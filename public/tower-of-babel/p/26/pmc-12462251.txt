RNF168 dephosphorylation ameliorates cognitive decline in Aβ-based mouse models of Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Miao-Jin Ji, Yun Li, Jiao Yang, Kangjunjie Wang, Shuning Sang, Hong Yang, Chenhao Tian, Xin Tang, Ji-Heng Cai, Tianhan He, Cheng Zhang, Huanyao Tang, Tiantao Cui, Xinran Meng, Xiang Cao, Jiaqi Zhu, Jie Wang, Jun-Li Cao, Daming Gao, Chao Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder among the elderly, with limited effective treatments available in clinical practice. Impaired glucose metabolism has long been observed in the brains of AD patients, yet the mechanisms linking metabolic signals to AD pathogenesis remain elusive. Our previous study demonstrated that growth signals regulate genomic stability through RNF168 phosphorylation. Here, we report that phosphorylation of RNF168 at Ser60 is significantly elevated in the hippocampi of Aβ-based mouse models of AD. Genetic dephosphorylation of RNF168 S60 enhances DNA damage response, reduces double-strand breaks (DSBs), and ameliorates learning and memory deficits in Aβ-based mouse models of AD. Mechanistically, RNF168 S60 phosphorylation impairs long-term potentiation (LTP) of mossy fiber-CA3 synapses in the hippocampus. Importantly, genetic dephosphorylation of RNF168 S60 rescues the deficits in Mossy fiber-CA3 synapse LTP, AD-related spine loss and Aβ pathology. Pharmacological inhibition of RNF168 phosphorylation by S6K1 inhibitor PF-4,708,671 alleviated learning and memory deficits. Furthermore, we demonstrated that the anti-hyperglycemia drug metformin improved learning and memory by inhibiting RNF168 phosphorylation. Our findings provide a novel therapeutic target for addressing synaptic dysfunction in Alzheimer’s disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40478-025-02115-8.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD), the most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder worldwide, is characterized by progressive cognitive decline, with synaptic dysfunction emerging as a pivotal early pathological hallmark [9, 12, 14]. While therapeutic strategies targeting Aβ and tau aggregation have largely faltered, accumulating evidence implicates synaptic failure—driven by Aβ toxicity, tauopathy, and epigenetic dysregulation—as a critical therapeutic target [33]. Intriguingly, activity-induced DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs), traditionally viewed as deleterious genomic lesions [16], have recently been implicated in regulating synaptic plasticity through transcriptional activation of neuronal early-response genes [21]. However, aberrant DSB accumulation induced by Aβ exacerbates synaptic impairment in AD, suggesting a dual role for DSBs in physiological and pathological contexts [34, 39, 40].

DSBs represent the most severe form of DNA damage, with oxidative stress, Aβ pathology, and neuronal hyperactivation being primary endogenous sources in mature neurons [39, 40]. Physiological DSBs, mediated by topoisomerase IIβ during long-term potentiation (LTP), are transiently repaired within 24 h [21, 39]. In Aβ-based AD models, however, Aβ disrupts this equilibrium by elevating baseline DSB levels and impairing repair kinetics, particularly in the dentate gyrus—a region crucial for memory encoding [39]. This DSB dyshomeostasis correlates strongly with synaptic failure, yet the molecular mechanisms linking DSB repair to AD progression remain elusive.

Central to DSB repair is the DNA damage response (DDR), where RNF168 orchestrates ubiquitin-dependent recruitment of repair proteins to maintain genomic stability [10]. Emerging evidence positions RNF168 as a nexus between metabolic signaling and DDR, exemplified by S6K1-mediated phosphorylation regulating its tumor-suppressive function [46]. The separate accumulation of the p53-binding protein 1 (53BP1) and the RAP80–BRCA1 complex leads to the recruitment of distinct functional factors, which subsequently initiate either the non-homologous end joining or homologous recombination repair pathway [24]. Notably, AD brains exhibit both aberrant DSB accumulation and impaired glucose metabolism [3, 5], raising critical unanswered questions: Does DDR dysfunction contribute to AD pathogenesis? How do metabolic signals modulate RNF168 activity in neurons? Could targeting RNF168 post-translational modifications rescue synaptic deficits?

Here, we identify RNF168 phosphorylation at Ser60 as a key mechanistic link between metabolic dysregulation and synaptic failure in Aβ-based AD models. Using two mouse models, we demonstrate that AD-associated hyperphosphorylation of RNF168 S60 impairs DDR efficacy, exacerbates DSB accumulation, and disrupts mossy fiber-CA3 synaptic plasticity. Genetic or pharmacological dephosphorylation of S60 rescues LTP deficits, dendritic spine loss, Aβ pathology and cognitive impairments. Furthermore, we delineate an AMPK/S6K1 (Ribosomal Protein S6 kinase beta-1)/RNF168 signaling axis connecting metabolic stress to genomic instability, and validate the therapeutic potential of S6K1 inhibitor PF-4,708,671 in restoring synaptic and cognitive function. Our findings establish RNF168 post-translational modification as a novel target for AD intervention, bridging metabolic dyshomeostasis, DDR dysfunction, and synaptic pathology.

Materials and methods

Mice

RNF168-SA/SE KI mice were generated with the CRISPR–Cas9 technique as previously described [46]. RNF168 Ser60 was substituted with alanine (SA) to mimic constitutive dephosphorylation, or glutamate (SE) to mimic phosphorylation. Genotyping was performed by Sanger sequencing of the S60 locus (Fig. S1). 5xFAD mice overexpress both mutant human amyloid beta (A4) precursor protein 695 (APP) with the Swedish (K670N, M671L), Florida (I716V), and London (V717I) Familial Alzheimer’s Disease (FAD) mutations and human presenilin 1 (PS1) harboring two FAD mutations, M146L and L286V. Expression of both transgenes is derived by neural-specific elements of the mouse Thy1 promoter [27]. All transgenic mice used in this study were backcrossed onto the C57BL/6J background. Six- to eight-week-old C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Gempharmatech Co., Ltd (Nanjing, China). Homozygous 5xFAD mice at 4-5-month-old were used - the early established onset window for concurrent Aβ plaque deposition, LTP deficits, and cognitive impairments in this model. This pathology initiation threshold was validated through our histopathological, electrophysiological and behavior assessments. As detailed in Fig. S1, male 5xFAD+/+ mice were crossed with female RNF168-SA+/+ or RNF168-SE+/+ mice to generate F1 5xFAD+/−; SA+/− or 5xFAD+/−; SE+/− progeny. Subsequent brother-sister matings of F1 heterozygotes produced homozygous 5xFAD+/+; SA+/+ or 5xFAD+/+; SE+/+ experimental cohorts. Genotypes were confirmed by PCR analysis or Sanger sequencing of tail snips. All mice were housed under a 12:12-h light–dark cycle at 22–26 °C in the center of experimental animals of Xuzhou Medical University, with free access to water and food. All experiments were carried out according to the guidelines of the Chinese Council on Animal Care and approved by the Xuzhou Medical University Animal Care Committee. Male mice were exclusively utilized for behavioral testing to eliminate confounds from estrous cycle variability. Mice were allocated into control and treatment group randomly by blind-catching from the same cage of littermates.

Aβ oligomer preparation and intrahippocampal injection

Aβ oligomer preparation followed an established protocol [7]. Briefly, one microgram of synthesized human Aβ1–42 peptide was dissolved in HFIP (400 µL, Sigma) and incubated at room temperature until clear solution forms. The Aβ1–42 was aliquoted into four tubes and opened in vacuum centrifuge overnight to evaporate HFIP and make the peptide pellet dry. The peptide was resuspended in DMSO to 5 mM and diluted by ACSF without glucose to yield a 0.1 mM stock solution, then incubated at 4 ℃ for 24 h. The solution was centrifuged at 14,000 g for 10 min at 4 ℃ and the supernatant was used. The concentration of Aβ was measured by BCA method. Oligomeric content (> 96% of species > 21 kDa) was verified by native PAGE using 4–20% gradient Tris-HEPES precast gels (Solarbio, #PG42010-N) followed by silver staining, as shown in Fig. S2.

After anesthetized with ketamine and xylazine (100 mg/kg and 10 mg/kg, i.p.), the mice were fixed in a stereo locator. A single dose of Aβ oligomer (0.1 mM, 2.5 µL each side) was stereotaxically microinjected into the bilateral dentate gyrus of hippocampus at the coordinates (anterior/posterior: -2.1 mm, medial/lateral: ±1.5 mm, dorsal/ventral: -1.8 mm). The vehicle or soluble Aβ1–42 oligomers were injected at 1 µL/min under the control of a microinjection pump and the needle was left in position for an additional 2 min. Throughout the surgery, core body temperature was monitored by a rectal thermistor probe and maintained at 36–38 °C by a heating pad. Three days post-surgery, mice underwent behavioral testing or electrophysiological recordings [18]. Following behavioral assessments, animals were euthanized for tissue collection.

Drug administration

Mice in the metformin-treated group received daily intraperitoneal injections of metformin (200 mg/kg; GLPBIO, GC60245) dissolved in sterile saline (0.9% w/v NaCl) for 14 consecutive days. On behavioral testing days, injections were administered 1 h prior to testing, consistent with established protocols [6, 44].

PF-4,708,671 (MedChemExpress, HY-15773) was dissolved in vehicle (17% DMSO, 10% Tween-80, 73% sterile saline) and administered intraperitoneally (50 mg/kg) daily for 7 days, with injections given 1 h prior to each behavioral session, according to similar studies [2, 23, 37].

Antibodies, peptides and kits

Antibodies, peptides and drugs used in this study are listed in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. The CometAssay® kit (Catalog # 4250-050-ES) was purchased from Trevigen, and the Rapid GolgiStainTM Kit (PK401) was from FD NeuroTechnologies.

Neutral comet assays

The DG region was isolated from the mouse hippocampus and digested into single cells with digestion medium (1.5 mM CaCl2, 0.2 mg/mL L-cystein, 0.5 mM EDTA and 15 units/ml papain) for 30 min at 37℃ in a water bath. Cells (1 × 105 cells /mL) were mixed with LMAgarose at a ratio of 1/10 (v/v) and immediately pipetted onto two well CometSlides™ (Trevigen’s CometAssay® kit, Catalog # 4250-050-ES). For cell lysis, the slides were immersed in 4 °C lysis solution for 1 h. Next, the slides were subjected to electrophoresis at 21 V for 40 min and stained with diluted SYBR® Gold For 30 min before immunofluorescence microscopy. Tail moment was analyzed with the CometScore software.

RNF168 immunoprecipitation (IP)

The lysates (containing 2 mg protein) were incubated with RNF168 or Flag antibody (1 µL) at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with protein A/G plus sepharose for 1 h at 4 °C. Then the sepharose was washed with lysis buffer for three times and mixed with 1 x protein loading buffer for further analysis.

Acid extraction of histones

Hippocampal DG cells were dissected, minced, and digested in 1 mL of digestion medium (1.5 mM CaCl2, 0.2 mg/mL L-cysteine, 0.5 mM EDTA, 20 U/mL DNase I, 15 U/mL papain) at 37 °C for 30 min with gentle agitation. Digestion was stopped by adding DMEM with 10% FBS.

Tissues were washed twice with HBSS, triturated using a 1 mL pipette tip (5×), and filtered through a 40 μm strainer. Cells were pelleted (300 × g, 10 min, 4 °C), washed with PBS, and resuspended in hypotonic buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 100 mM NaCl, 0.5% NP-40, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, protease inhibitors, 10 µM NEM). After rotation at 4 °C for 15 min, nuclei were pelleted (12,000 g, 10 min, 4 °C), washed, and resuspended in 0.2 M HCl for histone extraction (4 °C, 12 h).

Histones in the supernatant were precipitated with 33% TCA (2 h, on ice), washed twice with acetone, air-dried, and resuspended in hypotonic buffer for Western blot analysis.

Western blot

Tissues were homogenized in a lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 30 mM HEPES, 10 mM NaF, 1% v/v Triton X-100, 0.01% w/v SDS, and complete protease inhibitor mixtures, pH7.5), followed by pulse sonication for 30s. After centrifugation (12,000 g, 4 °C, 10 min), the supernatants were collected and the protein concentration was measured by BCA methed. Sample was mixed with 4 × protein loading buffer and incubated at 50℃ for 15 min. Equal amounts of protein were separated on 10% SDS polyacrylamide gel, and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. After 1 h in blocking solution (5% nonfat milk in TBST), membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody. After TBST washing, either anti-mouse IgG or anti-rabbit IgG peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied at 1:10,000, and blots were visualized with ECL detection kits. The WB images were acquired by Alliance Atom Auto software and analyzed by ImageJ Fiji.

Preparation of brain slices

After 40 min of acclimatization of the mice, the mouse was anesthetized by CO2, and the brain was quickly removed and soaked in chilled, oxygenated (95% O2, 5% CO2) slicing artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF, containing 80 mM NaCl, 3.5 mM KCl, 25 mM NaHCO3, 4.5 mM MgSO4, 0.5 mM CaCl2, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, 10 mM glucose, 90 mM sucrose). The trimmed brain was cut into 300-µm-thick brain slices with a vibratome (Leica, VT1200S), then the brain slices were transferred into the 32 °C oxygenated ACSF (95% O2, 5% CO2, ACSF; containing 125 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM KCl, 25 mM NaHCO3, 1 mM MgCl2, 2 mM CaCl2, 1.5 mM NaH2PO4, 10 mM glucose) to recover for 40 min. Incubate the brain slices at room temperature for 30 min before the experiment. The brain slices are then transferred to a recording chamber mounted on an Olympus BX51WI microscope and perfused with room temperature oxygenated ACSF.

Field excitatory post-synaptic potential (fEPSP) recording

Schaffer Collateral branch -CA1 pathway: The bipolar concentric electrode was placed on the distal side of the Schaffer Collateral branch (depth 10–100 μm) to evoke fEPSPs, and the 2 ~ 3MΩ borosilicate glass recording electrode filled with ACSF was placed in the radiation layer of the hippocampus CA1. Mossy fiber-CA3 pathway: the bipolar concentric electrode was placed near the junction of the granule cell layer and hilus near the midpoint of the suprapyramidal blade of the dentate [26]. For recording fEPSPs, the glass recording electrode was placed in the stratum lucidum of CA3 region. After evaluating basal synaptic transmission, was recorded the baseline for at least 30 min to reach a stable level with a stimulation frequency of 0.033 Hz, and the stimulation intensity was set to obtain a fEPSP slope of 30 ~ 50% of the maximum response. MF-CA3 fEPSPs were recorded in the presence of D-APV (25 µM) for 20 min before and during the delivery of strong tetanus stimulations (STET) for exclusion of the LTP produced by associational-commissural synapses on the CA3 pyramidal neurons. At the end of the recording, the mGluR2 agonist DCG-IV (1 µM) was perfused to confirm that fEPSPs were indeed evoked by mossy fiber synapses. LTP were induced by STET stimulation: 3 trains of 100 pluses stimuli, 100 Hz, 0.2 ms polarity; interval, 10 min [47]. Data were amplified by the Axon Instruments 700B amplifier and digitized with the Digidata1550B. Signals were sampled at 10 kHz and filtered at 1.2 kHz. Data were obtained using Clampex 10.6 and analyzed using Clampfit 10.7.

Golgi-Cox staining and sholl analysis

The FD Rapid Golgi Stain™ Kit (PK401) was employed according to the product manual. Briefly, the solution A and solution B in the kit were added to the light-protected glass bottle 24 h in advance. The next day, the brains were immersed in solution A + B and stored at room temperature for 2 weeks in the dark. The impregnation solution was replaced once after 24 h. Then, the brain was transfer into solution C and stored at room temperature in the dark for 3–7 days. The brain was cut into 120-µm-thick brain slices with a vibratome and was mounted on gelatin-coated slides. After sucking away the excess water, a small amount of solution C was added dropwise, and after 2 min, the slide was tilted to dry. The slide was rinsed three times in ddH2O, and stained for 10 min by freshly prepared staining solution consisting of one part solution D, one part solution E, and two parts ddH2O, and washed in ddH2O for 10 min. The sections were dehydrated and cleared in xylene and mounted with neutral gum. Images were obtained using an Olympus BX53 microscope with Olympus cellSens Standard software.

Dendritic arborization was quantified using ImageJ Fiji with the Sholl Analysis plugin. Concentric circles (10 μm radial increments) were centered on the soma, and dendritic intersections per radius were recorded. Apical dendritic spine density was determined by counting spines along 50–100 μm distal segments of CA3 pyramidal neurons (≥ 3 dendrites/neuron). Analyses were performed by an investigator blinded to experimental groups.

Behavioral assessments

The learning and memory behaviors were assessed with the Y-maze test, novel object recognition & novel position recognition test, and Morris water maze (MWM) test. Rotarod test was used to assess the motor function. The ANY-Maze or VisuTrack video tracking system was used to monitor and analyze behavior.

Y maze test

The Y-maze was consisting of three arms (30 × 6 × 15 cm), between each two arms at 120 degrees, with a movable divider each at the center. The mice were placed at the end of any arm of the Y-maze and allowed to explore freely for 10 min, the camera system recorded the behavioral changes of animals for 10 min, and record the following indicators: the total number of entries; the number of times that animal enters the maze arm; an alternation: successively enter all three arms of the Y-maze once. For repetitive entries, only first entries per arm within sequences were counted. Novel object recognition / novel position recognition test

Experimental mice acclimated ≥ 30 min in the testing room. During training (10 min), mice explored two identical objects. After a 2-h retention interval, mice were tested in either (a) Novel Object Recognition: One object replaced with novel item, or (b) Novel Position Recognition: One object moved diagonally. Exploration was video-recorded for 10 min using ANY-Maze™ software. Mice exhibiting > 10% preference (discrimination index) toward one object during training were excluded. Discrimination index = (Novel/Displaced exploration − Familiar exploration) / Total exploration time. Objects were cleaned with 70% ethanol between trials.

Morris water maze (MWM) test

The test assessed spatial reference memory. Mice underwent visible platform training (4 trials) to exclude motor deficits or swimming reluctance in a circular pool (diameter: 150 cm; height: 50 cm; water depth: 30 cm; maintained at 22 ± 1 °C). During acquisition (5 consecutive days, 4 trials/day), a hidden platform (1 cm below surface) remained fixed in one quadrant. Mice started from pseudorandomized cardinal positions (N, S, E, W) with 4 distal geometric cues on walls. Trials terminated when mice located the platform (remaining ≥ 5 s) or at 60 s (mice guided to platform). On day 6, a 60-s probe trial commenced with platform removed. Starting position was set to the quadrant farthest from the former platform location. ANY-Maze software tracked escape latency, target quadrant occupancy, platform crossings, and swim speed.

Rotarod test

The test was used to assess the exercise capacity of mice. Each mouse was placed in an individual compartment of the rotarod treadmill (ZH-600B, ZhengHua, China) and was required to keep walking to avoid falling off. Animals were first habituated for 60 s, and then the rod was constantly accelerated to 50 rpm in 309 s. The time taken for the mouse to fall off was recorded. In this test, three trials with a 10 min rest were carried out to avoid fatigue.

Statistics

Data are presented as mean ± SEM, and analyzed by Prism 8.0. The treatment of deviant values was according to Dixon’s “Q” parameter at the 95% confidence level [30]. The densities of the western blot bands were normalized to the internal loading controls. Student’s t-test was used in two group comparisons. For three or more group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for data from Gaussian distribution with the same standard deviation, and Kruskal-Wallis test was used for data from non-Gaussian distribution; followed by appropriate post-hoc tests. For all analyses, significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

DDR was impaired in the Aβ-based mouse models of AD

Consistent with previous reports of increased DSBs in AD brains [39], we observed elevated DNA damage in two Aβ-based AD models: 5xFAD transgenic mice (4 to 5-month-old homozygotes) and Aβ oligomer (AβO)-injected mice (2 to 3-month-old). Comet assay revealed prolonged tail moment in both models (Fig. S3A, Unpaired Student’s t test, t (168) = 8.64, p < 0.0001; Fig. S3B, Unpaired Student’s t test, t (100) = 5.56, p < 0.0001). Increased γH2A.X+ foci in neurons (Fig. S3C, D) and elevated γH2A.X levels – a sensitive marker of DSB formation – by immunoblot further confirmed DSB accumulation (Fig. S3E, Unpaired Student’s t test, t (4) = 3.45, p = 0.03. Fig. S3F: Unpaired Student’s t test, t (6) = 4.80, p = 0.03).

Given that RNF168 phosphorylation at Ser60 inhibits its E3 ligase activity and impairs DDR [46], we quantified pRNF168 levels. Both AD models exhibited increased pRNF168 (Fig. S3G: Unpaired Student’s t test, t (4) = 3.91, p = 0.02. Fig. S3I: Unpaired Student’s t test, t (4) = 3.30, p = 0.03). We previously demonstrated that S6K1 directly phosphorylates RNF168 at Ser60 [46]. Since S6K1 activation requires phosphorylation at Thr389 site, we examined pS6K1 level in DG tissues from 5x FAD and AβO-injected mice. Both Aβ-based AD models exhibited increased pS6K1 (Fig. S3H: Unpaired Student’s t test, t (4) = 5.40, p = 0.03. Fig. S3J: Unpaired Student’s t test, t (4) = 7.70, p = 0.02), with no change in total protein levels of S6K1 and RNF168 (Fig. S3G-J, the right panels). These results indicate DDR defects in AD mice, mediated by RNF168 hyperphosphorylation.

The phospho-deficient mutant of RNF168 enhances DDR and reduces DSBs in the DG of Aβ-based AD mouse models

To investigate the functional consequences of RNF168 phosphorylation in DDR, we compared phospho-mimetic (S60E; SE) and phospho-deficient (S60A; SA) mutants in an optogenetic activity-induced double-strand break (DSB) model [39]. Quantitative analysis of γH2A.X foci revealed comparable DNA damage loads between genotypes at 2 h post-blue light stimulation, confirming equivalent initial damage induction (Fig. S4). Strikingly, SA mutants exhibited accelerated damage repair, with much less residual γH2A.X signal compared to SE controls at 24 h post-induction (Fig. S4). This kinetic divergence suggests that phosphorylation at S60 serves as a regulatory switch, with the phospho-deficient mutant promoting more efficient DDR in the neurons of hippocampal DG.

We next examined this finding in Aβ-based AD mouse models. Ubiquitin blot analysis confirmed that RNF168-SA enhanced histone ubiquitination in the hippocampi of 5xFAD (Fig. 1A) and AβO-injected mice (Fig. 1D). Immunostaining for 53BP1 revealed increased nuclear foci in DG granule cells of RNF168-SA mice compared to wild-type and RNF168-SE mice (Fig. 1B & E), indicating enhanced NHEJ repair. Neutral comet assays further demonstrated that RNF168-SA significantly reduced DSBs in both Aβ-based AD models: In 5xFAD mice, tail moments were shortened in SA mutants (Fig. 1C: Kruskal-Wallis test, p < 0.0001). In AβO-injected mice, SA mutants exhibited similar DSB reduction (Fig. 1F: Kruskal-Wallis test, p < 0.0001). These results demonstrate that RNF168-SA enhances DDR and reduces DSBs in the DG of Aβ-based AD mice, highlighting the critical role of RNF168 phosphorylation in genomic stability.

RNF168 phospho-deficient mutant rescues learning and memory deficits in Aβ-based AD mice

To determine whether RNF168 phosphorylation state influences cognitive function, we assessed learning and memory behaviors in wild-type, RNF168-SE (phospho-mimetic), and RNF168-SA (phospho-deficient) mice. RNF168-SE mice exhibited impaired performance versus wild-type or RNF168-SA mice in novel position recognition test (NPRT; Fig. S5A, Kruskal-Wallis test, p < 0.0001), novel object recognition test (NORT; Fig. S5B, One-way ANOVA, F (2, 45) = 6.49, p = 0.003), and Y maze test (Fig. S5C: One-way ANOVA, F (2, 26) = 4.66, p = 0.019). No significant differences were observed in Y-maze total arm entries (Fig. S5C: One-way ANOVA, F (2, 26) = 1.21, p = 0.31) or rotarod performance (latency to fall: F (2, 32) = 0.58, p = 0.56; Fig. S5D), indicating absence of exploration or motor deficits.

In the Morris water maze, RNF168-SE mice showed prolonged escape latency during training versus wild-type or RNF168-SA mice (Fig. S5E, Repeated Two-way ANOVE, escape latency: column factor, p = 0.0004) and reduced time in the target zone during probing versus RNF168-SA mice (Fig. S5E, time spent in the target zone: One-way ANOVA, F (2, 24) = 3.73; p = 0.039). Platform crossings were also reduced in SE mice versus wild-type mice (platform crossing: Kruskal-Wallis test, p = 0.001). Swimming velocity was unaffected (Fig. S5E, swimming velocity: Kruskal-Wallis test, p = 0.632).

In 5xFAD and AβO-injected mice, RNF168-SA rescued cognitive deficits in NPRT (Fig. 2A: One-way ANOVA, F (3, 37) = 15.72, p < 0.0001; Fig. 2F: One-way ANOVA, F (3, 34) = 34.29, p < 0.0001), and NORT (Fig. 2B: One-way ANOVA, F (3, 38) = 11.23, p < 0.0001; Fig. 2G: One-way ANOVA, F (3, 33) = 12.34, p < 0.0001), while no significant difference was observed in rotarod test (Fig. 2D: One-way ANOVA, F (3, 43) = 0.81, p = 0.50; Fig. 2I: One-way ANOVA, F (3, 35) = 0.50; p = 0.684). In the Y-maze test, RNF168-SA mice exhibited a non-significant trend toward increased spontaneous alternation rates compared to wild-type controls. Conversely, RNF168-SE mice showed significantly reduced alternation versus both RNF168-SA and wild-type groups (Fig. 2C: One-way ANOVA, F (3, 29) = 14.86, p < 0.0001; Fig. 2H: One-way ANOVA, F (3, 38) = 6.23, p = 0.002). The total arm entries were significantly elevated in 5xFAD/SE mice relative to 5xFAD controls (Fig. 2C: One-way ANOVA, F (3, 29) = 4.54, p = 0.010), indicating enhanced exploratory activity in this genotype. The phospho-deficient mutant of RNF168 improves LTP, rescues spine loss, and alleviates Aβ pathology in the Aβ-based mouse models of AD

To investigate how RNF168 phosphorylation influences learning and memory, we assessed long-term potentiation (LTP) in hippocampal synapses (Fig. S6A, G). No differences in LTP (Fig. S6H, I: One-way ANOVA, F (2, 17) = 2.63, p = 0.10) or basal transmission (Fig. S6J-L: repeated two-way ANOVA, column factor, p = 0.08) were observed at Schaffer collateral-CA1 synapses (Fig. S6G) among wild-type, SE, or SA mice.

Discussion

Our study establishes RNF168 Ser60 phosphorylation as a critical molecular switch governing synaptic plasticity deficits in Aβ-based AD models through dual regulation of DNA damage response (DDR) and metabolic signaling. Three key findings emerge: (1) AD-associated hyperphosphorylation of RNF168 S60 disrupts DSB repair in hippocampal dentate gyrus (DG), impairing mossy fiber (MF)-CA3 synaptic plasticity; (2) Genetic or pharmacological dephosphorylation of S60 restores genomic stability, rescues LTP deficits, and reverses cognitive decline across distinct Aβ-based AD models; (3) The AMPK/S6K1/RNF168 axis constitutes a novel signaling nexus linking cerebral metabolic stress to synaptic failure. These discoveries advance our understanding of AD pathogenesis and propose actionable therapeutic targets (Fig. 6).

The complementary use of 5xFAD (chronic Aβ pathology) and intrahippocampal Aβ injection (acute, plaque-independent toxicity) [13] models provides crucial pathophysiological insights. Despite differing Aβ aggregation kinetics [11, 27], both models exhibited comparable RNF168 hyperphosphorylation and DSB accumulation (Fig. S3), suggesting that soluble Aβ oligomers—common to both paradigms—drive DDR dysfunction through post-translational modification rather than plaque-dependent mechanisms. This aligns with emerging evidence implicating soluble Aβ species as primary mediators of synaptic toxicity [33]. Notably, the rescue of cognitive deficits by S60 dephosphorylation in both models underscores the translational relevance of targeting RNF168 phosphorylation across AD subtypes.

The selective impairment of MF-CA3 LTP—but not CA3-CA1 LTP—by RNF168 hyperphosphorylation mirrors the anatomical progression of Aβ pathology. Three factors may explain this regional vulnerability: (a) Myelination Gradient: MF axons’ unmyelinated nature [26] renders them susceptible to Aβ-induced oxidative stress, contrasting with myelinated CA3-CA1 Schaffer collaterals protected until late disease stages [4]. (b) DSB Repair Heterogeneity: DG neurons exhibit heightened activity-dependent DSB generation [39] coupled with limited repair capacity, creating a “genomic stress hotspot” exacerbated by RNF168 dysfunction. (c) Metabolic Demand: The DG’s high neurogenesis rate and metabolic activity [48] may amplify AMPK/S6K1 signaling dysregulation, potentiating RNF168 phosphorylation. These factors collectively justify our focus on the DG as the primary investigative locus. While hippocampal circuitry elucidates spatial memory mechanisms, observed working memory improvements in SA mutants suggest broader cortical DDR involvement. Future studies mapping RNF168 phosphorylation dynamics in prefrontal-hippocampal networks could clarify system-level impacts.

Consistent with prior reports [39], we detected elevated DSB levels in both Aβ-based AD mouse models through neutral comet assays and γH2A.X staining (Fig. 1). This phenotype may originate from either: Enhanced DSB generation via endogenous stressors (Aβ oligomers, ROS, or physiologic neuronal activity [39], or impaired repair mechanisms, as suggested by our findings. While BRCA1-dependent homologous recombination (HR) – the predominant DSB repair pathway in proliferating cells – is known to be downregulated in J20 AD mice [38], the repair mechanisms in post-mitotic neurons remain unclear. Our work reveals two critical advances: (a) NHEJ dysfunction: Terminally differentiated neurons exhibit defective non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), evidenced by impaired 53BP1 foci formation (Fig. 1) and delayed γH2A.X repairment (Fig. S3, S4) in Aβ-based mouse models. (b) RNF168 regulation: Phosphorylation status (S60) of RNF168 governs NHEJ efficiency in mature neurons, as demonstrated by accelerated DSB repair in phospho-deficient mutants (S60A), and impaired repair kinetics in phospho-mimetic (S60E) mice (Fig. 1, Fig. S4). These findings establish RNF168 phosphorylation as a novel regulatory node for neuronal DSB repair, complementing known HR deficits in AD and providing a more complete mechanistic framework for genomic instability in neurodegeneration.

In this study, we observed decreased pAMPK levels in dentate gyrus (DG) tissue of AβO-injected mice, contrasting with reports of AMPK overactivation in certain AD models [20, 22, 36, 41, 50]. Our data demonstrate significantly reduced pAMPK specifically in Aβ-based AD models, aligning with emerging evidence of Aβ-induced AMPK inhibition in APP/PS1 mice [43] and in vitro systems (hippocampal cultures [32]; PC12 cells [35, 49]; BV2 cells [15]). Two key factors explain these discrepancies: (a) Model Specificity: AMPK overactivation reports derive primarily from APP/PS1 [36, 50], J20 [22], Tg19959 [50], or cortical neurons [22, 41], whereas our data from AβO-injected DG tissue (containing mixed cell populations) reflect distinct pathophysiological contexts. (b) Regional Heterogeneity: AMPK regulation exhibits subfield-specific patterns, with human AD studies localizing pAMPK predominantly around tau tangles in CA1 and temporal cortex [42], and murine models showing cortical overactivation [4]. The DG—characterized by unique metabolic demands, activity-dependent genomic fragility, and unmyelinated mossy fibers—forms a specialized microenvironment explaining its differential pAMPK response to Aβ pathology.

Previous studies have shown the accumulation of DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) in neurons and astrocytes in the hippocampi of AD patients and 5xFAD mice [40]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that DSBs induced by pharmacological agents or oxidative stress (including etoposide, camptothecin, corticotropin-releasing factor, and H2O2, among others) [8, 25, 45] significantly elevate Aβ generation and secretion both in vivo and in vitro. However, the underlying mechanisms remain elusive. The cell cycle checkpoint protein kinase 1 (Chk1)–cancerous inhibitor of PP2A (CIP2A) signaling pathway has been proposed as a key mechanism mediating DSB-induced Aβ overproduction. Activation of this pathway via DNA damage accumulation induces phosphorylation of APP at Thr668 in primary neurons, resulting in increased Aβ40 and Aβ42 levels [45], indicating that DSBs facilitate the APP cleavage process. Similarly, DSBs may also promote amyloidogenesis by enhancing beta-secretase (BACE1) expression [8, 31] and gamma-secretase activity [25]. Furthermore, electron microscopy and UV spectroscopy data have demonstrated that oxidative damaged DNA molecules can bind multiple Aβ peptides simultaneously, thereby facilitating their direct aggregation [1]. In this study, we observed a significant reduction in amyloid plaque burden in hippocampal and entorhinal cortex regions of 5xFAD/SA mice. Given the crucial role of RNF168 in DNA damage repair, RNF168 dephosphorylation significantly reduces damaged DNA accumulation, ultimately leading to decreased Aβ production and reduced plaque burden in mice. However, further studies will be needed to clarify the precise mechanism linking RNF168-mediated DNA repair to amyloidogenic processing.

This work extends our prior work [46], which established that S6K1 directly phosphorylates RNF168 at Ser60 under mTORC1 regulation. We previously demonstrated that rapamycin (an mTORC1 inhibitor) suppresses RNF168-S60 phosphorylation. In this study, we further show that disrupting the growth signaling-DNA damage repair axis via S6K1 inhibition (PF-4708671) or AMPK-mediated mTORC1 suppression (metformin) reduces RNF168-S60 phosphorylation in Aβ-based AD models. The AMPK/S6K1/RNF168 axis bridges two AD hallmarks: impaired glucose metabolism and genomic instability. Our data position metformin’s cognitive benefits [19] within a mechanistic framework: AMPK activation suppresses S6K1-mediated RNF168 phosphorylation (Fig. 5), enhancing DSB repair. This challenges the prevailing view of metformin’s neuroprotection as solely mitochondrial-dependent [17], proposing chromatin stabilization as a complementary pathway. The phosphorylation state of RNF168 is dynamically regulated by metabolic conditions. In wild-type mice, RNF168 remains predominantly dephosphorylated, supporting learning and memory functions akin to the SA mutant phenotype. Conversely, in Aβ-based AD mice, metabolic dysregulation induces RNF168 phosphorylation, resulting in cognitive deficits resembling the SE mutant.

PF-4,708,671’s efficacy—despite potential off-target effects [23]—validates S6K1 inhibition as a viable strategy. Notably, its blood-brain barrier permeability [29] and DDR enhancement create therapeutic advantages. However, three caveats warrant consideration: (a) Temporal Specificity: Chronic S6K1 inhibition may disrupt physiological protein synthesis; phase-controlled administration protocols should be explored. (b) Cell-Type Selectivity: Viral-mediated RNF168 modulation in specific neuronal populations could refine therapeutic precision. Nevertheless, PF-4,708,671 showed significant improvement in several behavior tests in Aβ-based AD mouse models, and this intervention suggested a potential translational treatment for AD.
