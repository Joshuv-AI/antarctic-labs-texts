Exploring the protective role of green tea extract against cardiovascular alterations induced by chronic REM sleep deprivation via modulation of inflammation and oxidative stress
Authors: Yonca Coluk, Emine Gulceri Gulec Peker, Sembol Yildirmak, Arif Keskin, Guven Yildirim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic Rapid eye movement (REM) sleep deprivation has been associated with various cardiovascular alterations, including disruptions in antioxidant defense mechanisms, lipid metabolism, and inflammatory responses. This study investigates the therapeutic potential of green tea extract (GTE) in mitigating these adverse effects.MethodsA total of 24 male Wistar albino rats were used in this study and divided into the control group (n = 8), Chronic-REM Sleep Deprivation (CRSD) Group (n = 8) and Chronic-REM SD + Green Tea 200 (CRSD + GTE200) Group (n = 8). After 21 days, a comprehensive analysis of paraoxonase (PON1), arylesterase (ARE), malondialdehyde (MDA), glutathione (GSH), nitric oxide (NOx), proinflammatory cytokines, and lipid profiles in aortic tissue, heart tissue, and serum was conducted in a sleep-deprived rat model.ResultsChronic REM sleep deprivation led to a significant reduction in PON1 and ARE levels in aortic (p = 0.046, p = 0.035 respectively) and heart tissues (p = 0.020, p = 0.019 respectively), indicative of compromised antioxidant defenses. MDA levels increased, and NOx levels decreased, suggesting oxidative stress and impaired vascular function. Lipid profile alterations, including increased triglycerides and total cholesterol, were observed in serum. Elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines (IL-6 and TNF-alpha) further indicated an inflammatory response (p = 0.007, p = 0.018 respectively). GTE administration demonstrated a protective role, restoring antioxidant enzyme levels, suppressing lipid peroxidation, and improving NOx levels.ConclusionThese findings suggest the therapeutic potential of GTE in alleviating the cardiovascular impairments of chronic REM sleep deprivation, emphasizing its candidacy for further clinical exploration as a natural intervention in sleep-related disorders and associated cardiovascular risks.Keywords: Arylesterase, Green tea extract, Paraoxonase, REM sleep deprivation

Introduction

Sleep is a fundamental physiological process essential for overall health and well-being. Long-term sleep loss is a growing issue in contemporary society and is associated with a variety of illnesses, including metabolic and cardiovascular disorders as well as psychological, neurological, and neurodegenerative diseases [1].

Sleep deprivation, as defined by the World Health Organization (WHO), includes acute sleep restriction and full sleep deprivation (total lack of sleep for more than 24 h). In addition, partial sleep deprivation (PSD) is defined as receiving four hours of sleep or less. Both chronic and acute sleep deprivation have complex, adverse impacts and are classified as sleep disorders. The physiological effects of sleep deprivation may involve modified regulation of the autonomic nervous system, increased oxidative stress, modified inflammatory and coagulation responses, and hastened progression of atherosclerosis [2]. These mechanisms link metabolic disorders and cardiovascular diseases with sleep deprivation. Recent epidemiological research has demonstrated a clear association between sleep deprivation (SD) and a higher incidence of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) such as obesity, diabetes, hypertension, arrhythmia, and coronary artery disease. Individuals sleeping fewer than six hours per day have an increased risk of developing coronary heart disease (CHD) when compared to those sleeping between six and nine hours daily [3].

Oxidative stress, characterized by an imbalance between the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and antioxidant defenses [4]. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) consist of oxygen-derived metabolites including hydroxyl radical, superoxide anion, hydrogen peroxide, and singlet oxygen. The primary intracellular sources of ROS include the mitochondrial electron transport chain and the NADPH oxidase complex [5]. Moreover, ROS can be produced by external factors such as radiation, metal ions, and phorbol esters [6]. Oxidative stress is implicated in the pathogenesis of many diseases, including cancer, through its involvement in fundamental biological processes such as tumorigenesis, apoptosis, and mutagenesis [7].

Natural dietary components with potential antioxidant activity and other health benefits have attracted attention in several chronic diseases due to their affordable cost, easy administration, and lack of adverse effects. The leaves of Camellia sinensis, a member of the Theaceae family, are utilized in the preparation of the well-known beverages, green, black, and oolong teas. Green tea has garnered global interest due to its natural properties, lack of toxicity, and potential for both preventive and therapeutic benefits in various medical conditions [8]. This makes green tea an intriguing subject for further study and exploration in the field of medicine. Green tea polyphenols exhibit both pro-oxidant and antioxidant characteristics, potentially influencing oxidative stress regulation under optimized conditions [9].

The most abundant polyphenols in tea are catechins, which are also referred to as flavanols. Epicatechin, epigallocatechin, epicatechin-3-gallate, and epigallocatechin 3-gallate (EGCG) are the four principal catechins found in tea. EGCG, which has the highest concentration, is primarily responsible for the health advantages associated with green tea consumption [10]. Various in vitro, animal, and human studies have shown that green tea polyphenols possess significant anti-inflammatory, anti-carcinogenic, anti-mutagenic, and antioxidant properties [11–14]. The primary polyphenol found in green tea, EGCG, demonstrates a robust history of functioning as an antioxidant in various experimental models. It exerts antioxidant capabilities over both the short and long term via multiple pathways [8]. It has shown that EGCG effectively neutralizes a broad spectrum of reactive oxygen species (ROS), including superoxide, hydrogen peroxide, hydroxyl radicals, peroxyl radicals, singlet oxygen, and peroxynitrite [15]. Brückner et al. demonstrated EGCG’s antioxidant effects in a dextran sodium sulfate-induced colitis model. EGCG ameliorated colitis symptoms, decreased malondialdehyde levels, and enhanced superoxide dismutase and glutathione peroxidase activities [16]. Liu et al. reported that Green tea polyphenols displayed a pH-dependent shift from antioxidant to pro-oxidant behavior. In acidic environments (pH 5.5–6.5), they acted as antioxidants, eliminating H2O2. Conversely, neutral to alkaline conditions (pH 7.0–8.0) promoted their pro-oxidant activity, resulting in P. aeruginosa growth inhibition. Also they performed gene expression analysis using quantitative real-time PCR. Results showed that exposure to green tea polyphenols for 10–60 min upregulated the mRNA levels of catalase, manganese superoxide dismutase, organic hydroperoxide resistance protein, SOS regulon repressor, and DNA repair protein genes, suggesting an induced oxidative stress response [17]. Green tea polyphenols have been shown to mitigate oxidative stress-induced cytotoxicity in a range of cell types, including human microvascular endothelium, osteoblasts, and bladder cells [9, 18–20].

Emerging evidence from experimental models suggests that green tea polyphenols possess the remarkable ability to prevent the development of cardiac hypertrophy. This protective effect appears to be mediated through a multifaceted approach, encompassing both reactive oxygen species (ROS)-dependent and -independent mechanisms. Notably, green tea polyphenols seem to exert their influence by inhibiting crucial intracellular signaling transduction pathways [21, 22].

A study investigating the effects of green tea on doxorubicin-induced cardiovascular abnormalities in rats found that doxorubicin treatment increased free radical production and lipid peroxidation. This was accompanied by elevated blood pressure and electrocardiogram (ECG) changes indicative of myocardial damage. However, green tea administration in these rats normalized blood pressure, reversed ECG changes, and increased glutathione (GSH) levels while decreasing lipid peroxidation, suggesting a reduction in oxidative stress [14].

There is existing research exploring the impact of green tea on cognitive tasks affected by sleep disorders [23, 24]. A study examining the effects of green tea on sleep deprivation-induced cognitive deficits in rats concluded that green tea could prevent memory impairment caused by 6 h of sleep deprivation, possibly through its influence on the antioxidant thiol defense system [24]. Nevertheless, as far as our knowledge, there has been no study examining the impact of green tea on the cardiovascular effects arising from sleep deprivation. In light of the aforementioned studies, the present study set out to examine the potential benefits of green tea extract (GTE) in relation to the cardiovascular deficits, inflammatory response, lipid profile, and oxidative stress that occur as a result of chronic REM sleep deprivation in rats. This work focused on these effects in the heart, aorta, and serum.

Materials and methods

Animals and experimental design

The study protocol was approved by Giresun University Animal Research Local Ethics Committee (18.12.2018/13). This study was performed in accordance with the principles of The Declaration of Helsinki.

We used 24 healthy male Wistar albino rats weighing 200–250 g in our study. These rats were obtained from Saki Yenilli Experimental Animals Inc. Ankara, Turkey.” Animals were housed under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle (lights on at 7.00 AM and lights off at 7.00 PM) in a temperature-controlled (24°C) room with ad libitum access to food and water. Following an acclimatization period of 1 week, all animals were placed in the tanks corresponding to their groups for half an hour a day for 5 days to reduce experimental stress. The rats were randomly allocated into three groups:

Control Group (n = 8): Rats received 4 mL of normal saline orally via gavage for three weeks.

Control Group (n = 8): Rats received 4 mL of normal saline orally via gavage for three weeks.

Chronic-REM Sleep Deprivation (CRSD) Group (n = 8): Rats experienced six hours of REM sleep deprivation utilizing the platform technique over 21 consecutive days(n = 8).

Chronic-REM Sleep Deprivation (CRSD) Group (n = 8): Rats experienced six hours of REM sleep deprivation utilizing the platform technique over 21 consecutive days(n = 8).

Chronic-REM SD + Green Tea 200 (CRSD + GTE200) Group (n = 8): Rats in this group underwent the same CRSD protocol as the Chronic-REM Sleep Deprivation group and received 200 mg/kg of green tea extract orally via gavage daily for 21 days [25]. The green tea extract utilized in this study is a commercially available product (Polyphenon 60 from green tea, Sigma-Aldrich).

Chronic-REM SD + Green Tea 200 (CRSD + GTE200) Group (n = 8): Rats in this group underwent the same CRSD protocol as the Chronic-REM Sleep Deprivation group and received 200 mg/kg of green tea extract orally via gavage daily for 21 days [25]. The green tea extract utilized in this study is a commercially available product (Polyphenon 60 from green tea, Sigma-Aldrich).

A multiplatform stainless steel water tank (measuring 145 × 44 × 45 cm) was constructed to induce chronic REM sleep deprivation. The tank comprises 14 platforms, each spaced 10 cm apart, measuring 16 cm in height and 6.5 cm in diameter. Animals were placed on the platforms, and when the water depth reached 15 cm, the rats were prevented from entering REM sleep by coming into contact with the water while they relaxed. For the control group rats, we prepared a second tank assembly of equal dimensions but without platforms. A stainless-steel grid was placed 15 cm above the bottom of the tank, completely covering the water surface and ensuring that the rats did not come into contact with the water while sleeping. A stainless-steel grid was placed 15 cm above the bottom of the tank, completely covering the water surface and ensuring that the rats did not come into contact with the water while sleeping [26].

On the initial day of the trial, all the animals underwent placement within the experimental setups at 10:00 AM and were left there for a duration of 6 h. At precisely 4:00 PM, they were taken back to their resting cages. To keep the rats above water for the same period, control group rats were positioned in tanks with wire mesh that had been prepared for them. The trial continued similarly for 21 days. After 21 days, general anesthesia was induced in all animals via intravenous injection of Xylazine hydrochloride (Rompun®-BAYER; 7.5–15 mg/kg) and Ketamine hydrochloride (Ketalar®, Pfizer, NY, NY; 40–60 mg/kg). Subsequently, intracardiac blood samples were obtained. To facilitate rapid tissue preservation, decapitation was performed, followed by immediate extraction of the heart and aortic tissues. The excised tissues were then flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen for storage at -80 °C alongside the collected blood samples until further biochemical analysis.

Determination of biochemical parameters

The malondialdehyde (MDA), reduced glutathione (GSH), and nitric oxide (NOx) levels present in the serum, aortic, and heart tissues were quantified following previously established methods. In brief, to measure MDA levels, samples were reacted with thiobarbituric acid and the resulting pink substance was detected using a spectrophotometer [27]. The GSH content in the samples was measured using the modified Ellman method [28]. In this test, DTNB (2,20-dinitro-5,50-dithiodibenzoic acid) reacts with the thiol groups and yields a yellow complex with a peak absorbance at 412 nm. To measure NOx, nitrate determination was carried out by means of vanadium chloride (VaCI3), and nitrite determination was accomplished using the Griess method [29]. Paraoxonase (PON1) activity was assessed utilizing paraoxon as a substrate and quantified by increased absorbance at 412 nm resulting from the creation of 4-nitrophenol. Arylesterase (ARE) activity was measured using phenylacetate as a substrate. The reaction mixture contained 2.0 mM phenylacetate and 2.0 mM CaCl2 in 100 mM Tris-HCl buffer was used to measure the initial rates of hydrolysis. Initial hydrolysis rates were determined by monitoring the increase in phenol concentration at 270 nm. Spectrophotometric analyses were performed using Shimazdu UV1280, UV-VIS. Serum levels of triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), Low-Density Lipoproteins (LDL), and High-Density Lipoproteins (HDL) were measured through the use of an automated biochemical analyzer (Olympus AU2700). The levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in the serum were assessed using commercial ELISA kits from Proteintech, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Prism software (v.7, GraphPad, USA). The results were presented as mean ± SD. One-way ANOVA was used to assess the statistical significance of the results at the p < 0.05 level, followed by post hoc Tukey multiple comparison test.

Discussion

We aimed to investigate the potential protective effects of green tea extract on cardiovascular alterations induced by chronic REM sleep deprivation in rats. Building upon previous research highlighting green tea’s benefits in cardiovascular health, we focused on examining changes in PON1 and ARE enzyme activities, oxidative stress, lipid profiles, and inflammatory markers within the aorta, heart, and serum. To our knowledge, this study is the first to evaluate the impact of GTE on cardiovascular outcomes in sleep-deprived rats. Our findings revealed that six hours of CRSD significantly decreased PON1 and ARE enzyme activities in the heart, aorta, and serum. However, the administration of 200 mg/kg GTE effectively restored these enzyme levels. Furthermore, chronic REM sleep deprivation increased lipid peroxidation and reduced nitric oxide levels while diminishing the antioxidant response in both the heart and aorta. Notably, GTE treatment attenuated these detrimental effects.

On the other hand, we found that REM SD showed negative effects on serum lipid profile and serum inflammatory cytokines. However, green tea extract administration ameliorated these parameters, approaching values observed in healthy controls. These results suggests that REM SD may increase the risk of cardiovascular disease and inflammatory response, but green tea consumption may have a protective effect against these effects. The mechanisms by which sleep deprivation contributes to cardiovascular disease are intricate and involve multiple factors, including inflammation, lipid metabolism, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction [30].

Research on sleep deprivation are limited and yields inconsistent findings, particularly regarding the effects of chronic sleep deprivation. Reports suggest that a week of chronic sleep deprivation, involving less than four hours of sleep per night, could alter endothelium-dependent vasodilation independent of its impact on the autonomic nervous system. This vasodilation has been shown to have an association with inflammation [31]. Cardiovascular diseases linked to sleep deprivation are reported to be associated with endothelial dysfunction in the literature [32]. A rat model of sleep deprivation confirmed the impact of sleep deprivation on nitric oxide production [33]. Moreover, sleep disturbances can trigger increased sympathetic activation, leading to oxidative stress and vascular damage [34].

The relationship between immune dysfunction and sleep deprivation is reciprocal, with sleep loss serving as a trigger for inflammation. A cross-sectional study demonstrated that SD increased the production of the inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α [35]. Furthermore, animal studies have demonstrated significant increases in pro-inflammatory cytokine levels and markers among sleep-deprived rats [36–38]. The complementary C3a-C3aR and C5a-C5aR pathways activate inflammation during acute SD. There is an increase in phagocytes such as monocytes and neutrophils in the peripheral circulation and an increase in IL-6 and C-reactive protein levels [39]. In the present study, we found that elevated levels of IL-6 and TNF-alpha in serum following REM sleep deprivation indicating an inflammatory response associated with sleep disturbances. GTE administration effectively reduced these cytokine levels due to its anti-inflammatory properties. Studies have demonstrated that green tea and its catechins (GTCs) influence the expression of inflammation-related genes and proteins, including TNF-alpha, IL-1, and MMPs [40–42].

Green tea contains high levels of catechins (8–20%), caffeine (2–4%), and amino acids (1–8%). Among the catechins, epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) is the most prevalent, followed by epigallocatechin (EGC). Theanine (L-theanine, N-ethyl-L-glutamine) is the primary amino acid found in green tea, although other amino acids like arginine (Arg) and glutamic acid (Glu) are also present [43]. Theanine plays a crucial role in modulating GABA synthesis from glutamate. While glutamate serves as the primary excitatory neurotransmitter in the brain, GABA functions as the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter. Maintaining a delicate equilibrium between these neurotransmitters is essential [44, 45].Caffeine and EGCG counteract the anti-stress effects of theanine, whereas EGC and arginine preserve these benefits. These results suggest that the synergistic interplay between theanine, caffeine, catechins, and arginine is pivotal in determining the overall anti-stress properties of green tea [46]. Unno et al. [47] showed that low caffeine grean tea ingestion demonstrated a significant positive impact on sleep quality, as well as a notable reduction in subjective fatigue levels.

Caffeine sensitivity varies widely among individuals due to genetic, metabolic, and behavioral factors. There are large interindividual variations in caffeine pharmacokinetics. The elimination half-life of caffeine ranges from 2.7 to 9.9 h [48]. Chronotype, representing a person’s natural inclination toward specific sleep and wake patterns, is another determinant of sleep quality [49]. Kerpershoek et al. [48] reported that increased caffeine intake was associated with decreased subjective sleep quality in students who did not consume caffeine in the evening. Nevertheless, caffeine consumption did not influence total sleep time or sleep initiation. Given the complex nature of green tea’s composition, its overall effects are influenced by individual variations and the balance of its constituents.

Paraoxonase and arylesterase are esterase enzymes encoded by the same gene and sharing similar active sites. While PON1 exhibits genetic polymorphisms, ARE does not. Despite differences in their natural substrates, PON1 demonstrates the capacity to hydrolyze phenyl acetate, a substrate inherent to ARE [50]. Furthermore, both PON1 and ARE possess the ability to hydrolyze organophosphates, aryl, and alkyl halides. Functionally, PON1 acts as an antioxidant by protecting LDL from oxidation and neutralizing other radicals, including hydrogen peroxide. The inherent protein structure denoted by ARE remains unaffected by alterations in PON1 [51]. PON1 activity decreases in oxidative stress conditions [52]. The decrease in PON1 serum activity by oxidative stress is mostly attributed to the changes in the redox status of the protein-free sulfhydryl group, which prevents the inhibition of PON1 activity caused by reactive oxygen derivatives [53].

Our findings of decreased PON1 and ARE enzyme levels in aortic and cardiac tissues following chronic REM sleep deprivation highlight the detrimental effects of sleep disturbances on antioxidant defense mechanisms. The diminished activity of these enzymes, essential for detoxification and preventing lipid peroxidation, indicates an elevated susceptibility to oxidative stress. Subsequent GTE administration effectively restored PON1 and ARE levels, underscoring GTE’s potential antioxidative properties in attenuating the adverse effects of sleep deprivation. Additionally, the significant increase in MDA levels in aortic tissue following sleep deprivation, indicative of elevated lipid peroxidation, corroborates previous findings on the oxidative stress associated with insufficient sleep. The concomitant reduction in MDA levels following GTE administration suggests a protective role in inhibiting lipid peroxidation, possibly through the modulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and free radicals. Nawi et al. [54] reported a significant increase in MDA levels within the aortic tissue of REM sleep-deprived rats. MDA, the end product of polyunsaturated fatty acid peroxidation, is widely accepted as a biomarker for lipid peroxidation. Kumar et al. [55] reported decreased PON1 arylesterase activity and increased LDL susceptibility to oxidation in D-galactose-induced aged rats. Despite the absence of significant changes in GSH levels during sleep deprivation, their notable elevation following GTE treatment suggests a direct impact of GTE on endogenous antioxidant systems. Our findings demonstrate that green tea extract can safeguard animals from the detrimental effects of sleep deprivation CRSD on cardiovascular oxidative status. As a potent antioxidant, green tea effectively inhibits free radical propagation and oxidative stress.

Within the antioxidant cell signaling pathway, NF-E2-related factor 2 (Nrf2), a pivotal regulator of antioxidant enzymes and detoxification genes, has been demonstrated to interact with Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1). his interaction is crucial for attenuating oxidative stress and enhancing overall antioxidant capacity. In vitro experiments involving the stimulation of epithelial MCF10A cells with EGCG exhibited a significant increase in the activity of antioxidative enzymes. Moreover, this stimulation facilitated the release of Nrf2, promoting its nuclear translocation [56]. These molecular processes collectively enhance antioxidative capacity, ultimately safeguarding cellular survival under oxidative stress conditions. Activation of the Nrf2-Keap1 signaling pathway plays a major role in the antioxidant effect of green tea polyphenols [57].

Impaired nitric oxide (NO)-mediated vasodilation is a contributing factor to atherosclerotic vascular disease and acute cardiovascular events. Several prospective studies have shown that reduced sleep duration at night is associated with dysfunction in endothelium-dependent vasodilation [58–60]. This may be attributed to decreased nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability, leading to impaired vasodilation. Jiang et al. reported that aortic NO and cyclic guanosine phosphate (cGMP) concentrations, as well as endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) phosphorylation levels decreased in rats subjected to five days of REM sleep deprivation [61]. Consistent with these findings, our results indicate that a decrease in NOx levels within aortic tissue following chronic REM sleep deprivation may contribute to impaired vascular function and increased cardiovascular disease risk. GTE administration significantly elevated NOx levels, suggesting a potential role in improving endothelial function. The dual impact of GTE on both antioxidant defense and NOx levels suggests a multifaceted protective mechanism against sleep deprivation-induced vascular alterations. Lipid peroxidation caused by free radicals results in endothelial damage [62, 63]. Furthermore, lectin-like oxidized low-density lipoprotein receptor-1 can induce endothelial cell dysfunction by inhibiting the expression of endothelial NOS. However, pretreatment with EGCG has been shown to decrease endothelial dysfunction by stimulating NO production through eNOS [64].

The results of this study showed that in heart tissue, the reduction in PON1 and ARE levels associated with sleep disorders highlights the systemic nature of oxidative stress and the potential involvement of cardiac tissue in this cascade. GTE intervention effectively restored these enzyme levels, underscoring its capacity to mitigate cardiac oxidative stress. Additionally, GTE exhibited protective effects against lipid peroxidation and NOx reduction in heart tissue, emphasizing its comprehensive impact on multiple facets of cardiovascular health.

SD has a significant effect on lipid metabolism, which can lead to atherosclerosis. In 2011, Kong et al. conducted a cross-sectional study that demonstrated a significant reduction in TC and LDL-C levels among children with long sleep duration compared to those with short sleep duration [65]. In 2020, Xing et al. conducted animal experiments that showed how acute sleep deprivation for 72 h in rats and mice can lead to elevated serum cholesterol levels and hepatic cholesterol accumulation. The experiments also revealed that nuclear receptor subfamily 1 group D member 1-mediated inhibition of cholesterol 7α-hydroxylase is responsible for this effect. The incongruous expression levels of enzymes involved in fatty acid and cholesterol metabolism were caused by the interruption of the rhythm of the inositol-requiring enzyme-1α pathway in the endoplasmic reticulum. This ultimately led to impaired lipid metabolism [66]. Experimental data suggest that green tea consumption is associated with beneficial effects on lipid profile, primarily by lowering the intestinal absorption of lipids. Green tea catechins appear to be the most potent inhibitor of lipid absorption; it has been shown to form complexes with lipids and lipolytic enzymes, thereby interfering with the luminal process of emulsification, hydrolysis, micellar solubilization, and subsequent uptake of lipids [67]. The altered lipid profile observed in serum, characterized by increased triglyceride and total cholesterol levels during REM sleep deprivation, aligns with previous studies linking sleep disturbances to dyslipidemia. GTE administration demonstrated significant amelioration, particularly in triglyceride and total cholesterol levels. The reduction in LDL levels further signifies the potential cardiometabolic benefits of GTE in the context of sleep-related disorders.

The comprehensive assessment of physiological markers highlights the potential therapeutic value of GTE in mitigating the adverse effects of chronic REM sleep deprivation. The observed improvements in antioxidant defense, lipid metabolism, and inflammatory cytokine regulation underscore the need for further clinical trials to elucidate the feasibility and efficacy of GTE supplementation as an adjunctive therapeutic strategy for sleep-related disorders and associated cardiovascular risks.

In conclusion, our study provides compelling evidence supporting the protective effects of green tea extract against chronic REM sleep deprivation-induced cardiovascular impairments. The multifaceted impact on antioxidant defense, nitric oxide levels, lipid metabolism, and inflammatory cytokine regulation suggests a comprehensive therapeutic potential. As sleep-related disorders continue to pose significant public health challenges, the exploration of natural interventions, such as GTE, offers a promising avenue for preventive and therapeutic strategies.
