Autonomy-supportive and controlling language in undergraduate biology syllabi
Authors: Ania A. Majewska, Chioma Anyanwoke, Lia Sanchez, Areeb Khan, Robert Richards
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSyllabi are important sources of information for topics covered, how to contact the instructor, and grading and attendance policies. In this study, we used the framework of self-determination theory to better understand how language in the syllabi might reflect autonomy-supportive or controlled style, which has been shown to influence student motivation. In addition, we characterized positive and negative emotion word usage with a natural language processing tool, LIWC. We used qualitative thematic analysis to identify themes in attendance policies under autonomy-supportive or controlled styles, as posited by self-determination theory. Our findings suggested that syllabi tend to be negative in emotional tone, yet somewhat less so in recent years. Furthermore, emotional tone was lower in syllabi of upper-division courses compared to lower-division courses. Controlling language style was most common, with the theme of responsibility appearing in most attendance policies. Our findings provide insights into the undergraduate biology syllabi, and the underlying messages in the text that may impact student motivation. Based on these findings, we provide recommendations for crafting syllabus text that supports student self-directed motivation.

INTRODUCTION

Syllabi are the first documents students receive in a college course, providing important information about the course and the instructor. Syllabi communicate course details, including topics covered, schedule of events, how to contact the instructor, rules, procedures, and policies on grading and attendance (1–4). Beyond logistical details, syllabi communicate messages about the instructor-student relationship through language choices (5, 6). Researchers have argued that wording in syllabi alone conveys classroom climate (1, 6, 7), influences first impressions (8), and can impact students’ decisions to remain or to withdraw from the class (9). Others conceptualize syllabi as rhetorical documents establishing power dynamics (5) and as communication tools that set the tone for the learning environment (10). Syllabi are universal in college classrooms, which warrants further research on these documents, specifically the messages they convey to the students.

Experimental studies using manipulated syllabus text indicate that the language and tone are important. For example, students who read “supportive” statements in the syllabus report are more likely to seek support from the instructor (11). Also, students presented with syllabi worded with positive “motivational” phrasing anticipate the instructor to be more helpful and the course to be more useful compared to syllabi without the positive language (12). Similarly, “friendly” language leads to more positive impressions of the instructor and greater expected success (12–14). On the other hand, punitive or authoritative statements in the syllabus result in students feeling uncomfortable about approaching the instructor and thereby create barriers to student-instructor interactions (11, 15). Despite the recognition that syllabus language can make a difference, systematic analysis of authentic syllabus documents is largely lacking.

Self-determination theory

Self-determination theory (SDT) offers a framework for understanding why certain instructional approaches may matter for student motivation and engagement. According to SDT, students with self-determined motivation learn because they want to, while those with controlled motivation learn because they feel they have to or because of external pressures (16, 17). Research indicates that self-determined motivation is associated with better learning outcomes, help-seeking behaviors, greater enjoyment, and persistence (18–21). Conversely, controlled motivation is linked to negative outcomes, including dropout, procrastination, and anxiety (22–24). SDT proposes that experiencing self-determined motivation requires the fulfillment of three basic psychological needs: autonomy (feeling self-directed), competence (feeling effective), and relatedness (feeling connected to others) (17, 25). It is thought that instructors can promote self-determined motivation through the learning environment they create and interpersonal behaviors they display (26, 27).

Instructor’s autonomy-supportive and controlling behaviors differ markedly in their characteristics and impacts. Autonomy-supportive behaviors include providing choices to students, offering explanatory rationales for tasks and activities, acknowledging and accepting students’ perspectives and feelings, and using invitational language (28). Meta-analytic evidence demonstrates that autonomy-supportive teaching is associated with positive learning outcomes, and experimental interventions have documented that autonomy-supportive teacher training increases students’ motivation (29–31). In contrast, controlling behaviors include using pressuring or commanding language, demanding compliance without rationale, employing threats and punishments contingently, ignoring student perspectives, criticism, and displaying impatience (32). Controlling teaching behaviors predict need frustration (i.e., undermining students’ feelings of autonomy, competence, and relatedness), amotivation, and negative affect (31). Importantly, research indicates that high control is detrimental to student outcomes even when teachers are perceived as autonomy-supportive (33).

Much of prior SDT research examined teacher interaction patterns (verbal and behavioral), and less is known about written communication, such as syllabi, which can have sustained influence on students’ experiences. Studies on syllabi created for hypothetical courses indicate that autonomy-supportive language leads to students’ positive perceptions, feelings, and expectations for the course compared to controlling language (8, 34). However, it is unclear how real syllabi use autonomy-supportive or controlling language. Furthermore, research on biology course syllabi, where student motivation and persistence are critical concerns (35), is particularly important.

Here, we analyzed over 300 unique undergraduate biology syllabi from a large R1 research university to (i) quantify emotional tone values and examine whether language patterns vary by course level (lower versus upper-division) and by class size (number of enrolled students), as these factors may affect persistence; and (ii) identify themes in attendance policy language through qualitative coding guided by SDT’s distinction between controlling (e.g., consequences) and autonomy-supportive (e.g., flexibility) language style. Our work provides a characterization of the language used in biology syllabi, moving beyond experimental manipulations to reveal what instructors communicate in practice. Finally, we translate findings into recommendations for revising syllabus text to be more autonomy-supportive while maintaining necessary structure and clarity in undergraduate courses.

METHODS

Data set compilation

We acquired publicly available undergraduate biology syllabi from the University of Georgia’s course bulletin (https://bulletin.uga.edu/), a webpage that allows students to explore and compare majors and courses. For each document, we examined the course outline to confirm that the course focused on biology. Syllabi were categorized as lower and upper-division based on the course number, where 1000 and 2000 level courses were considered lower-division and 3000 and higher were considered upper-division. We also acquired a class size of each course in a given semester based on historical records of enrollment for every course offered. Based on the suffix of the course, we noted whether the syllabus was a lab course. We converted the documents to text files and reviewed each one to identify the presence of the grading policy and attendance policy. If present, we extracted the text of these sections and saved them in a separate text file for further analysis. Duplicate syllabi were identified based on text similarity. We developed a custom script in Python (version 3.11.2) to calculate the percentage of shared vocabulary between pairs of syllabi, and syllabi that exhibited 90% or greater overlap in vocabulary were flagged as potential duplicates. After a manual review to confirm duplicity, we removed duplicate syllabi.

A total of 356 biology course syllabi were obtained across 22 departments, representing required and elective courses as well as those for biology majors and non-majors. We did not determine required versus elective courses and majors versus non-majors status for each course, as this information was not readily available. We removed 50 duplicates based on the similarity of text, resulting in a total of 306 unique syllabi. Our final syllabus data set spanned the years 2006–2022.

Quantitative analysis

We used a validated tool for measuring verbal expression of emotion, the Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC; https://www.liwc.app/) (36, 37), which requires a purchase of a license and is intended for academic research in a university setting. LIWC examines language use by counting words in psychologically meaningful categories, including emotional processes (positive/negative emotion), which show internal consistency and have been validated against behavioral outcomes and personality measures (36–39). LIWC works by comparing each word in a text against an internal dictionary of words and word stems sorted into categories and requires a minimum of 50 words to provide a reliable result (36). LIWC’s internal dictionary is based on decades of scientific research on language and psychology (36). The tool calculates the percentage of words in each category, providing a quantitative value of language patterns. It is important to note that this tool does not emphasize plain language assessment, but instead, it focuses on the psychological and emotional dimensions of text rather than readability or clarity. LIWC’s emotional tone variable is a summary measure that combines positive and negative emotion word usage into a single score, provided on a scale of 0–100, where a low value (below 50) reflects more negative language and a high value (above 50) represents more positive language (40).

Qualitative coding

SDT provides a robust framework for analysis of syllabus text and interpretation of the language used by instructors. We performed qualitative coding on attendance policies to identify themes within controlling and autonomy-supportive language styles posited by SDT (17). Accordingly, controlling language uses commands, emphasizes external consequences and punishments, applies pressure through “must” and “should” statements, and overlooks students' perspectives (27, 41, 42). In contrast, autonomy-supportive language provides choice and flexibility, offers rationales for requirements, acknowledges student perspectives and feelings, and uses invitational rather than pressuring phrasing (42, 43). We focused on attendance policies because we assumed they are most likely to provide controlling and autonomy-supportive messages to students.

We followed the qualitative coding methodology described by J. Saldaña (44). Three coders independently reviewed a subset of 20 attendance policies to develop the initial coding framework. Through an iterative process of reading and comparison, we identified recurring patterns and developed initial codes. For an additional 10 attendance policies, the coders met regularly to discuss emerging themes, merge similar codes, and clarify definitions, resolving discrepancies through discussion until consensus was reached. This approach established a comprehensive framework with mutually exclusive, clearly defined codes within the two categories.

Once distinct codes were established, we created a codebook with descriptions and examples for each theme. The remainder of the attendance policies were divided among the three coders to code the text assigned to them. During annotation, we marked text with their apparent theme and organized the syllabus references into a dedicated column, paired with their unique syllabus numbers for identification. Given that coders worked collaboratively throughout the initial codebook development process, interrater reliability metrics were not calculated, as these were not appropriate for our methodology (45).

Statistical analysis

We used simple linear regression to test whether LIWC emotional tone values of the entire syllabus document, grading policy, and attendance policy were related to course division and class size. We used logistic regression to determine whether language style (controlling vs. autonomy-supporting) was related to class size, course division, or time (years). We used R programming for all statistical analysis (46).

RESULTS

Quantitative analysis

Syllabi included lower (n = 43) and upper (n = 263) division courses with enrollment ranging from 3 to 406 students (mean = 70.53). Grading and attendance policies were found in 200 out of the 306 syllabi and spanned 2009 to 2022. LIWC emotional values for the entire syllabus text ranged from 7.27 to 79.96 (mean = 36.19). The tone values for grading and attendance policies ranged from 1 to 99 (grading mean = 26.89, attendance mean = 37.07) and therefore were, on average, “negative.”

Controlling language

Responsibility

The most common theme in attendance policies consisted of text conveying student obligations, commands, expected actions, or responsibilities, for example: “Students are expected to attend and participate in all class sessions” or “If you miss class, you are responsible for the lecture material/discussion and any assigned work. Ask your classmates what you missed.” Politeness was expressed in several instances: “Please make every effort to attend all class meetings.” While most expected actions mentioned attendance, participation, or following up on missed content, few policies expressed expectations about attentiveness, positive affect, or engagement: “Your attendance, alertness, participation, and enthusiasm are expected.” In some syllabi, prohibitions were mentioned and capitalized text was used to emphasize the point: “DO NOT email me to inquire about what was covered that day...”. The use of exclamation points for emphasis, such as “Attend class!!!!!!", was found but was not common. One instructor used a particularly judgmental language that is noteworthy: “If you feel that you will not be attending class often, then it is probably a good idea to drop the class, as your grade will suffer accordingly. Additionally, this would make room for someone who is actually interested in taking the course.” The statement implies judgment about student motivation and the worthiness of the opportunity to take a course, all of which can negatively impact students.

Consequence

Consequence emerged as the second most common controlling theme in attendance policies. These policies tended to outline negative outcomes of what would happen to students based on their attendance choices. The negative outcomes described what students would lose or forfeit by not attending class, for example: “Poor attendance may negatively impact your participation grade.” Instructors also used conditional “if” statements, such as: “If an unannounced pop quiz is given on the day of your absence, your grade for it will be a 0.”

Rigidity

Another theme within controlling language was rigidity, where instructors described specific actions that are not permitted or are nonnegotiable. Instructors often used the word “only” to emphasize their inflexibility, for example: “Absences will only be excused in the event of a documented illness, or unforeseen and unavoidable circumstance,” or “Tardiness and leaving lab early without permission are not tolerated in lab.” Text addressing the inability for students to make up missed work also fell under this theme: “Weekly quizzes, in-class questions, and exams cannot be made up under any circumstances.” The instructor also signaled rigidity through direct, negative statements. They used phrases such as “Don’t ask me to make an exception for you, because I won’t.” Statements under this theme, such as “DO NOT email me to inquire about what was covered that day, I will not respond," tended to use capitalization and express negative sentiment.

Autonomy-supporting language

Flexibility

Flexibility formed a key autonomy-supporting theme in attendance policies. The text outlined conditions for completing assignments or exams missed due to an absence, for instance: “Missed assignments can be made up with permission of the TA (documentation of reason for absence required).” Some instructors enumerated the number of permitted absences: “However, as unfortunate circumstances do occur throughout the semester, three excused absences are allowed.”

Success

Success was a less common theme, including descriptions of how to do well or be successful in the course, such as “Regular class attendance is essential for success in this course” or “Attending lectures will greatly facilitate your success in this course—both by helping you grasp the ideas, and through participation points.” Although a description of what “success” meant for a given course was lacking, the messaging in the text insinuated strategies for achieving high grades. Statements within this theme used encouraging language, demonstrated care for students, and linked attendance with positive outcomes, for example: “You will be most successful in this class if you attend class in person, so please make every effort to attend.”

Sickness accommodation

Accommodation due to illness was not a common theme, where the text mentioned illness and provided alternative options for attendance, and makeup of missed work or exam, for instance: “if you are seriously ill (with written proof from doctors stating that you are too ill to attend the lab) or have a family emergency (with valid proof) or some circumstances beyond your control, you may be excused to miss labs.” Not surprisingly, this theme was linked to the COVID-19 pandemic: “In the event that you display COVID-like symptoms, we ask you to stay home, get tested, and email the class TA in advance of the class so arrangements can be made to make up any missed quizzes.”

Reward

We defined expressing an outcome of an action in a positive way as a reward, for instance: “Students who have less than three unexcused absences will earn 12 extra credit points toward their final grade.” The wording in this theme provided an incentive structure by presenting attendance as an opportunity to earn points. This was the least common theme encountered in the sample.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we share findings from an analysis of over 300 undergraduate biology syllabi from a large R1 institution. Notably, our results indicate that syllabi typically convey a negative emotional tone, as indicated by low emotional value scores and controlling language in attendance policies. Here, we first discuss the quantitative and the qualitative results through the lens of SDT, discuss implications for instructors, and finally address the limitations of our work.

We found a wide range of emotional tone values for attendance and grading policies; however, on average, the emotional tone tended toward negative values across syllabi. This finding is in agreement with prior work on tones conveyed in syllabi by pharmacy instructors, which suggests mostly “cold” tones (47), although we note differences in methodology in how tones were assessed. From a SDT perspective, negative emotional tone in syllabi may undermine students’ sense of relatedness, the need to feel connected to and cared for by their instructors (17). When course materials convey cold or negative tones, students may perceive their instructors as unsupportive or unapproachable, which could negatively impact motivation (31). Indeed, experimental and qualitative studies consistently find that “cold” or harsh syllabi lead to more negative views of the instructor, lower perceived support, and reduced willingness to seek help (12, 48, 49). However, we note that the negative emotional tone values we found here might be due to the formal and direct language used in these documents, which may not necessarily reflect instructors’ intentions to communicate a negative emotional tone.

Interestingly, emotional tone has increased slightly over time in syllabi, suggesting greater use of “warmer” language in recent years. Furthermore, we found that emotional tone in syllabi was lower for upper-division courses compared to lower-division courses. One possible explanation for this pattern might be related to how instructors perceive their audience and assume upper-division students are more experienced and already familiar with academic norms and expectations, leading to “colder” communication in the syllabus. At the same time, in lower-division courses, instructors might use more positive and welcoming language for the newcomers. However, research suggests that all students, regardless of experience level, benefit from autonomy-supportive communication (29). The assumption that advanced students require less warmth or support in course materials may inadvertently create an environment that does not support their needs, even for experienced learners. Further work is needed on faculty perceptions of first-year versus more senior students to enhance our understanding of the emotional values that appear in syllabi and whether emotional values reflect instructor assumptions about students.

Our thematic analysis of attendance policies revealed that controlling language is present in nearly all syllabi examined. Controlling language was evident in statements that used judgmental or harsh phrasing and sarcasm. We found statements that demanded compliance without providing a rationale, ignored potential legitimate student circumstances, and rejected student perspectives, characteristic of the controlling style. This may be partly explained by the fact that many instructors do not anticipate close reading of syllabus text by the students. Indeed, some work suggests that syllabus reading rates are generally low, with one case study finding that fewer than 40% of students read syllabi, and as many as 79% of lecturers in the same study believing that only a few students do so (50). Thus, the language instructors use may not receive scrutiny before the document is shared with students. Such statements nonetheless may reflect controlling teaching behaviors, which research shows can undermine student motivation, increase need frustration, and negatively impact engagement (30–32).

While evidence on the frequency of autonomy-supporting versus controlling language in higher education is remarkably limited, prior work suggests instructors generally report being autonomy-supportive (51). For instance, A. Huić et al. (52) found that university teachers in Croatia rated themselves as having more autonomy-supportive behaviors than controlling behaviors. However, instructor reports do not always align with student reports, suggesting that instructors may underreport or underrecognize their controlling style (53). More work is needed to understand this discrepancy. Instructors may employ controlling language in their written course materials while maintaining more autonomy-supportive language and practices in face-to-face interactions.

Our findings of predominantly controlling rather than autonomy-supportive indicate that instructors rely on this approach to written communication with students, potentially undermining the autonomy support they may provide in other ways. It is important to note that some of the controlling language was subtle (e.g., “you are responsible for the lecture material”) and at times accompanied by clearly controlling phrasing (e.g., “DO NOT email me”). While the former may aim to support student independence, when paired with controlling wording, the overall effect may be the opposite of this goal. Furthermore, such messaging may give the impression that the instructor is not friendly and unwilling to support student learning. More work is needed to better understand how certain wording choices in syllabi influence students and their perceptions of the instructor.

While controlling language dominated, many syllabi included autonomy-supportive language. Invitational language, acknowledging student perspectives and circumstances, and policies that built in flexibility for absences, as well as providing explanatory rationales for policies, reflected hallmarks of autonomy support (28, 42). Such statements tended to respect students’ personal lives while maintaining academic standards. Thus, autonomy support and academic rigor are not mutually exclusive; instructors can establish clear expectations and maintain standards while simultaneously supporting students' psychological needs.

We noted that syllabi varied considerably in what is considered an “excused” versus “unexcused” absence. What constitutes an excusable absence was inconsistent across instructors, with some stating that only extreme circumstances, such as the death of an immediate family member or a severe illness with medical documentation, would be excused. Other instructors provided more flexibility, allowing for excused absences due to mental health needs, personal emergencies, or missed classes, which were built into the curriculum with no penalty. This variation suggests that there is no standard policy for what is a legitimate reason for not attending class, which could lead to potential inequities in how students are accommodated in different situations.

Recommendations for instructors

Based on our findings and the framework of SDT, we offer several recommendations for instructors. First, we encourage instructors to examine their syllabi for language that may signal unapproachability or unwillingness to support student learning. We noted messages that may be demotivating for struggling students and that could discourage students from seeking help if they fall behind. Warm, invitational language that acknowledges student perspectives and demonstrates care can foster students’ sense of connection to the instructor. We suggest instructors choose wording that conveys a supportive environment that promotes help-seeking rather than language that creates barriers to student-instructor interaction.

To support students' need for autonomy, we recommend providing explanatory rationales for course policies to help students understand why some policies exist. Simply stating rules without explanation can feel controlling and undermine autonomy, whereas providing meaningful rationales helps students understand the purpose behind expectations (43). For example, rather than simply stating “Attendance is mandatory,” an autonomy-supportive approach would also provide an explanation: “Regular attendance is important because it allows you to engage with concepts in real-time and benefit from peer discussion.” Such autonomy-supportive statements are more aligned with teaching behaviors shown to enhance student motivation (28). Using encouraging language, expressing a growth mindset, demonstrating care for students, and linking attendance with positive outcomes while providing explanatory rationales are strategies to increase autonomy-supporting language.

We suggest rewording attendance policies to acknowledge legitimate reasons for absence and provide clear guidance on how students should proceed when unforeseen circumstances prevent them from participating in class. Acknowledging students’ perspectives and circumstances, a key autonomy-supportive behavior, signals respect for students as individuals who may face genuine challenges (32). What constitutes legitimate reasons should be agreed upon by all academic units in an institution, such that students have the same expectations regardless of the department of the instructor. Providing clear expectations, while flexibility within those guidelines, would support autonomy by recognizing that students may face diverse circumstances.

Finally, we encourage instructors to avoid controlling language patterns identified in our analysis, such as commanding statements (e.g., “DO NOT email me”), demands for compliance without rationale, and dismissive responses to student needs. Such language can frustrate students’ basic psychological needs and undermine the very outcomes instructors hope to achieve (42). By intentionally crafting syllabi using autonomy-supportive rather than controlling language, instructors can create more motivating learning environments from the first day of class and promote motivated and engaged learners who take responsibility for their education. For instructors who use generative AI in crafting a syllabus, prompting the tool to use autonomy-supporting language style is a simple way to employ this practice.

Study limitations

There are several limitations to this study that should be acknowledged. This study focused on a single undergraduate institution, and therefore, the implications of this study are limited in scope. Further studies could expand the data set to include multiple disciplines and universities for a broader perspective and could thematically analyze all of the syllabus text. Another limitation of this study is that we were not able to distinctively identify if the policies in each syllabus were crafted by the individual instructor or if they were mandated at the departmental level. While currently the only institution-wide required statement is on academic honesty, department or school-level mandated statements may be present. The uncertainty about mandated statements makes it difficult to separate some of the instructor’s autonomy in word choice and syllabus design.

The lack of student perspective of the syllabus text poses another limitation to this study. Since only written documents were included and no implementation of the syllabus policies or classroom dynamics was collected as part of this work, we can only make conclusions about the text itself. Furthermore, we did not capture how students perceive the text we examined. A study that incorporates student perspectives could help reveal deeper insights into the effects of syllabi on the learning environment and is essential to make informed decisions about language use in syllabi.

Finally, we want to acknowledge that the syllabi examined here reflect a single discipline and institution type, with unique disciplinary and institutional cultures. Therefore, the findings may not generalize to other fields or institution types where attitudes toward student autonomy may differ. Additionally, generational differences among instructors may account for some of the variation we noted in language and emotional tone value, as student-instructor communication norms likely vary across generations. Future work examining how institutional context, discipline, and instructor background and generation shape the language used in syllabi is needed.
