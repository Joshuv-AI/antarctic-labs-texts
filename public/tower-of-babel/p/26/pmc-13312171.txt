Everyday and healthcare discrimination and polysubstance use among U.S. adults
Authors: Delvon T. Mattingly, Meman Diaby, Shyanika W. Rose, Carrie B. Oser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInterpersonal mistreatment in everyday life and healthcare settings are social determinants of health linked to a range of outcomes. However, their associations with adult polysubstance use—particularly at the population level—remain less understood.MethodsWe analyzed survey data from the All of Us Research Program (version 8) (n = 259,033) and operationalized the Everyday Discrimination Scale (EDS; range, 0–5) and Healthcare Discrimination Scale (HDS; range, 0–4) as continuous measures. Substance use patterns were defined as mutually exclusive use categories of exclusive (any one), dual (any two), and poly (all three) current tobacco/nicotine, alcohol, and cannabis use. We fit multivariable multinomial logistic regression models to estimate associations between both forms of discrimination and substance use patterns.ResultsThe average EDS and HDS scores were 0.85 (SD=0.87) and 0.59 (SD=0.66); the prevalence of exclusive, dual, and poly use was 47.4%, 8.4%, and 0.6%. In adjusted models, increasing EDS was associated with higher odds of exclusive use (AOR: 1.05, 95% CI: 1.04–1.07), dual use (AOR: 1.12, 95% CI: 1.10–1.15), and poly use (AOR: 1.21, 95% CI: 1.13–1.30). Increasing HDS was inversely associated with exclusive use (AOR: 0.91, 95% CI: 0.90–0.92) and dual use (AOR: 0.96, 95% CI: 0.93–0.98) and not associated with poly use.ConclusionsInterpersonal discrimination represents a clinically relevant and potentially modifiable social determinant of polysubstance use. Efforts to reduce everyday discrimination and improve patient experiences within healthcare settings may play an important role in preventing higher-risk substance use patterns and advancing equity in substance use-related outcomes.

Introduction

Perceived discrimination refers to unfair or unjust treatment based on an individual’s supposed or observed social identity or phenotypic characteristics, such as age, sex, gender, race, ethnicity, sexual orientation, religion, disability, or other identifying attributes (Krieger, 2014, Williams et al., 2019). Actions or policies rooted in prejudice, bias, or hate lead to discrimination, and these scenarios can happen to anyone and occur in various public or private settings, including the healthcare system (Krieger, 2014, Williams et al., 2019). Within public health and biomedical research, discrimination is conceptualized as a social determinant of health that disproportionately affects disadvantaged and marginalized populations in the United States (U.S.) (Krieger, 2014, Mattingly et al., 2025a, Williams et al., 2019). Individuals belonging to minoritized racial, ethnic, gender, or sexual identity groups are more likely to encounter discrimination and may be particularly susceptible to its negative health effects (Krieger, 2014, Williams et al., 2019).

Discrimination has been linked to a range of adverse health outcomes, including substance use and addiction (Ebrahimi et al., 2024, Gilbert and Zemore, 2016). Population-based studies have examined associations between multiple forms of discrimination and substance use outcomes, generally documenting statistically significant relationships (Mattingly et al., 2025b, Mattingly et al., 2024, Mattingly et al., 2023). For example, everyday discrimination has been found to be linked to tobacco, alcohol, cannabis, and illicit drug use among U.S. adults (Carliner et al., 2016, Mattingly et al., 2025b, Mattingly et al., 2024, Mattingly et al., 2023, Qeadan et al., 2022, Rose et al., 2019). Mechanisms linking discrimination to substance use include stress-coping responses, whereby repeated or chronic exposure to discrimination acts as a psychosocial stressor that increases negative affect and psychological distress (Clark et al., 1999, Pascoe and Smart Richman, 2009). This phenomenon, in turn, may promote substance use as a maladaptive coping strategy. Discrimination has also been linked to dysregulation of stress-response systems, reduced self-regulation, social marginalization, and increased exposure to substance-using contexts, all of which may elevate risk for substance use and substance use disorders (Clark et al., 1999, McCabe et al., 2010, Pascoe and Smart Richman, 2009, Williams and Mohammed, 2009).

To expand on the existing literature linking discrimination to substance use, this study investigated the relationship between perceived everyday and healthcare discrimination and polysubstance use using data from the NIH-sponsored All of Us Research Program (AoU), an ongoing prospective biomedical cohort study. AoU aims to enroll at least one million adults across the U.S. to advance precision medicine, with a particular emphasis on populations historically underrepresented in biomedical research (All of Us Research Program Investigators, 2019). While the link between everyday discrimination and substance use—including polysubstance use—is well documented, far less is known about how healthcare discrimination shapes use behaviors. We hypothesized that both everyday and healthcare discrimination would be positively associated with substance use patterns, with stronger associations for everyday discrimination, particularly for polysubstance use.

Methods

Data and participants

We used controlled-tier data (version 8) from the AoU. As of October 2025, the AoU had enrolled approximately 872,000 participants, of whom about 598,000 had completed the initial steps of the program (All of Us Research Hub). Participants enrolled digitally and, after providing informed consent, completed a baseline survey (All of Us Research Program Investigators, 2019). Participants could subsequently be invited to complete additional surveys, contribute biometric data via smartphones or sensor devices, and attend in-person visits for biospecimen collection used in genomic analyses (All of Us Research Program Investigators, 2019).

We used data from three surveys: “The Basics”, or the baseline survey on demographics, “Lifestyle”, or the survey on substance use, and “Social Factors of Health”, or the survey on various social determinants of health. Detailed information about these surveys is available online (All of Us Research Hub). We used the cohort builder available in the Researcher Workbench to create a sample that included participants who received all three surveys. Among the 633,533 adults in version 8 who took the baseline survey, 578,477 took the Lifestyle survey, and 259,033 took the Social Factors of Health survey. Thus, our final analytic sample was 259,033. Figure S1 describes the selection of the analytical sample.

To address missing data, we used multiple imputation (MI) via the "mice" package in R. Depending on the variable type, predictive mean matching and logistic regression were applied for imputation (van Buuren and Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011). This method created five imputed datasets, maintaining the dataset's structure and key demographic and behavioral patterns while effectively managing missing values (Kalpourtzi et al., 2023). We pooled results across imputed datasets using Rubin's rules to ensure valid statistical inferences across imputations (Rubin, 1987). To inform the MI, we used data on age, gender, race and ethnicity, sexual orientation, annual household income, educational attainment, home ownership, employment status, nine items for everyday discrimination, seven items for healthcare discrimination, tobacco product use, alcohol use, cannabis use, and other illicit drug use. We excluded participants who responded that they preferred not to answer or did not know to any study variable to prevent issues with interpretation. Prior to conducting multiple imputation, overall item-level missingness was low (3.4% of data cells). However, 52.6% of participants had at least one missing value, indicating that missingness was dispersed across individuals rather than concentrated within variables. The distribution of all study variables, including missing values, is presented in Table S1.

Measures

Perceived discrimination

AoU assessed everyday and healthcare discrimination. For everyday discrimination, participants were asked “In your day-to-day life, how often do any of these happen to you?”: (1) “You are treated with less courtesy than other people are”, (2) “You are treated with less respect than other people are”, (3) “You receive poorer service than other people at restaurants or stores”, (4) “People act as if they think you are not smart”, (5) “People act as if they are afraid of you”, (6) “People act as if they think you are dishonest”, (7) “People act as if they’re better than you are”, (8) “You are called names or insulted”, and (9) “You are threatened or harassed”. Responses were recorded on a frequency scale ranging from 0 (never) to 5 (almost every day). We coded each item as continuous and created a summary score, or the Everyday Discrimination Scale (EDS) (Krieger et al., 2005, Williams et al., 1997), by summing the items and dividing by the number of items (range=0–5) (Cronbach’s alpha=0.87).

The healthcare discrimination measures were adapted from the EDS (Peek et al., 2011). In AoU, participants were also asked, “How often do any of these happen to you when you go to a doctor’s office or other health care provider?”: (1) “You are treated with less courtesy than other people”, (2) “You are treated with less respect than other people”, (3) “You receive poorer service than others”, (4) “A doctor or nurse acts if he or she thinks you are not smart”, (5) “A doctor or nurse acts as if he or she is afraid of you”, (6) “A doctor or nurse acts as if he or she is better than you”, and (7) “You feel like a doctor or nurse is not listening to what you were saying”. Responses were recorded on a frequency scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (always). As with coding the EDS, we computed the sum and average of the items (range=0–4) (Cronbach’s alpha=0.82) to represent the Healthcare Discrimination Scale (HDS).

Individual substance use

In the Lifestyle survey, participants were asked about their lifetime and more recent use of various tobacco products, drinking habits, cannabis, and other illicit drugs. We defined current tobacco product use (yes/no) as now every day or some day use of at least one of the following products: cigarettes, electronic nicotine products, traditional cigars, cigarillos, or filtered cigars (hereafter: cigars); hookah, and smokeless tobacco.

For alcohol use, participants were asked about lifetime use and subsequently how often they had a drink in the past year not counting small tastes or sips. Response options included: never, monthly or less, two to four times a month, two to three times a week, four or more times a week, and prefer not to answer. We defined current alcohol use (yes/no) as at least two to four times a month.

For cannabis use and other illicit drug use (i.e., cocaine, prescription stimulants for non-medical reasons, other stimulants such as methamphetamine, speed, crystal meth, ice, k2/spice, bath salts, inhalants, sedatives or sleeping pills for non-medical reasons, hallucinogens, street opioids, prescription opioids for non-medical reasons, and other drugs), participants were asked about lifetime use and then their use in the past three months (never, once or twice, monthly, weekly, daily or almost daily, prefer not to answer). We categorized current cannabis use (yes/no) as at least monthly use in the past three months.

For other illicit drug use, we created an ever use indicator (yes/no) denoting lifetime use of at least one substance. Only a small proportion of respondents reported using at least monthly in the past three months (n = 60; prior to MI); therefore, we operationalized lifetime use indicators and included this variable as a covariate.

Patterns of substance use

We defined patterns of substance use as a four-category mutually exclusive variable representing non-use, exclusive use (any one substance), dual use (any combination of two substances), and poly use (all three substances) among the current tobacco, alcohol, and cannabis use variables defined above. A summary of the measurement and coding of all substance use variables used in this study is presented in Table S2.

As shown in Table S2 and discussed in the previous section, tobacco, alcohol, and cannabis use were evaluated differently. We approximated current use of these substances based on the measures available in the surveys. The substance use pattern variable reflects approximate thresholds for current use despite differences in how current use was defined across substance types. At the very least, participants had to report recent use (i.e., daily/some-day use or at least monthly use) to be categorized as having current use.

Covariates

We included the following sociodemographic characteristics: age in years (continuous), gender (male, female, nonbinary/transgender/something else), nativity (US born, non-US born), race and ethnicity (Hispanic; non-Hispanic (NH) Asian, NH Black, NH Multiracial, NH White, and another NH race including participants who responded: NH American Indian and Alaska Native, NH Middle Eastern or North African, NH Native Hawaiian and other Pacific Islander races, or “none of these describe me”), and sexual orientation (heterosexual, lesbian or gay, bisexual, something else). We also included annual household income (<$50,000; $50,000-$100,000; >$100,000), educational attainment (less than 12th grade, 12th grade or GED, some college, college graduate, advanced degree), home ownership (home owner, renting, other arrangement), and employment status (employed, retired, student, unemployed) as proxies for socioeconomic status (SES).

“Something else” gender included respondents who answered, “none of these describe me”, “I’d like to consider additional options”, or a combination of responses. For sexual orientation, "something else" included respondents who selected, “none of these describe me” in reference to the listed sexual orientations. Sexual orientation was a select-all-that-apply question; when multiple orientations were chosen (0.46% of the sample), we classified respondents based on their first reported orientation. Other arrangements regarding home ownership included: “on a college campus; with a friend/roommate; with family; motel/hotel; hospital, rehabilitation center, drug treatment center, or other temporary institution; in a group home, nursing home, or other residential facility; transitional housing; emergency shelter or homeless shelter; anywhere outside (e.g., street, vehicle, abandoned building); other”. Employed respondents were “employed for wages; self employed”, unemployed respondents were “unable to work; homemaker; out of work.”

Statistical Analysis

We estimated the distributions (i.e., n (%) or mean (SD)) of all study characteristics among the entire sample pooled across the five imputed datasets. Then, we computed cross-tabulations between perceived discrimination and sociodemographic characteristics by patterns of substance use. For each set of comparisons, we pooled F-tests using Rubin’s rules (D1 method) across multiply imputed datasets. To estimate whether perceived discrimination (both everyday and healthcare) was associated with patterns of substance use, we fit bivariate (results not shown) and multivariable (adjusted for covariates and ever illicit drug use; outcome referent group: no-use) multinomial logistic regression, generating odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals.

We ran several sensitivity analyses. First, we refit each model after changing the referent group from no-use to exclusive use to assess whether associations were present for dual and poly use relative to respondents who used only one substance. Second, we fit additional bivariate and multivariable logistic regression models estimating the associations between perceived discrimination and each individual substance use outcome (six total models, three for everyday discrimination and three for healthcare discrimination). Third, we fit the main model and second sensitivity analysis using the complete-case sample prior to MI to evaluate the extent of variation introduced by the imputation procedure. We conducted all analyses in the Jupyter Notebook cloud environment using R version 4.4.0.

Covariate effects revealed that age is inversely associated with all use outcomes. Compared to female gender, male gender was associated with all use categories, whereas nonbinary/transgender/something else gender was associated with exclusive use. Compared with NH White adults, other racial and ethnic groups had lower odds of exclusive use. This pattern held for dual use, with exception to NH Black adults who had elevated odds. For poly use, Hispanic and NH Asian had lower odds compared with NH White, while NH Black adults had higher odds. Furthermore, individuals identifying as lesbian, gay, or bisexual had higher odds of all use categories compared with heterosexual individuals. Adults identifying as some other sexual orientation consistently had lower odds of use compared to heterosexual adults.

Compared to adults who make > $100,000/year, those making less had lower odds of exclusive and dual use. Lower education, relative to holding an advanced degree, generally was associated with lower odds of exclusive use but higher odds of dual and poly use. Home ownership differed, with renters having higher odds of all use categories and those with another arrangement having higher odds of dual and poly use, compared to home owners. For employment status, compared to employed adults, retired adults had higher odds of exclusive use, and student and unemployed adults had lower odds of exclusive and dual use. Students also had lower odds of poly use.

Finally, ever other illicit drug was associated with higher odds of exclusive use (AOR: 1.75, 95% CI: 1.71–1.78) and dual (AOR: 2.12, 95% CI: 2.05–2.20). However, this use pattern was inversely associated with poly use (AOR: 0.35, 95% CI: 0.27–0.45).

Sensitivity analyses

In sensitivity analyses re-estimating models with exclusive use as the referent group (vs. non-use), EDS was associated with higher odds of dual use (AOR: 1.07, 95% CI: 1.05–1.09) and poly use (AOR: 1.15, 95% CI: 1.07–1.24) (Table S3). Further, HDS was associated with higher odds of non-use (AOR: 1.10, 95% CI: 1.08–1.12), dual use (AOR: 1.05, 95% CI: 1.02–1.08), and poly use (AOR: 1.12, 95% CI: 1.03–1.22), compared to exclusive use. Analyses fitting multivariable logistic regression models for each individual substance use outcome are presented in Table S4. While EDS and HDS showed consistent associations with current tobacco product use and cannabis use, patterns differed for alcohol. Specifically, the association with current alcohol use was null for EDS, and HDS was inversely associated with alcohol use (AOR: 0.84, 95% CI: 0.83–0.86).

In the complete case analysis, we observed similar patterns of association between EDS and substance use patterns (Table S5). HDS also demonstrated similar associations for exclusive use; however, estimates for dual use were no longer statistically significant. In analyses using multivariable logistic regression models for each individual substance use outcome, the observed associations were generally consistent with those obtained after MI (Table S6).

Discussion

This study found associations between EDS and HDS and patterns of substance use among a large sample of US adults. Specifically, EDS was positively associated with exclusive, dual, and poly use, with increasing magnitude across use intensity. In contrast, HDS was inversely associated with exclusive and dual use, but not poly use. These findings suggest that discrimination experienced in everyday settings may be more consistently linked to escalating substance use patterns, whereas discrimination in healthcare settings demonstrates a more complex and less uniform relationship with use behaviors.

We hypothesized that increased discrimination would be more strongly associated with poly use than other use profiles. This pattern was observed for EDS but not HDS, underscoring important conceptual and contextual differences between the two measures (Krieger, 2014, Peek et al., 2011, Williams and Mohammed, 2009). Importantly, associations involving HDS differed depending on the referent group used in the models. Relative to non-use, HDS was inversely associated with exclusive and dual use and was not associated with poly use. However, when exclusive use was specified as the referent group in sensitivity analyses, HDS was associated with greater odds of dual and poly use. These findings suggest that healthcare discrimination may be more strongly associated with increasing complexity of substance use among individuals already using substances, rather than possibly with substance use initiation or any use versus non-use. A key distinction relates to healthcare access: by definition, adults reporting healthcare discrimination must have had contact with the healthcare system (Hausmann et al., 2008, Wang et al., 2024). Healthcare access is unevenly distributed across sociodemographic groups, with structurally marginalized populations experiencing disproportionately lower access and fewer recent encounters with healthcare providers (Gulati et al., 2025, Riley, 2012). As a result, individuals who lack healthcare access may not report healthcare discrimination despite potentially experiencing high levels of discrimination in other domains, including everyday social settings.

Findings from sensitivity analyses further highlight the nuanced role of healthcare discrimination. When exclusive use was used as the referent group, HDS was associated with higher odds of dual and poly use, suggesting that among individuals already using substances, experiences of healthcare discrimination may be linked to more complex use patterns. Conversely, when compared to non-use, HDS was inversely associated with exclusive use. Notably, substance-specific analyses suggested that these inverse associations were largely attributable to alcohol use. HDS was negatively associated with current alcohol use, which may partially explain why inverse associations emerged when non-use served as the referent category in omnibus models. This distinction is important because alcohol use, particularly non-heavy or socially normative drinking, may reflect a different behavioral and social context than other substances included in the analyses. This pattern may reflect existing prevention, screening, and treatment efforts within healthcare settings that are more effective at reducing or managing single-substance use (John et al., 2018, McNeely et al., 2024), or differences in healthcare engagement among individuals with varying use profiles (Bhondoekhan et al., 2023, Lorvick et al., 2018). Among adults, alcohol use in the U.S. is prevalent with 67% of adults reporting past year drinking (National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism, 2025). Thus, current use alone (vs. more problematic patterns of use like binge drinking) may therefore not be as correlated with discrimination as other substances. Together, these findings suggest that HDS may capture a combination of exposure to discrimination and interaction with healthcare systems, rather than functioning solely as a generalized social stressor in the same way as everyday discrimination (Peek et al., 2011).

Our findings have implications for public health, policy, and clinical practice. At the population level, results underscore the importance of addressing everyday discrimination as a pervasive social stressor linked to substance use patterns, particularly dual and poly use, suggesting that prevention efforts should extend beyond healthcare settings to broader social environments. From a policy perspective, interventions aimed at reducing structural and interpersonal discrimination across housing, employment, education, and public spaces may yield downstream benefits for substance use prevention and health equity (Ray et al., 2023). Findings involving HDS suggest that these efforts can also be extended to healthcare systems, where promoting anti-discriminatory practices may help reduce substance use and improve patient experiences and engagement, including among individuals receiving care for other health conditions (Serchen et al., 2021). At the same time, the mixed directionality of HDS associations highlights healthcare settings as potential points of intervention (e.g., referral to substance use treatment, or brief counseling), particularly for individuals already engaged in care. A recent study showed that primary care personnel recognize provider- and structural-level stigma toward people who use drugs and are generally receptive to anti-stigma interventions, although implementation may be constrained by broader insurer and regulatory barriers (Madden et al., 2024). Clinically, differences in associations between everyday and healthcare discrimination emphasize the need to both address bias within healthcare systems and expand access to screening and treatment services for individuals who may be at risk but not engaged in care (Priester et al., 2016). Together, these findings support multi-level approaches that address discrimination both within and outside healthcare settings to reduce substance-related disparities. Furthermore, future research should examine whether SES moderates the effects of EDS and HDS on substance use. Incorporating healthcare utilization measures, such as the number of healthcare visits, may also provide additional context for interpreting the HDS measure.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. We relied on self-reported survey data, which are subject to response biases. Similarly, using cross-sectional survey data precludes any temporal inferences. In addition, substance use was assessed differently across substances. Current use was defined based on conventional literature to approximate recent, and potentially more problematic use. Third, the prevalence of illicit drug use, including cannabis and other illegal substances, in this sample was relatively low compared to the general U.S. adult population, possibly reflecting the sample's composition. Cannabis use had a substantial proportion of missing responses, and even with the use of MI, the true prevalence of current use is likely underestimated. Thus, some degree of misclassification may have biased our estimates when defining adults to the poly use category. Fourth, the EDS and HDS measures assess the frequency of recurrent discriminatory experiences without a defined recall timeframe, limiting the ability to establish temporal ordering between discrimination exposure and substance use outcomes. Fifth, substance use outcomes were based on binary recency use measures and therefore did not capture frequency or intensity of substance use within that period.

Conclusions

This study found that everyday discrimination was consistently associated with increasing intensity of substance use, including poly use, whereas healthcare discrimination demonstrated more variable associations depending on the comparison group and substance type. Covariate effects further revealed marked disparities in poly use by individual identity and socioeconomic characteristics, underscoring the intersecting roles of social marginalization and structural disadvantage in shaping patterns of adverse use behaviors. Collectively, these findings suggest that perceived discrimination—particularly in everyday contexts—may contribute to more complex and higher-risk use profiles, while healthcare settings represent both a site of potential harm and an opportunity for intervention to reduce substance use disparities and promote health equity.
