Dynamic cytokine profiles across the progression of Type 1 diabetes mellitus are associated with diabetic retinopathy development
Authors: Francisco Martín-Loro, Fátima Cano-Cano, Almudena Lara-Barea, Laura Gómez-Jaramillo, María del Carmen González-Montelongo, María del Mar Roca-Rodríguez, Cristina López-Tinoco, Pablo Álvarez-Ramos, Gema Jiménez-Gómez, Antonio Campos-Caro, Manuel Aguilar-Diosdado, Ana I Arroba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractType 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) is an autoimmune disease characterized by chronic inflammation that contributes to diabetic complications such as diabetic retinopathy (DR). This cross-sectional observational study aimed to characterize circulating cytokine profiles across different stages of T1DM and assess their association with early retinal alterations. 74 individuals with T1DM were categorized according to disease duration: <3 months (A), ≥ 3 months to ≤ 1 year (B), ≥ 1 to < 5 years (C) ≥ 5 years (D). 23 subjects were included as healthy controls (H). 48 serum cytokines were quantified using a standardized human cytokine assay. Retinal morphology and vascular parameters were assessed through OCT and OCTA images. Early-staged patients (A) showed elevated concentrations of IL-1β, TNF-α, IL2-Ra and IL-8, compared to controls (H). IL-8 remained increased in group C, alongside with higher Eotaxin, IL-12(p70) and IL-17 and anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-4 and LIF. Retinal thinning and enlarged foveal avascular and neovascularization areas correlated with these cytokine patterns. Introduction

Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) is an autoimmune disease characterized by the progressive destruction of pancreatic beta cells. Affecting over 9 million people worldwide, T1DM represents approximately 2% of the total diabetic population and is frequently diagnosed during childhood1. Although T1DM has traditionally been considered a disease of childhood or adolescence, diagnoses among older adults are becoming increasingly common2. Despite emerging evidence regarding the role of innate inflammation and ongoing research into potential immunotherapies, exogenous insulin administration remains the principal treatment for T1DM3,4.

Furthermore, over the past few decades, glycemic control has remained the principal focus in improving the management of T1DM patients5. However, the activation of the innate immune system not only impairs insulin secretion and activity, but also contributes to the maintenance of inflammation, leading to global6 and specific diabetic complications such as diabetic nephropathy and diabetic retinopathy (DR)7. The pre-diabetic phase is characterized by the detection of beta-cells autoantibodies, and a chronic inflammation preceding to the T1DM diagnosis5. The immune cells, T-cells and CD8+ T cells, are involved in the beta cell death during T1DM8. In newly diagnosed T1DM patients, elevated pro-inflammatory activity has been detected due to the increase of specific monocytes, that contribute to T-cell activation and the progression of T1DM9.

Accumulating evidence indicates that numerous cytokines are detectable in the serum of T1DM patients and are secreted by different types of cells, serving as molecular mediators of both, the innate and adaptive immune responses10,11. According to this evidence, high levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines: tumor necrosis factors (TNF-α, TNF-β), interleukins (IL-1α IL-1β, IL-12, IL-10, transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), interferons (IFNγ, IFNα/β)12; and anti-inflammatory cytokines including IL-13, IL-4, IL-22, and IL-1013 has been detected in biological samples from T1DM patients14,15, contributing to low-grade cellular activation and inflammation, that may be linked to prompt the onset of diabetic complications, including DR and its progression16. DR is considered one of the most prevailing diabetic complications, which is associated with vision loss and blindness in the adult population17,18. Although the pathogenesis of DR is still under study, it is widely recognized that inflammation plays a key role in its development19. Several studies have reported the presence of high concentrations of inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, IL-8 and IL-2, in the serum, plasma and humor vitreous of DR patients, with different implications in DR pathophysiology20. Moreover, the analysis performed has not kept in mind the stage of T1DM progression, the inflammatory status and or its effect on DR development.

The inflammatory profile present at each stage of T1DM progression, coupled with immune response, is linked with the development of specific complications of T1DM, such as DR. Despite strong preclinical evidence demonstrating that targeting inflammatory pathways can prevent DR, a definitive relationship between innate immune mediators and DR remains undefined21.

Screening for the presence of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines during the different stages of T1DM progression can help identify immunological response-related biomarkers, leading to improved diagnosis/prognostic or preventing tools in the clinical management and treatment of T1DM, as well as specific complications such as DR, and their potential therapeutic value. However, comprehensive profiling of these cytokine patterns across disease stages remains limited, underscoring the need for studies that integrate systemic inflammation with early microvascular changes. In this context, the present study aims to characterize circulating cytokine profiles throughout T1DM progression and to explore their association with early retinal alterations, providing insight into inflammation-driven mechanisms underlying DR onset.

Materials and methods

Study participants

Seventy-four patients diagnosed with T1DM according to American Diabetes Association criteria22 were recruited from the Endocrinology and Nutrition Department of University Hospital Puerta del Mar in Cádiz. Patients were enrolled in this study based on the following criteria: (1) continuous insulin treatment since onset, (2) good glycemic control, (3) non-related chronic infectious diseases or (4) other autoimmune/inflammatory pathologies. Samples were obtained after a minimum of 8 h of fasting period and glycated hemoglobin (A1c) and/or degree of glycemic control (GMI) less than or equal to 7% and a coefficient of variability for blood glucose levels (%CV) less than or equal to 36% sall be accepted as objectives for good control of DM123. In addition, twenty-three volunteers from the same area and ethnicity as patients, with no history of chronic infectious or major illnesses (similar to those described for T1DM patients), nor any immunological alterations, were included as Healthy control group (H).

To study disease progression, enrolled patients were categorized into four groups based on the duration of the disease since onset: ≤3 months (group A), > 3 to ≤ 12months (group B), > 12 months to ≤ 5 years (group C) and > 5 years (group D). The ophthalmologic analysis was done in all patients from groups C and D. In this study, patient with Mid-Stage T1DM were included as Group C and Late-Stage T1DM as Group D. The presence of DR was evaluated following the criteria and scales from the International Council of Ophthalmology24.

Serum cytokine analysis

A routine biochemical blood analysis was conducted on all participants, and blood samples were collected to extract serum for cytokine biomarker analysis. Blood was collected in clot-activating tubes and allowed to clot at room temperature for at least 30 min before centrifugation at 1500 g for 10 min to obtain serum. This serum was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C until cytokine analysis. From each group, a random subset of 15 participants was selected for analysis.

Cytokine analysis was performed using Bio-Plex Pro™ Human Cytokine 48-plex Assay (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc., CA, USA) to evaluate cytokine concentration of the following cytokines: basic-FGF, Eotaxin, G-CSF, M-CSF, GM-CSF, CXCL1, HGF, IFN-γ, IFN-α2, IL-1β, IL-1Ra, IL-1α, IL-2Ra, IL-2, IL-3, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-7, IL-8, IL-9, IL-10, IL-12 (p40), IL-12 (p70), IL-13, IL-15, IL-16, IL-17 A, IL-18, LIF, IP-10, MCP-1, MCP-3, MIG, β-NGF, SCF, SCGF-β, SDF-1α, MIP-1α, MIP-1β, PDGF-BB, RANTES, TNF-α, TNF-β, CTACK, MIF, TRAIL, and VEGF. The analysis followed the manufacturer’s instructions and recommendations. All measurements were conducted using Luminex technology FLEXMAP 3D® equipment (Luminex Corporation, TX, USA) and each sample was assessed in duplicate for accuracy.

Retinal structure evaluation by optical coherence tomography (OCT)

The images were captured by expert personnel from the Ophthalmology Department at Puerta del Mar University Hospital in Cádiz, who routinely perform this work. All scans were conducted by a qualified operator and subsequently reviewed to ensure proper signal strength, centering, beam placement, and targeting. Retinography, optical coherence tomography (OCT) and OCT-angiography (OCTA) images were obtained by DRI OCT Triton (Topcon Medical Systems, Inc, Oakland, NJ, USA), a spectral-domain OCT using custom 6 × 6 mm acquisition protocol centered in the fovea. OCTA parameters were automatically computed by IMAGEnet 6 software (version 1.17.9720; Topcon Medical Systems, Inc, Oakland, NJ, USA). The area of the Foveal avascular zone (FAZ) was manually outlined in the superficial capillary plexus (SCP) using the caliper area tool in IMAGEnet 6 and was expressed in square millimetres (mm2). Vessel density (VD) for SCP (3 × 3 mm OCT-A images) was measured using the automatic software algorithm with the segmentation process detailed by Stanga et al.25.

Based on previous reports26,27, hyperreflective foci (HRF) were defined as discrete and bright dotted signals in the retina with clear boundaries identified within the retinal layers. Retinal hyperreflective foci (HRFs) manifest as unique, foci-like lesions measuring from 20 to 40 μm in diameter, showing reflectivity equal to or surpassing the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) band seen on SD-OCT. For HRF quantification, SD-OCT images were acquired from both eyes of T1DM patients in Groups C and D using DRI OCT TRITON equipment. The total number of HRF was quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.54 g). Quantification was performed by an investigator blinded to the experimental treatment groups.

Statistics

Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism (version 9). Initially, the normality distribution of every set of data was established using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Subsequently, one-way ANOVA for multiple groups of data was performed by the ANOVA F test (normal distribution, with Bonferroni post-hoc) or by the Kruskal-Wallis test (no normal distribution, with Dunn post-hoc). For comparisons of two groups of data, the Mann-Whitney U test (for no normally distributed data) was used. Bivariate correlations between SD-OCT parameters and cytokines were performed using Spearman´s rank correlation coefficient (no normal distribution). Excluded data are from those that did not reach minimal detectable values to be included in a curve analysis and those considered extreme outliers (3.5 times above or below the mean value). The predictive value of serum cytokines for the prognosis DR in T1DM patients was evaluated by receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curve analysis by using SPSS software (version 24.0), reporting sensitivity, specificity, area under the curve (AUC), and 95% confidence interval. A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Analysis of serum cytokines

The chronic inflammatory environment associated with T1DM progression and it has been extensively described and is closely linked to the development of specific complications, such as DR. To explore the potential association between serum cytokine levels and T1DM evolution, we investigated the cytokine profiles present at various stages of the disease.

Cytokines exhibit highly pleiotropic and redundant functions with some being secreted by multiple immune cell populations28. Although cytokines classification is challenging, we categorized the analyzed cytokines into four main groups: Pro-inflammatory, anti-inflammatory, chemokines and growth factors. However, they could be further classified into different groups based on alternative criteria.

Pro-inflammatory cytokines

Serum levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines were significantly modulated in T1DM patients compared with group H and, distinct cytokine patterns were observed across disease stages (Fig. 1A). Specifically, IL-1β was notably elevated in group A relative to group H (p = 0.028), and TNF-α was elevated in group A compared to all the studied groups (groups A vs. H, p = 0.097; A vs. B, p = < 0.001; A vs. C, p = 0.005; A vs. D, p = 0.014). Moreover, groups A, C and D showed significantly increased levels of IL-8 (p = 0.009, p = 0.002, p = 0.014, respectively) and IL-2Ra (p = 0.016, p = < 0.001, p = < 0.001, respectively) when compared with H group, while group B showed similar levels to H, but differed significantly from the remaining T1DM groups in the case of IL-8 (B vs. A, p = 0.028; B vs. C, p = 0.007; B vs. D, p = 0.041). The IL-17 displayed a specific increase only in group C compared with groups H and A (p = 0.026, p = 0.008, respectively).

Conversely, TNF-β levels were significantly decreased in all T1DM groups compared with controls (groups H vs. A, p = 0.006; H vs. B, p = 0.008; H vs. C, p = 0.0047; H vs. D, p = 0.035) and also, IL-16 decreased in group D compared with groups H, A and B (p = 0.022; p = 0.027; p = < 0.001, respectively) and also in group C there is a decrease compare to B group (p = 0.032), while IL-12 (p70) increased significantly in groups C and D compared with H and B (C vs. H, p = 0.024; C vs. B, p = 0.022; D vs. H, p = 0.049; D vs. B, p = 0.046; Supplementary Table 1). IL-18 fold changes were elevated in groups A, B and D (p = 0.036, p = 0.019, p = 0.024, respectively) with no significant changes in C group compared to H. Altogether, a large number of pro-inflammatory cytokines were up-regulated from the earliest stages of T1DM, whereas group C showed a relative decline in the number of elevated pro-inflammatory mediators.

Anti-inflammatory cytokines

The analysis of the anti-inflammatory cytokines revealed a consistent increase in IL-1Ra (H vs. A, p = 0.002; H vs. B, p = 0.018; H vs. C, p = 0.042; H vs. D, p = 0.046) and IL-13 (H vs. A, p = 0.004; H vs. B, p = 0.034; H vs. C, p = 0.020; H vs. D, p = 0.036) levels throughout T1DM progression compared with group H (Fig. 1B). In addition, significantly higher concentrations of IL-4 and LIF were detected in group C compared with group H (p = 0.010; p = 0.006 respectively). Also, C group present higher levels of IL-4 (C vs. A, p = 0.006) and LIF (C vs. A, p = 0.042; C vs. B, p = 0.010; C vs. D, p = 0.018) compare to other T1DM g Conversely, IL-9 levels were significantly decreased in all T1DM groups relative to group H (H vs. A, p = 0.021; H vs. B, p = 0.006; H vs. C, p = 0.036; H vs. D, p = 0.037). Overall, a broader number of anti-inflammatory cytokines showed increased expression in group C, suggesting an enhanced compensatory response during the middle stages of T1DM progression.

The heatmap analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1) identifies a sustained pro-inflammatory context accompanying the progression of T1DM. Anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-13 are significantly elevated across all study groups. IL-4 and LIF showed a moderated increase expression in Group C, whereas G-CSF and IL-2Ra are particularly increased in Group A. The analysis further demonstrates similarity in cytokine expression profiles between Groups A, D, and B compared to group C, respectively, in terms of systemic inflammatory context.

Chemokines

The analysis of chemokines fold change patterns included MCP-1, IP-10, Eotaxin, MIF, MIG, CXCL1 and MIP-1b (Supplementary Table 1). MCP-1 exhibited a sustained increase from group A to group D compared with group H (groups H vs. A, p = 0.005; H vs. B, p = < 0.001; H vs. C, p = 0.001; H vs. D, p = 0.006). Similarly, MIF and IP-10 levels were elevated in several patient groups when comparing with H group, reaching statistical significance in groups A (p = 0.025; p = 0.009, respectively) and B (p = 0.023; p = 0.003, respectively), corresponding to the early stages of T1DM progression. Higher levels were also observed in MIG and Eotaxin levels, with significant differences in groups B and C (p = 0.020; p = 0.038) or just in group C (p = 0.015) compared to healthy group H, respectively.

In contrast, decreased levels in groups B, C and D were found in CXCL1 (p = 0.039; p = 0.011, p = < 0.001) and MIP-1β (p = < 0.001; p = 0.037, p = 0.007, respectively) when comparing with controls.

The chemokines profile revealed a marked upregulation at T1DM onset, followed by a partial attenuation in intermediate stages suggesting dynamic modulation of inflammatory signaling throughout disease progression.

Growth factors

After assessing the chemokine profile, the study examined several growth factors such as HGF and SCF showing no discernible differences in their levels between the studied groups. However, the analysis of other factors such as M-CSF and G-CSF revealed a slightly increase in all groups compared to the control group, but only reaching statistically significant in groups A and D, in the case of M-CSF, and only for group C in the case of G-CSF (p = 0.010; Supplementary Table 1).

Retinal affectation and the identification of hyperreflective foci in late stages of T1DM

The global average perifoveal Retinal Nuclear Fiber Layer (RNFL) thickness (mean ± S.D) was significantly lower in the group D compared to the group H (285.36 ± 20.66 vs. 300.82 ± 11.36; p = 0.014). The Foveal Avascular Zone (FAZ) (mean ± S.D) was significantly lower in the group C compared to the group D (161.45 ± 54.89 vs. 296.48 ± 144.43; p < 0.001. The OCTA presented a significantly lower area in group D compared to group C (43.11 ± 2.49 vs. 40.59 ± 2.00; p = 0.002).

Associated with the inflammatory environment, hyperreflective foci (HRF) were identified in groups C and D, located in the inner retinal layers mainly. Associated with T1DM progression, the detection of a higher number of HRF in the inner and outer retinal layers is located in retinas from groups C or D with previous DR diagnostic (Fig. 2A-D). Correlations between retinal parameters and cytokines patterns

The retinal analysis performed under clinical specifications in T1DM patients from groups C and D showed correlations between retinal parameters and biochemical data (Supplementary Table 2). In the group C, mRNFL thickness correlated positively with IL-8 (r = 0.928, p = 0.008) and negatively with IL-17 (r = −0.900, p = 0.037).

In the case of group D, negative correlations were found between mRNFL thickness and IL-2Ra levels (r = −0.890, p = 0.039) and also, between OCTA vs. TNF-α and OCTA vs. MIP (r = −0.670, p = 0.048; r = −0.722, p = 0.028, respectively). Furthermore, positively correlations were found in group D between OCTA and IL-1Ra (r = 0.880, p = 0.021).

Discussion

Inflammation plays a pivotal role in the progression of T1DM and its associated complications, such as DR29. The physiopathology of T1DM leads to differential activation of several immune cells, contributing to modified the inflammatory responses during T1DM evolution. Actually, immunotherapies have been considered as a preventive therapy in T1DM30 and DR31. This study screens specific profiles of pro- and anti-inflammatory mediators during T1DM progression, from a recent diagnosis (0–3 months) to more than 5 years of evolution. These findings reveal immune-modulated patterns in T1DM patients, which could contribute to the development of targeted approaches. The chronic systemic inflammation accompanying T1DM, results in blood-retinal barrier (BRB) dysfunction32, leading to increased BRB permeability and consequent DR development, alongside the presence of inflammatory mediators in the retinal environment33 and the response of the retinal immune system34. Our findings demonstrate an early pro-inflammatory context in group A (0–3 months T1DM evolution), which is delayed in the C group (2–5 years T1DM evolution) for some of the analyzed pro-inflammatory mediators, while others exhibit an increase in pro- and anti-inflammatory mediators at the same time point. The systemic inflammatory context influences the inflammatory status of the retina, therefore consequently, our study delineates the inflammatory status of T1DM patients at different stages of the disease progression and its implications for DR development. The identification of biochemical patterns associated with the development of DR broadens the window of opportunity for clinical intervention in patients with T1DM. Early detection of inflammatory profiles during the course of T1DM, together with the identification of subclinical structural retinal alterations, may facilitate a more proactive and personalized approach to DR management. The inflammatory milieu associated with disease duration and progression appears to contribute to the subsequent emergence of retinal pathology.

As the data reflects, the T1DM patients exhibit a predominant pro-inflammatory state from the onset of diabetes, as previously reported9, characterized by higher expression of IL-1β and TNF-α in group A. Elevated IL-1β levels in early stage of T1DM patients have been associated with poorer T1DM management35. Cytokines display differential profiles among groups during T1DM progression, with a predominantly pro-inflammatory milieu underlying the disease and contributing to chronic inflammation36. Notably, IL-13 is elevated across all groups, potentially reflecting a compensatory anti-inflammatory response. Its activity may be further supported by the detection of LIF and G-CSF in Groups A and C, which may represent key modulators in T1DM progression and the emergence of associated complications. Modulation of the inflammatory environment based on systemic cytokine profiles has been central to multiple studies aimed at better characterizing T1DM pathophysiology and identifying novel therapeutic strategies37,38.

However, in the later stages of the disease, the onset of DR in T1DM patients does not show significant correlations with systemic IL-1β, IL-8 and IL-10 levels, although in aqueous and vitreous humor samples, significant correlations with DR have been established before7. This discrepancy may reflect local inflammatory processes in the ocular microenvironment that are not reflected in systemic circulation. The significant increase in serum levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-8, IL2-Ra, IL-18 and MCP-1 observed in early stages (group A) underscores chronic inflammation exposure, contributing to BRB disruption39 with an increase in immune cell activation and facilitating the DR development in later of T1DM stages (group C and D). In the Spanish National Health System, T1DM patients are included in retinal analysis programs for early DR detection starting five years after diagnosis. However, the pro-inflammatory profile detected in group A, could be a strong evidence for including molecular biomarkers into early DR detection, potentially integrating this approach into standard clinical practice.

Furthermore, recent findings suggest a positive association between predicted genetic for DR risk and IL-8 that could contribute to an increase in IL2-Ra, G-CSF and IL-12p7040, as confirmed by our results, which demonstrate a significant increase in group C compared to the other groups. In this exploratory study, we conducted an early assessment not only of systemic inflammatory parameters but also included an ophthalmological evaluation related to the development of diabetic retinopathy. These results align with the observed association between IL-8 and the reduced mRNFL thickness in the group C compared to group H. The negative correlation between HFR and serum IL-8 suggest a shift in the spatial dynamics of the immune response during disease progression, in which inflammation becomes predominantly localized at the retina level while the systemic response enters a state of regulation or exhaustion. This pattern is consistent with the concept of chronic inflammation as a tissue-focused rather than a uniformly systemic one, reflecting a compartmentalization of the immune response that has been described in other condition41,42.The progressive reduction in retinal thickness from group C to group D, suggests the presence of early events related to emerging retinal affectation without clinical symptoms in group C43, which advances to a significant reduction in group D, indicative of an advanced retinal affectation status. However, T1DM duration should not be interpreted as a surrogate marker of DR severity, and that the objective of the study is to identify systemic inflammatory signatures associated with early or subclinical retinal alterations during the progression of T1DM that may precede clinically detectable DR. In this context, the elevated IL-8 levels observed in both groups C and D, along with its correlation with structural retinal changes in the early stage of T1DM, suggest that IL-8 potential could serve as a potential biomarker for early DR detection.

Group C showed an increase in blood glucose levels, likely due to a desensitization to insulin therapy. The required increase of insulin doses concurs with the differential expression of IL-17 and IL-12 (p70) in group C. Both cytokines have a fundamental role in the development of DR, favoring the pro-inflammatory environment that triggers neurodegeneration40. Moreover, the increase in insulin dose may also be behind DR and its progression as shown in other studies44.

Evidence from studies assessing IL-12 family member levels in blood and ocular tissues supports an association between IL-12 and diabetic retinopathy (DR), suggesting a potential role for IL-12 in the pathogenesis of DR as part of the immune response cascade45. Elevated IL-12 (p70) levels observed in group C may play a contributory role in the pathogenesis of diabetic retinopathy associated with T1DM. IL-17 has a significant impact on the T1DM progression46 by modulating the retinal immune response. It plays a role in the phenotypic polarization of immune retinal cells, as microglia47. The activation and proliferation of microglia and resident macrophages can lead to either retinal protection or degeneration, depending on the molecular signature and polarity state of the immune retinal cells. We and others have demonstrated that microglial cells can adopt a protective role by transforming from a pro-inflammatory phenotype to a reparative phenotype48,49. In this regard, our findings indicate an acute increase in IL-17 levels in group C of patients, where the presence of HRF with a smaller diameter and high intensity, is representative of an inflammatory response mediated by microglial cells27. Correlation analysis with proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-8 and IL-9 suggests the coexistence of systemic immune exhaustion and persistent local inflammation. At this stage, the inflammatory focus may shift from systemic/humoral circulation to local retinal activation (microglial aggregates)50, promoted by elevated HbA1c levels and monocyte presence, and facilitated by loss of immune balance associated with decreased IL-9. An increase in HRF number reflects an active phase of the disease, suggesting that inflammation is occurring locally within the retina51. Therefore, our findings support a link between alterations in systemic inflammatory patterns and the presence of HRF, which are considered clinical markers of retinal inflammation in T1DM.

However, the anti-inflammatory cytokines patterns showed two profiles: those that remain elevated throughout the disease (IL-13 and IL-1Ra), and those that increase their expression, specifically in group C (LIF and IL-4). The elevation of the anti-inflammatory cytokine profile in group C may represent an early compensatory mechanism aimed at counterbalancing chronic inflammation, which could also influence retinal alterations during the intermediate stages of T1DM progression.

Furthermore, the anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-4 and IL-13 play a crucial role in the downregulation of the Th1 lymphocyte phenotype and are considered key mediators in pathology of the autoimmune diseases. The sustained elevation of IL-1Ra and IL-13 from early to advanced stages of our T1DM cohort may be associated with hyperglycemic disorders and the inhibition of the pro-inflammatory events triggered by T1DM progression52. Deficient IL-4 production observed at the onset of T1DM might contribute to the development of diabetes by allowing the inflammatory/autoimmune process in pancreatic islets to progress53. However, the increased cytokine levels detected in group C could indicate a shift in the cytokine profile towards the induction of anti-inflammatory responses. Similarly, LIF has been associated to a favorable response in pancreatic islet cell transplantation, with detectable levels in patients exhibiting good engraftment giving superior graft survival54. In our patients’ cohort, we observe a significant increase in LIF levels in group C, potentially contributing to the induction of anti-inflammatory response at this specific stage of T1DM progression. Correlation analyses indicate that cytokine alterations are not directly driven by total lymphocyte counts. Although exploratory, subgroup analyses suggest reduced Th1 frequencies in advanced T1DM stages without significant changes in Th2 proportions. These findings may reflect stage-dependent immune modulation rather than generalized lymphocyte expansion, supporting the hypothesis of a regulated inflammatory profile during diabetic retinopathy progression.

The detection of IL-13, LIF, IL-4 and IL-1Ra in biological fluids has been associated with neuroprotective effect in retinal disorders55–57, as demonstrated by studies linking their presence in systemic samples to the progression of DR58,59. In this regard, the elevated serum levels of these cytokines may be involved in supporting the immune protective functions of the retina.

Our analysis also revealed a correlation between altered retinal parameters and the expression of identified biomarkers, namely IL-8, IL-2Ra and IL-17. In patients at the onset of T1DMs, these biomarkers demonstrated predictive value for detecting retinopathy alterations in later stages, such as those observed in C group. Nevertheless, the ROC analysis was exploratory in nature, and the observed AUC should not be interpreted as evidence of definitive or perfect diagnostic performance. Further studies are essential to corroborate this association across different clinical contexts, as the precise significance of this relationship remains unclear. Additionally, it is important to note that the observed cytokine profile expression and the diagnosis of DR may not have a direct causal link.

Conclusion

The imbalance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokine production during T1DM progression highlights key immunological targets at both early and advanced stages of the disease, with potential implications for the subsequent development of DR in the years following diagnosis. Unique cytokine signatures identified in serum samples may hold predictive or prognostic value, providing insight into clinical immune-driven mechanisms underlying disease evolution. Cytokines such as IL-1β, TNF-α and IL-8 emerge as potential immunotherapeutic targets during the initial stages of T1DM, potentially playing a role in preventive management strategies by modulating the immune response to delay disease progression. Conversely, in the advanced stages, IL-17 appears to play a critical role in DR development and BRB disruption, highlighting its potential as a key target for immunomodulatory intervention at later phases of the disease. It is important to note that, future therapeutic approaches should not only aim to suppress pro-inflammatory activity but also enhance anti-inflammatory pathways mediated by cytokines such as IL-4, LIF, IL-13 or IL-1Ra. This comprehensive strategy could improve disease clinical management and control, particularly in relation to DR, and may represent a valuable path for future treatment options.

Study limitations

We acknowledge certain limitations in our study, including the small cohort size. Although the sample size in this study was sufficient to detect overall differences between groups in the main analyses, it reduces the statistical power for exploratory subgroup evaluations and may weaken the robustness of the correlations observed between serum biomarkers and retinal outcomes. In addition, the relatively small cohort may limit the extent to which the findings can be generalized to the wider population of individuals with T1DM who present diverse clinical profiles and varying levels of disease severity and progression. Therefore, these results should be validated in larger, independent cohorts. The study encompassed patients with early-stage DR via SD-OCT, as well as those with advanced DR. Based on these findings, it would be valuable to carry out a study that analyzes subgroups of patients with different degrees of DR and their associated inflammatory profiles. Furthermore, incorporating imaging tests earlier in the progression of T1DM progression, within the first three years, could facilitate the early detection of DR. Considering the constrained sample size, further validation within a larger clinical cohort is imperative. Additionally, the functional role of the cytokines patterns observed remains ambiguous; it is unclear whether they merely act as biomarkers of T1DM progression and DR development or play a more active role. Moreover, the diagnostic utility of these two putative biomarkers requires confirmation across diverse populations. Another limitation of this study is the potential for confounding factors that were not controlled, such as, cigarette smoking, alcohol intake, exercise and dietary habits among enrolled patients. We recognize as limitation that the age-related immune modulation cannot be entirely excluded in any clinical cohort, we believe that our age-matched control design and the predominance of post-pubertal participants substantially mitigate the impact of developmental confounders on our findings.
