Protocol for inducing focal striatal stroke in mice using photothrombosis
Authors: Bridget R. Boyle, Eileen Collyer, Andrea P. Berghella, Elena Blanco-Suarez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHere, we present a protocol for inducing focal striatal stroke in mice using photothrombosis. We describe steps for photothrombotic striatal surgery, magnetic resonance imaging, open field behavioral assessment of stroke-induced motor deficits, and post-stroke monitoring. We then detail procedures for tissue collection and immunofluorescence. This protocol provides a standardized approach for consistently modeling striatal stroke in mice, enabling mechanistic studies and preclinical therapeutic testing.

Before you begin

Subcortical ischemia contributes significantly to motor and cognitive deficits after stroke,1,2,3 yet animal models exclusively targeting subcortical brain regions remain limited. Photothrombosis offers spatial precision for focal injury, but adaptation to structures such as the dorsal striatum requires careful optimization of stereotaxic targeting, illumination depth, and post-operative monitoring.

This protocol provides a streamlined workflow for generating reproducible striatal photothrombotic lesions in mice and assessing the molecular, structural, and functional consequences. The approach integrates stereotaxic light delivery with longitudinal behavioral testing, complementary magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), histological validation to confirm lesion localization and volume, and characterization of cellular reactivity (astrocytes and microglia), typical hallmarks in response to stroke.

Innovation

The striatum, together with the motor cortex, are the most common brain regions affected by ischemic stroke in human patients.4 Traditionally, photothrombosis is predominantly used to generate focal stroke on cortical areas, as it represents a convenient technique with high reproducibility, low invasiveness and well-characterized behavioral correlates.5,6,7,8 This protocol introduces a modified approach using photothrombosis to target the dorsal striatum, providing a unique model for investigating subcortical stroke pathology.

The innovation of this method lies in the optimization of the photothrombotic technique to generate reproducible ischemic injuries in a subcortical brain region, the dorsal striatum. This adaptation maintains the high spatial control and minimally invasive nature of classical photothrombosis while extending its application to a region associated with sensorimotor function, procedural learning, and basal ganglia circuitry.

Additionally, the resulting lesion produces a consistent, quantifiable behavioral deficit in the open field assay, allowing for longitudinal assessment of functional recovery. Overall, this protocol expands the versatility of photothrombosis-based models and provides a powerful, reproducible platform to study striatal injury, and potentially apply it to target other subcortical regions.

Expected outcomes

MRI analysis

This protocol is expected to produce clear and quantifiable measurements of striatal infarct volume across multiple timepoints after photothrombosis. At early stages (1-day post-injury), T2-weighted MRI typically reveals a well-defined hyperintense lesion within the dorsal striatum that enables reliable delineation of infarct boundaries. By later stages (7-days post-injury), lesion volume is generally reduced as edema subsides, and early tissue remodeling occurs.14,15,16 Because imaging is performed on the same animal across time, this workflow supports longitudinal assessment of infarct evolution and provides high reproducibility for tracking injury progression or therapeutic effects (Figure 6).

Open field behavior

The open field assay is expected to detect locomotor and exploratory changes following striatal photothrombosis. Mice commonly display reduced average speed and total distance traveled at both early (1 day) and later (7 days) timepoints (Figures 7A and 7B), reflecting impaired movement initiation and decreased spontaneous locomotion associated with striatal dysfunction17,18. At early timepoints, reductions in center entries and center-zone exploration are often observed (Figures 7C and 7D), consistent with acute decreases in exploration17. These behavioral metrics provide quantifiable, time-dependent readouts of motor and exploratory deficits that can be used to evaluate striatal stroke severity and test potential interventions.

Immunofluorescence staining

This immunofluorescence protocol is expected to generate labeling of astrocytes (GFAP) and microglia (Iba1) in cryosectioned mouse brain tissue. Astrocytic (GFAP) and microglial (Iba1) reactivity is typically low at earlier stages but increases by 7 days post-injury, producing prominent GFAP+ and Iba-1+ areas in the peri-infarct and the injury core. Sham sections are expected to show lower GFAP and Iba1 labeling in homologous areas to the lesion. This staining pattern enables robust visualization of injury progression and cell-type-specific responses over time (Figure 8). In the present protocol we did not perform quantitative analysis; immunofluorescence was used qualitatively to confirm injury. Quantification and statistical analysis

Recorded data from either HOROS for MRI infarct volume or ANY-maze tracking software (average speed, total distance, traveled, time spent in center, etc.) are exported to a Microsoft Excel file for data organization. For statistical analysis and graph plotting, we used GraphPad Prism software version 10.0.0 (GraphPad software, Inc.). Unpaired Student’s t test was used to compare the stroke volume progression between day 1 and day 7 after stroke (Figure 6). We used repeated measures one-way ANOVA followed by Holms-Šídák multiple comparisons post-hoc test to perform statistical analyses of our behavioral data (Figure 7). Exclusion criteria included unsuccessful induction of striatal strokes or animal survival shorter than the required 7-days endpoint.

Limitations

The photothrombosis technique is commonly applied to cortical brain regions, where light penetration and anatomical accessibility are more favorable. In contrast, the striatum’s deeper location and anatomical structure can introduce variability in lesion size and placement. Photothrombotic injury produces delineated microvascular occlusion that may not fully replicate the diffuse white-matter associated pathology often observed in human subcortical strokes.4,5,19 While alternative models such as endothelin-1–induced ischemia or middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) could more closely mimic aspects of large-vessel or progressive ischemic injury, these approaches are more technically complex and less spatially precise. Our modified photothrombotic method reliably targets the striatum while acknowledging the limitations inherent to this model.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

The laser does not reach the target power output, or the laser beam is absent or weak.

Potential solution

If the laser fails to reach the target output (7 mW), first inspect all fiber optic connections, including FC/PC connectors and ferrules, for dust buildup or poor contact. Ensure that the laser diode controller is powered on and has warmed up for at least 10–15 min, as insufficient warm-up can cause unstable output. Confirm that the laser power setting is not still at 0 mW, which may be used as a safety precaution during setup. Clean connector tips gently using fiber optic cleaning wipes and isopropanol to remove dust or debris that may interfere with transmission. After cleaning, reconnect all fiber links securely and recheck the output on the power meter. If the power remains low or inconsistent, replace any damaged patch cables or connectors. To prevent recurrence, inspect and clean all connectors before each session and record power readings before each mouse to ensure stable laser performance. Do all this wearing an antistatic wrist band to prevent ESD.

If this route is selected, we recommend an initial dose of 100 mg/kg of rose bengal to maximize efficiency.

Problem 3

Insertion of the fiber optic cannula may cause a secondary lesion in the cortex.

Potential solution

While partial injury of the cortex along the trajectory of the fiber optic cannula insertion to reach the subcortical area is unavoidable, increased surgical dexterity can minimize tissue damage (Figure 9). This “secondary lesion” must be acknowledged and accounted for when interpreting experimental results. For this reason, it is essential to perform these procedures alongside sham surgeries rather than comparing outcomes to those of uninjured mice. Problem 4

Mice did not move or resulted in variable behavior in the open field testing. After striatal stroke some mice may not move around one day after surgery if their stroke is too large.

Potential solution

The mouse is stressed or there was insufficient handling/acclimation for the mouse to get used to the researcher and/or the arena. Increasing the habituation time, and ensuring a consistent environment (lighting, noise levels, etc) should help reduce stress.

Problem 5

Immunofluorescence staining produced non-specific labeling. High background or non-specific fluorescent signal could be caused by inadequate blocking, or inappropriate primary and/or secondary antibody dilutions.

Potential solution

Increase the blocking time (e.g., from 1 to 2 h) or increase the concentration of goat serum in the blocking buffer. Ensure that the goat serum is fresh, properly stored at cold temperatures (4°C), and not denatured or contaminated. Titrate primary and secondary antibodies to lower concentrations while maintaining adequate signal. Add additional 1× PBS washes (3–5 × 10 min) after both primary and secondary incubations. Whenever possible, choose monoclonal primary antibodies, as they typically produce lower background staining. Finally, reduce exposure intensity when using a fluorescence microscopy system to minimize background signal. It is also recommended to run appropriate negative controls (omission of the primary antibody) to assess background fluorescence and nonspecific signal from secondary antibodies.
