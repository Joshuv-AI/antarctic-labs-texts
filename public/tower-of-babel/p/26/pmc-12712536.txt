Anatomic Matching and Contouring of Femoral Trochlear Osteochondral Allografts
Authors: Nicholas Bertha, Daniel J Stokes, Jason L Dragoo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPerforming a fresh trochlear osteochondral allograft can be challenging, given the complex anatomy of the trochlea. The recipient’s anatomy can be difficult to match with the currently used allograft matching criteria, often leading to a nonanatomic articular surface. There is currently little information available to optimize graft congruence with the recipient patient for trochlear osteochondral allograft procedures. We describe a method for optimal trochlear graft matching preoperatively by using multiple measurements, including the trochlear width and sulcus angle at the proximal and distal aspects of the patient’s trochlea. Additionally, we describe a method for allograft preparation and insertion that relies on more points of reference to create a highly congruent articular surface.

Osteochondral lesions are present in approximately 60% of knees, although many remain asymptomatic.1 These lesions most commonly affect the medial femoral condyle and patella, while trochlear defects account for only 6% to 8% of cases.1 Despite their lower incidence, trochlear defects can be particularly challenging to manage, largely due to the complex anatomy of the trochlea compared to the relatively uniform femoral condyles.2,3

The trochlea can be described as 2 intersecting spheres that form a valley-like groove.4 Anatomic differences among patients exist, ranging from trochlear dysplasia with a flattened sulcus to those with a deep groove.5,6 The varying degrees of width, depth, and slope make performing an osteochondral allograft (OCA) transplantation difficult, as standard donor matching does not typically take this into account.

Early studies reported a poor prognosis for trochlear OCAs, attributing failures primarily to the challenging anatomy.7 However, advancements in surgical techniques have shifted this narrative, with recent literature showing excellent outcomes.8 Various approaches contributing to this result have been described, including the use of contoured guides to create more congruent grafts.9,10

Despite technological innovation, donor graft variability continues to be a significant challenge. Given the diverse morphology of the trochlea, even a perfectly harvested graft may fail to align with the anatomy of the recipient, and this mismatch can impair healing and integration, increase biomechanical stresses, and ultimately lead to graft failure. To mitigate graft mismatch, many OCA techniques, particularly for femoral condyle lesions, match donors by comparing condylar and plateau widths.2,11 Mologne et al.3 describe a technique for trochlear donor matching based on trochlear depth and width. While the information garnered from this approach aids in communicating the anatomy to the allograft procurement companies, this may not fully characterize trochlear variation.

We describe a technique for fresh trochlear OCA that involves donor allograft matching based on specific anatomic parameters that can be measured on a patient’s preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Intraoperatively, our method utilizes additional points of measurement to create a more precisely matched graft that may enhance incorporation and outcomes.

Surgical Technique

Preoperative MRI Evaluation

The supplementary video illustrates the preoperative measurement technique as well as the surgical procedure (Video 1). The patient’s MRI is analyzed to identify appropriate anatomic landmarks. These measurements are sent to the allograft procurement facility for donor matching. Magnet strength is not a limiting factor, as either 1.5 or 3 Tesla MRI systems can be utilized. Slice thickness is also not essential for the analysis. Measurements are typically derived from T2 proton density-weighted sequences, although the choice of sequence is not critical, provided the measurements are accurate.

Trochlear Width Measurements

The first set of measurements assesses the trochlear width proximally and distally. The proximal trochlear width is determined by measuring the distance between the medial and lateral edges of the trochlear articular cartilage on the most proximal slice of the MRI (Fig 1 A and B). The distal trochlear width is measured in the same manner on the axial slice just proximal to the intercondylar notch (Fig 1 A and C).

Measuring the proximal trochlear width (PTW) and distal trochlear width (DTW) on a left knee. (A) The PTW can be measured on the donor allograft at the proximal aspect of the trochlea and correlated to the same location on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). (B) The PTW is measured on MRI at the most proximal aspect of the trochlea. (C) The DTW can be measured on the donor allograft at the distal aspect of the trochlea just before entering the intercondylar notch. (D) The DTW is measured on MRI at the most distal aspect of the trochlear prior to entering into the intercondylar notch.

Sulcus Angle Measurements

The second set of measurements quantifies the steepness of the trochlear walls using the sulcus angle, measured both proximally and distally. Using the same axial cut as the proximal trochlear width, the proximal sulcus angle is determined by measuring the angle between a line from the deepest point of the sulcus to the peak of the medial trochlear ridge and another line from the deepest point of the sulcus to the peak lateral trochlear ridge (Fig 2 A and B). The distal sulcus angle is measured similarly on the axial distal trochlear width slice, typically showing a steeper angle than the proximal sulcus angle (Fig 2 A and C).

Measuring the proximal sulcus angle (PSA) and distal sulcus angle (DSA) on a left knee. (A) The PSA can be measured on the allograft using a goniometer at the proximal aspect of the trochlea. (B) The PSA is measured on axial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) that corresponds to the most proximal aspect of the trochlea on the sagittal image. (C) The DSA can also be measured on the allograft using a goniometer just prior to entering the intercondylar notch (the condyles were resected to allow adequate illustration with the goniometer). (D) The DSA is measured on axial MRI that corresponds to the distal aspect of the trochlea on the sagittal image, where you have just entered into the intercondylar notch.

Recipient Lesion Preparation

The patient is placed in a supine position. A medial parapatellar approach is performed to gain adequate visualization of the trochlea. The trochlear defect is identified, and a measuring guide (Allograft Cartilage Transplant Lesion Gauge; Musculoskeletal Transplant Foundation) is used to measure the defect, extending just beyond the defect perimeter. Standard clockface nomenclature is utilized to refer to specific locations on the cartilage, and measurements are recorded by the surgical team. The lesion gauge is positioned so that its superior edge is against bone, designating this point as the 12-o’clock position.

The first intraoperative measurement is the trochlear drop, which is the distance from the lesion gauge to the trochlea at the 6-o’clock position (Fig 3 A and B). Measurements beyond 10 mm may indicate that the gauge needs to be repositioned (distal tilt) to avoid excessive depth of the socket proximally. Next, the proximal medial uptick and proximal lateral uptick locations on the trochlear wall are identified. These are defined as the point on the clockface that the trochlear wall steepens, and the guide either comes in contact with the trochlear wall or is at the point closest to the gauge (Fig 4 A and B). The distal medial uptick and distal lateral uptick locations are marked in the same fashion. These 6 points of reference provide a more accurate representation of the trochlear anatomy for each patient.

At this point, attention is turned to preparing the recipient site. A guidewire is inserted through the center of the lesion gauge. A lesion reamer (Musculoskeletal Transplant Foundation) is then passed over the guidewire and reamed to the desired depth. The reaming depth is approximately the trochlear drop plus 1 to 2 mm at the 6-o’clock position. If the trochlear drop distance is ≥10 mm, one should change the alignment of the lesion gauge to decrease this number. Note that occasionally, the guide will need to be adjusted so that the 12-o’clock position does not contact cartilage. This depth of reaming provides an adequate wall for future graft placement. A depth gauge is then used to measure the defect at 6 points: the 12-o’clock position, the proximal medial and lateral uptick locations, the distal medial and lateral uptick locations, and the 6-o’clock/trochlear drop position.

Donor Allograft Harvest, Preparation, and Insertion

The donor bone graft is secured for preparation (GraftStation; Musculoskeletal Transplant Foundation). The alignment guide is used to position the lesion gauge on the graft. The lesion gauge is placed so that the measurements collected during the recipient site preparation are the same (12-o’clock position, trochlear drop, proximal medial uptick, proximal lateral uptick, distal medial uptick, distal lateral uptick). This distance is adjusted by sliding and/or tilting the gauge proximally or distally until the correct measurement is achieved.

Once aligned, the guiding arm is then locked in place to harvest the allograft. A reamer (GraftMaker; Musculoskeletal Transplant Foundation) is used to core out the allograft from the donor distal femur, reaming to a depth at least 5 mm greater than the largest measurement made on the recipient site. Reaming is performed under copious irrigation to prevent thermal necrosis. A sagittal saw is then used to cut perpendicular to the graft, releasing the OCA plug. If the plug is not easily removed from the donor distal femur graft, a freer elevator can be utilized to gently lever out the plug.

At this point, the plug needs to be cut down to an appropriate depth at all points. Using the previously recorded clockface measurements, the appropriate depths are marked on the plug (Fig 5). The plug is held with forceps (Musculoskeletal Transplant Foundation), and a sagittal saw is used to cut along these depth markings to appropriately shape the graft. The depths are verified and adjusted by recutting as needed. Once the desired depths are confirmed, the edges are gently rasped until smooth. Finally, a pulsed lavage irrigation system (Interpulse; Stryker) is used to remove the marrow elements from the donor graft.

Marking the depths of the allograft plug. The premeasured depths from the left femoral donor site are transposed onto the graft at the appropriate clockface positions and depths. This is then cut down to the appropriate size, utilizing a sagittal saw.

The donor plug is brought to the recipient trochlea and oriented according to the previously established reference markings. It is then gently inserted by hand into the recipient site until the graft is flush with the articular surface. Gentle tamping can be used only if required (Fig 6).

Final implantation of trochlear allograft. Arthroscopic image of the final trochlear osteochondral allograft implantation as visualized on a right knee while viewing with the arthroscope from a tangential angle to the graft.

Discussion

Preoperative MRI measurements provide a precise representation of the 3-dimensional anatomy of the trochlea. Obtaining a closely matched donor allows the graft to better fit the recipient trochlea and reduces the demand on a highly technical procedure by limiting the number of variables intraoperatively.

As the trochlea extends distally, its curvature increases, forming a deeper groove than it does proximally. The trochlear width also varies from proximal to distal. By incorporating both proximal and distal points of reference, the morphology of the sulcus can be more accurately represented compared to a single point. These reference points are easily identifiable on imaging and can be superimposed onto a donor allograft to enhance precise matching.

Providing the allograft transplant company with measurements from both the proximal and distal trochlea ensures that the entire distal femur is supplied as the donor graft. Without distal measurements, the condyles may be removed for other purposes.

One advantage of our intraoperative measurement technique utilizing 6 reference points is that it allows for a more accurate evaluation of the recipient site and corresponding donor graft. Although acquiring additional measurements extends the operative time compared to standard allograft procedures, this technique can mitigate subsequent delays by ensuring proper graft seating at the recipient site, as well as avoiding an unnecessarily deep recipient socket due to excessive trochlear drop (Tables 1 and 2).
