Multifactorial Hypercoagulability Causing Simultaneous STEMI and Pulmonary Embolism in Young Adults With Prothrombin G20210A Mutation
Authors: Sana Tahir, Bret Farrow-Cypel, Said Ashraf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe simultaneous occurrence of ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction and pulmonary embolism is rare, especially in young individuals without known cardiovascular disease. Both presentations imply an underlying hypercoagulable state or paradoxical embolism.Case SummaryA 27-year-old man experienced cardiac arrest during exercise and was diagnosed with both ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction and pulmonary embolism. Tests revealed thrombotic occlusion of the left posterior descending artery, lower extremity deep venous thrombosis, pulmonary arteriovenous malformation, and heterozygous prothrombin gene mutation. He was treated with mechanical thrombectomy and anticoagulation.DiscussionThis case demonstrates simultaneous arterial and venous thrombosis, likely from inherited hypercoagulability amplified by dehydration, alcohol, tobacco, and trauma, though paradoxical embolism cannot be entirely excluded.Take-Home MessageComprehensive thrombophilia evaluation and systemic shunt exclusion are essential in young patients with concurrent arterial and venous thrombosis.

History of Presentation

A 27-year-old man was brought to the emergency department after experiencing a sudden collapse while exercising. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation was initiated immediately, and return of spontaneous circulation was achieved within 1 minute of chest compressions. The patient was intubated en route to the hospital.Take-Home Messages•Concurrent ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction and pulmonary embolism in young patients without traditional cardiovascular risk factors should prompt evaluation for hypercoagulable states and possible right-to-left shunt physiology.•Early recognition with mechanical reperfusion and systemic anticoagulation, followed by individualized long-term antithrombotic therapy, is essential to prevent morbidity and recurrent thrombosis.

On arrival to the emergency department, the patient underwent a 12-lead electrocardiogram that showed ST-segment elevations in the inferolateral leads, consistent with an acute ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). He was emergently taken to the cardiac catheterization laboratory for further evaluation and management.

Past Medical History

The patient has a past medical history of alcohol and tobacco use disorder. Over the last several weeks, the patient consumed alcohol and energy drinks excessively. In addition, he had sustained a gunshot wound to the left leg several weeks prior. Further history obtained from the patient’s father revealed that the patient had a family history of a clotting disorder (factor V Leiden) in the patient's paternal grandmother.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnoses included acute myocardial infarction due to atherosclerotic coronary artery disease, coronary embolism (paradoxical or in situ), congenital or acquired arteriovenous shunt, for example, pulmonary arteriovenous malformation (PAVM) or patent foramen ovale (PFO), and an underlying hypercoagulable state.

Investigations

On arrival, laboratory results were largely unremarkable, except for a low potassium level of 3.3 mmol/L, an elevated troponin T of 94 ng/dL, and an elevated creatine phosphokinase level of 1139 U/L. The electrocardiogram revealed ST-segment elevations in the inferior and lateral leads (Figure 1). Computed tomography (CT) of the head did not show any acute or chronic infarcts, no mass, or hemorrhage. CT angiography of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis revealed an acute pulmonary embolism (PE) extending from the segmental pulmonary artery in the left upper lobe, and subsegmental arteries in the right lower lobe (Figure 2). The right ventricle (RV) was enlarged, with bowing of the interventricular septum, suggestive of right heart strain, also observed on the imaging. The patient was emergently taken to the cardiac catheterization laboratory, which demonstrated a 100% thrombotic occlusion of the proximal and distal segments of the left posterior descending artery (LPDA), while the remaining coronary vasculature appeared angiographically normal (Figure 3). Right heart catheterization revealed severe pulmonary hypertension, a mean right atrial pressure of 17 mm Hg, a pulmonary capillary wedge pressure of 19 mm Hg, and a mean pulmonary artery pressure of 37 mm Hg. Fick’s estimated cardiac output was 8.51, and the cardiac index was 4.3. The patient had a pulmonary artery pulsatile index of 0.88, suggestive of acute RV failure.

Stepwise oxygen content measurements revealed a systemic arterial oxygen of 197.2 mL O2/dL, superior vena cava oxygen content of 165.65 mL O2/dL, pulmonary artery oxygen content of 163.68 mL O2/dL, and pulmonary venous oxygen content of 197.2 mL O2/dL, without evidence of a diagnostic step-up or step-down. The calculated pulmonary-to-systemic flow ratio (Qp/Qs) was 0.92.

The patient also underwent a venous ultrasound that revealed a thrombus extending from the left femoral to the popliteal vein. An echocardiogram was performed, which showed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 50% to 55%, with a bubble study showing delayed appearance of the bubbles in the RV, raising concern for PAVM (Figure 4). A comprehensive thrombophilia evaluation was performed. Test results were negative for antithrombin III deficiency, factor V Leiden mutation, protein C deficiency, protein S deficiency, lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin antibodies, and β₂-glycoprotein antibodies. The patient was found to be heterozygous for prothrombin (factor II) G20210A mutation.Figure 4Echocardiography With Saline Contrast Showing Delayed Bubble/Contrast Appearance in the Right Ventricle 4 Minutes After Saline Was Injected

Management

The patient underwent a successful mechanical thrombectomy of the LPDA occlusion, with restoration of TIMI flow grade 3 (Figure 5). Intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) of the affected vessel showed no evidence of atherosclerotic plaque rupture, erosion, or dissection. The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit. The patient was started on aspirin 81 mg daily, clopidogrel 75 mg daily, high-intensity statin, heparin intravenous infusion, and a 6-hour infusion of eptifibatide. The patient briefly required vasoactive support with norepinephrine and epoprostenol infusions. The patient was liberated from mechanical ventilation on the second day of hospitalization.Figure 5Coronary Angiography Post-Thrombectomy

Outcome and follow-Up

Vasoactive support was weaned off, and the patient was eventually discharged 1 week later in stable condition on clopidogrel and rivaroxaban.

After hospital discharge, the patient continued with close outpatient follow-up with cardiology and hematology. Repeat CT angiography of the chest and lower extremity venous duplex ultrasonography demonstrated complete resolution of PE and deep venous thrombosis, with no evidence of recurrent thrombosis. Given the presence of an inherited prothrombotic mutation, the patient was advised to continue lifelong anticoagulation with rivaroxaban, which he has tolerated without bleeding complications to date.

Discussion

Concomitant presentation of STEMI and PE is extremely rare, particularly in young adults without any conventional atherosclerotic risk factors. In this case, a combination of genetic and behavioral factors likely resulted in a hypercoagulable state, leading to simultaneous venous and arterial thrombosis. Given the delayed appearance of bubbles in the left chambers, transesophageal echocardiography was not performed to rule out a PFO.

The patient was heterozygous for the prothrombin G20210A mutation, a well-established inherited thrombophilia with a 2.8-fold increase in the risk of thromboembolism.1 This baseline prothrombotic risk was likely amplified by several transient factors, including severe dehydration related to excessive alcohol and energy drink consumption, tobacco smoking, and recent traumatic injury to the lower extremity, resulting in venous stasis and endothelial injury. These lifestyle factors increase sympathetic tone, which increases platelet activation and procoagulant activity.2 Alcohol intoxication was shown in one study by Shen et al3 to increase the risk of PE by 3.53-fold. The exercise stress, especially in these dehydrated and proinflammatory conditions, even further increased thrombus propagation. Collectively, these factors met Virchow’s triad and likely contributed to the development of extensive deep venous thrombosis, PE, and coronary thrombosis.

Paradoxical embolism is defined as the passage of venous thrombus into the arterial circulation via a right-to-left shunt, whether intracardiac (such as PFO or atrial septal defect) or extracardiac (such as PAVM). The clinical diagnosis requires 3 essential elements: evidence of venous thrombosis, right-to-left shunt, and arterial embolism.4 Paradoxical embolism was initially considered, given the coexistence of venous thromboembolism and embolic myocardial infarction. However, invasive hemodynamic assessment demonstrated a Qp/Qs ratio of 0.92, and stepwise oxygen content measurements revealed no evidence of intracardiac or extracardiac shunting. In addition, both initial and repeat CT angiography failed to identify PAVM, arguing against the presence of a clinically significant right-to-left shunt.

Nevertheless, transthoracic echocardiography with agitated saline demonstrated delayed appearance of microbubbles, a finding that may be seen with small or intermittent intrapulmonary shunts that are below the detection threshold of invasive hemodynamics or standard cross-sectional imaging. Therefore, although paradoxical embolism was considered less likely as the primary mechanism, the presence of a minor or transient right-to-left shunt could not be entirely excluded.

Prompt diagnosis and management are essential because both STEMI and PE are life-threatening illnesses. The patient's coronary angiography showed thrombotic occlusion of the LPDA without underlying atherosclerosis or plaque rupture on IVUS. Coronary perfusion with TIMI flow grade 3 was achieved with thrombectomy, avoiding the risk of systemic thrombolysis in a young patient with potential bleeding complications. Right heart catheterization confirmed the diagnosis of RV failure from PE, guiding supportive management as well as inhaled pulmonary vasodilators. Anticoagulation with unfractionated heparin was initiated, according to acute PE guidelines.5

Given the absence of underlying coronary artery disease on IVUS and the embolic nature of the myocardial infarction, the patient was managed with anticoagulation and a single antiplatelet agent. Aspirin was discontinued early to reduce the bleeding risk. Long-term anticoagulation with rivaroxaban was selected based on the presence of an inherited prothrombotic mutation and the severity of the thrombotic presentation. This is evidenced by PIONEER atrial fibrillation-percutaneous coronary intervention and other trials that demonstrate that dual antithrombotic therapy with a P2Y12 inhibitor (clopidogrel) plus a direct oral anticoagulant results in fewer bleeding complications compared with triple therapy, without increasing ischemic events in patients with embolic myocardial infarction and no other underlying atherosclerotic disease.6

Conclusions

This case highlights a rare presentation of simultaneous STEMI and PE in a young adult, most likely explained by a severe inherited and hypercoagulable state, although PAVM could not be excluded. It underscores the importance of comprehensive thrombophilia evaluation, cautious interpretation of bubble studies, and individualized long-term anticoagulation strategies in young patients presenting with concurrent arterial and venous thrombosis.
