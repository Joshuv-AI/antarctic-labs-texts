Cerebral infarction: Surgical timing dilemma in Streptococcus agalactiae endocarditis — A case report
Authors: I-Hsuan Chou, Ling-Yuan Lee, Shin-Ying Sung, Jui-Tsung Chan, Chih-Hau Hsiao, Cheng-Hsuan Ho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfective endocarditis (IE) is a relatively uncommon disease with a high mortality rate. Early recognition and prompt antibiotic therapy are crucial for preventing complications such as acute heart failure, uncontrolled infection, and embolic events. Embolic-related stroke events significantly increase both morbidity and mortality. Stroke may be the first clinical manifestation of IE. We report the case of a 57-year-old male who initially presented with stroke symptoms and left-sided weakness. His condition deteriorated rapidly and progressed to septic shock within 6 h. Broad-spectrum antibiotics were started at 13 h when “possible endocarditis” was diagnosed. Approximately 43 h after arrival, the patient developed atrioventricular block, ventricular fibrillation, and cardiac arrest, requiring 45 min of resuscitation. The surgical plan was changed from delayed surgery (originally scheduled for 4 weeks later) to emergency surgery that same day. Unfortunately, the patient developed intracranial hemorrhage on day 4 and died on day 13. The final diagnosis was “definite endocarditis” caused by Streptococcus agalactiae, which was confirmed by both pathological findings and three major clinical criteria (microbiological, imaging, and surgical).Keywords: Infective endocarditis, Cerebral infarction, Neurological complication, Stroke, Surgery

Introduction

Infective endocarditis (IE) is relatively uncommon, with an annual incidence rate of 3–10 cases per 100000 people and a 30-day mortality rate of approximately 30 % [1], [2]. IE occurs more frequently in middle-aged men [2], and is defined as an infection and inflammation of the endocardium and heart valves [3]. Staphylococcus aureus is the most common cause of IE, accounting for 31 % of cases [4], [5], [6]. Other etiologies of IE include Viridans group Streptococci (17 %), Enterococci (11 %), Streptococcus bovis (7 %), other Streptococci (including nutritionally variant Streptococci) (5 %), fungi (2 %), HACEK (Haemophilus species, Aggregatibacter species, Cardiobacterium species, Eikenella corrodens, Kingella species) (2 %), and non-HACEK gram-negative bacteria (2 %) [4], [5], [6]. The clinical course of IE presents acutely with rapid progression or subacutely with low-grade fever and nonspecific symptoms [7]. IE remains challenging to diagnose owing to its diverse manifestations and changing epidemiology. The diagnosis of IE is based on the Duke-International Society for Cardiovascular Infectious Diseases Criteria (Duke-ISCVID), which classifies cases of IE as "definite endocarditis," "possible endocarditis," or "rejected endocarditis" [8]. Antibiotics remain the primary treatment for IE, with the duration and choice of therapy regimen determined by the affected valve type and the pathogen resistance patterns of the infecting organism.

IE-related complications include acute heart failure, uncontrolled infection, and embolic events. Embolic events from vegetation detaching and traveling through the bloodstream, causing dangerous embolisms in 20–50 % of IE patients, may result in both neurological and nonneurological complications [7]. Symptomatic cerebrovascular complications affect up to 35 % of IE patients, while the majority (>80 %) are silent events such as ischemia and microhemorrhage [7]. Intracranial or subarachnoid hemorrhage is less common (<10 %) [7]. Other neurological manifestations include infectious aneurysms (9–32 %) [9], [10], meningitis, and encephalopathy, whereas brain abscesses occur in fewer than 10 % of cases [7]. Notably, stroke may be the first clinical manifestation of IE and is a severe complication associated with increased morbidity and mortality [7], [11], [12], [13].

In this case report, we present a 57-year-old male who initially presented to the emergency department (ED) with stroke symptoms. He was diagnosed with IE during his evaluation in the ED. In addition to administering broad-spectrum antibiotics, the emergency physicians and surgeons struggled to determine the appropriate timing for surgery, highlighting the challenges in managing IE in emergency settings.

Case report

A 57-year-old married male patient had a history of hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and hyperlipidemia. He presented to the ED with sudden onset of slurred speech, left hemiparesis, and an inability to recognize family members three hours before arrival. His vital signs included a temperature of 37.9°C, a blood pressure of 101/64 mmHg, a heart rate of 134 bpm, a respiratory rate of 20 breaths per minute, and an SpO2 of 97 % on room air. Neurological examination revealed confused consciousness with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of E4V4M6, mild slurred speech, 3.0-mm pupils with trace light reflex Oculus Uterque (OU), and no obvious facial palsy. Muscle strength was 4/5 for all limbs according to the Medical Research Council Manual Muscle Testing scale. Deep tendon reflexes were globally diminished (1 +). The patient could not cooperate with the finger—nose—finger test or sensory testing. The Babinski sign was equivocal on the right foot and absent on the left. Cranial computed tomography (CT) revealed no intracerebral hemorrhage. According to the family, the patient had repeatedly sought medical attention for severe chest pain over the past month. Three days before the visit, the patient experienced back pain that limited his mobility. During this period, abnormal findings included an elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) level (15.93 mg/dL), mild pericardial effusion, and a mildly herniated intervertebral disc at L4/5 detected on CT. The patient was prescribed levofloxacin at that time. The laboratory test results from the current emergency visit included leukocytosis [white blood cell (WBC) count of 12,090/µL], an elevated CRP level (22.30 mg/dL), an elevated procalcitonin level (2.28 ng/mL), hyponatremia (129 mmol/L), an elevated troponin I level (96 ng/mL), and an elevated D-dimer level (>20 ng/mL). Other values were within the normal range (Table 1). Chest radiography revealed no pneumonia, and urinalysis was negative for pyuria. Abdominal CT revealed spleen enlargement, with no other significant infectious source. Meropenem was initiated as empiric therapy 3 h after ED admission due to the unidentified source of infection. After six hours, the patient presented with fever (38.7°C), decreased consciousness (E3V3M5), tachycardia (a heart rate of 143 beats per minute), and hypotension (a systolic blood pressure of 86 mmHg). The patient was intubated and administered inotropic agents (norepinephrine) as their condition rapidly progressed to septic shock. To provide broader antimicrobial coverage, ceftriaxone and acyclovir were added to the existing empiric meropenem therapy. Cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed multiple acute lacunar infarcts in the bilateral centrum semiovale, corona radiata, cerebrum, corpus callosum, and cerebellum, with normal signal voids over intracranial vessels (Fig. 1). Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed vegetation on the aortic valve with severe aortic regurgitation and moderate mitral regurgitation (Fig. 2). Thirteen hours after the patient's arrival, with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of E3VTM5, the diagnosis of "possible endocarditis" was established. This diagnosis met the 2023 Duke-ISCVID [8], which is based on one major clinical criterion (vegetation visible on TTE) and one minor clinical criterion (fever). Following the diagnosis of infective endocarditis, ceftriaxone was replaced with vancomycin to provide more targeted antimicrobial coverage. Cardiovascular surgeons were consulted regarding surgical intervention. However, due to concerns about hemorrhagic transformation during the systemic anticoagulation required for surgery, they recommended delaying the procedure until approximately 4 weeks after completion of the antibiotic course. The patient received broad-spectrum antibiotics, ventilator support, and inotropic agents while awaiting intensive care unit (ICU) admission. At 43 h after arrival, he developed ventricular fibrillation and progressed to cardiac arrest. Spontaneous circulation was restored after 45 min of resuscitation, including defibrillation, epinephrine administration and cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) followed by a transition to venous-arterial extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (E-CPR).

Owing to cardiogenic shock, the cardiovascular surgeons decided to change the surgical plan to perform emergency aortic and mitral valve replacement immediately. Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) performed during surgery revealed left ventricular hypokinesia with an ejection fraction of 30 %, pericardial effusion, and severe mitral and aortic valve regurgitation. Two vegetations were identified: one on the aortic valve (0.8 × 1.2 cm) and the other on the mitral valve (0.5 × 1.2 cm). The surgeon replaced both of them with porcine tissue valves. The pericardium was left open for natural drainage. The procedure lasted 264 min. The excised valve tissue was cultured in two separate sets, both yielding Streptococcus agalactiae, which matched the previous blood culture results (obtained 6 h after ED arrival) [14], [15], [16].

The patient was transferred to the ICU postoperatively while remaining unconscious. A noncontrast brain CT repeated on day 4 (one day after the operation) revealed intraventricular hemorrhage in all ventricles with mild hydrocephalus. The neurosurgeons performed emergency external ventricular drain (EVD) insertion and intracranial pressure (ICP) monitoring on the same day. Continuous venovenous hemofiltration (CVVH) was administered from day 4 to day 7 due to acute kidney injury with decreased urine output, lactic acidosis, and hyperkalemia. However, the family opted for hospice care due to the patient’s persistent comatose state. Life-sustaining treatment was withdrawn, and the patient died on day 13.

In summary, the final diagnosis was “definite endocarditis” caused by S. agalactiae, which met both the pathological criteria and three major clinical criteria (microbiological, imaging, and surgical) [8], complicated by cerebral infarction, cardiogenic shock, and postoperative intraventricular hemorrhage. Discussion

Antibiotics are the primary treatment for IE, and the most effective way to reduce embolic risk is their prompt initiation. The risk of new embolism peaks during the first few days after antibiotic initiation but decreases rapidly thereafter, particularly after 2 weeks [17], [18]. If embolism has not occurred, surgical intervention is considered based on the size of the vegetation. Urgent surgery (<7 days) can be considered for patients with aortic or mitral valve endocarditis with vegetation ≥ 10 mm, without severe valve dysfunction or clinical evidence of embolism, and with lower surgical risk [1]. However, most embolic events occur prior to hospital admission [19], as demonstrated in this case. Infective endocarditis-related stroke is a severe complication that substantially increases both morbidity and mortality [7], [11], [12], [13]. Unlike conventional stroke management, thrombolytic therapy is not recommended for cerebral infarction in IE patients [7]. All patients with IE should receive a surgical consultation, as surgery becomes necessary when antibiotics alone cannot cure or prevent complications including progression to death. Regarding the optimal timing of surgical intervention for endocarditis, there remains no definitive consensus. Current medical practice presents conflicting recommendations on whether surgery should be performed early (3–14 days) or delayed (3–4 weeks). Early surgery may improve long-term outcomes by preserving cardiac function, although it might increase the risk of preoperative mortality and neurological complications [20]. Delayed surgery avoids hemorrhagic conversion from anticoagulation during surgery and ischemic worsening from intraoperative hypotension [21]. However, the latest meta-analysis revealed similar mortality and recurrence rates between early (3–14 days) and late (3–4 weeks) surgery groups for IE patients who had cerebral infarction [21]. In this case, the surgeon initially recommended delaying surgery due to the stroke and concerns about hemorrhagic transformation. At consultation, his consciousness level was E3VTM5 (see Table 1), above the GCS threshold of 4 required for surgery [7]. Earlier surgical intervention might have prevented cardiogenic shock, although the hemorrhagic transformation could still have occurred. This clinical dilemma again emphasizes the difficult choice of timing for surgical intervention in IE stroke patients.

Beyond the timing of surgery, this case raises two additional important questions: how did the patient develop endocarditis, and was his severe back pain directly related to the infection? First, IE has both traditional risk factors, such as rheumatic valvulopathy and dental surgery, and newer risk factors, such as intravenous drug abuse, prosthetic valves, degenerative valve disease, implanted cardiac devices, and iatrogenic or nosocomial infections [22]. The patient had no history of valve or dental surgery, valve disease nor intravenous drug use. The patient was infected by S. agalactiae (Group B Streptococcus, GBS), which accounts for approximately 5 % of IE cases [4], [5], [6]. This bacterium, a commensal of the gastrointestinal and genitourinary tracts, is typically transmitted from mother to child; however, in adults, it is associated with conditions such as diabetes, HIV, or other immunocompromised states [23]. The patient had a negative HIV screening result (Table 1) but did have a history of diabetes. We suspect that the patient's infection developed due to diabetes and gastrointestinal disease, which likely allowed bacteria to penetrate the intestinal wall, enter the bloodstream, and subsequently colonize the heart valves.

Second, regarding the cause of severe back pain, the patient experienced chest pain one month before arrival, which likely coincided with initial bacterial colonization. As the infection progresses, spondylodiscitis (SD) may develop, occurring in 2–10 % of IE patients and serving as a minor diagnostic criterion. [7]. Studies of spontaneous SD indicate that up to 20–30 % of patients with SD may have concurrent IE [7], [24]. Determining whether the primary infection site is a heart valve or the spine can be challenging. MRI is the preferred diagnostic method for SD when IE-related complications are suspected. Unfortunately, we had insufficient time to confirm both hypotheses during the admission period.

Conclusion

This S. agalactiae IE case demonstrates management challenges in patients with neurological presentations. The progression from stroke to septic shock to death highlights three key issues: 1) the surgical timing dilemma between early intervention to halt disease progression versus delayed surgery to reduce neurological risks; 2) the importance of prompt antibiotics for embolic prevention, though many events occur pre-admission; and 3) IE's potential to initially manifest as stroke rather than cardiac symptoms. This outcome emphasizes the need for early diagnosis, multidisciplinary approach, and individualized surgical timing decisions, especially with cerebrovascular complications.
