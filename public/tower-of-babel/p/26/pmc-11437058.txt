Identification of a colony- and dose-dependent worker aggregation pheromone in the subterranean termite Reticulitermes virginicus
Authors: Yuki Mitaka, Anjel M Helms, Edward L Vargo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCollective behaviors of social insects are often regulated by pheromones. In subterranean termites, some workers forage for and exploit decaying wood for new food resources while forming tunnels from their nest. Colonizing new food resources requires workers to build and disinfect tunnels and chambers inside the nest and ingest decaying wood; therefore subterranean termite colonies should have mechanisms to establish and maintain groups of workers to perform these functions. Recently, an aggregation pheromone was identified in workers of the termite Reticulitermes speratus, which induces quick attraction of nestmate workers and prolonged aggregation to the site of attraction. In this study, we extended this work to another species of Reticulitermes and identified a worker aggregation pheromone in the termite R. virginicus. GC–MS analysis and bioassays demonstrated that this pheromone consists of 3-octanone, 3-octanol, and palmitic acid and shows a colony-specific, dose-dependent attractant response but not an arrestant response. Furthermore, these pheromone components were most likely emitted from the surface of the body. This suggests that aggregation pheromone composition and function differ significantly among termite species, even within the same genus. This study advances our understanding of the regulatory mechanisms of termite aggregation behavior.Keywords: Pheromone, Aggregation, Termite, Volatile, GC–MSSubject terms: Chemical ecology, Entomology, Animal behaviour

Introduction

Pheromones are semiochemicals involved in intraspecific communication, where chemical signals are released by a sender that change the behavior or physiological conditions of a receiver1. Although pheromones are used in numerous insect species2, social insects have developed especially sophisticated communication systems based on pheromones, which are involved in various social activities such as sexual behavior, defense, caste regulation, and foraging3. With regard to social insect foraging behavior, it is known that task allocation to foraging within the worker caste and recruitment of nestmates to a food source are regulated by pheromones4,5, highlighting their important role in the collective behavior of social insect workers.

Termites are eusocial insects living mainly in dead wood and soil, and their colonies, containing a large number of siblings, exhibit a sophisticated caste-based division of labor6. In termite societies, some individual groups of workers are responsible for collective foraging6. In the case of subterranean termites, foraging workers search for new sources of decaying wood, and after they find wood suitable for feeding or nesting, they recruit nestmate workers and start to exploit it. The workers connect their natal nest wood to the new wood via aboveground and belowground tunnels, allowing nestmates to reach the new wood7. The exploitation and colonization of new wood requires a large number of workers. Decaying wood has a low nutritional value and is a microbe-rich environment8. Thus workers need to digest such wood in a group, to exchange nutrients and immune substances with each other via trophallaxis, to remove contaminants from worker bodies by allogrooming, and to sanitize the spaces created by feeding on the wood. Therefore, workers should possess mechanisms to maintain their groups for these activities. It was long hypothesized that aggregation pheromones help maintain worker groups, but evidence of such pheromones was only recently discovered for termites.

Recently, an aggregation pheromone was identified in workers of the Japanese subterranean termite Reticulitermes speratus, which induces rapid attraction of workers and arrestment that keeps them in the same place for extended periods of time9. Aggregation pheromones typically consist of attractant and/or arrestant components; the attractant components attract conspecific individuals at a distance, while the arrestant components induce a passing conspecific to stay near the pheromone source for a long period of time10. The aggregation pheromone of R. speratus consists of 2-phenylundecane, palmitic acid, trans-vaccenic acid, cholesterol, and the two main cuticular hydrocarbons (CHCs), n-pentacosane and n-heptacosane. These compounds synergistically contribute to the attractant and arrestant properties of the pheromone. This pheromone plays a role in enabling foraging workers of R. speratus to attract nestmates to a new wood resource and to keep them there for long periods of time for efficient exploitation of wood9. Furthermore, it was recently shown that workers of the Formosan subterranean termite Coptotermes formosanus also use an aggregation pheromone with both attractant and arrestant activities11. The attractant components remain unknown, however, the arrestant components were identified as four fatty acids including palmitoleic acid, palmitic acid, (Z)-10-heptadecenoic acid, and stearic acid. Based on these previous studies, it seems likely that aggregation pheromones are widely used, at least in the family Heterotermitidae (subterranean termites), to which R. speratus and C. formosanus belong12, but it was unclear whether other heterotermitid termites also use aggregation pheromones with similar functions as those of R. speratus and C. formosanus and whether they are chemically similar to those previously identified.

In this study, we aimed to identify a worker aggregation pheromone for the dark southern subterranean termite, Reticulitermes virginicus. This species not only belongs to the same genus as R. speratus13 but also has much in common with R. speratus in terms of reproductive systems and nesting habits14,15. We compared the attraction activity of hexane extract fractions from R. virginicus workers and identified the aggregation pheromone components in active fractions by using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis and bioassays.

Results

Aggregation activity of worker extract fractions

We identified pheromone components using two-choice bioassays with fractions of worker extract followed by GC–MS analysis of active fractions. In the two-choice bioassay, two pieces of filter paper were placed on the bottom of a plastic dish: a test paper and a control paper. We compared the aggregation index (AI) score—defined as the number of workers gathered on the test paper minus the number of workers gathered on the control paper, divided by the total number of workers—across bioassays with each treatment. First, we compared AI scores at 5, 60, 120, and 240 min after the bioassay started among crude worker extract in hexane, and its fractions (hexane, 30% diethyl ether [hereafter, DEE], 50% DEE, and 100% DEE fractions). Five min after the start of the bioassay, the 30% DEE, 50% DEE, and DEE fractions showed higher AI scores than the hexane fraction and the hexane solvent-only negative control. The AI score of the crude extract was more than twice as high as the fractions (Fisher’s exact test with Bonferroni correction, P < 0.05, Fig. 1A). At later timepoints, however, the AI values of both the crude extract and its fractions gradually declined (Fig. 1A). These results suggest that the aggregation pheromone of R. virginicus has attractant activity but no arrestant activity, and that the active components are contained in the 30% DEE, 50% DEE, and DEE fractions.

Chemical composition of worker extract fractions

To identify compounds contained in the active fractions, each fraction of worker crude extract from the four colonies (A–D) was analyzed by GC–MS. In total, 20 compounds were consistently detected in all fractions from all colonies (Fig. 1B and Table 1). The hexane fraction contained CHCs. The 30% DEE fraction contained 3-octanone and verbenone. The 50% DEE fraction contained 3-octanol and cholest-3,5-diene. The 100% DEE fraction contained palmitic acid and oleic acid. Butyrated hydroxytoluene was also found in the 30% DEE, 50% DEE, and DEE fractions, however, this compound is a stabilizer inherent in the DEE solvent. Based on these results, we focused on six compounds (3-octanone, verbenone, 3-octanol, cholest-3,5-diene, palmitic acid, and oleic acid), which were detected in the 30% DEE, 50% DEE, and DEE fractions, as candidate components of the aggregation pheromone.

Dose-dependent activity of pheromone components

To narrow down the candidate compounds in each fraction, a bioassay was performed in which combinations of authentic standards were presented to workers. As a result, the candidate pheromone components were narrowed down to three compounds: 3-octanone, 3-octanol, and palmitic acid (see Text S1 in the electronic supplementary materials for process details).

We presented the RvAP Mix solution (3-octanone: 3-octanol: palmitic acid = 5: 0.8: 1,337) and its 10- and 100-fold diluted solutions to workers from three colonies (A, D, F) to test the dose-dependence of attractant activity of the artificial aggregation pheromone. We found that the dose response pattern differed among colonies (Fig. 2). In colony A, the AI values of all doses were significantly higher than the negative control AI value, while the AI value of the crude extract was much higher than that of the RvAP Mix solutions (Fisher’s exact test with Bonferroni correction, P < 0.05, Fig. 2). In colony D, the AI values of all doses and the crude extract were significantly higher than that of the negative control with the crude extract AI not significantly different from the RvAP Mixes (Fisher’s exact test with Bonferroni correction, P < 0.05, Fig. 2). Only the 100-fold diluted RvAP Mix tended to show the same attractant level as the crude extract for this colony. In colony F, only the undiluted RvAP Mix showed the same attractant activity as the crude extract, and there was no difference in AI value among the negative control and the diluted RvAP Mix solutions (Fisher’s exact test with Bonferroni correction, P < 0.05, Fig. 2). When results for all colonies were combined, all RvAP Mix treatments showed higher AI values than the negative control but lower values than the crude extract (Fisher’s exact test with Bonferroni correction, P < 0.05, Fig. 2). Comparison of the distribution of workers in bioassay dishes (presence on test paper, presence on control paper, or absence on both papers) among colonies and treatments also showed significant colony- and dose-dependent responses to aggregation pheromone in workers of R. virginicus (likelihood ratio test, colony: P < 0.001; treatment: P < 0.001; colony × treatment: P < 0.001).

Secretory origin of aggregation pheromone

Workers were dissected and divided into head, salivary glands, crop, midgut, hindgut, and “others” (combined thorax, legs, and abdomen) and extracted with hexane for GC–MS analysis to determine which body parts secrete the aggregation pheromone. The results showed that all aggregation pheromone components were mainly contained in the "others” (one-way analysis of variance [ANOVA] followed by Tukey’s HSD test, P < 0.05, Fig. 3A). Only 3-octanone appears to be present in both the head and “others” extracts, although there is no significant difference in abundance. However, the compound was detected in all samples of the “others” part extracts, whereas it was not detected in some samples of the head extracts. Therefore, 3-octanone, like the other two components, was estimated to be abundant somewhere in the "others." To determine the site of secretion of the aggregation pheromone, we dissected workers more finely, excluding the gut, and extracted the head capsule, mandible, labium, thorax, legs, sternal gland, and abdomen (excluding the sternal gland), respectively. As a result, 3-octanone was present almost exclusively in the abdomen although it was present in only small quantities and there was no significant difference in abundance between the abdomen and other body parts, while 3-octanol and palmitic acid were detected predominantly in the abdomen, followed by the head capsule and thorax (ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test, P < 0.05, Fig. 3B).

Discussion

We demonstrated that the crude extract of R. virginicus workers temporarily attracts nestmate workers but does not keep them in the same place for long periods of time (Fig. 1A). This suggests that the R. virginicus extract contains a worker aggregation pheromone with an attractant but not arrestant activity. GC–MS analysis and bioassays revealed that the aggregation pheromone comprises a blend of 3-octanone, 3-octanol, and palmitic acid and that these components were found mainly on the surface of the head capsule, the thorax, and the abdomen (Fig. 3). A small amount of 3-Octanone was detected in extracts from the head and “others,” especially in the abdomen (excluding sternal gland), but not the gut. 3-octanol was detected in the gut, but in amounts less than half of those in the “others”. Palmitic acid was also detected in the midgut and hindgut, but in much smaller amounts than in the “others” (Fig. 3A). 3-Octanone and 3-octanol are volatile substances16, and palmitic acid is weakly volatile17,18. It is therefore likely that the aggregation pheromone components of R. virginicus are released from the body surface as a body odor rather than being secreted from the gut. In contrast, the arrestant components of the C. formosanus aggregation pheromone are mainly present in the gut, from the midgut to the rectum, especially distributed in the midgut, suggesting that these components are secreted in the midgut and discharged from the body with feces through the anus11. Although the secretion site of the aggregation pheromone components in R. speratus is still unclear, it is certain that the pheromone is secreted into the nest material substrate outside the worker body, because all the aggregation pheromone components are contained in the filter paper when the paper is placed for 24 h in a rearing container with a group of workers9. Therefore, it appears that R. virginicus workers developed a different system of aggregation pheromone secretion than R. speratus and C. formosanus. Because R. virginicus, R. speratus, and C. formosanus speciated independently in different geographic regions from each other during the Miocene13, the origin of the components and secretion sites of aggregation pheromones may also be different among these termite species. However, we currently lack knowledge on how widely aggregation pheromones are used in heterotermitid termites and if so, how the pheromone components have diversified within heterotermitid termites. Therefore, further research is needed to elucidate the evolutionary processes underlying the production and secretory systems of aggregation pheromones in termites.

Our dose–response test demonstrated that the pattern of dose response to the artificial aggregation pheromone differed among colonies (Fig. 2). Of the colonies we used, workers of colonies D and F were attracted to certain doses of the artificial aggregation pheromone as much as to the crude extract, while workers of colony A were not as strongly attracted to all doses of the artificial aggregation pheromone as the crude extract (Fig. 2). Colony-specific responses may help to avoid attraction to the aggregation pheromone released from non-nestmate workers, but why should only R. virginicus have such a colony-specific difference in response to the aggregation pheromone? There are three possibilities: (1) the threshold or motivation to respond to the aggregation pheromone may change depending on their physiological condition (e.g., food requirement level, health condition) and environmental condition (e.g., temperature and humidity) in each colony, (2) the threshold for responding to the aggregation pheromone shows colony-specific levels independently of the internal and external factors, and (3) in addition to 3-octanone, 3-octanol, and palmitic acid, some colonies use other compounds to enhance the attractant activity of aggregation pheromones or as components of colony odor to distinguish colonies. Given that all colonies were continuously fed a sufficient amount of “brown-rotted pine wood mixed cellulose” (BPC) medium19 to serve as food and nesting material and were kept healthy at constant temperature and humidity until used for our bioassays, the first hypothesis is unlikely. The second hypothesis may explain the difference in dose response of workers in colonies D and F, and this difference may reflect colony size. In larger colonies with more workers, the response threshold for individual workers to aggregation pheromone is predicted to be higher because many workers release aggregation pheromone and the concentration of aggregation pheromone in the nest increases. Conversely, in smaller colonies, the response threshold is predicted to remain low because the concentration of aggregation pheromones released by workers is not as high. In R. virginicus, foraging areas do not overlap or adjoin each other among colonies, and one colony tends to dominate a particularly large area20,21. This suggests that the probability of workers perceiving aggregation pheromone released from workers in other colonies is low, and therefore, it is assumed that the response thresholds to aggregation pheromones remained different in each colony. However, because Reticulitermes termites are multiple-piece nesters (species which nest in multiple wood pieces connected by aboveground and underground tunnels)22,23, it is difficult to accurately determine the actual colony size in forests. The colonies of R. virginicus used in our study were collected from foraging areas, and the colony size of each colony was unknown. Thus, it is necessary to confirm in the future whether the colony-dependent dose response to aggregation pheromone truly reflects colony size. The third hypothesis may explain the level of response of workers in colony A to the artificial aggregation pheromone. As mentioned above, colonies of R. virginicus occupy large foraging areas20,21, suggesting that there is territorial competition among conspecific colonies over foraging areas. Nestmate recognition mechanisms must be at work in this process. Although it is hypothesized that termites recognize colony differences based on differences in composition ratio of CHCs24 or colony odor25–27, the mechanism of nestmate recognition remains unclear in termites. In this study, R. virginicus workers were nearly unresponsive to the hexane fraction containing CHCs (Fig. 1A). Also, they were nearly unresponsive to compounds other than aggregation pheromone components (verbenone, cholesta-3,5-diene, and oleic acid) detected by GC–MS analysis (Fig. S1 in the electronic supplementary materials), and we could not detect any colony-specific volatile in GC–MS analysis. Therefore, the compounds other than the aggregation pheromone components that contributed to the attractant activity of the crude extract in colony A remain unknown. Probably, some compound that was not detected by GC–MS may have been involved, or not only the mixture of 3-octanone, 3-octanol, and palmitic acid but also CHCs contribute to an attractant activity only in colony A. In summary, the colony-dependent differences in the dose response of R. virginicus can be explained both by differences in response thresholds to aggregation pheromone by colony and by the possibility that volatile components other than aggregation pheromone components (or CHCs) are also involved in attracting workers in some colonies. Future work on the relationship between response thresholds of aggregation pheromones, colony size, and nestmate recognition will provide a better understanding of how foraging strategies change with termite colony growth.

The aggregation pheromone of R. virginicus had only an attractant activity and no arrestant activity (Fig. 1A), unlike the aggregation pheromones of R. speratus and C. formosanus. In R. speratus, the major components of CHCs (n-pentacosane and n-heptacosane) and cholesterol are involved as aggregation pheromone components9. In C. formosanus, although the blend of fatty acids alone shows a high arrestant activity, the addition of cholesterol and CHCs further enhances the arrestant activity11. These indicate that even if R. virginicus workers find new decaying wood suitable for nesting and attract nestmate workers with their aggregation pheromone, they do not stay at the pheromone source as well as workers of R. speratus and C. formosanus do9,11. Because the foraging behavior of R. virginicus workers is sensitive to temperature and moisture content of soil20, it is likely that the workers move frequently within the foraging area depending on the weather conditions at the time. Therefore, non-volatile compounds such as cholesterol and CHCs, which would have been suitable for eliciting arrestant activity, were probably not needed as aggregation pheromone components, and R. virginicus may have developed an aggregation pheromone consisting solely of volatile components that diffuse quickly into the nest.

3-Octanone and 3-octanol are the main components of the odor from post-mortem workers of R. flavipes and are considered volatile early death cues28,29. These previous studies have shown that workers generate 3-octanone and 3-octanol approximately 30 min to 1 h after death, and workers are particularly attracted to 3-octanol28,29. However, unlike oleic acid, which has been shown to elicit corpse burial behavior28, these two volatile components have not been proven to directly cue distinct corpse recognition (processing) behaviors such as corpse retrieval, cannibalism, and burial. Since recognition of dead bodies is an important hygienic behavior in social insects, it is not surprising that these volatile compounds would show a strong attractant activity if they were indeed used for early detection of dead bodies. In fact, however, 3-octanone and 3-octanol showed only low attraction activity by themselves in R. virginicus (AI values were around 0.1, see Fig. S1 in the electronic supplementary materials). After being attracted to 3-octanone and 3-octanol, the workers simply stood at the odor source (or walked around it slowly) and did not exhibit any special behavior. Also, in R. speratus, these two volatiles were detected or not detected depending on worker extract samples9,30, and in C. formosanus, they were not detected in workers11,31. Thus, it is questionable whether 3-octanone and 3-octanol function as a widespread volatile early death cue in heterotermitid termites. Although these volatile compounds may be produced from components of worker’s body fluids and leaked out of the body after death in R. flavipes, at least in R. virginicus, these volatiles appear to be simply part of the aggregation pheromone component.

Our study demonstrated that workers of R. virginicus use an attractant aggregation pheromone and show colony-dependent dose response to the pheromone. This pheromone has no arrestant activity and consists of only three compounds including a short-chain ketone, an alcohol, and a volatile saturated fatty acid. This pheromone composition differs considerably from that of R. speratus, which consists of one aromatic compound, CHCs, two fatty acids and cholesterol9, and from that of C. formosanus, which consists of four fatty acids11. These facts imply that in R. virginicus, when workers maintain a group of nestmates at a new feeding site, a positive feedback occurs in which workers attracted by the attractant aggregation pheromone also release the same pheromone, resulting in a concentration gradient of the pheromone toward the feeding site and maintaining the worker group at the feeding site. Also, it is possible that the aggregation pheromone consisting of only volatile components fits the behavioral pattern of the workers, which move from one area to another in response to changes in temperature and moisture of wood and soil20. This research provides new insights into the evolutionary processes shaping aggregation pheromones in termites.

Materials and methods

Termite collection

Six colonies (colonies A–F) of R. virginicus were collected from forests in Huntsville and College Station, Texas, USA. Colonies A, B and E were collected in Lick Creek Park (College Station) in August 2022 and January 2023. Colonies C, D, and F were collected at the Sam Houston University Center for Biological Field Studies (Huntsville) in September 2022 and May 2023. Termites were identified based on morphological characters of the soldiers32 and comparison of the chemical profile of CHCs between a previous study33 and our GC–MS data (see the Results section). Each of six colonies were kept in a plastic box with a BPC medium made from loblolly pinewood19 at room temperature (about 25 °C) until used for experiments.

Chemicals

n-Hexane was purchased from Fisher Scientific International Inc. Hampton, NH, USA. DEE, 3-octanone, 3-octanol, (–)-verbenone, cholesta-3,5-diene, palmitic acid, oleic acid, n-octacosane, and silica gel (Silica gel 60, 0.063–0.200 mm particle size) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Inc., St. Louis, MO, USA.

Whole-body extraction of pheromone components from R. virginicus workers

To extract chemicals from R. virginicus workers, 100 individuals of the same size from each colony were extracted in a 5 mL glass vial with 1 mL hexane for 24 h. Crude extracts were derived from workers of four colonies (A–D). These extracts, with an equivalent concentration of about 0.1 worker/μL, were transferred to fresh 1.5 mL glass vials and stored at − 20 °C until needed. A 500 μL portion of the crude extract was subjected to chromatography on a silica gel column (6 mm inner diameter) containing 0.5 g silica gel. Elution of the column was sequential, starting with hexane (2 mL), followed by two DEE concentrations in hexane (2 mL each of 30% DEE and 50% DEE), and concluded with 100% DEE (2 mL). Aliquots of 200 μL from each fraction were transferred to a 200 μL glass conical insert in a 1.5 mL autosampler vial and then were reduced to 25 μL (equating to 0.2 workers/μL) with a gentle nitrogen stream. Subsequent chemical identification was performed through gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis. The leftover portion from each fraction was stored at − 20 °C.

GC–MS analysis

GC–MS analyses were executed on an Agilent Mass Spectrometer 5977B (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) in conjunction with an Agilent GC 7890B (Agilent Technologies). A sample volume of 2 μL was injected in splitless mode using an Agilent autosampler 7693 into an HP-5MS column (30 m length × 0.25 mm inner diameter × 0.25 μm film thickness, Agilent Technologies) with helium as the carrier gas (1.90 mL/min constant flow rate). The column temperature was held at 50 °C for 2 min, increased to 320 °C at 15 °C/min, and held at 320 °C for 10 min. The injection port was consistently held at 280 °C. MS data acquisition conditions were as follows: 34.59 μA ionization current, 70 eV ionization energy, 1217.54 V accelerating voltage, and a scan range from 40–550 m/z. Both the gas chromatographic system and the mass spectrometer were controlled by the MassHunter GC/MS Acquisition B.07.06.2704 (Agilent Technologies). Data interpretation was facilitated by the Openchrom software Lablicate Edition ver. 1.5.034. Candidate compounds were predicted from the Mass Bank database (NIST format, release version 2022.06)35. Pheromone components were identified according to Kovats indices and mass spectra, in comparison with commercial standards, and CHCs were estimated based on Kovats indices and mass spectra in comparison with a previous report 33. Methyl branch positions of mono- and dimethylalkanes were determined from characteristic mass fragments of their respective mass spectra36 as well as by their calculated Kovats indices37. The positions of double bonds were not determined.

Bioassay

Two-choice bioassays were conducted as described in a previous study9,11,38. Prior to the bioassays, around 1,200 termites from each colony were housed in a 140 mm plastic dish with a moistened filter paper (90 mm in diameter) under dark conditions at 25 °C for 2 days. After this acclimation period, groups of 20 workers were introduced into 90 mm plastic dishes containing two filter papers (30 mm in diameter) positioned on opposing sides of the dishes, which had sanded surfaces. The ‘test’ paper was treated with an extract (or fraction or authentic standard solution), while the solvent ‘control’ paper was treated with solvent only. Both papers were air-dried for roughly 5 min to evaporate solvents. We closed the lid of the dish and tested attractant activities of chemicals under no airflow condition during bioassays according to previous studies9,11,38 because our preliminary test revealed that termite workers flee downwind even in a very weak airflow. Vertical photographs of each dish were taken using an iPhone 8 (Apple Inc., Cupertino, CA, USA) to count the number of workers on both the test and control papers.

Search for active fractions of whole-body worker extract.

Search for active fractions of whole-body worker extract.

To compare aggregation activity among fractions (hexane, 30% DEE, 50% DEE, and DEE), workers from two colonies (A and B) were used. Ten replications per colony were made for each treatment (totaling 20 replications per treatment). For each replicate of each fraction treatment, a 40 μL aliquot of fraction solution (equivalent to 1 worker) was added to the test paper, and the same amount of solvent (hexane) was added to the control paper. In the positive control treatment, 10 µL worker crude extract (1 worker equivalent in hexane) made from the same colony of the worker used in the bioassays was added to the test papers and compared to a hexane-only solvent control. In the negative control treatment, 10 μL of hexane was added to the test paper and compared to a hexane-only solvent control. The number of workers on the test and control papers were recorded after 5, 60, 120, and 240 min to confirm both the attractant and arrestant activities of pheromone components.

(2)Determination of pheromone components.

Determination of pheromone components.

To determine pheromone components, we compared the attraction activity among various combinations of candidate compounds using workers from two colonies (A and D) for each fraction. Experimental process details are provided in Text S1.

(3)Dose response of aggregation pheromone.

Dose response of aggregation pheromone.

The attractant activity of the RvAP Mix [3-octanone: 3-octanol: palmitic acid = 5: 0.8: 1,337 (ng/paper)] was compared with the activities of two dilutions (RvAP Mix × 0.1 and RvAP Mix × 0.01), using workers from three colonies (A, D, and F). Ten replications per colony were made for each treatment. For each replicate of RvAP Mix treatments, 10 μL Mix solution in hexane (equivalent to 1 worker) was added to the test paper, and the same amount of hexane solvent was added to the control paper. In the positive control treatment, 10 µL worker crude extract (1 worker equivalent) made from the same colony of worker used in the bioassays was added to the test papers. In the negative control treatment, 10 μL hexane was added to the test paper. The number of workers on the test and control papers were recorded after 5 min to confirm the attractant activity of RvAP Mix solutions.

The aggregation level was quantified by an aggregation index (AI), defined as (NTest − NControl)/NTotal, where NTest is the worker count on the test paper, NControl represents the count on the control papers, and NTotal is the total workers in a bioassay dish (i.e., 20 workers). The index takes a value of − 1 to 1, and positive AI values indicates that more workers gather at test papers than at control papers. For statistical analysis, following our previous studies11,38, we compared the proportion of the total number of workers on the test papers to the total number of workers on both test and control papers among treatments, using Fisher’s exact tests. P-values were adjusted for multiple testing based on the Bonferroni correction. Also, to confirm the difference in dose response pattern against RvAP Mix solutions among colonies, we used an ordinal logistic regression followed by likelihood ratio tests. In the model, we scored the worker position on a bioassay dish using a three-point scale as follows: 3 = presence on control paper, 2 = presence on a test paper, and 1 = absence on both papers. The worker position was the response variable, and the colony, the treatment, the interaction between colony and treatment were the fixed factors. Both statistical analysis and data visualization were executed in R software ver. 4.4.0 39.

Quantification of pheromone components from worker body parts

Termite workers have numerous exocrine glands located in different body regions, including the mandibles, labium, salivary glands, legs, and sternal gland40, but it has been unclear from which part of the body the aggregation pheromone is secreted. Also, it is possible that termites produce the aggregation pheromone in their gut, because in cockroaches, which are closely related to termites, aggregation substances are contained in their feces41. To ascertain from which body parts the worker’s pheromone components are produced or secreted, we dissected workers into head, salivary glands, foregut (crop), midgut, hindgut, and the rest of the body parts (including thorax, legs, and abdomen, referred to as “others”). Subsequently, to determine which specific part of the head, thorax, or abdomen produces or secretes aggregation pheromone components, we dissected workers into head capsule, mandibles, labium, thorax, legs, sternal gland, and abdomen (excluding sternal gland). We used workers derived from four colonies (A, D, E, and F) and pooled 10 individuals for each body part and each colony to extract pheromone components in sufficient quantities to be detectable by GC–MS analysis. Each body part sample was extracted in a 1.5 mL glass vial containing 200 μL hexane for 24 h. The hexane extract was evaporated without desiccation using a gentle nitrogen stream and re-dissolved in 20 μL hexane solution containing 200 ng of n-octacosane as an internal standard for quantifying the aggregation pheromone components by using GC–MS analysis. For statistical analysis, we compared the amount of each compound among body parts using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test (P < 0.05). Both statistical analysis and data visualization were executed in R software ver. 4.4.039.
