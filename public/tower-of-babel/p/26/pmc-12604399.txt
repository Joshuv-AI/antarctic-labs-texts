Association between eating alone and motor function among community-dwelling older adults during the COVID-19 pandemic: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Ryosuke Mikiya, Chika Momoki, Yuko Sawada, Daiki Habu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study examined the association between motor function and dietary environment, depressive symptoms, cognitive function, and nutritional status during the stay-at-home period due to COVID-19 in community-dwelling older adults.MethodsThe questionnaire survey was conducted from 1 March to 24 April 2021, when the state of emergency by COVID-19 was lifted. A questionnaire was distributed to 900 community-dwelling older adults whose residence was known by the Social Welfare Council, which is responsible for community welfare, and the Community General Support Center, which provides comprehensive support for older adults. Of the 400 participants, 237 (mean age 73.1 ± 6.0 years) were analysed, excluding 66 with missing data, 45 with suspected cognitive decline (Dementia Assessment Sheet for Community-based Integrated Care System–8 items [DASC-8] score of ≥ 11), 9 with neurological or mental problems (according to Mini Nutritional Assessment–Short Form [MNA®-SF]), and 43 who needed support or long-term care. Motor function was verified using the Motor Fitness Scale (MFS). MFS scores of ≤ 11 and ≤ 9 for males and females, respectively, were assigned to participants in the low MFS group (n = 65) and others in the normal MFS group (n = 172).We collected questionnaire data regarding the presence or absence of eating alone, nutritional status (MNA®-SF), DASC-8 (cognitive function), and 15-item Geriatric Depression Scale (depressive symptoms). Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with low MFS.ResultsThe low MFS group had a significantly lower proportion of patients aged ≥ 75 years (P = 0.001, Cramer’s V = 0.215), a higher proportion of eating alone (P = 0.052, Cramer’s V = 0.135), and participants assessed as at risk of malnutrition using the MNA®-SF. Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that eating alone (odds ratio [OR]: 4.08, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.39–11.99) was independently associated with low MFS. The risk of malnutrition as assessed using MNA®-SF was associated with MFS, although it was not a significant factor (OR: 2.27, 95%CI: 0.96–5.38).ConclusionsEating alone was independently associated with low motor function in community-dwelling older adults during the COVID-19 pandemic. We proposed providing opportunities to eat together as a highly feasible intervention measure to reduce motor function decline in community-dwelling older adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06557-6.

Background

This study aimed to investigate the effects of severe restrictions on social participation and face-to-face negotiation implemented as measures against the COVID-19 pandemic on the motor function of community-dwelling older adults. The study question was to test the hypothesis that the motor function of community-dwelling older adults is influenced by the individuals’ depressive symptoms, cognitive function, nutritional status, and dietary environment, such as the presence or absence of eating alone under the life activity restriction.

Studies examining the motor function of community-dwelling older adults frequently collected motor function data by conducting performance tests, including muscle strength and walking speed tests [1, 2]. These assessments are performed during health check-ups conducted in settings outside the home, and the motor functions of older adults who do not attend these health check-ups have rarely been examined.

The Motor Fitness Scale (MFS), a self-administered questionnaire, is one of the methods for evaluating the motor function of older adults. The MFS comprises a 14-point scale consisting of the following three subscales: six items of mobility ability of life strength, four items of muscle strength, and four items of balance ability. Higher scores indicate higher subjective motor function [3, 4].

The nutritional status (Mini Nutritional Assessment–Short Form [MNA®-SF]) [5], depressive symptoms (15-item Geriatric Depression Scale [GDS15]) [6], cognitive function (Dementia Assessment Sheet for Community-based Integrated Care System–8 items [DASC-8]) [7], and dietary environment (whether eating alone or not), which were examined as factors that may affect motor function, were also evaluated using self-administered questionnaires distributed and mailed to community-dwelling older adults.

In this study, we examined the motor function of community-dwelling older adults and various factors affecting their motor function without waiting for the lifting of the restrictions on movement, despite prolonged stay-at-home restrictions, such as those experienced during the COVID-19 pandemic. We planned to obtain basic data to implement timely measures and responses.

Methods

Study design

This study used a cross-sectional design.

Participants

The participants were community-dwelling older adults aged ≥65 years, where the university to which the author belongs has been cooperating continuously. The participants were 900 people who were registered with the social welfare council established in the prefecture and municipality and the community comprehensive support centre, which provides comprehensive support for older adults in the community, and whose residence could be confirmed during the survey. During the period from 1 March to 24 April 2021, when the state of emergency declared due to the COVID-19 pandemic was lifted, self-administered questionnaires were distributed face-to-face by regional coordinators at postings and welfare halls in 14 regions managed by the social welfare council. The completed questionnaires were collected by mail to the university. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Morinomiya University of Medical Care (approval number: 2020-082) and was conducted with the consent of the participants.

Sample size determination

G*Power 3.1.9.2 was used to calculate the sample size. Based on a previous study [4], the sample size was calculated as 25% of the people with reduced motor function, with effect size, alpha error, and power of 0.5, 0.05, and 0.8, respectively. The sample size for groups 1 and 2 was calculated as 166 and 42 cases, respectively, with 208 total number of cases. The questionnaire collection rate was set at 60% [8], and the target distribution number was set as 350 questionnaires.

Outcome

The primary outcome was motor function, and MFS was used for evaluation. Since no standard value exists for MFS, we used the cut-off value (≤ 11 and ≤ 9 points for males and females, respectively) [4], which has been shown to be associated with the risk of long-term care needs in previous studies, as the low MFS group.

Survey items

We prepared a self-administered questionnaire integrating the following items: general information including age, sex, household situation (living alone or not), whether or not the person was certified as requiring support or care, height, and weight. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated using the height and weight measurements.

The assessment items were motor function, nutritional status, dietary status, cognitive and daily functioning, degree of depressive symptoms, and educational history (less than a high school, high school, and university).

Nutritional status was assessed using the MNA®-SF [5, 9]. Six items related to nutritional status (declined food intake over 3 months, weight loss over 3 months, gait status, presence of psychological problems, and BMI) were investigated. The participants were categorised into the following two groups: normal group with a score of ≥12 and at risk of low nutritional status with a score of ≤11.

Eating alone group was defined as eating alone on most days (at least once a week). Previous studies have defined ‘eating together’ as eating with someone at least once a day [10]; however, no set definition exists for solitary eating, and the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries in Japan conducts surveys almost daily and 4–5 times a week. Therefore, we defined eating alone as more than once a week. In contrast, the communal eating group was defined as eating with someone on more than 1 day per week.

The DASC-8 was used to assess cognitive and life function. The DASC-8 is a shortened version of the DASC-21 [7]; however, they correlate well, and the DASC-8 has good internal consistency.

The Japanese version of the GDS15, a scale used for older adults, was used to assess the degree of depressive symptoms. A score of ≥6 was considered as depressive symptoms, and a score of ≤5 was considered normal [6].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA), and the two groups of patients with low and normal MFS were compared. For comparisons of nominal variables, the χ2 or Fisher’s exact probability tests are expressed as the number of cases (%). Quantitative variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test and expressed as median (interquartile range). Cohen’s criteria were used to evaluate the effect sizes, which were set as small (0.10), medium (0.30), and large (0.50). The effect sizes for the nominal variables were calculated using the χ2 value, Cramer’s V, and those for the quantitative variables were calculated using the z-value, r.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with low MFS scores. To avoid multicollinearity, six independent variables were assessed as factors (sex, age, household status, MNA®-SF, eating alone, and GDS15). Age was entered as a continuous variable, and multicollinearity was confirmed via the variance inflation factor. Multivariate results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), with p<0.05 indicating the level of statistical significance for all statistical analyses.

Results

Overall, 400 people responded by post (response rate, 44.4%). Since this was a survey using a questionnaire, we aimed to ensure the reliability of the responses by excluding 45 participants with a score of ≤11 on the DASC-8 and 9 individuals with neurological or mental problems as assessed using the MNA®-SF. Furthermore, we excluded 66 individuals with missing MFS data and 43 individuals with motor dysfunctions that are difficult to recover and require support or long-term care that may affect the MFS evaluation. Discussion

Why was eating alone significantly associated with motor function in older adults? Many reports have indicated that motor function in older adults is related to depressive symptoms and nutritional status.

First, regarding the relationship with depressive symptoms, older adults who eat alone are reported to have depression tendencies, low BMI, and poor food intake diversity [11]. Additionally, it has been reported that depressive symptoms are associated with decreased grip strength in older adults [12]. Furthermore, a depression tendency has been reported as a predictor of reduced motor function after 20 years [13]. In the low MFS group, the proportion of participants with depressive symptoms was significantly high. This result aligns with those of previous studies, suggesting that eating alone among older adults was related to motor function decline through depression.

Regarding nutritional status, the multivariate analysis was not significant; however, a significantly higher proportion of those with low MFS scores were at risk of malnutrition. As mentioned above, older adults who eat alone have a low BMI and poor diversity of food intake [11]. A decrease in food diversity and anorexia also lead to malnutrition [14]. The proportion of males in the low MFS group was higher than that in the normal group. These results suggest that males tend to eat alone because they rarely participate in communal eating [15]. The lack of diversity in food intake associated with eating alone may be linked to the risk of malnutrition. In this study, we conducted a sub-analysis; the proportion of eating alone was lower in males. However, OR for low MFS was higher in females. It suggests that males may be more prone to low motor function due to eating alone [see Additional files 1 and 2]. A previous study reported that older adults whose nutritional status improved within the 1-year study period maintained their motor function [16]. Depressive symptoms and decreased activity associated with extreme restrictions on going out during the COVID-19 pandemic may have contributed to the risk of malnutrition. The nutritional status of older adults is complicated by the physiological, social, and economic problems associated with aging [17], and further studies are needed.

The initial response to the COVID-19 pandemic in Japan was extremely strict regarding total quarantine and social activity restrictions compared to the rest of the world, and it can be assumed that this initial response significantly impacted the motor function of older adults [18].

Aside from COVID-19, other infections may cause behavioural restrictions in the future. Assessment and efforts to maintain motor function in older adults while considering infection control should be established [19]. Previous reports have shown that in older adults, eating alone is associated with their health status [20]; however, this is the first study to show that it is associated with low motor function. Eating alone can be alleviated by the efforts of the surroundings and the community. This study highlights the need to pay attention not only to nutritional status but also to the eating situation of older adults. We also believe that this study demonstrates an approach to conducting surveys in environments where face-to-face interactions are challenging.

This study examined the effects of social participation and restrictions on interpersonal contact due to the COVID-19 pandemic on the motor function of community-dwelling older adults in a district in Japan. It examined the effects of Japan’s unique measures to cope with the COVID-19 pandemic on a limited number of participants. Furthermore, the environment surrounding community-dwelling older adults in this study is highly specific but highly individualised. For example, the Japanese diet is unique not only in Western developed countries but also in other Asian and Southeast Asian countries [21]. However, from a different perspective, it is considered to be a universal research topic because it may play a role in providing concrete and powerful hints for estimating the effects on motor function of community-dwelling older adults who are restricted from going out, restraining social behaviour, and prohibiting contact with others for a certain period (including natural disasters and wars) and considering measures to reduce such adverse effects.

This study had some limitations. First, the questionnaire responses were provided voluntarily, and we could not assess those who did not complete the questionnaire. The response rate in this study was 44.4%, which is lower than the 63.5% response rate of other questionnaires for older adults living in large cities [8]; therefore, it is not representative of older adults living in the region. The low response rate may be attributed to the burden due to the large number of question items. Additionally, the power of the survey was insufficient due to the large number of excluded participants. Second, the DASC-8 excluded cases of suspected cognitive decline; however, the self-administered questionnaire may have included some participants with mild cognitive decline. Third, as this was a cross-sectional study, the causal relationship between low MFS and other factors, such as eating alone and nutritional status, remains unclear. Fourth, since the study included older adults living in a single area, the results of this study may not be applicable to older adults in other areas. In the future, longitudinal follow-up studies are needed to clarify the causal relationships among eating alone, nutritional status, and other factors with motor function decline.

Conclusion

This study showed that eating alone is independently associated with low motor function in community-dwelling older adults during the COVID-19 pandemic. The initial response to the COVID-19 pandemic in Japan was extremely strict regarding total quarantine and social activity restrictions compared to the rest of the world, and it can be assumed that this initial response significantly impacted the motor function of older adults. Based on this study’s results, we propose that an opportunity to eat together is provided as a highly feasible intervention measure to reduce motor function decline in community-dwelling older adults.
