Prevalence and associated factors of schistosomiasis among domestic ruminants in Ethiopia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yihenew Getahun Ambaw, Simachew Getaneh Endalamew, Ambachew Motbaynor Wubaye, Simegnew Adugna Kallu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSchistosomiasis is a significant concern for animal and public health, particularly in low-income and rural areas with limited access to clean water and healthcare services. Evidence on the national pooled prevalence and risk factors of ruminant schistosomiasis in Ethiopia is limited, underscoring the need for further research to inform effective control strategies. This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in line with the PRISMA guidelines. An extensive review was conducted across five academic databases (PubMed, Google Scholar, ScienceDirect, Scopus, and Web of Science), with all searches completed between May 10, 2025, and June 1, 2025. Heterogeneity was assessed, and a random-effects model was used to determine the pooled prevalence of schistosomiasis in ruminants. The sources of variation were analyzed through subgroup analysis, meta-regression, and sensitivity analysis. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots, Begg's test, and Egger’s test. A total of 33 studies were included in this meta-analysis, revealing a pooled prevalence of schistosomiasis in ruminants of 14.4 %, 95 % CI: [11.2-17.6]. The analysis demonstrated a substantial degree of heterogeneity among studies, with I² = 98.2 %, Q=1764.5 and p < 0.001. Among the variables analyzed for heterogeneity, the species of domestic ruminant and geographical region were identified as significant risk factors. Subgroup analysis revealed that the prevalence of schistosomiasis varied by species: 15.9 %, 95 % CI: [12.5–19.3] in cattle, 8.4 %, 95 % CI: [0.0–20.3] in sheep, and 3.0 %, 95 % CI: [0.0–31.9] in goats. According to the geographical region-based subgroup meta-analysis, the Amhara region exhibited the highest prevalence among domestic ruminants at 18.6 %, 95 % CI: [15.4–21.7], followed by the Oromia region at 12.8 %, 95 % CI: [4.8–20.9]. This systematic review and meta-analysis highlight that schistosomiasis remains a persistent threat to domestic ruminants in Ethiopia. Thus, veterinary and public health professionals, research institutions, livestock owners, and policymakers are encouraged to collaborate in designing and implementing effective prevention and control strategies for schistosomiasis.Keywords: Ethiopia, Meta-analysis, Prevalence, Schistosomiasis, Ruminants

Introduction

Schistosomiasis is a neglected tropical disease of major global concern and ranks the second most significant parasitic disease in terms of socioeconomic impact, following malaria (Ekloh et al., 2024). It affects over 250 million people across 78 countries (Gebrehana et al., 2024), with an estimated 779 million people at risk, particularly in low-income regions with limited access to clean water and sanitation (Peng et al., 2025). Sub-Saharan Africa, which is home to only 13 % of the world’s population, accounts for nearly 90 % of all schistosomiasis cases. The disease causes an estimated 280,000 deaths annually (Peng et al., 2025).

Although much attention has been focused on the human burden of schistosomiasis, several Schistosoma species also infect livestock, creating serious veterinary and economic challenges (Díaz et al., 2023). Among domestic animals, especially ruminants, schistosomiasis is primarily caused by Schistosoma bovis (cattle and sheep), Schistosoma mattheei (cattle), and Schistosoma curassoni (goats and sheep) (You et al., 2018). These parasites cause significant health and productivity losses in African farming systems (Rinaldo et al., 2021). Furthermore, growing evidence of hybridization between human and animal Schistosoma species raises concerns about cross-species transmission and the emergence of novel zoonotic strains (Stothard et al., 2020). In Ethiopia, livestock are central to livelihoods and economic growth, particularly in rural areas (Erdaw, 2023). However, parasitic infections such as schistosomiasis continue to limit animal health, productivity, and ultimately, public health and food security (Megersa et al., 2024).

Ethiopia’s varied agro-ecological zones and widespread use of surface water for irrigation and livestock create favorable conditions for the transmission of snail-borne trematodes (Megersa et al., 2024). In the life cycle of schistosomiasis, the intermediate host is a freshwater snail, while the final or definitive host is a mammal. The intermediate hosts (snails) of trematodes in Ethiopia include Lymnaea, Biomphalaria, and Bulinus species. These snails have been identified in the Batu, Hawasa, and Asela areas (Megersa et al., 2024).

Schistosomiasis was first reported in Ethiopian livestock in the mid-20th century, with initial diagnoses relying on clinical signs and post-mortem examinations (Tolossa, 2022). Advances in technology have introduced copro-logical, serological, and molecular diagnostic methods. Ethiopia has implemented several national initiatives to combat schistosomiasis in livestock, including awareness campaigns, veterinary services, mass drug administration, integrated control strategies, and research collaborations (Tolossa, 2022).

Despite this risk, the distribution and risk factors of schistosomiasis in animals remain poorly understood. In Ethiopia, existing reports of ruminant schistosomiasis varied from 0.0 % (Kebede et al., 2023) to 32.3 % (Kerie and Seyoum, 2016b) and bear a high burden of human schistosomiasis; around 5 million people are infected, and 37.5 million remain at risk (Negussu et al., 2017). These studies are scattered, often localized, and vary in design, making it challenging to develop a comprehensive national-level understanding (Dagnaw et al., 2023).

In addition, WHO has set ambitious goals such as controlling morbidity by 2020, eradicating schistosomiasis as a public health issue by 2025, and interrupting transmission in member states and selected countries in Africa by 2025 (WHO, 2013). These control plans made significant progress in reducing the prevalence of schistosomiasis in Ethiopia (Gebrehana et al., 2024). However, several challenges remain that hinder the achievement of the objectives, including logistical issues with drug distribution, limited access to clean water, and insufficient ongoing community engagement activities (MoH, 2021). To achieve these objectives, it is essential to have a comprehensive understanding of the disease context in sub-Saharan Africa, as well as the role of various animal populations in the ongoing transmission of the disease in endemic regions (Kokaliaris et al., 2022).

Despite its substantial public health and economic implications over the past decades, there is still insufficient evidence on the national pooled prevalence and factors associated with ruminant schistosomiasis in Ethiopia, indicating a need for further study to develop effective control strategies and public health interventions. To address these gaps, a systematic review and meta-analysis of studies published between 2016 and 2025 was conducted. Since 2016, Ethiopia has experienced significant progress in agriculture, veterinary science, and livestock management, which reflect current trends and challenges. This study aimed to estimate the pooled national prevalence of schistosomiasis in domestic ruminants in Ethiopia and identify the most significant contributing factors. Our findings support disease control strategies, inform veterinary health policy, and highlight the zoonotic importance of animal schistosomiasis within the One Health framework.

Materials and methods

Establishment of the review methodology

This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the Condition, Context, and Population (CoCoPop) outline (Munn et al., 2015) to structure its findings. It adhered to the guidelines established by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Protocols 2020 (Parums, 2021) to ensure thoroughness in the development, execution, and reporting of the protocol. Before starting data collection, the study protocol was registered in PROSPERO (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews) on May 18, 2025 with (Registration ID: CRD420251049876).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The present study systematically analyzed all cross-sectional research articles published in peer-reviewed journals about the prevalence of schistosomiasis in domestic ruminants in Ethiopia. To ensure the incorporation of the most recent and pertinent evidence, only studies conducted between 2016 and June 2025 were considered. The scope of the research includes peer-reviewed journals, published in English that reported on the prevalence of schistosomiasis in domestic ruminants. Exclusion criteria for the research articles included: (a) a lack of sufficient information or the presence of missing outcomes, (b) studies focused on knowledge, attitudes, and practices concerning schistosomiasis in domestic ruminants, (c) personal opinions, conference proceedings, and review articles.

Literature search strategy

For this systematic review and meta-analysis, data were collected from five prominent academic databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Google Scholar, Science Direct, and Web of Science. To collect all pertinent articles from the existing databases, MeSH terms and keywords associated with the study population were applied with Boolean operators (OR, AND) to combine search terms properly. The literature search strategy, prepared for PubMed database initially, was modified to use in other databases and comprised the following structure: (Schistosomiasis) [MeSH Terms] OR (Schistosoma) OR ("Schistosoma bovis") OR ("Schistosoma mattheei") OR ("Schistosoma curassoni") OR (Bilharzia) AND (ruminants) [MeSH Terms] OR (cattle) OR (bovine) OR (sheep) OR (goats) [MeSH Terms] AND (prevalence) OR ("risk factors") OR (epidemiology) AND (Ethiopia) [MeSH Terms]. All full-text retrieved articles were imported and organized using EndNote software version 20, and duplicate records were removed through a manual review process by title and abstract. Further studies were identified manually by cross-referencing eligible studies, and a literature search was initiated on May 10, 2025, and completed on June 1, 2025.

Assessment of study quality and risk of bias

The quality of the studies included in this systematic review and meta-analysis was assessed using the critical appraisal tool from the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI), specifically designed for prevalence studies (Munn et al., 2015). This tool consists of nine items, each evaluated with one of four possible responses: yes, no, unclear, or not applicable. Two independent teams conducted the evaluation of the selected studies: the first team, consisting of SAK and AMW, and the second team, which included YGA and SGE. The teams utilized a four-level rating system; “yes,” “no,” “unclear,” and “not applicable”, to determine the quality of the articles. Any disagreements among the evaluators were resolved through collaborative group discussions and by consulting a panel of expert researchers (both teams). In the scoring system, each “yes” and “not applicable” response received a score of 1, while “no” or “unclear” responses (including instances where information was absent or deemed irrelevant) received 0 points. The total scores for each article were summed on a scale ranging from 0 to 9. Based on these scores, the studies were categorized into three quality levels: good (7–9 points), medium (4–6 points), and low (0–3 points). However, in this systematic review and meta-analysis, all 33 articles included in the final analysis received a score of at least four points, classifying them as having high or moderate quality.

Data extraction among eligible studies

The initial search results underwent a relevance screening process, allowing only studies that met the predefined inclusion criteria to advance the analysis phase. A standardized data extraction template was used for collecting information, carried out independently by two research teams. Any discrepancies in the collected data were resolved through discussions among the teams. The data extraction template included essential study parameters such as the author's name, species of ruminants studied, year of publication, geographic region or location, diagnostic methods, sample size, duration of data collection in months, and prevalence of ruminant schistosomiasis. All collected information was carefully organized and cross-verified using Microsoft Excel spreadsheet version 13 to ensure accuracy and consistency throughout the review process.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Retrieved articles were evaluated for eligibility by examining the full text, and variables that needed for analysis were registered. The study's results were presented using tables and forest plots. Data analysis was performed using the R programming language version 4.5.1 (R Core Team, 2025). Variance was estimated using the DerSimonian and Laird method, and a random-effects meta-analysis was conducted to account for study variability (DerSimonian and Laird, 2015). A forest plot was utilized to visually synthesize the distribution of schistosomiasis among domestic ruminants, displaying the 95 % confidence intervals (CI) associated with data from various studies. The Cochrane Q test was employed to quantify methodological variation between these studies (Cochran, 1954), along with the I2 statistic, which measures the proportion of total variability attributable to heterogeneity (Higgins and Thompson, 2002), and CI to estimate the potential range of true effects (IntHout et al., 2016). To investigate sources of variability, subgroup analyses were conducted based on geographic location, species of domestic ruminants, sample sizes of the studies, and publication years. A sensitivity analysis was performed by systematically removing one study at a time to assess the reliability of the overall evidence. Variables such as ruminant species and geographic location were analyzed using both univariable and multivariable meta-regression models within a random-effects framework to determine their impact on variability. To assess publication bias, Egger's regression, Begg's correlation test, and funnel plot symmetry checks were employed.

Results

Selection and identification of studies

During the initial phase of the systematic review process, 1,500 studies were screened for their relevance to the research question. Following this initial screening, 395 studies were excluded due to duplication. Additionally, 1,152 studies were removed after a detailed review of their titles and abstracts because they did not meet the inclusion criteria. Moreover, 20 studies were reduced for various reasons, including incomplete reporting of outcomes and published before 2016. Ultimately, 33 studies were included in the final systematic review and meta-analysis, as illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Study selection using a PRISMA flow diagram for a systematic review and meta-analysis of domestic ruminant schistosomiasis in Ethiopia, 2025.

Description of the included studies

This systematic review and meta-analysis included data from 33 studies published between 2016 and 2025, incorporating both field and abattoir settings. The total sample comprised 11,678 domestic ruminants, of which 1,718 animals (14.71 %) were positive for ruminant schistosomiasis. The primary geographical focus was on the Amhara (20) and Oromia (7) regions, which accounted for the majority of research documenting the prevalence of ruminant schistosomiasis in Ethiopia during the study period. Most studies were on bovine (28) species, followed by ovine (3) and caprine (2) species, respectively. Sample sizes across the included studies ranged from 112 to 768 ruminants, with the apparent prevalence of schistosomiasis in the study areas varying between 0.0 % and 32.3 % (Table 1).

Pooled prevalence of schistosomiasis among domestic ruminants in Ethiopia

The results of the meta-analysis indicated a significant level of heterogeneity across the included studies (I2= 98.2 %, Q(32) = 1764.5, p < 0.001). As a result, a random effects model was employed to estimate the overall prevalence of domestic ruminant schistosomiasis. According to this model, the estimated prevalence of schistosomiasis among ruminants was 14.4%, 95 % CI: [11.2; 17.6] (Fig. 2). The 95 % prediction interval was [0.0; 31.3], which represents the range within which the results of a future study, based on the same population, are expected to fall (Fig. 2).

Forest plot for pooled prevalence of schistosomiasis among domestic ruminant in Ethiopia.

Handling heterogeneity

The results of the meta-analysis revealed a statistically significant difference among studies (Q(32) = 1764.5, p < 0.001). The estimated variance was τ2 = 0.01, and the I2 value was found to be 98.2 %. To identify the source of heterogeneity observed in the pooled estimate from the random-effects model, sensitivity analysis, subgroup analysis, and meta-regression analysis were utilized. These techniques help in examining potential sources of heterogeneity and provide insights into the reliability of the findings.

Subgroup analysis

A subgroup analysis was conducted based on geographical regions (Amhara, Oromia, SNNPR, Tigray, and the combined Oromia and SNNPR), the type of ruminants, the sampling method, the publication year, and the sample size (Table 2). Significant variations in schistosomiasis prevalence were observed across different geographic areas and ruminant species. The prevalence of schistosomiasis in ruminants ranged from 1.8 % in Tigray to 18.6 % in the Amhara region (Table 2).

Sensitivity analysis

A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was also performed to assess how each individual study influenced the overall effect size estimate. The meta-analysis produced a pooled effect size of 14.4 %, which aligned with the CIs of every individual study included. Sensitivity testing further demonstrated the stability of these findings, as removing any single study from the analysis did not meaningfully change the overall prevalence estimate (Fig. 4). Thus, the meta-analysis results encompassing all included studies in this study were reliable.

Sensitivity analysis for the prevalence of schistosomiasis in domestic ruminants.

Influence analysis

To identify studies that have the highest influence on the observed heterogeneity in the current meta-analysis, the Baujat diagnostic plot was employed. In this plot, the horizontal axis represents the contribution of each study to overall heterogeneity, quantified by Cochran’s Q, while the vertical axis depicts the study's impact on the pooled effect size.

Thus, among the included studies, Kebede et al. (2023) and Kerie and Seyoum (2016b) were found to exert a substantial influence on the overall heterogeneity and the pooled effect size, respectively, in our meta-analysis (Fig. 5).

Baujat diagnostic plots for the prevalence of schistosomiasis among domestic ruminants.

Meta-regression models

To identify the sources of data variability in the study, we employed both univariable and multivariable meta-regression models. In the univariable analysis, publication year and sample size were treated as continuous variables, while study region, animal species, sampling methods, and diagnostic techniques were considered as categorical variables within a mixed-effects framework. We evaluated these variables for their linear relationships with effect sizes, the outcome variable. Notably, geographic region and animal species showed statistically significant associations with variability across the studies. The geographic region emerged as the most influential factor, accounting for 41.48 % of the overall heterogeneity (R² = 41.48 %). The multivariable model further explained 48.87 % of the heterogeneity (R² = 48.87 %). The regression coefficients from the multivariable model indicated that studies focused on ovine (β = -0.0724) and caprine species (β = -0.0724) were associated with reduced effect sizes compared to studies conducted on bovine species, after adjusting for covariates (Table 3).

Association of sex and the prevalence of domestic ruminant schistosomiasis

Among 33 studies incorporated in this meta-analysis, 24 reported the relationship between schistosomiasis prevalence and sex of ruminants (Fig. 6). Out of those, 4 studies found a significant association between sex of ruminants and the prevalence of schistosomiasis (Shiferaw and Deressa, 2017; Wudneh, 2018; Yihunie, 2019: Kifle et al., 2022). Conversely, the remaining 20 studies showed no significant associations. The pooled analysis of these 24 studies revealed a significant overall association between sex of the animal and schistosomiasis prevalence, with OR = 1.2, 95 % CI: [1.1-1.4]; p < 0.001) (Fig. 6). This finding demonstrated that the likelihood of developing schistosomiasis could be 20 % higher in females’ ruminant compared to males.

Forest plot for pooled odds ratio of sex and prevalence of schistosomiasis among domestic ruminant in Ethiopia.

Association of body condition and the prevalence of domestic ruminant schistosomiasis

Among 33 studies, 20 examined the relationship between ruminants' body condition score and the prevalence of schistosomiasis. Out of 20 reports, 10 found a statistically significant relationship between medium and good body conditions regarding the prevalence of schistosomiasis (Fig. 7). The overall effect size was: OR = 2.1, 95 % CI: [1.8–2.5] for medium versus good body condition. The pooled analysis revealed a significant association between medium and good body condition ruminants and the prevalence of schistosomiasis; ruminants with medium body condition were 2 times more likely to be infected compared to those in with good body condition.

Forest plot for pooled odds ratio of poor vs medium body condition and prevalence of schistosomiasis among domestic ruminant in Ethiopia.

On the other hand, 16 studies found a statistically significant association between poor versus good body condition and the prevalence of schistosomiasis in ruminants. However, four studies did not support this association (Adane and Regasa, 2023; Gebremeskel et al., 2017; Kebede et al., 2018; Tesfaye et al., 2025) (Figs. 8). The overall effect size was estimated as an odds ratio, OR = 4.5, 95 % CI: [3.7–5.3] for poor versus good body condition. This pooled analysis reveals a significant association between body condition and the prevalence of schistosomiasis, showing that ruminants with poor body condition are 4.5 times more likely to develop the disease compared to those with good body condition.

Forest plot for pooled odds ratio of poor vs good body condition and prevalence of schistosomiasis among domestic ruminant in Ethiopia.

Publication bias

The evaluation of publication bias was employed using a combination of graphical and statistical methods. Graphically, the funnel plot revealed an asymmetrical distribution of effect size estimates across the included studies (Fig. 9). To support this visual observation, we utilized quantitative statistical tests. Egger’s regression analysis produced a coefficient of 10.60 (z = 8.34; p < 0.0001), while Begg’s rank correlation test yielded a Kendall's score of 244.00 (z = 3.77; p < 0.0002). Both analysis indicated a statistically significant asymmetry in the aggregated data, providing strong evidence of publication bias or small-study effects that may have influence on the meta-analysis results.

Funnel plot for the pooled prevalence of schistosomiasis among domestic ruminants.

To address the issue of publication bias, a trim and fill analysis was conducted, resulting in the imputation of 17 imaginary studies on the left side of the funnel plot (Fig. 10). Ultimately, the overall pooled prevalence of domestic ruminant schistosomiasis in Ethiopia, based on all 50 studies (33 observed and 17 imputed), was found to be 3.17 %, 95 % CI: [0.00, 8.22], with 95 % prediction intervals [0.00, 24.23].

Trim and fill analysis by imputing imaginary studies.

Discussion

Schistosomiasis poses a significant challenge in Ethiopia, particularly impacting farm animals such as cattle, sheep, and goats (Megersa et al., 2024). These animals are crucial for livelihoods and food security (Erdaw, 2023). This study represents a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis on the prevalence of schistosomiasis and its associated risk factors in Ethiopian domestic ruminants.

The pooled prevalence of schistosomiasis in domestic ruminants in this meta-analysis was 14.4 %, 95 % CI: [11.2–17.6]. This result is consistent with reports from other countries, including 11.4 % in Iraq (Zangana and Aziz, 2012), 13.02 % in Egypt (Fawy et al., 2025), 14.7 % in the Philippines (Jumawan & Estano, 2021), and 15 % in Pakistan (Niaz et al., 2010). The close agreement across these regions may reflect similarities in ecological and husbandry conditions that facilitate the persistence of intermediate host snails and maintain active transmission cycles.

However, higher prevalence estimates have been documented in Bangladesh with prevalence rates of 47.5 % (Islam et al., 2011), 34.0 % in Tanzania (Makundi et al., 1998), 19.0 % in Kenya (Njonge, 2017), 33.9 % in Nigeria (Onyewuchi et al., 2025), 20.9 % in Malawi (Juhász et al., 2024), 21.4 % in Iraq (Ajaj and Gharban, 2025), and 47.7 % in Senegal (Léger et al., 2020), indicating a substantially heavier burden in certain settings. Such discrepancies may be attributed to the use of more sensitive diagnostic approaches such as ELISA, and PCR in some studies, as well as environmental and management factors that increase livestock exposure to infested water bodies. Copro-parasitological methods, as some studies employed in this analysis, are known to have reduced sensitivity for detecting Schistosoma eggs due to the irregular shedding patterns of trematode parasites (do Espírito-Santo., 2014), which could partly explain the lower pooled estimate.

The higher prevalence of schistosomiasis in Ethiopia could be due to several water bodies in the country that promote the growth and multiplication of its intermediate hosts (snails). Furthermore, the practice of allowing animals to graze freely in swampy and stagnant water pasturelands may serve as an additional risk factor for the disease. The support for this perspective is provided by Molla et al. (2022) and Mesifn et al. (2024), who emphasize the significance of swampy areas and water bodies for the prevalence of schistosomiasis. Previous studies have also revealed that the prevalence of schistosomiasis in a particular area is influenced by the presence of suitable intermediate and final hosts, local climate conditions, and the availability of water bodies, lakes, and rivers (Kabatereine et al., 2004; de Jesus et al., 2004).

On the other hand, the result of this systematic review and meta-analysis was higher than previous reports: 3.6 - 4.9 % in China (Liu et al., 2012), 0.5 % in France (Oleaga et al., 2019), and 8.5 % in India (Bulbul et al., 2022). The comparatively lower burden of schistosomiasis in these countries can be attributed to the implementation of several effective control measures, including mass drug administration, snail control programs, and improved water and sanitation services, as well as a commitment to reducing the disease burden (WHO, 2020).

Moreover, the higher prevalence could be due to climate change significantly affects Schistosoma infections by impacting the production, survival, and reproduction of freshwater snails and schistosomes in Ethiopia. Developing predictive models for their population dynamics and identifying regions at high risk is essential for managing schistosomiasis transmission in sub-Saharan Africa. Effective control measures are urgently needed, including infrastructure improvements to reduce flooding and the development of vaccines and new drugs to prevent reinfection and combat drug resistance (Adekiya et al., 2019). Policymakers are encouraged to develop and implement comprehensive strategies to address neglected tropical diseases by allocating sufficient resources to adopt a One Health approach. This integrated perspective, which recognizes the interconnection between human, animal, and environmental health, is essential for effective disease control and prevention.

Significant variations in schistosomiasis prevalence were observed across the different geographic areas and ruminant species. The variation in the prevalence of schistosomiasis among ruminants across different study areas could be attributed to several factors, including the presence of suitable habitats for the snail intermediate hosts, seasonal differences during the study, animal management practices, and varying agro-ecological conditions (Chai & Jung, 2019; Zhang et al., 2023). Additionally, factors such as the availability of permanent water bodies, moisture levels, temperature fluctuations, humidity, and irrigation techniques are critical elements that support the life cycle of Schistosoma and contribute to their diversity (Mesifn et al., 2024).

Subgroup analysis indicated that the highest prevalence of schistosomiasis in domestic ruminants was observed in the Amhara region, followed by Oromia. The Amhara region is characterized by high-altitude wetlands, perennial water sources, and extensive irrigation schemes, including the Lake Tana basin and the Koga irrigation project in Achefer district, which provide favorable habitats for intermediate snail hosts and facilitate parasite transmission (Megersa et al., 2024). These findings underscore the influence of both environmental and anthropogenic factors in sustaining schistosomiasis transmission and highlight the importance of region-specific control strategies.

In terms of ruminant species, the prevalence of schistosomiasis in goats and sheep is notably lower compared to cattle. This difference is largely attributable to variations in grazing and watering behavior. Cattle typically graze farther from homesteads and drink from rivers, dams, or other open water bodies, which are often contaminated with Schistosoma cercariae. In contrast, goats and sheep usually remain closer to households, with water often supplied by farmers, thereby limiting their exposure to infested environments. Our findings are consistent with previous studies conducted in Côte d’Ivoire, which reported a higher prevalence of schistosomiasis in cattle than in sheep and goats (Kouadio et al., 2020), as well as findings from Ethiopia (Megersa et al., 2024). A study in Iran also noted a lower prevalence of trematodes in goats and sheep compared to cattle, attributing this observation to their different grazing habits. Goats tend to feed on leaves and shrubs in elevated areas, while sheep graze in open land. Conversely, cattle often graze near springs and streams, exposing them to a higher risk of exposure to snail-infested freshwater (Kordshooli et al., 2016).

Moreover, the species-specific susceptibility to schistosomiasis may arise from differences in grazing behavior and physiological vulnerability. Cattle and sheep are known to graze closer to the ground and in wetter, marshy areas where Schistosoma larvae are more prevalent, thus increasing their exposure to infective metacercariae (Torgerson and Claxton, 1999; Megersa et al., 2024). Conversely, goats exhibit more selective browsing behavior, primarily feeding on shrubs and higher vegetation, which may lower their risk of infection (Kordshooli et al., 2016). Furthermore, experimental evidence suggests that goats may have a comparatively stronger resistance to trematodes, potentially linked to immune response dynamics or liver enzyme activity (Ruiz-Campillo et al., 2017). These findings highlight the need for species-targeted control measures, with priority given to cattle, which are more susceptible to schistosomiasis and other trematode infections.

The prevalence of schistosomiasis was found to be higher in female ruminants compared to males. Females were 20 % more likely to develop schistosomiasis (OR = 1.2; 95 % CI: [1.1, 1.4] than males. This finding aligns with previous research indicating a significantly higher prevalence in females, which may be attributed to physiological factors related to immunological conditions during gestation and lactation, making them more susceptible to infections (Pfukenyi and Mukaratirwa, 2018, Ajaj and Gharban, 2025; Fawy et al., 2025). This observation is further supported by Pfukenyi et al. (2005), who reported a much higher prevalence in pregnant and lactating animals. However, several researchers argue that gender may not significantly influence susceptibility to infection, suggesting that both males and females are equally prone to infection (Dagnaw et al., 2023; Mesifn et al., 2024; Tesfaye et al., 2025). This condition may result from both sexes being exposed to similar grazing and watering areas, leading to an equal chance of developing the disease (Habtamu and Mariam, 2011).

The analysis revealed a significant association between body condition and the prevalence of schistosomiasis in domestic ruminants in Ethiopia. Animals with poor and medium body condition were more frequently infected compared to those in good condition, a pattern consistent with previous studies (Molla et al., 2022; Megersa et al., 2024; Mesifn et al., 2024; Fawy et al., 2025; Tesfaye et al., 2025). Ruminants with poor body condition are often immunocompromised due to inadequate nutrient intake, coexisting infections, and other health stressors, making them more susceptible to parasitic infections such as schistosomiasis. In addition, Schistosoma infections themselves can exacerbate weight loss and further impair immune function (Niaz et al., 2010; Gebru et al., 2015), creating a cycle of increased vulnerability and parasite establishment.

The consequences of heavy Schistosoma infections extend beyond immune suppression. Infected animals may experience anemia, weight loss, ruminal disorders, and reduced overall productivity, leading to significant production losses (Pfukenyi and Mukaratirwa, 2018). Furthermore, parasitic infections can impair nutrient absorption and utilization, hindering growth and body condition (Mpofu et al., 2023). These observations underscore that the epidemiology of schistosomiasis is multifactorial, influenced by the interaction of host health, nutritional status, and parasitic load rather than a single determinant (Hajipour et al., 2021). Addressing body condition through proper nutrition and management may therefore play a critical role in controlling schistosomiasis in domestic ruminants.

Since there is no vaccine for schistosomiasis, chemotherapy remains the primary treatment method. However, the ineffectiveness of praziquantel against immature parasites, along with its widespread use, raises concerns about potential drug resistance (Cioli et al., 2014). To enhance effectiveness against schistosomiasis, the report suggests combining praziquantel and mefloquine with chitosan-based nanotechnology. This combination may also reduce biochemical responses and improve immune protection against Schistosoma infections (El-Qabbany et al., 2024).

The evolutionary history of schistosomiasis as a multi-host parasite that readily hybridizes underscores the importance of One Health in effective elimination interventions. One Health strategies, including livestock treatment, vaccination, environmental management, and snail control, are vital for reducing zoonotic spillover risks and preventing human resurgence, while also supporting the livelihoods of communities dependent on healthy livestock. Despite challenges in implementing these approaches sustainably, embracing One Health is essential for tackling schistosomiasis and other neglected tropical diseases (Díaz et al., 2023).

Limitation of the study

This meta-analysis has few limitations. There is lack of studies from some regions, which may bias national prevalence estimates of schistosomiasis due to unrepresented ecological and livestock management variations. Most studies relied on copro-detection, which can yield false-negative results and may not fully capture the true infection burden. Additionally, the studies spanned 2016–2025, potentially overlooking historical trends influenced by climate and agricultural changes.

Conclusions

The pooled prevalence of schistosomiasis in Ethiopia is noticeably high. The study also revealed being cattle, residing in the Amhara region, female, and poor body condition were significant contributing factors for the prevalence of schistosomiasis. Thus, veterinary professionals, policymakers, and research institutions need to prioritize evidence-based control programs focused on high-burden regions and targeted species-specific deworming campaigns, improved pasture management, and better access to animal health services for effective prevention and control of the disease. Furthermore, additional studies are needed to assess the economic impact of the diseases and the efficacy of anthelmintics against schistosomiasis in Ethiopia.
