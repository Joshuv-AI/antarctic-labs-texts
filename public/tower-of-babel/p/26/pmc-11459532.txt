Outpatient management of pediatric epistaxis: A cost analysis and clinical model
Authors: Joshua A Lee, Christopher Puchi, Kathleen R Billings, Jennifer M Lavin, Inbal Hazkani, Carole Glennon, Dana M Thompson, John Maddalozzo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective(s)Pediatric epistaxis is a common, often non‐operative condition encountered by Otolaryngologists. The present study seeks to (1) describe our outcomes of epistaxis management, (2) estimate the associated healthcare burden, and (3) propose a clinical model to optimize care coordination with primary care and advanced practice providers.MethodsRetrospective case series of pediatric patients treated outpatient for epistaxis by a single otolaryngologist from 2021 to 2022. The primary outcome after treatment with nasal lubricants was defined as (1) refractory epistaxis, (2) improvement, or (3) complete resolution. Cost data for office versus operative nasal cautery were analyzed.ResultsIn total, 122 patients were included for analysis. Over a follow‐up duration of 20.5 months (IQR 8–36), 24.6% of patients experienced refractory epistaxis, 41.8% of patients found improvement, and 33.6% had complete resolution (n = 122). Refractory epistaxis was associated with a family history of coagulopathy (p = .007), daily epistaxis episodes (p = .043), and anemia (p <.001). Average direct hospital costs associated with nasal cautery were $187 for in‐office cauterization and $2179 for intraoperative cauterization. Estimated patient/third party payors savings were $1617 and $15,412 for in‐office and intraoperative procedures, respectively, and $541.59 for specialty office visits alone. The average charge for laboratory work‐up was $576.ConclusionApproximately 75% of patients with epistaxis experienced improvement or resolution of symptoms with nasal lubrication alone. Refractory epistaxis was associated with a family history of coagulopathy, daily epistaxis episodes, and anemia. Otolaryngology visits for epistaxis were associated with a direct healthcare expense burden. Adaptation of our clinical model may mitigate these costs while improving patient care. Level of Evidence: 4.Keywords: algorithm, cost analysis, epistaxis, guideline, pediatrics

INTRODUCTION

Pediatric epistaxis is a common problem affecting approximately 30%–50% of children. 1 About 6% of patients with epistaxis will seek medical treatment. 2 Previous studies have shown that epistaxis accounts for nearly 0.5% of emergency department visits and up to one‐third of otolaryngology‐related emergency department encounters. 3 A number of studies have examined the prevalence and financial impact of epistaxis in the emergency room and inpatient setting. 4 , 5 , 6 However, outpatient referral patterns for pediatric epistaxis represent a notable proportion of otolaryngology office encounters. The management of such visits and associated costs have not been thoroughly investigated.

Recommendations to guide diagnostic work‐up and treatment decisions for epistaxis have been previously described by our institution. Key findings from Damrose et al. in 2006 were that laboratory screening for anemia and coagulopathies was useful in identifying hematologic conditions requiring further care by a specialist. The use of routine computed tomography imaging was not recommended, as there were no worrisome sinonasal masses found among the 99 patients studied. 7 A follow‐up study in 2014 by Patel et al. added the recommendation against routine nasal endoscopy for the evaluation of epistaxis, reserving the procedure for patients presenting with a constellation of symptoms and risk factors suggestive of juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibroma (JNA). 8

Integrating findings from our previous work, the aims of the present study were to (1) describe our outcomes of epistaxis management with a nasal lubrication regimen, (2) estimate the associated healthcare burden of patient encounters for epistaxis, and (3) propose a clinical model to optimize care coordination with primary care and advanced practice providers.

METHODS

Patient selection

This study was approved by the Ann & Robert H. Lurie Children's Hospital of Chicago Institutional Review Board (IRB# 2023‐5750). The study is a retrospective case series of all patients seen by a single pediatric otolaryngology provider (JM) over a 18‐month period (6/2021‐12/2022) with a primary diagnosis of epistaxis. Patients were identified by a search of the electronic health record using ICD 10 code R04.0 under the above single provider.

Clinical algorithm

Patients were managed according to a clinical algorithm (Figure 1) used by multiple providers at our institution. In brief, a focused history and physical examination were performed. Patients with risk factors for JNA, such as adolescent males, recurrent severe epistaxis, and associated nasal obstruction (particularly unilateral) received flexible fiberoptic nasal endoscopy. A complete blood count (CBC), prothrombin (PT), and partial thromboplastin time (PTT) were ordered for all patients, which was the standard practice of the senior author. Abnormal PT and PTT results were followed up with a repeat lab draw. Patients with iron deficiency anemia were referred back to their primary care provider, while those with thrombocytopenia, coagulopathies, or any hematologic lab abnormalities, were referred for a hematology consultation.

Clinical model for outpatient management of epistaxis.

For all patients, education on a nasal hydration regimen was described in detail with the aid of a printed handout. Patients were instructed to mix an over‐the‐counter eucalyptus oil‐based emollient with nasal saline in a 1:1 ratio and to deliver via an atomizer provided to the family. The parent or patient was instructed to administer the spray to both nostrils 3–4 times daily for at least 4–6 weeks. Additional recommendations included application of petroleum jelly to the anterior septum at night along with avoidance of digital trauma. All patients were told to follow up as needed for their epistaxis diagnosis. Nasal cautery was offered to those with epistaxis refractory to nasal hydration.

Data collection

Two authors, JAL and CAP, followed up with patients by phone or electronic messaging to confirm medication compliance, and to determine the primary outcome, defined as (1) refractory epistaxis, (2) improvement (i.e., epistaxis that the patient determined no longer warranted clinical attention), or (3) complete resolution. Patients were excluded from analysis if they were not compliant with nasal hydration. Additional patients were excluded if symptomatic details of epistaxis were inadequate in the setting of a medically complex visit (e.g., epistaxis documented during follow‐up after surgery for velopharyngeal insufficiency). Follow‐up duration was defined as the interval between the date of initial visit, and the date of phone/electronic contact for follow‐up.

Demographic data collected included age at the initial visit, sex, race, and ethnicity. Additional variables analyzed included date of initial encounter, personal history of coagulopathy, family history of coagulopathy, and use of medications associated with epistaxis (intranasal steroids, antiplatelet therapy, anticoagulation). Epistaxis symptoms were characterized according to date of onset, frequency (daily, weekly, monthly), duration of bleeding episodes, and laterality. The presence of nasal obstruction symptoms, anemia, thrombocytopenia, and prolongation of prothrombin time (PT) or Partial Thromboplastin Time (PTT) were recorded.

Cost analysis

A query of institutional‐specific direct costs and charges associated with nasal cautery was performed for procedures performed both in the office and in the operating room. Cost and charge data were averaged over 2021–2022. The average charge of a level 3 new patient non‐procedural office visit for epistaxis as well as the cost of laboratory work‐up was also obtained. Additional indirect and direct costs, including supplies for the nasal hydration regimen and time away from school and work, were not incorporated into the cost analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS, version 29.0 (IBM Corp). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and summarized by median and interquartile range. Categorical variables were summarized by frequency and percentage. Comparison of patient factors and outcomes were conducted using a Kruskal‐Wallis test for continuous variables, and a Chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. A p‐value of <.05 was considered significant.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

A total of 186 patients were evaluated for epistaxis by the senior author between 2021 and 2022. One patient was deceased at the time of the chart review. Two patients were excluded as they reported an inability to comply with nasal hydration. Sixty‐one patients were excluded because data regarding compliance or clinical outcome was not obtainable.

A total of 122 patients were included in the final analysis. Patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 9 years (range 2–19). There were more male (65.5%) compared to females (34.5%) patients. Two (1.6%) patients had a known history of coagulopathy, and 3 (2.5%) patients had a family history of coagulopathy. Most patients were not taking medications, while 9% of patients reported taking intranasal steroids, and 1 (0.8%) patient was on an antiplatelet medication. Prior to their initial consultation, 7.4% of patients had a history nasal cauterization (n = 122 for all proportions).

Patient symptoms and clinical course

Patient symptoms and clinical course are summarized in Table 2. Patients presented a median of 12 months after the onset of their epistaxis (IQR 13–36) with reports of bleeding occurring daily (22.2%), weekly (56.7%), or monthly (21.1%) (n = 90). The median duration of epistaxis was 5 min (IQR 3.0–36.0). Bleeding occurred bilaterally in 53.9% of cases, and unilaterally in 41.6% of cases (n = 89). Nasal obstruction was reported 67% patients (n = 94). Anemia was identified in 19.8% of patients, thrombocytopenia in 1 (0.9%) patient. The remaining 79.2% patients showing no lab abnormalities (n = 106). A prolonged PT or PTT was found in 6.5% of patients (N = 107), with 1 patient having known von Willebrand's Disease, and 1 patient having a known family history of coagulopathy. Clinic follow‐up was not recorded for 58.2% of patients, but 41.8% patients returned to clinic (n = 122). Most return visits were related to other common otolaryngologic problems (e.g., adenotonsillar hypertrophy). The overall median follow‐up duration was 20.5 weeks (IQR 8–36), with durations of 27 weeks (IQR 19–64) for telephone follow‐up and 8 weeks (IQR 4–16) for those who returned to clinic. Of the patients who were seen or reached in follow up, 41.8% of patients noted improvement in bleeding incidence, while 33.6% experienced complete resolution of symptoms. There was no significant improvement in 24.6% patients (n = 122). Nasal cautery was offered to patients who did not improve and was performed in 16.4% of all patients (n = 122).

Patient factors associated with epistaxis outcome

With respect to race, the rate of epistaxis resolution was highest among Asian patients (77.8%), followed by patients identifying as Other (31.8%), White (29.7%), and Black (20%). With respect to ethnicity, 54.7% of Hispanic patients experienced improvement in epistaxis, whereas this rate was 32% for non‐Hispanic and 0% for patient identifying as Other. Although racial (p = .002) and ethnic (p = .005) differences were statistically significant, these data were limited by disproportionate sample sizes within certain categories.

Patients with a family history of coagulopathy were more likely to experience refractory epistaxis (100%) with nasal hydration when compared to patients without a family history of coagulopathy (21.7%) (p = .01). Only 1 of the 3 patients with a positive family history also had a known coagulopathy (von Willebrand's disease). The other 2 patients were found to have normal PT and PTT results. There were no differences in bleeding outcomes between patients with and without a medical history of coagulopathy (p = .54).

Outcomes were not influenced by medication use (p = .21). Among patients with prior nasal cautery, none experienced complete resolution, when compared to 36.3% of patients without prior nasal cautery, although this was not significant (p = .07). Cost analysis

The average direct cost incurred to the hospital in overhead and materials associated with nasal cautery was $187 for in‐office cauterization, and $2179 for intraoperative cauterization. The average hospital charge for nasal cautery was $1617 for in‐office cauterization, and $15,412 for intraoperative cauterization. Assuming that nasal cautery would have been performed instead of our proposed regimen, these figures represent potential cost savings for 75.4% of our patients/payors. The average charge for non‐procedural visits was $541.59, representing additional savings for patients who did not require a referral to an Otolaryngologist. The average charge for laboratory work‐up was $576. See Table 4 for a summary of costs and charges.

DISCUSSION

The present study evaluates the outcomes of conservative nasal hydration as an initial office‐based treatment for epistaxis in accordance with our management algorithm. Our analysis showed that 41% of patients experienced clinically significant improvement (i.e., patients did not feel that further attention was warranted), and 33.6% of patients had complete resolution of epistaxis, amounting to 75.4% of patients who did not require further clinical evaluation. Certain patient factors were associated with refractory epistaxis, including a positive family history of coagulopathy, daily episodes of epistaxis, and anemia. The presence of these factors may be associated with an eventual need for nasal cautery after an adequate trial of nasal lubrication.

Patient compliance remains key to symptom improvement and reduction of refractory epistaxis. It is prudent to educate all caregivers on the benefit of compliance with nasal lubrication in the reduction of epistaxis events and to assess sociodemographic barriers to compliance. It is possible that environmental and sociodemographic factors such as cigarette smoke and air pollution exposure may cause chemical irritation of the nasal mucosa and may contribute to a higher incidence of epistaxis in some patients. 9 While our study included racial and ethnic factors into the analysis, our data was limited by sample size and we could not draw appropriate conclusions related to racial and ethnic disparities.

There is some data in the literature reporting on health disparities in epistaxis management. One large database study queried State Emergency Department Databases for New York, Florida, Iowa, and California for visits in 2010 and found that Hispanic children were more likely than children of other races to present to the Emergency Department with epistaxis. 10 In contrast, another study queried the National Ambulatory Medical Care Survey and National Hospital Ambulatory Medical Care Survey for visits between 2001 and 2010 and found that Black children were more likely than children of other races to present for ambulatory care, and white children were more likely to undergo an epistaxis control procedure. 9

Our cost analysis showed that average direct hospital costs associated with nasal cautery were $187 for in‐office cauterization and $2179 for intraoperative cauterization. Charges to patients/payors were $1617 for in‐office cauterization and $15,412 for intraoperative cauterization, with a baseline charge of $541.59 for an office visit alone and $576 for laboratory work‐up. Given the high rate of improvement/resolution with nasal lubrication regimens, along with the sizable volume of epistaxis consultations, there are cost‐saving opportunities not factored into these estimations related to indirect costs of care for the family, and additional direct costs to the institution. Our analysis showed that it was rare for patients to require cautery procedures for control of epistaxis. Shifting the initial epistaxis management from otolaryngologists to primary care providers is suggested. This would open access for patients with refractory epistaxis requiring nasal cautery. Although cautery was typically reserved for refractory epistaxis, there were mixed results when comparing the effectiveness of nasal hydration to nasal cautery. 11 Patients with refractory epistaxis should be cautioned that procedural interventions may not guarantee the resolution of symptoms, especially if a vessel cannot be easily identified.

A recent study determined that otolaryngologists provide a higher proportion of outpatient epistaxis care compared to primary care providers. 9 Our data support that this practice is not necessary, and that implementation of clinical care pathways with enhanced communication between primary care providers and otolaryngologists could reduce the cost of care. Our study is a survey of clinical management within a specialist office and our pathway may serve as an alternative model to help direct patients to the surgeon when most appropriate. For primary care or advanced practice providers seeking to integrate the clinical model in Figure 1, the authors recommend otolaryngology referrals for patients with risk factors for JNA requiring nasal endoscopy, or those with epistaxis refractory to conservative treatment requiring consideration for nasal cautery (see figure footnote). Overall, coordinated care pathways could allow improved patient satisfaction and direct and indirect cost savings for the management of epistaxis.

Limitations

There were several limitations to our study. First, as this was a retrospective study, our results may have been subject to patient recall bias. While patient compliance was verified on follow‐up, the duration and frequency of treatment were not consistently reported. Additionally, the characterization of epistaxis pre‐ and post‐treatment was subjective, but for this reason, asking patients if they no longer determined clinical attention was necessary was a meaningful clinical outcome. Although seasonality of symptoms was not included in the analysis, the primary objective of this study was to evaluate treatment outcomes rather than contributing risk factors. Approximately one third of patients initially reviewed did not have available data pertaining to clinical outcome or compliance. Therefore, it is possible that the true rate of refractory epistaxis was underestimated, and follow‐up phone calls performed closer to the date of encounter may have increased the response rate. The cost analysis performed in this study was based on institution‐specific data and may not be directly applicable to other institutions; however, it may generally represent the magnitude of the healthcare burden. The clinical pathway also includes laboratory work‐up for all patients as the senior author has found this to be important for patients seen at our institution who may have limited access to care; however, we recognize that other providers may recommend laboratory work‐up for select patients with recalcitrant bleeding. While a detailed nasal hydration regimen is described, this study is not limited in its support of any specific formulation of nasal hydration. Finally, it is worth emphasizing that our clinical pathway applies to the large majority of routine outpatient pediatric epistaxis visits. While pathologies such as JNA, and other sinonasal neoplasms often present with epistaxis, these conditions are exceedingly rare and often present with additional symptoms. We include a general screening recommendation in the clinical pathway for patients at greater risk of JNA. For comprehensive evaluation and acute management of epistaxis, we defer to the otolaryngology Clinical Practice Guideline for epistaxis. 11

Despite the limitations above, this study provides data to support a common stepwise management model used by many otolaryngologists that can be more broadly adopted by primary care and advanced practice providers to optimize patient care and mitigate healthcare costs.

CONCLUSION

Of the patients who were seen or reached in follow‐up, approximately 75% of pediatric patients referred for outpatient epistaxis experienced clinically significant improvement or resolution of symptoms with conservative treatment alone. Factors associated with refractory epistaxis include a family history of coagulopathy, daily episodes of epistaxis, and anemia. Otolaryngology visits for epistaxis are costly to both patients and providers. Based on our survey of clinical management, we describe a cost‐effective clinical model that may optimize care coordination between primary care providers, advanced practice providers, and Otolaryngologists.
