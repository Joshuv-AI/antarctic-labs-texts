Insights into HIU enhancement of salt-reduced surimi gelation properties: Dual regulation of endogenous transglutaminase activation and protease inhibition
Authors: Xia Gao, Meng Gui, Shengjun Chen, Xiao Hu, Juan You, Tao Yin, Yang Hu, Shanbai Xiong, Ru Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: High-intensity ultrasound, Salt-reduced surimi, Endogenous enzyme regulation, Gelation properties, Dynamic rheological properties

Introduction

Surimi-based products are popular with consumers due to their huge advantages of being high in protein and low in fat [1]. Gel properties serve as key quality indicators for surimi-based products, directly influencing consumer acceptance [2]. To achieve satisfactory gel properties, 2 %-3% NaCl is typically required in the practical production of surimi-based products to fully extract and solubilize salt-soluble proteins [3]. However, epidemiological evidence confirms that high-salt diet is a direct pathogenic factor for hypertension [4]. As a predominant contributor to cardiovascular mortality, hypertension-related complications demonstrate a progressively younger onset pattern globally [5]. From a public health intervention perspective, reducing dietary salt intake has become an urgent priority to achieve the goal target of a 25 % reduction in hypertension prevalence by 2030 compared to 2010 baseline levels [6]. Under these considerations, there is an urgent need for salt-reduced surimi-based products.

How to maintain satisfactory surimi gel properties while reducing NaCl content remains a critical challenge. Currently, the research concerning salt-reduced surimi gel primarily focuses on the use of NaCl substitutes, including KCl, IMP, etc. [[7], [8], [9]]. Additionally, exogenous additives such as L-Arg, L-Lys, YE and CMC have been demonstrated to effectively enhance the gel properties of salt-reduced surimi [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. While exogenous additives could compensate for gelation defects in salt-reduced surimi, they inevitably introduce new challenges including off-flavors and potential regulatory restrictions. Nonthermal physical field processing has emerged as a promising alternative for protein modification, where mechanical energy induces structural changes without chemical interventions [14]. Among these techniques, high-intensity ultrasound (HIU) – widely adopted in food industry applications – primarily functions through cavitation phenomena to achieve desired textural modifications [15]. Our previous study showed that HIU could improve the gel properties of salt-reduced (1.5 % NaCl, achieving a 25 %-50 % reduction from the conventional 2 %-3% salt level) silver carp surimi [16,17]. The surimi gelation is fundamentally governed by competing enzymatic actions: endogenous transglutaminase (eTGase) catalyzes intermolecular crosslinking to enhance gel strength, whereas endogenous protease induces protein degradation leading to gel deterioration [18,19]. Under these considerations, further investigation was conducted on eTGase and protease separately in terms of their physicochemical alterations under HIU [20,21]. However, in the practical production, surimi is a complex system containing both eTGase and protease, the studies of dual enzyme regulations under HIU are rarely reported.

In this study, the dual regulation of enzyme under HIU was systematically investigated by employing enzyme-specific additives. The puncture properties were evaluated. Furthermore, the protein degradation degree and intermolecular isopeptide bonding were quantified by TCA-soluble peptides and solubility. Meanwhile, the heat-induced gelation properties were characterized by dynamic rheology. Subsequently, the water distribution and microstructures of salt-reduced (1.5 % NaCl) surimi gels were analyzed using low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The study aimed to elucidate the mechanistic interplay between HIU-induced enzymatic modulation and gel performance, ultimately establishing a theoretical framework for optimized salt-reduced surimi processing.

Materials and methods

Materials

Silver carp surimi (AAA grade, 73.26 % moisture content) was provided by Jingli Aquatic Co., Ltd. (Honghu, Hubei, China). Protease inhibitors E-64 and PMSF were obtained from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All other chemicals were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of surimi gels

Surimi gels with different treatments were prepared following our previous publication [22] with minor modifications. For HIU-treated samples, 150 g of surimi was sealed in polyethylene bags (160 × 240 × 0.2 mm) and treated using a KQ-300DE ultrasonic processor (Kunshan Ultrasonic Instruments Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China) equipped with piezoelectric ceramic transducers fixed at the bottom of the cleaning tank. The HIU conditions were set as follows: 40 kHz frequency, 240 W power, 15 min continuous duration, and 20 °C temperature. The surimi (with or without HIU treatment) was initially chopped in a food processor for 3 min, followed by salt-chopping for 5 min with 1.5 % NaCl (w/w). The salt content (1.5 % NaCl) was selected based on our previous study [17], which confirmed that HIU significantly improved the gel properties of surimi at this reduced salt level – corresponding to a 25 %-50 % reduction compared to conventional levels (2 %-3% NaCl). During salt-chopping, the moisture content was adjusted to 78 % by adding cold water, along with the following additives (final concentrations): CaCl2 (10 mmol/kg), NH4Cl (1 mol/kg), PMSF (0.1 mmol/L), and E-64 (0.1 mg/mL). Samples without additives were denoted as the “None” group. The paste was then degassed, stuffed into polyvinyl chloride casings (25 mm diameter), and heat-treated using a two-stage method (40 °C for 1 h followed by 90 °C for 30 min). Finally, the gels were cooled under running tap water and stored at 4 °C overnight for subsequent analysis.

Determination of puncture properties

The puncture properties of surimi gels, including breaking force and deformation, were determined according to Xue et al. [23] using a texture analyzer (TA-XT Plus, Stable Micro Systems Ltd., Surrey, UK). The surimi gels were equilibrated at room temperature for 30 min and then cut into cylinders (20 mm in height). Compression tests were performed using a P/0.25S probe with the following parameters: pre-test speed of 2 mm/s, test speed of 1 mm/s, post-test speed of 2 mm/s, and trigger force of 5 g. All measurements were performed in quintuplicate with three independent repetitions.

Determination of TCA-soluble peptides

Determination of TCA-soluble peptides was performed according to Yu et al. [24] with minor modifications. Briefly, 3 g of gel sample was homogenized with 27 mL of cold 5 % TCA solution. The homogenate was then incubated at 4 °C for 1 h and centrifuged at 10,000 r/min for 10 min. The supernatant was collected, and TCA-soluble peptides were measured using Lowry’s method [25] with tyrosine as the standard.

Determination of solubility

The solubility was measured according to our previous publication [17]. Briefly, 1 g of minced gel sample was dissolved in 20 mL of 20 mmol/L Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0) containing 1 % SDS, 8 mmol/L urea, and 2 % β-mercaptoethanol. The mixture was homogenized, heated at 100 °C for 2 min, and then stirred at 25 °C for 4 h. Thereafter, the homogenizer was centrifuged at 9,000 r/min for 30 min. The precipitate was discarded, and cold 50 % TCA was added to 10 mL of the supernatant to a final concentration of 10 %. The mixture was then incubated at 4 °C for 18 h and centrifuged again at 9,000 r/min for 30 min. The resulting precipitate was remained, washed with 5 mL of 10 % TCA, and then dissolved in 5 mL of 0.5 mol/L NaOH. The protein concentration was determined using Lowry’s method [25]. The solubility was expressed as the percentage of protein content in the solution relative to the total protein content.

Determination of dynamic rheological properties

The dynamic rheological properties of surimi were analyzed following the method of Yang et al. [26] using an AR500 dynamic rheometer (TA Instruments Ltd., Manchester, UK) equipped with a 40 mm parallel-plate geometry. The gap between the platform and plate was set to 1 mm. Samples were loaded onto the platform at an initial temperature of 20 °C and covered with liquid paraffin to prevent evaporation. Temperature sweeps were performed at a rate of 2 °C/min from 20 °C to 90 °C. Storage modulus (G′) and phase angle (δ) were recorded throughout the measurements.

LF-NMR analysis

LF-NMR analysis was performed according to Zhu et al. [27] with minor modifications. Approximately 2 g of cylindrical surimi gel was wrapped and placed in a 10-mm-diameter glass tube, then analyzed using a nuclear magnetic resonance analyzer (NMI20, Niumag Electric Co., Shanghai, China). The spin–spin relaxation time (T2) was measured using the Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill sequence with the following parameters: 90°-180° pulse spacing of 1 ms, scan interval of 6,500 ms, 8 scans, echo time of 1.2 ms, and 1,200 echoes. Four to six replicate measurements were performed for each sample.

Observation of microstructures

The microstructures of surimi gels were observed using a SEM (TM 3000, Hitachi Co., Tokyo, Japan) following the method of Duan et al. [28]. Samples were cut into 1 × 1 × 1 mm3 cubes and fixed in 2.5 % (v/v) glutaraldehyde at 4 °C overnight. Subsequent dehydration was performed through a graded ethanol series: 50 %, 70 %, and 80 % ethanol (15 min each), followed by 90 % and 100 % ethanol (20 min each). Samples were then washed with tertiary butyl alcohol for 30 min. After critical point drying, samples were sputter-coated with gold and observed at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV with 10,000 × magnification.

Statistical analysis

All determinations were performed in triplicate unless otherwise stated. Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using SPSS 22 (SPSS Institute Inc., Chicago, USA). Statistics comparisons were made using Duncan’s multiple range test and the least significant difference method, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Figures were generated using Origin 9.0 (Origin Laboratory, Northampton, USA).

Results and discussion

Effects of additives on puncture properties of surimi gels under HIU

Fig. 1 depicts the variations in breaking force and deformation of surimi gels under different treatments. For control samples, the addition of CaCl2 significantly improved both breaking force and deformation (P < 0.05), suggesting enhanced puncture properties of surimi gels. This enhancement could be attributed to CaCl2-induced protein interactions, as puncture properties reflected the degree of protein aggregation [29]. Surimi naturally contains eTGase, which catalyzes protein crosslinking via acyl transfer reactions during gelation process [21]. Given that eTGase was Ca2+-dependent [30], CaCl2 addition activated eTGase, thereby promoting protein crosslinking. Furthermore, Ca2+ facilitated salt bridge formation between adjacent proteins, synergistically strengthening protein networks [31]. Consequently, CaCl2-incorporated surimi gels exhibited superior breaking force and deformation compared to pure surimi gels without additive (None). In contrast, NH4Cl addition significantly reduced breaking force and deformation of surimi gels (P < 0.05). As a known eTGase inhibitor [32], NH4Cl impeded acyl transfer reactions, suppressing protein crosslinking and ultimately compromising puncture properties. Notably, E-64 incorporation increased breaking force and deformation by 32.08 % and 10.34 %, respectively, versus the None group, demonstrating markedly strengthened puncture properties. This improvement stemmed from E-64′s specific inhibition of endogenous cysteine protease, which suppressed protein hydrolysis and mitigated the modori phenomenon (gel weakening). Similar findings were reported by Shi et al. [33] in Peruvian hake surimi gels. Interestingly, PMSF (endogenous serine protease inhibitor) showed no significant effect on puncture properties (P > 0.05). This was consistent with our prior findings that endogenous cysteine protease played a dominant role in the modori phenomenon [20].

As evidenced in Fig. 1, HIU significantly enhanced both breaking force and deformation of surimi gels across all additive groups compared to their respective controls (P < 0.05). This improvement could be attributed to two primary mechanisms: i HIU-induced cavitation effects promoted protein dispersion and activated eTGase [21,34], thereby strengthening intermolecular interactions during thermal gelation; and ii HIU concurrently suppressed endogenous protease activity [20], mitigating protein hydrolysis. These dual effects resulted in enhanced textural properties. Notably, the combination of HIU and CaCl2 induced conformational changes in myosin [35], providing more favorable substrates for eTGase-mediated crosslinking. This synergistic effect also contributed to significantly higher breaking force and deformation in HIU-CaCl2-treated samples. Among all gel samples, surimi gels treated by HIU and E-64 had the highest breaking force (814.08 ± 33.62 g), which increased by 51.17 % over the None group without HIU. To elucidate the mechanism behind the improved puncture properties of surimi gels with different treatments, further analysis was conducted on protein degradation.

Effects of additives on TCA-soluble peptides of surimi gels under HIU

As shown in Fig. 2, both CaCl2 and E-64 treatments significantly reduced TCA-soluble peptides in surimi gels compared to the untreated control (None; P < 0.05). The E-64-incorporated surimi gels exhibited the lowest values, indicating the most effective suppression of protein degradation. This degradation primarily resulted from the modori phenomenon mediated by endogenous protease [36,37]. As previously demonstrated that E-64 could inhibit cysteine protease activity, thereby decreasing degraded peptides. The reduction observed in CaCl2-treated samples could be attributed to enhanced ε-(γ-Glu)-Lys crosslinking catalyzed by Ca2+-activated eTGase, which might protect proteins from proteolysis by masking cleavage sites [38,39]. Similarly, Fang et al. [40] pointed out that surimi gels added with MTGase formed larger aggregates through protein crosslinking, which was more resistant to hydrolysis. In contrast, NH4Cl addition significantly increased TCA-soluble peptides (P < 0.05), consistent with its known inhibitory effect on eTGase. The resultant impairment of protein crosslinking reduced resistance to proteolysis, ultimately explaining the inferior puncture properties of NH4Cl-treated surimi gels (Fig. 1). Notably, PMSF treatment showed negligible effect on TCA-soluble peptides, suggesting limited contribution of serine protease to the modori phenomenon under the current experimental conditions, which was consistent with puncture properties (Fig. 1).

HIU obviously reduced TCA-soluble peptides in surimi gels across all additive groups compared to their respective controls (P < 0.05), demonstrating effective suppression of protein hydrolysis. This was due to the fact that endogenous protease was largely inhibited by HIU [20], which directly limited proteolytic activity. In addition, HIU was also proven to facilitate protein crosslinking via activating eTGase [21]. This reduced protease accessibility by sterically masking cleavage sites in the protein molecules [41]. Notably, the combined treatment of HIU and E-64 yielded the lowest TCA-soluble peptides among all groups. This observation strongly suggested that protease inhibition played a more substantial role than eTGase-mediated crosslinking in minimizing protein hydrolysis under HIU.

Effects of additives on solubility of surimi gels under HIU

It is well established that eTGase catalyzes acyl transfer reactions to form ε-(γ-Glu)-Lys bonds, which significantly influences gel properties. Solubility serves as a critical indicator of protein crosslinking degree, as the solvent used for solubilizing surimi gels could disrupt all chemical bonds except for non-disulfide covalent bonds (ε-(γ-Glu)-Lys linkage) [42]. Therefore, solubility is inversely correlated with the extent of ε-(γ-Glu)-Lys bond formation. Solubility of surimi gels with different treatments is shown in Fig. 3. In the control group, the NH4Cl and PMSF groups exhibited the highest solubility, followed by the E-64 and None groups, whereas the CaCl2 group showed the lowest solubility. The results indicated that CaCl2 effectively enhanced ε-(γ-Glu)-Lys bonds formation, likely due to its role in activating eTGase. Consequently, protein crosslinking was strengthened, resulting in reduced solubility. This aligned with the improved breaking force and deformation observed in CaCl2-incorporated samples (Fig. 1). In contrast, NH4Cl completely inhibited eTGase activity, suppressing protein crosslinking and the subsequent ε-(γ-Glu)-Lys bonds formation, thereby increasing solubility. Nevertheless, the addition of E-64 hardly affected protein crosslinking, as there was no significant difference in solubility between None and E-64 groups.

Compared to the corresponding control, HIU significantly reduced solubility across all treatment groups (P < 0.05), suggesting enhanced formation of ε-(γ-Glu)-Lys bonds. This effect might be attributed to dual role of HIU in activating eTGase and improving protein dispersion through cavitation [21,43]. Together, these combined effects likely promoted eTGase-mediated protein crosslinking, thereby increasing ε-(γ-Glu)-Lys bonds formation and ultimately decreasing solubility.

Effects of additives on dynamic rheological properties of surimi under HIU

Fig. 4 exhibits the gelation profiles of surimi under different treatments. In control samples (Fig. 4A1), the G′ of the untreated group (None) exhibited a slight decreasing trend as temperature increased. At low temperatures, protein was mainly interacted with others through weak chemical bonds (e.g., hydrogen and ionic bonds), while heating could easily disrupt these chemical bonds, thereby increasing the fluidity. With increasing temperature, G′ gradually increased and obtained the peak value at around 46 °C. This phase, termed “setting”, was characterized by eTGase-catalyzed intra- and inter-protein crosslinking, facilitating the preliminary formation of a three-dimensional gel network [44]. Subsequently, G′ sharply declined, reaching a trough near 55 °C, reflecting the modori phenomenon. This weakening was attributed to: (i) myosin tail unfolding, (ii) protein denaturation by endogenous protease, and (iii) heat-induced disruption of hydrogen bond [45]. When the temperature was above 55 °C, G′ increased again due to enhanced molecular interactions (e.g., hydrophobic forces and disulfide bonding), reinforcing the gel network.

Effects of activator or inhibitor on G′ (A) and phase angle (B) of surimi under HIU. Subscripts 1–2 represent the control group and HIU-treated group, respectively.

The CaCl2-treated group exhibited obviously higher initial G′ than None group, indicating stronger chemical interactions. As shown in Table 1, CaCl2 addition also lowered the setting temperature, reduced the first peak temperature, and increased the peak G′. These effects likely arose from Ca2+-induced protein conformational changes [35], which shifted setting to lower temperature, and from Ca2+-activated eTGase, promoting isopeptide crosslinking. However, by the end of heating, CaCl2 group showed marginally lower G′ than the None group, possibly due to a denser network trapping more water [46]. In contrast, NH4Cl-treated surimi displayed atypical gelation profiles, with no distinct setting or modori phases. This suggested complete eTGase inhibition by NH4Cl, which severely impaired protein crosslinking and gel network formation. Notably, this group exhibited the highest final G′, likely due to a particle-filling effect of NH4Cl mechanically reinforcing the surimi matrix. However, its higher phase angle (δ) than the None group indicated inferior gel quality, implying weaker cohesiveness despite elevated G′.

PMSF and E-64 increased G′ to 97.7 Pa and 83.9 Pa, respectively, at the modori trough, surpassing the None group (76.2 Pa) (Table 1), confirming their efficacy in mitigating gel deterioration. PMSF (a serine protease inhibitor) and E-64 (a cysteine protease inhibitor) functioned by suppressing protein degradation. However, both inhibitors reduced the G′ of first peak relative to the None group, suggesting potential interference with eTGase catalyzing via protein conformational changes.

HIU-treated samples (Fig. 4A2) followed trends similar to their corresponding controls but with markedly higher G' of first peak (except NH4Cl group), indicating enhanced eTGase-catalyzed protein crosslinking under HIU. The most pronounced improvement occurred in CaCl2-treated surimi, where HIU elevated the G' of first peak from 290.4 Pa (control) to 454.1 Pa. Synergistically, HIU and CaCl2 reduced the setting temperature, likely due to protein conformational changes [35]. These results demonstrated that HIU and CaCl2 jointly optimized surimi gelation by maximizing eTGase activity. Moreover, HIU increased G' of modori trough in all groups, corroborated by reduced TCA-soluble peptides (Fig. 2), implying HIU suppressed protein degradation.

Effects of additives on water distribution of surimi gels under HIU

Fig. 5 displays the water distribution and corresponding peak area proportion in surimi gels with different treatments. All surimi gels exhibited three typical peaks with relaxation time (T2) at 0.1–10 ms (bound water), 20–220 ms (immobilized water), and 360–1200 ms (free water), consistent with prior reports [47]. Compared to the untreated control (None), additive incorporation induced leftward shifts (shorter T2) in both bound water and free water peaks, with the E-64-treated group showing the most pronounced shift. These results suggested strengthened protein-water interactions. Notably, NH4Cl addition might further reduce relaxation time via NH3+-mediated OH– binding, though its precise mechanism warranted further investigation. For brevity, HIU-treated samples, which displayed water distribution patterns similar to their corresponding controls, were thus not shown.

Effects of activator or inhibitor on water distribution (A) and proportion of peak area (B) of surimi gels under HIU.

As shown in Fig. 5B, immobilized water accounted for the dominant fraction (∼95 %) in all surimi gels. The addition of CaCl2 obviously increased bound water, likely due to Ca2+-induced protein conformational changes that exposed additional hydration sites, thereby enhancing water-binding capacity. Both PMSF and E-64 addition reduced free water while increasing bound water and immobilized water, indicating improved water retention via conversion of free water to immobilized water. HIU further optimized water distribution by decreasing free water and variably increasing either bound water or immobilized water. This enhancement aligned with HIU-induced protein dispersion, which exposed more hydrophilic groups and strengthened protein-water interactions.

Effects of additives on microstructures of surimi gels under HIU

Fig. 6 displays the microstructures of surimi gels with different treatments. The untreated control group (None) exhibited a characteristic three-dimensional network structure. The addition of CaCl2 obviously enhanced gel matrix integrity, producing a more continuous network with reduced pore size compared to the None group, which explained its improved textural properties. In contrast, NH4Cl-added surimi gels failed to form an organized network, instead displaying discontinuous and coarse microstructures. This phenomenon likely resulted from NH4Cl-induced inhibition of eTGase activity, leading to disordered protein aggregation during heating and subsequent formation of irregular gel microstructures. Both protease inhibitors (PMSF and E-64) improved gel compactness and homogeneity, with E-64 showing the most pronounced enhancement. This improvement could be attributed to protease inhibition by these additives, which reduced protein degradation and promoted the formation of denser, more uniform microstructures.

Effects of activator or inhibitor on microstructures of surimi gels under HIU. A-E denote the additives as None, CaCl2, NH4Cl, PMSF, and E-64, respectively; Subscripts 1–2 represent the control group and HIU-treated group, respectively.

HIU further improved the microstructures across all treatment groups, with the CaCl2 group exhibiting the most significant improvement. HIU obviously reduced pore size of microstructures and enhanced compactness. As previously mentioned, HIU could activate eTGase and synergistically inhibit the endogenous protease. Meanwhile, HIU promoted the solubilization of salt-soluble proteins. These combined effects promoted more extensive and uniform protein interactions, facilitating the formation of denser microstructures. Notably, while HIU improved the microstructures of NH4Cl-treated surimi gels, their failure to form a continuous network highlighted the indispensable role of eTGase in heat-induced gelation of surimi.

Discussion

Recent studies have demonstrated that surimi gel properties could be significantly enhanced by modulating enzymatic activities through additives such as exogenous Ca2+, whey protein concentrate, and MTGase [48]. Our previous study revealed that HIU could improve the gel properties of salt-reduced surimi (1.5 % NaCl, achieving 25 %-50 % salt reduction compared to conventional 2 %-3% NaCl levels) [17]. Based on these findings, this study further investigated the relationship between endogenous enzymes (eTGase and protease) and HIU-induced modifications in salt-reduced (1.5 % NaCl) surimi gels using specific activators or inhibitors. Results showed that CaCl2 treatment under HIU provided the most substantial improvement, increasing breaking force from 538.52 ± 48.44 g to 760.72 ± 28.72 g and deformation from 9.40 ± 0.26 mm to 11.57 ± 0.31 mm (Fig. 1). In addition, the combined effects of HIU and Ca2+ not only promoted covalent crosslinking during heating but also induced protein conformational changes [35], lowering the setting temperature while elevating the G′ of first peak (Table 1), which facilitated the formation of a denser and more homogeneous gel network (Fig. 6). Notably, E-64 addition also enhanced gel properties by specifically inhibiting cysteine protease activity, as evidenced by reduced protein degradation (TCA-soluble peptides decreased from 0.28 ± 0.00 to 0.25 ± 0.00 μmol Tyr/10 g protein) (Fig. 2) and improved G′ of modori trough, although it did not alter the setting temperature (Table 1). Besides, the combination of HIU and NH4Cl improved the surimi gel properties, but it failed to form the typical three-dimensional gel network (Fig. 6), reflecting the importance of eTGase. Collectively, HIU enhanced the gelation properties of salt-reduced (1.5 % NaCl) surimi through dual mechanisms of eTGase activation and cysteine protease inhibition, with eTGase activation contributing more substantially than protease inhibition, as evidenced by its pivotal role in microstructural optimization and rheological enhancement.

The proposed mechanism for the dual role of HIU in enhancing the gelation properties of salt-reduced surimi is depicted in Fig. 7. In the low-salt environment, myosin formed insoluble filaments, limiting its solubility [3]. HIU promoted myosin solubilization, thereby enhancing its dispersion [16]. As temperature increased, the dispersed myosin unfolded, exposing sites for eTGase catalyzing. Subsequently, during the setting stage, eTGase was further activated by HIU [21], strengthening both inter- and intra-molecular protein interactions via ε-(γ-Glu)-Lys isopeptide bonds. This facilitated the formation of preliminary gel network. Upon heating to 55 °C, cysteine protease (especially Cat L) was effectively suppressed by HIU [20], protecting the gel network from deterioration by minimizing the production of degraded peptides. These combined effects ultimately contributed to the formation of compact and uniform microstructures stabilized by hydrophobic interactions and disulfide bonds, which retained more water, resulting in improved gelation properties of salt-reduced (1.5 % NaCl) surimi. Notably, in this low-salt environment, the hydration behavior of myosin is also essential for surimi gel properties, as it governs the accessibility of sites for eTGase catalysis. Factors like ionic strength and protein phosphorylation can modify protein hydration behavior [3,49]. Given the importance of HIU on critical processes like myosin solubilization and eTGase activation, understanding how HIU might influence myosin hydration is important for fully elucidating its mechanism in enhancing gelation properties of salt-reduced surimi. However, further investigation is needed.

Conclusion

For control samples, CaCl2 addition promoted chemical interactions among proteins through non-disulfide covalent bonds, strengthening the gelation properties of surimi. This further facilitated the formation of dense and compact gel microstructures, promoting the trapping capacity of water molecules. In contrast, NH4Cl addition impaired the gelation properties through seriously eTGase inhibition. Both PMSF and E-64 treatments improved textural properties by reducing gel deterioration. HIU significantly improved the gel properties across all samples, with CaCl2-treated samples showing the most remarkable improvement through setting temperature reduction, whereas the combination of HIU and PMSF or E-64 enhanced gel properties by minimizing protein deterioration. However, although the combination of HIU and NH4Cl improved the gel properties, it still failed to form the typical three-dimensional gel network, reflecting the importance of eTGase. Critically, since CaCl2 and NH4Cl were used to modulate eTGase while E-64 and PMSF targeted protease inhibition, these results collectively demonstrated that eTGase activation contributed more substantially than protease inhibition to the overall improvement of HIU-enhanced surimi gel properties, as evidenced by its pivotal role in microstructural optimization and rheological enhancement. In conclusion, HIU modulated gel properties of salt-reduced (1.5 % NaCl) surimi through dual effects: eTGase activation and protease inhibition. Moreover, eTGase activation was the determining factor in optimizing HIU-enhanced surimi gel properties, with Ca2+-activated crosslinking proving most effective for developing superior salt-reduced (1.5 % NaCl) surimi products.
