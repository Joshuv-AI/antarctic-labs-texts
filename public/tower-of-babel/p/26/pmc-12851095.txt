Development and initial clinical experience with a paclitaxel-coated balloon for the treatment of benign difficult esophageal strictures
Authors: Kenneth K Wang, Ian Schorn, Carlos Adorno
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsBenign esophageal strictures can result in dysphagia and decreased quality of life. A novel drug-coated balloon (DCB) for dilation was developed to dilate strictures while concurrently delivering paclitaxel to reduce recurrence. Initial preclinical histologic and pharmacokinetic (PK) data in addition to first-in-human pilot data are presented.MethodsThirty-nine healthy swine were treated by using 3 different paclitaxel dosing regimens or a bare balloon. Paclitaxel PK elimination profile in plasma and tissue as well as local tissue responses were evaluated. Nine adult human subjects were treated and followed up for up to 2 years with dysphagia scores, safety, and imaging.ResultsIn the animal studies, histologic and PK analysis revealed paclitaxel present in treated tissues through 28 days’ postprocedure, whereas plasma levels were minimal (1 ng/mL at 155 mg total drug dose). No local or systemic drug toxicity was found. Nine human subjects with an average initial stricture diameter of 6.4 mm and an average Ogilvie Dysphagia Score of 2.2 were treated. No major treatment-related adverse events occurred. The retreatment rate through 2 years was 11%. Five subjects completed 2-year follow-up, with an average 2-year Ogilvie Dysphagia Score of 0. Stricture diameter improved to 19.4 ± .9 mm at 1 year (n = 5).ConclusionsThe animal studies show that DCB treatment of esophageal mucosa appears safe with paclitaxel still present in tissue after 1 month but at minimal plasma levels. Initial results in humans indicate DCB can be safely applied with clinically relevant increases in esophageal lumen at 1 year with symptomatic improvement. (Clinical trial registration number: NCT03885310.)

Benign esophageal strictures are a common adverse event from esophageal injury from etiologies as diverse as gastroesophageal reflux to ionizing radiation, esophagectomy, and medications. The formation of these strictures is generally driven by inflammation and fibrosis leading to a narrowing of the esophageal lumen. Patients develop symptoms of dysphagia that limits solid food intake and even progress to the inability to handle liquids, leading to a severe decrease in nutrition and quality of life.1

Treatment for benign esophageal strictures includes dilatation, usually achieved by bougienage that is effective but frequently needs to be repeated. Currently used dilation methods include bougie dilators (eg, Maloney; Teleflex, Morrisville, NC, USA), wire-guided polyvinyl dilators (eg, Savary-Gilliard; Cook Medical, Bloomington, Ind, USA), or through-the-scope balloon dilators, which all appear to be effective in the short term.2, 3, 4 Recurrence of strictures after dilation occurs in 30% to 40% of patients within the first year.5 Dilation procedures can be repeated multiple times, with each dilation further injuring the esophageal mucosa, leading to a cycle of injury and stricture recurrence.6 Alternative treatments such as self-expandable metal stents, self-expandable plastic stents, over-the-scope bougies, and electro-incision have been introduced for strictures refractory to standard endoscopic dilation with limited success.

Drug-coated balloons (DCBs) coated with paclitaxel have been used successfully to reduce the rate of recurrence of vascular stenoses and urethral strictures after dilation.7, 8, 9 The current report describes the development and first-in-human clinical application of a DCB designed specifically for the GI tract.

Methods

Study device

This novel DCB (ProTractX3 DCB; GIE Medical Inc, Plymouth, Minn, USA) is a 3-stage, through-the-scope, balloon catheter used to dilate benign strictures in the esophagus. The distal end of the catheter has a semi-compliant inflatable balloon with a proprietary coating containing the active pharmaceutical ingredient paclitaxel at a drug dose of 8.6 to 12.9 mg depending on balloon size with a paclitaxel dose density of 3.5 μg/mm2 (the same as that supplied by common vascular dilation balloons). The device mechanically dilates the stricture while facilitating the application of paclitaxel, which not only provides immediate symptomatic relief through dilation but also limits fibrotic tissue re-growth in the dilated stricture. The device was created as a controlled radial expansion balloon with 3 distinct diameters based on inflation pressure used: 12 mm (12/13.5/15), 15 mm (15/16.5/18), and 18 mm (18/19/20) with a length of 5.5 cm.

Preclinical safety study design

A preclinical safety study was performed in 39 healthy male or female swine weighing between 15 and 22 kg to evaluate the clinical and histologic response of GI tissues to treatment with the DCB, as well as characterize the pharmacokinetic (PK) elimination profile of paclitaxel in the treated GI sites, systemic circulation, and select organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney). The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institution Animal Care and Use Committee (American Preclinical Services, Minneapolis, Minn, USA) before initiating the study and was performed in compliance with Good Laboratory Practices.

The porcine model was chosen to accommodate the relevant DCB sizes and endoscopes used for delivery. The study included 4 treatment arms; 3 DCB test arms designed to evaluate local tissue response to paclitaxel at different doses plus dilation, and 1 comparator control arm to evaluate local tissue response to standard balloon dilation (Table 1). The 3 unique DCB treatment arms varied as to the concentration of paclitaxel: (1) 2 treatment sites with single treatments per site (standard dose arm, 25.8 mg systemic dosage), (2) 6 treatment sites with single treatments per site (high-dose arm, 77.4 mg), and (3) 6 treatment sites with overlapping treatments per site (maximum dose arm, 154.8. mg) to demonstrate a safety margin that would be found in routine clinical use. Overlapping treatments were believed to deliver the maximal anticipated dose and formed the upper limit dose for this study. Sites within the esophagus, duodenum, and colon were treated with 18 × 55 mm DCBs or bare balloons. Animals had planned survival schedules from 1 hour to 28 days’ posttreatment to assess local tissue and plasma concentrations as well as histologic tissue characterizations at several time points.

Study success criteria were based on overall animal health and were defined as no clinically significant adverse events leading to early death or mortality due to treatment. Technical success was defined as successful delivery, inflation, and withdrawal of the devices in the esophagus, duodenum, and colon without procedural device-related adverse events, including death, major GI tract trauma, or device malfunction. Animals for both histologic assessment and PK characterization were treated with either the DCB (single or overlapping treatments) or a bare balloon, with the place of deployment marked for future tissue resection using endoscopically placed sutures. Animals were assessed daily for general health by a trained, on-site veterinarian. Animals were killed at the assigned time points, and a full necropsy was performed to grossly evaluate organs and resect treated segments for analysis. Sites of dilatation were excised en bloc and either snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for later PK analysis or marked with a sutured tag to signify directional orientation (proximal vs distal) and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for histologic assessment.

Paclitaxel concentration in plasma and tissue samples were assessed at a central laboratory using validated high-performance liquid chromatography/mass spectrometry. Histology was conducted at a central laboratory by a trained veterinarian pathologist. The fixed samples were further processed using Movat Pentachrome (for evaluation of collagen fibers) (Abcam, Boston, Mass, USA) and hematoxylin and eosin stains of proximal, middle, and distal portions of the treatment sites. In all, there were 72 histologic slides with hematoxylin and eosin and with Pentachrome of esophageal tissue receiving a DCB high-dose or bare balloon at 7 days and 28 days’ posttreatment, as well as 18 histologic slides per stain of esophageal tissue at 28 days’ posttreatment. Histology was graded in a blinded fashion with a semi-qualitative 5-point scale (0 = not present; 1 = present but minimal feature; 2 = notable feature, mild; 3 = prominent feature that does not disrupt tissue architecture and is not overwhelming, moderate; 4 = overwhelming feature that effaces or disrupts tissue architecture, marked or severe).

Clinical study design

The first-in-human single-center clinical study (INGEST [Gastrointestinal Drug-Coated Balloon for the Treatment of Symptomatic Recurrent Benign Esophageal Stricture]) is a pilot prospective cohort study performed in Paraguay to assess the safety and efficacy of the ProTractX3 DCB in treating chronic benign esophageal strictures (NCT03885310). Institutional review board of Sanatorio Adventista (Asuncion, Paraguay) gave approval, and written informed consent was obtained for all study subjects before treatment. Treatments were performed in a hospital surgical suite using general anesthesia per local standard. The target final esophageal dilation diameter was ≥15 mm with the final dilation being with the ProTractX3 DCB. To promote gradual, staged dilation and avoid multiple dosing of the DCB, predilation was employed using commercially available uncoated balloon dilation catheters (CRE Wireguided, Boston Scientific, Natick, Mass, USA) before application of the DCB. This allowed dilation of tight strictures and ensured the stricture was dilatable to ≥15 mm in the final dilation using ProTractX3 DCB. Subjects were followed up at 30 days, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years postprocedure.

Eligibility criteria included age 18 to 80 years, ability to provide informed consent, absence of serious comorbidities (ie, irreversible coagulopathy, severe cardiopulmonary disease) that would be a contraindication for dilatation, biopsy-proven benign esophageal stricture with diameter <10 mm measured via barium swallow, stricture length <4 cm, Ogilvie Dysphagia Score (ODS) between 2 and 4, and at least 2 previous dilation sessions within 1 year with recurrence of dysphagia. Patients were excluded if strictures were thought to be formed due to extrinsic esophageal compression or determined to be malignant. Dysphagia related to primary motility disorders or untreated erosive esophagitis were exclusionary.

All patients had baseline, discharge, and 3-month kidney and liver function laboratory studies (blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, alkaline phosphatase, alanine transaminase, aspartate transaminase, and bilirubin). A barium esophagram and an endoscopy with biopsy to define the histology of the lesion were performed at baseline. Endoscopy was repeated at 4 weeks and barium esophagram was repeated at 4 weeks and 1 year follow-up. The primary safety end point was the incidence of a composite of serious balloon dilation–related adverse events within 30 days’ postprocedure, including death, perforation, bleeding requiring intervention, severe odynophagia, infection requiring hospitalization or IV antibiotics, and dysphagia requiring re-intervention. Major efficacy end points included improvement in dysphagia symptoms as defined by the ODS and the Dysphagia Handicap Index (DHI), improvement in esophageal stricture diameter at follow-up via barium swallow, need for repeat dilation, and technical success.

The ODS is a simple 5-grade scale used to assess dysphagia in patients, with 0 representing the ability to eat a normal diet without dysphagia and 4 representing dysphagia for liquids.10 Recurrent dysphagia symptoms were defined as an ODS ≥2. The DHI is a 25-item self-administered questionnaire used for measuring the effect of dysphagia on the physical, functional, and emotional aspects of a subject’s daily life.11 Technical success was defined as a successful delivery of the DCB to the target stricture, inflation of the balloon to the desired pressure, and deflation and removal without device malfunction.

This pilot study was designed to assess initial safety and efficacy of the DCB to inform future clinical trials. As such, data are presented as descriptive statistics with no hypothesis testing or formal sample size calculations conducted. The analysis population for this study was a modified intention-to-treat population, including all subjects who were attempted to be treated with the study device and met eligibility criteria.

Statistical methods

Sample sizes for the animal studies were estimated based on prior experience using DCB in the urinary tract of pigs and based on the standard deviation of tissue levels of paclitaxel in these studies. It was estimated that we had 80% power to detect differences from normal tissue to treated tissues of 40% at a .05 significance level based on these studies. Statistical calculations were made using software Graph Prism version 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, Calif, USA). Continuous variables were compared by using 2-sample Student t tests; categorical variables were compared by using the χ2 test or Fisher exact test. P values <.05 were deemed to be significant. Human studies were descriptive given that this is a first-in-man study and no prior data existed. Continuous variables were expressed as mean, standard deviation, and median. Frequency counts and percentages were used to summarize categorical measures.

Results

Preclinical study

Study criteria for success for the overall animal health end point was met, with no clinically significant adverse events or premature death related to treatment with the DCB. Technical success was achieved in 100% of animals. There were no significant differences identified in comparisons of treatment and control groups regarding gross pathologic observations.

In general, all histologic findings in the DCB arms and the control arm had low scores as none of the 27 histologic markers exceeded a grade higher than 2 (mild) in either treatment arm. By day 7, histologic scores for plasma cells, epithelial dysplasia, excessive arrested mitosis, hypocellularity, ulceration, hemorrhage, hemosiderin, dilation, mucinosis, occlusion, vascular thrombi, and inflammation of serosa were all zero (0). The only statistically significant histologic difference between the high-dose treatment group and the control group at day 7 was for less edema in the DCB arm. At 28 days, 93%, 6.6%, and .04% of all 1296 slide scores were rated 0, 1, or 2, respectively. There were no statistically significant differences in any of the different histologic microscopic markers when comparing high dose, maximum dose, and control groups.

Histologic (microscopic) assessment at 7 or 28 days’ posttreatment under the conditions of this study did not reveal any subacute or late-forming GI tract perforation or drug-induced toxicity from single or overlapping (double dose) treatments with the DCB. No other adverse effects such as drug-related local or systemic toxicity were noted. Representative histologic images are presented in Figure 1. There was no clinically significant abnormal trending observed in clinical pathology results over the course of the study.

The measured systemic paclitaxel plasma concentrations were low, indicating very low absorption and systemic exposure in all arms. The average peak paclitaxel plasma concentrations for all 3 dosing regimens occurred at 24 hours and was .20 ng/mL, .52 ng/mL, and 1.19 ng/mL for the standard, high dose, and maximum dose treatment arms, respectively (Fig. 2). By day 3, paclitaxel concentrations for the standard dose arm was below the lower limit of quantitation (<33 ng/mL), while by day 5 the high and maximum dose arms were also below detection. Paclitaxel concentrations in downstream organs such as the liver, kidney, and lungs revealed that paclitaxel concentration was highest for all tissue types immediately posttreatment (1 hour) and declined over time, with average tissue concentrations below the limit of quantification (<.30 ng/g) after 28 days. The average paclitaxel concentration detected in the treated esophagus segment was 141,300 ng of paclitaxel per gram of tissue at 1 hour postprocedure, which was reduced to approximately 1 ng/g at 28 days (Fig. 3).

INGEST clinical study

Ten subjects were consented for participation in the INGEST study from February 2019 to July 2019. One subject was excluded after diagnosis of a malignant stricture during baseline testing. The other 9 patients were treated with the ProTractX3 DCB and completed the scheduled 3-month follow-up examinations. The baseline cohort characteristics are listed in Table 2. Subjects had an average of 2.1 ± .3 prior dilations, with a mean stricture length of 2.0 ± .7 cm and diameter of 6.8 ± 2.4 mm at the time of presentation.

Subjects were pre-dilated with bare balloons to an average diameter of 14.5 ± 2.5 mm, followed by dilation with the DCB to an average diameter of 17.1 ± 2.9 mm. Subject accountability and methods for missing data

All 9 subjects completed scheduled follow-up through 3 months; 1 subject experienced stricture recurrence at the 3-month visit and was subsequently re-treated with the ProTractX3 DCB. Two subjects were lost to follow-up after the 6-month visit, and 1 subject missed the 12- and 24-month visits, leaving 5 evaluable subjects at 12 and 24 months. None of the patients lost to follow-up have presented again to the site for repeat dilatation since the original study treatment.

Safety

No participants had major device- or procedure-related adverse events within the first 30 days. One nonserious adverse event was reported in the study (discomfort and tightness in throat when consuming meat). One death due to esophageal cancer in the treatment group was reported at 738 days’ postprocedure and was deemed not related to the study device or study procedure. It is believed this subject was misdiagnosed as having a benign stricture at the index procedure. This subject maintained an ODS of 1 during follow-up but continued to have an anatomical narrowing at the 6 months (last recorded follow-up).

Efficacy

A single subject experienced stricture recurrence requiring repeat dilation after treatment with the DCB. A Kaplan-Meier curve of subjects remaining free from repeat intervention is provided in Figure 4. Average ODS significantly decreased at each follow-up and showed sustainable improvement over 24 months. The average ODS decreased from 2.22 at baseline to .56 at 30 days (n = 9), and an average ODS of 0 was maintained at 2 years (n = 5) (Fig. 5). Discussion

Results of the preclinical study and the INGEST clinical study show initial safety and durable efficacy from the ProTractX3 DCB through 24 months in a pilot study cohort. In our porcine model, peak systemic paclitaxel levels were low (.20 ng/mL or 2 × 10–4 μmol/L), and dropped below the limit of quantification by 3 days’ postprocedure. For comparison, sustained paclitaxel levels of .05 μmol/L are associated with peripheral neuropathy.12 Average paclitaxel levels in treated tissue peaked immediately posttreatment and remained low but detectable through 28 days’ postprocedure. The observed peak systemic paclitaxel concentration (.20 ng/mL) in this preclinical model was several orders of magnitude lower than that reported for intravenous administration of paclitaxel for chemotherapy (Abraxane [Bristol-Myers Squibb, Princeton, New Jersey] for breast cancer, maximum plasma concentration, 18,741 ng/mL; Taxol for ovarian cancer, maximum plasma concentration, 3650 ng/mL). There were also no significant differences between uncoated balloon and DCB-treated tissue in swine gross pathology and histologic findings.

In the INGEST pilot study, the enrolled cohort consisted of subjects in a population associated with difficult strictures that required frequent repeat dilations, with a baseline average DHI score of 47.6 and an ODS ≥2 and a history of multiple prior dilations. An illustration of the currently used controlled radial expansion balloon is shown in Figure 6. The only potentially treatment-related adverse event reported was an isolated episode of likely food impaction that resolved without medical intervention. Improvement in average obstructive symptoms as measured by the ODS was observed at 1-month postprocedure and maintained through 2 years. The single patient who did not experience symptom relief after DCB retreatment had a complex stricture (3 cm length, 4 mm diameter at endoscopy) and had significant chronic proximal dilation of the esophagus. Even after good mechanical results, difficulties with swallowing coordination may be present in these cases.13

The ProTractX3 drug-coated balloon currently being used in clinical trials.

There are few viable alternatives to standard endoscopic dilation. Electroincision, in which a needle-knife creates radial incisions in the treatment area, can treat difficult esophageal strictures. However, the procedure still typically requires balloon dilation and is generally indicated for shorter anastomotic strictures <1 cm.14 Temporary self-expandable metal stents are another option for treating refractory benign esophageal strictures. However, the deployment process is tedious, and uncovered stents carry long-term risks of tissue ingrowth and incorporation into the esophageal wall, making future removal difficult. Although fully covered self-expandable metal stents prevent this type of tissue ingrowth, 35% of covered stents migrate down the treatment site. In addition, there is a highly significant 50% stricture recurrence rate poststent removal.15 Stenting also carries nontrivial risks of adverse events such as airway constriction, tissue necrosis and fistula formation, or development of new strictures due to the stent.16,17 Other devices, including plastic self-expandable stents and bio-absorbable stents, have had limited benefits with chronic strictures.

Limitations for this early-phase clinical study include a small sample size and an excessive lost to follow-up rate (unfortunately related to the occurrence of the COVID-19 pandemic). The lack of a control group makes comparison to the broader clinical outcomes after standard dilation difficult; however, patient characteristics in this study represent a known difficult patient population. The INGEST trial was designed as a first-in-human study to gain preliminary safety and efficacy data on a novel device. PK and histologic data collected in a healthy porcine model provide insight into local tissue response to treatment, although the translation of these results to human applications remains uncertain.

In conclusion, treatment of benign esophageal strictures with the DCB showed durable results of decreased symptoms and esophageal lumen gain throughout 24 months of follow-up in an early-stage pilot study. Tissue response in a preclinical setting showed no concerns for local drug toxicity and adequate drug transfer and absorption into treated tissue. A future randomized controlled trial in a larger population will provide additional information on the safety and efficacy of the ProTractX3 DCB in the treatment of benign esophageal strictures and is currently enrolling (NCT05561114). Primary dilatation of the stricture with the CRE designed ProTract DCB balloon is a future goal but will need to await the results of the randomized study.
