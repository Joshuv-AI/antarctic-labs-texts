Cell-cycle phase progression analysis identifies three unique phenotypes in soft tissue sarcoma
Authors: Mark M Cullen, Alexander L Lazarides, Patricia D Pittman, Etienne M Flamant, Kathryn L Stoeber, Kai Stoeber, Julia D Visguass, Brian E Brigman, Richard F Riedel, Diana M Cardona, Jason A Somarelli, William C Eward
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeLoddo et al. (Br J Cancer 100:959–70, 2009) established the prognostic significance of cell cycle markers and "Cell-Cycle Phenotypes" in breast carcinoma. This study aims to 1) identify prognostic cell-cycle markers in sarcoma, and 2) assess the prognostic potential of specific cell-cycle phenotypes in sarcoma.MethodsTissue samples from 128 soft tissue sarcomas were stained for four cell cycle-specific markers: Mcm2, Geminin, Plk1, and H3S10ph. Only primary soft tissue tumors (liposarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, synovial sarcoma, and undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma) were included in the analysis. Any tumor coming from a recurrent or metastatic lesion were excluded from the analysis. Three cell-cycle phenotypes (I, II, III) were derived from marker expression patterns. Prognostic significance was evaluated in a subset of primary soft tissue sarcomas using Cox regression for survival analysis.ResultsCompared to phenotype I, the phenotype III tumors had a decreased 5-year overall survival (HR 6.81 [2.36–19.61]; p = < 0.001), 5-year disease-free survival (HR 1.07 (1.02–1.18); p = 0.004), and 5-year metastasis-free survival (HR 4.34 [1.58–11.93]; p = 0.004).High expression of Plk1 was associated with decreased 5-year overall survival (HR: 4.04 CI [1.21–6.67; p = 0.02) and 5-year metastasis-free survival (HR: 2.91 CI [1.15–7.37]; p = 0.03). Geminin was also found to have a decreased 5-year overall survival (HR:2.84 CI [1.21–6.67]; p = 0.02). No statistical difference in prognostication were noted between phenotypes and the AJCC system.ConclusionsWe identified three unique sarcoma cell cycle phenotypes that have prognostic significance. This performs similarly to the AJCC staging system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-024-13043-6.Keywords: Cell-cycle, Sarcoma, Prognosis, Phenotypes

Introduction

Within the hallmarks of cancer [1, 2], those predominantly targeted by chemotherapeutics are linked to cell proliferation [3]. Oncogenic mutations and alterations in tumor suppressor genes facilitate unregulated growth, affording cells a proliferative edge [4]. To deepen our understanding of growth dysregulation in cancer, research has delved into the intricate machinery of the cell cycle governing growth and proliferation [5–7]. This study seeks to elucidate factors that could more accurately predict a patient's prognosis following cancer diagnosis and may uncover potential avenues for future research and therapeutic interventions in both carcinomas [5–11] and sarcomas [7, 11, 12].

Numerous markers of cancer cell proliferation have emerged as reliable prognostic indicators across various studies [5–7, 10, 13, 14]. Ki67 stands out as a prime example [5, 10, 11, 15], demonstrating prognostic value across a spectrum of tumors, including breast cancer [10], medullary thyroid cancer [5], colorectal cancer [16], soft-tissue sarcoma [11], and osteosarcoma [12]. Additionally, several other cell-cycle-specific markers have shown promising prognostic potential in different tumor types. For instance, Geminin, which halts DNA replication initiation within a single cell cycle [7, 17], and mini-chromosome maintenance proteins (Mcm), crucial for DNA unraveling during the S-phase [6, 7, 14, 18], have been associated with disease-free survival and overall survival in soft tissue sarcomas and breast cancer [6, 7]. Furthermore, studies have highlighted the prognostic significance of polo-like kinase (Plk), pivotal for chromatin segregation in the M-phase [17, 19, 20], along with Plk receptors and phosphorylation of H3 at serine 10, marking the transition into the M-phase, in breast cancer [17].

Among these markers, Mcm and Geminin garnered particular attention, as Loddo and colleagues (2009) identified three distinct "cell-cycle phenotypes" through histopathological analysis of breast cancer tumor specimens. Phenotype I denotes tumors in a quiescent state or in the G0 phase [6, 18, 21, 22], Phenotype II characterizes tumors in a delayed growth phase (G1;(6)), and Phenotype III represents tumors actively cycling (S-G2-M; [6]). Notably, Phenotype III emerged as a superior prognostic indicator of disease progression compared to Ki67 [6], a widely accepted marker for proliferation with significant phenotypic implications [5, 10–12, 16].

In the present work, we sought to determine the potential prognostic significance of cell-cycle markers Mcm2, Geminin, Plk1, and H3S10ph in sarcoma. Based on the cell-cycle markers we hoped to use these to create unique cell-cycle phenotypes and test their prognostic significance.

Methods

Study cohort

All data collection procedures were conducted with approval from the Institutional Review Board. 128 bone and soft tissue sarcomas were gathered following resection at an academic center between January 1998 and December 2016. These included tumors from primary, metastatic, and recurrent sarcomas. From these, the four most common soft tissue sarcomas were chosen to be studied, which included synovial sarcomas, leiomyosarcomas, liposarcomas, and undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcomas. Tumors were excluded if: 1) the original biopsy date could not be determined from the medical record, 2) the biopsy quality did not meet biorepository quality assurance protocols, 3) the tumor did not adequately stain for a particular antibody 4) the biopsy was taken from a locally recurrent or metastatic tumor and 5) if they were classified as a grade 1 liposarcoma. The resected tumors were evaluated by a pathologist (DMC), with expertise in sarcoma, and given a diagnosis, based on histological subtype and a grade using the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria [23]. Selection of formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded sarcomas was based on their quality and availability.

Retrospectively, patient- and tumor-specific data included grade, tumor size, mitotic rate, date of birth, date of diagnosis, date of local recurrence, date of metastasis, definitive last follow-up, and date of death were collected for the tumors used in this study. Patients showing signs of metastasis on pathology or imaging within one month of their primary diagnosis were classified as having metastatic disease.

Immunohistochemistry

For each specimen, a representative sample of tumor was carefully selected, and a biopsy was obtained to prepare a Tissue Microarray (TMA). The TMA sections, cut to 3 μm thickness, underwent dewaxing in xylene and rehydration through graded alcohol to water. Subsequently, these sections were pressure-cooked in 0.1 M citrate buffer at pH 6.0 for 2 min before undergoing immunostaining. Primary antibodies, including Mcm2, Geminin, Plk1, and H3S10ph (refer to Supplementary Table 1), were applied to the TMA sections. Negative control involved incubation without a primary antibody, while human colon and skin samples served as positive controls [6].

Protein expression and phosphorylation profile analysis

The labelling index (LI), as described by Dudderidge et al. [24], was employed to assess protein expression and histone H3s10 phosphorylation (H3s10ph) in each tumor. Criteria for identifying positive cells varied for each biomarker: Mcm2, Geminin, and H3s10ph were considered positive with any form of nuclear staining, while for Plk1, any form of nuclear or cytoplasmic staining was deemed positive. Please see Fig. 1 for representative examples of positive and negative stains of each tumor.

Representative examples of positive and negative stains of A) Mcm2, B) Geminin, C) Plk1, and D) H3S10ph

A single pathologist (DMC) examined each tumor in the TMA under a 40 × high-powered field, utilizing an Olympus BX46 microscope (Olympus Corporation, Shinjuku, Tokyo, Japan). Each microscopic image was meticulously evaluated for immunoreactivity, with intensity categorized as weak, moderate, or strong. Tumors were subsequently scored based on their highest intensity level. The percentage of positive cells was determined by dividing the number of immunoreactive cells in the field by the total cell count in that field.

Quantifying the amount of each cell-cycle markers

By optimizing each marker for every outcome measure, we identified both high and low expressions of the cell-cycle markers (Mcm2, Geminin, Plk1) and varied levels of phosphorylation (H3s10ph). The labelling index determined the extent of marker presence within cells, with a labelling index of 50 indicating that 50% of cells in the sample were positive for the marker.

Cut-off points (high and low) were established using the area under a receiver operating characteristic curve for each marker. We adopted the suggested threshold in the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS; Version 27, IBM, Armak, NY) as our cut-off point. This point was where the sensitivity and specificity were at their highest respective levels. Tumors below the determined cut-off value were categorized as having a low amount of the specific cell-cycle marker, while those equal to or above that value were classified as having high amounts of the marker. Tumors exhibiting correct staining and no evidence of the antibody of interest (labelling index of 0) were included in the low group.

After evaluation Mcm2 was expressed in 58 tumors, Geminin in 53 tumors, Plk1 in 58 tumors and H3s10ph in 58 tumors. We determined the labelling index cut-off values were 59 for Mcm2, 19 for Geminin, 10 for Plk1, and 33 for H3s10ph. Please see Table 1 for further details.

Phenotype I (G1 delayed/arrested)

Phenotype I tumors were characterized by low levels of Geminin and Plk1 expression, with fewer than 17% of the cells exhibiting Geminin and fewer than 10% of the cells exhibiting Plk1 expression (refer to Table 2).

Phenotype II (S-phase)

Phenotype II tumors were distinguished by high levels of Geminin expression, greater than 17% of their cells expressing geminin, alongside low levels of Plk1 expression, with less than or equal to 11% of their cells expressing Plk1 (refer to Table 2).

Phenotype III (G2 and M-phase)

Phenotype III tumors exhibited high levels Plk1 expression, with 11% or more of their cells expressing Plk1 (refer to Table 2).

Outcome measures

The 5-year overall survival, disease-free survival, and metastasis-free survival, were computed from the onset of primary diagnosis, defined as either the date of primary biopsy or the identification of disease on imaging, until the initial confirmed manifestation of disease progression. Metastatic disease was characterized by evidence of disease in a location distant from the primary tumor, while locally-recurrent disease manifested as signs of disease progression within the tumor bed from which the primary sarcoma was excised or in the surrounding area. The determination of metastatic disease and locally-recurrent disease relied on the earliest indication of disease observed on imaging. Disease-free survival was determined by the earliest indication of either metastatic or locally-recurrent disease. In cases where there were no signs of disease progression or no recorded date of death, the endpoint of analysis for the patient was marked at 5 years or at the time of the last follow-up, with these patients being censored at that point in time. Patients who experienced mortality or any form of disease progression beyond the 5-year time frame were censored at the 5-year mark.

Statistical analysis

ROC curves and area under the curve (AUC) calculations were employed to determine the optimal cut-off points. Cox regression analysis was employed to assess the prognostic significance of each marker and cell-cycle phenotype on primary endpoints, including 5-year metastasis-free survival, 5-year disease-free survival, and 5-year overall survival. Multivariate analysis for each marker was conducted, which included patient age, tumor size, and grade (WHO classification). ANOVA with Tukey's post-hoc analysis was used to compare mitotic, tumor size, and grade between phenotypes. Finally, the AUC was used to compare the phenotypes created in this analysis to the more traditional AJCC system. Statistical significance was determined at p-values ≤ 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (Version 27, IBM, Armonk, NY),.

Results

Cell-cycle markers and characteristics associated with a worse prognosis in sarcoma

Increased Mcm2 was found to be associated with increased grade (2.82 vs. 2.44; p = 0.02) and mitotic rate (24.5 cells vs. 10.0 cells; p = 0.005). Increased Geminin was associated with increased tumor size (20.6 cm vs. 11.8 cm; p = 0.003) and mitotic rate (26.9 cells vs. 4.5 cells; p = 0.001). Plk 1 was associated with increased tumor size (18.0 cm vs. 10.7 cm; p = 0.04) and grade (3.0 vs. 2.5; p = 0.05). No association between expression and tumor size, grade or mitotic count were found in the H3s10ph group. Please see Table 3 for further details.

Comparison between the cell-cycle phenotypes of interest and AJCC/UICC TNM staging system

The AUC for 5-year overall survival for the phenotypes was 0.67 when compared to the AJCC system, which was 0.74. No difference was noted between these two tests (p = 0.34). The AUC for 5-year disease-free survival for the phenotypes was 0.59 compared to 0.68 in the AJCC system. No difference was noted between these two tests (p = 0.23). Finally, the AUC for 5-year metastasis-free survival was 0.64 in the phenotype group compared to 0.72 in the AJCC system. No difference was noted between these (p = 0.29).

In terms of survival analyses, both phenotype and the AJCC system are predictive of increasing risk. When both are included in the model, both can be used to predict 5-year overall survival (Phenotypes: HR 2.49 [1.48–4.20]; p = < 0.001 and AJCC System: HR 2.63 [1.37–5.04]; p = 0.004), decreased 5-year disease-free survival (Phenotypes: HR 2.25 CI [1.38–3.66]; p = 0.001 and AJCC System: HR 4.02 CI [1.86–8.71]; p = < 0.001), and 5-year metastasis-free survival (Phenotypes: HR 2.70 CI [1.61–4.54]; p = < 0.001 and AJCC System: HR 6.51 CI[2.66–15.96]; p = < 0.001).

Discussion

Analysis of the regulators governing G1-S and G2-M cell cycle transitions has provided valuable insights into disease progression prognoses across various cancers, including breast [6], pancreatic [25], and myeloid malignancies [26]. In sarcoma, the Complexity Index in Sarcoma (CINSARC) looks at gene expression and includes 67 different genes involved in chromosomal instability, mitotic check points and the cell cycle [27]. In soft tissue sarcoma, CINSARC has demonstrated prognostic power [28]. Although CINSARC includes genes that are a part of the cell-cycle, it does not include all of the key regulators and thus a significant knowledge gap persists regarding the correlation between these cell-cycle regulators and clinical outcomes in sarcomas. Hence, our study aimed to 1) identify if our markers of interest had prognostic significance in sarcoma and 2) determine whether distinct phenotypes of altered cell-cycle phase progression exist in sarcomas, and 3) determine if these phenotypes have prognostic significance.

We identified geminin and Plk1 as two markers that may have prognostic significance in sarcoma. Geminin is known to have prognostic significance both in breast cancer [6] and sarcoma [7]. The patients in this study that expressed high levels of Geminin were noted to have larger tumors and those tumors had increased mitotic rates, which are both critical aspects of tumor biology. This study reflected the findings in Hamamoto’s soft tissue sarcoma study [7], in that we noted that increased geminin expression was associated with a decreased overall survival.

To our knowledge, the prognostic significance of Plk1 in sarcoma has yet to be studied. In rectal carcinoma, increased Plk1 expression has been linked to a poorer overall survival [29]. The patients in this study that expressed increased Plk1 were associated with increased tumor size. Like rectal carcinoma, this study demonstrated poorer overall survival in those tumors with high expression of Plk1, as well as an increased risk of developing metastatic disease. We feel that studying these two markers could illuminate their role in understanding sarcoma biologyand could be beneficial in prognostication.

In our investigation, we employed a combination of cell cycle biomarkers to delineate three unique, sarcoma-specific phenotypes of cell cycle progression. These phenotypes were categorized as follows: Phenotype I (G1 – delayed/arrested), Phenotype II (S-phase), and Phenotype III (G2-M-phase). Interestingly, similar to the observations in breast cancer patients by Loddo et al., we observed a poorer overall prognosis among patients with Phenotype II and III tumors when compared to those with Phenotype I tumors. Specifically, patients categorized under Phenotype III in our study exhibited a heightened risk of mortality and those that were Phenotype II or III had a heighted risk of recurrence and metastasis. These phenotypes performed similarly to the current AJCC system of classifying sarcomas.

Limitations

This study is subject to several limitations, most notably our sample size. For the liposarcomas included in this group, we did exclude grade one liposarcomas, but do acknowledge that other forms of liposarcoma including myxoid, round cell, dedifferentiated, and fibroblastic have different prognoses and that it may not be appropriate to group these together. We also acknowledge that not including the anatomical site of tumor removal may be a limitation and that future study of this would be of benefit.

Conclusion

Sarcomas encompass a heterogeneous array of tumors, and the concept of defining distinct phenotypes to categorize them holds promise. In this study, we identify three unique sarcoma phenotypes, which hold prognostic significance and perform similarly to the current AJCC system. Additionally, we identified Plk1 and Geminin as potential future areas of study to both prognosticate sarcomas, as well as better understand sarcoma biology.
