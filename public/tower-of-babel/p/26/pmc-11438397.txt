The effect of thermal aging on microhardness and SEM/EDS for characterisation bioactive filling materials
Authors: Sinem Birant, Burak Gümüştaş
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of this study is to evaluate the surface microhardness, surface chemical composition of bioactive restorative materials pre- and post- thermal aging.MethodA total of 200 disc-shaped samples were prepared by using the materials: Cention N, ACTIVA BioActive Restorative, Equia Forte HT Fil, Glass Fill glass carbomer cement (GCP), and Fuji II LC. Vickers microhardness test were used to measure surface hardness. Scanning electron microscopy-energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM/EDS) was used to determine the characterization of the microstructures and elemental analysis of the materials. These measurements were repeated after thermal aging. One-Way ANOVA test, Bonferroni test and the Games-Howell test was used for data analysis. The significance level was accepted as 0.05.ResultsCention N had the highest vickers microhardness value before thermal cycle. The highest fluoride ion ratio among the materials before thermal aging was detected in the Equia Forte HT Fil and Fuji II LC groups. While a decrease in fluorideF ion was detected in all groups except the Cention N group after thermal aging. It is observed that ACTIVA BioActive Restorative has a more microporous and rougher surface in the scanning electron microscopy image after the thermal cycle than in the image before the thermal cycle.ConclusionsThe chemical properties of the materials and the properties of the filler particles may be related to the differences in the mechanical properties, surface characterizations and ion releases of the materials Thermal aging affected the microhardness, surface characteristics and elemental mass ratios of the studied materials. Alkasite bioactive materials are more similar to composite restorative materials and show better mechanical properties than other materials, but do not have the same effect on fluoride release.Clinical relevanceMost of the bioactive materials showed a decrease in the fluoride ion ratio after thermal aging, while no difference was found in the ion exchange of alkasite materials. Material selection should be made more carefully in caries-active individuals whose fluoride release is clinically important.Keywords: ACTIVA BioActive Restorative, Cention N, Microhardness, Thermal aging, SEM

Introduction

Restorative materials have been constantly developed for a variety of reasons, including dental caries, the persistent high prevalence of recurrent caries, the treatment of high-caries risk patients with a higher degree of restorative failure, and the minimal-intervention strategy [1, 2].

Material selection is an important factor for success in restorative treatment. The ideal restorative material should be similar to the dental tissue in terms of physical and chemical properties and should be able to remain in the mouth for a long time without being deformed. In addition, the restorative material should have features such as biocompatibility, ideal adhesive properties, aesthetics, sufficient mechanical strength, fast and easy application [3]. Fluoride release is becoming increasingly important among the properties of restorative materials in today’s dentistry. Because this feature allows physicians to approach more conservatively in removing caries, but also prevents the formation of secondary caries [4].

Glass ionomer cements are traditionally used as a treatment method in restorative dentistry mainly due to their biocompatible properties to dental hard tissues and their fluoride release [5]. Today, among the restorative materials containing fluoride, there are resin modified glass ionomers, compomers, giomers and composite restorative materials containing bioactive glass fillers, apart from glass ionomers. Recently, two new hybrid composites have been introduced that claim to release fluoride ions: ACTIVA BioActive Restorative (Pulpdent Corporation, Watertown, MA, USA) and Cention N (Ivoclar-Vivadent, AG, Schaan, Liechtenstein). These materials are also called bioactive due to their ionic release [6].

Cention N material shows a bioactive property. It has been developed to prevent demineralization by neutralizing acids with ion release. According to the manufacturer, Cention N contains three inorganic glasses: a conventional inert barium alumino-silicate glass, an ionomer glass based on a calcium barium alumino-fluoro-silicate and a basic calcium fluoro-silicate glass referred to as an “alkasite” filler [7]. Alkasides can be classified as a subgroup of resin-based composites [8, 9]. It is an anticariogenic material that can release calcium and hydroxyl ions [10]. This material can be used in class I, II and V restorations of primary and permanent teeth [11]. Cention N material is relatively more translucent than conventional and resin modified glass ionomer cements, therefore it exhibits more aesthetic properties [12].

ACTIVA BioActive Restorative is a bioactive-restorative material that can respond to oral changes via ion exchange [13, 14]. It has the mechanical and aesthetic properties of composite resins and the moisture tolerance of glass ionomers, chemical bonding to the tooth, release of ions and rechargeability [13]. ACTIVA BioActive Restorative is promoted as a bioactive restorative that forms apatite in marginal cavities and is claimed to release more fluoride than glass ionomer cements [7].

There are very few studies examining the changes in the mechanical properties and ion mass ratios of fluoride-containing bioactive materials after thermal aging. In particular, there is a deficiency in the literature regarding the mechanical and chemical properties of the current restorative materials used in the study, which may change due to the stress in their internal structures after thermal aging. In this study, microhardness, surface properties and ion changes, which have a significant effect on the clinical life of different fluoride-releasing bioactive materials after the thermal ageing procedure to reflect clinical use, were evaluated. The aim of this study is to evaluate the compressive strength, surface hardness, surface composition, ion release of 5 different bioactive materials, including 2 new restorative materials Cention N and ACTIVA BioActive Restorative, and to determine what changes these properties have reached after thermal aging. The null hypothesis tested was, thermal aging procedure had no effect on the microhardness, surface properties and element ratio of the restorative materials containing fluoride ion.

Materials-methods

The producers and contents of 5 different restorative materials used in the study are shown in Table 1. The study experimental design is shown in Fig. 1. The color of all materials was determined as A2, and disc-shaped samples were prepared as n:10 for each experiment by using stainless steel molds with a height of 2 mm and a diameter of 5 mm, in line with the manufacturers. Restorative materials were fixed on the upper and lower surfaces of the molds with a microscope slide using cellulose tape, and the restorative materials were polymerized for 20 s with a dental light device (Valo Grand, Ultradenti USA). The polishing of the samples was done under running water with 600, 900, 1200 grid sandpapers. The samples were kept in distilled water at 37 °C until the experiments were carried out. Each step of the finishing-polishing was applied for 30 s.

Expremental design

Sample size

The sample size calculation was done by considering an alpha value of 0.05 and 80% power to detect a difference of 25%. Considering a common standard deviation of 18% within a single group, the estimated minimum sample size per group should be 10 samples [15].

Surface microhardness measurements

A microhardness testing machine (HMV-2; Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) was used to measure the vickers microhardness (VHN) number of each sample. Vickers microhardness levels were measured at 100 g and 10 s. The value obtained by taking five measurements from each sample and taking the average of the values was included in the statistical analysis.

SEM-EDS analysis

Scanning electron microscope (Zeiss EVO LS 10, Carl Zeiss Ltd., Cambridge, UK) was used to determine the characterization of the microstructures of the materials examined. For chemical analysis of sample surfaces, measurements were made using SEM/EDS energy dispersive detector. Au-Pd coating was performed before SEM images of the samples. Evaluations were made on SEM images after coating. Each surface was obtained using the average of measurements taken from 3 different points and twice from each point. An energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) coupled to scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used for the analysis of the surface chemical composition of the samples. The weight percentages of calcium (Ca), phosphorus (P) and fluoride (F) elements on the surface of the samples were determined.

Thermal cycling procedure

After the materials were subjected to the specified experiments and analyzed, the first measurements were completed. After the first measurements, the newly prepared samples were aged in the thermocycling machine (Huber, SD-Mechatronik-Thermocycler, Germany) (between + 5, +55 °C) for 10,000 cycles before each experiment mentioned above. Thermal aging was done by immersing in tubs with deionized water for 40 s. The change between baths was set to be 20 s. Surface microhardness and SEM-EDS analyzes of the samples after thermal aging were performed by following the procedures.

Statistical analysis

The normality of the results was checked with the Shapiro-Wilk test. The homogeneity was confirmed by the Levene test. One-Way ANOVA test, Bonferroni test and the Games-Howell test was used for data analysis. The significance level was accepted as 0.05 in all analyzes.

Results

The microhardness values of the materials before and after the thermal cycle are shown in Table 2. Cention N had the highest VHN value before thermal cycle with 64.18; this value was significantly higher (p < 0.005) than those of Equia Forte HT Fil (52.29), GC Fuji II LC (41.71), ACTIVA BioActive Restorative (29.08), and GCP (32.22). The pre-thermal cycle VHN value for ACTIVA BioActive Restorative was significantly lower than those for Cention N, Equia Forte HT Fil and Fuji II LC (p < 0.005). No statistically significant difference could be detected between the VHN values before the thermal cycle for the ACTIVA BioActive Restorative and GCP groups (p > 0.005). A statistically significant decrease was detected in microhardness values after thermal cycle for all material groups (p < 0.005).

Discussion

Patient treatment options can be improved by being aware of the mechanical and physical properties of different dental materials [16]. Bioactive restorative materials are a new development brought about by technological advancements in restorative materials and ongoing advancements in dentistry. Bioactive materials exhibit superior properties over established restorative materials, including ion release, stimulation of remineralization, and regulation of environmental acidity, as demonstrated by their apatite structure [16, 17].

This study examined the effects of thermal cycle aging on the surface properties of bioactive restorative materials in order to mimic the changes of the materials over the course of clinical use. Thermal ageing was found to change the elemental structure, hardness and surface roughness of the materials and therefore this study hypothesis was rejected. Previous research has mostly focused on the mechanical characteristics of materials [18–20]. This study assessed the potential alterations brought on by clinical use as well as the characteristics of the materials following thermal aging. In our study, the materials’ initial microhardness values were compared, and the Cention N group showed the highest microhardness value. This material’s high degree of polymerization and highly cross-linked matrix, which give it strength and wear resistance, may be the cause of these result [21].

The absence of Bis-GMA, HEMA, and TEGDMA in alkasites may also help to explain this. The monomer of alkasite sequence’s principal ingredient was UDMA. Enhanced mechanical properties are obtained by combining UDMA, DCP, aromatic and aliphatic UDMA, and PEG-400 DMA [22].

Our study’s results are consistent with those of Karakaş et al.‘s investigation regarding the order of microhardness values of the materials ACTIVA BioActive Restorative, Cention N, and Equia Forte HT Fil [23]. In contrast to this study, the Ruengrunsom et al. study found that the Equia Forte HT Fil and Fuji II LC material groups had microhardness values that were higher than the microhardness value of the Cention N material [19]. However, the values for Cention N and ACTIVA BioActive Restorative are similar to our study [19].

A decrease in the microhardness values is seen when the measurements of the materials are evaluated after thermal aging. In the study of Alrahlah et al., a decrease in the mechanical values of ACTIVA BioActive Restorative and Fuji II LC restorative materials was detected after thermal cycling [24]. This suggests that increased ion release from bioactive materials due to aging causes the mechanical resistance of the material to decrease.

In our study, while the initial F ion ratios of the Equia Forte HT Fil and Fuji II LC groups were similar to each other and had high values between the groups, the F ion ratio of ACTIVA BioActive Restorative was found to be the lowest. It was reported in a different study that the ACTIVA BioActive Restorative group had the lowest F ion ratio, even though Equia Forte HT Fil was determined to have a high one [23]. This supports the fact that traditional glass ionomer-based materials release high F ions even though their solubility is higher [19]. It is also reported that ACTIVA BioActive Restorative releases less fluoride ions than glass ionomer-containing materials [18]. These material properties are in line with the EDS results from our study. A decrease in the F ion ratios of the material groups, except Cention N, was detected after the thermal cycle. In the Cention N material group, an increase in the ratios of F, Al, Si and Ba ions is observed after thermal aging. This can be explained by the size and density of the filler particles of the material [19].

Thermal cycling creates internal stresses within the material, and the difference in thermal expansion between the restoration and the surrounding tooth structure can lead to restoration-tooth separation and microseepage [25]. It is also reported that fluoride ions may be released due to dissolution in the material as a result of aging [26]. In another study using similar materials to our study but acidic aging instead of thermal aging, it was reported that bioactive restorative materials caused a significant increase in the ion release property of bioactive restorative materials due to aging with the pH change of the environment [27].

The increase in the dissolution of ions expressed on the outer surface of the material with aging in EDS analyzes compared to other materials suggests that there are more F, Al, Ba, Si ions in the deep layers of the filler particles of the material. The primary reasons for these variations are their composition and the setting reaction’s kinetics, as fluoride is easily trapped in the matrix [28]. The material composition and the degree of the reaction ultimately determine how many ions are released, and these factors can affect the diffusion [29].

Al release was higher in alkasite following thermal cycling than in ACTIVA BioActive Restorative despite having less bioactive glass filler. This is because the glass filler in alkasite had a higher Al/Si ratio and was more reactive than that of ACTIVA BioActive Restorative. This observed behavioral difference is most likely the consequence of the glass’s phase separation into phases rich in silicate and calcium phosphate [29]. SEM analysis provides micromorphological and microtopographic images that allow reliable evaluation of dental materials [30]. Due to this, SEM was chosen for this study’s evaluation of the materials’ surface analyses both before and after aging, as well as the examination of their surface topographies. It has been reported that the increase in the surface roughness of fluoride-containing materials after thermal aging is related to the amount of fluoride ion release [31]. When the data on surface properties are examined, it is seen that there is an increase in the surface roughness of materials due to aging. These findings are parallel to the results of Karakaş et al.‘s study, in which they evaluated material surfaces after acidic aging [23]. It may be related to the size of the filler particles in these materials. It is also reported that water absorption can hydrolyze the bonds of the material, resulting in the loss of polymer components [32]. Surface roughness is an important criterion when choosing a dental material [33]It affects the long-term success of restorative materials by reducing their durability and surface quality [33]. In clinical practice, increased surface roughness of a dental restoration results in plaque accumulation, secondary caries and loss of a material’s aesthetic properties, among others. Rough surfaces particularly favour the proliferation of bacteria responsible for the development of caries and periodontitis [34]. Another study investigating the effect of ageing on marginal integrity showed that dentin is more susceptible to thermal effects; enamel margins of composite restorations were not affected by thermal cycling until 1000 cycles/60 seconds dwell time, whereas dentin margins were affected after 500 cycles/60 seconds dwell time [35]. If the surface hardness and roughness of restorative materials are insufficient, the life of the restoration is shortened and the decrease in the surface hardness of the material due to water absorption also affects the surface properties and roughness of the material [36]. For these reasons, in this study, the first priority was to assess the surface hardness and properties of current bioactive restorative materials containing fluoride ion.

As a result the chemical properties of the materials and the properties of the filler particles may be related to the differences in the mechanical properties, surface characterizations and ion releases of the materials Thermal aging affected the microhardness, surface characteristics and elemental mass ratios of the studied materials. Alkasite bioactive materials are more similar to composite restorative materials and show better mechanical properties than other materials, but do not have the same effect on fluoride release. Material selection should be made more carefully in caries-active individuals whose fluoride release is clinically important. Therefore, the fluoride release of the material in caries active individuals is of great clinical importance in material selection. However, since this is an in vitro study, it does not represent all in vivo conditions and interactions. The ionic composition of dietary food/drinks, intraoral pH changes, lack of salivary enzymes are among the limitations in the evaluation of changes in the material. In addition, examining the fluoride recharge properties of the materials in future studies will provide a more holistic approach to the subject.
