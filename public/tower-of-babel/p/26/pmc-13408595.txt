Blocking the m6Am methyltransferase PCIF1 releases STAT1-mediated Th1 immunity to potentiate cancer immunotherapy
Authors: Jiansong Huang, Jing Zhou, Xiao Liu, Xiaoxu Liu, Ye Xiao, Kai Li, Chenbo Ding, Xuemin Cai, Wenli Mao, Changfen Xu, Yuxin Kong, Yu Deng, Xiaohan Song, Mei Yang, Daohai Du, Siqi Guo, Kaixian Chen, Cheng Luo, Chengqi Yi, Shijie Chen, Hua-Bing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Naïve T cells maintain a delicate balance between quiescence and rapid activation, which involves multiple layers of regulation beyond transcription. Here, we identify the RNA modification N6,2’-O-dimethyladenosine (m6Am) and its methyltransferase PCIF1 as critical enforcers of T cell quiescence. During CD4+ T cell activation, m6Am levels are dynamically downregulated. T-cell-specific PCIF1 knockout (cKO) mice exhibit potent tumor suppression, driven by enhanced Th1 differentiation and subsequent amplification of NK cell cytotoxicity. Mechanistically, PCIF1 represses STAT1 translation via m6Am modification of its mRNA, thereby constraining Th1 commitment. Activation-induced PCIF1 downregulation releases this translational brake, enabling rapid Th1 polarization. Crucially, we identify Suramin as a pharmacological PCIF1 inhibitor that disrupts m6Am modification, boosts Th1 responses, and suppresses tumor growth. Our findings establish the PCIF1-m6Am-STAT1 axis as a translational checkpoint governing T cell differentiation and suggest that targeting PCIF1 represents a potential strategy for tumor immunotherapy.

Introduction

Upon activation, naïve T cells rapidly undergo proliferation and differentiation after exiting the quiescent state, which requires the synthesis of a large number of proteins in a short period to ensure their normal function1–3. Therefore, naïve T cells need to delicately balance quiescent and active states, but the underlying mechanism remains largely unknown. Epigenetic alterations, encompassing modifications at the histone, DNA, and RNA levels4,5, exhibit remarkable dynamic changes and have been reported to regulate key processes underlying T cell proliferation and differentiation6,7. Our previous research showed that translation events constitute the most significant changes in genetic information processing during the early activation of naïve CD4+ T cells, and tRNA N1-methyladenosine (m1A) modification acts as a translational checkpoint to increase translation efficiency during T cell proliferation8. Another abundant mRNA modification, the N6-methylation of cap-adjacent N6,2’-O-dimethyladenosine (m6Am), is dynamically installed by the methyltransferase Phosphorylated C-terminal domain (CTD) interacting factor 1 (PCIF1) and has been reported to modulate translation efficiency9–12. However, its role in the immune system remains poorly understood.

In eukaryotic mRNAs, adenosine derivatives undergo distinct methylation modifications, including adenosine (A) and 2’-O-methyladenosine (Am)13,14. Am is predominantly located at the first transcribed nucleotide adjacent to the m7G cap, and N6-methylation of Am generates m6Am12,15, a modification exclusively catalyzed by PCIF1 through its WW domain-mediated interaction with the phosphorylated CTD of RNA polymerase II11,16–18. As an m7G cap-dependent methyltransferase, PCIF1 plays a critical role in regulating mRNA stability and translation11,12,19. Although m6Am has been established as a critical regulator of mRNA translation in tumor cells20,21, its potential role in orchestrating T cell activation and differentiation remains largely unexplored.

In this study, we note a marked reduction in m6Am levels during CD4+ T cell activation and differentiation. In a mouse model with T cell-specific PCIF1 knockout, we observe that the absence of PCIF1 in CD4+ T cells impedes tumor growth by enhancing Th1-mediated augmentation of NK cell antitumor activity. Mechanistically, our findings indicate that PCIF1 suppresses Th1 differentiation by inhibiting the translation of STAT1, a transcription factor essential for Th1 development. Furthermore, we identify that Suramin acts as a chemical inhibitor targeting PCIF1, preventing its m6Am modification activity. This action leads to a tumor-suppressive effect by enhancing Th1 cell function. Our findings unveil the PCIF1-m6Am-STAT1 axis as a pivotal translational gatekeeper of T cell differentiation and demonstrate that targeting this checkpoint overcomes immunosuppression to potentiate tumor immunotherapy.

Results

PCIF1 depletion in T cells hinders tumorigenesis

Our previous study revealed that RNA epigenetic modification can promote rapid T cell proliferation by regulating protein translation8. T cell activation and differentiation are biological processes with substantial protein synthesis demands accompanying T cell proliferation1–3; therefore, we questioned whether RNA epigenetic modifications also play a similar role in T cell differentiation and which modification plays a dominant role. To test this hypothesis, we isolated naïve CD4+ T cells from the spleens of wild-type (WT) mice and activated them in vitro with anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 to mimic T cell receptor (TCR) signaling, leading to their differentiation into Th0 cells with multiple Th lineage potentials22,23. Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometer (LC-MS/MS) analysis of Th0 cells at 0, 48, and 72 h post-activation revealed that among 11 common mRNA modifications, only m6Am exhibited a dramatic decrease at the 48- and 72-hour time points, while other modifications either increased or remained unchanged (Fig. 1a). This significant and specific change in m6Am levels suggests that it may be particularly involved in the regulatory mechanisms of T cell differentiation, possibly by affecting the translation of key proteins required for this process. Although m6Am modification has been reported to regulate RNA translation in tumor cells20,24,25, its role in the immune system, particularly in CD4+ T cell function, remains largely unexplored. Considering that PCIF1 is the only identified methyltransferase capable of catalyzing m6Am modification in mRNA, we constructed a conditional knockout mouse model with T cell-specific deletion of PCIF1 (Pcif1flox/flox Cd4Cre, cKO). To investigate the impact of m6Am modification on T cell differentiation, we used Pcif1flox/flox (WT) mice as littermate controls. Next, we conducted phenotypic assessments under homeostatic conditions in WT and cKO mice. No significant differences were observed in the composition, apoptosis, or proliferation capabilities of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the thymus and peripheral lymphoid tissues (Supplementary Fig. 1b–l, 7a, b), indicating that PCIF1 may not play a critical role in T cell homeostasis. Given the pivotal role of T cells in antitumor immunity, we utilized mouse tumor models to investigate the functional regulation of T cells by PCIF1. In an azoxymethane (AOM)-induced spontaneous colon cancer model26, cKO mice exhibited a reduced incidence and smaller volume of colon tumors compared to WT controls (Fig. 1c). Following isolation of colon-infiltrating lymphocytes, we observed that CD4+ and CD8+ T cells from cKO mice secreted significantly higher levels of IFN-γ and IL-2 (Fig. 1d–f). This enhanced T cell function extended to subcutaneous tumor models: cKO mice exhibited stronger suppression of tumor growth (Fig. 1g–i, k–m; Supplementary Fig. 1m, n) and prolonged survival (Fig. 1j, n) in both MC38 and B16-F10 models. Flow cytometry confirmed that tumor-infiltrating CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in cKO mice were more numerous and produced more IFN-γ and IL-2 (Fig. 1o, p; Supplementary Fig. 1o, p). We next evaluated whether PCIF1 deficiency in T cells could synergize with PD-1 blockade. Notably, in tumor-bearing WT and Pcif1 cKO mice, the combination of PCIF1 deficiency in T cells with anti-PD-1 therapy provided superior tumor control compared to PCIF1 knockout alone (Supplementary Fig. 3e–g). This finding demonstrates that loss of PCIF1 in T cells not only enhances intrinsic antitumor activity but also sensitizes tumors to immune checkpoint inhibition, pointing to an epigenetic strategy for improving immunotherapy efficacy.

In contrast, the depletion of CD4+ T cells completely abrogated this difference (Supplementary Fig. 2b–e). To directly assess the role of antigen-specific CD4+ T cells, we adoptively transferred PCIF1-deficient or WT OT-II CD4+ T cells into Rag1-/- mice bearing B16-OVA tumors. PCIF1-deficient OT-II T cells mediated significantly stronger suppression of tumor growth (Fig. 2d–f and Supplementary Fig. 2g), prolonged host survival (Fig. 2g), and produced more IFN-γ in tumors (Fig. 2h) compared to WT controls. These data establish that PCIF1-deficient CD4+ T cells are primary drivers of the enhanced antitumor response. We also confirmed, in agreement with Xiang et al. 27, that PCIF1-deficient CD8+ OT-I T cells alone exhibit stronger tumor suppression than WT OT-I T cells in Rag1-/- hosts (Supplementary Fig. 2h–k), underscoring the functional versatility of PCIF1 in regulating distinct T cell compartments.

Based on the central role of CD4+ T cells in coordinating antitumor immunity28,29, we next tested whether PCIF1-deficient CD4+ T cells could amplify the activity of CD8+ T cells. Co-transfer of PCIF1-deficient CD4+ T cells with WT OT-I CD8+ T cells into Rag1-/- mice resulted in superior tumor control compared to co-transfer with WT CD4+ T cells (Fig. 2i–k). This was accompanied by elevated secretion of IFN-γ and IL-2 from both T cell subsets (Supplementary Fig. 2l, m) and enhanced granzyme secretion by tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 2n), a phenotype consistent with that observed in cKO mice (Fig. 2l). Given that CD4+ T cell-derived IFN-γ promotes Th1-type polarization and functional optimization of CD8+ T cells30,31, we propose that the enhanced crosstalk between PCIF1-deficient CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells may partially explain the potent antitumor phenotype observed in cKO mice.

CD4+ T-NK axis confers PCIF1-mediated tumor killing

Considering that CD4+ T cells can also regulate the antitumor function of natural killer (NK) cells30,32–34, we then examined NK cell responses in our model. In the B16-F10 tumor-bearing model, cKO mice displayed increased infiltration of NK cells into tumors compared to WT controls (Fig. 3a). These tumor-infiltrating NK cells also secreted significantly higher levels of IFN-γ, IL-2, and granzyme B (Fig. 3b, c and Supplementary Fig. 3a). In contrast, PCIF1 knockout did not enhance the cytotoxic function of CD4+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 1s, t). To investigate whether PCIF1 deficiency in CD4+ T cells augments NK cell-mediated antitumor responses in cKO mice, we depleted NK cells in both WT and cKO mice using an anti-NK1.1 depleting antibody. NK cell depletion not only accelerated tumor growth in both groups but also abolished the differences in tumor growth between them (Fig. 3d–f and Supplementary Fig. 3b). These findings demonstrate that PCIF1 deficiency in CD4+ T cells critically enhances NK cell-mediated antitumor immunity through functional crosstalk.

To further validate the role of PCIF1 deficiency in CD4+ T cells in enhancing NK cell-mediated antitumor immunity, we adoptively transferred CD4+ T cells from WT and cKO mice into Rag1-/- mice followed by B16-F10 tumor implantation. Mice receiving CD4+ T cells from cKO mice exhibited significantly slower tumor progression and higher cytokine secretion compared to those receiving WT CD4+ T cells (Fig. 3g–j). Flow cytometric analysis further demonstrated enhanced cytokine-secreting capacity of NK cells within the tumor microenvironment of cKO recipients (Fig. 3k and Supplementary Fig. 3c, d). These results collectively establish that the suppression of tumor growth in PCIF1 cKO mice is driven by CD4+ T cell-intrinsic PCIF1 deficiency, which mechanistically enhances NK cell-mediated antitumor activity.

PCIF1 deficiency drives Th1 polarization of naïve CD4+ T cells

Given our in vivo tumor model observations that CD4+ T cells deficient in PCIF1 exhibit enhanced expression of Th1-type cytokines (Figs. 1o, 2h, Supplementary Fig. 2i), we hypothesized that PCIF1 may regulate antitumor functions by modulating Th1 differentiation. To test this hypothesis, we employed an in vitro T cell differentiation system. We first confirmed that PCIF1-deficient naïve CD4+ T cells did not exhibit elevated secretion of IFN-γ or IL-2 prior to activation (Supplementary Fig. 4d). Upon polarization, however, these cells displayed a strong bias toward Th1 differentiation and a concurrent decrease in Th2 potential (Fig. 4a, b and Supplementary Fig. 4a), with no significant change in their potential to form other helper T cell subsets (Supplementary Fig. 4b, c). This Th1-skewed phenotype was also observed in vivo, where cKO mice showed reduced tumor-infiltrating Th2 cells without altered Treg frequency (Supplementary Fig. 1q, r). Moreover, during Th1 polarization, both PCIF1 protein and global mRNA m6Am levels were dynamically downregulated (Fig. 4c, d). To further elucidate the mechanism by which PCIF1 regulates Th1 differentiation, we isolated naïve CD4+ T cells and Th1 cells from WT and cKO mice for RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq). Analysis of the sequencing data revealed a minimal number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between WT and cKO naïve CD4+ T cells (Fig. 4e, f and Supplementary Data 1, 2), consistent with the primary role of m6Am in translational regulation9–12. However, Th1-polarized cKO cells showed substantial transcriptional upregulation, including key Th1-associated genes such as Ifng and Il235 (Fig. 4e, f). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) assays confirmed that Th1 cells from cKO mice significantly upregulated the expression of Ifng and Il2 compared to WT cells, while no significant differences in the expression of these genes were observed in the naïve state (Supplementary Fig. 4e, f). Furthermore, under Th1 differentiation conditions, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment and upregulation of pathways related to cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions, JAK-STAT signaling, T cell receptor signaling, and Th1/Th2 cell differentiation in cKO cells, consistent with our observation that PCIF1 deficiency promotes a Th1 differentiation phenotype (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Data 7).

To determine whether PCIF1 regulates Th1 differentiation through its methyltransferase activity, we introduced WT and catalytically dead mutant PCIF1 expression vectors into PCIF1 knockout CD4+ T cells. We found that WT PCIF1 effectively suppressed the secretion of downstream cytokines IFN-γ and IL-2, whereas the catalytically dead mutant failed to exert this effect (Fig. 4h–k). Similar results were obtained in PCIF1-knockout Jurkat cells (Supplementary Fig. 4g, h). These findings demonstrate that PCIF1 relies on its m6Am methyltransferase activity to repress Th1 differentiation.

Finally, we evaluated whether PCIF1 deficiency-driven Th1 polarization enhances NK cell cytotoxicity. Co-culture of in vitro-derived cKO Th1 cells with NK cells augmented NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity (Supplementary Fig. 4i–k). To extend these findings to human cells, we knocked down PCIF1 in human naïve CD4+ T cells and differentiated them toward Th1 (Supplementary Fig. 4l, m). Similarly, PCIF1-deficient human Th1 cells enhanced the cytotoxic activity of co-cultured human NK cells (Supplementary Fig. 4n). These results demonstrate that PCIF1-deficiency-driven Th1 polarization potently enhances NK cell cytotoxicity across species.

PCIF1 deletion enhances Th1 differentiation via the STAT1-T-bet pathway

To elucidate how PCIF1 maintains T cell quiescence, we performed m6Am-seq and identified 669 genes with m6Am modifications in naïve CD4+ T cells (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Fig. 5a and Supplementary Data 3). Functional enrichment analysis showed that these m6Am-modified genes were significantly associated with pathways involved in Th1/Th2 cell differentiation and JAK-STAT signaling (Fig. 5c). To systematically assess the effect of PCIF1 on translation, we crossed WT and cKO mice with Ribo-Tagflox/flox mice and isolated naïve CD4+ T cells from the offspring for RiboTag-seq36. Our analysis revealed that 2491 genes exhibited increased translation efficiency (TE), while 136 genes showed decreased TE in the absence of PCIF1 (Fig. 5d, e and Supplementary Data 4). These results suggest that PCIF1 deficiency augments the translation of a broad spectrum of genes in naïve CD4+ T cells.

To identify specific mRNA targets through which PCIF1 exerts its translational repression in naïve CD4+ T cells, we intersected the 669 m6Am-modified genes with the 2,491 genes displaying elevated translation efficiency in PCIF1-deficient cells. This overlap identified 107 potential direct m6Am targets whose translation is suppressed by PCIF1, including Stat1 (Fig. 5f, Supplementary Fig. 5d, and Supplementary Data 5). Consistently, Pcif1 deficiency significantly reduced the m6Am level on Stat1 mRNA in CD4+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 5e). Given that STAT1 is a key transcription factor driving Th1 differentiation through induction of T-bet37,38, we further validated these findings using Ribo-Tag-qPCR, which confirmed enhanced translation of Stat1 in PCIF1-deficient cells (Fig. 5g). In naïve CD4+ T cells, where phospho-STAT1 is normally low relative to Th1-polarized cells, loss of PCIF1 specifically increased the protein levels of both total STAT1 and phospho-STAT1 (P-STAT1) without altering their mRNA abundance, as demonstrated by western blot and flow cytometry (Fig. 5h–j and Supplementary Fig. 5f–i). This post-transcriptional elevation indicates that PCIF1 regulates STAT1 primarily at the translational level. Consistent with this mechanism, elevated STAT1 protein was also observed in PCIF1-deficient Th1 cells (Supplementary Fig. 5j). Together, these results support a model in which PCIF1 deletion enhances Th1 differentiation by augmenting the STAT1 signaling pathway.

To directly demonstrate that PCIF1 regulates Stat1 translation, we mutated the first nucleotide after the 5’ methylation cap of Stat1 from A to T, thereby ablating the m6Am modification site. Luciferase reporter assays showed that this mutation significantly increased luciferase activity (Fig. 5k). Furthermore, re-expression of WT PCIF1, but not a catalytically dead mutant, in PCIF1-knockout Jurkat cells reduced STAT1 protein levels (Supplementary Fig. 5k, l). These results collectively demonstrate that PCIF1 regulates Stat1 translation through its m6Am methyltransferase activity.

Finally, to connect this regulation to Th1 lineage commitment, we examined T-bet, a direct target of STAT1. PCIF1 deficiency increased T-bet protein in both naïve CD4+ T cells, in vitro-polarized Th1 cells, and tumor-infiltrating CD4+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 5m–o). In addition, treatment with a STAT1 inhibitor abolished the elevated T-bet expression (Supplementary Fig. 5p) and the enhanced IFN-γ secretion in cKO Th1 cells (Fig. 5l). These results indicate that PCIF1 regulates Th1 differentiation by suppressing the STAT1-T-bet pathway.

PCIF1 is a potential therapeutic target for immunotherapy via Th1 boosting

While previous studies have shown that PCIF1 deficiency in tumor cells reduces their tumorigenicity20,24,25, our work demonstrates that PCIF1 loss in T cells potently promotes Th1 differentiation and enhances antitumor immunity. This prompted us to ask whether PCIF1 could be targeted pharmacologically to boost T-cell-mediated tumor killing. Therefore, we performed a Fluorescence Polarization (FP)-based high-throughput screening to identify an inhibitor of PCIF1 (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Since PCIF1 has been reported to methylate specific uncapped RNA39, we utilized the validated uncapped 5’ -Am RNA as the substrate of PCIF1 in the screening assay. We further found that PF007 inhibited the interaction between PCIF1 and Am-modified RNA with a half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) value of 0.64 ± 0.02 µM in the FP assay (Fig. 6c). To confirm that this binding inhibition translates into functional blockade, we assessed its effect on methyltransferase activity using an MTase-Glo assay, and observed that PCIF1-catalyzed RNA methylation was inhibited in vitro with an IC50 value of 10.8 ± 1.0 µM (Fig. 6d). To further assess the binding affinity of PF007 to PCIF1, we measured the dissociation constant (KD) of PF007 for PCIF1 as 4.88 ± 0.63 μM by isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) and 0.87 ± 0.06 μM by surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assay (Fig. 6e, f). We also observed that PF007 stabilized PCIF1 in Jurkat cells by a cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA), indicating direct target engagement in a cellular context (Supplementary Fig. 6d).

LC-MS/MS analysis confirmed that PF007 reduces m6Am levels not only on synthetic 5’ -m7GpppAm RNA but also in primary CD4+ T cells (Fig. 6g, h). The compound exhibited low cytotoxicity toward CD4+ T cells, with a half-maximal cytotoxic concentration (CC50) of 122.5 µM (Supplementary Fig. 6e). We next tested whether PF007 could promote Th1 differentiation in vitro, and found that PF007 effectively enhanced the polarization of naïve CD4+ T cells toward the Th1 lineage at 30 µM (Fig. 6i). Consistent with this effect, PF007 treatment significantly increased mRNA expression of Ifng and Il2 and elevated STAT1 protein levels in T cells (Supplementary Fig. 6f, g), mirroring the phenotype observed in PCIF1-deficient CD4+ T cells. Together, these data establish PF007 as a functional inhibitor of PCIF1 catalytic activity.

To evaluate the in vivo efficacy of PF007, we first established a subcutaneous tumor model in WT mice using B16-F10 cells and administered PF007 at a dose of 30 mg/kg via intraperitoneal injection. Compared to the control group, PF007 treatment significantly suppressed tumor growth (Fig. 6j–l). To further confirm that PF007 inhibits tumor growth by enhancing T cell function, we generated a PCIF1-deficient B16-F10 cell line (Supplementary Fig. 6i) and implanted these cells subcutaneously into WT mice. Notably, PF007 treatment still effectively inhibited tumor progression in this setting (Fig. 6m, n), indicating that its antitumor activity is largely mediated through the host immune system. Functional analysis of tumor-infiltrating immune cells revealed elevated secretion of IFN-γ and IL-2 by CD4+ T cells in PF007-treated mice, indicating that PF007 exerts its antitumor effects in vivo by boosting Th1 function (Fig. 6o, p). Furthermore, PF007 increased the number of tumor-infiltrating NK cells and their production of granzyme B (Supplementary Fig. 6j, k). In line with these in vivo observations, an in vitro co-culture assay confirmed that PF007-driven Th1 differentiation boosts the tumor-killing capacity of NK cells (Supplementary Fig. 6l). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that targeting the m6Am methyltransferase PCIF1 represents a promising epigenetic strategy for cancer immunotherapy by boosting Th1 function.

Discussion

Naïve T cells maintain a delicate equilibrium between quiescence and rapid activation, a process demanding stringent control over protein synthesis. Our study identifies PCIF1-mediated m6Am modification as a pivotal translational checkpoint enforcing this balance. We demonstrate that m6Am levels are dynamically downregulated during CD4+ T cell activation and Th1 differentiation. Furthermore, absence of PCIF1 derepresses Stat1 mRNA translation, thereby promoting Th1 differentiation and enhancing NK cell-mediated antitumor immunity. This axis not only advances our understanding of T cell quiescence at the RNA modification level but also identifies PCIF1-mediated m6Am modification as a druggable target for cancer immunotherapy (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Although m6Am is a well-established regulator of mRNA translation in tumor cells, its role in regulating immune cell function, especially T cell activation and differentiation, remains largely unexplored. Recently, a study demonstrated that PCIF1 orchestrates CD8+ T cell ferroptosis and activation to control antitumor immunity, highlighting its role in suppressing ferroptosis-related genes (e.g., FTH1, SLC3A2) and enhancing the efficacy of PD-1 blockade and CAR-T cell therapies27. Our study reveals that PCIF1 selectively restrains Th1 differentiation by repressing the translation of STAT1, a key transcription factor for Th1 development, through m6Am modification of Stat1 mRNA. Consequently, PCIF1 deficiency leads to elevated STAT1 protein levels, which in turn enhances STAT1 phosphorylation and upregulates the downstream master regulator T-bet, thereby biasing naïve CD4+ T cells toward the Th1 lineage. In contrast, mRNAs encoding lineage-defining factors for other T helper subsets, such as Gata3 (Th2) and Foxp3 (Treg), lack detectable m6Am modification, and their protein levels remain unaffected by PCIF1 knockdown. Thus, the PCIF1-m6Am axis functions as a specific checkpoint for Th1 commitment without broadly altering alternative CD4+ T cell fates. Furthermore, we identified Suramin as a pharmacological PCIF1 inhibitor, demonstrating its ability to enhance Th1 function and suppress tumor growth, thus offering a promising therapeutic strategy for cancer immunotherapy. This duality underscores compartmentalized RNA epigenetic regulation across different T cell subsets, with PCIF1 acting as a versatile enforcer of immune quiescence.

While our work establishes PCIF1-mediated m6Am RNA modification as a critical translational checkpoint in CD4+ T cell biology and antitumor immunity, key questions remain to be resolved: First, potential upstream mechanisms, such as TCR-triggered ubiquitination or transcriptional repression, that drive activation-induced PCIF1 downregulation require elucidation. Second, while STAT1 functions as the dominant effector, auxiliary Th1-polarization mechanisms may exist through other identified m6Am targets that warrant functional validation. Finally, although the multi-target nature of Suramin precludes its direct clinical use, it serves as a proof-of-concept tool validating PCIF1 as a druggable target for antitumor immunity. Future clinical translation will require the development of more specific PCIF1-targeted inhibitors through medicinal chemistry.

In conclusion, our findings establish the PCIF1-m6Am-STAT1 axis as a fundamental translational checkpoint in CD4+ T cell biology. By bridging RNA epigenetics to antitumor immunity, we decipher a molecular mechanism enforcing T cell quiescence and identify Suramin as a pharmacological tool to license Th1 responses. These insights offer a new epigenetic strategy to potentiate cancer immunotherapy.

Methods

Mice

Pcif1flox/flox mice were generated by Cyagen Biosciences Inc. (Suzhou, China). The mouse Pcif1 gene (NCBI reference sequence: NM_146129) is located on chromosome 2 and comprises 17 exons, with the translation start codon in exon 3 and the stop codon in exon 17. A targeting vector was designed to introduce loxP sites flanking exons 5–7. The vector also contained a neomycin resistance (Neo) cassette flanked by self-deletion anchor (SDA) sequences for positive selection, and a diphtheria toxin A (DTA) cassette for negative selection. Following electroporation of the targeting construct into C57BL/6 N embryonic stem (ES) cells, a correctly targeted ES cell clone (designated N-2E1) was identified and microinjected into C57BL/6 albino embryos, which were subsequently transferred into pseudopregnant CD-1 females. Founder animals were identified by coat color chimerism, and germline transmission was confirmed by breeding with C57BL/6 females and subsequent genotyping of the offspring. These mice were then crossed with Cd4Cre mice obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (JAX stock # 022071) to generate Pcif1flox/floxCd4Cre conditional knockout (cKO) mice. Concurrently, Pcif1flox/flox mice were used as wild-type (WT) littermate controls. Additionally, Ribo-Tagflox/flox mice from The Jackson Laboratory (JAX stock # 011029), which are from the B6N.129 strain, were bred with Cd4Cre mice to enable the introduction of an HA-epitope tag on the ribosomal protein RPL22 in all CD4-expressing cells. OT-I mice (JAX stock # 003831) and OT-II (JAX stock # 004194) mice were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory. Rag1-/- (Cat. NO. NM-KI-00069) mice were purchased from the Shanghai Research Center for Model Organisms (Shanghai, China). Mice used in this study were age-matched (8–10 weeks old) and sex-matched (male and female). Mice were randomly allocated to experimental groups, with each cage containing mice from various groups to ensure variability. All mice were housed and bred in a specific pathogen-free environment on a strict 12-hour day-night cycle with a regular chow diet at 20 °C–24 °C and 45–65% humidity within the animal facility of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. All mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation at the end of the experimental procedures. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, and the approval identifier is JUMC2023-086-A. The primers used for mouse genotype identification are listed in Supplementary Data 6.

Cell lines and culture

B16-F10, MC38, B16-OVA, B16-F10-Luc, Raji-Luc, and Pcif1-/- B16-F10 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Hyclone) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Transgene), while HEK293T cells were cultured in DMEM (Hyclone) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Transgene). All media contained 100 U/ml penicillin and 100 µg/ml streptomycin. Cells were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2. B16-F10-Luc and Pcif1-/- B16-F10 cells were generated in our laboratory. Other cell lines were from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences.

Azoxymethane (AOM) model of colorectal cancer

WT and Pcif1 cKO mice were intraperitoneally injected with azoxymethane (AOM; 10 mg/kg) once weekly for four weeks. Colonoscopy was performed six months after the final injection to assess tumorigenesis, and samples were collected.

B16-F10 and MC38 tumor model

Mice were subcutaneously inoculated with either 1×105 (in the OT-I direct transfer model only) or 4×105 tumor cells. Tumor nodule measurements began one week after injection, and the experiments were concluded when the tumor diameter reached 1.5 cm.

Antibody depletion tumor model

Mice received intraperitoneal injections of depleting antibodies or control IgG twice weekly. Each injection consisted of 150 µg of antibody in 250 µl PBS. The depleting antibodies included anti-CD4 (BE0003-1), anti-CD8 (BE0061), and anti-NK1.1 (BE0036), all obtained from BioXcell.

Rag1-/- mice tumor model

Naïve CD4+ T cells were isolated from mouse spleens using the EasySep Mouse Naïve CD4+ T Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies). A total of 1×106 naïve CD4+ T cells from WT or Pcif1 cKO mice were adoptively transferred into Rag1-/- mice. Two to three days post-transfer, a subcutaneous tumor model was constructed using B16-F10 cells as described above.

Tissue preparation

Thymus, spleen, and lymph nodes were passed through a 100-µm cell strainer. Splenocytes were lysed using red blood cell lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For isolation of colon-infiltrating lymphocytes, the colon was collected, opened longitudinally, rinsed with cold PBS, and cut into 1–2 cm segments. Segments were incubated in extraction buffer (PBS containing 2% FBS, 2 mM EDTA, and 1 mM DTT) at 37 °C with agitation (200 g) for 30 min to remove intraepithelial lymphocytes. After washing, the remaining lamina propria tissue was minced and digested in RPMI 1640 medium containing 2% FBS, 1 mg/ml collagenase II, and 0.5 mg/ml dispase at 37 °C with agitation (200 g) for 60 minutes. The resulting cell suspension was filtered, and lymphocytes were purified by Percoll density gradient centrifugation.

For isolation of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) from B16-F10 and MC38 tumors, tumors were excised, minced, and digested in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% FBS, 1 mg/ml collagenase II, and DNase I at 37 °C with agitation (200 g) for 30 min. The digested tissue was passed through a 100-μm cell strainer, and TILs were isolated from the resulting single-cell suspension by Percoll density gradient centrifugation.

Antibody staining and flow cytometry

Monoclonal antibodies against CD3 (clone 145-2C11), CD4 (clone RM4-5, RM4-4, GK1.5), CD8 (clone 53-6.7), CD25 (clone 3C7), CD44 (clone IM7), CD62L (clone MEL-14), CD45 (clone 30-F11), CD45RB (clone C363-16A), CD69 (clone H1.2F3), CD127 (clone A7R34), FOXP3 (clone MF-14), IFN-γ (clone XMG1.2), IL-2 (clone JES7-5H4), IL-17A (clone TC11-18H10.1), Ki67 (clone 16A8) and TCR-β (clone H57-597) were purchased from BioLegend. FOXP3 (clone FJK-16s) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Cells were first treated with rat serum to block Fc receptors, then stained with fluorescently labeled antibodies at a dilution of 1:200 at 4 °C for 30 min. For intracellular cytokine staining, cells were stimulated with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, 50 ng/ml; Sigma), ionomycin (1 μg/ml; Sigma) and GolgiPlug (1 μl/ml; BD Biosciences) for 4 hours. After surface antibody staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized with a Fixation/Permeabilization Solution Kit (BD Biosciences), followed by staining with antibodies against intracellular molecules. For intracellular transcription factor staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized using a FOXP3/Transcription Factor Buffer Set (eBioscience). Data were collected with a BD Fortessa X20 (BD Biosciences) and analyzed in FlowJo (Tree Star).

T cell isolation and stimulation

Naïve CD4+ T cells or total CD4+ T cells were purified using the EasySep Mouse Naïve CD4+ T Cell Isolation Kit or CD4+ T Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies), respectively, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Purified T cells were stimulated with plate-bound anti-CD3 (5 μg/ml) and anti-CD28 (2 μg/ml) in 96-well plates.

Cell proliferation and apoptosis assay

Proliferation status of naïve CD4+ T cells was determined using Ki67. Apoptotic status was determined using 7-amino-actinomycin D (7-AAD) and Annexin V staining. Cells positive for Annexin V or both Annexin V and 7-AAd were considered apoptotic, while cells negative for both markers were deemed viable.

T cell ex vivo differentiation

Naïve CD4+ T cells were purified and polarized using CellXVivo Mouse Th1, Th17, and Treg Cell Differentiation Kits (R&D Systems) according to the instructions. After 5 days of culture, cells were analyzed by flow cytometry. Naïve CD4+ T cells were purified from the spleens using the EasySep Mouse Naïve CD4+ T Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies) and cultured with anti-CD3 mAb (10 μg/ml; 145-2C11) and anti-CD28 mAb (2 μg/ml; PV-1) in the presence of defined mouse recombinant cytokines and blocking antibodies. In brief, Th1 was induced with IL-2 (2 ng/ml), IL-12 (10 ng/ml) and anti–IL-4 mAb (10 μg/ml; 11B11); Th2 was induced with IL-2 (2 ng/ml), IL-4 (10 ng/ml) and anti-IFN-γ mAb (10 μg/ml; XMG1.2); Th17 was induced with IL-6 (20 ng/ml), transforming growth factor (TGF)-β (2 ng/ml), anti-IL-4 mAb (10 μg/ml; 11B11) and anti-IFN-γ mAb (10 μg/ml; XMG1.2); Treg was induced with IL-2 (2 ng/ml), TGF-β (2 ng/ml), anti–IL-4 mAb (10 μg/ml; 11B11) and anti-IFN-γ mAb (10 μg/ml; XMG1.2). All cytokines were purchased from R&D Systems. After 4 days of culture, cells were harvested for RT-qPCR analysis.

In vitro cytotoxicity assay for human/mouse CD4+ T cells and NK cells

Luciferase-expressing B16-F10 or Raji cells (5×103 cells/well) were used as targets. Human or mouse NK cells were isolated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells or spleen using an NK cell isolation kit (STEMCELL). Human or mouse Th1 cells were generated by ex vivo differentiation. First, Th1 and NK cells were combined in equal numbers to form the effector cells. Subsequently, these effector cells were co-cultured with tumor cells at the indicated effector-to-target (E:T) ratios in triplicate in 96-well plates for 24 or 48 h in the presence of IL-2 (2 ng/ml). D-Luciferin (0.3 mg/ml) was added 10 min before imaging (BioTek, CYTATION1). Specific lysis was calculated as: (Fluorescence Control - Fluorescence Target) / Fluorescence Control × 100%, where ‘Target’ indicates co-culture of target cells with Th1 and NK cells, and ‘Control’ indicates target cells cultured alone.

Quantification of RNA modifications by Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometer (LC-MS/MS)

For m6Am, 150 ng mRNA was decapped with 10 U RppH (NEB, M0356S) in ThermoPol buffer at 37 °C for 3 h. The decapped RNA was purified by ethanol precipitation and then digested with 0.5 U nuclease P1 (Sigma, N8630) in 20 μl buffer containing 10 mM ammonium acetate (pH 5.3) at 42 °C for 6 h. Next, 2.5 μl 0.5 M MES buffer (pH 6.5) and 1 U rSAP (NEB, M0371S) were added, and the mixture was further incubated at 37 °C for 6 h. For other RNA modifications, 150 ng mRNA was digested with 0.5 U nuclease P1 in 20 μl buffer containing 10 mM ammonium acetate (pH 5.3) at 42 °C for 6 h. Next, 2.5 μl 0.5 M MES buffer (pH 6.5) and 1 U rSAP (NEB, M0371S) were added, and the mixture was further incubated at 37 °C for 6 h.

The digested nucleosides were separated by ultra-performance liquid chromatography with a C18 column, and then detected by triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer (AB SCIEX QTRAP 6500) in the positive ion multiple reaction-monitoring mode (MRM). The mass transitions of m/z 296.0 to 150.1 (m6Am), m/z 282.0 to 150.1 (m6A), m/z 286.1 to 154.2 (ac4C), m/z 258.1 to 112.1 (Cm), m/z 298.1 to 152.0 (Gm), m/z 259.1 to 113.1 (Um), m/z 282.1 to 136.0 (Am), m/z 296.0 to 164.1 (N66mA), m/z 258.0 to 126.1 (m5C), m/z 298.0 to 166.0 (m7G), m/z 282.0 to 150.1 (m1A), m/z 268.0 to 136.0 (A) and m/z 245.0 to 113.1 (U), m/z 244.0 to 112.1 (C), m/z 284.0 to 152.0 (G), were monitored and recorded. Concentrations of nucleosides in RNA samples were deduced by fitting the signal intensities into the standard curves.

m6Am RNA modification detection by m6Am-seq

This experimental protocol was adapted from the established m6Am-seq methodology40,41, with key modifications to enhance procedural efficiency and detection accuracy. First, total RNA was subjected to controlled fragmentation using magnesium-based RNA fragmentation buffer (NEB #E6150S) under thermal denaturation conditions (94 °C for 4 min), generating fragments averaging 150 nucleotides in length. For input control preparation, 10 ng of fragmented RNA was reserved as the input reference group. For m7G-IP enrichment, the remaining RNA fragments underwent sequential immunoprecipitation. The primary IP was performed using 2 μg of anti-cap-m7G antibody (MBL #RN017M) in IPP buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.4], 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% NP-40, 1 U/μl RNase inhibitor) at 4 °C for 16 h. Secondary capture was carried out with Protein A/G UltraLink Resin at 4 °C for 3 h, and m7G-containing RNAs were recovered via TRIzol/isopropanol precipitation. One nanogram of the cap-m7G-immunoprecipitated RNA was transferred to a new nuclease-free tube, designated as “m7G-IP,” and stored at −80 °C. The remaining RNA was then subjected to either “FTO (+)” or “FTO (-)” treatment as follows: Step 1. RNA was denatured at 65 °C for 5 minutes and immediately chilled on ice. Step 2. The demethylation reaction mixture was prepared as follows: 100 ng denatured RNA, 50 mM MES (pH 6.5), 100 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 1 U/μl SUPERase In RNase Inhibitor (Invitrogen, AM2694), 300 μM 2-ketoglutarate, 100 μM (NH4)2Fe(SO4)2·6H2O, and 1 μM purified FTO. Nuclease-free water was added to a final volume of 20 μl, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. Step 3. The demethylation reaction was diluted with 180 μl nuclease-free water, followed by addition of 200 μl phenol:chloroform:isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1) and thorough mixing. After centrifugation at 16,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C, the aqueous phase was transferred to a new tube and extracted with 200 μl chloroform:isoamyl alcohol (24:1). Following centrifugation under the same conditions, 200 μl of the supernatant was collected. Step 4. To the supernatant, 20 μl of 3 M sodium acetate (pH 5.2), 4 μl glycogen, and 550 μl ice-cold 100% ethanol were added. The mixture was vortexed and incubated at −20 °C for at least 1 hour to precipitate RNA. Step 5. RNA was pelleted by centrifugation at 14,000 g for 30 min at 4 °C. The pellet was washed twice with 1 ml of 75% ethanol, each wash followed by centrifugation at 14,000 g for 3 min. After carefully removing the supernatant, the pellet was air-dried and dissolved in 155 μl nuclease-free water. Step 6. For the “FTO (-)” sample, 5 μl of the remaining cap-m7G-immunoprecipitated RNA from Step 1 was diluted with 150 μl nuclease-free water. Subsequently, both FTO (+) and FTO (-) fractions were subjected to m6A re-immunoprecipitation using anti-m6A magnetic beads (EpiMark N6-Methyladenosine Kit), strictly following the manufacturer’s wash and elution protocols. Library construction and sequencing were performed in parallel for all experimental groups (Input, m7G-IP, FTO ± ). Strand-specific libraries were prepared using the SMARTer Stranded Total RNA-Seq Kit v2 (Takara #634413), and sequencing was carried out on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with PE150 mode. Pre-sequencing quality control was validated using a Bioanalyzer DNA High Sensitivity Chip. KEGG enrichment analysis was performed using R software (version 4.2.1) and the clusterProfiler package (version 4.4.4). Significance was determined by the method implemented in the software (nominal p < 0.05).

RNA-seq

T cells purified from each mouse were stimulated with plate-bound anti-CD3 (5 μg/ml) and anti-CD28 (2 μg/ml). Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA library construction and sequencing were performed by OEBiotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB) and sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform with 150-bp paired-end reads, following the manufacturer’s protocols. RNA-seq reads were aligned to the Mus musculus genome (UCSC mm10) using HISAT2 (v2.1.0) with the parameter ‘--rna-strandness rf’. Read counts were generated with htseq-count (v0.11.2) based on the mapped reads. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v1.36.0), with genes considered significantly differentially expressed if they met the thresholds of fold change ≥ 2, adjusted p-value < 0.05, and FPKM ≥ 1. DESeq2 was used to model gene expression data with a negative binomial generalized linear model, and the Wald test was applied to assess the statistical significance of differential expression. This test is two-sided. In our differential expression analysis, we report p values adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Volcano plots and principal component analysis (PCA) plots were generated using the ggplot2 package (v3.4.0) in R. KEGG enrichment analysis was performed using R software (version 4.2.1) and the clusterProfiler package (version 4.4.4). Significance was determined by the method implemented in the software (nominal p < 0.05).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA from CD4+ T cells was isolated with TRIzol (Invitrogen) and reverse-transcribed with PrimeScript RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara). qPCR was performed with iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, 1725124). The primer sequences used for qPCR are listed in Supplementary Data 6.

Immunoblotting

Total protein from CD4+ T cells was extracted with radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (Beyotime, P0013E) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no.78443). Antibodies against PCIF1 (Abcam, cat. no. ab205016), STAT1 (CST, cat. no. 14994S), P-STAT1 (CST, cat. no. 9167S), Tubulin (Servicebio, cat. no. GB15201-100), T-bet (CST, cat. no. 97135), m6A (Abclonal, cat. no. A17924), ACTIN (Cell Signaling Technology, cat. no. 3700S), GAPDH (CST, cat. no. 2118), GATA3 (Abclonal, cat. no. A21809), FOXP3 (Abclonal, cat. no. A27170), Rabbit IgG (CST, cat. no. 2729S), and mouse IgG (SANTA CRUZ, cat. no. sc-2025) were diluted in 5% nonfat milk buffer at the concentration of 1:1000 and incubated at 4 °C overnight. After washing three times with PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 buffer, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-rabbit IgG, HRP-linked antibody, Cell Signaling Technology, cat. no. 7074; anti-mouse IgG, HRP-linked antibody, Biodragon, cat. no. BF03001) were diluted in 5% nonfat milk buffer at the concentration of 1:10,000 and incubated at room temperature for 1 hour. The signal was detected by enhanced chemiluminescence with Pico ECL using ChemiDoc MP (Bio-Rad).

STAT1 inhibitor assay

During the first 24 h of Th1 differentiation from naïve CD4+ T cells, the STAT1 inhibitor Fludarabine (2.5 µM; MedChemExpress, 21679-14-1) was added.

Dual luciferase reporter assay

The dual-luciferase reporter assay was performed using the psiCHECK™−2 vector system (Promega). The 5’ UTR sequences of Stat1, including both WT and mutant (A-T) variants, were PCR-amplified and subsequently cloned into the psiCHECK™−2 vector using directional cloning. HEK-293T cells were co-transfected with 400 ng of Stat1 5’ UTR reporter constructs (WT or mutant) and 100 ng of PCIF1 overexpression plasmid using X-tremeGENE HP DNA Transfection Reagent (Roche, cat. no. 6366546001). Forty-eight hours after transfection, luciferase activities were measured using the Duo-Lite Luciferase Assay System (Yeasen Biotechnology, cat. no. 11402ES) on a BioTek Cytation 1 microplate reader (Agilent Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The primer sequences used for plasmid construction are listed in Supplementary Data 6.

Ribo-Tag RNA sequencing analysis

Fastq files from RNA sequencing and Ribo-Tag RNA sequencing were aligned to the mm10 genome using HISAT2 version 2.2.1, and StringTie version 2.2.1 was employed to count reads mapped to each gene. After normalization to FPKM values, the FPKM ratio of individual genes was determined by dividing the ribosomal fractions by their inputs. Volcano plots were generated using R software (version 4.2.1) and the ggplot2 package (version 3.4.4) to visualize the results of differential expression analysis. Significance was determined by the method implemented in the software (nominal p < 0.05).

mPCIF1-KO rescue and hPCIF1-KO rescue

Retroviral transduction was performed by transfecting HEK293T cells with an mPGK-retrovirus (with EGFP) plasmid encoding WT or APPA-mutant mPCIF1 along with packaging plasmids. Purified PCIF1-deficient naïve CD4+ T cells were activated in 96-well plates for 24 h. The cells were then spin-infected with packaged retrovirus at 800 g for 180 min in the presence of 8 µg/mL polybrene. After infection, we employed an in vitro Th1 differentiation system. WT and mutant hPCIF1 were constructed into a pLVX plasmid. Transfection was performed using X-tremeGENE HP DNA Transfection Reagent (Roche) to introduce the plasmid into the hPCIF1-knockout Jurkat cell line. The primer sequences used for plasmid construction are listed in Supplementary Data 6.

Protein expression and purification

The human PCIF1 (residues 174–668) gene with N-terminal GST tag was synthetically cloned into the vector pFastBac1 and expressed in SF9 cells using a baculovirus expression system. For protein expression, Sf9 cells were infected with recombinant baculoviruses at a density of 3×106 /ml and grown for 72 h to overexpress the recombinant PCIF1. The cells were then collected by centrifugation and stored at −80 °C.

For protein purification, thawed cells were lysed in a lysis buffer (40 mM Hepes pH 7.4, 200 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM PMSF, 1×cocktail) using a high-pressure cell disruptor. The cell lysate was cleared by centrifugation at 16,000 g for 40 min. The supernatant was bound to Glutathione Sepharose 4B beads (GE Healthcare) at 4 °C for 2 hours. After the beads were washed with lysis buffer, the proteins were eluted with elution buffer (40 mM Hepes pH 7.4, 200 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 20 mM GSH). The proteins were further purified by Superdex 75 (10/300 column, GE Healthcare). Purified PCIF1 was concentrated at 5 mg/ml and stored at −80 °C.

Fluorescence polarization (FP) assay

The in-house compound library consisted of approximately 7500 compounds, including a commercial FDA-approved drug library (HY-L022M; MedChemExpress). For PCIF1 inhibitor high-throughput screening, the compounds were diluted using FP buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.0, 50 mM NaCl) at 50 µM. The FAM-labeled substrate 5’-Am RNA (5’-AmGCGAAAUGGGAGGGUUA-FAM-3’) was synthesized by Nanjing GenScript Biotechnology Corporation. FP assay was performed at 25 °C in 384-well plates and the reaction volume was 40 µL. 150 nM PCIF1 was pre-incubated with 50 µM compounds for 30 min, and then FP-signals were detected immediately by Envision Readers (PerkinElmer) after adding 50 nM FAM-labeled RNA. The negative control well did not contain a compound (substituted by DMSO), while the positive control well contained FAM-unlabeled RNA.

The IC50 values of compounds were determined by assessing the binding of PCIF1 to RNA across a range of inhibitor concentrations. The results were analyzed in GraphPad Prism 8.0.

Protein thermal shift (PTS) assay

The PTS reaction system contained 5 µM PCIF1, 0.2% SYPRO Orange (Sigma-Aldrich, cat#S6650), and different concentrations of compounds in 10 µL assay buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl) using 96-well PCR plates. The assay used a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems), and wells were heated from 25 °C to 95 °C. Each concentration of Suramin (PF007) was tested in triplicate and the protein melting temperature was calculated by the Protein Thermal Shift software ver. 1.3 (Life Technologies). The protein thermal melting curves were plotted using GraphPad Prism 8.0.

PCIF1 methyltransferase assay

PCIF1 methyltransferase activity was measured using MTase-Glo™ Methyltransferase Assay Kit (Promega). Briefly, 1 µM PCIF1 protein was pre-incubated with compounds for 30 minutes. 7.5 µM 5’-Am RNA (5’-AmGCGAAAUGGGAGGGUUA-3’) and 60 µM S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) were then added in wells, and incubated in the reaction buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.6, 1 mM DTT) at room temperature for 2 hours. According to the Kit’s protocol, the reaction byproduct, S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH), was converted to luminescence signal following the addition of Kit’s detection reagents. The signals were measured by EnVision Readers (PerkinElmer). The curves were plotted in GraphPad Prism 8.0.

Alternatively, 180 nM of PCIF1 protein was pre-incubated with 60 µM of Suramin for 30 minutes. Subsequently, 125 nM RNA (5’-m7GpppAmGGAUAGCAGCAUGUCCUUCGACUAUCUACCUGUCCAGUAGCCUUCGAUCAUGCUGUCUGACUUGCUGACAUCAUUCUAGUGCCAUACUUCAGC) and 15 µM of SAM were added to the tubes. These components were allowed to react in the reaction buffer at room temperature for 2 hours. The methyltransferase activity of PCIF1 was then assessed through LC-MS/MS analysis based on the m6Am modification.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assay

SPR assay was carried out to evaluate the binding affinity of PCIF1 protein and Suramin (PF007) on a Biacore T200 instrument (GE Healthcare) with SPR buffer (20 mM Hepes pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl) at 25 °C. The protein was first immobilized on a CM5 chip (GE Healthcare) in 10 mM sodium acetate (pH 4.5). After the chip was equilibrated in the SPR buffer, the compound was injected at a series of concentrations for 120 s, followed by a 300 s delay for dissociation. The dissociation constant (KD) values were determined by Biacore T200 evaluation software (GE Healthcare). The curves were plotted in GraphPad Prism 8.0.

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC)

ITC experiments were performed using the Microcal ITC 200 isothermal titration calorimeter instrument (GE Healthcare) at 25 °C. Freshly purified PCIF1 was dialyzed at 4 °C overnight and diluted to 40 µM with dialysis buffer (20 mM Hepes pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl) before being loaded into the sample cell. Suramin (PF007) was diluted to 800 µM with dialysis buffer and injected into the cell with a syringe. After one 0.4 μl injection, Suramin (PF007) was titrated into PCIF1 by nineteen 2 μl injections at 180-second intervals. The binding affinity was calculated using a single binding-site model by MicroCal PEAQ-ITC Analysis Software.

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

Jurkat cells were treated with 60 μM of Suramin (PF007) or 0.2% DMSO for 6 hours, and then collected and washed with PBS. Cells resuspended in PBS containing 1×protease inhibitor cocktail were heated at 43-50 °C for 3 min and then rapidly cooled to 25 °C. The samples were subjected to three freeze-thaw cycles with liquid nitrogen. The supernatant was aspirated by centrifugation at 15,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C and boiled at 99 °C for 10 min after adding 6× SDS-PAGE sample loading buffer. The samples were subsequently detected by Western blot analysis.

Statistics

Samples or mice were grouped according to treatments or genotypes and thus were not randomized. Data collection and analysis were not performed blind to the conditions of the experiments. Unless indicated otherwise, comparisons were performed using unpaired Student’s t-test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), two-way ANOVA, or the log-rank test, with GraphPad Prism 8. Data are expressed as the mean ± SEM. Values of p < 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance: ns (no significance).
