Theoretical study on loading history dependence of dynamic failure strength for brittle materials
Authors: Xiao Yang, Zhiling Bai, Zhuoping Duan, Ji Duan, Xiaoqi Zhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Whether the dynamic failure strength of brittle materials is an intrinsic material property has been debated, owing to its dependence on loading history. To further study such a dependence, a characteristic time failure criterion for brittle materials is developed and introduced into the dynamic damage evolution law to establish a time-dependent dynamic uniaxial constitutive model in the pre-peak load branch. The criterion and the constitutive model are validated by the previous literature data. The results show that, the dynamic failure strength of brittle materials at high strain rates is not a material property due to its loading history dependence. But both the rate enhancement effect and loading history dependence of dynamic strength is a macroscopic mechanical behavior of the material rather than a structural response. When regarded as a mechanical behavior, it is accompanied by changes in the stress-strain curve, whereas a structural response presupposes that the stress-strain curve remains unchanged under quasi-static and dynamic loadings. The microcrack inertial effect is one of the physical mechanisms behind the strain rate enhancement effect and loading history dependence of dynamic failure strength for brittle materials. Macroscopically, the loading history dependence of dynamic strength for brittle materials results from the time-dependent dynamic stress-strain curve. The dynamic strength and stress-strain response of brittle materials should correspond to the full strain or stress history rather than an instantaneous or average rate of the history curve.

Introduction

Under dynamic loading, the dynamic failure strength of brittle materials is often higher than the quasi-static strength, which is the so-called strain rate enhancement effect. It has always been a popular issue in dynamic continuum mechanics. So far, in addition to the structural inertia effect, research on the strain rate effect has gradually evolved into two views with distinctive characteristics.

The classical viewpoint suggests that the dynamic failure strength is a rate-dependent intrinsic property of materials, which is a natural extension of the quasi-static failure strength, and the rate effect is the mechanical behavior of the material. Under such a consideration, many experimental studies1–3 have been carried out to measure the dynamic strength at different strain rates, and based on the experimental data, various empirical or semi-empirical rate-dependent models1,4,5 of dynamic strength have also been obtained as the dynamic failure strength criterion, which are further coupled into the dynamic constitutive model of the material for numerical simulation5–7. Meanwhile, some physical mechanisms1,4,8(e.g., thermal activation effect, the Stefan effect and microcrack inertia effect) have also been proposed to explain the rate enhancement effect. However, there is an intrinsic deficiency in this view. The classical view often treats strength as a state function of strain rate rather than a path-dependent function of history. When the rate-dependent dynamic strength criterion is used to describe material failure, it ignores the loading history dependence of material failure, because the material failure results from the process of microscopic bond breakage9 and mesoscopic damage evolution10.

Considering the time dependence of material failure, an alternative view on the rate effect of the dynamic failure strength has been developed. Ou and his co-workers11–15 creatively proposed a dynamic load-carrying capacity (DLC) theory. According to this theory, the dynamic failure strength is not a material property due to its dependence on the loading history, and the strain rate effect on the failure strength of brittle materials is a structure response rather than a mechanic behavior of the material. The analytical study of DLC theory was conducted by the incubation time fracture criterion (ITFC)16. The ITFC is an integral criterion for stress with respect to time, which emphasizes that the dynamic failure of materials possesses a preparatory process (i.e., the breakage of bonds and development of the microdefects) characterized by an incubation time16. It has also been applied in the theoretical study of rate dependence of fracture toughness16. Similar to the ITFC, there are other time-integrated failure criterion in previous studies including Tuler–Butcher criterion17, Nikiphorovski–Shemyakin criterion18, Klepaczko’s criterion19, Dorogoy’s criterion20 and the modified ITFC21. According to the DLC theory11–15, these time-integrated criterions also imply the loading history dependence of the dynamic failure strength. Recently, other studies22,23 have also reached similar conclusions to the DLC theory. Based on the crack extension duration of the stress increase from quasi-static to dynamic strength, Binder et al.22 argued that the high-dynamic compressive and tensile strength gain is a structural effect. Loeffler et al.23 carried out some SHPB experiments of concrete under trapezoidal stress history loading, and the results show that the time dependence of failure process in brittle materials plays a significant role in the observed dynamic strength enhancement.

Compared with the classical view, the view focusing on the loading history dependence of material failure seems more reasonable. However, it still exists two intrinsic issues. On the one hand, it does not clearly explain the physical mechanism for the loading history dependence of dynamic failure strength, which also results in the conclusion for treating the rate effect as a structural response11–15. On the other hand, if the strain rate enhancement effect of dynamic failure strength is a structural response, the stress-strain relationship of brittle materials should be consistent under quasi-static and dynamic loadings, but that is inconsistent with the results of dynamic mechanical property experiments1. Dynamic failure is the result of deformation and damage to the material under dynamic loading. In brittle materials, dynamic failure strength refers to the peak stress of dynamic uniaxial stress-strain curve. Therefore, it can be inferred that the loading history dependence of dynamic failure strength should result from the time dependence of dynamic mechanical behavior in brittle materials, i.e., the dynamic uniaxial stress-strain curve in the pre-peak load branch should also exhibit loading history dependence. Unfortunately, the time-integrated failure criterion is not a material model, and the DLC theory employs an ideal linear elastic material model in the pre-peak load branch. The viewpoint represented by the DLC theory11–15 does not take into account the possible loading history dependence of dynamic stress-strain response in brittle materials. Moreover, although some time-dependent dynamic constitutive models24–28 for brittle materials have been used, the loading history dependence of both the dynamic stress-strain relationship and the dynamic strength, as well as the correlation between them, have not received attention.

Therefore, this paper aims to, based on the DLC theory, further investigate the loading history dependence of dynamic failure strength from the perspective of time-dependent dynamic mechanical behavior in brittle materials, thereby investigating the physical mechanisms of such a loading history dependence. Inspired by the previous time-dependent failure criterions19,29, a characteristic time failure criterion is proposed to describe the time dependence of dynamic failure in brittle materials. And then, by incorporating this criterion into the dynamic damage evolution law, a time-dependent uniaxial constitutive model in the pre-peak load branch for brittle materials is established to study the loading history dependence of dynamic failure strength.

The paper is organized as follows. After this brief introduction, in Sect. 2, the details of the theoretical model for loading history dependence of dynamic strength are presented. And then, the model results are compared with previous literature data and discussed in Sect. 3. Finally, some conclusions are drawn out in Sect. 4.

That is to say, the rapid loading does not provide sufficient time for the material to generate new microcrack surface area, which is exactly the microcrack inertial effect and manifests as retarded damage. In previous studies24,26,31,32 on the dynamic damage constitutive law of brittle materials, the microcrack inertia effect was typically incorporated into the model, which leads to the retarded damage and further induces the strain-rate enhancement effect on dynamic failure strength at high strain rates. Meanwhile, in studies1,8 on the physical mechanisms of the strain rate effect on dynamic strength, the microcrack inertia effect is considered one of the primary mechanisms at high strain rates. In our damage constitutive model, when the time-dependent failure criterion is introduced into the dynamic damage evolution law, the microcrack inertia effect is phenomenological within the time-dependence of the damage evolution.

In Fig. 8, the loading history dependence of dynamic uniaxial stress-strain curve results from the dynamic damage evolution law incorporating the microcrack inertial effect. That is, at high strain rates, the microcrack inertial effect can produce the loading history dependence of dynamic uniaxial stress-strain curve in brittle materials. Similar conclusions were also reported in the study by Eibl and Schmidt-Hurtienne24. It should be noted that the microcrack inertia effect is not the only mechanism for the loading history dependence of dynamic stress-strain curve in brittle materials. In various physical mechanisms of the strain rate effect on dynamic strength, the viscous mechanism including the Stefan effect at low strain rates1 and phonon damping at high strain rates47 can also produce time-dependent dynamic mechanical behavior. For example, Häussler-Combe26 proposed a constitutive model to describe the strain-rate sensitivity of concrete through the introduction of viscoelasticity and inertial retarded damage, in which the viscous originates from the Stefan effect. Despite viscoelastic dynamic constitutive laws for brittle materials are rarely used to study the loading-history dependence of dynamic stress-strain curve, such models intrinsically possess this dependence. Such a dependence, considering more mechanisms, still requires further investigation.

When the dynamic uniaxial stress-strain curve of a brittle material possesses the loading history dependence, the peak stress as the dynamic failure strength also exhibits such a dependence as shown in Fig. 8. The dynamic strength is not a material property due to its loading history dependence10–14. However, both the rate enhancement effect and loading history dependence of dynamic strength should be a material behavior rather than a macroscopic structure response10–14,22. The microcrack inertial effect is one of the physical mechanisms behind both the rate enhancement effect and loading history dependence of dynamic strength for brittle materials. Physically, for the rapid microcrack propagation in meso-level, material particles on opposite crack faces displace with respect to each other. On the one hand, the inertial resistance to this motion produces the enhancement of driving force, which eventually behaves as the rate enhancement effect of dynamic strength. On the other hand, this motion also depends on the driving force history, which further results in the loading history dependence of dynamic strength. At the mesoscale, the microcrack inertial effect is a structural effect of the material, but at the macroscopic level, it emerges as a mechanical behavior of the material. As mentioned above, in addition to the microcrack inertial effect, the viscous mechanism including the Stefan effect1 and phonon damping47 should also contribute to both the rate enhancement effect and the loading-history dependence of dynamic strength.

Traditional researches primarily focused on the rate enhancement effect on dynamic strength while ignoring its loading history dependence, thus leading to the proposal of representative strain rate (e.g., the average slope of the strain history curve or the instantaneous slope at the failure time) dependent model for dynamic strength. However, the representative rate dependent model of dynamic strength is difficult to capture the delayed stress response under rapid changes in strain rate6,7,24. Because such a model implies instantaneous material failure and ignores the dynamic damage evolution process in brittle materials. To make up for this deficiency, Pereira et al.24 and Kong et al.7 proposed a corrected instantaneous strain-rate enhancement model of concrete by introducing a time scale into the current strain rate to control the evolution of strain rate, respectively. This correction represents an evolution of the original model from an instantaneous rate-dependent formulation to a time-dependent framework. Strictly speaking, due to the time-dependent damage evolution in brittle materials, the dynamic stress-strain relationship should correspond to the specific strain or stress history rather than a representative strain or stress rate picked from the corresponding history curve. Moreover, when the peak stress or dynamic strength exhibits the loading history dependence, the stress-strain curve in the post peak load branch should also possess such a dependence, which remains to be further studied.

Conclusions

To study the loading history dependence of dynamic failure strength for brittle materials, a characteristic time failure criterion is proposed and introduced into the dynamic damage evolution to establish a time-dependent dynamic uniaxial constitutive model in the pre-peak load branch of brittle materials. The criterion and the material model are validated by the literature data. Some numerical results are further obtained from the model. The main conclusions are as follows.
