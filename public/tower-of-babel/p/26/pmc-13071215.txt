Rapid improvement of renal microcirculatory homeostasis by liraglutide following diabetes induction
Authors: Yuan Li, Weiqi Liu, Yingyu Wang, Bing Wang, Xiang Xu, Bingwei Li, Mingming Liu, Xu Zhang, Ruijuan Xiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsRenal microvascular dysfunction is a central driver of diabetic kidney disease, yet the potential for rapid therapeutic reversal remains obscure. We investigated the effect of short‐term liraglutide on early changes in the renal microcirculation post‐induction of diabetes.Materials and MethodsType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) was induced in BALB/c mice via high‐fat diet and streptozotocin. Following induction, mice received insulin or liraglutide treatments for 1 or 2 weeks. Renal microhemodynamics was analysed using laser Doppler flowmetry and wavelet analysis. Histopathological evaluations via Masson trichrome and haematoxylin–eosin staining assessed fibrosis and microvascular integrity. Primary renal microvascular endothelial cells (RMECs) were isolated to evaluate angiogenic and migratory behaviours. Metabolomics analyses were performed to link metabolic changes with microcirculatory alterations.ResultsT2DM induced significant impairments in renal microcirculation, characterised by dysregulated microhemodynamics, disturbed oxygen saturation, altered haemoglobin distribution and compromised blood perfusion. These functional defects were accompanied by elevated creatinine, reduced cystatin C and impaired angiogenic and migratory capacity of RMECs under glucotoxicity. Both insulin and liraglutide treatments ameliorated renal microcirculatory oxygen profiles and improved renal function biomarkers. Histological data revealed decreased fibrosis and enhanced microvascular architecture post‐treatment. Metabolomic profiling revealed that liraglutide preferentially restored depleted amino acid levels associated with microvascular function, a change associated with the normalisation of metabolic profiles potentially involving peptide transport pathways.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that short‐term liraglutide restores renal microcirculatory homeostasis in the post‐induction phase, mitigating microvascular dysfunction and preserving kidney structure, highlighting the potential therapeutic value in preventing the progression of diabetic renal injury.

INTRODUCTION

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) precipitated by type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) represents a global health challenge. Affecting an estimated 40% of the diabetic population, 1 it stands as the principal driver of progression to end‐stage renal disease (ESRD). The sheer scale of the crisis is supported by a global CKD prevalence of 9.1%, culminating in approximately 1.2 million deaths annually and imposing an immense strain on healthcare infrastructures worldwide. 2 The epidemiological landscape in China has undergone a decisive shift, with diabetic kidney disease (DKD) supplanting glomerulonephritis as the leading cause of CKD since 2011,3, 4 highlighting a critical and evolving public health imperative. The progression from metabolic dysregulation to irreversible structural end‐organ damage often occurs insidiously,5, 6 creating a prolonged subclinical phase where therapeutic intervention could be most effective. At its core, renal injury in diabetes is fundamentally a disease of progressive microvascular decay, which compromises renal function.

The integrity of the renal microvasculature is a prerequisite for maintaining glomerular filtration and overall renal homeostasis. 7 The kidney's unique and highly complex glomerular and peritubular capillary networks, 8 engineered for massive ultrafiltration and solute exchange, are consequently exceptionally vulnerable to these systemic insults. 9 However, the hyperglycaemic milieu in T2DM initiates a cascade of pathological events that dismantle the architecture, leading to inexorable renal functional decline. Chronic hyperglycaemia inflicts multifaceted endothelial injury and promotes capillary rarefaction, 10 two signature lesions that synergistically cripple nephron function. The pathogenesis is complex, involving not only haemodynamic alterations, such as glomerular hypertension and hyperfiltration, 11 but also a host of biochemical insults that fuel microvascular damage and interstitial fibrosis.12, 13 The process is further amplified by systemic insulin resistance and the accumulation of advanced glycation end‐products (AGEs), which perpetuate a vicious cycle of endothelial dysfunction and renal deterioration. 14 Crucially, the renal‐specific microangiopathy is linked with, and predictive of, systemic vascular complications, which are the leading cause of mortality in this patient cohort. 15 Thus, the renal microvasculature serves as both a target of diabetic injury and a barometer for systemic cardiovascular risk, making its preservation a central therapeutic goal.

Current therapeutic paradigms remain insufficient. Although renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system blockade mitigates renal injury, 16 it fails to fully abrogate ESRD progression,17, 18 leaving a substantial residual risk driven by unaddressed non‐haemodynamic mechanisms. The therapeutic ceiling highlights a need for strategies that precisely neutralise the underlying microvascular pathology. Emerging evidence suggests that glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor (GLP‐1R) agonists exert direct vasoprotective effects,19, 20, 21 independent of glycaemic control. These agents may represent a paradigm shift, moving beyond haemodynamic modulation to target metabolic signalling pathways sustaining vascular injury. However, whether these benefits translate to rapid improvements in renal microhemodynamics and the specific metabolic mechanisms involved remain unclear. Characterising short‐term effects is essential to dissect direct vascular actions from the confounding benefits of long‐term glycaemic control. Therefore, our study integrates metabolomics with functional microhemodynamic assessments to test the hypothesis that short‐term liraglutide rapidly restores renal microcirculation in T2DM, uncovering the immediate metabolic shifts supporting renoprotection.

METHODS AND MATERIALS

Experimental design and confirmation of T2DM induction and treatment regimen

All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (CAMS) (CAMS‐IM‐IACUC‐2022‐AE‐1205). Male BALB/c mice (8 weeks old) were housed under standard controlled conditions. The T2DM mouse model was established using a combination of a high‐fat diet (HFD) and low‐dose streptozotocin (STZ) to mimic the pathogenesis of human type 2 diabetes. Specifically, mice (n = 40) were fed a 60% kcal HFD (D12492, HFK Bioscience, China) for 4 weeks to induce obesity and systemic insulin resistance. Following this dietary induction, the mice received intraperitoneal injections of low‐dose STZ (50 mg/kg; Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) dissolved in 0.1 M citrate buffer (pH 4.5) for two consecutive days. The dosage was selected to cause partial pancreatic β‐cell ablation, resulting in relative insulin deficiency superimposed on insulin resistance and establishing a clinically relevant model of metabolic dysregulation essential for driving early renal microcirculatory alterations.22, 23 Control mice were fed a standard chow diet and received vehicle injections of citrate buffer. Hypoglycaemia was prevented using glucose‐saline (0.2 g/kg) and 10% sucrose for 3 days. Diabetes was confirmed 12 days post‐STZ, defined as fasting blood glucose >200 mg/dL on two consecutive tests. Diabetic mice were then randomised into four treatment groups (n = 8 each) to receive either insulin (1 IU) or liraglutide (0.2 mg/kg) for 1 or 2 weeks.

HOMA‐IR score

To assess β‐cell function and insulin sensitivity, fasting plasma insulin (FINS) levels were quantified using a mouse insulin enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (EMINS, Thermo Fisher, USA) following the manufacturer's protocol. The homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA‐IR) index was subsequently calculated using the formula: HOMA‐IR = (fasting glucose [mmol/L] × FINS [μIU/mL])/22.5. These measurements were performed to characterise the metabolic phenotype and differentiate between absolute insulinopaenia and insulin resistance coupled with relative β‐cell decompensation.

Evaluation of renal microcirculatory profiles

To investigate renal microhemodynamics and oxygenation, we used the Enhanced Perfusion and Oxygen Saturation system (Perimed, Sweden), which combines diffuse reflectance spectroscopy and laser Doppler flowmetry following established protocols.24, 25 After anaesthesia with 2% isoflurane in 50% oxygen (RWD Life Science, China), the renal surface was exposed, and the probe was positioned with a holder for real‐time data acquisition. The parameters measured included red blood cell tissue fraction (RBC, %), oxygen saturation (SO2, %), total haemoglobin concentration (total Hb, μM), concentrations of oxygenated and reduced haemoglobin (oxygenated Hb, μM; reduced Hb, μM), and microcirculatory blood perfusion (BP) metrics: speed‐resolved perfusion (BP, %RBC × mm/s), total perfusion (total BP, %RBC × mm/s) and conventional perfusion (conventional BP, perfusion units). Next, 60‐s data were analysed using wavelet transforms in Python (version 3.7.0) with the Morlet wavelet algorithm. Time‐frequency scalograms were generated, and average amplitudes were calculated within frequency of 0.0095–0.04 and 0.0005–0.0095 Hz, corresponding to the nitric oxide (NO)‐dependent and NO‐independent endothelial oscillators.26, 27

Evaluation of renal function

Blood samples were collected, centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 20 min to separate serum, and then diluted 1:1 with saline. Biochemical analyses were performed at the Department of Clinical Laboratory, Peking Union Medical College Hospital, Beijing, China. Creatinine levels were measured using the sarcosine hydrogenase method, urea via the uricase‐peroxidase method, uric acid with the urease‐glutamate dehydrogenase method, glucose using the hexokinase method and cystatin C through the latex immunoturbidimetric assay. An automated biochemical analyser (Siemens, USA) was used for all measurements. Reference intervals for renal function parameters in mice were based on established literature. 28

Isolation and characterisation of primary renal microvascular endothelial cells

Primary renal microvascular endothelial cells (RMECS) were isolated following established protocols, 29 and cultured in KnockOut Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (Thermo Fisher) supplemented with 25 mM glucose, 10% FBS, 1% endothelial cell growth supplement (PromoCell, UK) and 1% each of penicillin/streptomycin, sodium pyruvate, and non‐essential amino acids (Thermo Fisher). Cultures were maintained at 37°C with 5% CO2. For phenotypic validation, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilised and blocked with 3% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA). Immunofluorescence was performed by overnight incubation with primary antibodies against CD31 (ab222783, Abcam, UK), von Willebrand factor (vWF) (ab287962, Abcam) and α‐smooth muscle actin (α‐SMA) (#19245, Cell Signalling Technology), followed by detection with Alexa Fluor 488‐ or 555‐conjugated secondary antibodies (Beyotime) and 4′,6‐Diamidino‐2‐Phenylindole (DAPI) counterstaining.

Wound healing assay

When RMECs reached 80% confluency, they were assigned to control (5.6 mM glucose), high glucose (35 mM glucose), and four treatment groups (35 mM glucose with 10−8 M insulin or 20 nM liraglutide) for 24 or 48 h. While 35 mM exceeds typical pathophysiological levels observed in vivo, the concentration was selected to induce a reproducible phenotype of endothelial dysfunction within the acute experimental timeframe. For the wound healing assay, RMECs were seeded in 96‐well plates, allowed to reach confluence overnight and serum‐starved for 4 h. A standardised scratch was made using a 96‐pin wound marker (Essen Bioscience, Germany), and cells were washed with Phosphate‐Buffered saline (PBS). Following the initial scratch, the wells were replenished with medium containing 0.5% FBS specific to each experimental condition. Plates were placed in an IncuCyte™ ZOOM system (Essen Bioscience) for continuous imaging. IncuCyte™ ZOOM software was used to measure wound confluence, relative wound density and wound width at 24 and 48 h. 30 A negative migration distance, indicating no net movement of cells into the wound area, was recorded as zero.

Tube formation assay

The assessment of RMECs capacity to form capillary‐like structures was conducted following established methodologies. 31 After culturing to full confluence and applying experimental pretreatment, 50 μL of Matrigel‐Matrix (356 234, Corning, USA) was added to each well of a 96‐well plate. RMECs were then seeded at 5 × 104 cells per 100 μL per well onto the solidified gel. Blood vessel‐like structures were observed and captured using the IncuCyte™ ZOOM system (Essen Bioscience). Total nets, loops, tubes, branching points, tube length and covered area were automatically quantified using the Wimasis Image Analysis System (https://www.wimasis.com) to assess the angiogenic capacity.

Masson and HE staining

Renal specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated through increasing ethanol concentrations and embedded in paraffin wax. Sections (4 μm) were cut using a microtome (Leica Biosystems, Germany), deparaffinised in xylene and rehydrated through decreasing ethanol concentrations. For HE staining, sections were stained with haematoxylin (1–3 min), differentiated with 1% hydrochloric acid alcohol (5–10 s), rinsed, counterstained with eosin (3–5 s) and rinsed again. For Masson trichrome staining, sections were stained with Ponceau S acid fuchsin (1–2 min), rinsed, mordanted with 1% phosphomolybdic acid (2 min), stained with benzidine blue (1–2 min) and treated with 0.2% acetic acid (10–30 s) before final rinsing and immersion in 70% alcohol. Stained sections were mounted with glycerol and examined under a light microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany) to assess histopathological changes in renal microvasculature and fibrosis.

Kidney metabolomics profiling

Untargeted metabolomic analysis was performed using an Agilent 1290 Infinity Ultra‐High‐Performance Liquid Chromatography (UHPLC) system coupled to an Agilent 6545 UHD Accurate‐Mass Quadrupole Time‐of‐Flight (Q‐TOF) mass spectrometer. For the targeted quantification of amino acids, Ultra‐High‐Performance Liquid Chromatography–Tandem Mass Spectrometry (UHPLC–MS/MS) analysis was conducted on the same UHPLC system paired with an Agilent 6470A Triple Quadrupole mass spectrometer. Raw data processing and feature extraction were performed utilising MassHunter Workstation Software (Agilent) in conjunction with custom scripts in the R programming environment (version 4.3.3). A description of the methodological parameters is provided in Supporting Information S1: Supplementary Methods and Table S1.

Gene expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and reverse‐transcribed into Complementary Deoxyribonucleic Acid (cDNA) using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara). Real‐time quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) was performed on a LightCycler 480 II system (Roche) using SYBR Green Premix Ex Taq (Takara). The sequences of the forward primer and reverse primer were Slc15a1 (5′‐ACCCCAGTCAATACCGTGTG‐3′) and R (5′‐TGTGTACTGCTTTTCGCCAG‐3′), Slc15a2 (5′‐CACCCATGCTAAGAGGGGATGTG‐3′) and R (5′‐GGCTGTTCACATCCTTTTCCAC‐3′). Vlyceraldehyde‐3‐Phosphate Dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was utilised as the endogenous control for normalisation. Relative Messenger Ribonucleic Acid (mRNA) expression levels of Slc15a1 (peptide transporters [PepT1]) and Slc15a2 (PepT2) were calculated using the 2−△△Ct method.

Immunoblotting

Kidney cortices were homogenised in Radioimmunoprecipitation Assay (RIPA) lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Roche). Protein concentrations were determined via Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) assay (Thermo Fisher). Equal amounts of protein (30 μg) were resolved by Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate–Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) and transferred onto Polyvinylidene Fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% non‐fat milk and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against PepT1 (1:1000; Protientech, China; 21726‐1‐AP), PepT2 (1:1000; Abcam; ab198270), and β‐actin (1:5000; Protientech; 60008‐1‐Ig). Following incubation with Horseradish Peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated secondary antibodies, immunoreactive bands were visualised using Enhanced Chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (Bio‐Rad) and quantified by densitometry using ImageJ software (version 1.54f; NIH, USA).

Immunohistochemistry and morphometry

Paraffin‐embedded renal sections (4 μm) were deparaffinised, rehydrated and subjected to heat‐induced epitope retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Sections were blocked with 5% goat serum and incubated overnight at 4°C with anti‐PepT1 (1:200) or anti‐PepT2 (1:50) antibodies. Detection was performed using the 3,3′‐diaminobenzidinecDAB substrate kit (Vector Laboratories) with haematoxylin counterstaining. Images were acquired using a Leica DM6000 B microscope.

Statistical analysis

Metabolomic data were normalised before multivariate analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) identified outliers and assessed inter‐group variance, while orthogonal projections to latent structures discriminant analysis (OPLS‐DA) revealed metabolic differences with R 2 and Q 2 metrics for model evaluation. Differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) were identified based on a dual‐threshold strategy, that variable importance in projection (VIP) scores (VIP >1) from the OPLS‐DA model and p values (p < 0.05) from unpaired two‐sided Student's t‐tests. To further control for false positives arising from multiple hypothesis testing in the high‐dimensional metabolomics dataset, the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method was applied, and adjusted p‐values (q‐values <0.05) were used to confirm significance. Non‐metabolomic data were analysed by one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test to adjust for multiple comparisons between groups and control the family‐wise error rate. Pearson's correlation coefficient examined the relationship between renal function and microcirculatory parameters, while Spearman's correlation explored relationships between metabolites and renal indices.

RESULTS

Liraglutide induces synergistic corrections in glycaemic, metabolic and renal structural parameters

We established a T2DM mouse model using a HFD and STZ, which resulted in significantly higher body mass compared to controls (p < 0.0001; Figure S1A). Liraglutide treatment significantly reduced body mass at 2 weeks (p = 0.0230), whereas insulin treatment did not. The liraglutide‐treated group showed significantly lower body mass than the insulin‐treated group at 2 weeks (p < 0.0001). While both treatments effectively reduced Fasting Blood Flucose (FBG) fetal bovine serum (all p < 0.01; Figure S1B), liraglutide demonstrated a benefit in body mass control. Additionally, the T2DM phenotype was further substantiated by significant hyperinsulinaemia, elevated HOMA‐IR and impaired glucose/insulin tolerance compared to controls (Figure S1C–F). Moreover, liraglutide treatment exhibited a superior, time‐dependent restoration of insulin sensitivity, resulting in significantly lower serum insulin, HOMA‐IR and Areas under the Curves (AUCs) for Intraperitoneal Glucose Tolerance Test (IGTT) and Insulin Tolerance Test (ITT) compared to insulin therapy at matched intervals. Histopathological assessments revealed extensive glomerular and microvascular fibrosis, as Masson trichrome illustrated in Figure S2A and interstitial oedema revealed in HE staining (Figure S2B) in T2DM mice. Both insulin and liraglutide treatments significantly reduced these structural injuries, restoring renal tissue architecture.

Liraglutide and insulin restore renal microcirculatory oxygenation independent of total blood perfusion

We next evaluated renal microhemodynamics. Microcirculatory BP metrics, categorised by velocity, remained stable across all groups with no significant differences observed (Figure S3A–C). In contrast, microcirculatory oxygen dynamics were significantly impaired in T2DM mice (Figure 1A). Following therapeutic intervention, the Ins‐2w, Lira‐1w and Lira‐2w groups exhibited a significant recovery, characterised by enhanced RBC tissue fraction, elevated total haemoglobin and increased oxygenated Hb levels (all p < 0.01). The restoration was reflected in haemoglobin distribution (Figure 1C), where treatment groups improved oxyhaemoglobin proportions (60%–66%) from the T2DM baseline (57%). Correlation analysis confirmed this dysfunction that while SO2 normally negatively correlates with reduced haemoglobin (reflecting oxygen unloading), the relationship inverted in the T2DM group, indicating impaired oxygen utilisation (Figure S4A). Spatial analysis further showed that T2DM mice had lower SO2 at similar perfusion levels, a defect rescued by both treatments (Figure S4B). These findings indicate that the early post‐induction phase is characterised by microvascular oxygenation failure rather than perfusion deficit, and failure is rapidly reversible.

T2DM suppresses endothelial oscillator function that is reversed by liraglutide

To investigate the basis of endothelial dysfunction, we analysed endothelial oscillator amplitudes using wavelet transforms, which reflect microvascular autoregulation (Figures S5 and S6). T2DM mice demonstrated significantly reduced amplitudes in both NO‐dependent and NO‐independent endothelial oscillators across all haemoglobin species (RBC tissue fraction, total Hb, oxygenated Hb and reduced Hb) compared to controls (all p < 0.001). Treatment with Ins‐2w, Lira‐1w and Lira‐2w significantly increased these endothelial amplitudes. For instance, Lira‐1w showed the most potent increase in RBC tissue fraction and total Hb amplitudes (NO‐independent and NO‐dependent, both p < 0.0001 vs. T2DM). Similar restorations were observed for oxygenated Hb (e.g., Lira‐1w vs. T2DM, p < 0.0001). Conversely, the Ins‐1w group paradoxically exhibited even lower amplitudes than the T2DM group in microcirculatory metrics (e.g., RBC, p = 0.0009). These data suggest that T2DM induces a severe suppression of endothelial autoregulatory function, which is potently reversed by liraglutide and sustained insulin treatment.

Renal dysfunction in T2DM correlates with compromised microcirculatory metrics

We next assessed systemic renal function. T2DM mice exhibited elevated creatinine compared to controls (p = 0.029), which was significantly reduced by insulin and liraglutide treatments (e.g., Lira‐1w, p < 0.001; Figure S7A). Urea levels were also elevated in T2DM and significantly decreased with both treatments (all p < 0.01). Conversely, cystatin C levels were unexpectedly lower in the T2DM group (p = 0.012) and were elevated post‐treatment by Ins‐2w (p = 0.027). These renal function biomarkers correlated significantly with the microcirculatory indices (Figure S7B). Elevated urea levels strongly correlated with lower SO2 (p = 0.024, r = −0.325) and reduced BP across all velocity categories (e.g., total BP, p = 0.0005, r = −0.482), providing a link between the decline in systemic renal function and the observed microcirculatory decay.

Liraglutide ameliorates glucotoxicity‐induced dysfunction in renal endothelial cells

To isolate the direct endothelial effects, we cultured primary RMECs, validated by CD31+/vWF+/α‐SMA− staining (Figure 2A). Under glucotoxicity, RMECs showed impaired angiogenic capacity in tube formation assays, with reduced covered area, total branching points and total tube length (p = 0.006, p = 0.002, p = 0.016, respectively) (Figure 2B,C). Liraglutide (48 h) treatment significantly reversed these defects (p = 0.002, p = 0.0002 and p = 0.010) and outperformed insulin in key metrics (e.g., total branching points, p = 0.012). Similarly, in a scratch assay (Figure 2D,E), glucotoxicity impaired cell migration. By 48 h, both liraglutide and insulin significantly improved migration (p < 0.0001), with liraglutide showing superior wound confluence. These results demonstrate that liraglutide confers potent, direct protective effects on RMECs against hyperglycaemic stress.

Untargeted metabolomics reveals broad disruptions in lipid and amino acid pathways

To uncover the metabolic mechanisms underlying the microvascular dysfunction, we performed untargeted renal metabolomics, identifying 2969 metabolites. PCA and OPLS‐DA analyses demonstrated inter‐group separation and high model validity (Figures S8 and S9). T2DM mice exhibited 436 altered metabolites (254 down, 182 up) compared to controls (Figures S10 and S11), with (S)‐9‐hydroxy‐10‐undecenoic acid most elevated and LysoPC (18:4) most decreased (Figure S11). Both insulin and liraglutide treatments substantially reversed these T2DM‐induced metabolic shifts (Figure 3A). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway enrichment analysis identified ‘Protein digestion and absorption’ and ‘ABC transporter’ pathways as significantly disrupted in T2DM (Figures S11 and S12). ‘Protein digestion and absorption’ was also the most commonly enriched pathway when comparing T2DM to the treatment groups (Figure 3B), alongside several lipid metabolism pathways. DEM distribution analysis confirmed a substantial overlap in the metabolic adaptations elicited by both treatments (Figure 3C,D).

Differential metabolic responses are driven by treatment modality and duration

Direct comparison of treatment modalities revealed significant metabolic differences, with 315 up‐ and 439 down‐regulated DEMs between Ins‐1w and Lira‐1w. Comparing durations (1‐ vs. 2‐week) also revealed hundreds of DEMs (Figure S13A). Despite these differences, 193 DEMs were common across all main comparisons (Figure S13B). KEGG analysis of these differential responses again highlighted pathways in lipid and amino acid metabolism, including ‘retrograde endocannabinoid signalling’ and ‘protein digestion and absorption’ (Figure S13C,D), reflecting the time‐dependent metabolic modulation by both drugs.

Liraglutide preferentially restores amino acid homeostasis associated with peptide transport pathways

Given the consistent enrichment of the ‘Protein digestion and absorption’ pathway, we performed targeted amino acid profiling (Figure S14). The confirmed that T2DM mice exhibited significant reductions in five key amino acids, glutamine, methionine, ornithine, phenylalanine and tryptophan (Figure 4A–D, Table S2). One week of liraglutide (Lira‐1w) treatment normalised the levels of all five. In contrast, ornithine remained suppressed in both insulin groups and the Lira‐2w group, indicating a differential and rapid metabolic response to liraglutide (Figure 4D). Mapping these differential amino acids to the KEGG pathway (Figure 5A,B) revealed that metabolites downstream of PepT, specifically glutamine, methionine and phenylalanine, were down‐regulated in T2DM and restored by Lira‐1w, suggesting that the short‐term liraglutide treatment's effect on amino acid homeostasis is associated with metabolic profiles potentially involving peptide transport activity, distinct from that of insulin. Finally, these differential amino acids correlated significantly with renal and microcirculatory function (Figure 5C). Glutamine, methionine, phenylalanine and ornithine all correlated with renal erythrocyte tissue fraction, total haemoglobin and oxygenated haemoglobin, illuminating an interconnection between these metabolic imbalances and the observed renal microcirculatory dysfunction.

Liraglutide restores renal peptide transporter expression and brush border integrity

To anchor the recovery of amino acid homeostasis observed in our metabolomics analysis, we focused on the renal PepTs PepT1 (Slc15a1) and PepT2 (Slc15a2), essential gatekeepers for peptide‐bound amino acid reabsorption in the proximal tubule. Quantitative analysis of gene and protein expression demonstrated a suppression of both transporters in the T2DM kidney (Figure 6A,B), paralleling the metabolic deficits. Crucially, while glycaemic control via insulin yielded only a modest, non‐significant increase in transporter abundance, liraglutide treatment potently restored both PepT1 and PepT2 proteins to near‐physiological levels. The pharmacological superiority was particularly evident for the high‐affinity transporter PepT2, where liraglutide efficacy significantly exceeded that of insulin (p < 0.05). Immunohistochemical examination further revealed that the diabetic kidney suffers from a loss of brush border integrity, characterised by fragmented and diffuse transporter staining, contrasting with the sharp, continuous apical linearity observed in controls (Figure 6C). Whereas insulin therapy failed to rectify this pathological fragmentation, liraglutide effectively re‐established the continuous linear architecture of the brush border, linking the metabolic correction to a structural restoration of the peptide transport system that extends beyond the benefits of glycaemic normalisation.

DISCUSSION

The central finding of our study is that significant renal microvascular dysfunction following diabetes induction is a rapidly reversible functional state. We demonstrate that 1–2 weeks short‐term treatment with liraglutide potently reverses microcirculatory hypoxia and endothelial dysfunction, preceding any substantial structural remodelling. The functional rescue is linked to the normalisation of metabolic pathways, namely amino acid homeostasis, suggesting a paradigm where direct metabolic reprogramming, independent of, or parallel to, glycaemic control, can serve as a primary therapeutic vector to restore microvascular homeostasis in early‐stage diabetes, opening a previously underappreciated therapeutic window.

A key finding that challenges current understanding is the observed dissociation between renal BP and microvascular oxygenation. Our in vivo data revealed that T2DM mice, at this early stage, did not exhibit a significant deficit in bulk microcirculatory BP (Figure S3). Instead, they revealed impairments in tissue SO2 and a pathological inversion of the SO2‐deoxyhaemoglobin relationship, unequivocally indicating impaired oxygen utilisation (Figure S4), indicating that the incipient diabetic microvascular lesion is not a simple failure of blood flow, but rather a functional failure of endothelial‐mediated oxygen transport and peripheral tissue exchange. The rapid correction of this oxygenation deficit by both insulin and liraglutide highlights the profound plasticity of this early impairment, long before capillary rarefaction becomes irreversible.

Additionally, our findings on endothelial oscillators provide a direct functional mechanism for the oxygenation failure. T2DM induced a marked suppression of both NO‐dependent and NO‐independent endothelial oscillator amplitudes (Figures S5 and S6), indicative of severe endothelial dysfunction and impaired microvascular autoregulation. However, comparative analysis indicates that the two treatment modalities exerted distinct vascular effects despite comparable glycaemic control. While both treatments effectively reversed the defects in microcirculatory SO2 and renal tissue architecture, liraglutide demonstrated superior efficacy in restoring endothelial autoregulatory function. Liraglutide rapidly reversed oscillator suppression within 1 week. Conversely, insulin treatment at 1 week did not immediately restore these oscillations and, in specific metrics, coincided with amplitudes lower than the diabetic baseline, suggesting a lag in vascular functional recovery compared to the structural and oxygenation improvements. At the cellular level, liraglutide demonstrated superior pro‐angiogenic and migratory effects in RMECS relative to insulin, 32 as evidenced by enhanced tube formation and cell migration in vitro. Moreover, our metabolomic profiling identified a specific restoration of amino acid homeostasis, particularly involving glutamine and methionine, solely in the short‐term liraglutide group. These data collectively suggest that liraglutide confers direct, rapid vascular protection and metabolic correction independent of weight loss or glucose lowering, whereas insulin‐mediated microvascular recovery appears delayed in the early treatment phase.

A comparative evaluation of the two therapeutic regimens clarifies the drivers of these improvements. The parallel restoration of renal tissue architecture and microcirculatory SO2 in both the insulin‐ and liraglutide‐treated groups suggests that these parameters are largely governed by the relief of systemic glucotoxicity. In contrast, the rapid recovery of endothelial oscillator activity and the selective normalisation of amino acid profiles were observed exclusively following GLP‐1R agonism. Accordingly, whereas haemodynamic stabilisation and structural repair appear closely associated with the attainment of euglycaemia, the re‐establishment of finely regulated endothelial autoregulatory function and metabolic homeostasis depends on liraglutide‐specific mechanisms. 33 These observations expose a fundamental limitation of glucocentric therapeutic approaches, indicating that normalisation of blood glucose alone is insufficient to actively restore impaired endothelial signalling networks. Rather, the selective recovery of oscillatory dynamics supports a model in which liraglutide directly engages vascular regulatory circuits, thereby uncoupling renal functional improvement from systemic glycaemic control. Collectively, these findings argue for therapeutic strategies that actively reinforce endothelial functional capacity, rather than relying solely on passive haemodynamic correction, to more effectively halt the progression of diabetic microangiopathy.

We propose a mechanism for liraglutide's renoprotection centred on restoring amino acid homeostasis. The observed metabolic shifts suggest a down‐regulation of peptide transport in T2DM, contrasting with increased PepT1 activity in catabolic type 1 diabetes,34, 35 likely reflecting suppression by chronic hyperinsulinaemia. The metabolic rectification appears underpinned by restoration of the renal peptide transport machinery PepT1 and PepT2.36, 37 We observed that while insulin‐mediated glycaemic control failed to sufficiently reverse the pathological fragmentation of the proximal tubule brush border, liraglutide treatment re‐established the linear apical architectural integrity required for efficient transport. The dissociation implies that the loss of reabsorptive capacity in T2DM is a structural defect that requires targeted regenerative signalling to reconstruct the physical interface of nutrient exchange. Liraglutide may reverse this via canonical GLP‐1R signalling, recruiting Protein Kinase A (PKA) and Epac38, 39 to modulate transporter dynamics. 40 Functionally, this restoration normalises amino acids critical for renal health,41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46 particularly substrates for Endothelial Nitric Oxide Synthasee (NOS)‐dependent perfusion. Furthermore, liraglutide may target intestinal PepT1,47, 48 stabilising systemic amino acid profiles. Thus, liraglutide's benefits are likely to extend beyond anti‐inflammatory effects,49, 50 reflecting a fundamental metabolic reprogramming of inter‐organ nutrient transport.

Our findings resonate with clinical observations, such as the Healthy Life in an urban Setting (HELIUS) Intraperitoneal Gucose Tolerance Test study, 51 which highlights the heightened susceptibility of the diabetic microvasculature to systemic macrovascular insults.52, 53 Selection of the HFD/STZ‐induced BALB/c model, rather than the genetic db/db strain, was driven by the objective to isolate early renal microhemodynamic fluctuations from established structural nephropathy. Unlike genetic models where aggressive progression often conflates initial haemodynamic variations with concurrent structural remodelling, the current protocol permits a temporal dissection of metabolic insults. The initial HFD phase introduces systemic lipotoxicity and endothelial stress prior to hyperglycaemia induction,54, 55 thereby mimicking pre‐diabetic vascular conditioning. The model highlights a stage of reversible functional maladaptation that precedes irreversible structural damage. While creatinine levels showed metabolic variability, the observed reduction in serum cystatin C indicated a state of glomerular hyperfiltration, a hallmark of incipient DN driven by microvascular dysregulation prior to the onset of overt structural damage and Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR) decline.

Additionally, our multi‐omics data provides a hypothesis; it is also important to recognise that the induction of diabetes in our experimental model represents an accelerated phenotype, which differs from the slow, insidious onset of insulin resistance characteristic of human type 2 diabetes. 56 Consequently, our data may not fully capture the chronic adaptive responses of the renal microvasculature to gradually accumulating metabolic insults over decades. To bridge this gap and definitively establish the prognostic value of the observed microcirculatory dysfunction, future research should shift towards longitudinal designs that track the temporal evolution of renal microcirculatory parameters. Specifically, studies utilising longitudinal intravital microscopy or functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) in models of early‐stage pre‐diabetes are needed to determine whether the dampening of endothelial oscillatory patterns precedes the onset of overt clinical markers, for example, microalbuminuria. Confirming the temporal sequence would validate endothelial oscillator dysfunction as a driver of renal pathogenesis and identify it as a window for early therapeutic intervention before irreversible structural remodelling occurs.

In summary, our study provides evidence that liraglutide acts as a rapid metabolic modulator that can repair microcirculatory homeostasis by correcting endothelial dysfunction and normalising amino acid profiles.
