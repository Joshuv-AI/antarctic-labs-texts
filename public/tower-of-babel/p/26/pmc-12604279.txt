Isolation, identification and characterization of compounds from the n-hexane fraction of Citrus limon seeds: cytotoxic and mosquito repellent activities
Authors: Mukaram Akintunde Adeniyi-Akee, Emmanuel Oloruntoba Yeye, Adibe Kingsley Mbachu, Kolade Ibrahim, Ogunyemi Oderinlo, Paul Matthew Osamudiamen, Sunday Olakunle Idowu, Olapeju O. Aiyelaagbe, Olajire Aremu Adegoke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe prevalence of malaria and other mosquito menace in Lagos State and many other parts of Nigeria has becomes so alarming. Citrus limon seeds have limited exploration, unlike the leaves, juice and the peels which are burnt as repellent and have been widely explored. This study was conducted to evaluate the repellent and cytotoxic effects of three known lipids, a known flavonoid and a novel fatty acid isolated from the n-hexane fraction C. limon.MethodsThe structures of the compounds were determined based on spectroscopic (IR, 1-D, 2-D NMR) and mass spectrometric experiments. Isolated compounds were evaluated for cytotoxicity and Anopheles gambiae mosquito repellent effects, using brine shrimp lethality assay (safety cut-off, LC50 > 100 µg/mL), and human bait method, respectively, while N,N-diethyl-meta-toluamide (DEET) and acetone were used as positive and negative controls, respectively. Repellent activity data were analysed using one-way ANOVA at α0.05.ResultsCompounds 1–5 identification gave, Palmitic acid (1), 14-Oxotricosanoic acid (2), n-Octyl stearate (3), 4-heptadec-7-enyloxy)carbonyl)butanoic acid (4) and 6,8 dimethoxy-3-undecyl-1H-[2]benzopyran-1-one (5). Compounds (1, 2 and 4) showed some level of toxicity with LC50 values less than 100 µg/mL, while compounds 3 and 5 are considered safe because of their LC50 values, that are greater than 100 µg/mL. Compound 4 and 5 are the most promising mosquito repellent agents at 5 mg/mL optimum concentration with repellencies of 77.87% and 75.15%, respectively, as compared to the standard, DEET (100%).ConclusionThe results offer additional scientific justification for the local usage of Citrus limon as mosquito repellent.

Background

Citrus plant belongs to a class of flowering plants in the rue family, the Rutaceae. Some economically important species in the family include the lemon (Citrus limon), the lime (Citrus aurantiifolia), the sweet orange (Citrus sinensis), the sour orange (Citrus aurantium), the tangerine (Citrus reticulata), the grapefruit (Citrus paradisi), the citron (Citrus medica), and the shaddock (Citrus maxima). Generally, peels, seeds, and pulps are dealt with as wastes, while, potentially, they can be source of pharmaceutical by-products [1, 2]. The species of the fruit are found to be of medical values and are also used in confectionary, toiletry, and perfume manufacturing industries. Citrus plants are grown globally and their fruits have been reported to contain different metabolites, such as limonene, terpenes, flavonoids, pectin and phenolics [3]. Their derived products have many therapeutic and nutritional benefits, for example, the medicinal potential of Citrus seeds, such as their proven antioxidative and anti-cancer effects, has made them to be considered beneficial and safe compounds or raw materials in pharmaceuticals and cosmetics production, therefore, huge attention has been placed on them [4].

Series of bioactive compounds have been isolated from C. limon, which include: Limonin, a furanoside diterpenoid, and β-sitosterol, a steroid [5]. Apigenin, luteolin, chrysoeriol, quercetin [6, 7], eriocitrin, diosmin, hesperidin [8], methyl jasmonate and methyl epi-jasmonate [9], 8-geranyloxypsolaren, 5-geranyloxypsolaren, 5-geranyloxy-7-methoxycoumarin, and phloroglucinol 1-β-D-glucopyranoside (phlorin) [10] are other reported compounds. 8-hydroxyhesperetin, cardamidin (6-hydroxynarigenin) and isocarthamidin (8-hydroxy narigenin) [11] have also been previously reported from the various species of Citrus.

In recent years, the cases of malarial infection in the tropical areas have been on the increase with the burden been borne more by the population of under-fives, with further complications of resistant falciparum strains developing on yearly basis. While advances have been made in the development of chemotherapeutic agents against malarial parasites, aspect of vector control is still a field that requires serious attention if the war against malaria will ever be won. The present study is aimed at isolation, characterizsation and the evaluation of mosquito repellent and cytotoxic activities of compounds from C. limon seeds against adult Anopheles gambiae mosquitoes.

Methods

Ethical approval for repellent assay using human volunteers

Ethical approval was obtained from the College of medicine, University of Ibadan, Nigeria’s Institute for Advanced Medical Research and Training (IAMRAT), with registration number NHREC/05/01/2008a and Ethics Board assigned number UI/EC/17/0308.

Recruitment of human volunteers

The human volunteers used in this study were recruited from two different areas, in and around University of Ibadan, Oyo State of Nigeria. Ethical permission was sought and obtained from the College of Medicine, University of Ibadan, Nigeria’s Institute for Advanced Medical Research and Training (IAMRAT), through an application which carried a written detailed proposal of the study. The ethical permission, together with the thought-provoking lecture by the team on the menace of malaria and the need to control it, helped to win the interest and co-operation of the leaders and the majority of the people in the study areas. Furthermore, the importance of the study in malaria control and the sources of the phytochemical extracts to be tested were explained in both English and the local language. The people, adult males and females only, were then allowed to declare individually, their consent and willingness to participate as volunteers. Again, these chosen volunteers were still given the opportunity to opt out at any time they wished.

Materials and chemicals

Melting points were recorded uncorrected. IR spectra were obtained in KBr on a Perkin Elmer FTIR system spectrum BX spectrometer. Column chromatography was carried out on silica gel (70–230 mesh, Merck). Thin-layer chromatography was carried out on pre-coated silica gel plates (Merck, PF254, 20 × 20) and spots visualized under UV (254 and 366 nm, short and long wavelengths) and/or by exposure to iodine vapour and sprayed with 30% H2SO4 followed by heating.

The 1H and 13C NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Avance Neo 400 and 150 MHz spectrometer, respectively in CD3Cl3 as solvent, chemical shifts (δ) are expressed in ppm and are referenced to the solvent signals. The 2D spectra: COSY, HSQC, HMBC connectivity was determined. The LC–MS and ESI–MS data were obtained using the JEOL-JMS600H-1 mass spectrometer. The repellency assay was carried out with female An. gambiae mosquitoes which were 5–6 days’ old and have been blood-starved for 24 h. Deet (N,N-Diethyl-meta-toluamide) and acetone were used as the positive and the negative controls, respectively. Mosquito larvae were collected from various habitats in Ibadan metropolis, Nigeria, and were authenticated at the Department of Zoology, Faculty of Science, University of Ibadan, Ibadan, Nigeria. They were left to hatch into adults inside the (30 × 30 × 30 cm) netted cage, at the insectary of the Department of Zoology, University of Ibadan, Nigeria. Adult female mosquitoes were maintained on 10% sugar solution at 27 ± 2 °C and 68 ± 2% humidity.

Plant material

Citrus limon seeds were purchased from renowned juice sellers at Oje market, Ibadan, Oyo state, Nigeria. The identity was confirmed at the Federal Research Institute of Nigeria, Jericho, Ibadan, Nigeria, with voucher number: FHL112112.

Extraction and isolation

The air-dried and pulverised seeds (800 g) of C. limon, was extracted with n-hexane, ethyl acetate and methanol respectively by maceration. This was carried out by soaking the plant material in the solvent for 72 h. The extracts obtained were concentrated using rotary evaporator (Buchi R215). The n-hexane extract (18 g) of the seeds of C. limon was adsorbed on 30 g of silica gel using a column (75 cm length and 17 cm internal diameter). This was loaded into a column packed with the same stationary phase (180 g) in hexane. The column was eluted using a step gradient of n-hexane, ethyl acetate, and methanol, starting with 100% hexane stepped down to 10, 20, 30, 40, 60, 80, 100% ethyl acetate, 50% methanol in ethyl acetate, and 100% methanol. The fractions obtained were coded as CLE1-CLE10 and were all screened for their repellent activities. All fractions with percentage repellencies greater than or equal to 70% were considered for further purification in order to isolate the most active compounds. The procedure was repeated four times to build up the yield of the extracts. The fractions which were coded CLE1 and CLE2 that were eluted with 100% of n-hexane and 10% of ethyl acetate in hexane were thereafter further purified.

The fraction coded CLE1 obtained from step gradient column chromatography of the n-hexane extract at mobile phase of 100% hexane was further subjected to open column chromatography on silica gel. Five grams of CLE1 was pre-absorbed on 5 g of silica gel inside a glass Petri dish. The dried powder obtained was introduced into a column packed with silica gel in hexane. The column was eluted with a mixture of hexane/ethyl acetate (100:0) and (95:5). 568 fractions (50 mL each) were collected and pooled based on Thin Layer Chromatography (TLC) characteristics into two fractions 1–235 and 236–568, coded CLE1* and CLE2*, respectively. CLE1* and CLE2* were also screened for repellent activities. CLE1*, which showed four (4) spots on TLC, and better repellent activity was purified further with a micro column, using 100% hexane and 2% ethyl acetate in hexane to yield two white isolates; compounds 1 and 2.

The fraction CLE2 was eluted with 10% ethyl acetate in hexane (10:90) that showed four (4) major spots on TLC plate. Four grams was loaded on silica gel column. Using a mixture of 7.5% ethyl acetate in hexane as the mobile phase to elute the column, a total of 158 fractions, 20 mL each were collected and combined into four sub-fractions labeled CLE2-A to CLE2-D, in accordance to their TLC Rf values. They were all tested for their repellent activities and sub-fractions CLE2-C (1.35 g) and CLE-D (0.86 g) that showed 67.50% and 70.50% activities respectively were further purified.

The sub-fraction CLE2-C (1.35 g) was subjected to both preparative TLC and mini-column for purification. The sample was prepared with dichloromethane and spread on the origin of the prep TLC plate. In all, 3 plates were used. The plates were developed inside the TLC tank using a mixture of acetone in hexane (20:80). Eight bands were collected from the three plates altogether, their TLC profiles were carried out and spots with similar Rf were pooled together to give 2 sub-fractions CLE2-Ca and CLE2-Cb that weighed 0.90 g and 0.25 g respectively. The sub-fraction CLE2-Ca that weighed 0.90 g, and showed three spots on TLC was later purified with a mini column, using 100% hexane and 0.5% acetone in hexane to yield compounds 3 and 4.

The sub-fraction CLE2-D (0.86 g) that showed three spots on TLC plate was subjected to both preparatory TLC and mini column for purification which was further purified. The mixture was purified further with a mini column using 0.5% A/H to yield compound 5.

Bioassays

Cytotoxicity assay [Brine shrimp lethality test]

Hatching of eggs

The seawater was poured into the partitioned plastic container with a side covered and the other closed. Brine shrimp eggs were added to the covered (dark) part and whole case was exposed to light. The hatching of eggs to larva occurred after 2 days and the larva moved to the open or exposed side of the case, they were attracted to light (phototaxis).

Cytotoxicity test of isolated compounds

The compounds were prepared at three doses; 1000, 100 and 10 in µg/mL. Preparation of stock solution was done by weighing 0.002 g (2 mg) and dissolving it in 2 mL of dimethyl sulfoxide in a test tube as previously reported by [12].

Mosquito repellency assay

Dose-percentage protection relationship [12, 13] and human bait technique which simulates skin condition to which repellent would be eventually applied were used as techniques for the mosquito repellency assay. The mosquito repellent assay was conducted using the method reported by [14]. Tests were conducted at room temperature and normal atmospheric pressure. Repellency was derived in percentage [15], using the equation below:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\% Repellency = \frac{{T_{a} - T_{b} }}{{T_{a} }} x 100$$\end{document}%Repellency=Ta-TbTax100where Ta = Controlled number of mosquitoes, Tb = Treated number of mosquitoes.

Statistical analysis

Computer software MS EXCEL 2003 and SPSS 14 version were used for the statistical analysis of the experimental data. One-way ANOVA in SPSS was used to calculate and compare the percentage repellency of the extracts at each concentration on plant basis. Results were also calculated as average ± SD.

Results and discussion

Structural elucidation and identification of the isolated compounds

The phytochemical analysis led to the isolation of five compounds of which one is novel. In an attempt to find compounds with good repellent activities from medicinal plants, Palmitic acid (1), 14-Oxotricosanoic acid (2), n-Octyl stearate (3), 4-heptadec-7-enyloxy)carbonyl)butanoic acid (4) and 6,8-dimethoxy-3-undecyl-1H-[2]benzopyran-1-one (5) were obtained from C. limon seeds. Bioassays

Brine shrimp lethality assay

The isolated compounds were assayed for their brine shrimp lethality. The assay showed that compounds (1, 2 and 4), had some level of toxicity with LC50 values less than 100 μg/mL, while compounds 3 and 5 are considered safe because of their LC50 values, that are greater than 100 μg/mL as presented in Table 1 and Fig. 3. Preliminary assessment of plants and their isolated compounds for their cytotoxicity can be carried out using brine shrimp lethality assay. This can be done to access their safety to biological cells prior to human consumptions. The assay can also be used to detect medicinal properties such as antimicrobial and antitumor activities of plants and their compounds, based on the LC50 values they exhibited [21]. Repellency assay of the isolated compounds from Citrus limon and their percentage repellencies

The isolated compounds were tested against adult female Anopheles gambiae mosquitoes, and the percentage repellency results are shown in Table 3.2. The repellent activities of compound 4 (77.87%) and compound 5 (75.15%) are the most promising at 5 mg/mL optimum concentration.

Palmitic acid (compound 1) with repellent effect of 65.91% alongside with other fatty acids were reported to be among the constituents in palm oil residue responsible for larvicidal activity against Aedes aegypti and synergistic effect [23]. The high activity of compound 4 is in line with the reported repellent activities of some other fatty acids. For example, C8910 (formulation of octanoic, nonanoic and decanoic acids) is a fatty acid repellent, which is eco-friendly and also categorized to be safe by the United States administration for food and drug. It is currently being commercialized as insect repellent in South Africa, and also approved by the United States agency for environmental protection, as fly repellent on livestock. Its dual activities against mosquitoes and other flies, as repellent and insecticide, are uncommon, compared to the standard repellent: DEET, (N, N-diethyl-m-toluamide) [24]. C8910 has also shown potential as an agent of malaria vector control, because of its adulticidal effect against pyrethroid resistant and insecticide susceptible Anopheles funestus [25]. The C8:0 to C12:0 chain length fatty acids of coconut oil were found to exhibit the predominant repellent activity against biting flies, ticks, bed bugs and mosquitoes. Repellency was stronger and with longer residual activity than that of DEET [26].

Saturated and unsaturated fatty acids have been reported to exhibit biting preventive effects against Aedes aegypti mosquito [27]. Plant seeds extracts containing fixed oil could be an abundant source of fatty acids that can prevent mosquito bites [28].

Compound 4; 4-heptadec-7-enyloxy)carbonyl)butanoic acid is a new compound being isolated for the first time from natural product and has not been reported for any biological activities, except its derivatives like pentadecanoic acid, which is present as a Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (GC–MS) constituent in the Cenchrus ciliaris leaf extract, and is employed by pharmaceutical companies and traditional healers to produce medicinal drugs, both plant based and synthetics [29].

Compound 5 has been reported for its in vitro antifungal activity against some human, animal and plant pathogenic moulds [20, 30]. Compounds 4 and 5 could be recommended for formulation or synergy with other existing fatty acids or plant-based repellent against mosquito bites.

Conclusion

Chromatographic investigation of the seeds of Citrus limon resulted in the isolation of palmitic acid, 14-Oxotricosanoic acid, n-Octyl stearate, 4-heptadec-7-enyloxy)carbonyl)butanoic acid and 6,8-dimethoxy-3-undecyl-1H-[2]benzopyran-1-one, with percentage repellencies of: 65.91, 63.63, 51.69,77.87 and 75.15%, respectively. This is the first time that 4-heptadec-7-enyloxy)carbonyl)butanoic acid is being isolated from natural source. All the isolated compounds have meaningful repellent activities, with pronounced activities in compounds 4 and 5. The results provide additional justification for the traditional use of Citrus limon as mosquito repellent.
