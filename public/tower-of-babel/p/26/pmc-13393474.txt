Overcoming surgical challenges with robotic assistance: robot-assisted nephrectomy of a failed renal allograft after complex en-bloc dual kidney transplantation
Authors: Xinji Yang, Zhanyi Zhang, Weilong Shi, Herong Zhu, Wenbin Zhang, Shudong Zhang, Qiming Zhang, Xiaofei Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We present the case of a 37-year-old male who underwent infant donor en-bloc dual kidney transplantation (EBKT) and subsequently developed left allograft failure with severe refractory urinary tract infection secondary to long-standing ureteropelvic junction obstruction and hydronephrosis. After failure of conservative anti-infective therapy to control recurrent infection, the patient underwent successful robot-assisted extracapsular allograft nephrectomy (ECAN), with the primary surgical indication of eradicating the source of refractory infection in the obstructed, completely non-functional allograft. The procedure was completed in 145 min with minimal blood loss (20 mL). The patient was discharged on postoperative day 5 with complete resolution of symptoms and preserved function of the contralateral viable graft. This case demonstrates that robot-assisted surgery offers a safe and precise surgical option for failed allograft nephrectomy after complex pediatric EBKT, with favorable perioperative outcomes in this specific scenario involving distorted anatomy and severe adhesions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12894-026-02148-8.

Introduction

Kidney allograft failure (KAF) remains a major clinical challenge that adversely impacts the long-term prognosis of renal transplant recipients (RTRs). Studies have consistently shown that patients with KAF face a substantially elevated risk of mortality. Transplant nephrectomy (TN) is the core intervention for symptomatic KAF, with primary therapeutic goals of eradicating allograft-related refractory infections, alleviating persistent clinical symptoms, and improving patients’ quality of life [1].

Infant donor en-bloc dual kidney transplantation (EBKT) is a specialized surgical technique in renal transplantation, which involves transplanting both kidneys from an infant donor, along with the donor abdominal aorta and inferior vena cava, into a single iliac fossa of an adult recipient. The inherent complexity of this procedure and the resulting unique anatomical configuration markedly increase the technical difficulty of subsequent TN in these patients. In recent years, the widespread adoption of robotic surgical systems has helped mitigate the technical challenges associated with these high-complexity operations.

In December 2024, we admitted a patient with a prior history of infant donor EBKT to Peking University Third Hospital, who developed refractory infection secondary to left graft failure caused by severe hydronephrosis. The patient subsequently underwent robot-assisted ECAN with favorable clinical outcomes and uneventful postoperative recovery. This case is reported in detail below.

Case presentation

A 37-year-old male was admitted to our center on December 20, 2024, with the chief complaint of “left graft hydronephrosis for over 3 years, accompanied by fever and gross hematuria for more than 1 month”. Six years prior, the patient had undergone infant donor EBKT at an external institution for end-stage renal disease (ESRD). The surgical technique involved placing both donor kidneys in the recipient’s right iliac fossa, with the donor aortic patch anastomosed end-to-side to the right external iliac artery, and the donor inferior vena cava patch anastomosed end-to-side to the right external iliac vein. A small incision was made on the right bladder wall, where the mucosa of both donor ureters was separately sutured to the bladder mucosa with interrupted stitches, followed by submucosal tunneling (Fig. 1).

Postoperatively, the patient adhered to a regular immunosuppressive regimen, with a stable serum creatinine (Scr) level of approximately 90 µmol/L (1.02 mg/dL). Left graft hydronephrosis was first detected on regular follow-up 3 years before admission. At that time, the patient was completely asymptomatic, with no fever, lumbago, lower urinary tract symptoms, or urinary tract infection. His serum creatinine remained stable at the ~ 90 µmol/L baseline, and radionuclide renography confirmed that the split renal function of the left graft was less than 5%, with nearly no residual renal function; the patient’s total renal function was completely dependent on the contralateral right graft, which contributed more than 95% of overall renal function.

After multidisciplinary consultation and thorough informed communication with the patient and his family, a strategy of active surveillance was adopted. The core considerations for this decision included: (1) the left graft had developed irreversible severe dysfunction with no salvage value; (2) the patient had no clinical symptoms or infection related to hydronephrosis at that time; (3) the patient’s history of complex infant EBKT resulted in severe anatomical distortion of the urinary tract, and the multidisciplinary team evaluated that the success rate of endourologic intervention was extremely low, with a high risk of iatrogenic injury to the ureter of the contralateral functional graft, which could lead to catastrophic loss of the patient’s only functional kidney; (4) the patient and his family fully understood the benefits and risks of invasive intervention versus surveillance, and refused endourologic intervention at that time, opting for regular follow-up instead.

Serial ultrasound follow-up demonstrated progressive aggravation of left graft hydronephrosis, with an anteroposterior diameter of 8.1 cm in October 2021, 8.8 cm in July 2022, and 17.4 cm in November 2024. Over 1 month before admission, the patient developed recurrent fever (peaking at 38.7 °C) accompanied by progressive lower urinary tract symptoms including frequency, urgency, dysuria, and gross hematuria, and was admitted to a local hospital for treatment. Urinalysis showed occult blood 1+, leukocytes 3+, and protein 2+, and urine culture confirmed bacterial infection. The patient received sequential, guideline-based anti-infective therapy with cefoperazone-sulbactam (5 days), linezolid combined with metronidazole (7 days), and meropenem (5 days). Although inflammatory markers decreased significantly (C-reactive protein [CRP] from 370 mg/L to 4.5 mg/L, procalcitonin [PCT] from 65 ng/mL to 0.2 ng/mL) after intensive antibiotic therapy, the patient’s lower urinary tract symptoms and gross hematuria persisted with intermittent chyluria, and fever recurred repeatedly after antibiotic withdrawal, meeting the diagnostic criteria for refractory infection originating from the non-functional left graft.

During treatment at the local hospital, the multidisciplinary team evaluated the feasibility of endourologic interventions (including cystoscopy, ureteral stenting, and percutaneous nephrostomy). However, the evaluation indicated severe anatomical distortion after EBKT: the left graft ureter was severely fibrotic and adherent, with no definable lumen identifiable on imaging, and the ureteral orifice of the left graft was anatomically adjacent to that of the contralateral functional graft. The team concluded that the success rate of endourologic intervention was extremely low, and the procedure carried a high risk of aggravating systemic sepsis and iatrogenic injury to the contralateral graft and its ureter, which could lead to loss of the patient’s only functional kidney. Accordingly, the patient was urgently referred to our tertiary transplant center for further surgical management.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and magnetic resonance urography (MRU) performed at the external hospital revealed severe hydronephrosis of the left renal pelvis, without hydroureter of the left allograft (Fig. 2A, B). Doppler ultrasound of the transplanted kidneys showed no blood flow signals in the left graft, confirming complete loss of residual function and that the left graft was the source of persistent infection. After comprehensive assessment at our center, the clinical diagnoses were established as: (1) complicated refractory urinary tract infection; (2) left renal allograft failure with severe obstructive hydronephrosis.

Upon admission, computed tomography urography (CTU) and magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) were performed to delineate the position and anatomical relationships of the vessels of both transplanted kidneys. The proximal abdominal aorta of the dual donor kidneys was anastomosed to the lateral wall of the right external iliac artery, with its distal end giving rise to the bilateral renal arteries entering the respective renal hila. Similarly, the proximal inferior vena cava of the dual donor kidneys was anastomosed to the lateral wall of the right external iliac vein, with its distal end giving rise to the bilateral renal veins. The donor abdominal aorta and inferior vena cava were in close proximity, and the left renal artery and vein were adjacent, forming a cruciform intersection with indistinct tissue boundaries (Fig. 3A, B). Coronal views showed that the donor abdominal aorta and bilateral renal arteries formed a T-shaped arterial pathway converging into the right external iliac artery (Fig. 3C). A protruding aortic patch from the dual donor kidneys was visible on the wall of the right external iliac artery (Fig. 3D). The donor inferior vena cava and bilateral renal veins formed a cruciform venous pathway converging into the right external iliac vein, and the orifices of both ureters converged into the right posterolateral bladder wall (Fig. 3E, F). The hilar structures of the dual donor kidneys were closely juxtaposed and intertwined, resulting in highly complex anatomy. Furthermore, expansion of the failed left kidney secondary to hydronephrosis compressed the central hilar region between the two grafts, resulting in a narrow operative space. In addition, chronic inflammation and recurrent infection led to unclear anatomical planes and significant perigraft adhesions, further increasing anatomical complexity and surgical difficulty.

Surgical procedure

After multidisciplinary consultation, robot-assisted extracapsular allograft nephrectomy (ECAN) was planned. The core surgical challenges of this case were: (1) severely distorted hilar anatomy and dense perigraft adhesions secondary to prior EBKT and chronic inflammation; (2) shared common aortic and vena cava patches between the failed left graft and functional right graft, with extremely thin, fibrotic hilar vessels of the failed graft; (3) mandatory strict preservation of the contralateral functional graft (responsible for > 95% of total renal function) and its vascular supply. The primary surgical goals were complete resection of the infected non-functional graft to eradicate the source of refractory infection, and safe, precise management of the complex hilar anatomy. The procedure was performed by senior urologists with extensive experience in robotic transplant surgery.

The patient was placed in the supine Trendelenburg position, and a transperitoneal approach was adopted with standardized trocar placement and robotic system docking (Fig. 4A, B). The robotic platform’s high-definition three-dimensional (3D) visualization and wristed instruments with 7 degrees of freedom were leveraged throughout the procedure to address the core surgical challenges.

Upon entering the abdominal cavity, the dual donor kidneys were observed in the extraperitoneal space, suspended from the anterior abdominal wall. The left graft showed severe hydronephrosis with thinned parenchyma, and an indistinct border with the functioning right graft due to dense adhesions. Pulsation of the external iliac artery was visible posterior to the dual kidneys (Fig. 5A). The robotic system’s magnified 3D visualization enabled meticulous identification of the fragile tissue planes between the two grafts, avoiding iatrogenic injury to the viable right graft during mobilization. Based on arterial pulsation, the approximate location of the donor abdominal aorta at the hilum was identified. Careful dissection along the hilar direction and posterior wall of the dual kidneys was performed under robotic magnification to expose the donor abdominal aorta, while preserving the vascular supply to the right graft. The patient’s native right ureter was also identified as it crossed the iliac vessels (Fig. 5B).

Dissection proceeded distally along the external iliac artery to preliminarily locate the aortic anastomosis. Intraoperative ultrasound (IOUS) was used in combination with robotic visualization to confirm the anatomical boundary of the donor aorta, differentiate between the shared vascular patch and the recipient’s external iliac artery, and avoid vessel wall injury. Dense adhesions and thickening of the vascular sheath secondary to chronic inflammation were carefully dissected using robotic wristed instruments, which provided precise, tremor-free manipulation in the narrow surgical field to minimize the risk of vascular injury.

The severely hydronephrotic left kidney obstructed the surgical field; therefore, its capsule was incised and intrapelvic fluid was aspirated to decompress the graft and improve visualization of the hilar structures (Fig. 5C).

Dissection continued along the hilum under robotic magnification, which enabled clear differentiation of the extremely thin left renal artery from the adjacent shared aortic patch and the origin of the right renal artery. After sufficient exposure of the left renal artery, a temporary vascular clamp was applied proximally (near the donor aorta). Immediately after clamping, bilateral femoral and dorsalis pedis artery pulses were palpated and confirmed to be strong, verifying uninterrupted blood supply to the contralateral graft and lower extremities. The external iliac artery around the anastomosis was re-examined to rule out compression or injury. Following safety verification, the left renal artery was ligated with non-absorbable Hemolock polymer clips and transected (Fig. 5D).

For hilar vascular control in this case, we explicitly selected Hemolock clips for ligation and ruled out linear stapler application, based on the following anatomical characteristics and clinical principles:

Rationale for the clip-based strategy in this case: This case was characterized by: ① shared common aortic and vena cava patches between the two grafts, with the left renal artery and vein originating directly from the shared patches; ② extremely small (infant donor-derived), severely fibrotic and distorted hilar vessels of the failed graft, with a diameter too narrow for safe stapler firing; ③ dense adhesions leading to close proximity (< 1 mm in focal areas) between the failed graft’s vessels and the vascular pedicle of the contralateral functional graft. The clip-based ligation strategy allowed for precise, stepwise, point-to-point control of the tiny hilar vessels, with full visualization of adjacent critical structures under robotic magnification. This strategy maximally avoided accidental injury to the shared vascular patch and the contralateral graft’s vessels, which was the core safety priority of the procedure.Rationale for excluding linear stapler: Intraoperative assessment and IOUS confirmed that the hilar structures of the failed left graft were too small, fragile, and adherent to the shared vascular patch to be safely skeletonized and transected with a linear stapler. Stapler application in this scenario would carry an extremely high risk of: ① accidental injury or ligation of the shared aortic/vena cava patch, leading to life-threatening hemorrhage or loss of the contralateral functional graft; ② incomplete ligation of the tiny, fibrotic vessel wall, leading to postoperative bleeding or pseudoaneurysm formation. Thus, a linear stapler was deemed unsuitable for this specific anatomical scenario.

The adjacent left renal vein was then exposed under robotic 3D visualization. Due to severe hilar adhesions and its close anatomical relationship with the contralateral graft vein, complete skeletonization of the vein was not feasible without risking injury to the adjacent functional venous structure. We continued to use the stepwise clip ligation-and-transection technique with Hemolock clips: multiple overlapping clips were applied to ensure secure, full-thickness ligation of the vein wall, and small incremental transections were made under direct robotic magnification. This allowed real-time observation of the venous lumen to avoid injury to the adjacent shared vena cava patch and contralateral renal vein, as well as residual thrombus within the lumen. The left renal vein was successfully ligated and transected with this technique (Fig. 5E). IOUS and direct visual inspection confirmed no thrombus within the lumen or injury to the vessel wall, as well as intact blood flow in the contralateral renal vein and shared vena cava patch.

After complete and safe management of the hilar pedicle vessels, robotic-assisted meticulous dissection was performed to circumferentially mobilize the left graft, while dividing dense perigraft adhesions without disrupting the capsule of the contralateral functional graft. Finally, the left transplanted ureter was dissected free down to the bladder wall and transected (Fig. 5F), completing full extracapsular resection of the failed graft. Hemostasis was confirmed under robotic magnification, a pelvic drain was placed, and the specimen was extracted through a midline supraumbilical incision. Total operative time was 145 min, with an estimated intraoperative blood loss of only 20 mL. Postoperative pathological examination confirmed chronic pyelonephritis with hydronephrosis of the left transplanted kidney, consistent with the preoperative diagnosis.

Perioperative outcomes

The patient had an uneventful postoperative recovery and was discharged on postoperative day 5. Serum creatinine remained stable at 90 µmol/L (1.02 mg/dL), body temperature normalized, and lower urinary tract symptoms and gross hematuria completely resolved. At the 1-year follow-up, the patient reported no recurrent urinary tract infections or related symptoms, with significantly improved quality of life. Serial Doppler ultrasound examinations confirmed a patent stump of the left renal vein without thrombosis, and normal blood flow velocity in the contralateral transplant renal vessels and recipient iliac vessels, verifying preserved perfusion and function of the remaining viable graft.

Discussion

Renal transplantation remains the most effective treatment for end-stage renal disease (ESRD). However, the persistent shortage of standard criteria donor (SCD) kidneys has led to the increasing use of infant donor en-bloc dual kidney transplantation (EBKT) to expand the donor pool [2–4], a strategy validated to achieve long-term graft survival comparable to that of adult donor kidney transplantation [1, 5–7]. Nevertheless, infant donor EBKT inherently results in highly complex vascular and urinary tract anatomy in adult recipients, and the risk of surgical complications and subsequent allograft failure remains non-negligible [6, 8]. When allograft failure occurs with refractory complications, transplant nephrectomy (TN) is indicated; however, the unique shared vascular patch and distorted anatomy after EBKT bring unprecedented technical challenges to the operation, which is far more complex than conventional TN for single renal allografts.

The present case reports a rare and high-risk clinical scenario: a failed left allograft after infant donor EBKT causing severe refractory urinary tract infection, in a patient whose total renal function was completely dependent on the contralateral viable graft. The primary and only indication for surgery in this case was to completely eradicate the source of persistent infection via radical resection of the obstructed, non-functional allograft, which was the core principle guiding our entire clinical decision-making and surgical planning. For this complex case, we selected robot-assisted extracapsular allograft nephrectomy (ECAN), and the patient achieved favorable perioperative and 1-year follow-up outcomes.

For failed renal allograft nephrectomy, open surgery remains the traditional standard approach, with the advantages of reliable hilar exposure and vascular control. However, for patients with a history of EBKT, severe intra-abdominal adhesions, and distorted hilar anatomy, open surgery is associated with significant surgical trauma, a high risk of injury to the contralateral functional graft, and slow postoperative recovery. Conventional laparoscopic nephrectomy is a minimally invasive alternative, but it has notable limitations in this scenario: the lack of flexible wristed instruments and limited two-dimensional visualization make it difficult to perform meticulous dissection in the narrow surgical field with dense adhesions, resulting in a relatively high risk of vascular injury and conversion to open surgery [9, 10]. In contrast, the robot-assisted surgical system offers unique technical advantages for this complex scenario, with high-definition three-dimensional magnified visualization and tremor-free, multi-degree-of-freedom wristed instruments. These features enable precise dissection of severely adhered tissue planes and tiny distorted hilar vessels, while maximizing protection of the shared vascular patch and the contralateral functional graft — the core rationale for our choice of the robotic approach in this case.

We conducted a comprehensive evaluation of all alternative treatment options preoperatively, and clarified the necessity of radical allograft nephrectomy. For this patient, endourologic interventions such as ureteral stenting or percutaneous nephrostomy were deemed infeasible and high-risk for three core reasons: first, long-standing ureteropelvic junction obstruction had led to complete loss of left graft function with no salvage value; second, severe fibrosis and adhesion of the left ureter, combined with distorted urinary tract anatomy after EBKT, resulted in an extremely low success rate of endoscopic intervention; most importantly, the procedure carried a high risk of iatrogenic injury to the ureter of the contralateral graft, which could lead to catastrophic loss of the patient’s only functional kidney. Meanwhile, intensive antibiotic therapy had failed to control the recurrent infection, and adjustment of immunosuppression would further increase the risk of systemic infection and rejection of the viable graft. Therefore, allograft nephrectomy was confirmed as the only radical and safe treatment option for this patient after multidisciplinary consultation.

The most critical surgical challenge in this case was the safe management of the hilar vessels, given the shared aortic and vena cava patches from the infant donor, as well as the extremely thin, severely distorted hilar vessels of the failed graft. For the vascular control strategy, we selected Hemolock clip-based stepwise ligation rather than the conventional linear stapler, and this individualized decision was fully supported by the anatomical characteristics of this case and existing clinical evidence. It is widely acknowledged that linear staplers are the first-line standard for hilar control in conventional single-graft nephrectomy, with well-proven efficiency and safety¹⁰. However, in this specific case, the hilar vessels of the failed graft were too small, fragile, and closely adjacent to the shared vascular patch and the contralateral graft’s vessels, with no safe space for stapler firing. The use of a stapler would carry an extremely high risk of injury to the shared vascular patch and the blood supply of the contralateral graft. In contrast, the clip-based ligation strategy, combined with robotic magnified visualization, allowed precise, point-to-point control of the tiny hilar vessels, which was the safest approach to preserve the integrity of critical vascular structures for this patient. This strategy has also been reported to be safe and feasible in complex robot-assisted transplant nephrectomy cases with distorted hilar anatomy in previous studies⁹.

Intraoperative ultrasound (IOUS) was another key measure to ensure surgical safety in this case. Throughout the procedure, IOUS provided real-time, accurate localization of vascular structures, helping to delineate the anatomical boundary between the failed graft’s vessels and the shared vascular patch, and verify the patency of the contralateral graft’s blood supply before and after vessel ligation. This is particularly important for EBKT-related complex nephrectomy, as anatomical landmarks are often obscured by dense adhesions, and IOUS can effectively reduce the risk of accidental vascular injury [11].

For this specific patient, complete extracapsular resection of the failed allograft eliminates persistent antigenic stimulation from the non-functional graft, which is a supplementary consideration given the patient’s willingness for potential re-transplantation in the future [12].

Limitations

This study has several important limitations that need to be clearly acknowledged. First, this is a single case report, with inherent limitations including selection bias and extremely limited generalizability of the surgical approach and clinical outcomes to a wider patient population. The findings of this case can only provide a technical reference for similar rare complex scenarios, and cannot be used as a general recommendation for conventional transplant nephrectomy. Second, we explicitly acknowledge the limitations of the clinical management in this case: earlier endourologic intervention is the standard first-line strategy for transplant hydronephrosis in general clinical practice, while intervention was not performed in this case due to the specific clinical context, anatomical risks, and the patient’s family’s informed decision, which is an important limitation of this report. Third, the robotic surgical approach has inherent limitations, including a steep learning curve, higher medical costs, and limited accessibility in some medical centers, which restricts its widespread application. Finally, the follow-up duration of this case is limited to 1 year, and the long-term impact of the surgery on the patient’s contralateral graft function requires further extended follow-up.

Conclusion

This case reports the successful application of robot-assisted transplant nephrectomy (RATN) for a failed allograft after infant donor en-bloc dual kidney transplantation, with the primary surgical goal of eradicating severe refractory infection from the obstructed and completely non-functional left graft. The inherent anatomical complexity of EBKT, combined with severe perigraft adhesions and hilar distortion, posed significant surgical challenges in this case. The robotic platform, with its high-definition three-dimensional visualization and precise operating instruments, facilitated meticulous dissection of the distorted hilar anatomy, safe vascular control, and complete extracapsular resection of the failed graft, with minimal intraoperative blood loss and rapid postoperative recovery in this patient. The patient’s complete symptom resolution, stable contralateral graft function, and favorable 1-year outcome confirm the feasibility and safety of RATN in this specific high-risk complex scenario. This experience provides a technical reference for the management of similar rare cases of failed allograft after infant EBKT, while the generalizability of this approach is limited by the nature of a single case report. Further multicenter studies with larger sample sizes are needed to verify the long-term benefits of RATN in this patient population.
