Curcumin therapeutic potential in gastric cancer prevention, treatment and metastasis suppression
Authors: Alireza Abdollahi, Samaneh Salarvand, Fereshteh Ameli, Kimia Mozahheb Yousefi, Aysan Nozheh, Zahra Modabber
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gastric cancer (GC) remains a significant global health burden, characterized by high mortality rates, often due to late-stage diagnosis, resistance to conventional therapies, and metastatic dissemination. This narrative review synthesizes current preclinical and early clinical evidence on the diverse roles of curcumin, a natural polyphenolic compound derived from Curcuma longa (turmeric), in the context of gastric cancer. A comprehensive search of databases, including PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, was conducted, and approximately 140 studies were critically appraised to identify mechanistic insights and translational barriers. In this context, curcumin’s potential in preventing GC is explored, specifically regarding its potent anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, modulation of precancerous lesions, and anti-Helicobacter pylori activity. Furthermore, the review details curcumin’s mechanisms in treating established GC, including its ability to inhibit cancer cell proliferation, induce apoptosis (via pathways like PI3K/Akt/mTOR, NF-κB, STAT3), and exert anti-angiogenic effects. Crucially, its capacity to inhibit GC metastasis by reversing epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), suppressing matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), and modulating key signaling pathways involved in invasion and migration is highlighted. Additionally, curcumin’s role in overcoming chemoresistance is discussed. Despite its promising pleiotropic actions, the clinical translation of curcumin is significantly challenged by its poor bioavailability. Moreover, various strategies to enhance systemic absorption are addressed, emphasizing the critical need for well-designed clinical trials to validate its efficacy and safety in human GC patients. This review underscores curcumin’s potential as a valuable complementary or integrative therapeutic agent in gastric cancer management.

Introduction

Gastric cancer: epidemiology, etiology, and current challenges

Gastric cancer (GC), often referred to as stomach cancer, remains a formidable global health challenge, representing a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. Despite a decline in incidence in some Western nations, it continues to exert a significant burden, particularly in East Asia and parts of South America, where it is often diagnosed at advanced stages, contributing to its high mortality rates. The disease’s global impact is underscored by its persistent ranking among the top five cancers in terms of incidence and mortality, accounting for hundreds of thousands of deaths annually [1, 2].

The etiology of GC is complex and multifactorial, involving an intricate interplay of environmental, lifestyle, and genetic factors. A pivotal risk factor is chronic infection with Helicobacter pylori, a bacterium strongly implicated in initiating and promoting gastric carcinogenesis. Prolonged H. pylori infection can lead to chronic gastritis, which, if left untreated, may progress through a cascade of precancerous lesions including chronic atrophic gastritis and intestinal metaplasia, ultimately increasing the risk of GC development [3, 4]. Dietary habits also play a crucial role, with high consumption of salted, smoked, and preserved foods, as well as low intake of fresh fruits and vegetables, being consistently associated with an elevated risk. Other contributing factors include tobacco smoking, excessive alcohol consumption, obesity, certain genetic predispositions (e.g., hereditary diffuse gastric cancer), and a family history of the disease [5, 6].

Current diagnostic strategies for GC typically involve endoscopy with biopsy, often followed by imaging techniques for staging. While early detection significantly improves prognosis, a substantial proportion of patients are diagnosed with advanced or metastatic disease, limiting curative options. The cornerstone of therapeutic approaches for localized GC is surgical resection, often combined with perioperative chemotherapy or chemoradiation. For advanced or metastatic GC, systemic therapies, including chemotherapy, targeted agents (e.g., HER2-targeted therapies), and increasingly, immunotherapies (e.g., PD-1 inhibitors), are employed to extend survival and improve quality of life [6, 7]. However, these treatments are frequently associated with considerable limitations. Patients often develop resistance to conventional chemotherapy, experience severe side effects that compromise their quality of life, and face high rates of disease recurrence and metastasis even after initially successful treatment. The aggressive nature of GC, coupled with the limitations of current therapies, underscores an urgent need for novel, more effective, and less toxic therapeutic strategies, thereby fueling a growing interest in exploring the potential of natural compounds for both cancer prevention and adjunctive therapy [7, 8].

Curcumin: source, chemistry, and traditional uses

Curcumin, a vibrant yellow pigment, is the principal curcuminoid found in the rhizome of Curcuma longa, a perennial herbaceous plant commonly known as turmeric. This spice, a staple in South Asian cuisine, has been revered for centuries not only for its distinctive flavor and color but also for its profound medicinal properties within various traditional healing systems, most notably Ayurvedic and Traditional Chinese Medicine [9, 10]. Chemically, curcumin is a polyphenolic compound with the systematic name (1E,6E)-1,7-bis(4-hydroxy-3-methoxyphenyl) hepta-1,6-diene-3,5-dione. Its unique chemical structure features two aromatic ring systems, each containing a hydroxyl and a methoxy substituent, linked by a seven-carbon chain with two α, β-unsaturated carbonyl groups. This diketone moiety is crucial for many of its biological activities. While curcumin is the most abundant and well-studied curcuminoid, turmeric also contains two other closely related derivatives: dimethoxy-curcumin and bisdemethoxycurcumin, which collectively contribute to the plant’s overall bioactivity [9–11].

Historically, turmeric, and by extension its active component curcumin, has been extensively employed for a wide array of therapeutic purposes. Its traditional applications span from addressing inflammatory conditions, such as arthritis and digestive disorders, to its use as an antioxidant and a wound-healing agent. It has also been utilized for its antiseptic properties and in the management of various skin ailments. The long history of its use in traditional medicine attests to its perceived efficacy and a generally favorable safety profile when consumed in typical dietary amounts. This extensive traditional background has provided a strong foundation for modern scientific inquiry into its pharmacological potential, particularly in the context of complex diseases like cancer [11–13].

The ongoing battle against gastric cancer necessitates a relentless pursuit of innovative and effective strategies, particularly given the limitations of existing treatments and the high rates of recurrence and metastasis. Despite significant advancements in surgical techniques, chemotherapy, and targeted therapies, many patients still face poor prognoses, highlighting an urgent need for novel interventions that can either complement conventional approaches or potentially serve as alternative therapeutic options upon further clinical validation. While individual studies have explored specific aspects of curcumin’s impact on gastric cancer, a comprehensive narrative review that synthesizes its potential roles across the entire spectrum (from prevention and treatment to the crucial inhibition of metastasis) is currently lacking. This review aims to bridge this gap by compiling and critically discussing the current preclinical and early clinical findings. We seek to illuminate the diverse mechanisms through which curcumin might exert its beneficial effects in the gastric cancer landscape, identify existing challenges, particularly concerning its bioavailability, and highlight promising avenues for future research and clinical translation. By consolidating this knowledge, we hope to provide a valuable resource for researchers, clinicians, and those interested in the potential of natural compounds in oncology.

Materials and methods

This study was designed as a narrative review to synthesize and critically discuss current evidence regarding the therapeutic potential of curcumin in gastric cancer prevention, treatment, and metastasis suppression. A narrative approach was adopted to facilitate the comprehensive integration of mechanistic, preclinical, and early clinical findings across diverse experimental models and translational contexts.

A comprehensive literature search was performed using major electronic databases, including PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. The search encompassed studies published up to 2025, with priority given to literature from 2020 to 2025 to ensure coverage of emerging mechanisms, delivery strategies, and translational insights.

Search terms were used individually and in combination using Boolean operators. Key terms included: “curcumin,” “gastric cancer,” “stomach cancer,” “stomach neoplasms,” “molecular pathways,” “signaling pathways,” “metastasis,” “apoptosis,” “angiogenesis,” “chemoresistance,” “drug resistance,” “bioavailability,” “nanoparticles,” “nanoformulations,” and “combination therapy.” Reference lists of included articles were manually screened to identify additional pertinent studies.

Eligibility criteria encompassed studies investigating the effects of curcumin, its analogs, or novel formulations across a spectrum of gastric cancer models, ranging from in vitro cell lines to human clinical subjects. To ensure a thorough mechanistic evaluation, research exploring molecular pathways relevant to carcinogenesis, tumor progression, and chemoresistance was prioritized. The selection process also incorporated studies focusing on bioavailability-enhancing strategies and advanced delivery systems, alongside those reporting preclinical or early clinical evidence of curcumin’s preventive, therapeutic, or adjunctive efficacy. Conversely, articles were excluded if they pertained to non-gastric malignancies, appeared as conference abstracts without full datasets, or were published as editorials, letters, commentaries, or non-peer-reviewed materials lacking specific mechanistic or translational relevance.

Pharmacological Properties and and Therapeutic Potential

Anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects

The development of gastric cancer (GC) is frequently a protracted process, often initiated or significantly promoted by chronic inflammation within the gastric mucosa. Conditions such as long-standing Helicobacter pylori infection, autoimmune gastritis, and chronic exposure to irritants create a persistent inflammatory milieu. This sustained inflammation is characterized by the infiltration of immune cells, release of pro-inflammatory mediators, and generation of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, all of which contribute to cellular damage, increased cell turnover, and an environment conducive to malignant transformation. The “inflammation-cancer axis” is thus a well-established pathway in gastric carcinogenesis, making anti-inflammatory interventions a rational strategy for prevention [14, 15].

Curcumin stands out for its potent and multifaceted anti-inflammatory properties. Its efficacy stems from its ability to directly modulate numerous signaling pathways and molecular targets critical to the inflammatory cascade. A primary mechanism involves the suppression of nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB), a master regulator of inflammatory and immune responses. By inhibiting NF-κB activation, curcumin effectively downregulates the expression of a vast array of pro-inflammatory genes, including those encoding cytokines such as interleukin-1 beta (IL-1), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) [15, 16]. These cytokines are well-known drivers of chronic inflammation and cellular proliferation in the gastric environment. Furthermore, curcumin has been shown to inhibit the activity of key enzymes involved in prostaglandin and leukotriene synthesis, specifically cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) and lipoxygenase (LOX), both of which are often overexpressed in inflammatory and cancerous conditions and contribute to tumor growth and survival. By dampening these inflammatory pathways, curcumin can potentially interrupt the chronic inflammatory cycle that fosters gastric carcinogenesis [16, 17].

Beyond its anti-inflammatory actions, curcumin also possesses remarkable antioxidant capabilities, which are equally crucial for its chemo-preventive potential. Oxidative stress, arising from an imbalance between the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the body’s ability to detoxify them, is a significant contributor to DNA damage, protein modification, and lipid peroxidation, all of which can initiate or promote carcinogenesis. Curcumin acts as a direct scavenger of various free radicals, including superoxide radicals, hydroxyl radicals, and nitric oxide radicals, thereby neutralizing their damaging effects [17, 18]. Moreover, it enhances the activity of endogenous antioxidant defense systems. It can upregulate the expression of critical antioxidant enzymes such as glutathione S-transferases (GSTs), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase, bolstering the cellular capacity to detoxify harmful oxidants. By mitigating oxidative DNA damage and protecting cellular components from oxidative insults, curcumin contributes significantly to maintaining genomic stability and preventing the accumulation of mutations that could lead to gastric cancer development. The combined effect of reducing chronic inflammation and combating oxidative stress positions curcumin as a promising natural agent for gastric cancer prevention [17–19].

Modulation of precancerous lesions

The progression of gastric cancer (GC) is often characterized by a sequential development from chronic inflammation to well-defined precancerous lesions, serving as critical intermediate steps towards overt malignancy. These lesions typically include chronic atrophic gastritis (CAG), marked by the loss of gastric glands, and intestinal metaplasia (IM), where gastric epithelial cells are replaced by intestinal-type epithelium. Both CAG and IM are recognized as significant risk factors for GC development, representing a window of opportunity for chemo-preventive interventions [19, 20].

Curcumin has garnered attention for its potential to modulate these precancerous changes, with some preclinical models suggesting the possibility of arresting or even partially reversing their progression. Preclinical studies, primarily utilizing in vitro models of gastric epithelial cells and in vivo animal models of gastritis and precancerous lesions, have provided insights into its mechanisms. Curcumin’s anti-inflammatory effects, as discussed in the previous section, are inherently linked to its ability to influence these lesions. By reducing the chronic inflammatory burden that perpetuates cellular damage and abnormal repair, curcumin can theoretically mitigate the progression from simple gastritis to more advanced atrophic and metaplastic changes [20, 21].

Furthermore, curcumin’s influence extends to cellular proliferation and differentiation, which are dysregulated in precancerous states. Research suggests that curcumin can exert anti-proliferative effects on aberrantly growing cells within atrophic and metaplastic gastric mucosa, potentially by inducing cell cycle arrest or promoting apoptosis in these compromised cells. Its ability to modulate various signaling pathways, such as those involving NF-κB, Wnt/β-catenin, and STAT3, which are often dysregulated in precancerous lesions, contributes to this regulatory capacity [21–24].

While the bulk of evidence comes from preclinical studies, there has been nascent interest in exploring curcumin’s effects in human subjects. For instance, some early clinical investigations have explored whether curcumin supplementation can impact biomarkers associated with inflammation and cellular changes in individuals with precancerous gastric conditions. Notably, investigations have looked into its effect on inflammatory markers such as IL-1, which is highly relevant in H. pylori-induced inflammatory processes and gastric lesion progression [23–25]. These early studies, though often small in scale, aim to determine if curcumin can indeed slow down or, ideally, reverse the histological abnormalities characteristic of CAG and IM, thereby offering a promising avenue for reducing GC incidence in high-risk populations. The potential for curcumin to exert epigenetic modulation, such as influencing histone acetylation/deacetylation patterns, also offers a sophisticated mechanism by which it might restore more normal gene expression profiles in precancerous cells, further highlighting its multifaceted approach to chemoprevention [25, 26].

Anti-H. pylori activity

Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection is unequivocally recognized as the strongest risk factor for gastric cancer (GC), responsible for a significant proportion of cases worldwide. This Gram-negative bacterium colonizes the gastric mucosa, inducing chronic inflammation (gastritis) that, over decades, can progress through a series of precancerous lesions to malignancy. Therefore, effective eradication of H. pylori is a cornerstone of GC prevention strategies. However, increasing antibiotic resistance and patient non-compliance to multi-drug regimens pose significant challenges to current eradication therapies [26, 27].

In this context, curcumin has emerged as a compelling natural compound with demonstrated anti-H. pylori activity, offering a potential complementary or alternative approach to managing this critical risk factor. In vitro studies have consistently shown that curcumin can directly inhibit the growth of various H. pylori strains, including those resistant to [27, 28]. Its antimicrobial effects are believed to stem from multiple mechanisms, including disruption of the bacterial cell membrane, inhibition of bacterial enzymes essential for survival, and interference with bacterial adhesion to gastric epithelial cells. Furthermore, curcumin’s ability to reduce H. pylori urease activity, an enzyme crucial for the bacterium’s survival in the acidic gastric environment, has also been reported [28–30].

Beyond its direct bactericidal or bacteriostatic effects, curcumin’s impact on H. pylori-associated gastric pathology extends to mitigating the host inflammatory response. As detailed previously, curcumin effectively downregulates key pro-inflammatory mediators and signaling pathways (e.g., NF-κB, COX-2, IL-8) that are activated by H. pylori infection. By simultaneously reducing the bacterial load and dampening the associated inflammation, curcumin can potentially break the vicious cycle of H. pylori-induced chronic gastritis and its progression towards precancerous lesions and ultimately GC [30–32]. While promising, most of the evidence for curcumin’s anti-H. pylori activity comes from in vitro and animal studies. Clinical trials investigating curcumin as a standalone treatment for H. pylori eradication or as an adjunct to standard antibiotic regimens are still limited but represent a crucial area for future research. If validated in human trials, curcumin could offer a valuable natural intervention to reduce H. pylori burden and its associated inflammatory sequelae, thereby contributing to the prevention of gastric cancer [29–31].

Inhibition of cancer cell proliferation and induction of apoptosis

A defining characteristic of cancer is uncontrolled cellular proliferation, driven by dysregulated cell cycle progression and resistance to programmed cell death (apoptosis). In the context of gastric cancer (GC), a key therapeutic objective is to halt this aberrant growth and induce the selective elimination of malignant cells. Curcumin has been extensively investigated for its profound ability to achieve these very outcomes in various GC cell lines and preclinical models [30–32].

Curcumin exerts significant anti-proliferative effects by interfering with the cell cycle machinery of GC cells. It has been shown to induce cell cycle arrest at different phases, most commonly in the G0/G1 phase or S phase [33, 34]. This arrest prevents cancer cells from proceeding through the division cycle, thereby limiting their unchecked expansion. The underlying mechanisms involve the modulation of key cell cycle regulators, such as cyclins (e.g., cyclin D1, cyclin E) and cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), often by increasing the expression of CDK inhibitors like p21 and p27. By disrupting the precise orchestration of the cell cycle, curcumin effectively puts a brake on the proliferative drive of gastric cancer cells. Beyond simply halting proliferation, a cornerstone of curcumin’s anti-cancer efficacy lies in its robust capacity to induce apoptosis, a form of programmed cell death crucial for eliminating damaged or transformed cells. In GC cells, curcumin orchestrates apoptosis through multiple convergent pathways. A prominent mechanism involves the modulation of the intrinsic (mitochondrial) apoptotic pathway. Curcumin often shifts the balance of pro-apoptotic and anti-apoptotic proteins within the Bcl-2 family, typically by upregulating pro-apoptotic members like Bax and downregulating anti-apoptotic members such as Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL. This perturbation leads to mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization, releasing cytochrome c into the cytoplasm, which in turn activates caspases, the executioners of apoptosis. Specifically, curcumin has been shown to activate initiator caspases (e.g., caspase-8, involved in the extrinsic pathway, and caspase-9, involved in the intrinsic pathway) and effector caspases (e.g., caspase-3), leading to the proteolytic degradation of cellular components and ultimately cell demise [34–37].

Furthermore, curcumin’s pro-apoptotic effects are intricately linked to its broad impact on various survival and signaling pathways that are often aberrantly activated in GC. It has been observed to inhibit the hyperactive PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, a central regulator of cell growth, survival, and metabolism in many cancers. Similarly, curcumin can modulate the MAPK (mitogen-activated protein kinase) pathway components, including ERK, JNK, and p38. Inhibition of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, frequently hyperactive in GC, by curcumin also contributes to its anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic actions [38, 39]. Moreover, its ability to suppress the constitutive activation of NF-κB and JAK/STAT3 (Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3) pathways, both critical for cancer cell survival, inflammation, and immune evasion, further underscores its comprehensive apoptotic induction capabilities. Emerging evidence also suggests that curcumin may induce ferroptosis, a distinct form of regulated cell death characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, in certain gastric cancer cell contexts, adding another dimension to its cell-killing repertoire. These multifaceted mechanisms collectively demonstrate curcumin’s significant potential as an agent for directly targeting and eliminating gastric cancer cells [39–41]. Anti-angiogenic effects

For any solid tumor, including gastric cancer (GC), to grow beyond a minimal size (typically 1–2 mm3) and metastasize, it critically depends on the formation of new blood vessels from pre-existing ones, a process known as angiogenesis. This newly formed vascular network supplies the tumor with essential oxygen and nutrients, removes metabolic waste products, and provides a conduit for cancer cells to escape into the circulation, initiating metastasis. Consequently, inhibiting tumor angiogenesis has emerged as a fundamental strategy in cancer therapy, aiming to starve the tumor and restrict its dissemination [41–43].

Curcumin has been extensively investigated for its significant anti-angiogenic properties in various cancer models, including those of gastric cancer. Its ability to disrupt the angiogenic switch in tumors is multifaceted, primarily involving the direct or indirect modulation of key pro-angiogenic factors and their associated signaling pathways. A primary target of curcumin’s anti-angiogenic action is Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF), a potent growth factor that plays a central role in stimulating endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and tube formation – the hallmarks of angiogenesis. Research indicates that curcumin can significantly downregulate the expression of VEGF and its receptors (VEGFRs) in gastric cancer cells and tumor microenvironments. This reduction in VEGF signaling impairs the tumor’s capacity to solicit new blood vessel growth [44, 45].

Beyond VEGF, curcumin’s anti-angiogenic effects extend to other critical molecular targets and pathways:

HIF-1α (Hypoxia-inducible factor-1 alpha): In hypoxic tumor environments, HIF-1α becomes stabilized and promotes the transcription of numerous genes involved in angiogenesis, including VEGF. Preclinical studies, both in vitro (e.g., using human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) in tube formation assays) and in vivo (e.g., chick chorio-allantoic membrane (CAM) assays or tumor xenograft models in mice), have consistently demonstrated curcumin’s ability to inhibit these angiogenic processes in GC. By selectively targeting the tumor’s lifeline, curcumin offers a promising strategy to restrict tumor growth, limit its nutrient supply, and ultimately impede its ability to metastasize, complementing its other direct anti-cancer effects [50–52].

Reversing chemoresistance and radio-sensitization

Despite advances in systemic therapies, chemoresistance remains a major hurdle in the effective management of advanced gastric cancer (GC), leading to treatment failure and disease progression. GC cells often develop resistance to widely used chemotherapeutic agents such as 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), cisplatin, and paclitaxel through various complex mechanisms. These mechanisms can vary widely, ranging from enhanced drug efflux, activation of cell survival pathways, impaired apoptotic signaling, and alterations in drug metabolism to even changes in microRNA expression that affect drug response [53]. A compelling aspect of curcumin’s therapeutic potential lies in its ability to circumvent or reverse chemoresistance, thereby enhancing the efficacy of conventional agents [54, 55].

Preclinical studies, both in vitro using resistant GC cell lines and in vivo in animal models, have provided substantial evidence that curcumin can act as a potent chemosensitizer. For instance, co-administration of curcumin with 5-FU or cisplatin has been shown to significantly improve the cytotoxic effects of these drugs on GC cells, leading to increased cell death and reduced tumor growth compared to chemotherapy alone. Modulation of drug efflux pumps: One common mechanism of resistance involves the overexpression of ABC (ATP-binding cassette) transporters, such as P-glycoprotein (P-gp/MDR1), which actively pump drugs out of cancer cells [56]. While the data on curcumin’s direct radiosensitizing effects specifically in gastric cancer are more limited compared to its chemosensitizing potential, the general principles apply. Radiosensitization refers to the enhancement of radiation therapy’s effectiveness. Given curcumin’s known abilities to induce cell cycle arrest, promote apoptosis, inhibit pro-survival pathways (like NF-κB, which can be activated by radiation and promote resistance), and its antioxidant properties that can sometimes balance radiation-induced damage, it holds theoretical promise as a radiosensitizer. However, more focused research using GC models is needed to fully elucidate and confirm these specific effects in the context of radiation therapy for gastric cancer [59–61]. Nevertheless, its established capacity to overcome chemoresistance makes it a highly appealing candidate for inclusion in combination therapies to improve the overall efficacy of current gastric cancer treatments. Immunomodulatory effects

The tumor microenvironment (TME) in gastric cancer, as in many malignancies, is a complex ecosystem that plays a critical role in tumor progression, immune evasion, and therapeutic response. Within this environment, a dysfunctional immune landscape often prevails, characterized by the presence of immunosuppressive cells (e.g., regulatory T cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, tumor-associated macrophages) and pro-tumorigenic cytokines, which collectively dampen effective anti-tumor immunity [62, 63]. Curcumin, beyond its direct cytotoxic and anti-proliferative actions on cancer cells, has garnered significant attention for its remarkable capacity to modulate the immune system, potentially shifting the balance towards an anti-tumorigenic response [62, 63].

Curcumin’s immunomodulatory effects are multifaceted and can influence both innate and adaptive immune components. It has been shown to enhance the activity of various immune cells critical for tumor surveillance and elimination. For instance, studies indicate that curcumin can augment the cytotoxic functions of natural killer (NK) cells and cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), which are crucial for directly recognizing and destroying cancer cells. It can also influence the differentiation and activation of dendritic cells (DCs), key antigen-presenting cells that initiate adaptive immune responses, thereby improving the presentation of tumor antigens to T cells [64–66].

Furthermore, curcumin can reprogram macrophages within the TME. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) often adopt an M2-like phenotype, which promotes tumor growth, angiogenesis, and immunosuppression. Curcumin has been shown to re-polarize these M2-like macrophages towards an M1-like phenotype, which is associated with anti-tumor activity and pro-inflammatory cytokine production. This re-education of macrophages can lead to a more hostile environment for tumor cells [67–69].

Crucially, curcumin influences the production of various cytokines and chemokines, helping to restore a more balanced immune profile. It can reduce the levels of immunosuppressive cytokines (e.g., IL-10, TGF-β) and increase the production of anti-tumor cytokines (e.g., IL-2, IFN-γ). By attenuating chronic inflammation (as discussed in Sect. 2.1) and modulating the immune cell repertoire, curcumin helps to dismantle the immunosuppressive barriers erected by gastric cancer cells, potentially allowing the host’s immune system to more effectively recognize and eliminate malignant cells [70–72]. While extensive in vivo and clinical validation specific to gastric cancer immune modulation is still evolving, these preclinical insights highlight curcumin’s potential as an immunomodulatory agent to enhance anti-tumor immunity within the complex gastric cancer microenvironment.

Inhibition of cell migration and invasion

Metastasis, the dissemination of cancer cells from the primary tumor to distant organs, is the leading cause of mortality in gastric cancer (GC) patients. This complex process involves a series of sequential steps, beginning with the detachment of cancer cells from the primary tumor, followed by their migration through the extracellular matrix (ECM) and invasion into surrounding tissues and blood vessels. A promising aspect of curcumin’s anti-cancer activity lies in its capacity to directly impede these critical steps of cell migration and invasion [73–75].

Curcumin exerts its anti-migratory and anti-invasive effects on GC cells through the modulation of several key molecular pathways and cellular components:

Modulation of adhesion molecules and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT): EMT is a crucial biological process where epithelial cells lose their cell-cell adhesion and apical-basal polarity and gain mesenchymal characteristics, thereby acquiring enhanced migratory and invasive capabilities. A hallmark of EMT is the downregulation of epithelial markers, such as E-cadherin, which is essential for cell-cell adhesion, and the upregulation of mesenchymal markers, such as N-cadherin and vimentin. Research indicates that curcumin can reverse or inhibit this transition in GC cells by restoring E-cadherin expression and/or suppressing the expression of mesenchymal markers and key EMT-inducing transcription factors (e.g., Snail, Slug, Twist). By maintaining cellular adhesion and epithelial phenotype, curcumin restricts the detachment and subsequent migration of GC cells [76–78].Inhibition of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs): For cancer cells to invade through the basement membrane and connective tissues, they must degrade components of the extracellular matrix. Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), particularly gelatinases like MMP-2 and MMP-9, are a family of zinc-dependent endopeptidases that play pivotal roles in this proteolytic degradation. Overexpression and increased activity of MMPs are frequently observed in aggressive GC. Curcumin has been consistently shown to downregulate the expression and activity of MMP-2 and MMP-9 in GC cell lines and in vivo models. This inhibition can occur at the transcriptional level (by suppressing upstream activators like NF-κB) or by direct enzymatic inhibition, thereby significantly impairing the ability of GC cells to degrade the ECM and invade surrounding healthy tissues [76–78].Regulation of cell motility-related proteins: Cell migration is a dynamic process driven by intricate rearrangements of the cytoskeleton, particularly the actin filaments, and the formation and disassembly of focal adhesions. Curcumin has been found to interfere with these cellular machinery components. It can affect the polymerization of actin, the formation of stress fibers, and the proper assembly of focal adhesion complexes, which are essential for cell attachment, spreading, and directed movement. By disrupting the cytoskeletal dynamics and cell-matrix interactions, curcumin directly hinders the ability of GC cells to move and spread [76–78].

Targeting signaling pathways involved in metastasis

The metastatic cascade in gastric cancer (GC) is not merely a consequence of cell migration and invasion but is intricately orchestrated by dysregulated intracellular signaling pathways. These pathways drive the acquisition of metastatic phenotypes, including enhanced survival in circulation, extravasation, and colonization of secondary sites. Curcumin’s anti-metastatic prowess extends significantly to its ability to modulate these pivotal signaling networks [79–81].

A central player in GC metastasis is NF-κB (Nuclear Factor-kappa B). Constitutive activation of NF-κB promotes the expression of genes associated with cell survival, proliferation, angiogenesis, and crucially, metastasis, including adhesion molecules and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs). Curcumin effectively inhibits NF-κB activation, thereby downregulating its downstream targets that facilitate epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), invasion, and distant spread of GC cells [80–82].

Similarly, the STAT3 (signal transducer and activator of transcription 3) pathway is frequently aberrantly activated in GC, contributing to tumor progression and metastasis. Activated STAT3 promotes the transcription of genes involved in cell proliferation, survival, angiogenesis, and particularly, the expression of genes that facilitate invasion and metastasis. Curcumin has been shown to suppress STAT3 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation, consequently inhibiting the expression of STAT3-regulated genes, which directly contributes to its anti-migratory and anti-invasive effects [81, 82].

The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is another critical driver of GC development and metastasis. Aberrant activation of this pathway leads to the accumulation of β-catenin in the cytoplasm and nucleus, where it acts as a co-activator for target genes that promote cell proliferation, stemness, and metastatic behavior, including EMT-related genes. Curcumin has been found to inhibit the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, leading to reduced nuclear β-catenin levels and a subsequent decrease in the expression of its pro-metastatic target genes in GC cells [82–84].

Furthermore, HIF-1α (hypoxia-inducible factor-1 alpha), often stabilized under hypoxic conditions prevalent in tumor microenvironments, plays a key role in orchestrating adaptive responses that promote tumor angiogenesis and metastasis. HIF-1α upregulates genes encoding various pro-angiogenic factors (like VEGF) and enzymes that facilitate invasion. Curcumin has been shown to suppress HIF-1α expression and its transcriptional activity, thereby mitigating hypoxia-driven metastatic processes in GC [82–84].

Beyond these major protein-signaling pathways, curcumin also exerts its anti-metastatic effects through the modulation of microRNAs (miRNAs). miRNAs are small non-coding RNA molecules that regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally and are crucial players in cancer progression and metastasis. Curcumin has been demonstrated to alter the expression profiles of various miRNAs in GC cells [85, 86]. For instance, it can upregulate tumor-suppressive miRNAs (e.g., miR-146a, miR-200 family, miR-212) that target pro-metastatic genes and pathways, or downregulate oncogenic miRNAs (e.g., miR-21, miR-155) that promote invasion and migration. This ability to epigenetically regulate gene expression via miRNAs adds another sophisticated layer to curcumin’s comprehensive strategy against gastric cancer metastasis. By simultaneously targeting multiple interdependent signaling nodes, curcumin effectively disrupts the complex machinery that drives the metastatic spread of GC [87, 88]. Preclinical evidence for anti-metastatic activity

The molecular mechanisms and pathway modulations discussed in the preceding Sects. 4.1 and 4.2 provide a strong theoretical basis for curcumin’s anti-metastatic potential. Critically, these theoretical underpinnings have been substantially corroborated by a wealth of preclinical evidence derived from both in vitro cell culture experiments and in vivo animal models specifically designed to mimic gastric cancer (GC) metastasis [89, 90].

In vitro studies using various human gastric cancer cell lines (e.g., AGS, BGC-823, SGC-7901, MKN-45) have consistently demonstrated curcumin’s direct ability to inhibit the migratory and invasive capabilities of these malignant cells. Assays such as wound healing assays, Transwell invasion assays, and scratch migration tests have shown that curcumin significantly reduces the distance migrated by cells and their ability to penetrate artificial membranes, often in a dose-dependent manner. These experiments provide direct evidence that curcumin interferes with the cellular machinery required for movement and tissue infiltration [91, 92]. Furthermore, in vitro analyses have confirmed that this inhibition is mediated by the suppression of key pro-metastatic factors like MMP-2 and MMP-9, the upregulation of adhesion molecules such as E-cadherin, and the reversal of EMT markers, precisely reflecting the mechanistic insights discussed previously [91, 92].

The ultimate validation for anti-metastatic agents, however, comes from in vivo studies using animal models, typically immunocompromised mice bearing human GC xenografts. In these models, curcumin administration has shown compelling results in suppressing tumor dissemination and actual metastasis to distant organs. Researchers have employed various approaches, including:

Orthotopic models: Where GC cells are implanted directly into the gastric wall of mice, allowing the natural progression of the disease, including local invasion and distant metastasis (e.g., peritoneal dissemination, liver metastasis). In such models, curcumin treatment has been observed to significantly reduce the incidence and burden of metastatic lesions [93–95].Tail vein injection models: While not fully recapitulating primary tumor development, these models directly assess the ability of cancer cells to survive in circulation and colonize secondary sites. Curcumin treatment in these models has demonstrated a reduction in lung or liver metastases, indicating its efficacy in inhibiting later stages of the metastatic cascade [93–95].

Bioavailability issues

Despite the extensive and compelling preclinical evidence highlighting curcumin’s multifaceted therapeutic potential in gastric cancer, its translation into effective clinical applications has been significantly hampered by a fundamental pharmacological challenge: poor bioavailability. Bioavailability refers to the proportion of an administered substance that is absorbed and becomes available in the systemic circulation to exert its intended therapeutic effects at target tissues. For curcumin, this measure is remarkably low, consistently reported across various studies [97–99].

The reasons for this limited bioavailability are multifactorial and primarily stem from its physicochemical properties and rapid metabolism within the body:

Poor absorption: Curcumin is highly lipophilic (fat-soluble) and poorly soluble in aqueous solutions, including the gastrointestinal fluids. This inherent insolubility severely restricts its dissolution and subsequent absorption across the intestinal barrier after oral administration. Much of the ingested curcumin passes through the digestive tract unabsorbed [100–102].Rapid metabolism: Once absorbed, curcumin undergoes extensive and rapid metabolism, primarily in the liver and intestine. It is subjected to various enzymatic transformations, including glucuronidation and sulfation, which convert it into more water-soluble, but often less biologically active, metabolites that are quickly eliminated from the body [101–103].Rapid systemic elimination: Even the small fraction of unmetabolized curcumin that enters the systemic circulation is quickly cleared, leading to very low plasma concentrations and a short half-life [101–103].

The profound impact of this low bioavailability is that despite promising in vitro results and animal studies, achieving therapeutically relevant concentrations of bioactive curcumin within gastric tissues or at metastatic sites in humans through standard oral dosing becomes exceedingly difficult. The concentrations required to exert the robust anti-cancer effects observed in laboratory settings are often orders of magnitude higher than what can be attained in human plasma and tissues following conventional oral intake. This limitation significantly impedes curcumin’s potential as a standalone or even an effective adjunctive therapy for gastric cancer, necessitating innovative strategies to overcome this pharmacokinetic barrier to unlock its full clinical promise [104–106].

Strategies to enhance bioavailability

Recognizing the critical limitation posed by curcumin’s poor bioavailability, extensive research efforts have been dedicated to developing innovative strategies to improve its absorption, reduce its metabolism, and enhance its systemic and tissue-specific delivery. These approaches aim to achieve therapeutically effective concentrations of curcumin in the human body, thereby unlocking its full clinical potential in gastric cancer management [107–110].

Several promising strategies have emerged:

Co-administration with piperine: One of the most widely studied and commercially available approaches involves co-administering curcumin with piperine, an alkaloid derived from black pepper. Piperine is known to inhibit various drug-metabolizing enzymes, particularly those involved in glucuronidation in the liver and intestine. By inhibiting these enzymes, piperine reduces the rapid breakdown of curcumin, allowing more of the active compound to be absorbed and remain in its unmetabolized form in the systemic circulation. This simple co-formulation has shown several-fold increases in curcumin’s bioavailability [107–110].Liposomal formulations: Liposomes are spherical vesicles composed of lipid bilayers that can encapsulate both hydrophilic and lipophilic compounds. Encapsulating curcumin within liposomes can significantly improve its aqueous solubility, protect it from degradation in the gastrointestinal tract, and enhance its absorption. Liposomal delivery systems can also potentially facilitate targeted delivery to tumor sites by leveraging the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, where nanoparticles preferentially accumulate in tumor tissues due to their leaky vasculature [107–110].Nanoparticle delivery systems: Beyond liposomes, a diverse array of other nanoparticle-based delivery systems has been explored. These include:Polymeric nanoparticles: Utilizing biodegradable polymers to encapsulate curcumin, offering controlled release and improved stability [111–113].Solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs) and nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs): These systems use solid lipids as carriers, providing high drug loading capacity, protection from degradation, and enhanced absorption through lymphatic pathways [111–113].Micelles: Formed by amphiphilic molecules, micelles can solubilize hydrophobic curcumin in their core, improving its dissolution and absorption. These nano-formulations offer advantages such as improved solubility, enhanced stability, protection from metabolism, and the potential for passive or active targeting to cancer cells [111–114].Phospholipid complexes (phytosomes): This strategy involves complexing curcumin with phospholipids (e.g., phosphatidylcholine). This forms a lipid-compatible molecular complex that can more readily cross biological membranes, leading to improved absorption and enhanced bioavailability. The phytosome technology aims to mimic the natural absorption of dietary fats [115–117].Structural modifications and analogs: Researchers are also exploring the synthesis of novel curcumin derivatives or analogs with modified chemical structures. The goal is to design curcumin-like molecules that are inherently more bioavailable and potent [115–117].Self-emulsifying drug delivery systems (SEDDS): These are isotropic mixtures of oils, surfactants, and co-solvents that form fine oil-in-water emulsions upon gentle agitation in aqueous media (like gastrointestinal fluids). SEDDS can significantly enhance the dissolution and absorption of poorly soluble drugs like curcumin by presenting them in a finely dispersed, readily absorbable form [115–117].

Clinical translation and current clinical trials

Despite the voluminous and compelling preclinical data supporting curcumin’s efficacy against gastric cancer (GC) in various in vitro and in vivo models, its translation into robust, widespread clinical application remains largely in its nascent stages. The primary hurdle, as discussed, has been its inherently poor bioavailability, which makes achieving therapeutically relevant concentrations in human tissues challenging with conventional oral dosing. Consequently, the number of completed and published clinical trials specifically focusing on curcumin’s effects in human GC patients is relatively limited when compared to the vast preclinical literature [118–121].

Early clinical investigations involving curcumin in cancer generally, and occasionally in gastrointestinal cancers, have primarily focused on its safety and pharmacokinetics, confirming its excellent tolerability even at high doses (several grams per day), with only mild and transient side effects. However, these trials often highlight the difficulty in detecting significant levels of unmetabolized curcumin in plasma or target tissues without advanced formulations [118–121]. For gastric cancer specifically, clinical trials exploring curcumin’s role are typically in early phases (Phase I or II) and often investigate its potential as an adjunct to standard therapies or as a chemopreventive agent in high-risk populations. For instance, some ongoing or recently completed trials might explore curcumin’s ability to modulate inflammatory biomarkers (like IL-1) or prevent the progression of precancerous gastric lesions (e.g., chronic atrophic gastritis or intestinal metaplasia), particularly in regions with high GC incidence. The National Cancer Institute (NCI) has previously sponsored trials examining curcumin’s role in inflammation and cancer prevention, some of which may include gastric indications or relevant biomarkers [122–124]. Combination therapy

Given the complex and heterogeneous nature of gastric cancer (GC), a single therapeutic agent is often insufficient to achieve durable responses, particularly in advanced stages. The current paradigm frequently involves multimodal approaches, combining surgery with chemotherapy, radiation, targeted therapies, or immunotherapies. In this context, curcumin’s multifaceted biological activities and its generally favorable safety profile make it an attractive candidate for combination therapy, aiming to enhance therapeutic efficacy, overcome drug resistance, and potentially mitigate the side effects of conventional treatments [125–127].

The rationale for combining curcumin with standard GC therapies is rooted in its ability to target multiple pathways simultaneously, many of which are distinct from or complementary to those targeted by conventional drugs. This pleiotropic action allows curcumin to:

Enhance chemotherapeutic efficacy: As discussed in Sect. 3.3, preclinical studies have extensively demonstrated curcumin’s capacity to chemosensitize GC cells to agents like 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), cisplatin, and paclitaxel. By inhibiting drug efflux pumps, suppressing pro-survival pathways (e.g., NF-κB, PI3K/Akt/mTOR), and augmenting apoptotic signaling, curcumin can lower the effective dose of chemotherapy required to achieve a desired cytotoxic effect, potentially reducing dose-limiting toxicities. This synergistic interaction has been observed in various in vitro and in vivo models of GC [128–131].Overcome drug resistance: A major challenge in GC treatment is the development of resistance to chemotherapy. Curcumin’s ability to modulate multiple resistance mechanisms, including those involving ABC transporters and survival pathways, positions it as a valuable adjunct to re-sensitize resistant GC cells to standard agents, thereby prolonging treatment effectiveness [128–131].Reduce side effects and improve quality of life: Conventional chemotherapy and radiation therapy are often associated with severe systemic toxicities that significantly impact patients’ quality of life. Curcumin’s well-documented anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties could potentially mitigate some of these adverse effects, such as mucositis, nausea, and myelosuppression, although more clinical evidence is needed specifically in GC patients undergoing these treatments. By reducing treatment-related toxicities, curcumin could enable patients to tolerate higher doses or longer durations of conventional therapy, leading to better outcomes [128–131].Synergy with targeted therapies and immunotherapies: While less explored in GC, curcumin’s ability to modulate various signaling pathways (e.g., HER2, EGFR, STAT3) and influence the tumor microenvironment suggests potential synergy with targeted agents or immunotherapies. The concept of combination therapy with curcumin is particularly appealing for GC, where aggressive disease often necessitates aggressive multi-agent regimens. By acting as a sensitizer, resistance modulator, and potential toxicity reducer, curcumin holds significant promise to improve the therapeutic index of existing gastric cancer treatments, offering a more comprehensive and potentially less toxic approach to disease management. However, well-designed clinical trials are crucial to validate these preclinical findings and establish optimal combination regimens and dosing strategies in human GC patients.

Safety and toxicity

A significant advantage of curcumin in the context of its therapeutic potential for gastric cancer, and indeed for various health conditions, is its generally favorable safety profile. Curcumin has been consumed for centuries as a dietary spice and traditional medicine, and is widely regarded as safe (GRAS designation by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration) when consumed at typical dietary levels [132–134].

However, when administered at the higher doses often explored in preclinical and clinical studies for therapeutic purposes, some side effects have been reported, though these are typically mild and transient. The most commonly noted adverse effects involve the gastrointestinal tract, including nausea, diarrhea, dyspepsia, and abdominal discomfort. Other less frequent but reported side effects include headaches, skin rashes, and yellow stool. These effects are generally dose-dependent, meaning they are more likely to occur at higher therapeutic dosages [132–134].

While rare, there have been isolated reports of potential liver injury (elevated liver enzymes) associated with very high doses of curcumin, though a definitive causal link is still under investigation and often associated with specific formulations or pre-existing conditions. It is crucial to monitor liver function in patients undergoing high-dose curcumin therapy, especially in clinical trial settings. Another important consideration for clinical application is the potential for drug interactions. Curcumin has been shown to modulate drug-metabolizing enzymes (e.g., cytochrome P450 enzymes) and drug transporters (e.g., P-glycoprotein). This enzymatic modulation means curcumin could theoretically alter the metabolism and efficacy of co-administered medications. Specific potential interactions include:

Limitations and challenges

Despite the substantial body of preclinical evidence supporting curcumin’s therapeutic potential in gastric cancer, several important limitations must be acknowledged when interpreting these findings. First, the heterogeneity of curcumin formulations (including free curcumin, structural analogs, micelles, liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, solid lipid nanoparticles, and nanoemulsions) creates significant variability in pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles. These systems differ widely in their solubility, stability, absorption, biodistribution, and cellular uptake, making direct comparison across studies difficult and limiting conclusions about optimal delivery strategies for clinical translation.

Second, many reported in vivo studies were performed with small sample sizes, often without explicit randomization or blinded outcome assessment, increasing the risk of selection and observer bias. Such methodological weaknesses reduce the reliability of preclinical efficacy signals and may overestimate therapeutic benefit. Furthermore, a substantial proportion of studies rely on single-laboratory models without independent replication, which raises concerns about reproducibility and robustness across experimental settings.

Third, considerable heterogeneity in experimental models exists. Studies employ diverse GC cell lines with distinct molecular backgrounds, and animal models vary across subcutaneous, orthotopic, and metastatic systems, each with different physiological relevance. These discrepancies complicate cross-study comparison and challenge the generalization of mechanistic conclusions.

Fourth, many investigations lack well-defined dose–response analyses or use dosing regimens that are unlikely to be clinically achievable due to curcumin’s inherently low bioavailability. High-dose administrations in animal models (often far exceeding human-equivalent doses) limit the clinical translatability of observed anti-tumor effects. Additionally, very few studies include comprehensive pharmacokinetic assessments or tissue distribution analyses, which are essential for determining whether therapeutically relevant concentrations are achieved in gastric tissue.

Fifth, the potential for herb-drug interactions poses a significant safety concern, particularly when curcumin is utilized as an adjuvant to conventional chemotherapy. As discussed, curcumin is known to modulate the activity of drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters. These interactions could inadvertently alter the metabolism, systemic exposure, and toxicity profiles of co-administered chemotherapeutic agents, underscoring the need for careful pharmacological monitoring in combination regimens. Finally, a distinct translational gap persists; while preclinical models consistently demonstrate robust tumor suppression, outcomes in human clinical trials have been more modest, largely attributed to rapid systemic elimination and insufficient tissue accumulation within the gastric mucosa.

Altogether, these limitations highlight the need for standardized experimental design, rigorous methodological practices, and harmonized reporting across curcumin studies. Addressing these issues will be critical to advancing curcumin-based therapeutics toward meaningful clinical evaluation in gastric cancer.

Future directions and recommendations

To facilitate the translation of curcumin from preclinical research to clinical practice in gastric cancer management, the research community should focus on several prioritized areas. High priority must be given to conducting well-controlled Phase II and III clinical trials that utilize advanced, bioavailable curcumin formulations, such as nano-micelles or phospholipid complexes, specifically testing them as adjuvants to standard chemotherapy or as chemoprevention strategies in high-risk groups. Of medium priority is the exploration of potential synergies between curcumin and modern oncological therapeutics, including immunotherapies like immune checkpoint inhibitors or targeted agents like anti-HER2 therapies, to overcome tumor resistance. Furthermore, important mechanistic research should delve into under-investigated areas, particularly the role of curcumin in modulating the gut microbiome and its subsequent impact on gastric carcinogenesis and the systemic immune response.

Conclusion

In conclusion, curcumin presents as a promising natural compound with anti-cancer activities against gastric cancer, targeting multiple hallmarks of malignancy. While the journey from bench to bedside is ongoing, particularly for robust clinical validation in humans, the continuous development of advanced delivery systems and the growing understanding of its complex molecular interactions suggests that curcumin may serve as a valuable candidate for future complementary or integrative therapeutic strategies in gastric cancer management. Its potential to enhance efficacy, overcome resistance, and perhaps mitigate toxicity of conventional treatments warrants persistent and rigorous clinical investigation, moving towards a more comprehensive and personalized approach to combating this challenging disease.
