Integrated Multi-Omics Profiling Identifies an Immunotherapy Vulnerable and Prognostic Associated Subtype in Cholangiocarcinoma
Authors: Lu Chen, Yuchao He, Hong Su, Xiangdong Tian, Haotian Liao, Dongming Liu, Meng Zhao, Huichuan Sun, Lisha Qi, Manqing Cao, Zhiyong Liu, Lin Guo, Guangtao Li, Chenhao Zhou, Yi Luo, Liwei Chen, Qiang Wu, Yunlong Cui, Huikai Li, Xiaolin Zhu, Feng Fang, Wei Zhang, Shaojun Zhang, Tianqiang Song, Hua Guo

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCholangiocarcinoma (CCA) is an aggressive malignancy with poor prognosis and limited treatment options. Molecular heterogeneity and the tumor immune microenvironment play crucial roles in CCA progression and therapeutic response, but comprehensive stratification remains insufficient for guiding treatment decisions.MethodsThis study performed integrated multi-omics profiling as well as immunohistochemical validation on 424 CCA patients. Multidimensional data, including genomic, epigenomic, transcriptomic, and immune profiling, were analyzed to identify clinically relevant molecular subtypes.ResultsFour distinct molecular subtypes of CCA were identified, each exhibiting unique clinical and immunological features: C1 (proliferation): driven by TP53/KRAS mutations and CpG island methylator phenotype (CIMP+) hypermethylation, showing T helper 17 cells infiltration and poor outcomes; C2 (Immune-Suppressed Macro_LYVE1 [ISM_LYVE1]): stroma-rich with LYVE1+ macrophages and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) activation; C3 (immune-activated Macro_C1QC (IAM_C1QC)): enriched in C1QC+ macrophages, CD8+ T-cells, and metabolic pathways, highly responsive to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB; 75% overall response rate); C4 (immune exclusion): FGFR2-altered and IDH1-mutant, with an immunologically cold phenotype.ConclusionWe validated ATP2B1 as a novel prognostic biomarker and developed a 160-gene classifier for subtype prediction. The C3 subtype’s exceptional ICB response, independent of conventional biomarkers (PD-L1/microsatellite instability/tumor mutational burden), highlights the clinical utility of this classification system in guiding precision immunotherapy for CCA.

Introduction

Cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) is one of the most aggressive malignancies, and its incidence has drastically increased representing approximately 2% of all cancer-related deaths worldwide annually [1]. CCA is categorized into three subtypes according to different anatomical locations: intrahepatic (ICC), perihilar (PHC), and distal (DCC) [2]. Radical surgical resection remains the primary curative treatment; however, the number of patients who benefit from this surgery is limited [3]. Gemcitabine combined with cisplatin and immunotherapy is the first-line standard treatment regimen for recurrent or advanced CCA. However, the high heterogeneity of CCA at the multi-omics and histopathological levels severely limits the effectiveness of chemotherapy [4]. Local recurrence and distant metastasis contribute to poor prognosis; nearly 50% of postoperative CCA patients experience recurrence within 12 months, and the 5-year survival rate is less than 10% [5]. Therefore, a CCA molecular characterization scheme is urgently needed to aid molecular subtyping, the discovery of candidate biomarkers, and the acceleration of individualized therapy development.

To date, large-scale genomic, transcriptomic, and epigenetic studies of ICC and extrahepatic CCA have partly revealed a panoramic view of CCA [6, 7]. The integrated genetic characterization of CCA remains unexplored, particularly regarding differences among anatomical subtypes. Additionally, CCA subclassification based on clinical application and molecular profiling with immune characterization has not been performed [6, 8]. Thus, immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy is a promising treatment strategy for CCA [9]. Immune characterization could contribute to the selection of patients with CCA who could benefit from ICB therapy, but predictive biomarkers beyond PD-L1 expression, microsatellite instability (MSI), and tumor mutational burden (TMB) are urgently needed to identify responsive patients [10–12]. Notably, CCA exhibits significant immune heterogeneity, with subsets of tumors displaying immune-excluded or immune-inflamed phenotypes; however, the molecular determinants of these states remain unclear.

We performed integrated multi-omics profiling of 424 patients to define molecular subtypes with clinical relevance. Our study revealed four consensus subtypes: proliferation (C1), immune-suppressed Macro_LYVE1 (C2), immune-activated Macro_C1QC (C3), and immune exclusion (IE; C4), each distinguished by unique driver alterations, immune microenvironments, and outcomes. We identify ATP2B1 as a novel prognostic biomarker and show that the C3 subtype, with C1QC+ macrophage infiltration and T-cell activation, responds well to ICB independent of conventional biomarkers. These findings link molecular taxonomy to tailored CCA treatment strategies.

Results

In this study, we recruited 424 patients with CCA of various subtypes from multiple Chinese centers to perform different sequencing analyses (N = 63) and IHC staining validation (N = 422) (online suppl. Fig. 1A). Fifty-three patients with paired tumor bile duct tissues and adjacent nontumor bile ducts/liver tissues, together with six additional nontumor bile duct tissues, were obtained for multi-omics sequencing and integrative analyses, including WGS, WES, RNA-seq, and WGBS (online suppl. Fig. 1A–C and online suppl. Table S1). A total of 366 patients were retrospectively identified for IHC staining and prognostic validation of the associated markers (online suppl. Fig. 1A and online suppl. Table S2). Eight patients with advanced or recurrent CCA who received immunotherapy alongside chemotherapy or switched to immunotherapy after chemotherapy failure were used as the validation set and analyzed using RNA-seq, clinical, pathology, and IHC staining of PD-1, PD-L1, and microsatellite stability (MSS)-associated markers, including MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, and MSH6 (online suppl. Fig. 1A, online suppl. Table S3).

Transcriptomic Classification Identifies Four Clinically and Biologically Distinct CCA Subtypes

To elucidate the molecular heterogeneity of CCA, we performed a consensus clustering analysis of RNA-seq data from 53 treatment-naïve tumors (online suppl. Fig. 1C, A). Protein-coding genes were first filtered by median absolute deviation (MAD), and the 2,934 most variable genes (top 20%) were selected as inputs for ConsensusClusterPlus (parameters: k = 2–6, 1,000 iterations, 80% sample resampling, and PAM algorithm with Euclidean distance). The optimal cluster number (k = 4) was determined by consensus CDF analysis, which showed a significant inflection at k = 4 with marginal improvement at k = 5 (online suppl. Fig. 2A–D). A heatmap of the top 2,934 with 30 representative expressed genes revealed distinct subtype-specific expression patterns, highlighting the unique transcriptional profiles of each cluster (Fig. 1a). The t-SNE and PCA plots demonstrated a clear separation of the 53 samples into four distinct subtypes (online suppl. Fig. 3A–C), confirming the robustness of the clustering.

This classification resolved four molecular subtypes with distinct transcriptional programs, each showing unique clinical features, biological differences and immune composition. Clinically, these subtypes are significantly associated with various histopathological features. C1 demonstrated varying degrees of association with vascular invasion (p = 0.0483), perineural invasion (p = 0.0648), and a higher frequency of CA19-9 (p = 0.0065), whereas C4 exhibited a strong correlation with FGFR2 fusion (p = 0.0017) and significantly lower CA19-9 levels (p = 0.0046) than the other clusters (Fig. 1a). The identification of FGFR2 fusions, predominantly in C4, was consistent with the overexpression of FGFR2, FGFR3, and FGFR4 (online suppl. Fig. 3D). The four clusters significantly differed in progression-free survival (PFS), with median PFS of 16.5, 9.5, 19, and 12 months for C1-C4, respectively (log-rank, p = 0.012, Fig. 1b).

Using enrichment analysis of 50 cancer hallmarks, we uncovered distinct biological differences among the various CCA subtypes. C1 exhibited a higher concentration of MYC targets, along with the activation of cell cycle signaling pathways, including E2F targets and the G2M checkpoint, as well as DNA repair pathways (Fig. 1c). Notably, Ki67 staining was significantly elevated in this cluster compared to that in the others (p = 0.005), indicating a higher rate of cell proliferation (Fig. 1d). Overexpression of proliferation marker genes, such as ERBB2, KRT7, and KRT19 [15] was consistently associated with this subtype (online suppl. Fig. 3D). C2 is distinguished by the significant activation of pathways associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [16]. This includes the TGF-β and hedgehog signaling pathways (Fig. 1c), both of which are well-known drivers of EMT [17]. Moreover, C2 was largely associated with a poor prognosis (Fig. 1b), in agreement with the findings of previous study [18, 19], in which homologous mesenchymal and basal-like tumors were linked to poorer clinical outcomes. The enhanced activity of these pathways in C2 cells suggests an increased potential for invasion and metastasis, which are often linked to the EMT. Tumors in cluster C2 also showed considerably higher levels of EMT marker genes, such as VIM and TGFB1, and the biliary marker PRDM5, providing further evidence that tumors in this cluster had lost epithelial characteristics and acquired a mesenchymal phenotype (online suppl. Fig. 3D). Cluster C3 showed significant alterations in gene expression profiles related to bile acid, fatty acid, heme, and xenobiotic metabolism. These metabolic changes suggest a key role of these pathways in C3’s biology, reflecting distinct biochemical processes within this subtype. This cluster also showed overexpression of genes encoding peroxisome components, a critical regulator of glucose and lipid homeostasis [20] (Fig. 1c).

Based on single-sample gene set enrichment analysis and the ESTIMATE algorithm, C1 was predominantly infiltrated by T helper 17 (Th17) cells within the tumor microenvironment (TME) (Fig. 1e), and IL-17 staining was significantly more prevalent in C1 (p = 0.011) than in the other clusters (Fig. 1d). Cluster C2 had a significantly higher percentage of stromal cells than the other clusters (Fig. 1f; vs. C1, p = 2.2e−08; vs. C3, p = 0.021; vs. C4, p = 5.9e−05). This cluster was enriched with activated fibroblasts, quiescent hepatic stellate cells, and myofibroblasts, as evidenced by increased α-SMA staining (p = 0.003) (Fig. 1d). Additionally, the immunosuppression score was substantially higher in C2 (Fig. 1e), suggesting that extensive and abundant fibrous stroma functions as a barrier, preventing immune cells from reaching the tumors [21]. Tumors at C3 had the highest immune scores (Fig. 1g; vs. C1, p = 0.0002; vs. C4, p = 0.0041). In addition, this class was highly enriched in activated CD4+ T-cells and dendritic cells (Fig. 1e), which may explain the enrichment of interferon alpha response [22] (Fig. 1c). Moreover, the considerable infiltration of gamma-delta T-cells was explained by the high enrichment of mTORC1 signaling [23] (Fig. 1c, e). Interestingly, another study found that enhanced glucose depletion within the TME is caused by signaling through immune checkpoint proteins in tumor cells, such as PD-1 and B7-H3 [24, 25]. Furthermore, enhanced T-cell chemotaxis, activation, and immune checkpoint expression were observed in this cluster (Fig. 1e) may indicate that patients in this group are more likely to benefit from immune therapy. C4, on the other hand, showed an immunological desert pattern with minimal enrichment of the molecular and functional TME signatures, as well as extremely modest expression of all 50 hallmarks (Fig. 1c, e). This cluster showed a significant attenuation of tumor and stromal immune signaling.

Differences in TME composition among the four subtypes were further validated at the single-cell resolution. Cluster analysis of scRNA-seq data from 29 CCA tumors (online suppl. Table S4) revealed that C1 and C4 were dominated by tumor cells, whereas C2 and C3 were enriched in immune cells, including myeloid and T-cell populations, consistent with their respective immune-excluded and immune-inflamed profiles (online suppl. Fig. 4A–D).

Genomic Characterization Revealed Subtype-Specific Alterations

Each subgroup showed a distinct profile of recurrently altered genes (Fig. 2a). In our CCA cohort, 4780 nonsilent somatic mutations were identified (3.08 coding mutations/Mb per patient on average; online suppl. Table S5). Notably, we excluded 1 patient identified as MSI-H (online suppl. Table S6) because the large number of mutations in this individual unduly influenced our analysis.

Cluster C1, previously noted for its proliferative characteristics, exhibited the highest TMB among the four clusters (Fig. 2b). Recurrent mutations were observed in TP53 (10/23 cases) and KRAS (8/23 cases), with SOS1, SMAD4, and APC mutations detected exclusively in this group. C4 harbored IDH1 mutations, specifically R132C and R132L (2/11 cases), which were not observed in other subtypes. Formal statistical comparisons were not conducted because of the limited sample size.

Copy number variations (CNVs) were estimated from both WES and WGS data (online suppl. Fig. 5A). The effects of CNVs on mRNA abundance were investigated, and we discovered that approximately 4787 (30.6%) gene mRNA abundance values were strongly associated with the gene copy number (online suppl. Fig. 3C). The CNVs burden was elevated in C1/C4 versus C2/C3 (p < 0.05, all comparisons), with no difference between C1 and C4 (p > 0.83) (online suppl. Fig. 5B). Despite the elevated CNVs burden, each of these clusters presented unique CNV profiles, indicating different genetic instability patterns and potentially distinct mechanisms underlying tumorigenesis.

CNVs were evaluated by pairwise comparisons of each of the four clusters with the remaining ones. Genes residing in aneuploid regions were documented in these comparisons (Fig. 2c). C1 had a more frequent 18q21.1 loss, containing oncogenes, including SMAD4, MALT1, and BCL2. Integrative CNV-RNA analysis revealed that SMAD4 loss was correlated with reduced SMAD4 expression (Spearman, r = 0.69, p < 5.96e−07) (online suppl. Fig. 5C).

Our molecular profiling (both mutations and CNVs) identified distinct alterations in the molecular modules between subtypes (Fig. 2d). Mutations in SOS1 were exclusively detected in C1, which is consistent with previously reported mechanisms, leading to hyperactivation of the RAS/MAPK pathway through SOS1’s function as a guanine-nucleotide-exchange factor that binds to receptor tyrosine kinase complexes and facilitates the activation of Ras proteins [26, 27]. Additionally, inactivation of SMAD2/SMAD4 contributed to the loss of TGF-β-mediated growth suppression.

Collectively, these alterations enhance cell proliferation and promote oncogenic progression. Conversely, C4 frequently harbors inactivating mutations in multiple chromatin-remodeling genes (including BAP1, ARID1A/ARID1B, PBRM1, and SYNE1/SYNE2), and patients with a mutation in any one of the chromatin-remodeling genes tended to have worse survival than patients in whom all of these genes were wild type [28]. Notably, four FGFR2 fusion genes were identified exclusively in C4 (4/11, 36.36%), including three novel fusions (FGFR2—PRDX3, —LGSN, —NRAP) (online suppl. Fig. 6A–D) and one known fusion (FGFR2-BICC1) [29], all of which retained their kinase domains and were validated (online suppl. Fig. 6A–E). C4 demonstrated marked enrichment of FGFR2 alterations, accompanied by elevated FGFR2 expression relative to FGFR2-wild-type tumors (online suppl. Fig. 6F). FGFR alterations can indirectly induce a “cold” TME with diminished T-cell infiltration and reduced immunogenicity [30, 31]. These findings underscore the diverse oncogenic programs that drive the phenotypes of different CCA subtypes, with the referenced literature serving to substantiate these observations and provide a foundation for further investigation into the underlying biology.

Epigenetic and Genetic Alterations Define Immune-Excluded Subtypes in CCA

To complement our investigation of genetic changes in CCA, we used WGBS to assess DNA methylation in 39 tumor samples and five non-neoplastic bile ducts (online suppl. Fig. 1A). CIMP is an epigenetic molecular subtype observed in multiple malignancies that silences the expression of well-known TSGs [32]. A total of 103 CIMP-CGIs that displayed significant hypermethylation were detected in our cohort. The methylation levels of these CGIs were used to perform hierarchical clustering across all tumors, as previously described [33]. CIMP-positive (CIMP+) tumors were classified into clusters with the highest number of hypermethylated CIMP-CGIs (Fig. 3a). Notably, CIMP+ tumors were enriched in C1 (15/19, 78.95%) and C4 (6/9, 66.67%), while CIMP− predominated in C2 (0/5) and C3 (1/6, 16.67%) (Fig. 3b). Decomposition of the immune components was performed using the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis algorithm and pan-cancer immune metagenes described in The Cancer Immunome Atlas [34] (Fig. 3c) showed that CIMP+ tumors had extremely weak expression of all immune cell types compared with CIMP− tumors. The ESTIMATE algorithm revealed immune scores (−1,267.70 to 2,637.82) for 39 tumor samples. The immune scores of CIMP− tumors were remarkably higher than those of CIMP+ tumors (p < 3.6e−06) (Fig. 3d).

In addition, compared with CIMP− tumors, CIMP+ tumors had significantly higher TMB and CNV burdens (p < 0.003 vs. p < 0.0009), indicating a positive correlation between hypermethylation and mutation or CNV burden (Fig. 3e). Fisher’s exact test was used to identify the association between CIMP+ tumors and genetics. TP53, KRAS, SMAD4, and APC mutations were highly enriched in CIMP+ tumors (Fig. 3e). Notably, patients in C1 who exhibit both CIMP+ status and mutations in TP53, KRAS, SMAD4, and APC tend to show an aggressive proliferative profile and reduced immune infiltration, suggesting the critical role of CIMP+ in driving tumor aggressiveness and immune evasion.

We then compared the PFS between patients with CIMP+ and CIMP− tumors. Patients with CIMP+ tumors had a median survival of 12.7 months, which was shorter than the median survival of 15.1 months in patients with CIMP− tumors (Fig. 3f). This finding is consistent with previous studies [35], which also showed that patients with CIMP+ tumors exhibited remarkably poor clinical outcomes. Notably, tumors positive for hepatitis B virus (HBV) were exclusively found in CIMP− tumors (Fig. 3e), indicating that HBV infection may play a protective role in CCA survival, as previously reported [36]. The integration of these molecular features into C1 suggests a complex interplay between DNA methylation patterns and genetic lesions, which may foster a more robust proliferative capacity while simultaneously creating an immunosuppressive niche. The synergy between CIMP+ and genetic alterations could be a critical factor in the biology of this subtype, highlighting the importance of considering both the genetic and epigenetic landscapes in understanding tumor behavior and developing therapeutic strategies for this subtype.

To assess which genes undergo transcriptional repression under the influence of methylation in the promoter regions, we performed integrative methylation and RNA-seq analyses (Methods section). We identified 30 genes with high levels of tumor-specific hypermethylation (online suppl. Fig. 7A; online suppl. Table S7). Among these genes, patients with low ATP2B1 expression displayed significantly reduced overall survival compared to their high ATP2B1 expression counterparts (p = 0.00092, log-rank test) (online suppl. Fig. 7B, C). For external validation, an independent cohort of 366 CCA samples from multiple centers was collected, and it was found that patients with low ATP2B1 expression exhibited significant associations with poor survival (p = 0.015, log-rank test) (online suppl. Fig. 7D, E). The ICC cell line RBE with stable ATP2B1 downregulation (KD) was established via transfection with lentiviral plasmids, and the transfection efficiency was verified by Western blotting. The wound healing assay showed that downregulation of ATP2B1 accelerated the migration of RBE cells compared to the corresponding SCR groups (online suppl. Fig. 7F), indicating a correlation between low ATP2B1 expression and CCAs malignant biological behavior of CCA. Taken together, these results suggest that ATP2B1 is a potential prognostic biomarker for CCA.

Subtype-Specific Immune Microenvironments Are Shaped by Divergent Macrophage Profiles

Building on the epigenetic and genetically defined IE in C1 and C4, we further explored the cellular mechanisms that shape immune heterogeneity across subtypes. Cell-cell communication analysis across the four molecular subtypes further showed that the C4 subtype, which was previously characterized as an immune desert, displayed minimal intercellular interactions, confirming its immunologically cold landscape (Fig. 4a). Notably, the myeloid populations exhibited subtype-specific interaction patterns, particularly between C1QC+ and LYVE1+ macrophages. C1QC+ and LYVE1+ macrophages exhibited distinct interaction strengths and patterns across C1–C3, indicating their differential roles in immune modulation (Fig. 4a). Furthermore, C1QC+ macrophages and CD8+ T-cells displayed the strongest interactions in the C3 subtype, whereas in C2, the interaction between LYVE1+ macrophages and CD8+ T-cells was more pronounced, suggesting divergent macrophage-mediated T-cell regulatory mechanisms underpinning the immune landscapes of these subtypes (Fig. 4b).

Trajectory analysis further demonstrated that C1QC+ macrophages resided in intermediate pseudotime states, marked by elevated MHC class II expression, indicative of an enhanced antigen-presenting capacity (Fig. 4c–e, h). In contrast, LYVE1+ macrophages were localized at the terminal end of the trajectory and displayed elevated M2 polarization scores, particularly in the C2 subtype (Fig. 4c–e, f, g). Concordantly, CD8+ T-cells in C3 exhibited stronger cytotoxic signatures and NK receptor scores, supporting an immune-activated TME in the C3 class (Fig. 4i–j). C1QC+ macrophages are associated with tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) and promote antitumor immunity. In muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC), they are enriched in TLS-positive tumors, exhibit enhanced phagocytic capacity, and are polarized toward the M1 phenotype, supporting their role in antitumor responses [37]. Multiplex immunofluorescent staining was performed to confirm the findings. C3 tumors exhibited a significantly higher infiltration of C1QC+CD68+ macrophages, whereas CD8+GZMB+ T-cells showed a mild increasing trend (Fig. 4k).

Taken together, these results indicate that the different immune states observed in the C2 and C3 subtypes may be driven by their dominant macrophage subsets: macro_LYVE1 in C2 and macro_C1QC in C3. These macrophages play a role in modulating CD8+ T-cell activity, contributing to the different prognostic outcomes associated with these subtypes.

Integrated Molecular, Immunological, and Clinical Landscapes Define Four Distinct CCA Subtypes with Therapeutic Implications

To synthesize the transcriptomic, epigenetic, immune, and clinical features of CCA, we integrated multi-omics data and visualized the four subtypes (C1-C4) using a comprehensive schematic (Fig. 5a–c). C1 and C4 were characterized by low immune infiltration but had distinct underlying biology. C1 displayed features of a proliferative phenotype, with an enrichment of Th17 cells and proliferating tumor cells with pronounced cell cycle pathway activation (Fig. 5a). In addition, C1 was enriched in a large proportion of DCC tumors (13/17), and tumors in this cluster had features similar to those in the recently proposed DCC proliferation cluster [19]. Molecular correlations with C1 included a significantly higher alteration burden (including mutations, CNVs, and epimutation burdens), high frequencies of TP53, KRAS, SMAD4, APC, and ERBB2 alterations, and enrichment of CIMP+ tumors (Fig. 5b). In summary, we named the C1 cluster “Proliferation” considering its major properties of cell cycle activation, ERBB2 alterations, high Th17 cell infiltration, and poor clinical outcomes (Fig. 5a, c). C4 showed a striking immune-desert pattern, consistent with its FGFR2-altered and CIMP+ molecular background, reinforcing its immune-excluded classification with a poor prognosis (Fig. 5a–c).

Pathological, molecular features, and clinical associated with CCA Molecular Classes. a Schematic representation of the four CCA subtypes, their defining features, and immune context. b Key somatic mutation events, CNV events, CIMP, and fusion events across the four classes. c Summary of the characteristics of the CCA molecular classes.

C2 and C3 exhibited high immune cell infiltration but represented fundamentally distinct immune states. C2 is dominated by LYVE1+ M2-like macrophages and abundant stromal fibroblasts with EMT and TGF-β signaling activation, forming an immunosuppressive barrier that restricts T-cell function (Fig. 5a). C3, in contrast, features an immune-activated environment with robust infiltration of C1QC+ macrophages, CD8+ T-cells, dendritic cells, and neutrophils. This composition supports active antigen presentation and cytotoxic functions, aligning with C3’s favorable prognosis and immune responsiveness (Fig. 5a).

Together, this integrative overview underscores how CCA subtypes are defined not only by transcriptional and genetic profiles but also by distinct immune environments, ranging from proliferation (C1), IE (C4), and immune-suppressed macro_LYVE1 (ISM_LYVE1) (C2) to immune-activated Macro_C1QC (IAM_C1QC) (C3), each with unique therapeutic implications (Fig. 5c).

Marker Gene Screening and External Data Validation

To externally validate the molecular classification of the CCA cohorts, we created a classifier that included 160 genes (online suppl. Table S8) within a maximum of 40 marker genes selected from each cluster. In our discovery cohort, the classifier assigned tumors to each of the four molecular classes with a global accuracy of 96% (Fig. 6a). For clusters C1, C2, C3, and C4, the classifier had accuracy of 100%, 100%, 96%, and 96%, respectively.

We developed a uniform classifier that included tumors from different parts of the CCA, including the ICC, DCC, and PHC. To externally validate the classifier, transcriptome data of 118 CCA samples from different anatomical locations in the GSE89749 cohort were analyzed (ICC = 83, DCC = 6, PHC = 29). The Nearest Template Prediction method implemented in the GenePattern module (Methods section) was used to predict the external cohort of the classifier. Finally, our classifier achieved a sensitivity of 91% in the external cohort (Fig. 6b, c) with high confidence (false discovery rate <0.05). External validation of our four molecular clusters supported the cross-validity of the model, regardless of the anatomical origin of the tumors or the technique used for transcriptome profiling. Nonetheless, it revealed significantly different molecular variations and immune microenvironments in CCAs. The availability of this technology may make the molecular subtyping of CCA more clinically feasible.

Next, we evaluated the clinical application of our established classifier in 8 newly recruited patients with CCA. As shown in Figure 6d, according to our classifier, of 4/8 patients were classified into cluster C3 (P1, P2, P4, and P8), whereas among the other 4 patients, one was classified into cluster non-C3, including one C1 (P7), two C2 (P3 and P6), and one C4 (IE) (P5). All 8 patients had advanced or recurrent CCA and underwent ICB therapy (PD-1 blockers) combined with chemotherapy or switched to ICB therapy after chemotherapy failure. The efficacy of ICB therapy in our cohort is shown in Figure 6e. Four of the eight patients exhibited CR or PR, while the other four exhibited progressive disease (PD) or stable disease (SD). The objective response rate (CR + PR) was 75% in cluster C3 (P1, P2, and P4) compared with 15% in non-C3 clusters (Fig. 6d, g).

High TMB, MSI, defective mismatch repair (dMMR), and/or PD-1/PD-L1 high expression are the main biomarkers currently used clinically to evaluate whether checkpoint inhibitors should be recommended for patients with CCA. To compare with these usual criteria, we performed PD-1, PD-L1, MSS-associated marker staining and TMB determination. The results showed that, except for P7, all the other 7 patients with CCA in our clinical validation cohort had MSS, MMR proficiency, low TMB, and negative PD-1/PD-L1 expression; however, clinical observations showed that four of them were highly responsive to checkpoint inhibitors (Fig. 6e, f). Our classification system and classifier effectively predicted and suggested that the 3 patients could benefit from immune checkpoint inhibitors. Studies have consistently reported that some patients with CCA with MSS, MMR proficiency, low TMB, and weak PD-L1 expression achieved a complete response following PD-1 blockade [38, 39]. Consequently, predicting immunotherapy responsiveness based on TMB, MSI, dMMR, and/or PD-1/PD-L1 expression is insufficient. The classification system and classifier created in this study could serve as an effective supplement to checkpoint inhibitors for high-response patient-selection.

Interestingly, P1, 1 of the patients in cluster C3 (Fig. 6h), was a 62-year-old male with DCC who underwent pancreaticoduodenectomy in January 2017. Twelve cycles of gemcitabine monotherapy were administered after surgery from February 2017 to January 2018, and no indicators of tumor recurrence were found during this period. However, lymph node metastasis was detected on MRI in April 2018. Unfortunately, the metastatic lymph nodes continued to progress after treatment with gemcitabine combined with oxaliplatin. The patient was started on a regimen of pembrolizumab (a monoclonal IgG4-kappa isotype antibody against PD-1) in late July 2018. Surprisingly, after five rounds of ICB therapy with pembrolizumab, MRI revealed that the enlarged lymph nodes had disappeared in October 2018. Thus, CR was achieved in less than 3 months, and the patient remained in remission for 76 months until the last follow-up.

Overall, we observed a significant difference in the response to ICB therapy between cluster C3 and non-C3 clusters (Fig. 6d, g, h). CCA patients with the characteristic cluster C3 immune microenvironment phenotype were more responsive than others (75% vs. 15%). This clinical observation requires further validation in a larger cohort, and patients are currently being recruited from our clinical center for this purpose. The clinical applications of our study are worth considering.

Discussion

The heterogeneity of CCAs at the multi-omics and histopathological levels severely limits the efficacy of traditional treatment [1]. Following the advent of ICB therapy and its potential therapeutic promise, it is critical to gain insights into the profiles of CCA and identify the population most likely to benefit from ICB therapy [40]. In this regard, the comprehensive and integrative clustering of CCA proposed in this study not only differs in terms of the immune microenvironment and genetic and epigenetic features, but also comprehensively covers CCA features at three different anatomical origins and four distinct classes (C1-C4) stratified by unique molecular drivers, immune landscapes, and clinical outcomes, not limited to one immune-linked subtype [18, 41]. Furthermore, of particular interest are molecular subtypes based on immune characteristics and their clinical applications facilitate the screening of populations that will potentially benefit from ICB therapy [42]. The C3 population (Immune-Activated Macro_C1QC) identified by the 160-gene molecular classifier offers a practical framework for guiding therapeutic decisions and underscores the limitations of current “one-size-fits-all” approaches in CCA management, assisting in determining whether medications are suitable for clinical application when validated in more external cohorts in the future [43].

Our classification covers all major anatomical subtypes of CCA, enabling unified clinical decision-making. It explicitly integrates and applies to all three major anatomical subtypes of CCA: ICC, PHC, and DCC. This allows us to provide a unified classification framework that is not restricted by tumor location, making it broadly applicable for guiding precision treatment across the entire CCA clinical spectrum. In contrast, previous major CCA classifications lack this universality. Josep M. Llovet et al. molecular subtype system focuses exclusively on extrahepatic CCA (eCCA) and cannot be extended to ICC or PHC [19]. Other classifications are primarily tailored to intrahepatic CCA (iCCA), with no valid application to DCC or perihilar CCA [7, 44]. Our molecular classification addresses the full heterogeneity of CCA, facilitating their utility in clinical settings where patients with different anatomical subtypes. In addition, we built our classification on comprehensive multi-omics integration, leveraging transcriptomic signatures, genomic driver alterations (e.g., FGFR2 fusions, IDH1 mutations), methylomic profiles (e.g., CpG island methylator phenotype [CIMP] status), and single-cell transcriptomic data (different immune environment). This multi-layered approach not only clarifies the biological basis of each subtype but also directly links molecular features to therapeutic mechanisms, providing a more holistic understanding of CCA biology. However, previous CCA classifications lack of epigenetic features (e.g., DNA methylation) or immune microenvironmental dynamics at single-cell resolution [7, 19]. Notably, we uncover different macrophage-T cell crosstalk that defines subtype-specific immune responsiveness.

Importantly, we validate our classification with both public databases and prospective clinical samples. Given CCA’s low incidence and the scarcity of eligible patients, collecting 8 prospective samples represents a considerable effort – and among these, 3 out of 4 C3 subtype patients benefited from ICB, providing direct clinical evidence of the subtype’s predictive value. Most prior CCA classifications, however, lack prospective clinical validation. For example, a recent ESCC molecular classification study used 155 clinical samples to establish its system but only validated it via TCGA; a granted Chinese patent (No. 202411659695.4) for an NSCLC prognostic model relied exclusively on TCGA [45]. Even major CCA schemes only validate findings in public databases or retrospective cohorts [7, 19], with no prospective clinical samples to confirm real-world predictive utility.

Our findings underscore the critical role of immune microenvironment heterogeneity in shaping CCA biology and response to therapy [46]. Among the four subtypes, C2 and C3 represent two contrasting immune phenotypes that highlight the complexity of the TME in CCA patients. Although both subtypes exhibit immune infiltration, their functional states and cellular compositions differ dramatically, leading to distinct clinical behaviors and therapeutic opportunities [47].

The C2 subtype is defined by a stroma-dominated TME, characterized by abundant LYVE1+ macrophages, activated fibroblasts, and HSs. This stromal abundance creates physical and biochemical barriers that restrict immune cell infiltration and function, contributing to an immunosuppressive niche [48, 49]. The enrichment of EMT-related pathways (e.g., TGF-β and hedgehog signaling) was consistent with the mesenchymal phenotype observed in C2 tumors. These pathways promote tumor invasiveness and foster an immunosuppressive milieu by recruiting regulatory T-cells (Tregs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [50]. The dominance of LYVE1+ macrophages, which exhibit an M2-like polarization state, further reinforces immune suppression [51].

These macrophages secrete anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-10 and TGF-β), inhibit cytotoxic T-cell activity, and promote angiogenesis, all of which contribute to tumor progression and therapy resistance [52]. Given the stroma-rich and immune-excluded nature of C2 tumors, conventional immunotherapy is likely to be ineffective. Instead, strategies targeting stromal components – such as TGF-β inhibitors, FAK inhibitors, or macrophage-depleting agents – may be necessary to remodel the TME and unmask potential immune responses [53].

In stark contrast to C2, the C3 subtype exhibits an immune-inflamed TME with robust infiltration of C1QC+ macrophages, dendritic cells and cytotoxic CD8+ T-cells. This immunogenic profile is correlated with enhanced antigen presentation, T-cell activation, and a favorable response to ICB. C1QC+ macrophages in C3 display an M1-like phenotype associated with pro-inflammatory cytokine production (e.g., IFN-γ and TNF-α) and enhanced phagocytic activity [54]. Their spatial association with TLSs suggests that they play a role in sustaining antitumor immunity, similar to that observed in other cancers. Notably, the C3 subtype’s favorable response to ICB occurred independently of conventional biomarkers, such as PD-L1 expression, MSI, or TMB, highlighting the limitations of current biomarker paradigms and the potential of our classification system to identify ICB-responsive patients who might otherwise be overlooked [38].

C3 patients demonstrated a striking sensitivity to ICB therapy, with a 75% objective response rate in the validation cohort. This contrasts sharply with the poor responsiveness observed in other subtypes, particularly C1 and C4, which exhibit immune-excluded phenotypes driven by proliferative signaling (e.g., TP53/KRAS mutations) and FGFR2 alterations, respectively. The interplay between genetic and epigenetic alterations further elucidates the mechanisms of immune evasion in CCA. C1 and C4 tumors, enriched for CpG island methylator phenotype (CIMP) positivity and mutations in TP53, KRAS, and IDH1, displayed suppressed immune infiltration, corroborating previous studies linking these alterations with impaired antigen presentation and T-cell exclusion [55]. Specially, IDH1 mutations in C4 might be associated with an “immune-desert” phenotype, consistent with preclinical evidence of metabolic immunosuppression [56]. As shown in the results of Figure 2, c4 harbored IDH1 mutations, specifically R132C and R132L, which were not observed in other subtypes. The observed trends were consistent with the established biological roles of TP53/KRAS mutations in promoting cellular proliferation [57]. Additionally, compelling evidence suggests that IDH1 mutations contribute to the development of an immunosuppressive TME in CCA. Spatial profiling studies have demonstrated that IDH1-mutant CCA spatially excludes CD8+ T-cells from the tumor core, leading to a distinctive “immune desert” phenotype [58]. Collectively, these findings suggest that IDH1 mutations play a pivotal role in immune evasion in CCA, with direct implications for immunotherapy responsiveness in this specific molecular subset of patients.

Furthermore, C1 showed a more frequent 18q21.1 loss (containing oncogenes, including SMAD4, MALT1, and BCL2 and integrative CNV-RNA analysis revealed a close relationship between SMAD4 loss and reduced SMAD4 expression. Our observations are highly consistent with recent findings that SMAD4 restricts reactive cholangiocyte expansion in response to liver injury and acts as a critical barrier against CCA development [59]. Inactivation of SMAD4 accelerates carcinogenesis by promoting preneoplastic biliary lesions and CCA development in the context of injury. Additionally, analyses of SMAD4-perturbed cholangiocytes and CCA cell lines revealed enriched pathways, including cell cycle regulation, MYC signaling, and oxidative phosphorylation, suggesting that SMAD4 may regulate cholangiocyte proliferation and CCA progression through these mechanisms. Our findings support this hypothesis. The frequent loss of SMAD4 at C1, along with the correlation between SMAD4 copy number loss and reduced expression, underscores the importance of SMAD4 in maintaining normal biliary-cell function. Downregulation of SMAD4 due to genetic loss may eliminate critical inhibitory signals, thereby enhancing the proliferative response of cholangiocytes to injury and promoting carcinogenesis. This is in line with the role of SMAD4 as a key downstream effector of the TGF-β signaling pathway that restricts cholangiocyte expansion and malignant transformation. C4 tumors had frequent heterozygous deletions containing numerous tumor suppressor genes (TSGs), such as ARID1A, VHL, CTNNB1, BAP1, ARID1B, and RB1. The presence of ARID1A deletions in C4 aligns with its “immune-excluded” phenotype, corroborating previous findings that the absence of ARID1A may lead to immune dysregulation [60].

Notably, in C1, the combination of CIMP+ and mutations in TP53, KRAS, SMAD4, and APC may contribute to the subgroup’s aggressive growth and low immune infiltration. This observation is consistent with recent reports. Tricarico et al. [61] found that age-related spontaneous hypermethylation and consequent silencing of specific genes could activate the APC-WNT pathway, causing differentiation defects. Additionally, Kerdivel et al. [62] demonstrated that CIMP+ in adrenocortical carcinoma is linked to increased expression of DNA methyltransferases DNMT1 and DNMT3A, driven by an increase in gene copy number and cell hyperproliferation, and that CIMP+ contributes to tumor aggressiveness by favoring immune escape. Moreover, Gu et al. [35] found that CIMP+ tumors are immune-depleted, and a significantly higher tumor mutation burden and copy number alterations confirmed the genomic heterogeneity and chromosomal instability of CIMP+. Collectively, these findings highlight the critical role of CIMP+ in driving tumor aggressiveness and immune evasion, supporting our conclusion that the cooperative effects of genetic and epigenetic alterations shape the TME toward an immune-excluded state. In addition, tumors positive for HBV were exclusively found in CIMP− tumors and mainly enriched in C3 class in Figure 3e. In some studies, HBV-positive patients exhibit a relatively favorable prognosis, suggesting that HBV may exert a protective role in specific subtypes (such as the CIMP− and C3 subtypes) [36, 63]. Patients with CCA with HBV infection showed increased antigen processing and presentation (APC) and T-cell infiltration, conferring a favorable prognosis compared with those without HBV infection [64]. This is consistent with our observation that CIMP− tumors with HBV enriched in C3 subtype are characterized by more immune cells infiltration, enhanced antitumor immunity, and C1QC+ macrophage that promote CD8+ T cell activation. In addition, FGFR2-positive iCCA (a molecularly defined subgroup) is enriched in patients with HBV seropositivity [65]. These data suggest that HBV infection may serve as confounder to shape the epigenetic and signalling landscape of the tumor, rather than merely reflecting background liver disease.

Notably, the calcium homeostasis-associated protein ATP2B1 was first identified at the methylation and transcriptome levels and was validated as a putative biomarker that may predict the clinical outcome of CCA [66]. Abnormal calcium homeostasis is associated with several pathological processes, including cancer [67]. Considering that ATP2B1 expression correlates with calcium intake and metabolic reprogramming [68], its malfunction may contribute to the progression of CCA. Previous reports have consistently revealed that the suppression of circular RNA ATP2B1 (circATP2B1) may drive the growth and metastasis of renal cancer [69]. Here, we found that low ATP2B1 expression correlated with aggressive tumor behavior and poor survival across independent cohorts, indicating that it is a promising biomarker for risk stratification. Functional assays confirmed its role in suppressing cell migration, consistent with its association with adverse outcomes.

Conclusion

We gained a better understanding of the molecular landscape of CCA. Molecular classes may identify possible therapeutic opportunities through molecular profiling and establish an evidence base for the development of drugs and biomarkers that will revolutionize clinical trials for testing systemic therapies. Molecular phenotyping provides a clinical framework for determining whether medications are suitable for clinical use and for predicting the sensitivity and effectiveness of individual therapeutics in patients with CCA based on standard molecular subtyping. The C3 subtype, in particular, offers an actionable paradigm for ICB therapy, whereas the C1 and C4 subtypes with poor prognoses may benefit from targeted approaches against proliferative drivers (e.g., ERBB2) or FGFR inhibitors, respectively. Although the number of clinically verified cases in this study was small and there are certain limitations, clinical trial research and related patents based on this research are actively being applied for by our center, and more clinical patients will be enrolled in the future for further verification. We expect that more patients will benefit from this treatment.
