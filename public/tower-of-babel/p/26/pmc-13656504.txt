Invertebrates gut viromes mediate microbial adaptation to pharmaceutical diversity under warming
Authors: Ya-Ning Wang, Jin-Ting Zheng, Xue-Peng Chen, Luo-Qin Shen, Ming-Ming Sun, Mao Ye, Yi-Fei Wang, Roy Neilson, Dong Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPharmaceutical pollution is an emerging environmental concern that can disrupt microbial communities and ecological processes, while climate warming adds further stress with broad ecological consequences. Soil invertebrates such as collembolans harbor gut microbiomes essential for host health and ecosystem stability, yet the responses of these communities—particularly viral communities—to combined pharmaceutical and warming pressures remain unclear. Here, we used controlled microcosm experiments with Folsomia candida to investigate how pharmaceutical diversity and fluctuating warming jointly shape gut microbiomes through bacteria–virus interactions.ResultsPharmaceutical diversity significantly reduced the alpha diversity of gut viral communities in F. candida, an effect not observed in surrounding soils. Diurnal warming increased the proportion of lysogenic phages and enhanced auxiliary metabolic genes (AMGs) such as ACADM and nrdA. Functional validation in Escherichia coli BL21 confirmed that these genes mitigate oxidative stress and improve host thermal tolerance. In contrast, diverse pharmaceuticals increased the proportion of lytic phages, likely driving nutrient turnover through a “kill-the-winner” dynamic that stimulated bacterial taxa involved in pharmaceutical degradation. Moreover, warming amplified the disruption of gut bacterial communities caused by pharmaceutical diversity and strengthened bacteria–virus co-occurrence networks.ConclusionsOur findings reveal that gut viruses act as pivotal regulators of microbial adaptation under concurrent chemical and climate stressors. By mediating host resilience and microbial dynamics, the gut virome provides mechanistic insights into ecosystem stability, and may also serve as an early indicator of combined pharmaceutical and warming stress in soil invertebrate systems, underscoring the need to integrate viral–microbial interactions into One Health framework for environmental risk assessment. Introduction

In recent decades, the widespread occurrence of pharmaceuticals in the environment and their potential ecological consequences have emerged as a growing concern [1]. Non-antibiotic pharmaceuticals account for approximately 95% of total pharmaceutical consumption [2] and are widely detected in environmental matrices such as landfill leachate, soil runoff, and surface waters, often at concentrations ranging from nanograms to milligrams per liter (e.g., carbamazepine, ibuprofen, gemfibrozil, and metformin) [3–5]. Up to 90% of ingested pharmaceuticals are excreted unchanged or as metabolites, allowing them to enter soils through wastewater irrigation or sludge application [6]. As a result, many pharmaceuticals can inadvertently perturb microbial communities by altering metabolic pathways or inducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production [7]. Importantly, environmental exposure typically occurs as complex, low-dose mixtures rather than isolated compounds [8]. Although the ecological effects of individual pharmaceuticals, such as carbamazepine [9], metformin [10], and ibuprofen [10], have been widely studied, the influence of pharmaceutical diversity as an ecological driver remains poorly understood. Increasing pharmaceutical diversity may impose compound-specific and synergistic selection pressures, potentially reshaping microbial community composition, functionality, and interspecies interactions [11]. Unlike traditional dose-response paradigms, this diversity-based mechanism is driven by the number of co-occurring compounds rather than their absolute concentrations. It may better reflect the complexity of environmental pharmaceutical exposure.

Soil invertebrates are both directly and indirectly exposed to pharmaceutical contaminants via ingestion, dermal contact, and interaction with contaminated soils. Folsomia candida is a widely used standardized model organism in soil ecotoxicology and has been extensively applied to investigate the impacts of environmental contaminants on non-target soil organisms [12]. As a representative collembolan species and one of the most abundant soil fauna, it contributes to organic matter decomposition and nutrient cycling by feeding on soil detritus and microbial communities, while also serving as a potential vector of soil contaminants and associated microorganisms [13]. Beyond effects on survival and reproduction, growing attention has turned to the gut microbiota as a sensitive indicator of pollution-induced stress [14]. Gut microbiota have vital roles in maintaining host health and facilitating nutrient cycling [15]. The responses of microbial communities to environmental stress can be interpreted through bacterial life-history strategies. These strategies describe coordinated trait syndromes shaped by physiological or evolutionary trade-offs, with different strategies favored under different environmental conditions [16, 17]. For example, pharmaceuticals such as carbamazepine and metformin have been shown to significantly alter the gut microbiome of soil invertebrates [18, 19]. Moreover, gut microbial assemblages often respond more rapidly and sensitively to environmental stressors than surrounding soil microbiota [20]. However, most previous studies have focused on the effects of individual pharmaceuticals on soil animals, leaving the influence of pharmaceutical diversity on gut microbiota largely unexplored.

Viruses, the most abundant and genetically diverse biological entities in the biosphere, are increasingly recognized for their pivotal roles in shaping microbiome structure and function [21]. Phages (viruses that infect bacteria [22]) can regulate microbial communities via lytic and lysogenic cycles [23], influencing community succession and diversity. Lytic phages reduce dominant bacterial populations, whereas lysogenic phages may enhance host adaptation to environmental stress by mediating horizontal gene transfer [24]. In particular, lysogenic phages often encode auxiliary metabolic genes (AMGs) that can modulate host metabolic pathways when expressed during infection [25, 26]. Environmental stressors, including warming and chemical pollutants, can alter viral community composition and shift phage–bacteria interactions. For example, warming may promote transitions toward lysogenic cycles [27], while certain pollutants can induce oxidative stress and trigger lytic phage activation [28]. Although phages are known to play important ecological roles in aquatic and soil environments [25, 29], their functions in the gut of soil animals remain poorly understood. Only limited evidence suggests that phages in the earthworm gut may alleviate benzo[a]pyrene toxicity through AMG-mediated functions [30]. However, the gut virome of collembolans and its interactions with bacterial communities remain poorly understood. This knowledge gap limits our understanding of how phages influence gut microbial responses to pharmaceutical pollution.

Temperature is a key environmental factor shaping microbial ecosystem structure and function by regulating metabolic activity, population dynamics, and resource competition [31]. Temperature can also interact with environmental pollutants, potentially amplifying microbial responses to stressors [32]. In soil invertebrates, elevated temperature may indirectly alter gut microbial composition and metabolic pathways by affecting host physiology and feeding behavior. For example, our previous study showed that the combined effects of warming and pollutants increased F. candida mortality and disrupted the structure and function of its gut microbiota [33]. Temperature also influences viral communities by affecting their diversity, relative abundance, and survival in soils, and long-term warming has been shown to alter virus–host interactions [25, 34]. However, little is known about how gut viromes in soil invertebrates respond to temperature changes. Furthermore, climate change involves not only increasing mean temperatures but also increased thermal fluctuations that can trigger varying adaptive responses of soil microbial communities [35]. However, most current studies have focused on linear warming scenarios, paying little or no attention to the effects of temperature fluctuations.

Thus, to address this question under controlled conditions, we established a microcosm experiment using the model soil invertebrate F. candida to investigate how pharmaceutical diversity and warming influence gut microbial communities, including bacteria and viruses. Seven widely used pharmaceuticals from different therapeutic classes (carbamazepine, thioridazine, ibuprofen, gemfibrozil, cetirizine, metformin, and citalopram) were selected because they are frequently detected in soils (up to 60%) and exhibit contrasting environmental behaviors and microbial effects [4, 36–40]. These compounds were combined to create four pharmaceutical diversity levels (0, 1, 3, and 6 compounds), encompassing 7 single exposures, 7 combinations of three, and 7 combinations of six pharmaceuticals. Two temperature regimes were applied: ambient (20℃) and elevated (23℃ with diurnal fluctuations). Within this factorial design, we assessed the individual and interactive effects of pharmaceutical diversity and warming on the structure, function, and interaction networks of gut microbiota. Specifically, we asked: (1) How does pharmaceutical diversity influence the composition, function, and stability of bacterial and viral communities? (2) Do viruses regulate microbial communities through shifts in life-history strategies and functional gene expression, and how are these responses modulated by warming and pharmaceutical diversity? (3) Does warming and pharmaceutical diversity co-regulate the gut micro-ecosystem by altering bacteria–virus interaction networks? We hypothesized that viruses modulate bacterial communities through distinct mechanisms under warming and pharmaceutical stress, potentially strengthening bacteria–virus interactions. Collectively, our findings provide a mechanistic perspective on how viral dynamics influence gut microbial responses to pharmaceutical mixtures under warming conditions.

Results

Effect of warming and pharmaceutical diversity on phage community composition and lifestyle

Overall, a total of 6970 phage contigs were identified across all soil and collembolan gut samples. Of the annotated viral operational taxonomic units (vOTUs), 42.0% were assigned to the family Siphoviridae (Fig. S1a–b), however, 49.2% were unclassified at the family level. In total, 6,879 vOTUs were detected in soil samples and 3447 in collembolan gut samples with 1,673 vOTUs shared across all samples (Fig. 1a). Across pharmaceutical treatments, 6,013 vOTUs were identified from soil (87.4%, Fig. S1c), and 929 vOTUs in gut samples (26.9%, Fig. S1d). Viral diversity significantly declined in the collembolan gut with increasing pharmaceutical diversity (Spearman’s ρ = −0.56, P < 0.001; Fig. 1b). A total of 87.55% of soil phages and 90.12% of collembolan gut phages were successfully classified into either lysogenic or lytic lifestyles (Fig. S1f–g). We then assessed how phage lifestyle strategies varied in response to warming and pharmaceutical diversity. Under elevated temperature (+3℃), the proportion of lysogenic phages increased from 73.4 to 75.3% in soil (F1,54 = 14.80, P < 0.001; Fig. 1c), and from 45.6 to 50.1% in collembolan guts (F1,53 = 20.11, P < 0.001; Fig. 1d). However, increasing pharmaceutical diversity did not affect the proportion of lysogenic phages in soil (Fig. 1e), but significantly decreased the proportion in the gut, from 49.6 (control, no pharmaceutical) to 45.9% under the highest pharmaceutical diversity (6 compounds) (F3,51 = 4.92, P < 0.01; Fig. 1f). Overall, the proportion of lysogenic phages was consistently lower in the gut than in the soil (F1,77.83 = 1718.4, P < 0.001; Fig. 1g). These patterns were supported by both a linear mixed-effects model and PERMANOVA analyses. The mixed-effects model indicated negligible variance associated with pharmaceutical combinations, whereas location (gut vs. soil), temperature, and pharmaceutical diversity significantly influenced lysogenic phage proportions, with a significant temperature×diversity interaction (Table S1a). Consistently, PERMANOVA showed that pharmaceutical diversity significantly structured phage lifestyle communities in the gut but not in soil (Table S1b–c), highlighting that the observed shifts were primarily driven by diversity gradients and environmental context rather than by specific pharmaceutical combinations.

Response of bacterial antiviral defense systems and phage functional genes under environmental stress in the collembolan gut

To further explore phage–bacteria interactions following the viral community shifts described above, we examined potential virus–host linkages in the collembolan gut. Using CRISPR-match, tRNA match, and genome-homology match approaches, we identified 1059, 722, and 238 virus–host linkages involving dominant bacterial phyla Actinobacteriota, Proteobacteria, and Bacteroidota, respectively (Fig. 2a). Temperature effects on virus–host associations diverged between environmental niches. In soil, warming increased viral connections with Actinobacteriota but reduced with Proteobacteria and Bacteroidota. In contrast, collembolan gut had decreased viral associations with Actinobacteriota and increased associations with Proteobacteria and Bacteroidota (Fig. S2). Moreover, 1,123 bacterial antiviral defense systems were identified within the collembolan gut microbiome, spanning 36 types across three known defense mechanisms including abortive infection, DNA synthesis inhibition and degrading nucleic acids (Fig. 2b). These were predominantly encoded by Actinobacteriota, with restriction modification (RM) systems being the most prevalent. Moreover, we identified 29 phage-encoded AMGs in the collembolan gut and 65 in soil, spanning 16 and 20 metabolic categories, respectively (Fig. 2c). The identified AMGs were involved in nutrient acquisition and transport, amino acid metabolism, sulfur metabolism, electron transfer and energy production, with higher relative abundances observed under warming treatment compared to 20℃. Ecological roles of lysogenic phages driven by warming in collembolan gut

Given the increase in lysogenic phages under warming, we next explored their potential ecological roles in the collembolan gut. Warming significantly increased the relative abundance of the phage-encoded AMGs ACADM and nrdA (t-test, P < 0.01; Fig. 3a). Among all detected AMGs, only these two genes responded significantly to warming and were therefore selected for further analyses and validation. Structural modeling using Phyre2 predicted both proteins with 100% confidence, suggesting they likely encode fully functional enzymes (Fig. 3b). Multitable co-inertia analysis (MCOA) revealed that the first two axes captured 87.1% of the total community aggregated traits (CATs) variation in gut bacteria, with broader dispersion indicating greater strategy diversity and narrower distribution reflecting functional constraint. Warming was associated with a loss of specific bacterial life-history strategies along the positive axis of MCOA1 (Fig. 3c). At 20℃, gut bacterial communities maintained diverse strategies, resulting in clear differences among pharmaceutical diversity treatments. In contrast, at 23℃, strategies converged and variation among treatments was reduced. Notably, MCOA1 showed a significant negative correlation with motility traits (Spearman’s ρ = −0.440, P < 0.001; Table S2), and positive correlations with environment responsiveness strategies, including membrane synthesis and repair (ρ = 0.730, P < 0.001), uptake systems (ρ = 0.584, P < 0.001), and quorum sensing (ρ = 0.287, P < 0.05; Table S2). Linear regression analysis highlighted that the lysogenic phage ratio was positively associated with traits such as uptake systems (ρ = 0.513, P < 0.01), chemotaxis (ρ = 0.421, P < 0.05), and sigma factors (ρ = 0.637, P < 0.001) under warming conditions (23℃), but not at 20℃ (ρ = −0.138 to 0.013, P > 0.05; Fig. S3a–f). Potential roles of lytic phages under pharmaceutical diversity stress in the collembolan gut

We next explored the potential roles of lytic phages under pharmaceutical diversity stress. With increasing pharmaceutical diversity, the proportion of lytic phages increased significantly at both 20 (Spearman’s ρ = 0.751, P < 0.001) and 23℃ (ρ = 0.380, P < 0.05; Fig. 4a), indicating a positive association across both temperature conditions. Virus–to–host relative abundance ratio (VHR) was significantly and positively correlated with host relative abundance (ρ = 0.481, P < 0.001; Fig. S5a). In addition, alpha diversity (Shannon) of gut bacterial communities in collembolans was significantly and positively correlated with the proportion of lytic phages (ρ = 0.471, P < 0.001; Fig. S5b), suggesting that increased viral lysis may facilitate bacterial turnover and diversity maintenance. Alterations in bacterial community stability, bacteria–virus interactions under combined environmental stressors in the collembolan gut

Building on the observed responses of lysogenic and lytic phages to warming and pharmaceutical diversity, we next assessed gut bacterial community stability and bacteria–virus potential interactions under combined stressors. To assess gut microbiome stability under pharmaceutical and thermal stress, we evaluated the Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio, average variation degree (AVD), and relative abundance of stress-related genes. At 23℃, increasing pharmaceutical diversity significantly, though slightly, reduced the F/B ratio (t-test, P < 0.01; Fig. 5a) whereas AVD values were elevated at the family level (t-test, P < 0.05; Fig. 5b). KEGG annotation revealed an increased relative abundance of ROS-related genes (ropD, ropC, ropZ, trxB) and SOS response genes (recF, CHO1, ruvA, uvrB) with increasing pharmaceutical diversity at 20℃ (t-test, P < 0.05; Fig. S7a–h). To further explore potential interactions between bacteria and viruses, bacterial taxa and vOTUs with mean relative abundance > 0.001% were selected to construct bacteria–virus co-occurrence networks. Eight meta-community networks were generated based on Spearman correlations (ρ > 0.6, P < 0.05) under different treatment conditions (Fig. 5c), and topological features of subnetworks were extracted and analyzed across pharmaceutical diversity gradients. At 20℃, core network topological features including node count, edge count, connectance, degree and centralization degree showed no significant correlation with pharmaceutical diversity (ρ = 0.223, P > 0.05; Fig. S8a–e). Phage ACADM and nrdA genes facilitate host thermal adaptation

Based on the observed increase of ACADM and nrdA under warming, we synthesized the genes and subcloned them into expression vectors. They were then transformed into Escherichia coli BL21 to investigate their role in host thermal adaptation. Recombinant strains were cultured at 37 and 40℃, and intracellular ROS levels and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity were quantified to calculate fold changes under warming. Compared with the empty-vector control, strains carrying ACADM and nrdA exhibited significantly attenuated ROS accumulation (0.86- and 0.92-fold, respectively; t-test, P < 0.01; Fig. 6a). Similarly, the increases in SOD activity were also markedly lower in these strains (0.67- and 0.65-fold, respectively; t-test, P < 0.01; Fig. 6a). Discussion

Our results revealed that viral communities in collembolan guts exhibited stronger responses than those in bulk soil to both pharmaceutical diversity and fluctuating temperature. Gut viral diversity declined significantly with increasing pharmaceutical diversity, and the proportion of shared vOTUs across treatments was markedly lower in gut samples than in soil. This heightened sensitivity likely reflects the unique biochemical and physiological conditions of the collembolan gut and the potential accumulation, concentration, and transformation of pharmaceuticals within the gut environment, consistent with previous studies showing that the gut microbiome of soil collembolans is more responsive to environmental stress than soil microbial communities [41]. In contrast, the dilution effect and physical shielding provided by the soil matrix, together with the high microbial diversity and functional redundancy of soil communities [42, 43], may buffer pharmaceutical impacts and contribute to the weaker and more stable viral responses observed in bulk soil. Nevertheless, the potential influence of methodological differences, including sample processing and DNA extraction procedures, cannot be fully excluded. Overall, our findings highlight that the collembolan gut microbiome can respond to environmentally relevant pharmaceutical exposures in ways that may not be detectable in soil microbial communities, supporting its role as an early indicator of ecological disturbance with potential cascading effects on soil ecosystems and higher trophic levels.

Our study revealed divergent viral responses to environmental drivers. Increasing pharmaceutical diversity shifted phage dynamics toward lytic strategies, while diurnal temperature fluctuations favored lysogeny. This divergence may reflect adaptive responses of phages to stress. Pharmaceuticals such as ibuprofen, carbamazepine, and gemfibrozil are known to damage bacterial DNA and trigger SOS responses [40, 44], inducing a switch from lysogenic to lytic [45], allowing phages to escape damaged hosts and seek more favorable conditions. Furthermore, increasing pharmaceutical diversity may exacerbate cellular stress, further promoting the transition of phages to the lytic cycle [46]. In contrast, warming may enhance metabolic activity in poikilotherms [47], promoting lysogenic integration into host genomes and long-term persistence. Indeed, increased temperatures have been shown to shift microbial communities toward lysogenic-dominated phage lifestyles [27]. Lysogenic phages can reside within host cells, minimizing exposure to external stress and reducing immediate demand on host resources [48]. This may support host survival and adaptation under warming. Environmental changes also reshape virus–host interactions [49]. This divergence in viral responses supports our hypothesis that viruses regulate bacterial communities through different mechanisms under warming and pharmaceutical stress, prompting further mechanistic investigation.

In our study, phage infections of Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes increased under warming, while infections of Actinobacteria declined. Related studies have also shown that warming increased the relative abundance of Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes, suggesting that phage communities may undergo adaptive shifts in response to changes in host composition [50]. Moreover, our results revealed a significant decline in the relative abundance of antiviral defense systems associated with the collembolan gut bacterial community under warming. This perhaps suggests that increased temperatures may impair host antiviral systems, enhancing phage infection and regulation through lysogeny. Such changes in phage–host interactions underscore the potential for warming to profoundly impact gut ecosystem function of soil biota as exemplified by the model species used in this study, altering both microbial community dynamics and host–microbe interactions. However, limited taxonomic resolution of phage annotation prevents identification of the specific viral lineages driving these changes, constraining detailed mechanistic interpretation of phage–host responses to warming.

In dynamic and stressed gut environments, viruses may enhance their survival and transmission by modulating host metabolism and ecological functions [26]. Our study revealed an ecological convergence of viral and host life-history strategies. MCOA and trait fitting analyses showed that the proportion of lysogenic phages positively correlated with host traits involved in signal recognition, transport, and motility. These stronger associations at 23℃ indicate that warming tightens the coupling between lysogenic phages and environmentally responsive host traits, enhancing viral persistence in stressed gut micro-ecosystems. For example, studies have shown that under increasing chromium-induced stress, phage communities co-evolve with bacterial hosts in composition, replication strategies, and ecological functions to adapt to extreme environments [51]. However, the absence of host-level phenotypic data (e.g., survival, reproduction, or activity) prevents direct validation of warming stress at the host physiological level, representing a limitation of this study. Viruses may also carry AMGs to boost host metabolic capacity under stress [52], thereby indirectly promoting viral replication. Previous studies have shown that long-term warming (+1.1℃) can significantly alter the functional composition of viral AMGs [53]. In our study, 29 AMGs were identified in the collembolan gut, with ACADM and nrdA significantly enriched under warming. These two genes contain complete domains and are mainly involved in fatty acid metabolism, amino acid synthesis, and energy production [54, 55]. ACADM, a key enzyme in fatty acid β-oxidation [56], may enhance host energy efficiency and metabolic homeostasis under heat stress, supporting viral replication. This suggests that viruses can optimize their ecological fitness under warming by enhancing host metabolic resilience via AMGs and selectively associating with adaptable microbial hosts. Previous studies have reported that phage genomes often harbor AMGs involved in oxidative stress mitigation, enabling microbial hosts to better tolerate environmental perturbations [57]. Consistently, our E. coli BL21 functional assays demonstrated that strains expressing ACADM and nrdA showed markedly lower ROS accumulation and attenuated SOD responses under warming compared with the control. These results confirmed that these phage-encoded AMGs play a direct role in alleviating heat-induced oxidative stress and enhancing host thermal tolerance. Collectively, these findings highlight a critical mechanism by which viruses regulate bacterial communities and promote host thermal adaptation under warming by encoding AMGs such as ACADM and nrdA (Fig. 6b).

Our results showed that, under increasing pharmaceutical diversity, lytic phages were significantly enriched in the collembolan gut. The VHR increased and correlated positively with host relative abundance. This pattern is consistent with the “kill-the-winner” (KtW) model [58], where phages suppress dominant bacterial populations through lysis [59]. Pharmaceutical exposure can impose sublethal stress on gut bacteria, interfering with microbial physiology through membrane perturbation, oxidative stress, or metabolic disruption, thereby altering bacterial growth and competitiveness [7, 40]. Consistent with this, pharmaceutical exposure led to a marked decline in bacteria such as Cutibacterium and Nocardioides, both rich in intracellular organic matter and highly susceptible to lytic infection [60, 61]. The lysis of these dominant taxa likely released intracellular substrates, providing nutrients for non-dominant taxa and reshaping nutrient allocation within the gut microenvironment [62, 63]. In addition, exposure to high pharmaceutical diversity increased the relative abundance of Gordonia and Microbacterium. Lytic phage relative abundance was positively associated with these genera, which are known for degrading aromatic compounds [64, 65]. Correspondingly, metabolic genes involved in degradation pathways (e.g., adhE, catE) [66, 67] were significantly enriched. Thus, lytic phages may not only restructure microbial composition but also facilitate the expansion of functional taxa via nutrient release and competitive exclusion [68]. Under this scenario, viruses act as active regulators of microbiome structure and function, rather than passive stress responders. This active role enables them to modulate microbial communities, facilitating their adaptation to pollutant stress and offering a new perspective for bioremediation strategies (Fig. 6b). Long-term studies are needed to assess whether chronic pharmaceutical exposure further reshapes virus–host dynamics and leads to distinct viral adaptation patterns.

Under the combination of increasing pharmaceutical diversity and warming, the collembolan gut micro-ecosystem exhibited signs of ecological imbalance. Specifically, we observed a reduced F/B ratio, an indicator of gut bacterial community stability that reflects shifts in dominant phyla [69]. This reduction coincided with increased AVD [70] and decreased expression of oxidative stress and SOS response genes [71], collectively indicating a loss of gut microbiome resilience under thermal stress. These findings were consistent with our previous observations (warming and pesticide co-stress), where microbial stability and metabolic diversity were reduced [33] indicating impaired microbial stability and weakened ecological feedback under multiple stressors. Previous studies on collembolan gut microbial responses to environmental stressors have primarily focused on bacterial indicators [69], often overlooking the role of viruses. With growing insights from viral metagenomics, it is now recognized that gut viruses also contribute to microbial homeostasis by influencing nutrient cycling, gene transfer, and ecological stability [72].

In this study, we addressed a recognized knowledge gap by constructing bacteria–virus co-occurrence networks to explore how combined stressors, such as pharmaceutical diversity and warming, reshape the collembolan gut microbiome and to identify the functional roles of viruses. At ambient temperature (20℃), pharmaceutical diversity had limited effects on network topology. However, under warming (23℃), increasing pharmaceutical diversity enhanced virus–host interactions, with a significant increase in node number, edge count, and network centralization. A similar trend was reported in a recent microplastic study, where increasing temperatures amplified the impact of microplastic diversity on microbial networks [73]. This suggests that warming amplifies the effects of pharmaceutical diversity on community structure and function, while also reinforcing virus–host interactions, thereby driving microbial network reorganization. Under combined stress, as in this study, bacterial community imbalance and the increased complexity of bacteria–virus interaction networks underscored the potential dual role of viruses: actively infecting hosts to ensure viral persistence, while simultaneously modulating host communities to enhance resilience to intensified environmental pressures (Fig. 6b). However, it should be noted that these inferences are based on co-occurrence network analyses and thus reflect putative virus–host associations rather than confirmed interaction mechanisms. Unlike traditional dose-response studies, our findings highlight that increasing pharmaceutical diversity can trigger complex ecological responses. Furthermore, viruses emerge as central players in this process, actively reshaping microbial networks, driving functional shifts, and influencing microbiome stability under multiple environmental pressures.

Conclusion

In this study, we used a controlled microcosm experiment to systematically evaluate the combined effects of increasing non-antibiotic pharmaceutical diversity and simulated climate warming (+3℃) on the gut microbiome of collembolans. Both pharmaceutical diversity and diurnal temperature fluctuations significantly altered the composition, functional expression, and interaction networks of gut bacterial and viral communities. Viruses played a central regulatory role in microbiome responses to environmental stress. Under warming, lysogenic phages encoded AMGs to enable host bacteria to cope with heat stress. Under pharmaceutical pressure, lytic phages promoted the expansion of key functional taxa through a KtW strategy. Furthermore, bacteria–virus networks exhibited increased topological complexity under combined stress. While our results reveal diversity-driven microbial responses and virus-mediated regulatory mechanisms, they likely reflect the combined effects of co-occurring pharmaceuticals. We did not explicitly test for synergistic or antagonistic interactions among individual compounds, and because pharmaceutical residues were not quantified during exposure, the observed responses should be interpreted based on nominal mixture concentrations rather than measured internal or residual levels. Nevertheless, this study highlights the need to expand microbial ecosystem risk assessment from single stressors to multiple interacting factors, including climate warming.

Materials and methods

Collembolan, pharmaceuticals, and soils

The collembolan F. candida used in this study was provided by Aarhus University, Denmark, and has been continuously cultured in our laboratory for over ten years. Cultures were maintained on a moist substrate composed of gypsum and activated charcoal (8:1 by weight, 0.5 cm thick) under a 16:8 h light/dark cycle, at 20℃ and 75% relative humidity. Distilled water and yeast (Angel Yeast Co., Ltd.) were added twice weekly. Prior to the experiment, synchronized individuals with minimal age variation were obtained following OECD guidelines [74]. Briefly, 50 similarly sized adults were transferred to fresh culture dishes to induce egg laying. After 3 days, juveniles were collected and cultured under the same conditions until they reached 10–12 days of age.

Seven commonly prescribed human non-antibiotic pharmaceuticals were selected: carbamazepine, thioridazine, ibuprofen, gemfibrozil, cetirizine, metformin, and citalopram (Table S5a). The total pharmaceutical concentration in the treated soil was set at 1.2 mg kg–1 dry weight, representing a relatively high environmental exposure level [75–78]. In the high-diversity treatment (6 pharmaceuticals), the total concentration was evenly distributed (0.2 mg kg–1 per compound), reflecting commonly detected environmental levels while maintaining a consistent total dosage across treatments [79].

The top 0–20 cm of agricultural soil was collected from Ningbo, Zhejiang Province, China (121.92°E, 29.79°N). The soil had a pH of 6.8, electrical conductivity of 90 μS cm–1, and organic carbon content of 0.75%. Soil was air-dried in the shade, cleared of debris, and passed through a 2 mm sieve. Stock solutions of the pharmaceuticals were prepared in a small volume of ethanol, diluted with ultrapure water, and mixed with dry soil to achieve the desired concentrations. The treated soil was then left under ventilated conditions for 24 h to allow ethanol to evaporate, ensuring any residual solvent was negligible. The soils were then freeze-dried, ground, and sieved again (2 mm). Moisture content was adjusted to 40% of water holding capacity using ultrapure water. All treated soils were pre-incubated under temperature-controlled conditions for 1 week prior to use, allowing the soil to equilibrate. Control soils underwent the same procedures but without pharmaceutical addition.

Experimental design

The combined effects of temperature (20 and 23℃) and pharmaceutical diversity (0, 1, 3, and 6 pharmaceuticals) on F. candida were tested. The 23℃ treatment was selected as a biologically relevant warming stressor based on previous evidence of reduced lifespan and cumulative detrimental effects in F. candida [80, 81]. To simulate climate warming, a fluctuating regime with a 3℃ increase within a 24 h cycle was applied using a climate chamber (Memmert ICP110, Germany) programmed with AtomCONTROL software. Temperature gradually increased from 20 to 23℃ over 12 h and subsequently decreased back to 20℃ during the following 12 h, and this cycle was repeated continuously for 28 days, mimicking natural diurnal temperature variation rather than imposing constant thermal stress. Each treatment included seven replicates (n = 7, Fig. S9). In the single-compound treatment (D1), the seven pharmaceuticals were applied individually. For the three-compound (D3) and six-compound (D6) treatments, seven unique combinations were generated, with each compound appearing at equal frequency across replicates to avoid bias from compound-specific effects (Table S5b). The diversity levels of 1, 3, and 6 compounds were selected to represent a gradient of increasing pharmaceutical mixture complexity. A seven-compound treatment was not included because it would produce identical compositions across replicates. All treatments maintained the same total concentration: 1.2 mg kg–1 for single-compound treatments, 0.4 mg kg–1 per compound in D3, and 0.2 mg kg–1 per compound in D6.

Twenty synchronized collembolans were introduced into 50 mL glass beakers filled with 40 g (wet weight) of treated soil. Beakers were incubated under the designated temperature conditions for 28 days, with this exposure period corresponding to the standardized duration commonly used in F. candida ecotoxicological assays [82]. Yeast was added twice per week as food, and deionized water was used to compensate for evaporation loss. At the end of the experiment, collembolans were recovered using a water-flotation method. Gut DNA was extracted immediately after collection. Representative soil samples (~ 5 g) were also collected and stored at −20℃ for later DNA extraction and analysis.

DNA extraction from collembolan guts and soil

To remove surface microbes, collembolan samples were treated with 2% sodium hypochlorite (NaClO) for 10 s, followed by three times with 1× PBS buffer to minimize NaClO impact on gut microbiota. Under sterile conditions, the guts were dissected and transferred into 1.5 mL sterile centrifuge tubes pre-filled with 100 µL 1× PBS. Samples were homogenized using sterile pestles to release gut contents before DNA extraction. Gut DNA was extracted using a DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (QIAGEN, Germany). Soil DNA was extracted using a FastDNA Spin Kit for Soil (MP Biomedicals, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocols including lysis, centrifugation, impurity removal, and DNA elution. These kits were chosen according to the distinct characteristics of gut and soil matrices to ensure optimal DNA yield and quality [33, 83]. DNA concentrations were measured with a Nanodrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, USA) and stored at −20℃ for downstream analyses.

Experimental verification of phage ACADM and nrdA genes

The two AMGs upregulated in the collembolan gut under warming, ACADM and nrdA, were synthesized and individually cloned into a pET28a vector. An empty pET28a plasmid without an insert was used as a control. Each plasmid was transformed into E. coli BL21 cells. Recombinant strains were cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth at 37℃ with shaking at 220 rpm. When cultures reached an OD600 of 0.3, cells were loaded with a DCFH-DA probe and incubated for 4 h at either 37 (optimal growth temperature) or 40℃ (optimal +3℃). Cells were then excited at 488 nm, and fluorescence measured at 525 nm to determine intracellular ROS levels. To assess SOD activity, recombinant strains were cultured under the same conditions at 37 and 40℃. At OD600 = 0.3, SOD activity was measured using a SOD-WST-8 assay kit (Jiangsu Aidisheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Metagenomic sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

DNA libraries were prepared using a NEBNext Ultra kit (NEB, USA) and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq4000 platform (PE150) by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology (Shanghai, China). Approximately 10 Gb of raw data were generated for each soil sample and 50 Gb for each gut sample. Raw reads were subjected to quality control using standard filtering procedures. Reads containing adapter sequences were removed, as well as reads with more than 10% ambiguous bases (N) and reads in which more than 50% of bases had a quality score (Q) ≤ 10. After quality filtering, high-quality clean reads were obtained in FASTQ format. For gut samples, host sequences were removed by aligning reads to four F. candida reference genomes (GCF_002217175.1, GCA_020920555.1, GCA_020923455.1, and GCA_034694505.1) using Bowtie2 in the “–very-sensitive” mode [84]. After quality control and host sequence removal, the average numbers of clean reads were 79,490,703 ± 8,895,378 for soil samples and 53,594,186 ± 17,452,929 for gut samples. Clean reads were then assembled into contigs using MEGAHIT [85], based on the De-Bruijn graph algorithm. Contigs shorter than 500 bp were discarded, and only contigs ≥ 500 bp were retained for downstream analyses. Assembly quality was evaluated using the N50 statistic. The average N50 values were 796 bp for soil samples and 5548 bp for gut samples. Open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted using Prodigal [86]. Redundant sequences were removed using CD-HIT (95% identity, 90% coverage), with the longest sequence from each cluster retained as a representative gene. Clean reads from each sample were then mapped to the non-redundant gene catalog using Bowtie2. Gene relative abundances were calculated based on transcript per million (TPM) values.

Taxonomic annotation was performed by aligning predicted gene sequences to bacterial entries in the NCBI NR database (version: 2021.11) using DIAMOND BLASTp (e-value ≤ 1e−5) [87]. The lowest common ancestor (LCA) algorithm in MEGAN was used to assign taxonomic ranks [88]. Functional annotation was also carried out using DIAMOND, aligning non-redundant genes against the KEGG, eggNOG, and CAZy functional databases (e-value < 1e−5). Only hits with the highest bit score and HSP > 60 bits were retained [89]. Based on alignment results, the relative abundance at different functional levels was calculated as the sum of the relative abundance of genes annotated to that functional level. Genome binning was conducted using VAMB (https://github.com/RasmussenLab/vamb). Bins with completeness > 50% and contamination < 10% were selected. Taxonomic annotation of bins was performed using GTDB-tk.

Virome annotation and bioinformatics analysis

Viral sequences were identified and recovered from metagenomic data to construct the virome. First, raw reads were quality-filtered using fastp (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp, v 0.20.0). Adapter sequences at both ends were trimmed. Reads shorter than 50 bp, with an average quality score below 20, or containing ambiguous bases (N) were removed. High-quality paired-end and single-end reads were retained. Clean reads were then assembled into contigs using MEGAHIT (https://github.com/voutcn/megahit, v 1.1.2), which is based on the succinct De-Bruijn graph algorithm. Only contigs with lengths ≥ 500 bp were retained as the initial assembly results. Viral contigs were identified and taxonomically annotated based on the RefSeq viral database and other classification approaches. Redundant viral contigs were clustered using CD-HIT with thresholds of ≥ 95% sequence identity and ≥ 90% coverage [90]. The longest sequence in each cluster was selected as the representative sequence of a vOTU. Genome completeness of vOTUs was evaluated using CheckV (v1.0.1, database v1.2) [91]. Contigs classified as prophages or assigned a “not-determined” quality level were removed. Bowtie2 was used to align high-quality reads from each sample to the non-redundant contig set (95% identity) [84], and vOTU relative abundances were calculated accordingly. Taxonomic annotation of viruses was performed using the CAT tool [92]. Viral lifestyle prediction was made with Deephage v1.0. vOTUs with a lifestyle_score < 0.4 were classified as lysogenic phages, while those > 0.6 were considered lytic [93]. For vOTUs not classified by Deephage, HMMER’s “hmmscan” was used to detect lysogenic marker proteins [94]. If such proteins were present, the vOTU was defined as lysogenic; otherwise, it was considered unclassified.

Virus–host associations were predicted using three approaches: CRISPR-match, tRNA-match, and genome-homology match. CRISPR spacers were identified in bacterial genomes using CRISPRone [95] and aligned to viral genomes using BLASTn (v2.9.0 +), with criteria of ≥ 95% identity and ≤ 2 mismatches. Viral tRNA genes were predicted using tRNAscan-SE (v2.0.9) [96] under default settings and aligned to bacterial genomes using BLASTn (≥ 95% identity). Direct viral-bacterial genome alignment was also performed with BLASTn to identify prophage-like regions, using the following filters: bitscore ≥ 50, e-value ≤ 1e−3, nucleotide identity ≥ 70%, and alignment length ≤ 2500 bp [97]. Antiviral defense systems in bacterial genomes were identified using the DefenseFinder platform, based on MacSyFinder models [98]. AMGs in phages were annotated using a combination of DRAMv and VIBRANT [99]. A gene was considered a potential AMG if it had an auxiliary score < 4 and carried an M label without A, V, or T tags. The 3D structures of candidate AMGs were predicted with Phyre2 (https://www.sbg.bio.ic.ac.uk/phyre2/html/page.cgi?id=index), and only structures with 100% confidence were retained as final AMG predictions.

Data analysis

Alpha diversity indices (Shannon) of viral and bacterial communities were calculated using the “vegan” package in R (v4.4.1) [100]. Phage lifestyle proportions among treatments were compared using one-way ANOVA when the data met normality and homogeneity of variance assumptions, with post hoc pairwise comparisons performed using estimated marginal means (emmeans) and Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) adjustment to assign letter groupings. When homogeneity of variance was violated, Welch’s ANOVA was applied, followed by Games–Howell post hoc tests. To further quantify the effects of temperature, pharmaceutical diversity, and location while accounting for variability among pharmaceutical combinations, linear mixed-effects models (LMM) were applied with pharmaceutical combination as a random effect [101]. In addition, differences in phage lifestyle composition were assessed using PERMANOVA based on Bray–Curtis distances [102]. Homogeneity of multivariate dispersions was examined using betadisper, and because gut and soil communities differed significantly in dispersion, PERMANOVA was conducted separately for each habitat. Bacterial MAGs were taxonomically classified using GTDB-Tk. Core gene alignments were generated to construct a bacterial phylogenetic tree. To identify multivariate axes that best explain the variation in CATs associated with collembolan gut bacterial communities, MCOA was conducted using the “ade4” package in R [103], based on life-history traits (Tables S3 and S4) and gene annotations from the eggNOG, CAZy, and KEGG databases. All linear regressions were performed using “ggplot2” [104]. Spearman correlations were used to assess significance, with P-values labeled on plots. Procrustes analysis, based on 999 permutations, was conducted using the “vegan” packages to compare the structural or functional similarity between microbial communities [100]. Lower M2 indicated better matching between microbial communities. LEfSe was used to identify any microbial taxa in the collembolan gut that were potentially sensitive to pharmaceutical diversity levels. Bacteria–virus interaction networks were constructed using the subgraph() function in “igraph”. Pairs with Spearman’s ρ > 0.6 and P < 0.05 were retained [105, 106]. Only bacterial taxa and vOTUs with mean relative abundance > 0.001% were included in the network; those > 0.002% were defined as representative nodes. Subnetworks were extracted from the global network to calculate sample-specific topological features. These features were then correlated with pharmaceutical diversity using Spearman analysis.
