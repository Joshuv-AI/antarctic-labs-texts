Artificial intelligence for pancreatic cyst dysplasia grading: a multicenter endoscopic ultrasound study
Authors: Miguel Mascarenhas Saraiva, Joana Mota, Belén Agudo, Francisco Mendes, Jessica Widmer, Tiago Ribeiro, António Pinto da Costa, Miguel Martins, Maria João Almeida, Daniel de la Iglesia, Carlos Esteban, Ana Garcia de Paredes, Maria Moris, Mateus F de Carvalho, Marcos Lera, João Ferreira, Filipe Vilas-Boas, Pedro Moutinho-Ribeiro, Susana Lopes, Mariano Gonzalez-Haba, Eduardo G De Moura, Guilherme Macedo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsPancreatic cystic lesions (PCLs) are increasingly detected because of the widespread use of imaging techniques. Among them, mucinous PCLs carry a higher malignancy risk, with intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms (IPMNs) being the most frequent subtype. Accurate stratification based on the degree of dysplasia—low-grade dysplasia (LGD) versus high-grade dysplasia or carcinoma (HGD/C)—is essential to guide clinical management and avoid unnecessary surgical interventions. This study aimed to develop and evaluate a deep learning model for stratifying IPMNs into HGD/C and LGD using endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) images.MethodsThis multicenter study included EUS images collected from 5 centers across Spain, Brazil, and the United States. Ground truth classification of IPMNs was established through cytologic and biochemical analysis of cyst fluid, EUS-guided through-the-needle biopsy, or surgical specimens. A deep learning model was trained to distinguish LGD from HGD/C. Model performance was assessed on the basis of sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and area under the precision–recall curve.ResultsA total of 51,046 EUS images were extracted from 30 examinations performed at 5 centers in Portugal, Spain, Brazil, and the United States. The model distinguished IPMNs with HGD/C from those with LGD with a sensitivity of 95.7%, a specificity of 88.7%, and an overall accuracy of 87.2%. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve was 0.951.ConclusionsTo our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to evaluate the potential of an artificial intelligence model for dysplasia grading of IPMNs. Prospective validation of our model is necessary to ensure clinical benefit.

Introduction

The detection of pancreatic cystic lesions (PCLs) has increased significantly in recent years, because of the widespread adoption of advanced imaging modalities.1,2 This heterogeneous group of lesions often presents with overlapping morphologic and imaging features, making their diagnosis particularly challenging.3 Although most PCLs are benign, a subset—particularly mucinous pancreatic cystic lesions (M-PCLs)—carries a significant risk of malignancy. The risk associated with M-PCLs varies according to lesion type and the degree of dysplasia. Accurate classification of these lesions is essential for guiding clinical management. It enables the avoidance of overtreatment, such as unnecessary surgical treatments and follow-up appointments, while also reducing the likelihood of missed opportunities for early diagnosis and intervention.1

Intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms (IPMNs) of the pancreas are the most common type of M-PCLs. They are characterized by their connection to the pancreatic ductal system and their ability to produce mucin.4 IPMNs are histologically classified into low-grade dysplasia (LGD) or high-grade dysplasia or carcinoma (HGD/C). Distinguishing between these 2 categories is critical for determining the appropriate clinical pathway—decisions between surgical intervention (in cases of HGD/C) and surveillance (for most IMPNs with LGD). This stratification involves clinical, laboratory, and imaging factors that help assess the degree of dysplasia.5

Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) plays a pivotal role in the evaluation of PCLs, as recommended by the 2024 Kyoto guidelines, by offering high-resolution imaging and enabling targeted tissue sampling. This contributes significantly to diagnostic accuracy, particularly in the assessment of IPMNs with suspected HGD/C.1 Nonetheless, despite the available tools, there are still significant challenges to overcome in accurately stratifying IPMNs. For example, traditional diagnostic methods, such as cyst fluid cytology, have low sensitivity (as low as 28.7%), although their specificity remains high.1

The application of artificial intelligence (AI) in medical practice is rapidly expanding, with proven applications across multiple fields, including pathology, radiology, and others. In the context of pancreatic lesions, AI models have shown promising potential to enhance diagnostic precision, particularly in differentiating LGD from HGD/C in IPMNs, thereby supporting more accurate and timely clinical decision-making.6

The aim of this multicenter study was to develop and validate an AI model capable of distinguishing IPMNs with HGD/C from those with LGD.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a retrospective, observational, multicenter study using EUS data collected from 5 medical centers across Spain, Brazil, and the United States.

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they had undergone EUS for the evaluation of PCLs, had high-quality video recordings available, and had a conformed diagnosis. An IPMN has been confirmed on the basis of a combination of morphologic criteria and cytologic or biochemical analysis of cyst fluid or histopathologic examination of through-the-needle biopsy or surgical specimens. PCLs were considered mucinous if cytology or histologic specimen revealed mucinous epithelial cells or, in their absence, carcinoembryonic antigen fluid levels >192 ng/mL and glucose levels <50 mg/dL.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and all data were anonymized in compliance with applicable data protection regulations.

Data collection

We collected EUS video recordings from 30 patients across 5 international medical centers: New York University (NYU) Langone Hospital, New York, NY, USA; Hospital Universitario Puerta de Hierro Majadahonda, Madrid, Spain; Hospital Vila Nova Star, São Paulo, Brazil; Hospital Universitario Marqués de Valdecilla, Santander, Spain, and Hospital Universitario Ramón y Cajal, Madrid, Spain.

Still images were extracted both from static frames acquired during the procedure and through frame decomposition of full-length video recordings, using the VideoLAN Client (software used for video processing) (VideoLan Project, Paris, France) media player. All images were reviewed by 2 gastroenterologists (M.L. and M.G-H.) with expertise in diagnostic EUS, with at least 3 years of autonomous EUS practice and over 500 previous diagnostic EUS examinations; nonrelevant or low-quality frames (blurred, showing <50% of the lesion diameter, lesion obscured by acoustic shadow or Doppler) were excluded.

The final data set consisted of 51,046 high-quality EUS images, including 42,914 images of IPMNs with LGD and 8132 images of IPMNs with HGD/C. Demographic and clinical data (age, gender, and diagnosis) were retrieved from the electronic medical records of each institution. All identifying information was removed, and each case was assigned a unique anonymized identifier.

All EUS procedures were carried out with linear echoendoscopes under sedation managed by an anesthesiologist.

Most examinations were conducted using the Olympus GF-UCT180 endoscope and Olympus EU-ME2 ultrasound (US) processor (Olympus Medical, Tokyo, Japan). Additional devices included the endoscope Olympus GF-UC140 and the US processors Hitachi ARIETTA 850 (Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan) and Aloka Alpha 5 (Hitachi Aloka, Tokyo, Japan). The definitive diagnosis of each M-PCL was established based on the following: (1) histopathologic analysis from surgical specimens; (2) tissue obtained by EUS-guided through-the-needle biopsy; and (3) cytologic evaluation of aspirated cyst fluid. IPMNs were classified histologically as either LGD or HGD/C, based on the highest degree of dysplasia. Cases of neuroendocrine tumors or solid pseudopapillary neoplasms were excluded.

Development of the model

A deep learning model was developed to automatically detect and differentiate IPMNs as either LGD or HGD/C. From a total 30 examinations, 51,046 EUS images were extracted. The data set was divided into 3 nonoverlapping subsets for training (70.0%), validation (20.0%), and testing (10.0%). The split was structured to ensure a representative distribution of lesion grade, image acquisition devices, and centers, using an image-level split. The study flowchart is shown in Figure 1.

Study flowchart for the training and testing stages. CNN, Convolutional neural network; EUS, endoscopic ultrasound; HGD/C, high-grade dysplasia or carcinoma; IPMN, intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm; LGD, low-grade dysplasia; NYU, New York University.

A pretrained YOLO11 nano-model (Ultralytics, Frederick, Md, USA) was retrained over 10 epochs using transfer learning on a proprietary data set of images and annotations. The Ultralytics SDK (Ultralytics) facilitated the loading and retraining of this model. Key parameters included a learning rate of 0.01, an image size of 224×224 pixels, and a batch size of 32. The model used Ultralytics' default composite loss for detection models, which combines box loss, classification loss, and distribution focal loss. Standard data augmentation techniques were applied, including scaling (0.5 mm), translation (0.1 mm), horizontal flipping (0.5 mm), and mosaic augmentation (1.0 mm).

Model performance and statistical analysis

Each model's prediction was compared with the reference standard diagnosis. Model performance was evaluated using weighted average metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, harmonic mean of precision and recall (F1-score), area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUROC) curve, and area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC), using the test data set. No formal sample size calculation was performed. The number of cases included reflects all available patients with high-quality EUS recordings and confirmed diagnoses across the participating centers. No hypothesis testing or P values were reported because the focus of this study was on model performance rather than intergroup comparisons.

Results

Clinical and demographical data

A total of 30 EUS procedures were included, conducted between June 2020 and July 2024, across 5 international centers: Hospital Universitario Puerta de Hierro Majadahonda (n = 14), Hospital Universitario Ramón y Cajal (n = 1), Hospital Universitario Marqués de Valdecilla (n = 3), Hospital Vila Nova Star (n = 1), and NYU Langone Hospital (n = 11).

The mean patient age was 68.2 (9.3) years. The gender distribution was equal: 15 female (50.0%) and 15 male (50.0%) patients. Among the 30 cases, 25 (83.3%) ultimately had cytologic or histologic evidence of LGD, and 5 (16.7%) had evidence of HGD/C. Supplementary Table 1 available online at www.igiejournal.org describes the distribution of cases across diagnostic methods for dysplasia grading. A total of 51,046 images were extracted: 42,914 LGD images and 8132 HGD/C images.

Performance of the deep learning model

The model provided real-time classification and localization of IPMNs during EUS, as demonstrated in Figure 2. Bounding boxes visually identified the lesion, providing simultaneous prediction for the grade of dysplasia.

The deep learning model achieved a weighted sensitivity of 95.7% (95% confidence interval [CI], 92.1-99.3), a specificity of 86.5 (95% CI, 77.7-95.3), and an accuracy of 91.1% (95% CI, 85.9-96.3) on the test set. The weighted F1-score was 0.9. The model reached a weighted AUROC of 0.951 and AUPRC of 0.983 (Fig. 4).

When analyzed per class, the model reached a sensitivity of 97.0% (95% CI, 94.6-99.4) and a specificity of 88.4% (95% CI, 77.0-99.8) for HGD/C and a sensitivity of 95.4% (95% CI, 91.1-99.7) and a specificity of 86.1% (95% CI, 75.8-96.4) for LGD.

The detection model falsely identified lesions in a total of 792 frames of the validation data set, corresponding to a false-positive detection rate of 7.2% (Fig. 5A and B). The rate of false-negative predictions was 2.6% (Fig. 5C-E).

Discussion

EUS constitutes a prime method for the diagnostic evaluation of PCLs, combining high-resolution imaging with the ability to perform tissue sampling. This is particularly relevant in the case of IPMNs, which are a highly prevalent group of PCLs with significant heterogeneity regarding clinical outcomes.7 Estimating the risk of progression to more-advanced lesions, including pancreatic cancer, is crucial to implementing safe and cost-effective surveillance programs. Although the updated Kyoto 2024 recommendations have reinforced the role of EUS, notably for its superior spatial resolution and ability to identify high-risk stigmata, risk estimates are predominantly achieved through cyst fluid analysis or histopathologic analysis of cystic epithelial lining. Traditional diagnostic methods, such as cyst fluid cytology, maintain limited sensitivity, reported as low as 28.7%,8,9 and other ancillary tests, such as EUS through-the-needle biopsy, have a non-negligible risk of adverse events.10 Newer molecular techniques based on next-generation sequencing techniques have been introduced, but are neither widely validated nor available.11

The continuously evolving role of AI is expected to overcome the shortcomings of standard techniques. Our AI model achieved strong diagnostic performance in differentiating IPMNs with HGD/C from those with LGD with a sensitivity of 95.7%, a specificity of 88.7%, and an AUPRC of 0.983 on the test set. These encouraging results underscore the potential of these algorithms for stratifying dysplasia grades of IPMNs during EUS procedures. The findings of this study may be regarded as a first step toward the development of an accurate software to assist in the selection of patients for the most appropriate treatment or follow-up strategy. This is particularly important in this setting because, even when high-risk stigmata criteria are used to guide surgical decisions, only 37% had invasive carcinoma, whereas 63% had noninvasive lesions, including LGD.12 Even the implementation of advanced modalities such as contrast-enhanced EUS may fail to detect subtle features indicative of HGD/C. Inaccuracy in the stratification of high-risk lesions contributes to the overtreatment of indolent lesions and missed or delayed intervention in malignant lesions. In this context, our study provides compelling evidence that AI can help address key limitations of current diagnostic approaches. Moreover, our model, which included images from different endoscopes and US processors from centers across 3 continents (Europe, North America, and South America), achieved an accuracy of 87.2% in distinguishing HGD/C from LGD, thus demonstrating clinically relevant performance while maintaining generalizability across multiple centers and imaging devices.

The integration of AI models for the characterization of IPMNs has been evaluated in previous studies. Corral et al13 developed, in 2019, a convolutional neural network (CNN) aiming to identify high-risk lesions, particularly those with HGD/C, using magnetic resonance images. The model included data from 139 patients and demonstrated a sensitivity and specificity of 75% and 78%, respectively. Overall, the performance of the model was not superior to the application of standard criteria as suggested by the main guidelines at that time.14,15 More recently, deep learning algorithms have been applied to standard EUS for histologic grading. Schulz et al16 developed a CNN based on a small number of images (n = 3355) from 43 patients with IPMNs, all of which had histologic confirmation. The performance of the CNN model was evaluated using a pool of 1823 images. Their system reached an overall accuracy of 99.6%, overcoming the estimates for the application of criteria defined in any clinical guidelines.14, 15, 16, 17, 18 Although the latter study includes patients with IPMN dysplasia grade confirmed by surgery, the conclusions drawn from the reported results are limited by the very low number of included images. In contrast to these studies, our multicenter report is the first, to our knowledge, to include data from 3 continents, including a high volume of histologically confirmed frames for training, validation, and final assessment of the model's performance. The introduction of AI presents several advantages compared with conventional analysis of medical imaging. Unlike conventional imaging-based assessments, which rely on variable interpretations of cyst size, mural nodules, or duct dilation, the AI model produces consistent and reproducible predictions directly from image data. This AI-powered technology has the potential to complement guideline-based strategies and enhance decision-making confidence among endoscopists. The clinical implications of these findings are substantial. Integrating AI models into EUS workflows could enhance dysplasia grading across institutions, reduce operator variability, and potentially minimize disparities between centers with different levels of expertise. Our confusion matrix analysis revealed a low number of false-positive results (7.2%) and even lower false-negative rates (2.6%). This cautious approach by the model minimizes the risk of missing high-risk lesions that require invasive treatment. Moreover, in line with the patient-centered approach of the Kyoto 2024 guidelines, this tool could reduce unnecessary surgical procedures and psychological burden, ultimately enhancing patient outcomes and lowering health care costs. More recently, AI applied to emerging techniques such as EUS-guided needle-based confocal laser endomicroscopy has shown potential to accurately identify patients with high-risk lesions.19 However, these ancillary techniques are not widely available and present high costs, thus limiting the applicability of these technologies.

This study has several notable strengths. First, it included a large and diverse data set, consisting of 51,046 EUS images, collected from 5 international centers across 3 continents, with careful attention to image quality and unequivocal histopathologic confirmation. The multicenter design, and inclusion of several models of endoscopes and US processors, enhances external validity and supports the model's interoperability across different imaging systems and acquisition protocols. Second, the model addresses a clinically relevant classification problem—dysplasia grading in IPMNs—which is essential for guiding patient management. Lastly, the model demonstrated excellent performance metrics, with high sensitivity, specificity, and AUPRC, indicating strong potential for integration into clinical practice to support more accurate and timely diagnostic decision-making. In addition to dysplasia grading, the model is capable of real-time lesion identification, visually delineating regions of interest with bounding boxes (Fig. 2), thereby enhancing interpretability and supporting intraprocedural decision-making. This functionality provides clinical and technical advantages by improving diagnostic transparency, guiding interventions, and reinforcing the confidence of the user in the technology.

Despite its promising results, this study also has some limitations that must be acknowledged. First, the study's retrospective nature limits the procedure standardization and does not allow for complete control of potential biases, namely selection bias. Furthermore, the limited patient sample represents a major limitation. Third, although the model was trained on a multicenter data set, subsequent validation in an independent external cohort is required to ensure the generalizability of the results. Fourth, the data set was unbalanced toward a higher proportion of LGD cases, and no explicit class imbalance strategies were applied. Although this imbalance is anticipated from real-life prevalence, it may affect the model's performance. Moreover, it must be acknowledged that the number of frames per included patient varied significantly (median, 229; interquartile range, 1-1680), which may create a potential source of bias. Fifth, although patient-level splitting was not applied, the data set was split at the lesion level to introduce a degree of spatial independence and avoid obvious data leakage. Finally, these results do not translate into direct clinical benefit–such benefit must be demonstrated in prospective studies comparing the performance of the model against standard practice. In this subject, it must be acknowledged that this study presents noncalibrated raw performance estimates, which are not directly interpretable for the estimation of clinical benefit. Finally, other PCLs or solid lesions with potential cystic degeneration, such as neuroendocrine neoplasms, were contemplated in this exploratory study; therefore, further studies with larger data sets should account for these lesions.

Our study presents a novel AI-based approach for differentiating the grade of dysplasia in IPMNs, transitioning from still-frame analysis to dynamic, real-time clinical decision support.

However, future prospective studies are needed to validate the model's performance in real-world settings, explore its integration with EUS equipment, and evaluate its clinical impact on patient management and health care resource utilization.
