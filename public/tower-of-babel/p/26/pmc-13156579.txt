Single-Center Perspective of Glomerular Pathology in Patients Treated with Checkpoint Inhibitors
Authors: Arjunmohan Mohan, Cristina Riaza-Ortiz, Belinda Galeano, Salvatore Mignano, Sam T Albadri, Alessia Buglioni, Mariam P Alexander, Sandra M Herrmann
Published: 2026

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed cancer treatment but are associated with a spectrum of immune-related adverse events reported in upto 85% of treated patients.1 Renal immune-related adverse events are less frequent (2%–5%) and commonly present as acute interstitial nephritis, whereas glomerular disease has traditionally been considered uncommon.1 However, reports and small series increasingly describe diverse glomerular lesions after ICI exposure, with potentially serious renal consequences.2, 3, 4, 5 We report an 11-year single-center cohort of biopsy-proven glomerular disease in patients treated with ICIs and discuss their clinical course, including management and outcomes.

Results

Between January 2014 and February 2025, 211 native kidney biopsies were performed in patients receiving ICIs (Supplementary Methods). Among these, 114 biopsies (54%) demonstrated acute interstitial nephritis with or without acute tubular injury, and 69 (33%) showed isolated tubular injury. The remaining 28 biopsies (13%) revealed glomerular pathology and comprised the analytic cohort (Supplementary Figure S1).

Patient characteristics are outlined in Supplementary Table S1. Eight patients had preexisting chronic kidney disease (CKD) (CKD3a, n = 5; CKD3b, n = 2; and CKD4, n = 1). Baseline proteinuria data were available for 17 of 28 patients (61%); proteinuria was higher in patients with nonthrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) (median urine protein-to-creatinine ratio: 1.6 g/g) than in patients with TMA (0.15 g/g).

Anti-PD-1 therapy was the most common ICI exposure. Twenty-three patients (82%) received ICI monotherapy, and 5 (18%) received combination ICI at the time of kidney injury. Exposure to medications associated with TMA (bevacizumab, lenvatinib, cabozantinib, axitinib, gemcitabine, pazopanib, and ramucirumab) was common (82% overall) and noted in all TMA cases (15/15) and less frequent in non-TMA cases (8/13, 61%). Concurrent ICI and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-pathway therapy was more common in TMA than in non-TMA (13/15 [87%] vs. 4/13 [31%]). The median duration of ICI exposure before biopsy was longer in TMA (12 [interquartile range: 6–23] months) than in non-TMA (4 [interquartile range: 2–5] months). ICIs were withheld or discontinued before biopsy in 23 patients (82%). In the TMA cohort, VEGF-directed therapy was withheld before biopsy in 10 of 15 patients (Supplementary Table S1).

Biopsy indications included acute kidney injury (often with proteinuria; 61%), nephrotic-range proteinuria or nephritic syndrome (32%), and isolated microscopic hematuria or proteinuria (7%). TMA was the most frequent lesion (15/28, 54%), characterized by renal-limited disease without clinical evidence of systemic TMA. Among non-TMA lesions, IgA nephropathy accounted for 21% of cases, including 1 crescentic IgA vasculitis. Single cases of membranous nephropathy, cryoglobulinemic glomerulonephritis (GN), proliferative GN with monoclonal IgG deposits, and pauci-immune GN were observed (Figure 1). No cases of minimal change disease, C3 glomerulopathy, or lupus nephritis were identified. Mild concurrent acute interstitial nephritis was present in 3 biopsies (11%). At biopsy, the median serum creatinine was 1.84 mg/dl, and the median urine protein-to-creatinine ratio was 2.6 g/g in both groups (Supplementary Table S2).

In the non-TMA subgroup, 8 patients (62%) received immunosuppression, most commonly corticosteroids (7/13, 54%). Two of these had concurrent acute interstitial nephritis on biopsy (Supplementary Figure S2). Rituximab was administered to 3 patients: 2 with severe IgA nephropathy (1 crescentic/vasculitic; 1 nephrotic) and 1 with cryoglobulinemic GN (Table 1). In the crescentic IgA vasculitis case, rituximab plus corticosteroids produced partial, transient improvement, with relapse during steroid taper. One patient with a remote history of cryoglobulinemic GN related to a low-grade lymphoproliferative disorder (hepatitis C serologies negative), previously in renal and hematologic remission, relapsed while receiving ICI therapy; rituximab provided transient disease control; however, kidney function declined and progressed to end-stage kidney disease.

Supportive care was the predominant strategy in TMA, used in 13 of 15 patients (87%). Of these, 9 patients received antiproteinuric therapy with renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system blockade and/or sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors, initiated or uptitrated after diagnosis to address hypertension and proteinuria. Two TMA cases received empiric eculizumab. In 1 case associated with ramucirumab and pembrolizumab, evidence of terminal complement activation was present, and eculizumab led to substantial improvement in proteinuria, permitting continuation of ICI and VEGF-pathway therapy under complement blockade, later transitioned to ravulizumab. In contrast, a gemcitabine-associated TMA case without sustained complement activation did not respond and progressed to end-stage kidney disease and hospice care. Neither patient had systemic hemolysis, and atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome and thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura were excluded.

Overall, 21 patients (75%) permanently discontinued ICI therapy. ICI rechallenge occurred in 7 patients (25%): 4 with TMA (27%) and 3 with non-TMA lesions (23%) (Supplementary Figure S2). Recurrent kidney injury occurred in 3 of 7 (43%) rechallenged patients (2 with TMA and 1 with IgA nephropathy). One patient with complement-activated TMA maintained stable kidney function during rechallenge under ongoing complement inhibition, whereas 2 non-TMA patients (IgA nephropathy and diabetic nodular sclerosis) tolerated rechallenge without recurrent acute kidney injury (Table 1). Among the 5 patients receiving VEGF-directed therapy at biopsy, this treatment was discontinued in all cases, with subsequent oncologic management individualized by treating oncologists.

Median follow-up was 9 (interquartile range: 2–22) months. Among 26 evaluable patients, 11 (42%) achieved complete renal recovery (6/15 [40%] with TMA and 5/11 [45%] without TMA), and 11 (42%) had partial recovery. Four patients (15%; 3 with TMA and 1 with cryoglobulinemic GN) remained dialysis-dependent; all died within 1 year. Overall mortality was 61% (17/28), driven primarily by cancer progression (Supplementary Figure S3).

Discussion

This single-center cohort provides clinicopathologic insight into glomerular disease occurring during ICI therapy. Three findings stand out. First, TMA was the dominant lesion, accounting for more than half of cases. Second, latency and exposure context differed: TMA occurred later and exclusively in patients exposed to known TMA-associated agents. However, VEGF-directed therapy was observed in several non-TMA cases, indicating that not all VEGF-exposed patients developed TMA. Third, although renal recovery was common, overall survival was poor and reflected advanced malignancy rather than kidney disease.

These observations underscore that glomerular disease during ICI therapy is heterogeneous and not uniformly immune-mediated. VEGF-pathway inhibition is a well-recognized cause of hypertension, proteinuria, and renal-limited TMA, whereas immune-mediated TMA or thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura directly attributable to ICIs appears uncommon.3, 4, 5, 6 The universal presence of VEGF-pathway inhibitor or gemcitabine exposure in our TMA cases supports drug-associated endothelial injury as the predominant mechanism. Because clinical features lack specificity, kidney biopsy is often required to distinguish immune-mediated GN from drug-associated TMA and to guide appropriate management.1,7

In contrast, non-TMA lesions likely reflect immune dysregulation triggered or unmasked by ICIs. IgA nephropathy was the most frequent immune-mediated lesion. Although incidental IgA nephropathy cannot be excluded, the temporal association, disease severity, and treatment response in several cases support a link with ICI exposure.3, 4, 5,8,9 Published series indicate generally favorable outcomes in ICI-associated IgA nephropathy, particularly in nonproliferative disease, whereas crescentic or vasculitic forms show less consistent recovery and a higher relapse risk after rechallenge.3, 4, 5,9 Consistently, 83% of our IgA cases achieved partial or complete recovery, with poor outcomes confined to crescentic disease or underlying advanced CKD. Membranous nephropathy and pauci-immune GN were histologically similar to idiopathic disease but demonstrated clinically relevant differences, including relapse with ICI reexposure in pauci-immune GN case.5,S1–S3

Two biopsies demonstrated advanced diabetic glomerulosclerosis, underscoring the importance of biopsy in avoiding misattribution of kidney injury to ICIs, particularly in patients with preexisting CKD or proteinuria. Decisions regarding ICI rechallenge remain complex; recurrence of acute kidney injury occurred in nearly half of rechallenged patients, emphasizing the need for individualized, multidisciplinary assessment and close monitoring. Despite the limited sample size and lack of a control group, the observed recurrence highlights the need for individualized, multidisciplinary decision-making, careful patient counseling, and close monitoring when considering ICI rechallenge.

Limitations include small sample size, retrospective single-center design, selection bias toward biopsied patients with more severe or atypical presentations, and variable follow-up with limited information on long-term outcomes. In addition, the absence of a clearly defined at-risk denominator prevents estimation of true incidence; the observed 13% reflects the biopsy cohort rather than all ICI-treated patients with kidney injury.

In summary, glomerular disease during ICI therapy is clinically meaningful and often multifactorial. TMA frequently reflects concurrent exposure to VEGF-pathway inhibitors or other TMA-associated agents; whereas immune-mediated glomerulopathies are less common and diverse. Kidney biopsy remains central to diagnosis and management, particularly when considering immunosuppression or ICI rechallenge. Larger prospective studies are needed to refine risk stratification and guide management strategies.
