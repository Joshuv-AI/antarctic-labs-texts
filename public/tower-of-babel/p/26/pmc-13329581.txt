Body mass index threshold for obesity should be personalized based on polygenic score: Analysis of european and east asian biobanks
Authors: Min Seo Kim, Injeong Shim, Beomsu Kim, Ron Do, Woong-Yang Park, Amit V Khera, Pradeep Natarajan, Patrick T Ellinor, Akl C Fahed, Hong-Hee Won
Published: 2026

Introduction

Despite the complex biology of obesity, we continue to use the oversimplified measure and fixed cutoffs for obesity of body mass index (BMI). To overcome the limitations of simplified approaches, efforts have been made to develop biological BMI [1,2]. Watanabe et al. demonstrated that metabolomics-inferred BMI and proteomics-inferred BMI captured the molecular heterogeneity of obesity better than a conventional BMI, and identified individuals with normal weight but disrupted metabolic health [2]. As such, adopting biological BMI could enhance disease risk prediction and alleviate stigma by propagating the nuance of environmental and biological determinants of obesity. Given that weight is highly heritable and polygenic score (PGS) captures biological drivers of obesity, we hypothesize that a genetically-inferred BMI, one that is personalized based on one’s PGS, is a better model to define and risk-stratify people living with obesity (PLwO).

We propose a genetically-inferred BMI to redefine cutoffs, moving away from the current one-size-fits-all approach used to define obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m2 in European and BMI ≥25 kg/m2 in Asian populations). It is plausible that European individuals with a high genetic risk for obesity might reach an equivalent risk of obesity-related morbidities even before their BMI reaches 30 kg/m2, while the opposite could be true for those with a low genetic risk. Here, we demonstrate this concept using two large European and East Asian biobanks – 335,835 European ancestry participants of UK Biobank and 68,596 Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study (KoGES) participants. Unlike clinical, proteomic, and metabolomic data which fluctuate during the life course, germline variants and PGS are present from the birth and remain constant. As genomic data become increasingly available in a future ‘genome-first’ health system, the genetically-inferred BMI concept may apply to diverse clinical settings.

Methods

Among the 335,835 (53.6 % female, mean age 57.37 ± 7.99 years, mean BMI 27.45 ± 4.71 kg/m2) participants in UK Biobank, 81,519 (24.3 %) met the classic criterion of PLwO based on a BMI ≥30 kg/m2. Among the 68,596 (64.0 % female, mean age 54.16 ± 8.31 years, mean BMI 24.12 ± 2.82 kg/m2) East Asian ancestry participants in KoGES, 23,726 (34.6 %) met the classic criterion of PLwO based on a BMI ≥25 kg/m2. We calculated ancestry-specific PGS for BMI, which were normally distributed. This study was conducted using the UK Biobank resource under application number 33,002, approved by the North West Multicentre Research Ethical Committee (11/NW/0382), with informed consent obtained from all participants. KoGES participants were recruited from the national health examinee registry and provided informed consent.

We compared conventionally-defined group of PLwO (BMI ≥30 kg/m2 in European and BMI ≥25 kg/m2 in Asian populations) to those within a normal BMI range (BMI 18.5–24.9 kg/m2 in European and BMI 18.5–22.9 kg/m2 in Asian populations) to determine the risk of the outcomes associated with obesity. Genetically-inferred BMI thresholds for obesity were then determined by identifying BMI cutoffs within each genetic risk group that corresponded to an equivalent risk level for composite cardiovascular diseases (CVD), CVD-related mortality, and all-cause mortality as observed in conventionally-defined PLwO compared to a normal BMI group.

Results

Among European ancestry participants, those with a high genetic risk (top PGS quintile) had a lower BMI threshold of 29.2 kg/m2 (95 % CI 29.0–29.4) and those with a low genetic risk (bottom PGS quintile) had a higher BMI threshold of 31.0 kg/m2 (95 % CI 30.4–31.5) for an equivalent risk of composite CVD outcomes (Fig. 1A). Among East Asian ancestry participants, those with a high genetic risk had a lower BMI threshold of 24.2 kg/m2 (95 % CI 23.6–24.9) and those with a low genetic risk had a higher BMI threshold of 25.4 kg/m2 (95 % CI 25.0–26.3) (Fig. 1A). Similar patterns were observed for CVD-related mortality (high genetic risk: 28.8 kg/m2; intermediate: 30.4 kg/m2; low: 30.8 kg/m2) and all-cause mortality (high: 29.5 kg/m2; intermediate: 30.0 kg/m2; low: 31.2 kg/m2) in UK Biobank.

(A) Genetically-inferred BMI thresholds based on equivalent risk of composite cardiovascular diseases (CVD) in European (N = 335,835; left) and East Asian (N = 68,596; right) ancestry participants. Equivalent risks for the outcomes (horizontal dashed lines) were calculated by comparing the risk of a given outcome in the conventionally-defined people living with obesity (PLwO) group (BMI ≥30 kg/m2 for European and BMI ≥25 kg/m2 for Asian) with the reference group (BMI 18.5–24.9 kg/m2 for European and BMI 18.5–22.9 kg/m2 for Asian). Models were adjusted for age, sex, genotype array, and the first 10 principal components of genetic ancestry. Notably, we observed that individuals with high PGS more often transitioned to conventionally-defined obesity in their follow-up BMI measurement than individuals with low PGS (Fig. 1B), indicating that PGS may reflect a future trajectory toward obesity. As such, adjusting BMI thresholds by genetic data would prove helpful in overcoming cross-sectional nature of BMI measurement and establishing more longitudinally nuanced BMI.

Personalizing the BMI threshold based on PGS may enable earlier and more precise identification of PLwO who would benefit from treatment. Current guidelines recommend that behavioral interventions be offered to adults with a BMI ≥25 kg/m2, [3] and anti-obesity medications should be considered for individuals with a BMI ≥30 kg/m2 or for individuals with comorbidities and BMI ≥27 kg/m2[3,4]. With the recent introduction of glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1) agonists as powerful weight loss medications that can even reduce major adverse cardiovascular outcomes by 20 %, [5] accurate identification of PLwO is more important than ever. We observed a dose-responsive decrease in BMI thresholds with increasing BMI PGS in both European (Fig. 1C) and East Asian ancestry participants, suggesting that individuals with high genetic risk should be directed toward interventions earlier than those with low genetic risk despite the same BMI. Compared to the conventional BMI threshold, the genetically-inferred threshold increased the PLwO population by 15.8 % and 22.4 % in the high genetic risk group of European and East Asian (Fig. 1D) ancestry participants, respectively. Conversely, in the low genetic risk group, the genetically-inferred threshold decreased the PLwO population by 28.8 % in European and 19.4 % in East Asian ancestry participants.

Discussion

Personalizing the risk continuum for conventionally-defined clinical measures based on polygenic background is an underexplored opportunity for precision medicine. Yet little research has demonstrated the clinical utility of personalizing clinical cutoff values based on PGS. In one prior example, the prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels–a screening tool for prostate cancer–was adjusted based on genotype information, improving prediction accuracy for aggressive prostate cancer, with 6–7 % of the population shifting above or below the conventionally-defined PSA threshold [6,7]. We observed similar findings for BMI, where net 3.9 % (European ancestry) and 6.1 % (East Asian ancestry) of participants above the conventionally-defined normal BMI range were subject to altered clinical decisions when accounting for genetic risk. For a trait like BMI ubiquitously used in public health, the impact of this shift at a population level translates into massive numbers [8]. In a “genome-first” future health, the clinical utility of PGS may need to be expanded to personalize clinically-defined thresholds and subsequently personalize eligibility for interventions. This initial application to BMI might inspire personalizing other routinely collected continuous measures, such as systolic blood pressure, HbA1c, and LDL cholesterol levels.

This study has limitations. First, we used BMI as a proxy for obesity, and the index may not sufficiently specify fat distribution. We selected BMI because PGS for BMI robustly predicts the risk for obesity and cardiometabolic diseases, [9] and most clinical recommendations use BMI to direct obesity management, [3,4] whereas other obesity indices have not yet been thoroughly integrated into clinical practice. Second, despite conducting multi-ancestral analyses, we were unable to offer ancestry-specific results for African American, Hispanic, and Central/South Asian ancestries due to limited sample sizes.

Conclusion

This study provides novel empirical evidence that the apt BMI threshold varies by individuals’ genetic risk for obesity. While measured BMI offers a snapshot of an individual's current obesity status, genetically-inferred BMI may capture a more protracted, long-term trajectory. The current one-size-fits-all BMI threshold leaves a non-trivial proportion of the population underdiagnosed or unnecessarily included in obesity management plans. BMI that considers individual’s genetic risk would provide an opportunity to reduce stigma by factoring biological insights into a crude measure and delivering precision medicine.
