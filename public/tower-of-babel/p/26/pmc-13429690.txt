Robust graph-transformer soft sensor for radar level estimation in dynamic LPG storage systems
Authors: Songqiao Bai, Shidong Fan, Pengcheng Wu, Bin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate liquid-level monitoring is vital for the safe operation of Liquefied Petroleum Gas (LPG) carriers. Conventional radar gauges are expensive and vulnerable to performance degradation under dynamic conditions. This paper proposes a Robust Graph Transformer Networks (RGTNs)–based Soft Sensor to enhance reliability and accuracy in radar level estimation. The RGTNs integrates a graph attention network for spatial dependency modeling and a Transformer for temporal feature extraction, enabling effective spatiotemporal fusion. Real-world experiments on an LPG carrier demonstrate that RGTNs achieves superior performance, with an R2 of 0.9704, MAE of 151.12 mm, and RMSE of 198.33 mm, outperforming existing models. By fusing radar measurements with thermodynamically coupled auxiliary sensors through learned spatial and temporal attention, the proposed framework provides a scalable and physically interpretable solution for liquid-level monitoring in LPG storage applications under dynamic marine operating conditions.Keywords: LPG carrier, Radar level monitoring, Soft sensing, Graph neural networks, Transformer architectureSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Liquefied Petroleum Gas carriers are pivotal to the global energy supply chain, ensuring the safe transport of liquefied petroleum gas while assuming growing responsibilities for environmental protection1. As global decarbonization advances, demand for LPG as a relatively low-emission fuel continues to grow, contributing to lower greenhouse-gas emissions and promoting the green transition of the maritime industry2, 3.

Liquid-level measurement is one of the core safety elements in the transportation process of LPG carriers4. Due to the high volatility and flammability of LPG, even minor fluctuations in liquid level within storage tanks can trigger significant pressure anomalies or vapor-space variations, affecting vessel stability, tank integrity, and overall system safety. Accurate liquid-level monitoring enables real-time awareness of cargo distribution during loading, unloading, and navigation, preventing risks such as overfilling, unbalanced loading, and liquid hammer. Therefore, developing a high-precision, interference-resistant, and redundant liquid-level monitoring system is essential to ensure the safe operation of LPG carriers, prevent accidents, and advance the goal of sustainable and green shipping.

Current industrial liquid-level measurement technologies include differential-pressure5, float (displacer)6, electric-contact7, magnetic-flap indicator8, magneto strictive9, ultrasonic10, and radar methods11. Traditional electric-contact instruments are simple and reliable but exhibit poor corrosion resistance and are prone to leakage under high-temperature and high-pressure conditions. The accuracy of differential-pressure, float, and magnetic-flap gauges is readily influenced by temperature, pressure, and the nature of the medium, and maintenance is difficult in extreme environments. Ultrasonic meters can achieve high accuracy, yet their performance is significantly affected by steam as well as process temperature and pressure. By contrast, radar level meters are largely independent of medium properties and operate stably under high pressure, low temperatures, and other harsh conditions, making them well suited to complex operating scenarios. Radar instruments have become the preferred choice in modern industrial applications due to their non-contact operation, strong resistance to interference, and long-term operational stability.

In current shipboard practice, microwave radar gauges installed on top of a still pipe are almost universally adopted as the primary Cargo Tank Measurement System, with float-type or capacitive gauges serving as the secondary backup11, 12. The radar primary / float secondary configuration has become the de facto standard since the late 1990s because radar gauging is non-contact, largely insensitive to medium properties, and can be maintained from outside the tank.

However, achieving and maintaining the gauging accuracy required for safe carriage and fiscal custody transfer remains challenging on board LPG/LNG carriers for two coupled reasons. First, the cargo tanks of seagoing gas carriers are subject to continuous six-degree-of-freedom motion and to internal sloshing, the latter exhibiting filling-level-dependent resonant peaks that strongly couple back to vessel roll and pitch11. The free liquid surface is therefore rarely planar nor stationary, which directly degrades both radar and float readings. Second, even when the cargo is at rest, the achievable accuracy of an industrial radar gauge is fundamentally bounded by the antenna pattern, antenna-inherent ringing, and for radar instruments mounted on a still pipe, intermodal dispersion within the overmoded circular waveguide formed by the still pipe itself6. Taken together, these factors mean that the nominal accuracy figure quoted by a radar gauge manufacturer is rarely sustained over an entire voyage in real sea states. This gap between specified and as-operated accuracy is precisely what motivates the redundancy- and soft-sensing-based approach pursued in the present work.

Classical data-driven soft sensors, such as principal component regression13, partial least square14, Gaussian process regression15, and artificial neural network16, perform well under steady-state conditions but often experience performance degradation in the dynamic, nonlinear, and non-stationary environments typical of shipboard operations. To address such limitations, the past five years have seen a rapid migration from these shallow regressors towards deep soft sensors based on stacked autoencoders, variational autoencoders, attention-based encoders, and transfer-learning frameworks, which are better able to capture nonlinear, time-varying, and multi-mode process behavior17, 18. Moreover, conventional models generally overlook spatial dependencies among sensors and offer limited interpretability. In contrast, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) explicitly represent sensors as nodes and their couplings as edges, enabling the exploitation of spatiotemporal correlations19. Through message passing, GNNs aggregate local information into global representations, thereby improving estimation accuracy and adaptability in complex conditions20. Consequently, GNNs-based soft sensing provides a more accurate and resilient alternative to traditional level-measurement methods, especially in dynamic operating environments.

Graph neural networks model sensor networks that exhibit spatial structure or physical connectivity by operating on relational graphs, thereby accelerating training and improving predictive performance. The theoretical foundations of Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) were first established in earlier research, followed by further refinements of graph-construction paradigms in subsequent studies21. Later developments incorporated semi-supervised node classification principles into GCN design, and it was eventually shown that even shallow GCN architectures can achieve performance comparable to the state-of-the-art semi-supervised methods of that time. Building on these advantages, numerous studies have introduced GCN-based approaches into industrial fault diagnosis. Chen et al.22 proposed a GCNs framework for robust fault localization in distribution networks. Structure analysis was combined with GCNs to incorporate prior knowledge for diagnostic tasks in23. Liu et al.24 presented an anomaly-detection approach based on state-space modeling and neural networks. A graph-based virtual sensor leveraging GNNs to exploit inter-sensor correlations for load prediction was developed in25. Yang et al.26 demonstrated the successful application of GNNs to bearing remaining useful life estimation. GNNs have also been employed to detect network–physical attacks in water-supply systems27 and to perform sensor calibration and fault detection in air-pollution monitoring networks28. Nevertheless, most existing research focuses on spatial-correlation modeling and static analysis, with relatively limited exploration of temporally aware forecasting.

In recent years, purpose-built deep-learning architectures for high-accuracy multivariate time-series anomaly detection have attracted widespread attention. Ren et al.29 introduced a method combining spectral residuals with convolutional neural networks to detect anomalies in service systems, while multivariate spectral frequency consistency was leveraged for unsupervised anomaly detection was explored in30. An unsupervised LSTM-based method was proposed in31, and Kim et al.32 employed invertible instance normalization to recover the statistical properties of time-series data. A single-scale architecture designed to extract both local features and global correlations was presented in33, and34 enhanced global information through data decomposition and reconstruction. Liu et al.35 developed a Transformer-based anomaly detection model that preserves inherent global trends via trend reconstruction. Condition-monitoring applications were further advanced through an LSTM–Autoencoder framework for wind turbines in36, and a CNN–BiLSTM model for tool-wear prediction in37. Additionally, CNNs combined with deep LSTM models have been employed for rolling-bearing degradation assessment28, 29.

Because conventional temporal and spatial prediction methods each have inherent limitations, integrating both dimensions through spatiotemporal forecasting can more comprehensively enhance prediction accuracy and reliability. Kong et al.38 proposed a spatiotemporal fusion attention method based on graph neural networks, which integrates temporal and spatial information while incorporating prior structural knowledge to enhance predictive performance. Their approach achieved strong results in remaining useful life prediction for both turbofan engines and steam turbines. Fu et al.39 introduced a spatiotemporal homogeneous feature extraction strategy, known as the dual-mixer model, which significantly improves prediction accuracy, particularly under complex environmental conditions.

Therefore, this study proposes a spatiotemporal prediction method that integrates graph attention networks with the Transformer architecture. By combining graph attention networks with the Transformer architecture, the proposed model jointly accounts for complex spatial and temporal dependencies, thereby improving the accuracy and stability of liquid-level monitoring. The model provides stable and reliable predictions under dynamically varying marine conditions and demonstrates greater robustness than conventional approaches in complex operating scenarios. Furthermore, the proposed framework scales efficiently to high-dimensional sensor signals and large-scale datasets, ensuring adaptability for practical shipboard applications. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

(1) A Graph-Attention–Transformer soft sensor is developed for radar level estimation in LPG cargo tanks under shipboard dynamic conditions, an application not addressed by existing soft-sensing literature.

(2) The graph topology is constructed from thermodynamics-motivated Pearson correlations rather than fully-connected or KNN adjacency, so the learned attention weights remain physically interpretable as inter-sensor couplings; the radar channel further plays a dual training–inference role that gives the framework intrinsic tolerance to radar degradation.

(3) Unlike observer-based fault estimators that require a known mechanistic model of the process, the proposed framework relies entirely on learned spatial and temporal encoders, making it applicable to systems such as marine cargo tanks for which no first-principles model is available.

Robust graph transformer networks

The proposed RGTNs integrates graph attention networks for spatial feature extraction and a Transformer architecture for temporal sequence modeling, enabling accurate and robust liquid-level estimation in LPG cargo tanks. The following three subsections will introduce this network model in detail. The framework of the RGTNs framework is illustrated in Fig. 1.

The overall framework of the proposed RGTNs.

The radar reading plays distinct roles in the training and inference stages of the framework. In the training stage, the radar reading serves as the regression target , with only time windows passing a quality-screening filter retained for supervision, ensuring that the model learns the input–output mapping from trustworthy labels. In the inference stage, the radar reading is included as Node 1 of the input graph alongside the four thermodynamic auxiliaries and the graph-attention layer assigns dynamic weights across all five channels rather than relying on the radar alone. The output is therefore a spatiotemporally fused estimate that remains valid even when the radar input is degraded.

GAT spatial feature extraction module

The measured value of the radar level gauge is not an isolated physical quantity, which is deeply coupled with the thermodynamic state inside the cargo tank. The temperature distribution at different heights (upper, middle, and lower) within the cargo tank directly reflects the interface between the liquid phase and the gas phase as well as the temperature stratification of the medium, while the pressure is directly related to the saturated vapor pressure of the medium and the liquid level height. Traditional models have difficulty effectively describing the nonlinear spatial interactions among these heterogeneous sensors. GAT through its powerful neighborhood aggregation and attention mechanisms, is precisely capable of quantifying such complex spatial correlations.

The core idea of GAT is that when aggregating the information of neighboring nodes, instead of assigning a fixed weight to each neighbor, a learnable attention mechanism is used to dynamically calculate the weight. This is crucial for LPG cargo tank systems because under different working conditions (such as feeding, standing, and discharging), the correlation strength between temperature sensors and radar level gauges changes dynamically.

Data preprocessing

All channels are sampled by the shipboard data acquisition system at a native rate of 1 sample per minute. The

radar level gauge has a manufacturer-declared accuracy of approximately ± 3 mm. Of the 26 available channels, five were selected as used in the RGTNs model (shown in Fig. 3): the radar level gauge of cargo Tank #1 (the prediction target node), the upper, middle, and lower temperature probes of the same tank and the tank-internal pressure sensor. The remaining 21 channels were excluded for one of three reasons. (i) Cross-tank channels belonging to Tank #2 were reserved for an independent validation experiment, since the two tanks operate quasi-independently. (ii) Pipeline and compressor channels (main pipe pressure, compressor inlet/outlet pressure and temperature) reflect transient flow events that are relevant to the cargo handling process but only weakly coupled to the steady-state liquid level inside a single tank. (iii) Safety-monitoring channels such as flammable-gas concentration and gas–liquid separator level remain near zero or saturate during normal operation and therefore carry negligible information for level estimation. The five retained channels are precisely those that participate in the thermodynamic equilibrium of the tank (vertical temperature gradient, vapour pressure, liquid surface), which makes the resulting graph physically interpretable.

Evaluation metrics

To quantitatively compare the prediction accuracy of each model, three evaluation metrics were introduced: Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) as shown Eq. (11), Mean Absolute Error (MAE) as shown Eq. (12), and Coefficient of Determination (R²) as shown Eq. (13).

MAE represents the average absolute difference between predicted values and true values, providing a direct measure of prediction error. RMSE represents the square root of the average squared differences between predicted values and true values. Both MAE and RMSE are commonly used to assess prediction performance, with smaller values indicating better model accuracy. However, both MAE and RMSE have limitations in model evaluation, as they may not accurately assess the performance across different feature dimensions. To address this, R² is used as an additional evaluation metric. R² provides a more precise measure of how well the model fits the data, with values closer to 1 indicating a better fit. R² is bounded within the range [0, 1], where higher values reflect stronger explanatory power of the model.

Results and visualization analysis

The model is implemented in PyTorch and trained on a single NVIDIA RTX 4060 GPU. The Adam optimizer is used with an initial learning rate of 1 × 10⁻³ and batch size of 64. Training is run for up to 200 epochs with early stopping on the validation RMSE (patience 15 epochs), and the checkpoint with the lowest validation RMSE is retained for testing. The architectural hyperparameters of the GAT and Transformer modules are selected on the validation set; the final values are K = 4 attention heads (F’ = 64) for the GAT layer, and L = 4 encoder layers with H = 8 attention heads and model dimension for the Transformer. The window length T was selected as 60 min through a sensitivity study on the validation set.

This model’s success is attributed to the effective combination of graph neural networks and Transformer, enabling it to simultaneously capture structural information within the data and long-term dependencies in the time series. In comparison to CNN-Transformer, the RGTNS model is better suited for handling data with complex structures and spatial dependencies, as GNNs can effectively model relationships between nodes, while Transformers capture global dependencies over long sequences, making it more adept at handling complex spatiotemporal patterns in radar level variations.

Although CNN-Transformer performs well, particularly in providing stable predictions for long time steps, it struggles to capture complex spatiotemporal dependencies, leading to relatively lower prediction accuracy. GAT-LSTM and GAT-TCN also perform well, especially in mid-term predictions, but they exhibit some errors in forecasting the later stages of radar level changes. CNN-LSTM and GraphSAGE-Transformer demonstrate lower prediction accuracy, particularly during rapid changes or abrupt shifts in radar level, as they struggle to capture global structural information. TCN-GRU, while capable of predicting well on certain datasets, shows poorer overall stability compared to the models mentioned above.

In this comparative experiment, various models exhibited different performance levels in radar level estimation tasks. The RGTNs model outperformed others in R², MAE, and RMSE—demonstrating significant advantages in fitting accuracy and error control. This performance is attributed to the effective integration of graph neural networks and Transformer models, enabling it to process graph-structured data while capturing complex dependencies within long time series. The GAT-TCN model, following closely behind, also shows high prediction accuracy, particularly with an R² value near that of RGTNs, indicating its effective performance through the combination of GAT and TCN for time-series data processing.

In contrast, the performance of GCN-GRU and GraphSAGE-Transformer is relatively weaker, particularly in the R² and RMSE metrics. The GCN-GRU, which combines GCN and GRU, may be limited by the GRU model’s ability to capture complex temporal dependencies, failing to fully leverage the potential of GNN. Meanwhile, GraphSAGE-Transformer underperforms, possibly due to the limitations of GraphSAGE in handling local structures and its inability to effectively integrate global information, leading to suboptimal data fitting. Similarly, CNN-LSTM and TCN-GRU show weaker performance, especially with significantly higher RMSE, suggesting potential underfitting or design limitations in handling complex time-series data. In particular, while CNN-LSTM excels in local feature extraction, its ability to capture long-term dependencies is limited, preventing the full utilization of LSTM in time-series modeling. Notably, CNN-Transformer performs better in accuracy and stability than CNN-LSTM, but still lags behind RGTNs and GAT-TCN, mainly due to its limited capacity to handle graph-structured data.

The RGTNs model emerges as the preferred choice for complex radar level prediction tasks, owing to its exceptional feature extraction capability and integrated modeling of temporal and graph structures. It demonstrates outstanding performance in fitting accuracy, error control, and generalization, endowing it with significant advantages for practical application scenarios. By contrast, models including GCN-GRU and GraphSAGE-Transformer are constrained by limitations in data processing and model integration—these shortcomings substantially compromise their prediction accuracy and robustness, necessitating further optimization.

Conclusion

This paper proposes a soft sensor framework based on Robust Graph Transformer Networks to overcome the limitations of conventional radar-based liquid-level monitoring in LPG storage systems under dynamic operating conditions. By integrating graph attention networks for spatial feature extraction with a Transformer architecture for temporal sequence modeling, the proposed framework effectively captures complex spatiotemporal dependencies among multi-sensor data, enabling accurate and robust radar level estimation.

Comprehensive experiments using real-world operational data from an LPG carrier demonstrated that the proposed RGTNs model consistently outperforms other representative spatiotemporal prediction models, including GAT-TCN, GCN-GRU, GraphSAGE-Transformer, and CNN-LSTM. It achieves superior accuracy and stability, as evidenced by its lowest MAE and RMSE and highest R² scores. The results confirm that the RGTNs model achieves higher prediction accuracy and stability than representative spatiotemporal baselines on a chronologically held-out test set spanning multiple loading, holding, and discharging regimes, indicating reliable generalization across the operating conditions encountered during the recorded voyages.

By incorporating soft-sensing technology and leveraging spatiotemporal fusion learning, this study provides a practical and scalable approach for intelligent liquid-level monitoring in LPG storage systems. The proposed model supports efficient real-time operation, offering high reliability and adaptability to complex marine conditions. Future research will focus on extending this framework to fault-tolerant control under sensor noise, missing data, and partial sensor degradation, building on the soft-sensor modelling foundation established in this work.
