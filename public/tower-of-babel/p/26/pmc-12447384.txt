A Cell-Surface-Targeted Fluorogenic Probe for Detection of Sulfatase 2 Activity
Authors: Yi Ding, Toshiyuki Kowada, Toshitaka Matsui, Norihiko Sasaki, Shin Mizukami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sulfatase 2 (Sulf-2), an extracellular sulfatase that modulates the sulfation pattern of heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs), serves as a critical biomarker for various pathological conditions. Monitoring Sulf-2 activity in living cell samples provides valuable insights for diagnostic applications and therapeutic evaluation of Sulf-2-related diseases. In this study, we developed a novel plasma-membrane-targeted fluorogenic probe, MAR-S, to visualize the activity of Sulf-2 secreted by living cells. Upon incubation with Sulf-2-containing culture supernatant from pancreatic cancer cells, MAR-S exhibited a significant increase in fluorescence at approximately 540 nm. Notably, MAR-S allowed for time-lapse monitoring of endogenous Sulf-2 activity in living cancer cells overexpressing Sulf-2, demonstrating its potential as a valuable tool for Sulf-2-related cancer diagnostics and therapeutic research.

Sulfatases constitute a conserved enzyme family that catalyzes the hydrolysis of sulfate ester bonds in diverse physiological substrates, ranging from small molecules such as sulfolipids and steroid sulfates to complex macromolecules such as proteoglycans and glycolipids. These enzymes are involved in multiple biological processes, including extracellular signal transduction, hormone regulation, and catabolic pathways. Currently, 17 distinct human sulfatases have been identified, with predominant localization in lysosomes and the endoplasmic reticulum. Among these, sulfatase 2 (Sulf-2), one of the two sulfatases exclusively localized in the extracellular matrix, has emerged as a focal point of recent research. Sulf-2 exhibits both arylsulfatase and endosulfatase activities and specifically catalyzes the 6-O-desulfation of heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs) at the cell surface and in the extracellular matrix. This desulfation process attenuates the binding affinity of HSPGs for specific signaling molecules, including growth factors and Wnt, thereby modulating associated signaling cascades. This distinctive mechanistic role differentiates Sulf-2 from other sulfatases in biological systems.

Abnormal elevation of Sulf-2 has been implicated in the pathogenesis and progression of various diseases. Notably, Sulf-2 expression levels exhibit 6–8-fold elevations in breast and colorectal cancers, and a 3-fold increase in lung cancer. , Consequently, precise and time-lapse detection of Sulf-2 activity in living samples is beneficial for elucidating its pathological functions and holds significant potential for clinical applications in both diagnosis and therapeutic monitoring of Sulf-2-associated malignancies.

Multiple analytical methodologies have been developed for the detection of Sulf-2 activity, including fluorescent probes and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) utilizing heparan sulfate substrates. Although the HPLC method offers superior sensitivity and selectivity, its complex operation and inability to facilitate real-time detection limit its practical applications. Activatable fluorescent probes represent robust analytical tools for enzyme activity detection, offering various advantages including high sensitivity and specificity, noninvasive detection capabilities, and rapid response. − In recent years, several fluorescent probes have been developed to detect sulfatase activity, including ERNathS with 4-hydroxy-1,8-naphthalimide as a fluorescent reporter for imaging steroid sulfatase (STS) activity, DQM-SULF based on aggregation-induced emission (AIE), and Hcy-SA, which is capable of visualizing sulfatase activity in living cells and animals through near-infrared fluorescence/photoacoustic (NIRF/PA) dual-modality imaging. However, most of these probes are used to detect intracellular sulfatase activity. 4-Methylumbelliferone sulfate (4-MUS) remains the only widely reported fluorescent probe for extracellular Sulf-2 activity detection. However, its short-wavelength excitation requirements and lack of effective cellular localization significantly constrain its applicability in living biological samples. − Therefore, there exists a critical need for the development of novel fluorogenic probes capable of monitoring Sulf-2 activity in living cells.

In the present study, we developed a novel cell-surface-targeted fluorogenic probe, MAR-S, for the detection of endogenous Sulf-2 enzymatic activity. MAR-S demonstrated a fluorogenic response upon incubation with Sulf-2, enabling discrimination between Sulf-2-overexpressing cancer cells and normal cells through differential fluorescence intensity. This sensing capability of MAR-S indicates its significant potential applications in pathological research and cancer diagnostics.

Results and Discussion

Probe Design and Synthesis

The rhodol fluorophore, a hybrid structure combining rhodamine and fluorescein, represents a promising scaffold for fluorescent probes due to its excellent photophysical properties, including sufficient photostability and high fluorescence quantum yield. − Rhodols exist in a dynamic equilibrium between a nonfluorescent spirolactone form and a fluorescent open form. Chemical modification at the O-position of rhodol typically shifts the equilibrium toward the nonfluorescent lactone form, while the deprotection restores the equilibrium toward the open form with high fluorescence. − Based on this mechanistic rationale, we designed MAR-S for the detection of Sulf-2 activity (Figure , upper scheme). In this probe design, the phenolic hydroxy group is derivatized as a sulfate monoester group, which was expected to promote the formation of nonfluorescent lactone form. Given that Sulf-2 exhibits arylsulfatase activity, we hypothesized that enzymatic hydrolysis of the sulfate ester would induce an equilibrium shift toward the highly fluorescent open form, resulting in a measurable fluorogenic response.

Design of a plasma-membrane-targeted fluorogenic probe, MAR-S, for detecting Sulf-2. MAR-S is enzymatically converted into fluorescent MAR-OH by Sulf-2. The upper panel illustrates the designed reaction scheme, while the lower panel shows a plausible reaction scheme based on spectral data.

A key feature of MAR-S is its localization on the extracellular surface of the plasma membrane (PM). The amino group of rhodol fluorophores can be modified with organelle-targeting moieties. Given that mature, active Sulf-2 is secreted to extracellular space, a long palmitoyl chain was incorporated as a PM-anchoring unit. In addition, a negatively charged aspartate moiety was introduced between the palmitoyl group and rhodol fluorophore to inhibit the permeation of MAR-S through the PM and to orient the sulfated fluorophore toward the extracellular region, thereby increasing the likelihood of interaction with secreted Sulf-2. Furthermore, incorporation of an azetidine ring was expected to enhance the photostability and brightness of the rhodol fluorophore by restricting the formation of a twisted intramolecular charge transfer (TICT) state.

MAR-S was synthesized starting from commercially available fluorescein (Scheme S1). Selective protection of one hydroxy group of fluorescein with a methoxymethyl (MOM) group afforded compound 1. Subsequent triflation followed by a Pd-catalyzed cross-coupling reaction yielded the asymmetric rhodol scaffold 3. Treatment with trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) in dichloromethane (DCM) removed the tert-butoxycarbonyl (Boc) and MOM groups, after which the resulting amine was coupled with Fmoc-Asp­(tBu)–OH to afford compound 4. Following Fmoc deprotection, a condensation reaction with palmitic acid produced compound 5. Deprotection of the tert-butyl ester with TFA in DCM produced MAR-OH. In parallel, sulfation of compound 5 using a sulfur trioxide trimethylamine complex, followed by tert-butyl ester deprotection under 95% TFA/H2O conditions, yielded the target compound MAR-S. All synthesized compounds were characterized using 1H NMR, 13C NMR, and high-resolution electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (HR-ESI-MS).

Photophysical Properties of MAR-S

The ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) absorption and fluorescence emission spectra of MAR-S and its hydrolyzed product MAR-OH were measured in an aqueous buffer (100 mM HEPES buffer, pH 7.4) at 37 °C (Figure A). MAR-S exhibited an absorption maximum at 483 nm and displayed weak fluorescence with a low quantum yield (Φf = 0.25). Compared to MAR-S, MAR-OH displayed a slightly red-shifted absorption maximum and a 13-fold increase in fluorescence intensity upon 515 nm excitation (Φf = 0.85). These results confirmed that MAR-S possesses a fluorogenic property upon desulfation; however, the residual absorption between 400 and 550 nm and the weak fluorescence of MAR-S indicated that it predominantly adopts the open form in aqueous solution (Figure , lower scheme), as previously proposed for a rhodol-based alkaline phosphatase probe. Because the introduction of the sulfate group on the phenolic hydroxy group does not fully drive the rhodol equilibrium toward the closed spirolactone form, MAR-S retained residual fluorescence, resulting in a limited dynamic range. To achieve more efficient fluorescence quenching in the sulfated state, strategies such as converting the benzoate to a benzyl alkoxide could be explored to increase the nucleophilicity.

Subsequently, we evaluated the stability of MAR-S in a neutral aqueous buffer. MAR-S exhibited sufficient stability in 50 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.4) at 37 °C for at least 3 h, with approximately 10% hydrolysis observed after 24 h of incubation (Figure S1). We further investigated the pH-dependent spectral properties of MAR-S and MAR-OH across a pH range of 3–10 (Figures B and S2). The absorption and emission spectra of MAR-S remained relatively constant from pH 6 to 10, indicating minimal spectral variation under physiological conditions. In contrast, MAR-OH, which has a pK cycl value of 6.0, predominantly existed in its open form at pH 7.4, resulting in markedly enhanced fluorescence intensity.

We investigated the fluorogenic response of MAR-S to a bacterial sulfatase isolated from Aerobacter aerogenes. Upon incubation with the bacterial sulfatase, MAR-S exhibited a gradual increase in fluorescence intensity over a 3 h period, whereas minimal fluorescence changes were observed in the absence of the enzyme (Figures A and S3). The reaction products were analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS), confirming the formation of the enzymatic product MAR-OH after incubation with the bacterial sulfatase (Figures S4–S5). Furthermore, the temperature dependence of the probe was examined by incubating MAR-S with bacterial sulfatase at different temperatures (27–43 °C) and measuring its fluorescence intensity after 1 h. In the absence of the enzyme, MAR-S showed minimal changes in fluorescence intensity, indicating sufficient stability. In contrast, in the presence of the enzyme, fluorescence intensity increased with rising temperature from 27 °C, reached a peak at 40 °C, and slightly decreased at 43 °C (Figure S6), suggesting that the enzymatic reaction proceeded most efficiently at the optimal temperature of the bacterial sulfatase. Collectively, these results indicate that MAR-S functions as a robust and selective probe for detecting sulfatase activity under physiologically relevant conditions.

We next evaluated the capability of MAR-S to detect Sulf-2 activity. Using a previously established protocol, we prepared a conditioned medium (CM) from cultured PK-45P cells, a human pancreatic cancer cell line known to overexpress Sulf-2. Sulf-2 secretion into the CM was verified by Western blot analysis (Figure S7A), and its sulfatase activity was validated using 4-MUS (Figure S7B). MAR-S (25 μM) was incubated with Sulf-2-containing CM in 50 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.4) at 37 °C. After 25 h of incubation, MAR-S exhibited an approximately 3-fold increase in fluorescence intensity (Figure B), demonstrating its responsiveness to Sulf-2 secreted by PK-45P cells. However, the fluorescence enhancement in response to Sulf-2 in CM was modest and required prolonged incubation. Previous studies have suggested that a chondroitin/dermatan sulfate glycosaminoglycan (GAG) chain covalently attached to the enzyme substrate-binding domain of Sulf-2 modulates its substrate affinity. Thus, Sulf-2 activity in CM samples may have been attenuated in aqueous solution, because the digestion of GAG chain does not occur due to the absence of GAG digesting enzyme.

To assess whether MAR-S specifically detects sulfatase activity in complex biological environments, we incubated the probe with potential extracellular interfering speciesincluding bovine serum albumin (BSA), hyaluronidase, glutathione (GSH), cysteine (Cys), reactive oxygen species (H2O2 and ClO–), metal ions (Na+, K+, Mg2+, Ca2+), and inorganic anions (SO4 2–, NO3 –). Upon excitation at 515 nm, none of these analytes produced an obvious fluorescence enhancement (Figure S8), indicating that MAR-S possesses excellent selectivity and resistance to interference from various biologically relevant species.

Cellular Evaluation of MAR-S for Monitoring Sulf-2 Activity

To evaluate the potential application of MAR-S for monitoring endogenous Sulf-2 activity in living cells, the cytotoxicity of MAR-S and its hydrolyzed product MAR-OH was first tested using the WST-8 cell viability assay. Both compounds exhibited no significant cytotoxicity in living cells at concentrations up to 20 μM (Figure S9), suggesting their good biocompatibility and suitability for live-cell applications.

Then, we investigated the subcellular localization of MAR-S and MAR-OH in CHO-K1 cells. MAR-OH exhibited strong fluorescence signals specifically localized to the PM, with slight intracellular signals. As expected, MAR-S also showed similar PM localization with significantly reduced intracellular fluorescence than MAR-OH (Figure A). These observations suggest that the incorporation of a long alkyl chain with a negatively charged carboxylate group effectively anchored the probe to the PM while orienting the sulfated rhodol moiety toward the extracellular region.

Next, we evaluated the capability of MAR-S to visualize Sulf-2 activity secreted from PK-45P cells using live-cell imaging. Upon incubation of PK-45P cells with 6 μM of MAR-S at 37 °C for 2 h, a significant increase in fluorescence signals was observed at the PM. CHO-K1 cells, which express negligible levels of Sulf-2 (Figure S10), served as a negative control. The fluorescence signals on the CHO-K1 cell surfaces showed no significant increase during the identical incubation period, although some intracellular fluorescent punctate signals were observed (Figure B). Quantitative analysis of the fluorescence intensity at the cell surface revealed a 2–3-fold increase in PK-45P cells, while CHO-K1 cells exhibited minimal changes in cell surface fluorescence under identical conditions (Figure C).

To further confirm that the observed cell surface fluorescence increase was due to secreted Sulf-2 activity, we performed an siRNA-mediated knockdown of Sulf-2 in PK-45P cells. Western blot analysis confirmed efficient downregulation of Sulf-2 expression (Figure S11A), and then MAR-S was applied to the cells. The suppression of Sulf-2 expression resulted in significant attenuation of fluorescence enhancement at the cell surface (Figure S11B,C), confirming that the fluorogenic response of MAR-S on the PK-45P cell surface was specifically induced by endogenous Sulf-2 activity.

Conclusions

We reported the development of MAR-S, a novel rhodol-based fluorogenic probe for in situ monitoring of endogenous Sulf-2 activity in living cells. MAR-S demonstrated a cell-surface-targeting ability and exhibited fluorescence enhancement upon Sulf-2-mediated desulfation. This probe enabled live-cell imaging of Sulf-2 activity secreted by pancreatic cancer cells. Quantitative analysis of cell surface fluorescence intensity revealed a significant difference between Sulf-2-overexpressing PK-45P cells and control CHO-K1 cells, which express minimal levels of Sulf-2. Although the relatively high basal fluorescence level may limit detection sensitivity, our findings highlight the potential utility of MAR-S for diagnostic applications in Sulf-2-overexpressing cancer tissues. Moreover, MAR-S offers a promising platform for the screening of Sulf-2 inhibitors as potential anticancer agents. Once validated for this application, the strategy could be extended to the development of inhibitors targeting other secreted enzymes, thereby accelerating drug discovery in the field of extracellular enzymology. If future studies continue to advance the application of MAR-S, the value of this probe is expected to increase significantly across the continuum of drug discovery, translational research, and clinical diagnostics.
