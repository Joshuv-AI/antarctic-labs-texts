Counting particles in cryo-electron microscopy may result in incorrect population estimates
Authors: Luke Evans, Lars Dingeldein, Roberto Covino, Marc Aurèle Gilles, Erik Thiede, Pilar Cossio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Counting particles is a common practice in estimating populations from cryo-electron microscopy data. Here, it is shown that counting particles is not robust to noise, whereas statistically rigorous population-estimation methods are more resilient and hold promise for future development.

Biomolecules continuously undergo conformational changes within the cell. The population of a given conformation refers to its relative frequency within an ensemble of states. Determining these populations is crucial for understanding how biomolecules achieve their biological function. Several recent studies reported populations from cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) using particle counts assigned to 3D classes or latent-space histograms1–10. At high signal, these counts can reflect true populations. However, we caution the cryo-EM community that, given the high levels of noise typical of cryo-EM images, these estimates can be substantially distorted. We show that reliable population estimates can be recovered by explicitly modeling the noise and statistics across the entire dataset.

Counting particles and the population inverse problem

Equation (1) accounts for both continuous and discrete heterogeneity, since we can write π(x) as a sum of deltas centered on a list of structures, which reduces the integral to a sum (Supplementary Information).

Some existing approaches for estimating π(x) are end-to-end from a user standpoint: given a set of images, one estimates a set of structures and populations of the structures simultaneously. Here, we focus on population estimation as a post-processing step: one that assumes candidate structures or alignments are already available, and whose final objective is to calibrate the populations associated with those structures.

The standard post-processing approach is to count or histogram particles. Such counting-based approaches estimate π(x) by tallying particle assignments and organizing them into specific classes or locations in an underlying space using the likelihood p(y∣x) (Supplementary Information). But in the presence of experimental noise, it is challenging to assign an image to a specific conformation with high precision. Choosing a conformation requires knowledge of π(x), which requires solving the inverse problem (1). If one assigns an image without this knowledge, one commits the base-rate fallacy: a statistical fallacy of ignoring global statistics when making inferences on a single sample12.

Discrete heterogeneity

We illustrate this failure mode on a discrete two-state system: the spike protein in two conformations (Fig. 1a). For two states, x1 and x2, the model for the inverse problem (1) simplifies to discrete heterogeneity with pdata(y) = p(y∣x1)π1 + p(y∣x2)π2, where π1 is the unknown probability of x1 and likewise for π2, with π1 + π2 = 1. For this example, we prepared several particle datasets with varying noise levels, all with a ground truth population 80/20 ratio of the 1-up and 3-down states (Supplementary Information and Fig. 1b). We used traditional 3D classification (yellow) that attempts to classify which 3D structure each image belongs to. Even with ground truth poses, these techniques fail quickly, giving incorrect population estimates as noise is increased. Hard and soft assignments (blue and green, respectively) show the same trend. We observed similar trends in heavily classified real images (<10% of the initially picked particles in EMPIAR-12098), which we prepared in an 80/20 mixture of the 3-up and 2-up spike states, respectively. Unlike in the synthetic example, here we rely on extensive 3D classification results to determine the baseline (80/20) population, which may not fully reflect the unknown true population. Nonetheless, with available alignments, all methods performed relatively well, though particle-counting approaches showed slightly lower accuracy (Fig. 1c, left). However, in a typical workflow, particle poses and contrast scaling are often uncertain and require refinement. We exemplified this uncertainty by adding noise to the images (Fig. 1c, right) or noise to the poses (Supplementary Information, Fig. 2). In these scenarios, the accuracy of particle counting methods decreased, while the ensemble reweighting and deconvolution retained more accurate population estimates. These results confirm that 3D classification can be inconsistent and unstable15.

Continuous heterogeneity

Continuous heterogeneity methods have recently shifted the cryo-EM community from discrete classification to latent-space embedding of individual images. However, latent-space distributions are still highly affected by noise. Inspired by the Flatiron Institute 2023 cryo-EM heterogeneity challenge16, we showcase a simple example: the rotation of the IgG domain from cryoBench17, where the underlying conformational density is described along one dihedral angle (Fig. 1d). Here, we sample a multimodal distribution on the dihedral (Fig. 1e, black dashed line) and compute images at high noise (Supplementary Information). We take RECOVAR14, a high-performing method in cryoBench, and assign particles to latent coordinates defined by the first two principal components. Latent-space analysis in continuous heterogeneity methods is typically performed by creating a histogram of the embedded image assignments (Fig. 1f, left). This predicts a nearly flat distribution, entirely missing the middle mode (blue—Fig. 1e). In contrast, solving the inverse problem (1), by denoising the latent space using the deconvolution approach14 (Fig. 1f, right), correctly finds all modes of the distribution (pink—Fig. 1e). Because RECOVAR implicitly estimates volumes directly from the images, we also assessed how the inverse problem behaves when provided with the ground-truth conformations. In this case, we supplied the true structures to the ensemble reweighting method13, which accurately recovered the underlying probability distribution over these conformations (orange, Fig. 1e).

Conclusions

Here, we demonstrated that noise-induced bias can affect population estimates when counting cryo-EM particles. This bias depends on the level of noise and uncertainty in the particle images, as well as the magnitude and distinguishability of the conformational changes. At low noise levels, counting or histogramming can provide reliable population estimates (Fig. 1b, left). However, determining the exact noise regime of real data is not possible, and high noise and uncertainty can hinder the accurate recovery of probability distributions through 3D classification or latent space histogramming. Thus, biological conclusions based on such analyses in cryo-EM should be drawn with caution. As expanded in the Supplementary Information, methods that account for noise and uncertainties by leveraging the statistics of the entire particle stack13,14 yield more reliable estimates. However, improving forward models, achieving robustness to pose misspecification and low SNRs, and determining how best to select or optimize the structures on which populations are evaluated remain active areas of development (e.g., ref. 18). In conclusion, we hope that this Comment encourages the integration of statistically rigorous methods for population estimation into widely used cryo-EM software packages, reducing the need for users to rely on external post-processing or qualitative interpretations.
