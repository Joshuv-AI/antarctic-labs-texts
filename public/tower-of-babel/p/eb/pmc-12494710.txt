Comparative study of indoor positioning datasets focusing on localization accuracy success rate and floor classification
Authors: Ayesha Ayub, Zuhairiah Zainal Abidin, Abdulraqeb Alhammadi, Naglaa F Soliman, Muhammad Asim Khan, Abeer D Algarni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIndoor positioning systems (IPS) are crucial for enabling location-aware services in GPS-denied environments such as buildings, hospitals, and industrial facilities. However, the lack of standardized, high-quality datasets remains a significant barrier to the development and fair evaluation of localization algorithms. This paper presents a comprehensive comparative analysis of four widely used indoor positioning datasets—BLE Indoor, SODIndoorLoc, TUJI1, and UJIIndoorLoc—evaluating their localization accuracy, success rate, and floor classification performance. Through exploratory data analysis and systematic experimentation using an XGBoost model with consistent hyperparameters, we identify how dataset-specific characteristics such as signal sparsity, access point (AP) density, device heterogeneity, and spatial layout affect localization outcomes. Results show that while high AP density can enhance accuracy, other factors like environmental complexity, weak RSSI signals, and multi-device variability significantly influence model performance. Based on our findings, we offer practical guidelines for dataset selection, feature engineering, and design improvements to support robust and generalizable IPS development. This study provides critical insights for both researchers and practitioners aiming to advance reliable indoor localization solutions.Keywords: Indoor Positioning, WiFi Fingerprinting, BLE Localization, Machine Learning, Dataset AnalysisSubject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Information technology

Introduction

Indoor positioning has gained significance owing to its wide-ranging applications in navigation, asset tracking, healthcare, smart buildings, and industrial automation1–4. In contrast to outdoor settings, where GPS consistently delivers accurate location estimations, indoor positioning encounters considerable difficulties due to signal attenuation, multipath effects, and dynamic environmental conditions5,6. Consequently, fingerprinting approaches using WiFi and Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) have emerged as popular and effective solutions, utilizing pre-collected signal strength maps to estimate locations accurately within indoor spaces7–9.

Although numerous datasets have been developed to assist with the evaluation and enhancement of indoor positioning algorithms, these datasets exhibit considerable variation in their characteristics, including environmental settings, number of access points (APs), spatial coverage, and data sampling methodologies10–12. Such discrepancies contribute to significant heterogeneity in the performance of positioning algorithms, making it impossible to generalize findings or evaluate algorithmic effectiveness across different datasets13,14. Additionally, many existing research largely focus on localization accuracy alone, overlooking other key metrics like as success rate and floor classification accuracy in multi-floor scenarios15.

Recent comprehensive reviews, such as the one conducted by Feng et al.16,17, have highlighted important limitations across widely available indoor positioning datasets. According to Feng et al., substantial gaps consist of the absence of temporal variability data collection, divergent or incomplete dataset metadata, and insufficient consideration of device heterogeneity and calibration issues. Particularly, temporal variations—such as changes in occupancy, signal interference, and environmental dynamics—are frequently left out, thus restricting the real-world applicability and robustness of developed models. Furthermore, inconsistencies in metadata presentation make it hard for researchers to select appropriate datasets, while inadequate device calibration procedures result in significant discrepancies and biases that impede the generalization of positioning models across different devices and environments16.

To address these challenges, recent machine learning-based approaches have been developed to mitigate device heterogeneity through feature transformation, domain adaptation, and signal normalization. For example, Ma and Shi18 introduced a few-shot learning framework to improve cross-device generalization, while Chen and Zhang19 proposed a deep metric learning model (DeepMetricFi) that enhances device-invariant feature representation. More recently, Chiu et al.20 employed a graph-based method to update fingerprints using unlabeled WiFi signals, showing resilience to device variation.

This paper undertakes a thorough comparative analysis of four publicly available indoor positioning datasets—namely UJIIndoorLoc21, TUJI123, SODIndoorLoc24, and a BLE-based dataset25—evaluating their performance across multiple essential metrics: localization accuracy (mean absolute error, mean squared error, root mean squared error, R², localization error), localization success rate, and floor classification accuracy. Unlike prior reviews or limited-scope comparisons, which primarily focus on descriptive or algorithm-specific insights, our approach provides a unified, model-driven benchmarking of dataset robustness using a fixed XGBoost framework. To further characterize the datasets, we introduce five diagnostic metrics—Effective AP Count (EAC), Sparsity Level (SL), Signal Entropy Score (SES), AP Redundancy Score (ARS), and Device Bias Index (DBI)—which quantify signal richness, redundancy, and device variability. Together, these metrics and evaluation results offer new insights into dataset reliability and generalization potential for indoor localization systems.

We conduct a comprehensive exploratory data analysis (EDA) to offer deeper insights into dataset properties and their effects on performance. This approach carefully examines essential features including environment size, the quantity and distribution of access points (APs), variability in signal strength (RSSI distributions), and device-specific biases. We benchmark localization performance using a fixed XGBoost model and introduce signal quality metrics to assess dataset robustness, bridging the gap between dataset structure and model evaluation.

Our analysis indicates that while datasets with greater densities of APs and RPs typically enhance localization performance, additional factors—such as environmental complexity, temporal dynamics, signal sparsity, and device heterogeneity—are equally significant. Furthermore, multi-floor datasets present further problems for floor classification and vertical positioning, frequently requiring more advanced modeling methodologies to get consistent accuracy across varying situations. Related work

Indoor localization is attracting significant interest owing to its crucial uses in navigation, healthcare, logistics, smart buildings, and industrial automation26,27. Among the numerous strategies examined, WiFi fingerprinting has surfaced as a prevalent strategy, using the ubiquitous presence of WiFi networks and WiFi-enabled devices. The premise of WiFi fingerprinting entails collecting Received Signal Strength Indicator (RSSI) readings from various Access Points (APs) and matching them with established reference locations to create a fingerprint map. Localization is accomplished by comparing real-time RSSI measurements with this pre-constructed map28,29. The reliability and precision of WiFi fingerprint-based localization are significantly dependent upon the quality of the underlying dataset. Factors such as spatial and temporal signal variability, device heterogeneity, environment complexity, and data collection methodology greatly influence the effectiveness of localization algorithms30.

Feng et al.16 performed an extensive review of more than 50 publicly accessible WiFi fingerprinting datasets and identified numerous significant issues impacting dataset efficacy. They observed an absence of uniformity in dataset formats, metadata comprehensiveness, and documentation standards, which complicates dataset selection and impedes repeatability. Many datasets, in particular, inadequately capture temporal variability, resulting in unjustified overestimations of algorithm robustness. Furthermore, device-specific biases arising from inadequate calibration among various collection devices were identified as significant problems. Interestingly, Feng et al noted that larger datasets with denser AP deployments and finer spatial grids do not necessarily lead to improved localization accuracy. Conversely, factors such as careful AP distribution, rigorous ground truth collecting, and appropriate management of missing data were recognized as essential predictors of dataset quality. Their efforts resulted in a series of proposed principles for the collection and dissemination of future datasets, highlighting open access, comprehensive metadata, incorporation of temporal variations, and documentation of environmental conditions.

Previous studies, including22,24,31,32, and33, focused on comparing specific WiFi fingerprinting datasets through limited comparative analyses that emphasize particular factors such as spatial density, temporal variations, or symmetric environments. Nevertheless, this research frequently exhibited limitations in scope, examining just a restricted number of datasets and without uniform evaluation frameworks. As a result, they did not adequately tackle the diversity and inconsistency challenges inherent in public databases.

Numerous benchmark datasets have been crucial to indoor positioning research. The UJIIndoorLoc dataset, which includes data from various buildings and floors, is among the most often cited resources. It offers substantial diversity concerning devices, users, and settings, yet is plagued by challenges associated with device heterogeneity and signal degradation. The SODIndoorLoc dataset enhanced prior datasets by offering a more densely populated grid of reference locations and improved data collection management, thereby facilitating more precise and reproducible evaluations of fingerprinting methods. The TUJI1 dataset provides high-resolution RSSI measurements obtained from various devices across short distances, primarily examining the effects of device variety and detailed spatial fluctuations. Meanwhile, BLE-based datasets, such as, extend localization research beyond WiFi by leveraging Bluetooth Low Energy signals, which present distinct signal behavior compared to WiFi and are increasingly utilized in IoT environments.

Despite these important resources, numerous datasets are deficient in critical information, like timestamped measurements, access point location metadata, or cross-device calibration, hence constraining their efficacy in the development of generic indoor locating solutions. Although numerous publications have proposed WiFi fingerprinting databases, limited research has conducted systematic comparisons among various datasets. Works such as22,31,32, and33 incorporated partial comparative analyses, concentrating mainly on particular datasets or assessing specific attributes such as spatio-temporal variations or environmental diversity. For example,22 conducted a comparative analysis utilizing clustering-based techniques on a restricted set of datasets, whereas31 and32 presented datasets with increased temporal variability and contrasted them with conventional datasets. However, these studies were often limited in scope, lacked standardized evaluation metrics, and did not provide large-scale, consistent comparisons across multiple datasets.

Machine learning techniques have proven essential to fingerprint-based localization. Conventional models like Random Forests (RF), Support Vector Machines (SVM), and k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) have been extensively utilized owing to their simplicity and effectiveness in high-dimensional signal spaces34. Recently, deep learning techniques such as Fully Connected Neural Networks (FCNN) and ensemble methods like XGBoost have exhibited enhanced performance, especially when integrated with careful feature engineering strategies19,35. Feature engineering techniques, including as AP selection, signal normalization, spatial filtering, and temporal smoothing, have demonstrated a substantial improvement in model performance by mitigating prevalent challenges, including missing data, noisy signals, and device-dependent fluctuations20,36,37. However, prior studies highlight algorithmic improvements without rigorously assessing the impact of dataset characteristics on model effectiveness. This gap necessitates comparison analyses that explicitly include dataset features.

Despite previous studies such as22,24,31–33 conducting partial comparisons of WiFi fingerprinting datasets, they were constrained by either the quantity of datasets examined, the scope of their evaluations, or the absence of standardized performance metrics. The majority of these research concentrated on certain dataset characteristics, such as temporal fluctuations or spatial density, without doing systematic machine learning assessments across many datasets under uniform conditions. Furthermore, the datasets analyzed were frequently limited in scale, tailored to specific building types, and did not account for device diversity. Feng et al.16 mitigated certain deficiencies by performing a comprehensive descriptive review of over 50 datasets. Nonetheless, their research was predominantly analytical and did not conduct empirical localization tests to measure the actual effects of dataset attributes on placement performance. Fingerprinting-based indoor localization

FIndoor locating using fingerprinting is widespread in areas with poor GPS signals. This method compares wireless signal properties to a pre-established database, called the radio map, to determine a user’s location. Fingerprinting methods use WiFi and Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) wireless technology40.

Despite differences in signal characteristics, WiFi and BLE fingerprinting exhibit a common operational process consisting of two separate phases: the offline stage, referred to as the training phase, and the online stage, known as the positioning phase. Offline Stage During the offline stage, signal measurements are collected at various known RPs throughout the environment. For each RP, signal characteristics such as Received Signal Strength (RSS), Round-Trip Time (RTT), or Channel State Information (CSI) are recorded from multiple transmitters i.e. Access Points (APs) for WiFi, Beacons for BLE41. These measurements are compiled to create a radio map, where each fingerprint uniquely corresponds to a specific location. The quality and granularity of the radio map depend on factors such as the density of RPs, the number of detected transmitters, and the environmental dynamics during data collection8.

Online Stage In the online stage, a user device collects live wireless signal measurements from surrounding transmitters. These real-time observations are compared to the fingerprints stored in the radio map using various localization algorithms, such as KNN, machine learning models, or deep learning architectures. The location corresponding to the most similar fingerprint is inferred as the estimated position of the user. The process of Wifi fingerprinting is also summarized in the Fig. 1.

Illustration of the WiFi fingerprinting process used for indoor localization. The workflow consists of five stages: (1) signal measurements are collected at predefined reference points (offline stage); (2) a radio map is constructed from these measurements; (3) the user’s device performs real-time scanning (online stage); (4) the current measurement is compared against the radio map; and (5) the location is estimated based on the closest fingerprint match. This process underpins most RSS-based positioning systems due to its infrastructure reuse and ease of deployment.

Several types of signal characteristics have been utilized for fingerprinting-based localization:

Received Signal Strength (RSS) RSS measures the power level of the received signal, typically expressed in dBm. It is the most commonly used signal attribute for both WiFi and BLE due to its widespread availability and simplicity. However, RSS is sensitive to environmental changes and device heterogeneity.

While BLE fingerprinting primarily relies on RSS values, WiFi-based fingerprinting can utilize a richer set of signal attributes (RSS, RTT, and CSI), offering greater flexibility42.

WiFi fingerprinting is often preferred for large-scale indoor localization systems due to the widespread availability of WiFi infrastructure in most environments, the greater range and stability of WiFi signals compared to BLE, and the richer set of available measurements, including RTT and CSI43. Additionally, leveraging existing WiFi networks reduces deployment costs and simplifies the localization setup, making WiFi fingerprinting a practical choice for real-world applications in malls, airports, hospitals, and smart buildings28. Critical analysis of datasets

This section provides a critical analysis of four useful datasets utilized in indoor localization research: the BLE-based indoor localization dataset25, the UJIIndoorLoc dataset21, the TUJI dataset23, and the SODIndoorLoc dataset24. Each dataset presents unique features for assessing localization algorithms, although they also entail obstacles like signal variation, device heterogeneity, and the environment factors. By analyzing critical properties such as spatial distribution, signal intensity variability, and the influence of environmental conditions, we seek to elucidate the advantages and drawbacks of these datasets, offering insights for the enhancement of indoor localization models.

Spatial layout and reference point (RP) coverage

The BLE-based dataset provides extensive spatial coverage, with reference points (RPs) placed every 2 meters across an area spanning 1.4–43.3 m (X-coordinate) and 1.5–22.5 m (Y-coordinate). As shown in Fig. 2, APs are distributed across rooms, enabling fine-grained fingerprinting. Local RSSI variations, influenced by walls and furniture, reflect typical indoor signal attenuation patterns.

Spatial distribution of fingerprinting reference points (color-coded by Room ID) and WiFi access points (black crosses) in the BLE-based dataset. This layout highlights the non-uniform density of reference points across rooms and the scattered deployment of APs. Several rooms, such as Room 45 and Room 16, exhibit denser coverage, while others are relatively sparse, potentially influencing localization accuracy due to uneven signal sampling and AP proximity.

The SODIndoorLoc dataset spans approximately 8,000 and includes three buildings—CETC331, HCXY, and SYL. Each buildings presenting distinct structural and signal-related challenges. CETC331 features an optimized AP deployment strategy based on Cramér-Rao Lower Bound principles, offering strong and balanced coverage across floors. In contrast, HCXY is characterized by long, narrow corridors that enable efficient localization along the main axis but introduce signal degradation and reduced accuracy in peripheral zones. SYL poses the most complex environment, with APs installed at varying heights and orientations across multiple floors, resulting in irregular signal propagation and challenging fingerprint consistency. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the spatial layouts and AP distributions vary significantly across the three buildings, highlighting the architectural complexity and signal variability that impact model generalization. This spatial diversity underscores the need for adaptive localization models capable of operating robustly across heterogeneous indoor environments.

Spatial layouts of RPs and access points across buildings in the SODIndoorLoc dataset. The variations in geometry, AP density, and layout complexity reflect real-world challenges in designing generalizable localization models.

The UJIIndoorLoc dataset spans three buildings with varying spatial characteristics. Figure 4a, b, and c show the distribution of RPs in Buildings 0, 1, and 2, respectively. Building 0 features a dense and uniform RP layout, supporting higher localization accuracy. In contrast, Buildings 1 and 2 have distinct geometries and AP deployment strategies, which may introduce cross-building biases. These variations highlight the importance of developing localization models that can generalize across heterogeneous spatial layouts and infrastructure designs.

3D spatial distribution of RPs across Buildings 1–3 in the UJIIndoorLoc dataset. Each point represents a unique fingerprint location, colored by floor. The contrast in sampling density, building geometry, and floor separation highlights the dataset’s diversity and presents distinct challenges for model generalization.

The TUJI1 dataset was collected in a structured indoor environment consisting of offices and corridors on a single floor. RPs were arranged in a fine-grained spatial grid with approximately 45 cm spacing, ensuring uniform coverage. As shown in Fig. 5, the layout supports detailed localization analysis. However, signal consistency is affected by obstacles such as furniture, partitions, and human presence, which introduce localized attenuation and RSSI fluctuations. The presence of both Line-of-Sight (LoS) and Non-Line-of-Sight (NLoS) conditions further contributes to multipath effects and variable AP coverage.

Spatial distribution of RPs in the TUJI1 dataset, showing a dense, uniform grid across a single-floor indoor layout. Consistent RP coverage supports robust localization, with minor sparsity at the periphery.

Across all datasets, several common challenges emerge. Datasets such as TUJI1 and UJIIndoorLoc benefit from fine spatial granularity, which enhances the resolution and accuracy of localization, but regions with weak signal coverage reduce the distinctiveness of location fingerprints. Datasets like SODIndoorLoc and UJIIndoorLoc highlight the impact of diverse building layouts on localization performance. Complex building geometries, such as long corridors or multi-floor environments, introduce unique challenges that require adaptive localization models capable of generalizing across different layouts. Additionally, all datasets face environmental challenges such as furniture and human presence, which cause signal attenuation and fluctuations in RSSI values, complicating localization accuracy. These environmental factors significantly impact signal propagation and introduce variability in the measurements across different areas of the environment.

In conclusion, the spatial distribution and coverage across the four datasets vary in terms of building layouts, reference point arrangements, and environmental factors. These variations impact the challenges encountered during localization, including signal sparsity, device heterogeneity, and environmental interference. Addressing these challenges will require the development of robust, adaptable localization models capable of generalizing across diverse indoor environments.

Experimental setup

In this study, our objective is to compare the quality and limitations of various indoor positioning datasets rather than to evaluate or optimize algorithmic performance. To ensure consistency and isolate the effects of dataset characteristics, we employ a single, high-performing machine learning model—XGBoost—with fixed hyperparameters across all datasets.

XGBoost was selected due to its ability to handle high-dimensional, sparse RSSI data and its robustness to overfitting, which are essential in indoor localization tasks. Although it is not directly evaluated in all prior studies, its foundation in decision tree ensembles is strongly supported in the literature. For instance, Polak et al.48 found that tree-based models like decision trees and random forests outperform simpler baselines such as KNN in RSSI-based localization. Similarly, Esmaeili Gorjan and Gil Jiménez45 highlight the effectiveness of ensemble and ML-based methods in improving indoor positioning accuracy. While alternative models like KNN, SVM, or RF are widely used, introducing multiple algorithms would add variability that obscures the true effect of dataset characteristics. By standardizing the model across all experiments, we ensure that performance differences are attributable to dataset properties— such as AP density, signal variability, or device heterogeneity—rather than algorithm-specific behavior.

Experimental procedure

For each dataset, the data is split into training and testing sets using an 80-20 split, with 80% allocated for training and 20% for validation. For datasets that already have separate training and testing datasets, the testing data is reserved exclusively for evaluating the model’s performance on unseen data. In such cases, only the training data is further divided into 80% for training and 20% for validation. The model is trained on the training set and evaluated on the validation set. To prevent overfitting, early stopping is used, with training halting after 10 rounds of no improvement in the validation performance. Once the model has been trained, it is tested on the test set to calculate the evaluation metrics, such as localization accuracy, floor classification and the localization success rate. Finally, the results across the datasets are compared to understand how dataset characteristics, such as the number of APs, RPs, and environmental conditions, influence the accuracy of localization.

Each dataset presents unique issues, from signal sparsity and RSSI variability to complex building structures, all of which influence the model’s performance.

Fingerprint quality characterization

To provide deeper insight into the intrinsic signal characteristics of each dataset beyond model performance, we introduce five diagnostic metrics: Effective AP Count (EAC), Sparsity Level (SL), Signal Entropy Score (SES), AP Redundancy Score (ARS), and Device Bias Index (DBI). These metrics help quantify key dataset-level traits, such as signal richness, completeness, device-induced variation, and signal correlation.

EAC reflects the number of distinct access points (APs) that contribute non-trivial and sufficiently diverse signal information. SL quantifies the proportion of missing signal entries, indicating the sparsity of observations. SES captures the average unpredictability of signal values per AP, serving as a proxy for signal variability. ARS measures inter-AP correlation, where high values may indicate redundancy or co-located APs. Finally, DBI reflects the variance in signal levels across devices for each AP, highlighting device-induced measurement bias.

Table 8 presents the values of these metrics across eight benchmark datasets. The BLE dataset shows a relatively sparse and low-diversity signal structure (SL = 0.7161, SES = 1.34), expected from a low-density BLE deployment with only 14 effective APs. In contrast, UJI datasets (B0–B2) show dense coverage (SL = 0) with moderate entropy and low redundancy, suggesting good spatial resolution with relatively uncorrelated signals.

CECT exhibits high entropy and low sparsity, yet the DBI is undefined due to use of a single collection device, limiting analysis of device-induced variation. On the other hand, HCXY and SYL, both part of the SODIndoorLoc benchmark, display excellent signal density with higher redundancy (ARS > 0.28) and notably high DBI values (51.07 and 27.10 respectively), revealing significant device heterogeneity that must be considered in localization modeling.

The TUJI dataset, designed for multi-device analysis, also demonstrates moderate entropy and low redundancy (ARS = 0.0225), while DBI remains measurable (5.87), reaffirming the dataset’s emphasis on heterogeneity and fine-grained sampling. Collectively, these results inform dataset suitability, guide preprocessing needs, and aid in the development of device-agnostic localization models.

Comprehensive dataset analysis and recommendations

This section synthesizes key observations from both the EDA and experimental results, highlighting how dataset-specific characteristics impact localization accuracy, and outlining recommendations for improved modeling and data collection.

Dataset challenges and localization performance

Despite its fine spatial granularity, the BLE-based dataset exhibits notable constraints. A significant share of RSSI readings are weak, often below -105 dBm, limiting fingerprint distinctiveness and degrading signal coverage. Device heterogeneity, observable in RSSI variation across devices in Fig. 10a, introduces systematic bias that undermines generalization. Additionally, its static, single-floor nature restricts real-world applicability. Nonetheless, due to dense RP distribution and stable dominant APs (e.g., WAP5/WAP15), the dataset achieves high performance, with a low MAE of 1.271 m and RMSE of 1.610 m (Table 6), validating the model’s robustness under constrained but dense conditions.

The SODIndoorLoc dataset shows significant inter-building performance variability. CETC331’s multi-floor structure causes inter-floor interference, as seen in misclassifications in Fig. 12d, resulting in an MAE of 1.99 m and RMSE of 1.33 m. HCXY’s elongated corridor leads to edge signal attenuation, reflected in its higher MAE of 1.6 m. SYL exhibits the highest error among the three (MAE = 2.2 m), driven by a complex layout and frequent RSSI fluctuations, as seen in Fig. 6d. While dense AP and RP deployment help maintain baseline performance, overlapping signals and device-induced variability necessitate calibration. Overall, SODIndoorLoc’s MAE ranges from 1.9–2.2 m depending on building layout and interference patterns.

The UJIIndoorLoc dataset poses the greatest modeling challenges. Placeholder values (100 dBm) from undetected APs reduce fingerprint completeness. Varying RP density and spatial heterogeneity across buildings as evident from Fig. 4a–c

introduce training inconsistencies. Device-specific signal biases, shown in boxplots in Fig. 9e–g, demand normalization strategies for generalization. High inter-AP correlation causes feature redundancy. These factors manifest in poor localization performance, particularly in building 2 (MAE = 8.2 m, RMSE = 11.3 m) and building 1 (MAE = 7.85 m), while building 0 fares comparatively better (MAE = 4.54 m). The TUJI dataset benefits from a dense RP grid and consistent AP layout, enabling strong localization despite signal sparsity in certain zones. However, device heterogeneity between Samsung S20 and POCO introduces fluctuating RSSI levels, visible in Fig. 9h, resulting in an MAE of 1.399 m and RMSE of 1.831 m. The dataset’s single-floor static setting limits generalization to complex environments, yet its performance reflects the model’s ability to handle device variation when infrastructure is dense.

Across datasets, three major patterns emerge. First, signal sparsity increases error disproportionately: BLE performs well under weak but dense signals, whereas UJIIndoorLoc building 2 suffers under sparse, incomplete fingerprints. Second, device heterogeneity consistently introduces error variability, as seen in BLE and TUJI. Third, architectural complexity, such as floor overlap and elongated geometries, affects classification accuracy and error distributions, especially in CETC331 and HCXY. These insights are consolidated in Table 9 and illustrated in Figs. 8h and 12d.

Conclusion and future work

This paper presented a comprehensive evaluation of four publicly available indoor positioning datasets—BLE Indoor, SODIndoorLoc, TUJI1, and UJIIndoorLoc—through unified machine learning benchmarking and exploratory signal analysis. By applying a fixed XGBoost framework across all datasets, we isolated the influence of intrinsic dataset properties such as AP density, signal sparsity, device heterogeneity, and spatial configuration.

In addition to conventional performance metrics, we introduced five diagnostic indicators—Effective AP Count (EAC), Sparsity Level (SL), Signal Entropy Score (SES), AP Redundancy Score (ARS), and Device Bias Index (DBI)—to characterize signal richness, redundancy, and cross-device variability. The findings underscore that dataset quality—not just AP or RP density—plays a critical role in localization performance. Datasets with dense and structured layouts achieved low error rates, while those with sparse signals or uncalibrated devices exhibited degraded results.

We recognize that long‑term temporal drift can impact localization performance. In future work, we will apply our comparative framework to multi‑month datasets to quantify these effects. We will extend our comparative evaluation benchmark by incorporating and systematically evaluating device-adaptation and calibration techniques, including few-shot learning frameworks for rapid per-device model tuning, deep metric-learning approaches to align feature distributions across devices, and graph-based fingerprint update strategies for on-the-fly correction of device-specific RSSI shifts.

A key limitation of current public datasets is the lack of explicit LoS/NLoS labels for individual fingerprints; future datasets should include per‑sample propagation annotations or develop systematic relabeling methodologies to enable robustness evaluations under varying propagation conditions. Moving forward, we will also focus on integrating additional signal modalities such as CSI and conducting model‑specific benchmarking using deep learning architectures. We also recommend standardizing dataset collection protocols—covering AP metadata, calibration procedures, and temporal annotations—to ensure reproducibility and fairness in indoor localization research.
