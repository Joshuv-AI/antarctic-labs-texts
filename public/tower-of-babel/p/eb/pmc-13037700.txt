Carbapenem and colistin-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae co-producing NDM-5 and OXA-48 in a teaching hospital from Tunisia
Authors: Nadia Jaidane, Wejdene Mansour, Saoussen Oueslati, Imene Mehidi, Nahed Al Laham, Ali Lazzem, Amal Berriri, Yomna Ben Lamine, Abdelhalim Trabelsi, Laurent Dortet, Walid Naija, Rémy A Bonnin, Bogdan I Iorga, Lamia Tilouche, Thierry Naas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesCarbapenemase-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae (CPKp) represents a major public health threat due to limited treatment options, especially in low- and middle-income countries where colistin often remains the last active antibiotic. Here we investigated clinical carbapenem and colistin-resistant Kp isolates (CCoRKp) from Tunisia between January and June 2023.Materials and methods Kp isolates were identified by MALDI-TOF, broth microdilution susceptibility testing, NG-Test CARBA5 lateral flow immunoassay, Carba NP test, plasmid analysis and by whole-genome sequencing (WGS) for MLST, genetic relatedness and resistome characterization.ResultsAmong the 263 Kp isolates collected, 101 (38.4%) were carbapenem resistant and 34 (12.9%) were carbapenem and colistin resistant. Twelve isolates exhibited an extremely drug-resistant phenotype, with in vitro activity retained only for eravacycline and aztreonam/avibactam, two agents that are currently unavailable for clinical use in Tunisia. Nine out of 10 patients who were treated with broad-spectrum antibiotics, including colistin and imipenem, died. WGS revealed OXA-48 and NDM-5 (12/12), CTX-M-15 (9/12) and the 16SRNA methylase ArmA (11/12) and identified ST 101 (n = 5), ST147 (n = 3) and ST 383 (n = 4), suggesting multi-clonal outbreak. While OXA-48 producing ST101 and ST147 were already present in a 2013 collection, ST383 has never been reported in the hospital nor in Tunisia.ConclusionHere we report double carbapenemase producing and colistin-resistant Kps with limited therapeutic options. Major efforts are needed in infection control and availability of novel molecules in Tunisia to restore safe conditions in hospitals.

Introduction

Klebsiella pneumoniae (Kp) is a major hospital-acquired enterobacterial pathogen and a main cause of nosocomial infections.1,2 Frequent exposure to antibiotics in hospital settings and Kp’s propensity to acquire resistance genes, have led over the years to the development of a sophisticated networks of enzymatic and nonenzymatic antibiotic resistance mechanisms against most clinically available antibiotics, including last-resort drugs such as carbapenems.3,4 Kp has been recognized by the Infectious Diseases Society of America as one of the six most significant and dangerous pathogens (ESKAPE pathogens) responsible of drug-resistant hospital infections, especially due to the increasing occurrence carbapenem-resistant Kp (CRKp) isolates.5 In 2024, CRKp was recognized by the World Health Organization, as the top priority bacteria for which novel antibiotics are urgently needed and the recent Global Antibiotic Resistance Surveillance report 2025 revealed that CRKps have progressed at a median annual change of 15.3% between 2018 and 2023 (https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240116337).6

Carbapenem resistance in Kp may arise through different mechanisms including over-expression of efflux pumps, porin mutations together with the production of cephalosporinases or ESBLs, or the production of carbapenemases: enzymes capable of efficiently hydrolysing carbapenems. Carbapenemase-producing Kp (CPKp) are usually MDR and sometimes even XDR.2,7 The number of carbapenemases described in the literature is constantly increasing, but five of them (KPC, NDM, VIM, IMP and OXA 48-like) have been particularly successful and frequently encountered in Kp.8,9

Treatment of CPKp infections may sometimes be challenging as only few, if any, therapeutic options are available and thus leading to higher morbidity, mortality and healthcare costs.10 Treatment of infections with double or multiple carbapenemase-producing bacteria that are increasingly isolated worldwide may even be more challenging, as these isolates have increased chances of co-resistance to other classes of antibiotics and may carry more virulence factors, further complicating the clinical management of infections.11 NDM + OXA-48-like co-producing Kp isolates were the most prevalent double carbapenemase producers globally.11 The development of novel antibiotics such as cefiderocol, ceftazidime/avibactam or aztreonam/avibactam has made it possible to effectively treat again infections, that were previously considered to be virtually therapeutic dead ends.12 However, as these molecules are not available in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC), the treatment of CRKp infections relies mostly on colistin, an old cyclic polypeptide antibiotic, discovered in 1947, that was abandoned due to toxicity problems in 1970s.13 Countries that increasingly use colistin report emergence of colistin resistance due to the alteration of the lipid A biosynthetic pathway and modification of LPS surface by addition of 4-amino-4-deoxy-l-arabinose (Ara4N) and/or phosphoethanolamine (PEtN).14–17 Addition of Ara4N is mediated through the pmrHFIJKLM operon and pmrC, which are under the control of PmrAB, PmrD, PhoPQ, and the small transmembrane protein MgrB.16,18 In addition, the crrB gene also has been described to be involved in colistin resistance in Kp.18 Addition of PEtN is mediated by plasmid-encoded mcr-like genes that were initially described in Escherichia coli in China, and have since been described worldwide in Enterobacterales recovered from human and animal samples.19

The convergence of carbapenem and colistin resistance in Kp (CCoRKp) strains represents a major global health challenge, especially in LMICs that are already disproportionately burdened by high mortality rates, and for which the loss of the two last-resort drugs for treating severe Gram-negative infections would be disastrous.3 In Tunisia, the epidemiological situation of CRE has deteriorated in recent years, with hospitals reporting in 2023 in average 20% (range 3.9–28.7%) of CRKps and 1.4% (range 0.4–4.6%) CR E. coli isolates a national level (https://www.infectiologie.org.tn/pdf_ppt_docs/resistance/1740045766.pdf).20 This has largely been attributed to the rapid spread of OXA-48-producing Enterobacterales, particularly Kp.20 The emergence of colistin resistance among Kp (CoRKp) and Acinetobacter baumannii clinical isolates has been documented as early as 2013 in Tunisia.17,21 WGS of 13 CoRKp isolates revealed five different sequence types (ST11, ST15, ST101, ST147 and ST392) expressing various acquired β-lactamases, including CTX-M-15 ESBLs and OXA-48-like carbapenemases, and mgrB related colistin resistance. Here we investigated the molecular mechanisms sustaining carbapenem and colistin resistance in Kp clinical isolates recovered 10 years later from the same hospital.

Materials and methods

Bacterial isolates, susceptibility testing and carbapenemase confirmation assays

Kp isolates recovered from clinical samples of the university hospital Sahloul in Sousse, Tunisia were collected between January to June 2023. Susceptibility testing results obtained by Vitek2 (BioMérieux, Marcy-l’Etoile, France) were recovered from the laboratory informatic system (LIS) of the clinical microbiology laboratory.

CCoRKp were further investigated by collecting microbiological, epidemiological and clinical data to monitor and understand their spread. In addition to the recent data, historical data on carbapenem and colistin resistance were also retrieved from the LIS from January 2019 to December 2022. Bacterial identification of CCoRKp isolates was confirmed using MALDI-TOF-MS (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) and subsequently confirmed by WGS. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed by broth microdilution (BMD) (Sensititre, ThermoFisher) and interpreted according to EUCAST 2025 breakpoints (https://www.eucast.org/), except for veterinary-restricted agents interpreted using CA-SFM 2023 criteria. Colistin MICs were determined by BMD using the UMIC Colistine® kit (kit (Biocentric, Bandol, France). Tigecycline MICs were interpreted using EUCAST 2025 ECOFFs, and cefiderocol MICs were determined by BMD under iron-depleted conditions and interpreted according to EUCAST 2025. CoRKp isolates recovered in 2013–2015 from the same hospital were included for genetic relatedness studies.17 Home-made Carba NP test and NG-Test Carba 5 lateral flow immunoassays (NG-Biotech, Guipry, France) were used to confirm the presence of carbapenemase as previously described.22,23

Genetic support of carbapenem resistance

Plasmid DNA was extracted using the Kieser method and electroporated into E. coli Top10 strain as previously described.24,25 E. coli transformants were selected on ertapenem (0.5 μg/mL) containing TSA plates. Plasmids were visualized using electrophoresis on 0.7% agarose. E. coli NCTC 50192, which harbours four plasmids (7, 48, 66 and 154 kb) was used as size markers during electrophoresis.26

Whole-genome sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

Total DNA of CCoRKp isolates were extracted using the PureLink™ Genomic DNA Mini-Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, Les Ulis, France) following the manufacturer’s instructions and stored at −20°C. DNA libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II FS DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, Evry, France) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and run on a NextSeq 500 sequencer (Illumina, Evry, France) to generate paired-end 150-bp reads. WGS data were assembled de novo using the CLC genomics v.10.2 program (Qiagen, Les Ulis, France), and contigs were analysed using software available at the centre for genomic epidemiology (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/, accessed 24 August 2025), namely MLST v.2.0, ResFinder v.4.1, PlasmidFinder v.2.1 and VirulenceFinder v.2.0, to determine the ST, acquired resistance gene, plasmid replicon types and potential virulence genes, respectively. In addition, virulence factors were searched using virulence factor database (VFDB) (https://www.mgc.ac.cn/VFs/, accessed 20 August 2024) and was also used to search for virulence factors. The virulence scores were assessed according to the Kleborate tool (https://github.com/klebgenomics/Kleborate, accessed 3 September 2025).27 Reference plasmid sequences were retrieved from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, accessed 24 October 2025). Contigs carrying carbapenemase genes were mapped to reference plasmids, using CLC genomics v.10.2 program (Qiagen, Les Ulis, France). Mutations in the quinolone-resistance-determining region of gyr and par genes and genes encoding PBP (PBP1 to PBP6) were also compared with those of Kp MGH 78578 (GenBank accession number CP000647) used as the reference sequence.

For the geophylogeny study, 102 Kp ST383 genomes and their corresponding metadata were retrieved from Pathogenwatch (https://pathogen.watch/). Together with the four Kp ST383 genomes from this study, they were used for a cgMLST analysis using Chewbbaca (https://github.com/B-UMMI/chewBBACA)28 with the scheme scgMLST629_S (629 loci)29 downloaded from the Pasteur Institute and adapted for use with Chewbbaca. Allele calling at 95% sequence identity produced a cgMLST phylogenetic tree. The beta-lactamases from these genomes were identified using Abricate (https://github.com/tseemann/abricate) with the ‘ncbi’ database. The tree and the metadata were used to generate a Microreact representation that can be retrieved at https://microreact.org/project/tzZq1Sy6VvfBvQjEyLkH8G-kpst383tunisia and an iTOL representation is provided in Figure S2 (available as Supplementary data at JAC-AMR Online).

Nucleotide sequence accession number

This Whole-Genome Shotgun project has been deposited at DDBJ/ENA/GenBank under the accessions JBGRDP000000000-JBGRDZ000000000. The version described in this paper is version JBGRDP010000000-JBGRDZ010000000 (Table S1).

Results

Demographic, clinical and microbiological data

During the 6-month prospective period in 2023, 101 out of 263 clinical isolates (38.4%) were carbapenem resistant, of which 34 (12.9%) were identified as CCoRKp. To contextualize this emergence, a retrospective in silico analysis was performed using the Sahloul University Hospital Laboratory Information System (2019–2022). While carbapenem resistance significantly increased from 9% (51/550) in 2019 to 18% (122/676) in 2022, the prevalence of CCoRKp remained marginal during this period, with only two isolates identified (one in 2021 and one in 2022). A total of 21 viable CCoRKp isolates could be recovered for molecular and phenotypic characterization. Phenotypic screening using the Carba NP test and NG-Test Carba 5 revealed that 23.8% (n = 5) produced OXA-48-like only, 9.5% (n = 2) produced NDM-like only, 57.1% (n = 12) co-produced OXA-48-like and NDM-like enzymes and 14.3% (n = 3) were non-carbapenemase producers, suggesting alternative resistance mechanisms such as porin loss and/or overexpressed efflux pumps. The 12 double carbapenemase producers were further characterized. They were collected from patients hospitalized in different wards with several comorbidities including prolonged ICU stays (9/12) with mechanical ventilation, intravascular devices and heavy surgery (8/12) (Table 1). Ten out of the 12 patients received empiric treatment composed of broad-spectrum antibiotics including in four cases colistin and imipenem, two drugs used in Tunisian ICUs for severe Gram-negative infections. Seven patients died and the remaining five were discharged or transferred to the physical medicine and rehabilitation ward. All the 12 isolates exhibited an extremely drug-resistant phenotype, with in vitro activity retained only for eravacycline and aztreonam/avibactam, two agents that are currently unavailable for clinical use in Tunisia. Although low tigecycline MICs were observed (0.25 mg/L), no susceptibility interpretation was applied due to the absence of EUCAST breakpoints for Kp.

WGS analysis of CCoRKp isolates

The draft genome of the 12 CCoRKp isolates resulted in a mean number of contigs of 147 with an L50 of 19 and an N50 of 109 452 bp. Average GC content of 56% and genome size of 5.7 Mb are compatible with the genome of Kp MGH 78578 (GenBank accession no. CP000647). CCoRKp isolates carried between 17 and 23 resistance genes, among which 5 to 6 were ß-lactamase genes, including carbapenemase (blaOXA-48, blaNDM-5) and ESBL (blaCTX-M-15) genes (Table 2). Resistance genes against other antibiotics including aminoglycosides [aac(6’)-Ib, aph(3’)-Ia, aph(3’)-VI, armA], sulfonamides (sul2, sul1), trimethoprim (dfrA5), chloramphenicol (catA1, CatB3), fosfomycin (fosA), rifampicin (arr-3) and the quaternary ammonium resistance genes (qacE) were detected in most strains (Table 2).

Fluoroquinolone resistance was mediated by plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance gene qnrS1 (12/12), and a bifunctional aminoglycoside and fluoroquinolone-resistance gene aac(6’)lb-cr (3/12) (Table 2), in addition to chromosomal mutations in the quinolone resistance-determining region of gyrA and parC genes, resulting in amino acid changes S83F/Y and D87N in GyrA and S80I in ParC (Table S2).30 Amino acid (AA) changes detected in AcrR and RamR, two other proteins incriminated in quinolone resistance are probably polymorphisms.

As no plasmid-mediated colistin resistance genes, mcr-1 to mcr-10 were detected, mutations in chromosomal genes involved in colistin resistance in Kp (mgrB, the two-component regulatory systems phoPQ and pmrAB, the EptA and crrB genes) were searched (Table 3).31,32 Mutations were detected in all mgrB genes resulting in F28C substitution, while isolate Kp9_O108J7 carried an extra ΔK2–I6 frameshift and V7G/I8S substitutions, producing a truncated 38-AA MgrB protein (Table 3). In addition, several nucleotide changes leading to AA changes, compared with reference sequences in pmrA, pmrB, phoP, phoQ, arnBCADTEF operon and eptA (pmrC) genes, were identified (Table 3). Complete deletion of phoPQ was observed in isolates Kp3_O108J1. CrrAB, a two-component system regulating pmrAB via crrC, was only present in three isolates with a C68S change in CrrB (Table 3).33

MLST and phylogeny

The CCoRKp isolates belonged to three different STs: 5 ST101, 3 ST147 and 4 ST383, known for their worldwide descriptions and causing enormous burden due their frequent association with various carbapenemase genes, e.g. blaVIM blaNDM, blaOXA-204, blaOXA-48, blaOXA-181, blaKPC and the ESBL gene blaCTX-M-15.34,35 The ST101 were highly related to Kp isolates from the same hospital isolated in 2015, and carrying only OXA-48 (Figure 1).17,36 Similarly, ST147 was already present in 2013 collection but expressing OXA-204 carbapenemase. ST383 has not been described in the isolates of 2013–2015.17 Geophylogenetic analysis of the global ST383 population showed that the four ST383 isolates recovered in this study formed a well-supported monophyletic branch embedded within a larger cluster of ST383 strains that have been reported from multiple continents since 2013. All isolates within this broader cluster shared highly similar combinations of β-lactamase genes, consistent with a close genetic relatedness across geographically distant strains. Four strains collected in Italy, the UK and the USA during the period 2019–2021 are very similar to our four ST383 strains (Figure S2 and Microreact website (https://microreact.org/project/tzZq1Sy6VvfBvQjEyLkH8G-kpst383tunisia).

Genetic support of carbapenem resistance

All CCoRKp isolates possessed several plasmids varying in size, as revealed by Kieser plasmid analysis (Figure S1).24 All isolates carried roughly 7-kb, 66-kb, 180-kb plasmids in size, except ST147 isolates that carried in addition a ∼60-kb plasmid. While carbapenem-resistant determinants could be transferred by electroporation to E. coli Top10 (data not shown), colistin resistance was not transferable, further suggesting the chromosomal nature of this resistance determinant. PCR experiments on transformants confirmed the presence of blaCTX-M and blaNDM genes on the 180-kb plasmid, whereas blaOXA-48 was associated with the 66-kb plasmid (data not shown). PlasmidFinder revealed the presence of several incompatibility groups, namely IncColRNAI, probably corresponding to the 7-kb plasmid, IncL corresponding to the 66-kb OXA-48-carrying plasmid, IncFIB probably corresponding to the 180-kb plasmid carrying NDM and IncHI1B probably present on the IncFIB plasmid (Table 2).37

Virulence profile

Basic pathogenicity factors such as lipopolysaccharide, capsular polysaccharide, type 3 fimbriae, the siderophores aerobactin (iuc) and the intrinsic siderophore enterobactin (Ent) that belong to the core genome of all Kp were identified. Data output by Kleborate revealed that 9/12 Kp isolates had a virulence score of three or more (indicative of yersiniabactin plus aerobactin/virulence plasmid detection) and a resistance score of 2 (corresponding to carbapenem resistance). Interestingly, colistin resistance was not detected by Kleborate. In addition, it is interesting to note that the isolates from 2013–2015 had lower virulence score, suggesting that the additional plasmid carrying blaNDM gene brought several virulence factors.

Susceptibility to last-resort antibiotics including veterinarian restricted molecules

As all the CCoRKp isolates were resistant on routine antibiograms to all available antibiotics in Tunisia except tigecycline, we determined the susceptibility of four representative isolates, chosen based on the ST and resistome, to 47 antibiotics including several newly commercialized antibiotics and some veterinarian restricted molecules. All isolates were resistant to fluoroquinolones including the novel delfloxacin molecule. High MICs were obtained for most ß-lactams including carbapenems with or without relebactam or vaborbactam (MICs > 16 mg/L), and to ceftazidime/avibactam combination (MIC >16 mg/L) (Table 4). All isolates were resistant to amikacin and gentamicin, except Kp21_O109A9 isolate that is lacking ArmA 16SRNA methylase. The tested isolates remained susceptible only to aztreonam-avibactam (4/4), eravacyclin (4/4), chloramphenicol (4/4, using veterinary breakpoints) and fosfomycin (4/4, using E. coli breakpoints for urinary tract infections). Only two isolates were susceptible to cefiderocol with MICs of 1 mg/L (2/4). Three isolates were susceptible to apramycin and neomycin (3/4), two aminoglycosides used in the veterinarian field. Of all these tested molecules, only tigecycline and fosfomycin are available in Tunisia. Tigecycline is not recommended for bacteraemia and no breakpoints for Klebsiella sp. are available,37 even though these molecules are used in many countries off label as very last treatment option for Kp infections (EUCAST 2025 guidelines, https://www.eucast.org/clinical_breakpoints).38

Discussion

This study revealed 38.4% carbapenem resistance among Kp isolates, markedly higher than the ∼20% local average reported in Tunisian hospitals in 2023. Carbapenem resistance increased from 9% in 2019 to 34% in 2023, probably reflecting clonal dissemination of at least three sequence types within Sahloul Hospital. In our earlier study covering isolates up to 2015,39 colistin consumption was low, whereas the present analysis shows that the rise in carbapenem resistance was temporally associated with a marked increase in colistin use, which grew from 6.1 DDD/1000 patient-days in 2018 to >20 in 2021 (antibiotic consumption data retrieved from pharmacy records; data not shown). This temporal association coincided with the emergence of CCoRKp, consistent with the hypothesis that increased colistin pressure may favour the selection of colistin-resistant strains.15 While CCoRKp isolates were rare (0.1% between 2019 and 2022), in 2023 they accounted for 12.9% of the Kps. Colistin-resistant Kp isolates were initially described from the same hospital in 2013 associated with OXA-48-like (6/13) and NDM-1 (1/13) carbapenemases.17 In this study, a clear shift occurred from single carbapenemase (mostly OXA-48) in 2013, to double carbapenemase (OXA-48/NDM-5) co-producers in 2023.

bla OXA-48 gene is present in all isolates on a ∼66-kb plasmid likely corresponding to the IncL prototypical OXA-48 plasmid.40 BlaCTX-M and blaNDM are carried on a large >180-kb plasmid corresponding likely to IncFIB, an incompatibility group frequently associated with antibiotic resistance determinants, such as blaCTX-M-15, and blaNDM genes.40,41

Co-production of carbapenemases has been increasingly described globally with Kp and NDM/OXA-48 is the most frequently encountered combination.11 The co-production of NDM and OXA-48-like carbapenemases in Kp is characterized by the convergence of high-risk international clones and highly transmissible plasmid backbones rather than isolated sporadic events.11 Across Europe, the Arabian Peninsula, North America, and the Mediterranean basin, epidemic lineages such as ST11, ST14, ST15, ST101, ST147, ST167, ST340 and ST383 have been repeatedly identified as genetic platforms capable of accumulating multiple carbapenemase genes through compatible plasmids with horizontal gene transfer representing the principal driver of blaNDM-like and blaOXA-48-like dissemination, complemented by clonal expansion of successful lineages often associated with additional resistance determinants such as 16S rRNA methylases.42–48 Mechanistically, the complementary hydrolytic activities of NDM (efficiently hydrolysing nearly all β-lactams except aztreonam) and OXA-48-like enzymes (notably active against penicillins and carbapenems) result in broadened resistance phenotypes, increased carbapenem MICs, particularly for ertapenem and meropenem and reduced effectiveness of β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations, thereby contributing to therapeutic limitation and increased mortality; the situation becomes even more concerning with hypervirulent STs. In North Africa, and particularly Tunisia, this international dynamic is mirrored within a Mediterranean context where OXA-48-like enzymes have become endemic and NDM has progressively emerged.49

The first Tunisian report of an ST11 isolate from a Libyan patient co-harbouring blaNDM-1 and blaOXA-48 on transferable plasmids marked a critical epidemiological shift, signalling both introduction of globally disseminated clones and the establishment of a mobilizable resistance reservoir.50 Subsequent surveillance demonstrated successive clonal waves dominated by ST101 and ST147 carrying OXA-48 or OXA-204 on IncL and IncA/C plasmids, indicating sustained endemicity driven by both clonal expansion and horizontal plasmid transfer.51 More recent outbreaks involving co-production of OXA-48-like and NDM enzymes, sometimes coupled with colistin resistance through mgrB disruption, illustrate a stepwise evolution toward multidrug and near pan-drug resistance within already established carbapenemase-producing backgrounds.52 Collectively, these data suggest that the Tunisian epidemiology does not represent an isolated phenomenon but rather a localized expression of a global pattern in which successful plasmids, high-risk clones, antimicrobial selective pressure and international patient mobility converge to promote the stable maintenance, amplification and hospital dissemination of dual-carbapenemase genotypes.

The CCoRKp isolates remained susceptible only to tigecycline and fosfomycin, among the antibiotics available in Tunisia. Tigecyclin is approved to treat complicated skin and skin structure infections, complicated intra-abdominal infections and community-acquired bacterial pneumonia but not for hospital-acquired or ventilator-associated pneumonia and bacteriemia.37 In addition, several studies have shown an increased risk of death when intravenous (IV) tigecycline is used, thus suggesting its use only as a very last therapeutic option. For fosfomycin, no breakpoints other than for E. coli isolated from urinary tract infections are available. Even though EUCAST does not recommend breakpoints for IV use of fosfomycin nor for other Enterobacterales then E. coli, in many countries facing high level of carbapenem resistance, it has been used to treat infections with MDR Kp isolates.53,54

The tested isolates remained susceptible to newer molecules, such as aztreonam-avibactam, known to be active on MBL producers and eravacycline, a derivative of tigecycline with improved PK/PD properties (4/4), not yet available in Tunisia, and veterinary-restricted antibiotics such as chloramphenicol (4/4) and apramycin and neomycin (3/4). Apramycin is currently under evaluation in human clinical studies, as it is active against GNB-carrying genes encoding aminoglycoside modifying enzymes or 16S-RMTases, with the exception of the aac(3)-IV gene, the AAC(2)-encoding gene apmA and genes encoding the rarely found 16S-RMTases methylating the A1408 residue of the 16S rRNA (i.e. npmA and npmB).55,56 16S-RMTases are frequently encountered in NDM-producing isolates as they are usually carried on the same plasmid.57 Resistance of isolate Kp3_O108J1 to apramycin could not be explained, and none of the known resistance gene leading to high level resistance was found. Only 2/4 isolates were susceptible to cefiderocol with MICs of 1 mg/L, which is close to the breakpoints. These results are in line with previous studies that have revealed that NDM-producers have frequently MICs close or slightly above breakpoints.58

All the CCoRKp were resistant to fluoroquinolone because of plasmid-encoded genes (qnr and aac6’1b-cr) as well as mutations in the quinolone resistance-determining region of gyrA leading to AA changes S83F/Y and D87N and parC leading to S80I of ParC proteins. Colistin resistance in Kp involves mostly mutations or disruptions in the mgrB gene.59 The F28C substitution in MgrB detected across all strains, is probably at the origin of colistin resistance, as previously demonstrated through complementation assays,17 but other AA changes in other proteins involved in the LPS biosynthesis, such as PmrA, PmrB, PhoP, PhoQ, ArnBCADTEF and EptA (PmrC), cannot be ruled out (Table 3).

The CCoRKp isolates belonged to three STs (ST101, ST147 and ST383) that are considered as high-risk clones given their global high prevalence and frequent association with various broad-spectrum ß-lactamase genes, e.g. blaVIM blaNDM, blaOXA-204, blaOXA-48, blaOXA-181, blaKPC and the ESBL gene blaCTX-M-15. Kp ST147, initially reported in 2008 in Hungary and Spain, has since been described globally, becoming one of the most successful Kp clone responsible of hospital outbreaks, probably because of its multidrug resistant phenotype due to mutations and the acquisition of carbapenemase encoding genes (blaNDM, blaOXA-48 and blaKPC). Kp ST147 is endemic in Tunisia and has been reported in hospital outbreaks associated with OXA-48-like carbapenemases.17,60,61 Other studies reported the spread of these bacteria from Tunisia to various European countries, such as France and Poland.62

There are limited data on the prevalence of ST101 in Tunisia. It was initially described in 2010 from a single Kp isolate producing OXA-48 from Tunis, and subsequently in 2013 in several Kps producing OXA-48 from Sousse.17,63 These isolates had a very low virulence score. The ST101 isolates of this study that come from the same hospital are highly related to those of 2013 isolates except that they co-produce OXA-48 and NDM, and that their Kleborate virulence score rose from 0 or 1 to 3 or 4.17 These results are in line with the global trends, which suggest the presence of KpST101 among invasive carbapenem-resistant and hypervirulent isolates circulating in Europe.

Kp ST383 has rarely been described in Tunisia, with one single description in Tunis, in 2010 associated with OXA-48 carbapenemase. In this study, we report the local emergence of Kp ST383 co-producing OXA-48 and NDM. Kp ST383 is considered as an emerging high-risk clone due its global spread and carbapenem resistance profiles.64 In the geophylogenetic analysis, the four Kp ST383 strains characterized in this study occupied a well-defined branch within a larger, globally distributed cluster of Kp ST383 isolates that have been reported from multiple continents since 2013. The fact that all strains in this cluster share very similar genomic content and combinations of beta-lactamase genes strongly suggests that they derive from a recent common ancestor and reflect ongoing clonal dissemination. Four Kp ST383 strains collected in 2019 in Italy and UK and in 2020 and 2021 in the USA show a striking genetic similarity to the four described in this study. This finding indicates that lineages closely related to the Tunisian strains are already circulating in geographically distant regions, suggesting the possibility of unrecognized international transmission events. However, in the absence of detailed epidemiological data (e.g. travel history, patient movement or contact tracing), it is not possible to reconstruct the directionality of spread or to determine the precise transmission routes by which ST383 was introduced into Tunisia. The detection of Kp ST383 in many countries in health care settings may suggest an increased virulence as revealed with a virulence score of 3/5.65 Adding carbapenem resistance may result in severe and difficult to treat infections including pneumonia, urinary tract infections, bloodstream infections and liver abscesses.66,67 Wu et al. proposed that hypervirulent Kp isolates should meet at least two of the following criteria: (i) positive string test; (ii) rmpA-positive and (iii) aerobactin-positive.68 The Kp ST101 and Kp STS383 isolates from this study even though with virulence scores of 4 and 3, respectively, are positive only for aerobactin, thus do not meet the hypervirulence definition.

Conclusion

Here we present the occurrence of three CCoRKp lineages co-producing NDM-5, OXA-48 and CTX-M-15 from Tunisia. Our work further illustrates the shift from single OXA-48 carbapenemase producers to double carbapenemase OXA-48 and NDM-5 producers along with increased virulence score. The presence of multiple carbapenemases increases the chances of co-resistance to other classes of antibiotics and more virulence factors, further complicating the clinical management of infections.11

The increase in carbapenem resistance has led to increased use of colistine, which in turns resulted in increased colistin resistance leaving very few therapeutic options. Tigecyclin and fosfomycin could be interesting alternatives, but access to recently commercialized molecules, especially those active on MBLs, is crucial to efficiently treating CCoRKp infections in Tunisia. Until these novel drugs become available in Tunisia, major efforts must be set up to implement robust surveillance, antibiotic stewardship and infection control programmes, especially in ICUs where all the risk factors (severely ill patients, antibiotic consumptions) that contribute to the development and spread of AMR are present.
