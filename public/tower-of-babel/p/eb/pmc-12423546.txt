Evaluation of the Risk of Adverse Maternal and Neonatal Outcomes Among Immigrant and Native‐Born Women: A Retrospective Observational Analysis
Authors: Júlia Siscart, Laura Gascó, Maria Catalina Serna, Míriam Orós, Daniel Perejón, Blanca Salinas‐Roca
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsAlthough ethnic disparities in birth outcomes have been well documented, it is unclear whether certain immigrant groups have a higher risk of suffering from perinatal complications compared to native‐born Spanish women. The objective of this study was to identify prenatal and perinatal complications among immigrant populations and residents of a region of Catalonia.MethodsA retrospective observational cohort study was conducted among pregnant women in the Lleida health region between 2012 and 2018. Perinatal indicators of obstetric outcomes were compared between the immigrant and native‐born Spanish population. The risk was calculated using univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis.ResultsA sample of 17,281 women, accounting for over 92% of the pregnant population in the Lleida health region, was analyzed. Among this sample, preeclampsia occurred in 2.4% of sub‐Saharan women. They also presented the highest percentage of cesarean sections of 22.5%. Apgar score in the first minute < 7 was observed in 4.6% of newborns of sub‐Saharan mothers compared to 2.3%–2.5% of the rest (other immigrant populations plus native populations). The populations from Latin America had a higher risk of cesarean sections (21.6%) and macrosomia (8.7%). Women from Eastern Europe had a lower percentage of cesarean sections (15.9%). The Maghreb population had a lower percentage of cesarean sections (15.5%) and a lower birth weight (3.2%). However, a higher macrosomia percentage of 10.7% was observed.ConclusionsDifferences are observed in the risk during pregnancy and in the newborn between the immigrant and the national population. These data corroborate the exposure to greater risk by the immigrant population, a preventive approach and early treatment is necessary to avoid complications in the mother and the newborn.Keywords: adverse pregnancy outcomes, international immigration, pregnancy

Introduction

In 2019, the global population of international migrants was estimated at 272 million, marking an increase of 51 million compared to 2010 [1]. Spain has undergone significant demographic growth, largely driven by migration. The share of immigrants in the Spanish population grew from 2.2% in the 1991 Census to 13.5% in the 2011 Census. By 2023, immigrants represented 17.2% of the population in Catalonia and 21% in Lleida. According to data from the Statistical Institute of Catalonia (Idescat), immigration has become a prominent global social issue. Factors such as conflict, human rights violations, socioeconomic inequalities, and employment opportunities frequently drive individuals to migrate in pursuit of a better quality of life [2]. The root causes of immigration are multifaceted, arising from a complex interaction of factors both within and beyond individual control. These include political, socioeconomic, and educational influences, as well as immediate triggers such as natural disasters, violence, and conflict [3].

Several studies have highlighted the difficulties faced by immigrant populations in accessing healthcare services due to political, linguistic, and socioeconomic barriers, which may contribute to higher morbidity rates [4, 5, 6]. The interplay between socioeconomic deprivation and the stressors associated with the migration process can further worsen these challenges [7]. Migrant women often begin their migration journey during their reproductive years, a period in which exposure to new environments, contexts, and lifestyles may elevate both biological and psychosocial risks, thereby increasing their vulnerability [5]. Some studies have reported a higher incidence of obstetric and perinatal complications among immigrant women compared to native‐born residents [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. However, other research indicates that perinatal health outcomes for immigrant women may be similar to, or even better than, those of the native population.

Studies conducted within Spanish territory often lack disaggregation by country of origin or focus on specific complications [15, 16, 17]. In contrast, a recent systematic review and meta‐analysis investigating the link between immigration status and adverse perinatal outcomes revealed a heterogeneous pattern, suggesting that immigrant women are at an increased risk for certain significant adverse pregnancy outcomes [18]. Given the inconclusive nature of the available evidence, this study aimed to explore the risk of adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes among immigrant women compared to their native‐born counterparts in the host country.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Data Collection

We performed a retrospective observational cohort study with pregnant women in the Lleida health region.

Information on women who gave birth at Arnau de Vilanova Hospital between January 1, 2012 and December 31, 2018, was extracted from the CMBD database (Conjunt Minim de Base de Dades). These data encompassed the period from the last menstrual period up to the date of delivery. Specifically, data from all eligible patients assigned to a primary care unit were obtained from the E‐CAP computerized clinical history database of the Catalan Health Institute, along with prescription data from the Social Security database maintained by the Servei Català de Salut. The Arnau de Vilanova Hospital serves as the primary public reference hospital for the Health Region. Consequently, it handles a substantial proportion of deliveries within the entire Health Region. In Spain, the healthcare system offers universal accessibility.

This article is part of the Iler Pregnancy Project, a retrospective cohort study conducted in Lleida. This study is a component of the larger ILERPRENANT project, which relies on a shared database. The primary objective of the project was to assess the population prevalence of various pregnancy‐related pathologies, as well as patterns of therapeutic prescriptions and medication adherence [19, 20].

Study Population

All women who have had a birth at the Arnau de Vilanova University Hospital in Lleida during the study period were included in the study. Patients without a personal identification code (PIC) or with medical histories missing multiple data points were excluded from the study. To assess the representativeness of the sample, we computed the proportion of pregnant women included in our study relative to the total number of pregnant women in the Lleida health region. These data were sourced from the “Instituto de Estadística de Cataluña” (Idescat) database (Table 1).

Obstetric variables related to pregnancy and delivery were extracted from the medical records of the mothers involved in the study. The following perinatal care indicators were evaluated: age; region of origin (sub‐Saharan Africa, Latin America, Asia and the Middle East, West Europe, Eastern Europe, and Maghreb) [21]; body mass index (BMI); number of pregnancy and twin pregnancy; delivery mode (vaginal delivery vs. CS); cesarean section; diabetes mellitus (code O24.9 at internacional classification of diseases [ICD]‐10.); hypothyroidism (codes EO3.9 and EO2 at ICD‐10) arterial hypertension (codes I10–I16 at l'ICD‐10); dyslipidemia (code E78 at l'ICD‐10); depression (codes F32.0–F32.9, F33.0–F33.3, F33.8, F33.9, F34.1, or F41.2 at l'ICD‐10); preeclampsia; risk during pregnancy; fetal complications included: (a) prolonged or preterm delivery (PTD), defined as a GA at birth < 37 weeks; risk during pregnancy; newborn weight, classified as low weight (< 2500 g), normal weight (2500–4000 g), and macrosomia (> 4000 g) [20]; and score of the Apgar test in the first minute and in the fifth, classified as good when ≥ 7 points and bad when < 7 points. Pregnant women received healthcare in accordance with the prenatal care protocol established by the Catalan health system [22].

Data Analysis

A descriptive analysis was performed, summarizing numerical variables with means and standard deviations, while categorical variables were presented as absolute and relative frequencies. Group comparisons were conducted using Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) for numerical data and χ 2 tests for categorical data. Multivariate logistic regression was utilized to investigate associations between country of origin and other variables, with adjusted odds ratios (OR) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) reported. Data analysis was carried out using R version 4.3.2.

Results

Participants' Demographic Characteristics

Initially, we selected a cohort of 21,375 women who delivered at Arnau de Vilanova Hospital in Lleida from 2012 to 2018. From this initial cohort, 1625 individuals were excluded due to the absence of a personal identification code (CIP), and 2573 were removed due to incomplete clinical records. Consequently, the final data set comprised 17,177 patients (Figure 1).

Sample of pregnant women studied.

The population distribution according to the region of origin was sub‐Saharan Africa (5.6%), Latin America (4.8), Asia and the Middle East (1.4%), Western Europe (63%), Eastern Europe (10.3%), and Maghreb (14.9%). There was a similar percentage in the different years of study (Table 1). The higher age was observed in women from Western Europe (middle age: 30.9 years), and lower in women from Eastern Europe (middle age: 28.7 years). The BMI of patients was 25 ± 5.2. In the Maghreb population, the median BMI was 27 ± 5.7.

Obstetric and Perinatal History

Women from sub‐Saharan Africa had the highest percentage (8.3%) of more than four previous pregnancies, compared to only 1.2% among Eastern European women. Preeclampsia occurred in 2.4% of sub‐Saharan women, a figure higher than the rest of the groups. They also presented the highest percentage of cesarean sections with 22.5%. No significant differences were observed in the duration of the pregnancy, although there were differences in the evaluation as a high‐risk pregnancy, which in the group of sub‐Saharan women was 37.9%.

Distribution of Adverse Maternal and Neonatal Outcomes

Maghreb women had more children with macrosomia. While those from sub‐Saharan Africa had a higher percentage of low‐weight children. Also, the Apgar score in the first minute < 7 was observed in 4.6% of newborns of sub‐Saharan mothers compared to 2.3%–2.5% of the rest (Table 2).

Multivariate Analysis by Region of Origin

The multivariate model according to country of origin shows an increased risk in the sub‐Saharan population of cesarean section, preeclampsia, high‐risk pregnancy, and a higher percentage of newborns with an Apgar < 7 in the first minute compared to the autochthonous population. Regarding the population from Latin America, they have a higher risk of cesarean sections (21.6%) and macrosomia (8.7%) with respect to regional women (18.5% risk of cesarean section and 5.6% risk of macrosomia). With a better percentage of prolonged pregnancy. As for women from Eastern Europe, a lower percentage of cesarean section (15.9%) and pregnancy risk was found. The Maghreb population had a lower percentage of cesarean sections (15.5%), a lower high‐risk pregnancy and a lower birth weight (3.2%), although a higher percentage of macrosomia was found (10.7%) (Figures 2, 3, 4, 5, 6).

Discussion

The objective of this study was to assess prenatal and perinatal complications among immigrants and residents in a region of Catalonia. The findings indicated an association between immigration status and the risk of adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes. In this context, sub‐Saharan women presented a higher risk of cesarean section, Apgar < 7 in the first minute, preeclampsia, and high‐risk pregnancy than native women. However, a lower proportion of prolonged pregnancy was observed. Those from Latin America had a higher risk of cesarean section and macrosomia of the newborn, as well as a lower proportion of prolonged pregnancy than native women. Those from Eastern Europe had a lower prevalence of cesarean sections and a lower risk of high or very high‐risk pregnancy. In relation to women from the Maghreb, a lower percentage of cesarean sections and very high‐risk pregnancies were corroborated, as well as a lower percentage of low birth weight newborns, although a greater number of macrosomic newborns were observed.

Recently, there have been urgent calls to address racial and ethnic disparities in maternal morbidity and mortality [23, 24]. Ethnic disparities in birth outcomes are well‐documented [25]. Some immigrant and ethnic groups are more susceptible to adverse pregnancy outcomes, whereas others demonstrate more favorable perinatal health indicators despite facing socioeconomic vulnerabilities.

Migrant women from sub‐Saharan Africa faced more than double the risk of requiring emergency cesarean sections compared to women from Norway, irrespective of prepregnancy BMI [26]. In Spanish studies, Gombau‐Giménez and colleagues observed increased rates of emergency cesarean sections among sub‐Saharan women, comparing 245 women from countries where female genital mutilation is practiced to 490 Spanish women in terms of obstetric complications [27]. In this study, cesarean deliveries were conducted in 20.82% of the overall participant population, exceeding the recommended standard set by the WHO [28]. The rate of cesarean sections among sub‐Saharan women was twice that of their Spanish counterparts. Additionally, a study by Cacciani and colleagues in Italy found that being of Western or sub‐Saharan African origin was significantly associated with adverse perinatal outcomes. Specifically, the 5‐min Apgar scores were lower among West and sub‐Saharan Africans as well as Central and South Americans [29].

In the analysis of the population of Latin America, Eastern Europe, or the Maghreb in terms of cesarean section, Rio and colleagues corroborated that the risk of cesarean sections varied markedly by area of origin, being higher for Latin Americans (OR = 1.09, 95% CI = 1.05–1.13) and lower for East Europeans (OR = 0.61, 95% CI = 0.57–0.66) and Maghrebians (OR = 0.60, 95% CI = 0.57–0.63) [30]. These results are consistent with those of our study, although the study by Rios and colleagues did not include representation of the sub‐Saharan population.

Regarding the results of the Apgar score, a monocentric observational study carried out in Brussels, after adjustment for confounding factors, observed no differences in labor and delivery complications. Although recent immigrant newborns had a lower umbilical cord blood pH (< 0.05), a lower 1‐min Apgar score (p < 0.01), and more frequently required respiratory assistance (p < 0.05) [31].

The findings from the systematic review and meta‐analysis indicated an association between immigration status and the risk of cesarean sections as well as a 5‐min Apgar score of < 7. Specifically, immigrant women exhibited a significantly higher risk of emergency cesarean sections (pooled OR = 1.1, 95% CI = 1.0–1.2) and 5‐min Apgar scores below 7 (pooled OR = 1.2, 95% CI = 1.0–1.3) compared to those of native origin [32].

In the analysis of preeclampsia and eclampsia, data from the PreCARE cohort of pregnant women in Paris demonstrated that sub‐Saharan African immigrant women have a twofold increased risk of developing severe preeclampsia compared to European‐born women, with one‐fifth of this risk attributable to prepregnancy obesity [33]. In Europe, immigrant women from sub‐Saharan Africa, Latin America, and the Caribbean are at a heightened risk for preeclampsia [34]. Our results indicated that only sub‐Saharan women exhibited an elevated risk of preeclampsia.

These findings are consistent with a study analyzing disparities in preeclampsia and eclampsia among immigrant women from various regions worldwide giving birth in six industrialized countries, utilizing provincial or regional obstetric delivery data from Australia, Canada, Spain, and the USA, along with national data from Denmark and Sweden [35]. The cross‐country comparative analysis of linked population‐based databases revealed that, compared to immigrants from Western Europe, those from sub‐Saharan Africa and Latin America and the Caribbean faced a higher risk of preeclampsia (OR: 1.72; 95% CI: 1.63–1.80 and 1.63; 95% CI: 1.57–1.69, respectively) and eclampsia (OR: 2.12; 95% CI: 1.61–2.79 and 1.55; 95% CI: 1.26–1.91, respectively) after adjusting for parity, maternal age, and destination country.

Regarding macrosomia in the newborn, in the Italian study, the immigrant cohort experienced a higher rate of macrosomia (1.8% vs. 0.6%; p = 0.001). Latin Americans also carried a greater risk of fetal macrosomia (3.6%; p < 0.008) while the rate of very low birth weight was higher among Europeans and Africans (2% and 1.8%, respectively; p < 0.04) [36]. In our study, we observed similar results regarding a higher frequency of macrosomia in Latin American women, while the presentation of low birth weight was higher in women from Africa in the Italian study. In our study, women from the Maghreb had a higher frequency of macrosomia and a lower percentage of low birth weight. Possibly, this is due to the division of African women into two groups (sub‐Saharan and Maghreb). These differences may be due to the fact that these two populations have different characteristics.

This study has some data shared with another study of Siscart [20]. Both studies analyze the health outcomes of 17,177 women who gave birth at Arnau de Vilanova Hospital in Lleida between 2012 and 2018, focusing on the impact of region of origin on obstetric and chronic health outcomes. The population was classified by region: sub‐Saharan Africa, Latin America, Asia and the Middle East, Western Europe, Eastern Europe, and Maghreb. Significant differences in health outcomes were observed across these groups. Sub‐Saharan African women exhibited higher rates of preeclampsia (2.4%) and cesarean sections (22.5%), with a higher incidence of low birth weight and lower Apgar scores. Women from Latin America had higher rates of cesarean sections (21.6%) and macrosomia (8.7%). In contrast, Eastern European women had lower rates of cesarean sections (15.9%) and pregnancy risks. The study also highlighted a higher prevalence of chronic conditions in women from sub‐Saharan Africa and Maghreb, such as hypertension (7%) and diabetes mellitus (14.4%). Multivariate analysis revealed a significantly increased risk of obesity, diabetes, and chronic diseases in women from sub‐Saharan Africa, Maghreb, and Latin America, while a lower risk of depression was found in these groups compared to native European women. These findings emphasize the importance of considering regional and socioeconomic factors in maternal healthcare strategies.

Difficulties and Limitations of the Study

Regarding the limitations of the study, it is necessary to mention the lack of information regarding socioeconomic data of the population that may have an important impact on the results, as well as the lack of information regarding the duration of residence of the immigrant population. Additionally, it is important to acknowledge the potential impact of missing data during the collection process. Specifically, we did not include pregnant women whose care was provided at facilities outside of the Social Security system. However, these cases are estimated to account for only ~2.2% of the total pregnant population in the Lleida health region [37]. Given the comprehensive coverage of the Spanish National Health System, it is unlikely that this omission significantly influenced the outcomes of our study. Other limitations include the absence of data on the duration of residence in the host country and socioeconomic status variables, which are important factors that can influence the results. We aggregated races and ethnicities to ensure adequate group sizes for comparison with related studies; however, we acknowledge the inherent heterogeneity within these groups, which warrants further investigation.

Conclusions

In the analysis of the results of this study, heterogeneous differences were observed in the risk during pregnancy and in the newborn between the immigrant and native‐origin populations. The sub‐Saharan population has a higher risk of cesarean section, eclampsia, and an Apgar < 7 in the first minute. The Latin American population has a higher risk of cesarean section and macrosomia. The population from the Maghreb has a lower proportion of cesarean sections and a higher risk of macrosomia, while the population from Eastern Europe had a lower risk of cesarean section, low birth weight, and preeclampsia.

Our results corroborate the high risk of complications in some immigrant populations, highlighting the necessity for a preventive approach and early treatment to avoid complications in the mother and newborn. Regarding preeclampsia, data indicate that 60% of maternal deaths associated with preeclampsia are preventable, highlighting this as a crucial area for intervention [38]. This review underscores the essential need for researchers, healthcare professionals, and policymakers to prioritize the health of pregnant women with immigrant backgrounds and their varied health outcomes. Addressing these issues is crucial for enhancing the integration of immigrants into the host country's healthcare system and improving health outcomes. Additional population‐based research is necessary to clarify the factors contributing to these diverse outcomes. More studies are needed to analyze the causes of differences in maternal and child health in migrated women, as well as access to and utilization of the health system, to ensure equity in healthcare.
