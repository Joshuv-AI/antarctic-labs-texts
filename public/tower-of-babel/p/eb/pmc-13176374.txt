Technological Interventions for Dry Eye Disease: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Network Meta-analysis of 3-Month Outcomes
Authors: Dror Ben Ephraim Noyman, Clara C Chan, Joshua C Teichman, Itamar Arbel, Or Yosefi, James Myerscough, Michael Mimouni, Margarita Safir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn recent years, various technological therapeutic modalities have emerged aiming to target the underlying pathophysiology of dry eye disease (DED).MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, Scopus, and Embase was conducted through July 23, 2025, for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating technological interventions for DED, including intense pulsed light (IPL), thermal pulsation (LipiFlow), TearCare, iLux, low-level light therapy (LLLT), transcutaneous electrical stimulation (TES), and quantum molecular resonance (QMR) therapy. Eligible studies reported outcomes for tear break-up time (TBUT), Schirmer test, corneal staining, meibomian gland quality, or symptom severity at 2–4 months post-treatment. Data extraction followed PRISMA guidelines. A frequentist random-effects network meta-analysis was performed, with standardized mean differences calculated for comparative analyses and P-scores used to rank treatment efficacy.ResultsForty-seven RCTs involving 3581 patients from 16 countries were included. TearCare with meibomian gland expression (MGX) and IPL with a heated eye mask (HEM) ranked highest for TBUT improvement (P-scores 0.924 and 0.898, respectively). QMR therapy demonstrated the greatest efficacy for corneal staining (P-score 0.977). TearCare with MGX ranked first for meibomian gland secretion quality (P-score 0.999). IPL-based modalities showed consistent superiority in symptom relief, with IPL plus HEM achieving the highest improvement (P-score 0.910). No serious adverse events were reported.ConclusionsAt 2–4 months, TearCare with MGX and IPL-based protocols demonstrated superior efficacy across objective and subjective DED outcomes compared with other technologies and conservative treatments. Further standardized long-term comparative studies are warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40123-026-01360-x.Keywords: Dry eye disease, Meibomian gland dysfunction, Intense pulsed light

Key Summary Points

This systematic review and network meta-analysis evaluated the intermediate-term (2–4 months) efficacy and safety of technological interventions for managing dry eye disease (DED).TearCare combined with meibomian gland expression (MGX) and intense pulsed light (IPL)-based protocols demonstrated the most consistent intermediate-term efficacy across several DED signs, including tear break-up time and meibomian gland secretion quality, while quantum molecular resonance (QMR) therapy showed the greatest efficacy for corneal staining.IPL-based modalities ranked highest for patient-reported symptom improvement.These findings support the role of technological therapies in DED management but highlight the need for further standardized long-term comparative studies.

Introduction

Dry eye disease (DED) is a multifactorial disorder of the tear film characterized by ocular discomfort, visual disturbance, and potential damage to the ocular surface. It is among the most common reasons for ophthalmic consultation worldwide, significantly impairing quality of life and productivity [1]. Traditional therapies, including artificial tears, topical anti-inflammatory medications, and lifestyle modifications often provide only transient relief, as they do not address the underlying disease mechanisms [2].

Therefore, in recent years, numerous novel technological treatments for DED have emerged. These technologies directly target the pathophysiologic components of DED, particularly meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD), which accounts for most DED cases [3]. These technologies act through different mechanisms, including photobiomodulation, localized heat delivery, mechanical gland expression, or neuromodulation, to restore tear film stability and reduce ocular surface inflammation.

Photobiomodulation-based therapies include intense pulsed light (IPL), intense regulated pulsed light (IRPL) and low-level light therapy (LLLT). IPL delivers high-intensity light pulses (500–1200 nm) to the periocular skin, targeting blood vessels and meibomian glands. Its therapeutic effects in DED are attributed to inflammation reduction, improved meibomian gland function, and eradication of demodex mites [4–7]. IPL has demonstrated efficacy in DED secondary to MGD, rosacea, demodicosis, blepharitis, and aqueous tear deficiency (ATD) [4–8]. When combined with MGX, outcomes may improve by facilitating the secretion of newly formed meibum onto the ocular surface. Synergistic effects have also been reported with diquafosol sodium, a mucin secretagogue [9]. IRPL, a variation of the IPL, produces homogeneous, precisely sculpted light pulses within the same wavelength range (580–1200 nm), potentially enhancing safety and treatment consistency [8]. LLLT employs light-emitting diodes (590–633 nm) that do not induce a thermal effect but penetrate the skin to modulate cellular metabolism and exert anti-inflammatory effects [10]. Additionally, although not light-based, transcutaneous electrical stimulation has been proposed to improve DED by promoting tissue regeneration and enhancing local metabolism, while transnasal electrical stimulation is believed to activate the trigeminal–parasympathetic pathway through stimulation of the nasal mucosa, thereby facilitating increased tear secretion [11]. Finally, the quantum molecular resonance (QMR) technology represents a novel energy-based approach that delivers non-thermal, low-intensity oscillating electric fields to stimulate cellular regeneration and enhance meibomian gland metabolism, without significant tissue heating [12–14].

Heat-based technologies include systems such as the LipiFlow® Thermal Pulsation System (Johnson & Johnson, Jacksonville, FL, USA), which applies controlled heat and intermittent pressure to the eyelid margin, effectively improving meibomian gland function [15]. The Systane® iLux (Alcon, Fort Worth, TX, USA) is a handheld, battery-powered device that uses LEDs to heat the meibomian glands via a disposable tip [16]. The TearCare® System (Sight Sciences, Menlo Park, CA, USA) delivers heat to the tarsal plates for 15 min followed by manual MGX [17], while the MiBoFlo® Thermal System (MiBo Medical, Dallas, TX, USA) combines localized heat with simultaneous gland massage [18]. The last subgroup, mechanical DED treatments, encompasses manual MGX alone or meibomian gland probing (MGP) using a 1–2-mm intraductal stainless-steel probe. The latter is believed to stimulate activation of lid margin meibomian gland precursor cells, thereby promoting epithelial regeneration of the glands [19].

In our previous systematic review and network meta-analysis, we evaluated short-term (1-month) outcomes of technological treatments for DED, identifying IPL-based modalities as well as TearCare combined with MGX as the most effective approaches for improving DED signs. IPL-based treatments were found to be significantly superior in improving patient-reported symptom scores compared to other technologies and conservative treatments [20].

While short-term improvements are important for patient satisfaction and adherence, DED is a chronic condition, making assessment of treatment effect durability critical. Understanding the medium- and long-term outcomes of these interventions can help refine clinical decision-making, optimize patient care, and guide future therapeutic strategies. Despite the growing use of these technologies and evidence supporting their short-term benefits, data regarding their relative efficacy and safety remain limited. Therefore, this systematic review and network meta-analysis aims to evaluate the 2–4-month efficacy and safety of various technological interventions for DED, providing a comprehensive comparison of their medium-term outcomes.

Methods

This systematic review was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (registration ID 1113397).

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors. As such, institutional review board approval and informed consent were not required.

Search Methods

A systematic search was conducted using PubMed, Scopus, and Embase, reviewing available published studies up to July 23, 2025. As this was an extension of a previous study for a different timepoint [20], the same search command was employed (subject to variations between the databases’ search platform syntax rules) was: ("dry eye*" OR meibom* OR "posterior blepharitis*") AND ("Intense Pulsed Light" OR "IPL" OR "LipiFlow" OR "TearCare" OR "iLux" OR "MASK-E" OR "Low-level light therapy" OR "LLLT" OR Technology* OR "radio frequency" OR "radiofrequency" OR "OptiVize" OR "quantum molecular resonance" OR "QMR" OR "electrotherapy" OR "Rexon" OR "Rexon-Eye" OR "neurostimulator" OR "neurostimulation"), where “*” stands for wildcard use, and “OR” and “AND” are Boolean operators.

Eligibility Criteria

We filtered the results for RCTs that described any technological intervention (i.e., device-related) for dry eye in at least one of the arms of the study versus any other intervention. No restriction was set for population age and publication language. We included only studies reporting at least one outcome for the following effects: meibomian gland quality, tear secretion (e.g., tear break-up time [TBUT] and Schirmer), corneal surface health, and patient satisfaction at a 2-month interval between 2 and 4 months after beginning treatment. Abstracts or papers without a full description of their methods were excluded.

Study Selection

The review was conducted according to the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines [21]. Two reviewers (DBEN, MS) independently filtered out publications by title, then by abstract, and finally by full paper text after retrieval. Selected publications were approved by a senior investigator (MM) (Fig. 1). Search results which could not be retrieved directly (i.e., through the electronic database or the journal's portal) were attempted for retrieval using a professional institutional library service, then authors contacted directly via email, before deeming them unretrievable.

Interventions that contributed data to the network but were outside the study scope were excluded from forest plots and summary tables for clarity; however, they are presented in the network diagrams and study characteristics tables and were included in the analyses [20].

Data Collection and Risk of Bias Assessment

Data were extracted and tabulated in Excel (Microsoft Corporation, 2018) in a pairwise comparison format.

Risk of bias (RoB) was assessed according to the Cochrane’s guidelines by three independent authors (DBEN, IA, OY) with the justification and quotes from the publication documented in the spreadsheet mentioned above [22]. A fourth author (MM) made the final decision in case of disagreement between any two. Due to the nature of the industry and prior experience [21], the Other Risk category, referred to by the Cochrane Society as potential systematic errors or study flaws not captured by standard domains, was deemed based on whether there were any financial interests to the study (i.e., if the study was funded by a company with interests and devices in the DED treatment market or any of the authors was an employee or a consultant of such a company). RoB plots are displayed via the Cochrane’s RevMan program (Version 5.4.3, The Cochrane Collaboration, 2020) in Supplementary Material- Fig. 1, and the summary graph of them is displayed in Fig. 2. The following variables were independently collected: sample size, demographics, treatment protocol, follow-up duration, adjunctive treatment, and outcome measures.

Where effect sizes and standard deviations (SDs) reported were relevant for the same effect– the mean effect size was calculated, and the higher SD was used as a conservative approach.

The most validated measurement method for each outcome was addressed solely, preferring Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI) and Standard Patient Evaluation of Eye Dryness Questionnaire (SPEED) scores as symptom measurements, and Meibomian Gland Yielding Secretion Score (MGYSS) and Meibomian Gland Score (MGS) scores as meibomian gland quality scores, over other methods.

After testing data skewness [23], medians and interquartile ranges reported were used to calculate approximated means and SDs for each according to the Cochrane Society’s guidelines [24–26], assuming a normal distribution.

Missing data were estimated in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systemic Reviews of Interventions [27]. A graphical extraction tool (PlotDigitizer, 3.1.5, 2023) was used to extract data presented solely in graphical form [27].

For outcomes reported as events—the natural logarithm of the odds ratio and its standard error (SE) were used to re-express the effect size as a standardized mean difference (SMD) with its SE, respectively [28]. Thus, allowing dichotomous and continuous data to be combined [29].

Data Synthesis and Analysis

To support clinical transitivity, four experienced clinicians (MM, JM, CCC, JCT) were consulted regarding the appropriateness of pooling treatment subtypes and protocols (i.e., interventions delivered with varying parameters) until a unanimous decision was made. Accordingly, lubrication, warm compresses, eyelid massage, eyelid hygiene, and any combination of these were classified as conservative therapy. Meibomian gland expression was categorized as a distinct intervention, separate from massage. Technological therapies delivered alone were analyzed separately from the same technologies combined with other treatments (e.g., IPL alone was analyzed separately from IPL plus MGX). See Supplementary Material-Table 7 for a full description.

For each outcome, a frequentist network meta-analysis was performed using the "netmeta" R package. Given the anticipated variability in patient populations, interventions, and study designs, a random-effects model was selected based on the clinical, real-world nature of the studies. The transitivity and consistency assumptions were quantitatively evaluated using the net-splitting method (details provided in the Supplementary Material).

Treatment performance relative to placebo was assessed using the standardized mean difference (SMD) estimated from the network model, with significance tested via a z-test and displayed in forest plots, where values to the left of the central axis indicate a favorable outcome.

All treatments (including placebo) were ranked according to their estimated effects using the P-score—a frequentist measure representing the average certainty that a treatment is superior to competing treatments [30].

Pairwise differences between treatment effects were presented in league tables, displaying direct effects (above the diagonal, when available) and network-combined effects (below the diagonal) for all pairwise comparisons. For ease of interpretation, treatments were ordered along the diagonal from best (top left) to worst (bottom right) based solely on their P-scores. One must remember that these convey relative ranking probabilities rather than deterministic values, and do not represent absolute clinical value.

The distribution of studies contributing to each comparison was visualized with a network graph, in which treatments are shown as vertices and the thickness of edges is proportional to the number of studies providing that direct comparison.

Unless otherwise specified, data are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Because adverse events (AEs) were reported without sufficient standardization across studies, they were not included in the quantitative analysis.

Results

The literature search yielded 5397 publications published between 1984 and 2025 (Fig. 1). A total of 225 papers were removed due to duplication and another 4305 were removed by automated tools for being non-comparative in nature. After screening for relevancy by title and abstract, 158 studies were sought for the retrieval of full papers. We were unable to retrieve three studies despite the use of a professional academic retrieval system and contacting of the authors. Three studies were excluded from quantitative analysis as they did not add data to the network [31–33]. Eighteen meta-analyses were found relevant.

Ultimately, 47 eligible publications remained, describing the treatment of 3581 patients with MGD, with a mean age range of 24 to 75 years, who were treated by either a technological device or competing interventions (Table 2) [8, 9, 13, 14, 17, 18, 34–75]. The studies were conducted in 16 different countries: Australia, Austria, China, France, India, Iran, Israel, Italy, Japan, New Zealand, Singapore, South Korea, Spain, Thailand, United Kingdom, and the United States of America. Fourteen distinct devices used in various settings and adjunctive treatments were included in the analysis. Other interventions which did not fall under the technological treatment category but were within network boundaries (as they were compared to a technological device) were MGX, diquafosol sodium, fumigates traditional Chinese medicine and various conservative treatment regimens (Table 2).

Schirmer

Fourteen studies were included [8, 13, 35, 39, 43, 45, 47, 48, 51, 52, 67, 70, 72, 73] (I2 = 48.3%, 95% CI [0.0%; 81.1%]). IPL was the most studied technological intervention and was included in six direct comparisons followed by LipiFlow with four. However, when accounting for IPL subtypes and adjunctive treatment (e.g., IPL and MGX), IPL was included in nine comparisons (Supplementary Material—Fig. 2A).

Transcutaneous electrical stimulation was ranked first with a P score of 0.815, followed by IPL with LLLT (P score 0.679) and LLLT alone (P score 0.658). The rest of the interventions were graded lower than conservative treatment (P score 0.646). No treatment significantly outperformed conservative treatment (Supplementary Material—Fig. 2B and Supplementary Material—Table 2).

Conservative treatment and IPL were the main nodes of two sub-networks, connected only by two direct comparisons between them (Supplementary Material—Fig. 2A). Thus, node split method could not be used to reveal any significant inconsistencies between direct and indirect evidence due to the network structure.

Adverse Events

Reporting of adverse events (AEs) was scarce and of low quality; thus, the evidence was qualitatively analyzed. Twenty studies (42.5%) reported occurrence of AEs in some manner. However, only ten of these (21.3%) reported non-null events, and only two (4.3%) quantitively reported non-null events for both arms, with a depiction of the characteristics of the events themselves. Only two (4.3%) prospectively defined the outcome measurement used for assessing AEs and described their acquisition methods. We were therefore unable to ascertain the assumed safety level of each intervention (Supplementary Material—Table 6).

Discussion

This systematic review and network meta-analysis evaluated the 2 to 4-month efficacy and safety of technological interventions for DED, expanding upon our prior 1-month analysis [20]. Integrating 47 randomized controlled trials from 16 countries, it represents the most comprehensive synthesis to date of medium-term outcomes, enabling a comparative assessment of the relative efficacy and safety of a broad range of technological treatments for DED. Such evidence is crucial for both patients and doctors in order to support evidence-based decision making in managing this highly prevalent ocular disorder.

Consistent with our earlier findings, TearCare combined with MGX and IPL-based protocols were superior in most examined parameters. TearCare with MGX and IPL with a heated eye mask (HEM) achieved the highest efficacy for tear break-up time (TBUT), both outperforming conservative care and other technologies. Given that the TearCare specifically targets MGD through localized thermal therapy followed by MGX, its strong efficacy is expected in this case [17]. According to a previous report by Yin et al., IPL treatment induces improvements in meibomian gland microstructure, gland density and reduction of peri-glandular inflammation [5]. Thus, the combined anatomical, functional, and anti-inflammatory effects of IPL along with further meibum liquification by the HEM likely explain the high therapeutic performance in of this treatment method in our study. It is important to note that meibomian gland secretion quality assessment at 2–4 months followed the same pattern as TBUT, further supporting the reliability of our findings since a more liquid consistency of the meibum leads to less tear film evaporation, and therefore longer TBUT [2].

Notably, Schirmer test results have been less extensively evaluated in the context of technological treatment modalities for DED, probably because many of them target MGD specifically, while the Schirmer test evaluates the aqueous component of the tear film. Transcutaneous electrical stimulation was found to be the most effective, followed by photobiomodulation-based technologies (IPL, LLLT). While the mechanism of action for both technologies differs, both induce an anti-inflammatory effect, which may play a more significant role with the Schirmer test compared to other DED parameters [76]. Further studies are needed to further evaluate this effect. Interestingly, acupuncture, which demonstrated superiority in Schirmer test results at 1-month follow-up in our previous meta-analysis [20], did not maintain its effect at later follow-up in the current study, suggesting limited durability and raising cost-effectiveness considerations for clinical use.

Regarding corneal staining, QMR therapy demonstrated notably a strong effect, suggesting potential advantages in epithelial healing and inflammation modulation through bioelectrical stimulation mechanisms [10]. Although promising, these findings are derived from a limited number of RCTs, necessitating cautious interpretation and further independent replication. The combinations of TearCare with MGX and IPL with HEM were the second- and third-highest-ranking treatments to affect corneal staining, presumably due to the same mechanisms as for TBUT and meibum grading.

Finally, our analysis of patient-reported outcomes reaffirmed a well-recognized challenge in DED research: the frequent discordance between clinical signs and subjective symptoms [2]. The symptom outcomes followed a pattern similar to the 1-month results [20], with IPL-based therapies (particularly IPL + HEM and IPL combined with MGX or diquafosol sodium) ranking highest, despite being reported slightly inferior in other DED parameters. This finding may be attributed to the broader therapeutic spectrum of IPL, which simultaneously targets meibomian gland dysfunction, ocular surface inflammation, Demodex infestation, and lid margin neovascularization [4–7, 34, 77, 78]. In contrast, thermomechanical systems such as TearCare with MGX and LipiFlow, while effective in improving several objective parameters in this study, produced no significant improvement in patient-reported symptoms when compared to conservative management.

This study has several limitations. First, substantial heterogeneity in study design, treatment protocols, and outcome definitions, particularly for meibomian gland quality and secretion, complicates direct comparisons across trials. Although SMDs were used to mitigate variability, the lack of uniform outcome measures highlights the need for standardized methodologies in DED research. Second, the limited number of head-to-head randomized trials restricts the ability to determine the relative superiority of specific technologies. In this context. pooling of treatment subtypes (e.g., IPL devices with different pulse parameters or filter settings) was necessary to preserve analytical power but may have reduced precision. Differences in adjunctive measures, such as the use of MGX or heated masks, could also have influenced outcomes. While this is a standard trade-off in meta-analyses, and although transitivity was deemed by expert clinicians in the field, one of the foremost weaknesses of meta-analyses is the loss of resolution in return for broader comparisons. Moreover, as DED is a chronic and multifactorial disease in nature, further analyses investigating subgroups may be warranted for better generalizability and refining of the results. Nevertheless, as this meta-analysis dealt with RCTs, one must remember that the pooled effect is ideally in essence the clean effect of the interventions themselves. Third, potential bias related to industry sponsorship remains a concern, despite careful assessment and transparent reporting. In the current meta-analysis 30% of studies demonstrated potential conflicts of interest related to industry funding, which could significantly impact results. Fourth, while the 3-month follow-up provides valuable medium-term insights, it remains insufficient for a chronic disease such as DED. Longer follow-up is needed to evaluate the durability, retreatment needs and safety of the examined treatment modalities beyond the 2–4 months examined in this study, the knowledge of which could significantly impact the total cost-effectiveness of each treatment modality. Finally, adverse event reporting was inconsistent and largely qualitative, precluding meaningful safety comparisons. Future studies should include long-term, standardized comparative trials to assess the sustained efficacy and safety of these interventions. Elucidating the mechanisms underlying the synergistic effects of combination therapies, such as IPL with adjunctive modalities, may further refine and optimize treatment strategies.

Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE)

According to the GRADE framework, the certainty of evidence in this network meta-analysis is overall moderate-high across most outcomes. The evidence consists of 47 RCTs (3581 patients across 16 countries). However, certainty was downgraded for risk of bias, as the overall risk of bias was mixed with an overall medium risk, driven largely by the expected limitation of participant blinding inherent to procedural interventions, and with other bias related to industry interests identified in 30% of studies. Certainty was also downgraded for indirectness, given the substantial heterogeneity in study designs, treatment protocols, and outcome definitions (notably for meibomian gland secretion scores), and due to pooling of treatment subtypes (e.g., IPL devices with differing parameters) and adjunctive measures (e.g., MGX/heated masks) was sometimes necessary to preserve analytical power, potentially reducing clinical specificity and precision. While node-splitting did not demonstrate significant inconsistency between direct and indirect evidence where assessable, the limited number of head-to-head trials and, for some outcomes, network structure constraints contributed to imprecision. Certainty regarding safety and adverse events was very low, as AEs reporting was inconsistent and largely qualitative, unfortunately precluding meaningful quantitative comparisons.

Conclusions

To conclude, at 2–4 months post-treatment, both TearCare combined with MGX and IPL-based modalities demonstrated the most consistent and sustained efficacy across multiple parameters of dry eye disease. QMR therapy shows promising results in corneal surface restoration, meriting further exploration. These findings reinforce the expanding role of advanced technological interventions in the sustained management of DED and highlight the importance of continued long-term evaluation of their comparative performance and safety. The results of this analysis should be taken in context of its limitations. Further head-to-head RCTs, and subgroup analyses are warranted to refine the relationships and validate the results.
