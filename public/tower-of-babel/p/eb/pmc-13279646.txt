Comment on “Minimally invasive surgery for diverticulitis: a single-center retrospective study in the United States focusing on splenic flexure mobilization in elective sigmoid colectomy”
Authors: Ho Seung Kim
Published: 2026

The role of splenic flexure mobilization (SFM) during left-sided colorectal surgery has long remained controversial. While some surgeons advocate routine mobilization to ensure a tension-free anastomosis, others favor a selective approach because of concerns regarding operative complexity, prolonged operative time, and potential morbidity [1].

In the previous issue of the Journal of Minimally Invasive Surgery, Doyle et al. [2] present an important retrospective analysis evaluating SFM during elective minimally invasive sigmoidectomy for diverticulitis. Their study is notable because it specifically focuses on benign diverticular disease. The authors evaluated 117 patients undergoing elective minimally invasive sigmoidectomy performed by board-certified colorectal surgeons who selectively utilized SFM. The primary clinical value of this study lies in its identification of preoperative predictors associated with the need for SFM.

Among various factors, fistula formation was identified as an independent predictor of SFM. Chronic transmural inflammation associated with fistulizing diverticulitis often results in dense fibrosis, shortened mesentery, distorted tissue planes, and the need for more extensive distal dissection. Encountering a colovaginal or colovesical fistula inevitably necessitates extensive dissection, adhesiolysis, and subsequent mobilization to achieve adequate proximal conduit length for a tension-free anastomosis.

Interestingly, although SFM was associated with longer operative time, greater blood loss, and higher conversion rates, postoperative morbidity and mortality were not significantly increased. This finding is particularly reassuring because many surgeons hesitate to perform SFM owing to technical difficulty and concerns regarding splenic injury or additional morbidity. The present study suggests that, in experienced hands, selective SFM can be safely incorporated even in complicated diverticulitis cases.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design inherently introduces selection bias. Decisions regarding SFM were surgeon-dependent and not standardized. Additionally, the study reflects the experience of a high-volume tertiary referral center with experienced minimally invasive colorectal surgeons, potentially limiting generalizability. The relatively small number of patients undergoing SFM also limits statistical power. Therefore, the results should be interpreted cautiously.

Despite these limitations, the study by Doyle et al. [2] provides valuable contemporary data regarding SFM in minimally invasive surgery for diverticulitis. Rather than supporting either routine or complete avoidance of SFM, the findings reinforce a selective and patient-specific strategy. In particular, surgeons should anticipate the potential need for SFM in patients with fistulizing diverticulitis and severe inflammatory disease.

As minimally invasive colorectal surgery continues to evolve, future prospective multicenter studies will be necessary to further clarify the optimal indications and timing for SFM. Nonetheless, this study contributes meaningful evidence supporting the safe application of selective SFM in complicated diverticulitis within modern minimally invasive colorectal practice.
