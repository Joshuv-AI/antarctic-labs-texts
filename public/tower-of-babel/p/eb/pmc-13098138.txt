Prevalence of Strongyloides stercoralis in haemodialysis patients in Vienna
Authors: Haris Omic, Yassan Mahdi-Nour-Ali, Simon Hoffmann, Karim Abd El-Ghany, Isabella Egger, Manfred Eigner, Farsad Eskandary, Rhea Jabbour, Martin Jansen, Matthias Lorenz, Bernhard Ludvik, Marcus Säemann, Thomas Stulnig, Selma Tobudic, Johannes Werzowa, Julia Walochnik, Wolfgang Winnicki, Ludwig Wagner, Bruno Watschinger, Luzia Veletzky, Daniela Gerges

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and hypothesisStrongyloides stercoralis is a neglected parasitic infection endemic to tropical regions, with rising prevalence in Europe. In kidney transplant (KTX) recipients, high-dose corticosteroids can induce a hyperinfection syndrome, which carries high mortality but is preventable with anthelminthic therapy. Screening for S. stercoralis is currently not included in pre-KTX protocols in Europe. This study assessed the prevalence of S. stercoralis among all haemodialysis patients in Vienna.MethodsAll consenting haemodialysis patients in Vienna (n = 704) underwent enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay testing for S. stercoralis immunoglobulin G (IgG). A detailed questionnaire captured soil exposure, medical and transplant history, travel, symptoms and immunosuppressive therapy. Positive serologies were confirmed at the Austrian reference centre for parasitology and in case of positivity followed by stool examination for larvae.ResultsOf 704 patients, 50 (7.1%) were seropositive for S. stercoralis IgG, including 4 with prior KTX; stool examination showed larvae in one case. Male sex [odds ratio (OR) 6.18, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.44–26.54], absolute or relative eosinophilia (≥0.19 × 10⁹/L, ≥1.95%; area under the curve = 0.87 and 0.84), and birth in the Middle East and North Africa (OR 4.75, 95% CI 1.26–17.93) were independently associated with seropositivity, while birth in Western Europe was protective (OR 0.51, 95% CI 0.29–0.91; P = .023). Males were more likely to report occupational soil exposure (P = .002). Travel history, pet ownership, soil contact, gastrointestinal symptoms, pruritus, C-reactive protein and immunosuppression were not associated with seropositivity.ConclusionsRelevant S. stercoralis seroprevalence was found in a Central European haemodialysis population. Implementation of targeted screening for S. stercoralis should be considered in pre-KTX protocols in Europe.

INTRODUCTION

Strongyloidiasis is a parasitic infection caused by the soil-transmitted nematode Strongyloides stercoralis. It is estimated to affect around 614 million people worldwide [1, 2]. As infection is usually clinically inapparent in immunocompetent hosts, the true burden is likely to be underestimated. Under-ascertainment is further compounded by mass drug-administration programmes for onchocerciasis and lymphatic filariasis, which use ivermectin and incidentally clear S. stercoralis infections before they are ever diagnosed [3]. Unlike other helminthiases, strongyloidiasis has never been the focus of a dedicated control programme or World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines directed towards its control and elimination until recently. In 2024, the WHO published its first guideline on the public health control of strongyloidiasis [2].

Foremost endemic to tropical and subtropical regions, S. stercoralis is increasingly reported from temperate areas, including Central Europe [4]. Clinical concern centres on hyperinfection syndrome, which occurs almost exclusively in immunosuppressed patients—particularly those receiving glucocorticoids [5–7]. It is characterized by an accelerated autoinfective cycle, where larvae disseminate beyond the gut and lungs to organs such as the liver, brain and heart, thereby disrupting mucosal barriers leading to bacteraemia and fulminant sepsis [5, 8]. A short course of ivermectin is the therapy of choice, providing an efficient, curative treatment with minimal side effects [2].

Kidney transplant (KTX) recipients are especially vulnerable to hyperinfection syndrome as immunosuppression induction protocols universally include high-dose corticosteroids. Importantly, fatal hyperinfection has even been reported in recipients living in areas with low endemicity and without travel history to endemic regions or migration background [7, 9–11]. Although sporadic cases of hyperinfection syndrome in KTX recipients have been reported, the only available incidence data stem from a multicentre study in Brazil, which found 2.9 cases per 1000 patients. However, the global incidence in KTX recipients remains unknown [9, 12, 13]. Haemodialysis—which commonly precedes KTX for several years—therefore provides a critical window for screening. Nevertheless, systematic testing for S. stercoralis is not part of pre-transplant evaluations in European transplant centres [14, 15].

To inform evidence-based pre-transplant screening recommendations, we conducted a city-wide seroprevalence study of S. stercoralis among all adult haemodialysis patients in Vienna, Austria—a metropolitan centre of more than 2.0 million residents—to assess the burden of S. stercoralis infection in a major European urban population.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and eligibility

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Medical University of Vienna (EK 2228/2023), the Ethics Committee of the City of Vienna MA15 (EK 24-102-0824), and the Ethics Committee of Krankenhaus Barmherzige Brüder (15-05-2024), and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent before any study-related procedures were carried out. The manuscript is reported in line with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies.

We conducted Strong-HD-VIE as a multicentre, cross-sectional seroprevalence study encompassing the entire adult maintenance haemodialysis population in Vienna, Austria. All nine haemodialysis centres in the metropolitan area participated (Supplementary data, Table S1). Patients were included between 1 February 2024 and 30 November 2024.

Eligibility criteria included: age ≥18 years, chronic haemodialysis for ≥3 months and provision of written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were acute renal replacement therapy, peritoneal dialysis or prior curative treatment for strongyloidiasis. As all eligible patients were invited, no formal sample size calculation was required. The study represented a census of the target population (N = 1030).

Patient demographics and baseline laboratory data—including complete blood count, relative and absolute eosinophil count, levels of total immunoglobulin E (IgE), C-reactive protein (CRP) and other routine indices—were recorded. Participants also completed a structured questionnaire as described below.

These data were used to characterise risk factors for S. stercoralis seropositivity.

Sample collection, transportation and processing

For serological analysis, 8 mL of blood were drawn directly from the extracorporeal circuit during routine haemodialysis, thereby avoiding additional venepuncture. After collection and transfer to the laboratory, the 8 mL sample was centrifuged and frozen at –80°C until enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) testing was performed. Remaining samples were stored in a biobank.

ELISA testing for Strongyloides stercoralis IgG

As provided by the manufacturer’s protocol (Bordier Affinity Products SA, Crissier, Switzerland), human plasma was diluted at 1:200 in the provided dilution buffer. The resultant mixture was incubated at the pre-coated plate at 37°C for 30 min. Following a washing procedure using an ELISA washing machine, the conjugate was diluted at 1:50 and the provided dilution buffer was incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. After a second wash, the substrate was reacted for 30 min again at 37°C and read by an ELISA reader at 405 nm. All extinction values were calculated against the reference standard provided with each test package. A test was considered positive when the absorbance of a sample divided by the weak positive reference standard was ≥1. The test sensitivity was measured as 89.5% and specificity as 99.8% in previous studies [7, 16, 17]. All patients who tested positive for S. stercoralis IgG by initial ELISA underwent confirmatory testing with a second ELISA (Bordier Affinity Products SA, Crissier, Switzerland), performed at the Institute of Specific Prophylaxis and Tropical Medicine, Medical University of Vienna. Confirmatory testing was performed in this study setting because our laboratory is not Conformité Européenne (CE) accredited for S. stercoralis ELISA, and initiation of therapy required an officially validated positive laboratory result.

Stool sample collection and parasitological examination

All patients with a positive confirmatory ELISA for S. stercoralis IgG were asked to provide stool samples, which were collected on three consecutive days and analysed using a modified sodium acetate–acetic acid–formalin (SAF) method, whereby the formalin was substituted by ethanol. The SAF technique allows for rapid and direct identification of larvae, increasing sensitivity compared with direct smear examination.

Briefly, fresh stool specimens were suspended in SAF, incubated for 30 min, strained through gauze, and centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the sediment resuspended in sterile saline (0.9%) and examined microscopically at ×100 and ×400 magnification for the presence of rhabditiform larvae, filariform larvae or adult parasites.

For molecular testing, DNA was extracted from stool using the QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification targeted the small subunit ribosomal RNA (SSU rRNA) gene of S. stercoralis. The reaction mix contained 2 µL of DNA, 1 µL of each primer (10 µM) and 4 μL of the SolisFAST® Master Mix 5x (Solis Biodyne, Tartu, Estonia) in a final volume of 20 µL. Thermal cycling was performed on a Biometra TAdvanced thermocycler (Analytic Jena, Jena, Germany) under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 98°C for 2 min, followed by 45 cycles of denaturation at 98°C for 6 s, annealing at 57°C for 25 s and extension at 72°C for 25 s, with a final extension at 72°C for 1 min. Positive and negative controls were included in each run. Amplification products were visualized on a 2.5% agarose gel stained with Midori Green Advance (Biozym, Vienna, Austria) and examined under ultraviolet light.

Treatment protocol

In cases of confirmed seropositivity, if patients reported no prior treatment for strongyloidiasis, their travel history—particularly to sub-Saharan Africa—was reassessed. Patients with a relevant travel history underwent additional testing to exclude concomitant microfilaremic Loa loa infection. If no history of travel to Loa loa–endemic regions was identified, or if Loa loa microfilaremia was excluded, patients received ivermectin treatment. The regimen consisted of ivermectin 200 μg/kg body weight for two consecutive days in patients not listed for KTX, as recommended for non-immunocompromised patients, and 200 μg/kg on two consecutive days, repeated after 10 days (total of four doses), in patients actively listed for KTX or under active immunosuppressive therapy. Ivermectin therapy was initiated after samples for stool examination were provided.

Statistical analyses

We employed a multifaceted statistical approach to analyse the seroprevalence of S. stercoralis. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics. The seroprevalence rate was calculated, and associations with relevant variables, such as travel history, region of birth, immunosuppression and laboratory parameters were assessed.

Adherence to a Gaussian distribution was evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed variables were presented as means ± standard deviations, and skewed data as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) (25th–75th percentiles). Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages. Comparisons between groups were made using Pearson’s chi-square test for categorical variables and appropriate non-parametric tests for continuous variables.

Univariable logistic regression was used to assess associations between potential risk factors and S. stercoralis seropositivity. Variables with a P-value <.10 in univariable analysis were included in a multivariable logistic regression model to identify independent predictors. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. Model discrimination was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Optimal thresholds for continuous variables (e.g. eosinophil count) were determined using the Youden Index.

Subgroup analyses were performed to explore variations in seroprevalence across predefined clinical and demographic strata. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with P-values <.05 considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using SPSS® Statistics [IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0.2.0 (20) for macOS] and figures were generated using GraphPad Prism [Version 10.3.0 (217) for macOS].

Sex considerations

Both, male and female patients were included without restriction, and all analyses were performed irrespective of sex. Although sex-disaggregated analyses were not a predefined objective of this study, the balanced inclusion of women and men across all haemodialysis centres reflects the typical sex distribution of the local end-stage renal disease (ESRD) population [18].

RESULTS

Study population and seroprevalence

All 1030 adult ESRD patients undergoing maintenance haemodialysis in Vienna were screened for eligibility. Of these, 720 (69.9%) provided written informed consent and were included in the study. Serology testing was performed in all 720 participants, and 704 (97.8%) completed the questionnaire; the remaining 16 patients (2.2%) were all negative for S. stercoralis. Stool microscopy and/or S. stercoralis PCR were performed in 23 patients (Fig. 1).

Median age was 67.8 (IQR 54.9–77.1) years and 467 (64.9%) patients were male. One-hundred-twelve (15.7%) patients had a history of solid-organ transplantation, including 107 (14.9%) prior KTX recipients. Among the 704 patients, 110 patients were on the waiting list for KTX, of which 89 were actively listed as currently transplantable. Overall, 42.6% (300/704) had current or prior immunosuppression, predominantly systemic corticosteroids (36.3%), followed by calcineurin inhibitors [11.5% (tacrolimus 8.8%; ciclosporin 2.7%)] and mycophenolate (8.7%), largely reflected prior organ transplantation (15.7%) (Table 1). Diabetes mellitus was present in 29.9%, previous malignancy in 9.4% and autoimmune disorders in 15.7%, and 6.1% reported a prior parasitic infection (Table 1).

Among these 704 patients, 50 (7.1%) were seropositive for S. stercoralis. Of these, 47 patients received ivermectin. Dosing was adjusted according to KTX-listing status: 38 patients who were not actively listed received 200 μg/kg body weight for two consecutive days. Stool diagnostics and symptom correlation

Confirming active S. stercoralis infection via stool testing is challenging due to intermittent larval excretion and the parasite’s extraintestinal tissue residence [17]. ELISA thus remains the diagnostic modality recommended by WHO, especially in screening of risk groups [2]. Nonetheless, stool samples from seropositive patients were collected on three consecutive days for microscopy and PCR analysis. Of the 50 seropositive patients, 23 (46%) provided stool samples (Fig. 1). Of those, one tested positive by PCR and showed S. stercoralis rhabditiform larvae in direct microscopy, all others were negative.

Gastrointestinal symptoms such as diarrhoea, bloating, constipation and abdominal pain were reported by 190 (27.3%) patients, while 154 (22.1%) reported pruritus or exanthema (Table 1). However, there was no significant difference in symptom prevalence between seropositive and seronegative patients for either gastrointestinal symptoms (P = .15), pruritus or exanthema (P = .95) (Table 2).

Environmental and clinical risk factors

Given the soil-borne transmission of S. stercoralis, we evaluated both general and occupational soil contact. Soil exposure—either general (reported by 44.0% of patients) or occupational (5.8%)—did not correlate significantly with seropositivity (P = .17 and P = .62, respectively) (Table 2; Fig. 2).

Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses of risk factors associated with S. stercoralis. (A) Univariable logistic regression showing OR and 95% CI for demographic, clinical and exposure-related variables. (B) Multivariable logistic regression including variables with P < .10 in univariable analysis. Statistically significant associations in the multivariable model were observed for male sex (OR 6.18, 95% CI 1.44–26.54), absolute eosinophil count (per 0.1 × 10⁹/L increase; OR 3.94, 95% CI 2.33–6.67) and birth in the MENA region (OR 4.75, 95% CI 1.26–17.93). Birth in Western Europe was inversely associated with seropositivity (OR 0.51, 95% CI 0.29–0.91; P = 0.023).

In addition, pet ownership was documented as dogs and cats can sporadically harbour infections especially in endemic areas [19]. Pet ownership, reported by 392 (57.8%) patients, showed no significant association with seropositivity (P = .19). Previous parasite exposure or parasitic infection were also not significantly associated with seropositivity (P = .45 and P = .54, respectively) (Table 2; Fig. 2).

Geographic and migration-related risk factors

Given the tropical/subtropical endemicity of S. stercoralis, we examined patients’ regions of birth and travel history. Of 704 questionnaire respondents, 427 (60.6%) were born in Austria and 18 (2.6%) elsewhere in Western Europe, for a total of 445 (63.2%) Western European–born participants. Eastern Europe comprised 147 (20.9%) of the study population, MENA 46 (6.5%), South/Southeast/East Asia 35 (5.0%), sub-Saharan Africa 20 (2.8%), Central Asia 7 (1.0%), South America 3 (0.4%) and North America 1 (0.1%) (Table 1). Birth in the MENA region was significantly associated with seropositivity (P = .03), whereas no other geographical region showed a significant effect (Table 2; Fig. 2). Logistic regression analysis did not demonstrate an association between the time spent in MENA (per year) and ELISA seropositivity (OR 1.003, 95% CI 0.95–1.05; P = .91). Median duration of residence in MENA was similar between ELISA-negative (24 years, IQR 18.5–39.5) and ELISA-positive individuals (26 years, IQR 20–35), with no significant difference observed (P = .90).

Travel to tropical areas was reported by 498 (72.6%) patients and did not differ between seropositive and seronegative groups (74.5% vs 72.5%; P = .77). Likewise, travel to the MENA region was comparable in seropositive and seronegative individuals (30.0% vs 25.4%; P = .48) (Table 2).

Sex-based differences in risk factors

More than half of included patients (64.9%) were male. Male sex correlated highly significantly with the presence of IgG against S. stercoralis (P < .001) (Table 2). Males were significantly more likely than females to report occupational exposure to soil (7.8% vs 2.1%, P = .002). Females had a higher proportion of Eastern European origin compared with males (25.5% vs 18.4%, P = .03), while a greater percentage of males originated from MENA countries (8.3% vs 3.2% P = .009). Men were more than twice as likely as women to report a MENA background (P = .009). No significant sex differences were observed for overall soil contact (P = .34), Central Asian origin (P = .22), travel history or previous parasitic infections (all P > .05). Similarly, there were no differences by sex in origins from Austria, Western Europe, sub‐Saharan Africa, Southeast Asia, North America, South America or Oceania (all P > .05).

Laboratory predictors and eosinophilia

Among the 704 participants median CRP was 2.07 mg/dL (IQR 1.02–4.65). Median absolute eosinophil count was 0.10 × 109/L (IQR 0.02–0.2), median relative eosinophil levels 1.5% (IQR 0.4–2.9) and total IgE 41.8 U/mL (IQR 12.12–101.20) (Table 1). While CRP levels did not differ by serostatus, IgE and both absolute and relative eosinophil counts were significantly higher in seropositive patients (all P < .001). ROC analysis identified an absolute eosinophil threshold of ≥0.19 × 109/L [area under the curve (AUC) 0.87 (95% CI 0.81–0.92)] and a relative threshold of ≥1.95% [AUC 0.84 (0.79–0.90)] as optimal predictors for S. stercoralis seropositivity (Table 2; Fig. 3).

Prediction of S. stercoralis using absolute and relative eosinophil counts. (A) Absolute eosinophil count (cut-off ≥0.19 × 10⁹/L) yielded an area under the curve at ROC analysis of 0.87 (95% CI 0.81–0.92). (B) Relative eosinophil percentage (cut-off ≥1.95%) yielded an AUC of 0.84 (95% CI 0.79–0.90). Optimal thresholds were determined by Youden’s index.

Predictors of Strongyloides stercoralis IgG positivity

In univariable logistic regression analyses, absolute eosinophil count, geographic origin and sex were all significantly associated with ELISA positivity. Each 0.1 × 10⁹/L increment in absolute eosinophil count was associated with a 3.83-fold increase in the odds of seropositivity (OR 3.83, 95% CI 2.58–5.68; P < .001), while each 1% increase in relative eosinophil percentage conferred 86% higher odds (OR 1.86, 95% CI 1.53–2.27; P < .001). Birth in Western Europe was protective (OR 0.51, 95% CI 0.29–0.91; P = .023), whereas birth in the MENA region more than doubled the odds of seropositivity (OR 2.57, 95% CI 1.08–6.08; P = .032). Male sex was a significant risk factor (OR 3.56, 95% CI 1.57–8.04; P = .02). Although each 10 U/mL increase in IgE was associated with 4.5% higher odds of seropositivity (OR 1.045, 95% CI 0.999–1.094), this association was not statistically significant (P = .06). No significant associations were observed for other regions (Central Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, South/Southeast Asia, North or South America), nor for travel history, pet ownership or other clinical exposures.

In multivariable logistic regression, adjusting for confounding factors geographic origin (MENA vs Western Europe), absolute eosinophil count and sex, all three remained independently associated with IgG seropositivity. Birth in the MENA region was associated with a 4.75-fold increase in odds (OR 4.75, 95% CI 1.26–17.93; P = .021) compared with Western Europe. Every 0.1 × 10⁹/L increase in absolute eosinophil count conferred 3.94-fold higher odds (OR 3.94, 95% CI 2.33–6.67; P < .001), and male sex was associated with 6.18-fold greater odds of seropositivity (OR 6.18, 95% CI 1.44–26.54; P = .014) (Fig. 2).

DISCUSSION

We had hypothesized that S. stercoralis infection would be present at a clinically significant rate among haemodialysis patients in Vienna, and that traditional risk factors such as travel history or soil exposure would predict seropositivity. This city-wide seroprevalence study, encompassing nearly the entire haemodialysis population of Vienna—a metropolitan area of over 2 million residents—identified S. stercoralis IgG antibodies in 7.1% of patients. This represents the first comprehensive, population-level assessment of S. stercoralis burden in a European haemodialysis cohort, underscoring the continued relevance of this neglected helminth infection in non-endemic, high-income settings. Seropositivity was independently associated with male sex, birth in the MENA region and elevated eosinophil counts—both absolute and relative—while no associations were observed with travel history, soil exposure, prior parasitic infections or pet ownership. These findings highlight the importance of considering targeted S. stercoralis screening in haemodialysis patients, particularly in the context of pre-transplant evaluation.

A previous seroprevalence study of S. stercoralis among KTX recipients in Vienna, initiated after the identification of a case of hyperinfection syndrome, reported a prevalence of 3%—notably lower than the 7.1% observed in our haemodialysis cohort [7]. This discrepancy may reflect evolving demographic characteristics of the ESRD population in Vienna, potentially influenced by increased migration from endemic regions, but it may also relate to Austrian transplant listing criteria, which require a documented residence permit of more than 1 year [20]. Language barriers and limited health system literacy may further impede access to pre-transplant assessment, compounding these effects. Data on S. stercoralis prevalence among KTX recipients in Europe remain limited. In the UK, no population-level or transplant clinic data are available [14]. In Brazil, a retrospective multicentre case–control study identified 46 cases of severe S. stercoralis infection among 15 860 transplant recipients [9]. Our findings align with a recent single-centre study from Houston, Texas, which reported a seroprevalence of 9.9% among KTX candidates prior to transplantation [21].

With over 40% of its population born abroad and representation from more than 180 nationalities, Vienna exemplifies demographic diversity in Central Europe, providing an optimal epidemiological setting to investigate the prevalence of S. stercoralis among a highly heterogeneous haemodialysis population [22].

Importantly, in our cohort S. stercoralis seropositivity was not associated with gastrointestinal symptoms or pruritus/exanthema, despite previous reports suggesting that S. stercoralis infection may contribute to gut dysbiosis and even accelerate chronic kidney disease progression [23]. This aligns with existing literature showing that strongyloidiasis in immunocompetent individuals is often asymptomatic or present with only mild, nonspecific gastrointestinal complaints [24]. Furthermore, ESRD itself predisposes patients to gastrointestinal symptoms—including constipation, diarrhoea, abdominal pain and reflux—due to factors such as uraemia, immobility, fluid restriction and polypharmacy [25]. Similarly, pruritus is highly prevalent in haemodialysis patients, largely attributable to uraemic pruritus and mineral bone disorder, particularly hyperphosphatemia [26, 27]. These overlapping symptom profiles substantially limit the clinical utility of gastrointestinal or dermatological symptoms in prompting diagnostic testing for strongyloidiasis in this population.

Stool-based detection did not correlate with seropositivity in our study, reflecting the well-known diagnostic limitations of direct methods in strongyloidiasis [17]. The parasite’s intermittent larval shedding and frequent extraintestinal residence reduce the sensitivity of stool microscopy and PCR, even when performed on multiple samples. Although the Baermann technique and PCR remain the preferred stool-based methods, their diagnostic sensitivity remains suboptimal despite high specificity [17]. A recent cross-sectional study by Tamarozzi et al. in Ecuador evaluated multiple diagnostic strategies and identified the Bordier ELISA—used in our study—as the most suitable serological tool, especially when combined with stool PCR on consecutive days [17]. Although the Bordier ELISA applied in our study has shown high specificity (≈99.8%) and good but imperfect sensitivity (≈89.5%) in previous evaluations, some infections may have remained undetected; hence, our reported prevalence likely underestimates the true burden of S. stercoralis in this population [7, 16, 17]. Current WHO guidelines also recommend combining serology with stool testing, but emphasize that a negative stool result does not rule out infection [2].

These same guidelines recommend annual mass drug-administration with single-dose ivermectin in settings where S. stercoralis prevalence exceeds 5%—a threshold surpassed in our study cohort (7.1%) [2]. While the risk of re-exposure may be limited in haemodialysis patients, the clinical risk remains significant due to the potential for severe, life-threatening hyperinfection in the setting of immunosuppression and KTX. This supports a proactive approach to screening and treatment in this vulnerable population. Notably, no population-based Austrian/Vienna seroprevalence estimates are available; the WHO ≥5% threshold is referenced here as a public-health benchmark rather than a directive for haemodialysis cohorts.

Given that strongyloidiasis is a soil-transmitted helminth infection, we hypothesized that contact with soil might be associated with seropositivity; however, no such association was observed. Similarly, pet ownership, prior parasitic infections, and known contact with parasites were not linked to S. stercoralis seropositivity. These findings suggest that environmental exposures within Austria are unlikely to be a major source of infection and further support the role of early-life exposure in endemic regions as the primary driver of infection risk in this population.

Birth in the MENA region and male sex were independently associated with S. stercoralis seropositivity, whereas travel to tropical or MENA countries was not. These findings support the hypothesis that early-life, long-term exposure in endemic regions represents a greater risk factor than short-term travel and are consistent with epidemiological evidence showing high endemicity in parts of the MENA region, particularly Egypt and Palestine [28, 29]. The association with male sex appears to reflect higher occupational exposure to soil among men, a pattern corroborated by our data.

Eosinophil levels, both absolute (≥0.19 × 10⁹/L) and relative (≥1.95%), emerged as strong predictors of seropositivity, despite the known occurrence of eosinophilia in haemodialysis patients due to hypersensitivity to dialysis circuit components [30]. Notably, however, applying these cut-offs would have missed approximately 15% of S. stercoralis seropositive patients, underscoring the need for screening even in patients without eosinophilia or with counts below these thresholds. Importantly, the identified cut-off values for both absolute and relative eosinophils lie below the conventional upper laboratory reference limits (0.4 × 10⁹/L and 4.0%, respectively), indicating that traditional definitions of eosinophilia do not predict S. stercoralis seropositivity, while our lower cut-offs do.

While US transplant guidelines recommend screening for S. stercoralis in transplant candidates from or with prolonged travel history to endemic areas, no such recommendation exists in current European protocols [15, 31]. Pre-transplant infectious disease screening in Europe typically focuses on hepatitis B (HBV), hepatitis C (HCV), HIV, tuberculosis, the cytomegalovirus and other Herpesviridae. Notably, the seroprevalence of HBV (3.2%) and HCV (0.7%) in Europe, as reported by the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, is substantially lower than the 7.1% S. stercoralis prevalence identified in our haemodialysis cohort [32, 33]. As other infections on the pre-transplant panel, S. stercoralis may be diagnosed with a single, inexpensive serological test, and effective, well-tolerated treatment with ivermectin is readily available. Confirmatory testing, as conducted in this study, is not routinely required in clinical practice. As outlined above, we applied two ivermectin regimens according to immunosuppression status or KTX listing, reflecting expert consensus. We captured current and prior immunosuppression to contextualize clinical risk, given that chronic S. stercoralis infection may be unmasked by future induction therapy. Follow-up serology was not performed, as S. stercoralis IgG typically persists after successful therapy. However, in the single stool-positive case, post-treatment stool microscopy and PCR confirmed parasitological clearance. Incorporating S. stercoralis screening into pre-transplant workups would therefore address a significant—and currently neglected—risk in this vulnerable population, enabling targeted intervention and reducing the threat of hyperinfection syndrome following immunosuppression. Screening for S. stercoralis can be readily integrated into routine serological testing during the pre-transplant workup, and repeat testing should be considered only after travel to tropical, subtropical or MENA regions. Based on current clinical observations, we do not recommend testing KTX donors, as these helminths do not reside in renal tissue and hematogenous transmission has not been demonstrated. Nevertheless, this question warrants further investigation. Pre-emptive anthelminthic treatment cannot currently be recommended for all listed pre-KTX patients, as data on population-wide seroprevalence and the incidence of hyperinfection syndrome remain scarce. However, in cases of uncertainty—particularly in patients with a migration background or travel history to endemic areas—an empiric course of ivermectin or thiabendazole should be considered prior to transplantation [34].

This study has several strengths, including near-complete coverage of the entire haemodialysis population in Vienna, the use of standardized serological testing, and detailed clinical and demographic profiling. Limitations include the reliance on serology alone, as stool-based diagnostics are known to have limited sensitivity and pose difficulties in sample collection as shown by the limited provision of 50% in our seropositive cohort. While the cross-sectional design enabled a robust assessment of seroprevalence and associated factors, it limits conclusions regarding temporal relationships or causality. In addition, serological testing cannot definitively confirm active strongyloidiasis, although a negative result effectively rules out infection. Although approximately 30% of eligible patients did not participate, primarily due to unwillingness to complete the questionnaire, demographic information available from the dialysis centres indicated no major differences in age or sex distribution between participants and non-participants, suggesting that selection bias is unlikely to have substantially influenced the prevalence estimate. In addition, peritoneal dialysis patients were not included in our study because their outpatient visits are infrequent, making systematic recruitment challenging.

In summary, our study reveals that S. stercoralis infection is more prevalent among haemodialysis patients than many other infections routinely screened for before KTX. Key predictors—male sex, MENA origin and elevated eosinophil counts—can guide targeted approaches, but most cases would be missed by symptom or exposure-based screening alone. Unlike other forms of organ failure, the chronicity of haemodialysis provides a unique window for systematic infectious disease work-up. Given the high seroprevalence and low cost of testing, we advocate for incorporating routine S. stercoralis screening into pre-transplant protocols across Europe to prevent hyperinfection syndrome particularly in asymptomatic S. stercoralis carriers, while also improving epidemiological knowledge and informing future public health policies.
