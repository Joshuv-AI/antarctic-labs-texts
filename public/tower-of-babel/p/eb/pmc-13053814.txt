Comment on Nutritional Counseling for Patients With Gastric Cancer After Subtotal Gastrectomy: A Randomized Clinical Trial
Authors: Pınar Peker
Published: 2026

I read with interest the article by Lee et al. [1], which described a randomized double-blind trial of intensive nutritional counseling (IC) and simplified counseling (SC) in patients who underwent subtotal gastrectomy for early gastric cancer. The authors concluded that the use of IC did not result in a clinically significant difference in postoperative weight loss compared with SC, indicating that standard counseling is sufficient for most patients [1].

This clinical trial was well designed. Its internal validity was optimized using a prospective randomized protocol, high follow-up rate, and homogeneous inclusion of early stage patients. The authors also indicate that application of both anthropometric and biochemical outcomes to provide a comprehensive perspective on postoperative recovery is warranted [2,3].

Certain aspects of this report deserve further debate. Weight loss after gastrectomy is multifactorial, with factors including decreased gastric capacity, hormonal changes, and malabsorption of nutrients [4]. International guidelines including the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) 2021 and European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO)-supportive nutrition recommendations highlight the crucial role of early personalized nutritional assessment and multimodal management in this context [5,6]. Behavioral counseling may thus be insufficient to achieve these complex metabolic changes. Combining nutritional education with metabolic or pharmacological interventions (i.e., ghrelin analogs and anamorelin) may yield better results [7,8].

It is also important to note that patients who received adjuvant chemotherapy were excluded, despite having even higher nutritional risk. This could be important because data from large adjuvant trials (e.g., ACTS-GC and CLASSIC) indicate that treatment-related malnutrition is common [9,10]. Assessing the effects of IC in this high-risk stratum would enhance the external validity of the results.

Oddly, despite the IC group consuming more calories and meals, weight gain and quality-of-life metrics did not differ. Previous observations have implied that nutrient intake, muscle maintenance, and metabolic function are more important indicators of recovery than total energy intake [11]. Future trials should assess body composition parameters including skeletal muscle index and phase angle. Psychological and behavioral factors contribute to adherence to dietary therapy [12].

The authors presented high-quality evidence that standard counseling may be adequate for low-risk postoperative patients, consistent with the ESPEN and ESMO recommendations for an integrated approach, including dietary counseling, physical activity, and pharmacological support. Personalized multimodal approaches involving pharmacological and psychological treatments and physical (exercise-based) therapy to optimize postoperative nutrition and recovery should be further investigated in future studies.

I am grateful for the authors’ valuable efforts to enhance nutritional therapy for patients with gastric cancer.
