Cell-free DNA dynamics and progression-free survival in metastatic gastroesophageal adenocarcinoma: insights from the REGIRI-PRODIGE 58 ancillary study
Authors: Alix Mérigout, Emmanuelle Samalin, Anthony Turpin, Hélène Senellart, Ludovic Evesque, Olivier Bouché, Faiza Khemissa, Christelle De La Fouchardière, Anthony Lopez, Slimane Dermeche, Damien Botsen, David Tougeron, Aziz Zaanan, Meher Ben Abdelghani, Emmanuel Guardiola, Olivier Dubreuil, Valérie Ly Le Brun, Audrey Hennequin, Nicolas De Sousa Carvalho, Alexandre Harlé
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cell-free DNA (cfDNA) analysis is an emerging tool for diagnosis, prognosis, and monitoring in cancer. This ancillary study of the REGIRI-PRODIGE 58 trial (NCT03722108) evaluated cfDNA dynamics in patients with metastatic gastroesophageal adenocarcinoma (mGA) treated with regorafenib plus irinotecan. Plasma samples from 34 patients in the experimental arm were analyzed (239 samples at baseline, post-treatment initiation [days 2, 8, 15 of Cycle 1; days 1, 8, 15 of Cycle 2]). cfDNA was extracted, quantified by fluorimetry, and characterized by fragment analysis. Baseline concentrations were dichotomized into “low” and “high” subgroups using a 2.07 ng/µL threshold. Changes from baseline to the final timepoint defined four dynamic subgroups (low–low, low–high, high–low, high–high). At baseline, 27 patients (79.4%) had low cfDNA concentrations and 7 (20.5%) high ctDNA. Progression-free survival (PFS) was longer in the low group (median 4.51 months) versus the high group (median 1.60 months; p = 0.0084). Longitudinally, the low-low subgroup had longer PFS than the high–high subgroup (3.61 vs 1.45 months; p = 0.0004). Baseline and dynamic cfDNA concentrations were significantly associated with PFS in patients with mGA treated with regorafenib plus irinotecan, supporting cfDNA as a potential prognostic biomarker for validation in larger cohorts.

Introduction

Gastric and esophageal cancers account for 7.7% (768,793) and 5.5% (544,076) of global cancer deaths in 2020, respectively1. Metastatic gastroesophageal adenocarcinoma (mGA) has a poor prognosis, primarily due to late diagnosis, with 5-year net survival rates of 16% in men and 20% in women in France (2010–2015)2. By 2040, the International Agency for Research on Cancer projects 2.64 million deaths from gastric cancer and 1.82 million from esophageal cancer1.

First described in 19483, cell-free DNA (cfDNA) is a non-invasive biomarker for cancer screening, early diagnosis, prognosis, therapeutic monitoring, and detection of resistance or recurrence4. In gastroesophageal cancer, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) is detectable in 57% of localized and 100% of metastatic cases5. Postoperative ctDNA detection has shown prognostic value and predicts recurrence in gastroesophageal cancer6.

The PRODIGE 58-UGCI 35-REGIRI trial (NCT03722108)7, a multicentre, open-label, randomized phase II study, evaluated regorafenib plus irinotecan (REGIRI) versus irinotecan alone as second-line treatment in patients with mGA previously treated with fluoropyrimidine and/or platinum-based chemotherapy. In total, 89 patients were randomized (REGIRI arm, n = 44; IRI arm, n = 45). Patients received irinotecan (180 mg/m², 90-min infusion) on days 1 and 15 of each 4-week cycle, with regorafenib (160 mg/day, days 2–8 and 16–22) added in the REGIRI arm. The trial was stopped early due to an unfavorable benefit/risk ratio, with 52.3% of REGIRI patients experiencing grade ≥3 treatment-related adverse events compared to 23.3% in the IRI arm. In the primary analysis, median progression-free survival (PFS) was 2.8 months (95% CI, 1.8–4.3) in the REGIRI arm and 3.0 months (95% CI, 2.0–4.6) in the IRI arm (p = 0.76; HR = 0.93; 95% CI, 0.6–1.45). Median overall survival (OS) was 6.5 months (95% CI, 4.8–9.0) in the REGIRI arm and 6.8 months (95% CI, 5.2–9.5) in the IRI arm (p = 0.76; HR = 1.11; 95% CI, 0.7–1.74)8.

This ancillary study investigates the prognostic value of plasma cfDNA concentrations in patients from the REGIRI arm, focusing on their association with PFS and OS.

Results

Patients

Of the 36 patients in the REGIRI arm with blood samples collected, 34 were included in the cfDNA analysis (2 withdrew consent). A total of 239 plasma samples from these 34 patients were analyzed.

cfDNA dynamics over time

Longitudinal cfDNA concentrations remained stable in most patients, except for two with extreme variations (Fig. S1). One patient with a marked increase in cfDNA (3.04–15.60 ng/µL) had a PFS and OS of 1.08 months. Another with a steep decline (13.32–0.58 ng/µL) had a PFS of 3.15 months and an OS of 6.93 months.

Patients were categorized into four subgroups based on cfDNA dynamics (baseline to final timepoint): low-low (n = 25), low–high (n = 2), high–low (n = 2), and high–high (n = 5). Median PFS was significantly longer in the low-low (non-shedders) group (3.61 months) compared to the combined low–high, high–low, and high–high (shedders) group (n = 9; 1.45 months; p = 0.0033; HR = 0.34; 95% CI, 0.11–1.06; Fig. 2C). A non-significant numerical difference in median OS was observed between the low-low (8.52 months) and shedders (6.18 months) groups (p = 0.3250; HR = 0.68; 95% CI, 0.28–1.65; Fig. 2D).

Exploratory analysis of early cfDNA dynamics (Cycle 1 Day 1 to Cycle 1 Day 15) did not reveal a statistically significant association with PFS (log-rank p = 0.61) nor OS (log-rank p = 0.5), consistent with the overall stability of cfDNA levels observed in most patients during the first two cycles (Figs. S1 and S2).

Subgroup analysis

No significant differences in baseline cfDNA concentrations were observed according to HER2 status (p = 0.381), MSI status (p = 0.967), histological type (p = 0.967), synchronous metastases (p = 0.486), tumor size (p = 0.066), treatment response (p = 0.801), or metastatic sites (p > 0.999). Detailed analyses are provided in Fig. S3.

Discussion

This multicentre ancillary study, conducted across 17 French centers, investigated the prognostic value of plasma cfDNA concentrations in patients with histologically confirmed mGA previously treated with fluoropyrimidine and/or platinum-based chemotherapy. Our findings demonstrate a significant association between baseline cfDNA levels and PFS, with patients in the low cfDNA group (<2.07 ng/µL) exhibiting a median PFS of 4.51 months compared to 1.60 months in the high cfDNA group (≥2.07 ng/µL; p = 0.0084). Additionally, longitudinal cfDNA dynamics (low-low vs. shedders) were significantly associated with PFS (p = 0.0033), highlighting cfDNA as a potential non-invasive biomarker for disease progression in mGA. Comparison between treatment arms would have been valuable to distinguish treatment-specific from disease-progression effects; however, no blood collection was performed in the control arm of the REGIRI study.

The observed correlation between cfDNA levels and PFS aligns with prior studies on circulating biomarkers in gastroesophageal cancers. For instance, Bettegowda et al. reported that circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) is detectable in 100% of mGA cases, supporting the feasibility of cfDNA analysis in advanced disease5. Similarly, Huffman et al. demonstrated that postoperative ctDNA detection predicts recurrence in gastric and esophageal cancers, suggesting a prognostic role for circulating biomarkers6. Our study extends these findings by focusing on cfDNA quantification without the need for targeted mutation analysis, offering a rapid and cost-effective approach for prognostic stratification. In our cohort, the mean baseline cfDNA concentration was 1.61 ng/µL (range: 0.16; 13.32 ng/µL), consistent with levels reported in cancer patients (1; 10 ng/mL) compared to healthy individuals9. Notably, two patients with extreme cfDNA variations (one with a steep increase to 15.60 ng/µL, PFS = 1.08 months; one with a decline to 0.58 ng/µL, PFS = 3.15 months) underscore the potential of longitudinal cfDNA monitoring to reflect tumor dynamics. Our findings align with broader literature on total cfDNA as a prognostic biomarker across solid tumors. Fluorometric or quantitative cfDNA assessment has been associated with poorer survival in non-small-cell lung cancer, melanoma, pancreatic cancer, and breast cancer, supporting its utility even without mutation-specific analysis10–12. These observations reinforce the clinical relevance of total cfDNA quantification in advanced disease settings, where tumor burden often dominates the cfDNA pool.

A key limitation of this study is the measurement of total circulating cfDNA rather than tumor-specific ctDNA, due to the absence of sequencing data in this ancillary study. While total cfDNA is a composite biomarker influenced by non-oncologic processes (inflammation, necrosis, cardiovascular stress), multiple lines of evidence indicate that, in patients with advanced metastatic disease, the tumor compartment is the dominant source of circulating DNA9,13,14. The observed prognostic association in our cohort is therefore consistent with this biological context, although sequencing-based confirmation of the ctDNA fraction would strengthen causal inference and further refine the clinical interpretation of cfDNA dynamics. Without sequencing, we cannot ascertain the tumor-specific origin of the measured cfDNA, limiting our ability to link concentrations directly to tumor burden.

Notably, the single patient with a prolonged PFS (>30 months) exhibited an initial rise in cfDNA followed by stabilization at a level close to the study threshold (2.17 ng/µL at C2D15). Given the analytical performance of the assay, this profile is best interpreted as molecular stability rather than progression, which may explain the favorable long-term outcome despite the early increase. Future studies should incorporate targeted sequencing or whole-genome approaches to validate ctDNA presence and characterize its mutational landscape, as demonstrated in other cancers15,16. Such analyses could refine the clinical utility of cfDNA in mGA, potentially identifying actionable mutations for targeted therapies.

Another limitation is the use of lithium heparin tubes for plasma collection, which are suboptimal for cfDNA analysis due to the risk of hemolysis and genomic DNA contamination17. To mitigate this, we restricted our analysis to fragments of 160 bp and multiples up to 480 bp, minimizing germline DNA interference. However, specialized blood collection tubes, such as Cell-Free DNA BCT® (Streck, La Vista, NE, USA) or CE-IVD Cell-Free DNA Collection Tubes (Roche, Basel, Switzerland), are recommended to enhance sample integrity18. Future studies should adopt these tubes to improve the reliability of cfDNA measurements.

Another key limitation of our study is the relatively small sample size (n = 34), which was constrained by the early termination of the parent REGIRI-PRODIGE 58 trial due to an unfavorable benefit/risk ratio and the availability of high-quality serial plasma samples8. This limits the statistical power and generalizability of our findings, which therefore require validation in larger, independent cohorts. Nevertheless, this prospective ancillary cohort remains unique: it provides one of the very few published datasets with dense serial cfDNA sampling (239 samples) in a homogeneous population of patients with refractory metastatic gastroesophageal adenocarcinoma treated in the second-line setting with regorafenib plus irinotecan. In this specific clinical context, published cohorts evaluating cfDNA dynamics are scarce, making the present results particularly valuable as hypothesis-generating data. This context underscores the need for non-invasive biomarkers like cfDNA to optimize patient stratification in second-line mGA treatment. Compared to other second-line therapies, such as ramucirumab plus paclitaxel (median PFS: 4.4 months)19, our low cfDNA group’s PFS (4.51 months) suggests that cfDNA could identify patients with better outcomes, even in a regimen with limited efficacy.

Given the paucity of studies on cfDNA in mGA, our findings provide a valuable contribution to the field. However, validation in larger, independent cohorts is essential to confirm the prognostic utility of cfDNA quantification. Future research should also explore the integration of cfDNA with other biomarkers (e.g., CEA, CA19-9) and imaging modalities to develop comprehensive prognostic models for mGA. Additionally, investigating cfDNA dynamics in response to other second-line therapies could further elucidate its role in treatment monitoring and resistance detection.

This ancillary study of the REGIRI-PRODIGE 58 trial demonstrates that baseline plasma cfDNA concentrations and longitudinal cfDNA dynamics are significantly associated with PFS in patients with mGA. These findings highlight cfDNA as a promising non-invasive prognostic biomarker for mGA, offering a rapid and cost-effective approach to stratify patients in second-line treatment. Future prospective studies incorporating targeted or sWGS are needed to dissect the ctDNA fraction, confirm tumor origin, and potentially improve prognostic performance and therapeutic guidance in patients with mGA.

Methods

Study design

The ancillary cfDNA REGIRI study was conducted as part of the PRODIGE58-UCGI35-REGIRI trial, a comparative, interventional, prospective, phase II, randomized, open-label, multicentre study sponsored by Unicancer and conducted within the PRODIGE group. This research was performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The trial was approved by the French regulatory authorities on 9 November 2018 and by the Comité de Protection des Personnes Ouest V, Rennes (CPP Ouest V-Rennes, France) on 10 October 2019. All patients provided written informed consent prior to participation in the study and for the ancillary biomarker analyses. This study is registered under EudraCT number 2018-002374-46 and ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT03722108. This ancillary study retrospectively evaluated the association between plasma cfDNA concentrations and cancer progression in patients enrolled in the REGIRI-PRODIGE 58 trial. Clinical data were extracted from the REGIRI study database, with patients followed until disease progression, death, loss to follow-up, or study completion. All patients provided written informed consent for participation in the REGIRI trial and ancillary studies, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and local ethical guidelines.

Plasma samples were collected exclusively from patients in the experimental (REGIRI) arm according to the study protocol. Patients from the irinotecan-only control arm did not have blood collection. Plasma samples were collected at predefined timepoints during the first two treatment cycles (Fig. 3). Samples were obtained on Cycle 1: Day 1 (pre- and post-irinotecan infusion [90 min]), Day 2 (24 h post-irinotecan and pre-regorafenib), Day 8 (post-regorafenib), and Day 15 (post-regorafenib); and Cycle 2: Day 1 (post-irinotecan), Day 8 (post-regorafenib), and Day 15 (post-irinotecan). cfDNA extraction and analysis

cfDNA was extracted using the QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (Qiagen, Düsseldorf, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Total cfDNA concentrations were quantified by fluorimetry using the Qubit 1X dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Fragment size analysis was performed using the Fragment Analyzer (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Only DNA fragments in the 160-480 bp range were included in the quantification: This range captures the characteristic nucleosomal ladder (mono-nucleosome ~160–180 bp, di- ~300–350 bp, tri- ~450–500 bp) while minimizing inclusion of high-molecular-weight genomic DNA (gDNA) contamination from leukocyte lysis or pre-analytical artifacts, in accordance with established pre-analytical guidelines for circulating cell-free DNA analysis15,20. No samples were excluded from the evaluation based on these metrics. Baseline cfDNA concentrations were dichotomized into “low” (<2.07 ng/µL) and “high” (≥2.07 ng/µL) subgroups using the optimal cutoff determined by the surv_cutpoint function in the survminer R package (v.0.4.9). Longitudinal changes in cfDNA concentrations were assessed by comparing baseline and final timepoint measurements (typically Cycle 2, Day 15), yielding four subgroups: low-low (low at baseline, remaining low), low–high (low at baseline, increasing), high–low (high at baseline, decreasing), and high–high (high at baseline, remaining high).

Study variables and outcomes

Baseline patient characteristics (age, sex, prior treatments, tumor response to prior therapy) and tumor assessments (location, Siewert classification, histological type, RECIST 1.1 (Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1) criteria, TNM (Tumor, Node, Metastasis), MSI (Microsatellite Instability), EBV (Epstein-Barr Virus) and HER2 (Human Epidermal growth factor Receptor 2) status, metastasis sites) were documented. Outcomes from the REGIRI study, including disease control rate (DCR), best overall response (BOR), objective response rate (ORR), first progression date, PFS, and OS, were analyzed. PFS was defined as the time from randomization to disease progression (per RECIST 1.1) or death from any cause. OS was defined as the time from treatment initiation to death from any cause, with patients alive at the data cutoff censored.

Statistical analyses

PFS and OS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. The optimal cfDNA cut-off value (2.07 ng/µL) was determined using the surv_cutpoint() function from the survminer R package (version 0.4.9). This function implements the maximally selected rank statistics approach21 via the maxstat package. For each continuous covariate, all possible cutpoints were evaluated (excluding the lowest and highest 10% of observations; minprop = 0.1) by computing the log-rank test statistic with maxstat::maxstat.test(smethod = “LogRank”, pmethod = “none”). The cutpoint maximizing the test statistic (i.e., providing the most significant separation of Kaplan–Meier curves) was selected. Variables were then dichotomized into “low” and “high” groups using surv_categorize() for subsequent survival analyses. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p-values were calculated using the log-rank test via the survfit function in survminer. Additional analyses, including comparisons of cfDNA subgroups, were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
