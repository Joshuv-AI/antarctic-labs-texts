Multimodal brain structural and functional analysis in systemic lupus erythematosus patients without overt neuropsychiatric manifestations: associations with disease duration, organ damage, and neurocognitive function
Authors: Jeonghwan Lee, Ji Hyoun Kim, Chi-Hoon Choi, In Ah Choi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSystemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disease that often affects the central nervous system (CNS), leading to structural and functional brain alterations. Although neuroimaging studies have reported significant cortical and white matter changes in SLE, findings remain inconsistent, particularly regarding disease duration and organ damage. This study aimed to comprehensively investigate multimodal brain alterations in SLE using structural and functional neuroimaging.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 30 SLE patients without overt neuropsychiatric manifestations and 34 age-matched healthy controls (HCs). Neuroimaging data were acquired using a 3 T magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scanner. Structural analyses included cortical and subcortical volume measurements via FreeSurfer (30 SLE, 34 HC) and white matter connectometry based on diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) using DSI Studio (29 SLE, 28 HC). Interhemispheric functional connectivity was assessed using resting-state fMRI in the CONN toolbox (27 per group). Clinical assessments included the SLEDAI-2 K, SLICC Damage Index, and SLICC Frailty Index, along with neurocognitive tests that assessed working memory, psychomotor speed, and executive function. Statistical analyses involved ANCOVA adjusted for age and intracranial volume, along with correlation analyses to explore associations between neuroimaging findings and clinical or neurocognitive measures.ResultsSLE patients exhibited significantly reduced volumes in the right occipital lobe (F = 11.274, η2 = 0.153, corrected p = .008) and left thalamus (F = 10.502, η2 = 0.140, corrected p = .028). DTI revealed reduced fractional anisotropy (FA) in the corpus callosum, right cingulum, and brainstem. FA values negatively correlated with disease duration, especially in the left and right inferior fronto-occipital fasciculi and the left cingulum. Patients with organ damage exhibited further FA reductions in the brainstem and left cingulum. FA decreases were also associated with poorer cognitive performance. While global interhemispheric functional connectivity was preserved, patients with moderate disease activity showed reduced connectivity in the frontal operculum and insula.ConclusionsEven in the absence of overt neuropsychiatric symptoms, SLE patients demonstrated structural brain changes—specifically reduced occipital and thalamic volumes and widespread white matter disruptions—associated with disease duration, organ damage, and neurocognitive dysfunction. Functional interhemispheric connectivity was globally preserved but impaired in the moderate activity subgroup.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13075-025-03640-6.

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disease characterized by multisystem involvement, including the skin, joints, kidneys, and central nervous system (CNS) [1]. Neuropsychiatric (NP) manifestations are relatively frequent in SLE and span a wide clinical spectrum, from subtle symptoms like mood disturbance and cognitive dysfunction to major syndromes such as seizures and demyelination. The American College of Rheumatology (ACR) 1999 nomenclature defines 19 NP syndromes that may occur in SLE, involving both the central and peripheral nervous systems. However, not all NP events in SLE are attributable to the disease itself. Attribution of NP events to SLE—thereby defining neuropsychiatric SLE (NPSLE)—is guided by attribution models that incorporate both clinical judgment and exclusion of other potential causes such as infection or metabolic disturbance. Examples include the Ainiala criteria, the SLICC attribution models (Model A and B), and the Italian attribution model proposed by Bortoluzzi and colleagues [2, 3].

This symptomatic heterogeneity has presented a challenge for clinicians in understanding NPSLE. Therefore, while some features of NPSLE, especially those related to altered coagulation, as the overlap with antiphospholipid syndrome, have been better characterized and treated using evidence-based algorithms, other features, such as cognitive and affective manifestations, remain poorly understood [4, 5]. Nevertheless, increasing evidence supports the impact of SLE on the CNS, including structural and functional brain alterations, even in the absence of overt NPSLE events. Recent advancements in neuroimaging have facilitated the exploration of these changes [6, 7]. Importantly, symptoms like headache, mild cognitive dysfunction, and mood changes significantly impair patients’ quality of life, yet occur frequently in the general population and lack disease specificity. As such, these features are often excluded from formal NPSLE classifications, despite possibly representing subtle CNS involvement.

Growing evidence suggests that CNS involvement in SLE extends beyond overt NPSLE events. Neuroimaging studies have identified both structural and functional abnormalities in SLE patients without clinically defined NPSLE [8–10]. Structural neuroimaging studies and meta-analytic reviews have consistently reported widespread cortical and subcortical abnormalities in patients with SLE, including reductions in gray matter volume, hippocampal volume, corpus callosum integrity, and cortical thickness in regions such as the frontal and temporal lobes [6, 8–11]. These anatomical changes have been associated with cognitive deficits, underscoring their clinical relevance [12, 13].

Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) has revealed widespread microstructural white matter abnormalities in SLE [14–16]. Reductions in fractional anisotropy (FA)—a metric reflecting the directional coherence of water diffusion—have been observed in key tracts such as the corpus callosum, cingulum, and anterior corona radiata [17, 18]. While not always correlated with current disease activity, FA values have shown negative associations with disease duration and cumulative organ damage, suggesting that white matter disruption may reflect the long-term effects of systemic inflammation or prior subclinical NP events. [19, 20].

Emerging research from 2023–2024 has further implicated microvascular brain injury in SLE as a contributor to both structural disruption and functional disconnection, providing a biological substrate for observed abnormalities in functional connectivity [21–24]. Resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) studies also have provided insights into the functional changes associated with SLE [25]. One analytical approach, interhemispheric functional connectivity evaluates the brain functional synchronization between corresponding regions in opposite hemispheres [26]. Disruption of this connectivity, often linked to corpus callosum pathology, has been observed in SLE patients and is associated with altered cognitive function [27–29]. Notably, both decreased and increased interhemispheric connectivity have been reported, depending on the region, suggesting complex patterns of dysfunction and compensation.

Despite these advances, much of the existing research has focused on SLE patients with overt neuropsychiatric syndromes, potentially overlooking subtle or subclinical CNS changes in patients without attributable NP events. Additionally, variability in imaging techniques and analytic approaches has contributed to inconsistent findings, particularly regarding associations with disease duration, activity, and damage indices [6]. To address these gaps, our study employed a multimodal imaging approach to comprehensively evaluate structural and functional brain changes in SLE patients. By excluding patients who are currently experiencing overt NPSLE events and utilizing advanced methods such as cortical surface-based morphometry, white matter connectometry, and interhemispheric functional connectivity analysis, our study aims to uncover subtle structural and functional brain alterations and their clinical correlates in this understudied population.

Method

Study participants

SLE patients were recruited from the outpatient clinic of the Rheumatology Department at Chungbuk National University Hospital, and healthy controls (HCs) were recruited through community advertisements. All participants were aged between 19 and 55 years. SLE patients were included if they met the 2012 Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics (SLICC) classification criteria. Exclusion criteria for the SLE group included severe disease activity defined as an SLEDAI-2 K score > 12 and the presence of active neuropsychiatric lupus manifestations at the time of recruitment, based on clinical assessment. Patients with a history of past or subclinical neuropsychiatric symptoms (e.g., remote seizure history or mild mood symptoms) were not excluded if they were clinically stable and did not meet criteria for active NPSLE. HCs with a history of autoimmune diseases requiring systemic glucocorticoid or immunosuppressive therapy were excluded from the study. Participants from both groups were excluded if they had a history or current diagnosis of substance use disorders (excluding tobacco), major psychiatric disorders (e.g., major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia) under treatment, or neurological conditions affecting brain structure, such as head trauma, stroke, Parkinson’s disease, or seizures. Mild depressive symptoms, anxiety, or subjective cognitive decline were not considered exclusion criteria. A total of 30 participants in the SLE group and 34 participants in the HC group were included in the study.

Rheumatological evaluations

For the SLE group, the disease duration, prednisolone-equivalent dosage of current oral glucocorticoid use, antibody profiles (including antinuclear antibody [ANA], anti-dsDNA antibody, anti-cardiolipin antibody, anti-beta2 glycoprotein I antibody [anti-β2GPI], lupus anticoagulant, anti-Smith antibody, and anti-RNP antibody), medication history, disease activity by SLEDAI-2 K (with scores of 6 or below considered mild, 7–12 as moderate, and above 12 as severe), damage by the SLICC Damage Index, and frailty by the SLICC-Frailty Index (FI) were assessed. The SLEDAI-2 K score, MRI, and neurocognitive assessments were performed on the same day for all participants, except for five participants for whom all procedures were completed within a maximum of 10 days.

Neuropsychological assessments

Participants'neurocognitive functions were evaluated using T-scores from the Digit Span, Finger Tapping, and Stroop Color-Word tests. The Digit Span test assessed working memory by requiring participants to recall number sequences presented at one-second intervals [30]. The Finger Tapping test measured motor speed by recording the average tapping rate over three 10-s trials [31]. The Stroop Color-Word test evaluated executive function, focusing on inhibitory control and reaction times across tasks such as word reading and color naming [32]. Additionally, psychological assessments included the Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) [33], a clinician-administered scale for assessing depression severity; the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) [34], measuring state anxiety levels; the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) [35], evaluating perceived stress levels.

MRI acquisition

MRI data was obtained using a 3 Tesla Philips Ingenia magnetic resonance imaging scanner (Philips Medical Systems Nederland B.V., Eindhoven, Netherlands) located in Chungbuk National University Hospital. The T1- magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE), DTI and rs-fMRI data were obtained using the following parameters. T1-MPRAGE: repetition time = 6.5 ms, echo time = 2.9 ms, field of view = 220 mm, flip angle = 8°, slice thickness = 1 mm, in-plane resolution = 0.375 × 0.375. DTI: b-values of 0 and 1000 s/mm2, 32 directions of diffusion gradients, repetition time = 8716.46 ms, echo time = 64 ms, flip angle = 90°, slice thickness = 2 mm, in-plane resolution = 1.72 mm. rs-fMRI: repetition time = 3000 ms, echo time = 35 ms, flip angle = 90°, slice thickness = 4 mm, in-plane resolution = 1.72 mm, acquisited for 5 min during the resting state.

Analysis of MRI image

Cortical and subcortical volumetric analysis

Cortical and subcortical structure volumes were analyzed T1-MPRAGE using FreeSurfer 7.2.0. (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/) following the standard ‘recon-all’ pipeline. The technical details of these procedures are described in prior publications [36–38]. Quality control involved visual inspection of reconstructed surfaces, and manual edits were performed when necessary, following standard FreeSurfer guidelines. Cortical surfaces were parcellated into 68 regions using the Desikan-Killiany atlas, and regions were grouped into the frontal, temporal, parietal, and occipital lobes using the ‘mri_annotation2label’ command with the ‘–lobeStrict’ flag. Additionally, key subcortical structures, such as the thalamus, caudate, putamen, hippocampus, and amygdala, were segmented for analysis. Volumes of cortical lobes and subcortical structures were compared using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), adjusted for age and estimated total intracranial volume (eTIV), calculated using FreeSurfer’s atlas-scaling method, to control for confounding factors. F-values and effect sizes were reported, with significance adjusted using the Bonferroni method. When significant differences were identified in cortical lobes, parcellated regions within those lobes were further compared between groups. Partial correlation tests, adjusted for age and eTIV, were performed to explore relationships between ROI volumes and disease activity variables.

DTI analysis

DTI scans were preprocessed and analyzed using DSI Studio software [39]. Preprocessing steps included brain extraction from the b0 image to generate a brain mask, along with eddy current and motion distortion corrections using the FSL eddy tool. The preprocessed images were reconstructed in MNI space using the Q-space diffeomorphic reconstruction (QSDR) method [40], applying a diffusion sampling length ratio of 1.25 and resampling to a 2 mm isotropic resolution. For quality control, QSDR reconstruction results with an R2 value below 70, indicating poor goodness-of-fit, were excluded [41]. This resulted in the inclusion of 29 participants from the SLE group and 28 from the HC group in the final analysis. Group comparisons of white matter tractography and correlational analyses were conducted using a connectometry database based on DTI FA metrics from 57 participants. FA, a scalar metric derived from DTI, reflects the degree of directional coherence of water diffusion within white matter tracts, with lower FA values indicative of disrupted microstructural integrity or demyelination [42]. The parameters for connectometry analysis were as follows: seeding regions were placed across the whole brain, excluding the cerebellum, and tracks were refined through 16 iterations of topology-informed pruning [43]. The T-score threshold of 2.5 was selected based on established practice in connectometry studies to ensure detection of robust local effects. An FDR threshold of 0.001 was applied to minimize false positives. A total of 4,000 permutations were used to construct the null distribution for track length significance testing, following the standard non-parametric procedure implemented in DSI Studio [44].

rs-fMRI analysis

rs-fMRI data were analyzed using the CONN toolbox, based on SPM12 [45]. Preprocessing steps included realignment with correction for susceptibility distortion interactions, slice-timing correction, outlier detection, segmentation, normalization to MNI space, and smoothing. Outlier scans were defined by displacements exceeding 0.9 mm or global BOLD signal changes greater than five standard deviations [46, 47]. Functional and anatomical data were coregistered, normalized to standard MNI space, and resampled to 2 mm isotropic voxels. Spatial smoothing was applied using an 8-mm Gaussian kernel [47, 48]. Functional data were denoised by regressing out potential confounding effects, including scrubbing of outlier volumes based on the default ART-based procedure in the CONN toolbox, followed by bandpass filtering between 0.008 and 0.09 Hz to isolate relevant BOLD signal fluctuations as per the standard CONN default setting optimized for resting-state analyses [49]. For quality assurance, five participants were excluded as outliers, exceeding the 97th percentile of scan validity or normalization quality, resulting in 27 participants from both the SLE and HC groups included in subsequent analyses. Interhemispheric correlation maps were generated to quantify the strength of homotopic functional connectivity, calculated as the normalized Fisher-transformed bivariate correlation coefficient between the BOLD signal of each voxel and its contralateral counterpart [26]. Group comparisons of interhemispheric connectivity were conducted using a general linear model (GLM) with age as a covariate. Voxel-wise analyses were performed with cluster-level multiple comparison correction using FDR, applying a p < 0.001 voxel-level threshold and a family-wise error-corrected cluster-size threshold of cluster-level FDR-corrected threshold of p < 0.05 [49, 50].

Statistical analysis of demographic and neurocognitive tests

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.3). Missing data for one participant in the neuropsychological test results were imputed using the Multiple Imputation method with Predictive Mean Matching (PMM), incorporating demographic information, and implemented with 20 imputations using the mice package [51, 52]. The predictor matrix was configured to exclude self-prediction by setting diagonal values to zero. Imputation diagnostics, including trace plots and convergence statistics, were reviewed to ensure the stability and validity of the imputed values. Demographic variables and T-scores of neurocognitive tests for the study participants were compared between the two groups using Student’s t-test, with means and standard deviations presented. Gender ratios and categorized MADRS and PSS were compared using the chi-square test. For cortical and subcortical volumetric analyses, statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, with Bonferroni correction applied for multiple comparisons.

Result

Demographic and clinical characteristics

The mean age of the participants was 37.40 ± 10.44 years for the SLE group and 38.21 ± 9.70 years for the HC group, with no statistically significant difference between the two groups (p = 0.671). The gender distribution was not statistically different between the groups, with 28 females and 2 males in the SLE group, and 31 females and 3 males in the HC group. Three participants in the SLE group were classified as having mild depression based on MADRS scores, while the remaining SLE participants and all HC participants were categorized within the normal range, with no statistically significant difference of distribution (p = 0.195). On the PSS, which assesses perceived stress, the SLE group reported experiencing higher levels of stress compared to the HC group (p = 0.026). Additionally, the STAI revealed significantly higher anxiety levels in the SLE group than in the HC group (p < 0.001). In the SLE group, all the participants were positive for antinuclear antibody (ANA), while 3 participants (10%) had lupus anticoagulant and 3 participants (10%) had anti-beta-2 Glycoprotein I antibody (anti-beta2GPI). At the time of the study, 22 participants were on immunosuppressants. The mean prednisolone-equivalent dose of current oral glucocorticoids in the SLE group was 3.29 ± 2.96 mg/day (range: 0–15). The mean disease duration in the SLE group was 77.20 ± 72.85 months (range: 6–311 months). 5 participants had a SLEDAI 2 K score categorized as mild disease activity, while 25 had a moderate disease activity. Regarding the SLICC Damage Index, 23 participants had no damage, while 7 had damage. The SLICC Frailty Index was 4.30 ± 2.30 (range: 0.50–8.50). All clinical data were summarized in Table 1. Cortical subcortical structural volumes analysis

In the analysis of cortical lobes, volumes of occipital lobes in the bilateral hemispheres of the SLE group were significantly lower than those of the HC group (Table 3). There were no significant differences between the two groups in the volumes of the frontal, temporal, and parietal lobes. Among the subcortical structures, the volume of the bilateral thalamus in the SLE group was significantly lower compared to the HC group. Among the parcellated regions comprising the bilateral occipital lobe, the cuneus, and the right lingual area were significantly smaller in the SLE group (Table 4, Fig. 1). No regions showed significant correlations with indices related to rheumatological evaluation. White matter tractography analysis

Through connectometry analysis, white matter tracts with significantly lower FA values in the SLE group compared to the HC group were identified. The major identified tracts included the Corpus Callosum (Forceps Major, Body, and Forceps Minor), the Left Fornix, the Right Cingulum, the Brainstem, and the Left Inferior Longitudinal Fasciculus (Fig. 2-A). In the SLE group, longer disease duration was significantly associated with lower FA values of the white matter tracts, including the Left Inferior Fronto-Occipital Fasciculus, Left Cingulum, and Right Inferior Fronto-Occipital Fasciculus (Fig. 2-B). Additionally, the SLE group with organ damage exhibited significantly lower FA values in white matter tracts, including the Brainstem and Left Cingulum. Significant associations were also observed between white matter tract FA values and neurocognitive function in the SLE group. The T-score of the Digit Span test showed a negative correlation with FA values in the Bilateral Cingulum (Supplement Fig. 1-A). Similarly, the T-scores of the Finger Tapping test and the Stroop Color-Word test exhibited significant negative correlations with FA values across a broader network of white matter tracts, including the Corpus Callosum, Bilateral Thalamic Radiation, Right Cingulum, Right Superior Longitudinal Fasciculus, and Right Arcuate Fasciculus (Supplement Fig. 1-B, 1-C).

Interhemispheric functional connectivity analysis

The SLE group showed no significant difference in interhemispheric connectivity compared with HC group. Additional analyses revealed that SLE group with moderate activity based on SLEDAI-2 K had significantly impaired interhemispheric connectivity in region comprising frontal operculum and insular cortex (Supplement Fig. 2).

Sensitivity analysis of group comparisons with additional adjustment for anxiety (STAI) and stress (PSS)

To further validate our primary findings, we conducted sensitivity analyses for group comparisons of brain volumes and white matter integrity, additionally adjusting for anxiety (STAI) and stress (PSS) scores along with age. In the cortical and subcortical volume analysis, after controlling for age, STAI, and PSS, the SLE group continued to exhibit significantly lower volumes in the left and right occipital lobes compared to the HC group (left: corrected p = 0.008; right: corrected p < 0.001) (Supplement Table 1). For the thalamic volumes, while the left thalamus showed a trend-level difference after correction (corrected p = 0.070), the right thalamus did not reach statistical significance (corrected p = 0.182). In the white matter connectometry analysis, adjusting for age, STAI, and PSS, the SLE group still demonstrated significantly lower FA values compared to the HC group (Supplement Fig. 2). The major affected tracts included the Corpus Callosum, Bilateral Cingulum, Left Fornix, and Bilateral Corticopontine tracts. These findings suggest that the group differences observed in brain structure and white matter integrity were robust even after accounting for anxiety and stress levels.

Post hoc power analysis

To evaluate whether our sample size was sufficient to detect between-group differences in brain structures, we conducted post hoc power analyses for both cortical and subcortical volumes, as well as DTI-based white matter microstructure. Structural volume analysis focused on four ROIs with significant between-group differences (corrected p < 0.05). Observed effect sizes (η2) were converted to Cohen’s d and used to estimate achieved power (α = 0.05, total N = 64). The estimated Powers were 0.814 for the left occipital lobe, 0.917 for the right occipital lobe, and 0.888 and 0.861 for the left and right thalamus, respectively, indicating adequate sensitivity to detect group-level differences in these ROIs.

For the DTI connectometry results, we estimated effect size and statistical power post hoc using mean FA values extracted from the significant tract cluster identified in Fig. 2A. The mean FA for the SLE group (n = 29) was 0.480 ± 0.046, and for the HC group (n = 28) was 0.525 ± 0.029, yielding a Cohen’s d of 1.16. Using this effect size, the resulting Power was estimated at 0.993, indicating excellent sensitivity for detecting FA group differences.

Discussion

In this study, the SLE group exhibited a mean disease duration of 77.20 ± 72.85 months, with the majority showing moderate disease activity and no organ damage, as per the SLICC Damage Index. The SLE group demonstrated significantly higher levels of anxiety and stress, consistent with previous studies [53–55]. In terms of neurocognitive test results, there are impairments in working memory, psychomotor speed, and executive function, which align with findings from prior research [53, 55–60].

This study further investigated structural and functional brain changes in patients with SLE. The SLE group exhibited significant volume reductions in the bilateral occipital lobes, particularly in regions such as the bilateral cuneus, lateral occipital areas, and right lingual gyrus. Additionally, the SLE group exhibited significantly smaller thalamic volumes in both hemispheres, indicating subcortical involvement in SLE-related brain changes. White matter tractography analysis revealed significantly lower FA values in the SLE group compared to the HC group, particularly in the Corpus Callosum (Forceps Major, Body, and Forceps Minor), the Left Fornix, the Right Cingulum, the Brainstem, and the Left Inferior-longitudinal-fasciculus, indicating disrupted white matter tracts integrity. In the resting-state functional MRI analysis, no significant differences in interhemispheric functional connectivity were observed between the SLE and HC groups. However, within the SLE group, patients with moderate disease activity exhibited significantly reduced interhemispheric connectivity in regions comprising the frontal operculum and insular cortex.

Our study identified significant reductions in cortical volume within the occipital lobe and in subcortical thalamic volumes in SLE patients. These findings align with several studies investigating cortical and subcortical structural changes in SLE, which have reported significant alterations across multiple brain regions. Cortical thinning has been frequently observed in both neuropsychiatric SLE patients, particularly in the frontal and temporal regions, using both surface-based morphometry and region of interest analyses [61, 62]. Even in pediatric and adolescent populations with SLE, similar cortical volume reductions have been observed, suggesting that these regions may be particularly susceptible to the structural brain alterations observed in SLE [63]. In addition, thinning in the lingual gyrus and superior frontal cortex has been linked to disrupted resting-state functional connectivity, reflecting an interplay between structural atrophy and functional network disruptions [62]. A systematic review of structural MRI studies reveals reductions in cortical volume and thickness in regions such as the frontal cortex and occipital lobe, often accompanied by cognitive impairments, including deficits in memory and executive function [6]. Beyond cortical changes, subcortical structures, particularly the thalamus, have consistently been implicated in SLE-related brain alterations. Reduced thalamic volumes have been observed in SLE patients and are associated with impaired episodic memory [64]. Patrica et al. reported gray matter atrophy in the posterior thalamus in SLE patients without NPSLE events, suggesting that thalamic involvement may occur even in the absence of overt neuropsychiatric symptoms [19].

DTI studies have consistently reported significant reductions in FA values in SLE patients, particularly in major white matter tracts, such as the corpus callosum and anterior corona radiata, suggesting microstructural damage to the white matter [17]. Several studies have explored the relationship between disease duration and white matter integrity, observing a negative correlation between FA values of the forceps minor of the corpus callosum or the left corticospinal tract and disease duration [18, 20]. However, previous studies have not found significant correlations between FA values and the SLICC/ACR Damage Index [18]. Our findings corroborate these observations, as significantly reduced FA values were observed in the SLE group, particularly in interhemispheric tracts such as the corpus callosum and the right cingulum. Moreover, significant negative correlations were found between FA values and disease duration, and patients with organ damage demonstrated lower FA values in specific white matter tracts, suggesting that cumulative organ damage may contribute to microstructural changes in white matter. Additionally, our study identified negative correlations between the integrity of broad white matter tracts and neurocognitive tests reflecting working memory, psychomotor speed, and frontal-executive function, suggesting that brain microstructural damage is associated with impaired neurocognitive function in SLE patients.

Interhemispheric connectivity, primarily mediated by the corpus callosum, is crucial for the coordinated functioning of the brain [65]. In previous studies conducted on SLE patients, impaired interhemispheric functional connectivity has been observed in regions such as the cuneus, angular gyrus, and middle temporal gyrus [28]. Additionally, a negative correlation has been reported between the interhemispheric connectivity of the insula and attentive function, suggesting that disruptions in interhemispheric pathways may contribute to cognitive dysfunction in SLE patients [29]. Our study found that, although there were no significant differences in interhemispheric functional connectivity between the SLE and HC groups, SLE patients with moderate activity exhibited reduced connectivity in the frontal operculum and insular cortex. These findings suggest that structural damage to interhemispheric pathways may underlie functional connectivity impairments, particularly in patients with more severe disease manifestations [26].

The structural and microstructural alterations observed in our study—particularly reduced thalamic and occipital volumes, along with widespread FA reductions in major white matter tracts—are suggestive of underlying microvascular and immune-mediated mechanisms. Prior studies have shown that blood–brain barrier (BBB) disruption in SLE correlates with gray matter loss and cognitive impairment [57, 66] while perfusion MRI findings in non-NPSLE patients demonstrate cerebral hypoperfusion, consistent with chronic microvascular compromise [24, 67]. Our findings of white matter tract disruption—most prominently in the corpus callosum, cingulum, and brainstem—parallel diffusion MRI studies linking endothelial dysfunction and neuroinflammation to microstructural damage [22, 68]. Furthermore, the observed associations between lower FA values and both disease duration and organ damage support the notion that cumulative vascular burden may underlie white matter injury. Complementing this vascular hypothesis, neurotoxic autoantibodies—such as anti-dsDNA, anti-NMDAR, and anti-ribosomal P—have been shown to exacerbate neural injury by penetrating a compromised BBB and inducing synaptic or glial disruption [54], offering an additional immune-mediated mechanism that may underlie the brain changes observed in SLE patients without overt neuropsychiatric manifestations.

This study has several Limitations that should be considering when interpreting the findings. First, the sample size was relatively small, particularly for subgroup analyses within the SLE group, such as comparisons based on disease activity or organ damage, which may Limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader SLE population. Second, although comprehensive neuroimaging analyses were conducted, the cross-sectional design limits causal inferences regarding the relationships between disease variables, neuroimaging findings, and cognitive outcomes. Longitudinal neuroimaging studies are necessary to establish temporal associations between disease progression, brain structural and functional alterations, and cognitive function. Third, although the study accounted for age and intracranial volume as covariates in the analyses, other potential confounding factors, such as medication use, comorbidities, and lifestyle factors, were not fully controlled for. Although anxiety and perceived stress were statistically adjusted in supplementary analyses with no change in main findings, mood was not included as a covariate, given that only 3 of 64 participants scored in the mild depression range and the rest were within the normal range according to the MADRS. Lastly, cognitive assessments were relatively limited in scope, focusing on specific domains such as working memory and psychomotor speed. A broader neuropsychological battery could provide more comprehensive insights into the cognitive impairments associated with SLE.

Despite these limitations, the study has several strengths. The multimodal imaging approach, incorporating structural MRI, DTI, and rs-fMRI, provides a comprehensive evaluation of cortical, subcortical, and white matter changes in SLE. This integrative approach highlights the interplay between structural and functional brain alterations in this population. Our study utilized advanced analytical methods such as connectometry for white matter analysis and voxel-based interhemispheric functional connectivity analyses, enhancing the sensitivity and specificity of the findings. Future research would benefit from longitudinal imaging to track disease progression, broader neuropsychological batteries, and the application of advanced diffusion imaging techniques such as neurite orientation dispersion and density imaging (NODDI), which enables estimation of neurite density and orientation dispersion, providing more biologically specific insights into white matter microstructure [69].

Conclusion

SLE patients without overt NPSLE with mild to moderate disease activity demonstrated significant reductions in occipital lobe and thalamic volumes, along with compromised integrity in specific white matter tracts, including the Corpus Callosum, the Left Fornix, the Right Cingulum, the Brainstem, and the Left Inferior Longitudinal Fasciculus. Moreover, disruptions in white matter tracts were significantly correlated with longer disease duration and organ damage. Moreover, disruptions in white matter tracts were associated with neurocognitive dysfunction, including deficits in working memory, psychomotor speed, and frontal-executive functions. In contrast, resting-state interhemispheric functional connectivity was preserved. This study provides novel evidence linking cumulative organ damage to white matter microstructural alterations in SLE, which are associated with cognitive dysfunction. This suggests that early clinical intervention may be warranted in patients presenting with cognitive symptoms.
