Experimental investigation of al₂o₃ nanoparticle-enhanced natural ester oil as a biodegradable liquid dielectric for transformers
Authors: S. Marisargunam, T. Mariprasath, P. Saraswathi, Mohit Bajaj, Milkias Berhanu Tuka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Mineral oil has been widely used as a liquid dielectric in transformers, serving as both an insulating and heat dissipation medium. However, its poor biodegradability, low fire resistance, and environmental concerns limit its long-term viability. To address these challenges, this study investigates natural ester oil (NEO) as an eco-friendly, biodegradable alternative, offering enhanced insulation and thermal performance. The research focuses on evaluating NEO’s key characteristics, aging behavior, and failure probability, with and without Al₂O₃ nanoparticle enhancement. A 60-day accelerated thermal aging test at 100 °C was conducted to assess the degradation resistance of NEO compared to mineral oil. The effects of partial discharge, creepage failure, and electrical stress were analyzed, and a Weibull statistical model was applied to predict insulation failure probabilities. Experimental results confirm that NEO demonstrates superior breakdown strength, lower degradation rate, and higher fire safety. The addition of Al₂O₃ nanoparticles significantly improves dielectric properties, reduces viscosity, and enhances the thermal stability of NEO, making it more suitable for transformer applications. This research establishes nano-enhanced NEO as a superior alternative to mineral oil, offering higher insulation performance, improved thermal durability, and enhanced fire safety. The findings advocate for its adoption as a sustainable, high-performance dielectric for modern transformers, promoting both reliability and environmental compliance.

Introduction

A transformer is a key piece of equipment in a power system. A few minutes of transformer failure harmed the entire power system. The majority of failures are the result of insulation deterioration. The insulating design of transformers is currently a critical issue because the transmission voltage range is continually rising to meet the needs of consumers. As a result, the insulation system employed within the transformer should be able to endure large transients. When it comes to the efficient functioning of electrical equipment, the role of electrical insulation is necessary. The loss of energy during the operation of power equipment causes temperature increases and is therefore unavoidable. Because of this, it is necessary to disperse the heat that is produced as a result of the energy losses, particularly when the load conditions are high. When this is not done, the equipment ages more quickly than it should, which eventually leads to its breakdown. The circulation of particular liquids, which also ensures the electrical insulation of energized conductors, can be used to dissipate heat when the appropriate conditions are met. Since the beginning of the 20th century petroleum-based oil, also known as mineral oil, has been the primary insulating liquid in industrial power systems. This is due to the oil’s excellent resistance to aging, low viscosity, quick availability, and low cost1,2.

Insulating liquids based on polychlorinated biphenyl (PCB) were first used in the early 1930s. This was done because it was necessary to operate distribution transformers in areas that required stringent fire security regulations. They did not start raising concerns about the environment until the 1960s. On the other hand, ever since the 1970 s, there has been a growing backlash against the utilization of transformer oils that are composed of PCBs. Even though the majority of PCB oils have been replaced with PCB-free liquids at this point, the latter are not typically acknowledged as alternatives to mineral oils in power equipment. Numerous alternatives to mineral oil have been researched by researchers and engineers over the past four decades to meet the demand for an ecologically friendly, non-combustible, and environmentally safe insulating liquid that can be used in power equipment. During this period, significant progress has been made3–5. Mineral oil is frequently employed as an insulating and cooling agent in electrical transformers owing to its advantageous dielectric characteristics, cost-efficiency, widespread accessibility, and thermal conductivity. The substance functions as a highly efficient electrical insulator, effectively mitigating the risks of arcing and short circuits, while simultaneously facilitating the efficient dissipation of heat that arises from the operation of transformers. Mineral oil, despite its flammability, is regarded as comparatively less flammable when compared to some alternative substances. Consequently, precautionary steps are implemented to effectively limit potential fire hazards. Furthermore, the chemical stability of the substance contributes to the extension of the operational lifespan of transformers6–8.

Mineral oil, a frequently employed liquid dielectric substance in transformers, does possess specific limits that necessitate careful attention. The flammability of the substance presents a substantial safety hazard, especially in interior settings or locations where fire prevention measures are of utmost importance9. Although many safety precautions can be implemented to limit the aforementioned risk, it continues to be a matter of concern. Furthermore, the utilization of mineral oil is not considered environmentally benign due to its origin from hydrocarbons, which might result in detrimental environmental consequences in the event of a spill or leakage. The escalating implementation of environmental rules and the mounting worries regarding environmental impact have generated a heightened interest in insulating fluids that possess enhanced environmental friendliness. Moreover, mineral oil exhibits a very limited temperature operating range, so rendering it less appropriate for applications that are exposed to significant temperature fluctuations or elevated ambient temperatures. The relatively elevated viscosity of the substance may have an impact on the effectiveness of heat dissipation under specific circumstances. At present, renewable energy resources play a vital role in maintaining stability. Researchers and industry professionals have been investigating alternate insulating fluids, such as natural esters or synthetic oils, to mitigate the restrictions associated with safety, environmental impact, and performance in transformer applications10,11.

Solid insulation is an essential constituent in power transformers, predominantly comprised of cellulose-based substances such as pressboard or paper that have been coated with insulating fluids. Solid insulation plays a critical role in transformers by offering both electrical insulation and mechanical support since it is meticulously arranged in layered designs. This design feature guarantees effective heat dissipation, facilitates the isolation of winding turns and enhances the ability to tolerate both mechanical and electrical loads12. As transformers undergo the natural process of aging, the state of their solid insulation becomes of utmost importance, requiring periodic testing and maintenance to evaluate its quality and guarantee the reliability of the transformer. The integration of solid insulation systems with liquid dielectrics is of paramount importance in ensuring the secure and optimal functioning of power transformers within the electrical grid13,14.

Extensive research highlights the critical need for an advanced liquid dielectric that not only provides effective electrical insulation but also exhibits high biodegradability, superior fire resistance, and suitability for deployment in environmentally sensitive and coastal regions. Addressing these requirements, this study investigates natural ester oil (NEO) as a sustainable and high-performance alternative to conventional mineral oil for transformer insulation. A comprehensive comparative analysis is conducted to evaluate the dielectric, thermal, and chemical properties of NEO and mineral oil, emphasizing the limitations of mineral oil, particularly its poor biodegradability, lower fire safety, and non-compliance with modern environmental regulations. This study establishes NEO as a functionally and environmentally superior dielectric fluid that can meet the evolving demands of the power industry15.

In recent years, the integration of nanomaterials into conventional oils has gained considerable attention due to their potential to enhance critical thermophysical properties. Among various nanomaterial-oil combinations, the use of MgO nanoparticles dispersed in Therminol 55 has shown promising results. Studies have reported that this nanofluid formulation significantly improves the thermal conductivity of the base oil while simultaneously reducing its viscosity and density. The observed enhancement in thermal conductivity is largely attributed to the reduction in viscosity, which promotes better fluid flow and heat transfer characteristics. Moreover, the density of the nanofluid was found to decrease with increasing temperature, indicating a strong temperature-dependent behaviour. Research also highlights that the thermal conductivity improvement is closely linked to the optimal concentration of nanoparticles added to the oil. Overall, these findings suggest that the incorporation of nanoparticles can effectively tailor the thermal, viscous, and density-related properties of oil-based fluids, making them more efficient for industrial heat transfer applications16-17.

Furthermore, this research examines the performance enhancement of NEO through the incorporation of aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃) nanoparticles, which are known to improve thermal stability, dielectric strength, and fire resistance. Experimental investigations demonstrate that nano-enhanced NEO exhibits a higher breakdown voltage, improved thermal conductivity, reduced viscosity, and superior aging resistance compared to both unmodified NEO and mineral oil. The statistical reliability analysis, including Weibull distribution modeling, further confirms the lower failure probability of nano-enhanced NEO under electrical stress. These findings establish nano-enhanced NEO as a highly viable insulating fluid for transformers, offering enhanced operational reliability, improved safety margins, and greater compliance with modern sustainability standards.

Measurement methods

A breakdown test cell conducts a specialised test to measure the breakdown strength of oil, also known as dielectric strength. An oil breakdown strength kit, as shown in Fig. 1, measures the breakdown strength, see. This test evaluates the oil’s resistance to electrical pressure before it begins to conduct electricity. First, the test cell is cleaned, dried and the electrode arrangement are as shown in Fig. 2, and then a sample of oil is deposited inside, covering the electrodes. Connecting a high-voltage source, a voltage-measuring instrument, and a current-measuring instrument to the test cell constitutes an apparatus setup. In the beginning, a low voltage is administered to detect any initial breakdown. The voltage is then progressively increased while the current and voltage are monitored. The breakdown point, such as sparking or flashover, is recorded, and the time required for breakdown may also be measured. This procedure is repeated with samples of fresh oil to ensure consistency. For accurate results, it is essential to adhere to applicable standards and safety precautions and to maintain and calibrate equipment properly15.

Using a capacitance bridge, one can measure the capacitance of a specialized test cell or capacitor filled with the liquid sample to determine the dielectric constant (also known as relative permittivity) of liquid dielectrics as shown in Fig. 3. The Capacitance Bridge, test cell, sample container, and reference material with a known dielectric constant—typically air or a standard liquid—are among the necessary tools. Make sure the liquid dielectric sample is pure and at the proper temperature before taking any measurements. By calibrating the capacitance bridge with the reference material, a baseline measurement is established. Measure the capacitance after assembling the test cell with the liquid dielectric, making sure it covers the gap between the electrodes. Next, using the observed capacitance and the known dielectric constant of the reference material, the dielectric constant of the liquid is computed. Understanding the electrical characteristics of liquid dielectrics, which are used in many different applications, such as electrical transformers and capacitors, requires following this process16,17.

Fully automated capacitance and tan delta set.

To quantify the specific resistivity of liquid dielectrics, a resistivity meter is utilized, wherein a set of electrodes is put into the liquid sample contained within a receptacle. A predetermined voltage is delivered across the electrodes, and the resulting current and voltage drop are monitored. The specific resistivity (ρ) of the liquid dielectric can be determined by utilizing Ohm’s law, which involves considering the measured resistance (R), the cross-sectional area (A) of the sample, and the distance between electrodes (L). To ensure precision, it is advisable to calculate the average of repeated measurements. Additionally, it is important to take into account temperature control and correction when dealing with a specific resistivity that is influenced by temperature. The meticulous recording of measurement conditions and outcomes is of utmost importance when characterizing the electrical properties of liquid dielectrics, which find utility in diverse electrical and engineering domains18.

The Karl Fischer coulometric titration technique is widely recognized for its exceptional precision and sensitivity in electrochemical analysis. It is extensively employed to quantify the moisture content, specifically in materials with minimal or trace amounts. The technique used utilizes specialized Coulometric Karl Fischer equipment comprising a generator electrode, a working electrode, and a reference electrode. The Karl Fischer coulometric reagent is employed, which comprises iodine, sulfur dioxide, a base, and a solvent. The preparation of the sample involves the accurate measurement of its weight, followed by its placement within the titration cell. The cell is subjected to a regulated electric current, resulting in the production of iodine at the generator electrode. Subsequently, the iodine undergoes a chemical reaction with the water present in the sample, resulting in the formation of iodide ions. The working electrode is responsible for the detection of iodide ions, and the quantification of the reaction is achieved by measuring the total charge involved. There exists a clear correlation between the quantity of charge and the level of water content present in the sample. The aforementioned technique exhibits a high degree of dependability, particularly in scenarios involving samples with minimal moisture content or where an extraordinary level of accuracy is necessary. Consequently, it holds immense value within diverse sectors such as pharmaceuticals, food, and petrochemicals, where it is employed for the objectives of quality control and research. To get precise and reliable outcomes in Karl Fischer’s coulometric titration, it is imperative to employ appropriate calibration, rigorous methodology, and strict adherence to standardized protocols. To quantify viscosity using a Redwood viscometer following the specifications outlined in IS 1448 (Part 25) as shown in Fig. 4, the initial step involves arranging the viscometer apparatus. This equipment comprises a cylindrical receptacle equipped with an immovable orifice as well as a calibrated glass tube that is graduated in Redwood seconds. Please ensure that the container is filled with the liquid sample whose viscosity is to be measured, taking care to ensure that the sample is at the designated test temperature. The nozzle should be opened to facilitate the passage of liquid through the calibrated glass tube. Quantify the duration required for a consistent quantity of fluid to traverse from the aperture to a designated location on the calibrated cylinder. The duration, measured in units of Redwood seconds, exhibits an inverse relationship with the viscosity of the liquid, whereby liquids with higher viscosity require a longer duration to flow. The Redwood seconds value can thereafter be employed to compute the viscosity of the liquid following the established standards, enabling the assessment and characterization of the fluid’s viscosity properties for many industrial applications20,21.

According to the IS 1448 (Part 2) 1992 standard, the Pensky-Martens Closed Cup Test Method is a widely used way to find out the flash point of many flammable liquids, such as petroleum products. The flash point refers to the minimum temperature at which the vapor above a liquid undergoes combustion upon exposure to an open flame or another ignition source, given a set of specified test conditions. The utilization of this approach is of utmost importance in the evaluation of the potential fire and explosion risks associated with many types of fuels, solvents, and chemicals. The experimental procedure involves subjecting a small portion of the liquid to controlled circumstances within a Pensky-Martens closed cup apparatus as shown in Fig. 5, wherein it is heated. The specimen is subjected to a progressive increase in temperature while the operator intermittently introduces a flame or electric spark near the liquid’s surface to ascertain its combustibility. The flash point is the temperature at which ignition occurs and is then measured. The IS 6104 − 2006 standard, developed in India, offers guidance on the measurement of interfacial tension (IFT) for petroleum products, namely oils and crude oils, as compared to designated liquids such as water21.

Fig. 5Pensky-martens apparatus (Closed cup).

Pensky-martens apparatus (Closed cup).

The assessment of interfacial tension involves the measurement of the force necessary to distort or rupture the interface between two immiscible liquids. This physical property is typically quantified in units such as dynes per centimeter or millinewtons per meter. To quantify interfacial tension (IFT) using the IS 6104 − 2006 standard, it is necessary to adhere to the prescribed processes outlined within the aforementioned standard. The process generally entails the preparation of samples, the establishment of the interfacial tension equipment, the introduction of the sample and contrasting liquid into the test cell, and the measurement of the force or energy required to separate the two liquids at the interface. Accurate interfacial tension (IFT) measurements are very important in the oil industry because they help with figuring out surface-active properties, making sure that safe and effective drilling, production, and refining operations happen, and making sure that additives work well. It is recommended to consistently consult the most recent edition of the standard in order to obtain accurate and specific testing procedures22.

To measure the density of insulating oil according to IS 1448 (Part 16) (2002), you’ll need a density bottle, a vacuum pump, a thermometer, an analytical balance, and the insulating oil sample. The procedure involves cleaning and drying the density bottle, filling it with the oil sample at a specified temperature, and then weighing it accurately using the analytical balance. The density bottle is then evacuated using the vacuum pump to remove entrapped air. After resealing the bottle, the mass of the evacuated density bottle with the oil sample is measured. The density of the insulating oil is calculated by comparing the mass of the density bottle with and without the oil sample and accounting for the known volume of the bottle. This method provides a reliable means of determining the density of insulating oils, which is crucial for quality control, monitoring changes in oil properties, and ensuring the proper functioning of electrical equipment like transformers. Always follow the specific instructions outlined in IS 1448 (Part 16) (2002) for accurate and standardized density measurements23.

Mathematical modelling and analysis

Thermal modeling of transformers is vital for several reasons, including ensuring the safe and efficient operation of these critical electrical components. The essential reasons for thermal modeling include loading capacity, degradation access, enhancing dependability, cooling system assessment, and so on. To disperse heat, transformers are outfitted with cooling devices like fans or oil-based cooling solutions. Thermal modeling aids in the optimization of cooling system design and operation to guarantee effective heat dissipation and temperature management. It enables engineers to make educated judgments on cooling equipment and techniques. Transformers have a maximum load-carrying capability, and exceeding it can cause overheating and damage. By estimating the temperature rise under various load situations, thermal modeling can assist in determining the transformer’s safe operating load levels. This data is critical for accurate transformer size and load management. Transformers convert electrical energy from one voltage level to another. Some energy is wasted as heat during this process due to the impedance of the transformer windings and core. Overheating a transformer can cause insulation deterioration, lower efficiency, and, eventually, transformer failure. Thermal modeling predicts and analyses temperature distributions within the transformer, assisting in the prevention of overheating.

Improving dependability: By understanding transformer thermal behavior, it is feasible to design and operate them in a way that maximizes dependability. Avoiding excessive temperature cycling, minimizing hotspots, and maintaining consistent temperature distribution are all part of this. Transformers may endure heat aging over time, which can hasten insulation breakdown and shorten their lifespan. Thermal models may forecast temperature rises within the transformer over time and hence follow the aging process. This data allows preventive maintenance and replacement decisions. In the electrical business, adherence to safety standards and regulations is critical. Thermal modeling may assist in demonstrating that a transformer design fits these requirements by verifying that it performs within defined temperature limits under a variety of operating scenarios. Transformer losses, which include copper and core losses, add to energy waste. Thermal modeling may be used to optimize transformer design in order to reduce losses and enhance energy efficiency. Thermal modelling-driven energy efficiency gains can also have a positive environmental impact by lowering energy consumption and greenhouse gas emissions24,25. Because of its adaptability in modeling time-to-failure and time-to-event data, Weibull distribution analysis is an indispensable tool. As a result, it is essential for reliability engineering, survival analysis, and a variety of other domains. Because of its malleable shape parameter, it can record different failure mechanisms, which helps in estimating lifetimes, improves quality control, and provides insight into maintenance plans. The Weibull distribution is useful in a variety of fields, including medical survival analysis and manufacturing defect distribution, because it quantifies failure patterns, compares populations, and evaluates risks. This makes decision-making easier. Because it may be applied to aging studies, analyses of deterioration, and risk assessments, this distribution is an essential tool for understanding and managing the dynamics of failure and event occurrences across sectors and disciplines29–31.

The Weibull distribution has applications in the field of insulating media because it makes it possible to conduct analyses and make predictions regarding the lifespan and breakdown characteristics of the materials that are used in electrical systems. It makes it easier to estimate lives and identify failure modes by fitting observed data to the distribution. These failure modes can include early-life failures, wear-out failures, or random failures. The versatility of this distribution is beneficial for assessing reliability, gaining insight into maintenance techniques, and guaranteeing constant performance. Additionally, in manufacturing, it helps in quality control by measuring breakdown characteristics, and in risk management, it helps quantify potential risks that are linked to insulation failures. Both of these benefits are derived from its application in the field of risk management. In addition, the distribution provides data for aging studies, which helps shed light on deterioration processes that are essential for the continued operation of reliable and secure electrical systems. The normal distribution and the Weibull distribution are two different kinds of probability distributions with different uses. It is often used to show the distribution of lifetimes, failures, and event occurrences that change over time, such as early failures, wear-out failures, and random failures. The tail behaviour of the Weibull distribution can vary depending on the shape (β) and scale (⅄) factors, which can sometimes lead to heavy tails that can produce extreme results30, [231]. Effect of nanoparticles on NEO and MO

Ester oil blends with nanoparticles, which significantly improves the oil’s properties. In power transformers, ester lubricants, such as synthetic ester or natural ester fluids, are frequently used as dielectric insulating fluids. There are numerous ways in which the incorporation of nanoparticles into these ester oils can modify their properties and performance. Ester oil can be made to conduct heat better by adding nanoparticles like carbon nanotubes or graphene. This change can make it easier for the transformer to get rid of heat, leading to better cooling and a more efficient transformer. Some nanoparticles can make ester oil’s dielectric qualities, like its breakdown voltage and dielectric constant, better. This can make the electrical protection better and make it less likely that the transformer will break down. When nanoparticles are in ester oil, they can help stop partial discharges from happening inside the transformer. It is important to keep the insulation in good shape and extend the life of the generator. Ester oil can get its antioxidative properties from nanoparticles, which help it avoid oxidation and breakdown over time. This can make the transformer last longer and cut down on how often the oil needs to be changed.

Nanoparticles can make ester oil work better as a lubricant, lowering wear and friction in transformer parts like bushings and tap changers. Ester oils are already known to be fire-resistant, but nanoparticles can make them even more fire-resistant by forming a barrier that stops flames from spreading and limits the release of flammable gases if something goes wrong. The thickness of ester oil can change based on the type and number of nanoparticles added. It’s important to think carefully to make sure that the change in viscosity doesn’t hurt the way the generator cools and works. The different types of nanoparticles and their effects are mentioned below32. Nanodiamonds are crystals made of carbon that are very good at transferring heat. When added to transformer oil, it can greatly improve its ability to move heat, making the transformer more effective at cooling. Carbon nanotubes, whether they have one wall or many, are very good at moving heat. They are used to make transformer oil more thermally conductive, which helps it get rid of heat more quickly. Graphene is another material made of carbon that is very good at transferring heat. When graphene nanoplatelets are mixed into transformer oil, they can improve the oil’s ability to move heat and lower temperature differences inside the transformer. Transformer oil can be made stronger by adding nano-alumina particles to it. They help cut down on partial discharges, which makes the transformer’s shielding work better. Nano-silica particles can be added to transformer oil to make it a better insulator. They make the insulation stronger and help lower the chance of an electrical breakdown.

People use nano-sized zinc oxide particles because they can keep electricity from going through them. They can help make transformer oil more dielectric and reduce partial shocks. Some transformer oils have nano clay bits added to them to make them more electrically and mechanically stable. They can make the shielding better and make the oil less thick. Nano-TiO2 particles can make transformer oil more insulating and stop sludge and deposits from building up inside the transformer. You can add nano-MgO particles to transformer oil to make it a better electrical insulator and lower the risk of an electrical breakdown. Polymer nanocomposites are used in some transformers. Nanoparticles are mixed into a polymer matrix. These nanocomposites can have better thermal, electrical, and mechanical qualities all at the same time. The type of nanoparticle used depends on what the generator needs and how its performance needs to be improved. To make sure the transformer works safely and reliably, it’s also important to think about how well nanoparticles work with the oil and other parts of the transformer and how stable they are over time. Before using nanoparticle-enhanced transformer oils for commercial purposes, they are usually put through a lot of testing and evaluation33.

In transformer applications, it can be hard to improve both dielectric properties and thermal conductivity with a single nanoparticle because these two properties often have different needs. Materials with high electrical resistance are usually used to improve electricity, while materials with good thermal conductivity are usually used to improve thermal conductivity. However, some nanoparticles have traits that are a good mix of these two. Nano-alumina, also called aluminum oxide nanoparticles, is a nanomaterial made from aluminum oxide (Al2O3), a chemical that is found in nature in large amounts. When aluminium oxide is broken down to the nanoscale, it has unique properties and can be used in many different areas, such as electronics, materials science, and nanotechnology34. Nano-alumina particles usually range in size from a few nanometres to a few hundred nanometres on the nanometre scale. The nanoscale size gives the material its qualities. Compared to bulk aluminium oxide, the surface area of ano-alumina particles is a lot bigger. Because they have a lot of surface area, they can be very reactive and good for a lot of different surface changes and functionalization processes. Nano-alumina isn’t as good as some other nanomaterials like carbon nanotubes or graphene, but it can still help improve the way heat moves in some situations. It can help get rid of heat more quickly, which is important for applications that need to control heat well.

Nano-alumina is known for its dielectric qualities, which make it a good addition to materials that keep electricity from getting through. Discussion

This study provides a clear comparison of the performance characteristics of mineral oil (MO), natural ester oil (NEO), and nanoparticle-enhanced NEO, particularly in relation to their dielectric behavior, aging resistance, and suitability for use in power transformers. The results indicate that NEO outperforms mineral oil in key areas such as breakdown voltage (BDV), resistance to partial discharge, and thermal aging stability.

One of the most notable findings is the significant improvement in BDV observed with NEO, especially when enhanced with aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃) nanoparticles. The dielectric strength of NEO increased consistently with rising nanoparticle concentration, which suggests that these additives help distribute the electric field more uniformly and suppress the formation of localized discharges. This directly contributes to improved insulation reliability under electrical stress.

The reliability of these insulating oils was further supported by Weibull statistical analysis. The results showed that NEO, and particularly nano-enhanced NEO, had lower failure probabilities compared to mineral oil, especially under thermal stress. This suggests that NEO has a longer service life and is better suited to handle the demands of long-term transformer operation.

Partial discharge behavior was also analyzed using statistical measures such as skewness and kurtosis. The data showed that NEO consistently produced fewer discharges than mineral oil, regardless of the electrode configuration or aging conditions. This can be attributed to the chemical makeup of ester oils, which typically includes strong polar bonds and fewer impurities, reducing the likelihood of discharge initiation.

Another important aspect examined was the behavior of these oils under thermal aging. While NEO showed higher moisture content compared to mineral oil, this did not negatively impact its insulation performance. In fact, NEO’s moisture equilibrium with cellulosic materials in transformers helps maintain stable dielectric conditions. Moreover, unlike mineral oil—which tends to form acidic by-products during aging—NEO degrades more slowly and produces less harmful substances, preserving both the oil and solid insulation.

Al₂O₃ nanoparticles played a key role in overcoming some of NEO’s limitations. For example, although NEO typically has higher viscosity than mineral oil, the inclusion of nanoparticles helped reduce its viscosity and improve flow characteristics. This is particularly beneficial for transformer cooling and fluid circulation. Additionally, nano-enhanced NEO demonstrated higher fire point temperatures and improved thermal conductivity, which enhances safety and efficiency in transformer operations.

These results highlight a strong connection between oil composition and its electrical and thermal performance. Mineral oil, while still widely used, shows limitations in terms of breakdown strength and aging behavior. NEO provides a more environmentally friendly and electrically robust alternative. When combined with Al₂O₃ nanoparticles, it becomes an even more attractive option, offering better insulation performance, longer lifespan, and improved thermal safety.

Conclusion

This study establishes natural ester oil (NEO) as a biodegradable and sustainable liquid dielectric for transformers, addressing the environmental and performance limitations of conventional mineral oil. The experimental analysis confirms that NEO exhibits superior electrical insulation properties, including higher breakdown voltage and greater resistance to partial discharge and creepage failure. From a chemical perspective, NEO contains a higher moisture content than mineral oil; however, this does not compromise its insulating properties, as cellulosic insulating materials maintain moisture equilibrium. Additionally, thermal aging tests indicate that NEO undergoes slower degradation, releasing higher molecular weight fatty acids that do not react with solid insulation materials. In contrast, mineral oil releases lower molecular weight fatty acids, accelerating the degradation of cellulosic insulation. Although NEO has higher viscosity and lower thermal conductivity, which may impact fluid flow efficiency in transformers, these limitations are mitigated through the incorporation of aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃) nanoparticles. The nanoparticle enhancement significantly reduces viscosity and improves thermal conductivity, thereby enhancing the overall suitability of NEO as a dielectric fluid. Weibull statistical analysis further validates that nano-enhanced NEO has a lower failure probability compared to mineral oil, reinforcing its superior reliability under electrical stress. These findings establish nano-enhanced NEO as a technically and environmentally superior alternative to mineral oil, offering enhanced insulation performance, improved thermal stability, and greater fire resistance. The results strongly support the adoption of NEO as a sustainable, high-performance dielectric fluid for distribution transformers, ensuring long-term reliability, operational efficiency, and compliance with modern environmental standards.
