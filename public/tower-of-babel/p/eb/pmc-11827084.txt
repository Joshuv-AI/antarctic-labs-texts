Novel technique for measuring salt concentrations in food using silver dichromate
Authors: Jiale Liang, Shin-ichi Ishikawa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCuring with salt reduces water content in food and extends its shelf life; therefore, understanding salt permeability in food is critical to food quality and safety. Although there are many new techniques to measure salt concentrations in food, they cannot be applied in practice owing to drawbacks such as the need for expensive equipment or the inability to image quickly; therefore, development of a fast and simple measurement method is crucial. Silver dichromate is converted to silver chloride when it encounters chloride ions. Based on this principle, we soaked radishes in NaCl solutions of different concentrations and added a layer of silver dichromate to it. Salt concentrations was observed in 5 min, as areas with NaCl concentration of ≥1 % appeared white, whereas the other regions appeared red. A strong correlation (r = 0.94/0.96) was observed between the area of salt concentrations measured using this method and that measured using the conventional salt measurement method, demonstrating the reliability of the method. This method will help advance research related to salt concentrations in food and its application in industrial scenarios.Keywords: Salt, White radish, Mohr method, Silver dichromate, Visualization

Introduction

Commercial production methods have a long history of using salt to cure food by reducing its water content and thereby extending its shelf life (Nolan et al., 1992; Varga et al., 1979). Understanding salt content and permeability of food cured with salt has important implications in food quality and safety (Smith et al., 1987).

The traditional method used for measuring salt penetration is complex and involves determining the extent of salt concentrations by measuring the salt content of a specific area. This method requires a considerable number of samples and time. Although new methods to measure salt concentrations in food such as computed tomography, nuclear magnetic resonance, fluorescence imaging, and isotope labeling (Manzocco et al., 2013; Shi et al., 2023; To et al., 2023; Vestergaard et al., 2004) have emerged, they are impractical owing to their high cost or inability to provide quick imaging. These limitations highlight the need for a fast, inexpensive, and efficient method to detect salt concentrations.

Salt is composed of sodium and chloride ions. Salt measurements using the above methods are based on the sodium ion concentration or the sodium chloride molecular distribution. However, the permeability of sodium and chloride ions is similar when salt is used in pickling food (Khavrutskii et al., 2009; Shi et al., 2024), and hence, we can indirectly measure the sodium ion concentration by measuring the chloride ion concentration to understand the mechanism of salt concentrations in food.

The Mohr method, widely used to measure the concentration of chloride ions in solution (Doughty and Waters, 1924; Sezey and Adun, 2019), is based on the principle of precipitation reactions. Addition of silver nitrate to a solution containing chloride ions produces a white precipitate of silver chloride. When all the chloride ions have combined with the silver ions, the remaining silver ions in the solution combine with the dichromate to produce a red precipitate. Order of precipitation is determined based on the solubility of silver chloride and silver dichromate. Red colored silver dichromate changes into white colored silver chloride on encountering chloride ions (Baxter et al., 1909). Therefore, this experiment aims to calculate the salt permeability rate by measuring the area percentage of silver chloride in the sample. Combined with the salt concentration measured by the salt concentration meter, the correlation coefficient between the permeation area and the salt concentration is calculated to demonstrate the effectiveness of this method in measuring salt permeability.

If the above theory is correct, it will significantly reduce the cost of testing the salt content in food and improve the efficiency of salt concentration testing. Because the experimental method is simple and quick, even ordinary people can quickly master it. Therefore, this method can be used in the food industry and daily life to detect salt penetration in food quickly. It has broad application prospects in detecting salt penetration in cooked food and reducing salt intake.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

The experimental sample was Japanese white radish obtained from the Seiyu online supermarket in Sendai, Miyagi Prefecture, Japan. The sample was stored at a low temperature (1 °C, 95 % relative humidity) (Xiao et al., 2014; NARO, 2024) and transported to the laboratory within 24 h. Air conditioning was used to maintain an ambient temperature of 25 ± 1 °C, and the sample was set aside for 12 h to reach the test temperature (25 °C). We spread three layers of kitchen paper to prevent water in the white radishes from evaporating, which might have affected the experiment. Distilled water was sprayed on the surface of the radishes, and they were covered with a plastic wrap. Salt (Seawater, Setouchi, Nihonkaisui Co., Ltd. Sakaide, Kagawa, Japan) was purchased from the Aeon supermarket. Reagents were obtained from Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation (Chuo, Osaka, Japan) and were of analytical grade.

Immersion method

The experimental design was a 5 × 5 factorial arrangement of treatments, using five concentrations of NaCl and five immersion times.

Samples were extracted from the middle 1/3 of a white radish using a metal mold with a diameter and height of 65 and 30 mm, respectively. Deionized water was used to prepare 2500 mL of NaCl solution at concentrations of 0, 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 % (w/v), and poured into square plastic containers (3700 mL capacity). A plastic holder was used to completely immerse the six samples in NaCl solutions, and the samples were spaced adequately to prevent uneven penetration of NaCl owing to the samples coming into contact with each other. The containers were closed to prevent evaporation of water, and the samples were immersed for 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h.

All the samples were maintained at an ambient temperature of 25 ± 1 °C to ensure consistent results.

Coloring methods

A 5 % (w/v) potassium dichromate solution and 10 % (w/v) silver nitrate solution were prepared using deionized water. Silver nitrate solution was poured into a spray bottle (China).

Samples were removed from the NaCl solutions, and their surfaces were dried using a Crecia Kim Towel (Nippon Paper Industries Co., Ltd. Chiyoda, Tokyo, Japan). Samples were carefully cut from the middle, horizontally and vertically, using a clean tool. These cuts created two different samples, one with a transverse section and the other with a vertical section. The tool was carefully cleaned with deionized water after handling each sample to prevent NaCl staining from affecting the results. The samples were dried again using Kim Wipes (S-200; Nippon Paper Industries Co. Ltd. Chiyoda, Tokyo, JP). Furthermore, 200 μL and 120 μL of potassium dichromate were added on the transverse and vertical sections, respectively, and spread evenly. Silver nitrate solution (487.4 μL) was sprayed three times from 15 cm above the sample, and the sample was set aside at room temperature 25 ± 1 °C for 5 min.

Photographic methods

All photographs of the experiments were captured using a Digital Imaging Lightbox (DIB-0806; Universal Scientific Industrial Co. Ltd., PT Enschede, Netherlands). The box had a lid, and a camera was positioned over the box to capture the images. A circle and rectangle were marked directly below the camera to photograph each sample at the same position. Samples were placed in the box and the light was switched on. The box was closed to prevent external light from affecting the image.

The images were subjected to minimal post-processing (background cropping and proportional enlargement) to maintain the integrity of the original images without altering its color or light.

Measurement of stained area and salt concentration

White colored areas in the sample represent the penetration of NaCl, whereas the red colored areas represent the lack of penetration. Total area of the sample was calculated using the ImageJ software (Version 13.0.6; Bethesda, Maryland, USA). Subsequently, the background was removed, and the threshold was adjusted (set to 100) to calculate the red colored area. All samples were removed from the blender and squeeze-filtered using Kim Wipes. The filtrate was analyzed using a salt meter (PAL-SALT; Atago Co., Ltd. Minato, Tokyo, Japan).

It is important to note that the area of NaCl penetration is referred to as the white area, despite its yellowish appearance for ease of understanding. Fig. 3, Fig. 4 can be referred for visual clarification.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the arithmetic means of triplicate measurements with the standard deviation for each parameter. Results are reported as mean ± standard deviation. Differences between means were analyzed using one-way variance using SPSS Statistics (SPSS IBM; Urayasu, Chiba, Japan), and the Tukey's test was performed to determine whether the differences were statistically significant (p < 0.05). The Pearson's correlation coefficient was calculated to examine bivariate correlations using SPSS Statistics software.

Results and discussion

Explanation of experimental principle

The microscopic view depicted in Fig. 1A reveals that the chloride ions are more concentrated in the outermost layer and the concentration decreases toward the interior. In the macroscopic view, the entire surface of the radish appears white as the chloride ions have no color. The entire surface appears yellow when dichromate is applied on the surface (Fig. 1B). As the dichromate and chloride ions are anionic, they do not react with each other. When silver nitrate is sprayed on the surface, the silver ions preferentially react with the chloride ions to form a white silver chloride precipitate, because silver chloride is less soluble than silver dichromate. Although a small number of chloride ions in the center combine with silver ions to form silver chloride, the low concentration of the chloride ions in the center causes excess silver ions to react with the dichromate to form the red colored silver dichromate. Silver chloride forms a white precipitate; areas with a high concentration of chloride ions appear yellowish macroscopically owing to the concentration of dichromate ions, which is higher than the concentration of silver chloride. By contrast, areas with a low concentration of chloride ions appear bright red (Fig. 1C). Thus, by observing the distribution of the two colors we can identify the areas where NaCl has penetrated.

Salinity measurement threshold and optimal shoot time

As mentioned in Section 3.1., areas with high chloride ion concentrations appear white, whereas the areas with low chloride ion concentrations appear bright red. We aimed to determine the concentration of chloride ions that was required for the complete conversion of silver dichromate (red area) to silver chloride (white area).

Therefore, prior to the formal experiment we performed an additional experiment to determine the threshold for measuring the chloride ion concentration. The experimental procedure, measurement method, photography method, and statistical method were the same as described in the Materials and Methods section. The samples were immersed in five different concentrations of NaCl solutions for 1 h and the surfaces were dried, stained, and photographed at 1, 3, 5, 7, and 9 min. The proportion of the total area covered by the white area was calculated.

The results of this experiment (Fig. 2) revealed that the white area of the white radish pickled in deionized water (0 % NaCl) did not change significantly with the increase in time, indicating that the chloride ions in radish were not sufficient to cause a color change. White area on the surface of the radish immersed in 0.5 % NaCl solution increased slowly, although color change on the surface was not clearly visible in approximately 20 % of the cases (Fig. 3A). By contrast, the white area increased rapidly within a short period when observing samples immersed in 1 % NaCl solution covering more than 94 % of the area in 5 min (Fig. 3A). NaCl solutions with a concentration of more than 1 % converted the bright red silver dichromate produced into a white silver chloride precipitate. Therefore, we can conclude that the threshold for measuring the salt concentration in this experiment is at 1 %, above and below which the sample appears white or bright red, respectively.

The waiting time after staining was a critical factor that affected the experimental results. Ions in the high-concentration solution spontaneously diffuse into low-concentration solution. Chloride ions diffuse from the areas of the radish with a higher concentration into the central area with a lower concentration owing to a longer waiting time, thereby resulting in an overestimation of the salt penetration (large white area). Conversely, the silver ions do not react sufficiently with the chloride ions when the waiting time is short. Consequently, a large amount of silver dichromate remained without reacting with the chloride ions resulting in an underestimation of the salt penetration (small white area). White radishes pickled in 1 % NaCl developed 94 % white area in 5 min of staining after which there were no significant changes, indicating that the optimal time for capturing images was at 5 min.

Picture analysis after staining

We observed that the white areas of the samples soaked with 0 and 0.5 % NaCl were not clearly visible on the surface and transverse section (Fig. 3). Results obtained while analyzing the white areas using ImageJ were consistent with our initial observations (Fig. 4A–C). We observed a significant increase in the white areas on the surface of samples soaked in 0.5 % NaCl solution compared to that on samples soaked in 0 % NaCl solution (p < 0.05; Fig. 4A). However, owing to the low percentage of silver chloride, the white areas could not be discerned with the naked eye (Fig. 3A). When observing the transverse and vertical sections, the white area did not expand with the increase in soaking time, indicating that the salt did not penetrate the sample at this concentration, although, a small amount had adhered to the surface of the sample (Fig. 3B and C). We observed that the salt concentrations of white radish soaked in 0 and 0.5 % NaCl solutions were similar (Fig. 4D), consistent with our analysis of the white area.

Fig. 3A reveals that the red areas were practically invisible on the surface of samples soaked in 1, 2, and 4 % NaCl solutions, indicating that soaking the samples for more than an hour in ≥1 % NaCl solution causes the silver dichromate generated on the surface of the samples (red areas) to completely transform into silver chloride (white areas). A similar result was obtained when the white areas were analyzed using the ImageJ software (Fig. 4A). While observing the transverse and vertical sections, the NaCl concentration in the white area was revealed to be above 1 % and the NaCl concentration in the red area was below 1 %. Therefore, the NaCl concentration threshold for the transformation of the red area into white was at 1 %. These results are in line with our hypothesis described in Section 3.2. Salt in the 0.5 and 1 % NaCl solution did not penetrate the interior of the samples as the immersion time increased; instead, it adhered to the surface of the samples (Fig. 3, Fig. 4), owing to the osmotic pressure inside the white radish. A study by Ohtsubo and Miyagawa reported that the osmotic pressure of white radish was 6.90 ± 0.6 atm, which translates to a NaCl solution of approximately 0.9 % concentration; hence, NaCl solution with a concentration of >0.9 % is required for the salt to penetrate the interior of the white radish (Ohtsubo and Miyagawa, 1990). These findings were consistent with the results observed (Fig. 4D). The salt penetration of white radishes soaked in deionized water (0 % NaCl) did not differ significantly from the samples soaked in 0.5 and 1 % NaCl solutions.

Intersection of the red and white areas were clearly observed in the samples soaked in 2 and 4 % NaCl solutions, the white area gradually increased with an increase in soaking time (Fig. 3B and C; Fig. 4B and C). This also means that the NaCl concentration gradually increased with an increase in soaking time, and the results are consistent with Fig. 4D.

Correlation analysis between areas of color change and salt concentration

Fig. 5A and B reveal the robustness of our methodology. The correlation coefficients between the white areas of the transverse and vertical sections of the samples under different pickling conditions and salt concentrations were 0.94 and 0.96, respectively, indicating a strong correlation (p < 0.0001). White areas of the different samples reveal an approximately linear correlation with the salt concentration. The strength of this correlation underscores the reliability of our findings. Therefore, this method can be used to deduce the salt penetration by observing the white area, thereby enabling the visualization of salt concentrations.

Fig. 5C reveals that the correlation coefficient between the white areas of the transverse and vertical sections under different pickling conditions was 0.98, thereby indicating a strong correlation (p < 0.0001). White areas of the transverse and vertical sections of different samples reveal an approximately linear correlation, which proves that when using this method to test the salt concentrations area, the test results are not affected by the different cutting methods.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we can visualize the areas where salt at concentrations above 1 % has penetrated the sample after soaking in NaCl solution, and the distribution of white and red areas can be used to determine the salt penetration range visually. This method is simple to perform, inexpensive, and offers rapid imaging advantages. The use of this method facilitates the elucidation of the principles influencing salt concentration, food composition, and the internal structure of food on the uniformity of salt concentrations and penetration. It plays an important role in the development of low-salt food, saving cooking time, improving cooking efficiency, and other practical applications.

Compared with traditional methods for detecting salt penetration (such as Computed Tomography (CT), Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR), and Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), the most significant advantage of this new method is that it can quickly visualize the salt concentration in food without the need to purchase expensive machinery, significantly reducing the cost of salt visualization. The testing method is so simple and quick that even an ordinary person without relevant knowledge can quickly master the operation method to perform simple salt visualization, significantly reducing the threshold for salt visualization. This method may have powerful application capabilities in food industry production and daily life.

The salt concentration threshold for this test is 1 %. Here, the amount of silver dichromate generated determined the threshold for detection of salt penetration. Lowering the amount of silver dichromate generated can reduce the detection threshold, increase the precision of detecting salt content, and identify areas where lower concentrations of NaCl solutions diffuse. The samples used in this test were white radishes; however, we also conducted the same test on potatoes and carrots and made similar observations. We plan to use this method to observe the diffusion of salts in other food products and conduct more in-depth studies. However, this method also has limitations. For example, factors such as the pH value of the soaking solution or sample, amino acid content, and color will affect the salt detection results. Therefore, testing the sample's composition before using this method is necessary to prevent external factors from affecting the test results.
