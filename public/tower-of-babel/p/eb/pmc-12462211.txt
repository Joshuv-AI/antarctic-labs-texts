Diagnostic accuracy of T-SPOT.TB and TST in detecting active tuberculosis in patients with rheumatic immune diseases: a fully matched comparative study
Authors: Hangxing Wang, Yuanchun Li, Lifan Zhang, Xiaoqing Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivesPatients with rheumatic immune diseases (RD) are considered a high-risk population for developing active tuberculosis (ATB). Timely and accurate diagnosis of ATB in RD patients is critical for optimizing treatment outcomes and improving prognosis. Both interferon-gamma release assays (IGRA) and the tuberculin skin test (TST) are immunological methods employed in the diagnosis of tuberculosis. However, the diagnostic accuracy of these tests in RD patients, who often experience immune dysfunction, remains underexplored. This study aims to compare the diagnostic accuracy of TST and T-SPOT.TB in RD patients with suspected tuberculosis symptoms.MethodsThis prospective study included RD patients presenting with any of the following symptoms—fever, cough, night sweats, or unexplained weight loss (all symptoms recommended by the World Health Organization for tuberculosis screening)—from September 2014 to September 2015. Both T-SPOT.TB and TST were performed, and patients were categorized into ATB and non-ATB groups based on clinical diagnosis (including microbiologically confirmed and clinically diagnosed cases). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed to compare the diagnostic accuracy of T-SPOT.TB and TST for ATB and to determine the optimal cutoff values. Sensitivity, specificity, predictive values, and likelihood ratios were calculated, along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The concordance between T-SPOT.TB and TST in diagnosing ATB was also evaluated.ResultsA total of 300 RD patients were enrolled in the study. Of these, 35 (11.7%) were diagnosed with ATB, 258 (86.0%) were excluded from ATB, and 7 (2.3%) had an unclear diagnosis. Among the RD patients, the ATB group exhibited significantly higher frequencies of night sweats (34.3% vs. 14.0%, p=0.002) and unexplained weight loss (17.1% vs. 3.1%, p<0.001) compared to the non-ATB group, while no significant differences were observed between the groups for fever and cough. The area under the ROC curve (AUROC) for T-SPOT.TB was 0.89 (95% CI 0.82-0.95), while the AUROC for TST was 0.74 (95% CI 0.63-0.84), with T-SPOT.TB demonstrating significantly superior diagnostic accuracy (AUROC difference 0.15, 95% CI 0.06-0.24, p=0.001) (Figure). The optimal cutoff for T-SPOT.TB in diagnosing ATB was 24 spot-forming cells (SFCs) per 10^6 peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), with sensitivity, specificity, positive likelihood ratio, negative likelihood ratio, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value of 88.6%, 84.9%, 5.86, 0.13, 44.3%, and 98.2%, respectively. The optimal cutoff for TST was a 5mm induration diameter, yielding diagnostic metrics of 57.1%, 88.8%, 5.08, 0.48, 40.8%, and 93.9%, respectively. The sensitivity of T-SPOT.TB was significantly higher than that of TST (p=0.003), while no significant difference in specificity was observed (p=0.193). As the T-SPOT.TB spot count and TST induration diameter increased, the likelihood ratios for diagnosing ATB also increased. The agreement between T-SPOT.TB and TST in diagnosing ATB in RD patients was moderate (kappa=0.466, p<0.001), and parallel testing with TST did not improve the sensitivity of T-SPOT.TB.ConclusionIn RD patients with suspected ATB symptoms, both T-SPOT.TB and TST offer valuable diagnostic assistance. T-SPOT.TB demonstrates superior diagnostic accuracy, particularly in terms of sensitivity. Higher spot counts on T-SPOT.TB or larger induration diameters on TST should raise clinical suspicion for the presence of concurrent ATB.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a major global health burden due to its high incidence, difficulty in early diagnosis, and associated mortality. According to projections by the World Health Organization (WHO), China is expected to report approximately 520,000 new TB cases and 27,000 TB-related deaths in 2024, ranking fifth globally in TB burden [1]. In recent years, the global prevalence of rheumatic immune diseases (RD) has been on the rise, with over 20 million RD patients currently residing in China [2–4]. Due to the nature of these diseases and the administration of immunosuppressive therapies [5], RD patients experience heightened susceptibility to a range of infections, among which active tuberculosis (ATB) constitutes a significant and grave complication [6].

When ATB coexists with RD, it presents with a constellation of challenges, including complex clinical manifestations, diagnostic ambiguity, difficult treatment regimens, prolonged infectious periods, and elevated mortality rates. The atypical nature of the symptoms, compounded by the potential masking effects of corticosteroid treatments, exacerbates the diagnostic difficulty, particularly in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), where misdiagnosis of ATB can range from 23–62% [7]. Furthermore, treatment aimed at managing RD often exacerbates the immunosuppressive state of these patients, increasing the risk of disseminated TB and significantly raising the incidence of extrapulmonary TB compared to the general population [8]. Studies have documented that the mortality rate in SLE patients with ATB can range from 15.6 to 54.5%, a figure that is four to fifteen times higher than that of the general ATB population [7–11]. Consequently, rapid and accurate diagnosis, coupled with timely treatment, is of paramount importance for preventing disease transmission and mitigating mortality in this high-risk cohort. According to current WHO guidelines, neither TST nor IGRA is recommended for the diagnosis of TB disease due to their inability to distinguish latent from active infection [12]. However, in China, national standards and expert consensus (WS288-2017) permit the auxiliary use of these immunological tools in situations where microbiological evidence is lacking [13]. This reflects the diagnostic reality in high-burden, resource-constrained clinical settings. Notably, the UK PREDICT TB study demonstrated that approximately one-third of TB progressors tested negative on both TST and IGRA, underscoring the limited sensitivity of immunological assays in predicting disease development [14].

The definitive diagnosis of TB is primarily reliant on microbiological testing. However, in RD patients with ATB in China, the rate of microbiological positivity is only about 30% [15]. Currently, in vivo skin tests and in vitro interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs) are the internationally recognized methods for detecting Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) infection [16, 17]. National standards and expert consensus in China recommend these methods as auxiliary diagnostic tools for ATB in the absence of microbiological confirmation [13, 18]. Notably, both of these methods are immunological in nature, and in patients with autoimmune diseases, immunosuppressive treatments may influence the sensitivity of these tests to varying extents [19]. Therefore, the diagnostic accuracy of TST and IGRA in RD patients with impaired immune function remains insufficiently explored, and their clinical utility warrants further elucidation.

This study employs a fully matched design to evaluate and compare the diagnostic accuracy of TST and T-SPOT.TB (a form of IGRA) in RD patients presenting with suspected TB symptoms, aiming to explore their potential applications in clinical practice.

Methods

Patient and sample collection

This study was based on the Epidemiological Study and Therapeutic Evaluation of Rheumatic Patients with Tuberculosis (ETHERTB) [20]. This study enrolled RD patients from 13 tertiary medical centers across the eastern, central, and western regions of China, between September 2014 and September 2015. All participants presented with at least one symptom related to TB screening, as recommended by the WHO, including fever, cough, night sweats, or unexplained weight loss, leading to a clinical suspicion of ATB. At the time of enrollment, all patients underwent both T-SPOT.TB testing and the TST. All patients underwent both the TST and T-SPOT.TB testing during the same clinical visit, and both procedures were conducted on the same day. The final diagnosis of ATB or non-ATB was made based on routine clinical practice, incorporating both microbiological confirmation and clinical judgment. Microbiological confirmation included positive results from smear microscopy, mycobacterial culture (solid or liquid media), or nucleic acid amplification tests (e.g., Xpert MTB/RIF assay), with identification of Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex, in accordance with national TB diagnostic guidelines. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age > 15 years, (2) a confirmed diagnosis of RD, (3) presentation with at least one TB-related symptom, and (4) completion of both T-SPOT.TB and TST testing. The exclusion criterion was pregnancy. Based on microbiological and clinical evaluation, participants were further categorized into four subgroups: (1) Microbiologically confirmed ATB; (2) Clinically diagnosed ATB; (3) Indeterminate TB status; (4) Non-ATB. This classification was made to better reflect the spectrum of TB certainty and to facilitate subgroup analysis.All eligibility decisions were independently reviewed by two board-certified rheumatologists. In cases of disagreement, a third senior rheumatologist was consulted to reach consensus. For patients without microbiological confirmation, a clinical diagnosis of active TB was established based on the presence of TB-suggestive symptoms (e.g., persistent cough, fever, night sweats, or unexplained weight loss), chest imaging consistent with TB, exclusion of alternative diagnoses, and a favorable response to empirical anti-TB treatment, following the diagnostic criteria outlined in the Chinese national guideline (WS288-2017). This approach is consistent with previously published studies, including the work of Ewer et al. [21], which applied similar criteria in non-confirmed TB cases.

T-SPOT.TB Testing Procedure: The T-SPOT.TB test was performed following the manufacturer’s instructions provided with the T-SPOT.TB kit (Oxford Immunotec Ltd, Oxford, UK). For each participant, 4 mL of peripheral venous blood was collected, and the blood was anticoagulated with heparin. The peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were subsequently isolated and purified, then adjusted to a working concentration of 2.5 × 10^6 cells/mL. The prepared cell suspension was then combined with Mycobacterium tuberculosis-specific antigens and added to a pre-coated 96-well microplate. For each sample, both positive and negative controls were set up in separate wells. The plates were incubated in a CO2 incubator for 16 to 18 h. Following incubation, the plate was washed, and an alkaline phosphatase-labeled mouse monoclonal antibody against human interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) (50 µL) was added, followed by incubation at 4–8 °C for 1 h. The subsequent steps included further washing, color development, and the enumeration of spot-forming cells (SFCs) using the Ispot Reader Spectrum ELISPOT reader (AID, Germany).

TST Procedure: Simultaneously with the collection of peripheral venous blood for T-SPOT.TB testing, all participants also underwent TST according to standard protocol. Specifically, 0.1 mL of a solution containing 5 IU of purified protein derivative (PPD) was injected intradermally. The injection site was evaluated 72 h post-injection for redness, swelling, and induration, which were measured to assess the TST response. All TSTs were administered and interpreted by trained personnel following a uniform protocol.

Both TST and T-SPOT.TB testing were performed by staff who were blinded to the final clinical diagnoses of the RD patients.

Microbiological confirmation was based on both solid (Löwenstein–Jensen) and liquid (MGIT) culture techniques. All sputum samples were processed within 24 h of collection. Laboratory analyses were conducted at certified microbiology laboratories affiliated with each of the participating centers, following national diagnostic standards for tuberculosis. Microbiological confirmation was based on bacteriological examinations (positive smear microscopy or positive mycobacterial culture, using solid or liquid media, with identification of Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex) and molecular biological tests (positive nucleic acid detection for MTB). Molecular testing was primarily conducted using the Xpert MTB/RIF assay (Cepheid, USA). In some centers, conventional PCR protocols aligned with national diagnostic standards were used where Xpert was not available.

Clinical data collection

TB contact history was assessed through a structured questionnaire administered at enrollment, which included queries regarding prior household or occupational exposure to individuals diagnosed with active TB. TB-related symptoms, including unexplained weight loss, were self-reported by patients during the clinical interview and verified against physician judgment and medical records when available.

Statistical analysis

All data analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0. First, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was employed to assess the normality of continuous variables. For data that followed a normal distribution, values were expressed as means ± standard deviation (x ± S), and differences between two groups were compared using the independent-samples t-test. For data that did not follow a normal distribution, values were presented as median (interquartile range) [M(Q1, Q3)], and the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test (also known as the Wilcoxon rank-sum test) was used for comparison. Categorical data were expressed as frequencies n (percentages %), and group differences were analyzed using the chi-square (χ²) test. In cases where the theoretical frequency in any cell was less than 5, Fisher’s exact test was applied. A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves and corresponding Area Under the Curve (AUC) values were used to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of TST and T-SPOT.TB. Cohen’s kappa coefficient was calculated to assess the level of agreement between TST and T-SPOT.TB results. Likelihood ratios (positive and negative) were computed using standard statistical formulas. A two-sided P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics of RD patients

Among the 293 RD patients included in the study, 35 (11.9%) were diagnosed with ATB, while 258 (88.1%) were excluded from the ATB diagnosis. Among the 35 patients diagnosed with active TB, 23 (65.7%) had pulmonary TB (PTB), 7 (20.0%) had extrapulmonary TB (EPTB), and 5 (14.3%) had both pulmonary and extrapulmonary manifestations. Of the ATB cases, 31.4% (11/35) were confirmed microbiologically, including 8 cases with positive smears, 3 with positive cultures, and 3 with positive nucleic acid tests. Some patients were positive on more than one test. In microbiologically confirmed ATB cases, T-SPOT.TB was positive in 11/11 (100%), and TST in 6/11 (54.5%). In clinically diagnosed ATB cases, T-SPOT.TB was positive in 20/24 (83.3%), and TST in 14/24 (58.3%).

The proportion of males in the ATB group (25.7%) was significantly higher than in the non-ATB group (10.5%), with a p-value of 0.010, suggesting that male RD patients are more likely to develop ATB. The average age of the ATB group was 41 ± 15 years, compared to 37 ± 12 years in the non-ATB group; however, the difference between the two groups was not statistically significant (p = 0.117). The average BMI in the ATB group was 20.3 kg/m², significantly lower than the 22.6 kg/m² in the non-ATB group (p = 0.004), indicating that a lower BMI may be associated with an increased risk of ATB. The proportion of patients with diffuse connective tissue diseases in the ATB group (57.1%) was significantly lower, while the proportion of patients with systemic vasculitis syndrome and spondyloarthritis was higher, compared to the non-ATB group (p < 0.001). In contrast, the non-ATB group was predominantly composed of diffuse connective tissue disease patients (91.1%). There was no statistically significant difference in disease duration between the ATB group [median 19 months (IQR 6–71)] and the non-ATB group [median 17 months (IQR 2–47)] (p = 0.278). The incidence of night sweats (34.3% vs. 14.0%, p = 0.002) and unexplained weight loss (17.1% vs. 3.1%, p < 0.001) was significantly higher in the ATB group compared to the non-ATB group, while the incidence of fever and cough, although different, did not reach statistical significance. No significant difference was found between the groups in terms of TB exposure history (p = 0.458); however, the proportion of patients with a history of TB in the ATB group (14.3%) was significantly higher than in the non-ATB group (5.0%) (p = 0.033). The proportion of patients using corticosteroids in the ATB group was lower (68.6% vs. 95.3%, p < 0.001), and the maximum daily dose was also lower. There was no significant difference in the use of biologic agents between the two groups (p = 0.170). The ATB group had significantly higher platelet counts (PLT), erythrocyte sedimentation rates (ESR), and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels compared to the non-ATB group (p-values of 0.021, 0.010, and < 0.001, respectively), while there were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of white blood cell count (WBC), neutrophils (N), lymphocytes (L), or hemoglobin (Hb) levels (Table 1).

The optimal cutoff value for T-SPOT.TB in diagnosing ATB in RD patients was determined to be 24 SFCs/10^6 PBMCs. At this threshold, the sensitivity, specificity, positive likelihood ratio, negative likelihood ratio, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value were 88.6%, 84.9%, 5.86, 0.13, 44.3%, and 98.2%, respectively. For TST, the optimal cutoff was a skin induration diameter of 5 mm, with corresponding diagnostic metrics of 57.1%, 88.8%, 5.08, 0.48, 40.8%, and 93.9% (Table 2). The sensitivity of T-SPOT.TB was significantly higher than that of TST (p = 0.003), though the specificity did not differ significantly between the two (p = 0.193). The concordance between T-SPOT.TB (positive at ≥ 24 SFCs/10^6 PBMCs) and TST (positive at induration diameter ≥ 5 mm) in diagnosing ATB among RD patients was moderate (kappa = 0.466, p < 0.001). Performing a parallel test with TST did not enhance the sensitivity of T-SPOT.TB. Among the 11 RD patients with microbiologically confirmed ATB, T-SPOT.TB had a sensitivity of 100%, while TST had a sensitivity of 54.5% (6/11) (Table 2).

Discussion

This study, based on a fully matched case design, systematically compared the diagnostic efficacy of T-SPOT.TB and TST in diagnosing ATB in patients with RD. In RD patients with suspected active TB and compromised immune function, T-SPOT.TB demonstrated superior sensitivity, specificity, and diagnostic accuracy (AUC) compared to TST. This study provides valuable evidence for the immunological-based auxiliary diagnosis of ATB in RD patients, especially for optimizing the diagnostic approach in patients with immunoregulatory or immunosuppressive conditions.

TST induces a delayed-type hypersensitivity response (Type IV Hypersensitivity) through Mtb antigens, reflecting the host’s immunological memory to Mtb. However, its sensitivity and specificity are constrained by several factors. In RD patients undergoing immunosuppressive therapy, the delayed-type hypersensitivity response may be inadequate, leading to a significantly higher risk of false negatives with TST. Additionally, TST specificity is susceptible to interference from prior BCG vaccination and exposure to non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM), resulting in an increased false positive rate [22, 23]. In contrast, the IGRA detects the level of interferon-gamma released by T cells in response to specific antigens (ESAT-6 and CFP-10), offering greater specificity that is not affected by BCG vaccination or most NTM exposures [24, 25]. However, IGRA’s higher cost remains a limitation. While IGRA demonstrates stable performance in patients with mild to moderate immunosuppression, its sensitivity can be affected in patients receiving high-dose corticosteroids or those with severely impaired immune function, particularly in the screening of latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI) among RD patients [26–28]. A meta-analysis by Zhou et al. [29] confirmed that the decline in TST positivity rates among immunosuppressed populations was greater than that of IGRA (OR 0.36 [95% CI: 0.31–0.41] vs. 0.53 [95% CI: 0.46–0.61]). Additionally, a meta-analysis on the impact of immunosuppressive therapy on LTBI screening in autoimmune disease patients demonstrated that immunosuppressive treatment negatively impacts TB screening results [30]. The Peto odds ratios for TST, QuantiFERON-TB (QFT), and T-SPOT.TB were 0.51 (95% CI: 0.42–0.61), 0.65 (95% CI: 0.50–0.84), and 0.81 (95% CI: 0.59–1.10), respectively. Among these, the standardized method for washing peripheral blood mononuclear cells in T-SPOT.TB significantly enhanced its sensitivity, making it the most effective of the three tests. These findings are consistent with our results, further supporting T-SPOT.TB’s superior performance over TST in immunosuppressed populations.

This study included patients with RD based on the symptom screening criteria recommended by the WHO, such as fever, cough, night sweats, and unexplained weight loss. The results revealed that there were no significant differences in the incidence of fever and cough between patients with or without concurrent ATB, suggesting that traditional screening markers may lack sensitivity in patients with immune dysfunction. The immune dysregulation in RD patients may obscure or mimic typical infection-related symptoms, particularly in those undergoing immunosuppressive treatment. Moreover, fever and cough, as nonspecific inflammatory manifestations, could be influenced by the underlying activity of the RD itself and the ongoing treatment, further diminishing their diagnostic value. In contrast, night sweats and weight loss were significantly more prevalent in ATB patients, suggesting that these may serve as more important diagnostic red flags in RD patients. Rheumatic diseases often manifest as fever, malaise, and weight loss, which overlap with the clinical presentation of TB. This overlap contributes to the challenges of diagnosing ATB, resulting in potential misdiagnosis or delayed diagnosis. Guo et al. [26] found that in patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) complicated by TB, the most common symptoms were fever (83.3%), cough (69%), and weight loss (45.2%), while SLE with pulmonary TB typically presented with fever (92.7%), cough (63.4%), and weight loss (48.8%). Similarly, patients with systemic sclerosis (SS) and TB commonly experienced fever (68.2%), cough (54.5%), and malaise (40.9%). Fever was the most frequent symptom observed in RD patients, which contrasts with our findings, possibly due to differences in sample size and the study populations included. Liu et al. [31] previously observed that in a cohort of Behçet’s disease (BD) patients, those with ATB were more likely to exhibit systemic TB intoxication symptoms, including fever (85.7%), night sweats (38.1%), and unexplained weight loss (61.9%). These symptoms are likely related to the metabolic disturbances and systemic inflammatory responses caused by Mtb) infection. Overall, symptom screening in RD patients should prioritize more specific manifestations, such as night sweats and weight loss, to improve diagnostic accuracy.

Our study demonstrated that the diagnostic efficacy of T-SPOT.TB was significantly superior to that of TST (AUC 0.89 vs. 0.74). T-SPOT.TB exhibited a considerably higher accuracy in diagnosing ATB in RD patients, particularly in terms of sensitivity, which reached 88.6%, far exceeding TST’s sensitivity of 57.1%. This result aligns with findings from Wu et al. [32], who observed a similarly high diagnostic efficacy of T-SPOT.TB in BD patients, particularly with respect to its high sensitivity (88.9%) and negative predictive value (98.3%), indicating T-SPOT.TB’s significant value in excluding TB infection. Additionally, a meta-analysis by Dai Y. et al. [33] further confirmed this, showing that T-SPOT.TB exhibited a high sensitivity of 88% [95% CI: 86–91%] and specificity of 89% [95% CI: 86–92%], with an AUC close to 0.95, demonstrating its stable and excellent diagnostic performance across various studies.

In this study, the optimal cutoff value for T-SPOT.TB in RD patients was found to be 24 SFCs/10^6 PBMC, which is lower than the 70 SFCs/10^6 PBMC identified by Wu et al. [32]. This discrepancy may reflect the specific needs of populations with immune dysfunction. For TST, a cutoff of 5 mm aligns with the industry standard, particularly in patients receiving immunosuppressive therapy for more than one month, as this threshold effectively balances sensitivity and specificity. Moreover, in RD patients receiving moderate-to-high doses of glucocorticoid therapy, a 5 mm cutoff should be considered to avoid missed diagnoses. The consistency between T-SPOT.TB and TST was relatively low (kappa = 0.466), likely due to the lower sensitivity of TST. The parallel testing of both methods did not significantly enhance the sensitivity of T-SPOT.TB, suggesting that in RD patients, a strategy based on a single, highly effective test may be more suitable. Therefore, in resource-rich settings, T-SPOT.TB, with its higher sensitivity and specificity, is recommended as the preferred immunological diagnostic tool for ATB in RD patients. In areas with limited resources or where T-SPOT.TB testing is not available, TST remains a viable, cost-effective alternative. Additionally, both testing methods demonstrated that as the frequency of T-SPOT.TB spots and the induration diameter of TST increased, the likelihood of diagnosing ATB significantly increased. This feature provides an important clinical insight: when the test results are strongly positive, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for ATB and pursue further evaluation using additional diagnostic tools.

Several studies from high TB burden countries have reported similar challenges in diagnosing active TB among immunocompromised populations. For instance, in India and Peru, where latent TB infection is highly prevalent, both TST and IGRA have shown limited sensitivity for ATB diagnosis in patients with immune-mediated diseases [34, 35]. In Brazil, IGRA is selectively used due to its cost, and its clinical value is still debated. These international findings are consistent with our observation that while T-SPOT.TB offers improved sensitivity over TST, it cannot replace microbiological confirmation [36, 37]. Our study adds to this growing body of evidence by providing performance data from a large Chinese RD cohort using both TST and IGRA.

This study does have several limitations: (1) The proportion of pathogen-diagnosed cases was low, though this reflects the actual clinical situation, and the diagnosis was made as accurately as possible. (2) The study primarily included patients with diffuse connective tissue diseases, especially SLE, which may introduce selection bias. (3) While the study enrolled RD patients with suspected TB symptoms, some RD patients with ATB exhibited atypical or even asymptomatic clinical presentations, and further research is needed to determine whether these results are applicable to such cases. 4)Another limitation is that we did not account for individual BCG vaccination history, which may have influenced TST results. However, BCG coverage is nearly universal in China, making stratification impractical in this cohort. Furthermore, it is possible that some patients in the non-ATB group had latent TB infection (LTBI) or subclinical disease that was not detected at the time of study enrollment. Since TST and IGRA do not differentiate latent from active infection, this could have contributed to false-positive results in the non-ATB group and may have affected the specificity estimates of the tests. HIV testing was not performed systematically across all study sites. While no enrolled patients were known to be HIV-positive at the time of inclusion, undiagnosed HIV infection could have influenced immune responses to TST and IGRA, potentially affecting sensitivity estimates. In addition, although Chinese guidelines permit the auxiliary use of TST and IGRA in certain clinical contexts, these tests have limited sensitivity, particularly in immunosuppressed individuals. This concern is echoed by the UK PREDICT TB study, which reported that nearly one-third of TB progressors were negative on both tests. Moreover, WHO guidelines do not support their use for ATB diagnosis, highlighting the need for improved non-sputum-based diagnostics. Future studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to explore how different RD subtypes (e.g., SLE, RA, vasculitis) and varying degrees of immunosuppression influence the diagnostic performance of TST and T-SPOT.TB.

In clinical settings where microbiological confirmation is unavailable or infeasible, particularly in extrapulmonary TB or immunosuppressed populations, T-SPOT.TB may serve as a valuable adjunct to guide treatment decisions.

In conclusion, for RD patients presenting with suspected ATB symptoms, such as fever, cough, night sweats, or unexplained weight loss, both T-SPOT.TB and TST offer valuable diagnostic support. T-SPOT.TB demonstrates superior accuracy, particularly with its higher sensitivity. The likelihood of ATB should be heightened as the T-SPOT.TB spot count increases or the TST induration diameter enlarges, emphasizing the need for further investigation in such cases.
