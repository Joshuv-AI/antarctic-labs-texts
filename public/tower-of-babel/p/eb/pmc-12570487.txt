Factors affecting survival in patients with colorectal cancer: an umbrella review
Authors: Lulin Yu, Jing Yuan, Simin Lou, Anmin Huang, Xinyu Cai, Jingmin Ji, Xinyang Dai, Xudong Mao, Yingying Mao, Leitao Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the strength and validity of the evidence for reported associations between multiple factors and colorectal cancer (CRC) survival.MethodsWe searched PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews from inception to 20 June 2024 for systematic reviews and meta-analyses of observational studies on factors and CRC prognosis. The quality of the methodology was evaluated using A Measurement Tool to Assess systematic Reviews 2 (AMSTAR-2).Results90 meta-analyses investigated 191 associations (123 main evidence and 68 supplementary evidence) with CRC overall survival (OS). Factors were categorized into: demographic characteristic, perioperative condition, pathology of neoplasia, clinical laboratory indicators, comorbidities, behavior patterns and others. Totally, 6 associations were recommended as strong evidence (class I). While the presence of chemotherapy-induced neutropenia (CIN) (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.40, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.32–0.51) denoted a better OS outcome, the existence of log odds of positive lymph nodes (LODDS) (HR = 3.49, 95% CI 2.88–4.23), liver cirrhosis (HR = 3.10, 95% CI 2.68–3.59) and the increment of metastatic lymph node ratio (mLNR) (HR = 1.62, 95% CI 1.39–1.88), pretreatment neutrophils-lymphocytes ratio (NLR) (HR = 2.17, 95% CI 1.83–2.58) and pretreatment lymph node ratio (LNR) (HR = 3.13, 95% CI 2.36–4.15) were considered as poor predictors of OS. Notably, 2 strong evidence was also found in supplementary evidence including extranodal tumor deposits (ENTDs) (HR = 1.72, 95% CI 1.46–2.04) and absolute monocyte count (AMC) (HR = 1.83, 95% CI 1.47–2.29).ConclusionBeyond high-risk pathological signs of lymph node status and ENTDs, optimizable and cost-effective laboratory indicators, such as CIN, NLR, LNR and AMC, may effectively predict the prognosis of CRC. Furthermore, our study provides valuable insights for stratified CRC patient management, reinforcing the development of evidence-based prognostic survival strategies and recommendations.Trial registrationThis protocol was registered with PROSPERO under CRD42024560003.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-06876-7.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Risk factor, Protective factor, Overall survival, Umbrella review

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the second leading cause of cancer-related death in the United States, with 8% incidence rate and 9% mortality rate [1, 2]. Alarmingly, around 20–25% of CRC patients have metastasized at initial diagnosis [3], progressing to stage IV where the five-year survival rate is as low as less than 14%. Despite therapeutic advances, including cytotoxic chemotherapy, targeted therapies, and laparoscopic surgery [4–6], long-term survival remains unsatisfactory.

Given the high mortality rate of CRC, improved understanding of disease etiology and progression is necessary to identify potential contributors of mortality and guide intervention. Previous observational studies [7, 8] have examined the effects of diverse factors on CRC survival in a variety of target populations and settings, incorporating obesity [9], treatment [10, 11], physical activity [12, 13], and clinical examinations [14]. However, few factors have been acknowledged with consistency. While some studies associate adiposity with prolonged survival [15, 16], Parkin’s and Simillis’s studies report insufficient evidences for this link [17, 18]. Similarly, when clinical trials consistently report a significant improvement in survival among CRC patients undergoing perioperative treatment [10, 11], Japanese JCOG 0603 trial showed no significant OS benefits [19].

Umbrella reviews allow for analysis of broad topic areas, and examine the strength and confidence of these associations using the results of published systematic reviews and meta-analyses, thus determine which associations are supported by strong evidence [20]. To our knowledge, prior umbrella reviews mainly focused on the associations between specific factors and CRC incidence, suggesting that more coffee intake [21] and physical activity [22] were recommended as protective factors, whereas elevated platelets [14] and some comorbidity, like type 2 diabetes mellitus [23], may increase the risk. These reviews often overlook prognostic factors of CRC survival, including pathological characteristics, perioperative clinical variables, laboratory test indicators, comorbidities, etc.

Therefore, the aim of this integrative review is to identify and evaluate various factors reported in meta-analyses of observational studies that influence the prognosis of CRC, gaining a better understanding of the mechanisms in CRC survival (Fig. 1).

Methods

The study protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024560003). Following a standardized methodology [24], we reported findings in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) and Meta-analysis Of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE) guidance [25, 26].

Literature search

Two investigators (LLY and JY) independently searched PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews for papers published between database inception to June 2024. Search terms included: (colorectal OR colonic OR colon OR rectal) AND (cancer OR carcinoma OR tumor OR adenomas OR neoplas* OR maligna*) AND (meta-analysis OR systematic review). We manually searched the bibliography of the selected reviews for eligible studies. Disagreements were resolved via discussion among LLY, JY and JMJ. The selection process is detailed in S1 Figure.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The PICOS criteria for this review were as follows:

Population- (1) adults with CRC of any age; (2) CRC here included colon cancer (CC), rectal cancer (RC), and all metastatic cancers originating from the colorectum; (3) not confined to patients who have undergone surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, in specific stage or have specific gene mutations;

Exposure- (1) at least one factors from observational studies investigating the prognosis of CRC patients; (2) factors were defined as any attribute, characteristic, or exposure of an individual that may influence the survival outcome of CRC patients;

Primary Outcome- (1) provided overall survival (OS), which was defined as the period without limitation (e.g., the time of diagnosis, treatment, grouping) to death from any cause; (2) provided sample size and hazard risk (HR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) in a form of forest plot;

Study Design- 1) observational studies (e.g., cohort, case-control, cross-sectional studies).

Studies were excluded when met the following criteria: (1) as we only included observational studies, any factors regarding medical and surgical treatment would be excluded to maintain the credibility of this umbrella review; (2) solely explored the relationship between factors and the incidence or prevalence of CRC; (3) investigated the association between factors and precancerous colorectal lesions; (4) data specific to CRC could not be extracted directly in articles that reporting multiple cancers without clarifying whether there was a shared feature. We also excluded non-systematic reviews and/or meta-analysis articles (narrative review, commentaries, umbrella review, and primary articles), editorials or letters, conference abstracts and grey literature.

When more than one meta-analysis existed for an identical association in the same population, we included the most recent meta-analysis with the largest number of studies.

Data extraction

From each article, two investigators (JY and XYD) recorded the following data when available: (1) study characteristics included first authors of the meta-analysis and the original research articles included in it, publication year, study designs, factors, comparisons, number of included studies; (2) patient characteristics included number of CRC patients (and death events when available) in each study included in a meta-analysis, any treatment if mentioned, clinicopathological features of tumor (e.g., sites, stage, and metastasis); and (3) outcome measures included metrics used for analyses, maximally adjusted individual estimate size (e.g., HRs for OS or 5-year OS), as well as their 95% CIs.

Data synthesis

To ensure a complete and comprehensive investigation, all primary outcomes along with the subgroup data from each eligible meta-analysis, would be included and extracted first. Further data synthesis was then conducted to identify main evidence and supplementary evidence. Main evidence criteria were as follow:1) if the subgroup analysis in a meta-analysis was conducted based on different features of population, the one with the largest sample size was viewed as the main evidence; 2) if the factor was sampled at a specific time (e.g. pretreatment), the same kind of factor with no limitation of sample time (if available) would be chosen as the main evidence; 3) when both the factor and the population from different associations were identical, the most recent one with the largest samples would be selected as the main evidence; 4) if an inclusive relationship of population group existed in different associations which investigated the same factor, the one with broader population coverage was designated as the main evidence. The remaining associations that failed to meet the criteria aforementioned would be considered as supplementary evidence correspondingly, possessing more specific stratification characteristics.

Data analysis

Estimation of summary effect—For each exposure-population pair, we recalculated and adjusted the summary effect and corresponding 95% CI by using both the random effects model (REM) and fixed effects model (FEM) [24].

Assessment of heterogeneity—The heterogeneity between original studies was assessed using the I2 value [27, 28], along with respective 95% CIs, and 95% prediction intervals (PIs) [29]. The latter not only reflected heterogeneity between studies, but also represented the range in which a future study may lie [29].

Assessment of bias—Bias in each association was evaluated by small study effects [23] and excess significance tests [30]. Small study effects could be indicative of the publication bias, and was assessed by the regression asymmetry test proposed by Egger. We considered the presence of small study effects when the Egger’s test p ≤ 0.10 [31, 32]. Excess significance bias was assessed by comparing the observed number of studies (O) in the published literature that reported nominally statistically significant results (p < 0.05) with the expected number of statistically significant studies (E) [30]. The difference between O and E was assessed using a two-sided binomial test [33, 34]. The power calculations of each original study assumed the plausible effect size as the largest study in each meta-analysis [35]. A threshold of p ≤ 0.10 was set to indicate statistical evidence of excess significance in individual meta-analysis [32].

Assessment of credibility ceilings—Credibility ceilings was implemented as a sensitivity analysis tool to address the spurious precision of combined effect estimates due to potential methodological limitations of observational studies [36]. The approach indicated that for each observational study, there exists a probability c (credibility ceiling), that the true effect size is in the opposite direction to that indicated by the study’s point estimate. In this review, we systematically re-estimated the pooled effect sizes and its heterogeneity using 10% c values, testing the stability of the conclusions under 10% skepticism toward individual study [36, 37].

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata V.16.0 and R version 4.4.1.

Quality assessment

To evaluate the methodological quality of the included systematic reviews, the AMSTAR-2 [40] (A Measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews 2) tool was used. It is comprised of 16 items, each of which can be scored one point according to the quality of the meta-analysis. Based on the overall score, we then considered the results of high (H), moderate (M), low (L) or critically low (CL). Assessment was mainly focused on 7 domains including possession of a predefined protocol, conducting a thorough literature search, providing a list of risk of bias (RoB) assessment, performing the review with appropriate methods, accounting for potential RoB, carrying out publication bias. Quality assessment was independently conducted by two individual investigators (LLY and JY). Any discrepancy was resolved by consensus of two investigators and the third reviewer (JMJ). The process of quality assessment could be seen in S2 Figure.

Results

Characteristics of meta-analyses

Overall, 90 eligible meta-analyses encompassing 191 associations were identified (Fig. 2). 123 main associations were evaluated, including demographic characteristics (n = 2, 2%), perioperative conditions (n = 24, 20%), pathologies of neoplasia (n = 29, 40%), clinical laboratory indicators (n = 40, 33%), comorbid (n = 15, 12%), behavior patterns (n = 1, 1%), and other factors (n = 12, 10%) (S3 Fig). Additionally, 68 associations were included as supplementary evidences to enhance the existing results (S3-S4 Tables). Study population comprised: CRC (n = 63, 51%), RC (n = 11, 9%), CC (n = 2, 2%), CRC with liver metastasis (CRLM) (n = 11, 9%), CRC with ovarian metastasis (CROM) (n = 9, 7%), CRC with peritoneal metastasis (CRPM) (n = 8, 7%), metastatic CRC (mCRC) (n = 12, 10%), non-metastatic CRC (non-mCRC) (n = 7, 6%) (S4 Fig). Comprehensive descriptions of each meta-analysis were demonstrated in S2 Table.

Articles retrieved and assessed for eligibility

Quality assessment

All 90 articles were assessed using AMSTAR-2 tool, with 2 (2%) ranked high, 17 (19%) ranked moderate, 48 (53%) ranked low and 23 (26%) ranked critically low (S5 Fig). As for the 7 AMSTAR-2 critical domains, 24 (27%) conducted a protocol, 89 (99%) performed a comprehensive literature search, 76 (84%) provided a list of excluded studies with justification, 87 (97%) used a proper technique for assessing the risk of bias (RoB), 90 (100%) performed the review with appropriate methods, 89 (99%) account for potential RoB in the discussion, and 70 (78%) carried out publication bias. Judgment on each domain for each meta-analysis was available in S7 Table.

Summary effect size

All estimated summary effect sizes were conducted in both random and fixed effect estimates, showing in S3 Table. When adopting a threshold of p < 0.05 for statistical significance, 107 of the 123 main associations (87%) were significant with random effect models. At a threshold of p < 10− 3, 75 of the 123 associations (61%) remained significant while of 10− 6, 48 (39%) remained.

The estimates derived from largest studies tended to exhibit more conservative than that from random effect estimates, with 95 (77% of total 123 main associations) associations yielding a HR greater than the point of largest study (Fig. 3A). The magnitude of the observed summary random effect estimates mostly ranged from 0.40 to 3.61 (Fig. 3B). Demographic characteristic

Any attributes of CRC patients (age, gender, anthropometric indices) would be defined as demographic characteristics, kind of stable characteristics that may influence the prognostic of CRC survivals. However, in this review, only 2 factors were included in the final analysis.

In total of 2 (2% of 123 main associations) factors, age was considered as weak evidence with only marginal effect in predicting prognosis of CRLM patients (S3-S4 Tables. and S8 Fig.). And no association with postmenopause condition and CROM patients were found in present study (S3-S4 Tables and S9 Fig). Another 2 evidences, also investigating age based on different population and cut-off, were included as supplementary evidences, showing suggestive evidence and no significance respectively (Fig. 6 and S3-4 Tables).

Perioperative condition

Factors that were explicitly identified as perioperative (including preoperative, intraoperative and postoperative) ones in meta-analyses were incorporated in this category.

17 (19% of total 90 articles) meta-analyses generated 28 (2% of total 192 associations) associations with 24 (20% of 123 main associations) main evidences and another 4 supportive evidences (Fig. 6).

Except for RDW-CV demonstrated no significance with the prognosis of CRC patients, 23 of 24 (96%) factors showed significant associations (p < 0.05). Though no strong evidence was detected, 8 (30%) associations were ranked as highly suggestive evidence, including preoperative sarcopenia in RC patients with resection (HR = 2.07, 95% CI 1.67–2.57), preoperative sarcopenia in CRC patients with resection (HR = 1.83, 95% CI 1.57–2.14), pre and postoperative carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) in CRLM patients with hepatectomy (HR = 1.62, 95% CI 1.45–1.82; HR = 2.66, 95% CI 2.10–3.38, respectively), preoperative neutrophils-lymphocytes ratio (NLR) in CRC patients with surgery (HR = 1.66, 95% CI 1.36–2.02), preoperative lymphocyte monocyte ratio (LMR) in CRC patients with surgery (HR = 0.62, 95% CI 0.53–0.72), preoperative platelet-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) in CRC patients with surgery (HR = 1.53, 95% CI 1.31–1.78), and prognostic nutritional index (PNI) in CRC with surgery (HR = 1.87, 95% CI 1.45–2.42) (Fig. 5). Among them, the enhancement of PNI and preoperative LMR emerged as protective factors for patients with surgery while other 7 factors were considered as risk factors.

High CRP and GPS sampled after operation in CRC patients were significantly associated with poor OS outcome and both of them were graded as suggestive evidences. Weak evidence was observed in the remaining 13 factors (54%) with only marginal effect in predicting prognosis of CRC (S3-S4 Tables and S7-S9 Figs).

Pathology of neoplasia

Any characteristics (such as location, stage, histology, etc.) pertaining to both primary and metastatic tumors will be encompassed in this category.

From 17 (19% of total 90 articles) eligible articles, 29 (24% of 123 main associations) associations were extracted, followed with another 6 supplementary evidences (Fig. 6 and S3-S4 Tables).

Strong evidence was presented in 2 meta-analyses including log odds of positive lymph nodes (LODDS) (HR 3.49, 95% CI 2.88–4.23) and metastatic lymph node ratio (mLNR) (HR 1.62, 95% CI 1.39–1.88), both indicating an increment of risk for poor prognosis in CRC patients (Fig. 4). 8 (28%) evidences were classified as highly suggestive, including primary tumor site in KRAS-wild CRLM patients with hepatectomy (HR 0.71, 95% CI 0.62–0.83), mCRC treated with palliative strategy in 1-line (HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.59–0.74) and CC patients (HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.79–0.85), surgical margin in CRLM patients with hepatectomy (HR 1.65, 95% CI 1.46–1.87), perineural invasion in CRC patients (HR 2.03, 95% 1,79 − 2.30), tumor budding (TB) in mCRC patients (HR 1.60, 95% 1.33–1.93), extra-nodal tumor deposits (ENTDs) in CRC patients (HR 1.98, 95% CI 1.57–2.51) and MRI-detected extramural venous invasion (mrENTDs) in RC patients after neoadjuvant treatment (HR 1.90, 95% CI 1.49–2.42) (Fig. 5).

Suggestive evidence was found in 5 (17%) associations including primary tumor site in CRLM patients treated with hepatectomy or ablation for both OS and 5-year OS outcome, completeness of cytoreduction (CC) score in CRPM patients treated with cytoreductive surgery (CRS) plus hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC), LODDS 1 in CRC patients and baseline mENTDs in RC patients. 10 (34%) and another 4 (14%) associations were classified as weak evidence and no significance respectively in the present study. (S3-S4 Tables and S7-S9 Figs).

12 associations investigating primary tumor site in CRLM, CC, CRPM, mCRC patients (8 for main evidences and 4 for supplementary ones) generated totally 5 highly suggestive, 2 suggestive, 2 weak and 3 no significance (Fig. 6). Results of each supplementary evidence were consistent with that of the main evidence respectively, revealing that right side primary tumor may act as an ideal indicator of poor prognosis in the patients aforementioned. However, when it comes to KRAS-mutated patients, no evidence was found with primary tumor site.

Notably, when the presence of ENTDs was regarded as highly suggestive evidence (Figs. 4 and 6), indicating a poor outcome in CRC patients, its subgroup was graded as strong evidence in CRC patients with positive lymph nodes (pLN) (HR 1.72, 95% CI 1.46–2.04). Additional information was presented in Fig. 4 and S3-S4 Tables.

Clinical laboratory indicators

All laboratory test indicators, particularly those related to blood workups, would be categorized in this section.

About one third of the associations (n = 40, 33% in 123 main associations) from 31 (34% of total 90 articles) meta-analyses were classified in clinical indicators, yielding 3 (8%) strong evidences, 14 evidences for highly suggestive (35%) and suggestive (35%) respectively, 6 (15%) weak evidences and 3 (8%) for no significance.

Among 3 strong evidences, elevated pretreatment NLR in CRLM patients (HR 2.17, 95% CI 1.83–2.58) and positive lymph node ratio (LNR) in RC patients with neoadjuvant therapy (HR 3.13, 95% CI 2.36–4.15) were correlated with poor outcome while the presence of chemotherapy-induced neutropenia (CIN) (HR 0.40, 95% CI 0.32–0.51) predicted a prolonged outcome for mCRC patients with chemotherapy (Fig. 4).

As for 14 highly suggestive evidences, the elevated of LMR in CRC and mCRC patients (HR 1.57, 95% CI 1.30–1.90; HR 0.60, 95% CI 0.51–0.70, respectively), 25(OH)D (HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.61–0.78), lymphocyte C-reactive protein ratio (LCR) (HR 0.44, 95% CI 0.36–0.55), albumin-to-globulin ratio (AGR) (HR 2.58, 95% CI 1.90–3.51) were associated with decreased risk. And the increment of pretreatment NLR (HR 1.92, 95% CI 1.58–2.32), PLR (HR 1.59, 95% CI 1.32–1.91), pretreatment C-reactive protein to albumin ratio (CAR) (HR 2.03, 95% CI 1.81–2.27), pretreatment fibrinogen level (HR 1.51, 95% CI 1.28–1.78), absolute monocyte count (AMC) (HR 1.71, 95% CI 1.48–1.97), baseline C-reactive protein (CRP) (HR 1.88, 95% CI 1.55–2.29), D-dimer (HR 2.17, 95% CI 1.67–2.81) and the presence of circulating tumor cells (CTC) conversion (HR 3.61, 95% CI 2.10–6.20) and CTCs (HR 2.86, 95% CI 1.96–4.17), symbolized terrible prognosis (Fig. 5).

Remaining 23 main evidence, ranging from suggestive to no significance, was presented in S3-S4 Tables and S7-S9 Figs.

Another 39 supplementary materials were also extracted, and all of them drew the same conclusion with main evidences (Fig. 6). It is also worth noting that a subgroup of AMC (HR 1.83, 95% CI 1.47–2.29) was graded as strong evidences as demonstrated in Fig. 4 while its main evidence not.

Comorbid

Diseases detected either at the time of CRC diagnosis or during perioperative period would be classified as comorbidities.

About 12 related-diseases from 14 (16% of total 90 articles) meta-analyses were categorized in this part, generating 15 (12% of 123 main associations) main comparisons and another 5 supplementary associations with consistent conclusions.

Above these, only liver cirrhosis (HR 3.10, 95% CI 2.68–3.59) was examined as strong evidence, denoting a poor OS outcome of CRC patients (Fig. 4).

Highly suggestive evidence was observed in 5 (33%) meta-analyses and all of them indicated poor outcome, including sarcopenia in RC patients (HR 2.37, 95% CI 1.61–3.49), myosteatosis in resected CRC patients (HR 1.89, 95% CI 1.64–2.19), inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) in CRC patients (HR 1.29, 95% CI 1.17–1.42), surgical-site infection and anastomotic leak (AL) in resected CRC patients (HR 1.59, 95% CI 1.31–1.94; HR 1.57, 95% CI 1.39–1.77, respectively) (Fig. 5).

As for 3 (20%) suggestive evidences, except for concomitant hypertension (HTN) serving as a protective indicator for mCRC patients, postoperative complications (POCs) and sarcopenia for CRC patients were recommended as potential risk indicators (S3-S4 Tables and S7 Fig). 1 (7%) weak evidence and 5 (33%) evidences without significance were both demonstrated in S3-S4 Tables and S8-S9 Figs.

Behavior patterns

Various life modalities, incorporating exercise routines and dietary intake would be categorized in this section. In this review, only 1 (1% of total 123 associations) factor was identified and no association was found between supplementary Vitamin D intake and OS outcome (S3-S4 Tables and S9 Fig).

Others

Factors that did not fit into the categories aforementioned would be included in this section.

To sum up, 12 (10% of 123 main associations) main evidences from 7 (8% of total 90 articles) meta-analyses, yielded totally 7 (58%) highly suggestive evidences, 3 (25%) suggestive evidences, 1 (8%) weak evidence and 1 (8%) no significance.

In this category, 9 (75%) of main associations were related with clinical index, such as Glasgow prognostic score/modified Glasgow prognostic score (GPS/mGPS) (n = 6), controlling nutritional status (CONUT) score (n = 1), geriatric nutritional index (GNRI) (n = 1) and peritoneal carcinoma index (PCI) (n = 1). All of the 6 associations regarding GPS/mGPS were recommended as highly suggestive evidence, including pretreatment mGPS in CRC patients with chemotherapy or surgery (HR 2.23, 95% CI 1.79–2.78), pretreatment GPS in resected CRC patients (HR 2.3, 95% CI 1.90–2.79), GPS/mGPS in resected CRC patients (HR 2.14, 95% CI 1.71–2.67), GPS/mGPS in CRC patients with chemotherapy (HR 2.68, 95% CI 2.04–3.52), GPS/mGPS in Asian or non-Asian CRC patients (HR 2.68, 95% CI 2.04–3.52; HR 1.65, 95% CI 1.37–1.98, respectively) and elevated scores tended to correlate with poor prognosis (Fig. 5). Regarding other indexes, except for high GNRI predicting promising outcome in resected CRC patients as highly suggestive evidence (Fig. 5), high PCI in CRPM patients and CONUT score in CRC patients were regarded as suggestive evidence, representing horrible outcome (S3-S4 Tables and S7 Fig). Remaining 5 associations were presented in S3-S4 Tables and S7-S9 Figs.

In line with present results, 12 subgroups of GPS, extracted as supplementary evidences, were also ranked as highly suggestive evidence. More differences on population level could be seen in Fig. 6 and S3-4 Tables.

Discussion

Principal findings

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first umbrella review which systematically synthesized and comprehensively appraises the epidemiological evidence for the association between factors and CRC survival. We evaluated a wide variety of demographic characteristics, perioperative conditions, pathology of neoplasia, clinical laboratory indicators, comorbid, behavior patterns as well as other factors. This study totally identified 8 factors with strong (class I) evidence: despite that CIN conferred a significant survival advantage, LODDS, ENTDs, liver cirrhosis, mLNR, elevated AMC, increased pretreatment NLR and LNR predicted poorer OS outcomes. Notably, among 42 highly suggestive evidence, 14 (33%) were linked with clinical indicators. This suggested certain cost-effective measurements—including predictors for poorer OS (e.g., elevated NLR, PLR, CAR, fibrinogen, D-dimer, CRP, and CTC presence/conversion), and indicators for better OS (higher LMR, LCR, AGR, and 25 [OH] D)—may serve as optimizable predictors for specific patient populations.

Based on the criteria aforementioned (Table 1), most of the main associations (87%) showed nominally significant results (p < 0.05). However, only 5% of these associations were classified as strong evidence. Moreover, a considerable portion (34%) were graded as highly suggestive evidence, due to substantial heterogeneity (n = 27, 69%), hint of biases encompassing small study effects (n = 22, 56%) and excess significance (n = 22, 56%). Observed heterogeneity and hints of biases in highly suggestive evidence mainly attributed to limited stratification in exposure or population, insufficient sample size, and quality of original studies. These meta-analyses supported that the presence of CIN denoted a better OS outcome while the existence of LODDS2, liver cirrhosis and the increment of mLNR, pretreatment NLR and LNR may predict a poor OS. Of note, in the supplementary evidence, we also found some strong evidence: ENTDs in CRC patients with pLN, and AMC in CRC patients with surgery. Beyond that, some of the examined associations were either statistically insignificant (13%), or supported by weak evidence (26%). The presence of these low-validity evidence would not influence the main results we have found, due to different population stratification. However, cautions should be taken when interpreting them. Since most of the low-validity evidence were with a limited sample size and original articles, the bias and confounding were inevitable, which may undermine the significance of these factors. Thus, it underscored the necessity for future researchers to undertake studies with a larger sample size and more rigorous methodologies.

Evidence rationality

Lymph node positivity is recognized as compelling evidence of a pathological factor. As proposed by William Halsted more than a century ago, the natural progression of most solid cancers initially involves advancement and dissemination through the metastasis to lymph nodes, with lymph node positivity serving as a critical milestone in malignant progression. Clinical data from definitive studies further substantiated this, revealing that selective lymph node ablation can improve overall, local disease-free, or disease-free survival rates in various solid malignancies [41, 42]. Lymph nodes serve as critical immune organs, particularly in initiating effective antitumor immunity and responding to immunotherapy. Recent studies have delved into the mechanisms underlying anti-tumor immunity and tumor metastasis pathways, giving rise to an emerging research focus - the indispensability of tumor-draining lymph nodes in immunotherapy [43, 44]. However, regular ablation will inevitably interrupt the drainage pathway. This contradiction also reminds us of the valuable case study on how to effectively administer immunotherapy in cases of lymph node positivity. Indeed, the constraints inherent in observational studies limit our ability to establish a causal link between “lymph node positivity” and the “death” outcome in CRC. Therefore, it may be more practical to integrate the optimal lymph node treatment strategies with auxiliary diagnostic examinations. Ultimately, a more comprehensive mechanistic underlying lymph node biology will provide more instrumental information in CRC prognosis.

In reviewing current evidence, 40 (33%) main associations involved clinical laboratory indicators, with 2 classified as strong evidence and 14 were recommended as highly suggestive evidence. As routinely available and cost-effective biomarkers, clinical indicators are extensively studied across diverse pathologies. Beyond oncology, they demonstrate significant predictive value in disease progression—evidenced by elevated pro-BNP predicting heart failure progression [45], increased galectin-3 correlating with gestational diabetes risk [46], and elevated NLR forecasting poor glycemic control in type 2 diabetes mellitus [47]—supporting their potential utility as independent prognostic tools. In the study by Uri et al. [48], blood-based biomarkers were identified as effective in boosting participation in screening programs, leading to a significant reduction of 52% in CRC mortality when compared to no screening. Among patients with mCRC undergoing first-line chemotherapy at Sanremo Hospital, researchers found that only four variables were able to independently predict survival rates. Among them, absolute leukocyte reduction (CIL-1) predicts better OS for patients with metachronous metastases, whereas relative leukocyte reduction may have prognostic significance in patients with synchronous metastases undergoing primary tumor resection [49]. These studies have contributed substantially to the understanding of the outcomes and opportunities for universal blood-based detection methods for CRC, highlighting the necessity of developing truly universal and cost-effective integrated approaches. These biomarkers also intersect with a significant related topic—inflammation. Beyond white blood cells, NLR and CRP are also closely related to systemic inflammation [50, 51]. The tumor microenvironment produces pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1, -6, -8, -17, -23, TNF-α) and chemokines (CCL2, CXCL8, CCL11), which attract immune cells such as macrophages, neutrophils, dendritic cells, T and B lymphocytes, and natural killer cells. Leukocytes may make up 50% of the total tumor mass [52, 53], and more neutrophils are involved in remodeling the extracellular matrix, which promotes angiogenesis and tumor development [54, 55]. Notably, two associations involving CTCs in patients with chemotherapy were identified as highly suggestive risk factors. CTC is considered to be the precursors of tumor metastasis [56]. As reported by Wei [57], the interplay between CTC and tumor associated macrophages (TAMs) could accelerate metastatic progression: CTC in blood may release CCL2, recruiting peripheral monocytes and driving their differentiation into TAMs. Concurrently, CTC-secreted factors polarize neighboring TAMs toward pro-tumorigenic phenotypes, thereby facilitating tumor invasion and metastasis. Throughout this cascade, macrophages demonstrate functional plasticity in CRC—polarizing into pro- or anti-inflammatory phenotypes. This dichotomy mirrors macrophage behavior in inflammatory conditions like graft-versus-host disease [58], where M1 dominance mediates acute tissue damage while M2 polarization propagates chronic fibrosis. Interestingly, our study revealed divergent prognostic values among systemic inflammatory biomarkers: elevated LMR and LCR were associated with improved survival outcome, whereas increased NLR, PLR, AMC and CRP correlated with poorer OS. This parallels findings in allergic inflammation, where serum IgE and eosinophil counts—though both clinically useful—exhibit no significant correlation despite their shared role as allergy biomarkers [59]. Such dissociations underscored that biomarkers within the same pathological pathway might operate through independent mechanisms, necessitating context-specific interpretation rather than presuming universal covariation.

Liver cirrhosis stands out as the only strong evidence in comorbid category. A recent meta-analysis [60] demonstrated higher incidence of complications and reduced survival rate after CRC surgery in CRC patients with liver cirrhosis compared to those without liver cirrhosis. Consistently, Charles and colleagues [61] reported the same results. As a chronic pathological condition, liver cirrhosis may not only affect the progression of cancer, but also pose a serious complication in cancer-related diseases. However, liver cirrhosis does not affect the availability or tolerance of postoperative chemotherapy. Above two studies support the screening of liver cirrhosis in patients with CRC, suggesting that it should not be regarded as a contraindication for radical treatment for CRC patients.

Practical clinical applications

Current findings provided novel insights into an integrated CRC management framework. It highlighted significant clinical utility in muti-dimensional assessment, encompassing clinical indexes, perioperative conditions, tumor pathology, and nutritional status.

Clinical indicators, especially those linked with systematic inflammatory, functioned as cost-effective biomarkers. A range of readily available clinical indicators, including NLR, PLR, CAR, AMC, CRP, as well as GPS (a composite index), supported the association between systematic inflammation and poor prognosis. These metrics provided clinicians with straightforward and cost-effective measurements to evaluate inflammatory condition and predict survival.

Moreover, perioperative status was also crucial for prognostic assessment in surgical patients, enabling identification of high-risk patients to guide more intensive adjuvant therapy and closer postoperative surveillance. Our evidence indicated that preoperative sarcopenia, elevated NLR, PLR, and pre- and postoperative CEA were all potential predictors of poor survival outcomes.

Ten highly suggestive evidence highlighted the significance of tumor pathology in predicting CRC prognosis. Our study supported that recognized pathological—perineural invasion, TB, ENTD, and mrEMVI—were associated with reduced survival outcomes. Beyond that, current evidence identified LODDS, mLNR, R1 surgical margin as key indicators linked to poor prognosis, indicating inadequate radical resection or lymph node assessment. This highlighted the necessity to achieve high-quality R0 resections with adequate lymph node dissection and meticulously evaluate status of lymph node in clinical settings.

Nutritional status, as a modifiable factor, represented an important independent prognostic factor. Two highly suggestive evidences demonstrated that higher GNRI and preoperative PNI were associated with improved OS, highlighting the significance of systematically assessing and improving nutritional status in CRC patients. Ensuring sufficient nutritional support is fundamental not only for maintaining physiological function but also as an effective intervention for enhancing long-term survival outcomes. Consequently, integrating PNI and GNRI into routine preoperative assessment is recommended to identify patients at nutritional risk and implement individualized nutritional intervention plans.

Strengths and weaknesses

Umbrella reviews have become an important tool for integrating and synthesizing evidence across the research field [62]. Our review is the most detailed and comprehensive attempt to date, providing a holistic evidence map and quantitative assessment of all non-treatment factors influencing the prognosis of CRC. While prior studies have focused on the identification and quantification of risk factors in incidence [63–68], this study specifically explores risk factors for survival.

Our research findings are consistent with existing evidence, reinforcing the robustness and credibility of multiple associations revealed in previous studies, involving factors such as neutropenia [69, 70], NLR [71], CRP level [72], and platelet status [73]. By integrating updated evidence, our review underscores the significance of these identified factors and expands their applicability in the field [74, 75]. For instance, while previous extensive research established a close link between obesity and poor prognosis [76], our analysis found that obesity failed to exhibit statistically significant meaning in predicting the prognosis of CRC patients. While sarcopenia has garnered moderate evidence for indicating a poor OS in CRC, our study presents varying degrees of evidence: for RC patients, sarcopenia is highly suggestive of a relationship with poor OS, while in patients with CRC, suggestive evidence exists. Though Meghan and her colleagues conducted a previous umbrella review on NLR and cancer prognosis, based on literature published up to May 29, 2020. Our umbrella review broadened the categories of CRC populations to include CC, RC, perioperative CRC, CRC patients undergoing radiotherapy and chemotherapy, mCRC, among others, reaching a total of 8 distinct groups. Indeed, we have identified high-level evidence of NLR’s prognostic significance in patients with CRLM. In addition to the population, this review also collected information on clinical variables, diseases and treatments, including refinement of treatment periods (e.g., preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative staging), patient-specific characteristics (e.g., lymph node infiltration, pathological invasion, gene mutations, etc.), treatment options (e.g., whether surgery, radiotherapy and chemotherapy status, etc.) and geographical factors, which are often overlooked in previous reviews. Analyzing these differences significantly enhances our understanding of CRC prognosis.

Our studies also have limitations. Firstly, cautions should be taken when interpreting the results of umbrella review. As a tertiary investigation, umbrella review fails to directly evaluate or re-synthesize the primary studies and has inherent limitations in appraising existing meta-analyses. Unlike primary studies where investigators maintain rigorous control in the conduction process, meta-analyses were inherently susceptible to inevitable heterogeneity. Substantial heterogeneity mainly arose from non-standardized measurement instruments, inconsistent diagnostic time, divergent definitions of factors, and variability in participants’ baseline characteristics (e.g., surgical history, chemotherapy regimens, disease stages, and demographic parameters). These factors limited a more precise stratification in population level. Secondly, we included only studies used in previously published meta-analyses, which may miss certain individual studies if those were not identified in the meta-analyses. At last, AMSTAR-2 was used as a core tool to assess the quality of included articles. However, its framework is not completely suitable to the guidelines set by meta-analyses, and consequently some of the required evaluation criteria are usually not reported in the original study. The evaluation process needs to incorporate a thorough assessment of the completeness of information and the quality of evidence.

Implications for future research

We found that, except for tumor pathology factors, our strong evidences primarily consisted of blood test indicators. While tumor pathology serves as a “red flag” at diagnosis, test indicators are considered as key factors that offer opportunities for intervention. These indicators can be used as therapeutic targets to assess their potential as the predictors of CRC prognosis [77, 78], or even as an evidence basis for experimental treatments that reduce or increase these cells to improve outcomes. For example, when hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocyte, and platelet (HALP) score were assessed before treatment [79], a low HALP score was identified as a reliable negative prognostic biomarker for survival outcomes of cancer patients. Another study has shown that the use of colony-stimulating factors plus antibiotics in individuals with chemotherapy-induced febrile neutropenia had no effect on overall mortality but reduced the participants’ length of hospital stay [67]. Since the cost of detection indicators is relatively low, it is easier to incorporate medical treatment into daily diagnosis and treatment, and achieve widespread popularization of education. Furthermore, the integrated application of these factors can enhance the interdisciplinary construction in CRC diagnosis and treatment, offering more comprehensive, economical and scientific global evidence to support clinical decision-making.

Conclusions and recommendations

Our analysis identified 8 factors with strong evidence. Beyond high-risk pathological signs of LODDS and ENTDs, there are optimizable and cost-effective predictors that associated with the OS of patients with CRC, notably test indicators such as CIN, mLNR, NLR, LNR and AMC. Research that follows consistent standardized definitions and procedures can improve the quality and level of evidence of publications. These research results not only strengthen the authority of existing guidelines and recommendations, but also provide new insights for optimizing the stratification strategy of patient prognosis management by introducing a detailed perspective for different populations, time points, and influencing factors.
