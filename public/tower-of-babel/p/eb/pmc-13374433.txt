Renal injury and hypocalcaemia in newborns with birth asphyxia at a tertiary care centre: a prospective observational study
Authors: Subhash Chandra Tard, Gajanand Singh Tanwar, Pramod Kumar Berwal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract BackgroundBirth asphyxia, also known as perinatal hypoxia-ischaemia, is a significant cause of neonatal morbidity and mortality. It occurs when a newborn experiences insufficient oxygen during birth, potentially leading to multisystem injury including renal dysfunction and disruption of calcium homeostasis. Understanding the impact of birth asphyxia on renal function and serum calcium levels is critical for early detection and timely intervention.Materials and MethodsOf 185 term neonates assessed for eligibility (27 excluded—congenital anomalies n=10; septicemia n=9; respiratory distress syndrome n=8; see flow chart), a total of 158 term neonates with birth asphyxia were enrolled. Renal function parameters (serum creatinine, serum urea) and serum calcium levels were measured at admission (within 2 hours of birth), at approximately 48±4 hours and at approximately 72±4 hours of life. All samples were analysed on a Beckman Coulter AU480 automated analyser by a blinded technician. Data were analysed in SPSS V.25 (IBM); descriptive tests included independent-samples t-tests (or Mann–Whitney U for non-normal data) and χ² tests as appropriate and multivariate logistic regression was used to adjust for potential confounders; there were no missing data. Acute kidney injury (AKI) was defined using modified neonatal KDIGO (kidney disease: improving global outcomes) criteria based on serum creatinine values and urine output, and hypocalcaemia was defined as serum calcium <8.0 mg/dL.ResultsAKI was observed in 88/158 (55.7%) neonates and hypocalcaemia in 92/158 (58.2%). Elevated serum creatinine was noted in 110/158 (69.6%) and elevated urea in 104/158 (65.8%) neonates. These abnormalities were more common in neonates with severe asphyxia. Serum creatinine and urea levels peaked at 48 hours, while serum calcium levels declined significantly within the first 48 hours, particularly in severe cases.ConclusionA high incidence of AKI and hypocalcaemia was found in neonates with birth asphyxia. These findings highlight the importance of routine biochemical monitoring and early intervention to mitigate complications and improve neonatal outcomes.

Introduction

The incidence of perinatal asphyxia is 1–1.5% in most developed countries and is inversely related to gestational age and birth weight.1 In India, the incidence of perinatal hypoxia is as high as 8–9%, and it accounts for 28.8% of neonatal deaths and 45.1% of fresh stillbirths.2 According to the latest estimates by the WHO, approximately four million babies die each year before they reach the age of 1 month; 98% of these neonatal deaths occur in developing countries.3

The WHO defines perinatal asphyxia as a failure to initiate and sustain breathing at birth.3 The National Neonatal Perinatal Database (NNPD) 2000 provided a similar definition: moderate asphyxia was defined as slow gasping respiration or an Apgar score of 4–6 at 1 min of age and severe asphyxia as no breathing or an Apgar score of 0–3 at 1 min.4

According to the American Academy of Pediatrics and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, the criteria for designating perinatal asphyxia include: profound metabolic or mixed acidemia (pH<7.00) in cord blood, persistence of Apgar score of 0–3 for longer than 5 min, neonatal neurologic sequelae (eg, seizures, coma, hypotonia) and multiple organ involvement (kidneys, lungs, liver, heart, intestine).5

As per NNPD 2003, data collected from 17 tertiary neonatal intensive care units in India revealed Apgar scores <7 at 1 min in 9% of all intramural deliveries.6 A total of 2.5% of babies had Apgar scores <7 at 5 min. Bag and mask ventilation was used in 4.5% of newborns, and fewer than 1% required cardiac compressions and/or medications at birth. Perinatal asphyxia accounted for 20% of neonatal deaths, and manifestations of hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy were observed in approximately 1.5% of all neonates. It was also the most common cause of stillbirths, responsible for nearly one-third of cases.6

Although routine biochemical monitoring is practised in many neonatal intensive care units managing birth asphyxia, most existing studies focus primarily on neurological outcomes or report isolated laboratory abnormalities. There is limited data, particularly from low- and middle-income settings, describing the temporal evolution of renal function and calcium homeostasis in relation to the severity of HIE during the early neonatal period. This study was therefore undertaken to evaluate time-based trends in renal parameters and serum calcium levels during the first 72 hours of life and to examine their association with the severity of HIE.

Materials and methods

This hospital-based, prospective observational cohort study was conducted in the department of paediatrics at S.P. Medical College and P.B.M. Associated Group of Hospitals, Bikaner, Rajasthan, India. The study was carried out over a period of 10 months, from 1 December 2023 to 30 September 2024.

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the institutional ethics committee of S.P. Medical College, Bikaner. Written informed consent was secured from the parents or legal guardians of all neonates before their inclusion in the study.

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research; parents/guardians provided informed consent for their infants’ participation and for sample collection.

The sample size was calculated using the formula: n=Z² × P × (1 − P) / d², where Z=1.96 for a 95% CI, p=11.7% (estimated prevalence) and d=5% (margin of error). Substituting these values gave a sample size of approximately 158 and, accordingly, a total of 158 neonates were enrolled. No drop-outs occurred; actual enrolment matched the calculated sample size.

Inclusion criteria comprised term newborns (≥37 weeks gestation) with a history of birth asphyxia, an Apgar score of <7 at 1 min of life, clinical signs of HIE and those requiring immediate resuscitation, including bag-and-mask ventilation. Neonates with congenital anomalies of the kidneys or urinary tract, those with septicemia or those diagnosed with respiratory distress syndrome were excluded from the study. Consecutive enrolment was used to minimise selection bias.

Eligible term neonates meeting the inclusion criteria were enrolled consecutively. For each participant, detailed maternal variables were recorded including hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (chronic hypertension, gestational hypertension, pre-eclampsia), diabetes mellitus (pre-existing and gestational), prolonged labour (defined as labour duration >12 hours), gestational age, mode of delivery, birth weight and the presence of meconium-stained amniotic fluid. A comprehensive clinical and neurological examination was performed on admission and again at discharge. Based on the Apgar score at 1 min of life, neonates were stratified into three categories: mild (score of 6), moderate (score of 4–5) and severe (score ≤3). Therapeutic hypothermia was administered to neonates diagnosed with moderate and severe HIE in accordance with institutional protocol, while neonates with mild HIE did not receive hypothermia.

Peripheral venous blood samples were collected at admission (0 hours, within 2 hours of birth), at approximately 48 hours (48±4 hours) and at approximately 72 hours (72±4 hours) of life. These samples were analysed using an automated analyser to determine serum urea, serum creatinine and total serum calcium levels. Urine output was recorded at 6-hourly intervals from day 3 onward. The 0-hour and 48-hour creatinine values were used to assess early renal trajectory and transitional renal adaptation, whereas definitive AKI staging was based on the 72-hour creatinine value and urine output in accordance with modified neonatal KDIGO (kidney disease: improving global outcomes) criteria.

Acute kidney injury (AKI) was defined using modified neonatal KDIGO criteria based on serum creatinine values and urine output. For AKI diagnosis and staging, we used serum creatinine values obtained at day 3 (72 h) and urine output assessed at 6-hourly intervals from day 3 onward and expressed as mL/kg/hour. Serum creatinine values during the first 48 hours were not used for definitive AKI staging because they may reflect maternal creatinine and transitional neonatal renal physiology. However, these early values are clinically important because failure of the expected postnatal decline or a paradoxical rise may indicate evolving renal injury before formal AKI staging is reached. Hypocalcaemia was defined as total serum calcium <8.0 mg/dL. Elevated serum creatinine was defined as >1.2mg/dL and elevated serum urea as >40mg/dL.

Data entry was carried out using Microsoft Excel, and statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.25.0 (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA).

Continuous variables were expressed as mean±SD or median with IQR, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Data were analysed using independent-samples t-tests, Mann-Whitney U tests for non-normal distributions and χ2 tests, as appropriate. Comparisons of biochemical parameters over time were assessed using appropriate repeated-measures statistical methods. There were no missing data.

To account for potential confounding factors, multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed for AKI. Variables entered into the model included HIE stage (I, II, III), Apgar score at 1 min, use of therapeutic hypothermia (yes/no), maternal hypertensive disorders, diabetes mellitus, pre-eclampsia and prolonged labour. Adjusted ORs (aORs) with 95% CIs were calculated. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 158 asphyxiated neonates were included in the study (figure 1: flow diagram of 185 assessed → 27 excluded → 158 analysed). Of these, 106 (67.1%) were males and 52 (32.9%) were females, resulting in a male-to-female ratio of approximately 2:1. This indicates a higher prevalence of birth asphyxia among male neonates.

On multivariate logistic regression analysis, higher HIE stage remained independently associated with AKI after adjustment for Apgar score at 1 min, therapeutic hypothermia and maternal risk factors. Prolonged labour was also an independent predictor of AKI (aOR 1.92, 95% CI 1.05 to 3.51; p=0.034). Discussion

Perinatal asphyxia remains a major contributor to neonatal morbidity, precipitating a cascade of systemic insults that extend beyond the brain. Our findings illustrate that hypoxic-ischaemic events provoke both transient renal injury and disturbances in calcium homeostasis in term neonates, with the magnitude of these derangements correlating directly with the severity of the hypoxic insult.

We observed a pronounced male predominance—106/158 (67.1%) males versus 52/158 (32.9%) females—consistent with the ‘male disadvantage’ described by Dahan et al and Bruckner et al, who attributed increased male susceptibility to less mature antioxidant defences and hormonal factors impairing oxidative stress tolerance.7 8 Severe asphyxia (Apgar ≤3 at 1 min) affected 100/158 (63.3%) of infants, aligning with findings by Levene et al and Shankaran et al, who reported that low Apgar scores predict higher resuscitation needs and increased multisystem complications.9 10 The predominance of stage II HIE—82/158 (51.9%)—over stage I—42/158 (26.6%)—and stage III—34/158 (21.5%)—reflects the original descriptions by Sarnat and Sarnat, and findings from the PRIME trial, both of which highlight that moderate encephalopathy is the most common and carries a substantial risk of end-organ dysfunction.11 12

Importantly, the association between HIE severity and AKI persisted after adjustment for Apgar score, maternal comorbidities, prolonged labour and therapeutic hypothermia. This indicates that the severity of hypoxic-ischaemic insult itself is a major determinant of early renal injury. The independent association between prolonged labour and AKI further underscores the contribution of perinatal stress to renal vulnerability. Therapeutic hypothermia did not demonstrate an independent association with AKI in the adjusted analysis, suggesting that while cooling may influence renal physiology, the primary driver of injury remains the hypoxic-ischaemic event.13

More than half of the infants—88/158 (55.7%)—developed AKI, consistent with the 50–60% incidence reported by Drukker and Guignard, Perlman, Guillet et al and Bonifacio et al in similar populations.1417 Disruptions in calcium homeostasis were also evident: mean serum calcium declined from 9.03±1.03 mg/dL at admission to 8.30±0.94 mg/dL at 48 hours, with 92/158 (58.2%) of neonates developing hypocalcaemia. These findings align with the work of Hankins et al and Martinez-Biarge et al, who highlighted post-asphyxial calcium dysregulation and its association with adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes.18 19

We observed a transient rise in serum creatinine from 1.41±0.07 mg/dL at admission to 1.64±0.09 mg/dL at 48 hours, followed by normalisation to 1.02±0.03 mg/dL at 72 hours paralleling patterns reported in cooled asphyxiated neonates by Selewski et al.20 In neonates, serum creatinine at birth largely reflects maternal creatinine and is expected to decline over the first 48–72 hours as neonatal renal function becomes established. In our study, the rise in creatinine at 48 hours instead of the expected decline suggests an abnormal early renal trajectory, consistent with evolving renal injury after hypoxic insult. Therefore, although formal AKI staging was based on 72-hour creatinine and urine output, the first 48-hour values were clinically useful as an early indicator of renal dysfunction.21 Serum urea followed a similar trend, reflecting adaptive haemodynamic and tubular changes post-hypoxia, as described by Volpe and Iacobelli et al.22 23 Cowett and Oh emphasised the high incidence of hypocalcaemia in this population, attributing it to parathyroid suppression and intracellular calcium shifts.24 Stojanovic et al further emphasised that early biochemical markers such as elevated creatinine and urea can identify AKI within the first 48 hours, supporting our approach of serial monitoring at admission and at 48 and 72 hours.25

Importantly, neonates with severe asphyxia demonstrated significantly higher rates of biochemical derangements: hypocalcaemia in 55/100 (55.0%), AKI in 57/100 (57.0%) and elevated urea in 61/100 (61.0%), indicating a clear dose-response relationship between hypoxic severity and organ dysfunction, as supported by Bonifacio et al and Arguelles et al.17 26

Although maternal comorbidities were included in adjusted analyses, their independent effects did not reach statistical significance, possibly due to limited subgroup sample sizes; additionally, detailed KDIGO stage-wise severity analysis and assessment of serum phosphorus and magnesium were not performed and represent important areas for future research, particularly in the context of therapeutic hypothermia.

Conclusions

Birth asphyxia significantly affects renal function and calcium homeostasis in term neonates. In this study, AKI was observed in 88/158 (55.7%) neonates, elevated serum creatinine in 110/158 (69.6%) and hypocalcaemia in 92/158 (58.2%), with these abnormalities correlating with the severity of asphyxia. Biochemical markers including serum creatinine, urea and calcium demonstrated dynamic shifts over the first 72 hours of life, reinforcing the value of early and serial monitoring.

Unlike earlier research focused primarily on neurological outcomes or survival, this study presents a time-based analysis of renal and electrolyte trends in relation to asphyxia severity and HIE staging. This targeted approach emphasises the clinical relevance of incorporating biochemical surveillance into early neonatal care.

Our findings support the routine use of biochemical screening protocols in neonatal intensive care units to enable timely detection and management of renal and electrolyte disturbances. Such proactive measures may improve outcomes and reduce complications in neonates affected by perinatal asphyxia.
