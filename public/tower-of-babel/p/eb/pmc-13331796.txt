From compression to denervation: Multimodality imaging of cervical rib–related arterial thoracic outlet syndrome
Authors: Srikanth Nalla, Pravin Sudhakar Patil, Sumavally Maydari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Thoracic outlet syndrome (TOS) encompasses a spectrum of disorders caused by compression of neurovascular structures at the thoracic outlet. Arterial thoracic outlet syndrome (aTOS) is rare, accounting for less than 1% of cases, yet carries significant morbidity due to arterial injury, thrombosis, and distal embolization. We present a 34-year-old woman with progressive pain, paresthesia, and bluish discoloration of the left upper limb. Doppler ultrasound demonstrated reduced ulnar artery calibre with a tardus-parvus waveform, indicating distal arterial compromise. CT angiography revealed a fused cervical rib causing focal compression of the left subclavian artery with associated poststenotic dilatation. The patient underwent surgical decompression with cervical rib excision, thrombus evacuation, and arterial reconstruction, resulting in restoration of distal perfusion. Postoperatively, MRI of the shoulder—performed for persistent pain—demonstrated edema of the infraspinatus and teres minor muscles without fatty infiltration, suggestive of acute denervation edema. This case illustrates the full imaging continuum of aTOS, from structural vascular compression and distal ischemia to postoperative neural involvement, underscoring the critical role of multimodality imaging in both diagnosis and follow-up.

Cervical ribs represent the most important osseous predisposing factor for aTOS. Chronic repetitive compression of the subclavian artery leads to endothelial injury, resulting in stenosis, poststenotic dilatation, mural thrombosis, and distal embolic phenomena [3].

While vascular manifestations of aTOS are well described, postoperative neuromuscular complications—particularly imaging evidence of denervation—remain infrequently reported. Only limited reports in the literature have described postoperative denervation changes on MRI in arterial thoracic outlet syndrome, and reports demonstrating the complete imaging continuum from vascular compression to postoperative neural changes remain uncommon. This case therefore provides a comprehensive radiological correlation of both vascular and postoperative neural manifestations.

Case report

A 34-year-old woman presented with a 3-month history of progressive pain, numbness, and bluish discoloration of the left upper limb. There was no history of trauma or systemic illness.

On examination, the left hand appeared mildly cyanotic. The ulnar pulse was absent, and the radial pulse was diminished. Motor function was preserved.

Doppler ultrasound of the left upper limb demonstrated reduced caliber of the ulnar artery with intraluminal echoes and a tardus-parvus monophasic waveform, consistent with proximal arterial obstruction and distal flow compromise [4].

CT angiography revealed bilateral cervical ribs, with a fibro-osseous fusion on the left side forming a rigid costoclavicular constraint. This resulted in focal compression of the subclavian artery, with associated poststenotic dilatation, indicative of chronic hemodynamic stress [3]. The distal ulnar artery appeared attenuated but patent.

A diagnosis of arterial thoracic outlet syndrome with subclavian artery thrombosis and distal embolic compromise was established.

The patient underwent surgical excision of the cervical rib, thrombus evacuation, and arterial reconstruction. Intraoperatively, the subclavian artery was noted to be dilated with intraluminal thrombus. Postoperative Doppler demonstrated restoration of arterial flow with a biphasic waveform in the ulnar artery, representing significant improvement compared to the preoperative monophasic waveform, with reappearance of distal pulses.

During follow-up, the patient reported persistent shoulder discomfort. MRI of the shoulder demonstrated diffuse STIR hyperintensity involving the infraspinatus and teres minor muscles, with preserved muscle bulk and absence of fatty infiltration. These findings were suggestive of acute to subacute denervation edema, which may reflect postoperative traction-related neural irritation involving the C5-C6 nerve roots, although intraoperative nerve monitoring was not performed to confirm this mechanism [5].

Discussion

Pathophysiology of arterial TOS

Arterial thoracic outlet syndrome results from chronic mechanical compression of the subclavian artery, most commonly due to a cervical rib or anomalous first rib. Repetitive arterial injury leads to intimal damage, progressive stenosis, poststenotic dilatation, and eventual thrombus formation [2,3]. Distal embolization may result in limb ischemia, as observed in this case.

Role of multimodality imaging

Accurate diagnosis and management of aTOS require a multimodality imaging approach:

CT angiography (CTA): CTA plays a central role in delineating osseous anomalies and their relationship to vascular structures. The presence of poststenotic dilatation is a key indicator of chronic high-grade compression and increased risk of thrombosis [3].

Doppler ultrasound: Doppler ultrasound provides functional assessment of distal perfusion. The presence of a tardus-parvus waveform reflects upstream arterial obstruction and is a critical clue to hemodynamic compromise [4].

MRI: MRI is valuable in the postoperative setting, enabling detection of denervation changes that may otherwise remain clinically under-recognized [5].

Comparison with published literature

Previously published reports on arterial thoracic outlet syndrome have predominantly focused on vascular manifestations such as subclavian artery stenosis, aneurysm formation, and distal thromboembolic complications. Farzam et al. [6] described a case of arterial thoracic outlet syndrome secondary to a fused cervical rib with pseudoaneurysm and distal embolism demonstrated on CT angiography; however, postoperative imaging evolution and neuromuscular follow-up were not reported. A recent comprehensive review on thoracic outlet syndrome imaging acknowledged the role of MRI in detecting denervation-related changes, although illustrative cases demonstrating postoperative denervation following surgery for arterial thoracic outlet syndrome appear to be rarely described in the literature [7].

In contrast, the present case demonstrates the complete imaging continuum of cervical rib–related arterial thoracic outlet syndrome, including dynamic vascular compromise, distal hemodynamic alteration on Doppler ultrasound, postoperative vascular recovery, and subsequent MRI findings suggestive of denervation edema involving the infraspinatus and teres minor muscles. To the best of our knowledge, this combination of sequential multimodality imaging findings spanning vascular compression to postoperative neural involvement has been only infrequently documented in the literature.

Postoperative denervation: An infrequently reported entity

A key distinguishing feature of this case is the demonstration of denervation edema involving the infraspinatus and teres minor muscles, corresponding to involvement of the suprascapular and axillary nerves (C5–C6 roots).

The imaging findings—STIR hyperintensity without fatty infiltration—are suggestive of acute to subacute denervation edema and may reflect postoperative traction-related neural irritation [5]. Conclusion

This case highlights the full disease spectrum of arterial thoracic outlet syndrome, extending beyond vascular compression to include postoperative neural involvement.

A multimodality imaging approach is essential not only for diagnosis but also for recognizing infrequently reported postoperative findings such as denervation edema, which has important implications for patient management.
