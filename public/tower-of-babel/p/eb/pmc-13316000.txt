Development of a First-tier Risk Assessment Tool for Occupational Hazards Among Public Officials
Authors: Sangjun Choi, Hayoung Lee, Juhyeon Jeong, Seonjin Lim, Soo-Young Lee, Jaehyun Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPublic officials face diverse job-related hazards, yet systematic approaches to identify risk factors are limited in the Republic of Korea. This study developed a risk assessment tool to evaluate occupational risk factors among public officials and describes its development process and practical use.MethodsWe conducted text analysis of injury/illness narratives from 25,317 approved compensation claims (2019–2023) to extract and classify key risk factors. The tool was designed as a first-tier self-assessment in which respondents rate the frequency and intensity of exposure to each risk factor on a 4-point Likert scale. The product of the two scores yields a four-level risk rating. The tool was developed in Microsoft Excel and consists of six worksheets.ResultsText analysis of the claim narratives identified 24 risk factors (psychological 5, organizational 2, ergonomic 5, physical 7, chemical 3, and biological 2); collision/fall risk was the most common (54.7%). In the Excel-based tool, users rate frequency and intensity for each item, which automatically generates a four-level risk rating. Items rated as high/very high prompt entry of detailed work situations. The tool also allows the addition of extra risk factors and provides guidance on second-tier in-depth assessments for high-risk results.ConclusionThis tool may help public officials across diverse job types easily screen job-related risk factors. Further studies should evaluate its usability and applicability across public service occupations.

Introduction

Public officials are essential workers who protect public safety and citizens' rights and ensure the stable delivery of public services. Ensuring safe and healthy working conditions for this workforce is therefore a critical task closely linked to the sustainability of the public sector. According to the Statistical Yearbook published by the Ministry of Personnel Management, the total number of public officials in the Republic of Korea was 1,181,527 in 2024, accounting for approximately 5% of the economically active population; by affiliation, 64.6% were employed in the central government, 33.1% in local governments, and 2.3% in the judiciary, legislature, and other sectors [1]. Among central government employees, public service-specific occupations—particularly education (365,555), police (143,894), and firefighting (66,819)—constitute nearly half of all public officials. Police officers and firefighters, as first responders deployed at the earliest stage of disasters and emergencies, are frequently engaged in field dispatch, rescue, suppression, and emergency medical response, and thus are likely to face a wide range of health hazards, including physical risks [2,3], occupational injuries [4,5], hazardous environmental exposures [6], and psychological trauma [7,8].

Occupational health research on public officials in the Republic of Korea has been reported only for selected occupations, primarily police officers, firefighters, and education-related public officials. A recent Korean study using approved compensation claim data reported that education-related public officials had a significantly higher risk of work-related mental disorders than administrative and technical public officials (hazard ratio [HR] = 2.16), with elevated risks also observed for specific subcategories such as depression (HR = 2.07) and acute stress (HR = 2.78) [9]. In another study analyzing National Health Insurance claims data from 2002 to 2014, police officers and firefighters showed an overall higher risk of disease incidence than general administrative public officials. Police officers had markedly increased risks of cardio-cerebrovascular diseases, including angina pectoris (HR = 1.52), acute myocardial infarction (HR = 1.84), and cerebrovascular disease (HR = 1.36) [10]. In contrast, firefighters exhibited a greater burden of injuries requiring hospitalization and musculoskeletal disorders, and a significantly higher risk of traumatic stress disorder (HR = 1.40) [10]. Moreover, general administrative public officials who frequently interact with citizens may be required to engage in emotional labor, including surface acting—displaying emotions without genuinely feeling them—which has been associated with increased burnout and greater exposure to job-related stress [11]. Collectively, these findings underscore the need for a systematic occupational safety and health approach that encompasses the full spectrum of public officials.

In the Republic of Korea, the occupational health and safety (OHS) system for all workers, including public officials, is governed by the Industrial Safety and Health Act. However, public officials are exempt from several key provisions for accident prevention—such as requirements related to OHS management systems and mandatory training—highlighting the need to improve the OHS framework for public officials [12]. Since 2022, the Serious Accidents Punishment Act—initially applied to workplaces with 50 or more employees—has required companies and organizations to establish an adequate OHS management system and to prevent serious accidents through job-related risk assessment (RA), leading to the development and application of various RA approaches across multiple industries [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17]]. Nevertheless, job-related RAs have not yet been implemented for public officials, despite their diverse job characteristics, underscoring the need to develop an appropriate RA tool.

Accordingly, this study aims to present the rationale and development process of a tier-1 RA tool designed to enable public officials to rapidly screen diverse job-related risk factors in a standardized manner, and to systematically describe the tool's structure (assessment items, scoring scheme, and risk rating method) and its practical application. Furthermore, we expect that the tool will support organization-level identification and prioritization of risk factors and facilitate follow-up actions (in-depth assessments and implementation of control measures), thereby contributing to the prevention of work-related injuries and adverse health outcomes among public officials.

Materials and methods

Design principles for tool development

RA has been widely adopted as a core system for managing OHS in many countries, including the Republic of Korea [18], the United Kingdom [19], Finland [20], the United States [21], and Japan [22]. The fundamental concept of RA is to identify potential hazards to which workers may be exposed, evaluate the level of risk, and implement appropriate control measures when necessary. In addition, workers' participation should be ensured throughout the entire RA process.

In this study, we developed a tier-1 RA tool for public officials by incorporating the conceptual framework of RA presented in Fig. 1. In developing the tier-1 tool, we considered the following principles. First, it should be applicable across as many occupational groups of public officials as possible. The target population for the RA tool comprised all public officials covered by the Public Officials' Compensation Act. Accordingly, the scope included not only national and local civil servants, but also other eligible employees of national or local government agencies, such as auxiliary police officers, auxiliary forest protection officers, and civilian employees of the military; however, active-duty military personnel are not covered by the Act and were therefore excluded. Second, it should enable identification of a broad range of potential job-related risk factors. Third, it should be designed for self-assessment by the workers performing the tasks. Fourth, the resulting risk ratings should be easy to interpret and readily linked to second-tier in-depth assessments and third-tier risk control and improvement actions. In practice, the tier-1 screening is completed by the public official performing the job (assessor) as a self-administered evaluation based on their routine tasks and perceived exposure conditions. The tier-1 results are intended to support communication and consultation within the workplace (e.g., with supervisors or relevant administrative personnel) and to inform prioritization of follow-up actions. When high-risk items are identified, the tool recommends tier-2 in-depth assessments, which may involve validated questionnaires (psychosocial/job stress) and/or expert-led evaluations (musculoskeletal or work environment assessments), depending on the hazard domain.Fig. 1Conceptual framework for the tiered risk assessment process used in tool development.

Risk factor selection for the tool

To ensure that a broad range of job-related hazards could be screened, we reviewed only compensation claims that were formally reviewed and approved under the Public Officials' Compensation Act over the most recent five years (2019–2023) and analyzed the narrative descriptions of accident or illness circumstances to derive a standardized set of risk factors; submitted but nonapproved claims were not included.

In the Republic of Korea, public officials seeking work-related compensation are required to submit an incident report to the Government Employees' Pension Service (GEPS). This report requests detailed information on when and where the event occurred, what the employee was doing at the time, how the accident or illness occurred, and the reasons or contributing circumstances. Based on the injury and illness narrative texts, the circumstances of each incident were first classified into seven categories: (1) performance of assigned duties, (2) performance of nonassigned duties, (3) job-related training, (4) business trips, (5) work-related public events outside regular duties, (6) commuting, and (7) break or rest periods. Because the primary objective of this study was to identify the types of risk factors to which public officials are predominantly exposed when performing their job duties, cases with low direct relevance to job performance were excluded from the analysis (e.g., commuting incidents and break or rest periods). Accordingly, we analyzed deidentified narratives from 25,317 approved claims related to job performance, provided by the Ministry of Personnel Management.

The initial review of de-identified claim narratives generated 70 candidate risk factors representing diverse event-level circumstances. The coding process was conducted over five months (February 15–July 15, 2025) by three public-administration experts (SL, HL, and JJ) and five graduate students in the same field. First, one expert (HL) selected 6,086 approved claims (2019–2023) using proportional sampling across occupational categories. These narratives were reviewed to develop a preliminary coding framework, yielding 48 candidate risk factors. A coding guide was then created, including operational definitions and representative keywords for each factor. Next, six coders (including expert JJ) coded the remaining approved claims using the 48-factor framework and coding guide. During full-scale coding, 26 additional factors were identified. After iterative discussions among the three experts, overlapping factors were merged and a final set of 70 risk factors was confirmed. To assess content validity, we consulted eight officials involved in reviewing public officials' compensation claims. Based on their feedback, the 70-factor framework was considered sufficiently comprehensive and mutually exclusive for characterizing occupational risk factors across public-sector occupations.

In the second-stage standardization, an industrial hygiene specialist (SC) consolidated and reclassified the candidate factors into 24 standardized risk factors within six domains (psychological, organizational, ergonomic, physical, chemical, and biological). This reclassification was guided by three considerations: (1) conceptual coherence and shared exposure mechanisms, (2) feasibility and interpretability for a first-tier, self-administered screening tool, and (3) empirical relevance in the claim data. Items with very low frequencies and those reflecting primarily subjective organizational perceptions (e.g., “insufficient staffing in the organization”) were not retained as standardized factors. To strengthen construct validity and facilitate comparability, the final set of standardized risk factors was mapped to hazard domains used in the Korean Working Conditions Survey [23], which was developed with reference to the European Working Conditions Survey. To mitigate the potential loss of specificity inherent to standardization, the tool includes an open-ended module that enables users to add and rate additional job-specific risk factors.

Risk rating criteria

For risk rating, each risk factor was assessed using two 4-point Likert scales: exposure frequency (likelihood) and exposure intensity (severity). For frequency, the public official completing the tier-1 self-assessment (hereafter, the assessor) indicated how often they encountered situations related to each risk factor during a typical working day, using a 4-point scale ranging from “rarely” to “≥ 3/4 of working hours.” For intensity, the assessor rated either the perceived exposure level or the degree of discomfort or stress experienced when exposed to the risk factor, using a 4-point scale ranging from “none” to “very high.” The assessor selected one level for each scale, and an overall risk score was calculated as the product of the frequency and intensity scores (range: 1–16). Based on the total score, risk was classified into four levels: very high (12–16), high (6–9), medium (3–4), and low (1–2). The tool was designed to recommend an appropriate second-tier in-depth assessment when a risk factor was rated as high or very high. The most frequently identified risk factor in the reviewed claims was collision/fall (54.7%), followed by vehicle operation (14.7%) and client aggression (14.0%). Structure of RA tool

The RA tool was developed in Microsoft Excel to facilitate self-assessment by public officials and consists of six worksheets: Introduction, RA1–RA4, and Reference. The English and Korean versions of the tool are available for download in Supplementary Materials 2 and 3, respectively. The first worksheet (Introduction) provides an overview of the tool and instructions for using each worksheet, and the sixth worksheet (Reference) presents the risk rating criteria shown in Fig. 2. The remaining worksheets (RA1–RA4) correspond to the stepwise assessment process.

In RA1, users select exposure frequency and intensity for each of the 24 risk factors from drop-down lists. The risk level is automatically calculated according to the criteria in Fig. 2, and high-risk items are visually flagged using cell colors (very high = red; high = orange) to facilitate rapid identification. RA2 is linked to RA1 and, as illustrated in Fig. 3, automatically highlights high-risk items in pink, prompting users to describe the specific high-risk work situations. RA3 allows users to document and rate additional job-related risk factors beyond the predefined 24 items. Finally, RA4 provides guidance on second-tier in-depth assessment options when high-risk items are identified, as shown in Fig. 4.Fig. 3Illustrative example of automatic flagging of high/very high-risk items in RA1 and corresponding documentation in RA2.Fig. 4Recommendations for second-tier assessments based on high-risk results from the tier-1 risk assessment of the 24 items.

For second-tier in-depth assessments of psychological risk factors, we proposed the use of structured instruments developed to evaluate health impacts related to psychological hazards, such as emotional labor (KELS-11) [24], anxiety (GAD-7) [25], depression (PHQ-9) [26], burnout (KBOSS) [27], and workplace violence (KWVS-13) [28]. For organizational and ergonomic risk factors, the tool guides users to undergo an expert-led musculoskeletal risk factor assessment.

For physical, chemical, and biological hazards, when any related item is rated as high or very high, users are advised to request an in-depth investigation by industrial hygiene professionals or accredited occupational exposure assessment agencies to 1) identify the specific hazardous agents relevant to the task (e.g., via task review and safety data sheets/product information where applicable) and 2) determine the need for workplace exposure assessment and environmental measurements. In addition, if any risk factor is rated as high or very high, the tool suggests conducting a job stress assessment (KOSS-19 [29]). Key characteristics, scoring criteria, and recommended applications of these six tier-2 psychosocial and job-stress instruments are summarized in Supplementary Table S3. The second-tier instruments for psychosocial hazards and job stress (e.g., KELS-11, GAD-7, PHQ-9, KBOSS, KWVS-13, and KOSS-19) are structured, validated questionnaires that provide quantitative severity scores based on standardized response options and established interpretation criteria. Because these tools are self-administered and psychometrically validated, they can support relatively consistent and reproducible assessments even when the assessor is not a specialist in occupational mental health, while still enabling referral to professional evaluation when high scores are identified.

Discussion

RA is widely recognized as a core instrument within the OHS management process, because it provides a structured basis for identifying hazards, estimating risk levels, and prioritizing preventive actions [30]. Accordingly, many countries have institutionalized RA by embedding it within legal and regulatory frameworks and requiring organizations to conduct RA as a mandatory element of OHS management to prevent occupational accidents and illnesses [[18], [19], [20], [21], [22]]. Despite the broad and heterogeneous job characteristics of public officials, job-related RA has not been systematically implemented for this workforce in the Republic of Korea, even though public officials can be exposed to diverse physical, psychosocial, and environmental hazards. This gap is particularly important given that public officials remain excluded from several core OHS provisions, underscoring the need for practical tools that can support organization-level hazard identification and prioritization.

In this study, we developed a first-tier, self-administered RA tool intended to screen a broad range of job-related risk factors among public officials in a standardized manner. The tool's content was grounded in real-world compensation claim narratives: 70 risk factors were initially extracted from 25,317 approved claims and then standardized into 24 core risk factors across six domains (psychological, organizational, ergonomic, physical, chemical, and biological). The claim-based approach also provides empirical insight into which hazards were most frequently documented, with collision/fall risk being the most common, followed by vehicle operation and client aggression (Table 1).

A key strength of the proposed RA tool is its practicality and accessibility for broad deployment. The tool was developed in Microsoft Excel and structured into six worksheets. Users select exposure frequency and intensity for each item via drop-down lists; risk levels are automatically generated and visually flagged (e.g., red/orange) to facilitate rapid recognition of high-risk items. The linkage between RA1 and RA2 further supports contextual interpretation by prompting users to document specific high-risk work situations when an item is rated high or very high (Fig. 3). At the same time, standardizing diverse public-sector hazards into 24 risk factors inevitably involves trade-offs. During the second-stage standardization, items with very few cases or those primarily reflecting subjective judgments in narratives were excluded, which may lead to under-representation of certain emerging, occupation-specific, or organizational-level hazards. To mitigate this limitation and acknowledge the diversity of public service work, the tool includes an “additional risk factors” worksheet (RA3), allowing users to add and rate hazards beyond the predefined list. This feature supports adaptability across heterogeneous public-sector roles and can inform future updates to the standardized item set. A practical implication of this tool is its suitability for settings where a dedicated OSH infrastructure is not yet fully established in the public sector. In such contexts, a self-administered tier 1 screening tool can serve as a feasible entry point for systematic hazard identification. Nevertheless, effective progression to tier-2 in-depth assessment and tier-3 risk control would be facilitated by strengthening organizational OSH capacity in public-sector institutions (e.g., designated units and trained personnel).

A further strength of this study is that it offers a screening framework that complements, yet differs from, existing internationally recognized RA resources. For example, EU-OSHA's Online interactive Risk Assessment (OiRA) platform provides structured, sector-specific tools that are highly useful for micro- and small-sized enterprises [31], and the ILO Ergonomic Checkpoints [32] and Stress Prevention Checkpoints [33] offer practical improvement ideas applicable to general workplaces. However, these approaches are typically designed for specific sectors, settings, or single domains. Similarly, school-focused tools such as the UK classroom health and safety checklist [34] or NIOSH-related school safety checklists [35] are tailored to particular environments and user groups. In contrast, the present tool was developed to provide a standardized, unified first-tier screening framework that can be used by public officials themselves across heterogeneous roles—from policing and firefighting to education, general administration, corrections, and public health services—while covering multiple hazard domains (psychological, organizational, ergonomic, physical, chemical, and biological). By operationalizing 24 standardized risk factors derived from duty-related compensation claim narratives, the tool helps ensure that no major job group or hazard domain is overlooked during initial screening and facilitates consistent prioritization for follow-up assessments and control actions.

Another important contribution is that the tool is not intended to stop at screening or to imply that the identified items fully represent the causal pathways underlying adverse events. Rather, the tier-1 results are designed to function as a triage step that flags priority hazards and triggers tier-2 in-depth assessments aimed at clarifying severity and uncovering upstream determinants. When high-risk results are identified, the tool provides guidance for second-tier assessments tailored to hazard domains—suggesting validated questionnaires for psychosocial hazards (e.g., emotional labor, anxiety, depression, burnout, workplace violence) and recommending a comprehensive job-stress assessment when any item is rated as high or very high. This structure allows users to evaluate organizational and psychosocial contributors that may operate upstream of events (e.g., long working hours or staffing constraints → fatigue and stress → impaired performance and increased injury risk). For organizational and ergonomic risks, the tool recommends expert-led musculoskeletal assessments to identify task-specific biomechanical drivers and feasible controls, while for physical/chemical/biological risks it directs users to expert-led work environment assessments (including measurements where appropriate) to support exposure characterization and control planning. By explicitly linking tier-1 screening to tier-2 evaluations and subsequent control measures, the tool facilitates a practical pathway from hazard identification to root-cause–informed risk management, rather than treating events as isolated “simple risk factors.”

Although the present RA tool was developed using approved duty-related compensation claims in the Republic of Korea, its potential utility is not confined to the Korean setting. The tool targets hazards that are common to public service delivery across many countries (e.g., public-facing work and client aggression, emergency response, vehicle operation and traffic-related risks, collision/fall events, ergonomic burdens, and environmental exposures), and it is structured to cover broad functional areas of government activity. Notably, the OECD's Classification of the Functions of Government (COFOG) provides an internationally standardized framework to categorize government functions and facilitate cross-national comparisons [36]. Because many public-sector occupations share functional similarities across COFOG domains, despite differences in administrative systems, a standardized first-tier screening approach may have value beyond a single national context. Furthermore, the tool's hazard domains (psychological, organizational, ergonomic, physical, chemical, and biological) align with internationally recognized occupational safety and health taxonomies. Accordingly, with context-specific tailoring (e.g., terminology, examples, and country-specific hazards) and local validation, this RA tool may be adaptable as a practical screening framework for public-sector workers in other countries.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the risk factor set was derived from approved compensation claims, which may not capture hazards that do not result in claims, are underreported, or are more strongly related to chronic outcomes and near-miss events. Nevertheless, this claim-based approach also differentiates our method from many existing RA tools developed for specific industrial settings. In contrast to industry- or task-specific checklists that are often tailored to relatively homogeneous processes, the present tool was designed to function as a first-tier screening instrument across heterogeneous public-sector occupations. To achieve broad coverage, the core items were derived from real-world duty-related injury and illness narratives accumulated over five years, reflecting hazards actually reported during public service work. Although reliance on approved claims may miss hazards that do not lead to claims or are underreported, we attempted to address this limitation by incorporating an open-ended module that allows users to add and rate additional job-specific risk factors beyond the 24 standardized items. This structure supports both standardization for comparability and flexibility for diverse public-sector contexts.

Second, tier-1 ratings are self-reported and may be influenced by perception, recall, and differences in risk awareness across jobs. Third, although the tool was designed to be intuitive and scalable, its psychometric properties (e.g., test–retest reliability) and practical performance (e.g., usability, acceptance, and feasibility across agencies) require formal evaluation. Future studies should therefore 1) conduct field testing across diverse public service occupations, 2) examine the consistency and validity of the risk ratings against external indicators (e.g., incident records, exposure measurements, expert assessments), and 3) iteratively refine and update the standardized risk factor framework using accumulated RA3 entries and new evidence.

In conclusion, the RA tool developed in this study provides a simple and practical first-tier screening approach to identify a broad range of job-related risk factors encountered across diverse public service duties. By enabling public officials to self-assess exposure frequency and intensity and linking high-risk findings to recommended second-tier assessments, the tool may support timely risk identification and follow-up actions at the organizational level. Overall, this RA tool has the potential to contribute to the prevention of work-related injuries and illnesses and to the protection of public officials' health.
