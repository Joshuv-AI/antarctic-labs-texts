Scotland's internal medicine simulation strategy: A 5-year journey
Authors: Joanne Kerins, Suzanne Anderson-Stirling, Jemma Pringle, Victoria Ruth Tallentire
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Alignment to curriculum and multimodal design has been key to the success of this national simulation strategy.•The Scottish internal medicine simulation strategy develops a sense of belonging and identity for trainees as well as offering procedural opportunities.•Robust evaluation has allowed iterative adaptations over time, influenced by trainee need.Keywords: Simulation, Internal medicine training, Postgraduate training

Background – the need for a national simulation strategy

Since the implementation of the European Working Time Regulations, which aimed to protect doctors by limiting excessive working hours, there has been an overall reduction in training time for junior doctors in the UK.1 In addition, trainees report a lack of training opportunities in the workplace, particularly for learning procedural skills.2,3 The new internal medicine (IM) stage 1 curriculum was implemented in the UK in 2019, heralding the commencement of a revised 3-year training programme.4 The curriculum, outlined by the Joint Royal Colleges of Physicians Training Board, asserts a requirement for simulation-based education.4,5 Simulation has been well received as a training modality in preparing doctors for becoming medical registrars6, 7, 8, 9 and providing procedural training opportunities.10, 11, 12 With this transition to IM stage 1, the opportunity to embed simulation training on a national scale was seized in Scotland. It involved the development of bespoke training courses for all IM stage 1 trainees in Scotland, in each year of their training programme (known as IM year 1, IM year 2 and IM year 3).

The Scottish Surgical Boot Camp, which has been running successfully for the past 10 years,13 along with the success of other medical education boot camps,14,15 provided inspiration for the initial IM year 1 (IMY1) course. In anticipation of the introduction of the new IM stage 1 curriculum, a proposal was submitted to the Scottish government in February 2019 for the development of an equivalent course for IM, which was subsequently supported and approved. Building on this, additional support was received for the implementation of national courses in IM year 2 (IMY2) from 2020, and IM year 3 (IMY3) from 2021. The courses are delivered to every stage 1 IM trainee in Scotland. Each year cohort includes over 100 trainees spread across the 30,000 square miles of Scotland, so the logistical and organisational undertaking is substantial. The overarching aim of the IM simulation strategy is to embed evidence-based educational practice to enhance skills, improve efficiency and augment patient safety, while preparing trainees for the challenges of IM and their careers as physicians. We will detail some of the key aspects of the design, delivery and impact of the simulation strategy.

Materials and methods

Funding proposal

The IM stage 1 simulation programme was initially funded directly by the Scottish government in response to a bid developed by the associate postgraduate dean for IM simulation, supported by the team involved in Scottish physician training. As the value of the courses has become more obvious and the impact clearly demonstrated, funding for the strategy has been incorporated into core business for NHS Education for Scotland (NES). The courses are funded in addition to trainees’ usual study leave budgets, amounting to just over £1,000 per trainee over 3 years of training. Value for money has been a major consideration, particularly in recent years, and lowest cost options and reusable materials are consistently pursued.

Design

The journey began in 2019, with a planning day involving key stakeholders, representation from all four deaneries in Scotland, and 14 core medical trainees (CMT, the precursor to IM training). The planning event was endorsed by the NES medicine simulation collaborative and Medicine Specialty Training Board simulation subgroup.

The resulting simulation strategy involves a 3-day bootcamp in IMY1, a 1-day skills day in IMY2 and a 2-day ‘registrar ready’ course in IMY3. During development, course learning objectives were aligned to curriculum outcomes.16 The courses in IMY1 and IMY3 are delivered at a central location, the Scottish Centre for Simulation and Clinical Human Factors (SCSCHF) in Larbert, Scotland. These two courses include the opportunity to be residential throughout, with accommodation and travel costs covered. In IMY2, the skills days are hosted in two different locations: The Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Glasgow and Aberdeen Royal Infirmary. IMY1 bootcamp

Eighteen trainees attend each bootcamp and are allocated into three groups of six, mixing trainees from different training regions in Scotland. The course content includes three core components aligned with the three domains of learning: cognitive, affective and psychomotor.17

Cognitive – immersive simulation

Experiential learning theory underpins high-fidelity immersive simulation followed by a facilitated debriefing.18 The sessions aim to replicate Kolb's experiential learning cycle, a four-part process of concrete experience, reflection, conceptualisation and experimentation.19,20 Scenarios are designed to achieve both technical and non-technical learning outcomes (eg competence in direct current cardioversion and improved understanding of the components of situational awareness), with six scenarios over 3 days. This allows each trainee to experience leading an individual scenario with the chance to be actively involved in multiple scenarios. The immersive simulation provides the opportunity for concrete experience and active experimentation, with video used to aid reflection-on-action during debriefings.21

Affective – communication workshops

Communication workshops allow discussion and rehearsal of a series of challenging communication tasks relating to patients, relatives and colleagues. Communication workshops, undertaken in groups of six, use small group learning theory to promote active participation with a trainee-directed approach.22 For example, the session on interprofessional communication is a free-flowing discussion, led by trainees.23,24 This provides the opportunity to reflect on attitudes and behaviours in the workplace, including sociological concepts often considered part of the ‘hidden curriculum’.25

Psychomotor – simulation-based mastery learning of procedural skills

Robust simulation-based mastery learning (SBML) in three main procedural skills offers the opportunity for trainees to achieve portfolio sign off as ‘ready for supervised practice on a real patient’.26 A flipped classroom approach is utilised, with pre-learning prior to the session facilitating maximal hands-on practice time.27 The use of SBML checklists28 encourages the provision of individualised specific feedback alongside formal assessment of skills to provide evidence for trainees’ annual reviews of competence progression (ARCP).29 Learning materials are accessible after the course, allowing trainees to refer back to them, should they wish, prior to undertaking the procedure in clinical practice.

IMY2 skills day

During IMY2, trainees attend a 1-day skills course in groups of 18 across two sites: Aberdeen (for trainees based in the north and east regions of Scotland) and Glasgow (for trainees based in the west and south-east regions of Scotland). The day includes a session on non-invasive ventilation and SBML sessions for central line insertion, arterial line insertion, and ultrasound-guided peripheral venous cannulation. It also provides an opportunity for refresher training for the procedural skill of intercostal chest drain.2 Trainees are introduced to point-of-care ultrasound to allow safe central venous access and facilitate difficult peripheral venous access.

IMY3 registrar ready

In IMY3, trainees return to SCSCHF for a 2-day course focused on the role of the ‘medical registrar’. It is recognised that the transition to this role can be challenging.30 Workshops focus on decision making and prioritisation, and leading and managing the medical team. The immersive simulation scenarios follow the same format as those in the IMY1 bootcamp, but are appropriately more complex and multi-faceted. Where scenario facilitation requires specific, in-depth subject matter knowledge, additional faculty with relevant expertise are sought to co-deliver the session. For example, a scenario incorporating a patient on dialysis is co-facilitated by a nurse from the dialysis unit and a renal registrar. In response to trainee feedback, a novel element recently added to the IMY3 course is shadowbox simulation,31 which allows learners to view a scenario through the lens of an expert, using video with pauses for facilitated discussion to develop decision-making skills.31 This provided a more interactive session for real-world discussions of the management of shock and agitation in clinical practice.

Delivery

The salient factors relating to the successful delivery of the simulation programme are meticulous planning, consistent faculty and multimodal delivery. The planning of course dates and strategic allocation of trainees is led by the associate postgraduate dean and training programme directors, supported by administrative colleagues from the Clinical Skills Managed Educational Network (CSMEN). Course planning aims to avoid any clashes with membership examination dates, junior doctor changeover dates and other key events in the training calendar. Diligent allocation avoids too many doctors being absent from a single department or, in the case of smaller hospitals, removing the entire medical workforce.

The courses are delivered by a small cohort of experienced faculty. Quality is monitored and assured by an even smaller group. Faculty have backgrounds in a variety of medical specialties and are of varying grades. However, only those with subject matter expertise, as well as teaching experience relevant to the area they are delivering, are invited to participate. All faculty delivering SBML for procedural skills must participate in bespoke train the trainer sessions to ensure consistency in this aspect of assessment of multiple procedural skills.32 All faculty facilitating immersive simulation are experienced in and have attended specific training in this methodology.18 This means that a very large number of trainees receive a training experience that can be assured to be both consistent and of high quality. This consistency has also presented an opportunity to promote culture change nationally, for example regarding approaches to procedural skills.3

The central location of the courses is not only convenient for trainees, but also allows faculty to be pulled from all of the densely populated regions of Scotland. Six consultants from Dundee, Glasgow, Edinburgh and local to Forth Valley have small amounts of job planned time (typically 0.5–1 programmed activity sessions) to provide an agreed number of days training per year. This core faculty is augmented by senior trainees across a range of specialties, including medicine, critical care, anaesthesia and emergency medicine, who are supported by the consultant team to enhance their simulation facilitation skills and gain competencies in teaching as required within their own training portfolios. Fellows and simulation technicians employed by SCSCHF provide an additional layer of support and consistency.

The multimodal delivery of the courses reflects trainee need. At the outset of the simulation strategy, we asked trainees to rank their first and second preferred learning styles. Trainees attend SCSCHF in IMY1 and IMY3, where they receive an introductory talk and orientation to the immersive simulation set-up. This aims to promote psychological safety33 and ensure that learners understand the ethos of the course. Trainees move between various areas of the simulation centre, with different faculty and a unique delivery style for each session. The sessions run strictly to time, with a catered lunch and opportunity for trainees to socialise during breaks.

Research and evaluation

At the outset of the training programme, we applied for ethical approval from NES (reference number NES/Res/14/20/Med) for evaluation and research of the IM simulation strategy. Pre- and post-course questionnaires are completed by all trainees at every course. This was initially using the Questback platform, but has since been transferred to Microsoft Forms with associated QR codes for ease of completion. Data are tracked for trainees as they progress through each year of their training. Interviews exploring the transfer of training to clinical practice were undertaken with IMY1 trainees in the months following their first course. Interviews have also been conducted with a cohort of trainees who have completed the whole strategy (IMY1, IMY2 and IMY3 courses), as well as faculty involved in training delivery and key stakeholders.

Every year since commencement of the programme, an annual review day is held with faculty, trainee representatives, training programme directors and other key stakeholders to share feedback and review all aspects of the courses, making modifications where required.

Results

In addition to the overall strategy evaluation, several in-depth projects have been carried out. Here we will share the impact from these strands of inquiry within each mode of delivery: immersive simulation, SBML and communication workshops. We will also describe work exploring overall course impact as well as describing some of the course adaptations.

Immersive simulation

The immersive simulation research strand involved analysing video recordings of immersive simulation to explore teamwork and leadership behaviours and communication of IM trainees. The Team Emergency Assessment Measure (TEAM) tool was utilised34 to assess the teamwork skills of IM trainees during high-fidelity immersive simulation.35 This study found that improved team performance, as measured by the TEAM tool, was associated with faster defibrillation by IM trainees in simulated cardiac arrest. This work therefore links non-technical skills directly to resuscitation skills, and patient chance of survival. This reinforces the importance of training in teamwork and leadership skills which is one of the key learning outcomes for the immersive simulation component, particularly for resuscitation scenarios. The use of this tool for peer observation is being considered to prompt reflection on these skills in future courses.

Questionnaire feedback highlighted that the key learning themes from immersive simulation were leadership and teamworking skills, communication strategies, confidence with emergency management of unwell patients, decision making and cognitive load.

Simulation based mastery learning

Using data tracking trainee confidence in procedures over the course of their training, SBML, as part of the IM simulation strategy, has been shown to boost confidence in intercostal chest drain insertion.2 There was evidence of skill decay between training sessions, likely due to lack of procedural exposure in clinical practice between courses. These data reinforced the need for this skill to be revisited during IMY2, after being initially taught in IMY1.

Key learning themes from the questionnaire data regarding SBML included procedural preparedness, asepsis and safety, technical skill confidence and continuous learning and improvement.

Communication workshops

The communication workshop research strand involved audio-recording interprofessional communication workshops to explore social identity development of consenting IM trainees. The aim was to understand how IM trainees learn to collaborate with other professions and specialties.24,36 This workshop in the IMY1 bootcamp provides space for reflection and discussion with peers relating to the strategies utilised to avoid conflict, like perspective taking in the workplace.37,38 Perspective taking involves recognising the priorities and workload of others in different professional groups, such as other professions and other specialties, and focusing on shared goals of safe patient care.

Key learning themes from the trainee questionnaires were effective handover, dealing with difficult situations, documentation and legal considerations, personal wellbeing and perspective, and professional development and support.

Overall course impact

Transfer of training from the IMY1 bootcamp to the clinical workplace has been the focus of interviews conducted with trainees.3 Positive factors that enhanced transfer of learning were close alignment to the training curriculum and opportunities to use communication and leadership skills in practice. Barriers to training transfer included a lack of procedural opportunities, lack of equipment availability and, at times, an unsupportive workplace culture.3 Interviews of those trainees having completed the whole simulation strategy highlighted the ways the courses made the trainees feel valued and fostered a shared identity.39

Adaptations

Key to the success of the IM simulation courses has been the embedded research and evaluation strategies. In addition to iterative adaptations in response to real-time trainee feedback, annual review days allow reflection on feedback and the opportunity for trainee and faculty collaboration to modify various aspect of the design and delivery of the courses.39

For example, the IMY3 course now includes shadowbox simulation31 in response to trainee feedback.

Discussion- reflecting on a 5-year journey

The Scottish IM simulation strategy has grown to be a well-regarded and highly valued training intervention providing opportunities for developing procedural competence and non-technical skills. It facilitates a rare occasion for IM trainees from across Scotland to meet and share experiences, fostering a sense of community and contributing to their identity development as physicians in training. IM trainees value the opportunity to come together as a group and have protected training time away from the pressures of the clinical workplace. This is particularly pertinent in the aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic, when many other training opportunities were cancelled or hosted virtually.40 Such in-person social benefits have been explored in the surgical bootcamp and provide further impetus to continue to deliver this in-person training.41 Social interaction is particularly important for group cohesion, self-worth, and development of teamwork skills,42 all of which have been reflected upon by trainees and faculty of the IM simulation strategy.

A needs assessment, informed by the IM stage 1 curriculum and enquiring about learning style preferences of trainees, reinforced the need for the incorporation of instructional design elements that emphasise psychomotor, affective and cognitive domains of learning. This variety is also beneficial for trainee engagement during courses spanning multiple days. A systematic review of bootcamps in the surgical setting outlined that:

‘A successful bootcamp should be multimodal, target trainee syllabuses, provisioned at transition, with deliberate practice.’43

From our experience in the medical context, the same principles have led to the success of the IM simulation strategy and would likely be useful to others planning similar endeavours in their contexts.

The IM simulation strategy has benefitted from enthusiastic leaders and faculty advocating for its implementation, maintenance and continual improvement. Employing a research fellow has facilitated a programme of integrated, nuanced educational research. Dedicating time to implementing meaningful evaluation has allowed the tracking of data across years, generating high-level oversight of impact. There are limitations to the data collected, for example the questionnaires ask about self-perception of confidence,44 rather than a measure of procedural competence. However, the triangulation of data gathered in interviews has produced a comprehensive understanding of trainee perspectives on the impact of this training.

Annual review days continue, fuelled by the desire for ongoing improvement to the courses through further adaptations. Trainees have expressed a wish to have their IMY3 course earlier in their training programme, although faculty and simulation centre availability is challenging if all three courses occur at the start of the academic year. From faculty interviews there have been some suggestions for future modifications, including co-debriefing to reduce cognitive load and as a faculty development strategy.45 Given the work around the development and measurement of teamworking skills, we are considering formal incorporation of a behavioural marker system for non-technical skills, to enhance trainee engagement with this process.34

Conclusions

The Scottish IM simulation strategy has been successfully implemented, with robust evaluation and research strands since the transition to IM stage 1 in 2019. It stands as a successful example of a national simulation strategy, aligned to the relevant curriculum, from which we hope others can draw inspiration. It incorporates varied instructional design approaches firmly grounded in educational theory and is appreciated by trainees and faculty alike. With increased pressures in the clinical environment, protected training is necessary to support IM training and progression. It also fosters a sense of belonging and value in trainees, hopefully counteracting the challenges of increased burnout and reduced retention. Ongoing evaluation with passionate and enthusiastic faculty aims to sustain the IM simulation strategy for the next five years and beyond, ensuring alignment with trainee needs and that it remains firmly embedded in the realities of the clinical workplace.
