Targeting the Epigenetic Remodeler GCN5 Prevents Vascular Oxidative Stress and Endothelial Dysfunction in Obesity: Insights in Patients with Cardiometabolic Disease
Authors: Sarah Costantino, Shafeeq Mohammed, Alessandro Mengozzi, Emiliano Duranti, Valentina Delfine, Martin A Geiger, Nazha Hamdani, Stefano Taddei, Stefano Masi, Agostino Virdis, Francesco Paneni

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEpigenetic changes are important modulators of gene expression. Introduction

Approximately 880 million adults and 159 million children and adolescents are currently affected by obesity [1]. The number of people with obesity has more than doubled since 1990 for adults and quadrupled for children and adolescents, indicating that the disease is assuming epidemic proportion across the globe [2]. While obesity is a risk factor for several chronic diseases (e.g. chronic kidney disease, cancer), 67.5% of deaths related to high BMI are attributable to cardiovascular disease [2–4]. The impairment of endothelial function is an important step in the pathophysiology of several cardiovascular diseases in metabolic patients, including myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, peripheral artery disease and heart failure [5, 6]. Emerging evidence is shedding light on the importance of epigenetic changes in the regulation of gene expression and disease phenotypes [7, 8]. Plastic changes of chromatin induced by environmental stressors (i.e. metabolic stress, pollution) are able to modify chromatin accessibility to transcription factors thus leading to altered expression of genes implicated in cardiovascular disease development, namely genes regulating inflammation and oxidative stress [9]. The recent development of compounds able to target chromatin modifications, known as “epi-drugs”, is becoming an attractive opportunity to reset gene expression changes, thus reducing the risk of comorbidities [7, 10, 11].

Histone acetylation is a key epigenetic mark fostering enhanced gene transcription [12]. Among different enzymes implicated in this process, the histone acetyltransferase gene non-derepressible 5 (Gcn5) is emerging as a master regulator of proliferation, metabolism and inflammation, and its activation has been linked to several disease phenotypes including cancer, diabetes, malaria and osteoporosis [13, 14]. Gcn5 catalyzes the transfer of acetyl groups to histones and transcriptional co-activators, thereby modulating chromatin accessibility and transcriptional regulation. Its acetyltransferase activity is dynamically regulated in a phosphorylation-dependent manner. Notably, a signaling module comprising the cofactor CITED2 and protein kinase A (PKA) can phosphorylate Gcn5, thereby switching its substrate specificity between histone and non-histone targets. This PKA-mediated, CITED2-facilitated phosphorylation enables Gcn5 to acetylate distinct protein substrates depending on the prevailing metabolic state [15, 16]. Other histone acetyltransferases (HATs) have also been implicated in vascular pathology. The transcriptional coactivators p300 and CBP, although closely related, exert non-redundant effects in vascular cells. The GNAT-family HAT PCAF (p300/CBP-associated factor) contributes to vascular disease by enhancing NF-κB–mediated inflammatory gene expression and has been linked to endothelial senescence, atherosclerotic plaque progression, and neointimal hyperplasia [17, 18]. TIP60, a MYST-family HAT, is abundantly expressed in cardiovascular tissues and regulates transcriptional programs and stress responses—such as autophagy and DNA repair—that are essential for endothelial and vascular homeostasis [19]. Beyond its catalytic activity, Gcn5 engages in functional complexes with several cofactors, including CITED2, the related HAT PCAF, and the mitochondrial deacetylase Sirt3, thereby integrating into broader epigenetic regulatory networks [20–23]. In experimental diabetes, activation of Gcn5 leads to repression of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ coactivator 1-α (PGC-1α) a major orchestrator of cellular metabolism and mitochondrial biogenesis [24]. Moreover, genetic or pharmacological inhibition of GCN5 has been shown to reverse insulin resistance-induced mitochondrial damage and dysfunction in cardiomyocytes [25]. Taken together, these findings indicate that Gcn5 could represent an important molecular target in the context of metabolic disease such as obesity and diabetes.

In obese patients, accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) is a major event underpinning impaired nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability and endothelial dysfunction [26–30]. ROS scavengers have shown to rescue endothelial dysfunction in patients with obesity and hypertension, further highlighting the pivotal role of altered redox balance in the setting of cardiometabolic disease [31–33]. Of interest, Gcn5 activation was identified as a mechanism underlying oxidative stress generation in leucocytes via transcriptional regulation of the NADPH subunit Nox2 [34]. Given the pivotal role of oxidative stress and NADPH signaling in endothelial dysfunction [35, 36], in the present study we investigated whether Gcn5-driven epigenetic changes are involved in oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction in obesity. In human endothelial cells, mouse vessels and vascular specimens from obese patients, we show that Gcn5 is a master regulator of Nox2 expression, oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction. Pharmacological inhibition of Gcn5 was able to attenuate obesity-related oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction, thus indicating Gcn5 as a potential therapeutic target to prevent or attenuate vascular disease in the setting of cardiometabolic disturbances.

Methods

Experiments in human aortic endothelial cells

Human aortic endothelial cells (HAECs, passages 5–7) were purchased from Clonetics (Allschwil, Switzerland) and grown in fibronectin-coated 75 cm2 flasks in optimized endothelial growth medium-2 (EGM-2, Clonetics, Allschwil, Switzerland) supplemented with 10% FBS. The cells were detached by using Tripsin/EDTA for 2 minutes and reseeded in fibronectin-coated 10 cm-cell culture dishes or 6 multiwell plates. HAECs were cultured in EGM-2 containing 2% FBS and exposed for 48 hours days to palmitic acid (200 uM) to mimic the effects of obesity. Palmitic acid (Sigma, P5585) was dissolved in ethanol, and a 500 mmol/L stock solution was stored at − 20°C. This stock solution was combined with 5 mmol/L of fatty acid-free bovine serum albumin (BSA) at a molecular ratio of 10:1 (fatty acid:albumin) in serum-free medium.

Small interfering RNA transfection

Transient transfection with commercially available human Gcn5 siRNAs (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Nunningen, Switzerland) was performed using Lipofectamine Reagent (Invitrogen, Basel, Switzerland). As a control, a predesigned scrambled siRNA was used (Microsynth, 5′ UAC ACA CUC UCG UCU CU dTdT 3′). Cytotoxicity was assessed by a colorimetric assay to detect lactate dehydrogenase (LDH; Roche, Basel, Switzerland).

Pharmacological inhibition of Gcn5 in HAECs

A selective inhibitor of Gcn5, cyclopentylidene-[4-(4′-chlorophenyl)thiazol-2-yl)hydrazone (CPTH2), was used in PA-treated HAECs at the final dose of 50 μmol/L for 48 h [37].

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

Chromatin immunoprecipitation was performed in HAECs and human vessels by using the Magna ChIP Assay Kit (Millipore, Billerica, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions [38, 39]. Briefly, samples were fixed for 10 minutes with 37% paraformaldehyde. After stopping cross-linking by the addition of 0.1 M glycine, the tissues were sonicated and centrifuged. ChIP were performed by using 10 μg of anti-Histone H3 (K14) antibody (Cat # MA5-32814, Invitrogen, Switzerland) and 10 μg of anti-GCN5 antibody (sc-365321, Santa Cruz, Swizterland). An equivalent amount of IgG (Invitrogen) was used as a negative control. Washes and elution of the IP DNA were performed according to the Magna ChIP protocol. The promoter region for Nox2 gene was identified by employing Eukaryotic promoter database (EPD) and specific primers were designed. Quantifications were performed using the comparative cycle threshold method and input are reported as the n fold difference in antibody-bound chromatin against the input DNA.

Real time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from human myocardial specimens as well as from human cardiomyocytes using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Before extraction, human myocardial samples were lysed by using a Precellys homogenizer. Conversion of the total cellular RNA to cDNA was carried out with Moloney murine leukemia virus reverse transcriptase and random hexamers (Amersham Bioscience, Piscataway, USA) in a final volume of 33 μl, using 1 μg of cDNA according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Real-time PCR was performed using the SYBR Select Master Mix (Applied biosystems, Thermo Fischer Scientific, Zug, Switzerland) on a Quant Studio 5–7 cyclers (Life Technologies, Thermo Fischer Scientific, Zug, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. GAPDH or TBP were used as endogenous control for normalizing RNA concentration. The amplification program consisted of 1 cycle at 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles with a denaturing phase at 95˚C for 30 s and an annealing and elongation phase of 1 min at 60˚C. A melting curve analysis was performed after amplification to verify the accuracy of the amplicon. Differences in Ct values between test gene and endogenous controls (GAPDH and TBP, ΔCt) were calculated and used for statistical analysis.

Measurements of 3-nitrotyrosine levels

The levels of 3-nitrotyrosine (3-NT), a marker of oxidative damage mediated by peroxynitrite, were measured by a commercially available ELISA kit (ab116691, Abcam) in vascular homogenates and cell lysates.

Assessment of nitric oxide levels

The levels of nitric oxide (NO) were detected by using a colorimetric activity assay (ab65328, Abcam) in vascular homogenates and cell lysates.

Mitochondrial swelling assay

Forty µg of isolated mitochondria from murine vascular specimens in swelling buffer (250 mmol/L sucrose, 10 mmol/L MOPS, 5 µmol/L EGTA, 2 mmol/L MgCl2, 5 mmol/L KH2PO4, 5 mmol/L pyruvate, 5 mmol/L malate) were incubated with 150 µmol/L of calcium chloride (CaCl2) in a final volume of 200 µL in a 96-well plate for 20 minutes, as previously reported.[40] Absorbance was read every 30 seconds at 520 nm [40].

Caspase-3 activity assay

Caspase-3 activity was assessed by using a colorimetric activity assay (ab39401, Abcam), according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Organ chamber experiments

Male mice (C57BL/6N, 8-week-old) were used for the experiments and maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle with unrestricted access to food. Mice were fed a normal (10 kcal% fat, D12450J) and high fat diet (60 kcal% fat, D12492) for 18 weeks. At the end of the experiment, animals were euthanized by intraperitoneal administration of 50 mg/Kg sodium pentobarbital. The entire aorta from the heart to the iliac bifurcation was excised and placed immediately in cold modified Krebs-Ringer bicarbonate solution (pH 7.4, 37°C, 95% O2; 5% CO2) of the following composition (mmol/L): NaCl (118.6), KCl (4.7), CaCl2 (2.5), KH2PO4 (1.2), MgSO4 (1.2), NaHCO3 (25.1), glucose (11.1), and calcium EDTA (0.026). The aorta was cleaned from adhering connective tissue under a dissection microscope, and either snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80°C or used immediately for organ chamber experiments. For isometric tension studies, aorta was cut into 2 mm rings. Each ring was then connected to an isometric force transducer (Multi-Myograph 610 M, Danish Myo Technology), suspended in an organ chamber filled with 6 mL Krebs-Ringer bicarbonate solution (37°C, pH 7.4), and bubbled with 95% O2, 5% CO2. After a 30 minute equilibration period, rings were gradually stretched to the optimal point of their length–tension curve, as determined by the contraction in response to potassium chloride (80 mmol/L). Several rings from the same aorta were studied in parallel. Responses to Ach (10-9 to 10-6 mol/L) were recorded during submaximal contractions to NE (10-7 mol/L). Endothelial vasorelaxation was assessed in aortic rings from obese mice, in the presence or in the absence of ex vivo treatment with the Gcn5 inhibitor CPTH2 (at the final dose of 50 μmol/L) for 3 hours. The NO donor SNP (10-10 to 10-5 mol/L) was added to test endothelium-independent responses. Relaxations were expressed as a percentage of the precontracted tension. Animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care Committee of the University of Zürich, Switzerland.

Study population

The study population included 10 patients with severe abdominal obesity and 10 age-matched non-obese control subjects (Table 1). Obese patients were recruited among consecutive patients referring to the Department of Endocrinology (University of Pisa, Italy) for screening in view of laparoscopic bariatric surgery. The exclusion criteria were history or clinical evidence of hypertension (blood pressure >140/90 mmHg), smoking history, ethanol consumption (more than 60 g or one-half liter of wine/day), hypercholesterolemia, diabetes mellitus, overt cardiovascular disease, renal dysfunction or menopause. Patients taking cardiovascular or metabolic drugs were also excluded. Control subjects were recruited among patients hospitalized in the Surgery Unit (University of Pisa, Italy) to undergo laparoscopic surgery for cholecystectomy. The study protocol was approved by Local Ethical Committee and, in accordance with institutional guidelines, all participants were aware of the investigational nature of the study and gave written consent for their participation.

Anthropometric and biochemical measurements

Sitting blood pressure (average of 3 measurements) was measured with a mercury sphygmomanometer, by means of a standard cuff and a large cuff in lean and obese individuals, respectively. Venous blood samples were taken for measurement of standard haematology and insulin (radioimmunoassay). Insulin resistance was estimated by the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) [32].

Functional experiments in small visceral fat arteries

Endothelium-dependent and endothelium-independent relaxations were assessed in a pressurized myograph by measuring responses to cumulative concentrations of acetylcholine (Ach, 1 nmol/l–100 µmol/l, Sigma Chemicals, St. Louis, Missouri) and sodium nitroprusside (0.01–100 µmol/l, Sigma Aldrich, St Louis, USA), respectively. Vessels were pre-contracted with norepinephrine (NE, 1 µmol/l, Sigma Aldrich, St Louis, USA).

Statistical analysis

Comparisons of continuous variables were performed using unpaired two-sample t-test and Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test. Multiple comparisons were performed by one-way analysis of variance, followed by Bonferroni correction. Adjusted P values were calculated by multiplying raw P values by g, where g indicates the number of comparisons. Between variable correlations were assessed by Spearman’s test. Probability values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism Software (version 7.03).

Results

Gcn5 regulates Nox2 transcription in human endothelial cells exposed to metabolic stress

In order to mimic the effects of obesity on the vascular endothelium, primary human aortic endothelial cells (HAECs) were exposed to palmitic acid (PA). PA treatment for 48h led to the upregulation of the histone acetyltransferase Gcn5 as compared to control cells (Fig. 1A). Given the role of Gcn5 in regulating the pro-oxidant enzyme Nox2 in immune cells [34], we next determined Nox2 expression and reactive oxygen species levels in our setting. Gcn5 elevation was associated with Nox2 upregulation, increased ROS formation, reduced nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability and cell death in PA-treated HAECs (Fig. 1B-E). To investigate the active involvement of Gcn5 in Nox2 transcription, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay experiments. We found a strong enrichment of Gcn5 and its chromatin mark (acetylated lysine 14 at histone 3, H3K14ac) on the promoter of Nox2 in PA-treated vs control HAECs (Fig. 1F-G). Of note, both gene silencing and pharmacological inhibition of Gcn5 by CPTH2 prevented PA-induced Nox2 upregulation, increased oxidative stress and apoptosis, as assessed by caspase-3 activity (Fig. 2A-D).

Gcn5 inhibition rescues obesity-related endothelial dysfunction

To test the relevance of our in vitro findings, we next investigated the ex vivo effects of Gcn5 inhibition on endothelial vasorelaxation in obese mice. Obese mice displayed impaired endothelial vasorelaxation to acetylcholine as compared to lean controls (Fig. 3A). Endothelium-independent relaxation was comparable in obese and controls animals, suggesting a pivotal contribution of the vascular endothelium in our setting (Fig. 3B). In line with our observations in endothelial cells, vessels from obese mice showed high levels of oxidative stress and Nox upregulation as compared to controls (Fig. 3C-D). ChIP assay confirmed an increase of Gcn5/H3K14 occupancy on Nox2 promoter in vessels from obese vs control mice (Fig. 3E-F). Consistent with Nox2 upregulation and enhanced oxidative stress, vessels from obese mice displayed reduced mitochondrial integrity, as shown by mitochondrial swelling assay (Fig.3G). For the assessment of organelle swelling, isolated mitochondria were challenged with calcium overload and the rate of swelling determined by light scattering. Organelles from vessels of lean mice showed stable absorbance throughout the 20-min time-course. In contrast, organelles from obese vessels displayed mitochondrial swelling (Fig. 3G). The latter observation pinpoints Gcn5 as a pivotal epigenetic orchestrator of Nox2 in the vasculature. Of interest, pharmacological inhibition of Gcn5 by CPTH2 attenuated endothelial dysfunction in obese mice (Fig. 3A). The observed improvement of endothelial function was associated with reduced oxidative stress and preserved mitochondrial integrity (Fig. 3C and G). This set of experiments in mouse vessels indicate a casual involvement of Gcn5 in obesity-related, ROS-induced vascular dysfunction.

Gcn5 upregulation and endothelial dysfunction in obese patients

To investigate whether our findings hold true in the human vasculature, we next checked Gcn5 expression in visceral fat arteries (VFA) from obese patients and age-matched controls. The characteristics of study population are shown in Table 1. Isometric tension studies in VFA showed an impairment of acetylcholine-induced vasorelaxation in obese subjects when compared with the controls (Fig. 4A), whereas response to sodium nitroprusside (SNP) did not differ between the two groups (Fig. 4B). Pre-treatment with the antioxidant ascorbic acid restored relaxation to acetylcholine in vessels from obese patients, suggesting that ROS contribute to endothelial dysfunction in this setting (Fig. 4C). Gene expression of Gcn5 and its target Nox2 was significantly higher in obese when compared with control VFA (Fig. 4D-E). Moreover, messenger RNA levels of Gcn5 negatively correlated with endothelium-dependent vasorelaxation in VFA from obese patients (Fig. 4F). In conclusion, our results suggest that Gcn5 is a new epigenetic target regulating Nox-2-driven vascular oxidative stress in experimental and human obesity. Discussion

In this work, we demonstrate that the histone acetyltransferase Gcn5 plays an important role in the epigenetic regulation of vascular oxidative stress in obesity. Our conclusions are supported by the following observations: (i) Endothelial expression of Gcn5 is increased in conditions of metabolic stress and associates with Nox2 upregulation, oxidative stress, defective NO availability and cell apoptosis; (ii) Gcn5 epigenetically regulates Nox2 by leading to increased H3K14 acetylation on its promoter; (iii) Pharmacological inhibition of Gcn5 by CPTH2 prevents obesity-related oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction in vitro and ex vivo; (iv) Gcn5 is upregulated in vessels from obese patients and negatively correlated with endothelial vasorelaxation.

Growing evidence suggests the growing importance of epigenetic remodeling in the phenotypic alterations observed in obesity [35]. Epigenetics is defined by the complex interaction between the genome and the environment [8]. While the genome is relatively stable over time, the epigenome is much more sensitive to external stimuli such as metabolic stress, pollution, cigarette smoking or noise. The plasticity of the epigenome plays a crucial role in the context of complex multifactorial diseases such as obesity [41]. In this setting, specific epigenetic marks remodel gene expression to foster inflammation, oxidative stress and cardiovascular dysfunction. Notably, epigenetic changes are heritable and are being transmitted to the offspring both via a maternal and paternal transmission [42]. The latter aspect deserves attention and might contribute to molecular alterations and early vascular dysfunction among children and young adults. This could contribute to explaining the high prevalence of cardiometabolic traits and endothelial dysfunction in obese children [43]. Hence, understanding the link between chronic metabolic stress, epigenetic regulation and vascular dysfunction is of paramount importance for the development of new mechanism-based strategies.

An important source of vascular ROS in obesity is the Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide Phosphate Oxidase (NADPH oxidase), a multiprotein complex that is expressed both in phagocytes and endothelial cells [31]. In mice, high-fat diet-induced obesity is associated with an increased expression of the p47phox subunit and activation of the NADPH oxidase, mediating an overexpression of Toll-like receptor (TLR) − 2 and − 4 and upregulation of proinflammatory cytokines and pathways involved in vascular remodeling [44]. The role of NADPH oxidase hyperactivation in the obesity-related vascular dysfunction has been confirmed in humans using in vivo and ex vivo approaches [31, 45, 46]. In the present study, we show that epigenetic changes induced by Gcn5 are required for the upregulation of a major NADPH subunit, namely Nox2. ChIP assays confirmed Gcn5 and H3K14 enrichment on Nox2 promoter while the use of a specific epi-drug inhibiting Gcn5, namely CPTH2, blunted Nox2 levels and vascular oxidative stress. In line with our results, previous work has demonstrated a pivotal role of Gcn5 in orchestrating Nox2 expression in leucocytes [34]. In this study, the activation of Gcn5 was essential for the generation of high superoxide anion levels. Given the important role of leucocyte-derived oxidative stress as a defensive mechanism against pathogens [47], we speculate that Gcn5 might be protective in immune cells while its dysregulation in the vasculature is detrimental. Further work is needed to elucidate cell-specific changes in Gcn5 expression and their contribution to different ROS species.

In line with our results, a growing body of evidence suggests a prominent role of Gcn5-induced chromatin remodeling in the pathogenesis of several comorbidities such as cancer, osteoporosis and diabetes [13]. GCN5 plays a critical role in the tumorigenesis and pathogenesis of different cancers [14]. It is ectopically expressed in various human cancers and is associated with cancer development, progression, and poor prognosis [13]. In diabetes, Gcn5 has emerged as an important regulator of hepatic gluconeogenesis and a suppressor of PGC1α signaling [24]. The latter is master regulator of cellular metabolism critically involved in mitochondrial biogenesis, organelle integrity and metabolism [48]. Consistent with these results, in our study we show that Gcn5 inhibition by CPTH2 preserved mitochondrial integrity while preventing endothelial cell apoptosis. The detrimental action of Gcn5 in the setting of cancer, diabetes and obesity-related vascular disease could also contribute to the growing and bidirectional relation between cancer and cardiometabolic disease [49, 50]. In this regard, Gcn5 could represent a sensor of metabolic stress critically implicated in chromatin rewiring, ROS generation and DNA damage. Future studies should determine whether Gcn5 could serve as a potential biomarker of cardiometabolic stress and increased cancer risk. Potential challenges in the direction are represented by the fact that Gcn5 is not secreted, and tissue analyses or investigation of circulating cells (i.e. immune cells) would be required to assess its upregulation and effects on chromatin activation in humans.

In our study, we found that Gcn5 upregulation was associated with endothelial dysfunction and increased ROS accumulation, suggesting a detrimental role in obesity-induced vascular stress. However, previous studies have reported beneficial effects of Gcn5 activation in both the micro- and macrovasculature, indicating that its impact on vascular homeostasis may be complex and context-dependent. In the microvasculature, Gcn5 activation has been shown to promote endothelial cell function and angiogenesis through enhanced VEGF-A signaling and improved chromatin accessibility at pro-angiogenic gene loci [51, 52]. Conversely, in the macrovasculature, Gcn5 activation may contribute to vascular stability by inhibiting vascular smooth muscle cell migration and neointima formation via increased α-tubulin acetylation, which enhances microtubule stability and limits directional cell migration [53]. This duality suggests that while Gcn5 activation could support beneficial microvascular remodeling and angiogenesis in settings such as ischemia or wound healing, it may also help prevent pathological macrovascular remodeling in conditions like atherosclerosis or restenosis [54]. The net vascular effect of Gcn5 modulation is therefore likely to depend on the vascular bed involved and the underlying disease context.

Our findings provide important insights into endothelial dysfunction and ROS generation; however, this study did not specifically investigate inflammatory pathways, which are critical contributors to vascular disease. Previous studies have demonstrated a potential role for Gcn5 in vascular inflammation. In endothelial and vascular cells, Gcn5 can regulate inflammatory signaling by acetylating non-histone substrates such as NF-κB p65, thereby enhancing NF-κB–dependent transcription of pro-inflammatory genes. Moreover, Gcn5’s interactions with cofactors such as CITED2 and PCAF integrate it into broader epigenetic networks governing cytokine expression, leukocyte adhesion, and endothelial activation. Notably, members of the GNAT family of HATs, including Gcn5, have been linked to endothelial senescence, oxidative stress, and reduced NO bioavailability—processes closely intertwined with vascular inflammation [17, 18].

In addition, growing evidence supports the involvement of the PGC-1α/NF-κB axis in obesity and vascular dysfunction. Although direct evidence of altered PGC-1α expression in the obese vasculature remains limited, metabolic stress and chronic inflammation in obesity are known to downregulate PGC-1α in vascular tissues. This is particularly relevant given that Gcn5 can acetylate and inhibit PGC-1α, thereby attenuating its anti-inflammatory and mitochondrial biogenesis functions [55]. Reduced PGC-1α activity may, in turn, facilitate NF-κB activation and sustain inflammatory signaling, establishing a feed-forward loop that exacerbates endothelial dysfunction.

We should also acknowledge that histone acetylation is orchestrated by a diverse array of histone acetyltransferases (HATs), many of which can target overlapping lysine residues on histone tails. In the vascular context, HATs such as p300, CBP, PCAF, and TIP60 have been shown to modulate gene expression programs governing endothelial function, inflammation, and vascular remodeling. For instance, p300 and CBP both acetylate histone H3 lysine residues (e.g., H3K27) yet exert non-redundant effects on vascular smooth muscle phenotype, whereas PCAF acetylates histone H3K14 and has been implicated in NF-κB–driven inflammatory signalling [17, 18]. TIP60, a MYST-family HAT, also targets overlapping lysine sites and regulates stress-response pathways, including autophagy and DNA repair, which are critical for endothelial homeostasis [19]. Given this enzymatic overlap, it is plausible that other HATs could partially compensate for reduced Gcn5 activity at specific lysine residues. Such redundancy may preserve basal acetylation levels while altering the transcriptional specificity or magnitude of downstream gene expression responses. This concept is supported by evidence that Gcn5 inhibition can induce compensatory upregulation of PCAF, highlighting the interconnected and adaptive nature of histone acetylation networks.

Our study has some limitations. First, we focused on the epigenetic regulation of Nox2 without considering other players of the game involved in ROS generation; however, previous work has identified Nox2 as a main epigenetic target of Gcn5 in immune cells [34]. Other NOX isoforms may also play important roles in vascular dysfunction. NOX1 contributes to endothelial dysfunction through galectin-3–dependent upregulation, leading to excessive ROS production, impaired vasodilation, and participation in TNF-α–mediated hypertensive signaling. NOX4 exerts context-dependent effects, namely an anti-inflammatory action in adipose tissue via KLF4. However, NOX4 activity was also linked to increased ROS production, leading to oxidative damage of cellular components like proteins and lipids [56, 57]. Taken together, these observations highlight that—in addition to NOX2—NOX1 and NOX4 represent distinct contributors to obesity-related vascular pathology, underscoring the need for therapeutic strategies that selectively target specific NOX isoforms [56, 57]. Second, our study has investigated Gcn5 role in endothelial cells whereas its function in other vascular cells, namely smooth muscle cells, fibroblasts and pericytes remain to be determined. This could help to broaden our view on Gcn5 as a vascular epigenetic target and could unveil new functions of the genes on other signaling pathways related to vascular damage such as inflammation and senescence. Third, ex vivo experiments were carried out in male mice and information on the role of Gcn5 in the vasculature of female mice is lacking. Future work should address sex and gender-related aspects in obesity-induced Gcn5 activation.

In conclusion, in this study we unveiled Gcn5 as new epigenetic regulator of vascular oxidative stress in obesity. The development of new epi-drugs testing Gcn5 inhibition in preclinical and human models of cardiometabolic disease represents an attractive avenue to tackle vascular dysfunction in metabolic disorders. Future work should focus on developing and testing more selective GCN5 inhibitors in robust pre-clinical models, and eventually in human studies, to evaluate their potential in preventing or treating vascular dysfunction in metabolic disorders.
