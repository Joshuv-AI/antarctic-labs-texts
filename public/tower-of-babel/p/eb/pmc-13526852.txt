Damage evolution mechanism and energy response of sandstone under triaxial compression
Authors: Bo Zhou, Xukun Wu, Bobo Li, Jianhua Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the mechanical behavior, damage evolution, and energy response of sandstone under high confining stresses, a critical issue in deep underground engineering. A series of triaxial compression tests were conducted on sandstone specimens under confining stresses of 3, 6, 9, 12, and 15 MPa to systematically analyze the influence of confining stress on the complete stress-strain response and the associated energy evolution. Based on the experimental results, a statistical damage constitutive model incorporating energy dissipation was developed, in which the failure probability of microscopic elements is described by the Weibull distribution and damage evolution is driven by energy dissipation. The results show that increasing confining stress significantly enhances both peak and residual strength: as confining stress increases from 3 MPa to 15 MPa, peak strength increases by 78% (from 43.365 MPa to 77.191 MPa) and residual strength increases by 196% (from 16.301 MPa to 48.241 MPa), while the failure mode transitions from brittle to ductile behavior. Energy analysis reveals that the deformation and failure process is governed by energy accumulation and dissipation; the pre-peak stage is dominated by elastic energy accumulation, whereas the post-peak stage is characterized by a sharp increase in dissipated energy. Elevated confining stress substantially enhances the energy storage limit, which at 15 MPa reaches 0.3322 MJ/m3—58.4% higher than at 3 MPa. The established constitutive model accurately captures the full-stage mechanical behavior from compaction and elasticity to yielding and residual strength, with theoretical curves showing good agreement with experimental data. Model parameters m, F0, and q exhibit clear physical meanings, reflecting strength, brittleness, and residual strength characteristics, respectively. Furthermore, an energy catastrophe criterion for instability failure is proposed. Analysis based on this criterion shows that as confining stress increases from 3 MPa to 15 MPa, the damage energy release rate Yc increases by over 120% (from 0.043 to 0.096), and the failure intensity index FId rises from 3.76 to 5.40, indicating a stronger tendency toward dynamic catastrophe under high confining stresses. This study clarifies the controlling role of confining stress on the mechanical behavior, energy evolution, and failure mode of sandstone, providing quantitative indicators and a theoretical basis for stability analysis and hazard prevention in deep engineering rock masses.

Introduction

With the continuous expansion of global resource extraction and underground space development to greater depths, the complex mechanical environment characterized by “triple-high and intense disturbance”—specifically high in-situ stress, high ground temperature, high seepage pressure, and intense mining-induced disturbance—has emerged as a critical challenge in the field of rock mechanics and engineering1. Under such conditions, engineering surrounding rock is subjected to significantly elevated in-situ stress levels, and its mechanical behavior exhibits pronounced confining stress effects and nonlinear characteristics. As a common sedimentary rock in underground engineering, the deformation, damage, and failure process of sandstone under high-stress conditions is, in essence, a non-equilibrium thermodynamic process driven by external energy input and governed by internal energy transformation and dissipation2. This process is accompanied by the accumulation of elastic energy and the release of dissipated energy. Once the accumulated energy exceeds the storage limit of the rock mass itself or the dissipation becomes unstable, it may trigger dynamic disasters such as rockburst or roof disaster, posing a serious threat to the safety of deep engineering projects3,4. Therefore, it is of critical theoretical and practical significance to thoroughly elucidate, from the perspective of energy evolution, the governing mechanism of confining stress on the damage and failure behavior of sandstone, and to establish a constitutive model capable of accurately describing its mechanical response. This work is essential for the scientific assessment of deep rock mass stability, as well as for the prediction and prevention of engineering disasters.

Damage mechanics has been introduced into rock mechanics to characterize key behaviors under complex stress states, including nonlinearity, irreversible deformation, and strength degradation5. Since Dougill pioneered the application of damage concepts to geological materials, significant research efforts have been dedicated to developing damage constitutive models for rock6. Early work in this area focused on analyzing stress-strain compression curves to model the mechanical properties of rock masses, leading to the formulation of models such as the J-Mazars model, linear models, and linear elastic models7–11. While these models have been applied in engineering to represent stress and strain changes within specific stress ranges, notable deviations between model predictions and experimental data have often been observed. Subsequent studies categorized damage mechanics according to the scale of defect structures in the medium into macro-continuum approaches12,13 and micro-meso theories14. To address material heterogeneity, statistical models employing mathematical statistics have gained prominence. Many of these models, often based on the random distribution of micro-defects, utilize Weibull or power functions to describe the probability distribution of micro-element strength15,16. Alternative methods, such as using axial strain to represent micro-element strength, have also been proposed17. Furthermore, statistical constitutive models incorporating different strength criteria (e.g., Mohr-Coulomb, Drucker-Prager, Hoek-Brown) have been established to calculate damage variables that quantify rock degradation under load. For instance, Deng and Gu18 introduced the maximum entropy density function into the damage variable to derive a statistical model describing rock behavior under strain. Similarly, Zhou et al.19 developed a damage constitutive model for rock under cyclic stress and temperature based on statistical damage theory.

While macroscopic stress-strain curves are commonly used to describe the mechanical behavior of rock, they often fail to adequately capture its degradation characteristics20. Under complex stress conditions, the failure of rock is fundamentally an energy-driven instability process, making energy evolution a critical and effective perspective for characterizing mechanical properties21. Research into the energy characteristics of rock materials has revealed consistent patterns. Studies on coal under varying gas pressures show that total absorbed energy, along with dissipated and elastic strain energy, increases with stress prior to failure22. During loading, strain energy accumulates in rock and can be released abruptly upon reaching a critical threshold, leading to rupture23. Triaxial compression tests further elucidate energy transfer and transformation during deformation and failure. For instance, analysis of granite under different loading paths indicates that strain energy changes exhibit distinct stage characteristics, and the strain energy conversion rate can reflect the deformation and damage degree under various conditions24. Similarly, studies on dense sandstone have quantified the laws of energy dissipation and release associated with crack growth under different confining stresses, analyzing the relationship between releasable elastic strain energy, dissipated energy density, and confining stress25. The influence of confining stress on energy evolution has also been examined through triaxial cyclic loading-unloading tests26. Throughout progressive failure, rocks continuously undergo energy absorption, accumulation, and dissipation27. The continuous dispersion of energy initiates and propagates micro-cracks, gradually damaging the internal structure28. Although energy remains balanced throughout the deformation process, a sudden release of elastic strain energy is a primary cause of violent failure in coal29. Consequently, damage development in stressed rock is closely tied to energy dissipation characteristics. To explicitly link damage to energy change, cyclic loading tests have been employed, confirming that sample failure is closely associated with energy release30. Based on energy mechanisms, a damage coefficient has also been proposed to analyze the mechanical properties and damage evolution in rock from an energy perspective31.

In summary, while existing research on rock damage and failure mechanisms has extensively focused on developing constitutive models or exploring energy evolution principles, studies that specifically investigate the correlation between damage-induced deformation characteristics and energy dissipation features in sandstone under load remain scarce. Most available work is limited to qualitative analysis of their relationship. Furthermore, research that integrates both damage and energy perspectives to analyze the deformation and failure laws of sandstone under triaxial compression, while considering the effects of both confining stress and gas pressure, is particularly rare. To investigate the damage and failure mechanisms of sandstone during compression, this study considers the influence of varying confining and gas pressures on its mechanical properties. We derive the expression for the plastic strain function of sandstone during triaxial compression, analyze its plastic strain behavior and stress-strain response, and propose mathematical expressions for various energy parameters during the compression process under the influence of confining and gas pressures. From an energy perspective, a damage stress-strain function is theoretically formulated to characterize the degradation of sandstone. This function incorporates a heterogeneous Weibull distribution function, an energy dissipation function, and the plastic strain function. Building on this foundation, a damage constitutive model for sandstone based on energy dissipation under confining and gas pressure effects is developed. The model is used to examine the damage evolution behavior of sandstone during compressive failure, thereby elucidating the relationship between energy dissipation and damage-induced deformation characteristics in sandstone.

Testing program

Specimen preparation

The specimens used in this study were cored from a natural sandstone block obtained from a geological stratum in a mining area of China. To ensure the uniformity and comparability of the test results, all specimens were extracted from the same intact rock block, which was free of visible macroscopic fractures. The preparation of the specimens strictly followed the standard procedures suggested by the International Society for Rock Mechanics (ISRM).

First, cylindrical rock cores with a diameter of 50 mm were drilled from the parent block using a diamond drilling technique. These cores were then cut into rough specimens with a height of approximately 100 mm using a rock saw. To ensure the flatness and parallelism of the two end surfaces, the specimens were precision-ground using a high-accuracy double-face grinder. The final specimens were standard right cylinders with dimensions of 50 mm in diameter and 100 mm in height. The non-parallelism and non-perpendicularity of the end surfaces were controlled to be less than 0.02 mm.

After preparation, all specimens were subjected to basic physical property tests, including dry density and P-wave velocity measurements, to screen for consistency in initial physical properties. P-wave velocity was measured prior to testing, and only specimens with velocities between 2700 m/s and 3000 m/s were selected to ensure uniformity. Visually intact, defect-free specimens with comparable physical properties were then oven-dried at 105 ℃ for 24 h to eliminate moisture effects and subsequently stored in a desiccator until testing. Experimental procedure

The test system employed is multi-functional triaxial test system (Fig. 1), which is located in the State Key Laboratory of Geomechanics and Geotechnical Engineering Safety, Wuhan, China. This system is capable of independently and precisely applying axial pressure, confining stress, and pore pressure. It is equipped with high-accuracy sensors for the real-time monitoring of axial load, axial displacement, and circumferential displacement.

The conventional triaxial compression test method was employed to investigate the influence of confining stress on the mechanical behavior and energy evolution of the sandstone. Five different confining stress (σ3) levels were specifically selected: 3, 6, 9, 12, and 15 MPa.

Prior to testing, the dried sandstone specimen was jacketed with heat-shrink tubing and a sealing jacket, and then mounted inside the triaxial pressure cell. The confining stress was first applied hydrostatically at a rate of 0.5 MPa/s until the predetermined target value was reached and maintained constant. Subsequently, axial load was applied under displacement control at a constant rate of 0.1 mm/min (corresponding to a quasi-static strain rate of approximately 1.67 × 10⁻⁵ s⁻¹). Loading continued until the specimen experienced complete failure and entered the residual strength stage, thereby obtaining a complete stress-strain curve. Throughout the loading process, data for axial stress, axial strain (measured by a built-in Linear Variable Differential Transformer, LVDT), and circumferential strain (measured by a circumferential extensometer mounted on the specimen’s mid-height) were automatically and synchronously recorded at a sampling frequency of 10 Hz.

This experimental procedure was designed to systematically reveal the complete mechanical response of the sandstone, encompassing the stages of initial compaction, linear elasticity, plastic yielding, post-peak softening, and residual flow. It provides a robust experimental foundation for the subsequent development of an energy-based damage constitutive model.

Sandstone typically contains a large number of randomly distributed pores and microcracks, exhibiting inherent heterogeneity and stochastic defect distribution. Under external loading, its damage-mechanical behavior cannot be adequately characterized by a single parameter, resulting in a broad range of mechanical strength. To capture this heterogeneous nature, it is noted that behaviors such as the propagation of pre-existing pores and fractures, the initiation and growth of new voids, and the associated damage and plastic deformation all consume a certain amount of energy. Energy dissipation is closely linked to the damage-induced deformation of sandstone: the greater the work done by dissipated energy under a given stress state, the more severe the damage degradation of the sandstone35. When investigating the damage evolution law during the deformation and failure process of sandstone, it is essential to consider the influence of energy dissipation on its damage-induced deformation. Taking the test results under a 3 MPa confining stress as an example, during the initial compaction stage, the deviatoric stress increases and the pre-existing pores and microcracks within the sandstone gradually close. Both the axial strain and lateral strain increase slowly, with the lateral direction showing slight dilation due to the Poisson effect. The volumetric strain, being predominantly compressive, remains positive and continues to increase, and the curve exhibits a concave-upward shape, indicating that the net volume of the rock is continuously compressed. When the axial strain reaches 0.0029, the specimen enters the linear elastic stage. In this stage, the deviatoric stress shows an approximately linear relationship with both axial strain and lateral strain; the magnitudes of both strains increase linearly. However, axial compression remains dominant, causing the volumetric strain to stay positive and continue rising, consistent with the generalized Hooke’s law. The elastic modulus remains constant throughout this stage. The deviatoric stress increases from 9.928 MPa to 39.013 MPa during linear elastic deformation. As the axial strain reaches 0.0111, the specimen enters the yield stage. Cracks inside the sandstone begin to propagate and new microcracks initiate. The growth rate of axial strain slows, while the absolute value of lateral strain starts to increase markedly, indicating accelerated lateral dilation. Consequently, the rate of increase in volumetric strain slows significantly, and after reaching a peak it begins to decrease, marking the transition from volumetric compression to dilation. The stress-strain curve adopts a convex‑upward shape. Under 3 MPa confining stress, the peak deviatoric stress of the specimen is 43.365 MPa, corresponding to an axial strain of 0.0151. Beyond this point, the specimen enters the stress drop stage. Macroscopic fracture occurs, leading to a partial loss of load-bearing capacity. The deviatoric stress drops sharply, and the absolute value of lateral strain increases rapidly. The volumetric strain turns from positive to negative, showing pronounced volumetric dilation, and the curve exhibits a steep decline. In this stage, the deviatoric stress falls from 43.365 MPa to 16.301 MPa, while the axial strain increases to 0.0203. Upon entering the residual stage, the rock maintains a residual strength through friction between broken fragments. The absolute values of axial strain and lateral strain continue to grow but at gradually decelerating rates. The volumetric strain changes slowly in the negative direction, and the overall curve gradually stabilizes.

With increasing confining stress, a significant enhancement in the mechanical properties of the rock specimens can be observed. Under confining stresses of 3, 6, 9, 12, and 15 MPa, the peak stresses of the sandstone are 43.365, 55.566, 61.434, 69.186, and 77.191 MPa, respectively. Relative to the initial 3 MPa confining stress, these represent increases of 28%, 41%, 59%, and 78%. The corresponding deviatoric stresses at the residual stage under these confining stresses are 16.301, 26.132, 34.389, 45.483, and 48.241 MPa, showing increases of 60%, 111%, 179%, and 196% relative to the 3 MPa case. It is evident that under high confining stresses, the sandstone retains a considerable load-bearing capacity even after failure, and the residual capacity increases with higher confining stress. The stress drops observed at 3, 6, 9, 12, and 15 MPa are 27.06, 29.43, 27.05, 23.70, and 28.95 MPa, respectively. Although the magnitude of stress drop shows no clear correlation with confining stress, the curves indicate that increasing confining stress reduces brittleness and enhances plasticity in the sandstone. It has also been observed that under high confining stress, while rock deformation exhibits plastic behavior, the fracturing process is more intense. Therefore, in deep underground engineering, greater attention should be paid to the effects of excavation disturbances to prevent instability incidents.

Although the fitting accuracy of the theoretical curves in this stage is somewhat lower than in the pre-peak stage, both the theoretical and experimental curves show a similar sharp drop at the peak, and their overall trends remain highly consistent. Judging from the evolving trends of the theoretical and experimental curves-across the initial compaction, linear elastic, yield, stress-drop, and residual stages-the proposed sandstone damage constitutive model demonstrates good rationality and feasibility.

The verification based on the mechanical behavior of sandstone under different confining stresses reveals that the parameters q, m, and F0 in the model all exhibit a systematic dependence on confining stress: as the confining stress increases, the values of q, m, and F0 show an increasing trend. The physical mechanism underlying this trend is that higher confining stress effectively suppresses the random propagation and localization of micro-cracks. This suppresses the brittle, discrete damage mode observed under low confinement and promotes a more homogeneous, plasticity deformation mode under high confinement. Consequently, the defect distribution becomes more concentrated and uniform, as reflected by the increase in the parameters q and m, while the macroscopic strength and damage stress threshold increase significantly, as reflected by the rise in F0. This regularity not only provides a reasonable statistical-damage-based explanation for the intrinsic mechanism of the brittle-to-plasticity transition in sandstone, but also verifies the effectiveness of the proposed constitutive model in capturing confining-pressure effects. It thereby offers a theoretical basis for analyzing damage evolution and predicting the strength of rock masses in deep underground engineering.

Discussion

Energy characteristic analysis

The macroscopic mechanical response, characterized by the stress-strain relationship, is essentially the result of the distribution, transformation, and dissipation of externally input energy within the rock mass. The damage evolution process in sandstone under stress fundamentally involves the mutual conversion between recoverable elastic energy and irreversible dissipated energy. Therefore, building upon the triaxial compression tests on sandstone under different confining stresses and the verification of the constitutive model, this section aims to further reveal the intrinsic physical nature and energy-driven mechanism of its damage and failure. From the perspective of energy conservation and transformation, this approach quantitatively analyzes how confining stress regulates the accumulation and release of energy and influences damage dissipation, thereby providing a deeper physical interpretation of the energetic origin of parameter evolution and the mechanism underlying the macroscopic transition of sandstone from brittle fracture to plastic flow.

According to the energy calculation formula for the damage degradation process of sandstone under confining stress, the evolution of total absorbed energy (U), elastic energy (Ue), and dissipated energy (Ud) with increasing axial strain for sandstone under confining stresses of 3, 6, 9, 12, and 15 MPa is shown in Fig. 5. During the crack closure stage, both total energy (U) and elastic energy (Ue) increase with axial strain. Although dissipated energy (Ud) shows a slight increase, its magnitude is very small. The curve of elastic energy essentially coincides with that of total energy, with a negligible difference between them, indicating that the energy dissipation during the compaction and closure of the well-developed microcracks within the sandstone is very low.

In the linear elastic stage, the elastic energy curve begins to diverge from the total energy curve as axial strain increases, yet the total energy is still primarily converted into elastic energy, which is stored in the deformation of the sandstone’s internal structure. The proportion of dissipated energy (Ud) relative to the total energy (U) remains small and increases slowly, mainly consumed by friction between microcracks during the further compaction process.

During the yield stage, the elastic energy continues to increase but at a gradually decelerating rate, and its proportion within the total energy (U) continuously decreases. Meanwhile, the dissipated energy used for the initiation and propagation of microcracks within the sandstone’s internal structure gradually increases, marking the onset of sandstone failure.

Upon entering the stress drop stage, the sandstone undergoes brittle failure when the axial stress reaches the peak compressive strength. This occurs because the stored elastic energy reaches its maximum and is released instantaneously, causing a large number of microcracks within the rock mass to connect and coalesce into macroscopic fractures, leading to its failure.

In the residual stage, due to the interaction between friction and confining stress, the sandstone retains a certain load-bearing capacity even after failure. The conversion of total energy (U) is still dominated by dissipated energy (Ud), which drives further damage deformation. As deformation and failure progress further, the asperities within the sandstone’s internal structure are worn down or sheared off, resulting in relatively pronounced plastic deformation.

Under different confining stresses, the characteristics of the energy curves during the triaxial compression failure process of sandstone are similar. However, there are differences in the energy values when the axial stress reaches the compressive strength. It can be seen that the change in confining stress has a significant influence on the energy characteristics of sandstone at the peak stress point. The total absorbed energy (U) at the peak stress point increases with higher confining stress, and both the elastic energy (Ue) and dissipated energy (Ud) also show an increasing trend.

The reason for this is that under increased confining stress, the enhanced peak strength and elastic modulus of sandstone enable it to absorb more total energy during the damage and failure process. Consequently, the elastic energy (Ue) accumulated within its internal structure also increases, leading to a larger instantaneous release of elastic energy (Ue) when the stress reaches the peak point. Therefore, increased confining stress results in greater accumulation of elastic energy within the sandstone. Upon failure, this energy is converted into larger amounts of dissipated energy, which can potentially trigger dynamic hazards such as rockbursts and roof failures.

The relationship between sandstone damage and energy

The nonlinear damage failure of sandstone under triaxial stress is fundamentally driven by the accumulation and dissipation of energy. Therefore, investigating the deformation and failure characteristics of sandstone requires identifying the evolution law linking its damage-induced deformation features and energy dissipation. This section statistically analyzes the energy dissipation and damage variable values during sandstone compression under different confining stresses to examine their relationship. Figure 9 illustrates the correlation between damage and energy dissipation in sandstone under various confining stresses. The figure shows that under triaxial stress, energy is consumed by plastic damage and deformation behaviors within the sandstone’s structure, such as the propagation of pre-existing pores and fractures, and the initiation and growth of new ones. In other words, as the external load increases, the continuous accumulation and release of dissipated energy intensify the degree of damage degradation in the sandstone. Moreover, the trends of energy dissipation and damage variation during deformation and failure exhibit strong consistency with increasing axial strain. During triaxial compression under different confining stresses, both energy dissipation and damage evolution in sandstone undergo three distinct phases: slow growth, rapid growth, and another phase of slow growth, collectively exhibiting an “S-shaped” evolution pattern.

At the initial loading stage, due to the well-developed pre-existing pores, fractures, and joints within the sandstone and the relatively low applied stress, the generated plastic deformation is minor. Consequently, the damage variable, which represents the degree of sandstone degradation, develops slowly. Simultaneously, the total energy absorbed by the sandstone is primarily converted into elastic energy and stored internally. Thus, both the energy dissipation and the damage variable remain low and increase at a slow rate during the early phase of loading.

As axial stress continues to increase, the propagation of pre-existing pores and fractures, along with the initiation of new microcracks, dissipates a substantial amount of energy, leading to a marked rise in dissipated energy. This period is characterized by the rapid development of plastic deformation and internal structural damage.

When the sandstone approaches failure, the elastic energy accumulated within the rock is released abruptly and in large quantities. The sharp increase in dissipated energy causes numerous internal microcracks to interconnect and coalesce into macroscopic fractures, resulting in significant plastic deformation and severe damage in the sandstone. Accordingly, the damage variable increases dramatically within a short period.

Subsequently, the sandstone enters the residual deformation stage. During this phase, the asperities within the sandstone’s internal structure are worn down or sheared off, and under the effect of confining stress, compaction and closure phenomena occur. The rates of energy dissipation and damage evolution slow down and gradually stabilize.

Throughout the entire deformation and failure process of the rock mass, the evolution of energy dissipation and damage under different confining and gas pressures consistently follows an “S-shaped” trend. This indicates an intricate link between continuous energy dissipation and internal damage within the rock, further validating the rationality of using energy parameters to quantitatively analyze and calculate the damage variable during the compressive failure process of sandstone.

The influence of model parameters on the characteristics of calculated curves

Building upon the verification of the model, an analysis of its parameters was conducted to further validate its rationality. Figure 10 illustrates the influence of different values of q, m, and F0, respectively, on the shape of the sandstone damage constitutive model curve under a confining stress of 6 MPa, while keeping other parameters constant.

As shown in Fig. 10, with an increase in the Weibull distribution parameter m, the peak strength of sandstone increases, the post-peak curve becomes steeper, and the stress drop rate rises. This indicates a reduction in the ductility and an enhancement of the brittleness of the sandstone. Therefore, the parameter m reflects both the strength and the brittleness characteristics of the rock. With an increase in the Weibull distribution parameter F0, both the peak stress and strain of the sandstone increase, signifying a higher peak strength for the rock. However, the theoretical curve shows no significant change in shape, indicating that F0 primarily reflects the magnitude of the rock strength. As the damage correction coefficient q decreases, the post-peak curve becomes gentler and the stress drop rate slows, demonstrating that q mainly governs the characteristics of residual deformation and residual strength.

In summary, the shape of the constructed damage constitutive model curve is jointly determined by the Weibull distribution parameters m, F0, and the damage correction coefficient q.

Among these, the destabilization and failure of the rock solid skeleton constitute the “physical threshold” for outburst initiation: under the effect of excavation disturbances, damage in the sandstone accumulates until reaching a critical state. When the energy release rate of the solid structure exceeds its dissipation capacity, the originally confined rock is instantaneously transformed into ejected kinetic energy. Therefore, the essence of establishing an energy-based rock instability criterion lies not only in describing the fracturing behavior of the material but also in providing a quantitative indicator for the “instant of instability initiation.” Such a criterion can characterize the regulating effect of stress on the opening process of the “energy gate,” thereby transforming the macroscopic outburst mechanism into a computable and monitorable energy catastrophe criterion. This study lacks quantitative characterization of mesoscopic damage mechanisms. Future work should integrate mesoscopic observation techniques such as CT scanning and acoustic emission positioning to conduct macro-meso collaborative analysis, revealing the energy-driven mechanisms of crack initiation and propagation under confining stress. Additionally, the current research is based on conventional triaxial quasi-static loading conditions, and the universality of the model parameters for different lithologies requires further verification. Subsequent studies should extend to complex conditions such as true triaxial stress states, multi-field coupling, and dynamic disturbances, and establish quantitative relationships between model parameters and basic physical and mechanical indices through comparative tests on various rock types. Furthermore, the instability criterion based on the energy catastrophe criterion needs engineering validation using field monitoring data. Future efforts should focus on developing dynamic disaster early warning methods centered on energy evolution to provide more reliable technical support for the stability assessment of deep engineering rock masses.
