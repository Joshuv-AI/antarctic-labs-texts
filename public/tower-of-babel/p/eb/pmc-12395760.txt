Menstrual blood-derived endometrial stem cells ameliorate neuroinflammation and apoptosis through JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway in NPC1 mutant cell and mice
Authors: Lihong Guan, Keli Xu, Minlin Yang, Yangyang Li, Xiaoying Li, Han Li, Ciqing Yang, Liang Qiao, Yanli Liu, Zisen Jia, Jinghui Wei, Fen Yang, Juntang Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNiemann-Pick disease type C1 (NPC1) is caused by NPC1 gene mutations, resulting in Purkinje cell degeneration and death, glial cell activation, and progressive neurodegeneration. Menstrual blood-derived endometrial stem cells (MenSCs) have been explored as a promising tool for treating neurodegenerative diseases due to their wide range of sources, non-invasive nature, and regular collection methods.ObjectivesThis study aims to investigate whether MenSCs can improve neuroinflammation and apoptosis in NPC1 mutant cell (Npc1KO BV2 cell line) and mice (Npc1−/− mice), and explore their underlying mechanisms.MethodsMenSCs were transplanted into the 4-week-old Npc1−/− mice cerebellum through stereotaxic injection, and their effects on weight, behavior, and survival were assessed. The activation of glial cells and the survival of neurons were detected by immunofluorescence technology, and the expression level of related inflammatory factors and apoptotic proteins was detected by western blotting. The transcriptome changes in cerebellum after MenSCs transplantation were analyzed by transcriptome sequencing. The mechanism by which MenSCs treat NPC1 was validated at the cellular level using the activator butyzamide.ResultsMenSCs transplantation could slow down the rate of weight loss and improve motor coordination in Npc1−/− mice, but had no significant improvement in lifespan. MenSCs could mitigate the activation of glial cells, alleviate neuroinflammation, reduce cell apoptosis, and increase the number of mature neurons and Purkinje cells in the cerebellum. Transcriptome analyses results indicated that the JAK/STAT signaling pathway changed across different groups. Compared with age-matched Npc1+/+ mice, the protein expression levels of P-JAK2 and P-STAT3, the ratios of P-JAK2/JAK2 and P-STAT3/STAT3 were increased in the cerebellum of 5-week-old Npc1−/− mice in the PBS group; compared with age-matched Npc1−/− mice in the PBS group, the protein expression levels of P-JAK2 and P-STAT3, the ratios of P-JAK2/JAK2 and P-STAT3/STAT3 were decreased after MenSCs transplantation. Finally, treating Npc1KO BV2 cells with butyzamide further confirmed that MenSCs relieve inflammation and apoptosis caused by Npc1 gene mutations through JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway.ConclusionOur study demonstrate that MenSCs ameliorate neuroinflammation and apoptosis in NPC1 mutant cell and mice through JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04575-0.

Introduction

Niemann-Pick type C (NPC) disease is a rare genetic disease caused by changes in the NPC1 or NPC2 genes. The NPC1 protein encoded by the NPC1 gene is a key cholesterol transport protein located on the late endosomes/lysosomes membrane [1]. The NPC1 protein, together with the cholesterol binding protein NPC2 in the lysosomal lumen, exports cholesterol released from low-density lipoprotein to receptor compartments such as the endoplasmic reticulum, golgi apparatus, mitochondria, and plasma membrane NPC1 [2]. NPC1 loss-of-function mutations in humans cause excessive accumulation of unesterified cholesterol and other lipids (glycosphingolipids, sphingomyelin, and sphingosine) in late endosomes/lysosomes, leading to a rare hereditary neurodegenerative disease known as Niemann-Pick disease type C1 (NPC1) [3]. NPC1 patients exhibit systemic, neurological and psychiatric symptoms [4]. The main systemic symptoms are hepatosplenomegaly [5]. The pathological manifestations of the nervous system include neuronal swelling, axonal demyelination, reactive glial hyperplasia, neuronal loss, and Purkinje cell loss, resulting in the neurologic manifestations including seizures, gelastic cataplexy, dystonia, and/or spasticity [5]. Psychiatric symptoms involve bipolar disorder, schizophreniform illness and major depression [6, 7]. BALB/cNctr-Npc1m1N/J (Npc1−/−) mouse (RRID: IMSR_JAX: 003092) exhibit symptoms such as cholesterol accumulation, decreased neurons, and weight loss, which are highly similar to those of human NPC1, making them an ideal animal model for NPC1 research [7]. Bi et al. [8] reported that glial cell-mediated inflammatory responses occur in the early stages of brain development in Npc1−/− mice postnatally. The accumulation of TLR4 in cerebellar glial cells of Npc1−/− mice leads to excessive secretion of IL-6, resulting in the activation of STATs in both neurons and glial cells, which may be the basis for the activation of glial cells in the brains of Npc1−/− mice [9].

Currently, there is no curative treatment for NPC1, and the existing therapeutic strategies are largely focused on mitigating clinical symptoms. Miglustat has been approved by European Medicines Agency (EMA) for the treatment of neurological manifestations in NPC1, but has not been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [3, 5]. 2-hydroxypropyl-betacyclodextrin (HP-β-CD) has been reported to delay cerebellar Purkinje cell death and slow down nervous system progression, and can prolong lifespan [10]. In clinical trials, intrathecal injection of HP-β-CD in the lumbar spine improved neuronal cholesterol homeostasis and reduced neuronal pathological changes in patients [11]. However, all participants experienced mid-to-high frequency hearing loss, although this expected adverse event could be improved using hearing aids. Recently, clinical studies have shown that N-acetyl-l-leucine (NALL) can improve neurological symptoms in children and adult NPC patients and enhance their quality of life [12, 13]. However, it will take a longer period to determine the long-term effects of this drug on patients with NPC [13]. In September 2024, the FDA approved two drugs for the treatment of NPC-Miplyffa (arimoclomol) and Aqneursa (levacetylleucine) [14]. When used in combination with miglustat, Miplyffa can improve neurological symptoms associated with NPC in children aged 2 years and older as well as in adults [15]. However, the drug may cause allergic reactions such as hives and angioedema, as well as side effects including respiratory tract infections, diarrhea, and weight loss. According to the instructions of Aqneursa, it can effectively cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and is suitable for the treatment of the neurological manifestations of NPC in adult and pediatric patients weighing ≥ 15 kg. However, the unique molecular targets of Aqneursa for NPC treatment remain unclear. The common side effects of Aqneursa include abdominal pain, dysphagia, upper respiratory tract infection, and vomiting. Therefore, it is still urgent to find a novel therapeutic strategy for NPC1.

Stem cell therapy is a promising and rapidly developing therapeutic method for various neurological diseases. Embryonic stem cells and various adult stem cells are currently being utilized for in vitro, in vivo, and translational clinical studies to explore their potential therapeutic applications in human diseases, such as neurological diseases [16, 17], pulmonary dysfunctions [18, 19], and cardiovascular diseases [20, 21]. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are a type of adult stem cells with multidirectional differentiation potential. MSCs have been studied more and more as a therapeutic method for various neurological diseases. In the past decades, MSCs from bone marrow (BM-MSCs) [22–24], adipose (ADSCs) [25], and human umbilical cord (hUCB-MSCs) [26–28] have been used in preclinical research on NPC1, exhibiting improvements in cholesterol storage, delays in the death of cerebellar Purkinje cells, and enhancements in motor coordination. However, only human amniotic epithelial stem cells (hAESCs) have been shown to increase the body weight of Npc1−/− mice and extend their lifespan [29]. Moreover, the source of these MSCs remains scarce and hard to procure. As a new type of MSCs, human menstrual blood-derived endometrial stem cells (MenSCs) have attracted more and more attention from researchers due to their advantages of non-invasive acquisition, abundant sources, high proliferation rate, low immunogenicity, no ethical problems, and are being used for in vitro and in vivo treatment of a series of diseases [30, 31]. Except that we have revealed that conditioned medium of MenSCs can alleviate inflammation and apoptosis of Npc1 gene knockout N2a cells [32], there is currently no preclinical or clinical treatment report using MenSCs transplantation for NPC1.

This study focuses on the therapeutic effects of MenSCs on Npc1−/− mice and its underlying mechanism. By performing cerebellar stereotaxic injection of MenSCs, we found that weight loss and motor coordination in Npc1−/− mice were significantly improved. In addition, MenSCs could mitigate the activation of glial cells, alleviate neuroinflammation, reduce cell apoptosis, and increase the number of mature neurons and Purkinje cells in the cerebellum of Npc1−/− mice. Furthermore, transcriptome analysis, western blotting, pharmaceutical adminstration together demonstrated that MenSCs relieve inflammation and apoptosis caused by Npc1 gene mutations through JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. This finding provides a novel therapeutic strategy for the treatment of NPC1.

Materials and methods

Extraction, culture, and identification of MenSCs

The method of extracting MenSCs from human menstrual blood is adapted from our previous published article [32]. Menstrual blood was collected from healthy women aged 25–40 using menstrual cups. Monocytes were then isolated via Ficoll (Cat NO., LTS1077, TBD, Tianjin, China) density gradient centrifugation. The cells were cultured in DMEM complete medium (Cat NO., ZQ-101, Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotech, Shanghai, China) at 37 °C and 5% CO2 condition. When cell confluence reached 80%, passage was performed at a ratio of 1:2. The phenotype of Passage 2 (P2) MenSCs was evaluated by flow cytometry using an MSCs surface marker detection kit (Cat NO., 562245, BD Pharmingen, San Jose, United States), in accordance with the International Society for Cell Therapy (ISCT) criteria for MSCs. In addition, the differentiation potential of P2 MenSCs was assessed using lipogenic, osteogenic, and chondrogenic differentiation kits (Cat NOs., CHEM-200010, CHEM-200011, and CHEM-200012, Unicorn League Biotech, Shanghai, China).

Animal feeding and experimental design

Heterozygous Npc1 mutant mice (Npc1+/−) were obtained from Jackson Laboratory (RRID: IMSR_JAX: 003092, United States) and were used to breed wild type mice (Npc1+/+) and homozygous Npc1 mutant mice (Npc1−/−) in breeding cages with a 2:1 female-to-male ratio. All mice were housed in an Individually Ventilated Cage System (Fengshi Laboratory Equipment Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China) under controlled environmental conditions: temperature maintained at 20 ~ 25 °C, relative humidity at 40 ~ 60%, ventilation rate of 25 times/h, light and dark alternation of 12 h/12 h, free to eat and drink. Their cages were randomly placed on the shelves without any fixed order or position. A total of 105 mice were used in this study, including 35 Npc1+/+ mice and 70 Npc1−/− mice. Experimental conditions were designed using a randomized order of participants. All experimental data were collected and analyzed using blind methods, with the operators and designers being unaware of each other’s identities. This study was conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

MenSCs transplantation

4-week-old Npc1−/− mice were randomly divided into 2 groups using a lottery method: the PBS group (n = 35) and the MenSCs group (n = 35). Mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 0.5% pentobarbital sodium (cat NO., 57-33-0, Sigma-Aldrich, United States), and secured in a brain stereotaxic apparatus (RWD, Shenzhen, China). Subsequently, 2 µL of PBS or MenSCs suspension (1 × 105 cells) was injected into mouse cerebellum (coordinates related to Bregma: 2.00 mm, 6.20 mm, -2.50 mm) at an injection rate of 0.5 µL/min. Wild type mice (Control group, n = 35) were used as positive controls. Following the stereotaxic injection, mice were weighed and tested with rotarod weekly, and their survival period was recorded. Due to the inevitable mortality rate during surgery, at least 30 mice per group were ensured for the final analysis. Mice exhibiting signs of humane endpoints were immediately euthanized. The surviving mice were allocated as follows: at least 10 mice per group for behavioral analysis, 5 mice per group at each sampling time point for preparing frozen sections for filipin and immunofluorescence staining, 3 mice per group at each sampling time point for western blotting experiments and transcriptome analysis. The sample size for this study was determined based on both statistical requirements and practical feasibility. An a priori power analysis was conducted, targeting an effect size of 2, with an α level of 0.05 and a desired power of 0.9, indicating a minimum requirement of 5 animals per group. In addition to the statistical parameters, we also considered practical constraints and adhered to ethical guidelines, ensuring the use of the minimum number of animals necessary to achieve reliable results while prioritizing the reduction of animal use and suffering.

Rotarod test

To evaluate the motor coordination and balance of the mice, a rotarod test was conducted weekly on each group of mice (n = 14 per group) after transplantation. Prior to the official testing, all mice underwent pre-training on an automated 6-lane rotarod apparatus (www.softmaze.com, Shanghai, China) for 3 consecutive days. After pre-training, the mice were tested on the rotarod 3 times consecutively at a speed of 20 rpm, with a maximum duration of 10 min per trial and a 30 min interval between trials. The latency time for each mouse to fall from the rod was recorded. The final results were calculated as the average of the 3 trials. For Npc1−/− mice, if they remained on the rod for the full 10 min without falling, the data were considered invalid, and the test was repeated after a 30 min interval.

Tissue separation and processing

At each sampling time point, mice (n = 5 per group) were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 0.5% pentobarbital sodium. Subsequently, they were transcardially perfused with 0.9% saline, followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (Cat NO., G1101-500ML, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). The cerebellums were then dissected and post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h at 4 °C. After fixation, the cerebellums were dehydrated in 18% sucrose for 24 h, embedded in OTC (Cat No., 4583, SAKURA, Japan) and stored at -80 °C. Serial coronary sections (20 μm) were prepared using a rotary low-temperature microtome (DAKEWE CT520, Shenzhen, China) for subsequent filipin staining and immunofluorescence staining.

Filipin staining

To visualize intracellular unesterified cholesterol, cell slides or frozen tissue sections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 20 min. Ater washing 3 times with PBS, the samples were stained with 1 mg/mL filipin (Cat NO., SAE0088, Sigma-Aldrich, United States) in dark for 1 h. Following another 3 washes with PBS, an anti-fluorescence quenching agent (Cat NO., 108483E, Epredia, United States) was applied to seal the slides. Fluorescence was then observed and analyzed using a confocal microscope (Nikon C2si+, Nikon, Japan).

Immunofluorescence staining

Frozen cerebellar tissue sections were placed in a 40 °C biological tissue baking machine (YIDI Medical Appliance Co., LTD, Zhejiang, China) for 20 min. The cell slides or frozen sections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 20 min. After washing 3 times with PBS, the cell membrane was permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Cat No., A110694, Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) for 5 min. The sections were then blocked with 5% BSA (Cat No., P0260, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at room temperature for 1 h and incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4 °C. Following 3 more PBS washes, the secondary antibody was added and incubated at 4 °C for 6 h in the dark. The list of antibodies is provided in Supplementary Table S1. The nuclei were stained with DAPI (Cat No., MBD0015, Sigma-Aldrich, United States). After sealing the sections with an anti-fluorescence quench agent (Cat NO., 108483E, Epredia, United States), the fluorescence was observed and photographed using a confocal microscope.

Culture of BV2 and Npc1KO BV2 cells and their co-culture with MenSCs

The BV2 cell line was obtained from China Center for Type Culture Collection (Cat NO., GDC0311, CCTCC, Wuhan, China) and cultured in DMEM complete medium at 37 °C in a humidified incubator under a 95% air/5% CO2 condition. The Npc1KO BV2 cells are Npc1 gene knockout BV2 cell lines constructed by CRISPR-Cas9 technology in our previous work [33]. Npc1KO BV2 cells were co-cultured with MenSCs or only DMEM medium with a transwell system (Cat NO., 3412, Corning Incorporated, United States) for 48 h.

Butyzamide treatment: Npc1KO BV2 cells and MenSCs (P2-P6) with good growth status were digested and centrifuged. The cells were then resuspended and adjusted to a density of 5 × 10⁴ cells/mL in DMEM complete culture medium. Subsequently, 2 mL of Npc1KO BV2 cell suspension was seeded into a 6-well plate, while 1 mL of the MenSCs suspension was added to the upper chamber of the transwell insert. The cells were co-cultured for 48 h. After this period, the culture supernatants from both the upper and lower chambers were discarded, and the cells were gently washed 3 times with PBS. Fresh DMEM complete culture medium containing the butyzamide (3 µM) was then added for further cultivation for an additional 24 h.

Protein extraction and western blotting

At each sampling time point, mice (n = 3 per group) were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and their cerebella were immediately collected on ice. Proteins were extracted from the tissues using RIPA buffer (Cat NO., CW2333S, Cwbio, Jiangsu, China) supplemented with protease inhibitor (Cat NO., CW2200S, Cwbio, Jiangsu, China). The concentration of the extracted proteins was measured using a BCA Protein Concentration Determination Kit (Cat NO., P0012, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The denatured proteins were separated with 10% SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Cat No. BSP0161, Pall Life Science, United States). Non-specific banding sites on the membrane were blocked with 5% non-fat-milk (Cat NO., abs9175, Absin, Shanghai, China) in TBST at room temperature for 2 h. The membrane was then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation wtih HRP-labeled secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h. The list of antibodies used is provided in Supplementary Table S1. For detection, an appropriate amount of ECL developer (Cat No., E422-02, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) was applied to the PVDF membrane, and the chemiluminescent signals were captured using the Amersham Imager 600 (GE Healthcare Bio-Sciences AB, Japan).

Mouse cerebellum transcriptome sequencing and analysis

Total RNAs were isolated from the fresh cerebella of 5-week-old mice (n = 3 per group) using Trizol reagent (Cat No., 15596-026, Invitrogen Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), while integrity was evaluated with a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent, Santa Clara, CAUSA). Sequencing libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs Inc; Ipswich, Massachusetts, USA), which involves mRNA purification through poly-A enrichment using poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads, followed by mRNA fragmentation, cDNA synthesis, double-end repair, 3′ adenylation, ligation of Illumina PE adapter oligonucleotides, selection of cDNA fragments (400–500 bp), and PCR enrichment. The products were then purified using the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, CA, USA) and quantified with the Agilent High Sensitivity DNA assay on a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent, Santa Clara, CAUSA). The sequencing libraries were then sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform (150 bp paired-end reads) at Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Raw mRNA sequencing reads were processed using fastp (version 0.22.0) to remove adapters and any reads with an average quality score below Q20, resulting in clean reads. The clean reads were aligned to mouse reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.1.0). Gene expression levels were quantified in fragments per kilobase of transcript per million fragments mapped (FPKM) using HTSeq (version 0.9.1). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two sample sets were identified using DESeq (version 1.38.3). A false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted P-value of less than 0.05 and |Log2FoldChange| greater than 1 were considered significant. Additionally, bi-directional clustering of all DEGs across samples was performed using the Pheatmap package in R (version 1.0.12). The heat map was generated to visualize gene expression patterns, showing the expression levels of the same gene across different samples and the expression patterns of different genes within the same sample. Pearson correlation was used to calculate the distance, and the Complete Linkage method was applied for clustering. The Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using the GSEA (version 4.1.0). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways enrichment analyses for the DEGs were performed using DAVID (https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov/summary.jsp), with all mouse genes as the background.

Statistical analysis

Image J software was used to scan and analyze the gray values and average fluorescence intensity of western blotting and immunofluorescence results. GraphPad Prism 7.0 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA) was used to perform statistical analysis. Data are presented as the arithmetic mean ± standard error of the mean. All experiments were independently repeated at least 3 times. The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, while the homogeneity of variances was evaluated using the Brown-Forsythe test. For datasets that met the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc multiple comparisons test was employed for comparisons among 3 groups. For datasets that does not meet the normality assumption, the nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis test was used for comparison among 3 groups. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Biological characters of MenSCs

MenSCs were isolated from menstrual blood of healthy women using Ficoll density gradient centrifugation. As shown in Fig. S1A, MenSCs displayed spindle-shaped morphology, with swirling and radiating in distribution. To assess the stemness of MenSCs, the flow cytometry was conducted by using the surface markers of stem cells. The results showed that the expression levels of CD90, CD73, and CD105 in P2 MenSCs were greater than 98% (Fig. S1Ba-c), while the expression levels of CD34, CD45, CD11b, CD19, and HLA-DR were less than 2% (Fig. S1Bd), which meets the minimum criteria for mesenchymal cells reported by ISCT. Furthermore, MenSCs possessed the ability to differentiate into adipocytes, osteocytes, and chondrocytes under differentiation induction medium (Fig. S1C-E).

MenSCs transplantation improves body weight loss and motor coordination ability of Npc1−/− mice

In order to investigate the effect of MenSCs on NPC1 model mice, 4-week-old Npc1−/− mice were subjected to stereotaxic injection of MenSCs into the cerebellum (Fig. 1A). It was observed that the weight loss of Npc1−/− mice was alleviated within 2–4 weeks after MenSCs transplantation (Fig. 1B), and this effect peaked at 3-week post-transplantation (P < 0.05, Fig. 1C). However, no significant change in body weight was detected 4 weeks after MenSCs transplantation. Rotarod test results showed improved motor coordination ability at 1 and 3 weeks after MenSCs transplantation (P < 0.05, Fig. 1D). The lifespan of mice was slightly prolonged after MenSCs transplantation, but was not statistically significant (P > 0.05, Fig. 1E and F).

MenSCs transplantation alleviates accumulation of unesterified cholesterol

Filipin staining was applied to detect the impact of MenSCs transplanation on the accumulation of unesterified cholesterol in Npc1−/− mice. Compared with Npc1+/+ mice (Fig. 1Ga and Fig. 1Gd), the accumulation of unesterified cholesterol in the cerebellar lobules of Npc1−/− mice treated with PBS (Fig. 1Gb and Fig. 1Ge) was significantly increased (Fig. 1Ha, P < 0.05 and Fig. 1Hb, P < 0.0001). 1 week (Fig. 1Gc) or 3 weeks (Fig. 1Gf) after MenSCs transplantation, the accumulation of unesterified cholesterol in the cerebellar lobules of Npc1−/− mice was significantly reduced (Fig. 1Ha, P < 0.01 and Fig. 1Hb, P < 0.05) compared with Npc1−/− mice treated with PBS. In addition, Npc1 knockout in BV2 cells resulted in a significant increase in the accumulation of unesterified cholesterol (Fig. 1Ib and Fig. 1J, P < 0.0001), compared with the control group (Fig. 1Ia). Furthermore, BV2 cells with Npc1 gene knockout displayed a reduction in the accumulation of unesterified cholesterol when co-cultured with MenSCs (Fig. 1Ic and Fig. 1J, P < 0.01).

MenSCs transplantation decreases the activation of glial cells in the cerebellum of Npc1−/− mice

Immunofluorescence against Iba1 and CD68 were performed to mark microglia in the mouse cerebellum 1 week (Fig. 2A) and 3 weeks (Fig. 2B) after MenSCs transplantation. Compared with age-matched Npc1+/+ mice (Fig. 2Ab and Fig. 2Bb), the number of Iba1+ cells in the cerebellum of 5-week-old (Fig. 2Af and Fig. 2E, P < 0.0001) and 7-week-old (Fig. 2Bf and Fig. 2G, P < 0.0001) Npc1−/− mice in the PBS group were significantly increased. 1 week (Fig. 2Aj and Fig. 2E, P < 0.01) and 3 weeks (Fig. 2Bj and Fig. 2G, P < 0.05) after MenSCs transplantation, the number of Iba1+ cells in the cerebellum of Npc1−/− mice decreased significantly, compared with age-matched Npc1−/− mice in the PBS group (Fig. 2Af and Fig. 2Bf). Compared with age-matched Npc1+/+ mice (Fig. 2Ac and Fig. 2Bc), fluorescence intensity of CD68 in the cerebellum of 5-week-old (Fig. 2Ag and Fig. 2F, P < 0.01) and 7-week-old (Fig. 2Bg and Fig. 2H, P < 0.001) Npc1−/− mice in the PBS group were significantly increased. 1 week (Fig. 2Ak and Fig. 2F, P < 0.01) and 3 weeks (Fig. 2Bk and Fig. 2h, P < 0.05) after MenSCs transplantation, fluorescence intensity of CD68 in the cerebellum of Npc1−/− mice decreased significantly, compared with age-matched Npc1−/− mice in the PBS group (Fig. 2Ag and Fig. 2Bg).

MenSCs regulate the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway

The JAK/STAT pathway is crucial for addressing various challenges faced by the immune system, from resisting infections to maintaining immune tolerance, enhancing barrier function and preventing cancer [34]. When IL-6 and IL-10 increase, JAK2 activates the signal transduction transcription factor STAT3 [35]. Therefore, we detected the expression of genes related to the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. Western blotting results showed that compared with age-matched Npc1+/+ mice, there was no difference in the protein expression levels of JAK2 and STAT3 in the cerebellum of 5-week-old and 7-week-old Npc1−/− mice in the PBS group, while the protein expression levels of P-JAK2 and P-STAT3 were increased (Fig. 7A and C). In addition, compared with age-matched Npc1+/+ mice, the ratios of P-JAK2/JAK2 and P-STAT3/STAT3 were both significantly increased (Fig. 7B, P < 0.01) in the cerebellum of 5-week-old Npc1−/− mice in the PBS group; the ratio of P-JAK2/JAK2 was significantly increased (Fig. 7D, P < 0.05) in the cerebellum of 7-week-old Npc1−/− mice. After MenSCs transplantation, compared with age-matched Npc1−/− mice, the protein expression levels of P-JAK2 and P-STAT3 were decreased (Fig. 7A and C). The ratios of P-JAK2/JAK2 (P < 0.01) and P-STAT3/STAT3 (P < 0.05) were both significantly decreased (Fig. 7B) in the cerebellum of 5-week-old Npc1−/− mice in the PBS group; whereas there was no significant difference in the ratios of P-JAK2/JAK2 and P-STAT3/STAT3 (Fig. 7D, P > 0.05) in the cerebellum of 7-week-old Npc1−/− mice in the PBS group.

Discussion

NPC1 is a rare neurodegenerative disease characterized by abnormal intracellular lipid transport, which can lead to abnormal accumulation of lipids in tissues and organs, causing a series of visceral and neurologic symptoms [36]. Until now, drugs can only provide symptomatic treatment for NPC1. The mechanisms of drug therapy for NPC1 are not fully understood and there are several side effects. The functions of MSCs and their derivatives, such as immune regulation, anti-inflammation, angiogenesis promotion, neurogenesis, antioxidant, and anti-apoptosis, have been widely studied in neurological disorders, lung injury, liver and kidney diseases, cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases [37]. Owing to their non-invasive collection, abundant and periodic sources, high proliferation rate, low immunogenicity, and absence of ethical concerns, MenSCs, as a type of MSCs, have represented promising therapeutic effects in neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease [38, 39]. In this study, we primarily utilized MenSCs to explore and elucidate their potential beneficial impacts on NPC1.

NPC1 can lead to the accumulation of unesterified cholesterol, gait disturbance, and premature death in patients [5, 40]. Npc1-/- mice also exhibit accumulation of unesterified cholesterol in late endosomes/lysosomes, weakened motor coordination ability, and shortened lifespan [41]. This study found that after transplanting MenSCs into the cerebellum of Npc1−/− mice using stereotaxic injection technology, the mice showed a reduction in unesterified cholesterol accumulation similar to that observed with the transplantation of BM-MSCs [22–24], ADSCs [25], and hUCB-MSCs [26, 27], as well as an increase in body weight similar to that seen with the transplantation of hAESCs [29]. However, MenSCs did not significantly prolong the lifespan of mice, which may be due to our insufficient sample size. In addition, at the cellular level, we found that the unesterified cholesterol in Npc1KO BV2 cells was significantly reduced through non-contact co-culture of Npc1KO BV2 cells with MenSCs. Therefore, we speculate that MenSCs may reduce the accumulation of unesterified cholesterol caused by the deficiency of the Npc1 gene by secreting the paracrine factors. In summary, the cerebellar transplantation of MenSCs can reduce the accumulation of unesterified cholesterol in Npc1−/− mice and increase body weight. We speculate that it may also have a certain therapeutic effect on human NPC1 disease.

Neuroinflammation is a key characteristic shared by many diseases, and it plays a particularly significant role in the pathogenesis of central nervous system (CNS) disorders [42]. Microglia are a type of resident mononuclear macrophage in the CNS, and participate in the homeostasis and immune response of the nervous system [43]. Astrocytes are the most abundant type of glial cells in the brain, forming the BBB along with brain microvascular endothelial cells, pericytes, and the basement membrane [44]. They maintain the basic stability of the brain tissue’s internal environment and play a significant biological role in sustaining the normal physiological state of the CNS [45]. Microglia and astrocytes can respond to inflammatory signals and promote inflammation, participating in the regulation of multiple life processes in the nervous system under both physiological and pathological conditions [46]. In this study, immunofluorescence staining revealed that microglia and astrocytes were in an activated state in the cerebellum of 5-week-old and 7-week-old Npc1−/− mice. After cerebellar stereotaxic injection of MenSCs, a significant reduction in the activation of microglia and astrocytes in the cerebellum of 5-week-old and 7-week-old Npc1−/− mice was observed. Western blotting detected a significant decrease in the expression levels of pro-inflammatory factors IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-18 in the cerebellum after MenSCs transplantation. Given that MenSCs introduced via stereotaxic injection exhibit limited migration within the mouse brain and typically vanish after 3–4 weeks [47], we speculate that MenSCs may alleviate neuroinflammation associated with Npc1 gene deficiency by secreting the paracrine factors. Additionally, after non-contact co-culturing MenSCs with Npc1KO BV2 cells, the expression levels of anti-inflammatory factors IL-4 and IL-10, as well as pro-inflammatory factors IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-18 in Npc1KO BV2 cells were significantly reduced. In another study we conducted, we found that the condition medium of MenSCs can significantly decrease the expression of the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 and the pro-inflammatory factor IL-18 in Npc1KO N2a cells [32]. These results further confirm that MenSCs may improve neuroinflammation through paracrine effects. In addition, in our previous research, secretome profiling of MenSCs revealed multiple neuroprotective and immunomodulatory factors that inhibit apoptosis, suppress microglial activation, and dampen both inflammatory and innate immune responses[39]. However, their therapeutic relevance in NPC1 remains to be confirmed in vivo.

Purkinje cells, the largest and most complex neurons of the cerebellar cortex, are its sole output [48]. Their axons project directly and innervate the deep cerebellar nuclei, playing a significant regulatory role in both motor functions and non-motor functions, including cognition and emotion [49]. Npc1−/− mice exhibit a loss of Purkinje cells in the cerebellum, which clinically corresponds to the manifestation of cerebellar ataxia [49, 50]. In this study, after transplanting MenSCs into the cerebellum of Npc1−/− mice, there was a significant rescue of Purkinje cell loss, and motor coordination ability was also improved to some extent, showing a similar effect to the transplantation of BM-MSCs [22–24], ADSCs [25], and hUCB-MSCs [26, 27]. Additionally, MenSCs transplantation can also increase the number of mature neurons in the cerebellum. However, over time, the rescuing effect of MenSCs on Purkinje cells and mature neurons significantly diminishes. In this study, we also examined the expression levels of apoptosis-related factors. Western blotting results revealed that 1 week after MenSCs transplantation, the protein expression levels of pro-apoptotic factors Bax and Bad, and the core apoptotic regulatory factor Cleaved-caspase3 in the cerebellum were significantly reduced. Furthermore, after non-contact co-culturing MenSCs with Npc1KO BV2 cells, the protein expression levels of pro-apoptotic factors Bax and Bad, and the core apoptotic regulatory factor Cleaved-caspase3 in Npc1KO BV2 cells were significantly decreased, while the expression levels of anti-apoptotic factors Bcl-2 and Bcl-xl were significantly increased. These results further demonstrate that MenSCs and their paracrine factors can reduce the neuronal cell death caused by the absence of the Npc1 gene. However, we consider that the long-term effects of single-dose stem cell therapy at the animal level are limited. In this study, we employed a single injection of MenSCs, and the results showed that the effects 1 week after injection were better than those 3 weeks after injection. This may be because the survival time of MenSCs in the body and the duration of action of their paracrine factors are limited. We speculate that multiple injections of MenSCs might achieve better therapeutic outcomes. However, it is necessary to consider the potential damage to animals caused by multiple stereotaxic injection, which is also one of the limitations of this study. Therefore, in the future, we will delve into the therapeutic effects of paracrine factors from MenSCs for NPC1. We aim to ensure therapeutic efficacy while striving to minimize potential side effects. This will provide a more solid scientific basis for the clinical translation of this therapy.

The JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway is a cytokine stimulated signaling pathway involved in important biological processes such as cell proliferation, inflammation, differentiation, apoptosis, immune regulation, and hematopoiesis [51]. After the increase of the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-6, JAK2 activates the signal transducer and activator of transcription factor STAT3, resulting in the production of phosphorylated STAT3 (P-STAT3) [52, 53]. Once P-STAT3 enters the nucleus, it can lead to cell apoptosis, promote cellular inflammation, and alter the cell cycle [35]. In this study, KEGG pathways enrichment results indicated that the JAK/STAT signaling pathway changed across different groups. Western blotting results exhibited that in Npc1−/− mice and Npc1KO BV2 cells, the levels of P-JAK2 and P-STAT3 were significantly elevated. However, after cerebellar transplantation of MenSCs in Npc1−/− mice or non-contact co-culturing Npc1KO BV2 cells with MenSCs, the levels of P-JAK2 and P-STAT3 were markedly reduced. It indicates that the absence of the Npc1 gene leads to the activation of the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway, and MenSCs can inhibit this pathway, as well as suppress the expression of pro-inflammatory factors IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-18, and pro-apoptotic factors Bax and Bad (Fig. 9). When the activator butyzamide of the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway is used to further activate the pathway, the effect of MenSCs in inhibiting the expression of pro-inflammatory factors IL-6 and IL-18, and pro-apoptotic factors Bax and Bad is poorer compared to when butyzamide is not added. Previous studies have shown that exosomes from BM-MSCs can regulate the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway [54, 55]. Therefore, we speculate that exosomes from MenSCs may improve neuroinflammation and cell apoptosis associated with the Npc1 gene deficiency by regulating the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. However, which specific factors play a regulatory role still needs further investigation. In addition, validation at the animal level is also essential.

Conclusions

Cerebellar stereotactic injection of MenSCs can increase body weight and reduce cholesterol accumulation in Npc1−/− mice. Moreover, MenSCs transplantation in the cerebellum of Npc1−/− mice reduced glial cell activation and neuronal apoptosis by inhibiting the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. More importantly, MenSCs have a significant role in preventing the loss of mature cerebellar neurons and promoting the survival of Purkinje cells, ultimately improving the motor coordination ability of Npc1−/− mice. Therefore, MenSCs may be a beneficial method for treating NPC1, and this research will contribute to a better understanding of the role of MenSCs in various neurodegenerative diseases.
