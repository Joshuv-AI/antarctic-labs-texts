Clinical Outcomes of RA FE‐TLIF Compared to FE‐TLIF in Patients With Lumbar Spondylolisthesis: A Retrospective Study
Authors: Lin Zhao, Zhengxuan Peng, Lei Cao, Mingdong Lu, Zhanxiang Wu, Ning Ding, Sheng Zhou, Jie Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundLumbar spondylolisthesis (LS) is a spinal disorder that often necessitates surgical intervention. However, evidence on the comparative clinical value of robot‐assisted full‐endoscopic transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (RA FE‐TLIF) versus conventional FE‐TLIF in early‐grade (Grades I and II) LS remains limited, leaving uncertainty about its true clinical value in this patient population. This study aims to compare the clinical efficacy and safety of FE‐TLIF with RA FE‐TLIF in patients with Grade I and II LS.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 47 patients who underwent surgical treatment for LS between April 2022 and April 2023 at our hospital. Patients were divided into two groups: 22 underwent RA FE‐TLIF, and 25 underwent FE‐TLIF. Key outcomes measured included operative time, intraoperative blood loss, postoperative recovery time, fusion rate, screw placement accuracy, Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), the Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) scores, and the incidence of postoperative complications. Statistical analyses were performed using the independent‐sample t test for continuous variables and the chi‐square test for categorical variables, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05.ResultsThe RA FE‐TLIF group exhibited significantly shorter operative times and lower intraoperative blood loss compared to the FE‐TLIF group (p < 0.05). Postoperative recovery, as measured by hospital stay, was also shorter in the RA FE‐TLIF group (p = 0.001). VAS and JOA scores indicated greater pain relief and functional improvement in the RA FE‐TLIF group, with statistically significant differences observed at both 1 month and final follow‐up (p < 0.05). The incidence of postoperative complications was lower in the RA FE‐TLIF group, though this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.144). Complete fusion rates were 95.45% in the RA FE‐TLIF group and 88.00% in the FE‐TLIF group, with no significant difference (p > 0.05). Screw placement accuracy was higher in the RA FE‐TLIF group (97.73%) than in the FE‐TLIF group (89.00%), with a significant difference (p < 0.05).ConclusionRA FE‐TLIF demonstrates superior clinical outcomes compared to FE‐TLIF in the treatment of LS. These findings support the broader adoption of RA FE‐TLIF as a preferred surgical technique for this condition.Keywords: clinical outcomes, full‐endoscopic transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion, lumbar spondylolisthesis, robot‐assisted surgery, surgical technique

Introduction

Lumbar spondylolisthesis (LS) is a common spinal disorder characterized by the anterior displacement of one vertebra over the adjacent one, leading to a range of symptoms including low back pain, radiculopathy, and neurogenic claudication [1, 2]. The condition can significantly impair a patient's quality of life, particularly when conservative treatments fail to provide adequate relief [3]. Various surgical techniques have been developed to address LS, with the goal of stabilizing the affected segment, relieving nerve compression, and restoring spinal alignment [2, 4].

Traditionally, open lumbar fusion procedures were the standard treatment for LS, but these approaches are associated with significant muscle and soft tissue disruption, leading to prolonged recovery times and increased postoperative pain [5]. In recent years, minimally invasive techniques such as full‐endoscopic transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (FE‐TLIF) have gained popularity due to their ability to achieve similar outcomes with less tissue trauma [6, 7]. However, the technical complexity and limited indications of FE‐TLIF require careful patient selection and skilled surgical expertise [8].

The emergence of robot‐assisted surgical techniques has provided new avenues for the treatment of LS [9, 10]. These techniques, particularly robot‐assisted FE‐TLIF (RA FE‐TLIF), aim to further reduce surgical morbidity while maintaining or improving clinical outcomes [11]. The precision offered by robotic systems, combined with the minimally invasive nature of endoscopic approaches, makes RA FE‐TLIF an attractive option for spinal surgeons [12].

This study specifically focuses on Grades I and II LS. These grades represent the less severe forms of the condition, where the vertebral slippage is limited to 25%–50% of the vertebral body's width [13]. Grades I and II spondylolisthesis are often the first to be managed surgically when conservative treatments fail [14]. Patients with these grades are more likely to benefit from minimally invasive procedures, as the degree of slippage is less severe, making it possible to achieve adequate decompression and stabilization with less invasive techniques [15, 16]. The outcomes of these less severe cases can provide important insights into the effectiveness of new surgical technologies, such as RA FE‐TLIF, in managing LS without the confounding factors associated with more advanced cases. Although robot‐assisted TLIF has demonstrated technical advantages—such as improved pedicle screw accuracy and reduced perioperative complications—existing evidence is primarily derived from complex, high‐grade, or multilevel degenerative cases [17, 18]. Its clinical value in early‐grade, single‐level LS remains underexplored, particularly given the preserved anatomical structures and narrower working corridors in such patients. Moreover, the longer operative times, higher costs, and greater technical demands of robotic systems raise concerns about their practicality in low‐complexity cases [17]. Whether Grades I and II LS represent a preferential indication for RA‐TLIF remains uncertain, warranting focused investigation.

To address this gap, this study aims to evaluate the clinical efficacy and safety of RA FE‐TLIF in patients with Grades I and II LS. Specifically, the purposes are: (i) to compare perioperative outcomes of RA FE‐TLIF and conventional FE‐TLIF, including operative time, blood loss, fluoroscopy frequency, screw accuracy, fusion rate, and hospital stay; (ii) to assess differences in postoperative pain relief and functional recovery; (iii) to compare postoperative complication rates between the two techniques.

Methods

Study Design and Patient Selection

This retrospective study was conducted at Gansu Provincial People's Hospital, focusing on patients who underwent surgical treatment for LS between April 2022 and April 2023, and follow‐up was completed in April 2024. Patients who underwent the RA FE‐TLIF surgery or FE‐TLIF surgery were based on clinical and radiological criteria, as well as the surgeon's expertise and patient preference. A total of 47 patients were included in the study, and they were divided into two groups based on the surgical technique employed: 22 patients in the RA FE‐TLIF group and 25 patients in the FE‐TLIF group. This study was approved by the ethics committee of Gansu Provincial People's Hospital (2024–587).

Inclusion criteria: (1) a diagnosis of LS associated with significant symptoms such as intermittent claudication or low back pain that affected daily living activities; (2) a lack of improvement or worsening symptoms following a minimum of 3 months of conservative management; (3) radiographic confirmation of single‐segment LS through anteroposterior, lateral, and dynamic X‐ray imaging, and treated with single‐segment pedicle screw fixation; (4) Meyerding classification of Grades I or II spondylolisthesis; and (5) patient consent for surgery and follow‐up participation.

Exclusion criteria were applied to exclude patients with (1) other lumbar spine pathologies at the surgical level (e.g., spinal tumors); (2) previous surgery at the same level; or (3) general health conditions that contraindicated surgery.

Surgical Techniques

In the FE‐TLIF group, under general anesthesia, the patient was positioned prone, and C‐arm fluoroscopy confirmed the target level. A small incision (1.5 cm) was made for polyaxial titanium alloy pedicle screw placement (diameter: 5.5–7.0 mm, length: 40–50 mm; Medtronic, Minneapolis, USA), guided by fluoroscopy. A working channel was established under fluoroscopic guidance, followed by endoscopic neural decompression, including removal of osteophytes, facet joint, lamina, and ligamentum flavum. The disc material was excised, and endplates were prepared for fusion. A polyetheretherketone interbody cage (height: 8–12 mm; length: 22 mm; Medtronic), filled with autogenous bone graft, was inserted and positioned under fluoroscopy. Finally, connecting rods were attached, screws were tightened, and a drainage tube was placed before wound closure. The RA FE‐TLIF procedure, using the Ti‐robot (Tinavi Medical Technologies Co. Ltd., China), began with the patient under general anesthesia in a prone position (Figure 2). The C‐arm fluoroscope identified the vertebral level of slippage. After sterile preparation and draping, a tracker was securely attached to the patient's back. Lumbar spine images were obtained and used to create a 3D reconstruction, allowing precise planning of pedicle screw trajectories and the endoscopic entry point. Following the robotic system's guidance, polyaxial titanium alloy pedicle screws (diameter: 5.5–7.0 mm, length: 40–50 mm; Medtronic) and endoscopic guidewires were inserted through a 0.9 cm incision. Sequential dilators expanded the incision, and the working cannula and endoscope were introduced for visualization. Under endoscopic guidance, hypertrophic bone, parts of the articular processes, and sections of the lamina were removed to expose the nerve roots and intervertebral disc. The disc material was excised, and the cartilaginous endplates were cleaned. A polyetheretherketone interbody cage (height: 8–12 mm; length: 22 mm; Medtronic), filled with autogenous bone graft, was inserted into the disc space, and its stability was confirmed. Pedicle screws were inserted, connecting rods were placed, and a drainage tube was positioned. The surgical area was irrigated, and the incision was closed to complete the procedure. Postoperative Care and Follow‐Up

Postoperative care protocols were standardized across both surgical groups to ensure consistent management. All patients received prophylactic antibiotics for the first 24 h after surgery to prevent infection, and drainage tubes were removed 48 h postoperatively. Beginning on postoperative day 1, patients were encouraged to perform exercises for strengthening the lumbar and lower limb muscles while in bed. Ambulation, supported by a lumbar brace and crutches, was initiated on postoperative day 3. Patients were monitored through follow‐up visits at 1 month and 12 months after surgery, with the final follow‐up conducted at 12 months, during which clinical evaluations and radiographic assessments were performed to track recovery and identify any complications.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome measures of the study included operative time, intraoperative blood loss, the number of intraoperative fluoroscopy images, pedicle screw placement accuracy, fusion status, postoperative hospital stay duration, and the incidence of surgical complications. Operative time was defined as the duration from the initial incision to the final suture. Intraoperative blood loss was measured by collecting all fluids in the suction canister and subtracting the volume of irrigation fluid used during the procedure. Additionally, blood absorbed by surgical sponges was quantified by weighing them before and after use to ensure accurate measurement. The number of intraoperative fluoroscopy images was counted to assess radiation exposure, and the length of postoperative hospital stay was recorded to evaluate recovery speed. Surgical complications, including dural tears, nerve root injury, infections, and hardware failure, were meticulously documented and compared between groups.

Pedicle screw placement accuracy was evaluated using the Gertzbein–Robbins classification system [19], defined as follows: Grade A, screw completely within the pedicle; Grade B, cortical breach ≤ 2 mm; Grade C, cortical breach > 2 mm but ≤ 4 mm; Grade D, cortical breach > 4 mm but ≤ 6 mm; and Grade E, cortical breach > 6 mm. Grades A and B were considered accurate placements, and the accuracy rate was calculated as: Accuracy (%) = (Number of screws in Grades A and B/Total number of screws) × 100%.

Fusion status was evaluated at the last follow‐up according to previously described grading criteria [20]: complete fusion was defined as trabecular bone bridging the entire disc space with cortical endplate remodeling; partial fusion was defined as trabecular bone extending from the endplates into the disc space without complete bridging; and no fusion was defined as no evidence of trabecular bone formation arising from the endplates.

Secondary outcome measures focused on pain and functional recovery, evaluated using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) [21] and the Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) [22] score, respectively. The VAS was used to measure pain intensity on a scale from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst pain imaginable), with separate scores recorded for lower back and leg pain at preoperative, 1‐month postoperative, and final follow‐up time points. The JOA score assessed overall neurological function, with a total score ranging from 0 to 29 points, covering three domains: subjective symptoms (0–9 points), clinical signs (0–6 points), and limitations in daily activities (0–14 points). Higher scores indicated better functional outcomes, and these were similarly recorded at preoperative, 1‐month postoperative, and final follow‐up visits.

Statistical Analysis

Data collected from the study were analyzed using SPSS software version 21.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and all variables satisfied the assumption of normal distribution. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared between the RA FE‐TLIF and FE‐TLIF groups using the independent‐sample t‐test. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi‐square test. Statistical significance was determined with a p‐value threshold of less than 0.05.

Results

Patient Demographics and Baseline Characteristics

A total of 47 patients were included in this study, with 22 patients in the RA FE‐TLIF group and 25 patients in the FE‐TLIF group. The baseline characteristics of the two groups were comparable, with no significant differences observed in gender distribution, age, duration of symptoms, Meyerding grade of spondylolisthesis, or body weight. Specifically, the RA FE‐TLIF group had 7 males and 15 females, with a mean age of 47.91 ± 8.45 years, while the FE‐TLIF group included 13 males and 12 females, with a mean age of 49.40 ± 7.66 years. The duration of symptoms averaged 13.95 ± 5.24 months in the RA FE‐TLIF group and 13.40 ± 4.75 months in the FE‐TLIF group. Both groups had a similar distribution of Meyerding Grades I and II spondylolisthesis, and there was no significant difference in body weight between the groups (Table 1).

Surgical Outcomes

The surgical outcomes revealed significant differences between the RA FE‐TLIF and FE‐TLIF groups. The mean time required for pedicle screw placement was significantly shorter in the RA FE‐TLIF group (18.71 ± 1.79 min) compared to the MIS FE‐TLIF group (27.19 ± 2.12 min, p < 0.001). Additionally, the total operative time was significantly less in the RA FE‐TLIF group (126.08 ± 13.87 min) than in the FE‐TLIF group (151.28 ± 18.65 min, p < 0.001). The RA FE‐TLIF group also experienced significantly less intraoperative blood loss (86.44 ± 18.02 mL) compared to the FE‐TLIF group (159.22 ± 18.93 mL, p < 0.001). Furthermore, the number of intraoperative fluoroscopy images required was significantly lower in the RA FE‐TLIF group (3.11 ± 0.73) compared to the FE‐TLIF group (14.60 ± 2.14, p < 0.001). The length of postoperative hospital stay was also shorter in the RA FE‐TLIF group (3.86 ± 1.08 days) compared to the FE‐TLIF group (5.04 ± 1.14 days, p = 0.001) (Table 2). Complete fusion was achieved in 95.45% of patients in the RA FE‐TLIF group and 88.00% in the FE‐TLIF group, with no statistically significant difference between the two groups (p > 0.05). Postoperative CT scans showed a higher screw placement accuracy in the RA FE‐TLIF group (97.73%) than in the FE‐TLIF group (89.00%), with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.05).

Postoperative Complications and Management

The incidence of postoperative complications was lower in the RA FE‐TLIF group than in the FE‐TLIF group, though the difference was not statistically significant. In the RA FE‐TLIF group, there were two cases of spinal cord edema, while in the FE‐TLIF group, complications included one case each of dural tear, nerve root injury, incision infection, deep‐seated infection, and pedicle screw loosening, along with two cases of spinal cord edema. The overall complication rate was 9.1% in the RA FE‐TLIF group compared to 28.0% in the FE‐TLIF group (p = 0.144), suggesting a trend towards fewer complications with the RA FE‐TLIF procedure (Table 3). All complications were managed promptly according to standard postoperative protocols. Intraoperative dural tears were repaired under microscopic visualization, followed by postoperative bed rest and prophylactic antibiotics. Nerve root injuries were treated with corticosteroids to reduce inflammation and neurotrophic agents to promote recovery. Incision and deep‐seated infections were managed with appropriate wound care and targeted intravenous antibiotics based on culture results, with surgical debridement performed when necessary. Pedicle screw loosening was monitored radiographically, and revision surgery was performed in cases with symptomatic instability. Spinal cord edema was treated with corticosteroid pulse therapy and dehydration agents, accompanied by close neurological monitoring.

Pain and Functional Outcomes

Both the RA FE‐TLIF and FE‐TLIF groups demonstrated significant improvements in pain and functional outcomes postoperatively. In the RA FE‐TLIF group, the mean VAS score for lower back pain decreased from 6.60 ± 0.99 preoperatively to 1.17 ± 0.99 at the final follow‐up, whereas in the FE‐TLIF group, the VAS score decreased from 6.78 ± 1.15 to 1.09 ± 0.73. Although both groups showed significant improvement, the reduction in VAS score at 1 month postoperatively was more pronounced in the RA FE‐TLIF group (p = 0.007). The VAS score for leg pain improved in both groups, with a significant difference observed at the final follow‐up (p = 0.025), favoring the RA FE‐TLIF group (Table 4).

Discussion

Main Findings of This Study

This study demonstrates that RA FE‐TLIF provides significant advantages over FE‐TLIF in the treatment of Grades I and II LS. The results indicate that RA Endo‐TLIF is associated with shorter operative time, reduced intraoperative blood loss, fewer intraoperative fluoroscopy images, higher screw placement accuracy, and shorter postoperative hospital stays. Additionally, the RA FE‐TLIF group exhibited superior pain relief and functional recovery as evidenced by the VAS and JOA scores. Although the difference in complication rates was not statistically significant, the RA FE‐TLIF group had a lower overall incidence. These findings suggest that RA FE‐TLIF may represent an advancement in the surgical management of LS, particularly for cases classified as Grades I and II.

Precision and Efficiency in Perioperative Outcomes

The enhanced perioperative outcomes observed with RA FE‐TLIF—particularly shorter operative time, reduced intraoperative blood loss, fewer intraoperative fluoroscopy images, higher screw placement accuracy, and shorter postoperative hospital stays—can be attributed to several key mechanistic factors. The robotic system provides precise control over the placement of pedicle screws and the endoscopic entry pathway, which minimizes tissue damage and ensures accurate decompression of neural elements [23, 24]. Higher screw placement accuracy in the RA FE‐TLIF group highlights the advantage of robotic assistance in spinal instrumentation. Accurate pedicle screw placement is essential for spinal stability and fusion success, as improper positioning can lead to complications such as neural injury, implant failure, pain, and pseudarthrosis [25, 26]. The robotic system integrates preoperative imaging with real‐time intraoperative guidance, enhancing screw accuracy and reducing the likelihood of revision surgery [27, 28]. Compared to conventional FE‐TLIF, which relies heavily on fluoroscopic guidance and may require prolonged tissue manipulation, robotic assistance improves precision while minimizing unnecessary trauma and intraoperative bleeding. In our study, the RA FE‐TLIF group required fewer fluoroscopy images due to the use of C‐arm 3D scanning, which provides a comprehensive view of the surgical area in a single scan [29]. This method replaces multiple fluoroscopic images typically needed in FE‐TLIF procedures, reducing radiation exposure for both patients and the surgical team [30]. By minimizing fluoroscopy dependence, robotic navigation significantly decreases intraoperative radiation risks, a crucial advantage in complex spinal surgeries where cumulative radiation exposure can be substantial [31].

Pain Relief and Functional Recovery

Our data indicate that RA FE‐TLIF produced significantly greater improvements in VAS scores and JOA scores. These findings may be explained by enhanced soft tissue protection and more precise working channel trajectories in RA FE‐TLIF [32]. Unlike conventional FE‐TLIF, which depends on intraoperative fluoroscopy and manual adjustment to establish the working channel, RA FE‐TLIF enables integrated preoperative planning of both the pedicle screw and endoscopic access trajectories. By utilizing preoperative 3D imaging to reconstruct spinal anatomy, the robotic system facilitates precise planning of individualized working paths that account for each patient's unique vertebral morphology [33]. This precision in access path planning not only improves the accuracy of instrumentation but also reduces muscle dissection and preserves surrounding soft tissues. As a result, patients in the RA FE‐TLIF group experienced less intraoperative blood loss and faster functional recovery, evidenced by significantly shorter hospital stays and earlier improvements in VAS and JOA scores [11, 34]. These findings suggest that robotic assistance, by optimizing the working channel and minimizing iatrogenic trauma, is especially beneficial in Grades I and II spondylolisthesis where preserving intact anatomy is crucial.

Postoperative Complications

The incidence of postoperative complications was lower in the RA FE‐TLIF group than in the FE‐TLIF group, though the difference was not statistically significant. This trend is consistent with previous reports indicating that robotic‐assisted and endoscopic spinal procedures can reduce iatrogenic injury rates and support their favorable safety profile [17, 32, 35]. However, it is important to note that the technical complexity of robotic‐assisted techniques introduces a learning curve for surgeons, which may influence outcomes such as operative time and complication rates [36]. In the earlier stages of adopting RA FE‐TLIF, longer operative times and potentially higher complication rates may be observed until surgeons become proficient with the technology. Previous studies have indicated that mastering robotic‐assisted procedures typically requires completing approximately 20 to 300 cases, highlighting the importance of training and institutional support for achieving optimal results [37]. In our study, RA FE‐TLIF demonstrated shorter operative times and lower complication rates, likely due to the proficiency of surgeons experienced with robotic systems, which reduces human error and enhances the accuracy of key surgical steps [38].

Clinical Implications in Grades I–II LS

LS, particularly at Grades I and II, often presents with symptoms such as low back pain and radiculopathy, which significantly impact patients' quality of life [39]. Surgical intervention is typically considered when conservative treatments fail, with the primary goals being to relieve pain, restore spinal stability, and improve functional capacity [14]. The findings from this study are particularly relevant given the increasing incidence of LS in the aging population and the growing demand for surgical solutions that offer quicker recovery and less postoperative morbidity [40]. Although both FE‐TLIF and robotic‐assisted spinal surgery have been studied, their combined application—RA FE‐TLIF—has rarely been systematically evaluated in this specific population [17]. By directly comparing RA FE‐TLIF with conventional FE‐TLIF in Grades I and II LS, our study shows that the combined approach results in less blood loss, shorter hospitalization, fewer complications, and faster recovery. By demonstrating that RA FE‐TLIF can achieve these objectives more effectively than FE‐TLIF, this study provides a strong rationale for considering RA FE‐TLIF as a preferred surgical option for early‐grade spondylolisthesis. The results of this study are consistent with and extend the findings of previous research that highlights the benefits of minimally invasive and robotic‐assisted spinal surgery. For instance, a study by Okumura et al. [41] has reported similar reductions in operative time and blood loss with robotic‐assisted TLIF compared to conventional techniques, although their study did not specifically focus on full‐endoscopic approaches. In contrast, our study provides direct evidence of the added benefits of combining robotic assistance with a full‐endoscopic approach, offering a novel contribution to the literature. Other studies, such as those by Kalina [42] and Sura et al. [43], have demonstrated improved outcomes with FE‐TLIF over open techniques, but the comparison with RA FE‐TLIF in our study suggests that there are still further gains to be made with more advanced technologies.

Limitations and Prospects

This study, despite its promising results, has several limitations. First, its retrospective design may introduce biases, including selection bias, that could affect the outcomes. Second, while the sample size is sufficient for detecting key differences, it is relatively small, and larger multicenter studies are needed to confirm these findings and ensure their generalizability. Third, although this study compared intraoperative fluoroscopy frequency between groups, detailed data on actual radiation exposure—including dose and duration of robotic C‐arm scanning—were not recorded in the surgical records and therefore could not be analyzed. This may limit a comprehensive evaluation of the true radiation burden associated with RA FE‐TLIF. Additionally, the follow‐up period, although suitable for early outcome assessment, may not reflect the long‐term durability of surgical results, such as spinal fusion rates and sustained pain relief. Future research should prioritize prospective, randomized controlled trials comparing RA FE‐TLIF with other minimally invasive and robotic‐assisted techniques, involving larger patient cohorts and longer follow‐up periods. Longitudinal studies focusing on radiographic fusion rates, implant longevity, reoperation risk, and patient‐reported outcomes will be essential to fully assess the durability and clinical utility of robotic‐assisted endoscopic fusion. Moreover, studies should evaluate the cost‐effectiveness of RA Endo‐TLIF, considering the higher initial costs of robotic systems, to better understand their economic impact and potential for wider clinical adoption.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that RA FE‐TLIF offers superior clinical outcomes compared to FE‐TLIF in treating Grades I and II LS. The significant reductions in operative time, intraoperative blood loss, and postoperative recovery time, coupled with improved pain relief and functional recovery, underscore the clinical advantages of RA FE‐TLIF. These findings highlight the potential of RA FE‐TLIF as a preferred surgical approach for early‐grade LS, offering enhanced patient outcomes and supporting its broader adoption in clinical practice for better management of this condition. Nevertheless, given the higher initial costs associated with RA FE‐TLIF, further research is warranted to evaluate its cost‐effectiveness and long‐term economic impact, which will be essential for determining its broader applicability in clinical practice.
