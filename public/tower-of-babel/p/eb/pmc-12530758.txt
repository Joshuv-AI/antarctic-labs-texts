Fibrous dysplasia: Rare sphenoidal and parietal involvement
Authors: Salah Ben Elhend, Hatim Belfquih
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Fibrous dysplasia (FD) is a rare congenital disorder characterized by the replacement of normal bone with fibrous tissue. Two forms are described: monostotic, polyostotic. FD shows a slight female predominance and is often asymptomatic. Frequently, this condition is an incidental radiographic finding. Craniofacial involvement is present in 50% of polyostotic and 25% of monostotic cases. Typically affects contiguous bones; noncontiguous involvement is rare. Sphenoid/ethmoid and parietal co-occurrence is uncommon. Classic radiographic signs include ground-glass appearance and cortical thinning/displacement. Magnetic resonance imaging is reserved for complication assessment. Curative surgery is reserved for compressive complications and palliative surgery for deformities. Modern imaging technique include image-guided surgery, 3D reconstruction: optimize functional/esthetic outcomes.Case Description: We present an illustrative case of a female patient who developed progressive sphenoid and parietal bone FD, representing the uncommon noncontiguous pattern of involvement. Initial presentation at age 12 featured headaches and a painless cranial mass. Surgical intervention includes craniotomy and acrylic bone cement cranioplasty.Conclusion: FD is a frequently asymptomatic congenital condition, often discovered incidentally. While craniofacial involvement typically affects contiguous bones, our case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of rare sphenoid-parietal co-involvement. Surgical intervention remains pivotal for functional and aesthetic rehabilitation.

INTRODUCTION

Fibrous dysplasia (FD) is a rare, noninherited congenital disorder caused by a GNAS gene mutation, leading to defective Gsα protein signaling. This disrupts stromal cell differentiation into osteoblasts, adipocytes, and hematopoietic cells (OMS, 2020),[8] replacing normal bone with fibrous tissue. The result is extensive marrow fibrosis and impaired local hematopoiesis. Craniofacial involvement is frequent in polyostotic FD, typically affecting adjacent bones. However, noncontiguous lesions are rare, and combined sphenoid-parietal FD is uncommon (Javaid et al., 2019).[4]

We report a case of a young female patient monitored at our institution for 5 years for FD. At age 12, she presented with headaches and a palpable cranial mass. Initial computed tomography (CT) imaging revealed small, separate FD lesions in the sphenoid and left parietal bones.

Serial CT scans documented progressive enlargement of both lesions. Despite initial reluctance from the patient and family, surgical intervention became necessary due to worsening cranial disfigurement and intractable headaches.

CASE REPORT

A 12-year-old female initially presented with chronic headaches. Over time, she developed a painless, firm, fixed scalp mass that progressively increased in size. Initial CT scan revealed a “ground-glass” lesion involving the center of the sphenoid bone [Figure 1], with preserved cortical integrity. In addition, there was a similar parietal bone lesion [Figure 2] corresponding to the bony protrusion [Figure 3], reported by the patient. Regular CT monitoring was initiated due to the absence of neurological complications. There are no endocrine abnormalities in laboratory tests (ruling out McCune–Albright syndrome). After 5 years of monitoring, intervention was indicated due to refractory headache progression and significant cosmetic deformity impacting psychosocial well-being [Figure 4]. Surgical approach includes left parietal craniotomy with resection of dysplastic bone and custom silicone cranioplasty [Figures 5-7]. Outcome marked by successful resolution of symptoms, excellent esthetic result [Figure 8], and full social reintegration achieved.

DISCUSSION

FD is a congenital, benign bone disorder, but nonhereditary transmission characterized by the replacement of normal bone with immature, fibrous tissue containing disorganized osteoid. This pathology stems from an activating mutation in the GNAS1 gene (chromosome 20q13), which dysregulates the Gαs subunit of G-protein signaling (Javaid et al., 2019).[4] The resultant aberrant enzymatic cascade impairs osteoblast differentiation, leading to defective bone formation. FD accounts for approximately 7% of benign bone lesions, with an estimated prevalence of <1:2000 (Couturier et al., 2016).[2] However, this figure likely underestimates the true frequency due to the often asymptomatic nature of the condition. The simultaneous involvement of the sphenoid and parietal bones represents an uncommon presentation of fibrous dysplasia, whereas craniofacial FD typically affects the frontal, maxillary, or mandibular bones. Rarity of the sphenoid-parietal association in FD is due to different embryological origins and noncontiguity.

The simultaneous involvement of the sphenoid and parietal bones in FD is exceptional, due to two key factors:

Distinct embryological origins

Sphenoid bone: Develops through endochondral ossification (from a cartilaginous model). Derived from mesoderm and the chondrocranium.Parietal bone: Forms intramembranous ossification. Originates from paraxial mesoderm and neural crest cells (Jin et al., 2016).[5] These differences involve distinct molecular signaling pathways, reducing the likelihood of co-involvement.

Anatomical noncontiguity

It is another key factor explaining this rare association: FD exhibits a predilection for contiguous bones (Katz and Nerad, 1998).[6]Sphenoid-parietal involvement suggests an extended polyostotic form (McCune–Albright syndrome) and rare FD phenotype, warranting genetic and endocrine evaluation. Standard radiography reveals three patterns: sclerotic type – dense, homogeneous “ground-glass” lesions; lytic type – cystic-like lesions with sparse trabeculae and tissue-density lacunae; and mixed type – pagetoid appearance. Characteristic CT findings are bone thickening, loss of corticomedullary differentiation, thinned but regular cortical tables, possible suture crossing, ground-glass opacity, sometimes with cystic or fibrous components. Magnetic resonance imaging in FD does not always demonstrate the classically expected hypointense signals on both T1- and T2-weighted sequences. FD demonstrates characteristic magnetic resonance imaging features dictated by its histopathological composition. Lesions typically exhibit sharply marginated boundaries, T1 signal: Intermediate to hypointense, T2 signal: Intermediate to hyperintense, consistent postcontrast enhancement (degree correlates with disease activity). Cystic degeneration manifests as focal T2 hyperintensity, while increased trabeculation produces T2 signal attenuation (Chang et al., 2022).[1] These imaging biomarkers reliably differentiate active (avid enhancement) from quiescent lesions. The diagnostic yield of scintigraphy is limited in monostotic FD; however, it serves as an effective tool for mapping disease distribution in polyostotic cases.

To date, no standardized, evidence-based guidelines exist for the surgical treatment of craniofacial FD. While conservative medical management remains the standard for asymptomatic, nonprogressive cases, surgical resection with reconstruction, anatomically feasible and clinically indicated, is the definitive treatment for symptomatic presentations. Regardless of the specific surgical technique chosen, the primary objectives of reconstruction should include preservation of functional integrity, reduction of visible deformities, prevention of secondary craniofacial distortion, and minimization of long-term complications (Diyora et al., 2022).[3] Bone burring is effective for volume reduction and creating a smoother osseous contour. En bloc resection is considered the gold standard due to its association with lower recurrence rates. However, anatomical constraints and functional concerns often limit its feasibility. Camouflage procedures serve as a valuable adjunct, particularly in cases where radical resection is impractical. Lipofilling enhances facial symmetry and improves contour irregularities (Lopez-Garibay et al., 2023).[7] The optimal surgical approach must be individualized, balancing the goals of disease control, functional preservation, and esthetic restoration. Further research is needed to establish standardized treatment protocols.

CONCLUSION

The simultaneous involvement of the sphenoid and parietal bones in fibrous dysplasia is exceptionally rare and presents unique diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. This case highlights the importance of vigilant, long-term follow-up to monitor disease progression and timely intervention when symptoms or cosmetic concerns arise. When indicated, surgical resection with appropriate reconstruction can achieve excellent functional and esthetic outcomes. An individualized, multidisciplinary strategy remains the cornerstone of management, and further reports are warranted to guide optimal treatment for these uncommon craniofacial presentations.
