Spinal signatures of entrenched and treated neuropathic pain in male mice
Authors: Lipin Loo, Kazuma Fujikake, Bryony L Winters, Grace Cunliffe, Sasha Saad, Teleri Clark, Zina Hamoudi, John Manion, Leslie Caron, Olivia C Davis, Diana Tavares-Ferreira, Stephanie Shiers, Joseph E Powell, Theodore J Price, G Gregory Neely
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic pain caused by peripheral nerve injury involves altered spinal pain transduction, where enhanced excitatory and/or reduced inhibitory pathways can drive pain. The cellular transcriptional changes that sustain entrenched neuropathic pain remain poorly understood. Here we use single-nucleus and spatial transcriptomic approaches in male mice to investigate spinal cellular and molecular programs associated with entrenched and treated neuropathic pain. We show that nerve injury leads to broad upregulation of synaptic and excitatory pathways in dorsal neuronal and glial populations. We confirm that these core populations are conserved in the human spinal cord, and show that an effective pain therapy dampens neuropathic transcriptional programs. These data identify spinal cell populations and pathways associated with neuropathic pain states and therapeutic reversal.Subject terms: Transcriptomics, Chronic pain

Introduction

Neuropathic pain (e.g., sciatica, back pain, cancer pain, diabetic pain, accidental injury) arises when nerves are injured and nociceptive thresholds for activation change1. This can include enhanced peripheral responses to painful stimuli (hyperalgesia), or normally innocuous stimuli being perceived as painful (allodynia)2. Excessive excitation then drives further sensitization in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord, culminating in an imbalance of excitation/inhibition within the central nervous system3. Anti-neuropathic drugs (including anti-epileptics and antidepressants) inhibit hyperactive pain signals and can provide pain relief to some patients4. However, these therapies can also have significant side effects. Most patients with neuropathic pain do not experience adequate pain relief from available therapies and more precise therapies are needed5. This is in part because we do not yet understand the underlying molecular changes that drive and maintain chronic neuropathic pain.

At the cellular level, multiple studies have reported that neuropathic injury leads to a loss of inhibitory neurons or downregulation of inhibitory pathways within the spinal cord6–8. Others have shown nerve injury leads to enhanced synaptic transmission and spinal long-term potentiation9,10. Whether chronic pain is maintained primarily by one of these mechanism or by a combination of both remains unclear.

The advent of high-throughput single-cell RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) methods has enabled high-resolution profiling of transcript expression in the peripheral and central nervous systems across various developmental, disease, and species contexts11–13. Single-cell RNA sequencing studies of the 14 day post SNI lumbar cord or of FACS-sorted spinal microglia linked key glial responses to chronic pain development and maintenance14,15. However, due to limitations in sequencing depth, cellular coverage, and spatial information of these studies, there remains a significant gap in knowledge in the cellular and molecular changes occurring in the neuropathic spinal cord, particularly in neurons.

To expand our knowledge of the cellular and molecular changes driving neuropathic pain, here we performed high depth single nuclei and spatial transcriptomics on healthy, neuropathic, and treated spinal cords. We did not observe a change in the proportion of spinal inhibitory neurons after nerve injury, and can rule out loss of inhibitory neurons as a cause of neuropathic pain. We did however observe transcriptional upregulation of pathways controlling excitation, synaptic development, and long-term potentiation, and these changes were observed in excitatory and inhibitory neurons as well as glial populations. Moreover, we confirm these changes with high resolution spatial transcriptomics and functional recordings, and these affected populations are conserved in the human spinal cord. Building on this understanding, we exploited a model of long-lasting analgesia, where transplanting GABAergic neurons into neuropathic spinal cords provide consistent and long term pain relief16–18. Notably, pain relief suppressed many of the transcriptional programs elevated in neuropathic pain, providing insight into spinal pathways to target for pain management. Overall these data provide a high-resolution cellular resource for understanding neuropathic pain mechanisms, information that can help us better understand entrenched pain, and most importantly guide the generation of new pain killers targeting the underlying cause.

Results

Engagement of a subset of cells underlie neuropathic pain

To define the cellular and molecular changes that occur during entrenched neuropathic pain, we conducted single nucleus RNAseq (snRNAseq) analysis of the entire lumbar spinal cord in healthy (Sham) and neuropathic pain (Spared Nerve Injury, SNI) conditions (n = 9 per condition, Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1). Mechanical hypersensitivity in SNI mice was confirmed through Von Frey testing prior to nuclei isolation (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Our primary goal here is a deep characterization of neuronal populations, which are often less abundant than glial cells in total nuclei preparations. To achieve sufficient neuronal depth, we employed Fluorescence-Activated Nuclei Sorting (FANS) using an anti-NeuN antibody (Supplementary Fig. 1b shows gating strategy). We sorted both NeuN-positive (NeuN+) and NeuN-negative (NeuN-) populations. To maintain representation of non-neuronal cells while enriching for neurons, we then recombined the NeuN+ and NeuN- fractions at ratios of 90:10, 70:30, and 75:25 for replicates 1, 2, and 3, respectively for each condition.

A total of 23,343 Sham and 31,192 SNI spinal nuclei were isolated from these mice (n = 3 biological replicates, 9 male mice, with nuclei pooled from 3 mice per replicate), with a median detection of between 2500 and 4000 genes per cell which represents ~ 2-3x improvement in gene detection from previous work14 (Supplementary Fig. 2a-b). Unsupervised clustering of spinal nuclei identified major classes, including Slc17a6/Cacna2d1+ Glutamatergic excitatory neurons (Glu1-18), Gad1/Gad2/Slc6a5+ inhibitory GABAergic neurons (Gab1-20), and Chat+ Cholinergic neurons (Cho1-3), as well as non-neuronal cells such as Aqp4+ Astrocytes, Ctss+ Microglia1 and 2, Mbp+ Oligodendrocytes (Oligo1-3), Pdgfra+ Oligodendrocyte Precursor Cells (OPC), Flt1+ endothelial cells, and Dnah12+ ependymal cells (Fig. 1b–d). Subsequently, the manual annotation of our own dataset underwent further comparisons using machine learning annotation based on a harmonized spinal nuclei dataset13 (Supplementary Fig. 3a) and co-embedding (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Compared to the reference datasets (Rosenberg and Sathyamurthy) in co-embedding, our enrichment approach increases the number of detected neuronal nuclei and less non-neuronal nuclei, especially meningeal clusters (Supplementary Fig. 3c).

For automated cell annotation, two complementary plots are generated to visualize the relationship between unsupervised cell clusters and predicted cell types. The first, a facet grid, displays bar charts where each row represents a cluster, showing the count distribution of predicted cell types within it (Supplementary Fig. 3d). The second, a heatmap, provides a more quantitative overview by depicting the percentage of each predicted cell type found within each cluster, with color intensity indicating the proportion (Fig. 1e). This heatmap also employs hierarchical clustering to group similar clusters and cell types. Both plots demonstrate strong agreement: most Cluster IDs predominantly mapping to a single, consistent predicted cell type, such as “Ependymal” or “Astrocyte.” Notably, the “Glu” clusters consistently align with excitatory (Glutamatergic) predicted cell types, while the “Gab” clusters map to inhibitory (Gabaergic) predicted cell types. The overall pattern indicates that the unsupervised clustering successfully identified finer-grained biological subtypes or states within the existing cell populations, suggesting a robust and biologically meaningful clustering that aligns well with the predicted cell type annotations. A few exceptions were the Glu2, Oligo2, Gab11, and Gab2 clusters, which showed alignment to multiple predicted cell types (highlighted at the bottom of Fig. 1e). This likely reflects mixed‑lineage transcriptional profiles or technical contamination (doublets or ambient RNA). This behavior was restricted to these clusters and did not affect the annotation of other neuronal or glial populations. We retained these clusters for completeness and for subsequent spatial localization but flagged them as low‑confidence and did not use them for mechanistic interpretation in downstream analyses.

No significant changes were detected in cell proportion when comparing healthy and neuropathic spinal populations (Fig. 2a, b). Absolute proportions to total neuronal and non-neuronal cells are utilised here considering the enrichment strategy. Our data show no significant death of GABAergic neurons, consistent with a previous imaging study16. Microglia expansion associated with the development of neuropathic pain has been reported at days 3, 7, and 14 post-SNI but subsides by 5 months15. In our data, we did not detect differences in microglia population proportion, suggesting expansion subsides as early as day 28.

While we don’t observe changes in cell numbers, we hypothesise that entrenched neuropathic pain is driven by transcriptional reprogramming. Consistent with this, a global heatmap revealed distinct gene expression patterns between Sham and SNI clusters (Fig. 2c). Pathway analysis of differentially expressed genes (adjusted p value < 0.05, Ingenuity Pathway Analysis, Qiagen, Supplementary Data 1) revealed significant pathway activation in subsets of Glutamatergic, GABAergic and glial populations (Z-score >1.96 in at least one of the pathways, Supplementary Fig. 4a). The affected pathways can be split into 6 large functional categories (Nervous System Signaling, Neuronal Diseases, Biosynthesis, Growth Factor, Cell Cycle & Death and Immune & Stress Response, Supplementary Fig. 4a). Several Glutamatergic (Glu1-Glu7), GABAergic (Gab1, Gab2, Gab3 and Gab5) and Non-neuronal (Microglia1, Oligo1 and Oligo2) populations show broad pathway activation across all categories and are thus referred to as “perturbed” cells. Many pathways involved in neuronal plasticity and synaptogenesis (Synaptogenesis Signaling Pathway, Glutamate/Glutaminergic Receptor Signaling Pathway, CREB Signaling in Neurons, Neurovascular Coupling Signaling Pathway, Synaptic Long Term Potentiation (LTP)) were upregulated in “perturbed” neuronal and glial clusters, with the exception of Oligo2 (Fig. 2d, full volcano plots for synaptic plasticity for every cluster in Supplementary Fig. 5). Importantly, the SNI spinal cord also displays elevated Neuropathic Pain Signaling in Dorsal Horn Neurons (under Neuronal Diseases in Supplementary Fig. 4a), indicating coordinated activation of pain‑relevant transcriptional programs during chronic neuropathic pain.

To resolve these pathway‑level changes at gene‑level and cell‑type resolution, we next examined differential expression within synaptic plasticity programs on a per‑cluster basis. Cluster‑specific volcano plots confirmed predominant upregulation of synaptic genes in SNI relative to Sham across multiple perturbed populations (Supplementary Fig. 5, gene list in Supplementary Data 2), consistent with the observed pathway Z‑scores. Among glutamatergic neurons, Glu5 emerged as a representative population showing robust and convergent activation across multiple synaptic plasticity pathways. Focusing on this cluster, pathway‑specific volcano plots revealed coordinated upregulation of genes involved in synaptic organization, neurotransmitter signaling, and activity‑dependent transcriptional regulation, including components of glutamatergic receptor signaling, CREB‑dependent programs, and potentiation‑related mechanisms (Fig. 2e).

While there was no loss in GABA cell numbers, disinhibition of pain signaling via a loss of function in GABA receptor signaling could still drive persistent pain. However, transcriptionally, negligible changes were observed in GABA-mediated pathways (GABAergic Receptor Signaling Pathway (Enhanced) and GABA synthesis, release, reuptake, and degradation; Supplementary Fig. 6). Together, these data identify subsets of neurons and glia as transcriptionally plastic populations in the injured spinal cord and provide a mechanistic link between pathway‑level activation and gene‑level remodeling during entrenched neuropathic pain.

Spatial localization of “Perturbed” neuronal and glial populations in pain circuits for functional validation

While snRNAseq enables high-resolution transcriptome profiling, it lacks spatial context. To address this, we next localized “perturbed” cells using whole-transcriptome-capture spatial transcriptomics in mouse and human tissue (Visium). In the mouse spinal cord, white matter exhibited lower transcript expression compared to gray matter (Supplementary Fig. 7a-b). Unsupervised clustering with Seurat and BayesSpace (Supplementary Fig. 7) reveal consistent distribution of clusters from dorsal to ventral horn neuronal populations, with clear demarcation by the expression of oligodendrocyte-associated genes like Mbp, neuronal synaptic gene Snap25, glutamate transporter Slc17a6, and glutamate decarboxylases Gad1 and Gad2 (Supplementary Fig. 7e). Next, we localized clusters manually using marker genes identified from snRNAseq and through a machine-learning deconvolution method, Robust Cell Type Decomposition (RCTD)17, with snRNAseq data as the reference. Together, these approaches localized the “perturbed” excitatory clusters (Fig. 3a, b), which can be divided into two spatial groups: Hsd17b2⁺ Glu1 and Ndst4⁺ Glu2 occupying deeper laminae, while Tac2⁺ Glu3, Maf⁺ Glu4, Tac1⁺ Glu5, Nfib⁺ Glu6 neurons, and Myo5b⁺ Glu7 were enriched in the dorsal horn (Fig. 3c). These distributions were consistent with those observed in human spinal cord tissue (Fig. 3d, full comparison for each cluster in Supplementary Fig. 8), consistent with prior reports showing minimal divergence between mouse and human spinal transcriptional architecture19.

We also determined the distribution of “perturbed” GABAergic (Fig. 3e–h) and non-neuronal cells (Fig. 4). Specifically, Meis2⁺ Gab1 localized to deep laminae, Rarb⁺ Gab3 and Gab4 to middle laminae, and Pdyn⁺ Gab5, Rorb⁺ Gab6, and Klhl14⁺ Gab7 to the superficial dorsal horn. Gab2 (also Meis2+) struggled to place, consistent with its mixed transcriptional profile observed in the snRNAseq annotation (Fig. 1). Ctss+ Microglia1 have dispersed distribution, while Mbp+ Oligo1 and Oligo2 can be differentiated by positioning, with the latter localizing more in the gray matter, consistent with the predicted neuronal contamination profile (Fig. 4c). These laminar localizations align with known pain-processing circuits: excitatory clusters enriched in superficial laminae (I–II) correspond to nociceptive relay neurons that undergo LTP and drive hypersensitivity20, while deep lamina clusters (V–VI) include wide dynamic range (WDR) neurons implicated in wind-up21. Perturbed GABAergic populations in middle and superficial laminae suggest disrupted inhibitory gating, and glial localization in gray matter supports glial-neuronal interactions that maintain chronic pain states22.

Having identified the laminar distribution of perturbed neuronal populations, we next assessed whether dorsal horn neurons show functional changes associated with neuropathic injury. Notably, several transcriptional pathways including CREB signaling, were upregulated in perturbed dorsal horn neurons (Fig. 2d, e), consistent with molecular programs known to enhance neuronal excitability23,24. To assess whether neuropathic injury is accompanied by changes in intrinsic excitability, we performed whole‑cell patch‑clamp recordings from dorsal horn neurons in Sham and SNI mice (Fig. 5a). Neurons from neuropathic cords exhibited a significant increase in action‑potential firing in response to depolarizing current injections (Fig. 5b, c), indicating heightened intrinsic excitability in dorsal horn neurons after nerve injury. Attempts to extend these recordings to deeper laminae (where Glu1 and Glu2 reside) were limited by heavy myelination, which impaired stable patch‑clamp access. As a result, electrophysiological validation was most robustly achieved in dorsal populations accessible to reliable physiological interrogation.

High resolution spatial transcriptomics confirms elevated synaptic plasticity as a core signature of the “perturbed” clusters in the neuropathic dorsal horn

To confirm the upregulation of transcriptional programs controlling neuronal plasticity and synaptogenesis in “perturbed” cells, we designed a custom panel of probes consisting of 300 cell-type markers and differentially expressed synaptic-related genes identified from the snRNAseq dataset, and applied it to high-resolution spatial imaging (Xenium, gene panel information in Supplementary Data 3). Using this method, we were able to identify the canonical cell markers (Slc17a6, Gad2, Chat, Aqp4, Ctss, Mbp, Dnah12) and localized main Glutamatergic and GABAergic subtype markers to the dorsal horn (Maf, Nts, Nmu, Tac2, Tac1, Rorb, Pdyn, Runx2, Klhl14) (Fig. 6a). Using machine learning annotation with our snRNAseq data as reference, the “perturbed” neurons were also localized to consistent locations as in Visium deconvolution, substantiating deconvolution results from the whole-transcriptome analysis (Fig. 6b).

We next asked if synaptic gene expression changes from snRNAseq could be validated spatially. Normalized transcript abundance in the ipsilateral SNI dorsal horn was compared to the ipsilateral Sham controls (Fig. 7a, b) and the differentially expressed synaptic genes were plotted alongside their corresponding snRNAseq differential expression values (adj p value < 0.05 for both assays, Supplementary Fig. 9). Using this orthogonal high resolution approach, 60.7% of the synaptic genes differentially expressed in “perturbed” snRNAseq clusters were confirmed to be upregulated in SNI tissue (Supplementary Fig. 9a). Of particular interest, Glu5 neurons exhibited the highest concordance between snRNAseq and Xenium at 83.3% (Fig. 7b). Increased expression of Vti1b, Grm7, Ap2b1, Stxbp4, and Grin2b in Tac1⁺ Glu5 neurons suggests coordinated enhancement of vesicle trafficking25, presynaptic terminal regulation26,27, receptor endocytosis28, SNARE‑regulated vesicle dynamics29, and NMDA‑dependent synaptic plasticity30, aligning with the heightened excitatory drive observed in neuropathic dorsal horn circuits31 (Fig. 7c). The confirmed Microglia1 (Cdh2, Gria3, Grm5) and Oligo1 (Cdh12, Dab1) changes suggest glial contributions to neuropathic plasticity through synaptic adhesion and regulatory signaling22,32,33 (Supplementary Fig. 9b).

We focused electrophysiological recordings on Glu5 neurons because of their high concordance in snRNAseq and Xenium data, and their superficial dorsal horn location makes them readily accessible for stable patch‑clamp recording, unlike deeper lamina populations such as Glu1, where heavy myelination severely limits recording feasibility. Furthermore, Glu5 neurons uniquely express Tac1 (Fig. 3b), enabling reliable cell‑type‑specific fluorescent labeling for targeted physiological interrogation. To achieve this, we crossed Ai9 tdtomato reporter mice with Tac1-cre animals to mark dorsal horn Tac1+ neurons34 (Fig. 7d). Following Sham or SNI surgery, spinal cords were retrieved 28 days post-injury for recordings of spontaneous excitatory post-synaptic currents (sEPSC; Fig. 7e). Tac1+ Glu5 neurons from SNI animals exhibited significant increase in sESPCs amplitude (Fig. 7f–j), functionally corroborating the enhanced synaptic transcriptomic signatures detected in both snRNAseq and spatial imaging datasets.

GABAergic transplant reverses plasticity and provides pain relief

Previous research by our group and others demonstrated that a single transplantation of GABAergic neurons effectively alleviated pain long-term13–15. Motivated by these findings, we sought to investigate whether these inhibitory transplants exert their analgesic effects by reversing or modulating neuropathic pathways. To address this, we generated GABAergic neurons from human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs; Supplementary Fig. 10a–c), transplanted these cells into the dorsal horn of the spinal cord, then profiled the transplanted spinal cords one-month post-SNI to identify resolved pathways, offering potential targets for future drug development (Fig. 8a).

Long lasting analgesia was observed in mice that underwent transplantation, consistent with previous reports35–37 (Fig. 8b). Co-clustering of the Sham, SNI, and SNI+Transplant datasets showed the human cells adopted diverse final identities (Fig. 8c–e), indicating that the graft does not remain confined to a single neuronal fate. These identities spanned all major spinal cell classes, with an apparent enrichment toward the Oligo2 cluster (Fig. 8f). Although the Oligo2 signal in the Sham versus SNI analysis could be explained by nuclei‑level contamination, this explanation does not apply to the human transplant dataset. The transplanted cells show hybrid transcriptional identities that are not compatible with contamination, as species‑aware ortholog mapping filters out any mouse‑derived transcripts from the human donor cells.

Despite being differentiated using a cortical inhibitory neuron protocol35, these transplanted cells diversify into multiple lineages while still preserving a GABAergic molecular core. A substantial fraction retained expression of GABAergic markers (Fig. 8g), including the neuronal marker Map2, the transcription factors Pax5 and Pax8, the GABA‑synthesizing enzymes Gad1 and Gad2, and the GABA transporter Slc6a1, consistent with their pre‑transplant differentiation state (Supplementary Fig. 10c). Together, these data indicate that while transplanted cells engage GABAergic programs, they also adopt additional molecular identities in vivo.

Finally, we compared transcriptional differences between the transplanted and neuropathic spinal cord, and found that many synaptic pathways affected in SNI were reversed where positive z-scores (red) in “perturbed” neuropathic neurons and glial shifting to negative z-scores (blue) in “painkiller” transplanted spinal cords (Fig. 8h). Notably, GABAergic receptor signaling was enhanced across all clusters, consistent with the expected effect of introducing inhibitory neuron transplants and supporting the marker expression patterns described above. To assess whether this pathway‑level shift toward toward downregulation extended to specific excitatory neuronal populations, we further examined differential expression of synaptic plasticity genes in Glu5 neurons, where pathway‑specific volcano plots reveal coordinated downregulation of synaptic plasticity programs following transplantation (Fig. 8i, volcano plots for all clusters in Supplementary Fig. 11, gene list in Supplementary Data 4, pathway information in Supplementary Data 5). Overall, our data suggests that introducing inhibition via GABAergic cell therapy provides analgesia, at least in part, by reducing expression of spinal synaptic signatures, while simultaneously boosting GABAergic signaling.

Discussion

Chronic neuropathic pain is a complex condition characterized by an imbalance between excitation and inhibition in the nervous system. While it is well established that chronic pain involves alterations in both excitatory and inhibitory neuronal circuits, the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. Recent studies have also highlighted the critical role of glial cells, such as microglia and macrophages, in modulating pain processing through changes in their gene expression profiles. Our study aimed to address this knowledge gap by investigating the transcriptional changes in both neuronal and glial populations within the spinal cord, a key region involved in pain processing. By integrating high-depth snRNAseq, Visium spatial mapping, high-resolution Xenium imaging and electrophysiology at day 28 time after injury, we sought to identify cell-type specific, spatially anchored and functionally validated insights into the neuropathic spinal cord.

Two single cell transcriptomics studies focused on microglia have linked a loss of Cd163 expressing MRC1+ spinal macrophages and ApoE upregulation in spinal microglia to neuropathic pain14,15. While these works have highlighted glial contribution to neuropathic pain, in these studies spinal neuron populations were either not evaluated at all15, or populations were under-represented due to limitations in isolating neurons via single cell techniques14. To investigate the role of neuronal populations in neuropathic pain, we used single nuclei sequencing which avoids glial biases introduced by harsh single cell dissociation methods38, and multiple spatial sequencing approaches were used to confirm our single nuc data. Our strategy of enriching neuronal nuclei (NeuN, or Rbfox3) before spiking in non-neuronal nuclei, coupled with higher depth sequencing, increased the detection of unique transcripts and enabled comprehensive identification of neuronal populations and this empowered our subsequent differential expression analysis. Overall, our work here represents a comprehensive high-resolution transcriptomic reconstruction of the neuropathic spinal cord, identifying neuronal changes while confirming glial activation.

Genes upregulated across neuronal and glial populations were enriched for multiple coordinated synaptic plasticity programs, including synaptogenesis, glutamatergic receptor signaling, and long‑term potentiation, indicating a broad transcriptional shift toward enhanced excitatory capacity in the neuropathic dorsal horn. The coordinated upregulation of these synaptic plasticity pathways, as validated spatially and functionally, supports a model in which entrenched neuropathic pain is maintained not by a single potentiation mechanism, but by a multi‑layered excitatory remodeling program across defined neuron subtypes and glia9,20,22,33,39. Notably, we did not observe a decrease in the number of GABAergic neurons or reduced transcription of inhibitory machinery, indicating that the dominant molecular signature at the chronic time point is excess excitation. Previous work has shown that loss of inhibitory neurons is not required for the development of neuropathic pain16,40,41. However, potential inhibitory dysfunction can arise through non‑transcriptional mechanisms (e.g., translational regulation, posttranslational modulation) as drivers of inhibitory dysfunction42 or through rapid disinhibition operating on synaptic timescales43,44.

We identified the key dorsal horn neuronal and glial populations affected after nerve injury, and these populations are conserved in human spinal cords. Notably, dorsal horn neurons exhibit enhanced intrinsic excitability. Besides this, Xenium further pinpointed Tac1+ Glu5 neurons as a major driver of plasticity, with ex vivo recordings confirming increased excitatory current amplitudes. Additionally, our observation of strong synaptic pathways upregulation in Microglia1 and Oligo1 implicates glial-neuronal interaction in driving neuropathic pain. This finding is consistent with numerous electrophysiological studies in the spinal dorsal horn9,28,32, including two studies demonstrating that microglia are necessary for maintaining plasticity in the spinal dorsal horn and chronic pain22,33. Our work reveals a complex interplay between glial cells and neurons in neuropathic pain, but we acknowledge that further investigation is necessary to understand the dynamics driving these changes. Future research should focus on elucidating the precise contributions of peptidergic neurons, Pdyn+ Gab5 neurons, microglia, and oligodendrocytes to excitatory changes during neuropathic pain, as well as exploring potential therapeutic opportunities by targeting these populations or pathways. Overall the transcriptional changes we report here represent a state of increased synaptic activities in the neuropathic dorsal horn.

Unlike gabapentin, which provides only short-term pain relief and requires frequent administration, we employed a model of continual and long-lasting analgesia to identify pathways modulated for sustained pain relief. This interventional dataset further linked circuit programs to behavior as inhibitory “painkiller” transplants induce analgesia at least in part by reducing excessive synaptic plasticity in the neuropathic dorsal horn. Despite adopting diverse final identities, transplanted cells retained GABAergic molecular features and were associated with broad shift toward downregulation of neuropathic synaptic pathways with positive z‑scores in SNI shifting to negative after transplantation, and GABAergic receptor signaling enhanced across clusters. However, we observed widespread transcriptional dampening in the transplanted spinal cord and achieving higher efficacy analgesia may require targeted resolution of synaptic activities in affected neurons.

There are some limitations to this study. For example, while analyzing transcriptomics within nuclei means access to spinal populations that are not easily isolated into single cells (like complex neuronal populations), this access is at the expense of detecting cytoplasmic transcripts. Consequently, while we capture neuronal populations with high resolution, we may be missing a complete understanding of the true neuropathic condition. All experiments were conducted in male mice; given that sexual dimorphism in microglial signaling and pain mechanisms is well established, future work should include both sexes to fully capture these differences. We also acknowledge that further transplant experiments could not be performed due to safety concerns, as approximately 20% of rodents develop paralysis following intraspinal transplantation. Because of this constraint, we were unable to generate additional tissue for electrophysiological validation or anatomical tracing. Lower-resolution Visium experiments may limit detection of rarer transcripts due to crowding effects. Using high resolution Xenium imaging, we were able to detect these rarer transcripts but were limited to a panel size of 300 genes at the time of the experiment, and this only allowed for validation of a subset of synaptic genes identified from snRNAseq. Newer larger panels are becoming available and will empower future studies for comprehensive validation. Nonetheless, data derived from nuclei represent the nascent transcripts most reflective of cellular conditions at the time of dissection. Moreover, our findings are validated using orthogonal spatial technologies and functional electrophysiological recordings, and are in line with a large body of work investigating spinal mechanisms of neuropathic pain over the last few decades1 suggesting that single-nucleus RNAseq is a valid approach for gene expression profiling in neuronal tissue. This is especially true in tissue of the nervous system, where obtaining viable single-cell preparations is challenging.

Overall, we provide high resolution data of the healthy and neuropathic spinal cord, including transcriptional programs that change with injury and treatment. Our findings here underscore the critical importance of pathways promoting synaptic plasticity in maintaining neuropathic pain. Targeting these and other pathways described here may help develop next generation painkillers that can specifically drive lasting pain relief.

Ethics statement

Animals were housed at 20-24 °C, 40-70% humidity, on a 12 h light:dark cycle (lights on at 07:00, off at 19:00). All human tissue procurement procedures and ethical regulations were approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Texas at Dallas under protocol Legacy-MR-15-237. Spinal cords were procured from organ donors through a collaboration with the Southwest Transplant Alliance, an organ procurement organization (OPO) in Texas. The Southwest Transplant Alliance obtains informed consent for research tissue donation from first-person consent (driver’s license or legally binding document) or from the donor’s legal next of kin. The distribution of donor medical information is in compliance with HIPAA regulations to protect donor privacy.

Spared nerve injury (SNI)

SNI was performed as previously described45. Briefly, 8–12 week old mice were anesthetized with Isoflurane before the skin was incised with a scalpel and the muscle was bluntly dissected to isolate the left sciatic nerve. The tibial and common peroneal portions of the nerve were ligated with Ethilon 4-0 nylon suture material (Johnson and Johnson International) and a portion of nerve was removed taking care not to touch the sural nerve. The incision was then closed with wound clips (Reflex, FineScience Tools). NOD SCID Mice were provided with 2.5 mg/kg enrofloxacin (Baytril, Bayer) in normal saline 0.9% (Pfizer) daily by subcutaneous injection for 7 days. Mechanical hypersensitivity was assessed on 21 days post-nerve injury.

Single nucleus RNAseq

At day-28 post-surgery, animals were euthanised with CO2 before spinal tissue dissection. Spinal nuclei were obtained as previously described38 and were stained with NeuN (Abcam ab190195, 1:200) and Hoechst (1:2000). Sorted neuronal and non-neuronal nuclei were pooled at ratios of 90:10, 70:30 and 75:25 for replicates 1, 2 and 3 respectively for each condition before being processed for single-nucleus sequencing using the 10X Genomics Chromium Single Cell 3′ Kit (v3 chemistry) and sequenced at a depth of at least 60,000 reads per nucleus (Protocol CG000183_ChromiumSingleCell3__v3_UG_Rev-A). Reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (refdata-gex-mm10-2020-A) using 10X Genomics Cell Ranger (v7.1.0, include-introns) to generate a filtered gene expression matrix. This matrix was migrated to Seurat_4.3.0, filtered to remove nuclei that contain >5% mitochondrial and ribosomal reads, and low transcript ( < 500) and high transcript ( > 10,000, to remove doublets) and normalized with SCTransformV2 before integration (anchors of 3000 variable genes in Supplementary Data 6) and clustering at 0.8 resolution with algorithm 2. Cluster identities were determined using the combinatorial marker code in Table 1 of Russ et al. where possible (known clusters). The clusters were compared via label transfer with Russ et al. as reference and facet grid plots were generated for ease in comparing cluster similarity.

Differential expression analysis and ingenuity pathway analysis

The gene expression profile of each cluster was compared between SNI and Sham conditions using the FindMarker function in Seurat, applied to the log‑normalised expression matrix and including all detected genes, rather than restricting the analysis to the ~3000 variable genes used during data integration and anchoring. Statistical significance was determined using the nonparametric Wilcoxon Rank Sum Test. Genes with adjusted P value < 0.05 were compiled and subjected to pathway analysis using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA). Pathways with significant Z‑scores (<–1.96 or >1.96) were aggregated and visualised with the pheatmap R package. Significant pathways were subsequently grouped into broader functional categories based on IPA classification.

Mouse spinal cord spatial transcriptomics (visium)

Lumbar spinal cord tissue obtained from day 28 SNI mice were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen before embedding in tissue freezing medium (Leica, 14020108926) as instructed by manufacturer (10X Genomics, TissuePreparationGuide_Rev_A). Embedded tissues were sectioned at 10 μm before placement within fiducial frames of 6.5 mm×6.5 mm capture areas on a Visium Spatial glass slide. These sections then undergo H&E staining (MethanolFixationandHEStaining_RevB), followed by imaging on a Leica DM6B and at ×10 magnification. Imaged sections are permeabilized, reverse-transcribed before denaturation. The collected cDNA are further amplified and indexed before sequencing to achieve 75 million reads, which equated to approximately 42,501 reads per spot for the presented data. Generated sequences were aligned with Space Ranger version 2.0.0 with mm10-2020-A as reference, before downstream analysis with Seurat and RCTD. Space Ranger was used for alignment, barcode and feature calling, and spot assignment. All barcodes with detected transcripts were retained for downstream analyses; no additional spot‑level filtering based on UMI or gene thresholds was applied. All summaries from this run is included in supplementary materials.

Human spinal cord spatial transcriptomics (visium)

The lumbar spinal cords from two human organ donors were immediately frozen using dry ice upon extraction and subsequently stored at –80 °C (Southern Transplant Alliance, human ethics protocol MR-15-237; for donor demographics, see Supp. Table 1). These experiments were performed before our current standardized RNA QC pipeline was established; therefore, RIN/RQN values were not generated for these donor samples. As an alternative quality check, we performed the 10x Visium Tissue Optimization assay on these same sections, which confirmed robust RNA signal and appropriate permeabilization prior to library preparation.The blocks of spinal cord were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound within a cryomold by incrementally adding small amounts of OCT over dry ice to prevent thawing. Visium tissue optimization and spatial gene expression protocols were followed exactly as described by 10x Genomics using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) as the counterstain. The tissue was sectioned at a thickness of 10 μm onto each capture area (6.5 × 6.5 mm) of Visium slides. Optimal permeabilization time was obtained at 18-min incubation with permeabilization enzyme. Imaging was conducted on an Olympus vs120 slide scanner. The sequencing run data (Illumina BCL files) were processed with Space Ranger (v1.1) from 10x Genomics. Samples were demultiplexed into FASTQ files and aligned with brightfield images. The pipeline detected barcode/UMI counts and mapped reads to the human transcriptome (GRCh38.p10). It generates feature-barcode matrices that contain raw counts and barcoded information with spatial coordinates. On average, we obtained over 64 million reads per sample, 26394 reads per barcode and a total of 23157 genes on average per sample.

Robust cell type decomposition (RCTD)

Seurat object was generated from Visium data and normalised using SCTransformv2. The RNA counts, spatial coordinates and number of UMI were extracted to create a “puck” with RCTD’s SpatialRNA function. Using our snRNAseq data as reference, the Visium data was annotated with run.RCTD. The annotated weights were normalized per spot so cell type probabilities sum to 1 for each spot. Finally, normalized cell type probabilities per spot were plotted (red = 0.5, blue = 0 probability).

Xenium data acquisition and analysis

A customized 300-gene panel was developed with our snRNAseq data. The panel was designed based on three criteria: (i) canonical marker genes to identify all major cell types and clusters detected in snRNAseq; (ii) genes significantly differentially expressed in SNI versus Sham, prioritizing those in key “perturbed” populations; and (iii) genes from pathways strongly implicated by our analysis, particularly Synaptic LTP, Synaptogenesis, and Glutamatergic signaling (Supplementary Data 2). Sham and SNI lumbar spinal cord collected at day 28 were prepared as formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks. 10 μm sections were overlaid on Xenium slides before deparaffinization, decrosslinking and probe hybridisation with the predesigned custom standalone probe panels. The DNA probes are flanked by two regions that independently hybridize to the target RNA and also contain a gene specific barcode sequence. The probes hybridized to their complementary target RNA in an overnight incubation.

The following day, excess unbound probes were washed away in the post hybridization wash step. After removal of unbound probes, a ligase was added to seal the junction between the probe regions that have hybridized to RNA. Ligation of the probe ends on the targeted RNA region generates a circular DNA probe. This ligation ensures a unique level of probe specificity to the target region. The ligation products were enzymatically amplified to generate hundreds of copies of the gene-specific barcodes. Autofluorescence quenching was conducted to diminish unwanted autofluorescence and enhance signal-to-noise ratio in the treated FFPE tissue sections. Next, nuclei were stained with DAPI (derived from Xenium Nuclei Staining Buffer) to assist in identification of tissue or regions of interest during an instrument overview scan. Finally, tissue sections on Xenium slides assembled into Xenium Cassettes were loaded into the Xenium Analyzer for imaging and decoding.

Xenium data analysis

Xenium data was loaded onto R using Seurat’s LoadXenium function before normalization with SCTransformv2. The normalized data is then annotated using RCTD as described above. The weights are normalized for each spatial barcode and the resultant cell type annotation with the highest weights are plotted using plot_puck_continous function. Ipsilateral and contralateral dorsal regions were split using the Crop function in Seurat and saved as individual objects following subsetting with subset_opt. The ipsilateral and contralateral objects were merged before differential expression analysis was conducted for the “perturbed” cells with the FindMarker function. Statistically significant genes with adjusted p-value < 0.05, determined via Wilcoxon rank sum test, were plotted against their snRNAseq values for comparison.

Stem cell differentiation

HiPSCs (ATCC, ACS1030) were purchased directly from ATCC and didn’t undergo further validation. They were prepared as previously described35. Briefly, hiPSCs were dissociated using TrypLE (Invitrogen) and grown for two weeks in suspension cultures in ultra-low attachment plates to facilitate the formation of embryoid bodies (EBs). To induce neural differentiation, cells were treated with SMAD inhibitors LDN193189 (100 nM) and SB431542 (10 µM) from days 0 to 14. For Medial Ganglionic Eminence (MGE) induction, cells received Wnt inhibitor IWP2 (5 µM), SHH activator SAG (Smoothened Agonist, 0.1 µM), and growth factor FGF8 (100 ng/mL) from days 0-21, with Rock inhibitor added on day 1. After two weeks in suspension culture, EBs were transferred to polyornithine (PLO) and fibronectin (FN)-coated surfaces. At day 21, EBs were dissociated, and cells were replated onto PLO/FN-coated plates in differentiation media containing 10 ng/mL BDNF, 10 ng/mL GDNF, and 2.5 µM gamma secretase inhibitor DAPT for further differentiation and maturation. GABA interneurons were dissociated at 25 days of differentiation and resuspended in injection media consisting of Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS) containing 10 ng/mL GDNF, 20 µM Boc-Asp(OMe) fluoromethyl ketone (broad-spectrum caspase inhibitor), and 50 nM Necrosulfonamide (MLKL inhibitor). These neurons underwent single-cell RNA sequencing with 10X Chromium and DropSeq, as previously described8 (Fig. 8c, d). Integration and clustering of these data was conducted as described above. This differentiation was conducted again in 2 separate batches to be injected in 2 cohorts, described below.

GABAergic neuron transplantation and subsequent single nuclei profiling and analysis

Mice underwent laminectomy in the L1 and received GABAergic neuron intraspinal injection as previously described13. An incision is made along the back of anesthetized mice (Ketamine (Ketamil)/Xylazine cocktail (100 mg/8 mg/kg) by intraperitoneal injection) and L1 is removed by careful pedicular dissection using ophthalmic vannas spring scissors (World Precision instruments). A 2 µl injection targeting the lumbar dorsal horn was made using a stereotaxic apparatus (Kopf) and a Hamilton Syringe with a custom designed needle (29 gauge with point style 4 and 30° Angle, 1.5 inch length) ipsilateral to the injury using the posterior central spinal artery as a landmark. The superficial fascia was sutured using Vicryl 5-0 Reverse cutting sutures (Johnson and Johnson) and the incision was closed with 2–3 wound clips. Bupivacaine 8 mg/kg (Pfizer) was injected and irrigated subcutaneously/cutaneously around the wound edges and Enrofloxacin was provided daily for 10 days.

Mechanical hypersensitivity of these mice were assessed on day 0 (pre-surgery), 7, 14 and 21 after surgery. On day 28, their lumbar spinal cords were dissected and nuclei were prepared and sorted for snRNAseq as described above. The obtained snRNAseq data was aligned with Cellranger v7.1.0 with mixed species reference genome (refdata-gex-GRCh38-and-mm10-2020-A). The aligned data were co-clustered with earlier Sham and SNI mice data. Differential expression analysis was conducted using the FindMarker function of Seurat, comparing SNI+Transplant spinal cord vs SNI spinal cords. The differentially expressed genes for each cluster are then mapped to their corresponding pathways using IPA and plotted in a heatmap against pathways that were elevated in SNI (over Sham).

Ex vivo electrophysiology

Mice were deeply anaesthetised with isoflurane, decapitated and the lumbar region of the spinal cord was removed. Parasagittal spinal cord slices (300 μm) were cut using a vibratome (VT1200S, Leica Microsystems) in ice-cold sucrose cutting solution of composition (in mM): 185 Sucrose, 25 glucose, 25 NaHCO3, 2.5 KCl, 1.2 NaH2PO4, 10 MgCl2, 0.5 CaCl2. Slices were then incubated for 10 min at 34 °C in a N-Methyl-D-Glucamine (NMDG) recovery solution (in mM): 93 NMDG, 2.5 KCl, 1.2 NaH2PO4, 30 NaHCO3, 20 HEPES, 25 glucose, 5 sodium ascorbate, 3 sodium pyruvate, 2 thiourea, 10 MgCl2, 0.5 CaCl2 (pH 7.4), before being transferred to warmed (34 °C) artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) of composition (in mM): 126 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.4 NaH2PO4, 1.2 MgCl2, 2.4 CaCl2, 11 glucose and 25 NaHCO3; equilibrated with 95% O2 and 5% CO2. The slices were then maintained in a submerged chamber containing ACSF for at least 1 h at room temperature prior to recording. For recording, spinal cord slices were individually transferred to a chamber on an upright microscope with fluorescence capability (Olympus BX51) and superfused continuously with ACSF (34 °C, flow rate 2.5 ml.min-1). LI/LII neurons in the translucent substantia gelatinosa layer of the superficial dorsal horn were visualised with a ×40 water-immersion objective using Dodt gradient or DIC-IR contrast optics. Whole-cell patch clamp recordings (Multiclamp 700B, Molecular Devices) were made using glass electrodes (3-5 MΩ) that were filled with a K-gluconate based internal solution containing (in mM): 135 K-gluconate, 4 NaCl, 0.5 EGTA, 10 HEPES, 1 MgCl2, 2 MgATP, 0.3 NaGTP, and 0.1% biocytin (pH 7.3; osmolarity, 280–285 mOsm·L − 1). All recordings were filtered (3 kHz low-pass filter) and sampled (20 kHz) for online analysis using pCLAMP 11 software (Molecular Devices) or Axograph 1.1.8 (Axograph Scientific Software, RRID: SCR_014284). All off-line analysis was performed using Axograph 1.1.8.

Firing frequency was obtained from incremental depolarising steps (0-190pA, 500 ms, 10pA step interval), measured in current clamp mode with bridge balance and pipette capacitance compensation applied and monitored throughout experiments. Spontaneous excitatory post synaptic currents (sEPSC) were made from Tac1+ neurons that were visually identified by their expression of TdTomato. Neurons were voltage clamped at –70 mV and series resistance ( < 25 MΩ) was compensated at 60% and monitored throughout the experiment. Gabazine (10μM; Abcam) and strychnine (500 nM; Sigma) were added to the circulating ACSF to block GABAergic and glycinergic fast transmission respectively. sEPSCs were sampled continuously in 5 second epochs, with a 0 s sweep-to-sweep interval. sEPSCs above a preset threshold (3–4 SDs above baseline noise) were automatically detected by a sliding template algorithm, then manually checked off-line.

For post-hoc analysis of TdTomato labeled neurons, spinal cord slices were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde dissolved into 0.1 M phosphate buffer solution (PB), immediately after recording. 12–24 h after fixation, slices were washed 3 × 10 min in 0.1 PB, then stored free floating in 0.05% sodium azide at 4oC until processing. For immunofluorescence staining, slices were blocked and permeabilised with 5% horse serum, 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA), 0.3% Triton X-100 in 0.1 M PB for 1 hr at room temperature before being incubated over night with Streptavidin-Alexa Fluor-647 (1:1000 Themo Fisher Scientific, S32357) in 1% BSA, 0.1% Triton X-100, 0.1 M PB. Slices were washed 3 × 10 min with 0.1PB before mounting in Fluoromount-GTM (ThermoFisher Scientific, 00-4958-02). The sections were imaged with Nikon C2 at 40X.
