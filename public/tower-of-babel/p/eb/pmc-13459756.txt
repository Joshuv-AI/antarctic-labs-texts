Recurrent cardiac hydatid cyst: a rare case of initial misdiagnosis and successful surgical management
Authors: Sara Afshar, Yasamin Moeinipour, Hoorak Poorzand, Kamran Ghods, Arash Amadeh Taheri, Ghazaleh Afkhami Teimouri, Aliasghar Moeinipour

Background

Cardiac hydatid cyst (CHC), caused by Echinococcus granulosus, is exceptionally rare and accounts for only 0.5–2% of all hydatid cases [1]. Despite its rarity, CHC poses a serious clinical threat due to potential complications such as mechanical obstruction of cardiac chambers, valvular dysfunction, arrhythmias, embolization, or even fatal anaphylactic shock following cyst rupture [2, 3]. These features make CHC a challenging medical emergency despite its infrequent occurrence.

The current standard of care for cardiac hydatid disease is prompt surgical resection of the cyst, which immediately alleviates mass effect and averts rupture risk [4]. However, surgery alone is seldom curative. A prolonged course of adjuvant Albendazole therapy is indispensable post-operatively to eradicate any residual protoscolices and thereby prevent recurrence of CHC [4]. In practice, strict long-term adherence to this anti-parasitic regimen is often a formidable challenge. Patient non-compliance with prolonged albendazole prophylaxis is a major barrier to definitive cure. Moreover, interruption of therapy is associated with preventable relapses, which may be severe and clinically complex [5]. Indeed, observational evidence highlights that incomplete or premature discontinuation of albendazole treatment frequently precedes the development of recurrent hydatid cysts in previously treated patients [5].

Beyond recurrence, another critical challenge is the diagnostic ambiguity of cardiac hydatid cysts, which may closely mimic other structural cardiac abnormalities such as double-chambered right ventricle (DCRV) or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. This overlap often leads to initial misinterpretation on echocardiography, delaying definitive treatment.

Herein, we report a rare case of a large recurrent right ventricular hydatid cyst, which was initially misdiagnosed as DCRV on echocardiography. This recurrence occurred five years after pulmonary hydatid surgery in a patient with documented non-adherence to albendazole therapy. This case emphasizes the dual importance of accurate preoperative diagnosis and strict postoperative medical compliance in preventing life-threatening cardiac recurrences.

Case presentation

A 43-year-old Turkmen male from a remote rural region of Turkmenistan was referred to our cardiothoracic center with progressively worsening exertional dyspnea (New York Heart Association [NYHA] Class III) and severe non-productive cough over the preceding months and occasional paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (PND). Notably, the patient’s history was significant for a surgical resection of a pulmonary hydatid cyst five years prior, non-adherence to albendazole therapy, and loss to follow-up. On admission, his oxygen saturation on room air was 87%. Physical examination revealed signs of right-sided heart failure, including pitting edema of the lower extremities and ascites.

Preoperative chest radiograph demonstrated mild cardiomegaly with bilateral basal hazy opacities and small pleural effusions, suggestive of congestive changes (Fig. 1). Preoperative coronary angiography was also performed and demonstrated normal coronary arteries.

From the outpatient evaluation to the preoperative ICU stage, laboratory results showed a progressive rise in inflammatory indices, including WBC count, CRP, and LDH, with the highest recorded values observed in the preoperative period.

A mild and persistent eosinophilia was noted throughout hospitalization, reinforcing the parasitic etiology of the process. Notably, D-dimer levels were significantly elevated from early admission (637 ng/mL; ref < 349), In contrast, Troponin I remained negative across all measurements, effectively ruling out concurrent myocardial injury.

Renal indices (urea and creatinine) also remained within normal limits throughout.

Electrocardiography (ECG) revealed a normal sinus rhythm with a ventricular rate of approximately 65 beats per minute, normal PR and QT intervals, and no evidence of ischemic changes or conduction abnormalities.

The initial echocardiographic impression in both transthoracic and transesophageal studies suggested a DCRV, characterized by prominent hypertrophied muscle bundles in the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT). However, these findings were later correlated with the postoperative intraoperative discovery of a large hydatid cyst within the right ventricle, indicating that the echocardiographic features initially interpreted as muscular hypertrophy were, in fact, due to the cystic lesion compressing and distorting the ventricular anatomy. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed marked hypertrophy of the RVOT with prominent muscular bundles causing right ventricular obstruction and a turbulent systolic jet with a peak gradient of approximately 31 mmHg. Estimated mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) was 21 mmHg, with a pulmonary valve acceleration time (PVACT) of 98 ms, suggestive of mild pulmonary hypertension. Right ventricular size was within normal limits (basal diameter: 40 mm; mid-cavity diameter: 3.3 cm), and systolic function was preserved (TAPSE: 21 mm; S′: 9.6 cm/s) (Fig. 2). Preoperative echocardiography also demonstrated moderate tricuspid regurgitation, most likely secondary to compressive distortion of the tricuspid annulus and impaired leaflet coaptation caused by the cystic mass.

Further evaluation with transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) demonstrated multiple cystic-appearing masses within the right ventricle (Fig. 3). However, the size of the cyst could not be accurately measured on echocardiography because the overlapping cyst walls created the appearance of right ventricular hypertrophy and prominent right ventricular muscle bands. In the long-axis, short-axis, and off-axis views, these lesions produced the appearance of muscular hypertrophy with multiple intracavitary cystic structures contributing to flow turbulence (Figs. 4 and 5). The overlapping cyst walls particularly in the RVOT simulated thickened muscle bundles, closely mimicking DCRV. The available echocardiographic video clips have been attached as Supplementary Material for further visualization of the dynamic findings.

Based on these echocardiographic features, the working preoperative diagnosis was DCRV, and recurrent hydatid disease was not specifically suspected before surgery. Accordingly, no dedicated hydatid serological assays or advanced cross-sectional cardiac imaging (computed tomography [CT scan] or cardiac magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]) were obtained prior to the operation.

The patient was taken to the operating room for a combined procedure via a median sternotomy. After induction of general anesthesia, median sternotomy was performed. Following full systemic heparinization (3 mg/kg intravenous heparin; activated clotting time approximately 450 s), cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) was established via arterial cannulation of the ascending aorta and bicaval venous cannulation of the superior vena cava (SVC) and inferior vena cava (IVC). After initiation of CPB, both venae cavae were encircled and occluded with tapes to allow right atriotomy. Upon opening the right atrium, a large partially perforated cystic mass was visualized within the right ventricle, involving the infundibular region and extending toward the pulmonary trunk and pulmonary valve, arising from the interventricular septum (Fig. 6).

Intraoperative photograph after right atriotomy, showing the massive, multi-vesicular hydatid cyst within the right ventricular cavity prior to extraction

The cyst cavity contained membranous and necrotic material, which was carefully evacuated from the right ventricular cavity (Fig. 7). The cavity was then thoroughly irrigated with 5% hypertonic saline as a scolicidal agent to prevent recurrence or dissemination, with an approximate contact time of 5 min before suction and final saline irrigation. The cyst consisted of two to three whitish internal laminated layers, which were removed intact without difficulty. In contrast, the external pericyst was markedly fibrotic and densely adherent to the surrounding myocardium; therefore, it was carefully shaved and partially excised from the right ventricular infundibular region to relieve the RVOT obstruction while avoiding excessive myocardial injury. After complete cyst resection, the anterior tricuspid leaflet was directly repaired using 5 − 0 polypropylene sutures, and the adequacy of repair was confirmed intraoperatively using a saline test, resulting in satisfactory restoration of valve competence. Following successful cyst excision and valve reconstruction, the right atrium was closed. Hemostasis was secured, and the sternum was approximated with steel wires, followed by layered closure of the subcutaneous tissue and skin. Retrocardiac and pericardial drains were inserted, and the patient was transferred to the ICU in stable hemodynamic condition.

Postoperative course and follow-up

Postoperatively, he remained on ventilatory support for approximately 8 h and was extubated uneventfully on the day of surgery after adequate hemodynamic stabilization. Histopathological examination of the excised cyst confirmed hydatid disease, after which adjuvant antiparasitic therapy was initiated once the patient was clinically stable. No major postoperative biochemical deterioration was observed, and laboratory abnormalities gradually improved following cyst resection and cardiac stabilization. Abnormal laboratory values and their temporal trends during hospitalization are summarized in Table 1.

He was discharged in good clinical condition on postoperative day 5 and subsequently relocated abroad, but was monitored for 6 months through remote transthoracic echocardiographic surveillance. At predefined intervals (approximately every 4–6 weeks), TTE was performed at a local cardiology center and the digital images were transmitted electronically to our team for review. Serial echocardiograms demonstrated a stable right ventricular geometry, preserved tricuspid valve function, and no evidence of new intracardiac cysts or recurrent mass‑like lesions. Albendazole therapy was re-initiated at a dose of 400 mg twice daily (approximately 10–15 mg/kg/day) and was planned to be continued for at least 6–12 months in repeated 28-day cycles separated by 14-day drug-free intervals, with regular monitoring of liver function tests, in line with current recommendations for postoperative prophylaxis in complicated recurrent hydatid disease. During the 6‑month follow‑up period, the patient remained asymptomatic, reported good functional capacity, and demonstrated full adherence to the prescribed antiparasitic regimen.

Discussion and conclusions

This report highlights a rare and life-threatening manifestation of recurrent Echinococcus granulosus infection, compounded by both prolonged non-adherence to antiparasitic therapy and an initial diagnostic misinterpretation. Five years after successful surgical treatment of a pulmonary hydatid cyst, our patient re-presented with a massive right ventricular hydatid cyst that was initially misdiagnosed as a DCRV on echocardiography. Despite the rare occurrence of CHC, it carries a high risk of catastrophic complications if left untreated. Acute cardiac cyst rupture can lead to embolic phenomena, tamponade, or anaphylactic shock [6], and published case series report double-digit fatality rates for CHC [6]. In our case, the hydatid cyst’s location in the heart and resultant valvular destruction created a dire scenario directly attributable to the patient’s lapse in medical therapy.

Cardiac involvement most commonly affects the left ventricle, whereas isolated right ventricular localization is uncommon, comprising only about 10% of CHC cases [7]. In this patient, previous pulmonary hydatid disease together with premature discontinuation of albendazole likely contributed to delayed recurrence. One hypothesis is that during the initial infection (or surgical manipulation thereof), microscopic parasite elements escaped into the circulation and eventually lodged in the myocardial capillary bed of the RV. The distribution of cysts in the heart is known to follow the pattern of coronary blood flow [6]. Parasite larvae that bypass the hepatic and pulmonary filters may seed the right ventricular myocardium and remain dormant for years before proliferating after albendazole discontinuation. This is consistent with previously described hematogenous spread via coronary microcirculation or pulmonary capillaries after leakage from a primary cyst [8]. Notably, truly secondary CHC due to postoperative parasite dissemination is exceedingly rare in the literature [5]. Our case, therefore, provides a unique example of this phenomenon, illustrating how a breakdown in the continuum of care allowed a latent cardiac focus to flourish over time.

This case also illustrates the considerable diagnostic and operative challenges of CHC. Preoperatively, intracardiac hydatid cysts can be difficult to distinguish on echocardiography from other right-sided lesions such as hypertrophy, thrombus, or benign tumors [9], particularly when located near the RVOT or tricuspid valve, where shadowing and limited acoustic windows impair image quality. Motion artefacts and suboptimal views further contribute to diagnostic ambiguity, so expertise in image interpretation and, when feasible, supplementary cross-sectional imaging (CT or cardiac MRI) are often required to define the lesion and its extent. In our patient, the echocardiographic appearance of the cyst closely simulated DCRV, as its compressive effect on the RVOT created a false impression of hypertrophied muscle bundles and turbulent flow. This overlap highlights how hydatid disease can mimic structural heart disorders and underlines the need for a high index of suspicion and multimodal imaging in patients from endemic areas, where any atypical right ventricular “mass” or “hypertrophy” should prompt consideration of parasitic etiology.

In our patient, the right ventricular cyst was large and partially ruptured, yet its hydatid nature became evident only at surgery and was subsequently confirmed by histopathological examination of the excised specimen, as no dedicated preoperative hydatid serological testing or advanced cross‑sectional cardiac imaging had been performed. Once identified, the surgical management in this case was exceptionally challenging. The patient required an emergent combined operation encompassing major procedures: complete excision of the RV hydatid cyst (which had perforated and was endangering the patient), and repair of the severely damaged tricuspid valve. The hydatid cyst excision, in particular, demanded meticulous technique, and saline irrigation with scolicidal agents to neutralize Echinococcus material intraoperatively. Furthermore, reconstructing the tricuspid valve (instead of replacing it) was preferred, given the active infection risk, but this was made technically difficult by the extensive tissue destruction caused by the cyst. Taken together, this case presented an extreme scenario at the intersection of parasitology and cardiac surgery. The fact that the patient survived and recovered without major complications is a testament to thorough interdisciplinary surgical expertise.

As a single-patient case report, our observations have inherent limitations. The patient was referred from another country for surgery and left our country approximately one week after the operation, which restricted our ability to provide in-person, long-term follow-up. Consequently, current follow-up is limited to the early postoperative period and remote echocardiographic monitoring, so the long-term durability of the tricuspid valve repair and the patient’s sustained adherence to albendazole cannot yet be confirmed. In addition, the absence of serial pre-admission imaging prevents precise reconstruction of when the cardiac cyst began to develop during the five-year interval after the initial pulmonary surgery, and advanced immunological or molecular analyses of the cyst were not performed, limiting detailed characterization of the parasite and the mechanisms underlying recurrence. Moreover, no preoperative CT, cardiac MRI, or dedicated hydatid serological/immunological testing was performed, because the working diagnosis was a purely structural RVOT obstruction (presumed DCRV) based on echocardiography and there was no specific preoperative suspicion of a CHC.

Conclusions

This case highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of clinical suspicion for cardiac hydatid cysts, particularly in patients from endemic regions or those with a previous history of hydatid disease in other organs. The initial diagnostic misinterpretation in our case demonstrates how cardiac involvement can closely mimic structural heart disorders. Accurate diagnosis requires the integrated assessment of clinical presentation, laboratory findings, and multimodal imaging.

Equally important, long-term continuation of antiparasitic therapy after surgical intervention remains essential to prevent recurrence. Even after an apparent surgical cure, discontinuing albendazole prematurely can allow dormant parasitic elements to reactivate and lead to life-threatening complications.
