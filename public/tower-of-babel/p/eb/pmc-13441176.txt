Insulin gene-modified 3D stem cells for enhanced islet regeneration and systemic reversal of type 1 diabetes
Authors: Qian Li, Xiao Ma, Kefei Yuan, Jiaxin Li, Li Fu, Nanxi Liu, Jianchuan Hu, Xiangzheng Chen, Yanna Zhang, Xiancheng Chen, Yong Zeng, Zhen Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractType 1 diabetes (T1D) is a chronic autoimmune disease characterized by the destruction of pancreatic β cells, leading to lifelong insulin dependence and an increased risk of severe complications. Three-dimensional stem cells (3D SCs) culture systems have emerged as a superior alternative by more accurately mimicking the in vivo microenvironment and enhancing stemness maintenance, regenerative efficiency, and paracrine secretion. However, studies exploring the application of 3D SCs in T1D remain limited. Here, we developed a novel serum- and cytokine-free orbital-shaking system. It enables efficient and large-scale reprogramming of somatic cells into 3D embryonic-like stem cell spheroids (Sph-Es) characterized by robust pluripotency and improved safety. To enhance therapeutic utility, Sph-Es were irradiated and transduced with INS-expressing adenoviral vectors to generate Sph-R-Ins, allowing transient insulin production without permanent genomic modification. In STZ-induced T1D mice, Sph-R-Ins improved glycemic control and glucose tolerance and increased mouse insulin and C-peptide responses, indicating improved endogenous islet function. Donor-cell tracking analyses showed no pancreatic engraftment, supporting an indirect mode of action. Additional transcriptomic, immunological, and ex vivo studies indicated that the therapeutic benefit was accompanied by ECM-related signaling changes, reduced inflammatory infiltration, enhanced M2 macrophage polarization and Treg-associated immune regulation, improved metabolic signaling, and spheroid-derived paracrine support of islet function. Together, these findings establish a mechanically guided 3D stem cell–gene therapy platform with both endocrine and immunometabolic benefits in T1D.

Three-dimensional culture enhances the stemness and therapeutic potential of induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), yet current methods are not suitable for large-scale production. Here, we used a dynamic suspension system to reprogram somatic cells into embryonic-like spheroids (Sph-Es) with pluripotency confirmed both in vitro and in vivo. After irradiation and INS-expressing adenoviral engineering (Sph-R-Ins), we show that Sph-R-Ins promotes pancreatic islet repair, reshapes the immune microenvironment toward an anti-inflammatory profile, and alleviates hepatic insulin resistance, with a favorable safety profile. These findings offer a novel strategy for applying 3D iPSCs in type 1 diabetes treatment.

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization, the global prevalence of diabetes has increased dramatically with lifestyle changes. The 2025 International Diabetes Federation (IDF) Diabetes Atlas reported that diabetes affected approximately 589 million adults worldwide in 2024, and this number is projected to rise to 853 million by 2050.1 Among the major forms of diabetes, type 1 diabetes (T1D) and type 2 diabetes (T2D) account for the vast majority of cases. T1D is a chronic autoimmune disease characterized by immune-mediated destruction of pancreatic β cells, resulting in absolute insulin deficiency and persistent hyperglycemia. Although lifelong exogenous insulin administration remains the standard treatment, it cannot fully recapitulate the physiological dynamics of endogenous insulin secretion.2 Consequently, many patients remain vulnerable to acute metabolic decompensation as well as long-term vascular and neurological complications.3 Although pancreatic islet transplantation offers a promising alternative by restoring endogenous insulin production,4,5 its broader application is severely limited by donor islet shortages, immune rejection, poor graft survival, and high cost. These limitations underscore the need for alternative regenerative strategies that not only improve glycemic control but also address the underlying loss of β cell function.

Stem cell-based therapies have emerged as promising approaches in regenerative medicine and diabetes treatment. Embryonic stem cells (ESCs) and induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) could be directed toward insulin-producing lineages and thus hold considerable potential for cell replacement therapy.6,7 In addition, engineering stem cells to overexpress betatrophin can induce β-cell proliferation in diabetic mice.8 However, most current strategies rely on conventional two-dimensional stem cells (2D SCs) and prolonged multistep differentiation protocols, which are often associated with variable efficiency, incomplete maturation, and safety concerns related to residual undifferentiated cells. In contrast, three-dimensional stem cells (3D SCs) more closely mimic native cell–cell and cell–matrix interactions and have been reported to exhibit improved survival, enhanced lineage potential, and stronger paracrine activity than their 2D counterparts. These features suggest that 3D stem cell platforms may offer a more suitable and clinically relevant basis for regenerative therapy.9,10

Mechanical cues are increasingly recognized as key regulators of stem cell fate,11,12 and emerging evidence suggests that circadian programs are remodeled during somatic cells reprogramming.13,14 Here, we developed a novel method to reverse mature human somatic cells into embryonic-like stem cells by modulating the physical-dynamics environment without genetic or chemical reprogramming factors.15 This gene-free strategy offers improved safety and scalability under xeno-free conditions. To explore the therapeutic utility of this platform in T1D, we generated irradiated spheroids (Sph-R-Es) and engineered them with INS-expressing adenoviral vectors (Sph-R-Ins) for transient insulin delivery. In T1D mice, this strategy improved glucose homeostasis through both insulin supplementation and spheroid-derived paracrine activity. These findings support the concept that combining 3D stem cell spheroids with gene delivery may improve endocrine function while also alleviating immune and metabolic dysregulation in diabetes.

In conclusion, our study introduces a novel approach that combines 3D stem cell spheroids with targeted gene delivery to tackle both the endocrine and immune challenges of T1D. This platform offers a scalable, safe, and clinically viable regenerative strategy for diabetes and other immune-mediated disorders.

Methods

Mice

Immunodeficient female NOD-SCID and male C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks old) were obtained from HFK Biotechnology (Beijing, China), all with Animal Quarantine Conformity Certificates. Arntl-/- and +/- knockout models on a C57BL/6 background were generated via CRISPR/Cas9 targeting exons 6-9 (Cyagen Biosciences). Mice were housed in laminar flow cabinets at 23 ± 1 °C, 45% ± 15% humidity, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle (7:00 am-7:00 pm), with free access to AIN-93M diet and autoclaved reverse osmosis water. All procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Sichuan University (No. 20201068A) and performed in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the Animal Welfare Act.

Cell culture

PIG1 (ATCC), Hosec (Science Cell), FHS 74 Int (Science Cell), and HPAEpiC (Science Cell) were used. PIG1, Hosec and FHS 74 Int were cultured in DME/F12 (Hyclone) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (P-S) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. HPAEpiC cells were cultured in DMEM/High Glucose (Hyclone) with 10% FBS and 1% P-S under the same conditions. At approximately 80% confluency, cells were trypsinized, centrifuged at 300 g for 3 min, and resuspended in fresh medium.

Dynamic formation of Sph-Es

Sph-Es were generated using a dynamic suspension culture system consisting of a horizontal orbital shaker placed inside a CO2 incubator to maintain stable temperature and gas conditions. Cells were seeded into 10 cm dishes at 1 × 106 cells per dish in 6 mL of serum-free DMEM/F12 medium. The orbital speed was set to 30-60 swings/min, empirically optimized to support spheroid formation and cell viability across multiple somatic cell types. The medium was refreshed every 2 days, and the total culture duration was 15-20 days, after which mature spheroids formed.

Immunocytochemistry analysis

Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 5 min, washed with TBST and PBS twice, permeabilized, blocked, and incubated with primary antibodies (Table S1; 1:100) at 4 °C overnight. Following incubation, cells were washed twice with PBS and then incubated for 90 min at room temperature with appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (Table S1; 1:100). Images were captured using a Zeiss confocal microscope.

Flow cytometry analysis

Sph-Es were dissociated into single cells (1-1.5 × 106 cells/sample). After fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilization, cells were stained with antibodies. 20 000 events were captured for each sample. Data were analyzed and graphed using FlowJo 10.0.7.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent according to the manufactures’ instructions. cDNA was synthesized with PrimeScriptTM RT Kit. Quantitative real-time (qRT)-PCR was performed using SYBR® Premix Ex TaqTM II on a Real-Time PCR System with gene-specific primers (Table S2). The data were analyzed using the ΔΔCt method. All primers were designed via NCBI Primer-Blast. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 7.0, and group differences were evaluated by a one-tailed Student’s t-test, with P < .05 considered significant.

Karyotyping

Cells were treated with 0.5 μg/mL colchicine for 2-2.5 h, dissociated with Accutase, and subjected to hypotonic treatment with pre-warmed 0.075 mol/L KCl. After the addition of the hypotonic solution, the cell suspension was gently inverted and then fixed in methanol: glacial acetic acid (3:1). The fixed cells were dropped onto clean glass slides, briefly air-dried, baked at 80 °C for 2 h, and stained with Giemsa for karyotype analysis.

Teratoma formation

For teratoma formation, Sph-Es were resuspended in serum-free Matrigel and injected into the hindlimb root of immunodeficient NOD-SCID mice (∼60 spheroids per mouse). Teratoma size was monitored monthly using calipers. After 4-5 months, mice were euthanized, and teratomas were collected for final measurement.

Functional pluripotency assessment and directed differentiation of Sph-Es

Tri-lineage differentiation and hepatocyte-like cell (HLC) differentiation were performed using commercial kits (R&D Systems, SC027B and SC033). Culture plates were coated with RGF Basement Membrane Extract for 1-2 h at room temperature. Sph-Es were dissociated into single cells and seeded onto coated plates at 1.10-1.25 × 105 cells/cm2.

For tri-lineage differentiation, cells were maintained in kit-provided induction media for ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm. Lineage commitment was confirmed by immunostaining for OTX2, BRACHYURY, and SOX17. For HLC differentiation, cells were cultured in kit-recommended media with bFGF during the initial stage; the media were replaced daily or every other day. Hepatic differentiation was assessed by ALBUMIN (ALB) immunofluorescence, ALB secretion ELISA (Elabscience), urea production assay (Beyotime), and CYP4503A4 immunostaining (MCE, HY-P84018).

High-throughput transcriptome sequencing

Total RNA was isolated from tissues using Trizol reagent (Ambion, Life Technology). RNA quality was assessed on a BioAnalyzer 2100 (Agilent), and samples were stored at −80 °C. Libraries were prepared using NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina, purified with Beckman AMPure XP beads, and quantified by ABI 7500 real-time PCR with KAPA SYBR green fast universal qPCR master mix. Samples were sequenced on HiSeqTM 4000 platform.

Whole-genome bisulfite sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted using the EasyPure Genomic DNA Kit (TransGen Biotech). DNA was bisulfite-converted with the EZ DNA Methylation-Gold Kit (Zymo Research), and libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform, generating approximately 90 Gb raw data per sample.

Data processing and methylation analysis

Quality was assessed with FastQC, and adapters were trimmed using Trimmomatic. Clean reads were aligned to the hg19 with Bismark, and duplicates were removed. Only CpG-context cytosines were retained. Methylation at each CpG was calculated as the fraction of methylated reads. Sites with FDR < 0.05 were considered methylated. Locus-level methylation profiles for OCT4, NANOG, and DPPA4 were visualized with IGV. Differentially methylated regions (DMRs) were identified using DSS (v2.12.0), and associated genes were defined as those with promoters (2 kb upstream) or gene bodies overlapping the DMRs.

Construction of recombinant adenoviruses vectors

The human INS coding sequence (RefSeq NM_000207) was amplified from a commercial cDNA template (GeneChem, Shanghai) using gene-specific primers. The PCR product was inserted into the adenoviral shuttle plasmid (pDC315-3FLAG-SV40-EGFP) via AgeI/NheI restriction cloning followed by In-Fusion recombination. The recombinant vector was confirmed by DNA sequencing.

Adenovirus amplification and purification

HEK-293 cells (5 × 105/well) were seeded in a 12-well plate 24 h before transfection. The shuttle plasmid and pBHGlox-AE1, 3Cre plasmid (Microbix) were co-transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 until cytopathic effects appeared. Medium and cells were harvested, then frozen at −80 °C and thawed at 37 °C three times to release adenoviruses. Clonal isolates were obtained by limiting dilution and amplified via serial passaging in HEK293 cells. Viral particles were purified and concentrated using the Adeno-X™ Virus Purification Kit (Takara), and the infectious titer (PFU/mL) was determined by plaque assay on HEK293 cells.

Sph-Es transduction

There are about 500 Sph-Es in a 10 cm dish, dynamically cultured for 20 days, replaced with 5 mL fresh medium. Fifty microliter virus solution was added into the dish. After 6 h, 3 mL of fresh medium was added. The operation of the control virus Ad-EGFP was the same.

T1D model

Seven-week-old male C57BL/6 mice (20-22 g) were injected intraperitoneally with streptozotocin (STZ; Sigma-Aldrich) at 40 mg/kg/day for five consecutive days. STZ was freshly dissolved in 0.1 mol/L sodium citrate buffer at 10 mg/mL and protected from light during preparation and injection. Tail-vein blood glucose was monitored under fasting conditions using an Accu-Chek Active glucose meter (Roche). Mice were considered diabetic when fasting blood glucose exceeded 16 mmol/L at 3 days after the final STZ injection. To evaluate pancreatic inflammation, insulitis (immune infiltration) was scored on H&E-stained pancreatic sections in a blinded manner using a 0-4 scale: 0, no infiltration; (1) peri-insulitis; (2) mild insulitis (lymphocyte infiltration in <50% of the islet); (3) severe insulitis (lymphocyte infiltration in >50%); and (4) complete destruction.16 CD45 immunofluorescence on adjacent sections was used as supportive evidence of leukocyte infiltration.

Stem cell therapy

Spheroid-based cell preparations were administered subcutaneously once weekly for 4 weeks. For each injection, mice received 1 × 10³ spheroids, corresponding to approximately 1 × 106 cells.17,18 Prior to transplantation, spheroids designated for the irradiated groups were exposed to 160 Gy irradiation to eliminate proliferative capacity and thereby reduce the risk of uncontrolled growth/teratoma formation while preserving short-term biological activity. The spheroid suspension was gently mixed immediately before and during injection to maintain a uniform distribution and minimize settling.

Blood glucose monitoring and IPGTT

Fast mice overnight for approximately 16 h by transferring mice to clean cages with no food but with enough water. Weigh the mouse and calculate the volume of 20% glucose solution required (2 g of glucose/kg body mass) for intraperitoneal injection. The blood glucose levels are measured at 15, 30, 60, and 120 min after glucose injection by placing a small drop of blood on a new test strip and recording the measurements.

Isolation and culture of primary islets

Primary islets were isolated as previously described.19 Briefly, the pancreas was perfused via the common bile duct with 4 mL collagenase V solution and digested at 37 °C for 15 min. Digestion was terminated by adding 5 mL HBSS containing 2% FBS. An additional 5 mL HBSS (2% FBS) was then added, and the tissue suspension was transferred to a 10 cm dish. Islets were hand-picked under a stereomicroscope, collected into fresh Hanks’ solution supplemented with 1%P-S, and maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO2 until further use.

Islet co-culture with Sph-R-Es conditioned medium and GSIS

Primary islets were transferred to Sph-R-Es supernatant (or matched control medium) and co-cultured for 48 h. For GSIS, islets were washed and pre-equilibrated in KRBB containing 2.8 mM glucose for 1 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, 20 islets per well were incubated in fresh KRBB with 2.8 mM glucose or 16.7 mM glucose for 1 h. Supernatants were collected for insulin measurement. Insulin was quantified using a mouse insulin ELISA kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Islet was lysed using buffer with PhosSTOP (Sigma-Aldrich).

Statistical analysis

The GraphPad software package system was used for most statistical analyses. Data were subjected to one-way ANOVA and t test. Numerical values are reported as means ± SEM standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was assumed for P < .05. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) in bioinformatics was performed for different gene modules with the R Package (version 3.2) as well as Cytoscape (version 3.6.0) based on whole transcriptome RNA-seq. Heatmaps were generated by the R package with a hierarchical clustering algorithm.

Results

Systemic establishment of Sph-Es from various germ layers

To determine whether mature somatic cells from all three germ layers could be reprogrammed into embryonic-like pluripotent spheroids (Sph-Es), four cell types (PIG1 from ectoderm, Hosec from mesoderm, FHS 74 Int, and HPAE Pic from endoderm) were subjected to a controllable dynamic suspension environment for 15-20 days (Figure S1A). Spheroid formation followed a similar trajectory across all cell types: early aggregates appeared by days 3-6, naïve spheroids by days 9-12, and well-formed Sph-Es by days 15-20 (Figure S1B). Under these conditions, estimated wall shear stress ranged from approximately 0.009 Pa at 30 swings/min to approximately 0.053 Pa at 80 swings/min (≈0.09-0.53 dyn/cm2). Confocal dynamic EGFP scanning illustrated spherical architecture and hollow germ layer development in Sph-Es derived from all four cell types (Figure 1A). By day 20, Sph-Es reached an average diameter of approximately 210-280 μm with a yield of approximately 240-280 spheroids/mL (Figure S1C). qRT-PCR confirmed significant upregulation of pluripotency-associated genes in Sph-Es compared to parental somatic cells (Figure 1B). Confocal dynamic detection showed that the expression of pluripotent markers gradually increased from day 3 (negative) to day 20 (strong) during conversion of all four cell types (Figure 1C and D and Figure S1D). Immunofluorescence confirmed that pluripotency markers were undetectable in all parental somatic cells (Figure S1E), and qRT-PCR showed gradual induction of key pluripotency genes during spheroid formation (Figure S1F), supporting that pluripotency emerges de novo rather than from rare pre-existing stem-like cells.

We next performed bulk RNA sequencing to compare the global transcriptomes of parental somatic cells, Sph-Es, and H9 cells. Unsupervised PCA showed a clear separation between Int and Int-derived Sph-Es (Figure S1G). Hierarchical clustering further showed that the overall transcriptional profile of Int-derived Sph-Es was more similar to H9 than to Int (Figure 1E). The pluripotency-associated regulators confirmed this trend (Figure 1F). DNA methylation analysis of core pluripotency loci revealed reduced promoter methylation in Sph-Es, indicating epigenetic reconfiguration toward a pluripotency-associated state (Figure 1G). Together, these transcriptional and epigenetic analyses support that Sph-Es acquire pluripotency-associated molecular features and adopt a state closer to H9 than to the starting somatic cells. Karyotype stability is critical for clinical application. Karyotype analysis of Sph-Es derived from all three germ layers revealed no abnormalities (Figure S1H and I).

Sph-Es with full differentiation potential

To assess the pluripotency-associated potential of Sph-Es, we performed a directed tri-lineage differentiation assay. Sph-Es generated representative cell types of all three germ layers (Figure 2A). In parallel, Sph-Es differentiated into hepatocyte-like cells (Sph-Es-HLCs; Figure 2B and C) and exhibiting hepatic functions (Figure 2D). These results support the multilineage differentiation capacity of Sph-Es in vitro. Furthermore, the in vivo teratoma formation was observed in all four Sph-Es types, displaying similar structural patterns (Figure S2A). MRI scanning revealed teratoma with similar cystic formation patterns and a clear boundary/no invasion (Figure 2E). Quantitative assessments showed comparable teratoma mean areas across all groups (Figure S2B). Histological analysis demonstrated the teratoma covering architectures from all three germ layers (Figure 2F), further confirming their pluripotent capability.

In addition, we also found that Sph-Es transplantation in SCID mice leads to the development of human multicentric hematopoietic tissue over a five-month period. Initially, cartilage formed by Month 1 (M1), then hollows to create a bone marrow cavity (M2). Primitive stem cells migrated and begin forming hematopoietic tissue (M3), which matures and expands, filling the cavity by M5 (Figure S2C). Confocal scans indicated that CD34 and CD38 expression increased over time, with CD34 exceeding CD38 levels by M5 (Figure 2G and H). Concurrently, the hematopoietic maturation index rose from 0% to over 80% (Figure S2D), colony counts increased to about 107 (Figure S2E), and the tissue area expanded to around 30 mm2 (Figure S2F). In all, the formation of mature human multicentric hematopoietic tissue further illustrates the pluripotency of Sph-Es.

Dynamic suspension signal resets mature rhythm to embryonic stem biorhythm

Pluripotent stem cells typically exhibit attenuated circadian oscillations, with robust rhythmicity emerging during differentiation. During somatic cell reprogramming, circadian programs could be remodeled alongside changes in cell identity.20,21 Mechanical constraints have also been reported to drive nuclear and chromatin remodeling, promoting the acquisition of stem-like properties in the absence of exogenous biochemical factors.22,23 Consistent with this framework, transcriptome analysis revealed upregulation of candidate genes associated with mechanically driven reprogramming and proliferation (NODAL, TCF4, WNT5A, and TIMP1) in Sph-Es relative to parental somatic cells, supporting activation of cell-state transition programs during conversion (Figure 3A). KEGG enrichment further indicated activation of stemness-related signaling pathways, including signaling pathways regulating pluripotency of stem cells and the Wnt signaling pathway (Figure 3B). Given the established link between Wnt signaling and pluripotent cell identity, these pathway-level changes provide additional transcriptomic evidence for engagement of pluripotency-associated regulatory networks during Sph-Es induction.24

Circadian rhythms are also known to influence pluripotency during somatic cell reprogramming.13 Notably, shaking culture has been reported to attenuate circadian rhythms via the TEAD–FBXL3–CRY axis.25 In line with this, RNA-seq analysis showed that TEAD2 and FBXL3 were upregulated, whereas CRY2 expression was reduced in Sph-Es relative to parental cells (Figure 3C), suggesting a link between mechanotransduction and reconfiguration of clock-associated programs during Sph-Es induction.

To more rigorously assess rhythmic behavior, parental somatic cells, corresponding Sph-Es, and H9 were synchronized with dexamethasone and collected at ZT0, 8, 16, 24, 32, 40, and 48 h for qRT-PCR analysis. The core clock genes ARNTL, CLOCK, PER3, and CRY2 showed no clear oscillatory pattern in Sph-Es, and a similar nonoscillatory profile was observed in H9 (Figure 3D). High-content immunofluorescence further demonstrated that these clock-related factors were detectable in Sph-Es together with pluripotency-associated markers, whereas the parental somatic cells displayed only limited expression of clock-related factors (Figure 3E and Figure S3A-F). Collectively, these findings suggest that clock component expression is retained or reconfigured during Sph-Es conversion, whereas robust circadian oscillations remain dampened in the pluripotent-like state.

To explore the functional relevance of circadian genes during reprogramming, CRISPR/Cas9 technique was used to establish Arntl+/+; +/- or -/- model. Sph-Es conversion trends and alkaline phosphatase (AP) expressions from different bone marrow (BM) stromal cells with Arntl+/+; +/-or -/- subjected to 20 days of orbiting-dynamics environments demonstrated that knocking out Arntl rhythm-related genes inhibited the reprogramming to stem cells, and only the cells with perfect Arntl+/+ could be subverted into panoramic Sph-Es, with positive AP reactivity (Figure 3F-H). Together, these transcriptomic, synchronization, and genetic perturbation data support that dynamic mechanical culture is associated with activation of mechanotransduction-related programs and remodeling of clock-associated regulators during Sph-Es induction, with Arntl contributing to efficient acquisition of pluripotency-associated features.

Infusion of Sph-R-Ins ameliorated hyperglycemia in T1D mice

To prevent teratoma formation risk, Sph-Es were ameliorated to be safer by irradiation.26 Safety of irradiated (Sph-R-Es) and nonirradiated (Sph-NR-Es) was assessed in NOD-SCID mice. Sph-R-Es significantly reduced teratoma formation (Figure S4A), with residual masses mainly containing vascular-like structures (Figure S4B), indicating reduced proliferation and differentiation after irradiation. T1D was induced by consecutive low-dose streptozotocin (STZ) injections.27 Diabetic mice developed sustained hyperglycemia under both fasted/refed conditions and impaired glucose tolerance in IPGTT. (Figure S4C and D). Histological revealed islet atrophy and increased immune infiltration (Figure S4E).

To determine whether transient insulin provision could improve early glycemic stabilization and thereby enhance the overall therapeutic outcome of irradiated spheroids, we engineered Sph-R-Es with an INS-expressing adenoviral vector (Sph-R-Ins). Confocal EGFP scanning showed approximately 80% transfection efficiency at 48 h (Figure 4A). Sph-R-Ins retained expression of pluripotency-associated markers (Figure 4B and Figure S4F and G). Insulin transcripts and secretion increased after Ad-INS transduction, with no major difference between irradiated and nonirradiated spheroids (Figure S4H). Sph-R-Ins secreted insulin in response to glucose stimulation (Figure S4I).

T1D mice received weekly subcutaneous injections of the indicated cell-based preparations for four weeks (Figure S4J). Sph-R-Ins improved body weight and lowered blood glucose more rapidly than Sph-R-Es (Figure 4C and Figure S4K). Both groups enhanced glucose tolerance in IPGTT and insulin sensitivity (ITT), with greater improvement in Sph-R-Ins (Figure 4D, E and Figure S4L and M). Pancreatic immunofluorescence revealed restored insulin secretion (Figure 4F), improved islet morphology, endocrine marker expression, and reduced immune infiltration (Figure 4G). Quantification showed increased islet number and area, with the largest gains in the Sph-R-Ins group (Figure 4H), consistent with lower insulitis scores (Figure S4N). Serum mouse insulin and C-peptide (basal/glucose-stimulated) were significantly elevated in the Sph-R-Ins group (Figure 4I). Ex vivo co-culture of primary islets with Sph-R-Es conditioned medium enhanced insulin secretion (Figure 4J), supporting improved endogenous islet function.

To further determine whether Sph-R-Es offer superior therapeutic benefits over conventional 2D SCs, we included a 2D SCs treatment group for direct comparison. Sph-R-Es achieved better glycemic control, higher body weight recovery, and improved glucose tolerance than the 2D SCs group (Figure S5A-D). Histological analyses also revealed better preservation or regeneration of islets in the Sph-R-Es group, with increased islet numbers, larger islet areas, and stronger insulin staining compared to 2D SCs (Figure S5E-H). Collectively, these results demonstrate that Sph-R-Ins not only retain pluripotency and insulin secretion ability but also outperform both nonmodified Sph-R-Es and conventional 2D SCs therapies in ameliorating hyperglycemia and promoting islet repair in T1D mice.

Sph-R-Ins regulates the immune microenvironment in T1D mice

To investigate whether Sph-R-Ins modulates the immune microenvironment in T1D mice, we first assessed systemic cytokine profiles using a Luminex liquid suspension assay. Sph-R-Ins treatment significantly reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-17A, IL-1α, IL-1β) while elevating anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10, IL-4 and IL-12; Figure 5A and B). Flow cytometry of splenic immune subsets revealed a marked increase in CD206+ M2 macrophages (Figure 5C), as well as a significant expansion of CD4+CD25+FoxP3+ regulatory T cells (Tregs; Figure 5D).

To further elucidate the immunomodulatory effects of Sph-R-Es, we examined whether secreted factors from Sph-R-Es could directly influence immune cell polarization. Supernatant from Sph-R-Es was applied to RAW 264.7 macrophages, as flow cytometry confirmed its immune activity (Figure 5E). In vivo, immunofluorescence staining of pancreatic sections revealed reduced infiltration of CD4+IFN-γ+(Th1) and CD4+IL-17+ (Th17) cells in Sph-R-Es mice (Figure 5F). Consistently, splenic immunofluorescence staining showed that the Sph-R-Ins treatment significantly suppressed Th1 and Th17 cell populations, reflecting a restored peripheral immune balance (Figure 5G). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Sph-R-Es alleviates hyperglycemia while restoring peripheral immune tolerance in T1D.

Sph-R-Ins activates islet developmental programs and tissue repair pathways

To explore the molecular mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of Sph-R-Ins, we performed bulk transcriptomic profiling of pancreatic tissue from Sph-R-Ins and T1D mice. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were visualized by a volcano plot, highlighting transcripts significantly upregulated or downregulated in the Sph-R-Ins group (Figure S6A). Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that DEGs were enriched in biological processes related to endodermal development, tissue formation, and stem cell differentiation, while GO cellular component terms highlighted associations with extracellular matrix (ECM) components and focal adhesion (Figure S6B). Hierarchical clustering further confirmed coordinated upregulation of gene modules linked to embryonic and vascular development in Sph-R-Ins mice (Figure 6A). In addition, to further evaluate the endocrine functional status at the transcript level, we examined expression patterns of additional markers representing endocrine lineages and islet function within the bulk RNA-seq dataset. (Figure 6B and Figure S6C). To validate the RNA-seq results, we performed qRT-PCR on isolated pancreatic islets. Developmental and stemness-associated genes were elevated in both Sph-R-Ins and Sph-R-Es groups, with significantly higher expression in Sph-R-Ins compared with Sph-R-Es (Figure 6C), consistent with improved early glycemic stabilization in the Sph-R-Ins group. Islet developmental/progenitor-associated markers (Pdx1, Nestin, Ngn3, Pax4, Nkx6.1, and Nkx2.2) were upregulated after the initial treatment in Sph-R-Ins mice (Figure 6D). Following the final injection, these markers also increased in Sph-R-Es mice, consistent with a time-dependent response (Figure 6E). Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed the presence of NKX6.1-positive cells within islets in Sph-R-Ins group (Figure 6F). Collectively, these transcriptional and immunostaining data support enhanced activation of endogenous islet developmental programs in the Sph-R-Ins group compared with diabetic controls.

To exclude donor-cell engraftment as a source of pancreatic insulin signals, we tracked Ad-INS–engineered spheroids using an EGFP-labeled insulin adenovirus. EGFP-positive signals were not detectable in the pancreas, liver, spleen, or kidney at the endpoint (Figure S6D). Consistently, species-specific qRT-PCR detected mouse Ins transcripts in pancreatic tissue, whereas human INS transcripts were undetectable (Figure S6E), supporting a host-derived origin of pancreatic insulin expression.

Finally, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs between Sph-R-Ins and T1D group identified pathways related to ECM-receptor interaction, focal adhesion, and PI3K-Akt signaling (Figure S6F). These results align with previous studies showing that ECM components are essential for maintaining tissue architecture and promoting regeneration.28,29 qRT-PCR further revealed that the relative expression levels of genes Col6a1, Col6a2, Col6a3, and Col4a1 related to collagenase VI and collagenase IV were upregulated in the Sph-R-Ins mice (Figure S6G).

Since neovascularization is crucial for islet regeneration, we examined vascular remodeling.30 KEGG analysis revealed enrichment in hematopoietic stem cell-related pathways. qRT-PCR showed increased expression of vascular-associated genes Cd53, Cd93, Egfl7, and Kdr in the Sph-R-Ins group (Figure S7A). Confocal immunofluorescence further demonstrated Ki67 positivity within vascular regions of islets, indicating active neovascularization post-treatment (Figure S7B).

Sph-R-Ins ameliorates insulin resistance in the liver

Long-term subcutaneous insulin injection could cause insulin resistance.3 To assess whether Sph-R-Ins affects insulin resistance in T1D mice, we examined histological and molecular markers in metabolic tissues. H&E staining revealed that T1D mice exhibited marked hepatic steatosis and inflammatory infiltration, as well as disorganized skeletal muscle fiber structure, all of which were significantly alleviated in Sph-R-Ins-treated mice (Figure 7A). Given the central roles of liver and muscle in glucose homeostasis,31 we next evaluated metabolic enzyme expression. qRT-PCR showed that the mRNA level of glucokinase (Gck), citrate synthase (Cs), and isocitrate dehydrogenase (Icdh) involved in glucose catabolism-related enzymes in the liver and muscle in Sph-R-Ins mice were higher than in T1D mice (Figure 7B and C). Conversely, glucose-6-phosphatase (G-6-p), fork head box protein O1 (Foxo1) and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (Pepck) related to gluconeogenesis in Sph-R-Ins mice were lower than in T1D mice (Figure 7D), suggesting suppressed hepatic gluconeogenesis. Insulin resistance is also known to impair glucose transporter expression, particularly glucose transporter 4 (Glut4) in muscle and Glut2 in liver.31 We found that both Glut2 and Glut4 mRNA levels were significantly increased in Sph-R-Ins mice compared to the T1D group (Figure 7E), indicating improved glucose uptake.

Chronic insulin resistance could cause inflammation in the liver.32 To assess inflammatory status, we measured hepatic cytokine expression. Pro-inflammatory markers were significantly reduced, while anti-inflammatory were markedly elevated in Sph-R-Ins-treated mice (Figure 7F and G). These results suggest that Sph-R-Ins not only alleviates liver inflammation but may also alleviate hepatic insulin resistance through modulation of inflammatory cytokine profiles.

Discussion

As a leading biological therapy, iPSCs exhibit self-renewal and multi-lineage differentiation capabilities, typically achieved via transcription factor overexpression or small molecule induction.33–35 However, conventional 2D SCs reprogramming systems face major translational barriers, limiting clinical efficacy.33,34 In this study, we established a novel strategy to reprogram diverse mature somatic cells into Sph-Es using a variable physical-dynamics environment over 20 days. The resulting Sph-Es displayed hallmark pluripotency markers and could differentiate into hepatocyte-like cells. Collectively, our proof-of-principle study demonstrates that a serum-free dynamic 3D culture environment dominated by physical mechanical cues can induce diverse somatic cells to acquire embryonic-like pluripotent features, highlighting the potential of mechanical regulation in cell fate modulation.

Current stem cell–based strategies for T1D primarily focus on differentiating stem cells into islet progenitors or β cells to restore insulin secretion.35,36 Beyond differentiation, stem cells also possess intrinsic homing and paracrine functions,37,38 enabling migration to damaged tissues and secretion of factors that support islet repair and immune modulation,39 as demonstrated using human umbilical cord–derived stem cells in diabetic mice.40

Building upon these properties, we developed a novel 3D stem cell system integrating the regenerative and immunomodulatory functions of Sph-Es with ad-INS transfer (Sph-R-Ins). In our system, adenoviral insulin gene transfer further enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of 3D stem cell spheroids (Sph-R-Es), as evidenced by superior glycemic control in the Sph-R-Ins group compared to Sph-R-Es alone. Leveraging stem cell homing capacity, Sph-R-Ins effectively targeted injured islets, supported their structural repair, and provided timely insulin supplementation. Although Sph-R-Ins significantly improved hyperglycemia in T1D mice, blood glucose levels did not fully normalize to those observed in nondiabetic controls. This contrasts with previous studies using stem cell–derived islets, where direct transplantation can transiently restore normoglycemia. These differences likely reflect distinct therapeutic objectives: transplanted islets provide immediate insulin secretion to acutely lower blood glucose, whereas Sph-R-Ins are designed for sustained paracrine support, immunomodulation, and endogenous β‑cell repair. Our data indicate that Sph-R-Ins improve islet structure, increase β‑cell marker expression, and modulate the local immune microenvironment, supporting sustained functional recovery rather than short-term glucose normalization. Moreover, Sph-R-Es secreted bioactive factors that may contribute to islet preservation and regeneration. Overall, while Sph-R-Ins do not acutely normalize glycemia to nondiabetic levels, they demonstrate durable, mechanistically supported improvements in islet function, highlighting a complementary therapeutic approach to conventional islet transplantation. To evaluate the therapeutic advantage of our 3D system, we compared its glucose-lowering efficacy with conventional 2D‑cultured stem cells. Only 3D spheroids achieved sustained effects, consistent with reports that 3D culture enhances stem cell retention and paracrine activity.41 The superior persistence of Sph-R-Ins likely reflects a native‑like microenvironment with improved cell–cell and cell–matrix interactions, enhancing post‑transplantation survival and function.

Stem cells exert immunomodulatory effects through direct cell-cell interactions and soluble paracrine factors,39 including suppression of CD4+/CD8+ T cells, induction of Tregs, M2 macrophage polarization, and inhibition of B cell, NK cell, neutrophil, and dendritic cells. Sph-Es also reduce infiltration cells into damaged tissues, promoting immune tolerance and homeostasis. The spleen, as the largest secondary lymphoid organ, plays a central role in T1D by integrating peripheral immune signals and orchestrating systemic immune response, actively participating in the initiation and perpetuation of autoimmune processes.42 In our study, Sph-R-Ins treatment increased M2 macrophages and CD4+CD25+FoxP3+ Tregs while reducing systemic pro-inflammatory cytokines and elevating IL-10 levels. These findings highlight that Sph-R-Ins reshapes the splenic immunological microenvironment, reinforcing its dual roles in tissue regeneration and immune regulation, and may help protect pancreatic islets from further autoimmune destruction via systemic homing and paracrine signaling.

Transcriptomic profiling revealed that Sph-R-Ins activated tissue repair pathways, notably ECM-receptor interaction, focal adhesion, and PI3K-Akt signaling, which are closely linked to β-cell survival, proliferation, and regeneration.43 In pancreatic islets, key ECM components (collagen IV/VI, laminins Lama2/3/4) were markedly upregulated, indicating basement membrane reconstruction—essential for preserving islet morphology and protecting β cells from immune-mediated damage.44 Previous studies showed that ECM scaffolds can restore basement membrane integrity, enhance islet adhesion, preserve insulin secretion, and reduce immunogenicity.45 The basement membrane acts as both a mechanical framework and a biochemical barrier limiting immune infiltration and anoikis.46 Collagen IV/VI and laminins sustain β-cell identity, enhance insulin gene transcription, and promote proliferation.47 These effects are partly mediated through focal adhesion kinase (FAK) and downstream PI3K–Akt signaling, which together promote stem cell survival and tissue regeneration. Additionally, islet development and angiogenesis genes (e.g., Hspg2, Nkx6.1) were upregulated, indicating enhanced islet maturation and vascularization. Although T1D is primarily characterized by insulin deficiency, patients are also susceptible to insulin resistance,48 particularly in the liver. This complication is often exacerbated by prolonged use of exogenous insulin. In our study, Sph-R-Ins therapy effectively alleviated hepatic insulin resistance, further supporting its potential as a comprehensive treatment strategy.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we have developed a novel gene-free reprogramming strategy that leverages a variable physical-dynamics environment to convert diverse mature somatic cells into embryonic-like pluripotent spheroids (Sph-Es) within 15-20 days. This mechanical cue-driven, serum-free 3D culture system offers a safe and scalable alternative to conventional genetic or chemical reprogramming approaches. Building on this platform, we further engineered irradiated Sph-Es with transient insulin gene delivery (Sph-R-Ins) to address type 1 diabetes. In T1D mice, Sph-R-Ins alleviated hyperglycemia by promoting islet repair and intra-islet vascular regeneration, reshaped the splenic immune environment by inducing regulatory T cells and M2 macrophages while suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines, and improved hepatic insulin sensitivity through metabolic gene modulation. Collectively, these findings establish Sph-R-Ins as a promising regenerative and gene-based therapeutic strategy for T1D and highlight the potential of mechanical regulation in stem cell reprogramming and tissue repair.
