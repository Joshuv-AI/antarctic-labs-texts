Circadian rhythm entrainment factors and premenstrual syndrome: a cross-sectional study of the roles of light exposure, exercise, and meal timing
Authors: Sawako Sasai, Megumi Nishikawa, Yoshiko Matsushima, Hiroto Matsuyama, Madoka Osawa, Sayaka Uiji, Isuzu Nakamoto, Saeka Ajiki, Shigeyuki Ogawa, Eiko Masutani, Tomoko Wakamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPremenstrual syndrome (PMS) is a common condition affecting women, involving physical and emotional symptoms. Since circadian rhythms may be disrupted during the premenstrual phase, regulating them could alleviate PMS. The purpose of this study was to determine how lifestyle factors (light exposure, exercise, and food intake) related to circadian rhythm entrainment contribute to PMS severity based on chronotype.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in Japanese women aged 20–49 years. PMS severity was assessed using the Menstrual Distress Questionnaire. Lifestyle habits related to circadian entrainment were also surveyed. The final sample comprised 564 women (249 morning types and 315 evening types). Multiple regression analysis was performed with PMS score as the objective variables and lifestyle related to circadian rhythm as the independent variables. Two models addressed multicollinearity: Model 1 included natural light exposure, screen-time after sunset, and first meal timing; Model 2 included screen-time, exercise, and meal timing. Ethical approval was obtained.ResultsAmong the 564 women, the numbers of reports of mild or worse PMS symptoms, were 526 (93.3%) for pain, 510 (90.4%) for water retention, 265 (47.0%) for autonomic reactions, and 482 (85.5%) for negative affect, many experienced PMS symptoms. For morning types, 4 or more hours of screen-time after sunset was associated significantly with higher negative affect scores (Model༑;β = 1.46; 95% CI, 0.09 to 2.83; p = 0.037), (Model2༛β = 1.43; 95% CI, 0.06 to 2.80; p = 0.040). For evening types, less than 2 h of daytime light exposure was associated with increased negative affect (β = 0.91; 95% CI, 0.15 to 1.66; p = 0.019) and water retention (β = 0.42; 95% CI, 0.05 to 0.79; p = 0.026). Similarly, exercise was other associated with increased water retention (β = 0.50; 95% CI, 0.14 to 0.86; p = 0.007). Meal timing was not significantly associated with any entrainment factors.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that circadian rhythm entrainment through appropriate light exposure and exercise timing can alleviate PMS. The consideration of individuals’ chronotypes may enhance the effectiveness of lifestyle-based interventions for PMS relief.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-026-04439-6.Keywords: Premenstrual syndrome, Circadian rhythm, Chronotype, Light exposure, Exercise

Background

Menstruation is a female physiological process, with 400–450 cycles occurring from menarche to menopause over an average of 36 years [1, 2]. Many women of reproductive age experience physical and/or emotional symptoms during the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle, known as premenstrual syndrome (PMS) [3]. PMS entails pain, swelling, anxiety, irritability, and mood swings, with symptoms and severity varying among individuals [4]. An estimated 80–90% of women experience at least one premenstrual symptom [5], 20–40% of women report impacts of such symptoms on their work and family lives [6], and 3–8% of women are diagnosed with premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD), which is characterized by severe symptoms [7]. Further, PMS may be associated with the future development of hypertension [8], is associated with postnatal depression [9], and women with PMDD are at greater risk of suicide [10]. Thus, PMS has long-lasting impacts on the quality of life of many women. However, the mechanisms underlying this condition remain unclear.

Although selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors [11] and oral contraceptives (OCs) [12] are sometimes indicated for severe PMS, the following challenges remain. For example, these medications can have side effects such as nausea and moodiness, have contraindications [13], and may not be suitable for women planning pregnancy. In addition, taking OCs at younger ages has also been reported to increase future major depressive disorder [14] and risk of suicidal behavior [15], and long-term mental health problems also remain. Thus, there are some risks for young women taking OCs for long term.

Whereas as non-pharmacological treatment, studies of women with PMDD have reported that light therapy at 10,000 lx for 30 min [16] and light therapy at 2,500 lx for 120 min [17] improved depressive symptoms. These suggest that PMS might be associated with circadian rhythms. Humans have endogenous circadian clocks that regulates hormonal secretion and sleep-wake rhythms autonomously for approximately 24 h [18, 19]. The circadian rhythms of the system are controlled by central clocks in the suprachiasmatic nucleus, which transmit signals to the peripheral clocks of the organs [20]. However, circadian rhythms are easily disrupted before menstruation. In experiments on mice and rats, the expression rhythm of clock gene expression was not influenced by the estrous cycle in the suprachiasmatic nucleus, but the peripheral tissues (liver, ovary, and uterus) were influenced by the estrous cycle [21]. It may lead to a misalignment between the central and peripheral clocks. Therefore, the premenstrual phase, when female hormones are rapidly fluctuating, is when circadian rhythms are easily disrupted. Sleep is one of the circadian rhythms and it is known that some women suffer from changes in sleep quality and insomnia before menstruation [22]. Decreased sleep quality in premenstrual phase may be driven by a likelihood of disrupting circadian rhythms.

Also, most human circadian clocks are slightly longer than 24 h [23]. Furthermore, there are individual variation, such as morning types who are good at getting up early and night types who are good at staying up late, which are referred to as chronotypes [24]. This characteristic may lead to differences in bed time and wake-up time between weekdays and free days, which may disrupt the internal clock. In contrast, humans have mechanisms to reset, i.e., entrainment, their biological clocks [18]. Factors that play this role are called entrainment factors and are part of lifestyle habits such as light exposure, exercise, food intake and social contact [25, 26]. Entrainment factors have varying effects, depending on their timing [27]. For example, exposure to bright light in the morning can reset the circadian clock to align with a 24-hour cycle, whereas light exposure at night can result in delay and disruption of the rhythm [28]. Similarly, the timing of food intake [29] and exercise [30] can regulate or disrupt circadian rhythms. There have been several reports on the association of PMS with exercise and food, which play a role in entrainment factors. Aerobic exercise [31, 32] and the consumption of nutrients such as calcium [33, 34] and carbohydrates [35, 36] can effectively alleviate PMS symptoms. However, the association about the timing of when these lifestyle behaviors are performed, i.e., from the viewpoint of entrainment factors, is not clear.

In recent years, the following studies have shown these lifestyle factors to be associated with PMS. In Korea, PMS was associated with anemia, underweight and smoking for working population [37]. In China, college students’ moderate physical activity and moderate to vigorous physical activity can be effective in reducing PMS symptoms [38]. OCs use in East Asian countries is 2.5%, which is lower than 15.1% in North America and 31.5% in Western Europe [39]. Non-pharmacological treatments for PMS, including aromatherapy [40] and cognitive behavioral therapy [41], are currently being researched. This situation may demand non-pharmacological treatment for PMS alleviation. Furthermore, to determine the association PMS and lifestyle, it is necessary that women are not regular OCs users. The reason is that OCs regulate hormonal rhythms. Therefore, surveys on the association between menstrual rhythms and lifestyle habits could provide new findings if conducted in Japan, where the use of oral contraceptives is 2.9% [39].

This study was conducted to clarify the relationships between PMS and lifestyle habits that act as circadian rhythm entrainment factors (light exposure, exercise, and food intake). These insights may support non-pharmacological approaches to relieving PMS symptoms through adjustments to daily habits.

Methods

Study design, participants, and data collection

A survey on PMS and lifestyle habits was conducted with women in Japan aged 20–49 years who had menstruated within the previous 3 months. The data collection took place in Japan between January and February 2022. Compared to Northern Europe, Japan experiences less variation in daylight duration across seasons. Participants were recruited online through the Septem Institute website. At the beginning of the questionnaire, an informed consent statement explained that participation was voluntary and that personal data would be protected. Only those who agreed to the terms proceeded to complete the questionnaire online. The survey was conducted anonymously, and the researchers were unable to identify individual respondents. This cross-sectional study was approved by the ethics committees of Septem Soken Co., Ltd. (SOKEN-IRB-26 and SOKEN-IRB-26-1) and the Kyoto University Graduate School and Faculty of Medicine (R3351).

We collected data on participants’ age; menstruation within the previous 3 months; menstrual cycle; usual waking time, bedtime, and first meal time; medication (female hormone and tranquilizer) use; and work shifts. To verify the accuracy of responses, dummy questions with identifiable answers were included. Participants were adults aged 20–49 years. Adolescents were excluded because circadian and sleep–wake rhythms are still maturing during this developmental stage, and lifestyle patterns may be strongly influenced by parental supervision. To reduce such confounding, only adults with established circadian stability were included.

A total of 1,681 filled-out questionnaires were collected. Questionnaires from respondents of inappropriate sex or age and those with incorrect answers to dummy questions, missing responses, or system errors were excluded. Additionally, questionnaires from women who had not menstruated for more than 3 months, had menstrual cycles outside the 25–38-day range, worked night shifts or shift work, or were taking female hormones, oral contraceptives, or tranquilizers were excluded (Fig. 1).

Measures

PMS

The primary outcome of PMS was assessed using four subscales of the Menstrual Distress Questionnaire (MDQ) covering well-established syndrome components [42, 43]: pain (6 items), water retention (4 items), autonomic reactions (4 items), and negative affect (8 items; S1 Table). We asked participants about their PMS symptoms over the previous 3 months. Response options were 0 (none), 1 (mild), 2 (moderate), 3 (strong), and 4 (severe). Subscale scores were calculated. This widely used scale was defined by factor analysis conducted by Moos (1968), and there is literature supporting its validity [44]. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were calculated for each subscale: pain (α = 0.85), water retention (α = 0.70), autonomic reactions (α = 0.70), and negative affect (α = 0.91), indicating acceptable to high internal consistency in our sample.

Lifestyle habits related to entrainment factors

The independent variables (entrainment factors) were light exposure, exercise, and meal timing. However, social contact was not assessed due to the enactment of self-restraint measures during the COVID-19 pandemic. Participants were asked about their lifestyle habits in the previous month.

Daytime light exposure was assessed using the question “How much time per day do you spend in a natural light environment?” Response options were 0–1 h, 2–3 h, 4–5 h, 6–7 h, 8–9 h, and 10 or more hours. Responses were dichotomized as less than 2 h and 2 or more hours. This is important since up to two hours of time spent outdoors is related to chronotype [45].

Nighttime light exposure was assessed using the question “How much screen-time (television viewing and computer or smartphone use) after 17:00 (the average sunset time in January in Japan) do you engage in per day?” The response options were the same as for daytime light exposure, and responses were categorized as less than 2 h, 2 to less than 4 h, and 4 or more hours based on a previous study [46].

Participants were asked about their usual activity levels and classified into three categories: sedentary (sitting during most of the day), moderate (Standing activities such as commuting, housework, shopping, and walking for 4 to 5 h), and vigorous (heavy labor or frequent exercise). This classification was based on ‘The Japanese Food Guide Spinning Top’ [47]. developed by the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare and the Ministry of Agriculture. Forestry and Fisheries in Japan, and is commonly used in Japan to assess physical activity levels. For the purposes of this study, we combined the moderate and vigorous categories into a single group labeled “moderate-to-vigorous,” and compared it with the “sedentary” group. This decision was based on previous research indicating that cycling for two sets of two hours (a total of four hours) has an advancing effect on circadian phase [48]. Since the entrainment effect is similar for physical activities lasting 4–5 h or longer, separating the vigorous category from moderate was deemed unnecessary. Additionally, as exercise performed during the daytime is known to advances the circadian phase [30], we defined daytime exercise combined with natural light exposure as an entrainment factor. Ultimately, Participants with moderate to vigorous activity levels and 2 or more hours of natural light exposure were allocated to an exercise group. All other participants were allocated to the “other” group.

Participants were asked about the timing of their first meals after waking. As more time passes after waking, other factors such as light exposure and physical activity begin to influence the individual. Therefore, responses were categorized as within 0.5 h, more than 0.5–within 1 h, and more than 1 h.

Chronotypes

Chronotypes were asked using one question based on the Morningness-Eveningness Questionnaire [49], similarly, some articles ask chronotypes in one question [50]. The question was follows: “Some people are good at getting up early, and some people are good at staying up late. If you were asked whether you’re a morning type or an evening type, what would you say?” Response options were definitely morning, moderately morning, neither, moderately evening, and definitely evening. Responses were grouped into three categories: “morning type” for definitely morning and moderately morning, “neither type” for neither, and “evening type” for moderately evening and definitely evening. Only data from participants with morning and evening types were included in the analysis, excluding neither type. This decision was made to focus on individuals exhibiting the most pronounced morning and evening chronotypes, as including neither types may introduce a gradient effect that could obscure the associations in this study.

Statistical analysis

As the effect size for light therapy for PMDD in a systematic review was − 0.20 [95% confidence interval (CI), − 0.48 to 0.07] and the effectiveness of this therapy is uncertain [51], we calculated the sample size based on a multiple regression model with a medium effect size and 10 explanatory variables using G*Power 3.1 [52]. For a significance level of 5% and power of 80%, the total sample requirement was determined to be 118 individuals. Furthermore, according to the sample size formula for multiple regression analysis, N > 50 + 8p [53], where p is the number of predictors (10), a sample of 130 individuals was needed. Based on these considerations, we determined that a sample of 200 morning types and 200 evening types after the application of the exclusion criteria and accounting for response errors was needed.

The data were analyzed as follows. First, means and standard deviation (SD) of the participant age, waking time, bedtime, and PMS scores were calculated for both chronotypes. The unpaired t test was used to compare variables between morning and evening types. Second, bivariate relationships between PMS scores and entrainment factors were examined using analysis of variance. Natural light exposure, screen-time after sunset, exercise, and first meal timing served as independent variables. Third, multiple regression analyses were conducted for the morning and evening types. Due to the strong collinearity between natural light exposure and exercise, two models were used: model 1 included natural light exposure, screen-time after sunset, and first meal timing, and model 2 included screen-time after sunset, exercise, and first meal timing. Both models analyses, age was included as a covariate. JMP version 18.0.2 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) was used for the statistical analysis, with the significance level set to 5%.

Results

Participant characteristics

The sample comprised 249 morning types and 315 evening types. Relative to morning types, evening types were significantly younger [39.7 (SD, 6.5) vs. 38.4 (SD, 7.6) years, p = 0.034], woke up significantly later (5:55 vs. 6:50 a.m., p < 0.001), and went to bed significantly later (10:44 p.m. vs. 12:08 a.m., p < 0.001; Table 1).

PMS scores

Of the total of 564 women, the numbers of women reporting mild or worse PMS symptoms, were 526 (93.3%) for pain, 510 (90.4%) for water retention, 265 (47.0%) for autonomic reactions, and 482 (85.5%) for negative affect, many women experienced PMS symptoms. The negative affect score was significantly higher for evening types than for morning types [6.8 (SD, 6.7) vs. 5.7 (SD, 5.9), p = 0.047; Table 1]. Other subscale scores did not differ significantly between groups.

Associations between PMS scores and entrainment factors among morning types

Analysis of variance revealed no significant relationship between PMS scores and entrainment factors for morning types (Table 2). However, multiple regression model 1 showed that negative affect was significantly more severe for women in this group with 4 or more hours of screen-time after sunset (β = 1.46; 95% CI, 0.09 to 2.83; p = 0.037). Model 2 showed that longer screen time at night was associated with significantly higher negative affect (β = 1.43; 95% CI, 0.06 to 2.80; p = 0.040). No significant association was found between any other PMS score and natural light exposure, exercise, or first meal timing in this group (Table 3).

Associations between PMS scores and entrainment factors among evening types

Analysis of variance revealed significant associations of natural light exposure with water retention (p = 0.032) and negative affect (p = 0.039) for evening types. Additionally, exercise was associated significantly with negative affect (p = 0.005; Table 4). Multiple regression model 1 showed that women in this group with less than 2 h of daylight exposure had significantly more severe water retention (β = 0.42; 95% CI, 0.05 to 0.79; p = 0.026) and negative affect (β = 0.91; 95% CI, 0.15 to 1.66; p = 0.019). Model 2 showed that water retention was significantly more severe among evening types in the “other” exercise group (β = 0.50; 95% CI, 0.14 to 0.86; p = 0.007). No significant association was found between any other PMS score and screen-time after sunset or first meal timing in this group (Table 5).

Discussion

The results of this study indicate that the circadian clock entrainment factors of light exposure, exercise and meal timing are associated with PMS. These associations differed between morning and evening chronotypes. In this section, we will discuss how each of these entrainment factors may be related to PMS.

Light exposure

Among morning types, engagement in 4 or more hours of screen-time after sunset was associated with higher negative affect scores in premenstrual phase. Prior population-based study has linked more than 4 h of daily screen time with increased cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality compared with less than 2 h [46], and adolescent studies have associated time spent on video games or social networking with depression [54]. and psychosomatic complaints [55]. These surveys focused on screen-time in free time outside of school or work. Although those studies included men and did not consider chronotype, the present findings extend this literature by suggesting that prolonged evening screen time in morning types may adversely affect mental and physical health. Moreover, night-time light exposure disrupts sleep and circadian timing [56], and extended evening screen use delays bedtime and shortens sleep duration [57]. Sleep patterns also vary across the menstrual cycle, with some women experiencing premenstrual insomnia [58]. Taken together, prolonged evening screen - time may disturb circadian rhythms and/or sleep, exacerbating negative affect in PMS.

Next, the effect of daylight exposure: among evening types, negative affect scores were significantly higher when daytime exposure to natural light was less than hours, and water retention scores were likewise higher when light exposure was less than two hours. In short, insufficient daylight was associated with worse PMS symptoms in evening types. Sunlight is crucial for vitamin D synthesis [59], and inadequate natural light has been linked to poorer mental health, including depression and insufficient sleep [60], as well as physiological changes such as higher cortisol and lower nocturnal melatonin [61]. Thus, daytime light exposure is vital for both mental and physical health. Comparing daily life under artificial lighting with camping without artificial light, prior work shows earlier sleep onset and melatonin secretion during camping, with a larger phase advance in evening types [62]. This suggests that natural light exerts a stronger phase advancing effect in evening types and is a key entrainment factor for circadian regulation. Solar illuminance can reach up to 100,000 lx on sunny days and several thousand lux on cloudy days [63, 64], far exceeding typical indoor lighting and sufficient to reset the circadian clock. Light exposure also promotes serotonin secretion, important for mood stability [63, 65], and women with severe PMS have lower serotonin levels than those with milder symptoms [66]. Because serotonin secretion and mood influence each other bidirectionally [67], adequate daytime light exposure may stabilize mood via serotonergic pathways, which could, in turn, further support serotonin secretion.

In brief, evening screen time and daylight exposure have different effects on PMS symptoms and mood depending on chronotype. Prolonged evening screen use worsens negative affect in morning types, while insufficient daylight is linked to more severe symptoms in evening types.

Exercise

Exercise was associated with reduced water retention among evening types. Prior studies link exercise to cardiovascular benefits [68], improved blood pressure [69], and reduced depression [70]. While those studies often included men and did not stratify by chronotype, their overall implications align with our observation that exercise supports health, including the cardiovascular symptoms. During the luteal phase, progesterone increases plasma volume and extracellular fluid [71]. and can promote oedema via vascular endothelial effects [72]. Women with PMS show altered plasma fluid-regulatory hormones and disturbed fluid distribution during the luteal phase [73]. The renin–angiotensin system, integral to blood pressure and volume regulation, also exhibits circadian rhythmicity [74, 75]. Therefore, entrainment of circadian rhythms by light and exercise aids in fluid homeostasis. In this study, the timing of exercise was important, we used the combined variable of light. Higher coefficients were observed in Model 2, which included light and exercise, compared with Model 1, which used only light exposure, suggesting that the beneficial effects were attributable to exercise. Taken together, these findings suggest that combining daytime exercise with light exposure may contribute to improved circadian entrainment and water retention in women with evening chronotypes.

Meal timing

Another entrainment factor examined in this study was first meal timing. This study did not identify an association between meal timing and PMS symptoms. It is generally well known that breakfast has health benefits [76]. Similarly, skipping breakfast is associated with cardiovascular disease and all causes of mortality [77]. Thus, the first meal after waking up is important for mental and physical health. However, in this study, the association between PMS symptoms and the timing of the first meal was not clear. Many participants may have engaged in household chores or childcare soon after waking, which may have influenced circadian entrainment before their first meals. Additionally, the duration of fasting affects the resetting of the circadian clock [78]. Therefore, the observed associations might have been influenced by prior-day eating patterns or other behavioral factors. Since this study did not identify an association with PMS symptoms, further research is needed to determine the association with meal timing.

This study has several limitations. First, due to its cross-sectional design, causal relationships between lifestyle habits and PMS could not be examined. Longitudinal or interventional studies are needed to clarify the causal effects of lifestyle modifications on PMS. Second, self-reported data were used to assess both PMS severity and lifestyle habits, which may have introduced recall bias and subjectivity. The subjective nature of the outcome measure, based on self-reported questionnaire scores, may have increased variability due to individual interpretation and reporting bias. Indeed, the PMS scores showed relatively high standard deviations, indicating substantial within-group variability. This variability likely reduced statistical power and attenuated the overall model fit, as reflected in the low R² values (0.01–0.06), despite some predictors having relatively large beta coefficients. These small effect sizes should not be dismissed, however, given that PMS recurs each menstrual cycle and imposes a cumulative burden. Future studies could improve measurement reliability using objective methods, such as actigraphy, to assess sleep-wake patterns and light exposure. Third, the point(s) in the menstrual cycle at which specific relief methods are most effective remains unclear. Fourth, the study population was limited to Japanese women recruited online, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings to other ethnic groups and populations with different lifestyles. It should also be noted that this study was conducted in Japan, where seasonal differences in day length are less pronounced compared to Western countries. Therefore, the impact of chronotype and seasonal variation on PMS symptoms might be less marked in this population, which could affect the generalizability of findings related to circadian and seasonal influences. Fifth, this study focused on adult women aged 20 years and older. Nevertheless, it has been reported that premenstrual syndrome (PMS) during adolescence can significantly impact academic performance and daily activities, underscoring the need for non-pharmacological interventions across a wider age range. Future research including younger populations is warranted to further elucidate the relationships throughout the entire reproductive lifespan.

Taken together, these limitations suggest that while the current findings are informative, future research should aim to incorporate objective measurements and diverse populations to enhance the validity and applicability of results.

Conclusion

The results of this study show that appropriate light exposure and exercise timing are associated with PMS symptom relief. Thus, circadian rhythm entrainment may alleviate these symptoms. Furthermore, the consideration of individuals’ chronotypes may enhance the effectiveness of lifestyle-based interventions for PMS relief.
