Comparing [18F]FDG–positron emission tomography and breast magnetic resonance imaging to predict pathological complete response and 3-year invasive disease-free survival in HER2-positive early breast cancer patients: an unplanned exploratory analysis of the PHERGain trial☆
Authors: JJ García-Mosquera, JM Pérez-García, M Ruiz-Borrego, A Stradella, B Bermejo, S Escrivá-de-Romaní, L Calvo Martínez, G Gebhart, K Kerrou, M Gion, G Antonarelli, S Santasusagna Canal, J Rodríguez-Morato, L Mina, M Sampayo-Cordero, A Llombart-Cussac, J Cortés
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe PHERGain trial demonstrated that an [18F]2-fluoro-2-deoxy-d-glucose ([18F]FDG)–positron emission tomography (PET)-based, pathological complete response (pCR)-adapted strategy could be safely utilized to avoid chemotherapy (CT) in patients with human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-positive early breast cancer (EBC) receiving neoadjuvant dual anti-HER2 blockade [trastuzumab and pertuzumab (HP)]. Due to the limited availability of [18F]FDG–PET, this study evaluated breast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) as an alternative for early treatment response assessment.Patients and methodsGroup B patients (n = 285) initially received two cycles of HP, with subsequent CT introduction if [18F]FDG–PET showed no response. [18F]FDG–PET and MRI were conducted before randomization and after two cycles (early). An additional MRI was carried out before surgery (late). Concordance between [18F]FDG–PET, MRI reduction, and MRI response by RECIST v.1.1 was evaluated, along with accuracy to predict pCR and 3-year invasive disease-free survival (iDFS) rates.ResultsEarly imaging assessment showed good accuracy (78.2%) between [18F]FDG–PET and breast MRI tumor reduction (any shrinkage), but not when applying RECIST v.1.1 response criteria (≥30% decrease in the sum of diameters of target lesions). There were higher pCR rates in [18F]FDG–PET responders with early MRI reduction (39.0% versus 29.6% if no reduction) or MRI response (44.0% versus 30.4% if no response). [18F]FDG–PET non-responders without MRI reduction had the lowest pCR (21.7%) and 3-year iDFS (75.3%) rates despite receiving CT. Among [18F]FDG–PET responders, early MRI complete responses (CRs) were uncommon, but extending CT-free treatment increased early MRI CR (9.3%-31.7%) and objective response rates (55.1%-70.0%). Late MRI CR predicted pCR better in hormone receptor (HR)-negative than in HR-positive tumors (positive predictive value: 85.5% versus 61.5%).ConclusionsAlthough [18F]FDG–PET is the recommended imaging technique for guiding treatment in HER2-positive EBC patients following the PHERGain strategy, this unplanned analysis suggests that tumor shrinkage assessed by breast MRI could be a viable alternative for adaptive strategies in settings where [18F]FDG–PET is not available.Key words: HER2-positive early breast cancer, trastuzumab, pertuzumab, pathological complete response, [18F]FDG–PET, magnetic resonance imaging

Introduction

Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-positive breast cancer (BC) is a clinically and biologically heterogeneous disease characterized by the amplification of the ERBB2/HER2 gene and/or overexpression of its related kinase receptor protein.1 This tumor subtype accounts for ∼15%-20% of all invasive BC and it has both prognostic and therapeutic implications.2 The introduction of anti-HER2 therapies for the treatment of HER2-positive BC has significantly improved outcomes for both early (EBC) and advanced BC patients3 and has enabled the possibility of reducing or even skipping chemotherapy (CT) in selected subgroups of patients in the early-stage scenario.4 Additionally, pathological complete response (pCR) has been shown to have a strong association with better long-term outcomes in EBC, particularly in triple-negative and HER2-positive tumors,5 and represents a critical endpoint in trials evaluating de-escalation approaches.

The PHERGain (NCT03161353) study previously demonstrated the feasibility of using an [18F]2-fluoro-2-deoxy-d-glucose–positron emission tomography ([18F]FDG–PET)-based, pCR-adapted strategy to safely avoid CT for HER2-positive EBC patients treated with neoadjuvant dual anti-HER2 blockade with trastuzumab and pertuzumab (HP).6 The trial met its first primary endpoint with 37.9% of patients included in group B (adaptive strategy) achieving a pCR at the time of surgery after having an [18F]FDG–PET response following two initial HP cycles ([18F]FDG–PET responders) and completed eight cycles of the same CT-free treatment.6 The second primary endpoint was also successfully achieved with a 3-year invasive disease-free survival (iDFS) rate of 94.8% among all patients who followed this adaptive strategy.7 Although this would require further clinical investigation and prolonged follow-up, these data support this approach as a new therapeutic option to discuss in our daily clinical practice with a subgroup of HER2-positive EBC patients, allowing for a significant number of patients to safely avoid CT.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is widely accessible and commonly used to assess the extent of locoregional disease, monitor response to neoadjuvant therapy, and assist in surgical planning. Also, [18F]FDG–PET is frequently used for the assessment of metastatic and locoregional status (or extension) and for monitoring response to neoadjuvant treatment; however, it is more expensive and less accessible than MRI. Therefore, given the limited availability of [18F]FDG–PET imaging and its high cost, the evaluation of breast MRI as an alternative tool for assessing early treatment response in PHERGain is warranted.8

In this article, we present an unplanned exploratory analysis of patients included in group B of the PHERGain trial that aims to explore the potential role of breast MRI in monitoring response to neoadjuvant CT-free dual anti-HER2 blockade with HP as an alternative to [18F]FDG–PET in guiding the decision to add CT. The specific objectives were as follows:

•To explore the concordance between [18F]FDG–PET and breast MRI in terms of early response assessment in HER2-positive EBC patients after two cycles of neoadjuvant HP (group B).

To explore the concordance between [18F]FDG–PET and breast MRI in terms of early response assessment in HER2-positive EBC patients after two cycles of neoadjuvant HP (group B).

•To compare the accuracy of early [18F]FDG–PET and breast MRI in predicting pCR and 3-year iDFS in HER2-positive EBC patients following the PHERGain strategy (group B).

To compare the accuracy of early [18F]FDG–PET and breast MRI in predicting pCR and 3-year iDFS in HER2-positive EBC patients following the PHERGain strategy (group B).

•To assess breast MRI responses according to RECIST v.1.1 at early (after two cycles) and late (after eight cycles) assessments, and their ability to predict pCR, in HER2-positive EBC patients treated exclusively with neoadjuvant HP (group B early metabolic responder patients according to [18F]FDG–PET).

To assess breast MRI responses according to RECIST v.1.1 at early (after two cycles) and late (after eight cycles) assessments, and their ability to predict pCR, in HER2-positive EBC patients treated exclusively with neoadjuvant HP (group B early metabolic responder patients according to [18F]FDG–PET).

Patients and methods

PHERGain study design and participants

This unplanned exploratory analysis was retrospectively conducted on patients from group B of the PHERGain trial, an international randomized phase II study involving 356 women with untreated, HER2-positive, stage I-IIIA, operable EBC (≥1.5 cm tumor size) with at least one breast lesion evaluable by [18F]FDG–PET. The two primary endpoints of the PHERGain trial were (i) the pCR rate among group B [18F]FDG–PET responder patients who completed the CT-free treatment, and (ii) the 3-year iDFS among all group B patients. The trial design has been previously described.7

Briefly, group B patients (n = 285) were part of the adapted-strategy arm and were initially treated with two cycles of dual anti-HER2 blockade with HP [plus endocrine therapy in the case of hormone receptor (HR)-positive disease]. Following this, patients underwent an [18F]FDG–PET scan (first response-adapted step) and a breast MRI to assess response. [18F]FDG–PET responders continued the same dual anti-HER2 blockade for six additional cycles until surgery, while [18F]FDG–PET non-responders were switched to six cycles of neoadjuvant CT (carboplatin plus docetaxel) in combination with dual anti-HER2 blockade. Importantly, the decision to add CT was based solely on the [18F]FDG–PET results. After surgery, all patients were evaluated for pathological response (second response-adapted step). [18F]FDG–PET responders who achieved a pCR continued the same dual anti-HER2 blockade until completing 18 cycles, while those not achieving a pCR received 6 cycles of adjuvant CT (carboplatin plus docetaxel) with HP, followed by 4 additional cycles of HP. In [18F]FDG–PET non-responders, exclusive dual anti-HER2 blockade with HP was continued for up to 18 cycles (Figure 1).6,7

[18F]FDG–PET assessment: definition of [18F]FDG–PET responders

Whole-body [18F]FDG–PET scans were obtained at baseline and after two cycles of neoadjuvant treatment according to the procedures of the European Association of Nuclear Medicine (EANM) through their EANM Research Limited (EARL) subsidiary. Image analysis and central quality assurance were carried out by the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) criteria by two independent reviewers at two imaging laboratories who were masked to the patient characteristics, treatment allocation, and clinical outcome. All patients who presented target lesions with at least a reduction of 40% of maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) from baseline were categorized as [18F]FDG–PET responders.

Breast MRI assessments: definition of MRI reduction and MRI response by RECIST v.1.1 including partial response and complete response

Breast MRI was conducted at three key points: at baseline, after two cycles of neoadjuvant therapy (early breast MRI), and after eight neoadjuvant cycles just before surgery (late breast MRI) (Figure 1). Response evaluation was carried out locally. Breast MRI response was defined as partial response (PR) or complete response (CR) as per RECIST v.1.1 criteria. Breast MRI CR was defined as the disappearance of all target lesions. Breast MRI PR was defined as a ≥30% decrease in the sum of diameters of all target lesions compared with baseline. Breast MRI reduction was defined ad hoc as any shrinkage in the diameter of target lesions compared with baseline. All these radiological definitions excluded the presence of new lesions or progressive disease in non-target lesions. However, progression of disease was still reported by MRI when present.

Definitions and assessments of outcomes: pCR and 3-year iDFS

pCR was defined as the disappearance of invasive cancer in the breast and axilla (ypT0/is ypN0) as determined by a local pathologist after surgery using the recommendations promoted by the Breast International Group-North American Breast Cancer Group.9

Three-year iDFS was defined as the percentage of patients who were alive without ipsilateral invasive breast tumor recurrence, ipsilateral locoregional invasive BC recurrence, distant recurrence, contralateral invasive BC, second non-breast primary cancer, and death from any cause at 3 years after surgery.

Analysis strategy

To explore the concordance with regard to response assessment between [18F]FDG–PET and breast MRI, we examined the agreement between [18F]FDG–PET assessment (in terms of [18F]FDG–PET response or non-response) and breast MRI assessment (in terms of breast MRI reduction or non-reduction and breast MRI response or non-response by RECIST v.1.1) after two cycles of HP in group B patients.

To compare the accuracy of early [18F]FDG–PET and breast MRI in predicting pCR and 3-year iDFS, we analyzed the rates of pCR and 3-year iDFS in [18F]FDG–PET responders and non-responders according to breast MRI radiological response (in terms of breast MRI reduction or response by RECIST v.1.1) in group B patients.

To assess early and late breast MRI responses, we initially analyzed the rates of breast MRI radiological response according to RECIST v.1.1 after two and eight cycles of HP in group B [18F]FDG–PET responder patients. Subsequently, we determined the ability of early and late breast MRI PR and CR to predict pCR according to HR status, by calculating the positive predictive value (PPV) for each subgroup.

Statistical analysis

The accuracy between [18F]FDG–PET response and breast MRI reduction was defined as the number of patients with breast MRI reduction and [18F]FDG–PET response [true positive (TP)] plus the number of patients without breast MRI reduction and [18F]FDG–PET non-response [true negative (TN)] among all patients evaluated with both techniques [TP + TN + false positives (FP) + false negatives (FN)]. The accuracy between [18F]FDG–PET response and breast MRI response was also calculated. Both were estimated with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using the Clopper–Pearson method.

The F1-index was also calculated to account for the unbalance between the number of [18F]FDG–PET responders and non-responders {F1-index = TP/[TP + 0.5 × (FP + FN)]}. PPVs [PPV = TP/(TP + FP)] and negative predictive values [NPV = TN/(TN + FN)] were also reported.

The rate of patients with pCR and 3-year iDFS was reported for [18F]FDG–PET and MRI responders, non-responders, and patients with and without MRI reduction.

The number of patients with pCR was calculated among MRI complete responders and partial responders after cycle 2 or 8 of HP. Only patients without neoadjuvant CT were included. The rate of patients with pCR for patients with CR or PR after cycle 2 or 8 of HP between HR-negative and HR-positive subgroups was also described as a PPV.

The estimation of the pCR rates was conducted with Clopper–Pearson 95% CIs.

The 3-year iDFS was calculated using the Kaplan–Meier estimator. The 95% CI was estimated using the ‘log–log’ method. The statistical analysis plan for pCR has been described previously.7 For additional statistical considerations, please refer to the previous publications.6,7

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 71 patients were assigned to group A and 285 patients to group B. These patients were recruited from 45 centers across seven European countries between 26 June 2017 and 24 April 2019. Patient baseline characteristics were balanced between groups. HR status was positive in 236 (66.0%) patients, 172 (48.0%) patients had lymph node involvement, and 269 (76.0%) had clinical stage II. Complete baseline characteristics have been previously described.7

Accuracy between [18F]FDG–PET and breast MRI in terms of early response assessment after two cycles of neoadjuvant HP

After two cycles of anti-HER2 dual blockade with HP, 79.6% (227/285) of patients from group B were [18F]FDG–PET responders, 82.5% (235/285) achieved a breast MRI reduction, and 47.0% (134/285) had a breast MRI response (either partial or complete) by RECIST v.1.1.

The highest level of accuracy with [18F]FDG–PET was observed when breast MRI was evaluated in terms of reduction versus response by RECIST v.1.1. A total of 200 out of 227 (88.1%) [18F]FDG–PET responders had a breast MRI reduction (PPV), whereas 23 out of 58 (39.7%) [18F]FDG–PET non-responders had no breast MRI reduction (NPV). These results showed a high accuracy and F1-index between [18F]FDG–PET response and breast MRI reduction of 78.2% (95% CI 73.0% to 82.9%) and 86.6% (95% CI 83.4% to 89.6%), respectively.

In terms of breast MRI response by RECIST v.1.1, a total of 125 out of 227 (55.1%) [18F]FDG–PET responders had a breast MRI response (PPV), whereas 49 out of 58 (84.5%) [18F]FDG–PET non-responders did not have a breast MRI response (NPV). These results showed a lower accuracy and F1-index of 61.1% (95% CI 55.1% to 66.7%) and 69.3% (95% CI 63.5% to 75.0%), respectively.

Value of early [18F]FDG–PET and breast MRI assessment in predicting pCR and 3-year iDFS

The pCR rate was numerically higher for patients with breast MRI reduction or response by RECIST v.1.1 compared with patients without any tumor shrinkage, independent of [18F]FDG–PET response status (Figure 2). The highest pCR rate was found in [18F]FDG–PET responders with a breast MRI response by RECIST v.1.1, reaching a pCR rate of 44.0%. The lowest pCR rate was found in [18F]FDG–PET non-responders without a breast MRI reduction, achieving a pCR rate of 21.7%.

Additionally, the 3-year iDFS rate for [18F]FDG–PET responders was excellent (>94.8%), regardless of the presence of breast MRI reduction or response by RECIST v.1.1, which makes early metabolic response a valuable predictive factor. Interestingly, [18F]FDG–PET non-responders without a breast MRI reduction had the lowest 3-year iDFS rate, with a 3-year iDFS of 75.3% (Tables 1 and 2).

Ability of early and late breast MRI PR and CR to predict pCR in patients treated exclusively with neoadjuvant HP ([18F]FDG–PET responders)

We evaluated the rates of PR and CR (evaluated by breast MRI) after two (early) and eight (late) treatment cycles and their correlation with pCR. In patients treated exclusively with dual anti-HER2 blockade (n = 227), 9.3% (21/227) achieved an early CR and 45.8% (104/227) had an early PR. Conversely, 31.7% (72/227) had a late CR and 38.3% (87/227) obtained a late PR. The objective response rate (ORR) after two HP cycles was 55.1%, increasing to 70.0% after eight cycles of HP (Table 3).

The value of [18F]FDG–PET prediction based on HR status suggested that PPV for pCR differed in regard to HR status. In HR-negative tumors, [18F]FDG–PET responders showed a lower PPV for pCR (75.0%). In contrast, for HR-positive tumors, [18F]FDG–PET responders had a numerically higher PPV for pCR (82.0%).

The value of early and late breast MRI responses to predict pCR according to HR status was also explored (Figure 3). After two cycles of neoadjuvant HP, 11.4% (8/70) of patients with HR-negative tumors achieved an early CR, and this rate had a 2.5-fold increase to 28.6% (20/70) after eight cycles. Early breast MRI CR had a PPV of 87.5% (7/8) (95% CI 47.3% to 99.7%) for achieving pCR after eight cycles of HP, which was comparable to the PPV of 85.0% (17/20) (95% CI 62.1% to 96.8%) for late breast MRI CR. Early breast MRI PR had a PPV of 38.9% (14/36) (95% CI 23.1% to 56.5%) for achieving pCR after eight cycles, which was marginally higher than the PPV of 28.0% (7/25) (95% CI 12.1% to 49.4%) for late breast MRI PR.

Discussion

In this analysis, we explored the potential of breast MRI as an alternative imaging modality to [18F]FDG–PET for predicting early treatment response in HER2-positive EBC patients included in group B of the PHERGain clinical trial.

Firstly, we found a high association between [18F]FDG–PET response and breast MRI reduction after two cycles of neoadjuvant CT-free anti-HER2 dual blockade with HP, with an accuracy and F1-index of 78.2% (95% CI 73.0% to 82.9%) and 86.6% (95% CI 83.4% to 89.6%), respectively. It is important to emphasize that this high level of agreement was specifically observed when breast MRI was evaluated in terms of ‘reduction’, defined as any shrinkage in the diameter of target lesions compared with baseline rather than ‘response by RECIST v.1.1’. This high concordance between the two imaging modalities suggests that breast MRI, when assessed based on tumor size reduction, could potentially serve as a viable alternative for monitoring early treatment responses in centers where [18F]FDG–PET is not available.

Secondly, our comparison of early [18F]FDG–PET and breast MRI assessments in predicting pCR and 3-year iDFS yielded results that underscore the potential complementary role of these imaging modalities in identifying patients with poorer prognosis. Indeed, in line with recently published data,10 while achieving an [18F]FDG–PET response was prognostic of superior pCR and 3-year iDFS rates regardless of the breast MRI status, patients with inferior pCR and 3-year iDFS rates showed both [18F]FDG–PET non-response and no breast MRI reduction, being 21.7% and 75.3%, respectively, despite receiving standard treatment with CT and dual anti-HER2 blockade. These results suggest that patients who do not respond on both imaging modalities are at a higher risk of poor outcomes, highlighting the potential utility of combining these imaging tools in identifying patients who may benefit from more aggressive or alternative treatment strategies. Finally, our analysis of breast MRI responses in [18F]FDG–PET responders treated exclusively with dual HER2 blockade revealed important insights. Despite identifying few early MRI CR after two cycles (9.3%), longer duration of CT-free treatment with HP, up to eight cycles, showed a higher rate of MRI CR (increasing up to 31.7%) and ORR (rising from 55.1% after two cycles to 70.0% after eight cycles). Notably, the value of breast MRI in predicting pCR varied by HR status. In HR-negative tumors, late breast MRI CR showed high PPV for pCR (85.0%). In contrast, for HR-positive tumors, late breast MRI CR had a lower PPV for pCR (61.5%). These findings suggest that breast MRI could be a valuable tool for monitoring response to CT-free treatment, particularly in HR-negative, HER2-positive EBC, which is aligned to recently published data,10 and could potentially guide decisions on treatment duration and de-escalation strategies. Our results are in line with previous studies showing that MRI predictive performance is worse in HR-positive in comparison to HR-negative patients.11

The evaluation of treatment response using [18F]FDG–PET presents both advantages and challenges in clinical practice. Its proven ability to predict pCR during neoadjuvant treatment in BC patients and its capacity to detect subclinical metastases at baseline are significant strengths.12,13 However, the high cost, limited availability, and required expertise for [18F]FDG–PET interpretation pose practical constraints.14 Considering these limitations, our findings suggest that breast MRI could serve as a feasible alternative for assessing response to CT-free dual HER2 blockade treatment in HER2-positive EBC patients in centers where [18F]FDG–PET is not available. Breast MRI offers several advantages, including its non-invasive nature, wider accessibility, lower cost, and potential as a dynamic biomarker. Nonetheless, it is crucial to remark that until more comprehensive data comparing various imaging modalities are available, [18F]FDG–PET remains the recommended method for implementing the PHERGain strategy in clinical practice. Moving forward, it would be valuable to conduct further prospective research exploring the use of breast MRI as an alternative method for assessing early treatment response.15 This approach could potentially broaden the applicability of response-adapted strategies in HER2-positive EBC management, ultimately leading to more personalized and efficient treatment protocols.

This exploratory analysis has several limitations that must be acknowledged. Firstly, the unplanned nature of the analysis introduces potential biases, as it was not initially designed to address the specific questions explored here. Notably, treatment adaptation was based on [18F]FDG–PET response rather than breast MRI response, which prevents a direct formal comparison between these two modalities. As a result, the true independent predictive value of breast MRI cannot be accurately determined within this study design. Additionally, the local evaluation of breast MRI imaging data may have impacted the consistency and accuracy of the results, particularly due to variations in imaging techniques and interpretation across different centers. The use of an ad hoc, non-RECIST definition of MRI reduction may have further introduced subjectivity and variability in response assessment. The relatively small sample size further limits the generalizability of the findings, especially when we analyze subgroups according to HR status to assess the predictive value of early and late MRI for pCR. Finally, while MRI shows promise as an alternative to [18F]FDG–PET for early treatment response assessment, its predictive value for outcomes, such as 3-year iDFS, requires further validation in prospective studies with larger cohorts. Moreover, alternative MRI parameters, such as functional tumor volume, have also demonstrated utility in predicting pCR15,16 and may offer additional value beyond simple tumor diameter reduction.

In conclusion, although [18F]FDG–PET is the recommended imaging technique for early treatment response in HER2-positive EBC patients following the PHERGain strategy, this unplanned exploratory analysis suggests that tumor shrinkage assessed by breast MRI could be a viable alternative to guide neoadjuvant, CT-free treatment after the initial two cycles of HP in a dynamic, imaging-based, pCR-adapted strategy when [18F]FDG–PET is unavailable. However, further prospective validation is needed before MRI-based tumor reduction can be considered an integral biomarker in this context. The high concordance between [18F]FDG–PET and breast MRI reduction, along with the predictive value of breast MRI when combined with [18F]FDG–PET for predicting pCR and 3-year iDFS outcomes and the high accuracy of breast MRI CR in predicting pCR, particularly in HR-negative tumors, suggests that breast MRI could play a valuable role in guiding treatment decisions. However, the complex study design and limitations underscore the need for further research to validate these findings and explore their integration into clinical practice. Notably, the PHERGain strategy has shown excellent results through an adaptive design in which the treatment strategies are dynamically tailored according to the individual patient’s response. However, despite these promising findings, this approach has not yet been adopted as standard practice. As we continue to refine our approach to personalized medicine in BC, the combination of dynamic imaging biomarkers with other prognostic tools holds promise for optimizing treatment strategies and improving patient outcomes.
