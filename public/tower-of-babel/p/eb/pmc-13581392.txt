Human cell-derived dermal-specific interwoven extracellular matrix for diabetic wound healing
Authors: Archita Sharma, Dhavan Sharma, Staci J Horn, Zeyu Wen, Jeffrey Ock, Jonathan Bova, Fred Clubb, Jason T George, Yuxiao Zhou, Feng Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetic wounds present significant clinical challenges due to their multifactorial and complex pathological characteristics. Although commercially available allogenic and xenogeneic tissue-derived acellular dermal matrices have been employed in chronic wound management, their utility is constrained by limited donor availability, potential immunogenicity, mismatched biomechanical properties, and batch-to-batch variability. Consequently, these limitations often result in incomplete wound closure and inconsistent therapeutic outcomes across patient populations. To overcome these challenges, we designed a completely biological and human dermis-specific acellular interwoven extracellular matrix (iECM). To closely replicate the ECM complexity and collagen bundle architecture of native dermis, a soft lithography-based approach was employed to guide human dermal fibroblast organization, enabling subsequent ECM deposition in an interwoven pattern. Post decellularization, the iECM retained both the compositional and structural features of native dermis, with a bulk elastic modulus (4 MPa) comparable to human dermal tissue (3–18 MPa). Upon implantation in a full-thickness diabetic rat wound model, iECM significantly accelerated healing rates by 80% compared to non-structured ECM by promoting granulation tissue formation, enhancing angiogenesis, accelerating resolution of inflammation, supporting uniform collagen deposition, and enabling complete re-epithelialization. By mimicking the architectural, compositional, and mechanical properties of native dermis, the highly biomimetic iECM presents a promising strategy for diabetic wound management.Statement of significance:Chronic wounds, like diabetic wounds, are a clinical challenge due to complex pathophysiology, impaired healing mechanisms, and limited effect of numerous treatment approaches like skin substitutes and wound dressings. This research work presents a completely biological, human cell-derived acellular dermal matrix fabricated using soft lithography, to mimic the interwoven architecture and mechanical properties of native extracellular matrix. Unlike traditional dermal matrices, the bioengineered iECM exhibits biomimicry, robustness, and regenerative potential, promoting collagen organization, angiogenesis, and re-epithelialization in chronic diabetic wound healing. This work introduces a bioinspired matrix, offering a promising advancement with the potential to transform chronic wound management.Keywords: Acellular dermal matrices, Extracellular matrix, Interwoven dermal architecture, Diabetic wound healing, Human dermal fibroblasts

Introduction

Chronic wounds, such as diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs), are a serious menace, leading to unresolved infections, impaired mobility, and risk of amputation [1]. The complex pathophysiology of DFU disrupts the timely resolution of the immune response, granulation tissue formation, and re-epithelialization [2,3]. Standard-of-care strategies, including antibiotics, wound dressings, and surgical debridement, often fail to suppress chronic inflammation, resulting in delayed healing [4,5]. Advanced strategies, such as electrospinning [6] and three-dimensional (3D) bioprinting [7], have been explored for wound healing but face notable challenges [8,9], including electrospun scaffolds’ difficulties in accommodating varied wound geometries [10], 3D printing’s poor reproducibility due to variability in tissue-derived extracellular matrix (ECM) inks [11], ethical concerns regarding ECM sourcing [12], and synthetic polymer inks lacking biological complexity and biomechanical properties [13]. Various commercial products, such as Tegaderm™, Dermagraft®, and TransCyte®, fabricated from synthetic materials or cellular components, have been developed to address these issues but face serious limitations. Tegaderm™, a polyurethane film, has poor adherence to wound beds [14], while Dermagraft® and TransCyte®, both cellular products, suffer from poor cell adhesion and biodegradability [15–17]. Currently, the most commonly used materials for DFU treatment are Acellular Dermal Matrices (ADMs), derived from xenogeneic sources (e.g., OASIS Wound Matrix™) or human donors (e.g., DermACELL® and AlloDerm®) [4,5,18]. These natural tissue-derived ECM matrices provide structural support and signaling cues for cell responses [19–21], promoting repair [22] and minimizing scarring [20, 23]. However, they face significant challenges, including donor-tissue scarcity, rejection, hypersensitivity from allo/xenogeneic sources, batch-to-batch variation, and scarring [4]. For example, DermACELL® ’s large pore size (ranging from a few mm2 to cm2) hinders cell migration and organized ECM deposition, leading to scaring [24] while AlloDerm® exhibits poor dermal fibroblast ingrowth due to its dense collagen fibril structure [25,26]. These shortcomings underscore the critical need for innovative biomaterial designs that eliminate reliance on donor tissues and more closely mimic native ECM to improve outcomes in the management of challenging DFUs.

Human cell-derived ECM-based ADMs have been developed to address these issues, providing easy pathogen screening and avoiding inflammatory or immunological responses. Nevertheless, their lack of native architectural complexity and donor tissue scarcity restricts their overall effectiveness [27,28]. The human dermis features an interwoven structure, as revealed by advanced multiphoton imaging (MPM) and biaxial tissue extension, showing a 90° angular difference between collagen and elastin fibers in the superficial and deep reticular layers [29]. Photoacoustic dermoscopy further highlights that the dermal microvascular network mirrors this interwoven collagen organization [29]. The significance of this interwoven structure has been demonstrated using an electrospun poly(epsilon-caprolactone)/type I collagen scaffold with crossed fibers, which significantly improved wound closure in diabetic rats compared to aligned and random nanofibrous scaffolds [30]. However, such scaffolds often fail to replicate the compositional, structural, and mechanical properties of the native dermis, which has an elastic modulus ranging from 3–18 MPa, compared to scaffolds’ moduli of 700 Pa-60 MPa [10,30–33]. Developing a matrix that mimics the dermal ECM’s compositional and structural complexity, along with its mechanical properties, is crucial for directing cellular behavior and accelerating healing.

Herein, we present for the first time an innovative human cell-derived ADM platform for DFUs: an interwoven ECM (iECM) matrix designed to closely mimic the structure of the reticular dermis. This iECM features several key attributes of human dermal ECM including (i) dermal-specific ECM components deposited by human dermal fibroblasts (hDFs), (ii) interwoven organization of ECM fiber bundles arranged at 90° angles with controllable pore size design; (iii) interwoven structure with a bulk elastic modulus of ~4 MPa, aligning with the elastic modulus range of native dermis; (iv) integrated thin ECM membrane to prevent fluid loss and enhance cell-matrix interactions, promoting uniform cell spreading, and (v) ease of fabrication, with a potential to achieve mass-production scalability via bioreactor-based culture. Our iECM is cell-derived and completely biological, addressing current bottleneck complications associated with commercial allo- and xenogeneic ADMs, such as tissue/organ scarcity, pathogen transmission risk, and adverse immunological or hypersensitivity responses. Our in vitro cell migration model indicated that iECM’s interwoven grid-like structure, topped with the thin membrane, guided fibroblast migration and organization following the anisotropic structural characteristics. A full-thickness excisional wound model in diabetic Lewis rats revealed that the unique synergistic design of the iECM significantly accelerated diabetic wound healing by promoting granulation tissue formation, angiogenesis, re-epithelialization, resolution of inflammation, and uniform deposition of collagen in the wound bed. iECM’s highly biomimetic composition, interwoven architecture, and mechanical robustness are pivotal in supporting physiological cellular responses and thereby accelerating diabetic wound healing.

Materials and methods

Interwoven and isotropic hDF-ECM fabrication

A silicon wafer with square micro-pits (500 μm width/length, 200 μm spacing, 170 μm depth) was fabricated using soft lithography at the AggieFab nanofabrication facility at Texas A&M University. Photomasks were designed in K-layout software and custom-ordered from CAD/Art Services, Inc. (Bandon, OR). Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) molds were cast from the silicon wafer using the Sylgard 184 Silicone Elastomer Kit (Dow Corning, Midland, MI) at a 10:1 base-to-cross linker ratio and cured at 65 °C for 4 h to create micropillared PDMS. Flat PDMS was cast using a flat plastic petri dish. Both micropillared PDMS and flat PDMS were coated with polydopamine and collagen-I, as previously described [34,35]. Briefly, PDMS was immersed in 0.01 % w/v 3-hydroxylamine hydrochloride (Dopamine-HCl) (ACROS Organics, Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) for 24 h, sterilized using an Anprolene AN75i Ethylene Oxide Sterilizer, and then incubated in 20 μg/mL bovine collagen (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) for 2 h before cell seeding [34]. Neonatal hDFs (ATCC, Manassas, VA; passage 4–5) were cultured for 5 weeks on micropatterned and flat PDMS to generate interwoven or random hDF sheets, respectively. The sheets were then decellularized using 1 M sodium chloride (NaCl), 0.5 % Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), 10 mM Tris, and 5 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetate (EDTA), as described previously [36,37] to obtain iECM and rECM.

Tensile testing

Bulk elastic modulus (E) of the scaffolds was determined using rectangular iECM/rECM sheets (25 mm x 6.25 mm x 0.2 mm; length x width x thickness). Tensile testing was performed on an Instron 5944 Universal Testing System (Instron Test Systems, Norwood, MA) equipped with a tensile clamp. Specimens were tested at a rate of 1 mm/min. Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM)

Micropillared PDMS and iECM samples were prepared for FE-SEM following a published protocol [36]. iECM sheets were fixed with 10 % formalin at 4 °C for 24 h, then washed and dehydrated through graded ethanol (10–100 %). After being desiccated overnight, samples were coated with platinum-palladium (Pt-Pd) alloy using a Cressington 208 HR sputter coater (Ted Pella Inc., Redding, CA) and imaged using an FEI QUANTA 600 FE-SEM (Hillsboro, OR).

Characterization of the iECM construct

The DNA content in hDF sheets and iECM was measured using Quant-iT™ PicoGreen™ dsDNA Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Major ECM components in iECM, including total collagen, fibronectin, elastin, and sGAG, were quantified using commercially available kits per manufacturer’s protocols: Total Collagen Assay Kit (Novus Biologicals, Centennial, CO), Fastin™ Elastin Assay (Biocolor, Carrickfergus, UK), Human Fibronectin DuoSet ELISA (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN), and Blyscan Glycosaminoglycan Assay (Biocolor).

Compositional evaluation of ECM sheets

Liquid Chromatography-Mass Spectroscopy (LC-MS)-based compositional analysis of ECM sheets was performed by Creative Proteomics (Shirley, NY). Three ECM (n = 3) sheets from each group (iECM and rECM) were digested by trypsin, identified, and quantified by applying a nano LC-MS/MS platform comprising Ultimate 3000 nano UHPLC system coupled with a Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with an ESI nanospray source. Detailed methods for sample preparation, nano LC-MS/MS Analysis, and data analysis are provided in the supplementary file.

Diabetic rat model

Animals

Animal experiments were performed following protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Texas A&M University. Male Lewis rats (6–8 weeks old, ~250 g) were purchased from Charles River Laboratories (Wilmington, MA) and maintained in an AAALAC-accredited facility.

Full-thickness wound creation and tissue collection

Lewis Rats were induced with Type I Diabetes Mellitus (TIDM) through intraperitoneal streptozotocin (STZ; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) injection (65 mg/kg, single dose) dissolved in citrate buffer following a 6–8 h fast. Normal water was provided throughout the process. Post-injection, rats were given normal food and 10 % sucrose water for 24 h to increase blood glucose levels, then switched to normal water. Blood glucose levels were monitored using a glucometer (Care-Sens®N), and rats with levels >150 mg/dl were included in the study. Next, the rats were anesthetized with isoflurane, and their backs were shaved with clippers and aseptically prepared for wound creation. 6 mm full-thickness circular wounds were created using a biopsy punch. Each wound site was implanted with two tissue constructs (iECM and rECM), spaced 2–3 cm apart. A splint ring was carefully placed around the wound, closed with suture, and covered with Tegaderm™ Film (3 M) and secured with a self-adhering bandage over the Tegaderm [38]. A portion of the skin from another random area of the back was also harvested and designed as a no-treatment (NT) control group. Animals were euthanized on days 3, 7, 14, and 28 post-implantation (n = 5 per time point) via carbon dioxide (CO2) in accordance with AVMA guidelines. Skin samples containing iECM and rECM (right and left sides, respectively), along with a random non-treated control skin sample, were collected from each rat. Tissues were embedded in Optimal Cutting Temperature (O.C.T.) compound (Sakura Tissue-Tek®, Torrance, CA), snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C. Samples were sectioned at 5 μm using a cryostat (Thermo Fisher Scientific, HM 525NX), mounted on HistoBond Slides (VWR, Radnor, PA), and fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde before staining.

Histological staining

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining was performed to assess tissue microanatomy. Tissue sections were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 15 min, washed with PBS and DI H2O, and stained with hematoxylin (Gill III, Merck, Rahway, NJ) for 5 min. Slides were differentiated by dipping them in a regressive solution, rinsing with water and 95 % ethanol, and counterstained with eosin (Eosin Y Alcoholic Solution, Sigma-Aldrich). Sections were dehydrated in 95 % and 100 % ethanol, respectively, cleaned in Xylene (Sigma-Aldrich-Histological Grade), and mounted in resinous media. The Olympus VS210 Virtual Slide Scanning System was used to capture the H&E staining images.

Masson trichrome staining (VWR KT034) was used to visualize collagen fibers. Sections were fixed in pre-heated Bouin’s Fluid at 56 °C for 15 min, cooled for 10 min, and rinsed in tap water and distilled water. Sections were then stained with Weigert’s Iron Hematoxylin solution for 5 min, followed by rinsing in running tap water for 2 min. They were then stained with Biebrich Scarlet/Acid Fuchsin Solution for 15 min, followed by distilled water rinsing. Sections were differentiated in Phosphomolybdic/Phosphotungstic Acid Solution for 1 min, stained with Aniline Blue Solution (5 min), and treated with 1 % Acetic Acid Solution (5 min). Sections were then quickly dehydrated in graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted with a mounting medium.

In vitro cell migration analysis

In vitro models were used to evaluate the wound healing mechanism of iECM sheets by assessing hDF migration by using two distinct seeding methods: 1. Membrane-guided migration: hDFs were seeded at a density of 10,000 cells/cm2 directly onto the membrane side of iECM sheets (Scheme S2, Supporting Information). Samples were collected on days 3, 7, 10, and 14 for analysis. 2. Zone-guided migration: A 6-mm diameter cell-free zone was created on a PDMS substrate. hDFs were seeded at 10,000 cells/cm2 onto the surrounding PDMS ring. After 24 h to allow cell attachment, an iECM sheet (grid side up) was placed at the center of the zone. Samples were collected on day 7 for analysis. In both models, rECM sheets were used as controls for comparison.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

IF staining of hDF-sheets, iECM, rat skin tissue sections, and cell migration samples was performed using a previously published protocol [39]. Samples were fixed in 4 % formaldehyde and blocked with 1 % bovine serum albumin (BSA), then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in 1 % BSA: collagen-I, fibronectin, and elastin with a 1:200 dilution, CD68 (1:200), CD206 (1:200), CD31 (1:200), and Ki67 (1:250). Samples were then stained with secondary antibodies: goat-anti-mouse Alexa Fluor™ 488, goat-anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor™ 488, goat-anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor™ 555, Alexa Fluor™ 594 Phalloidin, and Alexa Fluor™ 647 Phalloidin, all at 1:200 dilution in 1 % BSA. Cell nuclei were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole and dihydrochloride (DAPI). Images were captured using an Olympus VS120 Virtual Slide Scanner, Leica SP8 Confocal Microscope, and Zeiss Fluorescence Microscope at 10X, 20X, 40X, and 63X magnification. Quantitative analysis was conducted using QuPath-0.4.4 and ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical comparisons between experimental groups (sample size: n = 4 (in vitro) and n = 5 (in vivo) were performed by unpaired t-test and ANOVA, and Tukey's post hoc test using GraphPad Prism software. Results were expressed as mean ± SD and considered statistically significant for *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ***p < 0.0001.

Results and discussion

Fabrication and structural characterization of iECM

A completely biological and dermal-specific iECM was fabricated by decellularizing an interwoven hDF sheet cultured on a micropillared PDMS substrate, as illustrated in Fig. 1A. The PDMS was cast from a 6 cm x 6 cm silicon wafer, designed in KLayout software with square micropillars (500 μm width/length, 200 μm inter-pillar spacing, 170 μm depth) and fabricated in-house using soft lithography (Fig. S1A-B). The resulting PDMS substrate with a micropillar structure was confirmed by FE-SEM (Fig. 1B(i)). After 5 weeks of culture, hDFs grew into a 90°-interwoven sheet on the PDMS, predominantly filling the space between the pillars with minimal growth on top, as observed under light microscopy (Fig. 1B(ii)). Following decellularization, the iECM retained its architecture, with ECM fibers organized in an interwoven pattern (Fig. 1B(iii)). IF imaging revealed F-actin stress fibers (red) and cell nuclei (blue) in hDF sheets (Fig. 1C), both of which were absent after decellularization (Fig. 1D). In contrast, key structural proteins, including collagen I, fibronectin, and laminin fibers, were well preserved in the iECM post-decellularization (Fig. S3A-B). The DNA content in iECM (13.3 ± 2.43 ng/mg ECM dry weight) was significantly reduced after decellularization (Fig. 1K), meeting the standard for decellularized biomaterials (<50 ng/mg ECM dry weight) to minimize the risk of immune reactions upon implantation [40,41]. Confocal imaging revealed the iECM’s grid structure, with a thin ECM membrane located at the base (Fig. 1E). Quantitative analysis of the confocal data showed that the iECM has a total thickness of 49.1 ± 2.5 μm, which includes the underlying thin ECM membrane measuring 7.2 ± 0.9 μm (Fig. 1F). The iECM could be easily detached from the PDMS base and folded while maintaining its interwoven structure without shrinking, deformation, or tearing (Fig. 1G-H). By adjusting the size and shape of the PDMS substrates, square and circular iECM sheets in multiple sizes were fabricated, demonstrating the adaptability of the platform to different wound bed dimensions (Fig. S2A-D). The largest square-shaped iECM, based on our silicon wafer dimensions, measured 6 cm x 6 cm, covering an area of up to 36 cm2 (Fig. S2E).

Quantitative analysis of major ECM macromolecules showed that decellularization largely preserved key structural components of the hDF sheet. Total collagen content was maintained (217 ± 16 μg/scaffold vs. 210 ± 14 μg/scaffold; ~97 % retention), while elastin retention remained high (~73 %; 194 ± 35 μg/scaffold vs. 141 ± 26 μg/scaffold) following decellularization (Fig. 1J-K). In contrast, significant reductions were observed in fibronectin (3.3 ± 0.6μg/scaffold vs. 2.4 ± 0.4 μg/scaffold, p < 0.05) and sulfated glycosaminoglycans (sGAG; 17.0 ± 0.9 μg/scaffold vs. 6.9 ± 0.9 μg/scaffold, p < 0.0001), although fibronectin retention in iECM remained above 70 % (Fig. 1L-M). The preferential preservation of collagen and elastin is consistent with their thicker, cross-linked fibrillar structures [42], whereas the thinner fibronectin fibers (~ 5–25 nm) and hydrophilic sGAG are more susceptible to loss during detergent-based decellularization [43]. Importantly, retention of collagen and elastin supports maintenance of iECM’s structural and mechanical integrity, while effective removal of cellular components confirms successful decellularization.

Micromorphological, mechanical, and compositional evaluation of iECM

FE-SEM analysis revealed that the iECM exhibited a grid-like architecture composed of nanofibrous bundles oriented at orthogonal (90°) angles (Fig. 2A), closely resembling the interwoven organization of native collagen fibers [44,45]. Prominent ring-shaped, thick ECM bundles were observed surrounding grid corners and along grid sides (red arrows, Fig. 2Aii), corresponding to micropillar corners and straight walls where hDFs experienced higher localized tension during cell sheet formation prior to decellularization (Fig. 1Bii). Higher-magnification images further showed that iECM consists of tightly packed, entangled mesh-like nanofibrous bundles (Fig. 2Aiii). In addition, a continuous thin ECM membrane with a nanofibrous organization was present at the base of iECM (Fig. 2B). This basal membrane was designed to function as a physical barrier, protecting the wound from external insults such as microbial invasion while retaining moisture, a particularly important feature in infection-prone diabetic environments [46]. In contrast, rECM exhibited a randomly organized fibrous network (Fig. S2F, S3C).

The stress-strain curve obtained from tensile stretching showed that both iECM and rECM exhibited ductile behavior prior to failure (Fig. 2C). From a fiber-reinforced composite perspective, the ring-shaped thick bundles surrounding the grid forming the interwoven structure (Fig. 2Aii), together with mesh-like entangled nanofibers spanning within and between bundles (Fig. 2Aiii), provide multiple load-bearing pathways that enable efficient stress redistribution and enhanced energy dissipation. As a result, iECM underwent progressive failure rather than abrupt rupture. The wavy stress-strain profile observed prior to failure (Fig. 2C) likely arises from the progressive activation of spatially distributed, localized damage mechanisms, such as microcrack initiation and bundle-level failures, which remain isolated and do not immediately propagate. In contrast, rECM lacks both reinforcing bundles and an organized hierarchical architecture, resulting in limited stress redistribution and abrupt failure at substantially lower stress levels (~0.6 MPa). Quantitatively, iECM displayed significantly higher bulk elastic modulus (3.91 ± 0.5 MPa vs. 1.1 ± 0.2 MPa, p < 0.001), toughness and maximum stress compared with rECM (Fig. 2D). The strain at maximum stress was lower for iECM, indicating reduced deformability associated with the presence of mature, tensile-aligned ECM bundles (Fig. 2A). These findings are consistent with prior reports showing that hDF sheets stretched between micropillars realign ECM fibers along tension direction, with localized stiffness increases of up to 6-fold compared with unaligned regions [47]. Notably, the bulk elastic modulus of iECM (~ 4 MPa) falls within the reported range for native dermis (3–18 MPa) [30], highlighting its mechanical relevance. Importantly, this robust and self-sustaining ECM bundle architecture preserved structural integrity, enabling easy handling and placement on the wound bed without specialized techniques.

Comprehensive LC-MS proteomic analysis identified a total of 1486 proteins in both iECM and rECM scaffolds. Based on the Label-Free Quantification (LFQ) intensities (Supplementary Excel Sheet 1), proteins were categorized as upregulated (fold-change, FC > 1.5) or downregulated (FC < 0.67). Overall, the most abundant proteins exhibited largely similar compositional profiles between iECM and rECM (Fig. S3D). Fig. 2E-F summarizes the 15 most abundant proteins and the sum intensities of the rest of the proteins in each ECM. Among the dominant components, type VI collagen (α1(VI), α2(VI), and α3(VI)) is a non-fibrillar collagen [48], playing a crucial role in structural support and matrix assembly [49], with α1(VI) supporting re-epithelialization and α2(VI) and α3(VI) forming a meshwork that reduces the fibrosis [50] and anchors blood vessels [51]. Fibronectin, a key dermal protein, facilitates cell adhesion, migration, and recruitment of fibroblasts and keratinocytes to the wound bed [43], while Fibrillin-1 contributes to ECM elasticity and stability [52]. Thrombospondin-1 modulates cell-cell and cell-matrix interactions and supports scar-free healing through its anti-angiogenic properties [53,54]. Other abundant proteins, including transforming growth factor beta-induced protein (βig-H3), tenascin, and vimentin, are known to promote cell adhesion, proliferation, and migration [55–57], while aggrecan enhances ECM hydration [58]. EMILIN-1 (Elastin Microfibril Interface Located Protein 1) [59] and heparan sulfate proteoglycan [60,61] support ECM integrity, elastogenesis, and growth factor signaling, collectively facilitating regeneration and wound repair. Comparative analysis between iECM and rECM further identified 23 upregulated and 56 downregulated proteins (Supplementary Excel Sheet 2). Notably, upregulated proteins in iECM included desmin and Myosin II, which enhance actomyosin contractility and maintain cytoskeletal organization, contributing to the mechanical resilience of iECM. In addition, basement membrane-associated components, including collagen α-1(XVIII) (Endostatin) and collagen α-2(IV) (canstatin), were enriched in iECM, supporting its structural integrity and stiffness (Supplementary Excel Sheet 2 and Table S1). Together, these data demonstrate that while iECM and rECM share a largely similar proteomic composition, iECM enriches cytoskeletal-associated and basement membrane-associated proteins, resulting in a mechanically robust and biologically active scaffold that more closely recapitulates the hierarchical organization and functional properties of native dermal ECM.

Full-thickness excisional wound healing in diabetic Lewis rats

The wound healing efficiency of iECM and rECM was evaluated using a full-thickness excisional wound model in diabetic Lewis rats and compared with the no-treatment (NT) group (Scheme S1). All groups exhibited progressive wound closure over 28 days; however, iECM consistently promoted faster and more effective healing (Fig. 3A). As early as day 3, wound reduction in the iECM group (18.60 ± 6.95 %) exceeded that of rECM (10.3 ± 6.5 %) and NT (4.7 ± 2.0 %). This trend persisted through day 14 and culminated in near-complete closure by day 28 in the iECM group (81.1 ± 3.74 %), significantly outperforming rECM (65.8 ± 4.1 %) and NT (37.9 ± 5.1 %) (Fig. 3B).

H&E staining revealed distinct differences in healing progression among groups (Fig. 3C, S4). On day 3, all wounds showed disrupted skin architecture with inflammatory cell infiltration, including neutrophils (red asterisk and arrow in Fig. S4B), macrophages (MØ), and wound exudates, with no evidence of hair follicle regeneration. By day 7, granulation tissue formation was evident in all groups, accompanied by reduced inflammatory cells (MØ) and smaller wound beds (the area inside black dashed demarcated lines; Fig. 3C), with the iECM group exhibiting significantly greater epidermal thickness (p < 0.001; Fig. 3D). Notably, pronounced cytoplasmic vacuolization (green arrows, Fig. S4C), a spontaneous response to injury that facilitates sequestration of waste products and cellular debris [62,63], along with enhanced phagocytic MØ activity (yellow arrows), were observed in the iECM group, indicating active ECM remodeling and a transition toward tissue repair. By day 14, the iECM group displayed near-complete epithelial coverage and advanced healing (25 % granulation, 75 % repair), whereas rECM (50 % granulation, 50 % repair) and NT wounds (75 % granulation, 25 % repair) remained predominantly in the granulation phase. By day 28, complete epidermal formation was observed in all groups, with iECM exhibiting the greatest epidermal thickness, consistent with accelerated healing (Fig. S4A). Histopathological scoring (Table S2) further supported these observations at the wound surface and within the connective tissue core (a deeper region). On day 3, all groups exhibited moderate chronic-active surface inflammation, but connective tissue core inflammation varied: iECM showed mild chronic inflammation, rECM had mild chronic-active inflammation, and NT displayed moderate chronic-active inflammation. Healing scores were 3 for iECM and rECM, indicating granulation tissue formation, while NT scored 1 to 2, reflecting an active ulcer state. By day 7, iECM and rECM showed moderate acute inflammation with neutrophils at the surface and moderate chronic-active inflammation with a mixture of neutrophils and MØ in the connective tissue core, scoring 3 and 2, respectively. NT displayed moderate chronic-active inflammation at the surface and mild chronic-active inflammation in the core, with a score of 2. By day 14, both iECM and rECM exhibited severe chronic-active inflammation at the surface and within the connective tissue core, with cellular connective tissue scoring 4 at the edges and 1 at the center. In contrast, the NT group showed moderate chronic-active inflammation, scoring 4 in deeper regions and 1 at the center, resembling an active ulcer. By day 28, iECM showed moderate chronic inflammation in the connective tissue core (score 3 to 4), indicating a transition from granulation tissue to cellular connective tissue, accompanied by keratinized tissue tracks and an intact epithelial surface. Similarly, rECM exhibited mild chronic inflammation on the surface and core (score 3 to 4), reflecting a comparable transition. Meanwhile, the NT group displayed residual hemosiderin-laden MØ, indicating the later stage of granulation tissue healing.

Collagen deposition and organization were assessed using Masson's trichrome staining (Fig. 4A). At early time points (days 3 and 7), all groups exhibited immature, loosely organized collagen. By day 14, iECM-treated wounds displayed a well-organized collagen network, while the rECM group showed sparse and irregular fibers, and the NT group exhibited dense, disorganized collagen bundles typical of fibrotic scarring. By day 28, iECM developed a mature basketweave-like collagen architecture associated with flexibility and functional tissue regeneration [64], whereas rECM and NT retained thick, irregular collagen bundles, with the NT group showing more pronounced scarring, potentially due to glycosylation [65].

Given the critical role of MØ polarization in wound repair, MØ phenotypes were examined by CD68 (M1-like) and CD206 (M2-like) colocalization. iECM treatment resulted in a significantly higher M2-MØ ratio at all time points compared to rECM and NT groups (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4B). On day 3, the M2-MØ ratio in iECM was 23.3 ± 2.2 %, compared to 13.5 ± 2.2 % in rECM and 8.9 ± 2.2 % in NT. By day 7, all groups showed increased M2-MØ ratios. The highest ratio was observed on day 14, with iECM at 84.6 ± 2.7 %, rECM at 68.4 ± 2.6 %, and NT at 36.1 ± 2.6 % (Fig. 4E). These findings indicate that iECM treatment facilitates an earlier and stronger M1-to-M2 shift during the critical inflammatory-to-proliferative phase of cutaneous wound healing (days 3 to 14) [66].

The timely MØ polarization orchestrates key regenerative events, including angiogenesis and granulation tissue formation. CD31 immunostaining revealed significantly greater neovascularization in iECM-treated wounds, with vessel density peaking at day 14 and subsequently declining by day 28, consistent with normal vascular regression during healing (Figs. 4C, 4F) [67–69]. In parallel, Ki67+ staining demonstrated significantly higher proliferative activity in iECM-treated wounds from day 3 to 14 compared to rECM and NT-treated wounds (Fig. 4D, 4G), reflecting accelerated granulation and tissue regeneration. By day 28, Ki67+ expression decreased across all groups, indicating near-completion of re-epithelialization. Collectively, these results demonstrate that iECM accelerates diabetic wound healing by promoting timely MØ polarization, enhancing angiogenesis and cell proliferation, and supporting organized collagen remodeling, thereby facilitating efficient and functional tissue repair.

In vitro cell migration analysis to model iECM-induced wound healing

To elucidate how iECM accelerates wound healing and reduces scarring, an in vitro membrane-guided cell migration model was designed to mimic direct contact between iECM and wound bed (Scheme S2). As a predominant cell type in the dermis, hDFs were seeded on iECM and rECM scaffolds and tracked over 14 days to assess how iECM architecture regulates migration, cytoskeletal organization, and matrix remodeling.

On rECM, hDFs displayed a spread morphology with no apparent alignment or organized cytoskeletal structures (shown by F-actin staining), throughout the culture period, that is from day 3 to 14 (Fig. 5A i-iv). This disordered cellular organization was accompanied by irregular ECM deposition and is consistent with the dense, disorganized collagen bundles and increased scarring observed in vivo (Fig. 4A) [65, 70]. Similarly, hDFs initially cultured on the membrane side of iECM displayed random migration with prominent filopodia protrusions throughout the culture period (Fig. 5B i-xii). However, by day 14, hDFs penetrated the thin membrane and migrated directionally along the grid walls of iECM (Fig. 5C i-iv), indicating their strong response to the architectural cues.

Although fibronectin fibers appeared locally aligned on both rECM and the thin membrane region of iECM, this alignment on rECM did not provide sustained or spatially instructive guidance. Only the interwoven grid regions of iECM generated stable, region-specific cues that induced consistent cell and nuclear alignment (Fig. 5C i-iv). Quantitative analysis of cell and nuclear morphology (Fig. 5A v-viii and 5B xiii-xvi) using aspect ratio (AR) (Fig. 5D; Table S3) and nuclear shape index (NSI) confirmed this distinction (Fig. 5E; Table S3) [71,72]. On rECM, hDFs exhibited moderate elongation by day 10 (AR: 3.0 ± 0.04; NSI: 0.6 ± 0.03), followed by reduced elongation by day 14, possibly due to cell crowding and contact inhibition (Fig. 5A v-viii). In contrast, iECM supported progressive and spatially patterned elongation, with maximal elongation along grid sides and tops, followed by grid corners and grid centers (Fig. 5C v-xii).

Cytoskeletal organization further distinguished iECM from rECM. Within iECM grids, hDFs formed arc-like, ring-shaped F-actin structures supported by diagonally oriented stress fibers spanning the grid (Fig. 5F i). This configuration enables uniform intracellular force distribution and minimizes localized stress concentrations during migration [73,74]. In contrast, actin organization on rECM remained randomly oriented (Fig. 5F ii). Such cytoskeletal adaptations are consistent with findings by Kollmannsberger et al. [75], who demonstrated that ECM-generated tensile forces within confined geometries can drive reversible fibroblast-myofibroblast transitions at the highly tensed growth front in the absence of exogenous TGF-β stimulation. Consistent with this mechanotransductive mechanism, our zone-guided migration, with hDF migrating from edge to the center, revealed spatially confined α-SMA expression on iECM on day 7 (Fig. 5G, Fig. S6). α-SMA-positive hDFs were highly elongated and preferentially localized along grid walls and migration fronts (Fig. 5G i), where they co-localized with diagonally oriented actin bundles, suggesting transient myofibroblast activation in regions of elevated mechanical demand. In contrast, hDFs on rECM displayed randomly orientated α-SMA fibers across the migration zone (Fig. 5G ii), indicative of dysregulated and potentially persistent myofibroblast activation associated with fibrosis in vivo.

Matrix anisotropy and architecture, rather than biochemical composition alone, are well-recognized regulators of fibroblast mechanotransduction and tissue remodeling [76,77]. Consistent with this principle, collagen I deposition on iECM followed grid-defined migration tracks (Fig. 5G i), whereas rECM showed diffuse and disorganized collagen I distribution (Fig. 5G ii). The coordinated alignment of α-SMA, actin filaments, and collagen I on iECM indicates early, structured matrix deposition that limits prolonged myofibroblast activation. These findings align with mechanical feedback models in which actin-rich protrusions locally align matrix fibers, reinforcing directional tension and stabilizing transient activation states [78]. Moreover, collagen fibrils formed under sustained mechanical loading have been shown to exhibit reduced susceptibility to MMP-mediated degradation [79], suggesting that the interwoven, tension-bearing bundle architecture of iECM may confer enhanced structural stability in proteolytic wound environments.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that the improved wound healing observed with iECM treatment arises from its unique integration of architectural organization, mechanical robustness, and spatial guidance, rather than differences in ECM composition alone. Despite similar LC-MS profiles between iECM and rECM, the interwoven architecture of iECM generates directional mechanical cues that coordinate cytoskeletal organization, nuclear elongation, transient myofibroblast activation, and organized collagen deposition. The same cues facilitate MØ elongation and polarization toward a pro-regenerative M2-like phenotype through mechanosensitive pathways such as YAP/TAZ signaling [80, 81], while also promoting directional endothelial migration and tubulogenesis [82]. Together, this structure-mechanics coupling orchestrates coordinated cellular, immune, and matrix remodeling responses, resulting in accelerated wound healing with reduced scarring and improved tissue regeneration. Importantly, the inherent bioactivity, mechanical robustness, and tissue integration, and native-like matrix architecture of iECM also position it as a potential platform for combinatorial regenerative strategies, including integration with therapeutic cells, exosomes, or pro-angiogenic growth factors, particularly in compromised or ischemic wound microenvironments.

Conclusions

A robust, off-the-shelf, hDF-derived acellular iECM was developed to mimic the macromolecules and growth factors, and exhibited tensile properties comparable to human dermis, ensuring structural integrity. iECM implantation in a full-thickness diabetic wound model promoted rapid granulation tissue formation, angiogenesis, resolution of inflammation, and organized collagen fiber deposition, resulting in accelerated wound closure. With its dermal-specific composition and superior mechanical performance, iECM represents a promising solution for diabetic wound management.
