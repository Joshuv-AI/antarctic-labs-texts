In vitro antiproliferative effects of green synthesized silver nanoparticles from Brassica carinata microgreens on DU-145 prostate cancer cells and In vivo safety assessment
Authors: Dogfounianalo Somda, Joel L. Bargul, Sabina Wangui Wachira, John M. Wesonga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Background

Cancer remains a major obstacle to enhancing life expectancy and ranks as the second most common cause of fatalities, rendering it a critical public health issue.1, 2 In 2020, cancer accounted for 10 million fatalities worldwide.3 Prostate cancer, with 1.41 million new cases annually and 375,000 fatalities worldwide in 2020, ranks as the second most common cancer among older men globally.4, 5 Currently, cancer management is mainly through radiotherapy, cytoreductive surgery, and chemotherapy. However, these approaches are often associated with significant side effects and high costs, limiting their accessibility for many patients. Additionally, there is an emergence of resistance to anticancer treatments, which remains a major challenge to effective cancer therapy.6, 7, 8 Therefore, there is a pressing need to find innovative and more effective therapeutic alternatives with minimal side effects and cost-effective to improve the efficacy of cancer therapies.

Brassica carinata A. Braun (Ethiopian kale) of the Brassicaceae family is garnering growing interest in research due to its potential as a microgreen and functional food, driven by its rich phytochemical profile, which enhances its nutritional benefits and therapeutic attributes.9, 10 Microgreens are immature edible plants that are harvested when the first true leaves appear, typically within a period of 7 to 21 days post-sowing.11, 12 Thanks to their distinctive biological attributes, microgreens rapidly expand and hold great potential as a beneficial food source for human health.13, 14 The heightened interest in microgreens stems from their remarkable health benefits, their exceptional nutrient density, which can be up to 40 times greater than that of their mature plants.15 Moreover, studies have reported the medicinal properties and therapeutic potential of Brassica carinata microgreens, including antidiabetic properties16, antifungal and antibacterial properties,17 as well as anticancer activities.18, 19

Although herbal remedies exhibit impressive in vitro antiproliferative and antimicrobial activities, their in vivo effectiveness is often limited due to several factors, including poor water solubility, lipophilicity, and unsuitable molecular size, which prevent them from crossing the cell membrane. These properties hinder their absorption and systemic distribution, ultimately reducing their bioavailability and therefore affecting their therapeutic efficacy.20, 21, 22 Nanotechnology emerges as a cutting-edge alternative to address the aforementioned challenges and ultimately enhance the therapeutic efficacy of plant-based remedies. Unlike traditional approaches, nanosystems such as AgNPs offer the advantage of protecting bioactive compounds from degradation, ensuring their accurate delivery at optimal concentrations, and thereby enhancing their therapeutic potency.21, 23 Furthermore, recent investigations indicate that combining herbal medicines with nanobiotechnology (nanoformulation) results in improved pharmacological properties, including increased bioavailability, solubility, and stability. It also enhances the distribution of tissue macrophages, as well as minimizing systemic side effects.24, 25 Moreover, AgNPs have demonstrated anticancer properties, rendering them an attractive option for cancer therapy.26, 27 The production of AgNPs using plant extracts has gained prominence over other methods due to the numerous invaluable advantages it offers.28, 29 Thus, this approach is straightforward, rapid, efficient, high-output, scalable, sustainable, and affordable.30, 31 Most importantly, the presence of biomolecules in plant extract, such as glucosinolates, flavonoids, phenolics, and amino acids, plays a crucial role in the synthesis of AgNPs by acting as natural reducing, stabilizing, and capping agents. These compounds facilitate the reduction of silver ions (Ag+) to elemental silver atoms (Ag0), resulting in the formation of biocompatible and biodegradable AgNPs, which makes them suitable for biological applications, such as drug delivery and chemotherapy.32, 33 On the other hand, the remarkable enhancement in the biological activities of biosynthesized AgNPs is attributed to the fact that AgNPs produced with plant extracts not only possess their own therapeutic properties but also harness the biological activity of the bioactive compound in the plant extracts.31, 34

Despite their considerable therapeutic potential, the use of AgNPs worldwide has significantly increased human and environmental exposure to these nanoparticles. There are significant apprehensions about their possible detrimental impacts on public health and the environment.35 This concern is particularly justified given that AgNPs can build up in biological tissues, including the liver, kidneys, lungs, and other organs.36 Current information concerning the toxicity of AgNPs, including their biodistribution and metabolism, is scarce and inconclusive. Furthermore, there are no regulations and guidelines on the safe dosage of AgNPs to be administered to humans.37, 38 Regulatory agencies like the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), and the European Chemicals Agency (ECHA) are still developing regulations and guidelines to guarantee the safe and effective application of nanomaterials.38 The toxicological assessment of biosynthesized AgNPs contributes to the knowledge of the safety profiles of nanoformulations. This study sought to evaluate the anti-proliferation properties of biosynthesized BCM-AgNPs as well as their safety through in vivo subacute toxicity test.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

The chemicals and reagents employed in this research complied with analytical standards and were acquired from accredited suppliers. The normal Vero E6 (ATCC® CRL-1586™) cell line stemmed from green monkeys, and the prostate carcinoma cell line (DU-145, ATCC® HTB-81™) were procured from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Minimum Essential Medium (MEM) produced by ATCC, along with DMSO, foetal bovine serum, trypsin, were bought from Gibco BRL (Germany). MTT, PBS, and doxorubicin were acquired from Solarbio Life Sciences Company (Beijing, China). Formalin solution, ethanol, paraffin wax, and blood collection tubes were purchased from Legacy Lab Africa LTD (Nairobi, Kenya). Reagents for biochemical and hematological analyses were supplied by the manufacturers of the respective equipment.

Biosynthesis of silver nanoparticles mediated by BCME

This study used previously synthesized nanoparticles from Brassica carinata microgreen extracts (BCM-AgNPs), whose physicochemical characteristics were successfully characterized through spectroscopic and microscopic techniques.17 The BCM-AgNPs exhibited a maximum absorption peak at 420 nm and were stable over various pH levels, temperatures, and storage conditions. Moreover, they exhibited a crystalline size of 34.68 nm on average, a polydispersity index of 0.13 ± 0.02, a zeta potential of − 22.5 ± 1.16 mV, and displayed a spherical morphology. The functional groups implicated in the biosynthesis of AgNPs comprised alkanes, alkynes, aldehydes, esters, and nitro groups.17

In vitro anticancer study

Cell culture conditions

Cell culture was performed in MEM (GIBCO, USA) fortified with vital components for cellular growth, which include 1 % HEPES buffer (Gold Biotechnology, USA), 7.5 % Sodium hydrogen carbonate (LOBA Chemie, India), 1 % L-Glutamine (Sigma Aldrich, USA), 10 % Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS, Sigma Aldrich, USA), 1 % Penicillin/Streptomycin (Sigma Aldrich, USA). In order to achieve 70 to 80 % confluence, the cells were cultured in an incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) under standard conditions of 37 °C, 5 % CO2, and 95 % humidified atmosphere.

Cytotoxicity and antiproliferation assay

The cytotoxicity, as well as the antiproliferation activities of BCM-AgNPs, were evaluated through the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay as previously described.39 Vero and DU-145 cell suspensions, each adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 105 cells/mL, were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 104 cells/well and incubated overnight under standard conditions (37 °C with 5 % CO2 and in a humid atmosphere). 24 h post-incubation, the initial seeding media were replaced with 100 µL of fresh media, containing the treatments at concentrations ranging from 200 µg/mL to 6.25 µg/mL. The treatments included BCM-AgNPs, BCME, doxorubicin (positive control), and 0.1 % DMSO (negative control). The cells exposed to the different treatments were subsequently incubated under the aforementioned conditions for 48 h. Next, the media containing the different treatments was removed, and 100 µL of new media containing 10 µL of MTT at 5 ng/mL, freshly prepared, was added to each well. The plates were then kept for an additional 4 h under the same conditions.40 The MTT was eliminated, and 100 µL of 100 % DMSO was added to each well to dissolve the formazan crystals. A Tecan Infinite M1000 ELISA microplate reader was used to measure the absorbance at 570 nm, with 720 nm serving as reference absorbance for normalization.41 Each experiment was conducted in replicate and a minimum of three times. The cytotoxic effects on Vero cells and the anti-proliferative activity against DU-145 of the BCM-AgNPs were expressed as a proportion of the cells subjected to the treatment to the negative control, following the formula (Eqn. (1)) below:(1)Cellviability%=AbsorbanceofthetreatedcellsAbsorbanceofthenegativecontrolx100All treatments that inhibited cell proliferation by over 50 % were then used for further analysis. Subsequently, the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) and half-maximal cytotoxic concentration (CC50) values were determined using GraphPad Prism based on data from a two-fold serial dilution approach.42 To determine IC50 values, DU-145 cells were treated with BCM-AgNPs and BCME at concentrations ranging from 6.25 to 200 μg/mL, alongside the positive control (doxorubicin) at concentrations ranging from 3.12 to 100 μg/mL. Meanwhile, CC50 values were estimated in Vero cells exposed to BCM-AgNPs and BCME, at concentrations between 6.25 and 200 μg/mL, as well as to doxorubicin within the same concentration range (6.25 to 200 μg/mL).

The selectivity index (SI) is a critical metric in the development of anticancer drugs. It measures the ability of the compound to selectively target the cancerous cells while sparing the normal cells. A higher SI value indicates better selectivity, implying that the compound preferentially targets cancer cells over normal cells. Cell morphology study

The effect of BCM-AgNPs on the morphology of the DU-145 cell line was investigated according to the method outlined by Kamau et al.41 DU-145 cells were placed into 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and left to reach confluence over 24 h. Thereafter, they were subjected to various concentrations of BCM-AgNPs, which encompass the IC50, IC25, and IC12.5, along with BCME and the control at their respective IC50 concentrations. The cells underwent a 48-h incubation, and morphological changes were observed. The EVOS™ XL Core Imaging System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was employed to capture the images.

Cell migration assay

A two-dimensional wound healing assay was conducted in order to evaluate the impact of BCM-AgNPs on cell migration, as previously outlined.21, 40 Exponentially growing DU-145 cells were collected and placed onto 12-well plates at a density of 106 cells/mL per well and then incubated at 37 °C with 5 % CO2. The cells were then monitored until they reached a confluence of 95 % and formed a monolayer. Thereafter, sterile 100 µL pipette tips were employed to create an artificial wound vertically through the cell monolayer in each well, and perpendicular lines were made and used as a reference to the wound. After the scraping, the cells were gently washed with PBS to remove debris before being treated with BCM-AgNPs at their IC50 and IC25 concentrations, as well as the negative control (DMSO 0.1 %). The microscope EVOSTM XL (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was utilized to capture the images of the scratch areas at 0 h, 24 h, and finally at 48 h. The images were analysed using ImageJ software (imagej.nih.gov). Subsequently, the relative migration distance or the percentage of wound closure was computed based on the following formula (Eqn. Clonogenic or colony formation assay

The clonogenic assay evaluates cell survival and proliferative potential upon exposure to treatments. It is often employed to evaluate long-term cytotoxicity during the drug discovery process.44 In this study, a clonogenic assay was performed in order to evaluate the effect of BCM-AgNPs on the colony formation capacity of DU-145 cells. Du-145 cells were seeded at a density of 500 cells/well into six-well plates and then incubated overnight in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5 % CO2. Following overnight incubation, the cells were exposed to the predetermined IC50 and IC25 of BCM-AgNPs, along with the positive (Doxorubicin) and negative (0.1 % DMSO) controls. The treatments were left to act for 48 h; afterward, the treatment-containing media were replaced with fresh media. The cells were further incubated under the aforementioned conditions with regular media replenishment (every three days) until colonies had formed and completely covered the negative control wells (fourteen days). Upon completion of the experiment, the media was eliminated, and the colonies were gently flushed twice using PBS, then fixed with 75 % methanol and 25 % acetic acid for 1 h at ambient temperature.45 Thereafter, the fixed colonies were stained with 0.5 % crystal violet (sigma) for 5 h.46 After 5 h, the colonies were flushed and dried at ambient temperature, then subsequently counted through an EVOSTM Imaging system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Only colonies with more than 50 cells were included in the analysis.47 Data were expressed as the mean number of colonies per treatment obtained from a minimum of three replicate experiments.

Cell cycle assay

A cell cycle assay was carried out in order to investigate how BCM-AgNPs affect cell cycle arrest. Thus, DU-145 cells at the exponential growth phase were seeded at a density of 105 cells/mL in T-25 flasks and then maintained for 24 h at 37 °C in a 5 % CO2 humidified atmosphere. The cells were further subjected to BCM-AgNPs at their IC50 and IC25 concentrations, along with a 0.1 % DMSO (negative control), and then incubated for 48 h under the aforementioned conditions. Following a 48-h incubation period, the cells were flushed twice to eliminate any remaining treatment. Subsequently, they were collected via trypsinization and fixed in 70 % ethanol for 24 h. After the ethanol was discarded, the cells underwent centrifugation at 2000 rcf for 2 min, and then re-suspended in ice-cold PBS with 0.25 % Triton X-100 for 15 min. The resuspended cells underwent a second round of centrifugation at 2000 rcf for 2 min. After discarding the supernatant, the cells were re-dispersed into PBS alongside 10 μg/mL RNase A and 25 μg/mL propidium iodide, then incubated at 24 °C for 30 min in the dark.48 The distribution of cell cycle phases was further determined through a flow cytometer (BD Canto II, BD Sciences), and the acquired data were analyzed with FlowJo (version 10.10.0; Ashland, OR, USA).49

DNA fragmentation assay

In order to elucidate the effect of BCM-AgNPs on the mechanism of cell death, a DNA fragmentation assay indicative of cell apoptosis was carried out as outlined,50, 51 with few modifications. DU-145 cells were introduced into T-25 flasks at a density of 105 cells/mL and kept at 37 °C overnight with 5 % CO2. Next, the cells were subjected to BCM-AgNPs at IC50, IC25, and BCME at IC50, alongside the positive control (at IC50) and the negative control. The treatments were allowed to act for 48 h under the aforementioned conditions. Afterward, the cells were flushed twice with PBS to eliminate the treatment, then collected using trypsin, and 1 mL of fresh medium was added to the harvested cells to get a cell suspension. The suspension was further subjected to centrifugation at 1000 × g for 2 min. After discarding the supernatant, the resulting pellet was resuspended in 100 μL of PBS for DNA extraction.52 DNA extraction was done using the Quick-DNA™ Miniprep Plus Kit (Zymo Research, USA), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The cells were lysed by mixing the resuspended pellet with 200 μL BioFluid & Cell Buffer (Red) and 20 μL proteinase K and vortexed. Afterward, an equivalent volume of Genomic Binding Buffer was introduced into the digested sample and mixed gently. The lysates were purified by transferring them to the Zymo-Spin™ IIC-XLR Column, where they underwent centrifugation and washing. The DNA was subsequently eluted using 70 μL of DNA Elution Buffer. The extracted DNA was subsequently analyzed through electrophoresis on a 1.5 % agarose gel and stained with SafeView™ DNA Stain (Cat. No. R23-P05, Applied Biological Materials Inc., Canada). The Gel was run at 95 V for 45 min in a Tris/acetate/EDTA (TAE) buffer with a pH of 8.5, containing 2 mM EDTA and 40 mM Tris-acetate.53 DNA bands were visualized and recorded using the E-BOX Gel Imaging System (Vilber Lourmat, France) equipped with a 302 nm UV transilluminator and image capture software.

Gene expression profiling

RNA extraction

DU-145 cells were initially placed in T-75 flasks and allowed to adhere and grow for 24 h. Once cells reached about 80 % confluency, they were then exposed to BCM-AgNPs at their IC50 concentration alongside the negative control (0.1 % DMSO). After 48 h of incubation, the cells underwent three PBS washes and were further harvested through trypsinization. Thereafter, the harvested cells were resuspended in PBS for RNA extraction. Total RNA extraction was performed using the PureLink™ RNA Mini Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) as per the manufacturer’s guidelines. To quantify the isolated RNA, a NanoDrop, ND-2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) was used. The RNA was preserved at − 80 °C for downstream analysis.

Complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis

Total RNA extracted from various samples was normalized to a concentration of 100 ng/μL prior to cDNA synthesis.21 The cDNA was synthesized by employing a FIREScript RT cDNA synthesis kit (Solis BioDyne, Estonia), as per the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA synthesis was conducted in a 20 μL reaction volume comprising 10 μL of template RNA (100 ng/μL), 0.5 μL of dNTP mix (20 mM), 2 μL of 10 × RT buffer with DTT, 1 μL of FIREScript reverse transcriptase, 0.5 μL of RNase inhibitor (40 U/μL), 1 μL of oligo(dT) primer (100 μM), and 5 μL of nuclease-free water. Reverse transcription was performed using a SimpliAmp thermal cycler (Applied Biosystems) under the following conditions: primer annealing at 26 °C for 6 min, reverse transcription at 42 °C for 60 min, and enzyme inactivation at 85 °C for 5 min. The cDNA was preserved at − 20 °C for further analysis.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis and relative gene expression

The effect of the treatments on the expression level of selected genes was evaluated using qRT-PCR. The qRT-PCR analysis was carried out with the HOT Firepol® Evagreen® qPCR Mix Plus (ROX) 5x kit (Solis BioDyne, Estonia), using the Quant Studio™ 5 RT-qPCR system (Applied Biosystems, USA) following the manufacturer's guidelines. The master mix and primers without cDNA were included as a blank. The PCR conditions included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 60 s (1 cycle), followed by denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s (40 cycles), extension at 60 °C for 30 s (40 cycles), and a melt curve step for 60 s (1 cycle). Oligonucleotide primers targeting specific genes were procured from Macrogen (Europe BV, Netherlands) (Table 1). The melting curve was analyzed for each reaction to ensure that no unwanted amplification had occurred. These reference genes were selected due to their stable expression levels across different cell types and all experimental conditions. Moreover, the use of two housekeeping genes increases the reliability and accuracy of the normalization by mitigating the impact of potential variations in individual housekeeping gene expression. Animal studies

Experimental animals

A total of twenty-four (24) healthy female Wistar rats, aged between seven and eight weeks and measuring 150 – 200 g, were acquired at the Small Animal Facility for Research and Innovation (SAFARI) at Jomo Kenyatta University of Agriculture and Technology (JKUAT, Kenya). The animals were housed in standard cages for one week to acclimatize and were subjected to a 12-h cycle of light and darkness at room temperature. They had unlimited access to water and feed pellets. Clean-cut papers were placed on the floors of the cages to serve as bedding for the animals. The cages were cleaned up daily, and the papers were replaced with fresh ones.

Ethical approval

All protocols received ethical clearance from the Ethics Committee for the Use and Care of Animals at Mount Kenya University, Kenya (Ref. No. MKU/ISERC/3626). The experiments were designed to minimize animal pain and distress as much as possible, following the National Institutes of Health’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.56

Toxicological investigations on BCM-AgNPs

The sub-acute toxicity investigation of BCM-AgNPs was conducted as previously described.57 The study focused on investigating the prolonged effects related to low nanoparticles concentrations on haematological markers, serum biochemical indices, as well as histopathological markers in Wistar rats.58

Sub-acute toxicity

The subacute toxicity was investigated as per the guidelines number 407 of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), which outlines the standard protocol for a 28-day repeated-dose oral toxicity study in rodent models59 with slight changes from the method outlined by Jegnie et al.60 Twenty-four female rats assigned for the investigation were allocated into four groups of six rats each and allowed to acclimatize for four days before the study commenced. Thereafter, the rats underwent 16 h of fasting with free access to water before being administered BCM-AgNPs. The various doses of BCM-AgNPs were determined based on their demonstrated therapeutic activities in the in vitro study. The group (1) was assigned as the negative control group and was given distilled water at a volume of 1 mL per 100 g of body weight, while the test groups (groups 2 to 4) were subjected to BCM-AgNPs at 5, 10, and 20 mg/kg body weight per day, respectively, for 28 days. The treatments were given orally once daily through gavage at a volume of 1 mL per 100 g of body weight over 28 days.61, 62

Clinical evaluation

The animals were clinically evaluated as previously described.62, 63 Throughout a subacute toxicity study, all animals were meticulously monitored, with close observation during the first 4 h after the drug administration and subsequently on a daily basis, in order to identify any signs of toxicity, such as lethargy, mortality, and various general physical and behavioural changes, encompass diarrhoea, convulsions, tremors, salivation, lacrimation as well as feeding activities.37 To evaluate weight changes, all animals were weighed at the beginning of the study and subsequently weekly throughout the experiment. The water intake and feed consumption for each group were calculated daily using formulas (Eqn. (4) and (Eqn. AFGPD (g) refers to the amount of feed given the previous day in g, while AFRA24h (g) is the amount of feed remaining after 24 h (g).

Collection of samples

On day 29, following an overnight fast, animals were humanely euthanized under carbon dioxide (CO2) asphyxiation.64, 65 Thereafter, blood was collected via cardiac puncture into two tubes: one containing EDTA for haematological analysis and the other without additives for biochemical analysis, as described previously.61, 66 Subsequently, vital organs, namely the liver, heart, kidneys, lungs, and spleen, were surgically removed from the dissected rats, flushed with physiological saline, and weighed. The organs were further preserved in 10 % formalin saline for further histopathological investigation.67

Analysis of biochemical parameters

Blood samples in additive-free tubes were allowed to coagulate at room temperature for 1 h prior to centrifugation (3,500 rpm, 10 min). The resulting supernatant sera were subsequently used for biochemical analysis62. The biochemical parameters were investigated using an automated chemistry analyzer, the Architect c4000 (Abbott Diagnostics, Lake Forest, IL, USA), as per the manufacturer's instructions. These parameters included liver function markers, such as Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP), Alanine Aminotransferase (ALT/GPT), Aspartate Aminotransferase (AST/GOT), Gamma-Glutamyl Transferase (GGT), Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH), and Total Bilirubin (BIL-T). Protein metabolism was also assessed by determining levels of Total Protein (TP) and Albumin (ALB). Renal function was evaluated through measurements of Creatinine (CREA), Urea, and Blood Urea Nitrogen (BUN). Furthermore, electrolyte balance was assessed by evaluating the concentrations of Chloride (CL), Sodium (NA), and Potassium (K).68, 69

Analysis of haematological parameters

Haematological parameters were assessed using blood collected in EDTA tubes through an automated Mindray BC-5000 haematological analyzer (Mindray Medical International Limited, Shenzhen, China). The haematological parameters investigated included key blood cell counts, erythrocyte indices, and platelet indices.64, 37, 59 Which encompassed, WBC: Total White Blood Cell Count, LYMP: Lymphocyte Count, MID: Mid-sized Cell Count, GRAN: Granulocyte Count, LYMP (%): Lymphocyte Percentage, MID (%): Mid-sized Cell Percentage, GRAN (%): Granulocyte Percentage, RBC: Total Red Blood Cell Count, HGB: Haemoglobin Concentration, HCT: Haematocrit, MCV: Mean Corpuscular Volume, MCH: Mean Corpuscular Haemoglobin, MCHC: Mean Corpuscular Haemoglobin Concentration, RDW-CV: Red Cell Distribution Width Coefficient of Variation, RDW-SD: Red Cell Distribution Width Standard Deviation, PLT: Platelet Count, MPV: Mean Platelet Volume, PDW: Platelet Distribution Width, PCT: Plateletcrit.

Post-euthanasia, all rats underwent a macroscopic pathological investigation, encompassing an evaluation of the exterior surface, internal organs, and all orifices, as well as a record of any evident pathological changes. Thereafter, vital organs such as the heart, lungs, spleen, liver, and kidneys were surgically removed and weighed to determine their absolute organ weight. Histopathological investigation

The organs (the heart, lungs, spleen, liver, and kidneys), which were kept in 10 % neutral buffered formalin solution (fixation), were further subjected to histopathological investigations as follows. The fixed organs were then dehydrated with 100 % ethanol, embedded in paraffin wax, and allowed to solidify into blocks on a cold plate. Thereafter, they were sectioned with a microtome blade to a thickness of 3 to 4 µm.68, 72 The sectioned organs were further stained using haematoxylin and eosin according to standard laboratory procedures.73 Slides of the stained organs were ultimately made and scrutinized using a light microscope (Optika, Italy) at × 40 magnification to identify any histological alterations, including tissue integrity degeneration, necrosis, apoptosis, and leukocyte infiltration.68 Photomicrographs were captured with a manually installed digital camera, thereafter recorded, and further analyzed and interpreted by a histopathology specialist.74, 72

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 (La Jolla, CA, USA) and OriginPro 2024 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA). Results were then expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical comparisons between the different groups were performed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Dunnett's/Turkey’s post hoc test, as well as t-tests. A p-value of less than 0.05 was employed to indicate the statistical significance for all analyses conducted in the study.

Results

In vitro anticancer activity

Cytotoxicity and antiproliferation assay

Both BCM-AgNPs and BCME significantly inhibited the proliferation of DU-145 cells (p < 0.0001), reducing cancer cell growth by more than 50 % (Fig. 1). The Antiproliferative effect of BCM-AgNPs was significantly greater than that of BCME, suggesting that nano formulation significantly enhances antiproliferative properties, as shown in Fig. 1. A two-fold serial dilution method was employed in order to estimate the IC50 and the CC50 values. DU-145 cells were then subjected to BCM-AgNPs at concentrations ranging from 6.25 to 200 μg/mL (Fig. 2A), BCME at 6.25–200 μg/mL (Supplementary Fig. S1), and the positive control at 3.12 to 100 μg/mL (Supplementary Fig. S3). Meanwhile, Vero cells were exposed to BCM-AgNPs at 6.25–200 μg/mL (Fig. 2B), BCME at 6.25–200 μg/mL (Supplementary Fig. S2), and the positive control at 6.25–200 μg/mL (Supplementary Fig. S4). BCM-AgNPs exhibited an IC50 of 33.47 ± 1.92 μg/mL and a CC50 of 117.33 ± 1.35 μg/mL. BCME displayed an IC50 of 46.25 ± 0.88 μg/mL and CC50 of 213.76 ± 2.41 μg/mL, while Doxorubicin (positive control) showed an IC50 of 4.51 ± 1.10 μg/mL with a CC50 of 12.16 ± 0.83 μg/mL (Table 2). Notably, the results demonstrated that all treatments suppressed cell proliferation in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 1(A-B)). Effects of BCM-AgNPs on the relative organ weight

The evaluation of the impact of daily oral administration of BCM-AgNPs on the relative weight of essential organs (heart, lungs, spleen, liver, and kidneys) revealed no marked change (increase) in the relative weight of all assessed organs in the treated groups (5, 10, and 20 mg/kg) relative to those in the control group (Fig. 12), p > 0.05.

Gross pathological examination

The effects of BCM-AgNPs on vital organs were macroscopically evaluated. No alterations or lesions associated with the varying doses (5, 10, and 20 mg/kg) of BCM-AgNPs administered to the rats were observed. Moreover, the general aspect and structure of the vital organs remained intact. There were no visible changes in the organs of rats subjected to BCM-AgNPs relative to those of the control group (Supplementary Fig. S5).

Histopathological investigation

The histopathological investigation of vital organs (heart, lungs, spleen, liver, and kidneys) in both the control group and rats subjected to daily oral administration of BCM-AgNPs at varying doses (5, 10, and 20 mg/kg) for 28 days revealed normal morphological characteristics and an absence of pathological alterations, including inflammation, cellular infiltration, or necrosis, in the majority of organs, among them the heart, lungs, spleen, and liver (Fig. 13). However, minor localized blood congestion was found in the kidneys of both untreated (control) and BCM-AgNPs-treated rats (Fig. 13). The occurrence of blood congestion in both the control and BCM-AgNP-treated rats suggests that the cause might be environmental rather than a direct effect of BCM-AgNP oral administration.72

Discussion

Prostate cancer continues to pose a significant global health challenge, with rising incidence and fatality rates, particularly in developing nations that face substantial barriers to timely diagnosis and adequate treatment options.76 Current therapies like chemotherapy and surgery face challenges such as side effects, resistance, relapse, and high costs, further worsening patient outcomes.41 This underscores the imperative for novel and less detrimental therapeutic agents. This work was undertaken to evaluate the anticancer activity of BCM-AgNPs on DU-145 cancer cells and normal Vero cells, and to assess their safety through an in vivo subacute toxicity study.

The MTT assay is an accurate and trustworthy test of cell viability. It relies on the aptitude of succinate dehydrogenase, a mitochondrial enzyme, to convert tetrazolium salt (MTT) in metabolically active cells into purple formazan crystals. The formazan concentration, quantified by color intensity, correlates directly with viable cell numbers.77 The MTT assay demonstrated that both BCM-AgNPs and BCME exerted potent cytotoxic effects against DU-145 prostate cancer cells. These findings correlate with earlier investigations that have proven the cytotoxic properties of both the mature Brassica carinata18 and its microgreens on cancer cell lines.19 Furthermore, the observations are also in line with earlier research in which plant-mediated AgNPs from different plant extracts exhibited significant cytotoxic activity against gastric cancer,34 cervical and prostate adenocarcinomas,21 and cervical (HeLa229) cancer cells.78 Notably, BCM-AgNPs (IC50 of 33.47 µg/mL) demonstrated a significantly stronger cytotoxic effect on DU-145, relative to the crude BCME (IC50 of 46.25 µg/mL), implying that nanoformulation significantly enhances antiproliferative properties of plant extracts. These findings align with those reported by Zhao et al.,34 who found that the ethanolic extract of Sphaeranthus indicus demonstrated a modest cytotoxic effect against HGT-1 cells, while its bioengineered AgNPs displayed significantly improved anticancer effect. The incorporation of bioactive compounds, such as alkaloids and glucosinolates, into AgNPs through a plant-based synthesis approach results in a significant enhancement of their anticancer efficacy. Commonly present in plants, these compounds exhibit various anticancer mechanisms of action, encompassing proliferation inhibition, induction of apoptosis, and modulation of the immune system.79 Nanoparticle formulations enhance the delivery and efficacy of natural compounds by improving their solubility, bioavailability, and targeting abilities, enabling more effective and targeted cancer therapy. Furthermore, these plant-based AgNPs serve as potent tools for targeted therapy.79 They leverage the unique properties of nanoparticles and phytochemical bioactivity to eliminate tumor cells with precision, minimizing damage to normal cells.79 The enhanced anticancer effects of biosynthesized BCM-AgNPs may therefore stem from their nano-enabled properties, which enhance cellular uptake, solubility, bioavailability, and targeting abilities, driven by high surface area and small particle size.80

The selectivity index (SI) evaluates how selectively a compound exerts cytotoxic effects on cancer cells over normal cells.81 The threshold for high selectivity is established at a selectivity index value ≥ 2.82 BCM-AgNPs demonstrated a higher Selectivity Index (SI = 3.50) compared to the positive control (doxorubicin, SI = 2.69), suggesting its selective toxicity towards cancer cells and its promise as a treatment option for DU-145 prostate cancer cells. On the other hand, BCM-AgNPs altered the morphology of DU-145 cells in a manner that depended on the dose, compared to the negative control, as evidenced by the appearance of unusual characteristics, including cell shrinkage, irregular shapes, loss of intercellular connections, and detachment from the culture surface.41

Most cancer-related fatalities are attributed to metastases.83, 84 Preventing metastases is crucial to improving the prognosis of cancer patients. The in vitro cell migration assay revealed that BCM-AgNPs were able to significantly impede DU-145 cell migration in both time and concentration-dependent manner relative to the negative control. Thus, the wound area remained more than 60 % open, indicating a significant reduction of cell migration in DU-145 cells exposed to BCM-AgNPs (IC50) compared to the control. Similar observations were reported in previous studies on biosynthesized AgNPs, which demonstrated comparable anti-migratory effects across different cancer cells, namely HeLa, PC3, MCF-7, and A549 cells.78, 85, 86, 87 The anti-migratory effect of BCM-AgNPs may stem from their ability to inhibit key protein pathways regulating cellular adhesion and motility.21, 88

A foremost difficulty in cancer treatment is the post-therapy resurgence of the disease, which often leads to therapeutic failure. This relapse is driven by residual cancer cells that reacquire their malignant properties, including sustained proliferative signaling and resistance to apoptosis.21, 89 The clonogenic assay, therefore, serves as a vital tool in cancer drug discovery, as it assesses the post-treatment regrowth capacity of cancer cells. The capacity of BCM-AgNPs in inhibiting the colony formation ability of DU-145 cells upon treatment removal was evaluated through the colony formation assay. As a result, BCM-AgNPs markedly impeded the colony-forming capacity of DU‑145 cells in a manner that depends on the concentration relative to the control (p < 0.0002), underscoring their promising anticancer activity against DU-145. These results corroborate earlier investigations that reported the potential of biogenic AgNPs to hinder the capacity of several malignant cells to form colonies.90, 91, 92 However, given that the percentage of cell recovery following the treatment removal for the IC50 (14.99 %) was not 0 % indicates that the efficacy of BCM-AgNPs is limited in the long term, as they did not totally prevent residual cancer cells from re-growing after the treatment was withdrawn, as pointed out by Gavamukulya et al.21

The cell cycle plays a crucial role in the transition of normal cells into cancer. The different phases (G1, S, G2) control cells' movement through the cycle, ensuring that cells cannot proceed to the next stage if their DNA is damaged. Many cancer drugs exploit this by triggering cell cycle arrest through DNA damage and ultimately blocking tumor progression.48, 93 The cell cycle investigation findings demonstrated that BCM-AgNPs significantly hindered cell cycle progression at the S phase in a manner that depends on the concentration of BCM-AgNPs. This was evidenced by a higher proportion of cells in the S phase at both IC50 and IC25 concentrations, while fewer cells progressed to the G2/M phase. Given that the S phase (synthesis phase) is dedicated to DNA replication, the buildup of cells in the S phase after being exposed to BCM-AgNPs at their IC50 and IC25 concentrations could be explained by DNA damage or disruption of DNA synthesis induced by BCM-AgNPs.41 These findings are confirmed by further observations indicating that BCM-AgNPs triggered apoptosis by fragmenting DNA. Moreover, similar results have been reported by a number of studies.94, 95, 96

Many anticancer agents target apoptotic pathways, underscoring the importance of understanding apoptosis in the development of effective cancer treatments. DNA degradation is one of the biochemical changes observed during apoptosis.97 A DNA fragmentation assay was performed to elucidate the biological characteristics of apoptosis. The findings revealed that BCM-AgNPs cause cell death in DU-145 cancer cells via DNA fragmentation, as evidenced by the gel electrophoresis results, which clearly show diffuse smears across the gel in cells exposed to BCM-AgNPs, in contrast to the undamaged (intact) DNA found in the negative control. This result validates the anticancer efficacy of BCM-AgNPs via apoptosis induction and aligns with earlier research.98, 99 Gurunathan et al.98 reported that the accumulation of AgNPs within the nucleus might affect the DNA as well as the process of cell division. In addition, it is hypothesized that AgNPs would induce DNA damage by promoting the generation of reactive oxygen species and by activating the p53 protein, thereby mimicking the mechanisms associated with radiation-induced carcinogenesis.100, 101, 102

Gene expression analysis serves as a powerful and indispensable tool in anticancer research offering molecular level insights into how the product affects cancer cell behavior. RT-qPCR analysis was conducted to measure the mRNA levels of significant prostate cancer-related genes in order to understand how BCM-AgNPs act as an anticancer agent on DU-145 cells. The observed downregulation of the relative expression level of AR, BCL-2, AKT1, and ESR1 could be associated with the antiproliferative effect of BCM-AgNPs against the DU-145 cell. These genes are typically upregulated during cancer progression.103 BCL-2 is frequently upregulated in advanced androgen-independent prostate tumors, suggesting its role in disease progression.5, 104 AR signaling is crucial for the onset and progression of prostate cancer (PCa). In advanced stages of PCa, mutations in the AR and its overexpression lead to continued cell proliferation.105 Su et al.106 have reported that silencing both Akt1 and Akt2 significantly reduces the metastatic potential of CWR22rv1 human prostate cancer cells. Furthermore, previous research indicates that different isoforms of AKT, such as AKT1 and AKT2, might contribute to the advancement of prostate cancer from androgen-dependent stages to those that are hormone-refractory.107, 108 Okpako et al.109 have highlighted the involvement of ESR1 in Prostate cancer proliferation and migration in their study. The downregulation of the relative expression of AR, BCL-2, AKT1, and ESR1 in DU-145 cells subjected to BCM-AgNPs aligns with findings from the migration assay, in which BCM-AgNPs markedly hindered cell migration. This impeded migration is probably attributable to the repression of essential genes associated with cellular motility and invasive activity. Therefore, the mode of action of BCM-AgNPs on DU-145 cells could be the inhibition of the expression of genes strongly related to cancer cell migration and progression.21 On the other hand, exposure of DU-145 to BCM-AgNPs led to up-regulation of the expression level of Caspase9, P53, and Bax. Caspase 9 encodes the initiator caspase of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway, playing a crucial upstream role in the activation of programmed cell death.110, 40 The p53 signalling pathway plays a pivotal role in promoting apoptosis in tumor cells. As a key tumor suppressor gene, p53 governs the expression of downstream targets involved in DNA repair, cell cycle regulation, and the promotion of apoptosis.109 The Bax gene, which stands for Bcl-2-associated X protein, plays a pivotal role in controlling programmed cell death, which is essential for the elimination of cancerous cells.111 The observed upregulation of Caspase 9, p53, and Bax relative expression levels in DU-145 cells exposed to BCM-AgNPs provides clear evidence that BCM-AgNPs exert their anticancer effects on DU-145 by activating the intrinsic apoptotic pathway. Therefore, their mode of action on DU-145 cells probably involves stimulating genes involved in apoptosis. BCM-AgNPs exert their anticancer effects on DU-145 by activating the intrinsic apoptotic pathway, and on the other hand, by inhibiting genes involved in cancer progression, including those related to cancer cell migration and proliferation. Comparable findings have been documented.102, 112 A limitation of this study is the lack of in vivo antitumor efficacy data, which is necessary to further validate the anticancer potential of BCM-AgNPs observed in vitro. Future investigation should focus on evaluating the therapeutic efficacy of BCM-AgNPs in animal models with prostate cancer xenografts, as well as investigating potential synergistic effects with conventional chemotherapeutic agents.

Owing to their exceptional anticancer, antifungal, antimicrobial, and antiviral properties, AgNPs are extensively utilized in medicine.113 However, their extensive utilization raises considerable apprehensions regarding safety, biodistribution, and possible detrimental impacts on human health and the environment.38, 114 Subacute toxicity assesses the potential prolonged detrimental consequences arising from continuous daily exposure to the same substance.62 Subacute toxicity testing is prioritized due to its relevance to real-world drug use, as most drugs are administered repeatedly. It better predicts long-term toxic effects, organ damage, and biochemical changes. This study further evaluated the safety of BCM-AgNPs via a subacute toxicity assessment in female Wistar rats. The findings demonstrate that the daily administration of BCM-AgNPs (5, 10, and 20 mg/kg) over 28 days did not induce any toxic effects in rats.

The daily administration of BCM-AgNPs for 28 days in Wistar rats did not induce any toxicity-related morbidity or fatality. Observable clinical manifestations of toxicity encompass alterations in feeding habits (anorexia), alterations in physical appearance (dull hair, dehydration, paleness of mucous membranes), behavioural changes (lethargy, agitation, aggressiveness), and, notably, in certain instances, the onset of gastrointestinal disturbances (diarrhoea, vomiting).115 Diarrhoea is a symptom frequently observed in toxicity studies and may be a direct manifestation of the toxic effect of the substance assessed on the digestive system. In our study, none of these clinical signs were observed, which agrees with results from previous studies.37, 116 The results revealed that the daily administration of BCM-AgNPs had no impact on the feeding habits or water consumption of the treated rats. No notable change was seen in food or water consumption between the BCM-AgNP-treated groups and the untreated group (p > 0.05). The preservation of appetite and water intake in the treated groups suggests that the administration of BCM-AgNPs did not induce gastrointestinal dysfunction or metabolic disturbances that could lead to appetite loss or alterations in feeding habits.62

Body weight is an important criterion for assessing an animal's overall health status.117 The daily exposure to BCM-AgNPs (5, 10, and 20 mg/kg) did not result in body weight loss in the treated rats compared to the control. In addition, a steady and progressive increase in body weight was found in both treated and untreated groups throughout the investigation, aligning with the outcomes reported in other investigations.118 This indicates that prolonged oral exposure to BCM-AgNPs in rats did not hinder their growth or induce any physiological disruptions that might indirectly compromise their overall health.

The liver and kidneys play crucial roles in detoxifying the body by metabolizing toxic substances, such as medications, and facilitating their elimination. Therefore, the assessment of serum-specific biochemical markers related to these organs is fundamental for assessing the toxic effect of any substance on their function.119 Increased serum levels of liver enzymes, specifically ALT and AST, as well as renal nitrogenous waste products such as urea and creatinine, may indicate necrosis or damage to liver or kidney tissues, and lead to the release of these enzymes into the bloodstream.60, 120 Therefore, increased serum levels of these markers are commonly utilized as evidence of hepatotoxicity or nephrotoxicity.62 We measured serum levels of specific hepatic markers, such as AST, ALT, and ALP, to assess liver health status. The findings demonstrate a notable increase in AST levels in the treated rat group relative to the control. Nevertheless, this variation remains within the normal range.1 Moreover, this increase in AST levels was not dose-dependent. In addition, it was contradictory to the histopathological analysis results, which revealed no liver lesions. This increase could not, therefore, be considered as an indicator of the adverse effect of BCM-AgNPs on the liver. The blood levels of urea, BUN, and Crea were also measured to assess the nephrotoxicity of BCM-AgNPs. An increase in these markers would indicate kidney damage.121 Notably, the repeated administration of BCM-AgNPs did not result in any significant alterations in blood urea, BUN, or crea levels in the treated rats when compared to the untreated group. Assessing sodium, chloride, and potassium levels serves as a reliable marker for evaluating the functional status of the heart and muscles.122 Our results have shown no significant difference between the levels of these major ions in the group subjected to BCM-AgNPs and those in the control group. Finally, the serum level of LDH was found to be significantly higher in the group of rats subjected to BCM-AgNPs at 20 mg/kg and 10 mg/kg relative to the control. However, the values remained within the conventional normal range for rats.75 Moreover, although elevated LDH levels are generally associated with tissue damage,123 results from histopathological examinations of vital organs did not support this finding. As a result, the elevation in LDH levels may be regarded as an isolated occurrence or potentially attributed to sample handling procedures rather than being indicative of BCM-AgNPs toxicity. Further studies should assert this hypothesis.

The hematopoietic system, especially the bone marrow, is a primary target for harmful substances.124, 125 Therefore, assessing haematological parameters is a valuable approach for identifying potential toxic effects of these substances on the blood. Such evaluation helps identifying haematological abnormalities, including anemia, leukopenia, or thrombocytopenia, which may indicate underlying toxicity or physiological alterations.1 The haematological parameter assessment revealed a significant increase in WBC levels in rats treated with 20 mg/kg of BCM-AgNPs and in PLT levels in those treated with 20 mg/kg and 5 mg/kg. The increased WBC in rats subjected to the highest dose of BCM-AgNPs was considered to be an isolated finding unrelated to other toxicity parameters. It would constitute an immune response to BCM-AgNPs, regarded as a foreign entity. The significant increase in PLT count was not dose-dependent. Furthermore, the values remained within the normal range.75 Therefore, this increase cannot be interpreted as a response to the toxicity of BCM-AgNPs but rather as the result of external factors related to sample processing. Further in-depth studies would be essential to verify these hypotheses.

Induced toxicity manifests through morphological alterations in vital organs, encompassing changes in size, color, and texture.126 Macroscopic examination of vital organs post-autopsy revealed no significant differences in color, texture, or shape between the organs of treated rats and those of the control group. Moreover, Relative Organ Weight (ROW) is one of the key biomarkers used to assess potential treatment-induced damage to the internal organs of animals.127 The study revealed no notable differences in the ROW of the vital organs of the treated rats relative to the control (p > 0.05).

Drugs and xenobiotics are metabolized and eliminated mainly by the kidneys and liver. As the primary sites of biotransformation for these compounds, they are, therefore, the main targets of the adverse effects of toxic substances.125 No treatment-associated histopathological changes were observed in the vital organs of the treated rats relative to the control. No evidence of cellular injury, inflammatory response, necrosis, or structural disarray was seen in either the treated or control animal groups. All assessed organs exhibited normal architecture and cellular structure in both the treated and control groups. Notably, even at high doses, AgNPs did not exhibit cellular toxicity. These findings align with the biochemical parameter results of all treated rats, revealing no significant variations attributable to the treatment. Furthermore, Srisrimal et al.118 have reported similar findings in their study, which evaluated the in vivo toxicity of AgNPs using rats. Blood congestion was found in the kidneys of both the untreated rats and those subjected to BCM-AgNPs. This finding is unlikely to be associated with the treatment, as it is also present in the untreated group. This observation may be attributed to environmental factors,72 such as tissue handling during autopsy. Improper euthanasia or delayed fixation can cause artificial congestion. Additionally, euthanasia techniques like CO2 asphyxiation may compromise vascular integrity. This study aligns with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (UN SDGs), notably SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being), by addressing the pressing global health burden of prostate cancer through safer, plant-based nanotherapies. The green synthesis of BCM-AgNPs using Brassica carinata microgreens supports SDG 9 (Industry, Innovation, and Infrastructure) by promoting sustainable nanotechnology as an innovative advancement in nanomedicine. The eco-friendly method contributes to SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production) by utilizing plant-based materials, reducing reliance on toxic chemicals and energy-intensive, as well as expensive, processes, thereby lowering the environmental impact of drug development. The study, therefore, integrates the principles of sustainable health innovation, eco-friendly nanotechnology, and responsible scientific practice, thus aligning well with the UN SDGs and contributing meaningfully to global sustainability and health equity goals.

Conclusion

Various experimental approaches were employed to elucidate the mechanisms through which BCM-AgNPs hinder the proliferation and metastatic abilities of prostate cancer cells. These findings demonstrate the multifaceted anticancer effect of BCM-AgNPs against prostate cancer cells, ranging from their ability to selectively inhibit the proliferation and migration of DU-145 cells to inducing cell cycle arrest through DNA fragmentation, and notably, impairing the ability of DU-145 cells to form colonies. On the other hand, the subacute toxicity assessment indicated that BCM-AgNPs did not produce any adverse effects in female Wistar rats following 28 days of repeated oral administration, implying their safety and biocompatibility. These findings, therefore, underscore their potential as a promising candidate for anticancer therapy. However, owing to molecular variability across different cancer types, evaluating the anticancer activity of BCM-AgNPs on other types of cancer cell lines would further strengthen their potential as anticancer agents. Additionally, long-term toxicity studies, such as sub-chronic and chronic toxicity studies, are necessary to establish a comprehensive safety profile for human use.
