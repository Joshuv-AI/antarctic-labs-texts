Establishing humoral immunity to rabies in a person with multiple sclerosis through booster vaccination: A case report
Authors: D Dinov, P Hardeman, J Tessier, PV Sguigna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
It is known that certain disease-modifying therapies for multiple sclerosis impair humoral immunity; however, data on the effect of therapies on humoral immunity with human diploid cell rabies vaccine is limited. Here we present a case of a 31-year-old male with multiple sclerosis on ofatumumab who had a bat of unknown rabies status fly into his hand. Despite completing the standard rabies vaccination series per the World Health Organization recommendations, he did not establish humoral immunity until he received two additional booster vaccinations. This case highlights novel aspects of humoral immunity to rabies in a person taking ofatumumab.

Background

The development of highly effective disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) for multiple sclerosis (MS) has improved disease control steadily over the last three decades. As it pertains particularly to high-efficacy DMTs, balancing [their high efficacy with] their associated immunosuppressive properties, including impaired humoral immunity to vaccination, has become of increasing import. This was a prominent concern during the COVID-19 pandemic, where there was mixed evidence on the establishment of humoral immunity across DMTs, and the requirement for booster vaccines to achieve this immunity.1–4 Expanding the MS community's knowledge about the impact of DMTs on humoral immunity with vaccination is important to optimize the care and reduce risks for persons with MS.

In particular, the human diploid cell vaccine (HDCV) is an inactivated virus vaccine against rabies, a rare but nearly universally fatal disease. It was shown to establish humoral immunity in 100% of participants following a series of three vaccines in its original trial population nearly 70 years ago. 5 Given the lethality of the disease, it is recommended for all persons following high-risk exposure to rabies. 6 Herein we present a case of an MS individual on ofatumumab who had a potential rabies exposure and ultimately established humoral immunity to the virus following booster vaccinations.

Case report

A 31-year-old gentleman with no medical history other than relapsing-remitting MS, on ofatumumab presented to the emergency department shortly after an unprovoked bat flew into his hand. No bite marks or blood were observed, but the animal could not be retrieved, so he was labeled a CDC category III exposure.7,8 He had been compliant with ofatumumab since his MS diagnosis 3 years prior, with his last dose being 23 days prior to the event. He had no evidence of disease activity (i.e. no new symptoms, physical disability, or new MRI lesions). He had no prior rabies vaccination or potential rabies exposure(s).

On the day of potential exposure, a CBC with differential was without lymphopenia (ALC was 1.01 × 109/L), and immunoglobulins were within normal limits. He initiated the World Health Organization (WHO) post-rabies exposure prophylaxis guidelines, which include a rabies immune globulin in the affected limb (passive immunity), and his first of five dosages of the HDCV (active immunity).7,8 Given the risk of rabies, his ofatumumab was held per the guidelines until he completed the recommended five doses of HDCV. He received a sixth dose of the HDCV on day 28 as a first booster, obtaining a total of six rabies vaccinations (Figure 1). He was instructed to restart ofatumumab 2 weeks after his sixth HDCV, at which point a post-vaccination rabies serum IgG via a commercially available rapid fluorescent foci inhibition test was drawn. The resultant rabies IgG returned below the threshold level established by the WHO, 0.5 IU/mL, even despite passive immunity. Given this, his ofatumumab was again held, and he received another HDCV booster approximately 2 months following bat contact. Two weeks following the second booster vaccination, a repeat rabies IgG was measured and was found to be adequate (≥ 0.5 IU/mL). He restarted ofatumumab and has not had any new neurological symptoms or disability, nor has he developed rabies beyond the average incubation period, with the most recent follow-up being 5 months post-exposure.

Discussion

Establishment of humoral immunity to a vaccine antigen has been regarded as a biomarker of efficacy, a surrogate for the ability of a vaccine to protect its recipient against the intended pathogen. Humoral immunity is established in the HDCV by growing the Pitman Moore strain of the rabies virus in human diploid cells, inactivating them, and giving them as an intramuscular injection. 5 By growing it in human diploid cells versus neural tissue cells, there is a lower risk for neurological complications. In the original HDCV trial population, all participants established humoral immunity against the rabies antigen after 49 days. 5 No country mandates rabies vaccination for its entire population, but countries with high-disease burden recommend it for travelers, and those individuals who are high risk, such as those who handle animals known to harbor the virus. While there is limited data on the humoral immunity of the rabies vaccine in the MS population, one single-center study evaluating the rabies vaccine in 23 participants receiving teriflunomide and 23 patients receiving placebo, rabies seroprotective titers were obtained by all participants, similar to the initial vaccine trial population. 9

Reviewing the literature on the impact of ofatumumab, or any of the B-cell depleting DMTs, the authors could not find evidence on the establishment of humoral immunity with the rabies vaccine. As rabies is a neoantigen for our patient, one could extrapolate the evidence from other neoantigen vaccines, such as the evidence that ofatumumab may not limit humoral immunity to the COVID-19 antigen. 10 Possibilities include the depth and duration of B-cell depletion, or the differences in the vaccine technologies between the COVID and rabies vaccines that account for the discrepancy we found between these neoantigen vaccines.

The main novelty of our case is the absence of rabies IgG despite the standard WHO vaccine series with one additional booster, even following passive immunity. The absence of other immunosuppression and otherwise intact lymphocyte count, and immunoglobulin levels suggests there were no other typical detractors to the establishment of humoral immunity despite the near-continuous B-cell depletion through ofatumumab. Establishment of immunity to rabies is time-sensitive following potential exposure to the virus, and the duration of time it takes to obtain a rabies IgG level from the laboratory had a significant impact on the time between the standard vaccine series and the booster.

Conclusion

Current WHO recommendations, namely the rapid administration of the rabies vaccine, reduce the risk of infection. This is the first datum the authors can identify describing the impact of B-cell depleting therapy on the establishment of humoral immunity with HDCV. Our case suggests that there indeed may be value in booster vaccinations for those exposed to B-cell depleting therapy to establish humoral immunity. While there is no similar literature for this high-risk clinical situation, this is a single case report, and much caution should be exercised before generalizing these findings.
