Peripheral circulating proteomic profiling reveals serum CD80 is positively correlated with the efficacy of neoadjuvant PD-1 blockade combined with chemotherapy for esophagus cancer
Authors: Liangliang Wu, Chaohu Pan, Wei Wang, Yiming Gao, Xiaoli Cui, Shengzhi Xie, Bo Yang, Jing Yuan, Man Chen, Jing Zhang, Haitao Luo, Tianyi Liu, Guoqing Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeoadjuvant immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy has achieved remarkable success in esophagus cancer. However, identifying the patients who may benefit from the neoadjuvant treatment remains a great challenge. The aim of this study was to investigate peripheral blood biomarkers and explore their underlying mechanism.MethodsBaseline blood samples were collected and analyzed from esophagus cancer patients (discovery cohort n = 46, validation cohort n = 28) treated with neoadjuvant PD-1 blockade combined with chemotherapy. To explore the underlying mechanism of the identified biomarker, pre- and post-treatment transcriptome sequencing and PD-L1 immunohistochemistry data were analyzed.ResultsA total of 55 features, including complete blood count and serum proteins were quantified from blood samples. In the discovery cohort, CD80-high patients were found to be associated with a higher response rate (AUC = 0.686). The predictive value of serum CD80 in evaluating treatment efficacy was further confirmed in the validation cohort (AUC = 0.778). Moreover, we found that patients with CD80-high were associated with higher CD8 T cells and lower neutrophils in the tumor microenvironment. During neoadjuvant treatment, iTreg infiltration and PD-L1 expression levels were significantly increased, while the MHC class I signature score was significantly decreased in patients with CD80-low.ConclusionsPeripheral blood circulating soluble CD80 protein may serve as a potential non-invasive biomarker to predict clinical benefit for esophagus cancer patients treated with neoadjuvant PD-1 blockade combined with chemotherapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14874-7.Keywords: Esophagus cancer, Neoadjuvant immunotherapy, Chemotherapy, CD80, Tumor microenvironment

Introduction

Neoadjuvant immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) combined with chemotherapy has improved the clinical outcome in patients with esophagus cancer and changed clinical treatment strategies [1–3]. Despite these advances, nearly 50% patients with esophagus cancer were still resistant to the neoadjuvant immune checkpoint inhibitors combined with chemotherapy [4]. In addition, some patients even may experience serious side effects [1, 2]. Therefore, predictive biomarkers are needed to guide personalized neoadjuvant therapy for patients with esophagus cancer.

Multiple studies have identified the association between predictive biomarkers, such as PD-L1 expression [5, 6], tumour mutational burden (TMB) [7, 8], and infiltrating lymphocytes [9, 10], and treatment outcomes of ICIs therapy. Meanwhile, machine learning-based algorithms and potential biomarkers (such as the T Cell exhaustion-related prognostic feature, the systemic immune-inflammation index, integrated machine learning and genetic algorithm-driven multiomics score, and a novel plasma cell signature) have also been reported to correlate with tumor immunotherapy efficacy [11–14]. However, the predictive performance of these predictive biomarkers remains limited, which may be due to the temporal and spatial heterogeneity of tumor tissue. More importantly, there are still limitations in the access to tumor biopsies. All of these factors limit the clinical application of these biomarkers. Therefore, identification of effective biomarkers through non-invasive methods is critical. Due to the simplicity and convenience of non-invasive collection, peripheral blood is an ideal clinical sample for exploring predictive biomarkers for precision therapy.

Currently, a number of peripheral blood biomarkers that predict the response to ICIs therapy have been reported, including circulating cells [15], lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels [16, 17], inflammatory proteins [18, 19], tumor-associated antigens [20, 21], and soluble forms of immune checkpoint molecules [22, 23]. These cells and proteins are found to be involved in the development or progression of tumors, and some of them can regulate host immune activity [24–29]. Therefore, they all have the potential to serve as predictive biomarkers for esophagus cancer patients treated with PD-1 blockade combined with chemotherapy.

In this study, the relationship between baseline complete blood count and serum proteins, including soluble forms of immune checkpoint molecules and inflammatory proteins, and the clinical outcome in esophagus cancer patients treated with PD-1 blockade combined with chemotherapy was investigated. The association of the identified protein with clinical outcome was then confirmed in the validation cohort. Furthermore, pre- and post-treatment transcriptome sequencing and PD-L1 immunohistochemistry (IHC) data were analyzed to elucidate the potential mechanisms of the identified biomarker and clinical benefit.

Materials and methods

Study population and composition

This study constitutes a translational analysis of a phase II clinical trial [3]. Between November 2019 and July 2021, a total of 60 patients were enrolled in the study. However, due to limited sample availability, marker detection could only be performed on samples from 46 of these patients. These 46 patients formed the discovery cohort for further analyses. Given the promising clinical outcomes observed in the initial cohort, an additional 28 patients were enrolled between July and December 2021 at their own request, aiming to expand patient access to the beneficial treatment regimen. These 28 newly enrolled patients were recruited under the same trial protocol [3] and constituted the validation cohort. A total of 74 esophagus cancer patients were recruited and diagnosed at the Chinese PLA General Hospital. All patients, including the newly enrolled participants, provided written informed consent. This study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Ethics Committee of the General Hospital of the People’s Liberation Army. All patients received neoadjuvant PD-1 blockade combined with chemotherapy, and surgery was performed within 4 weeks of neoadjuvant therapy. Detailed treatment regimen is as follows: The neoadjuvant therapy contains 2 cycles with 3 weeks/cycle. (1) Intravenous infusion of nanoparticle albumin-bound paclitaxel (nab-paclitaxel) on Day 1 of each cycle, the dose is calculated by body surface area for 260 mg/m2. (2) Daily oral Tiggio capsule (S-1) taken from D1 to D14 of each cycle, the dose depends on body surface area for 40–60 mg/time. (3) Intravenous infusion of Teriprizumab on Day 4 of each cycle, 240 mg/time. Furthermore, the inclusion and exclusion criteria were identical for all patients as previously described [3]. The primary inclusion criteria are as follows: (1) Age ≥ 18 years, (2) Histologically confirmed esophagus cancer, (3) No prior systemic therapy, (4) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status 0 and 1, (5) Ability to read, agree and sign informed consent forms. Patients were excluded if they (1) Aortic and tracheal invasion (T4), (2) Pregnant or breastfeeding, (3) Previously received the immunotherapy, (4) Active autoimmune disease or immunodeficiency, (5) vaccination within 30 days prior to enrolment, (6) Severe liver, heart, kidney, endocrine or circulatory disease. The detailed information on all patients was summarized in Table S1 A-B. Blood samples were collected from all patients before treatment.

Treatment and clinical outcomes evaluation

After the neoadjuvant treatment, the clinical response was evaluated according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) [30]. Patients with complete response (CR) and partial response (PR) were classified as responders, while those with stable disease (SD) and progressive disease (PD) were classified as non-responders. Clinical endpoints included progression-free survival (PFS) (defined as the time from the first treatment to disease progression, death, or the last patient contact) and overall survival (OS) (defined as the time from the first treatment to death or the last patient contact).

Clinical features for discovery and validation cohorts

From blood tests, a total of 55 features were initially collected: 6 features from complete blood count (white blood cell (WBC) count, neutrophil (NEUT) count, lymphocyte (LYM) count, monocyte (MONO) count, platelet (PLT), and hemoglobin (HGB)), 2 features from comprehensive metabolic panel (albumin (ALB), and LDH), 9 features from tumor biomarkers (AFP, CA125, CA199, CA153, CA724, CEA, NSE, cyfra21-1, and SCC), 14 features from immuno-Oncology Checkpoint 14-Plex Human ProcartaPlex™ Panel 1 (CD27, CD28, CD137 (4-1BB), GITR, HVEM, BTLA, CD80, CD152 (CTLA4), IDO, LAG-3, PD-1, PD-L1, PD-L2, and TIM-3), 14 features from immuno-Oncology Checkpoint 14-Plex Human ProcartaPlex™ Panel 2 (MICA, MICB, Perforin, ULBP-1, ULBP-3, ULBP-4, Arginase-1, CD73 (NT5E), CD96 (Tactile), E-Cadherin, Nectin-2, PVR, Siglec-7, and Siglec-9), and 10 features from TM Panel 3 (B7-H6, CD276 (B7-H3), CD47 (IAP), CD48 (BLAST-1), CD134 (OX40), Galectin-9, ICOS Ligand (B7-H2), TIMD-4, S100A8/A9, and VISTA (B7-H5)). Details were listed in Table S2.

The blood sample from each patient was divided into four portions. One portion was utilized for complete blood count, and the remaining three were processed for serum preparation. Specifically, blood samples were transferred into clot activator tubes, centrifuged at 1500 × g for 10 min, and then the serum was collected. Two of the serum samples were separately employed for the detection of metabolic indicators and tumor biomarkers, while the last one was stored at − 80 °C for the detection of other features. The complete blood cell count data, metabolic indicators, and tumor biomarkers were performed using the SYSMEX XN 9000 blood cell analyzer (Japan), Roche Cobas8000 biochemical analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany), and Roche Cobas6000 automatic electrochemiluminescence analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) immediately after blood sample collection, respectively.

To measure the serum levels of 38 soluble immune checkpoint proteins, patients’ serum samples stored in − 80 °C were pretreated after thawing (13000 rpm, 10 min at 4℃) and tested by the Bio-Plex 200 System (Bio-Rad, USA). Bio-Plex Validation Kit and Bio-Plex Calibration Kit (Bio-Rad, USA) were used to verify the system’s performance. Experimental operation was performed as previously described [31]. In brief, the gradient diluted antigen standard samples were used to generate standard curves, Bio-plex Manager 5.0 software was used to obtain samples. The serum concentrations of each protein were obtained according to the standard curves and blank controls. The optimal cutoff value derived from the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to classify patients into high and low groups.

The 14 features from the immuno-Oncology Checkpoint 14-Plex Human ProcartaPlex™ Panel 1 were performed in both discovery and validation cohorts. The remaining features were only assayed in the discovery cohort.

Public RNA-seq data collection and processing

A total of 33 raw tissue transcriptome data from 23 patients in the discovery cohort were collected from our previous study [3], comprising 23 pre-treatment and 10 post-treatment samples. After obtaining the raw sequencing data, low-quality reads and adapters were filtered out using trim galore (0.6.7). Then, the Kallisto (v0.46.2) was used to calculate the reads counts and transcripts per million (TPM) with the human reference genome (version hg38/GRCh38) [32]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the DESeq2 package [33]. Genes with |log2FoldChange| > 1 and the P-value < 0.01 were considered as significant DEGs. The KOBAS-i web tool was used to annotate the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis with a p-value < 0.05 [34].

Major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I signature and infiltrating immune cells abundance analysis

Loss or low MHC class I molecules expression on the cell surface is one of the most crucial immune-escape mechanisms in tumors, leading to reduced recognition and cytotoxicity of CD8 + T cells [35–37]. Meanwhile, MHC I molecules play a pivotal role in antigen presentation to T cells and the regulation of natural killer cells activity [35]. Therefore, evaluating the MHC class I signature in tumors is significant for assessing antigen presentation. The MHC class I signature for paired RNA data was calculated by the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) (‘GSVA’ R package) [38] using a previously described gene set [39]. The ImmuCellAI, a gene set signature-based method, was used to estimate the enrichment levels of 24 immune cell types (including 18 T-cell subtypes, B cells, natural killer cells, monocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells) [40]. Immune cell infiltration was calculated on RNA-seq data. The detailed process was carried out by using the ImmuneCellAI online tool (https://guolab.wchscu.cn/ImmuCellAI/#!/analysis).

IHC of PD-L1

PD-L1 expression data for 31 patients pre- and post-treatment in the discovery cohort were obtained from our previous study [3]. The PD-L1 combined positive score was evaluated using a PD-L1 IHC assay.

Statistical methods

R software version 4.1.2 was used to perform the statistical analyses. Continuous and categorical variables were expressed as median (range) and frequency (percentage), respectively. Comparisons between groups were performed with the Student’s t-test in discovery and validation cohorts. Fisher’s exact test was used to evaluate categorical variables in discovery and validation cohorts. The ROC curve was used to measure diagnostic accuracy, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to evaluate the discriminatory ability of the identified biomarker in the discovery and validation cohorts. The significance of PFS and OS was determined with Kaplan–Meier curve analysis in the discovery cohort. Under the assumption of missing at random, missing data were not included in the relevant analysis. For CD80-high and CD80-low patients, paired tests were performed respectively to compare the pre- and post-treatment differences in DEGs, immune cells infiltration, MHC class I signature score, and the PD-L1 expression levels detected by IHC. All statistical tests used p < 0.05 for significance and were two-sided.

Results

Overview of the study

There were two major aims in our study (Fig. 1). The first aim was to identify the peripheral blood biomarker that could predict the clinical outcome of neoadjuvant PD-1 blockade combined with chemotherapy in esophagus cancer patients. To achieve this aim, baseline blood samples from 46 esophagus cancer patients (discovery cohort) were collected. Complete blood count and serum protein levels were quantitatively measured and correlated with clinical outcomes. The identified biomarker was then confirmed in the validation cohort. The second aim was to further elucidate the potential mechanisms of the identified biomarker and clinical benefit. To accomplish this aim, pre- and post-treatment transcriptome sequencing and PD-L1 IHC data were obtained from the study we previously reported [3] and their association with the identified biomarker was analyzed.

Overview of the study

A total of 74 patients were enrolled in this study, of which 46 from our previous clinical trial [3] were considered as the discovery cohort, and 28 newly enrolled patients served as the validation cohort. The baseline characteristics of all patients were shown in Table 1 and S1. The median age for 74 patients was 62 (range 44–79) years. 89.2% of the patients were male. Most patients were stage III-IV. The Tumor grade varied between patients. The treatment efficacy and safety of 46 patients from a clinical research trial have been described in detail in our previous work [3]. For all patients enrolled in this study, the CR, PR, SD, and PD rates were 0, 62.16%, 33.78%, and 4.05%, respectively. Objective response was achieved in 62.16% of the patients. A total of 47 patients underwent surgical resection. Thirteen patients (27.66%) experienced pathological complete response and twenty-four patients (51.06%) experienced major pathological response (MPR, defined as ≤ 10% viable tumor remaining within the primary tumor bed) (Table 2). The estimated six and twelve- month of OS and PFS rates were 97.3%, 71.62%, 87.84%, and 63.51%, respectively (Table S1).

Clinical outcome for 74 patients

Baseline CD80 level was associated with clinical outcome in the discovery cohort

To identify the peripheral blood biomarkers associated with treatment efficacy, 55 biological features including 38 soluble immune checkpoint proteins, were quantitatively measured before the neoadjuvant treatment in the discovery cohort (Fig. 2A and Table S3). Among them, the detection values of five features (including HVEM, ULBP-1, ULBP-3, B7-H6, and TIMD-4) were lower than the detection limit of the standard material in most samples (> 93%). Therefore, these five features were excluded from further analysis. Subsequently, the association of each marker with radiological response was analyzed. It was found that the expression levels of CD80 and GITR were significantly higher in responders. The expression level of OX40 showed an upward trend in responders, while CA153 displayed a downward trend in the same group, without reaching statistical significance (Fig. 2B). Then each marker was classified as high or low according to the median value, and its response rate was explored. The significant difference in response rate was found only between CD80-high and CD80-low patients (Fig. 2C). However, no significant difference was found in MPR rate between CD80-high and CD80-low patients (Figure S1). Furthermore, to analyze the performance of the baseline CD80 level to predict the response, we carried out the ROC analysis and found that the value of AUC was 0.686 in the discovery cohort (Fig. 2D). Compared to CD80-low patients, the PFS and OS were longer in CD80-high patients (Fig. 2E and F). These results indicated the potential predictive value of serum CD80 level.

Confirming the association of CD80 with radiological response in the validation cohort

To further confirm the predictive value of peripheral CD80 level, baseline serum samples collected from 28 esophagus cancer patients treated with neoadjuvant PD-1 blockade combined with chemotherapy served as a validation cohort. The measurement of CD80 protein was conducted using the identical method employed for the discovery cohort (Table S4, only CD80 level was presented). The level of CD80 was significantly higher in responders than in non-responders (Fig. 3A). According to the same cutoff value as the discovery cohort, the esophagus cancer patients in the validation cohort were divided into high and low groups. Improved response rate was observed in CD80-high patients (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, the ROC analysis was performed and the value of AUC was 0.778 (Fig. 3C). These results further confirmed the potential of serum CD80 protein in evaluating treatment efficacy.

Patients with CD80-high were associated with higher CD8 T cell and lower neutrophil before the treatment

To investigate whether peripheral CD80 protein was correlated with the immune status in the tumor microenvironment, the pre-treatment RNA sequencing data from 23 esophagus cancer patients in the discovery cohort were obtained from our previous study [3] (Table S5). The significantly up-regulated and down-regulated genes in CD80-high patients were 199 and 123, respectively (Fig. 4A). These significant DEGs were found to be associated with the signaling pathway of immunity (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, the abundance of immune cells was evaluated (Fig. 4C). It was found that CD8 T cells were significantly higher, while the neutrophils were significantly lower in CD80-high patients (Fig. 4D). In addition, the T helper 2 cells (Th2) were higher in CD80-low patients (Fig. 4D). Previous studies indicated that the expression of HLA genes and PD-L1 have an impact on the clinical outcome [41–44]. However, in our study, MHC Class I signature scores and PD-L1 expression were not significantly different at baseline between CD80-high and CD80-low patients (Table S1, Figure S2A and S2B).

Analysis of the dynamic changes in the tumor microenvironment revealed that PD-L1 is elevated in CD80-low patients after neoadjuvant therapy

To study the changes of tumor microenvironment (TME) during the neoadjuvant therapy, RNA sequencing and PD-L1 IHC data pre- and post-treatment were collected (Tables S1 and S5). The significantly DEGs in CD80-high and CD80-low patients were 389 and 914, respectively (Figure S3A and S3B). Among them, 133 genes were shared in CD80-high and CD80-low patients (Fig. 5A). These 133 genes were mainly related to metabolism signaling pathways (Fig. 5B). In addition, the uniquely regulated DEGs in CD80-high and CD80-low patients were related to the immune signaling pathways (Fig. 5C and D). These results suggest that the neoadjuvant treatment can modulate the metabolism and immune status of TME. Interestingly, the PD-L1 expression and PD-1 checkpoint pathway exhibited enrichment in both CD80-high and CD80-low patients by functional enrichment analysis. Subsequently, we checked the alteration of gene expression levels within this pathway in samples from the CD80-high (Figs. 5E) and CD80-low (Figs. 5F) patients. In the CD80-high patients, the expression levels of BATF2 and CD274 were decreased post-treatment, while FOS was increased. In contrast, the expression levels of MAP2K3, BATF, PPP3CC, AKT3, and ZAP70 within this pathway were increased post-treatment in the CD80-low patients. In addition, we found that the dysregulated genes in CD80-low patients were frequently involved in immunosuppressive signaling pathways, such as tryptophan metabolism (Fig. 5G) and Wnt signaling pathway (Fig. 5H) [45–51]. For example, in the Wnt signaling pathway, seven genes (MAPK9, PRKACA, RSPO3, PPP3CC, CAMK2G, CCND2, CCND3) were significantly increased post-treatment. Meanwhile, among the three genes (DKK4, SOST, and WNT11) that were significantly decreased post-treatment, DKK4 and SOST were found to negatively regulate the Wnt signaling pathway (Fig. 5H) [52, 53]. Additionally, we further analyzed the changes of infiltrating immune cells pre/post-treatment in the CD80-high and CD80-low patients. As shown in Fig. 5I and J, although CD8 and CD4 T lymphocytes were significantly increased post-treatment in CD80-low patients, immunosuppressive cells induced regulatory T (iTreg) cells were also significantly increased. Moreover, the MHC class I signaling pathway score was significantly decreased in CD80-low patients (Fig. 5K). Based on the PD-L1 IHC analysis, we further confirmed that PD-L1 expression was significantly increased in patients with CD80-low post-treatment (Fig. 5L). The above results suggested that the PD-L1/PD-1 checkpoint pathway and the Wnt signaling pathway exhibited varying degrees of dysregulation after neoadjuvant therapy, and PD-L1 showed a significant increase in CD80-low patients post-treatment.

Discussion

Compared to traditional biomarkers, such as TMB and PD-L1 expression status, serum biomarkers are a promising alternative to address the shortcomings associated with the analysis of tumor tissue. In this study, 55 features (including 38 serum immune checkpoint proteins) were detected, and their association with clinical outcomes in esophagus cancer patients treated with neoadjuvant PD-1 blockade combined with chemotherapy was analyzed. In the discovery cohort, CD80 was identified as a predictive factor of radiological response and PFS. To further strengthen this finding, the association of serum CD80 level with radiological response was confirmed in the validation cohort. Compared with CD80-low patients, CD80-high patients were associated with increased CD8 T cells and decreased neutrophils at baseline. During neoadjuvant therapy, iTreg infiltration and PD-L1 expression levels were significantly increased, while MHC Class I signature score was significantly decreased in CD80-low patients.

Several studies have reported the predictive biomarkers in esophagus cancer patients treated with immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy, such as PD-L1 expression and tumor infiltrating lymphocytes [3, 54, 55]. However, serum test has significant advantages over tumor tissue tests, such as accessibility and reproducibility. In addition, serum test can avoid the issue of intratumor heterogeneity in patients. In our previous study, serum IFN-γ, LAMP3, and Gal.1 were found to be associated with the clinical outcomes in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma patients treated with toripalimab plus nab-paclitaxel and S-1 [3]. However, their predictive value was not validated in another independent cohort. In this study, the association of serum protein CD80 with clinical outcome in esophagus cancer patients treated with neoadjuvant PD-1 blockade combined with chemotherapy was identified in the discovery cohort (AUC = 0.686) and further confirmed in the validation cohort (AUC = 0.778). Therefore, high serum CD80 protein level could be a predictive biomarker in esophagus cancer patients treated with neoadjuvant PD-1 blockade combined with chemotherapy, although this finding warrants further confirmation in independent cohorts.

T lymphocyte is regulated by different checkpoint molecules at the immunological synapse including CD80 [56]. CD80 is also known as B7-1, which is mainly expressed on antigen-presenting cells, such as dendritic cells, activated B cells, and monocytes [57]. The binding of CD80 to CD28 expressed by T lymphocytes trigger a co-stimulatory signal for T lymphocytes activation and survival [58]. Interestingly, in addition to providing the co-stimulatory signal through CD80-CD28 interaction, soluble CD80 can also neutralize immunosuppression mediated by PD-1-PD-L1 interaction and is more effective than PD-L1 or PD-1 antibodies in maintaining the production of IFNγ by PD-1(+) activated T lymphocytes [59]. Therefore, soluble CD80 may be more effective in promoting anti-tumor immunity than these checkpoint antibodies. In our study, patients with CD80-high were found to be associated with better clinical outcomes. At baseline, the CD8 T cells were higher in CD80-high patients, which may allow these patients to benefit from neoadjuvant PD-1 blockade combined with chemotherapy. In addition, iTreg and PD-L1 were significantly increased and MHC Class I signature scores were significantly decreased in CD80-low patients. These immunosuppressive factors and impaired antigen presentation may result in T cells not being effectively activated, thus promoting the immunosuppressive microenvironment.

To explore the relationship between biomarkers and prognosis and uncover the underlying molecular mechanisms, we applied a median-based stratification approach. Initially, we attempted quartile-based stratification but found it less effective in distinguishing treatment outcomes. Additionally, we observed that the median value was highly consistent with the cutoff derived from the ROC curve. Through comprehensive evaluations of multiple methods, we ultimately adopted the median threshold in our study.

There were several limitations in our study. First, the sample size in both the discovery and validation cohorts was relatively small, particularly for the RNA-seq data. Therefore, the obtained results were suggestive, which need to be further validated in larger, more diverse cohorts to ensure robustness and generalizability. Second, the follow-up period in the validation cohort was short and the correlation between CD80 with PFS and OS in the validation cohort could not be analyzed, which will be timely reported in the future.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that serum CD80 level was associated with clinical outcome in esophagus cancer patients treated with neoadjuvant PD-1 blockade combined with chemotherapy. Furthermore, the tumor microenvironment of patients with CD80-high and CD80-low at baseline and during treatment was elucidated. These results suggested that CD80 may be a predictive biomarker for esophagus cancer patients treated with neoadjuvant PD-1 blockade combined with chemotherapy, and targeting CD80 may represent a promising strategy to improve the efficacy of immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy in esophagus cancer patients. However, more prospective studies are needed to further validate these findings.
