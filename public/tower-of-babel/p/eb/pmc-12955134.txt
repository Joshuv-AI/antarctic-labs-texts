Privileged structure-based molecular fingerprints for organic electronic materials: towards intuitive machine learning interpretation
Authors: Tae Wook Yang, Seung Hyun Jo, Min Chul Suh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Molecular descriptors are central to the performance and interpretability of QSPR models, yet most existing fingerprints for organic electronics lack chemical relevance or interpretability. Here, we present the Organic Electronic Fingerprint (OEFP), a structure-based representation tailored for OLED and OPV materials. OEFP was constructed from a manually curated OLED dataset and publicly available OPV and chromophore datasets to ensure structural diversity. Synthetically accessible substructures were identified using fragmentation and ring decomposition methods, which capture the conjugated π-bonds crucial for organic electronic materials, and subsequently encoded as individual bits. In case studies of OPV HOMO energy prediction, OEFP achieved up to 13.7% lower MAE and 1.6% higher R2 compared to domain-mismatched structure-based fingerprint under random splits. Under scaffold-based splits designed to assess generalization, OEFP demonstrated substantially improved generalization, reducing MAE by 50–70% and increasing R2 by over 150% relative to the same baseline and achieved performance comparable to ECFP baselines evaluated at a similar fingerprint length when using count-based representations. SHAP analysis further enabled intuitive substructure-level interpretation, while OEFP’s chemically meaningful and synthetically relevant design supports rational molecular generation. Together, these results establish OEFP as an effective and interpretable molecular representation for machine learning applications in organic electronics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13321-026-01169-7.

Introduction

In recent years, machine learning (ML) has emerged as a powerful tool in chemistry and materials science, accelerating workflows in property prediction [1], virtual screening [2], and even inverse molecular design using generative models such as Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) [3, 4]. These advancements are reshaping how researchers navigate chemical space and identify candidates with targeted functionalities. In the domain of organic electronics, ML has gained considerable traction for predicting key molecular properties that influence device performance. Notable efforts have focused on estimating parameters such as highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO), lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy levels [1], photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) [5], bond dissociation energy (BDE) [6], power conversion efficiency (PCE) [7], and TADF (thermally activated delayed fluorescence) characteristics [2, 8]. These approaches have demonstrated the potential of ML to predict material properties rather than measuring or calculating and support the rational design of high-performance OLED and OPV materials. To enable such predictions, a variety of molecular representations have been explored, including molecular graphs [9], physicochemical descriptors [10], and molecular fingerprints such as the Extended-Connectivity Fingerprint (ECFP) [11]. While these representations enable accurate predictive models, descriptors such as KRFP, which are not domain-specific, or ECFP, which lack explicit structural meaning, fall short in interpretability. As a result, even when the model performs well, it becomes difficult to extract chemically meaningful insights from the output, limiting their utility for rational molecular design. For example, in extended-connectivity fingerprints (ECFP), a single bit may correspond to multiple, structurally distinct substructures depending on the molecular context. Moreover, these substructures are not explicitly defined or synthetically viable, hindering their use in downstream tasks such as rational molecule generation or modification. As a result, it becomes difficult to attribute model predictions to specific chemical motifs. In addition, general-purpose fingerprints [12, 13] tend to overlook domain-specific features such as conjugated cores or donor, acceptor moieties prevalent in organic electronic materials.

Recent high-profile studies on OLED and organic photodetector (OPD) molecules have highlighted the limitations of current molecular representations. For instance, a state-of-the-art model for predicting BDEs in OLEDs employed a VAE-based active learning loop with DFT calculations to achieve high accuracy [6]. However, it relied on global chemical descriptors—such as electrotopological-state (E-state) indices, cheminformatics descriptors, and Extended Topochemical Atom (ETA) descriptors—that offer limited interpretability and are not readily applicable to structure-based molecular design. Similarly, a frontier orbital prediction model [1] adopted Klekota-Roth fingerprints (KRFPs) [14]—originally developed for bioactivity prediction—as input features to model HOMO and LUMO energy levels. While predictive performance was strong, the domain relevance of the descriptors was limited, reducing their utility in interpreting results for organic electronic systems. These examples highlight the urgent need for a domain-specific fingerprint that bridges chemical intuition and machine learning in organic electronics, making it difficult to translate ML model predictions into actionable molecular design insights. Previous studies have attempted to construct molecular fingerprints using polymer units to analyze structural features that influence target properties such as power conversion efficiency (PCE) [15], and to design high-performing molecules based on these insights [7]. This highlights the need to develop domain-specific fingerprints that effectively capture the characteristic structural motifs of organic electronic materials. To address these limitations, we propose the Organic Electronic Fingerprint (OEFP), a structure-based, 5,363-bit fixed-length molecular fingerprint specifically designed for OLED and OPV materials (Fig. 1). OEFP encodes synthetically accessible, chemically meaningful, and optoelectronically relevant substructures derived from a curated dataset comprising 864 OLED molecules, approximately 2.3 million OPV candidates [16], and 6,865 chromophores [17, 18]. Using the Breaking of Retrosynthetically Interesting Chemical Substructures (BRICS) fragmentation algorithm [19], we extracted frequently observed fragments and developed an additional algorithm to identify unit rings from fused ring clusters, accounting for the importance of π-conjugation in optoelectronic performance. These structural motifs were then mapped to individual fingerprint bits, allowing for both predictive accuracy and interpretability. By bridging machine learning with chemical intuition, OEFP enables transparent feature attribution (e.g., via SHAP analysis [20]), facilitates rational design, and integrates seamlessly with generative workflows. Dataset

OLED

First and foremost, for OLED materials—our primary area of interest—publicly available datasets containing widely used OLED compounds are scarce. To address this, we manually curated a comprehensive dataset with 864 molecules by extracting information from the literature. This includes widely used host [21] and dopant materials [22] across the red, green, and blue emission spectrum, as well as representative materials used in common layers such as ETL (Electron Transport Layer) [23, 24], EIL (Electron Injection Layer) [25], HTL (Hole Transport Layer) [26], and HIL (Hole Injection Layer) [27]. Furthermore, we incorporated TADF (thermally activated delayed fluorescence) materials, including both donor–acceptor (DA) types [28] and multi-resonance (MR) types, which have significantly contributed to improvements in OLED efficiency and lifetime. The curated molecular structures were standardized and converted into Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System (SMILES) notation to enable consistent fragment extraction and efficient fingerprint generation. SMILES is widely used because it simplifies substructure matching and is compatible with most cheminformatics tools [29–31].

OPV

For OPV materials, we utilized the publicly available dataset from the Harvard Clean Energy Project Database (CEPDB) [16], one of the largest and most sophisticated computational screening efforts in organic electronics. The dataset contains approximately 2.3 million unique molecules generated through combinatorial assembly using 26 carefully selected molecular fragments. These fragments were linked and fused following chemical rules to yield small-molecule OPV candidates and oligomeric building blocks, creating a vast and diverse chemical space. What sets CEPDB apart is not only the scale of molecular coverage but also the rigorous computational strategy employed. The molecules were subjected to a multi-level screening pipeline involving quantum chemical calculations, cheminformatics descriptors, and property-based filtering. The CEPDB leveraged distributed computing via IBM’s World Community Grid [32] to achieve this unprecedented scale. The molecular candidates were assessed for key photovoltaic properties such as HOMO–LUMO gap, open-circuit voltage (VOC), and power conversion efficiency (PCE) using both first-principles calculations and machine learning models. Among these, high-efficiency candidates were prioritized for further study and database integration. The breadth, quality, and mechanistic relevance of this dataset make it well-suited for training and validating OEFP fingerprints in the context of OPV research. By applying OEFP to this dataset, we aim to extract domain-specific substructures with clear synthetic relevance and electronic function, ensuring the resulting fingerprint is robust, interpretable, and scalable across a wide range of OPV chemistries.

Chromophore

To further expand the dataset, we incorporated a comprehensive set of chromophore molecules reported by Joung et al. [17, 18], which added approximately 6,865 SMILES entries. These combined sources provide a robust and diverse representation of organic electronic molecular space [1].

Results and analysis

Fragmentation – BRICS algorithm

We employed the BRICS (Breaking of Retrosynthetically Interesting Chemical Substructures) algorithm [19] to fragment molecules into synthetically accessible units at chemically relevant disconnection points (Fig. 2a,b). To enhance the generalizability and reusability of resulting fragments, all BRICS-specific atom labels were replaced with a universal wildcard "[!#1]" during the SMARTS [33, 34] conversion process. This representation does not indicate the specific atom to which a bond was broken; rather, it conveys the common information that a bond was cleaved at that position. By abstracting away atom-specific details, the resulting fingerprint fragments avoid over-specialization and remain broadly applicable across diverse molecules. Following fragmentation, we compiled a frequency distribution of all generated fragments across the dataset and selected the top-N [14] most frequently occurring fragments. These fragments were then assigned to individual bits in the OEFP vector. This selection process ensures that OEFP captures the most structurally and functionally relevant motifs common to OLED and OPV molecules.

Importantly, the rationale behind using BRICS and selecting retrosynthetically accessible fragments extends beyond prediction tasks. OEFP was also designed with generative modeling in mind. When integrated with frameworks such as Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) [3, 4], the use of BRICS-derived substructures enables molecule generation that is not only chemically reasonable but also practically synthesizable. This aligns with the broader direction of data-driven de novo molecular design [35], where interpretability and synthetic accessibility are increasingly emphasized. This design choice is supported by prior work in drug discovery, where BRICS has been shown to outperform classical RECAP fragmentation [36] in reconstructing high-quality, 'drug-like' molecules with greater chemical coverage and diversity. As demonstrated in Degen et al., the BRICS framework offers a more sophisticated model of fragmentation and recombination and generates fragment spaces that preserve both synthetic feasibility and chemical complexity. By encoding OEFP with BRICS-based units, we simultaneously enhance model interpretability, synthetic relevance, and generative potential—key qualities for any molecular representation intended to bridge predictive modeling and chemical discovery.

BRICS alone could not fully capture the wide diversity of ring systems present in organic electronic materials, as complex fragments often emerged as combinations of multiple rings. By decomposing these fused clusters into their constituent rings, our approach ensures a lossless representation of the fundamental ring motifs, enabling OEFP to better account for the structural features critical to OLED and OPV materials.

Composition of OEFP

To construct the Organic Electronic Fingerprint (OEFP), we extracted molecular fragments using the BRICS algorithm from three representative datasets: OLED (462 fragments), OPV (605,747 fragments), and chromophore molecules (4,050 fragments). Our goal was to capture the structural diversity of organic electronic materials while keeping the fingerprint bit length comparable to existing fingerprints. Therefore, we selected top-ranked frequent fragments from each dataset while maintaining domain-specific proportional representation.

For the OLED dataset, which had the fewest fragments, we included all fragments with a frequency of 1 or higher to ensure adequate representation of molecular features (Fig. 3d). In the OPV dataset, we set the cutoff at a frequency of 32. This threshold captured 3,188 fragments—beyond which the fragment count increased sharply (e.g., 24,899 fragments at 31), rendering the fingerprint impractically long (Fig. 3e). To confirm that this frequency cutoff appropriately balances feature diversity and dimensional efficiency, we additionally compared predictive performance at two fragment counts (375 bits @ cutoff = 35 and 3,188 bits @ cutoff = 32) across both the CEPDB 10 k and National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) OPV 81 k datasets (Table. S1) [39, 40]. Similarly, for the chromophore dataset, we included fragments with a frequency of 2 or higher, resulting in 1175 fragments. Including single-occurrence fragments would have added 2,875 additional entries, again leading to excessive fingerprint length (Fig. 3f). In addition to BRICS fragments, we incorporated decomposed fused rings into OEFP—112, 73, and 641 ring fragments from the OLED, OPV, and chromophore datasets, respectively (Fig. 3b). Because ring fragments are fewer in number yet crucial for electronic properties, all were retained without frequency filtering. As a result of frequency-based selection, 602,559 OPV fragments and 2,875 chromophore fragments were excluded from OEFP. To ensure that the selected features still captured the key structural characteristics of the excluded low-frequency fragments, we performed a detailed atom-level coverage analysis. For each excluded fragment, we counted the number of non-hydrogen atoms and calculated the proportion of atoms that were matched by any of the 5,363 OEFP bits. Although described as atom-level matching, this effectively reflects subgraph-level correspondence, indicating how completely each functional moiety or structural motif is represented within OEFP.

As shown in Fig. 3c, for the OPV dataset, among 602,559 excluded fragments, 99.91% (602,006) were fully covered, and an additional 0.09% (553) exhibited coverage above 90%. For the more diverse chromophore dataset, 64.97% (1,868 of 2,875) of excluded fragments were fully covered and 85.94% (2,471) exceeded 90% coverage, while 98.57% (2,834) retained coverage over 50%. These results demonstrate that OEFP faithfully represents both frequent and rare structural motifs.

Together, the frequency-based optimization (validated in Table. S1) and the subgraph-level coverage analysis confirm that the 32 cutoff yields a compact yet chemically comprehensive fingerprint. To further examine whether a purely frequency-based fragment selection may discard rare but informative motifs, we performed an additional analysis on the NREL OPV dataset. Since the OPV fingerprint construction applies a frequency cutoff of 32, we specifically investigated whether fragments below this threshold contain low-frequency yet high-informativeness motifs that could materially affect model performance. We evaluated fragments with low frequencies using simple univariate association metrics between fragment presence and the target property, including the absolute difference in conditional means, absolute t-statistics, and effect-size–like correlation measures. This analysis was designed to identify fragments that, despite low occurrence, show a strong statistical association with the target. Based on this criterion, we identified a small subset of 22 fragments exhibiting relatively high informativeness scores. Notably, these fragments were not extremely rare. All selected motifs fall within a narrow frequency range (approximately 29–31 occurrences), and no fragments with substantially lower frequencies showed strong univariate associations. We then augmented the original OEFP representation with these 22 fragments and systematically evaluated the impact on predictive performance under both random and scaffold-based splits.

The results show that incorporating these fragments does not lead to a consistent improvement in model performance. Under random splits (Fig. S10a), prediction errors increased and R2 decreased, while under scaffold splits (Fig. S10b), only marginal and non-systematic changes were observed. Overall, the performance variations were small and dependent on the splitting strategy, indicating no robust benefit from explicitly including these low-frequency fragments. These results suggest that, for the OPV datasets considered here, the number of rare yet highly informative fragments below the frequency cutoff is limited, and their inclusion does not materially alter predictive performance. Accordingly, frequency-based fragment selection offers a practical balance between coverage and dimensional efficiency, while preserving the essential chemical features required for robust and interpretable machine-learning-based property prediction.

Validation via machine learning models — OPV

In machine learning for molecular property prediction, the choice of molecular descriptors and the size of the training dataset critically affect model performance. To assess the robustness and generalizability of OEFP across different data regimes, we randomly extracted subsamples of 1 k and 10 k molecules from the NREL OPV database and utilized the full 81 k training dataset. This design enabled a consistent evaluation of OEFP under varying data scales.

Before reporting model performance on the NREL benchmark, we assessed whether NREL can be treated as an external evaluation set relative to CEPDB. An exact SMILES match between the full CEPDB and NREL databases found only four duplicated molecules, suggesting negligible explicit leakage from identical structures. We further quantified molecular-level similarity using Morgan fingerprints (4,096 bits, radius = 2) and Tanimoto similarity. Because an exhaustive CEPDB–NREL all-vs-all comparison is computationally infeasible, we compared 81 k NREL molecules against a random subset of 200 k CEPDB molecules and recorded, for each NREL molecule, the maximum similarity value (Table. S2). The similarity distribution is centered at low values (mean 0.26; median 0.25), with p95 and p99 of 0.39 and 0.47, respectively, and fewer than 1% of NREL molecules showing a maximum similarity above 0.5. Together, these results indicate that most NREL molecules are only moderately similar to the CEPDB corpus at the molecular level, supporting the use of NREL as a practically external benchmark despite partial overlap in scaffold space.

For comparison, in addition to binary and count-based OEFP, we included KRFP (4,860 bits; privileged substructure-based, interpretable but not domain-specific) and ECFP4/6 (Extended-Connectivity Fingerprints with radii of 2 and 3, respectively), which capture general atomic environments irrespective of material domain. The bit length of 4096 was selected to maintain dimensional consistency with OEFP (5,363 bits) and KRFP (4,860 bits). All fingerprints were evaluated using an identical XGBoost regression framework, optimized via Bayesian optimization for six hyperparameters, ensuring a fair comparison of descriptive performance.

To test scalability, models were trained on datasets containing 1 k, 10 k, and 81 k molecules. The 1 k datasets (five random samples) were evaluated via fivefold cross-validation for statistical robustness, whereas the 10 k datasets (five random samples) and the 81 k dataset used an 8:2 train–test split shared across all fingerprints. For the full dataset, bootstrap resampling (1,000 samples × 5,000 iterations) was applied to the 16,000 test molecules to estimate the 95% confidence interval (Table. S3, Fig. S1-S8). An Message Passing Neural Network (MPNN) [41, 42] model was additionally trained on the 81 k dataset to serve as a deep-learning baseline [43, 44].

For the smallest dataset (1 k molecules) (Fig. 4a, d), OEFP reduced MAE by 4–5% relative to KRFP and achieved comparable R2 gains. Although ECFP4/6 achieved slightly lower MAE (0.139–0.120 eV), OEFP (count) retained competitive accuracy (0.141 eV) while offering domain-specific interpretability.

In the 10 k datasets (Fig 4b, e), OEFP again outperformed KRFP (ΔMAE = 1.3–9.1%, ΔR2 = + 0.9–1.6%) and remained close to ECFP (MAE = 0.091 eV vs 0.086–0.089 eV), demonstrating that OEFP preserves chemical relevance even with fewer bits.

For the 81 k dataset (Fig 4c, f), OEFP (count) maintained its lead over KRFP (count) with a 13% lower MAE (0.051 vs 0.059 eV) and higher R2 (0.971 vs 0.961). While ECFP yielded marginally better values (MAE ≈ 0.041 eV, R2 ≈ 0.982), OEFP (count) achieved 96–98% of ECFP’s predictive performance with substantially higher interpretability.

The graph-based MPNN achieved MAE = 0.037 eV, R2 = 0.987, validating its expected superiority in expressive power. When OEFP was used in the binary format, its performance lagged behind MPNN by 0.08 (MAE) and 0.112 (R2), whereas in the count format, the gap narrowed to only 0.014 eV (MAE) and 0.016 (R2). This indicates that count encoding enables OEFP to approach deep-learning accuracy while preserving interpretability and low computational cost.

Model performance on the NREL OPV dataset is primarily discussed using random data splits to enable direct comparison with prior studies. Under a random 8:2 split, Bemis–Murcko scaffold analysis revealed substantial overlap between the training and test sets, with 4,571 shared scaffolds out of 5,157 test scaffolds, corresponding to an overlap ratio of 0.89 and a Jaccard index of 0.48. To assess robustness under scaffold shifts, we therefore additionally performed a scaffold-based evaluation using an 8:1:1 scaffold split with no scaffold overlap, with the results summarized in Fig. S9. Under this scaffold-based split, performance differences became more pronounced. OEFP retained reasonable accuracy (MAE = 0.132/0.078 and R2 = 0.831/0.925 for binary/count), whereas KRFP showed a severe degradation, while ECFP4/6 showed slightly higher predictive performance. Notably, although the OEFP–ECFP gap increased relative to random splits, the OEFP–KRFP gap widened much more substantially, indicating that OEFP generalizes more robustly to unseen scaffolds. We attribute this behavior to the fact that OEFP is constructed from OPV-relevant substructures mined from the target domain, enabling transfer across different scaffold topologies.

Collectively, these findings confirm that OEFP provides a balanced fingerprint representation, combining predictive accuracy, structural interpretability, and scalability, particularly effective for organic electronic materials, where repeated or symmetric motifs make count-based encoding crucial for accurate property prediction. By design, OEFP is a substructure-based molecular representation that emphasizes local chemical environments, including heteroatom identity and position, short-range ring connectivity, and local bonding motifs. Accordingly, the OLED, OPV, and chromophore datasets considered in this work fall within the intended scope of OEFP, as their electronic properties are largely governed by such local structural features, whereas effects arising purely from very long-range conjugation or extended fused-ring delocalization are not explicitly encoded.

Conclusion

The Organic Electronic Fingerprint (OEFP) provides a domain-specific representation tailored for organic electronic materials, simultaneously achieving predictive accuracy and structural interpretability, thereby establishing its academic significance. Empirical validation across OPV datasets of varying sizes demonstrated that OEFP achieved up to 13.7% lower MAE and 1.6% higher R2 compared to conventional fingerprint such as KRFP, while an OLED case study confirmed its ability to provide intuitive interpretation of structure–property relationships through SHAP analysis, yielding direct insights into molecular design. At the same time, because OEFP is constructed from synthetically accessible substructures, it offers practical extensibility toward computer- and AI-assisted molecular design, providing a pathway to accelerated discovery of high-performance OLED and OPV materials.

Mean absolute error (MAE)

MAE [45] measures the average magnitude of errors by calculating the average absolute difference between actual and predicted values. Unlike mean squared error (MSE) [46] or root mean squared error (RMSE) [47], which square the errors and therefore emphasize larger deviations, MAE evaluates prediction errors linearly, making it easier to intuitively interpret how far, on average, the predictions deviate from the actual values. This makes MAE particularly useful when a straightforward assessment of model accuracy is desired. Where n is the total number of samples, yᵢ represents the actual value for the i-th sample, ŷᵢ is the predicted value for the i-th sample, and ȳ is the mean of the actual values. The numerator, Σ (yᵢ—ŷᵢ)2, is called the residual sum of squares (RSS), which measures the total squared error between predictions and actual values. The denominator, Σ (yᵢ—ȳ)2, is the total sum of squares (TSS), representing the total variance in the actual values. R2 quantifies how well the model explains the variance in the outcome variable. An R2 of 1 indicates perfect prediction, meaning all predicted values exactly match the actual values. An R2 of 0 implies that the model does no better than simply predicting the mean of the actual values for all observations.

SHAP method

The SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) [20] is a unified framework for interpreting machine learning model predictions based on Shapley values from cooperative game theory. It quantifies the contribution of each feature to the final prediction by fairly distributing the prediction difference across all features.8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Phi_{i} \, = \,\sum\nolimits_{{S \subseteq N\backslash \left\{ i \right\}}} {\frac{{\left| S \right|\,!\left( {\left| N \right|\, - \left| S \right|\, - \,1} \right)\,!}}{\left| N \right|\,!\,}}$$\end{document}Φi=∑S⊆N\iS!N-S-1!N!

The SHAP value фi represents the average marginal contribution of feature i to the model’s prediction across all possible subsets S of the remaining features (excluding i). Specifically, for each subset S, the model’s prediction is computed both with and without feature i, and the difference f(S ∪ {i})—f(S) indicates how much adding feature i changes the output. This difference is then weighted by the term (|S|! × (M—|S|—1)!) / M!, where M is the total number of features and |S| is the size of the subset S. The weighted contributions across all subsets are then summed to produce the SHAP value фi.

Message passing neural network

To evaluate the structure-based predictive performance of graph neural networks, we trained a message-passing neural network (MPNN) implemented with the PyTorch Geometric framework (v2.5). All molecules were converted from SMILES strings to molecular graphs, where each atom was represented by a 4-dimensional feature vector [Z, degree, formal charge, aromaticity], and each covalent bond was described by its bond-order type. A Graph Isomorphism Network (GIN) architecture was adopted to perform neighborhood aggregation, consisting of three GINConv layers with learnable multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs), followed by a global mean-pooling operation to generate graph-level embeddings. The pooled embeddings were processed through two fully connected layers (128 → 64 → 1) with ReLU activation and a dropout rate of 0.1. The dataset (81,000 OPV molecules) was randomly divided into training, validation, and test subsets with an 8:1:1 ratio. Training was performed using the Adam optimizer (learning rate = 1 × 10⁻3, batch size = 64) for 300 epochs with an L1 loss function. Model performance was evaluated using mean absolute error (MAE), root-mean-square error (RMSE), coefficient of determination (R2), and Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r). The model achieving the lowest validation MAE was saved and used for final test evaluation. All experiments were conducted with a fixed random seed (42) to ensure reproducibility. The complete training and inference pipeline, including SMILES-to-graph conversion, data splitting, model configuration, and checkpoint saving, was implemented in Python (PyTorch 2.8.0, RDKit 2023.09, CUDA-enabled GPU).
