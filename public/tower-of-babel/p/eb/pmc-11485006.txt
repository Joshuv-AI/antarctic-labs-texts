Relevance of red blood cell Lithium concentration in the management of Lithium-treated bipolar and unipolar disorders: a systematic narrative review
Authors: Manon Coyac, Lynn Jalabert, Xavier Declèves, Bruno Etain, Frank Bellivier
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite a variability in response and a narrow therapeutic index, Lithium (Li) remains the gold standard treatment for bipolar disorders (BD), and a treatment of choice for unipolar disorders (UD). Red blood cell Li concentration (RBCLiC) and red blood cell/plasma Li ratio (LiR) have been studied in many areas of mood disorders (such as acute or chronic Li efficacy, adherence, side effects (SE), intoxication management) as well as in several research domains. This systematic review aims to synthesize the existing literature.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review, based on preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and Metanalysis (PRISMA) guidelines, of articles published between 1972 and February 2023, indexed in the following databases: EMBASE, MEDLINE, Cochrane Library. The search terms were combinations of the following headings: “Lithium AND Plasma AND Erythrocyte AND Mood disorders”. The systematic review protocol was published to PROSPERO (CRD42023406154).Results and conclusionOut of the 252 identified studies, 57 met the selection criteria. The articles investigated the interest of RBCLiC and other blood parameters (PLiC and LiR) in various areas: (i) disease management (31 articles) (compliance/adherence (5 articles), SE/toxicity (13 articles), prediction of Li response/therapeutic efficacy for acute episode or for relapse prevention (17 articles)), (ii) Li blood parameters as trait markers of mood disorders subtypes (UD, BDI, BDII) (16 articles), (iii) Li blood parameters as state markers of mood episodes (11 articles), (iv) factors influencing Li blood parameters (age, gender, ethnicity, dosage and duration of Li treatment, co-medications with other treatments, seasonality) associated with RBCLiC or LiR (24 articles), and (v) potential pathophysiological mechanisms (30 articles).ConclusionOverall, this review suggests that RBCLiC or LiR could be of interest for tolerance monitoring. However, the heterogeneity of methods and results, coupled with the limited amount of data, does not allow clear conclusions to be drawn in the other areas explored in this literature review. Given the potential interest in exploring brain Li pharmacokinetics (PK)s, this review calls for further research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40345-024-00356-5.

Introduction

Mood disorders are recognized as prevalent, severe and burdensome psychiatric illnesses (GBD 2017 Disease and Injury Incidence and Prevalence Collaborators 2018) and represent a significant public health challenge due to their association with premature morbidity and mortality, including suicide. Lithium (Li) is the gold standard treatment for bipolar disorders (BD) (Cousins et al. 2020). It is also a treatment of choice for other mood disorders (Young 2017), associated with a decreased risk of suicide (Lewitzka et al. 2015). Current recommendations advise monitoring plasma Li concentrations (PLiC) to achieve target concentrations, which can widely differ among individuals (Nolen et al. 2019). Close monitoring of PLiC is essential for dose adjustments, prevention of side effects (SE) and toxicity, search of potential drug interactions, and adherence monitoring. Some studies suggest that the red blood cell concentration (RBCLiC) may exhibit a stronger correlation with brain Li concentration than with PLiC (Frazer et al. 1978, 1973), suggesting that RBCLiC could be more relevant to monitor the therapeutic/toxic effects. However, the recent guidelines for managing mood disorders treated with Li do not mention the measurement of RBCLiR, and the value of such monitoring remains controversial.

This systematic review aims to provide a summary of the areas in which the value of RBCLiC determination has been explored in the literature, in particular the relevance of such a pharmacological parameter in the management of Li-treated individuals with BD and UD. More generally, it aims to identify areas of research that remain to be filled in order to better position this pharmacological parameter, if any relevance.

Methods

English-language articles on RBCLiC and PLiC in mood disorders between 1972 and February 2023 were identified by searching MEDLINE, EMBASE and the Cochrane Library. The search terms are as followed: Lithium AND Plasma AND Erythrocyte AND Mood disorders. Two of the authors (LJ and MC) have independently reviewed all the studies identified by the search. To improve the clarity and transparency of the review process, the main criteria and recommendations of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Metanalysis (PRISMA) statement have been followed. The review has been published in PROSPERO (CRD42023406154). The identified records were first screened by titles and abstracts, and then by full texts. When full-text articles are not available directly from online databases, authors have been contacted to obtain a copy.

The inclusion criteria are as follows: (1) studies with adult individuals with BD or UD receiving Li, (2) studies with RBCLiC and PLiC. Studies are excluded if: (1) articles not available in full text or published in a language other than English, (2) books, reviews, letters, editorials, annotations, (3) studies in which individuals are not receiving Li when measuring RBCLiC and PLiC, (4) studies in which analyses are not stratified on patients with BD and/or UD receiving Li, (5) in vitro studies of RBCLiC and PLiC, not associated with concomitant in vivo analyses. Data extraction have been done using Covidence and Zotero, the software packages provided for this purpose, and then by hand.

Disease management

Compliance/Adherence

Five articles discuss the relevance of RBCLiC or LiR in assessing compliance/adherence (Brunswick et al. 1978; Gengo et al. 1980; Mallinger et al. 1997; Rosa et al. 2007; Yassa et al. 1984). Three of them suggest a positive association between RBCLiC and compliance/adherence (Brunswick et al. 1978; Gengo et al. 1980; Rosa et al. 2007). For instance, Rosa et al. (2007) demonstrated a positive correlation between RBCLiC levels and adherence, as assessed by the Li Knowledge Effect Scale, in a sample of 99 patients with BD (Rosa et al. 2007). Although univariate analyses showed positive correlations between both Lithium Knowledge Test (LKT) score and Medication Adherence Rating Scale (MARS) score with both PLiC and RBCLiC, only the Lithium Knowledge Test (LKT)/PLiC correlation and the Lithium Knowledge Test (LKT)/RBCLiC correlation survived after including the covariates. Confounders such as sex, age, years of education and the use of multiple drugs were controlled by using a linear regression model. Altogether, the interest of RBCLiC (compared to PLiC) has not been confirmed, so far.

SE/toxicity

Thirteen articles investigated associations between RBCLiC and Li-related SE (Ahmadi-Abhari et al. 2005, 2003; Dashti-Khavidaki et al. 2003, 2006; Dehpour et al. 1998; Elizur et al. 1982, 1972; Ghadirian et al. 1989; Johnston et al. 1979; McCoy et al. 1982; Nelson and Cohen 1976; Ong 1983; Zerbi et al. 1975). Examined SE included neurological aspects (cognitive evaluation, abnormal movements, electroencephalogram) (Ahmadi-Abhari et al. 2005; Dehpour et al. 1998; Elizur et al. 1982; Johnston et al. 1979; Ong 1983), renal SE (urinary concentration) (Dashti-Khavidaki et al. 2003; Dehpour et al. 1998), thyroid complications (disturbances of thyroid functioning, and goiter) (Ahmadi-Abhari et al. 2003), cardiovascular issues (hypertension, increase in corrected QT) (Dashti-Khavidaki et al. 2006; Ghadirian et al. 1989; McCoy et al. 1982). While three articles indicated a decrease in LiR (Dehpour et al. 1998; Johnston et al. 1979; McCoy et al. 1982), three others described an increase in RBCLiC (Ahmadi-Abhari et al. 2005; Dashti-Khavidaki et al. 2003) and in LiR (Elizur et al. 1982) in patients with SE compared to patients without SE. With regard to neurological SE specifically, RBCLiC may be more predictive of extra-pyramidal SE than PLiC (Ahmadi-Abhari et al. 2005). RBCLiC may also serve as a more accurate predictor of renal SE compared to PLiC (Dashti-Khavidaki et al. 2003). In conclusion, when considering side-effects as a whole, RBCLiC does not clearly show better predictive value as compared to PLiC. However, RBCLiC may better reflect the risk of extra-pyramidal SE than PLiC.

Prediction of Li response/therapeutic efficacity

The investigation of the comparative values of the different blood parameters is a complex issue covered by different aims and methodological approaches in the scientific literature (acute efficacy vs relapse prevention, retrospective vs prospective studies) (Catalano et al. 1988; Del Vecchio et al. 1981; Ehrlich et al. 1984; Johnston et al. 1980; Knorring et al. 1976a; Kocsis et al. 1982; Mallinger et al. 1997; Mendels and Frazer 1973; Mendlewicz et al. 1978; Nelson and Cohen 1976; Pradhan and Channabasavanna 1983; Rihmer et al. 1982; Sampath et al. 1980; Swann et al. 1987; von Knorring et al. 1976b; Yassa et al. 1984; Zerbi et al. 1975). Using different approaches, Mendels and Frazer (1973), Sampath et al. (1980), Nelson and Cohen (1976), and Swann et al. (1987) showed that LiR or RBCLiC better predict Li response in acute depression or mania than PLiC, and Ehrlich et al. (1984) and Zerbi et al. (1975), an association between acute Li response and LiR. With regard to relapse prevention, we first considered retrospective studies, which were found to have methodological weaknesses and variable treatment durations [Mendlewicz et al. (1978) (at least 2 years), Pradhan and Channabasavanna (1983) (at least 2 years), Knorring et al. (1976a) (from 0.3 to 7.5 years) and Catalano et al. (1988) (between 9 and 31 months)]. These studies concluded that mean LiR or RBCLiC was not associated with Li prophylactic response. The study by Kocsis et al. (1982) deserves more attention. In 41 patients with PLiC in the therapeutic range and a 22 months retrospective assessment of Li prophylactic response, no association was observed between LiR and Li prophylactic response (Kocsis et al. 1982). We then considered the 2 prospective studies. Rihmer et al. (1982) (2 years follow-up, 45 patients with BDI and II, PLiC in the therapeutic range at baseline) showed that mean follow-up LiR was significantly higher in Li-responders by comparison to Li-no-responders, but no information of PLiC during follow-up is provided, which implies the possibility that some Li-non-responders may be non-adherent. Johnston et al. (1980) (one year follow-up) reported a trend towards higher PLiC and RBCLiC in Li-responders when solely PLiC measured during the period preceding a relapse were considered in relapsing patients. But this association didn’t remained significant after taking into account all available PLiC and RBCLiC determined during the follow-up of relapsing patients (Johnston et al. 1980). Altogether, we may conclude that LiR or RBCLiC did not demonstrate a benefit as compared to PLiC to monitor relapse prevention efficacy.

Li blood parameters as trait markers of mood disorders subtypes

Differences according to mood disorders type (UD, BDI, BDII)

We identified 13 articles investigating the association between RBCLiC or LiR and mood disorders diagnoses. Among these, 8 did not demonstrate any significant association (Cusin et al. 2002; Del Vecchio et al. 1981; Ehrlich et al. 1984; Kim et al. 1978; Mendlewicz et al. 1978; Ryan et al. 1989; von Knorring et al. 1976b; White et al. 1979). Five studies suggested an increase in LiR in individuals with BD or UD compared with controls treated or not with Li (either healthy individuals or those with other psychiatric disorders) (Lyttkens et al. 1976; Ramsey et al. 1979; Rybakowski et al. 1978; Szentistvanyi and Janka 1979; Szentistványi et al. 1980). The results of three studies comparing RBCLiC and PLiC in subjects with UD or BD to Li-treated controls were unconclusive (Rybakowski et al. 1978; Szentistvanyi and Janka 1979; Szentistványi et al. 1980). Therefore, RBCLiC and LiR are unlikely to discriminate mood disorders subtypes (BD, UD and schizoaffective disorders). At most, LiR may be different in individuals with affective disorders when compared to Li treated healthy controls.

Regarding subtypes of BD, a study by Rihmer et al. (1982) showed a higher mean LiR in 19 patients with BDII as compared to 26 patients with BDI (Rihmer et al. 1982), while a second study by the same author found no significant difference in LiR between 63 patients with BDI and 23 with BDII (Rihmer et al. 1983).

We concluded that Li blood parameters are not trait markers of mood disorders subtypes.

Family history of affective disorders

Six articles investigated the potential influence of family history on LiR (Cusin et al. 2002; Del Vecchio et al. 1981; Kim et al. 1978; Rihmer et al. 1982; 1983; Verbanck and Mendlewicz 1981). Four studies reported an association between a higher LiR and positive first or second degree mood disorders family history (Del Vecchio et al. 1981; Rihmer et al. 1982; 1983; Verbanck and Mendlewicz 1981). In addition, in a study including 25 pairs of monozygotic twins, concordant pairs for affective disorders exhibited a significantly higher LiR than discordant pairs, and the twin concordance in relation to affective illness correlated to LiR (Verbanck and Mendlewicz 1981). These results suggest that LiR is heritable and that familial BD is associated with Li accumulation in RBC.

Li blood parameters as state markers of mood episodes

Differences according to acute mood episodes

Ten studies investigated the association between RBCLiC or LiR and mood episodes (Catalano et al. 1988; Elizur et al. 1982, 1972; Hashemi et al. 2006; Kim et al. 1978; Kocsis et al. 1982; Ramsey et al. 1979; Rosa et al. 2007; Swann et al. 1987; von Knorring et al. 1976b). Only three articles reported significant differences in LiR according to mood symptoms. Indeed, Swann et al. (1987) found a negative correlation between LiR and the Mania Diagnosis and Severity Scale (MDSS) score in 13 patients with a manic episode (Swann et al. 1987). Elizur et al. (1972) observed lower LiR in 16 patients with BD during manic episodes compared to periods of euthymia (Elizur et al. 1972). Another study described a higher LiR during depression (n = 33) or hypomania (n = 7) as compared to periods of euthymia (n = 89) in patients with BD or UD (Kim et al. 1978). In conclusion, regarding Li blood parameters as state markers in affective episodes, no clear conclusion can be drawn from the literature. These conflicting data may be related to methodological issues. Indeed, uncertainties on treatment adherence or stable Li dosage during acute episodes may have biased the interpretation of both positive and negative results.

Clinical features (psychotic symptoms)

In 186 patients with mood disorders followed for an average of 36 months, Cusin et al. (2002) did not observed a significant influence of psychotic features during manic episode on links between seasonality and LiR (Cusin et al. 2002).

Factors influencing Li blood parameters

Socio-demographic factors

The influence of the following socio-demographic and clinical characteristics of the disorders on LiR have been studied in 24 articles: age, gender, ethnicity, duration of mood disorders, dosage and duration of Li treatment, co-medication with other treatments, and seasonality.

Age

The results of the 11 articles exploring links between age and LiR, RBCLiC, and PLiC identified in this systematic review, are notably consistent, revealing no significant association between age and Li blood parameters (Ahmadi-Abhari et al. 2005; Azizabadi-Farahani et al. 1996; Catalano et al. 1988; Ghadirian et al. 1989; Hashemi et al. 2006; Kim et al. 1978; Lieberman et al. 1985; McCoy et al. 1982; Rosa et al. 2007; Swann et al. 1990; von Knorring et al. 1976b).

Gender

Nineteen articles explored the association between gender and RBCLiC or LiR (Azizabadi-Farahani et al. 1996; Catalano et al. 1988; Cusin et al. 2002; Hashemi et al. 2006; Kim et al. 1978; Lieberman et al. 1985; Lyttkens et al. 1976; McCoy et al. 1982; Mendlewicz et al. 1978; Ramsey et al. 1979; Rihmer et al. 1983; Rosa et al. 2007; Ryan et al. 1989; Rybakowski et al. 1978, 1989; Strickland et al. 1995; Swann et al. 1990; von Knorring et al. 1976b; Zaremba and Rybakowski 1986). Among them, fourteen found no association, while five reported significant differences based on gender. Three studies observed a higher LiR in women compared to men (Hashemi et al. 2006; Lyttkens et al. 1976; Zaremba and Rybakowski 1986). Cusin et al. (2002) observed significant variations of RBCLiC according to seasons only in women but not in men (Cusin et al. 2002). According to Swann et al. (1990), the time of the maximal RBCLiC was later in women (Swann et al. 1990). In conclusion, links between gender and Li blood parameters remain rather unconclusive, given the conflicting literature and the absence of clear mechanistic hypotheses.

Ethnicity

Only two articles investigated the association between LiR and ethnic origins in BD (Strickland et al. 1995; Ryan et al. 1989). However, the presence of biases, such as small and uneven sample sizes, along with variations in Li concentrations, including sub-therapeutic levels, precludes definitive conclusions.

Duration of mood disorders and number of previous episodes

Two studies did not found association between LiR and the duration of the BD or UD illnesses (Catalano et al. 1988; Ahmadi-Abhari et al. 2005), or the number of previous manic episodes (Ahmadi-Abhari et al. 2005).

Dosage and duration of Li treatment

Compelling evidence indicate that LiR is independent of the Li dosage (Ahmadi-Abhari et al. 1998; Azizabadi-Farahani et al. 1996; Dashti-Khavidaki et al. 2003; Dehpour et al. 1998; Hashemi et al. 2006; von Knorring et al. 1976b) and Li treatment duration (Ahmadi-Abhari et al. 1998; Azizabadi-Farahani et al. 1996; Dashti-Khavidaki et al. 2003; Dehpour et al. 1998; Hashemi et al. 2006; Lieberman et al. 1985; von Knorring et al. 1976b).

Co-medication with other treatments

Several investigated medications (anti-thyroid, anti-hypertensive, benzodiazepines, electroconvulsive therapy) did not show any association with RBCLiC or LiR (Ahmadi-Abhari et al. 1998; Kim et al. 1978; Rosa et al. 2007; Dashti-Khavidaki et al. 2003). While Dashti et al. (2003) reported a lower LiR in patients with BD treated with carbamazepine (Dashti-Khavidaki et al. 2003), Ahmadi et al. (1998) found no difference in LiR between 9 patients treated with Li and carbamazepine compared to 15 patients treated with Li alone (Ahmadi-Abhari et al. 1998). Although conflicting (Ghadirian et al. 1989), there is some suggestion that neuroleptics (particularly first generation one) co-prescription may decrease LiR (Ahmadi-Abhari et al. 1998; Azizabadi-Farahani et al. 1996; Dehpour et al. 1998; Hashemi et al. 2006). Two early and non-replicated studies reported a higher LiR in patients treated with neuroleptics (Kim et al. 1978; Ryan et al. 1989).

Seasonality

Cusin et al. (2002) found no significant difference in LiR according to seasons in 186 individuals with mood disorders (Cusin et al. 2002).

Pathophysiological mechanisms

Thirty articles investigated whether measuring RBCLiC could provide a better understanding of the mechanisms of action of Li and of the pathophysiology of BD. Some early studies suggested that the RBCLiC might better reflect brain Li concentration than PLiC under steady-state conditions (Frazer et al. 1973, 1978). Aligned with this hypothesis, several authors proposed that the red blood cell (RBC) transmembrane model could be considered as a model for transport across the blood brain barrier (BBB) (Swann et al. 1987; Swann et al. 1990; Mallinger et al. 1997; Szentistványi et al. 1980).

Research has identified key players involved in Li transport at the RBC membrane. According to Swann et al. (1990), Li transport across the RBC membrane is mainly determined by passive diffusion (“leakage”), ion pair transport as Li-CO3-, and via the Na+-K+ pump (sodium-potassium pump), Li acting as an analog of Na + (Swann et al. 1990). Several RBC in vitro studies have described multiple pathways mediating Li efflux under physiological extracellular Na+ concentrations: (i) Li+-Na+ counter-transport inhibited by phloretin (Pandey et al. 1984; Ostrow et al. 1978), (ii) passive diffusion of Li (Swann et al. 1990; Pandey et al. 1984; Mallinger et al. 1997), (iii) Na+-K+ pump sensitive to ouabain (Mallinger et al. 1980; Pandey et al. 1984; Ostrow et al. 1978), and the Na+-Na+ exchange system (Swann et al. 1990).

To summarize the subsequent findings, it is important to note that Li does not passively equilibrate between intracellular and extracellular RBC compartments. At least four mechanisms have been characterized for Li transport across RBC membranes: passive and bicarbonate-influenced diffusions, exchange at the Na+ site of the Na+-K+-ATPase pump, and extrusion against Na+ via the Na+-Li counter-transport (SLC) system. Although a passive gradient diffusion is the dominant mechanism of Li transport at the RBC membrane, the SLC system has been the most extensively studied (El Balkhi et al. 2009).

Swann et al. (1987) described a negative correlation between RBCLiC and several neuro-transmitters in the cerebrospinal fluid (3-methoxy-4-hydroxyphenylglycol, 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid, homovanillic acid, vanillylmandelic acid), but not with PLiC (Swann et al. 1987). More recent works do not support the notion that the RBC transport model could accurately reflect the transport mechanisms involved at the blood–brain barrier. Indeed, numerous studies have demonstrated that PLiC better reflect brain Li concentration than with RBCLiC (Plenge et al. 1994; Moore et al. 2002; Kato et al. 1993). In addition, as far as we know, systems responsible for regulating Li at the RBC membrane and at the BBB appear to be different. Using hCMEC/D3 cells, human hematopoietic stem-cell-derived BBB models (HBLEC), and human primary brain microvascular endothelial cells (hPBMECs), Luo et al. developed inhibition and/or transcriptomic strategies for Na+ transporters such as NHE (SLC9), NBC (SLC4), and NKCC (SLC12) to assess their role on Li transport in human brain endothelial cells (Luo et al. 2018). They showed that NHE1 and/or NHE5, NBCn1, and NKCC1 may play a significant role in the transport of Li through the plasma membrane of brain endothelial cells (Luo et al. 2021).

Discussion

The main finding of this review is that it is impossible to conclude positively or negatively on the value of RBCLiC, and that the respective comparative values of different Li blood parameters (RBCLiC, PLiC and LiR) remains unclear.

We concluded in favour of possible associations between LiR and SE, particularly neurotoxicity. While some data suggest a lower LiR with neuroleptics co-medication compared to Li monotherapy (Ahmadi-Abhari et al. 1998; Azizabadi-Farahani et al. 1996; Dehpour et al. 1998; Hashemi et al. 2006), the available data are too scarce and heterogeneous to draw firm conclusions (Canal et al. 2003; Chiu et al. 2014; Sadeghipour-Roudsari et al. 1998). The articles reviewed do not suggest that Li concentration in RBC or LiR is associated with mood disorder type, type of mood episodes, or response to Li. Although a few authors propose a higher LiR in women compared to men, most data do not support associations with age, gender, ethnicity, duration of mood disorders, psychotic features, dosage, duration, and the pharmaceutical form prescribed of Li treatment, co-medication with treatments other than neuroleptics, or seasonality. There is some evidence supporting an association between RBCLiC or LiR and family history of mood disorders, suggesting a potential role for genetic mechanisms in the regulation of these parameters.

Limitations

The primary limitations of this review include the restricted number of studies, small sample sizes, heterogeneity (in diagnoses, in clinical features of mood disorders or in definitions of response to Li), discrepancies in dosage and duration of Li treatment, and variations in Li-associated therapies. In addition, information on treatment adherence, somatic, psychiatric, and addiction comorbidities were frequently lacking and multivariate models considering potential confounding factors were rarely applied. It is important to note the marked heterogeneity of the methods used in the studies included in this review (for example, with regard to the quantification of RBCLiC, but also the delay between the determination of RBCLiC and the introduction of Li). In studies where only RBCLiC was studied and since RBCLiC is dependent on PLiC, we may wonder to what extent identical associations would not have been observed with a PLiC measurement.

Despite screening conducted by two authors, bias is inherent in article selection, partly influenced by the choice of inclusion and exclusion criteria. Twenty nine full texts were unattainable even after attempts to contact the authors.

The variability of Li response (efficacy and tolerance) remains poorly understood, and we know little about the cerebral pharmacokinetics (PK) of Li. Therefore further exploration and understanding of these parameters is crucial, and requires methodologically sound studies.

Prospects and future studies

This systematic review highlights the need for further research into the correlations between PLiC and RBCLiC, as well as the clinical relevance of these two parameters. We also note the lack of recent studies and the small sample sizes. Indeed, it is noteworthy that approximately 85% of the studies included in this literature review were published between 1972 and 2000. To date, RBCLiC is frequently employed in therapeutic drug monitoring (Lee et al. 1975; Schreiner et al. 1979; White et al. 1979) and in particular in France, without clear scientific evidence on its added value. Further investigations using modern analytical and modelling tools are warranted in several domains (Komoroski 2005; Riedl et al. 1997; Sachs et al. 1995; El Balkhi et al. 2009).i)Clinical approaches

i)Clinical approaches

Clinical approaches

There is a clear need for future large-scale studies using precise and high-quality methods with stratification into homogeneous sub-groups of patients according to standardized clinical characteristics. Longitudinal studies would allow to conclude whether the RBCLiC monitoring could add to PLiC only in predicting both response and SE. Future studies on these topics deserve particular attention on adherence monitoring, prospective evaluation of SE and relapse prevention efficacy, monocentric lab assays, precise and prospective assessment of co-medication and comorbidities. This literature clearly indicates that multivariate approaches taking into account potential confounding factors are needed. Different study designs are also warranted when investigating the interest of the different biological parameters in acute efficacy or in relapse prevention. Finally, differences according to immediate- versus slow-release Li are lacking.ii)Pharmacokinetics aspects

ii)Pharmacokinetics aspects

Pharmacokinetics aspects

Population pharmacokinetic (Pop-PK) utilizes model-based representations of PK with a statistical component to identify sources of variability (Lotrich et al. 2006). There is a need to establish a Pop-PK and pharmacodynamic (PD) model of Li (Pop-PK-PD) in individuals with mood disorders and to identify the covariates statistically influencing the PK model of Li and incorporating response and tolerability to Li, and possibly Li-7 magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) study. To date, it is known that the PK of PLiC and RBCLiC to reach steady-state conditions are different, and it appears that the elimination kinetics in the event of intoxication are relatively parallel (El Balkhi et al. 2009). No pharmacokinetic studies of Li in blood/erythrocytes and in the brain are published. Few studies have measured brain Li in Humans using 7Li spectroscopy (Plenge et al. 1994; Komoroski et al. 1993) or using Li MRI (Scott et al. 2019). However, the correlation between brain regional Li concentration measured by in vivo imaging technics and post-mortem Li concentration in the same regions, has been established in animal studies only (Hanak et al. 2015). As compelling data indicate that brain Li distribution is heterogeneous, future brain pharmacokinetic studies represent a major avenue.iii)Research on brain Li distribution

iii)Research on brain Li distribution

Research on brain Li distribution

Despite the heterogeneity in the literature, the idea that RBCLiC exhibits a stronger correlation with brain Li concentration than with PLiC under steady-state conditions seems either meaningless, on the basis on studies in rats showing that brain Li concentration better correlate with PLiC (Hanak et al. 2015). Moreover, Li-7 nuclear MRI in patients with BD revealed lower cerebral Li concentrations in non-responders to Li therapy, despite similar blood concentrations, indicating a weak correlation between blood and cerebral Li concentrations (Machado-Vieira et al. 2016). Since brain Li distribution is heterogeneous, it adds a level of complexity. Indeed, further research is needed to explore regional concentrations (using Li-7 nuclear magnetic resonance), alongside RBCLiC and PLiC. These investigations are also crucial with regard to the heterogeneity of brain Li distribution (Stout et al. 2017) with some regional concentrations being potentially confirmed as biomarkers of response and tolerance.

Future studies with in vivo or ex vivo brain imagery conducted on animal models may facilitate a deeper understanding of the correlations between RBC, plasma, and cerebral Li levels.iv)Molecular signatures

iv)Molecular signatures

Molecular signatures

The blood molecular signature of Li treatment is the subject of a growing body of literature. In particular, this field explores the elements of this molecular signature that would enable better prediction of the response to Li, in particular relapse prevention (Bellivier and Marie-Claire 2018). Part of this molecular signature is related to therapeutic mechanisms of action. xxii)Role of the brain blood barrier

Role of the brain blood barrier

Li transport through the brain barriers is crucial to achieve adequate concentrations in the brain parenchyma, where it interacts with its pharmacological targets (Luo et al. 2021). Regional permeability of the BBB and the blood cerebrospinal fluid barrier may represent variability factors affecting Li exchanges between blood and brain, and brain distribution. Li influences various transporters and drug-metabolizing enzymes at the BBB, inducing the expression of several proteins that are key-elements in the modulation of the BBB under pathophysiological conditions [Axin 2, Plasmalemma vesicle-associated protein (PLVAP), claudin 3 (Amasheh et al. 2002), p120, B catenin, occludin, claudin 5 (Komoroski et al. 1993), ATP Binding Cassette (ABC) and Solute Carriers (SLC) transporters, permeability-glycoprotein (Francis and Traill 1970), multidrug resistance-associated protein 2 (MRP2), ATP-binding cassette super-family G member 2 (ABCG2)/Breast Cancer Resistance Protein (BCRP) (Amasheh et al. 2002; Shawahna et al. 2017)]. Exploring these issues through studies combining magnetic resonance imaging, BBB permeability measurements and clinical genomics in future studies, would be of interest. For example, it might be interesting to investigate correlations between Li permeability within the RBC membrane compared with the BBB, and common and specific transporters at each of these barriers. Also, to which extend the RBC membrane may have some analogy with the BBB remain to be studied.

Conclusion

We have chosen to include in this systematic review of the literature very different fields of research on RBCLiC, which has shown that many areas of uncertainty persist concerning the place of this biological parameter. In particular, its added value for clinical management has not been clearly demonstrated. However, this area of research remains largely underexplored, and methodologically sound studies are still lacking. Furthermore, it is now well established that Li transport is regulated at the BBB, but two major questions remain to be elucidated: (i) the precise characterization of the transporters responsible for Li influx/exflux at the BBB, and the factors influencing this transport, (ii) the mechanisms that explain the regional distribution of Li in the brain.
