An efficient AAV vector system of Rec2 serotype for intravenous injection to study metabolism in brown adipocytes in vivo
Authors: Janina Behrens, Ingke Braren, Michelle Y Jaeckstein, Luka Lilie, Markus Heine, Finnja Sass, Judith Sommer, Dagmar Silbert-Wagner, Marceline M Fuh, Anna Worthmann, Leon Straub, Tarek Moustafa, Joerg Heeren, Ludger Scheja
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveRecombinant adeno-associated virus (rAAV) vectors are powerful tools for the sustained expression of proteins in vivo and have been successfully used for mechanistic studies in mice. A major challenge associated with this method is to obtain tissue specificity and high expression levels without need of local virus administration.MethodsTo achieve this goal for brown adipose tissue (BAT), we developed a rAAV vector for intravenous bolus injection, which includes an expression cassette comprising an uncoupling protein-1 enhancer-promoter for transcription in brown adipocytes and miR122 target sequences for suppression of expression in the liver, combined with packaging in serotype Rec2 capsid protein. To test tissue specificity, we used a version of this vector expressing Cre recombinase to transduce mice with floxed alleles to knock out MLXIPL (ChREBP) or tdTomato-Cre reporter mice.ResultsWe demonstrated efficient Cre-dependent recombination in interscapular BAT and variable effects in minor BAT depots, but little or no efficacy in white adipose tissues, liver and other organs. Direct overexpression of glucose transporter SLC2A1 (GLUT1) using the rAAV vector in wild type mice resulted in increased glucose uptake and glucose-dependent gene expression in BAT, indicating usefulness of this vector to increase the function even of abundant proteins.ConclusionTaken together, we describe a novel brown adipocyte-specific rAAV method to express proteins for loss-of-function and gain-of-function metabolic studies. The approach will enable researchers to access brown fat swiftly, reduce animal breeding time and costs, as well as enable the creation of new transgenic mouse models combining multiple transgenes.Keywords: Adeno-associated virus vector, Rec2, Cre recombinase, brown adipose tissue, Glucose transport, de novo lipogenesis

Introduction

Brown adipocytes are highly specialized cells designed to generate heat in a mechanism that relies on uncoupling protein-1 (UCP1), a mitochondrial proton transporter that separates the electron transfer chain from ATP synthesis, and a number of more recently discovered alternative mechanisms [1]. These cells are present in defined anatomical sites, forming brown adipose tissue (BAT) depots [2]. Thermogenic adipocytes are also found interspersed among white adipocytes in white adipose tissue (WAT) depots. The numbers of these so-called beige adipocytes increase after prolonged thermogenic stimulation, for example, by cold exposure or by pharmacological stimulation of β-adrenergic receptors [3,4].

Research of thermogenic fat cells has been greatly aided by the development of transgenic mice with the cell type-specific Ucp1 promoter driving expression of Cre recombinase, which allows Cre-loxP-driven knockout or overexpression in thermogenic adipocytes in vivo [5,6]. These Ucp1-Cre mice have been widely used to study thermogenic signalling [7], metabolite transport and combustion of energy substrates [[8], [9], [10]], adaptation of BAT to thermogenic demand [11,12], endocrine functions [13] and other aspects of BAT biology [14]. A disadvantage of using transgenic Cre for Cre-loxP recombination is that it requires time- and animal-consuming crossing of Cre mouse lines to mice carrying a floxed gene of interest. Furthermore, recent studies indicate other drawbacks of using the specific Ucp1-Cre mouse line that has been mostly used for BAT research [5]. For instance, it confers ectopic Cre recombinase expression in the brain and other organs, which may confound the interpretation of results claiming BAT-specific effects [15,16]. Moreover, the Ucp1-Cre transgene itself was reported to have effects on development and metabolism, e.g. by expressing Ucp1 from an additional copy located on the transgenic construct [17].

Vectors based on recombinant adeno-associated virus (rAAV) can be a good alternative to conventional transgenic expression. Depending on the serotypes of the capsid proteins used for packaging the rAAV, transduction with viral particles can be efficient and swiftly result in sustained expression of transgenes in vivo [18]. Of note, rAAV particles of serotypes 8 and 9 that carry a minimal Ucp1 enhancer-promoter have been used to transduce brown adipocytes of mice after intravenous application [19]. However, the infectivity of serotype 8 and 9 for adipocytes is very low, which results in very high experimental costs. Subsequent research revealed that rAAV particles of the recombinant serotype Rec2 can transduce white and brown adipocytes when injected locally in fat depots of mice [20,21]. When applied systemically, Rec2 rAAV showed tropism for the interscapular BAT (iBAT) depot and the liver, even without the use of a cell-specific promoter [22,23]. These publications have provided proof of concept for the use of rAAV to genetically manipulate brown adipocytes in vivo. However, the specificity and the general applicability of these vectors for metabolic studies was not further explored. No published studies ever compared rAAV-Ucp1-Cre in vivo recombination efficiency with that of germ line inherited Ucp1-cre transgenes. Furthermore, functional overexpression of major metabolite-handling proteins such as enzymes and transporters in this context has never been assessed. To generate a novel brown adipocyte-specific expression tool, we designed rAAVs that combine rat Ucp1 enhancer-promoter with the Rec2 serotype. To achieve BAT specificity, we added a mmu-miR-122 (miRNA122) target cassette to repress expression in hepatocytes. By employing this vector with Cre recombinase inserted, we show that iBAT but less so minor BAT depots are efficiently targeted, while no or only sporadic Cre activity is observed in white adipose tissue (WAT) and other organs. Using this novel rAAV method, it is possible to achieve reprogramming of lipid metabolism by efficient Cre-loxP-mediated knockout in BAT of adult animals. This was demonstrated by knocking out carbohydrate response element-binding protein (ChREBP, in the following called MLXIPL), a transcription factor that controls de novo lipogenesis (DNL) in BAT [12]. In addition, by overexpressing the glucose transporter SLC2A1 we provide proof of concept that this rAAV can be even used to directly alter metabolic fluxes in BAT.

Material and methods

Mice

All animal studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees at University Medical Center Hamburg-Eppendorf and Medical University Graz. Mlxipl-flox mice (previously called ChREBPα-flox mice) [24], transgenic Ucp1-Cre mice [5], tdTomato reporter (B6.Cg-Gt(ROSA)26Sortm9(CAG-tdTomato)Hze/J) and wild type mice, were on the C57BL/6J genetic background. Age and weight-matched mice aged 12–20 weeks, housed at 22 °C with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle with ad libitum access to water and food (chow diet, Altromin) were used for experiments. Unless indicated otherwise, male mice were used.

rAAV vector generation, virus production and titration

To generate the transfer plasmid for rAAV-Ucp1-Cre, an Ucp1 enhancer-promoter element, comprising an upstream enhancer (−2494 to −2283) and the promoter (−400 to +111) of the rat Ucp1 gene [25] was employed. This element, followed by the coding sequence of Cre recombinase, the woodchuck hepatitis virus post-transcriptional regulatory element (WPRE) and an SV40 poly (A) termination signal were inserted into vector pFBGR-Ultra (kind gift from Robert Kotin, University of Massachusetts, USA) to generate the transfer plasmid pAAV-Ucp1-Cre. A version of the vector without insert (pAAV-Ucp1-empty) was designed to generate a matching control rAAV. To generate the transfer plasmid for rAAV-Ucp1-Cre-miR122 (pAAV-ucp1-Cre-3xmiR122-WPRE-HGHpA), the CAG promoter in pAAV-CAG-Cre-3xmiR122-WPRE-HGHpA (Addgene plasmid 183776) was replaced with the rat Ucp1 enhancer-promoter element described above. This plasmid contains a codon-optimized Cre containing an SV40 nuclear localization sequence, and three miR-122 target sequences [26] inserted between iCre and WPRE. rAAV-Ucp1-Slc2a1-miR122 was constructed by exchanging iCre in rAAV-Ucp1-Cre-miR122 with the coding sequence of murine Slc2a1. Sequences of all vectors are provided in the Supplementary Methods Part and the plasmids are available upon request.

Recombinant AAV-Rec2 pseudotyped vectors were generated by co-transfection of HEK293-AAV cells (Cellbiolabs/Biocat, Heidelberg, Germany) with the respective pAAV transfer plasmid, the AAV packaging plasmid pRepCap Rec2 which provides the AAV rep and cap genes, and pHelper providing adenoviral helper functions (Cellbiolabs/Biocat, Heidelberg, Germany). Generation of rAAV particles was carried out as described previously [27] with some modifications. Briefly, 1 × 107 HEK293-AAV cells were transfected with 6 μg pAAV, 7.5 μg pRepCap Rec2 and 10 μg pHelper plasmid per 15 cm dish complexed with Max-polyethylenimine (MaxPEI, Polysciences, Eppelheim, Germany) at a PEI:DNA ratio of 5:1 (w/w). After 72 h, cells were harvested and resuspended in 5 ml lysis buffer (50 mM Tris base, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, pH 8.5). After three freeze-thaw cycles, lysates were incubated with 50 U/ml benzonase (Merck) for 1 h at 37 °C. Cell debris was pelleted and vector-containing lysates were purified using iodixanol step gradients and ultrafiltration (Amicon: Ultra Cartridges, 50 mwco, EMD Millipore, Schwalbach, Germany). The genomic titers of DNase-resistant rAAV were determined by alkaline treatment and subsequent neutralization particles and subsequent qPCR (95 °C for 2 min, followed by 35 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s) using the QPCRBIO SyGreen Mix Hi-ROX (Nippon Genetics Europe gmbH), ITR sequences (5′-GGAACCCCTAGTGATGGAGTT and 5′-CGGCCTCAGTGAGCGA) and a QuantStudio5 cycler (Applied Biosystems, Darmstadt, Germany). pAAV transfer plasmid was used as a copy number standard.

rAAV vector administration and organ harvest

Mice were intravenously injected with 1 × 1012 or 1 × 1013 rAAV vector particles in 100 μL saline per mouse. Organ harvest was performed two or three weeks post-injection, as indicated. The mice were fasted for 4 h, anesthetized with ketamine (180 mg/kg)/xylazine (24 mg/kg), and systemically perfused with PBS via the left heart ventricle. Organs for RNA, lipid and protein analysis were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen.

Tissue preparation and microscopy

Microscopy was performed with a Nikon A1 confocal microscope. For whole-mount fluorescence microscopy, organs were fixed in 2% para-formaldehyde for 2 h, incubated in 5% sucrose overnight and then transferred to 15% sucrose. Hematoxylin & eosin staining was performed on paraffin-embedded BAT tissues. Sections (5 μm) were cut on a Leica microtome and mounted on Histobond slides (Marienfeld-Superior). Deparaffination was performed with xylene substitute and dehydration by descending alcohol series. Sections were incubated for 15 min in hematoxylin solution (Sigma–Aldrich), washed for 15 min with running tap water and incubated for 1 min in eosin solution (1%). Sections were dehydrated with ascending alcohol series and mounted with Eukitt® mounting media (Sigma–Aldrich).

RNA isolation and gene expression analysis

Tissues were homogenized in 1 ml of TRIzol reagent (ThermoFisher) using a TissueLyser type 3 (QIAGEN; 20 Hz for 2 × 3 min). 250 μL chloroform was added and samples were mixed and centrifuged at 13,000 g at 4 °C for 15 min. Supernatant was mixed with 600 μL of 70% ethanol. Further purification was performed by using NucleoSpin RNAII Kit (Machery&Nagel) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Double-stranded DNA was digested using rDNase I (kit). 400 ng purified RNA were used for cDNA synthesis using High-Capacity cDNA Archive Kit (ThermoFisher). Reverse transcription PCR program was as followed: 1. 10 min, 25 °C; 2. 120 min, 37 °C; 3.5 s, 85 °C. Gene expression was assessed using Taqman assays supplied as assays-on-demands (AoDs, ThermoFisher) or SYBR green primers. AoDs: Tbp (00446973_m1), Mlxipl = Mlxipl isoform 1 (Chrebpα) (01196407_m1; spanning exons 1a-2), Mlxipl isoform 2/Chrebpβ) (customized, described in [12], spanning exons 1b-2), Fasn (00662319_m1), mIsg15 (01705338_s1), mIl6 (00446190_m1), Itgam (00434455_m1), Ccl5 (01302428_m1), mSlc2a1 (00441480_m1). SYBR green primers: Tbp (fwd. CTTCCTGCCACAATGTCACAG, rev. CCTTTCTCATGCTTGCTTCTCTG), AAV-ITR (fwd. GAACCCCTAGTGATGGAGTT, rev. CGGCCTCAGTGAGCGA), Cre (fwd. GAACGCACTGATTTCGACCA, rev. CCTGATCCTGGCAATTTCGG).

Protein isolation and western blotting

Tissues were homogenized in 10× (v/w) RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4; 5 mM EDTA; 150 mM NaCl; 1 mM Na-pyrophosphate; 1 mM NaF; 1 mM Na-vanadate; 1% NP-40) supplemented with completeMini protease inhibitor (Roche) using TissueLyser-type3 (QIAGEN; 20 Hz for 2 × 3 min). Samples were centrifuged for 10 min at 13,000 g and 4 °C and supernatant was collected without upper lipid layer contamination. Protein was quantified by bicinchoninic acid assay. Samples were diluted in RIPA buffer and 2-fold NuPAGE® LDS Sample buffer + Sample Reducing Agent (Invitrogen). 20 μg of total protein were separated in 10% Tris-glycine SDS-PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (GE healthcare) in a wet blotting system (blotting buffer: 20 mM Tris, 150 mM glycine, 20% (v/v) methanol) overnight at 200 mA. Membranes were stained with Ponceau Red (Sigma), cut and blocked for 1 h in 5% milk in TBS-T (20 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20). Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C in the corresponding primary antibodies diluted 1:1000 in 5% BSA (Sigma) in TBS-T. After washing 3 × 10 min in TBS-T, membranes were incubated for 1 h at RT in the corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibody diluted 1:5000 in 5% milk in TBS-T. After washing 3 × 10 min in TBS-T, detection was performed with Amersham Imager 600 (GE healthcare) using SuperSignal West Femto ECL (Thermofisher). Quantification was performed using Li-Cor Image Studio Lite. Primary antibodies: γTubulin (abcam, ab179503), ACC (Cell Signalling, #3662), FASN (BD Biosciences, #610962), MLXIPL (or ChREBP) (Novus Biologicals, NB400-135) UCP1 (RnD, MAB6158), Cre (Cell Signalling, #15036), SLC2A1 (or GLUT1) (abcam, ab652). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies: α-mouse (Jackson, #115-035-146), α-rabbit (Jackson, #111-03-144).

Lipidomics

Lipidomic analysis was performed as described [28,29]. Briefly, after addition of internal standards, lipids were extracted from 3 mg of snap frozen iBAT using a adjusted MTBE/methanol extraction protocol [30]. Lipid extracts were concentrated and reconstituted in running buffer (10 mM ammonium actetate, dichloromethane (50): methanol (50)) and analyzed by a flow injection analysis-tandem mass spectrometry (FIA-MS/MS)-based method using an ultra-high pressure liquid chromatography system (Nexera X2, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) coupled with a QTRAP® system (QTRAP® 5500; SCIEX) run in multiple-reaction-monitoring mode and equipped with a differential mobility spectrometer (DMS) interface operating with SelexION technology. Aquired raw data was processed and lipids were quantified using the Shotgun Lipidomics Assistant, a Python-based application [28].

Deoxyglucose uptake in vivo

Deoxyglucose uptake into BAT was determined in a mixed-meal test. To this end, an oral glucose (2 g/kg body weight) and triolein (4 g/kg, emulsified using 0.4 g/kg lecithin) gavage of day time-fasted mice (4 h) was performed with 14C-deoxyglucose (1.9 MBq/kg) as tracer. Two hours after gavage, organs were harvested as described above. Tracer uptake was determined by dissolving organs in 10× (v/w) Solvable™ (Perkin Elmer) and scintillation counting.

Statistics

Data were shown as mean ± SEM. Treatment groups were compared to control group by unpaired two-tailed Student's t test. Statistical analyses were conducted using Microsoft Excel 2016 (RRID: SCR_016137) and GraphPadPrism 9.0 (RRID: SCR_002798) and p < 0.05 was considered significant. The statistical tests used can be found in the figure legends.

Results

Construction of a rAAV vector that targets Cre recombinase to BAT

To generate a vector that confers Cre expression in brown adipocytes in vivo, a rAAV vector was constructed with a Cre-coding sequence cassette downstream of a rat Ucp1 enhancer-promoter construct that drives BAT-specific expression in transgenic mice [25] (rAAV-Ucp1-Cre, Figure 1A). For packaging of the rAAV, serotype Rec2 capsid protein [31] that targets both iBAT and liver when injected intravenously [22] was used. To determine the tropism and efficiency of the vector, and its ability to induce Cre-loxP-dependent deletion, rAAV particles at a low titer (1 × 1012 particles/mouse) or a high titer (1 × 1013 particles/mouse) were intravenously injected into Mlxipl-flox mice [12,24]. Organs were collected two weeks post-injection. Quantities of rAAV vectors, indicated by copy numbers of inverted terminal repeat (ITR), were high in BAT depots, lower in liver and very low or undetectable in the other organs studied (Figure 1B). Of note, iBAT showed 2–4 times more vector genome than deep cervical (dc) and subscapular (sc) BAT (Figure 1B). Cre recombinase expression followed a similar pattern with appreciable expression also in liver (Figure S1A). The efficiency of rAAV-Ucp1-Cre to induce Cre-loxP-dependent deletion was assessed by measuring Mlxipl expression, comparing it to un-injected Mlxipl-flox mice (Cre− control) and Mlxipl-flox mice that carry a conventional, genomic Ucp1-Cre transgene [5] (Cre+ control). In iBAT of mice that received the high titer of rAAV-Ucp1-Cre, Mlxipl reduction was comparable to Cre+ transgene controls (Figure 1C). We detected, however, only partial deletion of MLXIPL in scBAT and dcBAT, consistent with the lower ITR copy numbers in these BAT depots (Figure 1B). Low dose treatment had no effect on Mlxipl expression, indicating that the higher number of rAAV particles is mandatory for efficient Cre-mediated recombination. Consequently, only the high rAAV titer were used in subsequent experiments. No further increase in rAAV efficiency was observed when mice were studied 3 weeks after rAAV administration (Figure S1B, C).

Notably, as suggested by Cre expression (Figure S1A), knockout of Mlxipl was also observed in the liver after rAAV-Ucp1-Cre treatment with the high dose (Figure 1C), indicating that the Ucp1 promoter-enhancer element is less tissue-selective than previously reported for the corresponding genomic transgene [25]. To overcome the undesired Cre expression in the liver, we modified the vector by adding three target sequences for the hepatocyte-specific microRNA mmu-miR-122 [26] to the 3′UTR, an approach that had been previously applied to rAAV vectors targeting BAT [19,22]. The resulting rAAV-Ucp1-Cre-miR122 vector (Figure 2A) did not reduce liver Mlxipl mRNA when injected into Mlxipl-flox mice (Figure 2B), in line with the absence of Cre protein in liver (Figure 2C). In contrast, mRNA (Figure 2B) and protein levels (Figure 2D,E) of Mlxipl were reduced in iBAT compared to empty vector controls. The MLXIPL targets acetyl-CoA carboxylase-α (ACC1) and fatty acid synthase (FASN) were depleted, notably to the same degree as in the transgenic Cre+ controls. Together, these results show that mmu-miR-122 endogenously expressed at high levels in hepatocytes efficiently blocked Cre expression from the rAAV vector in liver. Similar to the results obtained with rAAV-Ucp1-Cre (Figure 1C), Mlxipl mRNA expression in scBAT was reduced only by 50% whereas it was unaltered in dcBAT and WAT depots (Figure 2B). AAV-Ucp1-Cre-miR122-mediated Mlxipl knockout in adipose tissues was similar in female and male mice (Figure 2B). Importantly, treatment with the vector did not increase UCP1 protein expression in iBAT to the same extent as observed in the Cre+ transgene controls (Figure 2D,E), an effect recently ascribed to the Ucp1-Cre transgene [17]. Moreover, the viral vector did not trigger inflammatory marker expression in iBAT or liver (Figures S2A, B), nor did it change iBAT morphology (Figure S2C). In summary, rAAV-Ucp1-Cre-miR122 confers Cre-mediated recombination selectively in BAT in a sex-independent manner without apparent side effects.

Visualization of rAAV-Ucp1-Cre-miR122-targeted cells in tdTomato-Cre reporter mice

To independently validate organ- and cell type-specificity, rAAV-Ucp1-Cre-miR122 was intravenously injected into tdTomato-Cre reporter mice [32]. The vector caused Cre-dependent recombination in a negligible number of liver cells (Figure 3, Figure S3), confirming effective miR122-dependent suppression of Cre expression. Cre activity was detected in most adipocytes in iBAT and axillary BAT (axBAT) depots but in fewer dcBAT adipocytes (Figure 3, Figure S3). Beyond BAT, a significant yet variable number of tdTomato-expressing cells was, surprisingly, found among multilocular adipocytes within mesenteric WAT (mesWAT) (Figure 3, Figure S3) whereas no or only sporadic tdTomato-positive cells were found in the other WAT depots, in skeletal muscle, heart, duodenum, ileum, pancreas, brain and kidney (Figure 3, Figure S3). Overall, this set of data confirmed efficient targeting of brown adipocytes resulting in functional expression of Cre recombinase.

Alterations of BAT metabolism by rAAV-mediated MLXIPL depletion and SLC2A1 (GLUT1) overexpression

To assess the metabolic consequences of Mlxipl deletion and hence DNL enzyme depletion, (Figure 2), we performed lipidomic analysis of iBAT from Mlxipl-flox mice treated with rAAV-Ucp1-Cre-miR122. Compared to Cre− controls, the relative abundance of the main DNL-derived fatty acids lauric acid (12:0), myristic acid (14:0) and myristoleic acid (14:1) in triglycerides (TG) were significantly lower in iBAT of both rAAV-Ucp1-Cre-miR122-treated Mlxipl-flox mice and transgenic Cre+ controls (Figure 4A). The major DNL product palmitoleic acid (16:1) was also lower in both groups, however, reached significance only in the Cre+ controls (Figure 4A), whereas palmitic acid (16:0) was not different between the groups. In contrast to the DNL fatty acids, the essential fatty acids linoleic acid (18:2) and linolenic acid (18:3) in TG were significantly higher in iBAT of rAAV-Ucp1-Cre-miR122 and transgenic Ucp1-Cre-mediated Mlxipl knockout mice (Figure 4A). Similarly, polyunsaturated fatty acids that are endogenously synthesized from essential fatty acids showed a trend for upregulation (Figure 4A). Changes in fatty acid pattern of phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) and phosphatidylcholine (PC) also followed the pattern of decreased DNL versus increased essential fatty acids (Figure S4A). Overall, the composition of the iBAT lipid pool was similar between rAAV-Ucp1-Cre-miR122 vector-treated and Cre+ transgenic control mice, indicating that a Mlxipl knockout intervention of 2 weeks is sufficient to reduce DNL in BAT.

To assess whether this novel vector can be utilized to achieve gain of function of proteins handling highly abundant metabolites, we designed a vector to overexpress the glucose transporter SLC2A1 (or GLUT1). Two weeks after single intravenous administration of the rAAV-Ucp1-Slc2a1-miR122 vector, we found strongly elevated Slc2a1 gene expression in iBAT (Figure 4B). Moreover, we observed a strong induction of Mlxipl isoform 2 (ChREBPβ) (Figure 4C), a surrogate marker of enhanced glucose metabolism [33], indicating that glucose transport was increased in SLC2A1-overexpressing BAT. One mouse (indicated by a red asterisk in Figure 4B–D) showed lower GLUT1 expression and Mlxipl isoform 2 response. This was a technical outlier caused by suboptimal injection, as clearly indicated by lower ITR copy numbers in this mouse (Figure S4B). Western blot analysis revealed that Slc2a1 overexpression in iBAT resulted in a prominent SLC2A1 protein band with apparent molecular weight of ca. 40 kDa (Figure 4D) and thus smaller than the expected size of ca. 54 kDa. Most likely, the overexpressed SLC2A1 is underglycosylated, as suggested by a previous study that found 40 kDa as size of SLC2A1 in human cells after treatment of cell lysates with glycosidases [34]. Despite the apparent underglycosylation, overexpressed SLC2A1 was functional, as demonstrated by markedly increased FASN protein (Figure 4E, Figure S4C) and higher uptake of radiolabelled deoxyglucose (Figure 4F) in iBAT of mice treated with rAAV-Ucp1-Slc2a1-miR122. The DNL enzyme FASN is downstream of SLC2A1, since its expression is under control of Mlxipl that is activated by high glucose influx [33]. It has to be mentioned that in liver of mice injected with rAAV-Ucp1-Slc2a1-miR122, Slc2a1 mRNA was moderately increased (Figure 4B). However, no Mlxipl isoform 2 response (Figure 4C), SLC2A1 protein induction (Figure 4D) or altered deoxyglucose uptake (Figure 4F) was detected in liver, showing that miR122 efficiently prevented translation and functional expression of the transgene in this organ. Tissue specificity was further supported by unaltered deoxyglucose uptake in WAT, muscle, heart, spleen and kidney (Figure S4D). Taken together, the rAAV vector with the Ucp1 enhancer-promoter and mmu-miR-122 target sequences can be used to perform loss-of-function and gain-and-function studies in brown adipocytes in vivo.

Discussion

In the present study, we describe a novel rAAV vector that can be systemically administered to achieve high and selective expression in murine BAT. This method of combining the three functionalities, Ucp1-promoter elements, Rec2 AAV serotype and mmu-miR-122 target sites is sufficient to manipulate energy metabolism in brown adipocytes without potential side effects such as unspecific expression of transgenes in the nervous system recently described for a widely distributed transgenic Ucp1-Cre line [15,17] or virus-induced inflammation as seen in adenoviral approaches. The low inflammation observed here is consistent with previous studies using other AAV serotypes that found no or only mild and transient inflammation in response to rAAV application, for example in the liver [35] or in BAT [19]. Low inflammation associated with the Rec2 serotype is a critical observation because high numbers of the rAAV particles are required for successful expression in BAT. It is important to note that the need for high rAAV titers as observed in this study is rather typical for rAAVs that exhibit generally low transduction efficiency [18].

An observation we made is that minor BAT depots such as dcBAT and scBAT are less efficiently targeted by Rec2 rAAVs, which limits the usefulness of the method to specifically study these depots. An explanation for the variable transduction efficiency could be lower blood flow in these minor BAT depots compared to iBAT. In line with this notion, evidence for lower blood flow in dcBAT compared to iBAT was reported recently [36]. However, in the interscapular BAT depot the recombination efficiency was astonishingly comparable to that of the transgenic Ucp1-cre line. This is a crucial finding because iBAT is the largest BAT depot [2,14], and together with the considerable transduction in the other BAT depots, this means that a large majority of brown adipocytes are reached by the vector and thus the study of systemic effects should be possible.

The rAAV method for expressing genes in brown adipocytes has clear advantages. First, injection into adult mice circumvents developmental effects of genetic intervention, providing an alternative for inducible transgenic models such as tamoxifen-dependent transgenic Ucp1-CreERT mice [37]. Second, animal breeding is reduced, since wild type mice can be employed for protein overexpression, and floxed mice for Cre-dependent recombination, which represents an important contribution to the 3R guiding principles for more ethical use of animals. Third, rAAV-Ucp1-Cre could be combined with other Cre-deleter strains such as adipoQ-Cre or Alb-Cre to study organ crosstalk. In addition, the rAAV-Ucp1-Cre-miR122 vector we generated appears to be more tissue-specific than the transgenic Ucp1-Cre mouse model [5] routinely used by many laboratories [7,8,[10], [11], [12], [13]]. These mice were recently reported to express Cre recombinase in ectopic sites relevant to metabolism such as the hypothalamus [15], and may by itself affect development and metabolism [17]. It needs to be taken into account, however, that the rAAV vectors used in the present study also show ectopic expression, for example in major WAT depots and intestinal cell types. Even when only observed in few cells of an organ, this may have undesired effects, especially when highly potent molecules such as endocrine hormones are overexpressed.

One aspect that was not addressed in the current study is the duration of expression imparted by the rAAV vector system. In general, rAAV vectors confer long-time expression up to several years [38]. In mouse muscle, rAAV-mediated expression lasted for more than a year [39] and in rat cardiomyocytes, expression was stable for at least three months [40]. Thus, it is likely that expression in BAT by the novel rAAV vector system is stable for several months, which needs, however, to be confirmed in future experiments.

When considering genetic intervention, the method would, for example, allow the expression of Flp recombinase [41] and Staphylococcus pyogenes Cas9 enzyme [42]. In conclusion, we validate a brown adipose tissue specific Rec2 rAAV approach and make it reproducible through the detailed information given in the methods part. In many countries the costs for transgenic mouse facilities rapidly increases and this new and inexpensive approach could open the path for researchers to study multiple transgene manipulation in brown adipose tissue without exponentially growing mouse colonies.
