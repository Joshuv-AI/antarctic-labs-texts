Arctigenin discovered by high-throughput screening ameliorates doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity as a novel natural KEAP1-NRF2 inhibitor
Authors: Xinyu Heng, Chao Ye, Shaohong Huang, Ke Zhang, Yankui Liu, Yueyue Wu, Ning Sun, Hai Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDoxorubicin (DOX), a widely used chemotherapeutic, is significantly limited by dose-dependent cardiotoxicity characterized by oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and ferroptosis. Despite decades of research, dexrazoxane (DXZ) remains the only approved cardioprotectant, yet its clinical utility is constrained by potential reduction in efficacy and systemic toxicity. Effective alternatives are therefore urgently needed. Through high-throughput screening of a 960-compound natural product library, we identified arctigenin (ATG), a major bioactive compound from the Asian medicinal plant Arctium lappa L., as a promising cardioprotective candidate against DOX-induced cardiotoxicity (DIC). Both in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated that ATG significantly attenuated DOX-induced cell death, reduced reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, inhibited ferroptosis, preserved mitochondrial function, and improved cardiac function and myocardial structural integrity in mice. Mechanistically, ATG activated the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2)-antioxidant response element (ARE) pathway by binding to the serine 602 (S602) residue of Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1), which competitively inhibited the KEAP1-NRF2 interaction. This binding facilitated the dissociation of NRF2 from KEAP1, resulting in reduced NRF2 ubiquitination, increased nuclear accumulation of NRF2, and enhanced transcription of downstream target genes. Collectively, our results identify ATG as a novel NRF2 activator that alleviates DIC through suppression oxidative stress and ferroptosis by directly targeting the KEAP1-NRF2-ARE axis.Keywords: Arctigenin, DOX-Induced cardiotoxicity, High-throughput screening, Oxidative stress, Ferroptosis, KEAP1-NRF2 protein-protein interaction (PPI)

Introduction

Doxorubicin (DOX), a cornerstone chemotherapeutic agent used for diverse solid tumors and hematologic malignancies, exhibits significant antitumor efficacy [1]. However, its clinical utility is severely limited by significant dose-dependent cardiotoxicity [2], characterized by progressive ventricular dilation, cardiac fibrosis, and ultimately heart failure [3]. Understanding the mechanisms underlying DOX-induced cardiotoxicity (DIC) is thus critical for improving cancer treatment outcomes. Currently, no effective clinical therapies prevent or alleviate DOX cardiotoxicity. Dexrazoxane (DXZ) remains the sole FDA-approved cardioprotectant [4], yet studies suggest it may compromise chemotherapeutic efficacy and induce adverse effects (e.g., hepatotoxicity, myelosuppression) at high doses [5]. Therefore, developing novel and low-toxicity cardioprotective agents against DIC is an urgent clinical need.

The precise mechanisms of DIC remain incompletely defined. Evidence implicates multiple pathways, including oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage, ferroptosis, and topoisomerase inhibition [6]. Oxidative stress is a pivotal trigger: the quinone moiety of DOX undergoes enzymatic reduction to a semiquinone, which rapidly transfers unpaired electrons to oxygen, generating substantial superoxide anion radicals [7]. DOX also selectively accumulates in cardiomyocyte (CM) mitochondria via affinity for inner membrane phospholipids [8], disrupting the respiratory chain, impairing electron transport, and amplifying reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. Moreover, DOX-iron complexes catalyze redox cycling, generating oxygen free radicals [9]. Elevated ROS oxidatively damage lipids, proteins, and DNA, compromising membrane integrity, including mitochondrial dysfunction, and culminating in CM death. Notably, recent evidence positions ferroptosis, an iron-dependent, lipid peroxidation driven cell death pathway, as a central executor in DIC pathogenesis [10]. Consequently, strategies targeting oxidative stress and ferroptosis represent promising approach to alleviate DIC.

Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1)-nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) is a crucial regulatory signaling pathway that protects against both endogenous and exogenous insults [11]. Under non-stress conditions, NRF2 predominantly resides in the cytoplasm, where it is continuously marked for degradation through interactions with the repressor protein KEAP1, and the Cullin 3 E3 ligase complex [12]. However, in response to stress or elevated levels of ROS, KEAP1 facilitates a reduction in NRF2 ubiquitination [13]. This stabilization enables NRF2 to translocate to the nucleus, where it binds to antioxidant response elements (AREs) within the regulatory regions of target genes that are essential for maintaining cellular redox balance, including quinone oxidoreductase 1 (Nqo1), heme oxygenase-1 (Hmox-1), solute carrier family 7 member 11 (Slc7a11) and glutathione peroxidase 4 (Gpx4), etc. [14]. Therefore, sustained activation of the NRF2 signaling pathway plays a pivotal role in the prevention and treatment of DIC.

Through high-throughput screening of a 960-compound natural product library for inhibitors of DOX-induced CM death, we identified arctigenin (ATG) as a potent candidate cardioprotectant. The efficacy of ATG was validated in multiple experimental models: human embryonic stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (hESC-CMs), H9C2 cells, and a murine DIC model. Crucially, we demonstrate that ATG directly binds the KEAP1 Kelch domain at serine 602 (S602). This binding competitively inhibits the KEAP1-NRF2 interaction, reducing NRF2 ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. This promotes the translocation of NRF2 into the nucleus and activates the transcription of antioxidant and anti-ferroptotic genes (e.g., GPX4, SLC7A11, NQO1, and HMOX1). As a result, ATG mitigates DOX-induced cardiac damage by concurrently suppressing oxidative stress and ferroptosis. The findings establish ATG as a promising therapeutic candidate for preventing oxidative stress and ferroptosis-mediated DIC.

Materials and methods

Reagents and antibodies

The natural product library was provided by Professor Yongjun Dang's research group (Chongqing Medical University, Chongqing, China). DOX (HY-15142), ATG (HY-N0035), DXZ (HY-76201), Cycloheximide (HY-12320), and MG-132 (HY-13259) were obtained from MedChemExpress (Shanghai, China). Puromycin (T19978) and Hygromycin B (T1022) were acquired from Topscience Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The following antibodies were used: anti-BCL-2-associated X protein (anti-BAX) (50599, ProteinTech, Wuhan, China), anti-B-cell lymphoma-2 (anti-BCL2) (26593, ProteinTech, Wuhan, China), anti-β-actin (66009, ProteinTech, Wuhan, China), anti-Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (60004, ProteinTech, Wuhan, China), anti-Histone H3 (anti-H3) (68345, ProteinTech, Wuhan, China), anti-NRF2 (HA721432, HuaBio, Hangzhou, China), anti-phosphorylated NRF2 (anti-p-NRF2) (ET1608, HuaBio, Hangzhou, China), anti-KEAP1 (HA721525, HuaBio, Hangzhou, China), anti-ACSL4 (ET7111-43, HuaBio, Hangzhou, China), anti-Solute carrier family 7 member 11 (anti-SLC7A11) (HA721868, HuaBio, Hangzhou, China), anti-heme oxygenase 1 (anti-HO-1) (ER1802, HuaBio, Hangzhou, China), anti-Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (phosphate) reduced form quinone dehydrogenase 1 (anti-NQO1) (ET1702, HuaBio, Hangzhou, China), anti-Glutathione peroxidase 4 (anti-GPX4) (ET1706-45, HuaBio, Hangzhou, China), anti-Ubiquitin (ET1608, HuaBio, Hangzhou, China), anti-FLAG (M20008, Abmart, Shanghai, China), and anti-Human influenza hemagglutinin (anti-HA) (M20003, Abmart, Shanghai, China).

Cell culture and human embryonic stem cell (hESC) differentiation

H9C2, AC16, HEK-293T, MCF-7, and HepG2 cells (Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shanghai, China) were cultured in DMEM (10-013-CV, Corning, New York, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, 2422662, Gibico, Waltham, MA, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere.

The hESC line H7 was maintained in mTeSR1 medium (85852, STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, Canada) on Matrigel (354277, BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA)-coated plates at 37 °C, with daily medium changes. Cells were passaged at 75%–85% confluency. Cardiac differentiation was performed as previously described [15]. CMs were purified by G418 selection, dissociated using collagenase I (SCR103, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) and 0.05% trypsin (25300, Gibco, New York, NY, USA), and seeded for experiments.

Plasmids and transfection

Full-length complementary DNAs (cDNAs) of NRF2 (HA-tagged) and KEAP1 (FLAG-tagged) were amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and subcloned into the pcDNA3.1 vector; empty pcDNA3.1 served as the control. KEAP1 and its mutants (FLAG-tagged) were similarly cloned into the pCDH vector (empty pCDH as control). Plasmids were transfected into HEK-293T cells using linear polyethylenimine (APExBIO, Houston, TX, USA) and into H9C2 cells using Neofect™ DNA transfection reagent (Neofect, Beijing, China), following manufacturers’ protocols.

High-throughput natural product screening

H9C2 cells (7500 cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates using a Multidrop Combi dispenser (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). After 24 h, cells were treated with 1 μM DOX alone or combined with 960 natural products (10 μM each) using an Explorer™ G3 Workstation (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). Following 24 h incubation, cellular adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP) production was quantified using the CellTiter-Glo® Luminescent Assay (G7570, Promega, Madison, WI, USA).

Cell viability detection assay

Cell viability was assessed via Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, C0005, Topscience). Cells (75% confluent in 96-well plates) were treated with compounds for 24 h, incubated with 10 μL CCK-8 solution for 2 h (37 °C/5% CO2), and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using the EnSight® Multimode Plate Reader (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA).

ATP production assay

ATP production was quantified using the CellTiter-Glo® Luminescent Assay (G7570, Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Cells (75% confluent in 96-well plates) were treated with compounds, followed by reagent addition and luminescence measurement using the EnSight® Multimode Plate Reader (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA).

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) detection assay

Cellular LDH release and murine serum LDH levels were measured using a Total Lactate Dehydrogenase Assay Kit (P0395S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The absorbance was measured using the EnSight® Multimode Plate Reader (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) at a wavelength of 450 nm.

Calcein-acetoxymethyl ester (AM)/propidium iodide (PI) staining

Calcein AM/PI double staining was performed to assess cell death using Calcein-AM/PI Double Staining Kit (C542, Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates and cultured until they reached approximately 75% confluence. Following treatment with DOX and ATG, the cells were labeled with Calcein AM and PI simultaneously for 30 min. Subsequently, fluorescence images were captured using the ECHO RVL2-K fluorescence microscope (ECHO, San Diego, CA, USA), and the fluorescence intensity was quantified using the EnSight® Multimode Plate Reader (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA).

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity, malondialdehyde (MDA) contents, and glutathione (GSH) contents detection assay

SOD activity, MDA levels, and GSH levels in cells and heart tissue were measured using the Superoxide Dismutase Assay Kit (S0101, Beyotime, Shanghai, China), Lipid Peroxidation MDA Assay Kit (S0131, Beyotime, Shanghai, China), and GSH Assay kit (S0053, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The SOD activity, MDA level, and GSH level for each sample were calculated by constructing a standard curve.

ROS detection assay

Intracellular ROS was detected using a ROS Assay Kit (S0033, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Cells treated with DOX/ATG were incubated with 2',7'-Dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) and Hoechst 33342 (S1022, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 30 min (37 °C), washed with phosphate buffered solution (PBS), and imaged using the ECHO RVL2-K fluorescence microscope (ECHO, San Diego, CA, USA).

Mitochondrial ROS (MitoROS) detection assay

MitoROS was measured using MitoSOX™ Red (MT14, Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan). Cells treated with DOX/ATG were incubated with MitoSOX™ and Hoechst 33342 for 30 min (37 °C), washed with PBS, and imaged using the ECHO RVL2-K fluorescence microscope (ECHO, San Diego, CA, USA).

Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) detection assay

MMP was assessed using JC-1 (C2003, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Cells treated with DOX/ATG were incubated with JC-1 for 30 min (37 °C), washed, and imaged using the ECHO RVL2-K fluorescence microscope (ECHO, San Diego, CA, USA). Mitochondrial damage was quantified as red/green fluorescence ratio.

Flow cytometry-based lipid peroxidation detection assay

Cells treated with DOX ± ATG for 24 h were incubated with 5 μM C11-BODIPY581/591 (L267, Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) for 30 min (37 °C), washed, trypsinized, resuspended in PBS/5% FBS, and analyzed via flow cytometry (BD LSRFortessa, FL1 channel, San Jose, CA, USA).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Cells were treated with DOX or co-treated with DOX plus ATG for 24 h. Then, cell samples were fixed in 3% phosphate-glutaraldehyde and stored at 4 °C in the dark overnight. The sections were dehydrated and stained with lead citrate and uranyl acetate for measurement. Finally, the samples were viewed using a Talos L120C TEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Animals and treatments

The animal care and experimental methods adhered to the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the National Institutes of Health (NIH Publication, revised 2011) and received approval from the Animal Care and Use Committee of Jiangnan University (Wuxi, China) (Approval No.: JN. No20230915c0601030 (241), Approval Date: September 15th, 2023). C57BL/6J male mice (8 weeks old) were purchased from GemPharmatech and fed adaptively for one week prior to experimentation. All mice were housed under specific pathogen-free, environmentally controlled conditions (Temperature: 20–25 °C; Humidity: 50% ± 5%) in individually ventilated cages, with unrestricted access to water and food.

Mice were randomly divided into five groups (n = 8): Saline-treated group (CON), DOX-treated group (DOX), 20 mg/kg ATG-treated group (LATG), 40 mg/kg ATG-treated group (HATG) and DXZ-treated group (DXZ, Positive control). The modeling of DIC and the dosage of drugs refer to the previous methods [16,17]. DOX was dissolved in sterile saline, while ATG was dissolved in corn oil. To generate DIC, mice were received intraperitoneal injections of DOX at a dose of 5 mg/kg/week for four weeks. The control group received equal volume of saline. Mice in LATG and HATG groups were administered ATG orally at doses of 20 mg/kg and 40 mg/kg, respectively, while the other groups received the same volume of corn oil. Mice in the DXZ group were injected intraperitoneally with DXZ at a dose of 50 mg/kg/week for four weeks, with the other groups receiving an equivalent volume of saline.

To evaluate the potential cardioprotective effect of ATG related to KEAP1, adeno-associated virus-scramble (AAV-scr), AAV-Keap1WT, and AAV-Keap1S602A were respectively injected through the tail vein of mice for 3 weeks to construct KEAP1 overexpression mice.

Cardiac function assessment by echocardiography

Mice were anesthetized via inhalation of isoflurane, maintained at a concentration of 1% to 1.5%. Echocardiography in mice was performed using a High-Resolution in vivo Imaging System (Vevo3100LT, FUJIFILM, Toronto, Canada). Echocardiographic M-mode images were acquired to calculate critical parameters, including left ventricle ejection fraction and fractional shortening, which serve as indicators of cardiac performance and function.

Histopathologic analysis

The heart tissue was treated with 4% paraformaldehyde fixation for 24 h, and then embedded in paraffin. The embedded sections were cut to a thickness of 5 μm for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) (C0105, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and masson's trichrome (Masson) staining (G1006, ServiceBio, Wuhan, China). The staining was performed according to the standard procedures following the manufacturer's recommendations.

Serum creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB) contents detection assay

Whole blood samples from mice were incubated at room temperature for 2 h and subsequently centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 20 min to isolatethe supernatant. The serum CK-MB levels were quantified using a Creatine Kinase MB Isoenzyme Assay Kit (H197, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China), in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

IHC staining was employed to assess the expression of 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), SLC7A11, GPX4, ACSL4, and the p-NRF2 entry into the nucleus in cardiac tissues. The sections underwent sequential treatment with citric acid buffer (10 min), 3% hydrogen peroxide (10 min), 10% goat serum (1 h), and then, tissues were incubated with the corresponding primary antibody overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with the anti-mouse/rabbit EnVision™+/horseradish peroxidase (HRP) reagent (PK10006, ProteinTech, Wuhan, China) for 1 h at 37 °C. After visualization with diaminobenzidine at room temperature, the positive targets were examined using a light microscope.

Western blot

Cells/tissues were lysed in RIPA buffer with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentration was determined via bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (23225, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA), separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes, blocked with 5% skim milk (1 h, room temperature), incubated with primary antibodies (overnight, 4 °C) and HRP-conjugated secondaries (1 h, room temperature), and detected by enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) (Tanon imaging system, Shanghai, China).

Reversed transcription-quantification polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from tissues or cells using TRIzol (Vazyme, R411-01, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA was quantified by a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using HiScript IV All-in-One Ultra RT SuperMix (Vazyme, R433-01, Nanjing, China). With (Acidic ribosomal phosphoprotein P0, 36B4) gene serving as the reference gene, the relative gene expression was calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences used in the analysis are listed in Table 1.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis

Whole-genome gene expression analysis was performed using hESC-CMs treated with DOX (n = 3) or co-treated with DOX plus ATG (n = 3). The total RNA was extracted using Trizol (Vazyme, R411-01) and stored at −80 °C. RNA isolation, quality control, library construction, and sequencing were performed by the Shanghai OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (www.oebiotech.com/, Shanghai, China). A P value < 0.05 and a fold change ≥1.2 were set as thresholds for significant differential expression. The hierarchical cluster analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) proved the expression patterns of genes in different groups and samples. Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis was performed on DEGs based on the hypergeometric distribution algorithm to screen for significant enrichment functional entries. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using cluster Profiler package. All codes are available from the corresponding author upon request.

Biotin-assisted pull-down and liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis

Biotinylated ATG (ATG-biotin) was synthesized by Chaoyuan Yifang (Shanghai) Biomedical Technology Co., LTD (Shanghai, China). ATG was mixed with potassium carbonate and sodium iodide, followed by dropwise addition of propargyl bromide. The mixture was stirred overnight and purified by chromatography. The intermediate was treated with copper(I) iodide and N,N-diisopropylethylamine, and stirred overnight. The final product was isolated by chromatography. hESC-CM lysates were incubated with biotin or ATG-biotin (3 h, 4 °C), pulled down with streptavidin beads, separated by SDS-PAGE, and stained (Coomassie). Specific bands were excised, digested, and analyzed by LC-MS/MS. ATG-biotin and KEAP1 binding was confirmed by Western blot.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking of ATG (PubChem CID: 64981) to the target proteins KEAP1 (PDB ID: 6TYM), FMR1 (PDB ID: 6DC6), LBR (PDB ID: 6V8E), HNRNPL (PDB ID: 6WZF), and DBN1 (PDB ID: 6TEC) was performed using Schrödinger Maestro 11.4. KEAP1 protein preparation was carried out using the Protein Preparation Wizard module, which included the addition of hydrogen atoms, removal of water molecules and hetero groups (specifically GOL and DMS molecules), followed by energy minimization with the OPLS2005 forcefield until a root mean square deviation (RMSD) threshold of 0.30 Å was reached. The receptor grid was generated using the Receptor Grid Generation tool by centering the grid box on the centroid of the co-crystallized ligand 08A within its binding pocket. The dimensions of the grid box were set to 20 Å × 20 Å × 20 Å to encompass the entire binding site. Similar procedures were applied to prepare the other proteins.

The 2D structure of ATG was obtained from PubChem and processed using the LigPrep module to generate energetically minimized 3D conformations with the OPLS2005 forcefield (RMSD cutoff 0.30 Å). Molecular docking was performed using the Glide module in extra precision (XP) mode, which evaluates ligand binding through geometric and energetic complementarity with the receptor. The resulting docking poses were ranked according to their Glide XP GScore, and the binding energy data were derived directly from these outputs. All molecular graphics were prepared using PyMOL (Version 2.5).

Docking of ATG (PubChem) to proteins (RCSB PDB) was performed using Schrödinger Maestro 11.4. Proteins were prepared (hydrogenation, water/ligand removal, energy minimization), and grids were generated. ATG was minimized (LigPrep) and docked (Glide XP mode). Figures were generated in PyMol.

Expression and purification of KEAP1 protein

Human Keap1 and Keap1-S602A mutant cDNA were respectively cloned into pET-32b (+)-TrxA-His and expressed in E. Coli BL21(DE3) (0.5 mM IPTG, 16 °C, 12 h). The TrxA-HIS-KEAP1 protein was initially purified by Ni-NTA chromatography (C600791, Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China), followed by enterokinase (P4237, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) digestion to cleave the TrxA-His tag. The tag was subsequently removed via a second Ni-NTA chromatography step, and the target protein was finally concentrated using an ultrafiltration device (UFC903096, Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). Protein purity was confirmed by SDS-PAGE.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assay

SPR (Biacore T200, Cytiva, Uppsala, Sweden) used a CM5 chip immobilized with KEAP1 protein or KEAP1-S602A mutant (amine coupling, pH 4.0). ATG (0.5% DMSO in PBS) was flowed at varying concentrations; affinity was calculated using Biacore T200 software.

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

The hESC-CMs pretreated with ATG (30 μM) or DMSO (12 h) were lysed, aliquoted, heated (40–70 °C, 4 min), cooled (4 °C, 3 min), centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 30 min), and analyzed by Western blot. For exogenous binding, lysates from HEK-293T cells transfected with wild-type (WT) or mutant Keap1 were incubated with ATG (100 μM) or DMSO (2 h, room temperature) and processed identically.

Drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) assay

HEK-293T cells transfected with WT/Mutant Keap1 were lysed, incubated with ATG (2 h), treated with pronase (100 μg/mL, 15 min, 37 °C), quenched with SDS-loading buffer (100 °C, 5 min), and analyzed by Western blot.

CO-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

H9C2 cells transfected with pcDNA3.1-Flag-Keap1 plasmids were lysed in 0.5% NP-40 buffer with protease inhibitors. Lysates were centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 15 min), incubated with Flag-beads (overnight, 4 °C), washed three times with PBS buffer, and analyzed by Western blot.

Ubiquitination assay

H9C2 cells transfected with pcDNA3.1-HA-Nrf2/Flag-Keap1 plasmids were treated with ATG (8 h) and MG132 (4 h). Ubiquitination was analyzed by Co-IP/Western blot. Cell lysates were treated by CO-IP and ubiquitination results were analyzed by Western blotting.

Cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay

The hESC-CMs treated with DMSO or ATG (6 h) were incubated with CHX (100 μg/mL). Lysates were collected at 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, and 60 min for Western blot.

Separation of nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins

Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (P0028, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was used to separate hESC-CMs cytoplasm and nuclear protein according to the manufacturer's instructions. Under low osmotic pressure, the cells expand sufficiently, then the cell membrane was destroyed, and cytoplasmic proteins were released. The nucleus was then precipitated by centrifugation. Last, nuclear protein was extracted by high-salt nuclear protein extraction reagent.

ARE-luciferase reporter assay

H9C2 cells were seeded in clear-bottom white 96-well plates (FCP963, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and cultured to 60% confluence. Cells were transfected with pGL4.37-ARE-luc2p and pRL-TK plasmids. After 24 h, DOX or ATG were administered for 24 h. Luminescence was quantified using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (E2920, Promega, Madison, WI, USA) on the EnSight® Multimode Plate Reader (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA).

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence staining was used to detect the expression and location of NRF2 in hESC-CMs. In brief, cells were plated on glass coverslips within 48-well plates and then underwent sequential treatment with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, 0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 min, and 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against NRF2 (1:400). Afterward, the cells were incubated with fluorescence-labeled secondary antibodies (1:1000) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclear staining was conducted using DAPI (1:1000). Immunofluorescence images were captured using the ECHO RVL2-K fluorescence microscope (ECHO, San Diego, CA, USA), and fluorescence intensity was analyzed with ImageJ software.

Keap1 stable knockdown cell construction

The short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting sequences of human and rat Keap1 genes were respectively designed through the website (www.sigmaaldrich.cn). Lentiviral particles (psPAX2/pMD2.G + pLKO.1-Puro-shKeap1 or control) were produced in HEK-293T cells, used to infect target cells, and selected with puromycin (10 μg/mL). Knockdown was confirmed by Western blot. The shRNA sequences are shown in Table 2.

Keap1 mutant stably expressing cell construction

WT and S602A mutant pCDH-Flag-Keap1 plasmids were constructed using the point mutation method based on human and rat keap1 messenger RNA (mRNA), respectively, and the control plasmid was pCDH. Lentivirus carrying pCDH-Flag-Keap1WT or pCDH-Flag-Keap1S602A was transduced into Keap1-knockdown H9C2/AC16 cells and selected with hygromycin B (500 μg/mL).

Adeno-associated virus serotype 9 (AAV9) vector packaging

The AAV-Keap1 vector has been acquired through Shanghai Genechem Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). A mouse Keap1 (NM_016679) sequence was created and then cloned into the pAAV9-cTNT plasmid to create the pAAV-scramble, pAAV-Keap1WT, and pAAV-Keap1S602A plasmids. Viral vectors were produced by transfecting AAV-293 cells, and purification of the virus particles was achieved using the iodixanol gradient approach.

Scratch wound healing assay

MCF-7 or HepG2 cells were cultured in 24-well plates until reaching confluence. Cells were wounded by a 200 μL plastic pipette tip to create a “scratch model”. Then, cells were were treated with DOX (1 μM) or DOX (1 μM) + ATG (10 or 50 μM). Images of the cells were captured at indicated time points by the ECHO microscope (ECHO, San Diego, CA, USA). Migration areas were analyzed by the Image J software.

3D multicellular tumor spheroids (MCTSs) assay

High-throughput screening identifies ATG as a potent protective agent against DOX-induced CM death

Given that the most significant adverse effect of DOX in vitro is cardiotoxicity mediated through CM death [2], we hypothesized that co-treatment with cytoprotective agents could mitigate DOX-induced cell death. To test this hypothesis, we established a high-throughput drug screening system using 96-well plates, with ATP production in H9C2 cells as the readout (Fig. 1A). A library of 960 natural products was screened by treating H9C2 cells with each compound at a concentration of 10 μM in combination with DOX. The primary screening identified 13 compounds that increased ATP production by more than 25%. Among these, ATG emerged as the most potent and effective candidate, consistently demonstrating protective effects against DOX-induced CMs death induced (Figs. 1B and C). As a result, ATG was selected as the focus for the subsequent phases of this study.

To validate the accuracy and validity of our screening results, we further treated H9C2 cells with DOX and evaluated the dose-dependent effects of ATG on cardiotoxicity induced by DOX. Cytotoxicity assays demonstrated that ATG itself exhibited low toxicity toward cardiomyocyte viability, with a half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of 420.3 ± 2.94 μM (Fig. 1D), which is substantially higher than the screening concentration of 10 μM. Both CCK-8 and ATP production assays indicated that the protective effect of ATG was dose-dependent at concentrations up to 20 μM (Figs. 1E and F). Calcein-AM/PI staining further confirmed that ATG significantly enhanced cell survival and reduced cell death induced by DOX (Figs. 1G–I). Furthermore, ATG significantly upregulated the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein BCL2 while inhibiting the expression of the pro-apoptotic protein BAX (Figs. 1J and K). All together, these results indicate that ATG effectively inhibits DOX-induced cardiomyocyte death.

Protective effects of ATG against DIC in hESC-CMs

Given the significant functional and physiological differences between H9C2 cell lines and human cardiomyocytes, we engineered a human embryonic stem cell (hESC) line (H7) by inserting a neomycin selection cassette following the last exon of the MYL2 gene (cardiac myosin light chain-2v), designating this cell line as MYL2Neo/WT-H7. Following cardiac-specific differentiation and G418 selection, we consistently obtained hESC-derived cardiomyocytes (hESC-CMs) with over 98% purity (Fig. 2A). These hESC-CMs were used to evaluate the protective efficacy of ATG against DIC. Cytotoxicity assays revealed that ATG had low toxicity in hESC-CMs, with an IC50 of 465.6 ± 3.68 μM (Fig. 2B). We optimized cell plating densities and DOX concentrations to achieve approximately 50% cell death within 24 h (Fig. 2C). Using CCK-8, ATP production and LDH release assays, we found that DOX significantly reduced cell viability, this reduction was dose-dependently attenuated by ATG co-treatment (Figs. 2D–F). Calcein-AM/PI staining further confirmed that ATG markedly suppressed DOX-induced cardiomyocyte death (Figs. 2G–I). Moreover, ATG counteracted DOX-induced cell death by upregulating the BCL2 protein expression and downregulation BAX protein expression (Figs. 2J and K). Taken together, these results indicate that ATG provides significant protection against DIC in human cardiomyocytes.

Cardioprotective effects of ATG against DIC in mice

Chronic DOX accumulation is a well-established cause of heart failure in humans [18]. To investigate the cardioprotective effects of ATG against DIC in vivo, we established a classic chronic DIC mouse model based on previously published protocols (Fig. 3A). DOX administration significantly reduced murine food intake in mice; however, co-treatment with ATG effectively reversed this decrease (Fig. 3B). High-resolution echocardiography revealed significantly impaired cardiac function in DIC mice compared to the controls, characterized by markedly reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and left ventricular fractional shortening (LVFS). Strikingly, ATG provided protection comparable to DXZ, significantly attenuating the DOX-induced decline in cardiac function after four weeks of treatment (Figs. 3C–E). Cardiac atrophy observed in DOX-treated mice was also ameliorated by ATG administration (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, the heart weight (HW)-to-tibia length (TL) ratio was significantly decreased in the DOX group, a reduction that was prevented by both ATG and DXZ treatments (Fig. 3F).

To assess myocardial damage, we analyzed biomarkers of cardiac injury and histopathological alterations. Biochemical analyses demonstrated that both ATG and DXZ significantly suppressed the DOX-induced elevation in serum CK-MB and LDH levels (Figs. 3G and H). Histopathological evaluation using H&E and Masson staining revealed myocardial disorganization, perivascular fibrosis, and interstitial fibrosis in DOX-treated mice compared to controls. These pathological changes were significantly ameliorated following ATG or DXZ administration (Figs. 3C and I). Subsequently, we investigated whether ATG mitigates DOX-induced myocardial injury by inhibiting cardiac cell death in vivo. ATG treatment reduced BAX protein expression while increasing BCL2 protein levels compared to the DOX-treated mice, consequently lowering the BAX/BCL2 ratio (Figs. 3J–M). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that ATG protects against cardiotoxicity in a murine model of chronic DIC, supporting its potential as a novel therapeutic agent for DOX-induced cardiac injury.

ATG ameliorates DIC via transcriptional upregulation of antioxidant defenses and suppression of ferroptosis pathways

In vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated that ATG significantly ameliorated DOX-induced cardiac injury and restored cardiac function. To elucidate the underlying biological processes responsible for the cardioprotective effect of ATG, we conducted an RNA-seq analysis to explore the obviously changed genes by treatment of DOX alone or co-treatment with ATG. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct transcriptional clustering between DOX-treated and ATG-co-treated group (Fig. 4A), indicating that ATG induces global transcriptional reprogramming in the context of DOX treatment. Comparative analysis identified 6361 DEGs in ATG + DOX and DOX groups, including 3759 upregulated and 2602 downregulated genes (Figs. 4B and C). Consistent with established mechanisms of DIC pathogenesis, we and others have previously demonstrated that DOX treatment (vs. Control) significantly dysregulates ferroptosis and oxidative stress pathways, characterized by downregulation of key genes such Gpx4, Slc7a11, and Hmox1 [10,[19], [20], [21]]. In the present study, KEGG enrichment analysis of these DEGs highlighted ATG's modulation of critical pathways involved in DIC, including oxidative phosphorylation, ROS homeostasis, ferroptosis, NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, and cytochrome P450 metabolism, all influenced by ATG (Fig. 4D). Volcano plot analysis confirmed ATG co-treatment upregulation of Hmox1, Nqo1, Gpx4, Slc7a11, and others protective genes (Fig. 4E).

GSEA further corroborated these findings, showing significant enrichment of oxidative stress- and ferroptosis-related genes sets in the ATG-co-treated cells (Figs. 4F and G). Collectively, these results suggest that ATG counteracts DIC by transcriptionally reversing the suppression of antioxidant defenses and ferroptosis suppressor pathways induced by DOX.

ATG alleviates oxidative stress and ferroptosis in cardiomyocytes through modulation of the KEAP1-NRF2 axis

Previous studies have demonstrated that excessive ROS induce oxidative stress damage and ferroptosis, contributing to DOX-induced cardiac injury [22]. Based on transcriptome analysis results, we further investigated the mechanisms underlying the antioxidant and anti-ferroptotic effects of ATG in hESC-CMs. DOX treatment significantly reduced SOD activity and GSH levels compared to controls, whereas co-treatment with ATG restored both parameters in a dose-dependent manner (Figs. 5A and B). ATG also significantly attenuated DOX-induced increases in MDA, a key lipid peroxidation marker (Fig. 5C). Consistent with these findings, DOX markedly elevated both intracellular ROS and mitoROS, indicating pronounced cellular and mitochondrial oxidative stress, which were significantly mitigated by ATG co-treatment (Figs. S1A, S1B, 5D, and 5E). Furthermore, ATG robustly suppressed DOX-induced lipid peroxidation, as measured by oxidized 4,4-difluoro-boradiazaindacene (BODIPY) fluorescence (Figs. 5F and G). JC-1 staining revealed a disruption in MMP in DOX-treated cells, indicated by an increase in green monomer relative to red aggregates (indicative of polarized MMP in controls). This loss of MMP was significantly restored by ATG co-treatment (Figs. S1C and D). TEM assay further revealed that ATG ameliorated DOX-induced mitochondrial damage, including reduced cristae density and distorted cristae morphology (Fig. 5H). These results demonstrate ATG preserves mitochondrial integrity by suppressing superoxide generation and lipid peroxidation.

The KEAP1-NRF2-ARE pathway represents a central defense mechanism against oxidative stress and ferroptosis [11]. Expression levels of NRF2 and downstream targets (HMOX1, NQO1, SLC7A11, GPX4) serve as key biomarkers of pathway activation. Mechanistically, oxidative stress triggers protein kinase C (PKC)-mediated NRF2 phosphorylation at Serine 40, enabling NRF2 dissociation from KEAP1 and subsequent nuclear translocation. Western blot analysis confirmed that ATG treatment reversed DOX-induced downregulation of total NRF2, phosphorylated NRF2 protein and its downstream proteins, including NQO1, HO-1, GPX4 and SLC7A11. ATG also suppressed the DOX-induced upregulation of ACSL4, a central mediator of lipid peroxidation (Figs. 5I–K). Together, these findings demonstrate that ATG activates the KEAP1-NRF2-ARE signaling axis to counteract oxidative stress and ferroptosis in DOX-treated hESC-CMs.

ATG mitigates DOX-induced cardiac oxidative stress and ferroptosis in mice

To evaluate myocardial oxidative stress injury and ferroptosis in vivo, we measured the levels of SOD, MDA and GSH. Compared with the controls, mice in the DOX model group exhibited significantly increased MDA levels, along with decreased SOD activity and reduced GSH content. ATG treatment significantly attenuated MDA generation and restored SOD and GSH levels (Figs. 6A–C). Excessive ROS production promotes lipid peroxidation, leading to the accumulation of toxic byproducts such as 4-HNE. IHC staining of cardiac tissues revealed a substantial increase in 4-HNE expression following DOX exposure, which was markedly suppressed by ATG treatment (Figs. 6D and E). qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated that DOX downregulated the mRNA expression of Nrf2, Hmox1, Nqo1, Slc7a11 and Gpx4, while upregulating the expression of Acsl4. ATG treatment robustly reversed these transcriptional alterations (Fig. 6F). These findings were further supported at the protein level by Western blot analysis, which confirmed that DOX treatment suppressed the expression of NFR2, HO-1, NQO1, SLC7A11, and GPX4, but increased ACSL4. Notably, ATG treatment effectively rescued the expression of these proteins (Figs. 6G and H). Since p-NRF2 translocate to the nucleus to initiate the transcription of downstream genes (including Nqo1, Hmox1, Slc7a11 and Gpx4), we examined its subcellular localization using IHC staining. DOX treatment inhibited NRF2 phosphorylation, while ATG administration significantly increased p-NRF2 levels and promoted its nuclear accumulation (Fig. 6I). Consistent with Western blot results, IHC staining revealed that the protein levels of SLC7A11 and GPX4 were downregulated by DOX administration, both of which were restored following ATG treatment (Figs. 6J and K). Collectively, these results demonstrate that ATG alleviates DOX-induced cardiac oxidative stress and ferroptosis in vivo by activating the NRF2-ARE signaling axis.

KEAP1 is identified as a direct target of ATG

To identify the molecular targets of ATG, we conjugated biotin to ATG (Figs. S2A–C) and performed affinity purification of ATG-binding proteins from cardiomyocyte lysates using the biotin-labeled probe, followed by LC-MS/MS proteomic analysis (Figs. 7A and B). ATG-biotin retained cytoprotective activity comparable to unmodified ATG in DOX-treated cardiomyocytes (Fig. S2D), confirming its functional validity. Pull-down assays and Coomassie brilliant blue staining experiments revealed a prominent protein band at approximately 70 kDa (Fig. 7B). In-gel digestion and LC-MS/MS analysis identified several candidate binding proteins within this band, including KEAP1, heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein L (HNRNPL), drebrin (DBN1), lamin-B receptor (LBR), and fragile X messenger ribonucleoprotein 1 (FMR1) (Table S1). Molecular docking analysis to assess predicted binding energy (ΔG) indicated KEAP1 as the top candidate direct target of ATG (Fig. S2E). This interaction was further validated by Western blotting using a KEAP1 specific antibody (Fig. 7C). Competitive pull-down assays with ATG-biotin and increasing concentrations of unlabeled ATG showed a dose-dependent reduction in the amount of KEAP1 pulled down (Fig. S2F), supporting its binding specificity. Both endogenous and exogenous CETSA, demonstrated that ATG treatment enhanced the thermal stability of KEAP1 compared to the vehicle-treated group across a range of temperatures, which implied that ATG bound to KEAP1 (Figs. 7D and E). To further confirm the direct binding of ATG with KEAP1, full-length KEAP1 was expressed and purified (Fig. S2G). SPR analysis confirmed specific high-affinity binding between ATG and KEAP1, with an equilibrium dissociation constant (affinity constant (KD) = 22.0 μM) (Fig. 7F).

To determine whether the protective effects of ATG against DIC in vitro are KEAP1-dependent, we generated Keap1-knockdown H9C2 and AC16 cardiomyocytes using shRNA-expressing lentivirus. Western blot analysis confirmed efficient KEAP1 knockdown (Fig. S2H). These cells were then treated with DOX alone or in combination with ATG. Moreover, ATG treatment did not further improve protective effects of ATG in KEAP1-knockdown cells exposed to DOX (Figs. 7G–I). Taken together, these results indicate that ATG protects against DIC by directly binding KEAP1, and that its cardioprotective effects are KEAP1-dependent.

ATG directly binds with S602 of KEAP1, inhibits NRF2 degradation and promotes NRF2 translocation into nucleus

Previous studies have established that KEAP1 regulates the ubiquitination of NRF2 [12]. However, whether ATG influences the KEAP1-NRF2 interaction remained unclear. Notably, Co-IP assay confirmed that ATG decreases the interaction between KEAP1 and NRF2 in H9C2 cells (Fig. 8A). We observed that ATG treatment reduced the ubiquitination levels of NRF2 (Fig. 8B) and slowed degradation rate of NRF2 following CHX treatment compared to controls (Fig. 8C). Moreover, ATG promoted nuclear translocation of NRF2 and activated downstream AREs (Figs. 8D and E, S3A, and S3B). Molecular docking predicted that ATG binds to KEAP1 by forming two hydrogen bonds with the carbonyl group interacting with residues S602 and the oxygen atom interacting with Gln530 (Q530) (Figs. 8F and S3C). To validate these predictions, we performed a DARTS assay in HEK293T cells overexpressing WT or site mutants KEAP1. DARTS results revealed that the S602A mutation abolished ATG binding, while Q530E mutation had minimal effect, indicating S602 as the critical binding residue for ATG (Fig. 8G). This finding was supported by CETSA, in which ATG failed to stabilize the KEAP1-S602A mutant against thermal denaturation compared to WT KEAP1 (Figs. 8H and I, S3D, and S3E). We further purified the recombinant KEAP1S602A mutant protein (Fig. S3F) and performed SPR analysis, which revealed markedly reduced binding affinity between ATG and the KEAP1S602A mutant protein, with an equilibrium dissociation constant (affinity constant (KD) = 5300 μM) (Fig. 8J). We also examined the binding of ATG to other serine active sites in the Kelch domain of KEAP1. The DARTS results indicated that ATG does not bind to other serine active sites, further confirming its highly specificity for the S602 site (Fig. S3G). As summarized in Fig. S3H, ATG directly binds to the S602 residue of KEAP1, disrupts KEAP1-NRF2 interaction, attenuates NRF2 ubiquitination and degradation, and thereby facilitates NRF2 nuclear translocation and subsequent activation of antioxidant and ferroptotic suppressor genes.

ATG directly binds with S602 of KEAP1 to activate the KEAP1-NRF2-ARE signaling axis in DIC

The interaction between KEAP1and NRF2 plays a central role in regulating NRF2-mediated antioxidant responses and ferroptosis, making it a promising therapeutic target for DIC and various other diseases [23]. To determine whether the ATG-binding site on KEAP1 is essential for ATG's cardioprotective effects, we reintroduced either WT or mutant (S602A) KEAP1 plasmids into KEAP1-knockdown H9C2 and AC16 cardiomyocytes to generate stable rescue cell lines (Fig. 9A). Notably, the protective effect of ATG against DIC was significantly diminished in cells expressing the S602A mutant KEAP1 compared to those expressing WT KEAP1 (Fig. 9B). Furthermore, ATG failed to reduced DOX-induced mitoROS accumulation in the KEAP1 mutant group relative to the WT group (Figs. 9C and D). Finally, ATG was unable to activate the NRF2 signaling pathway in KEAP1 mutant cells, as demonstrated by protein expression analyses (Figs. 9E–G). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that ATG exerts its cardioprotective effects by specifically binding to the S602 residue of KEAP1 in vitro.

To further investigate whether the protective effects of ATG against DIC in vivo depend on its interaction with KEAP1, we utilized cardiac-specific AAV9 to overexpress either WT KEAP1 or the ATG-binding deficient S602 mutant KEAP1 in mouse hearts, followed by an assessment of ATG's therapeutic efficacy (Fig. 10A). Western blot analysis confirmed the successful cardiac-specific overexpression of both KEAP1 variants (Fig. 10B). After the overexpression of WT KEAP1 and mutant S602 KEAP1 in the hearts of mice, a significant difference in average food intake was observed between the two groups (Fig. 10C). Compared to the WT group, the effects of ATG in improving the HW-to-TL ratio were absent in the mutant group (Fig. 10D). Following ATG treatment, serum levels of CK-MB and LDH did not decrease significantly in the S602 KEAP1 group (Figs. 10E and F). High-resolution echocardiography demonstrated that ATG treatment preserved the protective effects on LVEF and LVFS only in the WT group (Figs. 10G–I). Furthermore, H&E and Masson staining revealed that, compared to the WT group, the improvement effect of ATG on myocardial disorders and increased perivascular and interstitial fibrosis in mice treated with DOX was significantly reduced in the mutant S602 KEAP1 group (Figs. 10G and J). Collectively, these in vivo findings demonstrate that the cardioprotective effects of ATG against DIC are strictly depend on its binding to the S602 residue on KEAP1.

Discussion

Despite decades of investigation, no consensus exists on effective strategies to prevent or treat DIC. Although several antioxidants (e.g., vitamin E, N-acetyl cysteine) and natural compounds (e.g., resveratrol) have shown promise in preclinical studies, their clinical translation has been hampered by limited efficacy and off-target effects [24,25]. Therefore, the identification of novel therapeutic targets and more effective pharmacological interventions remains imperative.

In this study, we employed high-throughput screening of a natural products library and identified ATG, a natural lignan compound derived from burdock seeds (Arctium lappa L., Asteraceae), as a promising novel candidate for mitigating DIC in both in vitro and in vivo models. The principal findings of this study are as follows: 1) ATG administration effectively ameliorated DIC; 2) ATG attenuated DIC by inhibiting oxidative stress and ferroptosis; 3) ATG suppressed oxidative stress and ferroptosis in cardiomyocytes via activation of the KEAP1-NRF2-ARE pathway; 4) ATG facilitated NRF2 dissociation from KEAP1 and subsequent nuclear translocation by competitively binding to the S602 residue on KEAP1. Furthermore, the protective efficacy of ATG at a dosage of 40 mg/kg/day was found to be partially superior to that of DXZ in ameliorating DOX-induced myocardial damage, highlighting its potential for practical and clinical application in DIC treatment.

ATG is a natural lignan compound extracted from t burdock seeds (Arctium lappa L., Asteraceae), a traditional medicinal food in Asia [17]. Previous studies have attributed ATG's bioactivity to its anti-inflammatory, anticancer, and antioxidant properties [26,27], which are mediated through the modulation of pathways such as AMPK/SIRT1 [28], PP2A [29]. Notably, Chen et al. [30] found that ATG significantly suppresses the malignant phenotypes of cancer cells (such as those in lung cancer and gastric cancer) by potently inhibiting UBC12 enzyme activity and cullin neddylation. Shi et al. [31] demonstrated that ATG attenuates breast cancer progression by suppressing GM-CSF/TSLP/STAT3/β-catenin signaling. More importantly, our results consistently demonstrate that co-treatment with ATG did not diminish the inhibitory effects of DOX on cancer cell migration and proliferation. These findings suggest that ATG does not interfere with the primary antitumor actions of DOX in these in vitro models (Fig. S4). Although these studies highlight the potential of ATG in oncology, its role in cardiovascular protection, especially in DIC, had remained unexplored. While limited evidence suggested cardioprotective potential in ischemia-reperfusion models, its direct molecular target and mechanism in cardiomyocytes remained undefined. From a chemical structural perspective, ATG possesses phenolic hydroxyl and methoxyl groups, and its conjugated double bond structure supports significant free radical scavenging capacity, underpinning its antioxidant potential [32]. Our study now reveals that ATG acts through competitive inhibition of KEAP1 via S602 binding—mediating both antioxidant and anti-ferroptosis activities. This work redefines ATG as a direct KEAP1-NRF2 protein-protein interaction (PPI) inhibitor, representing a previously unreported mechanism of action for this compound. Although we cannot completely rule out contributions from other pathways, our current study provides multiple lines of evidence supporting the central role of the KEAP1-NRF2 axis in mediating the cardioprotective effects of ATG in our experimental model DIC. This discovery offers a mechanistic basis for expanding the therapeutic application of ATG to other oxidative stress and ferroptosis -related pathologies.

Regarding the pharmacokinetic profile, existing studies indicate that ATG exhibits favorable bioavailability and metabolic stability comparable to those of other natural lignans [33]. Preliminary animal models suggest that ATG reaches effective plasma concentrations within a therapeutic window and demonstrates tissue distribution consistent with cardioprotective [34]. In terms of safety, ATG has shown a favorable profile in preclinical toxicity studies, with no significant adverse effects reported at pharmacologically relevant doses [35]. Its natural origin and presence in traditional diets also suggest a relatively high safety margin. However, detailed toxicological evaluations and long-term studies are necessary to fully establish its safety profile in clinical contexts. Although direct evidence in chronic DIC is still under investigation, the sustained activation of NRF2-mediated antioxidant pathways supports its potential for prolonged cytoprotection. Future studies should focus on chronic toxicity, organ-specific accumulation, and the durability of its protective effects in vivo.

The pathogenesis of DIC is primarily driven by two interconnected mechanisms: ROS-induced oxidative stress damage and iron-dependent ferroptosis [36]. Oxidative stress as a primary instigator in DIC that creates a permissive environment for ferroptosis. lipid peroxidation, a specific form of oxidative damage, is the fundamental biochemical executioner of ferroptosis. This creates a vicious cycle: DOX-induced ROS promotes lipid peroxidation, initiating ferroptosis; in turn, ferroptotic cell death releases redox-active iron and peroxidized lipids, further propagating oxidative stress to neighboring cardiomyocytes and amplifying the initial injury [37]. Concurrently, it induces lipid peroxidation through ACSL4 upregulation and GPX4 suppression, ultimately leading to ferroptosis [38]. Our data confirmed that ATG concurrently restored redox homeostasis (reducing ROS, replenishing SOD/GSH) and inhibits ferroptosis (upregulating GPX4/SLC7A11, suppressing MDA/4-HNE). This dual-pathway efficacy is critical because isolated antioxidant approaches often fail clinically due to their inability to address ferroptosis—a key executor of DOX-induced cardiomyocyte death.

Upon translocation to the nucleus, NRF2 binds to AREs and activates the transcription of over 200 cytoprotective genes (e.g., Hmox1, Nqo1, Slc7a11, Gpx4) [11,39]. However, previous research has demonstrated that NRF2 is adaptively activated in DIC to resist oxidative cell death [40]. It is postulated that the reduction in NRF2 expression may be attributable to the decompensation of the endogenous anti-oxidative system. Furthermore, our results shown that DOX accelerated NRF2 degradation through KEAP1-mediated ubiquitination in DIC, leading to further pathway decompensation. We proved that ATG rescued NRF2 signaling at three levels: competitively blocking KEAP1 binding at S602 and reducing NRF2 ubiquitination; enhancing phosphorylated NRF2 nuclear accumulation; restoring NRF2 and ARE-driven gene expression. This multifaceted activation explains the substantial increase in NRF2 following ATG treatment, which restores compromised antioxidant defenses and inhibits the concomitant ferroptosis cascade. These findings thus establish NRF2 as a critical biomarker for therapeutic response in DIC intervention.

The earliest identified NRF2 inducers were primarily cysteine-reactive compounds that selectively target certain cysteine residues on KEAP1, particularly KEAP1 Cys151. By covalently modifying KEAP1, these compounds inhibit KEAP1–CUL3 E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, leading to subsequent stabilization of NRF2 [41]. Some studies have further demonstrated such inducers do not immediately dissociate the KEAP1-CUL3 complex during early stages of NRF2 stabilization, with dissociation occurring only at later time points [42]. Despite their efficacy, these inducers frequently exhibit off-target effects due to their electrophilic reactivity with other proteins containing reactive cysteine residues. Currently, three NRF2 inducers dimethyl fumarate (DMF), diroximel fumarate, and omevaloxolone have received clinical approval, with several other candidates currently under investigation in clinical trials. Although some cysteine-reactive activators have achieved clinical success, their broad reactivity and off-target effects pose significant challenges. In contrast, PPI inhibitors enable precise and sustained NRF2 induction by sterically hindering KEAP1-NRF2 binding, offer a promising alternative approach with potentially higher specificity for NRF2 activation, but their development is hindered by pharmacokinetic limitations. Our biophysical validation (PM ≈ 22.0 μM; DARTS/CETSA with WT and mutant KEAP1) confirmed that ATG binds directly and competitively to the KEAP1 Kelch domain. Unlike DXZ, which is associated with tumor resistance and systemic toxicity, ATG's targeted PPI inhibition minimizes off-target effects while preserving chemotherapeutic efficacy—a key translational advantage.

Under basal conditions, NRF2 interacts with the KEAP1 Kelch domain via two motifs: the Glu-Thr-Gly-Glu (ETGE) and Asp-Leu-Gly (DLG) motifs within its Neh2 domain, with each NRF2 binding two KEAP1 [43]. The crystal structure of these motifs reveals a “hinge-latch” sequential binding mechanism, wherein the ETGE-KEAP1 interaction is strong and stable, while the DLG-KEAP1 interaction is weak and unstable [44]. Following oxidative stress, NRF2 is released from KEAP1 and translocates to the nucleus, where it activates the antioxidant response [45]. Our results demonstrated that the binding of NRF2 to KEAP1 was increased in DIC, and ATG treatment significantly reduced this binding. This may occur because a portion of KEAP1 is released from the DLG motif of NRF2 in DIC, enabling it to bind other NRF2 via their ETGE motifs, thereby increasing overall KEAP1-NRF2 binding. For NRF2 to function effectively, sufficient NRF2 must occupy KEAP1 binding sites. ATG may act as a molecular wedge, disrupting the NRF2-KEAP1 ″hinge-latch” mechanism.

We identified S602 within the KEAP1 Kelch domain as a critical residue for ATG binding. The KEAP1S602A mutant disrupted ATG binding, NRF2 stabilization, and cardioprotection both in vitro and in vivo. Structurally, the S602 residue is located near the ETGE motif-binding pocket of KEAP1. Previous crystal structures analyses indicate that S602 is a hotspot for developing KEAP1-NRF2 PPI inhibitors [46,47], Previous studies have established S602 as a key site modulating KEAP1's binding affinity for NRF2 [48]. Several reported PPI inhibitors bind at the S602 site, thereby competitively inhibiting KEAP1-NRF2 binding [49]. Moreover, chemical modifications aimed at enhancing interactions with S602 have been shown to increase inhibitor potency [46,50]. Therefore, ATG represents a natural, potent, and specific KEAP1-NRF2 PPI inhibitor with broad clinical potential. Future studies should focus on optimizing the pharmacological properties of ATG, to enhance its binding specificity, reducing off-target toxicity and minimizing potential risks to achieve successful clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

In summary, our results establish that ATG as a potent therapeutic agent against DIC in both cellular and murine models. Mechanistically, ATG activates the NRF2 pathway by directly binding to the Ser 602 residue of KEAP1, thereby disrupting the KEAP1-NRF2 interaction and promoting NRF2 mediated antioxidant and anti-ferroptotic responses. These findings suggest that ATG may represent a novel strategy for treating DIC and other oxidative stress and ferroptosis-related cardiac disorders.
