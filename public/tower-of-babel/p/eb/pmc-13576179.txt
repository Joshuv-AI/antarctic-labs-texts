CLTC expression in oral squamous cell carcinoma: insights into prognostic value and immune microenvironment modulation
Authors: Zifeng Cui, Yan Gao, Kaige Chen, Jianguang Zhao, Kaicheng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) has a poor prognosis because of its high metastatic potential and frequent chemoresistance. Although CLTC is aberrantly expressed and involved in fusion events in several tumors, its role in OSCC remains poorly defined.MethodsWe integrated transcriptomic and clinical data from TCGA and GEO, scRNA-seq data, immune infiltration analysis, ligand-receptor based cell–cell communication analysis, drug sensitivity prediction, molecular docking, and in vitro functional validation to evaluate CLTC expression, prognostic value, and its association with the OSCC immune microenvironment.ResultsCLTC was significantly overexpressed in OSCC and several other cancers. High CLTC expression was associated with poorer survival and improved prognostic stratification when combined with tumor mutation burden (TMB). Immune analyses showed that high CLTC expression was associated with reduced CD8 + T-cell infiltration, decreased cytotoxic activity, and increased immune exclusion- and suppression-related signatures. Single-cell analysis showed that CLTC was broadly expressed across OSCC cell populations, with relatively high expression in epithelial, inflammatory epithelial, macrophage, inflammatory macrophage, and endothelial cells. Macrophage-centered communication analysis further revealed prominent MIF-, CXCL-, and SPP1-related signaling interactions between macrophages and tumor, stromal, and immune cell populations. Drug sensitivity prediction and molecular docking suggested potential associations between CLTC expression and responses to several chemotherapeutic agents, although these computational results indicate possible physical interactions rather than confirmed functional drug effects. In vitro experiments confirmed that CLTC was upregulated in OSCC tissues and that CLTC knockdown suppressed SCC25 cell proliferation, migration, and invasion.ConclusionCLTC may serve as a prognostic biomarker in OSCC and may contribute to tumor progression by promoting malignant cellular phenotypes and immunosuppressive remodeling of the tumor microenvironment. Future mechanistic studies are required to determine whether CLTC directly regulates chemotherapy response through clathrin-mediated trafficking, receptor recycling, adhesion signaling, or tumor–immune interactions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12957-026-04436-x.Keywords: CLTC, Oral squamous cell carcinoma, Tumor mutation burden, Immune microenvironment, Personalized therapeutic target

Introduction

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is one of the most common malignant tumors worldwide, accounting for approximately 2% of all cancer types, with over 350,000 new cases diagnosed globally each year [1]. Despite significant advancements in treatment modalities, including surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and multidisciplinary comprehensive sequential therapy, the 5-year survival rate of OSCC patients remains low, hovering around 50% [2]. Distant metastasis has always been a major challenge in the treatment of OSCC [3]. Although some progress has been made in the field of tumor invasion and metastasis, the underlying molecular mechanisms still need to be further explored. Therefore, a thorough understanding of the genetic and epigenetic molecular alterations in OSCC is crucial for improving the prognosis of OSCC patients. At present, although some biomarkers have been used for the diagnosis and prognosis assessment of OSCC, their clinical application effects vary due to the heterogeneity of the disease. Thus, further exploration of molecular targets and biomarkers in OSCC is of great significance for improving prognostic prediction and developing new therapeutic methods. Recent studies have increasingly used integrated pan-cancer and tumor-specific analyses to identify diagnostic, prognostic, and immune-related biomarkers. For example, PPME1 was reported to be upregulated in multiple cancers and associated with prognosis, immune regulation, and tumor-promoting pathways [4]. Similarly, BEND3 was identified as a potential prognostic and immune biomarker through integrated pan-cancer profiling and experimental validation [5]. Other biomarker studies, including those on CLIC6 and CORO1A, also combined public database mining, immune microenvironment analysis, functional enrichment, and experimental verification to clarify their diagnostic, prognostic, and immunological relevance in cancer [6, 7]. These studies highlight the value of integrating transcriptomic analysis, immune infiltration assessment, functional enrichment, and experimental validation for identifying clinically meaningful cancer biomarkers.

Gene dysregulation and fusion events contribute to tumor initiation, progression, and therapeutic resistance. CLTC has attracted increasing attention because of its aberrant expression and fusion events in multiple cancers. CLTC encodes the clathrin heavy chain, a major structural component of clathrin-coated vesicles that participates in receptor-mediated endocytosis, intracellular protein trafficking, and cell division [8]. In oncology, CLTC-related fusion events have been reported in several malignancies. For example, CLTC-ALK fusion has been identified as an important pathogenic event in inflammatory myofibroblastic tumors [9] and large B-cell lymphoma [10], and a CLTC-VMP1 fusion has been implicated in autophagy regulation and energy metabolism in cisplatin-resistant osteosarcoma [11]. However, the expression pattern, prognostic significance, and immune relevance of CLTC in OSCC remain insufficiently characterized.

In this study, we adopted multidimensional bioinformatics analysis and experimental validation to systematically dissect the expression profile and prognostic significance of CLTC in OSCC. Through transcriptomic analysis, we revealed the differential expression patterns of CLTC in normal and diseased tissues and further clarified its expression characteristics in various cancers by pan-cancer analysis. Based on the expression levels of CLTC, we divided patients into high-expression and low-expression groups and analyzed the differences in tumor mutation burden (TMB) between the two groups. We also conducted survival analysis to assess the impact of CLTC expression levels on patient prognosis. In addition, we constructed a nomogram model and validated its predictive performance using calibration curves and ROC analysis. At the level of the immune microenvironment, we explored the complex associations between CLTC expression and immune cell infiltration through immune infiltration and correlation analysis and further clarified the expression distribution of CLTC in different immune cell populations through single-cell analysis. Moreover, we identified gene modules co-expressed with CLTC and performed functional enrichment analysis on them. Finally, through ceRNA and drug sensitivity analysis, we aimed to explore the potential regulatory mechanisms and therapeutic targets of CLTC in OSCC.

Methods

Data sources and pan-cancer analysis

Transcriptomic and matched clinical data from 330 OSCC samples and 32 normal samples in the TCGA-HNSC cohort were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database. Transcriptomic data from 83 OSCC samples were obtained from the GSE65858 dataset in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Tumor mutation burden (TMB) data were downloaded using the TCGAbiolinks R package, and waterfall plots for the high- and low-CLTC expression groups were generated using the maftools R package. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data for eight OSCC samples (GSM5851565-GSM5851572) were obtained from the GSE195832 dataset. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) analysis for CLTC was performed based on the GeneMANIA database (http://genemania.org/). The expression of CLTC across different cancers was evaluated based on the TIMER database (https://cistrome.shinyapps.io/timer/).

Experimental methods

Human tissue sample collection

Tumor tissues and matched normal tissues were collected from patients with OSCC who underwent surgical resection at the Fourth Hospital of Hebei Medical University from January to June 2025. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) histopathologically confirmed OSCC; (2) no preoperative anticancer therapy (e.g., chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or immunotherapy); (3) no severe cardiac, cerebrovascular, or pulmonary disease, with patients deemed eligible for surgery; and (4) no history of other malignancies. The exclusion criteria were: (1) failure to meet histopathologic diagnostic criteria; (2) receipt of any preoperative anticancer treatment; (3) severe cardiac, cerebrovascular, pulmonary, or other comorbidities precluding surgery; and (4) presence of other malignancies, systemic infection, autoimmune disease, psychiatric disorders, or severe metabolic disorders. In total, eight pairs of tissues (tumor and matched normal) were included. Immediately after excision, specimens were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Fourth Hospital of Hebei Medical University (Approval No. 2024KY008). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment.

Cell culture and siRNA transfection

Normal human oral keratinocytes (HOK), human gingival epithelial cells (HGE), and the OSCC cell lines HSC3, SCC9, SCC25, and CAL27 were obtained from the Biobank of the Fourth Hospital of Hebei Medical University. Cells were maintained in high-glucose DMEM (Servicebio, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Biosharp, China) at 37 °C in a humidified CO₂ incubator (5% CO₂; Thermo Scientific, USA). Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting CLTC and a non-targeting negative control (si-NC) were synthesized by Hebei Haomao Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China). The siRNA sequences were as follows (sense, 5′ → 3′): si-CLTC#1, GCCCAAAUGUUAGUUCAAGAUTT; si-CLTC#2, CGGUUGCUCUUGUUACGGAUATT; si-CLTC#3, CCUGUGUAGAUGGGAAAGAAUTT. SCC25 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and transfected at 70% confluence with Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Transfections were performed in serum- and antibiotic-free medium. After 8 h, the transfection mixture was replaced with DMEM containing 10% FBS for subsequent culture. Transfected cells were incubated for an additional 48 h and then harvested for downstream assays.

Western blot

Cells were lysed on ice in RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors (Servicebio, China). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (Solarbio, China). Cell lysates were denatured in SDS-PAGE sample buffer (Biosharp, China) at 100 °C for 10 min. Proteins were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked in 5% nonfat dry milk for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit monoclonal anti-CLTC (1:2,000; R381847, Zenbio, China) and rabbit monoclonal anti-β-actin (1:10,000; R380624, Zenbio, China) primary antibodies. After washing, membranes were incubated with an HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:10,000; 511,203, Zenbio, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were developed by enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL; MCE, USA) and captured with a gel imaging system (Syngene, USA). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ (version 1.51).

CCK-8 assay

Cells from each group were seeded in 96-well plates at 1 × 10^3 cells/well in 100 μL of medium. At 0, 24, 48, and 72 h, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (Beyotime, China) was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. The optical density (OD) at 450 nm was recorded using a microplate reader (Biotek, USA).

Colony formation assay

Cells from each group were seeded in 6-well plates at 1 × 10^3 cells/well and cultured. The medium was changed every 72–96 h. Cultures were maintained until microscopic examination confirmed that most colonies comprised ≥ 50 cells, and the assay was then terminated. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 20 min, and air-dried at room temperature. Images were captured with a digital camera, and colony numbers were quantified using ImageJ (version 1.51).

Wound healing assay

Cells from each group were seeded in 6-well plates. At 90–100% confluence, a linear wound was created across the cell monolayer with a sterile 200 μL pipette tip. After gentle washing with PBS to remove debris, the culture medium was replaced with serum-free medium. Images were acquired at 0 and 24 h with an inverted microscope (Leica, Germany). Wound areas were quantified using ImageJ (version 1.51), and the migration rate (%) was calculated as [(area at 0 h − area at 24 h)/area at 0 h] × 100.

Transwell invasion assay

Matrigel (Beyotime, China) was diluted 1:8 in serum-free DMEM and used to precoat the upper chambers of the Transwell inserts (LabSelect, cat. 14,342). The coated inserts were incubated at 37 °C for 4 h to allow Matrigel gelation. For the invasion assay, 200 μL of serum-free medium containing 5 × 10^4 cells was added to each upper chamber, and 600 μL of DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber as the chemoattractant. After 24 h of incubation, non-invading cells on the upper surface of the membrane were gently removed with cotton swabs. Membranes were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.5% crystal violet. Images were acquired using a microscope (Leica, Germany), and invaded cells were quantified using ImageJ (version 1.51).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and data visualization were performed in R (version 4.2.1). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homoscedasticity was evaluated with Levene’s test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± SD. Western blot results from paired tissue samples were analyzed with paired t tests. Because the quantitative data met the assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity, multiple-group comparisons were conducted using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. All statistical tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Differential expression

Based on the median expression level of CLTC, we divided oral cancer patients into high and low expression groups, and differential expression analysis was performed. The threshold for differential expression was set at |logFC|> 0.5 and adj.p < 0.05, resulting in the identification of 617 differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses for the 617 DEGs were conducted using the “clusterProfiler” package in R software. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was also performed for the high- and low-expression groups, with significant pathways retained at p < 0.05.

Prognostic gene screening and prognostic model construction

To evaluate the clinical predictive value of CLTC in patients with oral cancer, we performed univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis for CLTC and clinical indicators using the survival package in R. Subsequently, we developed a nomogram based on age, tumor stage, T stage, N stage, and CLTC expression to predict 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival. The predictive performance of the model was evaluated using calibration curves and ROC curves.

Immune infiltration analysis

To evaluate the differences in the abundance of various immune cell infiltrations and immune function scores between the high- and low-expression groups, single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was performed using the “GSVA” package in R software. The correlation between CLTC and immune cells/functions was calculated using the Spearman correlation coefficient. Scores for immune cell types, immune suppression features, immune exclusion features, and immunotherapy biomarkers for samples in the high- and low-expression groups were calculated using the “IOBR” package in R software. Differences between the high- and low-expression groups were calculated using the Wilcox.test.

Analysis of scRNA-seq data

Basic analysis

The scRNA-seq data were processed and analyzed using the “Seurat” package in R software. Quality control (QC) was performed based on the number of detected genes (nFeature_RNA), total counts (nCount_RNA), and percentage of mitochondrial genes (percent.MT), with low-quality cells filtered out. Cells with nFeature_RNA between 200 and 7500, nCount_RNA less than 100,000, and percent.MT less than 20 were retained for further analysis. The retained cells were normalized using the LogNormalize method, high-variance features were identified and scaled, and the data dimension was reduced by principal component analysis (PCA). To correct for batch effects, the Harmony algorithm was applied, and downstream analyses were performed using the corrected PCA. Neighbors were identified based on the corrected PCA, and clusters were generated using the Louvain algorithm at a resolution of 1, followed by t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE). Clusters were annotated using the SingleR package and marker genes.

Cell communication analysis

Cell communication analysis was performed using the “CellChat” package in R software. Interaction information was provided by the human ligand-receptor interaction database (CellChatDB.human). After identifying overexpressed genes and interactions, communication probabilities were calculated, and interactions supported by at least 10 cells were filtered. An aggregated communication network was then calculated to summarize interactions between cell populations. The communication network was visualized using a circular plot to display the number and weight of interactions. Network centrality scores were calculated to identify major senders and receivers. Global communication patterns were identified by non-negative matrix factorization (NMF), and outgoing and incoming communication patterns were visualized using dot plots.

hdWGCNA analysis

To construct a co-expression network and perform weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), the “hdWGCNA” package in R software was used. The meta-cell expression matrix was normalized after dimension reduction by PCA and setting the KNN parameter (k = 25). The optimal power (power = 8) was selected based on scale-free topology criteria by testing different soft-thresholding powers. A co-expression network was constructed using the selected soft-thresholding power, and the resulting network was visualized using a dendrogram. Module eigengenes representing the expression profiles of each module were calculated, and the connectivity (kME) of each gene with module eigengenes was determined. Hub genes within each module were identified by selecting the top N genes with the highest kME values. Network plots for specific modules were generated using the ModuleNetworkPlot function, and correlations between different modules were assessed using the ModuleCorrelogram function.

Spatial transcriptomics analysis

Spatial transcriptomics data from four samples, including gene expression counts and spatial information, were analyzed using the “Seurat” package in R software. Low-quality cells were filtered out based on UMI counts, gene counts, and mitochondrial gene expression. The filtered data were normalized using the SCTransform function, followed by PCA and clustering. Spatially variable features were identified by two methods: differential expression analysis between clusters and the FindSpatiallyVariableFeatures function. Spatial transcriptomics data were integrated with single-cell RNA-seq data for joint analysis, and an interactive visualization tool was used to explore the spatial distribution of gene expression and cell types.

Drug sensitivity and molecular docking analysis

To assess the potential significance of gene expression levels in treatment strategies, drug sensitivity analysis was performed for the high- and low-expression groups using the “oncoPredict” package in R software in combination with the GDSC2 database (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/). Drugs with p < 0.05 were selected as representative drugs for display. Among them, two chemotherapeutic drugs commonly used in the treatment of oral cancer—5-fluorouracil (5-FU) (binding energy −5.3 kcal/mol) and paclitaxel (binding energy −8.6 kcal/mol)—were further used for molecular docking analysis with the CLTC gene product.

The molecular docking analysis steps were as follows: First, the three-dimensional structures of the small-molecule compounds 5-FU (CID: 1954) and paclitaxel (CID: 18,680,487) were obtained from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The structure information of the CLTC protein was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). Finally, molecular docking simulations were performed using the CB-Dock2 online platform (https://cadd.abshare.cn/cb-dock2/index.php) to predict possible binding sites and ligand–protein interactions.

Results

Expression landscape of CLTC

Figure 1 presents the technical roadmap of this study. Initially, we assessed the expression landscape of CLTC in the control and OSCC groups based on the transcriptome data from the TCGA cohort. As shown in Fig. 2A, CLTC is significantly upregulated in OSCC. A comprehensive analysis of CLTC gene expression levels across pan-cancer revealed significant expression differences (Fig. 2B). Specifically, the expression pattern of the CLTC gene across pan-cancer exhibited diversity. For instance, in BRCA tumor samples, compared with normal tissues, CLTC expression levels showed an upward trend, especially in the BRCA-Basal subtype. In addition, significant changes in CLTC expression were observed in hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), lung cancer (LUAD and LUSC), and cutaneous melanoma (SKCM) as well as their metastatic samples (Fig. 2C).

Expression grouping and prognostic validation

The mutational landscape of tumors stratified by CLTC expression is shown in Figs. 3A and 3B. In the high-CLTC expression group, the most frequently mutated genes were TP53 (77%), TTN (32%), and FAT1 (23%), with 151 of 158 samples (95.57%) carrying at least one mutation. In the low-CLTC expression group, TP53 was also the most frequently mutated gene (67%), followed by TTN (32%) and FAT1 (24%), with 151 of 160 samples (94.38%) carrying mutations. Missense mutations were the predominant mutation type in both groups. KM survival analysis showed that patients with high TMB had significantly poorer survival than those with low TMB (p = 0.012; Fig. 3C). Combined stratification by TMB and CLTC expression further refined prognostic classification, with the H-TMB + High-CLTC group showing the worst survival and the L-TMB + Low-CLTC group showing the best survival (p = 0.018; Fig. 3D). As shown in Fig. 3C, patients with high TMB (H-TMB) had significantly worse survival than those with low TMB (L-TMB) (p = 0.012). This indicates that TMB is an important prognostic factor in this cohort. Further stratification based on TMB and CLTC expression levels revealed different survival patterns (Fig. 3D). Patients with high TMB and high CLTC expression (H-TMB + High-exp) had the worst survival prognosis, while those with low TMB and low CLTC expression (L-TMB + Low-exp) had the best survival prognosis. The differences among the four groups were statistically significant (p = 0.018). This suggests that combined analysis of TMB and CLTC expression levels provides more refined prognostic stratification than a single factor.

Next, we evaluated the prognostic impact of CLTC expression in the TCGA cohort using Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival analysis. In the TCGA cohort (Fig. 4A), patients with high CLTC expression had a lower overall survival rate than those with low CLTC expression. To further assess the clinical value of CLTC in patients with oral cancer, we performed univariate Cox regression analysis of CLTC and clinical indicators (Fig. 4B). The results showed that CLTC, age, stage, T stage, and N stage were associated with the prognosis of oral cancer, where high CLTC expression was also associated with poorer survival outcomes (p = 0.020). These results indicate that CLTC expression is a robust prognostic biomarker across different patient cohorts. To integrate CLTC expression with clinical factors for more accurate prognostic prediction, we constructed a nomogram based on CLTC expression and clinical variables (including age, stage, T, and N) in the TCGA cohort (Fig. 4C, Tables S1, S2 in Supplementary material). The nomogram provides a visual tool for predicting patient survival probabilities at different time points. The calibration curve of the nomogram in the TCGA cohort showed good consistency between predicted and actual survival probabilities (Fig. 4D), indicating the reliability of the nomogram model. We further evaluated the prognostic performance of the nomogram through ROC curves. The AUC values for predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probabilities were 0.66, 0.753, and 0.793, respectively (Fig. 4E). Similarly, we constructed a nomogram based on CLTC expression and the same clinical factors in the GEO cohort (Fig. 4F). The calibration curve of the nomogram in the GEO cohort also showed good consistency between predicted and actual survival probabilities (Fig. 4G), validating the applicability of the nomogram model in an independent cohort. The ROC curve of the nomogram in the GEO cohort showed AUC values of 0.722, 0.762, and 0.760 for predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probabilities, respectively (Fig. 4H). In addition, we constructed a nomogram model using clinical indicators alone, performed calibration curve and ROC curve analyses, and calculated the C-index. The results showed that, compared with the nomogram model constructed using clinical indicators alone, the nomogram model integrating CLTC with clinical indicators achieved higher AUC and C-index values (Figure S1 in Supplementary Material).

To explore the biological functions and signaling pathways associated with CLTC expression, we performed differential expression and enrichment analyses between the high- and low-CLTC expression groups in the TCGA cohort. The heatmap (Fig. 5A, B, Table S3) showed distinct expression patterns of DEGs between the two groups. GO enrichment analysis (Fig. 5C) indicated that these DEGs were mainly enriched in extracellular matrix organization, collagen fibril organization, cell-substrate adhesion, and structural constituents of the ribosome. KEGG pathway analysis (Fig. 5D) demonstrated significant enrichment in ECM-receptor interaction, focal adhesion, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, and proteoglycans in cancer. GSEA was further performed using the complete ranked gene list from the high- versus low-CLTC comparison. GSEA further showed that CLTC-associated genes were enriched in several cancer-related pathways, including pathways in cancer, MAPK signaling, ECM-receptor interaction, and Hedgehog signaling (Figs. 5E-H, Table S4). Together, these results indicate that CLTC expression is bioinformatically associated with extracellular matrix remodeling, ribosomal function, energy metabolism, and cell adhesion-related pathways; however, these associations do not establish direct regulatory effects of CLTC on specific pathway genes.

Determine the expression trend of CLTC in single cells and the interactions among related cells

This paper first conducts quality control and batch correction on the original scRNA-seq data. Subsequently, we compared immune infiltration and immune function scores between the high- and low-CLTC expression groups. CD8 + T-cell infiltration was lower in the high-CLTC expression group (Fig. 6A), indicating reduced adaptive antitumor immunity. Similarly, immune function scores also differed between the two groups (Fig. 6B, Table S5 in Supplementary Materials). The CLTC-high group exhibited lower scores for cytolytic activity and inflammation-promoting functions, suggesting reduced antitumor immune activity. Correlation analyses further supported these findings, showing that CLTC expression was negatively associated with CD8 + T-cell infiltration and cytotoxic immune function (Figs. 6C-D).CLTC expression was negatively correlated with the infiltration of CD8 + T cells and T helper cells (Fig. 6C), suggesting that high CLTC expression may be associated with reduced adaptive antitumor immune responses. In terms of immune functions, CLTC expression was negatively correlated with cytolytic activity and HLA-related signatures (Fig. 6D), further supporting an association between high CLTC expression and impaired immune effector function. To avoid overinterpretation, inconsistent descriptions regarding macrophages and neutrophils were removed. These results suggest that elevated CLTC expression is associated with an immune-excluded and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, rather than immune activation. Subsequently, we used the IOBR algorithm to assess the differences in TME, immune suppression, immune exclusion, and immunotherapy biomarker scores between the high/low expression groups. The high-CLTC group displayed features consistent with immune exclusion and immune suppression, including higher exclusion-related scores such as fibroblast- and TGFβ-associated signatures, indicating potential barriers to effective immune-cell infiltration and cytotoxic immune activity. As shown in Fig. 6E, immune cell-related signatures, including CD8_Tcells and T follicular helper cells, were significantly higher in the CLTC-low group than in the CLTC-high group (p < 0.001), indicating a more immune-infiltrated and potentially more immunologically active tumor microenvironment in the CLTC-low group. The immune suppression-related feature scores, including MDSC_Wang_et_al and Pan_F_TBRS, also showed significant differences between the two groups (Fig. 6F). The higher scores of these suppressive features in the high expression group suggest more complex interactions between immune activation and suppression in this group (p < 0.05). Regarding immune exclusion, the scores for fibroblasts and TGFβ family members were higher in the high expression group (Fig. 6G), indicating potential barriers to immune cell infiltration. Finally, we evaluated immunotherapy-associated immune signatures, including TMEscore_CIR, CD_8_T_effector, and Immune_Checkpoint. The results showed that the TMEscore_CIR, CD_8_T_effector, and Immune_Checkpoint scores were lower in the CLTC-high group than in the CLTC-low group (Fig. 6H), further suggesting reduced immune activation and weaker CD8 + T-cell effector activity in CLTC-high tumors. Collectively, these findings suggest that CLTC-high tumors may be characterized by relatively reduced lymphocyte-mediated antitumor immunity, attenuated cytolytic and HLA-related immune functions, and increased myeloid- and stromal-associated immunosuppressive or exclusion features, indicating a potential association between high CLTC expression and a more immune-suppressed or immune-excluded tumor microenvironment.

To systematically characterize the expression distribution of CLTC within the OSCC microenvironment, we performed cell clustering, cell-type annotation, and gene expression analysis using OSCC scRNA-seq data. UMAP dimensionality reduction revealed that cells from OSCC tissues could be classified into several major cell populations, including epithelial cells, inflammatory epithelial cells, CAFs, myCAFs, endothelial cells, pericytes, T cells, plasma cells, dendritic cells, macrophages, and inflammatory macrophages (Fig. 7A). Cell-type annotations were subsequently validated based on canonical marker genes (Fig. 7B). Further comparison of cellular composition across different OSCC samples revealed marked differences in cell proportions among individual patients. Some samples were predominantly composed of epithelial cells or CAFs, whereas others were enriched for microenvironmental components such as T cells, inflammatory macrophages, or endothelial/pericyte populations, indicating substantial interpatient heterogeneity in the cellular composition of OSCC tumors (Fig. 7C). Based on this cellular landscape, we further examined the expression distribution of CLTC across different cell types. The results showed that CLTC expression was not restricted to a single cell population but was detectable across multiple cell types within the OSCC microenvironment. Notably, macrophages, inflammatory macrophages, epithelial cells, endothelial cells, and inflammatory epithelial cells exhibited relatively higher average expression levels of CLTC (Fig. 7D).

Macrophages exhibit extensive cell–cell communication activity within the OSCC tumor microenvironment

To define macrophage-centered communication in the OSCC tumor microenvironment, we analyzed ligand-receptor-based interaction number and strength across cell types. CLTC-high macrophages showed extensive interactions with epithelial cells, CAFs, endothelial cells, T cells, inflammatory epithelial cells, and inflammatory macrophages (Fig. 8A, B). As signal-sending cells, macrophages mainly communicated through MIF-CD74/CD44, MIF-CD74/CXCR4, and SPP1-related axes, whereas as signal-receiving cells, they received inputs from diverse tumor, stromal, and immune populations, particularly via MIF-CD74/CD44 and MIF-CD74/CXCR4 interactions (Fig. 8C, D). Consistently, pathway-related gene expression supported these communication patterns. MIF and its receptors CD74, CXCR4, and CD44 were broadly expressed in immune cells (Fig. 8E). CXCL2, CXCL3, CXCL8, and CXCR4 were enriched in macrophage populations, while ACKR1 was mainly expressed in endothelial cells (Fig. 8F). SPP1 was highly expressed in macrophages, and its receptors or co-receptors were distributed across epithelial, stromal, and immune cells (Fig. 8G). These results suggest that macrophages may contribute to OSCC microenvironment remodeling through MIF-, CXCL-, and SPP1-mediated communication.

Drug sensitivity and molecular docking analysis

To evaluate the association between CLTC expression and predicted drug sensitivity, we performed drug sensitivity analysis using the oncoPredict R package and the GDSC2 database. Eight drugs with significant differences between the high- and low-CLTC expression groups were selected for presentation (Figs. 9A-H and Table S6 in Supplementary Materials). Among these, 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), docetaxel, paclitaxel, cisplatin, and oxaliplatin are commonly used in oral cancer treatment. Molecular docking was then performed to explore the potential physical interactions between CLTC and two representative drugs, 5-FU and paclitaxel. First, we obtained the 3D structure data of 5-fluorouracil and paclitaxel from the PubChem database. Then, we acquired the protein structure data of the CLTC gene from the PDB database. Finally, detailed drug molecular docking analysis was performed using the CB-Dock2 online platform (Figs. 9I-J). The results showed that the binding energy of 5-fluorouracil to CLTC was −5.3 kcal/mol, while that of paclitaxel to CLTC was −8.6 kcal/mol, suggesting favorable in silico binding potential. However, these docking results only indicate possible physical interactions and do not demonstrate functional inhibition, biological efficacy, or therapeutic activity of these drugs through CLTC. These findings should be interpreted as preliminary computational predictions and require further biochemical and experimental validation.

CLTC upregulation in OSCC and functional validation

Western blot analysis of eight paired tissue samples showed that CLTC expression was upregulated in OSCC tissues (Fig. 10A). Further screening of OSCC cell lines revealed relatively high CLTC protein levels in SCC25 and HSC3 cells (Fig. 10B); therefore, these two cell lines were selected for subsequent in vitro functional assays. Among the three siRNAs targeting CLTC, si-CLTC#1 and si-CLTC#2 effectively reduced CLTC protein expression (Fig. 10C). Subsequent functional experiments demonstrated that CLTC knockdown inhibited the proliferative capacity of SCC25 cells (Fig. 10D-E) and attenuated their migratory and invasive abilities (Fig. 10F-G). Similar inhibitory effects were also observed in HSC3 cells following CLTC knockdown (Figure S2), suggesting that CLTC may contribute to the malignant biological behavior of OSCC cells.

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated CLTC expression, prognostic value, and immune microenvironment associations in OSCC using multi-omics data and experimental validation. Consistent with recent integrative biomarker studies that combined pan-cancer profiling, immune analysis, and experimental validation [4–7], our study applied a multidimensional framework to clarify the clinical and biological relevance of CLTC in OSCC. CLTC was upregulated in OSCC and several other cancers, and high CLTC expression was associated with poorer survival. Moreover, combined stratification by TMB and CLTC expression improved prognostic classification, with patients in the H-TMB + High-CLTC group showing the poorest outcomes. Although CLTC-related fusion events have been reported in several malignancies [9–11], the expression pattern, prognostic value, immune relevance, and functional role of CLTC in OSCC have not been well characterized. The novelty of this study lies in integrating bulk transcriptomic cohorts, immune infiltration analysis, scRNA-seq-based cell-type resolution, macrophage-centered cell communication, predicted drug sensitivity, molecular docking, and in vitro knockdown validation to provide a comprehensive view of CLTC in OSCC. These findings suggest that CLTC may have clinical relevance as a prognostic biomarker in OSCC.

Differential expression and enrichment analyses of patients with high and low CLTC expression identified several pathways associated with OSCC progression, such as extracellular matrix organization, ribosome, cytosolic ribosome, collagen binding, and integrin binding. However, these findings should be interpreted as pathway-level associations based on transcriptomic data rather than direct evidence that CLTC regulates key genes within these pathways. In the current study, we did not perform qPCR to examine representative ribosomal genes, mitochondrial ATP synthesis-related genes, or cell adhesion-related genes after CLTC knockdown. The organization of the extracellular matrix is closely associated with oral squamous cell carcinoma, as proteins upregulated in cancer-associated fibroblasts mainly involve the organization and depolymerization of the extracellular matrix and collagen metabolism, which can promote the proliferation and invasion of OSCC cells [8]. In OSCC, the lysine lactylation modification of ribosomal proteins significantly increases under hypoxic conditions, a modification that may affect ribosomal function and subsequently impact protein synthesis and tumor malignancy [9]. The relationship between cytosolic ribosome and OSCC lies in the downregulation of miR-99a in OSCC, with its target genes enriched in cytosolic ribosome-related pathways, suggesting that cytosolic ribosome function may be regulated by miR-99a and influence the occurrence and development of OSCC [10]. Mutations in Streptococcus mutans, a species within the Streptococcus genus, interact with host cells through collagen-binding proteins on their surface, affecting immune responses and inflammatory processes, and may thereby promote the development of OSCC [11]. The abnormal overexpression of the integrin β4 binding protein eIF6, which is associated with tumor size and clinical grading, enhances the proliferation, migration, and invasion capabilities of OSCC cells by promoting epithelial-mesenchymal transition and activating the AKT signaling pathway, making it a potential new therapeutic target for OSCC [12]. Further GSEA analysis identified key pathways including focal adhesion, platelet activation, and proteoglycans. Lysyl oxidase, highly expressed in cancer-associated fibroblasts in OSCC, catalyzes collagen cross-linking, increases matrix stiffness, and promotes OSCC cell invasion and epithelial-mesenchymal transition by activating the focal adhesion kinase phosphorylation pathway [13]. Platelet-activating factor receptors affect cisplatin chemosensitivity by regulating the phosphorylation of ERK and Akt and increasing the expression of cleaved caspase-3. Their specific inhibitor, ginkgolide B, can enhance the chemotherapeutic effects of CDDP and induce apoptosis, potentially becoming a new drug to improve the combined chemotherapy outcomes for OSCC patients [14]. In oral mucosal lesions, the small leucine-rich extracellular matrix glycosaminoglycans lumican and biglycan are expressed in both OSCC and oral lichen planus, with lumican significantly upregulated in OLP and OSCC tissues and biglycan significantly increased in active OLP lesions, changes that may indicate the malignant potential of OLP [15].

The nomogram incorporating CLTC expression and clinical variables showed acceptable predictive performance, supporting the potential clinical utility of CLTC. Immune analyses further showed that high CLTC expression was associated with reduced CD8 + T-cell infiltration and decreased cytotoxic activity, suggesting impaired adaptive antitumor immunity. In addition, higher immune exclusion-related scores, including fibroblast- and TGFβ-associated signatures, were observed in the high-CLTC expression group. Thus, the immune-related findings consistently indicate that CLTC is associated with immune exclusion and immunosuppressive remodeling of the tumor microenvironment. In locally advanced OSCC patients receiving neoadjuvant immunotherapy with camrelizumab combined with albumin-bound paclitaxel and cisplatin, patients with higher baseline CD4 + Tfh cell density were more likely to achieve major pathological remission, and the density of tertiary lymphoid structures increased after treatment, suggesting that CD4 + Tfh cells may serve as predictive biomarkers of treatment efficacy, with their interaction with CXCR5 on B cells potentially influencing treatment response [16]. Oral polymorphonuclear neutrophils may play a significant role in the occurrence and development of OSCC by enhancing the expression of cell surface markers and inflammatory cytokines and infiltrating cancer sites under the influence of inflammation and specific chemokines [17]. The immune checkpoint molecule PD-1 binds to PD-L1 on the surface of tumor cells and activates the p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase pathway, inducing cellular senescence and subsequent epithelial-mesenchymal transition, thereby promoting tumor metastasis. Circulating small extracellular vesicles carrying PD-1 are closely related to lymph node metastasis, and inhibiting sEV PD-1 holds promise as a potential therapeutic target for OSCC [18]. Cytolytic activity is positively correlated with regulatory T cell-related gene signatures, and OSCC with low TRS scores exhibits higher cytolytic activity, which may predict better immunotherapy response and clinical prognosis [19].

Finally, drug sensitivity analysis suggested that CLTC expression may be associated with predicted responses to several chemotherapeutic agents, including 5-fluorouracil, docetaxel, paclitaxel, cisplatin, and oxaliplatin. Molecular docking showed favorable in silico binding energies between CLTC and 5-fluorouracil or paclitaxel. However, because CLTC encodes a structural protein rather than a canonical enzyme or receptor, these docking results should be interpreted only as exploratory evidence of potential physical interaction and not as proof of therapeutic efficacy. Several plausible mechanisms may link CLTC to chemotherapy sensitivity. First, CLTC is a core component of clathrin-mediated endocytosis, which regulates internalization, recycling, and degradation of membrane receptors and transport cargoes [20]. Altered CLTC expression may therefore influence EGFR, integrin, or other receptor-trafficking programs that control PI3K-AKT, MAPK, STAT3, adhesion, and survival signaling, all of which can affect chemotherapy response [21, 22]. Second, CLTC also has non-endocytic functions during mitosis. It contributes to mitotic spindle stability and chromosome congression, suggesting a possible mechanistic connection with microtubule-targeting drugs such as paclitaxel and docetaxel [23]. Consistently, pathway-level biomarker analysis in cancer cell lines has linked CLTC essentiality to microtubule-related/stathmin pathway activity, supporting the hypothesis that CLTC-dependent mitotic regulation may modulate sensitivity to antimitotic agents [24]. Third, our enrichment results showed that high CLTC expression was associated with ribosomal function, energy metabolism, extracellular matrix remodeling, and cell adhesion-related pathways. These processes may indirectly influence drug response by altering proliferative state, metabolic dependency, cell–matrix adhesion, and apoptosis susceptibility. Compared with these previous studies, our work does not claim that CLTC directly determines sensitivity to these drugs, but provides a hypothesis-generating link between CLTC expression, predicted chemotherapy response, receptor trafficking, mitotic regulation, and tumor microenvironment remodeling. Therefore, the docking results and drug sensitivity predictions should be regarded as exploratory findings that require experimental validation. 5-Fluorouracil is a commonly used drug, but cancer cells often develop resistance to it. Propofol can reduce OSCC cell activity, promote cell apoptosis, and decrease the secretion of various growth factors. It enhances sensitivity to 5-FU by downregulating amphiregulin expression and holds promise as an effective strategy for combined therapy in OSCC [25]. In OSCC treatment, docetaxel combined with radiotherapy has comparable efficacy and lower toxicity than cisplatin combined with radiotherapy, and low ITGB1 expression is associated with better docetaxel treatment outcomes, indicating that docetaxel can serve as an alternative to concurrent chemoradiotherapy for high expression postoperative OSCC patients [26]. In OSCC treatment, the fatty acid synthase inhibitor orlistat can enhance the chemosensitivity of paclitaxel and cisplatin by downregulating cyclin B1 but has no effect on sensitivity to 5-fluorouracil. Moreover, combination therapy does not show superior inhibitory effects on cell proliferation, migration, and invasion compared to individual chemotherapeutic drugs [27]. Cisplatin resistance is one of the main factors leading to treatment failure. It was found that circular RNA circ-ILF2 is upregulated in CDDP-resistant OSCC cell lines, where it acts as a sponge for miR-1252 to regulate KLF8 expression, thereby promoting CDDP resistance. Additionally, circ-ILF2 induces the M2 polarization of tumor-associated macrophages, offering new ideas for immunotherapy, and circ-ILF2 holds promise as a potential therapeutic target for CDDP-resistant OSCC [28]. Oxaliplatin inhibits the proliferation and migration of OSCC cells by increasing reactive oxygen species production and activating PARP1-mediated parthanatos, and it suppresses tumor formation both in vivo and in vitro [29]. Molecular docking simulations revealed favorable binding energies for 5-fluorouracil and paclitaxel with CLTC, indicating a potential structural interaction. However, as these results are based entirely on in silico predictions, further in vitro and in vivo functional experiments are strictly required to determine whether this direct binding occurs physiologically and if it contributes to the anticancer efficacy or drug resistance mechanisms in OSCC.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size of the analyzed datasets was limited, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. Second, most associations identified in the bioinformatic analyses were correlational, and additional functional experiments are needed to clarify the causal role of CLTC in OSCC progression. Third, several potential confounding factors should be considered when interpreting the transcriptomic and survival analyses. The TCGA-HNSC cohort contains tumors from anatomically and molecularly heterogeneous head and neck sites, and OSCC-specific analyses may still be influenced by tumor subsite, HPV status, smoking and alcohol exposure, treatment history, pathological stage, tumor purity, stromal content, and immune-cell composition. Bulk RNA-seq-based CLTC expression may therefore reflect both tumor-cell-intrinsic expression and differences in microenvironmental composition. Fourth, the scRNA-seq analysis was based on eight OSCC samples, and cell-type annotation may be affected by dropout events, sample-specific variation, batch correction, and marker-gene selection. Although epithelial and inflammatory epithelial clusters were considered tumor epithelial-associated populations, future studies should further confirm malignant epithelial identity using copy-number variation inference, tumor-specific mutation information, or spatial co-localization. Fifth, the predicted drug sensitivity results were derived from computational modeling based on public pharmacogenomic datasets and may not fully capture OSCC-specific drug response, tumor heterogeneity, stromal interactions, or immune-mediated effects. Finally, molecular docking was performed using static structural models and cannot establish true binding affinity, intracellular accessibility, functional inhibition, or therapeutic efficacy.

Although this study systematically explored the expression pattern, prognostic value, immune relevance, and potential biological significance of CLTC in OSCC, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, part of the clinical evidence was derived from retrospective public datasets, and the tissue validation cohort was from a single center with a limited sample size, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. Prospective multicenter validation and in vivo animal experiments were not performed. Although CLTC protein upregulation was confirmed by Western blot and functional assays were further validated in a second OSCC cell line, rescue experiments and IHC validation in a larger tissue cohort were not included; therefore, the specificity and clinicopathological relevance of CLTC require further confirmation. In addition, although enrichment analyses suggested that CLTC may be associated with MAPK, PI3K-Akt, extracellular matrix remodeling, immune-related pathways, ribosomal function, energy metabolism, and cell adhesion, these findings remain largely transcriptome-level associations, and the precise molecular mechanisms remain unclear. The present experiments mainly focused on malignant phenotypes rather than the canonical role of CLTC in clathrin-mediated endocytosis. Future mechanistic studies should therefore incorporate CLTC perturbation in multiple OSCC cell lines, patient-derived organoids, and preferably in vivo xenograft or patient-derived xenograft models, followed by rescue experiments to confirm specificity. Further assays are needed to evaluate transferrin uptake, EGFR internalization/recycling/degradation, integrin trafficking, and downstream p-AKT, p-ERK, and p-STAT3 signaling to determine whether CLTC modulates OSCC progression or chemotherapy response through receptor trafficking. Because CLTC also participates in mitotic spindle organization, future studies should examine α-tubulin organization, TACC3-related spindle localization, chromosome congression, mitotic index, and sensitivity to microtubule-targeting drugs. Moreover, transcriptomic findings related to ribosomal function, energy metabolism, and cell adhesion should be validated using qPCR, Western blotting, proteomics, ribosome profiling, Seahorse metabolic assays, and adhesion/matrix remodeling assays. Macrophage-centered mechanisms should also be tested using tumor cell-macrophage and tumor cell-T cell co-culture systems, cytokine blockade, spatial transcriptomics, multiplex immunofluorescence, and functional immune-killing assays. Finally, although drug sensitivity prediction and molecular docking suggested a potential association between CLTC expression and responses to paclitaxel, 5-fluorouracil, cisplatin, oxaliplatin, and docetaxel, these findings remain computational and correlative. Direct drug-response assays should be performed to test whether CLTC knockdown or overexpression alters IC50 values, apoptosis, cell-cycle arrest, and DNA-damage responses after chemotherapy. These future studies would help clarify whether CLTC directly drives OSCC progression and treatment response or instead serves as a marker of broader malignant and immunosuppressive tumor states.

Conclusion

This study systematically revealed the abnormal overexpression of CLTC in OSCC and found that it is closely related to TMB, immune microenvironment characteristics, and patient prognosis. High expression of CLTC is associated with poorer survival outcomes. Moreover, high CLTC expression is associated with reduced CD8 + T-cell infiltration, decreased cytotoxic activity, and enhanced immune exclusion and suppression, and is bioinformatically associated with ribosomal function, energy metabolism, and cell adhesion-related pathways. However, direct regulation of key genes in these pathways by CLTC remains to be experimentally validated. In summary, CLTC is a prognostic biomarker with clinical value and also provides new ideas for future targeted therapy and immunointervention.
