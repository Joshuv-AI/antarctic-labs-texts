Protracted exposure to low-dose ionising radiation and cancer incidence among Canadian nuclear power plant workers
Authors: Patrick Hinton, Laura Andrea Rodriguez-Villamizar, Philippe Prince, Tim Prendergast, Minh T Do, Paul A Demers, Cheryl E Peters, Lydia B Zablotska, Paul J Villeneuve
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesIonising radiation is a human carcinogen; however, there are uncertainties about the shape of the exposure-response function at low doses. We evaluated the relationship between radiation dose and cancer incidence in a cohort of Canadian nuclear power plant workers (NPPWs) with protracted exposures to low-dose ionising radiation.MethodsThe cohort included 75 350 workers employed at one of five Canadian nuclear power plants any time between 1945 and 2010. Exposure to cumulative whole-body effective dose was determined through personal monitoring. A total of 4370 incident cancers were identified through record linkage of these workers to national cancer registries (1969–2010). Vital status was determined through linkages to national mortality and tax databases. Standardised incidence ratios (SIRs) were calculated to compare cancer incidence rates of the cohort with the Canadian general population. Poisson regression was used to characterise dose-response relationships via categorical and linear excess relative risk (ERR) models.ResultsSignificantly elevated SIRs were found for solid cancers (combined), melanoma, colon and prostate cancer, while a reduced SIR was found for lung cancer. Positive, but not statistically significant excess risks were found for melanoma (ERR/100 mSv=0.32; 95% CI: −0.23 to 0.87) and prostate cancer (ERR/100 mSv=0.12; 95% CI: −0.05 to 0.29). An inverse association was found for lung cancer (ERR/100 mSv=−0.18; 95% CI: −0.01 to –0.36).ConclusionsOur findings suggest that Canadian NPPWs have increased risks of prostate cancer and melanoma from low-dose ionising radiation exposure. Estimates should be cautiously interpreted due to the inability to adjust for demographic and lifestyle factors.Keywords: Radiation, Ionizing; Workers; Occupational Health

Introduction

The link between ionising radiation and cancer is well established.1 Initially, increased risks were primarily reported in individuals exposed to acute high-dose radiation, such as atomic bomb survivors and patients treated with radiotherapy.1 Risks associated with low-dose exposures (≤100 mSv) were extrapolated from these studies leading to uncertainties about the shape of the dose-response, dose-rate factors and effects of acute versus protracted exposures.1 2 Subsequently, a series of investigations were carried out in occupational cohorts of workers with higher than background exposures.2

Several multinational pooled cohort analyses have evaluated cancer mortality risks from protracted low-dose radiation exposures among nuclear energy workers.3,8 These studies are generally consistent with positive dose-response relationships for haematological malignancies, namely leukaemia and multiple myeloma, but less so for solid cancers. However, a 2023 update of the International Nuclear Workers Study (INWORKS) reported increased risks of lung (excess relative risk (ERR)/grey (Gy)=0.65; 95% credible interval (CrI): 0.24 to 1.07), prostate (ERR/Gy=0.44; 95% CrI: −0.06 to 0.91), colon (ERR/Gy=0.53; 95% CrI: −0.07 to 1.11) and all solid cancer (ERR/Gy=0.52; 90% CI: 0.27 to 0.77).6 8

Most studies of radiation exposure and cancer in nuclear worker cohorts have focused on mortality, rather than incidence.3,13 This is largely due to a lack of accessible incidence data in many regions. Characterising the risks between exposure to ionising radiation and incident, rather than mortality cancer, offers several advantages. Incident outcomes better allow for the characterisation of biologically relevant latency periods. Importantly for rarer radiogenic cancers, incident outcomes are more frequent and less prone to survival-related biases. This is important when investigating cancers with effective treatment options and favourable prognoses.

In Canada, a typical contemporary nuclear power plant worker (NPPW) receives an occupational radiation dose of approximately one mSv per year.14 15 As such, NPPWs represent a large occupational group exposed to radiation at levels much higher than the general population. Importantly, cohorts of these workers may provide long-term exposure follow-up and sufficient exposure variability to characterise dose-response relationships.14 These workers are predominantly exposed to external penetrating ionising radiation in the form of gamma rays (γ), and to a lesser extent alpha (α)-particles and neutrons. Internal exposures arise primarily from tritium (3H) beta (β)-particles present in heavy-water-modulated reactors, though these tend to be minimal.16

Previous studies of nuclear energy workers captured in Health Canada’s National Dose Registry (NDR), with follow-up through 1994, have investigated radiation-related risks for cancer mortality9 16 17 and leukaemia incidence in a sample of their offspring.18 These studies contributed to early multinational pooled mortality analyses of nuclear energy workers,3 4 7 but appear to have biased pooled estimates—especially for solid cancers. However, in a later revised analysis of cancer mortality in NDR nuclear energy workers, Zablotska et al9 found no dose-related increases in solid cancers, although an increased, but not statistically significant ERR per sievert (Sv) was found for leukaemia (ERR/Sv=5.06; 95% CI: −1.49 to 57.8). This revised analysis highlighted dose record errors associated with early workers employed at Atomic Energy of Canada Ltd. (AECL).

Canada’s NDR has recently been linked to national mortality and cancer incidence registries, extending the incidence follow-up by more than two decades (1988–2010).19 The NDR includes approximately 75 000 NPPWs as of 2010. Our main objectives include: (1) an external-cohort analysis comparing cancer incidence rates among Canadian NPPWs to the Canadian population and (2) an internal-cohort analysis to characterise the shape of dose-response patterns for multiple cancer sites.

Methods

The NDR of Canada

Comprehensive descriptions of the Canadian NDR have been published.14 16 20 21 Since 1951, the NDR has served as the centralised dose-record system of Canadians monitored for occupational exposure to ionising radiation. It is a dynamic registry with new workers entering and leaving regularly. Its centralised design allows for the capture of lifetime dose records for workers who move from one nuclear power facility to another as well as those employed in other occupations exposed to ionising radiation (eg, uranium miller to NPPW). While the Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission mandates radiation monitoring for all workers with a reasonable probability of receiving an effective dose ≥5 mSv in a 1-year period,22 in practice, all NPPWs undergo monitoring and are included in the dose record used in this study.14 As of 2018, the NDR contains records for nearly one million Canadian workers across 114 job classes, including medical radiation technologists, dentists and nuclear energy workers, among others.14

Study population: NPPWs

The NDR contains several categories of nuclear energy workers, including workers in uranium mines and mills, radioactive waste management, nuclear research facilities and NPPs. For this study, eligible workers had at least one annual monitoring dose record from 1969 onwards, from one (or more) of the following Canadian NPP employers:

Workers were identified using employer codes within the NDR database. The year 1969 represents the earliest year for which incident cancer cases are available within national cancer incidence registries. However, doses prior to this were included in cumulative dose measures. Individuals who worked for any year at AECL, a nuclear research facility, were excluded from the cohort out of an abundance of caution given concerns surrounding completeness and accuracy of their exposure data, particularly among early workers,9 and a specific focus on NPPWs in this study.

Given that the NDR functions first and foremost as an administrative database with the primary goal of helping regulatory authorities with their surveillance mandate,14 some data adjustments have previously been made and personal information data (eg, sex) were not always captured rigorously. Additional cohort restrictions were applied to remove these irregular records and permit appropriate internal and external cohort analyses (online supplemental figure 1). For example, we excluded workers with irregularities in their annual or cumulative dose records (ie, invalid doses). Restrictions regarding vital status include the exclusion of workers with an attained age of ≥110 without a corresponding death link at the end of mortality follow-up (likely representing errors in recorded personal identifiers such as birth date), workers with a missing birth year, and those for which vital status could not be identified through national mortality and tax files. Other restrictions include the omission of workers with an undefined or missing sex, a ‘negative’ duration of follow-up (due to linkage errors or diagnosis of cancer prior to the start of monitoring), and those with an age of first exposure of <14 or >80.

Dosimetry

Exposure information for NPPWs was provided by licensed Dosimetry Service Providers (DSPs).14 Individual dose monitoring, using personal badge dosimeters, is mandatory for all nuclear energy workers and DSPs who report all doses to the NDR.14 The current study focuses on cumulative ionising radiation exposures from both external and internal sources—represented using effective doses. Specifically, doses are expressed in mSv and calculated as the amount of energy absorbed per gram of tissue. This measure is weighted according to modifying factors specific to the relative biological damage elicited by different types of ionising radiation, as well as by appropriate tissue weighting factors. Internal doses (primarily from tritium) are generally small relative to external exposures in this workforce and have minimal impact on overall risk estimates, as shown in prior Canadian studies.9 16

For each NPPW, annual summary doses of penetrating whole-body radiation exposure were calculated. For most workers, doses were recorded on a quarterly basis. A 5-year lagged cumulative measure of dose was constructed by summing annual exposures and forms the basis for dose estimates used in main analyses. Consistent with previous analyses of the NDR,9 16 19 a dose of zero mSv was recorded where exposures were below the dosimeter detection limit (typically 0.1–0.2 mSv).16

Ascertainment of cancer incidence

The NDR was linked to national mortality and cancer incidence data by Statistics Canada using their Social Data Linkage Environment.23 Specifically, the updated record linkage of individual-level occupational histories of NDR workers, up to 2018, was completed for the following databases: National Cancer Incidence Reporting System (1969–1991), Canadian Cancer Registry (CCR; 1992–2010), Canadian Vital Statistics Death Database (CVSD; 1926–2020) and the T1 Personal Master tax File (1981–2021). The national tax file was used to confirm CVSD vital status. This linkage allowed for the follow-up period of the cohort to be extended through 2010 for cancer incidence, and through 2020 for mortality. As a place of residence was not tracked within the NDR, we could only identify incident cancers through 2010, as from 2010 onwards the CCR excludes data from the province of Québec.

The record linkage was performed using probabilistic linkage methodology and key variables used included: Social Insurance Number (SIN), date of birth, given names and surname, place of birth and sex. In Canada, SIN numbers uniquely identify Canadian residents; however, they were not introduced until 1964. Statistics Canada obtained sufficient personal identifiable information to conduct the probabilistic record linkage for 883 150 of the 999 600 (92.2%) unique individuals in the NDR.

Disease classification

For incident cancers that occurred before 1992, the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Ninth Revision (ICD-9) coding was used, whereas the ICD for Oncology, Third Edition (ICD-O-3) coding was used from 1992 onwards (online supplemental table 1). As the reporting procedures for non-melanoma skin cancer varied by province, it was excluded from analysis.

Statistical analysis

Workers contributed person-years from 1 January of the first year reported with an annual dose record (1969 or later) until the earliest date of: first (primary) cancer diagnosis; death; or 31 December 2010 (end of follow-up). Person-years were cross-tabulated by sex (male/female), attained age (5-year intervals) and calendar period (5-year intervals). For external analyses, standardised incidence ratios (SIRs) were calculated via indirect standardisation using Canadian cancer incidence rates (1969–2010) specific to the stratifications used in the tabulation of cohort person-years. Two-sided 95% CIs on the SIR estimates were calculated under the assumption that the observed incidence was Poisson distributed.24

Due to reporting requirements for data analysed within the Statistics Canada’s Research Data Centre Network, cancer sites with <10 cases were excluded from analyses, and all counts presented have been rounded to the nearest 10. However, the derivation of SIRs and ERRs is based on unrounded counts.

We used Poisson regression to conduct internal cohort analyses. Person-years were allocated by sex, 5-year age-groupings, calendar period, cumulative whole-body effective radiation dose (0, >0, 10, 50, ≥ 100 mSv), and potential effect modifiers: duration of exposure (<2, 2 to <4, 4 to <11, ≥11 years), birth cohort (<1930, 1930–1949, 1950–1959, 1960), age at first exposure (<35, 35–44, 45–54, ≥55 years) and time since last exposure (<10, 10–19, ≥20 years). Dose was modelled using both categorical and quasi-continuous (11 categories) forms. Workers with cumulative doses >500 mSv were excluded from internal analyses given our focus to estimate risk at lower doses. The person-year weighted mean (cumulative effective) dose in each cross-classified cell was used for quasi-continuous ERR analyses. Cumulative doses were lagged by 5 years for all cancer sites.

The linear ERR model is commonly used to model cumulative exposure to radiation and cancer risk. Mathematically, these models assume that the background cancer incidence rate r(d, x, z) depends on: cumulative exposure to radiation, d; a vector of covariates, x, used to describe the background cancer incidence rate and a vector of covariates, z, that can modify the exposure–response relationship.24 25 This can be written as:

The background cancer rate was modelled as r0x=e∝x accompanied by a linear ERR model in d was fitted, namely RR=1+βd, where β measures the unit increase in the ERR per unit increase in d. Variations in the exposure-response across possible effect modifiers (z) were assessed by comparing the above model to the model RR=1+βjd where βj represents the ERR per increase in d across category zj. Under the null hypothesis where there is no effect modification, the differences in model deviances follows a χ2 distribution with j-1 df. ERR models were scaled in relation to a 100 mSv increase in cumulative exposure.

All ERR models were fitted and maximum likelihood point and interval estimates were obtained using the AMFIT program (EPICURE software).26 Despite the exploratory nature of our analyses, no adjustments were made for multiple comparisons, in line with Rothman’s recommendations.27 Secondary and sensitivity analyses were performed for sites of interest, selected post hoc, for which model convergence was obtainable. The assessment of effect modification was performed for solid cancers, leukaemia and cancers of the prostate and lung. Sensitivity analyses were performed to: (1) evaluate differences in ERR estimates across alternate latency exposure periods (2 years for haematological cancers, 10 years for solid cancers) and (2) evaluate differences in solid and haematological-cancer ERR estimates across different study populations (all workers vs workers monitored ≥365 days vs workers first monitored ≥1985 vs workers who had ≥1 mSv cumulative exposure).28 Similar to analyses performed by Lee et al,28 subgroup analyses were performed to examine workers with improved dosimetry (first monitored ≥1985), to exclude shorter-term workers (≥365 monitoring days) and to focus on those with ‘meaningful’ exposures (≥1 mSv cumulative dose).

Results

In total, 4370 incident cancers were identified in the follow-up of the cohort. A total of 1 118 620 person-years at risk were accrued across the 75 350 (nmale=65 570, nfemale=9780) cohort members (online supplemental figure 1). The mean age at first radiation exposure (monitoring) was 34.62 (SD=11.56). The mean unlagged cumulative dose of the cohort was 12.00 mSv (SD=30.42 mSv), with males receiving a considerably higher cumulative mean dose (13.45 mSv, SD=32.30 mSv) when compared with females (2.24 mSv, SD=6.70 mSv). The distribution of cumulative dose was highly positively skewed (online supplemental figure 2).

We found no evidence of effect modification by duration of monitoring (ie, exposure), birth cohort, time since last exposure or age at first exposure across cancer sites of interest (table 5 and online supplemental table 4) except for the association between cumulative dose and prostate cancer by age at first exposure. Discussion

Our analysis of ~75 000 Canadian NPPWs provides updated radiation risk estimates for cancer outcomes, and includes more than 15 years of additional follow-up since this subcohort was last analysed.9 This is the first study to comprehensively characterise cancer incidence in the NDR NPPW subcohort. Study findings revealed that while Canadian NPPWs experienced increased risks for developing a number of solid cancers (melanoma, prostate, colon cancer), protracted occupational exposure to whole-body ionising radiation was not significantly associated with site-specific cancer incidence. However, the reported elevated SIRs combined with positive ERR estimates for melanoma and prostate cancer provide some indications of increased risks, despite confounding and screening-related limitations. Effect modification by age at first exposure was significant for the relationship between dose and prostate cancer (p=0.01), but not evident for other cancer sites. Risk estimates were similar across alternate exposure lag periods and study population subgroups.

The summary dose-response estimates for solid cancer (combined) in this study are similar to those found in other nuclear worker cohorts6 10 12 13 29 and the Life Span Study (LSS) of Japanese atomic bomb survivors.30 For example, our main estimate is approximately equivalent to an ERR/Sv of 0.20 (95% CI: −0.70 to 1.10), which is relatively consistent with the ERR/Sv of 0.22 (90% CI: 0.03 to 0.42) reported in the updated cancer incidence analysis of the UK National Registry for Radiation Workers cohort31 and the ERR/Sv of 0.19 (95% CI: −0.10 to 0.52) reported in the 2023 pooled mortality analysis of US nuclear energy workers.12 Our estimate also aligns with the US Million Person Study (MPS) which found an ERR/100 mGy of 0.01 (95% CI: −0.03 to 0.05) for all solid cancer mortality.13 Surprisingly, none of the haematological malignancies in our study displayed positive dose-response estimates other than non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma (ERR/100 mSv=0.25; 95% CI: −0.28 to 0.77). This finding, particularly relating to leukaemia, somewhat contrasts the most recent mortality analysis of this subcohort9 and several pooled analyses of nuclear energy workers.3,57 13 Similarly, while we found a negative dose-response for lung cancer (ERR/100 mSv=−0.18; 95% CI: −0.01 to –0.36), analyses of related cohorts4 8 10 12 and the LSS30 have typically observed positive relationships. Yet, the MPS reported an ERR/100 mGy of −0.04 (95% CI: −0.11 to 0.02) for lung cancer mortality, more consistent with our estimates.13

A key finding of our investigation relates to the elevated risk of prostate cancer apparent with both a statistically significant positive SIR, as well as suggestive dose-response estimates. A positive risk estimate for prostate cancer mortality was reported in earlier analyses of this subcohort (ERR/Sv=0.43; 95% CI: −1.47 to 17.7), who were followed through 1994, however, the precision of this estimate was poor as it was only based on 22 deaths.9 Our current analysis offers the important advantages of a larger cohort and the ascertainment of incident cancers, which combined, identified 1120 men diagnosed with prostate cancer. The identification of aetiological risk factors for prostate cancer is best ascertained using incident outcomes as the 5-year survival rate exceeds 90%.32

In other studies, Richardson et al reported an ERR/Gy of 0.44 (95% CI: −0.06 to 0.91) for prostate cancer mortality in the INWORKS cohort.8 The associated dose-response exhibited little curvature, supporting a linear model. In a recent analysis of prostate cancer incidence in the LSS, a stronger estimate of association was reported (ERR/Gy=0.57; 95% CI: 0.21 to 1.00).33 The risk of incident prostate cancer was considerably higher than for mortality in the LSS (ERR/Gy=0.33; 95% CI: <0 to 1.25), further highlighting the importance of incidence data.30 A number of incident analyses in other radiation-exposed populations have reported increased risks of prostate cancer, such as in Canadian and South Korean medical radiation workers,28 32 Nordic airline pilots34 and Australians who participated in British nuclear tests.35 The elevated prostate cancer ERR among workers first exposed at older ages (≥55 years) found in our study may reflect a combination of biological susceptibility, increased baseline risk in this age group and potential biases such as differential screening or healthy-worker survivor effects. In addition, smaller numbers of cases and overall person-years of follow-up in this subgroup may contribute to statistical uncertainty. These findings should be interpreted cautiously and warrant further investigation in pooled or larger cohort studies.

Compared with other occupational cohorts exposed to ionising radiation (eg, medical workers), epidemiological investigations of NPPWs present a few methodological advantages, including (1) stringent dosimetry practices and radiation protection regulations, including mandatory and frequent reporting—ensuring exposure misclassification is minimised and (2) greater variability and range of exposure across job classes—increasing statistical power when examining risks at low dose.14 Our study has several other notable strengths. First and foremost, the methodological advantages imparted through the use of incident, as opposed to mortality data, especially for rare cancers and those with good survival. Second, the centralised design of the NDR allowed for the capture of radiation doses accrued at non-NPP sources, especially for some early workers—reducing measurement error. Third, the NPPW subcohort is relatively large and has matured substantially since it was last analysed (for mortality). Last, doses are recorded based on individual-level, accurate and precise dosimetry.

Differences in hiring practices, the extent of temporary or subcontracted labour, and the inclusion of workers whose first monitored exposure occurred outside of the NPP setting may explain why our cohort exhibits an older mean age at first occupational radiation exposure (34.62 years) compared with some other nuclear worker cohorts.36 For example, job classes typically associated with temporary or subcontracted labour showed elevated mean ages at first exposure, including contractors (36.4 years) and construction (37.6 years).

We acknowledge the potential for screening-related biases relating to our prostate cancer findings. Consistent with the healthy-worker effect, NPPWs may be more likely to be screened for prostate cancer than the general male population in Canada—resulting in elevated SIRs. Overdiagnosis of prostate cancers is of particular concern with respect to this potential bias.37 These phenomena may contribute to risk estimate differences between occupational incident and mortality studies of prostate cancer. Despite this, screening biases would have a greater effect on external-cohort comparisons compared with internal ones, where the rates of screening are thought to be similar across NPPWs.

Similar to prostate cancer, melanoma was found to have a statistically significant positive SIR and suggestive dose-response estimates. Previous investigations of the Canadian NDR have also reported SIRs above unity for this site,19 including within dental workers.38 Recent mortality analyses of INWORKS8 and the US nuclear workers cohort12 support this association. It should be noted that our estimates may be subject to ethnicity-related biases. Given that rates of melanoma are markedly higher in Caucasian populations (when compared with other ethnicities), it is possible that our SIR is inflated if the NPPW subcohort is of greater Caucasian proportion than the general Canadian population. This may also confound internal dose-response estimates if radiation dose across NPPWs is related to ethnicity, but this is believed to be of lesser concern. We lacked the information on ethnicity required to investigate this potential bias.

The differences between some of our ERR estimates and those presented in earlier Canadian9 19 and international studies,3,58 11 12 particularly the null and negative ERRs for leukaemia and lung cancer, respectively, could be attributed to cohort differences (eg, all nuclear energy workers vs NPPWs), variation in study design, unmeasured confounding and biases, and greater statistical power afforded by incidence data. The use of incidence, rather than mortality, allowed for substantially more cases, which is especially valuable for cancers with relatively good survival (eg, leukaemia) or those affected by competing risks (eg, lung cancer). While our melanoma findings are consistent with some prior occupational studies,8 12 19 confounding or detection bias may still contribute. These site-specific patterns reinforce the need for cautious interpretation, but highlight the advantages of incidence data when evaluating risks from low-dose radiation exposure.

Like other studies of occupational radiation exposures and cancer, a key limitation of our analyses is the lack of information on confounding factors. Specifically, we could not control for lifestyle factors and non-occupational exposures, such as smoking history, alcohol consumption and medical radiation exposures. In particular, the absence of smoking data is a limitation for outcomes such as lung cancer, where smoking is a strong independent risk factor. That said, evidence from contemporary pooled nuclear worker studies (eg, INWORKS) suggests that confounding by smoking is likely minimal,6 39 given the typically weak or absent association between smoking and radiation dose in these settings. Integration of residential information alone would allow for the external linkage of area-level measures of socioeconomic status and would permit appropriate censoring of Québec residents—increasing statistical power by permitting extended follow-up to more recent years and substantially reducing potential confounding bias of dose-response estimates.

A number of other limitations are worth considering. First, recording of radiation doses below dosimeter detection thresholds will lead to an underestimation of true cumulative exposures. Yet, the dosimetry committee of IARC has previously suggested that the impact of this error on Canadian risk estimates is low.16 40 Additionally, Zablotska et al. (2004) found minimal difference in cancer risk estimates when sensitivity analyses were applied to correct this potential underestimation of cumulative dose in Canadian nuclear energy workers.16 Second, we lack detailed information on shielding, intensity and geometry of radiation exposures. This issue is thought to be minor and would represent non-differential error—biasing estimates towards the null. Third, the cohort analysed here is predominately male, and female workers were typically employed in positions with comparatively reduced exposure profiles. As a result, we were unable to perform analyses for select female-specific sites (eg, ovarian cancer) and provide sex-stratified estimates for many cancers. Last, our analysis may be subject to left truncation bias, as some workers may have accrued occupational radiation exposure prior to the start of cancer incidence follow-up in 1969, potentially introducing health-related selection into the cohort and attenuating risk estimates.

In summary, our study represents the first comprehensive characterisation of cancer incidence within the NPPW subcohort of the Canadian NDR. Our findings support a relationship between protracted ionising radiation exposures and prostate cancer and melanoma; however, cautious interpretation of risk estimates is warranted given the inability to control for demographic and lifestyle confounders. Our solid cancer estimates aligned closely with a number of recently published estimates from national and multinational pooled cohorts. Incorporating demographic, lifestyle and other socioeconomic measures into this cohort would prove valuable.
