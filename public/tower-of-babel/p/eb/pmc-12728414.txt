Sex differences in the progression of cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic syndrome
Authors: Satoshi Taya, Kentaro Ejiri, Hidehiro Kaneko, Yuta Suzuki, Toru Miyoshi, Atsushi Mizuno, Toshiyuki Ko, Takahiro Jimba, Tatsuhiko Azegami, Akira Okada, Katsuhito Fujiu, Norifumi Takeda, Hiroyuki Morita, Kaori Hayashi, Koichi Node, Masaomi Nangaku, Hideo Yasunaga, Norihiko Takeda, Shinsuke Yuasa, Maciej Banach
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsCardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) syndrome is a novel disease concept; however, sex differences in its progression remain uncertain. This study aimed to quantify the risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) events across CKM stages and to explore sex differences in this association.Methods and resultsWe included 1 332 436 individuals (581 423 males and 751 013 females) from the DeSC database between 2014 and 2023 who had no prior CVD (i.e. CKM Stage 4). CKM stages were categorized as follows: Stage 0 (no CKM risk factors); Stage 1 (excess or dysfunctional adiposity); Stage 2 [metabolic risk factors and chronic kidney diseases (CKD)], and Stage 3 (subclinical CVD). We used Cox models to examine the association of CKM stages with the risk of CVD events (newly developed CKM Stage 4), including myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and peripheral artery disease. The progression from CKM Stages 0 to 3 showed a dose-dependent increase in adjusted hazard ratios (HR) for developing CVD events, with the highest risk at Stage 3 [1.85 (95% CI: 1.80–1.90)]. A similar pattern was observed in both males and females. However, the magnitude of associations for CKM stages 1–3 differed between the sexes: HR by Stage 1, 1.12 (1.04–1.21) vs. 1.12 (1.07–1.16); by Stage 2, 1.78 (1.69–1.88) vs. 1.43 (1.39–1.48); by Stage 3, 1.99 (1.89–2.10) vs. 1.82 (1.76–1.88); and P-for-interaction values were 0.87, < 0.001, and 0.005, respectively.ConclusionIn this large nationwide cohort, CKM stage progression was associated with higher CVD risk in both sexes, with modest sex-specific differences. These findings highlight the value of CKM staging for early risk assessment, regardless of sex.Keywords: Cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic syndrome, Cardiovascular disease, Sex difference

Introduction

Over the past three decades, cardiac, renal, and metabolic conditions have been increasingly common globally.1 For instance, the prevalence of diabetes and chronic kidney disease (CKD) has been increasing, while the global number of cardiovascular disease (CVD) nearly doubled from 1990 to 2019.2 In clinical settings, dysfunction in the heart, kidneys, and metabolic system—including diabetes and obesity—frequently coexists, with these conditions sharing common risk factors and mutually exacerbating one another.3 A notable example is cardiorenal syndrome, which reflects the bidirectional and synergistic interplay between cardiac and renal dysfunction, underscoring the strong association between CVD and CKD.4,5 In response to this interconnection, cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) syndrome, recently proposed by the American Heart Association, is a new disease concept which focuses on the pathophysiological continuum linking metabolic abnormalities to obesity, renal failure, and CVD.6–10 CKM syndrome encompasses a continuum of health states, proposing 5 categories: Stages 0 (no risk factors); Stage 1 (Excess or dysfunctional adiposity); Stage 2 (Metabolic risk factors and CKD); Stage 3 (Subclinical CVD in CKM); and Stage 4 (clinical CVD), and is thought to progress over time.10 The concept of CKM staging highlights the pathophysiological progression of CKM syndrome (i.e. excess or dysfunction of adipose tissue), underscores the importance of detection of early CKM stages to prevent CVD events, and emphasizes the need for effective risk assessment and therapeutic approaches for each CKM stage in clinical practice.

Although this concept is supported by a growing body of evidence, several critical knowledge gaps remain. First, previous studies reported characteristics and mortality risks of each CKM stage;1,11,12 however, there is a lack of evidence for quantitative evaluation of its progression from each stage to CVD events (i.e. CKM Stage 4) other than mortality. Second, albeit females have a higher mortality risk but a lower prevalence of CKM Stage 3 compared with males,12 sex differences in the risk of CVD events according to each CKM stage are uncertain. Finally, whether the risk stratification utilizing CKM staging could be promising regardless of sex has not been fully investigated.

Given the growing attention to CKM syndrome, addressing these gaps is essential to improve prevention and treatment. Therefore, we aimed to quantify the risk of clinical CVD events according to CKM Stages 0–3 and to explore sex differences in the association, using a large nationwide cohort database in Japan.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was a population-based observational cohort study based on nationwide real-world data, the DeSC database (DeSC Healthcare, Inc., Tokyo, Japan).13 The DeSC database collects data on individual health insurance records and health check-up data (e.g. anthropometric measurements and blood tests). This database stores inpatient and outpatient administrative claims data. Diagnoses (confirmed or suspected) are registered using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The University of Tokyo (Approval no. 2021010NI) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Because all data in the DeSC database are anonymized, which poses minimal risk to participants’ privacy, informed consent from individual participants was not required.

We identified 1 849 187 individuals for whom we could obtain data registered in the DeSC database from April 2014 through August 2023, including body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, haemoglobin A1c, blood pressure, lipid profile, and renal function (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1). Individuals were excluded from the study participation if they met any of the following criteria: prior history of CVD events (i.e. excluding CKM Stage 4) including myocardial infarction (MI), stroke, heart failure (HF), atrial fibrillation (AF) and peripheral arterial disease (PAD) (n = 204 180); missing data on alcohol consumption (n = 80 114); and missing data on physical inactivity (n = 232 457). Finally, a total of 1 332 436 individuals were included in the final analytic population.

Definition of cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic syndrome stages

CKM syndrome stages (i.e. Stages 0–4) were defined based on the criteria in the statement from the American Heart Association, with modifications for the DeSC database (see Supplementary material online, Table S1). Briefly, Stage 0 included participants without CKM risk factors who had a normal BMI (<23 kg/m2, calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared) and a normal waist circumference (<80 cm for women or <90 cm for men), and did not meet criteria for the other stages. Stage 1 included those with obesity and metabolic disorders but without other CKM risk factors, who had either BMI ≥23 kg/m2, a waist circumference ≥80 cm for women or ≥90 cm for men, or prediabetes (haemoglobin A1c of 5.7–6.4%, or taking diabetes medications). Stage 2 included those with metabolic risk factors or CKD as moderate- to high-risk CKD according to Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria.14 Metabolic risk factors were defined as the presence of any of the following: elevated fasting serum triglyceride ≥135 mg/dL, hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥130 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥80 mmHg, and/or the use of antihypertensive medications), diabetes (haemoglobin A1c ≥ 6.5% and/or the use of diabetes medications), or metabolic syndrome. Metabolic syndrome was defined as the presence of three or more of the following: waist circumference ≥80 cm for women or ≥90 cm for men, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol <50 mg/dL for women or <40 mg/dL for men, triglycerides ≥150 mg/dL, hypertension, and diabetes. Finally, although the original framework defines CKM Stage 3 by the presence of subclinical CVD (e.g. diagnosed by coronary artery calcification or elevated cardiac biomarkers like b-type natriuretic peptide), and/or very-high-risk CKD or high predicted 10-year CVD risk, our study’s definition of CKM Stage 3 was based solely on very-high-risk CKD and/or high predicted 10-year CVD risk. For the definition of very-high-risk CKD, since there were no quantitative measures for urine protein in the DeSC database, we used a semi-quantitative qualitative measure representing negative (−) or trace (±), 1+, and ≥2+ as A1, A2, and A3 of albuminuria categories in the KDIGO criteria, respectively.15 We defined high predicted 10-year CVD risk using the Pooled Cohort Equation ≥10%.16,17 Stage 4 was defined as the outcome in our study as below.

Outcomes and covariates

Our primary outcome was the incidence of CVD events corresponding to CKM Stage 4, including MI, stroke, HF, AF, and PAD (see Supplementary material online, Table S1). Each event was defined in accordance with the ICD-10 as follows: MI (ICD-10 codes I210–I214 and I219), stroke (ICD-10 codes I630, I631–I636, I638, I639, I600–I611, I613–I616, I619, I629, and G459), HF (ICD-10 codes I500, I501, I509, and I110), AF (ICD-10 codes I480, I481, I482, I483, I484, and I489), and PAD (ICD-10 codes I7020 and I7021). We examined the incidence of outcomes and the time from baseline to the outcomes at each stage and compared them across the different CKM stages. Individuals were censored at outcome occurrence, insurance withdrawal (including death), or the end of the study follow-up in August 2023.

Smoking status (current or non-current/never smoker), the frequency of alcohol consumption (every day or not every day), and physical activity (inactive or active) were assessed using information from the self-administered questionnaires.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics stratified by CKM Stages 0–3 were summarized as median with interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables and as number (%) for categorical variables. We compared each variable between CKM stages using Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and χ2 test for categorical variables. We plotted the cumulative incidence of CVD events as CKM Stage 4 according to CKM Stages 0–3 using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared the cumulative incidence among strata using the log-rank test. To assess the association between CKM Stages 0–3 and the incidence of CVD events as CKM Stage 4, we estimated the hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) using Cox proportional hazards regression models: Model 1 for an unadjusted model; Model 2 adjusted for age and sex; and Model 3 further adjusted for alcohol consumption and physical activity. Although Model 3 was our main model, to assess the magnitude of CKM staging in the risk of CVD events over traditional cardiovascular risk factors, we additionally constructed Model 4, further adjusted for BMI, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, and smoking. We tested the interaction between males and females using likelihood ratio tests.

We defined CKM Stage 3 of those with very-high-risk CKD and CVD by a 10-year estimated CVD risk based on the Pooled Cohort Equation ≥10%; however, different CVD risk calculators were used for the definitions of CKM Stage 3 as subclinical CVD in previous studies. Thus, we further conducted several sensitivity analyses to confirm the robustness of our primary findings. As very-high-risk 10-year estimated CVD, first, we used the same Pooled Cohort Equation but with a different threshold of ≥20%. Second, we used the Predicting Risk of cardiovascular disease EVENTs (PREVENT) 10-year risk equation with a cut-off of ≥20%.10 Third, we used the Suita score, a specific CVD risk calculator for Japanese people, with a cut-off of ≥56 points.18 Fourth, albeit the original definition of CVD events as CKM Stage 4 does not include aortic disease (e.g. thoracic and abdominal aortic aneurysm) as PAD, we assessed the risk of CVD events including aortic disease among CKM stages (see Supplementary material online, Table S1). Finally, since ageing is another key driver of the progression of CVD events, especially in older adults, we evaluated the association between CKM stages and CVD events after stratification by age (<40 years, 40–59 years, ≥ 60 years) and tested the interaction using likelihood ratio tests. We ran the same Cox models above in all sensitivity analyses. Two-tailed P value <0.05 was set as statistical significance. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata software (version 18; StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Baseline characteristics

In a total of 1 332 436 individuals with the median age of 64 years (IQR: 53–68) and 43.6% being male (Table 1 and Supplementary material online, Table S2), those with CKM Stage 3 were the most prevalent (41.2%). Progression across CKM stages was associated with a higher cardiovascular risk profile including older age, higher blood pressure, dyslipidaemia, and lower estimated glomerular filtration rate. On the other hand, between each CKM stage, CKM Stages 1 and 2 showed a largely similar pattern in most risk factors except for blood pressure and proteinuria, and CKM Stage 3 had the most unfavourable risk profile especially for age (∼20 or ∼10 years older than CKM Stage 0 or Stages 1 and 2, respectively), cigarette smoking, and alcohol consumption.

The proportion of CKM stages varied between male and female participants (Tables 2 and 3). CKM Stage 3 was the most prevalent in males, while CKM Stage 2 was the most frequently seen in females (see Supplementary material online, Table S3). In both sexes, a largely similar pattern was observed in the risk profile for CVD among CKM stages as in the overall population, but female participants with each CKM stage were relatively older compared to male participants with the same CKM stage. Furthermore, males had a higher BMI, a higher proportion of cigarette and alcohol consumption compared to females. On the other hand, females with CKM Stage 3 had a greater progression in elevated blood pressure, dyslipidaemia, and lower estimated glomerular filtration rate compared to males with the same stage.

Progression of cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (stage in males and females)

During the follow-up period [median: 3.1 years (IQR: 1.4–4.8)], a total of 119 951 CVD events occurred as follows: MI, n = 2870 (2.4%); stroke, n = 31 306 (26.1%); HF, n = 62 670 (52.2%), AF, n = 9305 (7.8%); PAD, n = 5065 (4.2%), and multiple events, n = 8735 (7.3%) (see Supplementary material online, Table S4). The progression of CKM Stages 0–3 was significantly associated with the incidence of CVD events (i.e. CKM Stage 4) (incidence per 10 000 person-years [95% CI]: 103.4 [100.9–105.9], 140.1 [136.9–143.4], 204.7 [202.5–206.9], and 458.3 [455.0–461.7], respectively), showing the highest incidence rate by CKM Stage 3 with the shortest follow-up period (median 1.8 years) (Figure 1, Table 4 and Supplementary material online, Table S5).

A significant, dose-dependent association was also observed between increasing CKM stages (Stages 1–3) and the risk of CVD events compared with CKM Stage 0 as the reference (Table 4). After adjusting for traditional risk factors, the association was attenuated but still significant; specifically, even in Model 4 adjusted for component risk factors themselves included in the CKM concept (e.g. hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia) [HR 1.19 (95% CI: 1.15–1.23) in Table 4].

In the sex-stratified analysis, a similar dose-dependent association was seen between the progression of CKM Stages 0–3 and the incidence of CVD events as the results in the overall population. However, the magnitude of association for each CKM stage differed between males and females (e.g. in Model 3: HR by Stage 1, 1.12 [1.04–1.21] vs. 1.12 [1.07–1.16]; by Stage 2, 1.78 [1.69–1.88] vs. 1.43 [1.39–1.48]; by Stage 3, 1.99 [1.89–2.10] vs. 1.82 [1.76–1.88]; and P-for-interaction values of 0.87, < 0.001, and 0.005, respectively) (Figure 1B, 1C, and Table 4). For males, CKM Stage 2 showed a slightly attenuated but comparable adjusted HR compared with Stage 3 after adjusting for risk factors (e.g. 1.78 [1.69–1.88] vs. 1.99 [1.89–2.10] in Model 3, Table 4). For females, on the other hand, CKM Stage 2 showed a significant but weaker association with the risk of CVD events compared with Stage 3 (e.g. 1.43 [1.39–1.48] vs. 1.82 [1.76–1.88] in Model 3, Table 4).

Sensitivity analysis

In the sensitivity analyses, the distribution of CKM Stages 0–3 varied depending on the specific CVD risk calculators (see Supplementary material online, Tables S6-S9). However, the results remained broadly consistent with the primary analysis. First, using the Pooled Cohort Equation with an elevated risk threshold of ≥20%, the association between CKM stages and the risk of CVD events was strengthened, particularly for CKM Stage 3 (see Supplementary material online, Table S10 and Figure S2). Second, using the PREVENT equation, a significant association between CKM Stage 3 and the risk of CVD events was also seen (see Supplementary material online, Table S11 and Figure S3). Third, using the Suita score, a specific CVD risk calculator for Japanese people, CKM Stages 0–3 were also significantly associated with the risk of CVD events (see Supplementary material online, Table S12 and Figure S4). Fourth, upon including aortic disease in the definition of CVD events, a significant association with CKM stages was also observed (see Supplementary material online, Table S13 and Figure S5). Overall, each sensitivity analysis showed a pattern of results consistent with our primary results, including after sex-stratified analyses (see Supplementary material online, Tables S10–S13 and Figures S2-S5).

Furthermore, a significant association was found between CKM Stages 1–3 and the risk of CVD events across all age-stratified subgroups (<40 years, 40–59 years, ≥ 60 years). However, the magnitude of the association varied significantly among these subgroups, as supported by a significant interaction with age (P-for-interaction <0.001) (see Supplementary material online, Table S14).

Discussion

In this large nationwide cohort study in Japan, we evaluated the association between CKM Stages 0–3 and the incidence of CVD events (defined as CKM Stage 4) (Graphical Abstract). The progression of CKM Stages 0–3 was significantly associated with a heightened risk of CVD events, including MI, HF, stroke, AF, and PAD. This association was strongest in those with CKM Stage 3 (i.e. subclinical CVD), who demonstrated the highest HR compared to all other stages. A dose-dependent increase in the risk of CVD events was observed across CKM Stages 0–3 in both males and females. However, the magnitude of the association for each CKM stage differed between the sexes. Our sensitivity analyses, which utilized different definitions and outcomes, yielded results that were broadly consistent with those of the primary analysis.

Although our findings align with the previous studies, we found several unique contributions. First, while most prior studies demonstrated a significant association between CKM health and all-cause or CVD-related mortality,11,12,19 our study is, to the best of our knowledge, the first report to further show the association between the progression of CKM stages and the risk of CVD events. Second, compared with previous epidemiological studies including approximately 100 000 individuals,11,12,19 our study with larger sample size over one million participants allowed for a more robust assessment of the association between CKM health and CVD events. Third, although some previous studies have reported sex differences in CKM health,12 our findings further suggest that CKM staging may be a promising tool for the risk stratification of CVD events in both males and females.

We found a dose-dependent increase in the risk of CVD events as CKM Stages 0–3 progressed, suggesting that the cumulative burden of metabolic and kidney risk factors could exacerbate developing CVD events. As prior basic research has indicated, metabolic diseases and renal dysfunction can induce inflammatory and haemodynamic stress, thereby increasing the risk of atherosclerotic CVD, including coronary artery disease, HF, and AF.20 Furthermore, our findings quantified the degree to which this risk is amplified when these conditions are combined.21 Given the aetiological link between CVD and fatal events, this finding is plausible and underscores the importance of CKM Stages within the CVD cascade. Our stratification analysis showed that the association between CKM Stages 1–3 and the risk of CVD events was gradually attenuated with advancing age. This suggests that CKM syndrome may be a greater contributor to the risk of CVD events in younger adults, where the effects of ageing on disease are less pronounced.

Clinical implications and future directions

In our study, the association between the progression of CKM Stages 0–3 and the risk of CVD events was generally consistent between males and females. Previous epidemiological studies often included more males than females, which may have led to less accurate risk assessments for females when using existing risk calculators.22 However, we found a similar association between CKM stages and the risk of developing CVD events in females as well as males. This suggests that the CKM concept may hold promise for risk assessment in females, who typically face a poorer CVD prognosis than males. After sex stratification, modest sex-specific differences were observed. Although the underlying mechanisms are not fully understood, variations in sex hormones, adipose tissue distribution, vascular ageing, and inflammatory or metabolic pathways may play different roles in the progression of CKM-related disease between males and females.12 Additionally, differences in social and behavioural determinants of health should be considered in the context of CKM development,23 and further studies are warranted to explore these factors.

Our study had several limitations. First, because the study population was drawn from the DeSC database, which almost exclusively includes individuals of Asian descent, the application of these findings to other racial and ethnic groups requires careful interpretation. Second, the follow-up period was relatively short, with a median duration of 3.1 years. Third, the use of ICD-10 codes for defining both exposure and outcome may have led to an underestimation of clinical CVD diagnosis. Fourth, while the DeSC database is a comprehensive source of nationwide claims and health check-up data, our study cohort was limited to individuals who underwent health check-ups. Given that only approximately 60% of Japanese adults participate in annual check-ups, our study population may represent a healthier and more health-conscious subset, which could potentially introduce selection bias. Fifth, a simplified definition of proteinuria was employed in our study. Sixth, the definition of CKM Stage 3 in our study was more restrictive than in the original CKM framework. While the original framework defines CKM Stage 3 by the presence of subclinical CVD (e.g. diagnosed by coronary artery calcification or elevated cardiac biomarkers like b-type natriuretic peptide), and/or very-high-risk CKD or high predicted 10-year CVD risk, our study’s definition of CKM Stage 3 was based solely on very-high-risk CKD and/or high predicted 10-year CVD risk. Seventh, the Pooled Cohort Equation used to define CKM Stage 3 in our primary analysis is not well-validated in the Japanese population. To address this limitation, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using the Suita score, a CVD risk calculator specifically for the Japanese population. Finally, while we made every effort to adjust for confounding factors, unmeasurable and residual confounding factors may still have influenced the results. Therefore, the results of our study should be interpreted as exploratory.

Conclusions

In this large nationwide cohort, the progression of CKM Stages 0–3 was associated with the risk of developing CVD events (defined as CKM Stage 4), with a significant interaction observed between males and females. Despite the interaction, a similar dose-response relationship between CKM stages and CVD events was observed in both sexes. These findings highlight the importance of the CKM concept in understanding the progression of CVD events and suggest its potential for enhancing patient adherence to risk assessment and lifestyle modifications, irrespective of sex, even before the manifestation of overt disease.
