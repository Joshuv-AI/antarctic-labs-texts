Interviewer gender and loneliness disclosure in survey research: evidence from the European Social Survey and the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe
Authors: Thijs van den Broek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLoneliness is an important public health concern, but interviewer effects in face-to-face surveys may affect population-based monitoring efforts. Prior work suggests that people may be more open about sensitive topics to female interviewers than to male interviewers. This study examines whether interviewer gender influences the reporting of loneliness.MethodsData are from 276,073 face-to-face interviews from four rounds of the European Social Survey (ESS) and four waves of the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE). Country-specific logistic regression models were estimated to assess the association between interviewer gender and reported loneliness, adjusting for respondent characteristics. Interaction effects with respondent gender were also examined. Country-specific average marginal effects were combined using random-effects meta-analyses. Sensitivity analyses used a continuous measure of loneliness (short UCLA scale).ResultsAcross datasets, the effect of interviewer gender on reported loneliness was close to zero and not statistically significant (ESS: θ = −0.001, 95% CI: −0.006 to 0.005; SHARE: θ = −0.017, 95% CI: −0.034 to 0.001; Overall: θ = −0.004, 95% CI: −0.011 to 0.002). No evidence was found for stronger effects among male respondents (ESS: θ = 0.006; 95% CI: -0.006 to 0.019; SHARE: θ = -0.005; 95% CI: -0.027 to 0.018; Overall: θ = 0.002; 95% CI: -0.009 to 0.013). Although some between-country heterogeneity was observed, effect estimates remained centered around zero. Sensitivity analyses using the UCLA loneliness scale yielded comparable results.ConclusionsThese precise estimates suggest that interviewer gender is unlikely to be a source of bias in loneliness monitoring.

Introduction

Loneliness is the distressing emotional state that arises when individuals perceive a discrepancy between their desired and actual social relationships [1, 2]. Given its associations with mortality [3–5], morbidity [5–8], health behavior [9, 10], and the overall outlook on the future [11], it is increasingly recognized as an important public health concern [12, 13]. The high prevalence of loneliness in many high-income countries has even become to be referred to as an epidemic [14].

The growing recognition that loneliness is widespread, harmful, and shaped by social and structural conditions has led scholars to call for a public health approach that addresses structural drivers and prioritizes population level prevention [15, 16]. Crowe et al. [15] stressed the importance of adequate monitoring as a foundational element of public health strategies on loneliness [cf. 14]. High-quality population surveys are essential for effective monitoring. In the context of Europe, European Research Infrastructure Consortiums suitable to assess trends in and antecedents of loneliness include the European Social Survey (ESS) [17] and the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE) [18]. These are large-scale, cross-national surveys that have been fielded periodically since the early 2000s. Both ESS and SHARE collected information on loneliness in multiple rounds, and have been used for loneliness monitoring purposes [e.g., 19, 20, 21].

A key challenge to loneliness monitoring is that loneliness carries substantial social stigma [22, 23], meaning that it discredits people in the eyes of others [24]. Consequently, respondents may choose not to disclose feelings of loneliness in surveys to avoid negative judgment [25]. This nondisclosure risk may be especially relevant in face-to-face surveys, which is also the mode of data collection that surveys such as the ESS and SHARE have long primarily relied upon. Although face-to-face interviewing, in general, tends to be beneficial for data quality [26], the reliance on a heterogeneous pool of interviewers may introduce noise and bias, as the interviewer’s identity may influence responses [27, 28].

The current study sheds light on how the gender of the interviewer may affect respondents’ inclination to report loneliness in face-to-face survey interviews. Prior work suggests that respondents are more strongly inclined to disclose sensitive or potentially stigmatizing information when they feel emotionally comfortable and perceive the interaction as nonjudgmental [29]. Whereas Moum [30] did not find evidence that Norwegian Health Survey respondents were more prone to report mental health problems to female than to male interviewers, Chun et al. [31] found that military personnel in the Dominican Republic were more likely to report depression and post-traumatic stress disorder to female interviewers than to male interviewers. Similarly, Pollner [32] found that respondents in the Epidemiological Catchment Area study reported more depressive symptoms to female than to male interviewers, and argued that this may reflect that female interviewers are perceived as more sympathetic and may create interactional conditions that facilitate disclosure. In contrast, male interviewers may be experienced as more threatening or evaluative in sensitive domains, which can inhibit disclosure [29]. Given that loneliness is a sensitive topic as well [23], it may therefore be hypothesized that the likelihood of reporting loneliness is lower when being interviewed by a male interviewer as opposed to a female interviewer (Hypothesis 1).

The extent to which interviewer gender affects respondents’ inclination to conceal loneliness may furthermore differ between female and male respondents. In a recent survey experiment study, Langenkamp and Schobin [33] found that men were especially likely to conceal loneliness in fictitious interactions when their interaction partner was male as well. The authors tentatively suggested that this pattern may be linked to negative stereotypes of same-gender intimate interactions in men [cf. 34]. Assuming that these concealment dynamics translate from fictitious conversations to real interviewer–respondent interactions, it may therefore be expected that the negative effect of being interviewed by a male interviewer on the likelihood of reporting loneliness is stronger for male respondents than for female respondents (Hypothesis 2).

Effects of interviewer gender on respondents’ predisposition to conceal loneliness may potentially frustrate adequate loneliness monitoring. Such effects can, for instance, bias estimates of cross-national variation in loneliness [21, 35, 36] if there are systematic country differences in the gender composition of the interviewer pool. Drawing on more than 276,000 interviews from four waves of ESS data and four waves of SHARE data, the current study therefore examined to what extent interviewer gender is associated with the likelihood of reporting loneliness.

Data and methods

Data

ESS is a repeated cross-sectional, multi-country survey designed to measure and track changes in public attitudes, beliefs, and values across Europe [17]. Whereas ESS’s population of interest consists of all community-dwelling persons aged 15 and older [37], SHARE focuses on older adults. Specifically, this longitudinal, cross-national dataset is centered on the health, socio-economic status and social relations of European individuals aged 50 and older in Europe and Israel [18]. Both ESS and SHARE draw on population-based samples, although the types of sample frames differ by country due to cross-national variability in sampling resources [18, 37]. Thus far, 11 rounds of ESS data and 9 waves of SHARE data have been released. However, loneliness was only collected in ESS Rounds 3, 5, 6, 7, and 11 and in SHARE Waves 5, 6, 7, 8, and 9. The current study draws on these data, with the exception of ESS Round 3 and SHARE Wave 8, because information on interviewer gender is not available for these waves.

The total number of interviews in the data sources drawn upon was 478,159 (197,438 from ESS and 280,721 from SHARE). Only interviews that were collected in-person were considered in this study. Data collection for all SHARE waves and for ESS rounds 5–7 were collected via face-to-face-interviews. However, for ESS round 11, a small share of the interviews (n = 1,846) was conducted online via video interviews. These interviews were excluded from the sample. Information on interviewer gender was unavailable for 4,172 ESS interviews and for 143,685 SHARE interviews. The high level of missingness on this variable in the SHARE data primarily reflects the overall lack of interviewer information in (selected waves for) Czech Republic, Denmark, France, Israel, Italy, Luxemburg, the Netherlands, Slovenia, and Switzerland, but also individual non-participation in the interviewer survey. Subsequently, 5,568 ESS interviews and 46,214 SHARE interviews with missing or invalid information on variables of interest were dropped.

Finally, SHARE data from Cyprus (n = 396) and Malta (n = 205) were excluded, because the interviews in these countries were only conducted by 7 and 2 different interviewers, respectively. Including these data would have been problematic, because the country-specific analyses performed in the current study require clustering standard errors at the interviewer level, and simulation-based evidence suggests that cluster-robust variance estimators tend to be biased downward with fewer than 10 clusters [38]. Performing analyses on data with so few clusters would have resulted in underestimated standard errors and, consequently, inflated risks of type-I errors. The inclusion procedure described here resulted in a final analytical sample of 276,073 interviews (185,852 in ESS and 90,221 in SHARE). These interviews were conducted by 15,690 different interviewers (32.4% male).

Measures

In ESS, loneliness was measured with a single item from the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression (CES-D) scale [39]. Respondents were asked how often the statement “I felt lonely” had applied to them in the past week, with response categories being “none or almost none of the time”, “some of the time”, “most of the time”, and “all or almost all of the time”. Following prior work [19, 40, 41], responses were dichotomized with people who reported having felt lonely at least some of the time coded as lonely and their counterparts who reported having felt lonely none or almost none of the time coded as not lonely. SHARE respondents were asked “Now think about the past week. How often have you felt lonely?”, with response categories being “hardly ever or never”, “some of the time”, and “often”. Here, respondents who reported that they had felt lonely at least some of the time were coded as lonely, whereas their counterparts who reported that hardly ever or never had felt lonely in the week prior to the interview were coded as not lonely.

In addition to a direct loneliness question, the SHARE questionnaire also included the short version of the University of California Los Angeles (UCLA) loneliness scale [42]. This scale consists of three items: “how often do you feel you lack companionship?”, “how often do you feel left out?”, and “how often do you feel isolated from others?”. Each item has three response categories: “hardly ever or never” (scored as 1), “some of the time” (scored as 2), and “often” (scored as 3). The items were summed into an internally consistent (Cronbach’s α = 0.76) scale ranging from 3 to 9, with higher scores indicating stronger feelings of loneliness. The summed scale was only calculated for respondents with complete information on all three items.

This study’s central explanatory variable was interviewer gender (male; female). In order to mitigate potential omitted variable bias in the estimation of the effect of this variable, it should be considered that interviewers tend to be assigned to locally demarcated areas, often with specific demographics [27]. In addition to wave of data collection, several socio-demographic background characteristics known to be associated with loneliness [21, 36, 43, 44] were therefore in the models as controls: respondent gender (male; female), age in years, age in years squared, partner status (living with a partner; not living with a partner), educational attainment (up to lower secondary; upper secondary or vocational; at least bachelor’s degree or equivalent), and level of urbanization ((suburbs or outskirts of) large city; smaller city, village, or countryside). The reason for including age squared was linked to the curvilinear relationship between age and loneliness reported in prior work [45, 46].

Analytical approach

In addition to presenting basic descriptive statistics, data source- and country-specific conditional intraclass correlation coefficients were estimated using basic multilevel logistic regression models. These coefficients quantify the proportion of the residual variance in reported loneliness that is attributable to differences between interviewers within each data source and country, after adjusting for survey wave fixed effects.

For the main analyses, a series of regression models were estimated to gain insights into the association of interviewer gender and reported loneliness. Separate models were estimated for ESS data and SHARE data. Given that the magnitude of interviewer effects tends to vary across countries [27], country specific models were estimated, whereby standard errors were clustered at the interviewer level [47]. In initial data source- and country-specific logistic regression models, loneliness were regressed on interviewer gender, whereby respondent gender, age, age squared, and wave of data collection were adjusted for. These models enable assessing the male interviewer effect postulated in hypothesis 1. Data source- and country-specific average marginal effects of interviewer gender capture the estimated attributable risk of reported loneliness that comes with a male interviewer, i.e., the absolute difference between respondents interviewed by male interviewers and their counterparts interviewed by female interviewers in the adjusted predicted risk of reported loneliness. Subsequently, all models were re-estimated with an additional interaction term that allows the coefficient for interviewer gender to vary by respondent gender. The latter model specification allows assessment of the surplus male interviewer effect for male respondents postulated in hypothesis 2.

In sensitivity analyses with loneliness as measured with the short UCLA scale as the outcome, linear regression models were estimated. Coefficient estimates in this model reflect the associations of interviewer gender with the reported strength of feelings of loneliness.

Following the estimation of the source- and country-specific analyses, meta-analyses were performed to assess whether there is an overall male interviewer effect and a surplus effect for male respondents [48]. The unit-specific estimates obtained from the separate models were combined into an overall estimate using inverse-variance weighting. A random-effects specification was used to account for heterogeneity in effects across units. The meta-analysis furthermore quantifies between-unit heterogeneity in effect estimates, thereby allowing assessment of the variability of effects across units.

Given that the response categories on the single item loneliness measure prior to dichotomization differed somewhat between ESS and SHARE, the overall interviewer gender effects were estimated both separately for, respectively, ESS and SHARE data, as well as across both datasets. For the sensitivity analyses with loneliness as measured with the short UCLA scale as the outcome, the meta-analyses only drew on results from analyses of SHARE data, as this alternative loneliness measure was not collected in the ESS.

A replication file for all data preparation and analyses, including instructions on how to access the data, is available on the Open Science Framework (OSF): https://osf.io/wr5s9 .

Table 1 shows that the share of respondents reporting that they felt lonely at least some of the time was almost 5% points lower among ESS respondents interviewed by male interviewers than among those interviewed by female interviewers. Table 2 shows a similar pattern for SHARE respondents, where the prevalence of reporting loneliness was approximately 3% points lower among those interviewed by male interviewers. Although smaller in magnitude than in the ESS sample, this difference was statistically significant in the samples of both ESS and SHARE. Similarly, scores on the short UCLA loneliness scale were slightly, but statistically significantly, lower among SHARE respondents interviewed by male interviewers than among their counterparts interviewed by female interviewers.

Across Tables 1 and 2, ESS and SHARE respondents interviewed by male interviewers were significantly younger on average, albeit only slightly, and more likely to be male themselves than their counterparts interviewed by female interviewers. Moreover, the distribution of interviewer gender differed significantly across waves of data collection as well as across countries. The latter pattern may partly underlie the observed differences in respondent characteristics between the male interviewer and female interviewer subsamples. At the same time, it underscores the importance of examining interviewer gender effects, as such effects may in combination with the cross-national differences in interviewer composition established here introduce systematic bias in estimates of country differences in loneliness.

Figure 1 provides an overview of the interviewer level variation in reported loneliness by country and data source. Conditional interviewer-level intraclass correlation coefficients varied substantially across countries in both the ESS and the SHARE, indicating considerable cross-national heterogeneity in the extent to which reported loneliness clustered by interviewer. In most countries, the estimated intraclass correlation coefficients were modest, although several countries exhibited substantially higher levels of interviewer-related variation, with estimates exceeding 0.20 and, in some cases, approaching 0.30. Overall, the magnitude of interviewer effects showed no consistent pattern across the two surveys. For Norway and Iceland in the ESS, the estimated interviewer-level variance was effectively zero. Consequently, confidence intervals could not be calculated, because the variance component was on the boundary of the parameter space.

Meta-analyses

Figure 2 presents the results of the meta-analyses of the association between interviewer gender and the likelihood of reporting loneliness based on the single-item measure. For the ESS data, the pooled estimate was essentially zero (θ = -0.001; 95% CI: -0.006, 0.005), indicating no evidence of a systematic association between interviewer gender and reported loneliness. Country-specific estimates were tightly clustered around zero, and between-country heterogeneity was very low and not statistically significant (I² = 0.4%; Q(34) = 47.2, p = .07), suggesting that estimates were largely consistent across contexts. For the SHARE data, the pooled estimate was slightly negative, but this very small effect was not statistically significant (θ = -0.017; 95% CI: -0.034, 0.001). Variability across countries was substantially larger than in the ESS data and statistically significant (I² = 68.4%; Q(20) = 54.4 p < .001).

Results of meta analysis of male interviewer effect on estimated loneliness prevalence

When combining results across both datasets, the overall pooled estimate was effectively zero with a rather narrow confidence interval (θ = -0.004; 95% CI: -0.011, 0.002), indicating a high degree of precision. Between-country heterogeneity at the overall level was moderate and statistically significant (I² = 33.7%; Q(55) = 102.7, p < .001). There was no evidence of systematic differences in effects between ESS and SHARE (Qb(1) = 2.9, p = .08).

Figure 3 presents the results of the meta-analyses of the surplus male interviewer effect for male respondents. Across countries, the estimated interaction effects were generally small in magnitude and varied in direction. For the ESS data, the pooled estimate was, again, essentially zero with a rather narrow confidence interval (θ = 0.006; 95% CI: -0.006, 0.019), indicating no evidence that the association between interviewer gender and reported loneliness was significantly stronger for male respondents than for female respondents. There was moderate and statistically significant between-country heterogeneity (I² = 44.5%; Q(34) = 61.6, p < .001), indicating some variability in effect sizes across contexts. For the SHARE data, the pooled estimate was likewise close to zero (θ = -0.005; 95% CI: -0.027, 0.018), again indicating no evidence of a meaningful surplus effect for male respondents. Heterogeneity across countries was statistically significant and more pronounced than in the ESS (I² = 75.8%; Q(20) = 65.9, p < .001), reflecting greater variability in country-specific estimates.

Results of meta analysis of surplus male interviewer effect for male respondents on estimated loneliness prevalence

When combining results across both datasets, the overall pooled estimate was effectively zero, with narrow confidence intervals (θ = 0.002; 95% CI: -0.009, 0.013). Overall heterogeneity was moderate and statistically significant (I² = 58.8%; Q(55) = 130.9, p < .001), but there was no evidence of systematic differences between ESS and SHARE estimates (Qb(1) = 0.7, p = .42). Taken together, these findings provide no evidence for a surplus male interviewer effect among male respondents.

Importantly, given the large number of observations and the resulting precision of the pooled estimates, these results suggest that any interaction between interviewer gender and respondent gender is negligible in practice. This reinforces the conclusion that interviewer gender is unlikely to meaningfully influence reported loneliness, either among men or among women.

Sensitivity analyses

In addition to the main analyses of reported prevalence of loneliness, sensitivity analyses on the reported strength of feelings of loneliness, as measured with the short UCLA loneliness scale, were performed. These analyses drew on SHARE data only. Note that the estimates from these analyses cannot be directly compared to those of the main analyses; estimates of the main analyses reflect estimated attributable risks of reported loneliness whereas the estimates of the sensitivity analyses reflect the estimated impact in points on the short UCLA loneliness score.

Results of the meta-analyses of the association between interviewer gender and the strength of feelings of loneliness are presented in Fig. 4. Also in these additional analyses, the pooled estimate of interviewer gender was close to zero (θ = -0.037; 95% CI: -0.088, 0.014), indicating no evidence of a systematic association between interviewer gender and reported strength of feelings of loneliness. Between-country heterogeneity was substantial and statistically significant (I² = 60.7%; Q(20) = 47.4, p < .001).

Results of meta analysis of male interviewer effect on estimated strength of feelings of loneliness

Figure 5 displays the results of the meta-analyses of the extent to which the association between interviewer gender and the strength of feelings of loneliness were stronger for male respondents than for female respondents. The pooled estimate of this surplus interviewer gender effect for male respondents was close to zero and not statistically significant (θ = -0.019; 95% CI: -0.049, 0.012). Estimates were highly consistent across countries and between-country heterogeneity was not statistically significant (I² = 0.0%; Q(20) = 18.8, p = .54).

Discussion

This study analyzed over 276,000 ESS and SHARE interviews to examine whether interviewer gender influences the reporting of loneliness in large-scale face-to-face surveys. Across both ESS and SHARE data, no evidence of a systematic association between interviewer gender and reported loneliness was found. Neither did the analyses support the idea that interviewer effects were more pronounced among male respondents than among female respondents.

It is important, in general, to be cautious when interpreting the absence of a statistically significant effect [49], in this case of interviewer gender. Particularly when confidence intervals are wide, the non-significant variable of interest may still have an effect in the population. However, it is increasingly recognized that non-rejection of null hypotheses in well-powered settings can still provide meaningful information about processes in the population [50]. The present analyses are based on a very large number of observations, resulting in highly precise estimates with narrow confidence intervals. This implies that the absence of statistically significant effects is unlikely to reflect a lack of statistical power, but rather indicates that any potential interviewer gender effects are small in magnitude. Specifically, the confidence interval of the overall interviewer gender effect indicates that the range of plausible values for the effect of being interviewed by a male rather than a female interviewer on reporting loneliness lies between approximately − 1.1 and + 0.2% points. The range of plausible values for the corresponding overall effect on reported strength of feelings of loneliness lies approximately between − 0.09 and + 0.01 points on the short UCLA loneliness scale. The consistent finding of precise null results across two different measures and across two datasets focusing on the community-dwelling population aged 15 + and on older adults, respectively, provides strong evidence that interviewer gender is unlikely to meaningfully influence the reporting of loneliness in practice. Although some variation in effect estimates across countries was observed, these differences were generally modest in magnitude and remained centered around zero.

To illustrate the practical implications of the findings presented here, consider two countries in which 10% and 60% of respondents, respectively, are interviewed by male interviewers. The former rate roughly coincides with rates observed in the ESS for Finland, Latvia, Lithuania, and Russia, whereas the latter rate roughly coincides with rates observed in the ESS for Germany, Israel and Norway. Using the lower bound of the 95% confidence interval for the pooled interviewer-gender effect (-1.1% points), differences in interviewer-gender composition would account for at most a 0.55% point difference in estimated loneliness prevalence between the two countries (0.50 × 0.011 = 0.0055). Thus, even under a conservative interpretation of the results presented here, interviewer-gender composition is unlikely to meaningfully bias cross-national comparisons of loneliness prevalence.

Based on the idea that female interviewers may be perceived as more empathetic and less judgmental, thereby facilitating disclosure of sensitive information [29, 32], it was first hypothesized that respondents would be less likely to report loneliness when interviewed by male rather than female interviewers. Second, it was hypothesized that this effect would be stronger among male respondents. This expectation built on prior research suggesting that men may be particularly reluctant to disclose loneliness, especially in interactions with other men, due to gender norms and potential stigma around same-gender emotional vulnerability [33, 34]. Across datasets and across the two measures of loneliness considered, neither of these two hypotheses was supported. Langenkamp and Schobin’s survey experiment provided some evidence that the concealment of loneliness may be more pronounced in interactions with male partners, particularly among men and in contexts where loneliness is perceived as stigmatized, although these findings are based on hypothetical interaction scenarios [33]. The absence of similar differences by interviewer gender in ESS and SHARE data suggests that loneliness may be less interaction-sensitive in survey contexts than in interpersonal conversations with friends or co-workers.

The findings presented in this study differ from some previous studies of interviewer effects on the reporting of mental health problems. Several studies have found that respondents were more likely to report symptoms of depression or other mental health problems to female than to male interviewers [31, 32], although no evidence for such differences was found in other work [30]. One possible explanation is that reporting clinically defined mental health problems, such as depression or anxiety disorders, may be perceived as more sensitive than reporting feelings of loneliness, making responses more susceptible to interviewer characteristics. However, further research is needed to determine whether the effects of interviewer gender indeed vary according to the perceived sensitivity of the topic.

A key strength of this study is its use of large-scale, cross-national data from two major survey programs and the resulting high statistical power, allowing for precise estimation of even very small effects. Some limitations should also be considered. One limitation concerns the high rate of missingness on interviewer gender in the SHARE data. As information on interviewer characteristics was only available for a subset of interviews, the analytical sample may not be fully representative of the original survey population. If the availability of interviewer gender information is systematically related to interviewer or survey characteristics, this could introduce selection bias and potentially affect the generalizability of the findings. However, findings from SHARE were highly consistent with those from ESS, where missingness on interviewer gender was substantially lower.

Secondly, there were substantially more female interviewers than male interviewers in both surveys. Such unequal distributions do not, in themselves, bias the estimated interviewer gender effect, if the country-specific models are correctly specified. They do, however, reduce the precision of the coefficient estimates for interviewer gender, particularly in countries where one interviewer gender is rare. Consequently, country-specific estimates based on more balanced interviewer-gender distributions tend to be estimated more precisely and therefore contribute greater weight in the meta-analysis.

Finally, the present analyses isolate one specific source of potential measurement bias within face-to-face surveys. The absence of interviewer-gender effects should therefore not be interpreted as evidence that face-to-face interviewing is unaffected by social desirability bias more generally. Respondents may still be less willing to disclose feelings of loneliness in interviewer-administered interviews than in self-administered survey modes. Although the available evidence on mode effects in loneliness reporting remains limited, mode effects have been documented for other sensitive topics, such as mental health problems [30, 51, 52]. This suggests that the degree of privacy afforded by the interview mode may influence disclosure. Future research should therefore examine whether reported loneliness differs systematically between interviewer-administered and self-administered survey modes, for example by exploiting mixed-mode survey designs or experimental mode assignments.

Notwithstanding these limitations, the findings suggest that interviewer gender is unlikely to constitute a meaningful source of bias in survey-based loneliness research. This strengthens confidence in the validity and comparability of loneliness estimates derived from large-scale face-to-face surveys such as ESS and SHARE. Although broader concerns about the underreporting of loneliness remain, the present findings suggest that such underreporting is not systematically shaped by interviewer gender. Future research may build on these findings by examining other interviewer characteristics and survey modes to further assess potential sources of measurement bias.
