Modeling high-risk Wilms tumors enables the discovery of therapeutic vulnerability
Authors: Gui Ma, Ang Gao, Jiani Chen, Peng Liu, Rakesh Sarda, Jessica Gulliver, Yidan Wang, Carstyn Joiner, Mingshan Hu, Eui-Jun Kim, Herman Yeger, Hau D. Le, Xiang Chen, Wan-Ju Li, Wei Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryWilms tumor (WT) is the most common pediatric kidney cancer treated with standard chemotherapy. However, less-differentiated blastemal type of WT often relapses. To model the high-risk WT for therapeutic intervention, we introduce pluripotency factors into WiT49, a mixed-type WT cell line, to generate partially reprogrammed cells, namely WiT49-PRCs. When implanted into the kidney capsule in mice, WiT49-PRCs form kidney tumors and develop both liver and lung metastases, whereas WiT49 tumors do not metastasize. Histological characterization and gene expression signatures demonstrate that WiT49-PRCs recapitulate blastemal-predominant WTs. Moreover, drug screening in isogeneic WiT49 and WiT49-PRCs leads to the identification of epithelial- or blastemal-predominant WT-sensitive drugs, whose selectivity is validated in patient-derived xenografts (PDXs). Histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors (e.g., panobinostat and romidepsin) are found universally effective across different WT and more potent than doxorubicin in PDXs. Taken together, WiT49-PRCs serve as a blastemal-predominant WT model for therapeutic intervention to treat patients with high-risk WT.

Introduction

Wilms tumor (WT) accounts for nearly 6% of all pediatric cancers. Although patients with WT have a favorable prognosis, ∼50% of the patients with anaplastic WT experience recurrence. Currently, the standard of care for WT in the US, according to the Children’s Oncology Group, is surgery followed by chemotherapy, and treatment is dictated by the histology classification and stages.

WT is classically characterized by its multipotent “triphasic” histology, consisting of blastemal, epithelial, and stromal cells. WT exhibits features of the developmental kidney and is thought to arise from malignant transformation of undifferentiated embryonic renal stem cells.1,2 There are three main components in WT: blastema, which resembles undifferentiated embryonic metanephric mesenchyme; tubular epithelia, which resembles developing renal tubules; and stroma. Blastemal-predominant WTs have a higher recurrence and lower overall survival rates than epithelial-, stromal-predominant, and mixed-type WTs and are associated with treatment resistance.3,4,5 According to the International Society of Pediatric Oncology (SIOP), epithelial, stromal, mixed-type, and focal anaplasia are classified as intermediate-risk group, while blastemal and diffuse anaplasia belong to high-risk tumors3 and are often stage III and IV6 and metastatic.7 In accordance, the gene expression patterns of the advanced-stage WTs cluster with those from the blastemal tumors.8 In patients with high-risk WT, intensive postoperative chemotherapy often results in cancer remission3; the treatment is debilitating to some children and may increase the risk of negative long-term health issues.9 For example, doxorubicin, used in postoperative chemotherapy for patients with stage II and III WT, has been shown to increase the risk of cardiac disease.10 Therefore, there is a need to develop new therapeutic modalities to treat high-risk, metastatic WTs with fewer side effects.

The significant challenge in the development of novel therapies for WT is the lack of preclinical models that faithfully recapitulate high-risk blastemal WT. There are a limited number of authenticated WT cell lines available. WiT49 is a frequently studied cell line resembling the mixed cell types but exhibits epithelial characteristics.11,12,13,14 Tumors generated from isotopic injection of WiT49 cells in mice do not metastasize.13 On the other hand, the CCG-99-11 cell line, which is a representative of the blastemal type, can generate kidney tumors that metastasize to liver and lung.13 However, this cell line is not widely accessible to the scientific community. St. Jude’s Children’s Hospital has a collection of patient-derived xenografts (PDXs), of which some exhibit gene signatures resembling those of blastemal tumors.15 To the best of our knowledge, none of the PDXs has been reported to metastasize in mice.

The cell origin of high-risk WT is thought to be metanephric blastemal cells, which form pluripotent renal stem cells in the developing fetal kidney.16,17 We hypothesize that reprogramming the mixed-type WiT49 cells into induced pluripotent cells (iPSCs) may recapitulate the blastemal-predominant WT. Somatic cells can be reprogrammed to iPSCs by transducing four transcriptional factors: OCT4, KLF4, c-Myc, and SOX2. The iPSC cells share similar gene expression and epigenetic profiles to the embryonic stem cells and have pluripotency to differentiate to other cell types.18 Recently, several groups successfully reprogrammed cancer cells derived from breast cancer,19 bladder cancer,20 lung cancer,20 pancreatic cancer,21 gastrointestinal cancer,22 and melanoma23 into iPSCs. Most cancer-derived iPSCs acquired cancer stem cell properties and exhibited some levels of resistance to chemotherapy drugs.22,23,24 Importantly, several studies have shown that fully25 or even partially26 reprogrammed iPSC derived from kidney cells or renal cancer cells or iPSC-derived organoids27 can form WTs in vivo. All lines of evidence support that cancer cell-derived iPSCs may be a valid model for developing new therapeutics.

The current study aimed to develop a high-risk blastemal WT model using a cellular reprogramming approach to convert WiT49 cells into cells exhibiting pluripotent traits. We named partially reprogrammed cells as WiT49-PRCs. To that end, we characterized the resemblance of tumors formed from WiT49-PRCs and human blastemal WTs by gene expression, expression of protein markers, and response to anti-cancer drugs. Notably, the WiT49 tumors failed to metastasize, whereas WiT49-PRC tumors metastasized to the lung and liver. This represents the first model of high-risk blastemal tumors that faithfully recapitulates the progression and histology of the disease. Moreover, screening of Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved drugs in the isogenic cell lines, WiT49 and WiT49-PRCs, enables the discovery of histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors as putative therapeutic drugs for high-risk WT. The sensitivity of drugs in WiT49 and WiT49-PRCs mirrors those in epithelial- or blastemal-predominant PDXs. Thus, the paired WiT49 and WiT49-PRCs are suitable for drug screening, and existing FDA-approved drugs may be re-purposed to improve the treatment of patients with advanced WT.

Results

WiT49-PRCs acquire metastatic traits

Considering the poor differentiation of high-risk blastemal WTs, we reprogrammed WiT49, a WT cell line with epithelial characteristics, into iPSCs using a transgene-free method. Specifically, we transfected WiT49 cells with episomal vectors following our established procedure28,29 (Figure 1A). Morphologic changes in WiT49 cells during the reprogramming process were detected (Figure 1B). The reprogrammed clones on day 21 post transfection were selected (Figure 1B). First, we selected one clone (WiT49-PRC-1) and found that it displayed induced expression of pluripotency marker genes as compared with parental WiT49 (Figures 1C–1H). To assess the pluripotency of this clone, we analyzed the expression changes of the germ layer cell markers. Interestingly, transcriptional levels of the mesoderm-specific markers T-box transcription factor T (TBXT) and C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 4 (CXCR4) were not changed after reprogramming, while ectoderm-specific markers paired box 6 (PAX6) and nestin (NES), and endoderm-specific markers SRY-box 17 (SOX17) and forkhead box A2 (FOXA2) were significantly decreased in reprogrammed cells compared to WiT49 cells (Figures 1I–1N). To eliminate clonal-specific effects, three more reprogrammed WiT49 clones were validated by analyzing the expression changes of pluripotency markers (Figures S1A–S1F) and germ layer cell markers (Figures S1G–S1L). Cancer cells are known to be difficult to fully reprogram into iPSCs due to genetic and epigenetic changes.30,31,32 For example, Michele et al. induced pluripotency of colorectal cancer (CRC) cells by retroviral transduction of the Yamanaka factors. However, these CRC-derived iPSCs achieved only partial pluripotency, evidenced by poorly defined iPSC colony borders, central cell accumulation, and an inability to form teratomas. Additionally, they exhibited decreased expression of endodermal and mesodermal markers, while ectodermal marker expression remained unchanged.30 This contrasts with the fully regenerated iPSCs, of which the expression of all three germ layer markers is expected to decrease. Other studies reported similar findings, suggesting that limited pluripotency may be a common feature in reprogrammed cancer cells.33,34 For this current study, we referred to the reprogrammed WiT49 cells as partially reprogrammed cells (PRCs) based on the following findings: (1) the borders of the iPSC colonies were not well defined, and the cells piled up in the center (Figure 1B), (2) WiT49-PRC-1 cells failed to form teratomas, and (3) WiT49-PRCs exhibit decreased ectoderm- and endoderm-specific markers (Figures 1K–1N and S1I–S1L), while mesoderm marker expression remains unchanged (Figures 1I, 1J, S1G, and S1H).Figure 1Generation and characterization of transgene-free WiT49-PRC(A) Schematic diagram illustrating the timeline for the establishment of the reprogrammed cell line. To evaluate if the WiT49-PRC-1 cells acquire metastatic traits in an orthotopic model, WiT49-PRC-1 or WiT49 cells were injected into the kidney capsule of 9 or 12 NSG mice, respectively (Figure 2A). The engraftment rates of both WiT49-PRC-1 and WiT49 were 100%. Nine weeks later, the tumors were harvested from mice. Distinct morphologies were observed. While the WiT49 tumors were generally solid and round, the WiT49-PRC-1 tumors were soft and exhibited a coarsely nodular appearance (Figure 2B). Ki67 immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis showed comparable cell proliferation rates between the two groups (Figure 2B), and tumor weights were also comparable (Figure 2C). Since both WiT49 and WiT49-PRCs were engineered to stably express luciferase, their metastatic tendencies to the liver and lungs were gauged using ex vivo bioluminescence imaging. As reported, WiT49-xenografted tumors do not metastasize in vivo.13 Surprisingly, WiT49-PRC-1 tumors metastasized to both the liver and lungs (Figures 2D–2F). We immunostained metastatic cells in the liver and lung tissues of mice with human mitochondrial antigens. The data confirmed liver and lung metastasis of WiT49-PRC-1 but not WiT49 tumors (Figure S2A). We repeated the in vivo experiments using another WiT49-PRC clone (WiT49-PRC-2). The ex vivo bioluminescence imaging (Figures S2B–S2D) and human mitochondrial antigen staining (Figure S2E) confirmed that WIT49-PRC-2 tumors also metastasized to the liver and lungs, ruling out the clonal-specific effects. To further support that WiT49-PRCs acquired metastatic traits, we analyzed the expression levels of cancer metastasis markers (Snail2, Vimentin, and HLAB) in WiT49, WiT49-PRC-1, and PRC-2 clones by IHC staining and quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (RT-qPCR). The IHC results showed that the expression of three metastasis markers increased in tumors formed from WiT49-PRC-1 (Figure 2G) and WiT49-PRC-2 (Figure S2F) compared to WiT49 cells. RT-qPCR results also detected the increased expression of three markers in WiT49-PRC-1 (Figures 2H–2J) and additional 3 PRC clones (Figures S2G–S2I), compared to WiT49 cells. WiT49-PRCs recapitulate blastemal tumors in morphology and expression of signature genes of blastemal tumors

To determine if WiT49-PRCs exhibit characteristics of blastemal tumors, we first compared the histology of tumors derived from WiT49 or WiT49-PRC, the primary tumors of patients, and PDXs with established histological profiles. Out of four patient samples, two presented triphasic histology, while one was blastemal predominant and another one was epithelial predominant. Of the PDX tumors, two were characterized as blastemal predominant. Notably, the morphology of WiT49-PRC-1 tumors displayed small round blue cells with overlapping nuclei, recapitulating the features of blastemal-type WTs (Figure 3A). To identify the gene signature of WiT49-PRC-1 cells, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of the cultured WiT49 and WiT49-PRC-1 cells. RNA-seq analyses revealed that 2,915 genes were upregulated and 3,296 genes were downregulated upon reprogramming of WiT49 cells into PRCs. The upregulated genes include pluripotency genes such as POU5F1, SOX2, KLF4, and C-MYC. The downregulated genes include epithelial markers such as KRT18 and WNT4, as well as Lin28A, a pluripotency gene (Figure 3B). These results support the stem cell characteristics of WiT49-PRCs. Next, we compared the molecular characteristics of WiT49-PRC-1 cells with WT patient samples.15 Distinct gene signatures were derived from the pre-characterized high- or low-blastemal tumors at the St. Jude Children’s Hospital. Based on the expression levels of cell lineage-specific genes involved in kidney development and tumorigenesis,8,35 we analyzed the genes featuring the cap mesenchyme and nephron progenitors (blastema), and the early nephric epithelium within human fetal kidney cells (epithelium) in WiT49 or WiT49-PRC-1 tumor xenografts. Hierarchical clustering (Figures 3C and 3D) showed that patient tumors and PDXs classified as blastemal expressed high levels of genes associated with the cap mesenchyme or nephron progenitors (SIX2, EYA1, and SALL1), consistent with a recent report identifying SIX2, EYA1, and SALL1 as blastemal markers.8 On the contrary, the blastemal-low tumors showed higher levels of expression of nephric epithelium signature genes (e.g., KRT8, CDH6, and PDGFB), as well as PTPRO and PODXL which signify podocyte epithelial cells. These high expression levels of blastemal markers and low expression levels of epithelial markers in WiT49-PRC-1 cells (Figures 3C and 3D) further support that WiT49-PRC-1 remodels blastemal WTs. To further confirm that WiT49-PRC-1 cells express high levels of blastemal markers and low levels of epithelial markers, quantitative PCR was performed to detect the expression of selected blastemal and epithelial markers in WiT49, WiT49-PRC-1, and three additional WiT49-PRC clones. Consistently, the expression of blastemal markers (EYA1, SIX2, and HAS2) (Figures S3B–S3E) was increased, while the expression of epithelial markers (KRT18 and EPCAM) (Figures S3F and S3G) was decreased after reprogramming of WiT49. Moreover, gene set enrichment analysis of WiT49 and WiT49-PRC-1 revealed that transcriptional regulation of somatic stem cell and epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition pathways were upregulated and tissue development and cell differentiation pathways were downregulated in WiT49-PRC-1 as compared with WiT49 (Figure S3H).

We next determined if the blastemal and epithelial signature genes identified in RNA-seq could be validated at the protein levels in WT specimens, as well as tumors derived from WiT49 and WiT49-PRCs. Indeed, the IHC staining results showed that the blastemal markers (SIX2 and NCAM1) are highly expressed in blastemal-predominant tumors or the blastemal cells in mixed-type tumors, while KRT18, an epithelial marker, is highly expressed in epithelial-predominant tumors or epithelial cells in the mixed-type WTs (Figure 3E). EZH2, a histone-lysine N-methyltransferase enzyme, is expressed at high levels in blastemal-predominant tumors and blastemal cells of mixed-type tumors (Figure 3E). Concordantly, EZH2 mRNA was significantly increased in WiT49-PRC clones compared with WiT49 cells (Figure S3E). Because EZH2 is essential for stem cell maintenance in cancers including glioblastoma36 and breast and pancreatic cancer and is associated with pluripotency,37 our data implicate that EZH2 is a blastemal marker. Consistent with the expression patterns of epithelial or blastemal markers in primary tumors, SIX2, NCAM1, and EZH2 were found to be highly expressed in two blastemal PDXs while not detected in an epithelial PDX (Figure 3F). KRT18, on the contrary, was detected in epithelial PDX, but not in blastemal-predominant PDXs (Figure 3F). Notably, strong staining of SIX2, NCAM1, and EZH2 was detected in WiT49-PRC-1 (Figure 3G) and WiT49-PRC-2 (Figure S3I) but not in WiT49 tumors, whereas KRT18 exhibited the opposite expression pattern. Collectively, the histology results and the expression of blastemal or epithelial signature genes support that WiT49-PRCs recapitulate blastemal WTs.

Identification of drugs exhibiting differential sensitivity to WiT49 and WiT49-PRCs and more potent than doxorubicin in WiT49 cells

WTs are generally treated with chemotherapy drugs (e.g., doxorubicin). WiT49 exhibits mixed-type histology and WiT49-PRCs represent blastemal-predominant WT. Using our isogenic model, we screened 140 FDA-approved anti-cancer drugs and 69 epigenetic drugs (Table S1) with the following goals: (1) determine if epithelial or blastemal-predominant tumors elicit different sensitivity to drugs and (2) discover whether some drugs display stronger cytotoxicity to epithelial- or blastemal-predominant cells than doxorubicin. If the drugs outperform doxorubicin, they may be repurposed for WT treatment. Interestingly, our results showed that approximately 26% of drugs elicit >20% differential sensitivity to WiT49 as compared with WiT49-PRC-1 cells (Figure 4A; Table S1). Moreover, WiT49 cells are more sensitive to a subset of FDA-approved epigenetic drugs (e.g., HDAC inhibitors panobinostat, quisinostat, and romidepsin) than the three FDA-approved chemotherapy drugs employed in the treatment of WT (Figures 4A; Table S1). Because the primary screen was only performed once at the 1 μM drug concentration, we then selected lead candidates for validation in dose-dependent cytotoxicity assays in WiT49 and WiT49-PRC-1 cells. The results showed that panobinostat displayed similar sensitivity to WiT49 and WiT49-PRC-1 cells (Figure 4B). Interestingly, doxorubicin, cytarabine (Ara-C), and alisertib elicited ≥10-fold sensitivity to WiT49-PRC-1 cells compared to WiT49 cells (Figures 4C–4E). Panobinostat is a pan-HDAC inhibitor approved for the treatment of patients with relapsed multiple myeloma. We next investigated which HDAC is likely responsible for the cytotoxicity effects of panobinostat using selective inhibitors of HDACs. Among the HDAC inhibitors tested, romidepsin, which targets HDAC1, 2, and 3, is the most potent. Romidepsin exhibits 10-fold greater efficacy in WiT49-PRC-1 than in WiT-49 cells (Figure 4F). On the contrary, Cy10683 (HDAC2 inhibitor), RGFP996 (HDAC3 inhibitor), BRD4354 (HDAC5/9 inhibitor), and CXD101 (HDAC4, 5, 7, 9 inhibitor) are ineffective toward either WiT49 or WiT49-PRC-1 cells (Figures 4G–4J). The results imply that HDAC1 is likely the target of HDAC inhibitor that elicits a growth inhibitory effect on WT. These results were also validated in three additional WiT49-PRC clones (Figures S4A–S4I). The higher sensitivity of WiT49-PRCs to romidepsin than WiT49 cells further implies that romidepsin may be repurposed for the treatment of blastemal-predominant WTs. Since HDAC inhibitors have not been applied as a single agent for the treatment of solid tumors, we assessed the efficacy of panobinostat in combination with doxorubicin in WiT49 and WiT49-PRC-1 cells. Because several HDAC inhibitors including panobinostat, romidepsin, and vorinostat are FDA approved to treat different types of cancers, we anticipate that the side effects of HDAC inhibitors might be more tolerable for children than those of chemo drugs. To compare the responsiveness of WiT49 and WiT49-PRC-1 to doxorubicin and HDAC inhibitors in vivo, WiT49 or WiT49-PRC-1 cells were injected into the kidney capsule of NSG mice followed by randomized treatments. After 4 weeks, the tumor-bearing mice were divided into 8 groups and treated with doxorubicin (4 mg/kg, daily, i.v., WiT49, n = 6; WiT49-PRC-1, n = 7), panobinostat (20 mg/kg, daily, i.p., WiT49, n = 7; WiT49-PRC-1, n = 7), and romidepsin (4 mg/kg, daily, i.p., WiT49, n = 7; WiT49-PRC-1, n = 7) or vehicle (WiT49, n = 7; WiT49-PRC-1, n = 9) for 16 days. The results showed that doxorubicin strongly inhibited the growth of WiT49-PRC-1 tumors but was less effective toward WiT49 tumors. Panobinostat and romidepsin, on the contrary, strongly inhibited the growth of both WiT49-PRC-1 and WiT49 tumors as measured by tumor volume (Figures 5A and 5B), tumor weight (Figures 5C and 5D), and Ki67 levels (Figure 5E). Doxorubicin decreased Ki67 in WiT49-PRC-1 tumors, but was less effective in WiT49 tumors (Figure 5E). Because WiT49-PRC tumors metastasized to both the liver and lung, we next investigated whether doxorubicin or HDAC inhibitors can block metastasis of WiT49-PRC-1 cells to liver and lung by ex vivo bioluminescence imaging. The results showed that either panobinostat or romidepsin, but not doxorubicin, reduced the metastasis of WiT49-PRC-1 tumors to the liver and lung (Figures 5G–5I). WiT49 tumors, on the contrary, had non-detectable metastasis (Figure 5F). Consistent with imaging data, IHC data showed that the levels of cancer metastasis markers including Snail2, Vimentin, and HLAB were dramatically decreased by treatment with HDAC inhibitors in WiT49-PRC-1 tumors and slightly decreased by doxorubicin (Figure 5J). Panobinostat and romidepsin also inhibited the mRNA expression of cancer metastasis markers as shown by RT-qPCR (Figures S5A–S5C).

Blastemal WT PDXs are responsive to WiT49-PRC-sensitive drugs

WiT49-PRC tumors exhibit features of blastemal tumors and are sensitive to several FDA-approved drugs. This led us to investigate whether blastemal WTs are also responsive to the treatment of these drugs in vivo. Three WT PDXs derived from patients ranging from 6 months to 15 years old (St. Jude Children’s Hospital)38 were histologically characterized. One PDX is epithelial predominant (PDX226, SJWLM046152_X1), and two are blastemal predominant (PDX207, SJWLM049191_X1; PDX253, SJWLM046157_X1). We chose to compare the therapeutic response of doxorubicin and alisertib in these models because doxorubicin and alisertib elicited stronger cytotoxic effects on WiT49-PRCs than on WiT49 (Figures 4C, 4D, S4B, and S4C). Moreover, doxorubicin, alisertib, and panobinostat act through different mechanisms. PDXs were established by intramuscular transplantation and treated with doxorubicin (4 mg/kg, daily, i.v., PDX226, n = 12; PDX207, n = 6; PDX253, n = 8), panobinostat (20 mg/kg, daily, i.p., PDX226, n = 14; PDX207, n = 9; PDX253, n = 11), and alisertib (30 mg/kg, daily, orally, PDX226, n = 16; PDX207, n = 12; PDX253, n = 9) or vehicle (PDX226, n = 14; PDX207, n = 10; PDX253, n = 8) for 15 days. Tumor growth curves revealed that doxorubicin and alisertib elicited a stronger anti-growth effect on blastemal-predominant than epithelial-predominant PDXs. Panobinostat, on the contrary, strongly inhibited the growth of both blastemal and epithelial PDXs (Figures 6A–6C). Tumor weight measurement revealed that panobinostat and doxorubicin showed similar effects on blastemal PDXs (Figure 6E). Interestingly, in epithelial-predominant PDX (Figure 6D), panobinostat outperformed doxorubicin or alisertib. Moreover, we did not observe any weight loss in mice during panobinostat treatment, suggesting that panobinostat might be a safe and tolerable drug to replace doxorubicin. Concurrently, Ki67 expression was dramatically decreased in panobinostat-treated epithelial or blastemal PDXs. Doxorubicin and alisertib were found to decrease Ki67 in blastemal PDXs, but less effective in an epithelial PDX (Figure 6F). These results suggest that WiT49-PRC-sensitive drugs likely elicit strong anti-cancer effects on blastemal WTs. Discussion

Despite the advance in precision medicine for other cancers, chemotherapy remains the primary treatment for patients with WT. This paucity of therapeutic intervention is largely due to limited research models. Currently, only a few cell lines, PDXs, and organoids derived from WT PDX (PDxO) are available. CCG-99-11 is the only blastemal cell line that metastasizes to the lung and liver when transplanted into the kidney of mice.13 However, it remains unreported if any PDX or PDxO models possess metastatic capabilities.

Several studies underscore the importance of blastemal cells in this area of research. Van Leeuwen et al. found that, among various cell types, blastemal cells have the highest likelihood to metastasize in vivo and, notably, are sensitive to chemotherapy drugs.39 Our study agrees with several reports that the highly proliferative blastemal component is sensitive to chemotherapy, the standard treatment of WT.40,41,42 This may be partially due to the fact that chemotherapy usually targets rapidly dividing cells.40 However, blastemal-predominant tumors will develop resistance to chemotherapies, so blastemal WT remains to have a high relapse rate and is considered as “high risk.”43,44,45 For example, Huang et al. reported the higher metastatic potential of stage II blastema compared to other types of WT.45 According to SIOP guidelines, the metastatic WT with blastemal histology exhibits a poor prognosis after standard treatment.46 Consequently, generating models to recapitulate blastemal-predominant WTs will advance therapeutics for patients with high-risk WT.

We addressed the lack of high-risk blastemal and metastatic models by reprogramming WiT49 cells into WiT49-PRCs (Figures 1 and S1). Six genes, OCT4, SOX2, KLF4, c-MYC, NANOG, and LIN28A, were transiently transduced to WiT49 cells via episomal vectors to induce reprogramming. Remarkedly, these reprogrammed cells, when introduced into the kidneys, formed tumors that metastasized to the liver and lung (Figures 2 and S2). We have compared the expression of NANOG, MYC, and LIN28A in WiT49-PRC vs. WiT49 cells. Although NANOG expression change was below the cutoff of 2-fold in RNA-seq data, RT-qPCR results showed that NANOG expression increased from the range of 30- to 300-fold in four independent PRC clones, as compared with WiT49 cells (Figures 1D and S1B). Our RT-qPCR data showed that MYC was dramatically increased in PRC clones (Figures 1F and S1D); however, LIN28A was significantly decreased in PRC clones (Figures 1H and S1F). MYC is a central transcription factor that drives epigenetic reprogramming to induce a stem cell-like state and onset of tumorigenesis.47 Although our results cannot distinguish the contribution of MYC or NANOG expression alone or the epigenetic changes in PRCs per se result in the blastemal features of PRCs and the metastatic phenotype of WiT49-PRC tumors, our results can exclude the role of LIN28A in the metastasis ability of WiT49-PRCs as LIN28A is downregulated in WiT49-PRCs as compared with WiT49 cells.

The histology of WiT49-PRC tumors and the gene expression signatures of WiT49-PRCs recapitulated those of patient blastemal WT (Figures 3 and S3). However, it is important to note that blastemal cells are heterogeneous. Blastemal cells exhibit putative cancer stem cell features, which share similar molecular and histologic characteristics with nephron progenitor cells (NPCs). NPCs are derived from metanephric mesenchyme and also exist in the cap mesenchyme in the developing kidney. Like NPCs, WT blastemal cells express SIX2, EYA1, and SALL1 and lose the expression of epithelial markers (e.g., CK18). Although we also detected enhanced expression of SIX2, EYA1, and SALL1 in WiT49-PRCs compared to WiT49, we cannot conclude that WiT49-PRCs harbor all characteristics of blastemal cells. This is evident by the gene expression profiling of WiT49-PRCs when compared with those of blastemal-predominant tumors where WiT49-PRCs capture some but not all gene signatures of blastemal tumors. Nonetheless, the drugs that elicit sensitivity to WiT49-PRCs are also effective in blastemal WT PDXs, demonstrating that WiT49-PRC remains a valuable model for high-risk blastemal WTs. The gain of the metastatic phenotype after reprogramming of WiT49 enables us to evaluate the anti-metastasis effects of anti-cancer drugs. We indeed found that HDAC inhibitors panobinostat and romidepsin outperformed doxorubicin in blocking liver and lung metastasis of WiT49-PRC-1 in mice (Figures 5G–5I). Thus, WiT49-PRC is a valuable model for developing new therapies or re-purposing FDA-approved drugs for treating patients with metastatic WT.

WTs are increasingly recognized as a disease caused by epigenetic dysfunction. WTs are highly sporadic, with only about 5% of patients showing a familial predisposition. The genetic mutations that affect WT are diverse. Interestingly, the mutated genes are often epigenetic regulators. Thus, histone methylation, DNA methylation at imprinted loci, and chromatin modifications are frequently altered in blastemal WTs compared to normal kidney tissues.1 The epigenetic dysfunction in WTs provides the rationale for therapeutically targeting epigenetic enzymes to treat WTs. Our drug screening identifies hypersensitivity of both epithelial and blastemal-predominant WT PDXs to HDAC inhibitors. Specifically, panobinostat and romidepsin are found to be most effective for both epithelial- and blastemal-predominant tumors. Panobinostat is a pan-HDAC inhibitor, which showed some side effects due to targeting multiple HDACs. Romidepsin is believed to be a safer drug because it primarily targets HDAC1 and HDAC2. Type I HDAC enzymes are shown to be critical in self-renewal and differentiation of NPCs.48,49 HDAC1 and HDAC2 are highly expressed in WTs compared to the adjacent normal kidney tissue.50 Whether HDAC1 or HDAC2 is the therapeutic target in blastemal WTs warrants further investigation.

Nevertheless, several HDAC inhibitors are FDA-approved anti-cancer drugs. For example, panobinostat is approved for treating relapsed multiple myeloma, and romidepsin is approved for treating cutaneous T cell lymphoma. The effectiveness and safety of other HDAC inhibitors have also been assessed by many clinical trials for treating solid or hematological cancers.51 More importantly, several studies also found that WT cell lines are sensitive to HDAC inhibitors including vorinostat,52 panobinostat,53 and Merck 60.50 Even though the PDXs are not introduced to mouse kidneys, HDAC inhibitors that are effective in WiT49 and WiT49-PRC lines (Figures 4 and S4) and orthotopic models (Figures 5 and S5) remain to be effective in the in vivo PDX models (Figure 6), supporting that HDAC inhibitors may substitute chemotherapy for treating high-risk, metastatic WTs. Collectively, our data support the notion that multiple epigenetic drugs display higher cytotoxic effects than standard chemotherapy drugs used to treat WTs (Figure 4A).

In addition to HDACs, enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2), a histone methyltransferase in polycomb repressive complex 2 to catalyze H3K27me3,50 is often aberrantly expressed in WT and other forms of cancer. Interestingly, EZH2 expression is elevated by reprogramming WiT49 into WiT49-PRCs (Figures 3G, S3E, and S3I). EZH2 inhibitor tazemetostat has been FDA approved for the treatment of sarcoma. Therefore, further studies are needed to examine if blastemal PDXs are sensitive to EZH2 inhibitors in vivo.

Two additional drugs, cytarabine (Ara-C) and alisertib, were effective, particularly in WiT49-PRCs (Figures 4A, 4D, 4E, S4C, and S4D). Alisertib was also effective in blastemal WT PDXs (Figure 6). Alisertib is a selective Aurora A inhibitor clinically tested for the treatment of several cancer types. Cytarabine is an FDA-approved DNA damage drug for leukemia treatment. Our findings open new avenues for their usage in treating WT.

In conclusion, our reprogrammed WiT49-PRC is proven to be a valid cell line model recapitulating blastemal cells. This model has vast applications for drug discovery and assessment of the anti-metastatic potential of drugs in vivo. The application of WiT49-PRCs in a trial drug screening has led to the identification of two HDAC inhibitors that are highly effective toward both epithelial and blastemal-predominant WTs. Given the safety feature of HDAC inhibitors, they may replace chemotherapy to advance the treatment of high-risk WTs.

Limitations of the study

There are several limitations to this study. First, we only reprogrammed one WT cell line, WiT49, in this study. Another WT cell line 17.94 can be used for reprogramming to replicate the findings. Second, the goal of the study was to generate a novel model that recapitulates blastemal WTs, enabling the discovery of therapeutic vulnerability for high-risk WTs. As a proof of the concept, we only screened 209 drugs. To discover more FDA-approved drugs that may be re-purposed to improve the treatment outcome of patients with advanced WT, a large-scale drug screening should be conducted. Third, in the current paper, we validated several PRC-sensitive drugs using the PDX models in vivo because animal experiments are time-consuming and expensive, making it impractical to examine the in vivo effects of more drugs. Studies have shown that drug responses are largely preserved between PDxO and PDXs. To overcome the limitation of utilizing few PDXs in our study, PDxO models resembling epithelial, blastemal, or mixed-type WTs should be established from PDXs or primary tumors in the future. The PDxO models will be cost effective, enabling us to conduct the secondary screen in a medium throughput to identify more effective therapeutic agents for advanced WTs.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell culture

HEK293T cell line was purchased from ATCC. Both WiT49 and HEK293T cells were cultured in DMEM plus 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; VWR), and 1% antibiotics (penicillin-streptomycin, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and maintained at 37°C under a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. When reaching over 90% confluency, cells were trypsinized using Trypsin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and re-plated. The cell culture medium was changed every two days.

Cellular reprogramming via episomal vector transfection

Episomal vectors, including pEP4 E02S EN2K (plasmid #20925), pCEP4-M2L (#20926), and pEP4 E02S ET2K (#20927) supplied by Addgene (Cambridge, MA, USA) and gifted by Dr. James Thomson, were utilized as detailed in a prior publication.55 To create WiT49-PRCs, WiT49 cells underwent transfection with a mix of these episomal plasmids carrying OCT4, SOX2, KLF4, c-MYC, NANOG, and LIN28A (3 μg of pEP4 E02S ET2K, 3 μg of pEP4 E02S EN2K, and 2 μg of pCEP4-M2L), following our established protocol.28 This process involved using the Nucleofector II device with the U-20 program (Amaxa, Walkersville, MD, USA) and the Nucleofector kit R (Lonza, Hayward, CA, USA). Post-transfection, cells were seeded in a Matrigel-coated 6-well plate with Essential 8 (E8) Medium (A1517001) (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), with daily medium changes to E7 (E8 minus TGFB1) upon reaching 20–30% confluence. After three weeks, distinct WiT49-PRC clones displaying morphological changes indicative of reprogramming were identified and isolated using a pipette tip under microscopic observation. These clones were carefully selected based on their distinct morphological features, ensuring that changes were due to reprogramming rather than cell overcrowding. Selected clones were then expanded in a Matrigel-coated well with E8 medium to facilitate further growth and passaging. The entire reprogramming process is summarized in Figure 1A.

Animal models

Our study examined male NSG mice (Jackson Laboratory, Cat#5557) because Wilms tumors risk does not differ by sex, and both male and female mice have been used in the literature. All animal work was performed in accordance with protocols approved by the Research Animal Resource Center of UW-Madison, and the study was compliant with ethical regulations regarding animal research.

Orthotopic xenografts and in vivo metastatic analysis

1 X 106 luciferase stably expressed WiT49 and WiT49-PRC cells were resuspended in Matrigel and injected into the kidney capsule of 8-week-old NSG mice when the mice were anesthetized. A small incision (1–2 cm) was made in the back of the animal. The thin muscle layer on the left flank body wall was incised to expose the left kidney. A small incision was made in the kidney capsule. Using a Hamilton Syringe (700 series), WiT49 cells were injected into the renal subcapsule through the small incision. The kidney capsule incision was healed using a High Temperature Cautery Kit (Fisher Scientific). The incision in the thin muscle layer was then closed with 4-0 Polysorb suture (VWR), and the skin incision was closed with staples. After 9 weeks, tumor-bearing mice were injected with luciferin. After 10 min, mice were sacrificed. Liver and lung were collected and placed into the 6- well plates. Bioluminescent images were taken and bioluminescent intensity was analyzed. Tumors were collected and weighed.

The therapeutic efficacy in vivo was examined by orthotopic transplantation of WiT49-PRC and WiT49 to 8-week-old, male NSG mice followed by drug treatment. Briefly, WiT49-PRC and WiT49 cells were orthotopic transplanted into the kidney capsule of male NSG mice by using the same method. After 4 weeks, the tumor-bearing mice were randomized into the four treatment groups with doses corresponding to appropriate doses as previously reported in the literature. Briefly, mice were treated with doxorubicin (4 mg/kg, daily, IV), panobinostat (20 mg/kg, daily, IP) and romidepsin (4 mg/kg, daily, IP) for 16 days. The rough tumor size was estimated by injection of luciferin followed by measuring the radiance of tumors using bioluminescence imaging (BLI). The tumor volume was measured as radiance every 2–3 days and the experiment was terminated after 16 days. Lung and liver were dissected for ex vivo BLI. The radiance of tumors was measured every 2–3 days. Experiments were terminated after treatment for 16 days. Proliferation was measured by Ki67 staining.

Therapeutic efficacy in the PDX model

Wilms tumor PDXs were kind gifts from Dr. Michael Dye (Childhood Solid Tumor Network, St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital, Inc.).38 The therapeutic efficacy was examined in vivo by transplantation of epithelial and blastemal Wilms tumor PDXs to 8-week-old, male NSG mice, followed by drug treatment. Briefly, PDX cells were injected into the muscles of the limb. Tumor volumes were measured by using calipers with volume calculated by using the equation: (length × width× width)/2. When tumor size reached the size of 100 mm3, mice were randomized into the four treatment groups in appropriate doses previously reported in the literature. Briefly, mice were treated with doxorubicin (4 mg/kg, daily, IV), panobinostat (20 mg/kg, daily, IP) and Alisertib (30 mg/kg, daily, Orally) for 15 days. The tumor size was measured every 2–3 days. The experiments were terminated at day 15. Proliferation was measured by Ki67 staining.

Method details

Viral production and transduction of luciferase into WiT49 and WiT49-PRC cells

To produce lentivirus particles, HEK 293T cells were seeded into 10 cm dishes 24 h before transfection 293T cells were transfected at 90% confluency for each 10-cm dish, 30 μL polyethyleneimine (PEI) solution (Polysciences, Cat. # 23966), 2 μg VSVG, 4 μg psPAX2, and 4 μg luciferase lentiviral plasmids pHAGE PGK-GFP-IRES-Luciferase (Addgene #46793) was added to 1 mL of Opti-MEM. After 20 min of incubation at room temperature, the mixture was added dropwise to HEK 293T cells. After 48 h of incubation, the supernatant containing the viral particles was harvested, filtered with 0.45 μm filters (Steriflip, Millipore), and concentrated 100-fold using a Lenti-X concentrator (Clonetech). The concentrated supernatant was aliquoted and stored at −80°C. For viral transduction, WIT49 and WiT49-PRC cells were seeded into 6-well plates and exposed to the GFP-luciferase lentivirus for 48 h. GFP-positive cells were selected by using flow cytometry.

Total RNA extraction and real-time qPCR

The total RNA was extracted from WIT49 Parental and WiT49-PRC cells using the E.Z.N.An HP Total RNA Extraction Kit (Omega) under the manufacturer’s protocol. The total RNA quantity and were measured using NanoDrop 1000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) before reverse transcription using the SuperScript First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad). QPCR was performed to detect mRNA expression of genes of interest using the iQSYBR Green Premix (Bio-Rad), and the mRNA levels were calculated using the 2-ΔCt method. The primer sequences are listed in Table S2.

RNA-sequencing (RNA-Seq) and data processing and analysis

The RNA-seq library was prepared using the SMARTer Stranded RNA-Seq Kit (Takara Bio # 634839). Qualified libraries were deep-sequenced using an Illumina NextSeq 500 at Northwestern University (NUseq core). RNA-seq reads were aligned by STAR (version 2.5.2b)54 to the human genome (version hg38) with GENCODE basic gene annotations (version 27). Gene expression levels were quantified by RSEM (version 1.3.0), and differential expression was analyzed by edgeR (version 3.30.3). A differentially expressed gene was required to have at least 2-fold changes and an adjusted p-value <0.05.

To compare the similarity between WiT49-PRC cells with blastemal Wilms tumor patient and PDX samples, gene expression profiles of WiT49, WiT49-PRC-1, blastemal WT patient samples and blastemal PDXs were compared. We examined genes that are known to be associated with blastemal or epithelial cell lineages and that are also involved in kidney development and Wilms tumors. After normalization, we employed the limma method to remove the batch effects using edgeR (v3.40.2) in R. Each gene expression value was further standardized by subtracting the mean over all samples and dividing by the standard deviation.

HE and Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

Tumors were fixed in 10% (v/v) neutral-buffered formalin, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned (5 μm thickness). The slides were heated at 63°C for a half-hour, followed by deparaffinization and rehydration. For HE staining, sections were stained with hematoxylin for 2 min and eosin for 1 min, then washed and covered with a coverslip using CytosealTM XYL. For IHC staining, sections were put in antigen retrieval buffer and boiled for 23 min; the sections were allowed to cool to room temperature. Sections were blocked and incubated with the primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. Then, a secondary streptavidin-conjugated HRP antibody was used for 15 min at room temperature. The DAB detection kit was used to visualize expression signal intensity. The slides were counterstained with hematoxylin and then covered with a coverslip using Cytoseal XYL. The following antibodies were used, Ki67(Ab15580, Abcam), SIX2 (11562-1-AP, Proteintech), NCAM1 (701379, Thermo), EZH2 (5246S, Cell signaling technology), Vimentin (5741S, Cell signaling technology), HLAB (41915S, Cell signaling technology) human mitochondria (MA5-12017, Thermo), Snail2 (12129-1-AP, Proteintech) and Keratin 18(MA5-12104, Thermo).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using Microsoft excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, Washington, USA) and GraphPad Prism version 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical outliers were identified and removed by Interquartile Range analysis in Microsoft Excel. All data are shown as the mean ± SEM. Data were analyzed by the 2-tailed Student’s t test, p-value less than 0.05 was considered significant.
