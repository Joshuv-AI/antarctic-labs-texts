Beyond reflection: using the clinician educator milestones in faculty development
Authors: Era Buck, Flavio M Monteiro, Kimberly Turner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction. Faculty in academic medicine face increasing clinical and research demands, often limiting time for professional development and identity formation as educators. The ACGME Clinician Educator Milestones offer a structured framework for reflection and self-assessment, but their usefulness for broader applications such as needs assessment and program evaluation remains unexplored. We examined the potential of a milestone-based survey to support program evaluation and needs assessment in faculty development, and to gather preliminary validity evidence for its use beyond self-reflection. Methods. We adapted the ACGME Clinician Educator Milestones into a 20-item survey across four domains: Educational Theory and Practice, Well-Being, Welcoming Learning Environment, and Administration. The survey was administered to three cohorts of interprofessional faculty development participants. Cohort1 (n = 6) completed the survey post-program; Cohort2 (pre n = 10, post n = 14) received an enhanced educator identity curriculum; Cohort3 (n = 27) participated in an international immersive program. Responses were rated on a 6-point proficiency scale. Descriptive statistics and MANOVA were used to analyze changes in self-assessed competence for Cohort2. Results. All cohorts used the full range of proficiency levels. Cohort2 showed significant improvement from pre- to post-program (Wilks’ Lambda = .123; F = 3.207; p = .038). Domains emphasized in the curriculum – such as teaching fundamentals and learning environments – showed the greatest gains. Lower scores were consistently observed in administration and change management. Discussion. Preliminary findings support the use of the milestone-based survey for program evaluation and needs assessment in faculty development. Improvements aligned with curricular emphasis suggest content validity, while similar results across culturally distinct cohorts support external validity. Further research is warranted to explore the relationship between self-assessed competence and educator identity formation as well as the validity of use beyond reflection.KEYWORDS: Faculty development, educator identity, program evaluation, educator milestones, self-assessment, validity evidence, reflective practice

Introduction

Growing clinical and research demands encroach on faculty time available for educational activities and may hinder development of an educator identity. Recent literature [1] recommends explicit cultivation and assessment of educator identity for health professions educators. Factors influencing educator identity formation include personal and professional experience, development of competence, values, reflective practice, and socialization.

The ACGME Clinician Educator Milestones [2], a reflective tool developed for graduate medical education faculty and residents, outlines 20 competencies across four domains aligning with educator identity formation. These milestones have been recommended by numerous groups in medicine [3–5] and dentistry [6] for use as a framework for faculty development and career planning. They have been found useful for self-directed goal setting for clinician educators [7]. We sought to examine whether use of these milestones is suitable beyond reflection and self-assessment, e.g., needs analysis or program evaluation in faculty development. Consistent with modern validity theory [8–10] we collected preliminary evidence to frame the validity arguments about the meaning of scores generated by the milestone-based survey.

Materials and methods

This retrospective cohort study used existing data from 3 cohorts of health professions faculty who voluntarily engaged in faculty development programming. Cohorts 1 and 2 were participants in a longitudinal ‘educational scholars’ type faculty development program at a large state-supported academic health center. Cohort 3 were participants in an immersive faculty development program for a nascent academic health center in the Middle East. All faculty in the programs were invited to participate with a response rate of 58%.

We adapted the ACGME milestones [2] and formatted them as a survey administered to 3 cohorts of interprofessional participants on 4 occasions without training regarding the interpretation of the milestones. The primary adaptation of the milestones was reframing the original domain focused on equality for all learners as ‘Welcoming Learning Environment’ to accommodate a changing cultural climate within our state as well as for our international cohort. Cohort1 (n = 6) completed the survey at the conclusion (post only) of a 20-month longitudinal faculty development program with curricular content organized around educational practice, educational scholarship and minimal content related to educator identity formation. Cohort2 (pre (n = 10) and post (n = 14)) received similar content with an additional educator identity curricular thread. This additional thread was added as part of the routine updating of the curriculum for the longitudinal program. Cohort3 participated in an international immersive faculty development program focusing only on educational practice and educational scholarship. For Cohorts1 and 3, the survey served as a source of reflection and self-assessment. For Cohort2, the survey was administered twice, once at the beginning of the program and once on completion as a source of self-assessment and to explore the potential for program evaluation of the additional educator identity programming.

The survey comprised 20 questions in 4 domains, Educational Theory and Practice, Well-Being, Welcoming Learning Environment, and Administration. Each domain included 6 levels of proficiency on which the respondents rated their perceived development: emerging, novice, advanced beginner, competent, proficient, and expert. Scoring for the six levels ranged from 0 to 5.

We plotted mean scores for the domains for each cohort in addition to summarizing descriptive statistics. We used a MANOVA to compare the scores pre and post for Cohort2.

Results

The number of respondents per cohort varied with sample sizes limited by the overall size of the cohorts. All cohorts used the range of scores from novice to expert with Cohort 3 also using the emerging level to rate their competence. An overall mean across domains yielded Cohort1 (n = 6) M = 3.7 (SD = 1.0) with scores ranging from competent to proficient; Cohort2 pre (n = 10) M = 3.0 (SD = 0.5) with scores ranging from advanced beginner to competent; Cohort2 post (n = 14) M = 4.1 (SD = 1.3) with scores ranging from proficient to expert; Cohort3 (n = 27) M = 3.8 (SD = 1.5) with scores ranging from competent to expert.

A MANOVA compared pre and post scores for the cohort receiving an enhanced educator identity curriculum: Wilks’ Lambda = .123; F = 3.207 (df 20,9); p = .038.

As can be seen in Figure 1, mean scores across domains were lowest for the pre-program cohort participating in the enhanced educator identity curriculum and highest for the same cohort post-program. Similar patterns of variability are seen across domains for the 3 cohorts. Discussion

Although working with small sample sizes, this preliminary work supports the potential validity for use of the instrument in conducting needs assessment and program evaluation in faculty development in addition to its stated purposes of reflection and self-assessment. The variation among scores across domains may provide guidance for faculty developers about the needs of faculty participants and content to include and/or emphasize during programs. The demonstrated increase after a 20-month program compared to pre-program scores provides preliminary evidence for potential validity in program evaluation.

Domains emphasized during the program showed greatest improvement for the cohort providing pre/post data and consistently had high scores for all cohorts having completed faculty development programs. These domains included teaching and facilitating fundamentals, learning sciences, and learning environments, all of which were addressed in the longitudinal program and the immersive international program, thus providing additional evidence of validity for use in program evaluation. The scores for the culturally distinct faculty in the immersive program were similar to those of US-based faculty, providing evidence of external validity for the findings.

The limitations of this study include the small sample size and the reliance on self-report data. Mindful of the small sample size, we have limited the inferences made regarding the data. Beyond these preliminary data, future studies may use this report as a foundation to build larger sample sizes with more diverse data sources as well as examining differences among health care professionals.

These data provide preliminary support for the use of this framework for planning and evaluating faculty development programs. Next steps include continuing to examine the validity of the milestones as part of program evaluation as well as more granular investigation of the relationship between self-assessed competence and identity as a health professions educator.
