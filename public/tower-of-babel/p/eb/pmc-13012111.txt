Orthogonal disentanglement of single-cell multi-omics reveals private and shared drivers of tissue development and pathogenesis
Authors: Yi Fan, Yanchi Su, Gaoyang Hao, Fuzhou Wang, Xingjian Chen, Ka-Chun Wong, Xiangtao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceTissues develop and diseases progress through coordinated changes in gene expression and gene regulation, which can now be measured by single-cell multi-omics. However, these datasets contain both modality-specific signals unique to each omics layer and shared signals reflecting coordinated regulation, making integration and interpretation challenging. In this study, we present Omics Separation Modeling using Domain Adaptation (OmiDos), a deep learning-driven framework that disentangles shared biological programs from modality-specific effects to generate integrated, biologically informative representations. We demonstrate the performance and versatility of OmiDos across diverse single-cell multi-omics datasets, enabling more reliable cross-dataset integration and downstream analyses.

Recent advances in single-cell multi-omics sequencing technologies have enabled more comprehensive profiling of cellular states without relying on prior assumptions, providing an increasingly complete view of biological systems (1). Laboratories worldwide have produced hundreds of single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) or ATAC-seq (scATAC-seq) datasets, leading to the discovery of previously uncharacterized cell types and regulatory circuits (2). More recently, multimodal platforms capable of jointly profiling different molecular modalities within the same cell (i.e., paired data) have emerged, including SHARE-seq (3), 10x Genomics Multiome, DBiT-seq (4), and SNARE-seq (5). Together, these technologies allow for fine-grained dissection of how epigenetic modifications regulate gene expression at the single-cell level (6). This capability is especially transformative in studies of tissue function and disease progression, where single-omics assays often fall short in capturing the full spectrum of regulatory complexity.

Yet, gene regulation remains challenging to decipher due to the inherently multi-layered nature of these data (7). scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq layers differ significantly in sparsity, noise levels, and statistical distributions, complicating the identification of shared signals and often resulting in modality misalignment. Technical variation introduced by differing experimental protocols or clinical sources further confounds biological comparisons. Incomplete integration of chromatin accessibility data limits our ability to infer regulatory programs.

Additionally, standard dimensionality reduction techniques can obscure subtle cell subpopulations or transitional states, leading to the loss of critical biological information relevant to early pathogenesis or normal development. To address these challenges, a range of sequential multi-omics integration methods have been developed (8–14), typically performing joint analyses after modality-specific preprocessing and dimensionality reduction. In parallel, direct multi-omics modeling approaches leverage deep learning architectures to learn shared representations directly from raw multi-omics data (15–18).

Gene regulation is inherently asynchronous, as transcriptional programs, chromatin accessibility, and transcription factor activity operate on different temporal scales (3). These layers of regulation are coordinated, yet not necessarily synchronized, resulting in time-lagged or uncoupled molecular events across modalities. However, single-cell multi-omics technologies capture these complex processes at a single snapshot in time, limiting the ability to resolve temporal dependencies. As a result, multi-omics signals comprise both shared regulatory programs and modality-specific features arising from temporal asynchrony, biological specialization, or technical variation. Disentangling these shared and modality-specific factors is essential for interpreting regulatory mechanisms underlying dynamic cellular states. To our knowledge, most existing computational approaches do not explicitly model the structured distinction between shared and private sources of variation. By focusing primarily on constructing joint representations, they often overlook omic-specific information, ultimately obscuring the distinct contributions of regulatory layers to gene expression dynamics.

Here, we introduce Omics Separation Modeling using Domain Adaptation (OmiDos) (19), an unsupervised deep learning framework that disentangles shared and private representations across multiple omics layers. OmiDos models private representations for each modality to capture omic-specific properties, and shared representations to reflect cross-modal regulatory features, with explicit constraints to ensure their separation. These representations are learned through variational inference, with orthogonality constraints explicitly encouraging the disentanglement between shared and private latent components. Notably, this learning process is annotation-free, relying solely on the structure of the input data without requiring predefined cell labels. This orthogonality ensures that OmiDos preserves omic-specific information while capturing regulatory signals shared across modalities. To address misalignment in unpaired data, OmiDos incorporates an adversarial discriminator with noise contrastive estimation to reduce modality-specific confounding and encourage domain-invariant representation learning. Additionally, a decoder module regularized by maximum mean discrepancy facilitates batch correction while preserving biologically meaningful variation across datasets.

We evaluated OmiDos on 25 multi-omics datasets spanning 19 human and 6 mouse samples, demonstrating superior clustering accuracy, batch-effect correction, and misalignment resolution across diverse platforms and tissue types. These datasets encompass a wide range of tissues—including the brain, jejunum, lymph nodes, kidney, and embryonic structures—and represent both physiological and pathological conditions such as renal carcinoma, small lymphocytic lymphoma, and Alzheimer’s disease. In a mouse secondary palate development dataset covering embryonic days E13.5 to E14.5, OmiDos precisely identified a functional enhancer at chr16:32697085-32697585 regulating Muc4 expression. This enhancer exhibited strong activity aligned with the dynamics of epithelial cell differentiation and migration, underscoring its importance in palate morphogenesis. The application of OmiDos to medulloblastoma revealed a potential role deficiency in driving partial closure of the distal enhancer region of Neurod1, thus regulating the progression of medulloblastoma from normal to cancerous states.

Results

Methodology Overview of OmiDos.

OmiDos is a flexible, annotation-free deep learning framework designed to disentangle shared and omics-specific representations from single-cell multi-omics data. The inherent asynchrony of gene regulation, combined with the snapshot nature of single-cell multi-omics technologies, gives rise to modality-specific private signals and cross-modal shared signals. As a result, shared signals are susceptible to contamination from noise associated with modality-specific variation. To address this, OmiDos disentangles omic-specific representations that capture features unique to each omics layer, while shared representations, enforced via autoencoders, and explicit loss constraints reflect coordinated regulatory programs across modalities. By finding shared representations that are orthogonal to omic-specific ones, OmiDos effectively separates unique modality signals and generates representations that more faithfully reflect underlying gene regulatory mechanisms. OmiDos adopts a modular architecture capable of handling paired, unpaired, and batch-heterogeneous data, ensuring robust performance across a range of integration scenarios (Fig. 1).

The framework of OmiDos. (A) The OmiDos workflow. scRNA (Left) and scATAC (Right) data are input into private and shared encoding networks. The private encoders extract modality-specific features, while the shared encoder integrates common regulatory signals across omics. The resulting multi-omics embeddings support downstream analyses. (B) Model training process in OmiDos. scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq data are preprocessed into normalized and tiled matrices, then encoded through shared and private networks. The shared encoder learns cross-omic representations, constrained to be orthogonal to the private ones. The training objective includes reconstruction losses (ZINB for RNA, GMM for ATAC), unpaired integration loss via an adversarial discriminator and NCE, and batch-effect correction using MMD regularization in the decoder. Together, these components disentangle omic-specific and shared variation while ensuring cross-batch and cross-modality consistency. (C) The integrative representations learned by OmiDos enable diverse downstream analyses such as biomarker discovery, cis-regulatory annotation, trajectory reconstruction, and enhancer identification.

At its core, OmiDos employs modality-specific encoders to extract private latent representations from scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq, alongside a shared encoder that captures cross-modal regulatory programs (Fig. 1B). These components are disentangled via orthogonality constraints. A variational objective governs training, with Zero-Inflated Negative Binomial (ZINB) and Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) likelihoods used to reconstruct RNA and ATAC profiles, respectively. Reconstruction depends jointly on the shared and private latent spaces, ensuring that both cross-modal coordination and omics-specific variations are retained. To handle cases where data are unpaired, that is, collected from the same population but not from the same cells, OmiDos incorporates an adversarial discriminator and a noise contrastive estimation (NCE) module. This extension regularizes the shared encoder to learn domain-invariant signals by penalizing modality-predictive features and promoting consistency across data types, thereby mitigating modality-specific confounding. Importantly, this design enables OmiDos to perform robust integration even when cell-level correspondences are unavailable, a common limitation in current multi-omics datasets.

Additionally, OmiDos addresses batch heterogeneity by incorporating label-informed maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) regularization in its decoder layers. This is motivated by the hypothesis that batch effects occupy a subspace approximately orthogonal to true biological variation—a theoretical assumption supported by prior studies in representation learning and batch correction. This strategy enables harmonization of data across batches without sacrificing biologically meaningful structure. The representations learned by OmiDos facilitate a wide array of biological analyses (Fig. 1C), including biomarker discovery, trajectory inference, identification of cis-regulatory elements, and linkage of distal enhancer regions to their target genes. This broad applicability makes OmiDos a versatile tool for interpreting regulatory dynamics across single-cell multi-omics landscapes.

OmiDos Enhances Cell-Type Resolution by Disentangling Multi-Omics Signals.

Multi-omics integration requires resolving signals originating from distinct molecular layers. These signals generally fall into two categories: modality-specific (private) signals, which capture the intrinsic characteristics of individual omics layers (e.g., RNA or ATAC), and cross-modality (shared) signals, which emerge only through the joint modeling of multiple molecular modalities (20). Unlike conventional methods that merge all information into a unified latent space, OmiDos explicitly separates private representations (PREM) from shared representations (SHEM), enabling the disentanglement of these orthogonal signal sources. To systematically evaluate OmiDos, we performed extensive benchmarking across 21 single-cell multi-omics datasets, including 16 human and 5 mouse samples. These datasets cover a broad range of anatomical sites, such as the brain, jejunum, lymph nodes, kidney, and embryonic tissues, and encompass both physiological and pathological conditions, including renal carcinoma, small lymphocytic lymphoma, and Alzheimer’s disease.

We compared the SHEM outputs from OmiDos with those from eight state-of-the-art integration methods: GLUE, DeepMAPS, LIGER, MultiVI, iNMF, Harmony, Scanpy, and Seurat. Integration performance was assessed using four metrics: normalized mutual information (NMI) (21), adjusted Rand index (ARI) (22), runtime, and memory usage. Across all datasets, OmiDos consistently outperformed competing methods, achieving higher NMI and ARI scores in the majority of cases (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). Several methods performed well on the peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) dataset. In this dataset, cell types are relatively easy to distinguish (8). However, their performance declined notably on brain datasets, which are characterized by greater cellular heterogeneity (23). In contrast, OmiDos maintained robust clustering accuracy across both simple and complex tissue landscapes.

We hypothesized that the enhanced clustering performance of OmiDos derives from its capacity to orthogonally encode single-modality and cross-modality signals. To investigate this, we examined a representative paired bone marrow mononuclear cell (BMMC) dataset containing transcriptionally similar myeloid progenitors and their close descendants such as CD16+ and CD14+ monocytes. The subtle transcriptional distinctions among these lineages pose a significant analytical challenge (24). Remarkably, OmiDos generated nearly identical UMAP-based low-dimensional projections for PREM and SHEM (Fig. 2 D and F). Nonetheless, a more granular analysis of their respective community structures revealed meaningful divergences (Fig. 2 D and F), underscoring the benefit of modeling modality-specific contributions.

Both the PREM and SHEM preserved canonical immune cell identities when compared against curated annotations (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B), which accounts for their overlapping UMAP distributions. Given the established relationship between gene expression and cell identity, we further analyzed gene-level variation to interpret discrepancies in community structures (Fig. 2 E and G). Differentially expressed genes within PREM-derived clusters were enriched for established cell-type-specific markers (Fig. 2G), confirming the biological fidelity of the clusters—consistent with the understanding that cell identity is often defined by transcriptional signatures (25).

SHEM, however, revealed finer biological distinctions (Fig. 2E). Notably, S13 was marked by strong overexpression of PID1 (rank = 1, logfoldchanges = 4.7, pvalsadj = 9E-205), clearly separating clusters S6 and S10 from the CD14+ monocyte population (Fig. 2H). Previous studies have implicated PID1 in obesity-related traits (26), aligning with the donor’s clinical background.

Functional enrichment analysis linked PID1 to key pathways such as negative regulation of phosphorylation and metabolic regulation (Fig. 2J). PID1’s phosphotyrosine-binding domain and inferred role in immune regulation (27), supported by its coexpression with predicted interactors like NTM, an immunoglobulin superfamily protein (confidence score = 0.7), suggest a broader involvement in immune-related signaling. By disentangling and analyzing private and shared signals, OmiDos enables a more nuanced understanding of cellular heterogeneity across modalities and enhances the resolution of multi-omics analysis in biological systems.

OmiDos Elucidates the Role of a Candidate Distal Cis-Regulatory Element in Epithelial Cells.

Building on these findings, we next evaluated whether OmiDos could uncover gene regulatory mechanisms at the chromatin accessibility level, with a specific focus on cell type–specific cis-regulatory elements. We applied OmiDos to a well-annotated peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) multi-omics dataset comprising seven clearly defined cell populations, offering an ideal framework to assess regulatory resolution (28). To minimize bias arising from unequal sample sizes and to ensure robust detection of regulatory elements, we systematically downsampled all cell populations to a comparable level. Subsequently, we benchmarked OmiDos against several multi-omics integration algorithms, using NMI and ARI to quantitatively assess clustering accuracy and concordance with established cell type annotations.

The resulting clusters from OmiDos’s SHEM closely matched the annotated cell type (Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Figs. S6 and S7), supported by high NMI and ARI scores (Fig. 3B). The composition analysis also confirmed that OmiDos effectively identified both minor populations such as NK cells and rare populations such as dendritic cells (DCs) (29) (Fig. 3A). For instance, ZBTB16, a well-established NK cell marker (30), was accurately assigned by OmiDos (SI Appendix, Fig. S8A). A peak located within 200 bp of the ZBTB16 transcription start site (TSS) showed strong chromatin accessibility and H3K4me3 enrichment (SI Appendix, Fig. S8B), confirming its identity as promoter region. Similarly, OmiDos correctly identified the canonical DC marker gene FLT3 (31) along with its associated promoter region (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S9), highlighting its robustness in detecting cis-regulatory elements in various cell types.

Although the preceding analyses focused on static cell identities, regulatory programs are inherently dynamic. To investigate whether OmiDos can capture these dynamics during developmental transitions, a context where its ability to orthogonally encode private and shared signals is crucial, we applied it to multi-omics profiles from the embryonic day (E)13.5 and E14.5 mouse secondary palates (32). Palatal fusion involves tightly regulated epithelial cell adhesion, followed by the formation and removal of interepithelial seams. Although the morphological process has been well studied, the underlying cellular regulatory mechanisms are still poorly understood (33). Following cell type annotation using canonical markers, we focused our analysis on epithelial cells (Fig. 3D and SI Appendix, Figs. S10 and S11). UMAP projections from PREM and SHEM revealed comparable global structures (Fig. 3 E and F); however, closer examination uncovered differences in community architecture (34), particularly in peak-to-gene associations. Notably, genes from the Muc family, key epithelial markers, were differentially enriched between the PREM and SHEM. While the overall peak-to-gene relationships were broadly consistent, SHEM uniquely identified an additional regulatory interaction within the Muc network (Fig. 3 G and H).

Within a 50 kb upstream and 40 kb downstream window of the Muc4 TSS, both PREM and SHEM detected a comparable number of peaks; however, SHEM uniquely identified one additional interaction (Correlation = 0.55, FDR = 2.07e-12) associated with strong DNase-seq signals (Fig. 3I), indicative of a distal open regulatory region. Motif enrichment analysis revealed a significant enrichment of Rarb-binding motifs (Fig. 3K and SI Appendix, Fig. S14). In humans, RARB is a key transcriptional regulator involved in coordinating retinoic acid- (RA-) mediated morphogenic movements, cell growth, and differentiation during eye development. Induction of RARB overexpression in human lens epithelial cells leads to increased proliferation (35). Consistent with this, both Muc4 and Rarb were highly expressed at E14.5 (Fig. 3 J and K), supporting the predicted regulatory relationship. Together, these findings highlight the critical role of OmiDos in dynamically resolving regulatory programs by disentangling private and shared signals. Both PREM and SHEM captured a potential distal open regulatory region near Muc4, corroborated by DNase-seq evidence. Leveraging this foundation, SHEM uniquely incorporated this region into the epithelial-specific regulatory network as a putative distal enhancer. This distinction underscores the enhanced capacity of OmiDos to separate shared and cell type–specific regulatory components, thereby improving the interpretability of chromatin accessibility in the context of functional cis-regulatory network. By separating shared developmental cues from cell type–specific regulatory programs, OmiDos facilitates more precise reconstruction of transcriptional landscapes that underlie cellular identity during development.

OmiDos Resolves Multi-Omics Confounders in the Regulatory Architecture of T Cells.

Balancing batch effect removal with the preservation of biologically meaningful signals remains particularly challenging, especially given that many true biological signals are likely to be previously uncharacterized. An essential hypothesis has emerged, proposing that the batch effect is almost orthogonal to the biological subspace (36). This conceptual framework underpins the orthogonal disentanglement strategy adopted by OmiDos. Accordingly, OmiDos preserves shared biological characteristics across multi-omics batches while preventing the loss of unique regulatory signals during integration. Traditional methods often address batch effects within individual modalities, potentially resulting in considerable loss of shared biological information. To assess the effectiveness of OmiDos, we applied it to a bone marrow mononuclear cell (BMMC) dataset characterized by complex biological and technical batch structures. We further investigated whether variations in sample size could lead to spurious batch correction outcomes.

We benchmarked OmiDos against a range of state-of-the-art multi-omics integration tools, including GLUE, multiVI, BindSC, LIGER, scMoMaT, Seurat V3, Seurat V4, and Cobolt. Batch correction performance was evaluated using metrics such as kBET and Batch-ASW. OmiDos consistently outperformed other methods, achieving superior scores on both metrics, indicative of effective batch mixing (Fig. 4A). Additionally, elevated NMI and ARI values confirmed that OmiDos maintained biologically meaningful cell-type distinctions (Fig. 4A). Visualization further supported these findings (Fig. 4B and SI Appendix, Figs. S15–S30), showing that OmiDos effectively merged disparate batches into cohesive clusters composed of cells with shared biological characteristics. Across all metrics-including batch correction, clustering accuracy, and biological integrity-OmiDos demonstrated robust and consistent performance, independent of sample size variations.

To determine whether OmiDos aligns with the theoretical orthogonal framework for batch effects, we examined the training dynamics of the generated representations—PREM-RNA, PREM-ATAC, and SHEM—across successive iterations (Fig. 4C). At the outset, batch effects manifested as discrete clusters corresponding to individual batches. As training proceeded, these clusters gradually merged and reorganized into biologically coherent subpopulations. By the final iteration, PREM-RNA, PREM-ATAC, and SHEM exhibited well-defined clusters composed of mixed-batch cells, each corresponding to true biological identities. This dynamic evolution illustrates OmiDos’s capacity to remove batch effects while preserving underlying biological structure.

However, the emergence of a mixed cluster observed exclusively in PREM-ATAC and SHEM suggested potential partial disentanglement of regulatory signals in complex networks. Differential gene expression analysis confirmed the uniqueness of this mixed cluster at the gene expression level, identifying significantly elevated expression of BCL11B (avg_log2FC = 1.15, p_val_adj = 1e-123) (Fig. 4 D and E). Previous research has demonstrated that transcription factor BCL11B plays a critical role in the development of the central nervous system and the differentiation of T cells (37). In the immune system, BCL11B is activated during thymocyte differentiation and is indispensable for multiple stages of T cell precursor differentiation. This finding aligns closely with the composition of the mixed cluster, comprising 35.4% activated CD4+ T cells, 27.3% naive CD4+ T cells, 16.1% NK cells, and 15.0% CD8+ T cells, supporting its strong association with T cell differentiation (Fig. 4C). The exclusive appearance of this mixed cluster in PREM-ATAC and SHEM demonstrates OmiDos’s capacity to retain unique regulatory signals during integration.

Given the incomplete understanding of the genotype–phenotype relationship of the transcription factor BCL11B, we further investigated its regulatory network. We identified a proximal enhancer located 286 bp upstream of a BCL11B exon, marked by elevated DNase hypersensitivity (DNase = 3.98), H3K27 acetylation (H3k27ac = 4.37), and H3K4 trimethylation (H3K4me3 = 4.60) (Fig. 4F). Motif enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of the transcription factor CLOCK at this enhancer (Fig. 4G). CLOCK is a core component of the transcription–translation feedback oscillator driving the mammalian circadian rhythm, which influences both cellular timing mechanisms and immune cell lineage development. In T cells, CLOCK may modulate T cell activation and effector functions through rhythmic regulation of glycolytic enzymes (38). Therefore, we hypothesize that CLOCK binding at the proximal enhancer region regulates BCL11B expression, subsequently affecting T cell differentiation.

In summary, OmiDos effectively implements a theoretically grounded orthogonal disentanglement strategy to achieve robust batch correction across complex multi-omics datasets. By explicitly decoupling batch-associated from biological signals, OmiDos not only preserves essential cell-type distinctions but also enables the discovery of context-specific regulatory programs. The identification of the CLOCK-BCL11B regulatory axis exemplifies its ability to retain fine-grained regulatory information during integration.

OmiDos Reveals Regulatory Landscapes and Lineage in Unpaired Single-Cell Multi-Omics.

When scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq data are derived from the same biological sample but lack explicit cell-level pairing information, cell heterogeneity introduces significant noise into the integration process. In such scenarios, modality-specific (private) signals often dominate over shared biological signals, resulting in integrated representations that are biased toward one modality. A widely adopted strategy to mitigate this issue involves manually or adaptively reweighting the contributions of each modality. However, these approaches frequently produce latent embeddings in which shared and modality-specific features remain entangled and difficult to interpret. To assess the capacity of OmiDos to disentangle shared and private features in unpaired settings, we extracted the scRNA-seq and snATAC-seq profiles from the SHARE-seq dataset (24), which provides experimentally determined cell-level pairings as ground truth. We then compared the inferred cell alignments generated by OmiDos with the true pairings. In addition, we evaluated the preservation of cell-type structure in the integrated embedding using two quantitative metrics: Average Silhouette Width (ASW) and Graph Connectivity (GC). These metrics reflect the degree to which cells of the same type form distinct and cohesive clusters after integration. We benchmarked the performance of OmiDos against several state-of-the-art integration methods, including MultiVI, LIGER, Seurat, and Cobolt.

OmiDos successfully integrated unpaired scRNA-seq and snATAC-seq data, achieving superior cross-modality cell type mixing while preserving biologically meaningful clustering structures (Fig. 5A). It outperformed all baseline methods in both ASW and GC metrics, providing quantitative evidence of its integration effectiveness (Fig. 5B). To further evaluate the biological relevance of the learned embedding, we leveraged known cell-type annotations to compute average embeddings per cell type, subsequently constructing a pairwise correlation matrix for each method (Fig. 5C). The correlation matrix derived from OmiDos closely reflected the expected biological relationships between cell types. In contrast, competing methods exhibited spurious correlations between biologically distant cell types, undermining the biological plausibility of their integrated representations.

Notably, OmiDos accurately captured the strong correlation between hair shaft (HS) cells and transit-amplifying cells (TAC), a well-established lineage relationship in the hair follicle cycle (24) (Fig. 5C). To further validate that OmiDos preserved lineage-specific transitions while capturing shared biological signals, we performed high-resolution differential gene expression analysis. This revealed that HS and TAC-1 cells exhibit highly concordant DEG profiles, consistent with their developmental relationship (Fig. 5D). We additionally applied Slingshot to infer lineage trajectories within the OmiDos embedding (Fig. 5E). Pseudotime analysis along lineage 1, which spans from group 7 to group 2, revealed a coherent and progressive differentiation path. Furthermore, the overlapping distributions observed between groups 3 and 4 suggested the presence of transitional cellular states, further supporting the fidelity of the inferred trajectories.

To identify genes with significant expression dynamics along the inferred trajectory, we applied tradeSeq (39). Clustering of temporally dynamic expression patterns revealed distinct gene modules with characteristic temporal profiles (Fig. 5G). Genes in cluster 4 were enriched for cell cycle and mitosis-related functions, highlighting early proliferative activity in TAC-1 cells. Cluster 1 genes were associated with Wnt signaling and epithelial migration, suggesting active signaling during mid-stage differentiation. Cluster 2 genes were involved in skin development, epidermal differentiation, and neural tube formation, marking the terminal differentiation of HS cells. Finally, cluster 3 genes were related to mRNA processing, chromatin remodeling, and RNA splicing, underscoring the role of posttranscriptional regulation in the later stages of differentiation.

To further validate these inferred developmental dynamics at the gene level, we examined representative lineage-associated marker genes that reflect stage-specific transcriptional and epigenetic changes (SI Appendix, Figs. S39–S41). Specifically, we focused on Trps1 and Sox5, two transcription factors implicated in skin morphogenesis and lineage specification. Reduced expression of Trps1 in hair follicles has been linked to congenital hair loss and aberrant Stat3 signaling in trichorhinophalangeal syndrome (40). Sox5 modulates melanogenesis by acting downstream of microRNA-21a-5p in mouse skin melanocytes (41). Consistently, Trps1 was highly localized in terminally differentiated HS cells (Fig. 5H), underscoring its relevance in late-stage differentiation. Conversely, Sox5 was prominent in early differentiation stages of TAC-1 cells (Fig. 5I). Chromatin accessibility profiles (Fig. 5J) revealed increased accessibility at the Trps1 locus in late-stage groups (2 and 3), consistent with its role in terminal differentiation. In contrast, the Sox5 locus exhibited higher accessibility in early-to-mid-stage group 4, reflecting its function in early lineage priming and cell fate commitment.

In practice, unpaired datasets may originate from different populations. One common manifestation of this discrepancy is the presence of cell types that appear exclusively in one modality (24). To evaluate whether OmiDos depends on the same population assumption, We simulated population mismatch by sequentially removing one cell type from only one modality (SI Appendix).

OmiDos reliably reconstructs lineage relationships and regulatory programs when the same population assumption holds. However, the observations show that deviations from this assumption require more cautious interpretation. Under population mismatch conditions, OmiDos preserves key aspects of biological structure, such as stable ASW and GC scores (SI Appendix, Figs. S42–S45). At the regulatory level, OmiDos continues to recover a meaningful fraction of known peak gene interactions and partially captures modality specific signals through its orthogonal private subspaces (SI Appendix, Figs. S46–S48). Nevertheless, it may fail to maintain the expected lineage proximity between certain related cell types, such as HS and TAC-1.

In summary, OmiDos demonstrates robust performance in integrating unpaired scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq data by effectively disentangling shared and modality-specific signals. Through rigorous benchmarking and detailed biological validation, we show that OmiDos not only improves alignment and clustering quality, but also preserves biologically meaningful lineage trajectories and regulatory programs.

OmiDos Identifies Stat2-Linked Neurod1 Chromatin Dysregulation and Distal Enhancer Reprogramming in SHH Medulloblastoma.

Medulloblastoma (MB), a highly aggressive embryonal tumor of the developing cerebellum, accounts for around 25% of pediatric brain malignancies and remains a leading cause of cancer-related deaths in children (42). Among its molecular subtypes, the Sonic Hedgehog (SHH) subgroup (MBSHH) emerges from cerebellar granule neuron progenitors (GNPs) and constitutes approximately 30% of all MB cases. Persistent activation of the SHH pathway is a defining feature of this group, initially rendering these tumors susceptible to targeted inhibition. However, therapeutic resistance often develops, complicating long-term disease control (43). Recent advances in single-cell multi-omics technologies have transformed cancer research, allowing high-resolution characterization of tumor epigenomes (44). In this study, we employed OmiDos to perform an integrative analysis of multi-omics data derived from a well-established murine model of Sonic Hedgehog-type medulloblastoma (MBSHH). Our objective was to elucidate the epigenomic mechanisms that orchestrate the progressive transformation of GNPs into malignant tumor cells.

The MBSHH dataset captures a well-defined cellular lineage, encompassing cerebellar GNPs at postnatal day 7 (P7), preneoplastic cells (PNCs) at postnatal day 28 (P28), and fully transformed MB cells. At the transcriptomic level (Fig. 6A), GNPs exhibit high nFeature RNA and nCount RNA values and a low proportion of mitochondrial gene expression, reflecting their biological characteristics. These cells reside in the external granular layer of the cerebellum and, during normal development, exit the cell cycle, differentiate, and migrate inward to form mature granule neurons (44). However, when SHH signaling is dysregulated due to genetic mutations, GNPs undergo abnormal proliferation and transition into PNCs. By P28, this uncontrolled proliferation results in cerebellar hyperplasia, marked by a reduction in nFeature RNA and nCount RNA values and an increase in mitochondrial gene expression. As tumorigenesis progresses and additional oncogenic events are acquired, fully malignant MB cells emerge. These are characterized by a further decrease in transcriptomic complexity and an elevated proportion of mitochondrial transcripts, consistent with the metabolic and proliferative reprogramming observed in advanced tumor states (44).

To elucidate the epigenetic basis of this transformation, differentially accessible regions (DARs) were analyzed across the GNP-to-PNC-to-MB continuum. Annotation of these DARs revealed that the epigenome progressively acquired a proliferative signature, consistent with the transition from a developmental to a tumorigenic state. Notably, tumor-specific DARs were highly enriched in gene ontology terms associated with cerebellar development, suggesting that chromatin remodeling during tumorigenesis may favor a transcriptional environment conducive to oncogenesis. While conventional downstream analyses failed to fully capture the complexity of regulatory element dynamics, OmiDos revealed a notable trend: The proportion of distal regulatory elements decreased along the GNP-to-PNC-to-MB trajectory (Fig. 6C). Moreover, this shift was accompanied by dynamic changes in the distribution of these elements across distinct cellular subpopulations, highlighting regulatory heterogeneity that was previously unrecognized.

Additional support for these findings was provided by the dynamic variation in differential peak regions (Fig. 6D; FDR < 0.05, Log2FC > 1), where the number of differential peaks varied across the cellular continuum. Interestingly, similar patterns of regulatory imbalance have been observed in the Group 3 subtype of MB. In that context, structural alterations associated with enhancer hijacking—wherein distal genomic regions are repositioned near enhancers or superenhancers—have been shown to induce mutually exclusive overexpression of the transcriptional repressors GFI1 and GFI1B in approximately 15 to 20% of Group 3 MB cases, thereby contributing to tumorigenesis (44). To further dissect potential regulatory mechanisms specific to MBSHH, we extracted distal intergenic regions from each cell cluster and employed HOMER to identify enriched transcription factor motifs within accessible chromatin (Fig. 6E). A particularly notable result was the significant enrichment of the Neurod1 motif (P-value = 1e-18), a member of the Neurod family of basic helix–loop–helix (bHLH) transcription factors, consistently observed throughout the transition from GNPs to MB cells. Pseudotime analysis further revealed a progressive decline in Neurod1 RNA expression along this trajectory (Fig. 6 G and H). While the original study reported enrichment of multiple bHLH family members, the prominence of Neurod1 was not explicitly emphasized, despite its ranking as the second most enriched motif among known tumor-associated motifs. Intriguingly, chromatin dysregulation of Neurod1 has been implicated as a potential marker of MB aggressiveness, underscoring its relevance in tumor progression (43).

Hi-C interaction analysis identified multiple distal regulatory elements that may influence Neurod1 expression. Among nine candidate enhancers, one proximal element coincided with a known regulatory region previously implicated in Neurod1 control. More strikingly, four distal elements demonstrated even stronger expression correlations and exhibited heightened chromatin accessibility in differentiated tumor subpopulations (C2) relative to proliferative clusters (C3-C7). These patterns suggest that distal enhancer activity is linked to the acquisition of differentiated characteristics during tumor progression. The biological relevance of these elements is further supported by studies showing that deletion of homologous regions leads to marked reductions in Neurod1 expression in other neuronal contexts, such as retinal cells (44). In addition, OmiDos identified a chromatin accessibility peak near the Neurod1 promoter that was specific to GNPs and PNCs, and whose signal declined in fully transformed tumor cells (cor = 0.6, FDR = 7.06e-48). These results collectively indicate that Neurod1 is governed by a dynamic enhancer network, modulated in a lineage- and differentiation-dependent manner during MBSHH evolution.

To investigate whether Neurod1 is integrated within a broader, previously uncharacterized regulatory network, we conducted motif enrichment analysis of this specific peak. Stat2 motifs were significantly enriched at this locus. Prior studies using GFAP-IFNα transgenic mice have demonstrated that Stat1 and Stat2 play crucial roles in IFN-α signaling. Notably, loss of either Stat1 or Stat2 exacerbates neurological disease, implicating alternative signaling pathways. For instance, in the absence of Stat1, IFN-α-mediated neuropathology may be driven through Stat2/Brg-1-dependent mechanisms, whereas Stat2 deficiency may induce IFN-γ production via IL-12-like signaling, potentially modulating SHH activity and promoting medulloblastoma development (Cytokine-mediated inflammation, tumorigenesis, and disease-associated JAK/STAT/SOCS signaling circuits in the CNS) (45). Based on these observations, we hypothesize that chromatin dysregulation of Neurod1, a hallmark of MB aggressiveness, may be linked to Stat2 deficiency, as evidenced by the closure of Stat2-associated distal regulatory regions. This phenomenon may also contribute to the observed reduction in the number of distal regulatory elements during the transition from normal to malignant states.

In summary, our study uncovers a regulatory mechanism in MBSHH progression, wherein Stat2-associated chromatin dynamics appear to influence Neurod1 expression and tumor aggressiveness. The progressive loss of distal regulatory elements and the epigenomic reprogramming identified here provide insights into the molecular events driving malignant transformation in MBSHH. These findings highlight potential therapeutic targets within the chromatin regulatory landscape that warrant further investigation.

Conclusions

Characterizing gene expression and regulatory dynamics underlying both normal tissue function and disease progression requires integrative modeling of multi-omics single-cell data. In this study, we introduce OmiDos (Omics Separation Modeling using Domain Adaptation), a flexible, annotation-free deep learning framework that disentangles omics-specific and interomic shared representations through private-shared component analysis. Its modular architecture supports the incorporation of adversarial learning to address unpaired data misalignment and MMD regularization to mitigate batch effects while preserving biological variability. We comprehensively benchmarked OmiDos across diverse human and mouse datasets spanning different platforms and tissue types. OmiDos demonstrated consistently superior performance in clustering accuracy, batch-effect correction, and modality misalignment resolution, as evidenced by elevated NMI, ARI, Batch-kBET, Batch-ASW, and GC scores. Through disentangling shared and private signals, OmiDos enhances the resolution of cellular heterogeneity and enables robust cross-modal data integration.

Importantly, OmiDos uncovered biologically meaningful insights in developmental and disease contexts. In mouse secondary palate development, it identified a cell type–specific, distal enhancer regulating Muc4, elucidating its role in epithelial cell differentiation and migration. In medulloblastoma, OmiDos revealed that insufficient closure of a distal enhancer region linked to Neurod1 may underlie the progression from normal to tumor states, providing mechanistic insight into epigenetic reprogramming in cancer. Taken together, these findings establish OmiDos as a powerful tool for multi-omics data integration, addressing key challenges such as batch correction, modality-specific biases, and regulatory element identification. By effectively disentangling shared and private representations, OmiDos enhances the resolution of cellular heterogeneity and facilitates the discovery of biologically relevant regulatory elements.

Materials and Methods

The Overall Network Architecture of OmiDos.

OmiDos, inspired by domain adaptation principles (19), features a comprehensive architecture designed to differentiate and integrate biological data across modalities. The system comprises five principal components: three encoders, two decoders, and a modality classification model, structured to segregate and process shared and modality-specific biological features effectively. The core of the architecture is the shared encoder, denoted as Es(xn,m; ωs). This encoder is parameterized by ωs and is responsible for transforming input data from both RNA and ATAC modalities into a unified representation zs. This shared representation encapsulates common features pertinent to both modalities, facilitating their integration. Complementing the shared encoder are two modality-private encoders designed to capture unique characteristics of each data type. The RNA-private encoder, Erna(xn,rna; ωrna) and the ATAC-private encoder Eatac(xn,atac; ωatac), are tailored to manage the distinct aspects of RNA and ATAC data, respectively. These encoders are specially regulated to ensure that the private representations they generate do not overlap with the shared representation, maintaining the integrity of modality-specific features.

Then, the architecture also includes two decoders that are responsible for reconstructing the original data from their respective encoded states. The RNA decoder, Drna(zrna; ω^rna), focuses on RNA data reconstruction, whereas the ATAC decoder, Datac(zatac; ω^atac), is dedicated to reconstructing ATAC data. Finally, the modality identification model, G(zm; ωg), plays a critical role in identifying the modality of the data represented by zs. Implemented as a fully connected layer, this model directly maps shared latent representations to modality-specific predictions, determining whether they correspond to RNA or ATAC inputs.

OmiDos Training.

During the training of the OmiDos model, we define distinct loss functions for each modality, as well as for the shared and private representations. These loss functions are specifically designed to optimize the accurate reconstruction of the modality-specific data, and ensure effective regularization of both shared and private representations (SI Appendix).

To model the characteristics of scRNA-seq data, we use a ZINB distribution, which effectively accounts for the dropout events that are frequently observed in scRNA-seq. The ZINB model is parameterized by the mean μ, dispersion θ, and dropout probability π. The key idea is to define the reconstruction error as the likelihood of the ZINB distribution of the model, rather than reconstructing the input data itself. During reconstruction, the ZINB likelihood is defined strictly over integer-valued counts xn,rnacount (SI Appendix, Figs. S62 and S63). This modeling strategy is consistent with widely used ZINB model-based deep learning frameworks for single-cell analysis (15, 46).

To capture the characteristics of scATAC-seq data, we adopted a generative approach that combines a variational autoencoder (VAE) with a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) (47). The ATAC input xn,atac is encoded into a latent variable z via reparameterization, which is subsequently used by the decoder to generate samples. This process follows a joint distribution p(x,z,c) where z is the latent variable and c is a categorical variable representing the component assignment of the data (SI Appendix).

To ensure effective modality separation, we impose an orthogonality constraint via a difference loss between shared and modality-private representations (19) (SI Appendix).

To further enhance the alignment of shared representations across modalities, we introduce a similarity constraint using the MMD loss, denoted as Lsimilarity. This loss encourages the hidden representations zsrna and zsatac, produced by the shared encoder, to be as similar as possible, regardless of their originating modality. Additionally, we introduce a modality identification loss, defined as the negative log-likelihood of predicting the modality of a given shared representation:[1]Lmodality=−∑i=1N∑c=1Cyi,clogy^i,c,

where yi,c is the one-hot encoding of the modality label, and yi,c is the softmax output of the modality classification model G(zm; ωg). Lmodality supervises a lightweight modality classifier attached to the shared encoder. Crucially, this term is not used adversarially and does not aim to enforce modality separability. Excessively strong distribution alignment can produce overly aligned or degenerate latent representations that suppress informative biological variation (48, 49). To prevent such trivial alignment, Lmodality serves as a mild auxiliary regularizer that retains only the minimal and necessary amount of modality-discriminative signal. In this way, it helps maintain an appropriately expressive latent space without competing with the MMD objective. The MMD loss remains the principal driver of cross-modality alignment, while the small influence of Lmodality ensures that OmiDos achieves modality-invariant integration while still preserving biologically meaningful variation (SI Appendix, Fig. S64).

Although modality labels remain informative, the guidance they provide becomes substantially weaker in unpaired settings, making them insufficient to preserve the underlying biological structure in the shared latent space. Therefore, we adopt a Noise Contrastive Estimation (NCE) loss (50), denoted as LNCE:[2]LNCE=−logexpsim(zi,zj)/τ∑k=12Nexpsim(zi,zk)/τ,

where sim denotes cosine similarity, and zi and zj form a positive pair generated by applying two independent augmentations to the same input sample. In unpaired multi-omics settings, these augmentations are applied within each modality, ensuring that contrastive supervision does not rely on unavailable cross-modal pairing information. Importantly, LNCE does not attempt to predict modality labels. Instead, it follows the standard contrastive-learning principle by pulling together augmented views of the same biological signal while pushing apart unrelated samples. This allows the shared encoder to learn structure-preserving representations, which provides a more appropriate constraint for unpaired integration than Lmodality. A discriminator network GNCE (50) is attached to the shared encoder Es(xn,m; ωs) and trained using an adversarial domain adaptation loss LG. The goal of GNCE is to distinguish the modality source of the latent space z extracted by Es(xn,m; ωs) (SI Appendix, Fig. S65).

To mitigate batch effects, OmiDos integrates batch information b into the network following the Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE) framework (51). LCVAE is redefined as the negative conditional ELBO:[3]LCVAE(x,b)=−Eq(z∣x,b)[logp(x∣z,b)]+KLq(z∣x,b) ‖ p(z∣b)

In theory, the encoder of a CVAE tends to learn a latent representation z that is relatively insensitive to batch-related variation because of the conditioning mechanism. However, in single-cell multi-omics datasets, this deconditioning effect is often incomplete, and the latent space typically retains substantial batch-related residual signals that can interfere with downstream analyses. Applying an MMD penalty directly to the latent space can reduce these residual batch differences, but remains insufficient to fully eliminate their downstream impact (52). In contrast, the first layer of the CVAE decoder receives both the latent representation and the batch information as inputs, making it the point where latent features and batch-related signals are jointly integrated. Applying the MMD penalty at the decoder therefore provides a more direct and structurally informed means of constraining batch effects, making it more effective for correcting systematic differences across batches (SI Appendix, Fig. S66). By incorporating an MMD term, the batch loss is defined as:[4]Lbatch=LCVAE+βLMMD.

Selection of Hyperparameters.

OmiDos involves seven loss terms (Lmodality, Lrecon, Lorthogonality, Lsimilarity, LNCE, LG, and Lbatch). Because manual hyperparameter tuning is highly nontrivial in this coupled optimization landscape, we employed a Genetic Algorithm (GA) (53), implemented via the DEAP toolkit (54), to identify a stable and robust configuration (SI Appendix).

Through this automated GA search, we obtained an optimal hyperparameter configuration (λLmodality=0.2, λLrecon=1.1, λLorthogonality=0.7, and λLsimilarity=1.0). To evaluate generalizability, this configuration was fixed and assessed across 21 multi-omics benchmark datasets. As shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S67 C and D, all datasets achieved consistently high NMI scores and well-separated UMAP embeddings, demonstrating strong cross-dataset robustness. Thus, this configuration was used for all paired multi-omics experiments. For paired batch-correction tasks, the paired-related hyperparameters remained fixed, and the batch-specific coefficient for Lbatch was optimized using GA on the BMMC dataset. This yielded αLbatch=0.01. Applying the paired-setting hyperparameters to unpaired integration resulted in severe performance drop, confirming that unpaired integration requires a distinct configuration. A separate GA search was therefore conducted on the SHARE-seq dataset, producing the following unpaired optimal parameters: (βLNCE=0.2, βLG=0.3, βLrecon=0.8, βLorthogonality=0.7, and βLsimilarity=1.0).

Selection of MMD Bandwidth.

Because MMD is highly sensitive to the choice of kernel bandwidth, we systematically evaluated both single-bandwidth and multi-bandwidth settings. We defined 19 individual RBF bandwidths spanning 10−6 to 106, and constructed 5 multi-bandwidth sets to assess the performance of the multi-kernel MMD strategy (19). The multi-kernel MMD was implemented as a linear combination of multiple RBF kernels, κ(xi,xj)=∑nexp −12σn2‖xi−xj‖2, where σn denotes the SD (SI Appendix).

These results (SI Appendix, Fig. S68) indicate that the full-scale multi-kernel MMD configuration provides the most stable and scale-invariant bandwidth choice for OmiDos. Accordingly, we adopted the following multiscale bandwidth set for all experiments: σn= [10−6, 10−5, 10−4, 10−3, 10−2, 10−1, 1, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 100, 103, 104, 105, 106].

Evaluation Metrics.

We evaluate the performance of OmiDos using complementary metrics that assess clustering accuracy, batch correction, and structure preservation. Specifically, we use NMI and ARI to quantify agreement between predicted and true cell-type labels. We assess batch correction using batch ASW and the kBET, and we evaluate global manifold continuity using GC (SI Appendix).

Implementation Details and Comparisons with Baseline Methods.

To comprehensively assess the effectiveness of OmiDos, we compared it against several established approaches. Each method was implemented following the parameter configurations specified in the respective original publications (SI Appendix).

Downstream Analyses for Biological Interpretation.

To interpret the integrated representations, we performed a standard suite of downstream analyses, including differential gene expression, functional enrichment, differential chromatin accessibility, peak-to-gene linkage and motif-related analyses, as well as lineage and pseudotime inference with lineage-dependent gene identification (SI Appendix).
