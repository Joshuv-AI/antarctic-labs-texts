Converting From Paper to Electronic Charting for a Nurse‐Led Programme Serving High‐Risk Postpartum Women and Infants in Malawi
Authors: Joanne Chiwaula, Marcianna Nosek, Glory Mwafulirwa‐Kabaghe, Emmanuel Chimwezi, Dehlia Ramos‐Gonzalez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionGovernmental and non‐governmental organisations in low‐income countries have transitioned from paper data collection to electronic data collection at a slower pace than those in high‐income countries due to limitations of funding, internet stability and trained personnel, among other reasons. This paper chronicles the process employed by one small non‐governmental organisation in Malawi from the selection of a programme through implementation, describing challenges and successes along the way in order to facilitate the adoption process for NGOs in sub‐Saharan Africa or other low‐income countries.DiscussionUpgrading the data collection system presented a daunting challenge. Despite the significant learning curve for the entire team, the implementation phase proceeded smoothly. Although staff remained apprehensive, they also understood the need for change and fully invested in the process.ConclusionThe benefits of electronic charting outweigh the struggles involved in learning a new system. Key elements of the process that supported success were active engagement of users at all points during the transition, selection of a programme that was well‐suited for the size and needs of the NGO, and appropriate support from ancillary staff and outside experts.Keywords: data collection, electronic charting, infants, low‐income countries, Malawi, mobile apps, NGOs, postpartum, REDCap, sub‐Saharan Africa

Introduction

Over the past decade, the adoption of electronic data collection in health care and research has become nearly universal in high‐income countries. Although usage has also increased in low‐income countries, uptake has proceeded at a slower pace (Kumar and Mostafa 2019). Despite many benefits associated with electronic data collection, including improved data analysis, service delivery, monitoring and evaluation and secure data storage, issues such as unreliable or limited Wi‐Fi coverage, lack of trained personnel and general associated infrastructure costs have often posed seemingly insurmountable challenges (Akanbi et al. 2012). It is noteworthy that the benefits of electronic data collection are not limited to improved systems in individual organisations or governments but relate to general information sharing and its effects on development (World Health Organization [WHO] 2023). The disparate pace of usage between high‐ and low‐income countries contributes to disparities in research, as well as information sharing across and between sectors (Qureshi et al. 2021). These disparities not only impact patient care, epidemiology, health equity and progress towards the achievement of the Sustainable Development Goals but also the identification of and advocacy for development issues, grant availability and resource allocation (Koumamba et al. 2021).

The development of free open‐source data collection software and applications, which may be used offline, addressed many concerns faced by potential users in low‐ and middle‐income countries. Their release was followed by a corresponding increase in the use of electronic data collection (Anokwa et al. 2009). Like many organisations in Africa, Chimwemwe mu'bereki Association (CMA, translated Joyful Motherhood) (Chimwemwe mu'bereki Association (CMA) 2024), a Malawian nongovernmental organisation, experienced an increasing urgency to identify and adopt a system for improved data collection and data analysis. In 2020, the management of CMA, together with board members of sister US‐based nonprofit, African Mothers Health Initiative (AMHI) (African Mothers Health Initiative (AMHI) 2024) began a search for software that suited their context and data management needs. In December of 2021, the team selected Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) (“Research Electronic Data Capture [REDCap]” 2025) and began the process of building forms, training staff, and finally launched their new system in April of 2022. The aim of this paper is to share the process from selection of a mobile device through implementation, describing successes and lessons learned along the way in order to facilitate the adoption process for similar NGOs in sub‐Saharan Africa and other low‐income countries.

Background

In 2000, the under‐5 mortality rate for Malawi was 189 per 1000 live births, and the infant mortality rate was 104 per 1000 live births (National Statistical Office (NSO) and ICF 2000). In 2022, the under‐5 and infant mortality rates for Malawi were 40.1 and 30.1 per 1000 live births, respectively (United Nations Inter‐Agency Group for Child Mortality Estimation [UNIGME] 2024). Certain groups of infants have an increased risk of dying within their first year of life (potentially reaching 80% in sub‐Saharan Africa) (UNIGME 2024). These high‐risk infants include orphans, multiples (twins and triplets), and other infants without sufficient access to breast milk (e.g., infants with mothers who are critically ill) (Moucheraud et al. 2015). The majority of these infants are healthy at birth and have family members who are willing to raise them. However, due to an environment of extreme poverty, many deteriorate quickly and die within days to months. Similarly, while the maternal mortality ratio has dropped precipitously (from 749 per 100,000 live births in 2000 to 389 in 2020), the number of women experiencing severe maternal morbidities (SMMs) in sub‐Saharan Africa continues to eclipse the number of those who die during pregnancy, childbirth and the postpartum period and remains a significant issue of maternal health (Baykemagn et al. 2024). SMMs have been defined as specific pregnancy‐related illnesses/complications such as sepsis, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, haemorrhage, fistulas, ruptured uterus, etc. They may also be identified by periods of prolonged hospitalisation following delivery, emergent hysterectomy or ICU admissions (Geller et al. 2018).

SMMs often have significant long‐lasting consequences on the lives of women and their children, and in Malawi, many women do not receive even the routine recommended postpartum care (Say et al. 2004). These women are more likely to experience difficulty completing activities of daily living, suffer financial repercussions and depression and face an increased risk of mortality extending up to 1 year following the delivery (Pindani et al. 2020). Their young children also experience an increased risk of mortality (Ferreira et al. 2020). In response, CMA, a nurse‐run organisation, was founded in 2008 by a nurse midwife to provide targeted family‐based nursing care to women experiencing SMMs and high‐risk infants in Lilongwe, Malawi (CMA 2024). Currently, there are three Malawian nurses who provide intake and care out in the field, and the office is run by the programme manager, who is also a Malawian trained nurse.

CMA nurses provide health assessments, health education, nutrition support, community mobilisation and monthly cash stipends in communities generally located in rural areas (CMA 2024). Within a typical single calendar year, nurses visit patients in over 100 communities, the majority of which have limited or no Wi‐Fi access. From 2008 until April 2022, all documentation was paper‐based. Each enrolled client would have between 7 and 28 paper forms in their file (e.g., consent form, admission form, assessment form for each visit, emergency plan form and discharge form). From 2013 to date, CMA has averaged 170 new enrolments annually.

From 2008 to 2020, CMA made several unsuccessful attempts to facilitate data analysis by using Excel spreadsheets and Access. Initially, when nurses were trained to use Excel, there were several incidents when data were inadvertently deleted or rearranged. Data entry personnel were then hired to enter data from paper forms into these programmes. However, these personnel were not familiar with terms and abbreviations related to maternal and child health, which led to errors in the data. During these years, programme data were rudimentary and limited (e.g., admissions, deaths, discharges, number of visits conducted, number of food items distributed). Implementation Process

Mobile Application Selection

The team initially selected the Community Health Toolkit (CHT), an open‐source platform by Medic Mobile, as the preferred electronic data system. CHT was designed for maternal and infant care, supported offline use and could integrate into broader healthcare systems through SMS communication with field staff. However, it required coding for customisation and form development.

As CMA prepared to launch the system, it became evident that local technical support from skilled CHT technicians in Malawi would be necessary. While seeking assistance through the CHT community forum, it was suggested that CMA might benefit more from a simpler solution like REDCap. REDCap requires no coding for project or form creation, offers offline data entry through a mobile app with later syncing, has a user‐friendly interface and exports data easily to Excel, STATA and R. Ultimately, REDCap proved to be a better fit for CMA's needs.

REDCap, developed by Vanderbilt University, is a secure data collection and management platform offered free to nonprofit organisations (REDCap 2025). However, it requires users to have the infrastructure and technical staff to host it. CMA partnered with a Malawian member of the REDCap consortium, who agreed to host the project on their server. An AMHI board member (and second author) created REDCap forms guided by online resources and replicated CMA's paper forms electronically (CMA 2024). They also developed an instruction manual for nurses and office staff in Malawi. CMA upgraded its office computer, purchased basic Android tablets and installed the REDCap mobile app on each device. A key element of success was the inclusion of office staff and nurses' input prior to the launch, during the pilot stage and ensuring ongoing input. Informal interviews and focus groups were held with office staff and nurses, as applicable, to gather their input on concerns regarding the transition and use of the mobile application, the accuracy of the forms, and the ease of using the tablets and overall platform.

Ethics Statement

Transitioning from paper to electronic charting at CMA was a key component of its monitoring and evaluation plan. This process was classified as a quality improvement initiative rather than primary research; therefore, informed consent was not required. However, ethical approval was obtained from the University of San Francisco's Institutional Review Board (IRB Protocol #699) as part of CMA's ongoing monitoring and evaluation activities.

Training of Nurses & Office Staff

The created REDCap instruction manual included screenshots of the mobile app pages, login instructions, steps to navigate the different forms, and the process of uploading data to the server once connected to the internet. The Malawian REDCap consortium member conducted a 2‐day in‐person training for nurses and office staff, focusing on the nuances of the programme and its forms, as well as tablet usage. They also provided extended service and time for problem‐solving, adjustments and related discussions following the rollout. Extra time with pay was offered to nurses and staff for overall training and completion of the online data collection and management certifications.

At the time REDCap was rolled out, all three nurses had been working with CMA for nearly a decade. They were comfortable in their roles and with job expectations. While acknowledging the need for a new system, they expressed reservations about the plan to convert from paper to electronic charting. As nurses began using the forms, they provided input that ultimately led to improved specificity of data collected and ease of use. In addition, nurses reported glitches within the forms, which were subsequently addressed. To manage the time required for data entry, nurses only filled out forms directly on days when there were few visits. Other days, nurses documented key details in their diaries, which they later transferred to the tablets when in the office. Although this provided an added benefit of creating a written backup that could be referenced in the future, this new system introduced redundancy and raised privacy concerns. Ongoing Benefits and Challenges

Benefits of Monitoring and Evaluation

Following the pilot phase, CMA hired a part‐time data manager to assist with ongoing management of the programme and implementation of the monitoring and evaluation (M&E) plan. REDCap greatly facilitated the capacity to create reports using specified data sets for particular audiences. For example, in 2023, at a stakeholder's meeting with representatives from the District Maternity Hospital and other maternity wards within the district, CMA was able to share descriptive data on the enrolled women experiencing severe maternal morbidities (SMMs). CMA created charts breaking down the total number of women who had had hysterectomies following sepsis by delivery hospital, age and parity. As a result, meeting participants were able to visualise how their particular facility contributed to the SMM statistics as compared with other facilities, while also gaining an understanding of the personal impact on women served.

The development of an ‘At‐Risk Infant List’ illustrates another example of improvements following REDCap implementation. On a weekly basis, CMA's data manager uses REDCap to produce a list of all infants seen in the prior week, noting their three most recent weights. Any infant whose weight is unchanged or decreasing is added to the master ‘At‐Risk’ list. Every week, clinical staff review the list and discuss care plans for those infants. On a quarterly basis, all infants placed on the master list are reviewed, their weights from the past year are plotted on a growth curve and their cases are discussed by the team. This new process helps ensure that infants at risk of malnutrition are identified quickly and receive extra attention and individualised care.

Benefits and Challenges for Nurses and Office Staff

After 2 years of using REDCap, staff comfort and concerns regarding the programme were reassessed. All staff (nursing and office) agree that the benefits of electronic charting outweigh the struggles involved in learning a new system. Though nurses are now more comfortable with the electronic format of questions and prefer REDCap to paper forms, they continue to take field notes on paper. This redundant documentation adds time to data entry, and although it provides a backup reference, the transcription also introduces the risk of inaccurate or missing data. Contributions to Programme Quality Assessment and Improvement

To date, complete records for 550 CMA clients are stored in REDCap. The data manager reviews submissions as nurses upload forms, and CMA recently hired a second data specialist to support a comprehensive programme evaluation. Together, they identified inconsistencies in how certain questions were interpreted. For example, the question ‘Is immunization complete?’ was often taken to mean ‘Is immunization up to date?’ Similarly, binary questions, like ‘Normal? yes/no’, were sometimes misinterpreted, with ‘no’ used to indicate no problems instead of ‘yes’ for normal. They also found inconsistent formats in data entries, such as weights recorded as ‘2.5 kg’, ‘2500 g’ or ‘2500’. In response, the form was revised to accept only integer values. While these issues required extensive data cleaning, they underscored the importance of continuous communication between clinical and nonclinical staff to ensure data consistency and relevance.

In the infant care programme, survival remains the most critical outcome. Electronic data collection now enables real‐time tracking of infant weight and provides detailed data to tailor interventions both programme‐wide and individually. For instance, a spike in diarrheal illness in 2023 prompted increased hand hygiene education and distribution of soap, oral rehydration salts and zinc—resulting in improved survival rates. In the postpartum programme, access to electronic data revealed postpartum depression as a common symptom among women with severe maternal morbidities. In response, CMA is developing a more robust mental health support initiative.

Finally, the authors acknowledge that fragmented health information systems in low‐income countries hinder accurate national data collection (Koumamba et al. 2021). Although REDCap was chosen to meet CMA's internal needs, it has also improved data sharing. Regular meetings between CMA and District health officials revealed discrepancies in maternal death records. CMA often identifies maternal deaths through support requests for motherless infants—cases that may go unreported at the District level if they occur weeks after delivery or are not recognised as maternal deaths by families. As a small NGO, CMA recognises its responsibility to use collected data to advance maternal and child health in Malawi (Iyengar et al. 2012).

Discussion

Upgrading the data collection system at CMA presented a daunting challenge. Like many small organisations in low‐income countries, CMA's staff had limited computer experience, inconsistent and unstable Wi‐Fi connectivity and a limited budget. However, due to external pressure for improved reporting and a need for solutions to internal problems related to the paper‐based system, CMA committed to converting from paper to electronic charting. Despite the significant learning curve for the entire team, the implementation phase proceeded smoothly. Although staff remained apprehensive, they also understood the need for change and fully invested in the process. Additional essential elements that facilitated the successful transition include: the extensive support of the AMHI board member who secured funding; the on‐ground participation of the AMHI nurse‐educator; the training support from the Malawian REDCap specialist; the responsiveness of the data manager; the extra time and salary for nurses; the dedicated oversight of the programme manager; the leadership of the executive director; and the excellent communication between all parties involved in the transition. Within 3 months, all staff expressed appreciation for the new system.

As expected, REDCap addressed many of the data management issues previously faced by CMA. It improved data accessibility and analysis and eliminated storage issues, misfiling and potential privacy breaches due to lost files. Additionally, compared with other programmes, REDCap resulted in cost savings in that usage did not require an expert in programming and that CMA accessed a secure server managed by an outside organisation. Direct benefits impacting programmatic operations at CMA include improved tracking of individual patients' health as well as improved capacity to implement monitoring and evaluation initiatives.

CMA's success with REDCap is not surprising, as researchers in other low‐resource settings with limited internet access have reported similar outcomes (Ministry of Health 2015; Harris et al. 2019; Maré et al. 2022). Since its inception in 2004, REDCap has been used in 2.5 million projects across 164 countries (REDCap 2025). Harris et al. (2019) described this global network as the REDCap Consortium—a community of international administrators and organisations with expert software knowledge who support one another at no cost. This consortium was instrumental in CMA's implementation, as their initial contact in Malawi was a consortium member. In South Africa, researchers similarly reported a successful REDCap rollout in an academic‐healthcare institution, highlighting how easily users were trained despite resource constraints (Maré et al. 2022). Although their institution was significantly larger, with their efforts resulting in 3447 end users, they echoed CMA's experience in benefiting from hands‐on training and strong support teams within a low‐resource area (Maré et al. 2022). Furthermore, a key feature contributing to CMA's success was REDCap's mobile application, which allows offline data collection. Harris et al. (2021) explained how the introduction of this functionality in 2014 aimed to meet the need for a mobile device for field data collection without internet connection—a critical asset for CMA, where most of their nursing care takes place out in the field.

Conclusion

The transition to an electronic data system using REDCap resulted in substantial direct and indirect benefits for CMA. Improved data analysis capabilities have enhanced CMA's ability to measure programme impact, refine interventions and demonstrate the cost‐effectiveness of its services. This data‐driven approach supports better outcomes and contributes to CMA's financial sustainability by increasing donor confidence and engagement. CMA is committed to sharing REDCap‐generated data with the Ministry of Health and regional health facilities, fostering collaboration and enabling local stakeholders to use the information for their own programme improvements. The system tracks detailed information such as the location and causes of maternal deaths and severe morbidities, offering valuable insights for targeted interventions.

Most importantly, CMA addresses health issues that are widespread across sub‐Saharan Africa—such as high infant mortality and severe maternal complications—that currently receive limited focused attention. Having a modern data system allows CMA to generate evidence that supports an effective model of care, thereby strengthening its position in national and international health dialogues. This evidence base not only improves internal decision‐making but also enables CMA to advocate for change and share its model with the global health community.
