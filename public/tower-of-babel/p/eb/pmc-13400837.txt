Theoretical and practical challenges for capturing reserve and resilience in aging and Alzheimer's disease: A narrative review
Authors: Valentin Ourry, Maude Wagner, Colin Groot, Rory Boyle, Jacob W Vogel, David Bartrés‐Faz, Sylvia Villeneuve
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe concepts of reserve and resilience in aging and Alzheimer's disease (AD) are widely used but their operationalization remains challenging and often lacks conceptual and methodological consistency. In this narrative review, we describe the chronological history of these concepts and then summarize and discuss key theoretical and practical issues, along with proposed solutions and different perspectives, in the context of aging and AD. Theoretical challenges include risk versus protective factors, dynamic brain and cognitive trajectories, overestimation of resilience and inflection points. Practical challenges include proxy measures, study designs, residual approaches, and influential cases. We highlight the need for greater methodological harmonization, improved validation of proxy, and lifespan longitudinal approaches. Addressing these challenges will strengthen interpretability, reproducibility, and causal inference, ultimately facilitating the translation of reserve and resilience research into preventive and therapeutic strategies.Keywords: aging, Alzheimer's disease, modeling, Reserve, resilience, theoretical and practical challenges

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the leading cause of dementia, with its worldwide prevalence projected to triple in 2050. 1 Although clinical trials for AD and dementia have shown promising yet limited success, improved understanding of disease pathophysiology has led to an increased emphasis on dementia prevention and a shift toward personalized approaches. Approximately 45% of AD dementia risk is attributable to modifiable risk factors. 2 In AD, the accumulation of amyloid‐β and tau deposits–the main pathological hallmarks of AD–can be measured in vivo using positron emission tomography (PET) or fluid biomarkers, and can be quantified post mortem using immunohistochemistry. These proteinopathies are known to accumulate decades before cognitive symptoms. 3 Modifiable risk factors may directly influence AD pathology level and accumulation over time, or may modulate its impact on cognition through moderation, presenting a significant opportunity for AD prevention. 4 , 5 Note that moderation captures context‐dependent variation in effect size while mediation captures pathways of effect. Recent two‐year multidomain lifestyle interventions — combining physical and cognitive activity, healthy diet, social engagement and cardiovascular monitoring — have demonstrated robust benefits on global cognition in 2111 older adults. 6 These factors support and nurture the brain throughout the lifespan, potentially modifying the brain's tolerance to aging and pathological burden. 7

Definitions of the concepts of reserve and resilience (BOX A and next section) have been refined over the years through continued research and collaborative workshops. 8 Briefly, reserve is more general and defined as a heuristic to explain individual differences in cognition, function, or clinical status in the face of ageing and brain disease. 9 Meanwhile, resilience is a brain's ability to cope with accumulating senescent and pathologic changes, thereby preserving brain integrity or cognitive function in the face of significant senescence or AD pathologic burden 10 , 11 (BOX A and next section). Resilience effects can be broadly categorized into static and dynamic effects. Static effects refer to better premorbid performance or brain capital that contributes to beneficial outcomes in the presence of pathology. In contrast, dynamic effects are reactionary and activate only when pathology manifests. 9 , 12

BOX A: Definitions

Consensus definitions:

Reserve: A heuristic to explain individual differences in cognition, function or clinical status in the face of ageing and brain disease (Stern et al., 2020).

Brain reserve: Brain reserve has been used to reflect the neurobiological status of the brain (numbers of neurons, synapses, etc.) at any point in time. Brain reserve does not involve active adaptation of functional cognitive processes in the presence of injury or disease as does cognitive reserve (https://reserveandresilience.com/framework/).

Cognitive reserve: Cognitive reserve is a property of the brain that allows for cognitive performance that is better than expected given the degree of life‐course related brain changes and brain injury or disease (https://reserveandresilience.com/framework/).

Brain maintenance: Brain maintenance refers to the relative absence of changes in neural resources or neuropathologic change over time as a determinant of preserved cognition in older age (https://reserveandresilience.com/framework/).

Resistance: Brain's ability to avoid age‐related senescent and pathologic changes, thereby preserving brain integrity and cognition, despite risk factors such as advanced age or genetic predisposition to Alzheimer disease pathology (Arenaza and Vemuri, 2018; Bocancea et al., 2021). It is important to be precise and specify what resistance refers to (e.g., resistance to amyloid, tau or atrophy…).

Resilience: Brain's ability to cope with accumulating senescent and pathologic changes, thereby preserving brain integrity (brain resilience) or cognitive function (cognitive resilience) in the face of significant senescence or Alzheimer's disease pathologic burden (Arenaza and Vemuri, 2018; Bocancea et al., 2021). It is important to be precise and specify what resilience refers to (e.g., resilience to amyloid, tau or atrophy…).

Other terminologies:

Preserved differentiation: Differences of dynamic lifespan outcome between two groups or individuals where the levels of the outcome are different and their trajectories are similar (≠intercept, = slope), consistent with brain reserve (Walhovd et al., 2023).

Differential preservation: Differences of dynamic lifespan outcome between two groups or individuals where the levels of the outcome are similar and their trajectories are different (= intercept, ≠slope), consistent with brain maintenance (Walhovd et al., 2023).

Enhanced differentiation: Differences of dynamic lifespan outcome between two groups or individuals where the levels of the outcome and their trajectories are different (≠intercept, ≠slope). The difference in trajectory increases, consistent with the Matthew principle (Walhovd et al., 2023).

Reduced differentiation: Differences of dynamic lifespan outcome between two groups or individuals where the levels of the outcome and their trajectories are different (≠intercept, ≠slope). The difference in trajectory decreases, consistent with “developmental catch‐up” (Walhovd et al., 2023).

Ceiling effect: The ceiling effect refers to a situation where the score of a measure reaches the upper possible limit, preventing the detection of further variations and limiting the sensitivity of the measurement.

Proxy: A proxy is a surrogate variable or measure used to represent another variable or concept that is difficult to measure directly. For example, years of education is a commonly used proxy of cognitive reserve.

Residuals: Residuals are the differences between observed and predicted outcome variables for an individual relative to the population estimate from a model. Individuals with better outcome than expected are considered resistant/resilient, depending on the variables included in the model (Bocancea et al., 2021).

Class‐specific trajectory: A class‐specific trajectory refers to output from models, such as growth curve models or latent class mixed models, that reflects a distinct pattern of change in a variable over time in a specific subgroup of a population.

Inflection point: An inflection point is a moment in a trajectory when the slope changes direction. For example, an older adult with high reserve may maintain stable cognitive performance or daily functioning for a certain period, after which a decline begins; this turning point represents the inflection point.

BOX C: Opening questions and challenges

The distinction between cognitive reserve and cognitive resilience remains unclear, and reserve is sometimes conceptualized both as a factor and a process/mechanism, which can be challenging for the definition and interpretation.

Recent data suggest that high education is not as protective as low education is risky, raising the question of where the threshold between risk and protection should be set.

In preclinical research, it is essential to distinguish neuropsychological tests designed for detecting daily‐life functional impairment in clinical diagnosis from those targeting subtle cognitive changes more commonly used in research, while emphasizing the need for more challenging and variable tasks to capture early changes in preclinical Alzheimer's disease or dementia.

Is there truly an inflection point, or could it be an artifact depending on the cognitive outcome? Further analyses and data exploration are warranted at this point.

Over the past two decades, interest in reserve and resilience has risen, and this growth in interest has been accompanied by the proliferation of open‐access data that facilitate their investigation. 13 However, modeling and operationalizing reserve and resilience remains challenging because these theoretical concepts and their methodologies are often applied inconsistently across studies. 14 It is important to be aware of the current limitations, issues, and solutions in the study of reserve and resilience — ranging from theory to practice — to avoid inappropriate study designs, modeling, or interpretation. A better understanding of these challenges is essential to advance the field.

In this narrative review, we first briefly describe the chronological history of the concepts of reserve and resilience. We then summarize the theoretical and practical issues related to capturing and modeling these concepts, and discuss proposed solutions and perspectives in the context of aging and AD. We believe that this short review — from the subjective point of view of some experts — will help improve the quality and reproducibility of studies in the reserve and resilience field.

CHRONOLOGICAL HISTORY OF RESERVE AND RESILIENCE

Two decades apart, a pair of studies observed post mortem brain lesions due to AD in older adults who were cognitively unimpaired proximate to death. 15 , 16 These two studies were among the first to contribute to what eventually became known as the reserve hypothesis. According to this hypothesis, these individuals had a high neural reserve — a greater brain volume and a higher number of neurons — allowing them to withstand significant damage that would normally lead to cognitive impairment. These observations led to the concept of brain reserve 17 (BOX A and Figure 1) and have been corroborated by many studies, notably the Nun Study of Aging and AD 18 and the Religious Order Study. 19 This concept focuses on brain substrates (e.g., number of neurons, synapses) and has often been considered as a passive form of reserve. While brain reserve refers to neurobiological capital, fixed at any given time, brain maintenance (BOX A and Figure 1) refers to the relative absence of changes in neural resources or neuropathologic change over time as a determinant of preserved cognition in older age. In other words, it is modifiable throughout life and shaped by experience, allowing for reduced development or advancing brain changes related to age or neuropathology. 20 Since the 1990s, several studies have also highlighted an active form of reserve, 21 , 22 , 23 which would subsequently be termed cognitive reserve 24 (CR; BOX A and Figure 1). CR is a dynamic process and is based on the recruitment of effective strategies and/or compensatory processes of neural networks in response to a cognitive task. 25 , 26

More recently, a working group composed of several international experts defined the general term reserve as “a heuristic to explain individual differences in cognition, function or clinical status in the face of ageing and brain disease” 9 (BOX A and Figure 1). Reserve can therefore be conceptualized as a buffer between neuropathology and the onset of brain lesions, or between those brain lesions and their clinical expression. However, although a consensus definition of the terms described above (reserve and maintenance) has been proposed, 9 research groups studying reserve and resilience still use and discuss multiple theoretical concepts and methodological approaches, 14 and efforts to refine these constructs are ongoing. 8 , 12 , 27 , 28 In the context of AD, and complementary to the reserve concept, two concrete mechanistic models have been proposed: Resistance and resilience 10 , 29 (BOX A and Figure 1). Resistance refers to the ability to avoid or limit the accumulation of age‐related brain changes or AD pathology in individuals who are at high risk of AD (e.g., individuals with advanced age, apolipoprotein E (APOE) ‐ε4 carriers, or AD family history), suggesting that pathological processes have been delayed or prevented (BOX A). In contrast, resilience refers to the ability, either cognitive or brain‐related, to maintain brain integrity (brain resilience) or cognitive performance (cognitive resilience) despite the presence of brain age‐related changes or AD pathology (BOX A). In other words, resistance prevents the development of pathology, whereas resilience mitigates its impact once pathology has accumulated. Note that the concepts of reserve and resilience were first developed in the context of AD, but have since been extended to non‐AD neurodegenerative diseases 30 and aging.

Building on these conceptual frameworks, Walhovd et al. (2023) recently proposed a comprehensive data‐driven model describing the lifespan dynamics of brain and cognition 31 (Figure 1). This framework emphasized that individual differences in brain and cognition observed over the lifespan could be more strongly influenced by early‐life factors, such as genetics, prenatal environment, and early development, than by later factors in life. More comprehensive modeling is needed to better capture these longitudinal individual differences which is one of the aims of the present review.

THEORETICAL ISSUES AND SOLUTIONS

Risk versus protective effect

A first conceptual challenge in defining reserve lies in the fact that it is likely influenced by both risk and protective factors, which complicates the interpretation of observed associations with AD or dementia‐related outcomes. Distinguishing whether these associations primarily reflect vulnerability at low levels of reserve or protective effects at high levels of reserve therefore remains difficult (Figure 2A). This distinction is particularly relevant when considering proxies of reserve. For instance, several studies using education, one of the most common proxies, have reported that low educational attainment is associated with an increased risk of AD dementia, 2 , 32 , 33 whereas high educational attainment is associated with a reduced risk of AD dementia in some studies 25 , 34 , 35 but not in others. 35 , 36 Note that the association between education and AD dementia risk, sometimes not found, could vary across studies, partly due to differences in life expectancy or population characteristics. 37 Longitudinal studies have provided evidence that a high level of education, compared to low level of education, does influence the brain or cognition from early life (i.e., higher offset or intercept), but does not necessarily continuously influence the brain or cognition over time (i.e., no difference on slope or trajectory). 35 , 36 , 38 , 39 , 40 This raises the empirical question of whether the effect associated with low level of education as a risk factor for AD is more important than the effect of high level of education as a protective factor against AD. Beyond education and socioeconomic status (SES), the protective effects of reserve may vary depending on non‐modifiable individual characteristics, such as genetic risk or sex. For instance, the benefits of reserve may be especially pronounced in individuals who are APOE‐ε4 carriers 41 or in women, 42 suggesting that reserve (mainly education in these studies) may interact with underlying risk factors and buffer their deleterious effects. This could have direct implications for interventions aiming to build or maintain reserve: such interventions may be particularly effective in high‐risk individuals but have limited or no additional benefit in low‐risk populations. This distinction between risk and protective factors may appear trivial or semantic, since mitigating risk is inherently protective; however, reducing risk factors or enhancing protective factors may not yield effects of the same magnitude, nor target the same population depending on the reference norms used (Figure 2B and 2C).

One of the solutions to estimate the relative strength between risk and protective effects requires an appropriate reference category (e.g., average education based on the general population), although this varies across populations and depends on recruitment feasibility. Indeed, average educational norms differ substantially across populations, with approximately 8.7 years in the world population, 43 13.2 years in U.S. older adults, 44 3.3 years in India and 5.5 years in Mexico older adults, 45 and 16.1 years in Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) participants. 46 Note that ranking approaches (e.g., quartiles or quantiles) effectively capture relative differences within populations; however, they limit comparability across populations, as the same rank may correspond to substantially different absolute levels of education. This discrepancy, combined with the overrepresentation of highly educated individuals in research samples, complicates the understanding and characterization of individuals with low educational attainment and could be responsible for the asymmetry between risk and protective factors. Individuals with a low level of education or SES are often underrepresented in study samples, particularly in clinical trials, 47 highlighting the need to increase the representativeness of clinical trials and study samples by including individuals with low educational attainment and from lower SES backgrounds. Furthermore, interventions focusing on individuals at higher risk (e.g., those with a low level of education) may yield larger individual effects due to greater potential for improvement although some future dementia cases may emerge outside the targeted high‐risk population. 48 Moreover, a well‐balanced recruitment strategy — ensuring adequate representation of both low and high levels of reserve proxies — could reduce asymmetry in effect size estimates by providing a more symmetric distribution around the reference category.

In this framework, reserve may appear to be less effective as a universally protective trait than as modulator of vulnerability, meaning that it alters the strength of the relationship between underlying risk factors (e.g., pathology or genetic risk) and clinical outcomes. However, the distinction between risk and protective effects requires further study using more appropriate reference categories that are representative of global or country‐specific population. Taken together, delineating risk and protective effects would influence not only scientific interpretations, but also clinical interventions regarding whom to target.

Dynamic brain and cognitive trajectories

Understanding how brain and cognition can change over time is essential to capture reserve and resilience appropriately. To model the brain and cognitive trajectories, we usually apply statistical approaches for longitudinal data where the intercept parameter represents the initial level of the dependent variable (e.g., cognitive performance or brain volume) at a reference time point (often baseline or a specific age), and where the slope parameter indicates how fast the outcome increases or decreases over time. According to Walhovd et al., 2023, 31 there are theoretically 4 types of possible trajectories: (1) preserved differentiation (Figure 2D; ≠intercept, = slope), (2) differential preservation (Figure 2E; = intercept, ≠slope), (3) reduced or (4) enhanced differentiation (Figures 2F and 2G; ≠intercept, ≠slope) (BOX A).

In practice, accurately capturing “true” cognitive and brain aging trajectories is challenging, as it depends on both the modeling approach and the data used. Several key aspects should be carefully considered to obtain more realistic trajectories: using non‐linear models, having more than two time points or a broad age range, and limiting cognitive tests with ceiling or floor effects. The largest brain chart study to date, including magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans from 101,457 human participants with an age range spanning from 115 days post‐conception to 100 years, demonstrated that brain structure follows a non‐linear trajectory over time. 49 About cognition, two large studies — one including 170,795 participants aged over 50 years from 33 Western countries followed for up to 28 years, 39 and another including 51,280 participants aged 12 to 105 years from the U.S. population followed for over two decades 50 — showed that cognition follows a non‐linear trajectory over time. Note that the theoretical framework of reserve proposed by Stern 25 aims to explain interindividual differences in the relationship between brain pathology and cognitive outcomes through active and passive mechanisms while the descriptive data‐driven framework proposed by Walhovd 31 distinguishes patterns of interindividual differences in terms of level (intercept) and change (slope). These two frameworks reflect complementary approaches: one mechanistic and explanatory, mainly informed by functional MRI (fMRI) and neuropsychological studies, and the other descriptive, primarily based on structural MRI studies. In the next section, we will see how resilience can be overestimated with reduced age range and ceiling effect.

Overestimation of resilience

A reduced age range and ceiling effect of cognitive tests, combined with the use of linear models, could lead to an overestimation of resilience, that is, a spurious difference on slope. For instance, starting from a theoretical preserved differentiation scenario (Figure 2H), in which no slope difference is observed, and trajectories remain parallel between individuals with high versus low reserve/resilience, restricting the age range (Figure 2I) and the presence of ceiling effect (Figure 2J) can artificially create slope differences, that is, non‐parallel trajectories. To mitigate such overestimation, future studies should adopt a lifespan approach, a well‐balanced recruitment (see section risk versus protective effect above), and develop more challenging and variable cognitive tests capable of capturing earlier cognitive changes beyond daily functioning impairment in preclinical AD or dementia.

Reduced age range

A reduced age range, compared to the full lifespan, combined with the use of linear models, limits the capture of the true trajectory of brain features or cognition. 31 In a preserved differentiation scenario (Figure 2H), a model based on a reduced age range (e.g., from tx ; Figure 2I) may be unable to distinguish preserved differentiation from differential preservation. Worse, a linear model in this situation can potentially lead to spurious group‐by‐age interactions, that do not reflect the actual lifespan trajectories, which are non‐linear and parallel (Figure 2H). An overestimation of resilience can arise because a linear model imposes a constant slope. If two groups (e.g., high vs. low reserve) follow the same non‐linear trajectory but differ in their intercepts, at any tx, their respective slope or tangent can differ (Figure 2I). This difference in slopes will be captured by the linear model as a group‐by‐age interaction, even though the global trajectories are parallel. This statistical artifact can easily lead to the false conclusion that one group shows slower cognitive decline than the other, when in fact they follow the same non‐linear pattern throughout their lifespan.

The ideal way to address the above issues is through the collection of lifespan longitudinal data, which enables to capture variability that emerge across the entire lifespan by estimating non‐linear change in brain structure and cognition over time. Several cohorts illustrate this approach such as Lifebrain, 51 CARDIA 52 or Lothian Birth cohort. 53 The minimum timeframe over which non‐linear changes can be detected at the individual level is an important consideration. It is likely that many years are required before non‐linear trajectories become evident. Furthermore, collecting at least three time points per participant is essential for detecting non‐linear individual patterns. Note that time of follow‐up is statistically more important than number of observations to detect a change in structural MRI. 54 To reduce the risk of overestimating resilience in a more classical datasets with restricted age range (i.e., non‐lifespan approach), we recommend that future studies adopt non‐linear modeling approaches, such as generalized additive models 55 or other mixed models (see Longitudinal approaches sub‐section), which better capture the curvature of brain or cognitive trajectories over time. Life‐span approach also refers to including younger and middle‐aged participants, not only older adults, in cross‐sectional designs. Therefore, it is possible to appreciate non‐linear trajectories without longitudinal data by including participants across a broader age range (i.e., including young and intermediate adults), 49 although resilience can be assessed at the group level but not at the individual level with this design.

Ceiling effect

In the context of preclinical AD or dementia, major advances have been made over the past two decades in the development of neuroimaging and fluid biomarkers to detect early pathological changes. Some recent composite scores sensitive to subtle early changes have been introduced, such as the Preclinical Alzheimer's Cognitive Composite (PACC) 56 or global cognitive composite score. 6 The Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE), the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), and the Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale‐Cognitive Subscale (ADAS‐Cog), which were originally designed for clinical stage diagnosis and screening, are sometime used in the context of preclinical stage to detect subtle early changes. These tests remain useful for Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) and AD dementia screening and diagnosis. However, in the preclinical stage, they often suffer from limited psychometric sensitivity, particularly ceiling effects, 57 which are even more pronounced in individuals with high reserve (BOX A, Figure 2J). Applying clinical tools to preclinical stages may be inadequate, as they often lack the sensitivity to detect subtle cognitive changes occurring before clinical diagnosis.

There are two main issues related to the ceiling effects sometime observed in certain cognitive tests. First, cognitive tests used for screening are not designed to detect early subtle cognitive changes, but rather impairments that affect daily functioning. Detecting earlier changes, even if daily functioning is unimpaired, is important to predict future cognitive changes and implement preventive strategies. For example, in the context of AD treatments, an MMSE test score of 30 (i.e., maximum score, indicating fully intact cognition) at age 60 followed by another MMSE test score of 30 at age 70, would wrongly suggest that no cognitive change occurred. In reality, the likelihood of no cognitive loss over 10 years is low, but these subtle changes would likely remain undetected with a test designed for daily functioning or diagnosis. The ceiling effect can also mask the association between AD pathology and cognition, potentially leading to misleading conclusions. For instance, the reported association between amyloid‐β and cognition is often weak or inconsistent 58 because amyloid‐β deposition occurs a decade before cognitive symptoms emerge in daily life. However, there is evidence suggesting amyloid‐β deposition itself can induce subtle cognitive changes that are not captured by these tests. 59 , 60 Second, the ceiling effect can distort the interpretation of CR models. According to Stern's model, 25 individuals with higher reserve are expected to delay the inflection point of cognitive decline (see next section) compared to those with lower reserve. However, if the true trajectories reflect preserved differentiation (different intercepts but similar slopes, see previous section), reduced age range and ceiling effect could even artificially create different inflection points between individuals (Figure 2K). In reality, the slope, and therefore the timing of decline, could be similar but masked by the test's limited sensitivity. Note that different practice‐effects, such as test‐retest, may also artificially increase cognitive decline difference between cognitively unimpaired versus impaired individuals, as patients with impairment show reduced practice‐effects. 61 One solution to overcome these potential issues is to use validated cognitive composite scores sensitive to subtle early changes such as the PACC 56 , 62 or other global cognitive composite scores with or without executive and language tasks. 6 , 63 If no such composite scores are available, another solution could be to apply appropriate transformations for longitudinal cognitive outcomes that exhibit ceiling effects. 64

Inflection point

One intriguing approach to studying the dynamic nature of reserve and resilience is by examining the inflection point, that is, onset of accelerated decline when compensatory mechanisms fail to maintain daily cognitive functioning and individuals start to experience accelerated cognitive decline. 9 , 26 , 65 , 66 This notion might be somewhat analogous to the “all‐or‐none” principle in biology (e.g., neuronal action potentials), where a system maintains stability, or exhibit minimal loss, until a critical threshold is reached after which it undergoes a sudden and substantial change. The time when decline sets in is difficult to capture as it relies on repeated measurements within relatively short time intervals to estimate when inflection toward significant decline takes place. The inflection point is, however, flexible and can capture both the static and dynamic effects of resilience. The height of the inflection point along the axis of cognitive performance can capture premorbid functioning, that is, static effects. The position along the axis of pathology can capture the point when the compensatory effect of resilience fails, that is, dynamic effects.

There are several ways to capture sudden changes in a time series, such as Bayesian changepoint analysis 67 or piecewise mixed models with a single changepoint and two linear phases (also known as the linear‐linear or the broken‐stick mixed model). 68 Bayesian change point analysis — using probabilities — examines the statistical properties of a time series of data points and whether a significant deviation in these properties occurs between two points. The inflection point is then assigned to the time point after which the properties change, and the aforementioned shifts along the cognition and pathology axes are defined at that point. This approach has the advantage of providing data on key moments in a time course of measurements on an individual basis, rather than group‐level estimates that ignore the considerable inter‐individual variability that is associated with resilience effects. Bayesian change‐point analysis can even be applied prospectively, by allowing the model to detect a significant change in cognition as soon as the change occurs, allowing time for a clinician to react to this onset of decline. In contrast, piecewise linear mixed models require researchers to specify the shape of change over time when analyzing repeated‐measures data, allowing for the estimation of inflection points and individual variation in change patterns. These models are particularly useful for studying nonlinear progression in cognition over time and do not rely on probabilistic detection of change as the Bayesian analysis. Such approaches, however, require data on cognition before, as well as after, the onset of decline. An assessment of inflection points also relies on data where there is an inflection in the time series, and therefore only applies to individuals with at least some measure of pathology and decline. Capturing and delineating static and dynamic effects of resilience on an individual basis, approaches like these might be vital to inform future interventions aiming to increase reserve using personalized therapies. Nonetheless, given that cognitive measures can vary due to many factors unrelated to biological state, change points that rely on data accuracy should be interpreted cautiously and within confidence intervals.

PRACTICAL ISSUES AND PROPOSED SOLUTIONS

Proxy measures

Sociobehavioral variables, which summarize the life course exposure to cognitively stimulating experiences, such as educational attainment, occupational complexity and status, and engagement in leisure activity, are commonly used to study reserve and resilience. Epidemiological studies and meta‐analyses have related a reduced risk and delayed onset of dementia to higher educational attainment, 69 , 70 occupational complexity, 71 , 72 literacy, 73 , 74 verbal intelligence, 75 engagement in cognitively stimulating, 76 , 77 leisure, 8 , 78 physical 79 , 80 and social activities. 76 , 78 The face validity and accessibility of these variables have led to their widespread use as proxy measures of reserve. Proxy measures should not be interpreted as direct measures of reserve, but rather as indirect indicators of exposures that may contribute to reserve through multiple interacting mechanisms. Proxy measures have several limitations that may complicate the interpretation of reserve‐related findings and which, unaddressed, could hinder progress in translating reserve and resilience research into clinical impact. These limitations include failures to address the validity of proxies, inconsistent use, standardization, and validity of proxies, diverging effects of proxies in different contexts (e.g., neurodegenerative diseases), oversimplification of the concept of reserve, and the often‐overlooked confounding effects of SES. We will describe these limitations here and suggest practical solutions.

Inconsistency in the proxies used to operationalize reserve across studies

First, the sociobehavioral variables that are used as proxies of reserve vary across studies. 81 , 82 Moreover, the associations of these proxies with late‐life cognitive performance differ across cognitive domains. 83 Further compounding this problem, moderation effects on the relationship of AD pathology and neurodegeneration with cognition differ depending on the reserve proxies investigated. 84 This means that studies ostensibly investigating the same construct, reserve, may not be directly comparable depending on the proxies used.

One of the solutions to this issue may be to combine different proxies into composite measures, which is common in practice, for example, often using some combination of years of education with verbal intelligence, reading or vocabulary measures. 85 , 86 , 87 , 88 , 89 However, it is difficult to determine the optimal combination of proxies, and optimal combinations may be different depending on the population under investigation. Although data‐driven studies could attempt to identify and validate optimal proxy composites for studying resilience to different types of pathology, the empirical evidence supporting the use of composites instead of individual proxies is unclear. An analysis comparing different combinations of five common proxies found that a single proxy, verbal intelligence, had more consistent positive associations with cognition, independent of brain structure, compared to any of the composite proxies. 90 Similarly, questionnaires have been developed to combine multiple proxies, but they are also affected by the problems outlined above (i.e., the actual validity of these questionnaires in complete resilience models is unclear), as well as issues relating to their psychometric properties. 91 Furthermore, pooling proxies into composite measures may therefore be appropriate only when they reflect the same underlying dimension (e.g., education and occupational complexity), but not across distinct dimensions (e.g., education and physical activity) which influence reserve through fundamentally different mechanisms. Greater use of standardized and combined tools, as well as harmonized methods, deserves more attention and could represent a valuable approach to improving the operationalization of reserve across studies.

Diverging resilience effects for the same proxy in different neurodegenerative diseases

Second, as resilience is increasingly studied in relation to several non‐AD neurodegenerative diseases, 30 researchers must be careful to interpret a resilience effect of a given proxy in the context of the neurodegenerative disease in which it is studied. This is because some proxies may have contrasting effects in different diseases. The proxy of occupational attainment or complexity presents an interesting example of this issue. Occupational attainment or complexity is associated with better late‐life cognitive performance and slower cognitive decline in community‐based samples. 92 However, among a Dutch sample of patients with behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia (bvFTD), primary progressive aphasia (PPA), and AD, there was a higher frequency of teachers with PPA than with bvFTD or AD, and this frequency was also higher than the frequency of teachers in the broader Dutch population. 93 Teaching is characterized by occupational demands on communication, and therefore clinical symptoms of PPA may become more evident earlier in teachers than in other occupations with lower demands on communication skills.

Taken together, these observations highlight the need to avoid assuming that a resilience effect observed for a given proxy in one type of dementia will necessarily generalize to other neurodegenerative conditions. It should also be noted that contrasting resilience effects could sometimes reflect diagnostic and ascertainment biases, as individuals with lower educational attainment and reduced access to healthcare may be underdiagnosed or diagnosed later. In this context, some studies have reported that higher educational attainment is associated with a higher risk of developing Parkinson's disease. 94 , 95 However, a recent meta‐analysis did not support this association and suggested that the observed pattern may instead reflect differential access to diagnosis and healthcare services, as well as methodological and statistical biases. 96

Proxies may over‐simplify the construct of reserve and fail to adequately measure the exposure of interest

Third, certain proxies may over‐simplify the reserve construct, which we assume to be multi‐dimensional (it is influenced by different experiences) and dynamic (it is developed over the life course). The most widely used proxy, educational attainment, is not in line with these assumptions as it reflects formal educational attainment, most often acquired by early adulthood and, therefore, does not reflect the continuing exposure to cognitively stimulating experiences over the life course. Educational attainment as indexed by years of education is also a blunt measure that reflects the quantity, rather than the quality, of education. 97 , 98 Educational quality is affected by socioeconomic and structural factors, including class size, 99 student‐to‐teacher ratio, 100 and multi‐grade classrooms. 101 Higher educational quality is associated with slower cognitive decline, even when adjusting for years of education, 74 , 102 and accounting for educational quality attenuates racial disparities in late‐life cognitive performance. 103 , 104

Similarly, studies investigating the role of occupational complexity in reserve have often used employment type or rankings, 105 , 106 which provide limited insight into the cognitive demands and complexity of an occupation. An alternative is to generate more detailed occupational complexity measures data using occupational databases such as O*NET. To‐date, O*NET measures have been generated that relate to cognition and AD‐related outcomes, including overall cognitive requirements, 92 domain‐specific occupational requirements and skills, 107 environmental and hazardous exposures, 107 cognitive complexity of working with data, people, and things 90 , 108 and level of routine cognitive work involved. 109 Therefore, we highlight the need for more comprehensive proxies of reserve that better capture the complexity of this construct, for example, incorporating both educational quality and quantity, or detailing occupational complexity.

Proxies are confounded by SES

Finally, an overlooked aspect of reserve is the confounding effects of SES. Many of the proxies that we study reflect exposures that are mediated by SES. Indeed, two of the most common reserve proxies, educational and occupational attainment, are used as individual‐level indicators of SES. 110 , 111 Area‐level SES also influences the ability to access recreational resources, which provide opportunities to engage in cognitively stimulating leisure activities, 112 , 113 as there are fewer such resources available in lower SES areas. 114

Lower SES itself is associated with an increased risk of developing dementia, 115 , 116 , 117 earlier mortality in AD, 118 and higher rates of cognitive decline. 119 SES may influence AD‐related outcomes via several mechanisms, 120 aside from resilience, including the negative physiological impact of increased stress, 121 , 122 , 123 , 124 poorer access to treatment and care, 125 increased diagnostic delay, 126 increased co‐morbidities, 127 and poorer diet. 128

An emerging area of focus is the association between area‐level disadvantage and reserve. 112 , 113 Geo‐coded measures of area‐level disadvantage, or neighborhood deprivation, based on national census data, can now be relatively easily obtained from participants’ addresses in many countries. 129 , 130 , 131 , 132 , 133 Across countries, increased neighborhood deprivation is associated with lower later‐life cognitive performance in older adults, independent of individual‐level SES 130 , 131 or neuropathology, 134 and is related to faster disease progression in dementia. 135

Importantly, although SES captures a broad range of health‐relevant exposures and, therefore, is highly informative from a public health and intervention perspective, this same breadth complicates its interpretation as a specific proxy of reserve. Observed associations could reflect socioeconomic inequalities rather than brain‐related compensatory mechanisms. Therefore, greater efforts need to be devoted to collecting detailed individual‐level SES measures to better account for its confounding influence when investigating proxies such as education.

Improving the use of proxy measures

To address the limitations of proxy measures, several methodological improvements should be considered. First, greater harmonization and standardization of proxies across studies are needed to enhance comparability and reproducibility, along with the development and validation of proxy measures. Second, proxies should better capture the multidimensional and dynamic nature of reserve by incorporating more detailed, lifespan measures, such as educational quality and occupational complexity (e.g., using databases like O*NET). Third, the interpretation of proxy effects should be carefully contextualized within specific neurodegenerative diseases and their stages, as their meaning and impact may differ across conditions and disease stages. Finally, systematic assessment and adjustment for SES, including both individual‐ and area‐level indicators, are essential to reduce confounding and improve the validity of reserve‐related findings.

Study design

One crucial study design consideration when aiming to assess resilience effects is a cross‐sectional study design versus a longitudinal study design (Figure 3A). Each of these study designs has merits and disadvantages, and researchers should be aware of when each design is appropriate.

Cross‐sectional approaches

Generally, static effects of resilience might be examined using a cross‐sectional study design with the obvious advantage of not needing longitudinal data, which can allow larger available sample sizes. Due to their very nature, the dynamic effects of reserve should be assessed in a longitudinal study design. This relates, for instance, to effects of brain maintenance (i.e., maintaining brain integrity despite disease progression) and to the dynamic effects of CR that enable a relatively stable level of cognitive performance in the face of pathology (e.g., via compensatory mechanisms). In practice, most cross‐sectional approaches use 1) models of moderation/interaction effects between the predictor and the reserve proxy on a brain or cognitive outcome (see for examples 136 , 137 , 138 , 139 ) (Figures 3B), and 2) residual models, where residuals are the difference between the observed and the predicted values (see for examples 11 , 140 ) (Figure 3C, see the next section). Cross‐sectional assessment of inherently dynamic effects of reserve capture a snapshot in this process, which may complicate interpretation, particularly when samples include individuals at different stages of disease progression and may affect comparability across studies. To mitigate this limitation, findings from cross‐sectional studies should be interpreted within the temporal context of disease progression, as these designs capture only a single timepoint in an inherently dynamic process. Consequently, observed associations between reserve and AD pathologies may reflect different underlying mechanisms depending on when individuals are assessed during the disease course, such as apparent protection at earlier stages versus compensatory processes at later stages. 141

Residual approaches

Reserve has also been quantified as a residual, reflecting the variance in cognitive function that is not explained by brain structure or pathology and demographic factors. 142 The residual can be computed easily in a multiple regression analysis, where a measure of cognitive function is regressed on measures of brain structure or pathology and demographic variables. 11 , 35 , 140 , 142 , 143 , 144 , 145 Positive residuals from this regression reflect greater reserve as the observed outcome (brain or cognitive related) is higher than expected given the individual's brain structure or pathology (Figure 3C). Across studies, higher residual values have been associated with a reduced risk of progression to dementia and AD and with a slower rate of cognitive decline across studies. 11

One methodological issue is that there is considerable heterogeneity in the variables representing brain structure or pathology and cognition that have been used to calculate residual measures across studies. 11 This reduces generalizability of results, 146 and this issue further extends to the selection of demographic variables used in the reserve residual. For instance, education, the most commonly studied reserve proxy, 82 has sometimes been included as a demographic predictor variable in computing the residuals. 142 , 145 , 147 However, doing so removes its contribution from the residual itself, therefore generating an index of reserve that no longer capture one of the key determinant of reserve. 24

Another issue, as demonstrated by Elman, Vogel et al. 143 is that the residual is dependent on the strength of the relationship between the measure of brain structure or pathology with the measure of cognition. As a result, associations identified between the residual and a variable of interest may be driven by the measure of cognition used in the residual model rather than an association with reserve. These authors have argued that researchers seeking to identify potential protective factors associated with reserve should instead aim to build more complete models of cognitive decline and then iteratively add factors of interest to determine if they account for meaningful variance in cognitive decline.

Longitudinal approaches

Given the heterogeneity of cognitive and brain aging trajectories observed in old age, defining resilience focusing on a single point in time, using proxies or residuals, may be limited. To overcome several of the limitations raised by the cross‐sectional framework, recent longitudinal statistical approaches have been proposed to quantify the resilience continuum focusing on cognitive/brain change over time 144 , 148 , 149 , 150 , 151 , 152 , 153 , 154 , 155 , 156 or cognitive/brain levels over time 157 , 158 using standard or extended linear and non‐linear mixed‐effects models regressed on AD‐related markers or risk factors (e.g., brain structure, neuropathology, genetic risks). Mixed models of fixed and random effects have become a tool of choice to estimate late‐life marginal and individual cognitive trajectories, as they offer many practical advantages, including the treatment of intra‐individual correlations, intermittent missingness over time, and varying times and numbers of observations per participant. 159 Briefly, the fixed effects represent the average (marginal) effects of a predictor on the longitudinal outcome across the entire population, assuming it remains constant for all individuals. In contrast, the random effects account for heterogeneity in individual trajectories and represent the subject‐specific deviations from this average. For instance, if the random intercept and random slope parameters of an individual are positive, this indicates that an individual exhibits a higher initial level and a slower decline than what was expected (estimated) marginally. To assess resilience, some longitudinal approaches may focus solely on the interpretation of marginal effects, while others will also consider random effects, depending on the research question and the available data.

A first common longitudinal approach proposes to assess resilience by examining how hypothesized determinants of resilience (e.g., reserve proxy) modifies the association between AD‐related markers and cognitive/brain changes over time (see for example 138 , 148 , 149 , 160 , 161 ) (Figure 3D). For example, Bocancea and colleagues 149 used linear mixed models with longitudinal global cognition as the outcome to test whether key predictors (demographics, APOE‐ε4 status, and intracranial volume) moderate the effect of tau pathology on cognitive decline. In other words, the authors tested whether the association between tau and the annual rate of cognitive decline was different at distinct levels of the resilience‐determinant variable. Statistically and concretely, they adjusted their model on the predictor and tau as simple effects (i.e., effects on the initial level) and in interaction with time (i.e., effects on the rate of decline), and they evaluated whether the three‐way interaction Predictor × Tau × Time was significant or not. If this three‐way interaction was found to be not significant, they examined, at least, the interaction Predictor × Time, reflecting how the resilience determinant influences cognitive decline above and beyond tau pathology. When analyzing specific AD‐related markers, this approach, which focuses on marginal effects and assumes linear trends of cognitive/brain trajectories, is straightforward to implement, parsimonious, and likely to provide trustworthy estimates. However, when multiple AD‐related markers are available for study and when non‐linear (accelerated) trends need to be considered, this approach involves estimating a large number of parameters and three‐way interactions, which can heighten the risk of convergence issues, overfitting, and complicate interpretations.

A second longitudinal approach allows for quantifying resilience based on individual cognitive/brain trajectories estimated after accounting for multiple common AD‐related pathologies (see for example 144 , 151 , 155 ). We first describe a linear slope‐based method, followed by a second linear level‐based method that is more flexible and can accommodate non‐linear trajectories as illustrated in Figure 3E. In the first method, cognitive/brain trajectories are modeled using linear mixed models adjusted for demographics/pathology as simple effects and in interaction with time, while incorporating correlated random intercept and slope. The random slope parameter is used to define resilience, with positive/negative values reflecting high/poor resilience to the annual rate of cognitive/brain changes over time, given pathology. Following this definition, Participants A and B, illustrated in Figure 3E, both demonstrate high resilience to cognitive change, as their cognitive decline occurs more slowly than the marginal trajectories to which they belong (i.e., specific to their pathological profiles). This method can accommodate a large number of AD‐related markers in the model and provides a continuous measure of resilience that is likely easy to estimate, interpret, and use as an outcome afterward. On the other hand, this method assumes linear trajectories based on a single slope parameter and does not capture individual differences in baseline cognitive level. For instance, although Participant B (Figure 3E) shows a slower‐than‐expected decline in cognition over time, this individual consistently exhibits lower cognitive levels than the marginal trajectory. As slope is not the sole means of considering cognitive/brain trajectories, and because cognitive loss may accelerate over time, another complementary definition consists in integrating the individual cognitive levels differences over time, while allowing a more flexible (non‐linear) function of time. 157 , 158 Here, in the second method, resilience is defined as the average of the longitudinal differences between the person‐specific and the expected marginal estimations of cognitive/brain levels after controlling for pathology. Thus, from this perspective, Participant A (Figure 3E) exhibits high resilience in terms of levels, as this participant consistently presents higher cognitive levels than expected, given the pathological profile. In contrast, Participant B (Figure 3E) exhibits low resilience in terms of levels, as this participant consistently presents lower cognitive levels than expected, given the pathology profile. Like the cross‐sectional residual approach, these two resilience measures, defined based on individuals slopes and repeated levels over time, provide two continuous measures capturing two complementary dimensions of resilience, and which can be used as main outcomes or predictors a posteriori. Unlike residuals, these measures focusing on the estimated (error‐free) trajectories have the benefit of accounting for measurement errors inherent in cognitive measures and accommodating non‐linear changes in cognition over time. Nonetheless, they still rely on residuals, meaning that adding more variables to the models may reduce the error estimate and increase noise. Differentiating the components that contribute to outcomes (e.g., cognition) rather than the ones that relate to the residuals would be a good practice.

A third longitudinal approach consists of classifying individuals based on their probability to belong to a class‐specific trajectory, estimated using extension of linear mixed models, such as growth curve models and latent class mixed models (see for example 150 , 152 , 156 , 162 ) (Figure 3F). Compared with the longitudinal approaches discussed above, where individual trajectories deviate from a single mean (marginal) trajectory, here the models assume that the population is composed of two or more longitudinal latent classes of participants characterized by distinct marginal cognitive trajectories. For example, three distinct longitudinal cognitive patterns could be identified after controlling for AD‐related markers: “high initial level, no decline” (Figure 3F, Group 1); “moderate initial level, moderate‐to‐fast decline” (Figure 3F, Group 2); “low initial level, fast decline” (Figure 3F, Group 3). In this context, Participant A and Participant B depicted in Figure 3A would be more likely to belong to group 1 and group 3, respectively. In comparison to the two prior approaches applying linear mixed models with the assumption of homogeneity of the longitudinal process, this method is particularly useful to detect heterogeneity in cognitive/brain longitudinal patterns, and provides a binary or categorical (also called class) variable which can be used as a main outcome or predictor in subsequent analyses. This model provides less statistical power and less granularity than the continuous outcomes and requires long follow‐up periods to ensure model convergence, and accurately capture differential accelerations of cognitive change over time. Nonetheless, it remains promising, as additional variables can be incorporated to refine class definition and improve interpretability.

Together, these longitudinal methods, that do not rely on the error term, capture complementary dimensions of the resilience continuum and provide complementary information when estimating the putative effects of covariates. However, longitudinal estimation strategies to define resilience are relatively new. These approaches also imply difficult comparisons between cohorts as the model specification and the sample size can influence the estimates. Future research is needed to understand the potential influence of methodological choices, such as the time scale (e.g., chronological age, years before death), and time to account for AD biomarkers collected repeatedly during life (MRI or PET imaging).

Influential cases

Outliers are typically defined as extreme or aberrant values that deviate substantially from the group mean. These can generally be categorized into two types: methodological outliers, which arise from data acquisition or processing errors, and biological outliers, which reflect true inter‐individual variability. While methodological outliers should be excluded, biological outliers should not be systematically excluded from analyses for statistical reasons. Removing biological outliers, also called influential cases, excludes individuals who may exhibit exceptional resilience by deviating from norms. Some of the most compelling advances in our understanding of reserve and resilience have emerged from unique case studies. Several notable examples include a Reelin‐COLBOS heterozygous man showing resistance to a gene mutation related to autosomal dominant Alzheimer's disease (ADAD), 163 a woman carrying an ADAD mutation who remained cognitively intact for decades beyond the expected onset age due to a rare APOE‐3 Christchurch homozygous genotype, 164 and an individual with the presenilin 2 p.Asn141Ile mutation, classically associated with early‐onset AD, who exhibited no cognitive symptoms well beyond the expected clinical onset. 165 In addition, the 90+ Study described a participant who maintained normal cognition despite advanced age, comorbidities, and multiple neuropathological markers of dementia. 166 Such cases highlight that studying influential cases can yield valuable and unique information about resistance and resilience mechanisms in AD. To improve transparency and scientific insights, we recommend that standard practices include plotting individual data points and reporting model results both with and without biological outliers to confirm that the main analysis remains generalizable at the group level and not driven by a few extreme observations.

CONCLUSION

This review highlights several priorities for advancing research on reserve and resilience in aging and AD. First, greater methodological harmonization is needed, as current heterogeneity in proxy measures and modeling strategies limits comparability and cumulative knowledge. Future progress will require improved validation of proxy and composite measures, as well as greater consistency in the operationalization of reserve and resilience. Second, a lifespan approach based on longitudinal data is essential, as restricted age ranges, ceiling effects, short follow‐up periods, and simplified linear models may lead to overestimation of resilience. This lifespan approach with multiple time points, broader age ranges, and non‐linear models, such as generalized additive models and mixed‐effects models, are therefore necessary to better capture heterogeneous brain and cognitive trajectories. Third, future progress will depend on combining longitudinal approaches with individual‐level trajectory modeling and investigations of underlying molecular and biological mechanisms to better characterize resilience effects, as much of the existing literature remains largely descriptive and non‐causal. Nevertheless, despite these important methodological recommendations, studying reserve and resilience will likely remain inherently challenging due to the complexity, heterogeneity and long‐term nature of aging and AD. We believe this narrative review — based on expert perspectives — provides essential insights, knowledge and practical guidance for researchers aiming to study reserve and resilience, ultimately contributing to the improvement of the quality and reproducibility of future research in this field.
