From pre-tumor to tumor: Decoding the endoscopic-pathologic spectrum of neoplastic lesions in autoimmune gastritis
Authors: Fengmei Zhong, Yingchao Zhang, Ziguang Liu, Haipeng Feng, Yanqin Ma, Zhihong Zhang, Qinwei Xu, Li Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAutoimmune gastritis (AIG) is a chronic atrophic gastritis predisposing to neuroendocrine tumors (NETs), dysplasia, and gastric cancer (GC). In a cohort of 1,551 patients with suspected AIG, we identified 46 with neoplastic lesions and 134 without such lesions and compared their clinicopathological and endoscopic features. NETs typically appeared as polypoid/submucosal tumors with dilated vessels or central depression; epithelial neoplasms showed variable morphologies, with irregular microvessels under narrow-band imaging (NBI). Extensive corpus atrophy and antral patchy redness were independent risk factors for AIG-associated neoplasia, whereas female sex was protective. These findings support a risk model integrating clinical, endoscopic, and pathological features for individualized surveillance.Subject areas: Oncology, Pathology, Diagnostics

Introduction

Autoimmune gastritis (AIG), historically termed type A gastritis, is a chronic autoimmune disorder characterized by atrophic changes. It was first described as a distinct entity by Strickland and Mackay in 1973.1 In contrast to Helicobacter pylori (HP)-associated gastritis (type B), AIG is primarily driven by a T cell-mediated immune response. This process generates autoantibodies against the gastric H+/K+ ATPase (proton pump), ultimately resulting in the targeted destruction of parietal cells.2,3 The autoantibody response in AIG extends beyond parietal cells to include the production of anti-intrinsic factor antibody. Consequently, this impairs vitamin B12 absorption, thereby leading to the development of pernicious anemia.3,4

The hallmark endoscopic feature of AIG is corpus-predominant atrophy, a pattern historically termed “reverse atrophy.” Additional common findings include remnant oxyntic mucosa (ROM) and tenacious, adherent mucus within the gastric body.5 Cast-off skin appearance (CSA) and white globe appearance (WGA) can also be observed in advanced AIG under magnified endoscopic conditions. Gastric swelling accompanied by erythema (referred to as salmon roe-like appearance) and longitudinal arrangement of pseudo-polyps (referred to as bamboo joint-like appearance) can be observed in the early-stage AIG.3

The development of AIG can induce hypergastrinemia and promote the proliferation of enterochromaffin-like (ECL) cells. This linear progression of ECL cell expansion may advance through stages of micro-nodular hyperplasia and adenomatous hyperplasia, ultimately culminating in the formation of type 1 NETs.3,6,7 Furthermore, AIG is recognized as a premalignant condition associated with an elevated risk of GC.8 Epidemiological studies indicate that AIG patients face a 3-fold increase in GC risk compared with the general population, with an annual incidence of approximately 0.5%.9 This is corroborated by a Japanese cohort study, in which GC was identified in approximately 9% of AIG patients.5

Although the association between AIG and NETs is well established, the spectrum of other neoplastic lesions in AIG—including GC and various polypoid lesions—remains inadequately characterized. This study systematically analyzes a large cohort of AIG patients to delineate the clinical, endoscopic, and histopathological features of these concomitant neoplasms.

Results

Screening study and clinical characteristics in the neoplastic lesion cases

A cohort of 46 patients with AIG and concomitant neoplastic lesions was enrolled in this study. As detailed in Table 1, the study population was composed of 24 female and 22 male patients, with the median age at diagnosis of 54.5 years (range: 31–78 years). Concomitant autoimmune diseases (10/46, 21.7%), including Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, Sjögren’s syndrome, and anemia (10/46, 21.7%) were frequently observed. Serological profiling revealed that the majority of patients exhibited elevated serum gastrin-17 levels (45/46, 97.8%).

Endoscopic features in the neoplastic lesion cases

A total of 55 lesions were identified in the 46 enrolled patients, with nine patients presenting two synchronous lesions each. A majority of lesions were predominantly located in the gastric fundus and body.

Endoscopically, NETs presented as polypoid or submucosal tumor-like lesions (Figure 1A). Under white-light imaging, they typically appeared yellowish, often accompanied by dilated vessels (Figure 1B), central depression, erosion, or erythema on the surface. NBI revealed partial loss of the gastric pit structure within the lesion area, along with abnormally dilated vessels (Figure 1C).

Endoscopically, epithelial neoplasms (dysplasia and adenocarcinoma) exhibited diverse macroscopic morphologies, including type 0-I, 0-II, 0-IIa, 0-IIb, 0-IIc, 0-III, or mixed types (Figure 1D). Under white-light imaging, they appeared as reddish, yellowish, or faded lesions. NBI revealed well-demarcated borders in most cases, along with enlarged crypt openings, widened intercryptal areas (Figure 1E), and an irregular microvascular pattern (Figure 1F).

Endoscopic evaluation revealed that the background gastric mucosa in most patients exhibited features of advanced AIG (37/46, 80.4%). Early and intermediate stages were less frequently encountered. Advanced AIG is characterized by extensive atrophy in the gastric body and fundus. This is accompanied by mucosal thinning and loss of rugal folds, resulting in clearly visible submucosal vessels (Figure 1G). The intermediate stage of AIG is primarily characterized by atrophy confined to the gastric body, with residual fundic gland mucosa observed as widespread, flat elevations (Figure 1H). Notably, tenacious, yellowish-white mucus adherent to the gastric body and fundus was observed in a subset of patients, which could not be easily removed by water irrigation (Figure 1I). In its early stages, AIG is endoscopically characterized by erythema and edema in the gastric body (Figure 1J), which can present as a mosaic-like pattern, fissuring, or a salmon-roe appearance characterized by fine red granularity (Figure 1K). In contrast, the antral mucosa typically remains normal (Figure 1L).

Pathological feature in the neoplastic lesion cases

Thirty-four patients (34/46, 73.9%) underwent endoscopic submucosal dissection, 10 underwent gastric biopsy, one received radical gastrectomy for GC, and one underwent endoscopic full-thickness resection. Concurrent hyperplastic polyps were observed in 17 patients. Among the 55 lesions, the most prevalent were NETs, followed by gastric adenocarcinoma, high-grade dysplasia, and low-grade dysplasia.

Histologically, the tumor cells were arranged in characteristic organoid patterns, including nested, trabecular, and ribbon-like architectures, and were confined to the gastric mucosa and submucosa (Figures 2A and 2B). The adenocarcinoma cohort consisted exclusively of early-stage GCs, most of which invaded the lamina propria (5/11), muscularis mucosae (3/11), or submucosa (3/11). The majority were well-to-moderately differentiated tubular (Figure 3A) or papillary (Figure 3B) adenocarcinomas (7/11), whereas poorly differentiated adenocarcinomas were less frequent (4/11). The poorly differentiated subtype included two cases of signet-ring cell carcinoma (Figure 3C), one case of poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma, and one case of adenocarcinoma with enteroblastic differentiation (Figure 3D), characterized by tumor cells exhibiting a clear cell-like morphology with abundant glycogen and resembling fetal intestinal epithelial cells. In addition, precursor lesions—namely high-grade dysplasia (Figure 3E) and low-grade dysplasia (Figure 3F)—were observed adjacent to the adenocarcinomas.

The surrounding gastric mucosa demonstrates pathological features typical of AIG. Histologically, the majority of cases presents with mucosal thinning and a marked reduction or complete absence of oxyntic glands in the gastric body and fundus, frequently accompanied by pseudopyloric gland metaplasia (Figure 4A) or complete-type intestinal metaplasia (Figure 4B). A lymphocytic-predominant inflammatory infiltrate is observed throughout the mucosal thickness, occasionally with admixed plasma cells or eosinophils (Figure 4C). These inflammatory cells are diffusely distributed within the lamina propria.

In a subset of cases, early-stage AIG is characterized by a diffuse, periglandular inflammatory infiltrate surrounding the fundic glands, resulting in a decrease in parietal cells (Figure 4D). The damaged parietal cells often exhibit swelling and degenerative changes, with luminal cytoplasmic protrusions resembling a “boot-nail” appearance—a phenomenon referred to as pseudohypertrophy (Figure 4E). In advanced-stage cases, neuroendocrine cells may exhibit linear hyperplasia (defined as five or more cells arranged in a linear, curvilinear, or chain-like pattern), micronodular hyperplasia (solid micronodular cell nests composed of five or more cells), and nodular hyperplasia (aggregation of five or more micronodular foci) (Figure 4F).

Association between neoplastic lesions and clinicopathological characteristics

An additional cohort of 134 patients with AIG was included and compared in terms of clinicopathological characteristics with the previously described 46 patients who had AIG with neoplastic lesions. As illustrated in Table 3, the 46 tumor cases were categorized into the NET group (17 cases), the epithelial neoplasm group (26 cases), and the other group (3 cases). Significant differences in sex distribution were observed among the groups (p = 0.039). The proportion of female patients was 73.9% in the AIG group, compared to 58.8%, 50.0%, and 33.3% in the NET, epithelial neoplasm, and other groups, respectively, with a corresponding increase in male proportions. Serum iron levels differed highly significantly across the four groups (p < 0.001). Normal serum iron levels were found in 70.1% of the AIG group, 35.3% of the NET group, 57.7% of the epithelial neoplasm group, and 66.7% of the other group. Additionally, elevated serum iron levels were observed in the epithelial neoplasm group (1 case, 3.8%) and the other group (1 case, 33.3%; p < 0.05 for the other group vs. AIG group).

Association between neoplastic lesions and endoscopic characteristics

We subsequently performed endoscopic examination of the gastric body, fundus, and antrum mucosa in the 134 patients with AIG without neoplastic lesions. These findings were compared with those from 46 AIG patients with neoplastic lesions to evaluate the association between neoplastic involvement and endoscopic features.

As shown in Table 4, multiple significant differences in gastric mucosal endoscopic features were observed among the four patient groups. For ROM, the AIG group showed the highest detection rate (37.3%), whereas the NET (5.9%) and epithelial neoplasm (15.4%) groups exhibited markedly lower rates, and the other group showed 0% detection, with a statistically significant difference (p = 0.008). Extensive corporal atrophy was present in 25.4% of the AIG group but had significantly higher incidence in the NET (88.2%), epithelial neoplasm (73.1%), and other (100%) groups (p < 0.001). Patchy redness in the gastric antrum was most frequent in the epithelial neoplasm group (57.7%), followed by the AIG (23.9%) and NET (29.4%) groups, with 0% in the other group (p = 0.004). No statistically significant differences were observed among the groups for the remaining endoscopic features (p > 0.05).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis (Table 6) revealed that extensive atrophy of the gastric body (odds ratio [OR] = 8.023, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.141–30.058, p = 0.002) and antral patchy redness (OR = 5.948, 95% CI: 1.973–17.937, p = 0.002) were independent risk factors for neoplastic lesions. Collectively, these findings indicate that extensive gastric body atrophy and antral patchy redness are independent risk factors for neoplastic lesions in patients with AIG, whereas female sex may serve as a protective factor.

Discussion

AIG is a chronic atrophic gastritis that is more prevalent in the female population, with an overall estimated prevalence ranging from 0.5% to 4.5%.10,11,12 Serologically based diagnostic studies have reported prevalence rates as high as 8%–20%.13 In this study, we screened 1,551 patients with histopathologically suspected AIG. Among them, 46 patients with neoplastic lesions and 134 without such lesions were confirmed to have AIG based on positive serological antibodies combined with histopathological diagnosis, yielding a prevalence of 11.6% (180/1,551). The incidence rate among female patients was 7.9% (123/1,551). The overall incidence of neoplastic lesions was 3.0% (46/1,551), including NETs (1.1%, 17/1,551), GC (0.7%, 11/1,551), and dysplasia (0.97%, 15/1,551).

However, a large-scale meta-analysis demonstrated that the incidence of GC in AIG was 0.12 per 100 person-years, whereas the incidence of NETs was 1.09 per 100 person-years.12 According to an international multicenter study, the prevalences of gastric adenocarcinoma and NETs at the time of AIG diagnosis were 0.5% and 3.7%, respectively, in Europe.14 Although the prevalence and incidence rates reported in this study are somewhat higher than those previously reported—likely due to the limited cohort size—we argue that the true prevalence of AIG may be underestimated in clinical practice. This is because, in our retrospective cohort, not all patients with histopathological suspicion underwent serological testing as recommended, primarily owing to cost constraints. Accordingly, we propose that serological testing be performed for all histopathologically suspected cases where economically feasible and that confirmed patients receive regular high-quality endoscopic surveillance to prevent underdiagnosis of neoplastic lesions.

Among the 180 patients with AIG in this study, the endoscopic findings were categorized as early stage in 33.3% of cases (60/180), intermediate stage in 42.8% (77/180), and advanced stage in 39.4% (71/180). The antral mucosa appeared completely normal in 25.6% of cases (46/180). Although the antral mucosa is generally considered to be structurally normal in patients with AIG, Terao et al. found that less than 50% of patients exhibited endoscopically normal antral mucosa. They described endoscopic findings of “circular wrinkle-like pattern,” “patchy redness,” “raised erosion,” and “red streaks”.15,16 Comparative analysis of endoscopic features between the neoplastic and non-neoplastic groups revealed significant differences in the presence of residual oxyntic glands, extensive atrophy of the gastric corpus, and patchy redness of the antral mucosa. Antral patchy redness was identified as an independent risk factor for neoplastic lesions.

We think that a history of HP infection may be the primary cause of the aforementioned antral mucosal lesions and may also serve as a contributing factor to neoplastic lesion development. It has been established that a cancer-associated metaplastic cell expressing ANPEP/CD13 with neoplastic potential is present in both HP-associated gastritis and AIG.17 In addition, the pathogenesis of AIG entails molecular mimicry between HP antigens and host H+/K+-ATPase epitopes, together with the involvement of Toll-like receptor (TLR)-dependent pathways that mediate the immune response to HP.18 Our inability to fully evaluate the relationship between HP infection and neoplastic lesions is a limitation of this study. Future studies should focus more closely on the molecular mechanisms underlying the development of AIG in HP-negative cases (de novo AIG) as well as in patients with concurrent or prior HP infection, as this may facilitate a more individualized assessment of GC risk.

Histopathologically, AIG progresses through three stages. The early stage features injury to parietal and chief cells, mild periglandular inflammatory infiltration, and possible parietal cell hypertrophy. The intermediate stage shows marked loss of oxyntic glands, often with pseudopyloric and pancreatic metaplasia. The advanced stage is defined by complete loss of normal oxyntic glands, epithelial metaplasia, mucosal thinning, and potential neoplastic lesions such as dysplasia, NETs, and GCs.2,4,19,20,21 Some investigators have also proposed a pre-early (occult) stage, characterized by seropositivity for AIG-related antibodies without histologic evidence of atrophy or metaplasia, with the presence of only scattered inflammatory cells around the oxyntic glands.19,22

The full spectrum of histopathological features across all stages was observed in this study. Diagnosis of AIG is straightforward in cases with typical histopathological features. However, in clinical practice, the identification of early-stage AIG is crucial for early detection, timely intervention, and effective surveillance. This presents a substantial challenge for pathologists. Accurate diagnosis of early-stage AIG relies on the integration of expert clinicopathological diagnostic skills, comprehensive clinical knowledge, and detailed endoscopic and serological findings.

Notably, some studies have reported that hypergastrinemia plays a promoting role in the pathogenesis of both type 1 NETs and gastric adenocarcinoma.23,24,25 Marked hypergastrinemia can promote the development of intestinal-type gastric adenocarcinoma, a mechanism mediated directly by gastrin or indirectly through gastrin-stimulated stem cell proliferation.6,24 However, this study neither detected a significant difference in serum gastrin levels between the two groups nor identified elevated serum gastrin as a risk factor for neoplastic development. Instead, significant intergroup differences were observed in sex distribution and serum iron concentration. Extensive atrophy of the gastric corpus emerged as an independent risk factor for neoplastic lesions, whereas female sex was identified as a protective factor.

Studies indicate that although the incidence rate is higher in female patients, advanced tumor complications are more likely to occur in male patients.26 We hypothesize that estrogen may exert a protective effect against GC in female patients. This is consistent with the higher incidence of GC in men, whereas postmenopausal women and men show nearly comparable susceptibility to the disease.27 Whether the mechanisms through which estrogen confers protection in GC arising from AIG are identical to those in GC of non-autoimmune origin remains to be elucidated. Therefore, long-term follow-up surveillance is of paramount importance for male patients as well as for those with extensive corpus atrophy and antral mucosa-associated lesions, which is crucial for personalized tumor risk monitoring and patient management.

Clinical, endoscopic, and pathological features in patients with AIG complicated by neoplastic lesions exhibit strong and quantifiable correlations. Integrated assessment of these features enables more accurate risk prediction and represents a pivotal step toward the individualized and precise management of AIG.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design carries inherent biases. Second, as a single-center study, selection bias may exist. Third, the limited sample size of patients with neoplastic lesions, especially for certain rare subtypes such as adenocarcinoma with enteroblastic differentiation as presented here, restricts the robustness of statistical analyses. Future multicenter prospective studies are warranted to validate the risk-stratification model proposed herein and to explore the potential integration of molecular markers—such as methylation signatures or microbiome profiles—into the current feature-based framework.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human participants

This study was a retrospective one, screening 1551 patients who underwent gastric biopsy, gastrectomy or endoscopic submucosal dissection in the Department of Pathology of Shanghai East Hospital from 2019 to 2025. Finally, 46 patients diagnosed with AIG complicated by neoplastic lesions and 134 patients with non-neoplastic AIG were enrolled.

The inclusion criteria are as follows: 1) Patients diagnosed with AIG in our hospital; 2) All patients have tissue pathological specimens.3) All patients had at least one positive serological indicator (such as positive parietal cell antibody or positive intrinsic factor antibody or both antibodies positive); 4) All patients had complete endoscopic data. 5) The HP test is conducted using the carbon-13 breath test or pathological tissue immunohistochemical detection. In case of a contradiction between the results of the two methods, the pathological result shall prevail. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee for Clinical Research, Shanghai East Hospital (Number:2024 YS-167). Because the study used retrospective, de-identified clinical data and posed minimal risk to participants, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the ethics committees. This study was conducted by the Declaration of Helsinki.

This study did not involve animal experiments or cell lines.

Method details

Data collection

This study is a retrospective analysis. The clinical data (sex, age) and AIG-related parameters (history of autoimmune diseases, serum antibody levels, anemia status, serum gastrin levels, vitamin B12 levels, serum iron, and serum folate) were primarily collected from the inpatient and outpatient electronic medical record systems of Shanghai East Hospital. Pathological data (e.g., pathological diagnosis, HP infection status, immunohistochemical findings, and tumor pathological classification) were obtained from the pathology reporting system.

Endoscopic assessment

The endoscopic data of 46 enrolled patients with tumors and 134 patients without tumors were retrieved from the endoscopy reporting system of the Department of Gastroenterology, Shanghai East Hospital, and the endoscopic findings were reassessed, with a primary focus on the endoscopic staging of AIG. The assessment was performed in the gastric body and fundus, specifically evaluating early-stage features (mosaic cracking, redness or edematous nodules, granular mucosa, salmon-egg-like mucosa), intermediate-stage features (inverse atrophy, yellowish-white mucus, residual oxyntic glands), and advanced-stage features (extensive atrophy of the gastric body). Involvement of the antral mucosa (entirely normal mucosa, more than half normal, normal only near the pylorus, patchy redness, red streaks, circular folds) was also evaluated. Furthermore, endoscopic characteristics (color tone, margin, microvascular architecture, NBI findings) were assessed specifically in the tumor group.

According to the Japanese endoscopic diagnostic proposal for AIG—designated as AIG-atrophic stage.2,5,28 The classification is as follows. Early stage: 50% < ROM ≤100%, with endoscopic findings of redness and swelling of the gastric mucosa (granular, mosaic, or salmon roe-like appearance). Intermediate stage: 10% < ROM ≤50%, with endoscopic findings of oxyntic mucosa of various shapes and sizes, medium-to-large hyperplastic polyps, and the presence of WGA. Advanced stage: ROM ≤10%, with pronounced atrophy characterized by visible vasculature, adherent viscous mucus, and the presence of WGA and small hyperplastic polyps. Endoscopic staging of AIG and evaluation of the antral mucosa were performed by Y and Y, with any disagreements adjudicated by Professor Xu, who made the final determination.

Summary of pathological features

We summarized the pathological features of AIG in the enrolled cases, with a focus on evaluating lesions in the gastric fundus and body. The assessment encompassed the following aspects: 1) Glandular atrophy (reduced number of oxyntic glands and thinning of the glandular architecture due to atrophy); 2) Inflammatory cell infiltration (chronic inflammatory cell infiltration in the lamina propria, predominantly lymphocytes and plasma cells); 3) Cellular morphological alterations (reduction or loss of parietal cells, with residual parietal cells potentially exhibiting hyperplasia or pseudohypertrophy); 4) Neuroendocrine cell hyperplasia (hyperplasia of ECL cells in the gastric body and fundus, which may manifest as simple, linear, or micronodular hyperplasia); 5) Metaplastic changes (presence of intestinal metaplasia); and 6) Associated neoplastic lesions (classified into neuroendocrine neoplasms, epithelial neoplasms, and others).

All pathological histological diagnosis results were independently reviewed by two senior pathologists. In case of inconsistent results, a third senior physician would re-review and confirm the final conclusion. Histological assessments were conducted by F and H, with any discrepancies resolved by Professor Zhang, who served as the final arbiter.

Immunohistochemistry

All the paraffin sections of the pathological tissues were 3 μm thick, and immunohistochemical staining was performed using an automatic immunohistochemical staining machine (Leica, Germany). Positive external controls were included for all immunohistochemical sections. All NETs tumors included in this study were subjected to immunohistochemical staining for CgA (clone OTI8H7), Syn (clone EP158), SSTR2 (clone EP149), and Ki-67(clone MAB107). Pathological specimens of AIG involved in this study were subjected to immunohistochemical staining for HP (Rabbit polyclonal antibody). All immunohistochemical interpretations were independently reviewed by two senior pathologists. In case of inconsistent results, a third senior physician would re-review and confirm the final conclusion. Immunohistochemical assessments were conducted by F and H, with any discrepancies resolved by Professor Zhang, who served as the final arbiter.

A comparative analysis of clinicopathologic and endoscopic characteristics

A comparative analysis of clinicopathological and endoscopic features between the tumor group and the non-tumor group revealed statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of sex, serum iron levels, presence of ROM, extensive atrophy of the gastric body, and patchy redness of the gastric antrum.

Identification of risk factors associated with the development of neoplastic lesions

To further identify risk factors for neoplastic lesions in AIG, we performed multivariate regression analysis on variables that showed statistically significant differences between the two groups. With the presence or absence of neoplastic lesions as the dependent variable, and sex, serum iron level, ROM, extensive atrophy of the gastric body, and patchy redness of the gastric antrum as independent variables, the regression analysis identified extensive atrophy of the gastric body and patchy redness of the gastric antrum as independent risk factors for neoplastic lesions, whereas female sex was identified as a protective factor.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 25.0). Categorical variables are expressed as percentages, with between-group comparisons made using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Risk factors for neoplastic lesions in patients with AIG were assessed by logistic regression analysis. All tests were two-sided, and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
