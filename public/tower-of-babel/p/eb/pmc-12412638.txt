Chiral edge state control of thermoelectric effects
Authors: William Huynh, Boliang Liu, Simon Munyan, Sina Ahadi, Susanne Stemmer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Thermoelectric responses in two-dimensional electron gases subjected to magnetic fields have the potential to provide unique information about quasiparticle statistics. In this study, we show that chiral edge states play a key role in thermoelectric Hall bar measurements by completely controlling the direction of the internal thermal gradient. To this end, we perform measurements of the magnetothermoelectric responses of cadmium arsenide quantum wells. The magnetothermoelectric responses in the quantum Hall regime agree with theoretical predictions if one considers the role of chiral edge states, which flow in opposite directions on either side of the Hall bar and establish an internal temperature gradient that is perpendicular to the externally applied thermal gradient. We show that the results are self-consistent within this picture under different measurement conditions. We discuss potential applications of the findings, such as in nanoscale control of local temperature gradients and thermoelectric effects along with the characterization of other topological systems with chiral edges states.

INTRODUCTION

The thermoelectric properties of quantum Hall systems have attracted attention since the early 1980s, because they can provide a wealth of information. Under appropriate conditions, thermopower is directly related to the entropy per quasiparticle (1), which may indicate the exchange statistics of exotic quantum states, such as non-Abelian quasiparticles and Wigner crystals (2, 3), and provide information about localization (4) and entanglement (5, 6). The theory of thermoelectric effects in two-dimensional electron gases (2DEGs) in magnetic fields was developed in pioneering works by Girvin and Jonson (GJ) (7, 8) and others (9–14). For an isotropic 2DEG in a perpendicular magnetic field and subject to an in-plane thermal gradient, the Seebeck ( Sxx ) and Nernst ( Sxy ) coefficients, measured parallel and perpendicular to the temperature gradient, respectively, depend on the components of the thermoelectric and resistivity tensorsSxx=ρxxαxx−ρxyαxy(1)Sxy=ρxxαxy+ρxyαxx(2)where ρxx and ρxy are the diagonal and off-diagonal elements of the resistivity tensor and αxx and αxy are the corresponding elements of the thermoelectric conductivity tensor. Note that Sxx=Syy and Sxy=−Syx . The thermoelectric conductivity coefficients, αxx and αxy , are even and odd functions in magnetic field (B), respectively, e.g., αxy(B)=−αxy(−B) . Often, αxy is of particular interest as it corresponds to the entropy per carrier (12). In the quantum Hall regime, ρxy≫ρxx , and Eqs. 1 and 2 simplify toSxx≈−ρxyαxy(3)Sxy≈ρxyαxx(4)

In the quantum Hall regime, Sxx reflects αxy , which shows a series of peaks when the Fermi level is at the center of a Landau level (7). Sxx is symmetric in B, because both αxy and ρxy are antisymmetric. The off-diagonal component, Sxy , is dominated by αxx and alternates in sign, taking positive, zero, then negative values with increasing filling factor (7). Both Sxx and Sxy vanish at the quantum Hall plateaus.

Experiments broadly confirmed the expected behaviors of Sxx and Sxy (15–23), but quantitative discrepancies remain. One reason is that III-V heterostructures show strong phonon drag effects (24, 25), thus requiring extremely low temperatures to measure the diffusive thermopower. In the configuration considered by GJ, the thermal gradient is applied across the width of the Hall bar (y direction in Fig. 1). Barlas and Yang (26) suggested a Corbino disk geometry, in which only longitudinal responses need to be considered. Recent experiments not only reproduce the predicted sawtooth response of the Corbino thermopower, but also record large, unexplained thermoelectric signals at the Hall plateaus (27). Moreover, Corbino disks do not lend themselves easily to device geometries needed for quantum thermoelectric devices (28–30). The thermoelectric responses of Hall bar devices therefore remain of great interest.

In this respect, an important issue was pointed out in (31, 32). In particular, when an external heater is used to set the edge states on either side of the Hall bar to different temperatures, the boundary conditions are not adiabatic, unlike those considered by theory. It was instead suggested in (31, 32) to establish the external temperature gradient ( ∇Text ) along the length of the Hall bar (x direction in Fig. 1). As illustrated in Fig. 1A, ballistic, chiral edge states on one edge maintain the temperature set by the hot end until they encounter the cold end on the right, whereas the edge states on the opposite edge, which flow in the reverse direction, maintain the temperature of the cold end until they equilibrate at the hot end. As a result, the internal temperature gradient, ∇Tint , is normal to the edge current flow and to ∇Text . Reversing the direction of the magnetic field changes the chirality of the edge states and thus reverses the direction of ∇Tint . This picture is consistent with prior studies of heat transport in the quantum Hall regime (33, 34). To date, experimental evidence for the resulting thermoelectric effects is still lacking, despite important implications. Aside from adiabatic conditions, (31, 32) point out that heat transport will be quantized and the system will realize a nonequilibrium steady state. Moreover, manipulation of chiral edge states via electric gates could be used for applications based on local, nanoscale control of heat and thermoelectric responses.

In this study, we use the geometry shown in Fig. 1A to measure magnetothermoelectric responses of a narrow quantum well of cadmium arsenide (Cd3As2). While Cd3As2 is a three-dimensional (3D) Dirac semimetal in the bulk (35), prior experimental and theoretical studies have shown that thin (<25 nm) films are small gap insulators, which can be topological or trivial, depending on the precise thickness (35–37). We observe remarkably clear and pronounced diffusive thermoelectric effects in the quantum Hall regime. We show that the results can be explained self-consistently with chiral edge currents on either side of the Hall bar that independently maintain different temperatures and completely control the direction of the internal thermal gradient, as shown in Fig. 1A.

We begin with discussing the thermoelectric responses in the quantum Hall regime, away from charge neutrality, when the Fermi level is swept through the Landau levels of the conduction subband (VTG > −1.5 V in Fig. 2). We observe oscillatory behavior in Vx2ω whenever the Fermi level crosses a Landau level, where Vx2ω abruptly changes sign. For a direct comparison with Rxx , which peaks at the Landau levels, we show line cuts at different values of B in Fig. 3. At the quantum Hall plateaus, Vx2ω∼0 , as expected. The sign-changing behavior of Vx2ω is, however, in apparent contradiction with the predictions by GJ, discussed in the Introduction. In particular, from Eq. 3, one would expect to observe negative extrema in Sxx (i.e., Vx2ω ) at energies corresponding to half-filling and no sign changes.

The contradiction can be resolved if the chiral edge states control the direction of ∇Tint , as shown in Fig. 1A. In this case, Vx2ω corresponds to the thermoelectric voltage that develops along the direction transverse to ∇Tint . Therefore, from the perspective of ∇Tint , Vx2ω is really a Nernst signal. In generalVx2ω=SxxΔxT+SxyΔyT(5)

With Eqs. 3 and 4, ΔTint=ΔyT and ΔxT=0 , we obtain Vx2ω≈ρxyαxxΔTint . We note that ρxy≫ρxx is valid in the quantum Hall regime for this film (see fig. S4). Thus, Vx2ω reflects the longitudinal thermoelectric transport coefficient calculated by GJ ( αxx ), which changes sign at the Landau levels. Calculations presented in (32) consider this situation and predict sign-changing oscillations in Sxx ( Vx2ω ) at the Landau levels. As shown in fig. S5, Vx2ω is symmetric in B. This may at first seem unexpected, considering that ρxyαxx and, therefore, Sxy in Eq. 4 are antisymmetric in B. The key point is that reversing B also reverses the chirality of the edge states and, therefore, the direction of ∇Tint . As a result, Vx2ω(≈ρxyαxxΔTint) is symmetric in B. While the charge current is carried by both bulk and edge states when the Fermi level is in the delocalized states of a Landau level, as also assumed in the calculations in (32), which reproduce our data, the heat current is mostly carried by the edge states, so that ΔxT≈0 even in the Landau levels. We note that N chiral edge states from the filled Landau levels are present when the Fermi level is in the N-th Landau level, where Vx2ω oscillates. There is no indication that ΔxT≠0 , which would mix Sxx into the data and make the sign-changing oscillations in Vx2ω asymmetric. The central role of the chiral edge states in heat transport has also been pointed out in (33, 34).

Further confirmation that independent chiral currents with different temperatures control ∇Tint comes from measurements of the Nernst voltage, Vy2ω . Results for Vy2ω are shown in Fig. 4 for different values and directions of B. Here, Vy2ω was measured at 10 K to obtain a better signal-to-noise ratio. As shown in fig. S6, while the magnitude of Vy2ω continues to increase with decreasing temperature, it also becomes very noisy. This is likely due to limitations in the cooling power of the cryostat at low temperatures. The increase in noise is not as visible in the Vx2ω data, due to the larger signal. As the chemical potential moves through the center of a Landau level, Vy2ω shows peaks, whose sign depends on the direction of B. The fact that peaks are observed, rather than the sign-changing oscillations predicted by GJ (Eq. 4), confirms the interpretation that the edge states cause ∇Tint to be normal to ∇Text . For an isotropic system, with Eqs. 3 and 4, ΔTint=ΔyT and ΔxT=0, Eq. 6 simplifies to Vy2ω≈−ρxyαxyΔTint . Thus, Vy2ω reflects the behavior of αxy calculated by GJ, which peaks when the Fermi level is positioned at the center of the Landau levels, exactly what is observed here. Moreover, while the product of the two functions, −ρxyαxy , is symmetric in B, reversing the sign of B causes the direction of ∇Tint to flip, due to the chirality of the edge states. As a result, Vy2ω is antisymmetric in B, further confirming the interpretation that the different temperatures of the chiral edge states control ∇Tint . As shown in fig. S7 and table S1, the data shown in Fig. 4 can be used to estimate ΔTint , if we assume that the maxima of Vy2ω are fully quantized [see, e.g., (1)]. With this assumption, ΔTint was found to be ~5 mK, slightly less than ΔText (~12 mK). When B is oriented in the positive direction, Vy2ω shows some intermixing with αxx , as can be seen from the fact that there are minor sign-changing oscillations. No mixing is observed when B is oriented in the negative direction. As mentioned above, Vx2ω does not exhibit any mixing of signals for either B direction (see also fig. S5). It is possible that the higher measurement temperature for Vy2ω leads to some bulk heat transport and a nonzero ∇xT , but why this is only the case for one direction of B, and not the other, requires further studies.

Moving away from the quantum Hall regime, we briefly discuss the thermopower near the charge neutrality gap (marked by red stars in Fig. 2). There are no chiral edge states in the gap, and we expect ∇Tint and ∇Text to point in the same direction (a pair of helical edge states that may be present in the topological gap at B = 0 would not change this). At B = 0, Vx2ω changes sign from negative to positive as VTG is tuned through the gap from the electron side to the hole side, which is the expected behavior for the thermopower voltage. As can be seen more clearly from Fig. 3, Vx2ω passes through zero at the charge neutrality point, where Rxx peaks, which is the expected behavior. The thermopower voltage remains positive at large, negative VTG, reflecting the sign of the majority carriers (holes). The sign change in Vx2ω as VTG is tuned through the gap persists at finite B, which can be understood from Eqs. 1 and 5: ρxx≫ρxy in the gap (where ρxy goes through zero), so Vx2ω=SxxΔxT≈ρxxαxxΔxT reflects the expected sign change of αxx at charge neutrality (note that αxx is proportional to the energy derivative of the longitudinal conductivity). The extrema of Vx2ω have their lowest absolute values at 9 T, which is close to Bc, reflecting the gap closing and decrease in ρxx (see Fig. 3).

The behavior of the Nernst voltage in the gap is unexpected (see Fig. 4). From Eqs. 2 and 6, Vy2ω=−(ρxxαxy+ρxyαxx)ΔxT . In general, αxy is expected to vanish in the gap, while ρxy passes through zero. The fact that we observe a sign change of Vy2ω as the chemical potential crosses the gap may indicate that the second term controls the behavior of Vy2ω . At 9 T, close to the gap closing, Vy2ω shows (almost) a single peak that reverses sign upon reversal of B, indicating that ρxxαxy dominates the response, similar to graphene (16). More puzzling is the sign alternating Nernst voltage in the gap at B = 0 (Fig. 4D). This is not an anomalous Nernst effect, because Cd3As2 is nonmagnetic and αxy should vanish in the gap. One possible explanation is signal mixing (see also fig. S8). We leave understanding the behavior of the Vy2ω signal at B = 0 to future investigations.

DISCUSSION

To summarize, we have shown that thermoelectric responses provide remarkably clear evidence that chiral edge states control the internal temperature gradient and that they are the main heat conducting channels. The clarity of the results is likely due to the fact that phonon-related effects are minimized in Cd3As2 quantum wells. Not only is the thermal conductivity of Cd3As2 intrinsically very low (39–41), but embedding quantum wells in III-V layers, which have a different crystal structure, also likely leads to strong interface phonon scattering (42). Poor electron-phonon coupling results in the edge channels being the main heat conducting channels and makes cooling of the edge channels by interactions with the phonons in the bulk states inefficient. In contrast, in materials such as graphene, edge channels cool more efficiently (33), and the temperature gradient set up by external heaters can take over. We note, however, that signs of rotation of the thermal gradient due to chiral edge states, as observed here, can also be seen in data published in the literature (34, 43, 44). The thermoelectric signals observed here are an excellent match with theoretical predictions, as a result of a combination of well-defined thermal gradients, adiabatic conditions, and the excellent thermoelectric properties of Cd3As2, including its poor thermal conductivity. For example, we observe the expected zero thermoelectric response in the Hall plateaus, rarely seen (18) in data in the literature [see, e.g., (16, 34)].

Our findings open up many interesting applications to modulate and control temperature profiles at the nanoscale. For example, local electric gates can be used to control the edge states and thereby the direction of internal temperature gradients and thermoelectric responses, which may have interesting applications in quantum heat engines and device cooling. The findings could also prove to be highly useful in the study of other topological systems that host chiral edge states, such as chiral spin liquids or chiral topological superconductors, where the detection of chiral edge channels could serve as a definitive signature (45), or quantum anomalous Hall systems, where edge channel conduction has recently been called into question (46, 47). As we have shown here, thermoelectric responses can serve as signatures for the presence of chiral edge states via their effects on local, internal thermal gradients, resulting in the apparent switching of Nernst and Seebeck responses, as observed in this study. Since thermoelectric voltages are much easier to measure than, for example, thermal transport coefficients, it would be worthwhile to conduct thermoelectric measurements in these systems where chiral edge states have proven to be more elusive, using the measurement configuration used here.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample

The sample investigated in this study is an epitaxial, 20-nm-thin quantum well of (001) Cd3As2 grown by molecular beam epitaxy on an Al0.45In0.55Sb buffer layer on a (001)-oriented GaSb substrate, described in detail elsewhere (48, 49). Gated thermoelectric Hall bars (50 μm by 1500 μm) were patterned using standard photolithography and lift-off techniques. Device mesas were defined with argon ion milling. On-chip microheaters, a thermistor strip, and ohmic pads were deposited via electron beam deposition of Ti/Au (20/200 nm). Devices were treated with a low-power oxygen plasma prior to deposition of an Al2O3 gate dielectric via atomic layer deposition at 120°C. The dielectric was etched from the ohmic pads with dilute tetramethylammonium hydroxide. Last, a gate electrode of Ni/Au (5/150 nm) was deposited via thermal evaporation. An optical microscope image of the device is shown in Fig. 1B.

Measurements

Magnetotransport measurements were conducted in a Quantum Design DynaCool system with a base temperature of 2 K. Four-probe resistance measurements were performed using low-frequency ac lock-in techniques at 17.777 Hz. For thermoelectric measurements, an ac current of frequency ω ~ 4 Hz was applied to an on-chip microheater, which produced an alternating temperature gradient at frequency 2ω. The resulting longitudinal ( Vx2ω ) and transverse ( Vy2ω ) thermoelectric voltages were monitored with lock-in amplifiers at 2ω (20, 50). The chip was equipped with thermistors, which allowed for an estimate of ΔText (see the Supplementary Materials). ΔText was estimated to be ~12 mK, which is much smaller than the bath temperature. In the Supplementary Materials, we also estimate ΔTint , which was found to be somewhat smaller than ΔText . The thermoelectric voltages are proportional to the Seebeck and Nernst coefficients, e.g., Vi2ω=SijΔjT . Additional details can be found in the Supplementary Materials.
