Role of comprehensive HIV knowledge as a mediator between receiving HIV information during antenatal care visits and positive attitudes toward people living with HIV among reproductive-age women in Bangladesh
Authors: Chowdhury Abdullah Al Asif, Md. Abul Kalam, Nayla Ferdousi Haque, Sabrina Karim, Lima Rahman, Muhammad Imran, Paul Bouey, Rounak Khan, Mohammad Rifat Haider
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveComprehensive knowledge of HIV transmission and positive attitudes toward people with HIV (PWH) are key to HIV prevention and care. This study assessed the potential mediational role of comprehensive HIV knowledge between receiving HIV information during antenatal care (ANC) visits and positive attitudes toward PWH among reproductive-age women in Bangladesh.MethodsWe analyzed the 2019 Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey data (N = 5118). Comprehensive HIV knowledge scale (9-item; Cronbach's alpha = 0.706) and Attitudes toward PWH scale (2-item; Cronbach's alpha = 0.84) were constructed. Receiving HIV information was a dichotomous variable (yes/no). A multivariable structural equation model was controlled for age, ethnicity, education, rural/urban residence, media exposure, marital status, wealth index, and division. All analyses were performed using Stata 18.0.ResultsAfter adjusting for sociodemographic characteristics, receiving HIV transmission knowledge during ANC was not associated with positive attitudes toward PWH (B = 0.06, p = 0.101). Receiving HIV information was positively associated with comprehensive HIV knowledge (B = 0.39; p < 0.0.001), and comprehensive HIV knowledge was positively associated with positive attitudes toward PWH (B = 0.13; p < 0.001). The indirect effect of comprehensive HIV knowledge on the relationship between receiving HIV information during ANC and positive attitudes toward PWH was statistically significant (B = 0.05, p < 0.001).ConclusionGiven the mediational role of comprehensive HIV knowledge between receiving HIV information and positive attitudes toward PWH, providing HIV information during ANC should be a routine practice to capture the teachable moments to prevent HIV transmission.

Introduction

Bangladesh ranks 8th in the world according to population, is predominantly Muslim and has a low burden of HIV/AIDS (<0.01 %), with 16,000 people living with HIV (PWH) [1]. The HIV epidemic is concentrated among socially marginalized groups, known as ‘Key Populations’, which includes people who use drugs (PWUD), female sex workers (FSW), men who have sex with men (MSM) and transgender women [2]. In recent years, however, the number of new HIV infections among the general population has been rising [2]. In 2023, 27.7 % of new HIV infections occurred among the general population, with 24 % among women and primarily among those of reproductive age (15–49 years) [3]. Women of reproductive age are particularly vulnerable to contracting HIV because of the prevalence of intimate partner violence, lack of autonomy, and less access to health care [[4], [5], [6]]. Bangladeshi women cannot negotiate the use of protective measures, like condoms, and run the risk of getting HIV from male partners [7,8]. Moreover, in 2023, 17.6 % of new HIV infections occurred among the migrant population, and there is a lack of HIV screening upon arrival and departure of these migrants increases the risk of HIV transmission [3,9].

Although Bangladesh made some progress in Joint United Nations Program on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) 95–95-95 indicators (i.e., 95 % aware of their HIV status, 95 % of those are aware of their status are linked to care, and 95 % of those are on care are virally suppressed) [10], it is still lagging with 73 % of PWH aware of their status, 75 % of these individuals linked to HIV care, and 90 % of these persons who are virally suppressed [3]. Bangladesh is lagging far behind in the first two 95 s, which indicates that people are not aware of HIV status, and even after diagnosis, people face stigma and discrimination, which prevent them from getting HIV treatment [11].

The Bangladesh national HIV policy includes imparting HIV information and offering HIV testing at the first antenatal care (ANC) visit [12]. UNICEF data shows that ANC-based HIV testing has increased over the years (from <1 % to 2.5 % in 2022) [13], although the World Health Organization recommends HIV testing among all pregnant women [14]. Providing HIV information to pregnant women is vital to prevent mother-to-child HIV transmission. Moreover, pregnancy is a teachable moment, and mothers can be informed of HIV, and ways to protect them and their children from HIV can be a useful tool for preventing HIV transmission [4,15,16].

Women of reproductive age in Bangladesh lack comprehensive knowledge of HIV and its transmission. Lack of comprehensive knowledge along with conservative social norms may induce discriminatory attitudes toward PWH [17]. UNAIDS defines HIV-related stigma and discrimination as follows: “a process of devaluation of people either living with or associated with HIV and AIDS” [18]. HIV-related stigma is a major deterrent in HIV testing as well as getting linked to care [19]. Almost 45 % of the women of reproductive age in Bangladesh reported that PWH suffer from discriminatory behavior because of their illness [20]. Discriminatory attitudes toward PWH also lead to lower uptake in HIV tests during ANC [21].

Given the interrelationships among the provision of HIV information at ANC, the positive effects of comprehensive HIV knowledge on stigma and discrimination toward PWH, it is plausible to consider that the provision of HIV information/services during ANC will increase comprehensive HIV knowledge among pregnant women and lead to increased positive attitudes toward PWH among pregnant women. Little research has assessed these potential pathways, especially among reproductive-age women in Bangladesh. In this context, this study aims to (1) examine the association between receiving HIV information/service during ANC visits and positive attitudes toward PWH among reproductive-age women in Bangladesh, and (2) assess the mediating role of comprehensive HIV knowledge between receiving HIV information and positive attitudes toward PWH.

Methods

Data source and study variables

We used the most recent data from the Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS) conducted in 2019. MICS is a comprehensive and repeated cross-sectional survey that is multidimensional and nationally representative, carried out by UNICEF. Standardized questionnaires are used in this survey to collect data and identify important variables pertaining to women and children in Bangladesh. It focuses on early childhood development, child nutrition status, maternal and child health treatments, and reproductive health. Additionally, MICS gathers a standard set of socioeconomic data about people and households [22]. The datasets and results are accessible in public domain [23].

Sampling design and sample size

The MICS survey is a two-stage cluster sampling procedure where women of reproductive age are selected randomly and sampling techniques has been described in detail elsewhere [22]. Inclusion criteria for this study were: (1) women of reproductive age (15 to 49 years), (2) currently or ever married, (3) had given birth in the preceding two years of the survey, while exclusion criteria were: (1) had no children, and (2) did not have any response to receiving HIV information and/or testing during ANC. Therefore, the sample size selection process for the study involves several key steps aimed at focusing on married or ever-married women with recent childbirth experiences and understanding their awareness of HIV: A total of 64,377 women were interviewed in the 2019 wave; unmarried women were omitted from this total, resulting in 53,933 married or ever-married women. Subsequently, those without children were excluded, followed by those who hadn't given birth in the last two years, resulting in a final pool of 9285 women. Among them, those aware and unaware of HIV were identified, with 5759 and 3526 women, respectively. Out of 5759 women who were aware of HIV, 641 did not have any response to receiving HIV information and/or testing during ANC. Excluding the non-responses, our final sample size was 5118 (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Schematic diagram of the analytic study sample.Fig. 1

Outcomes

The two outcomes of this analysis are (i) comprehensive knowledge of HIV, and (ii) attitudes toward PWH. In the 2019 MICS survey, respondents who had ever heard of AIDS were asked nine questions relating to HIV knowledge and two questions related to positive attitudes (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). Nine Yes/No knowledge questions include: (1) people can reduce their chance of getting HIV by having just one uninfected sex partner who has no other sex partners; (2) people can get HIV from mosquito bites; (3) people can reduce their chance of contracting HIV by using a condom every time they have sex; (4) One can get HIV by sharing food with a person who has HIV; (5) people can get HIV because of witchcraft or other supernatural means; (6) it is possible for a healthy-looking person to have HIV; (7) HIV can transmit during pregnancy; (8) HIV can transmit during delivery; and (9) HIV can transmit during breastfeeding. For each item “0” represents “wrong,” “don't know,” or “non-response”, and “1” represents the correct answer [24]. We obtained the comprehensive HIV knowledge scale by summing up all nine responses, with a range of 0–9. Cronbach's alpha for the scale was 0.706.

Positive attitudes toward the PWH scale includes two questions: (1) Would buy fresh vegetables from a shopkeeper living with HIV, and (2) children living with HIV should be allowed to attend school with other children [25]. For each item “0” represents “no,” “don't know,” or “non-response”, and “1” represents “yes”. We summed up these two items to obtain the positive attitudes toward PWH scale with a range of 0–2. Cronbach's alpha for the scale was 0.856.

Covariates

Based on previous literature we included different covariates related to demographic, socio-economic, and household characteristics and locations: respondents' age (<30, ≥30 years), education (no education, primary, lower secondary, secondary or higher), ethnicity (other, Bangali), marital status (single/divorced/widowed/separated, married), household wealth quintile (poorest, poorer, middle, richer, richest), location (rural, urban), media exposure (none, at least once a week), division (Barisal, Chattogram, Dhaka, Khulna, Mymensingh, Rajshahi, Rangpur, Sylhet), and received any ANC information/service (no, yes). The household wealth index has been considered a proxy of economic status by considering long-term wealth, including the ownership of consumer goods, housing materials, and the status of water and sanitation [24]. If the women reported that they used any media, i.e., radio, TV, newspaper, and internet, at least once a week, the response was coded as “1”, and otherwise it was coded as “0”. If the women reported receiving any HIV information/service during ANC visits, e.g., HIV can transmit from mother to baby, things to do to avoid HIV, offer of an HIV test, and testing for HIV, the response was coded as “1” (yes), and if they did not receive any HIV information/service during ANC visits, the response was coded as “0” (no).

Statistical analysis

We conducted univariate, bivariate, and multivariable analyses for this study. The univariate analysis of each of the selected covariates and outcome variables was shown by adjusting the sampling weight of the survey (Table 1). Table 1 also shows a bi-variate analysis between covariates and outcome variables, where we used t-tests for binary covariates and F-tests for covariates with multiple categories to see significant differences in outcomes. We conducted path analysis to determine the association between receiving HIV information during ANC visits (exposure), comprehensive HIV knowledge (mediator), and positive attitudes toward PWH (outcome), both crude and adjusted for sociodemographic characteristics. We obtained direct and indirect standardized estimates between HIV information during ANC, comprehensive HIV knowledge, and attitudes toward PWH (Table 2 and Fig. 2). The standardized beta coefficients derived from the adjusted models reflect the change in the standard deviation of the outcome variable (attitudes toward PHW) for a one standard deviation change in the predictor variables (HIV information during ANC and comprehensive HIV knowledge, holding other variables constant. If the indirect effect is significant, we can conclude that comprehensive HIV knowledge mediates the relationship between HIV information during ANC and attitudes toward PWH. Ethics approval

All the data used in this study came from the public domain health survey datasets from the MICS 2019, which are accessible online and have had all personally identifiable information removed. The Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics (BBS) and UNICEF both reviewed and approved the MICS techniques. During the interview process, individuals gave their informed consent. Since this study used secondary data for analysis, it was deemed exempt from needing IRB approval.

Results

This study included 5118 MICS 2019 survey respondents, who were currently married, had a child within last two years, and heard about HIV (Fig. 1). Table 1 shows the distribution of sociodemographic characteristics, comprehensive HIV knowledge, and positive attitudes toward PWH among women of reproductive age in Bangladesh. The majority of the women were < 30 years old (76.2 %), had a secondary or higher level of education (61.7 %), were of Bangali ethnicity (99.3 %), were currently married (99.4 %), lived in rural areas (73.8 %), had media exposure at least once a week (78.9 %), and did not receive any HIV information/service during ANC visits (86.7 %).

A total of 69.5 % of women correctly believed that having one uninfected sex partner reduces the chance of getting HIV, while 66.1 % recognized that using a condom every time during sex could also reduce the risk. However, misconceptions persisted, with 53.6 % wrongly believing that HIV can be transmitted through mosquito bites. Only 11.3 % of women correctly answered all HIV knowledge questions, underscoring a gap in comprehensive understanding. Despite this, positive attitudes toward PWH were moderately prevalent. For instance, 51.8 % of women expressed willingness to buy fresh vegetables from an HIV-positive shopkeeper, and 58.1 % believed children living with HIV should attend school with other children. Yet, only 49.0 % held consistently positive attitudes across all items. In the bivariate analyses, there were statistically significant differences in comprehensive HIV knowledge and positive attitudes toward PWH among women by age, education, wealth quintile, location, media exposure, division, and receiving HIV information during ANC visits (Table 1).

There was a statistically significant association between receiving HIV information during ANC visits and positive attitudes toward PWH among women (β = 0.13; p = 0.002). However, the association attenuated to be not statistically significant after including the mediator (comprehensive HIV knowledge) in the model. Table 2 shows the direct and indirect standardized estimates between receiving HIV information during ANC visits, comprehensive HIV knowledge, and positive attitudes toward PWH. After adjusting for sociodemographic characteristics, receiving HIV information during ANC visits was positively associated with comprehensive HIV knowledge (β = 0.39; p < 0.001), and comprehensive HIV knowledge was positively associated with positive attitudes toward PWH (β = 0.13; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). The indirect estimate suggests that comprehensive HIV knowledge fully mediated the association between receiving HIV information during ANC visits and positive attitudes toward PWH among women (β = 0.05; p < 0.001). The results indicate a moderate indirect pathway between receiving HIV information during ANC and attitudes toward PWH through comprehensive HIV knowledge. It signifies that receiving HIV information during ANC visits leads to better comprehensive HIV knowledge, which in turn is associated with slightly more positive attitudes toward PWH. The mediation effect is statistically significant and suggests that comprehensive HIV knowledge is a key mediator linking health awareness during pregnancy to stigma reduction outcomes among women of reproductive age in Bangladesh. Both crude and adjusted models had excellent fit.

Discussion

The study findings suggest that receiving HIV information during antenatal care (ANC) visits is positively associated with comprehensive HIV knowledge, which in turn leads to more positive attitudes toward people living with HIV (PWH) among reproductive-age women in Bangladesh. Additionally, it assessed the mediating role of comprehensive HIV knowledge in this relationship. The findings reveal important insights into how health information disseminated during ANC visits can influence attitudes toward PWH and highlight the pivotal role of comprehensive HIV knowledge in shaping these attitudes.

The results demonstrated a statistically significant association between receiving HIV information during ANC visits and positive attitudes toward PWH among women (β = 0.13; p = 0.002). This finding aligns with previous literature that emphasizes the role of education and information dissemination in shaping attitudes toward PWH. A systematic review found that educational interventions significantly improved attitudes toward PWH among women, highlighting the potential of targeted health education programs during ANC visits [26]. Furthermore, a systematic review indicated that integrating HIV education into routine health services can effectively reduce HIV-related stigma and discrimination [27] . This suggests that ANC visits serve as a critical touchpoint for disseminating HIV-related information that can foster positive attitudes toward PWH. However, when comprehensive HIV knowledge was included in the model, this association was no longer statistically significant, indicating a mediating effect. This finding underscores the importance of comprehensive HIV knowledge as an intermediary that influences attitudes toward PWH. Similar research in Bangladesh demonstrated that increased HIV knowledge was associated with reduced stigma and discrimination against PWH, reinforcing the critical role of comprehensive HIV knowledge in fostering positive attitudes [28].

The mediation analysis provided further clarity. Receiving HIV information during ANC visits was significantly associated with comprehensive HIV knowledge (β = 0.39; p < 0.001), suggesting that ANC visits are effective in enhancing women's understanding of HIV. Similar results were found in Zambia where antenatal HIV education significantly improved HIV-related knowledge and attitudes among pregnant women [29]. This could be due to the structured environment of ANC visits, where health professionals can deliver targeted and clear information. Comprehensive HIV knowledge, in turn, was significantly associated with positive attitudes toward PWH (β = 0.13; p < 0.001). This indicates that women who possess a thorough understanding of HIV are more likely to hold compassionate and non-discriminatory attitudes toward those living with the virus.

The indirect effect of receiving HIV information on positive attitudes through comprehensive HIV knowledge was also significant (β = 0.05; p < 0.001), confirming that comprehensive HIV knowledge fully mediated the relationship between receiving HIV information during ANC visits and positive attitudes toward PWH. A study in sub-Saharan Africa highlighted that HIV knowledge significantly mediates the relationship between exposure to health information and attitudes toward PWH [30]. Similar findings were reported by Oginni et al. (2017), who emphasized the critical role of comprehensive knowledge in shaping attitudes toward PWH [31] . This full mediation suggests that the primary mechanism through which ANC-based HIV information influences attitudes is by enhancing comprehensive knowledge. The results also align with findings from studies conducted in Nigeria where HIV education during routine health visits improved HIV knowledge and reduced stigma [32]. Similarly, a study conducted in Kenya found that community-based HIV education interventions significantly improved knowledge and attitudes toward PWH [33]. These studies underscore the potential of ANC visits and other routine health service touchpoints as platforms for effective HIV education. Moreover, the findings from this study are consistent with the results of research conducted in other regions. In rural Ethiopia, comprehensive HIV knowledge significantly mediated the relationship between receiving HIV information and positive attitudes toward PWH [34]. Similarly, Nyblade et al. (2019) highlighted the role of comprehensive HIV knowledge in reducing stigma and fostering positive attitudes toward PWH in Vietnam [35]. Structured and well-delivered health information during ANC visits can have a lasting impact on HIV-related attitudes and behaviors. For example, studies in Tanzania and South Africa have indicated that ANC-based HIV education not only improves knowledge but also increases the uptake of HIV testing and prevention measures [36,37].

The implications of these findings are significant for public health strategies. To reduce stigma and discrimination against PWH, it is crucial to ensure that HIV information provided during ANC visits is comprehensive and understandable. Health professionals should be trained to deliver HIV education effectively, ensuring that women not only receive information but also comprehend and internalize it. This could involve using clear, culturally appropriate language, providing opportunities for women to ask questions, and offering written materials for further reference.

Some limitations of the study warrant mention. Due to the cross-sectional nature of the data, we could not assess the causal relationship. Obtaining HIV information during ANC is self-reported and subject to recall bias. There was some missing information regarding getting HIV information during ANC. However, we used a nationally representative data from reproductive-age women who had childbirth during last two years, which mitigates the recall bias and increases the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the present study highlights the significant role of ANC visits as a platform for disseminating HIV-related knowledge and information and improving attitudes toward PWH among women of reproductive age in Bangladesh. The full mediation by comprehensive HIV knowledge underscores the necessity of ensuring that HIV information provided during ANC visits is thorough and comprehensive. These findings have important implications for public health strategies aimed at reducing stigma and discrimination against PWH, suggesting that enhancing the content and delivery of HIV education during ANC visits could be a key component in these efforts. Integrating HIV education into ANC visits is crucial for reducing stigma and discrimination against people living with HIV (PWH). By enhancing the content and delivery of HIV information during these visits, healthcare systems can provide accurate details about HIV transmission, prevention, and treatment, empowering women to make informed health decisions. Regularly including HIV education in ANC visits normalizes discussions about the virus, reducing fear and misconceptions and fostering a more accepting attitude toward PWH. This approach encourages more women to get tested and seek treatment, improving health outcomes and reducing virus transmission. Leveraging existing healthcare infrastructure for HIV education enhances ANC services and supports broader public health goals of reducing HIV-related stigma and improving the quality of life for PWH.
