EEG β oscillations in aberrant data perception under cognitive load modulation
Authors: Haihong Yu, Wei Cao, Tie Fang, Jia Jin, Guanxiong Pei
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractData-driven decision making (DDDM) is becoming an indispensable component of work across various fields, and the perception of aberrant data (PAD) has emerged as an essential skill. Nonetheless, the neural processing mechanisms underpinning PAD remain incompletely elucidated. Direct evidence linking neural oscillations to PAD is currently lacking, and the impact of cognitive load remains ambiguous. We address this issue using EEG time-frequency analysis. Data were collected from 21 healthy participants. The experiment employed a 2 (low vs. high cognitive load) × 2 [PAD+ (aberrant data accurately identified as aberrant) vs. PAD- (non-aberrant data correctly recognized as normal)] within-subject laboratory design. Results indicate that upper β band oscillations (26–30 Hz) were significantly enhanced in the PAD + condition compared to PAD-, with consistent activity observed in the frontal (p < 0.001, = 0.41) and parietal lobes (p = 0.028, = 0.22) within the 300–350 ms time window. Additionally, as cognitive load increased, the time window of β oscillations for distinguishing PAD+ from PAD- shifted earlier. This study enriches our understanding of the PAD neural basis by exploring the distribution of neural oscillation frequencies, decision-making neural circuits, and the windowing effect induced by cognitive load. These findings have significant implications for elucidating the pathological mechanisms of neurodegenerative disorders, as well as in the initial screening, intervention, and treatment of diseases.Keywords: Cognitive load, Aberrant data perception, Frontal and parietal lobes, High-frequency β oscillationsSubject terms: Human behaviour, Cognitive neuroscience

Introduction

Data is becoming the cornerstone of societal development. We are progressively entering an era of artificial intelligence characterized by model-based, computation-intensive, and data-driven approaches1,2. Data has evolved from merely being a repository of information to serving as the foundation for decision-making processes3. From stock analysts and sales managers to healthcare professionals and factory operators, data-driven decision-making (DDDM) has become indispensable4. The ability to identify aberrant data has become a crucial skill in the workplace, facilitating the optimization of business processes, enhancement of user experiences, and improvement in productivity. However, how the human brain perceives aberrant data and whether this perceptual ability varies across different task intensities remain questions that require further investigation.

The perception of aberrant data (PAD) is the ability to recognize data information that significantly deviates from expected norms or standards, which is crucial for individual social functioning and cognitive decision-making5. Cognitive activities related to PAD include attention regulation6, cognitive conflict7, information integration8, and vigilance maintenance8, thereby enabling the timely detection and effective processing of aberrant data. Neural oscillations, which are rhythmic or repetitive neuronal activities present in the central nervous system, are extensively involved in these cognitive processes9,10. Research typically focuses on predefined canonical frequency band ranges that are thought to capture distinct oscillations. However, there is currently a lack of direct evidence linking specific frequency bands of neural oscillations with PAD.

β oscillations are closely associated with endogenous, top-down control processing, and there is a potential coupling relationship with the cognitive processing of PAD9,11,12. β oscillations are commonly linked with motor functions, exhibiting particularly pronounced activity during steady contractions, being attenuated by voluntary movements and greatest during holding periods following movements9,13,14. Furthermore, research has confirmed the important role of β oscillations in various cognitive functions related to attention regulation15,16, violation of expectations17, information integration18, cognitive resource allocation19, and vigilance maintenance20, which is highly consistent with the cognitive activities involved in PAD. However, one persistent mystery regarding β oscillations is their direct relationship with PAD, which remains unclear. The lack of objective evidence has led to ambiguous arguments, hindering further research in this field. Therefore, the primary objective of this study is to explore the relationship between them. We hypothesize that β oscillations can be used to characterize and distinguish different conditions of PAD processing.

The frontal and parietal lobes are considered key regions for PAD processing21,22; hence, β oscillations in relevant brain areas deserve special attention. Frontal β oscillations are known to mirror cognitive processes associated with stimulus evaluation and decision-making9,16,23. Meanwhile, β oscillations in the parietal regions are pivotal for functions such as attention allocation, cognitive control, and sensory integration24,25. The present study posits that PAD may induce joint activity of β oscillations within both the frontal and parietal regions.

Moreover, prior investigations have demonstrated that the coherence of β oscillations between frontal and parietal cortices signifies decision-making circuits spanning cortical regions, particularly prominent during situations involving free choice26. Additionally, in line with the “communication through coherence” hypothesis12, oscillations within the β frequency band may facilitate cross-talk between frontal and parietal cortices27. This study aims to investigate the consistency of β oscillations within the frontal-parietal regions, thus offering a framework for probing neural circuits underlying decision-making and communication mechanisms within the context of PAD.

As the integration of DDDM into work processes becomes more entrenched and the complexity of tasks escalates, the cognitive load placed on workers in managing substantial data volumes also intensifies. This heightened cognitive load has the potential to impact their abilities in perceiving aberrant data28,29. Previous studies have demonstrated that cognitive load, defined as the total amount of psychological resources required by the brain to execute specific tasks, significantly influences individuals’ abilities in anomaly perception, reaction time, and perceptual accuracy30–32. Under conditions of high cognitive load, individuals may experience compromised information processing abilities. Research suggests that excessive cognitive load can lead to delayed reactions to abnormal events and may even elevate the likelihood of overlooking such occurrences33. Complex tasks that require a significant amount of attention and memory resources may limit the ability to notice unexpected events or data34, and may also impair individuals’ situational awareness33,35. This implies that with an increase in cognitive load, the efficiency of perceiving anomalies may decrease. Given the conclusions in the aforementioned studies regarding the impact of cognitive load on PAD, we hypothesize that as cognitive load levels increase, the accuracy of PAD will decrease. Additionally, the β oscillation time window used to differentiate between varied PAD conditions may be delayed.

In summary, we aim to elucidate the direct association between β oscillations and PAD, while also examining the moderating effect of cognitive load on this relationship. We collected behavioral and EEG data from participants engaged in PAD task performance, manipulating cognitive load levels. Three primary scientific research inquiries addressed in this study include: (1) the potential utility of β oscillations in characterizing and distinguishing various conditions of PAD processing, (2) the presence of co-activation of β oscillations in frontoparietal regions during PAD and the consistency of this response, and (3) whether, under varying levels of cognitive load modulation, the temporal window of β oscillations in differentiating PAD conditions shifts. Addressing these questions contributes to a deeper understanding of neural oscillations, decision-making neural circuits and communication mechanisms, and temporal dynamics involved in PAD.

Methods

Participants

Twenty-one participants, comprising 10 males and 11 females aged 22 to 26 years (mean age = 23.86 years, SD = 1.24 years), were recruited from Ningbo University for this study. All participants were right-handed, in good health, and with normal or corrected-to-normal vision. They had no history of neurological diseases or adverse lifestyle habits, and they did not experience physical discomfort during the experiment. Prior to participation, individuals were instructed to avoid substances that could affect the central nervous system, refrain from strenuous physical activity and prolonged mental exertion, and ensure they were alert and rested. All participants underwent pre-experimental training, provided written informed consent, and were compensated upon completion of the experiment. This study received approval from the ethics committee and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki36.

Materials

The experiment employed a 2 (cognitive load: low vs. high) × 2 [PAD: PAD+ (aberrant data accurately identified as aberrant) vs. PAD- (non-aberrant data correctly recognized as normal)] within-subject laboratory design. This design comprised two blocks, each consisting of 120 trials, resulting in a total of 240 trials conducted during the experiment.

The block 1 aimed to evaluate participants’ ability to judge aberrant data under low cognitive load. Participants were presented with stimuli featuring four numbers and were tasked with identifying any number exceeding a predefined threshold indicating anomaly. The four numbers were positioned at the corners of a 3 cm × 3 cm square without displaying the square’s border. In the block 2, cognitive demand was increased by presenting stimuli with five numbers, requiring participants to perform a similar task of detecting aberrant perceptions. Five numbers were placed at both the corners and the center of a 3 cm × 3 cm square. The experiment set the threshold for aberrant detection at 80. Numbers were randomly drawn from a range of 20–99, ensuring that, within each block, half of the stimuli did not feature aberrant numbers, while the remaining half contained precisely one aberrant number (exceeding the threshold). Additionally, the distribution of the aberrant number across the stimuli was uniform, ensuring an equal probability of its occurrence in any designated position.

In each block, participants encountered two categories of stimuli: those containing aberrant data and those devoid of any aberrations. Their task was to discern the presence of aberrations, resulting in four potential outcomes: aberrant data correctly identified as aberrant, aberrant data incorrectly perceived as normal, non-aberrant data incorrectly perceived as aberrant, and non-aberrant data correctly recognized as normal. Aligned with the objectives of this study, our primary focus was on two specific scenarios: (1) aberrant data accurately identified as aberrant, referred to as PAD+; and (2) non-aberrant data correctly recognized as normal, referred to as PAD-.

In the experiment, the stimuli were presented on a 21-inch ViewSonic CRT monitor (ViewSonic, Inc., Walnut, CA). Digits were strategically positioned within a confined 3 cm × 3 cm area to ensure their placement within the participants’ foveal vision field, which spans approximately a 5° angle around the visual field’s center. At a viewing distance of 70 cm, this visual angle translates to a visual field diameter of approximately 3.67 cm and an area of about 10.56 square centimeters. This design aimed to enable participants to assimilate the visual information of all numbers in a single glance, thereby obviating the necessity for ocular scanning.

Procedure

To prepare for the experiment, participants were briefed on the study’s procedures and objectives, and written consent was obtained. They were also instructed on the experimental tasks and provided with necessary guidance, followed by essential pre-experimental training. Subsequently, assistance was provided for scalp cleansing and hair drying. Once completed, the electrode cap was fitted, and conductive gel was carefully applied at each electrode’s contact point with the scalp (Fig. 1).

Experimental flowchart.

In the formal experiment, there are three sub-stages with a total duration of approximately 45 min. Each stage is controlled using E-prime software (version 3.0, Psychology Software Tools, Inc., Sharpsburg, PA). In sub-stage A, participants complete block 1 of the task under a low cognitive load condition (see Fig. 2A). In each trial, participants first see a “+” sign in the center of the screen as a focal point of attention, which lasts for a random duration between 600 and 800 ms. A blank screen then appears for a random duration between 600 and 800 ms, followed by a stimulus image displaying four numbers in the center of the screen, which remains visible until a response is made or 2000 ms has elapsed. Participants’ task is to quickly determine if there is any aberrant data greater than 80 in the stimulus, and respond by pressing the designated key (the key assignment is counterbalanced across participants). After pressing the key, the stimulus image disappears, and the response time is automatically recorded. Then follows a random interval lasting between 1000 and 1500 milliseconds before the next trial begins. This sub-stage lasts approximately 10 min in total.

(A) Schematic diagram of trial procedure for low cognitive load experiment (block 1). (B) Illustration of mental arithmetic task used to increase subjects’ cognitive load. (C) Schematic diagram of trial procedure for high cognitive load experiment (block 2).

In sub-stage B, participants complete a mental arithmetic task designed to increase cognitive load37, in order to meet the higher cognitive load requirements for block 2. Participants are required to complete 30 mental arithmetic problems within approximately 12–15 min, with each problem consisting of four numbers and three basic operators (e.g., 16 × 7 − 53 + 12=?) (Fig. 2B). In Sub-stage C, participants engage in Block 2 of the task, which incorporates two modifications designed to sustain an elevated cognitive load (Fig. 2C). Initially, a mental arithmetic task, specifically mathematical calculations, precedes the presentation of the stimulus image, thereby increasing the cognitive load. Subsequently, the stimulus images for this stage contain five numbers, one more than those in Block 1, further intensifying the cognitive demands imposed by the task. This sub-stage lasts approximately 20 min. It should also be noted that the presentation order of stimulus images within each stage is randomized. Prior to the formal experiment, participants undergo 6 trials to adapt to the experimental task. Researchers advise participants to minimize eye movements and body movements during the recording phase.

Electroencephalogram (EEG) recording and preprocessing

The experiment utilized the Neuroscan (Compumedics Neuroscan, Charlotte, NC) and Curry 8 (Compumedics Neuroscan, Charlotte, NC) platforms to synchronously collect EEG data (Fig. 3A). Behavioral responses, including participants’ keypress choices (RESP) and reaction times (RT), were recorded in real-time into the E-DataAid module of E-prime 3.0 (E-Prime Psychology Software Tools Inc., Pittsburgh, USA). EEG signals were collected from 64 scalp sites using Ag/AgCl electrodes fitted within an elastic cap. The online reference electrode was positioned at FCz, with the ground electrode at AFz. Vertical electrooculography (EOG) data were obtained from electrodes placed 1 cm above and below the left eye, and horizontal electrooculography (HEOG) from the outer canthus of both the left and right eyes. Both EEG and EOG signals were bandpass filtered between 0.1 and 40 Hz and sampled at 250 Hz (Fig. 3B). Electrode impedance was kept below 5 kΩ.

(A) Core equipment and software used for EEG data acquisition; (B) The preprocessing of EEG data; (C) Fundamental components of EEG data analysis.

Time-frequency analysis, including preprocessing, utilized MATLAB scripts (R2021a, The MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA) within the EEGLAB toolbox (v2022.1, Swartz Center for Computational Neuroscience, University of California San Diego, San Diego, CA) framework. The continuous EEG data were initially band-pass filtered within the frequency range of 0.1 to 40 Hz and then re-referenced to the average mastoid. Data were then segmented from 1500 milliseconds pre-target appearance to 2000 milliseconds post-target, excluding segments that either met behavioral exclusion criteria or deviated by more than 5 standard deviations from the mean probability distribution38. The rationale for excluding data beyond 5 standard deviations is that such extreme deviations are likely due to noise or recording errors rather than meaningful neural activity. By using this threshold, we aimed to retain all intrinsic data, including individual-specific variations, while eliminating external noise artifacts. Independent components (ICs) analysis was performed using EEGLAB to identify and remove blink, eye movement, and muscle artifacts, ensuring data integrity through manual screening. On average, 237.26 trials per participant were retained, accounting for 98.86% of the total 240 trials. Owing to the high data quality, preprocessed datasets from all participants were included in further analyses.

Behavior and EEG data analysis

First, we analyzed the behavioral data from the experiment. We conducted a repeated measures ANOVA to investigate the main effects and interaction effects of cognitive load and PAD on reaction time and error rate.

Following this, we performed time-frequency decomposition and analysis of the EEG data from each trial across conditions. The time-frequency representations (TFRs) were generated for frequencies ranging from 1 Hz to 30 Hz, decomposed in 50 logarithmically spaced steps. This process involved multiplying the EEG signal’s power spectrum, obtained via Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), by the power spectrum of a complex Morlet wavelet (ei2πtfe−t2/(2α2)). In these equations, t denotes time, f denotes frequency, and σ represents the width of each frequency band, adjusted to 3 to 7 cycles in logarithmic intervals to optimize the balance between time and frequency resolution. An inverse FFT was then applied. Power was defined as the modulus of the resultant complex signal z: . The TFRs for each participant under each condition were averaged, converted to a decibel (dB) scale, and normalized at each frequency using baseline activity (− 300 ms to − 100 ms before the target) with the formula: dB power = 10*log10 (power/baseline), ensuring a concise, accurate, and coherent presentation suitable for academic publication.

Drawing on preliminary research that links β oscillations closely with tasks related to the PAD20,39, our study concentrated on analyzing the average amplitude of β-band oscillatory activity through repeated measures ANOVA (Fig. 3C). We further categorized the β-band into low frequency (L-β 13–20 Hz), mid frequency (M-β 21–25 Hz), and high frequency (H-β 26–30 Hz). We designated the prefrontal Fz and parietal Pz regions as our regions of interest, as these areas are associated with the cognitive functions required for PAD21,39–42. Our analysis focused on the window of 300 ms to 600 ms. We extracted average data at intervals of 50 ms and 100 ms for multiple rounds of repeated measures ANOVA. The analysis incorporated the Greenhouse-Geisser correction to adjust degrees of freedom.

Results

Behavioral results

Given the five comparisons conducted in response time analysis, we have adopted the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR control method to adjust the thresholds of individual p-values accordingly. The adjusted result is equivalent to setting the threshold at 0.03.

Repeated measures ANOVA indicated that participants’ average response time did not significantly vary with cognitive load, showing no notable difference [F(1, 20) = 1, p = 0.32, = 0.05] between average response times under low cognitive load (M = 1338.5 ms, SD = 136.05) and high cognitive load conditions (M = 1367.6 ms, SD = 123.16). A significant main effect for PAD was observed [F(1, 20) = 37, p < 0.001, = 0.65], with participants demonstrating shorter average response times when aberrant data was perceived (PAD+) (M = 1304.8 ms, SD = 121.7) compared to when it was not (PAD-) (M = 1401.3 ms, SD = 120.52), (Fig. 4A).

The results also indicated an interaction between cognitive load and PAD [F(1, 20) = 17.52, p < 0.001, = 0.47]. Discussion

This study conducted primary investigations around three key questions through the analysis of behavior and brain neural oscillations: (1) In terms of frequency-specificity, we elucidated the close association between H-β oscillations and PAD, providing insight into their potential utility in distinguishing different PAD conditions. (2) Regarding regional specificity, we elucidated the co-activation and consistency of responses within the frontoparietal regions during the PAD processing. (3) Concerning time-specificity, we delineated the cognitive load-induced window shifting effect of H-β oscillations for PAD recognition.

Previous research has underscored the intimate relationship between β oscillations and endogenous, top-down control processes, highlighting their pivotal role9,11,12. Specifically, frontal β oscillations have been implicated in cognitive activities associated with stimulus evaluation and decision-making processes9. While this theoretical framework provided the basis for our study, direct evidence linking β oscillations to PAD was lacking. This study, for the first time, confirms a close association between β oscillations in the frontal Fz region and individual PAD. Crucially, this effect was observed only in the H-β frequency band (26–30 Hz), with significantly increased H-β power during the PAD + condition compared to the PAD- condition. The augmentation of frontal H-β oscillations underscores its pivotal role in the cognitive control and decision-making processes associated with PAD.

Another significant finding of this study is the close association between H-β (26–30 Hz) oscillations in the parietal Pz region and PAD. Power values exhibited a significant increase during PAD + compared to PAD-. Positioned centrally within the parietal lobe, the Pz electrode serves as a pivotal site where β oscillations play a critical role in attention allocation, cognitive control, and sensory integration processes24,25. This underscores the involvement of multiple cognitive processes in the task of perceiving aberrant data. A central inquiry in systems neuroscience revolves around elucidating how cognitive functions arise from interactions among various brain regions26, especially in the context of complex tasks like PAD, which necessitate integrated actions across disparate anatomical locations in both spatial and temporal dimensions.

From the perspective of interregional connectivity, the consistency of H-β oscillations between the frontal and parietal cortices may mirror dynamic interactions of neural populations at different spatial scales between these two brain regions9, implicating specific decision-making neural circuits. Previous research has demonstrated heightened coherence in the frontal-parietal β band during periods of free decision-making compared to guided decision-making phases43. Free and guided decisions impose distinct cognitive control demands, akin to the PAD + and PAD- conditions in our current task, where β oscillation coherence denotes the active maintenance of decision circuits and cognitive sets between the frontal and parietal cortices23,26.

From the standpoint of information exchange, the coherence of β oscillations is intricately linked to brain network communication, particularly evident during endogenous, top-down interactions among brain regions12, laying a crucial foundation for synaptic information transmission within this dynamic and highly adaptable mechanism. Previous research has indicated that high-frequency oscillations facilitate functional coupling of neurons over shorter distances compared to low-frequency oscillations44. Whether the frequency of oscillations in the parietal and frontal lobes dictates the specific role of H-β in perceiving anomalous data due to spatial distance remains a question ripe for further investigation.

Moreover, this study revealed an intriguing phenomenon: a moderate increase in cognitive load resulted in a time shift in PAD recognition. Experimental findings demonstrated that as cognitive load intensified, the time window for distinguishing between PAD + and PAD- shifted from the 400–450 ms range of H-β oscillations in the frontal region Fz to 300–350 ms. This indicates a cognitive load-induced temporal advance effect: under low cognitive load, the differentiation between PAD + and PAD- could be made within the 400–450 ms window of H-β power, whereas under high cognitive load, this differentiation shifted to the 300–350 ms window. A similar trend was observed in the analysis of H-β oscillations in the parietal region Pz. We term this phenomenon the cognitive load-induced temporal modulation effect on PAD recognition, which may suggest a dynamic temporal mechanism (see Fig. 9).

(A) Temporal advance in the adjustment of cognitive load in the Fz region: the H-β (26–30 Hz) average power index (in dB) within the 400–450 ms time window can discriminate between PAD + and PAD- under low cognitive load, but this window advances to 300–350 ms under high cognitive load. (B) Temporal advance in the adjustment of cognitive load in the Pz region: the H-β (26–30 Hz) average power index (in dB) within the 400–450 ms time window can discriminate between PAD + and PAD- under low cognitive load, with the window advancing to 300–350 ms under high cognitive load.

This contrasts with the hypothesis posited in the current study, prompting the question: why does the window for brain recognition of anomalous data shift slightly earlier rather than later with increased cognitive load? This phenomenon could be attributed to two mechanisms engaged by the brain when faced with heightened load: the mobilization of additional resources and the optimization of resource allocation45–47. As cognitive load escalates, the brain endeavors to mobilize supplementary cognitive resources to handle the augmented influx of information. This may entail intensified activity in specific brain regions, heightened attentional focus, and the utilization of working memory capacity48,49. Studies suggest that the activity in the prefrontal cortex, implicated in higher cognitive functions like decision-making and attentional control, is heightened during the processing of high-load tasks50. Moreover, as cognitive load intensifies, the brain employs selective attention strategies to optimize resource allocation in response to the demands of diverse cognitive tasks51. This adaptability underscores the brain’s nuanced approach to managing tasks of varying cognitive load, minimizing unnecessary resource expenditure, and selectively prioritizing task-relevant information to enhance processing efficiency51,52. Consequently, tasks under high load may undergo expedited processing, thereby explaining the observed phenomenon of earlier temporal dynamics. This discovery enriches our comprehension of the intricate interplay between cognitive load and PAD recognition.

The results of behavioral data further corroborate the aforementioned findings. Results from the main effects analysis indicate that, compared to the PAD- condition, participants exhibited shorter reaction times in the PAD + condition. Subsequent analysis of interaction effects further confirms that under high cognitive load, there are significant differences in reaction times between the PAD- and PAD + conditions, whereas such differences are not observed under low cognitive load. This suggests that only when cognitive load reaches a certain threshold does the brain mobilize additional cognitive resources or optimize resource allocation during PAD processing, thereby expediting the processing task.

Behavioral data from this study also suggest that both low-load and high-load scenarios remain within participants’ manageable levels of cognitive load, thus facilitating the observation of the time advancement effect under high load. However, the brain’s capacity to mobilize additional resources and optimize resource allocation for enhanced efficiency under high load is restricted under extreme conditions53. For instance, cognitive overload resulting from a load increase beyond individual processing capacity may impede cognitive task performance efficiency52, potentially counteracting the time advancement effect or even eliciting a time delay effect. Further investigation in future research endeavors could delve into this phenomenon with greater depth.

The theoretical contributions of this study manifest in three primary aspects: Firstly, this research aids in distinguishing the roles of β oscillations across various frequencies. By precisely isolating high-frequency β oscillations and delineating their correlation with PAD, this work contributes to the nuanced understanding of the distinct functional roles of β oscillations in cognitive processing, thereby enriching existing theories on β oscillations54–56. Secondly, the consistency observed in H-β oscillations within the frontal-parietal cortex offers valuable insights for investigations into brain neural circuits and communication networks. The consistent occurrence of frontal-parietal cortex H-β oscillations establishes a foundation for studying decision-making neural circuits under PAD conditions23,26, while also suggesting potential avenues for exploring brain communication mechanisms under PAD12. Lastly, this study sheds light on the potential relationship between cognitive load and PAD. Moderate increments in cognitive load augment the brain’s capacity to detect anomalous data by advancing the temporal window. This finding extends theories on cognitive load and attention resource allocation51,52. Traditional theories propose that heightened cognitive load diminishes processing efficiency or extends processing duration57. In contrast, our study suggests that elevated cognitive loads may expedite processing speed to meet the demands imposed by cognitive load. H-β oscillations contribute to elucidating the mechanisms of cognitive resource mobilization and allocation in the brain under varying loads.

From the perspective of practical applications, the consistency of H-β oscillations in the frontal-parietal cortex provides an important neural marker for PAD, with potential value in the pathological mechanism research, preliminary screening, intervention, and treatment of neurodegenerative diseases58,59. Studies have shown that individuals with specific learning disabilities60, Alzheimer’s disease patients61, schizophrenia patients62, and others exhibit significant cognitive processing deficits or functional impairments in data processing, with poorer numerical processing and anomaly recognition abilities compared to normal populations. The consistency of H-β oscillations in the frontal-parietal cortex identified in this study may serve as a potential objective screening indicator for distinguishing pathological from normal populations63, and it also holds potential value for evaluating the effectiveness of disease interventions and treatments. In fact, β oscillations have already been clinically applied in the screening and diagnosis of Parkinson’s disease, showing significant causal relationships with motor impairments and providing important means for understanding the motor control deficits in Parkinson’s disease64–66. Drawing from these pathways, H-β oscillations hold promising prospects for characterizing PAD defects in some patient populations and their clinical applications.

However, this study is not without limitations, and future research could explore the following four aspects. First, the data types encompass both structured numerical data and unstructured data (such as graphics, videos, sounds, texts, etc.), yet our study exclusively focuses on numerical data as the monitoring target, which may present limitations. Future investigations should encompass a wider array of data types to ascertain the generalizability of the conclusions derived in this study. Second, the time-shift effect proposed in this study may have specific limitations, particularly in scenarios involving moderate increases in cognitive load. If cognitive load is significantly heightened, the brain’s ability to mobilize additional resources and optimize resource allocation to enhance efficiency may be constrained52,53. Future research should distinguish between varying levels of cognitive load intensity to further elucidate the mechanisms underlying cognitive load regulation. Third, future research could employ EEG-fMRI techniques to gain a deeper understanding of the β-oscillation neural circuits associated with PAD. This approach would help pinpoint specific brain regions responsible for electrophysiological changes and provide more detailed spatial evidence on the connectivity and information exchange within the frontal and parietal brain regions. Fourth, although the consistency of frontal-parietal H-β oscillations provides a valuable neural marker for PAD, translating this into a clinical tool for screening neurodegenerative diseases requires the collection of large-scale data across diverse pathological populations. This will allow for a thorough evaluation of the sensitivity and specificity of H-β oscillation markers. Additionally, neuroscientists could use neural modulation techniques to manipulate H-β oscillations, examining how changes in these oscillations relate to cognitive functions, including PAD, and uncover potential intervention strategies for neurodegenerative diseases.

Conclusions

PAD significantly influences individual social functioning and cognitive decision-making, and this study delves into the neural mechanisms of PAD based on EEG. The research unveils a close correlation between H-β oscillations in the frontal and parietal regions and PAD, offering insights into PAD identification under diverse circumstances. Furthermore, moderate augmentation of cognitive load demonstrates a time-shift effect on H-β oscillations for PAD identification. This study enriches our understanding of the neural oscillations underpinning PAD, decision-making neural circuits and communication mechanisms, as well as temporal dynamic mechanisms. It underscores the considerable value of H-β oscillations in representing PAD, holding promising applications in pathological mechanism research of neurodegenerative diseases, as well as in the preliminary screening, intervention, and treatment of diseases.
