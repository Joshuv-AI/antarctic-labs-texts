Water quality index and human health risk analysis in urban drinking water sources
Authors: Iqra Nasim, Rab Nawaz, Aysha Farwa, Sajjad Ahmad, Muhammad Atif Irshad, Sami A Al-Hussain, Ali Irfan, Magdi E A Zaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present study was conducted to assess the quality of drinking water in urban areas of Hafizabad and Pindi Bhattian through the Water Quality Index (WQI) and Human Health Risk Assessment (HHRA). Forty-eight samples were collected from four sources of drinking water, including filter plants, tube wells, tap water, and bore wells. Water samples were analyzed for physical, chemical, and biological parameters. Results for all the parameters were compared with World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines. Results showed that temperature, turbidity, and electrical conductivity frequently exceeded WHO guidelines, while pH was within its allowable range. The chemical parameters, such as dissolved oxygen, total dissolved solids, total hardness, and calcium hardness met WHO guidelines. However, E. coli was found higher than both WHO guidelines and National Environmental Quality Standards (NEQS) in tap water samples. Arsenic concentration was also higher than standards in tap water of Hafizabad. According to WQI, several urban sources, particularly tap water, had a very poor or unfit class for drinking purposes. HHRA found arsenic to have both non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic risks for adults and children. These findings highlight the need for improved treatment, distribution-system integrity, and routine monitoring to reduce long-term health risks in urban and peri-urban settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42789-1.Keywords: Drinking water quality, Arsenic contamination, E. coli, Risk management, Water supply systems, Human health risk assessment, Water quality indexSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Hydrology

Introduction

Groundwater is the primary source of drinkable water for industrial, agricultural, and municipal applications. However, due to a variety of economic operations and demographic pressures, its quality is steadily declining these days1. This is one of the major issues being faced today. Due to the unavailability of safe drinking water, billions of people around the world are compelled to consume contaminated water, which makes groundwater scarcity a pressing global concern2,3. Groundwater resources are being depleted fast because of the increasing population, urbanization, industrialization, and improvements in human living standards. Water quantity and quality is badly managed together with increased extraction rate. Higher pollutant levels in drinking water can endanger human health4,5.

Surface drinking water is mainly contaminated through direct sewage disposal into surface waters, improper waste disposal, runoff containing pesticides and fertilizers from agricultural lands, and industrial effluents6. Therefore, such a composition introduces hazardous chemicals and heavy metals together with pathogenic microorganisms into the water. These sources introduce a wide variety of contaminants to the water, including hazardous chemicals, heavy metals, and pathogenic microorganisms. Water pollution greatly threatens biodiversity in aquatic ecosystems, which leads to degradation of water quality and risk to human health, as pointed out by Ukaogo and Ewuzie7. Therefore, there is an urgent need to address such anthropogenic sources of water contamination, the conservation of water resources, and the enhancement of the resilience of ecosystems and the health of communities.

Globally, poor water quality severely affects the environment and human health. Environmentally, bad water quality supports eutrophication by habitat destruction and loss of biodiversity as it endangers aquatic ecosystems7. The impurities contained in drinking water can have potentially severe effects on human health, causing gastrointestinal diseases, mental disorders, reproductive problems, and cancer. The illnesses caused by viruses, pathogens, parasites, and bacteria are hepatitis, stomach problems, diarrhea, typhoid fever, renal failure, and vomiting can affect children, elderly people with weak immune systems as well as cancer and AIDS patients with compromised immune systems8. Drinking water containing high nitrate levels may cause methemoglobinemia. Fluoride is useful to human health. A small daily application strengthens teeth against decay. However, excessive intake of fluoride will be harmful and result in skeletal fluorosis and dental fluorosis. Children and old-aged people are among the most sensitive category of people to the effect of the situation4. A risk-based approach framework, international recognition is a tool for systematically addressing this widespread environmental health impact through safe water supplies that drinking water has been encouraged as a preventive and risk-based management in the context of global challenges by WHO. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends Water Safety Plans (WSPs) as the best approach to protect drinking water within a risk management framework. Water Safety Plans include risk assessment and propose control measures. Quantitative indices and health-based assessments support this risk-management approach by transforming complex water quality data into understandable information. The two main tools are the Water Quality Index (WQI) and Human Health Risk Assessment (HHRA). The WQI integrates multiple parameters into a single value, providing a simplified representation of drinking-water quality for decision-making. It reduces all available data to one value by considering the influence of several physical, chemical, and biological parameters. This Index provides a standard approach to water quality evaluation that is useful in deciding its potential uses, whether for human consumption or other purposes. WQI helps in pinpointing the source of pollution and trend monitoring, which eventually leads to remedial measures aimed at protecting human health as well as water bodies9–12.

These assessment approaches have been widely applied in many regions worldwide but their application remains uneven across developing countries. Assessing water quality necessitates comprehension of the geographical distribution of environmental attributes. However, monitoring water quality can be very expensive. Therefore, in order to meet these problems, the strategic usage needs to be reliable and adaptable. Widely utilized techniques, such as HHRA and WQI, are essential for classifying and demonstrating the status of water and health hazards in specific areas. Several research studies in different countries have employed these tools to measure water quality1,2,4,9,10. In particular, urban areas in developing countries such as Pakistan remain under-studied despite increasing pressure on drinking water resources. However, there are limited studies on water quality in urban areas of developing countries, including Pakistan. To address this gap, the present study was carried out in two urban areas of Punjab Province of Pakistan (Hafizabad and Pindi Bhattian) to assess the quality of drinking water from four source types, namely filter plants, tube wells, tap water, and bore wells.

While numerous studies in Pakistan have applied WQI or HHRA independently, or in limited combinations, the present study advances existing work in several important ways. First, it integrates a comprehensive quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC) framework, including method detection limits and replicate precision, which is rarely reported in regional WQI–HHRA studies. Second, the robustness of WQI classification is evaluated through both weight-based and concentration-based sensitivity analyses, providing a quantitative assessment of uncertainty that goes beyond conventional index calculation. Third, arsenic-related health risks are assessed using age-stratified exposure scenarios, allowing explicit comparison of non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic risks for adults and children. By applying these methodological enhancements to multiple urban drinking-water sources in Hafizabad and Pindi Bhattian, this study provides a more policy-relevant and decision-oriented assessment than earlier work conducted in Lahore, Karachi, Sujawal, and the Indus delta.

Methodology

Study site

The present study was conducted in two tehsils of Hafizabad District, Punjab Province of Pakistan (Hafizabad and Pindi Bhattian). The district is administratively divided into these two tehsils and relies heavily on groundwater as the main source of drinking water. Hafizabad is located at 32.0712° N latitude and 73.6895° E longitude, with an elevation of approximately 200 m above sea level (700 ft). Pindi Bhattian is located at 31.8966° N latitude and 73.2824° E longitude (Fig. 1).

Map of the study area with sampling points.

Water sampling

Water samples were collected from four major drinking water sources to determine the WQI in Hafizabad and Pindi Bhattian: (I) filtration plants, representing treated water after basic filtration but before distribution; (II) tap water, representing water supplied through the municipal distribution system after treatment; (III) tube wells, representing untreated deep groundwater extracted through motorized pumping; and (IV) bore wells, representing shallow or small-scale hand-pump/borehole groundwater sources, often used privately. The locations of these sources are shown in Fig. 2.

Sampling and location point at Hafizabad and Pindi Bhattian.

Twelve (12) water samples were collected from each of the four sources: filtration plants, tube wells, tap water, and bore wells. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate for quality assurance. This approach ensured internal consistency and enhanced the statistical power of the study. The water samples were transported to the laboratory at a temperature of about 4 °C in cool boxes containing ice packs. Samples were collected in 500 mL plastic bottles to avoid any contamination, with proper care and labeling on the bottles. The physicochemical analysis was carried out within 48 h from the time of collection, while bacteriological analysis started within 6 h and was always completed inside 24 h, according to APHA guidelines13. The microbial analyses samples were taken in sterile bottles under aseptic conditions so as not to introduce any contaminants into them. QA/QC procedures were strictly followed throughout sampling and analysis. All instruments were calibrated daily using certified standard solutions. Method detection limits (MDLs) were determined according to APHA standard methods. Field blanks were analyzed and showed no detectable contamination, confirming the absence of sample handling bias. Triplicate analyses were performed for all physicochemical, microbial, and arsenic parameters. Analytical precision was assessed using relative standard deviation (RSD), which generally remained below 10% for most physicochemical parameters, indicating good reproducibility. It should be noted that RSD values reported for parameters such as temperature, dissolved oxygen, turbidity, and E. coli reflect a combination of analytical reproducibility and inherent field or biological variability; higher RSD values for these parameters therefore indicate natural environmental heterogeneity rather than limitations in laboratory precision. Overall, the QA/QC results confirm the reliability and robustness of the data as shown in Table 1.

Results of all these parameters were compared with WHO guidelines. Pakistan has its own national standards for drinking water quality (Pak-EPA, 2008), but WHO guidelines were used in the present study. This makes it easy to compare with other studies conducted globally and ensure consistency with international public health benchmarks.

Bacteriological analysis of water quality

Samples were collected and handled according to WHO (1996) methods, while APHA16 criteria were followed for sample analysis in the laboratory. Additional sterilized bottles were used to take water samples at each site. Samples were brought to the laboratory for analysis. E. coli counts were determined by the most probable number (MPN) method, as illustrated by Prescott17. Within 24 h of collection, water samples were examined by MPN fermentation techniques. To facilitate the ease of isolation of E. coli from different water samples, each sample was marked with a serial number, type of water, location, date, and time of collection. For every sample a three-tier dilution series was prepared using three sets of five tubes (5–5–5 format). Specifically, 10 mL, 1 mL and 0.1 mL aliquots of the water sample were inoculated into tubes containing Lauryl Tryptose Broth (LTB) with inverted Durham tubes for gas detection. The inoculated tubes were incubated at 35 ± 0.5 °C for 24–48 h. Those tubes that showed turbidity and gas production were considered presumptive positive. Positive tubes were then streaked on MacConkey agar and eosin methylene blue (EMB) agar (Merck) for confirmation. Plates were incubated at 35 ± 0.5 °C for 24 h, and colonies exhibiting characteristic metallic sheen on EMB agar were selected. Later, classification at the species level was performed using cytochrome oxidase, triple sugar iron agar, urea, and indole tests as presumptive E. coli18. During bacteriological analysis, positive and negative controls were run in parallel to ensure accuracy in microbial identification. Duplicate analyses were carried out to check the precision of results. All analytical work was performed in the Analytical Lab of The University of Lahore, Pakistan, following WHO/APHA methods of testing. The study parameters (physicochemical and microbiological) are key indicators relevant directly to human health (included also in WHO guidelines). Specifically, arsenic level and E. coli have been included as priority contaminants due to public health recognition both nationally (Pakistan) and internationally.

Water Quality Index (WQI)

WQI is a widely accepted approach for water quality assessment. A mathematical formula consisting of quality parameters is used for the evaluation of water quality. WQI shows the combined impact of various water quality parameters. In the current study, weights were applied to parameters based on their significance to human health and water quality, as per WHO guidelines and previous literature19,20. For instance, higher weight has been assigned for parameters like bacteriological contamination (E. coli) than aesthetic parameters (e.g., hardness) because of direct health impacts reflected sensitively by WQI classification. A sensitivity check was performed for varying selected parameter weights (± 10–20%) and recalculating the index. The results indicated no change in the overall WQI category for the majority of samples, indicating that the classification is stable under reasonable variations in weight assignment. Human health risk assessment

Health risk assessment is used to determine the consequences of exposure to a metal, chemical, or microbiological agent. Extent of the effects is determined by the dose and duration of exposure. This method is used to assess the quality of the water and control the effects of drinking water that has been contaminated. Risk assessment is based on the drinking water pathway and average daily consumption of water25. HHRA was assigned in both carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic categories. In the present study, ingestion intake rate considered as humans orally intake drinking water to consume contaminants has been evaluated according to guidelines provided by US Environmental Protection Agency (USEPA). These include an evaluation of Carcinogenic Risk (CR), Hazard Index (HI), Hazard Quotient (HQ), and Chronic Daily Intake (CDI).

Results

Human settlement is widespread in the selected study sites on the basis of sources of water. The water quality in these locations is seriously threatened by anthropogenic activities. Additionally, drinking water from certain sources and the quality of the water are important issues for public health.

Physicochemical analysis of water quality

The mean pH value of all samples was within the WHO guidelines of 6.5–8.5. Although statistically significant differences were detected among some sources (Fig. 3), pH values remained within the WHO guideline range across all sources. Tube wells and bore wells generally showed slightly higher pH compared to filter plant and tap water, indicating greater buffering from groundwater geochemistry rather than anthropogenic influence and safe limits reported by previous studies31. Therefore, there is no direct health risk associated with consumption of water having this range of pH. However, it plays an important role in the stability and corrosion or scaling tendency of water in the distribution system32. Higher pH (> 8.0) may reduce the efficiency of chlorination, and lower pH (< 6.5) increases corrosivity, affecting pipes as well as the possible release of metals into drinking water33. Water temperatures for Hafizabad filter plants were found to be between 18.5 and 42.9 °C. Some samples had higher values than the WHO guideline, and temperature has an effect on chemical and biological reactions, which ultimately has an impact on turbidity and electrical conductivity. Turbidity showed statistically significant variation among sources (Fig. 3), with tube wells in Hafizabad exhibiting the highest values, frequently exceeding the WHO guideline of 5 NTU. Water from bore wells and filter plants always displayed the clearest appearance with the lowest turbidity. They either effectively remove particles or probably just naturally “filter” things out. Turbidity has much significance in microbes; cloudier water allows germs to hide from disinfectants and hence make it easier for them to stay around34. Low turbidity enables effective disinfection. The adequacy of low turbidity in enabling effective disinfection has to be confirmed through microbiological testing. The mean electrical conductivity (EC) in all the water samples exceeded WHO guidelines, ranging from 1120 to 3350 µS/cm. Statistically, tube wells and local tap water exhibited significantly higher EC than filter plant and bore well sources in both cities, reflecting a greater contribution of dissolved ions from deeper groundwater and distribution-system interactions. Such elevated EC levels reflect a high concentration of dissolved ions, which may arise from the natural hydrogeology of the area (e.g., mineral dissolution from alluvial deposits), saline water intrusion, or anthropogenic inputs such as agricultural runoff and industrial effluents. Similar EC levels have been documented in groundwater across Punjab province and other urban centers of Pakistan, including industrial zones near Khurrianwala (≈ 2,408 µS/cm), urban supplies in Karachi with values up to 3,448 µS/cm, and widespread tube wells in Gujranwala exceeding safe thresholds35,36. These findings show that elevated EC is a common characteristic of local groundwater, reflecting both natural geochemical conditions and human activities. Dissolved oxygen (DO) levels in drinking water samples from four selected sources ranged from 3.6 to 8.6 mg L− 1 and were within guidelines, except for one tube well source in Pindi Bhattian. Total dissolved solids (TDS) in the samples were within the WHO guidelines, with a maximum value of 197.8 mg L− 1. Although EC and TDS are usually correlated, EC is primarily influenced by the presence and mobility of ionic species, whereas TDS reflects the total mass of dissolved matter. Elevated EC alongside relatively low TDS shows that water chemistry may be dominated by a smaller set of highly mobile ions (e.g., sodium, chloride, sulfate, and nitrate) that enhance conductivity without substantially increasing the total mass of dissolved solids. Conversely, the absence of high concentrations of heavier salts such as calcium or magnesium carbonates may clarify the modest TDS levels. Such ionic imbalances are not uncommon in groundwater systems of alluvial Punjab, where saline intrusion and evaporite dissolution can elevate EC disproportionately to TDS. Bacteriological analysis of water quality

E. coli concentrations in the water samples ranged from 0.0 to 0.4 CFU/100 mL, with an average of 0.4 CFU/100 mL as shown in Fig. 5. While these levels are low, any detection of E. coli indicates fecal contamination and a potential risk of waterborne disease, as the WHO guideline is zero CFU/100 mL for safe drinking water. Although E. coli concentrations were generally low, statistically significant differences were observed among sources (Fig. 5). Tap water showed higher E. coli counts compared to other sources, particularly in Hafizabad, indicating contamination likely introduced during distribution rather than at the source. The water distribution system contains very old pipes of asbestos cement and galvanized iron and in some places the distance between the water supply line and sewer line is very close and this can be a reason for bacteriological contamination. Previous studies have demonstrated that aging pipes and inadequate separation from sewer infrastructure can increase microbial entrance. Additionally, water pH plays a critical role in pipe corrosion, which may further promote microbial growth or leaching of contaminants, thereby affecting water quality. Water Quality Index

The Water Quality Index (WQI) was calculated using the weighted arithmetic index method, which integrates multiple physicochemical parameters into a single dimensionless value. In this approach, unit weights (Wi) were assigned inversely proportional to the recommended drinking-water standards, and quality ratings (qi) were computed based on measured and ideal values of each parameter. The weighted sub-indices (Wi × qi) were then summed to obtain the final WQI for each water source. A worked example and stepwise calculations for all parameters are provided in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2. Although E. coli was assigned a high relative weight due to its critical importance for drinking-water safety, it was not numerically incorporated into the WQI calculation in this study because both its measured value (Vi) and permissible standard (Si) are zero. Under the weighted arithmetic index formulation, this results in a quality rating (Qi) of zero and therefore no contribution to the final index. Consequently, the WQI values presented here primarily reflect physicochemical water quality conditions. Results indicated that the water quality in Hafizabad and Pindi Bhattian was poor based on physiochemical factors except for the filter plants of Pindi Bhattian. Overall, alarmingly, in terms of filter plant, bore well, tube well, and local tap, the WQI for site 1 (Hafizabad) was classified as “unsuitable to very poor” water quality for drinking purposes (88.2, 61.5, 73.4, and 111.3, respectively). For site 2 (Pindi Bhattian), WQI falls between “excellent to poor” water quality for filter plant, bore well, tube well, and local tap water (11.5, 57.0, 64.3, and 67.3, respectively). These classifications indicate relative physicochemical water quality performance among sources within the study area and do not necessarily imply the absence of contaminant-specific health risks. This indicates that the water samples in this area contain physicochemical parameters that are outside guideline limits, contributing to degraded WQI classifications; however, health risks associated with specific toxic contaminants are further evaluated through human health risk assessment, as illustrated in Fig. 7. The relative weights of individual physicochemical parameters were systematically varied by ± 10% and ± 20%, while measured concentrations were held constant. The resulting changes in weighted sub-indices and final WQI values are presented in Table 6 and 8. Results indicate that moderate variations in parameter weights produced only limited changes in final WQI values and did not substantially alter the overall water quality classification for most sources.

Chronic risk

The present study explores the potential health consequences of long-term arsenic exposure, the primary contaminant in the studied area’s drinking water. This drinking water poses extreme risk to locals. Carcinogenic risk assessment

Figure 8 displays the results of estimated Cancer Risk (CR) for two age groups: children and adults. The average values among every ten thousand people in Hafizabad and Pindi Bhattian were found to be 5.0 × 10− 3 for adults and 2.0 × 10− 3 for children. However, results of the study show that CR values exceed, for both age groups, the standards set by USEPA of 1 × 10− 6 to 1 × 10− 4. This means that there is likely an elevated cancer risk among both children and adults due to consumption of arsenic-contaminated water within this study area. The problem of detected contamination of drinking water sources in Hafizabad as well as Pindi Bhattian can be linked to several factors, which contribute to the alarming state of groundwater contamination in the entire area.

Discussion

Physicochemical characteristics and their implications

Safe drinking water is a burning issue in urban areas of Pakistan. This study reflects the same issues quantitatively through WQI classification and arsenic health risk metrics. Tap water sources in Hafizabad were classified as unfit for drinking (WQI > 100), whereas tube well and bore well sources generally fall into poor to very poor categories, indicating multiple supply pathway potential health risks. The degraded WQI classifications are consistent with arsenic hazard quotient values greater than unity for both adults and children, with a maximum value of HQ observed for children up to 15.1 due to their increased vulnerability because of lower body weight. Carcinogenic risk values more than the USEPA acceptable range (10− 6 – 10− 3) in some cases almost equal to 10− 3 show high long term cancer risks if the same situation prevails and people continue consuming contaminated water.

Turbidity, temperature and color are high in Hafizabad and Pindi Bhattian samples. Turbidity has main health related aspects. When turbidity levels are higher than the EPA and WHO guideline values for drinking water; it means there is a presence of suspended particles which could contribute to particle-associated microorganisms and reducing the efficiency of disinfection by sheltering microbial pathogens36. Elevated temperatures can support bacterial regrowth, and elevated temperatures result in a change in taste, while the brown to dark brown color reported in some samples informs us about organic contamination within them. Similar results have been reported from other areas like Swabi, Sindh37, but here it seems more intense with regard to both parameters, thus depicting localized risk zones. Nasrullah38 reported that most dissolved metals hardly ever influence taste unless accompanied by complexing agents; therefore, our study area’s aesthetic problem is most likely related to turbidity and organic load.

The study also found high values of electrical conductivity, total dissolved solids, and hardness in some samples. High EC is basically an indication of more ionic content, which may be due to industrial discharge or a subsurface ore body disturbance39. Quite interestingly, in most of the sample’s TDS was within permissible limits, which means specific dissolved ions other than general salts are contributing disproportionately to EC levels. Although hardness primarily affects water usability through scale formation and reduce soap efficiency, it poses minimal direct health risk. When compared with findings from Faisalabad, Gilgit Baltistan, and Quetta40, water sources in Hafizabad and Pindi Bhattian exhibited acceptable Ph and hardness levels, with localized EC elevations warranting further hydrogeochemical investigation.

Interactions among physicochemical parameters and contamination mechanisms

Correlation among the measured parameters provides an insight into the underlying dynamics that govern water quality in the study area. EC and TDS are always strongly positively correlated in this particular study. Values were observed to be much higher for EC than for TDS which could probably be attributed to highly mobile ions such as sodium or chloride without determining specific ion composition. Elevated temperature and turbidity appeared to interact synergistically, as higher temperature accelerate chemical reactions and favor microbial persistence in samples exceeding WHO turbidity limits.

Similarly, pH also played a significant role in influencing pipe corrosion, which may have enabled E. coli ingress through aging asbestos cement and iron pipelines, hence linking chemical stability to bacteriological contamination. Arsenic concentration had very strong positive relationships with both HQ and CR, thereby confirming it as the dominant risk factor within this study area. WQI values reflected the combined effects of EC and arsenic because all sites with high levels of these parameters fall into poor or very poor categories-for example, Hafizabad tap water. While bore wells with lower loads achieve better scores. WQI results thus support/underscore the interactive nature between physicochemical, microbial, and toxicological parameters in shaping overall water safety.

Microbial contamination and public health implications

One of the most alarming findings of this study was the detection of E. coli in few water samples at concentrations exceeding the World Health Organization (WHO) guideline values. E. coli is a good indicator of fecal contamination and reflects microbial deterioration of water quality. From the public health point of view, this result becomes more alarming since fecal contamination has a direct relationship between increasing risk on waterborne diseases with immediate health risk to the population exposed. Long term consumption of such water increases the burden of diarrheal disease in the community, also cholera and typhoid41,42. Similar results have been obtained from the Punjab, including the Vehari district, where large-scale contamination of E. coil has been reported, indicating a systemic challenge to the water safety of the province41. Hafizabad and Pindi Bhattian microbial risk varies but is consistently above safe threshold; hence, urgent intervention is needed. While this study’s quantitative HHRA focused on arsenic due to available models, the detection of E. coli points towards the urgent necessity of including microbial risk assessment in future studies. Typically, the effects caused by microbial contamination are more acute and massive in terms of diarrhea, gastroenteritis, cholera, and typhoid, with an immediate burden higher than that of chemical exposures. A full quantitative microbial risk assessment (QMRA) was beyond the scope of this study; thus, since E. coli was detected, there is an urgent need for improved disinfection within water supplies. Hence, future work should incorporate a microbial risk analysis together with a chemical HHRA to give a better composite indication regarding drinking water safety.

Arsenic contamination and human health risk

Arsenic, a highly toxic heavy metal, was found above the NEQS limits in both cities posing serious long-term health issues. Arsenic is mainly known to act as a carcinogen with other effects involving skin lesions and cardiovascular and neurological diseases. The arsenic levels reported for Hafizabad are comparable to those previously reported for Lahore43,44, thus showing contamination over a wide area of Punjab. Considering an NEQS limit of < 0.05 mg L− 1, high concentration detected implies anthropogenic input together with inadequate regulation on industries45.

HHRA results demonstrate that arsenic poses a long-term health risk, while microbial contamination constitutes an acute hazard. Elevated turbidity and frequent E. coli detection indicate immediate microbial risks, particularly for children and immunocompromised individuals. High EC and TDS suggest a high mineral content, which could be responsible for gastrointestinal disturbances in short-term consumption periods and long-term renal impacts. Detection of arsenic above WHO and NEQS thresholds-consistent with HHRA outcomes-underscores long-term carcinogenic and systemic toxicity risks. In a summary integration of these findings with WQI classifications, where most samples were ranked “poor” or worse, results explicitly show the convergence between microbiological hazard and chronic chemical exposure vulnerability pathways in Hafizabad & Pindi Bhattian.

Integrated risk interpretation using Water Quality Index (WQI)

WQI results mostly fall in the categories of “bad”, “very poor” or “unfit for drinking” for Hafizabad and Pindi Bhattian. This is a baseline fact of local water quality that urgently demands intervention. Outbreaks of hepatitis attributed to unsafe water and millions of annual diarrhea cases have been reported from across the country46. There are also reports from different parts of the country about outbreaks of hepatitis and millions of annual diarrhea cases. While these numbers give an idea about the magnitude at a national level, this study pinpoints both acute (short-term) and chronic (long-term) risk situations specifically pertaining to urban populations of Hafizabad & Pindi Bhattian.

A comparison with the studies conducted in Sujawal, New Karachi and Nagarparkar47,48 also reveals a poor WQI value. Therefore, there is a parallel trend of physicochemical degradation in different parts of Pakistan. However spatial variation was observed with higher arsenic load found in Hafizabad and relatively high microbial contamination detected from Pindi Bhattian. It is important to note that E. coli was not included as part of the numerical formulation for computing WQI therefore WQI classification should be interpreted as indices regarding physicochemical quality rather than composite measures regarding microbial safety.

The apparent divergence between WQI-based source rankings and HHRA outcomes reflects the conceptual differences between these assessment tools. WQI integrates multiple physicochemical parameters to indicate overall water quality status, whereas HHRA in this study focuses specifically on arsenic-related health risks via the ingestion pathway. Consequently, sources such as bore wells and filter plants, which exhibited lower WQI values and were classified as ‘good’ to ‘poor’, still showed hazard quotient (HQ) values exceeding unity for children and adults due to arsenic concentrations. This highlights that WQI-based suitability should be interpreted as relative physicochemical performance rather than comprehensive safety, and underscores the importance of complementary risk-based assessments for priority contaminants.

Water is degrading day by day in Hafizabad and Pindi Bhattian. This has made the people more prone to health problems, most especially the vulnerable group which includes children and pregnant women. Therefore, turbidity, bacterial contamination, arsenic, heavy metals, and nitrates provide a pathway for waterborne diseases to cause long-term carcinogenic effects on organs of the body systems such as neurological developmental delay or immune system problems that eventually result in death. Immediate action is needed through proper management intervention by government agencies responsible for public health protection.

Sources of contamination and pathway analysis

Most probably, this is a pattern of contamination that results from different pathways which converge. There were several reasons for drinking water contamination in the study areas. Aquifers have been compromised by the indiscriminate use of fertilizers in agricultural practices5; attempted remedy by mending of defective sewage systems37; and bleaching and disposal of raw industrial and municipal waste49, which must be the pathways through which harmful contaminants infiltrated the ground. It has also been found that there is widespread arsenic contamination in groundwater supplies, which has set off another health crisis50.

Strengths, limitations, and management implications

One major strength of the study is the integrated application of the water quality index and human health risk analysis because it provides a very powerful and comprehensive assessment by directly integrating numerous parameters associated with human health, hence the linkage of water quality to public health. The relevance of the study to city dwellers becomes apparent when urban areas are considered as the main source of drinking water supply with the eventual aim that it would be useful for urban planning and public health activities. There are several limitations to this study which should be acknowledged. The analysis was restricted to ten parameters, thereby excluding such contaminants as nitrate, lead, mercury and emerging pollutants including pharmaceuticals. The cross-section study design also precluded an assessment of seasonal variability. Addressing these limitations in future studies would make the findings more robust and generalizable. The results of the study indicate that in Hafizabad where arsenic concentrations were found to be high there is a need for well installations of community filtration plants using arsenic removal technology regular well screening and blending high and low sources o farsenic together In Pindi Bhattian consistent chlorination or UV disinfection couples with routine monitoring is required microbial risk aging distribution infrastructure sewer separation household water practices public awareness campaign.

The integrated interpretation of WQI classification and age-specific human health risk assessment provides a practical approach to risk prioritization for urban water management. For instance, filter-plant sources in Pindi Bhattian were always marked by ‘excellent’ WQI values with comparatively lower arsenic risk metrics, while municipal tap-water supplies in Hafizabad reported both ‘unfit’ WQI status and high non-carcinogenic as well as carcinogenic risks. This means intervention should be treatment efficiency improvement, controlling arsenic intrusion into the supply system through distribution network rehabilitation works instead of applying interventions uniformly across the city. The results transpire to develop Source-Specific Risk Profile based Water Safety Plans (WSPs) for evidence-based decision support at Urban Drinking Water Management.

Conclusion

The quality of drinking water is the main issue of health in developing countries, where, contaminated water with harmful and toxic substances, as well as microbial agents, results in epidemics of waterborne diseases. The present investigation in the urban areas revealed alarming results; only bore well water samples complied with safe drinking standards according to WQI. The results show a widespread deterioration of drinking water quality in the study area, as indicated by poor to unfit (WQI) classifications across several sources and high arsenic-related health risks found through human health risk assessment. It should be noted that HHRA in the present study was conducted specifically for arsenic exposure; therefore, conclusions regarding toxicological risk are limited to this contaminant, while WQI reflects broader physicochemical water quality conditions. Although no epidemiological data were collected, the presence of microbial and chemical contaminants may pose a risk of gastrointestinal illnesses among residents. The risk assessment clearly indicated the risks that are involved in the consumption of water with respect to human health. The human health risk assessment indicated high risk with water consumption. The risks were mainly carcinogenic due to arsenic, for adults at 5.0 × 10⁻³ and for children at 2.0 × 10⁻³, both far above the acceptable levels. This calls for an urgent improvement in water quality to protect public health.

Limitations and recommendation for future research

The present study has some limitations, which can be interpreted as avenues for further research. Firstly, the sample size of 12 (for each source) is quite small and may not capture all spatial or temporal variations. A larger sample size in future studies could be attempted to cover comprehensiveness. Secondly, the study did not fully address the behaviour of highly mobile ions such as sodium and chloride; it is recommended that further investigation should explicitly deal with their behaviour and impact. Thirdly, future researchers should conduct sensitivity analysis on the HHRA exposure parameters to clearly define the uncertainty associated with ingestion rates, body weight, and duration of exposure that this study assumed from literature values. The microbiological risk assessment shall be discussed to complement this physicochemical and chemical risk assessment.

Future research should focus on: (a) specific source contaminations such as industrial effluents, domestic wastewater, and surface runoff, (b) seasonal variation of the water contamination and its effect on human health, (c) exploring advanced water treatment technology to remove arsenic and biological contaminants from drinking water in developing countries (d) integrating a community-based participatory approach for creating awareness and involving the general public in water quality monitoring to make mitigation measures effective.
