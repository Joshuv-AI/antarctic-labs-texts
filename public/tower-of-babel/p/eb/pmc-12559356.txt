Mechanical and microscale characterization of foamed concrete with Tianqi aluminosilicate binder
Authors: Afiya Abdul Sattar, Md Azree Othuman Mydin, Altamashuddinkhan Nadimalla, Mohd Mustafa Al Bakri Abdullah, Paul O Awoyera, Olaolu George Fadugba
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPersistent research work has aided the development of supplementary cementitious materials, contributing to both sustainable development and mitigating environmental impacts. This study utilized Tianqi aluminosilicate (TAS) as partial replacement for Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC) in foamed concrete (FC) mix. The mechanical, transport, and microstructural characteristics of the concrete were explored. The formed concrete mixes were developed by varying TAS from 0 to 40%, in steps of 10% for OPC to identify optimal performance. The mechanical characteristics (compressive, flexural, splitting tensile, and elastic modulus) improved by 18–25% over control and peaked at 20% TAS. At 20% TAS substitution, the transport properties (water absorption and permeability) improved significantly, which corresponds to 35% and 28%, reduction, respectively. SEM analysis revealed that TAS refined pore structure, yielding a denser matrix with homogeneous hydration product distribution. The result revealed foam stability and uniformity in mixes containing TAS, and an improvement in mechanical and durability of the concrete. Filler effect and pozzolanic activities of TAS were identified as two key factors responsible for the observed results. There was pore refinement improved secondary hydration in the concrete matrix. The results show that 20% TAS substitution improves strength and durability while lowering OPC use and striking the ideal performance balance. From the results, TAS proved to be a sustainable supplementary cementitious material aiding the durability of the mixes. This work advances eco-friendly construction practices by demonstrating TAS’s viability in high-performance FC applications.Keywords: Tianqi aluminosilicate, Foamed concrete, Mechanical properties, Transport properties, Sustainable constructionSubject terms: Engineering, Civil engineering

Introduction

Over the years, there has been increasing popularity in the use of foamed concrete (FC), owing to its low density, improved sound insulation, and thermal properties, ability to withstand fire and earthquakes1–3. Evidence has demonstrated that these advancements result in a reduction of cost of construction and duration through lowering the requirement for formwork and minimising on-site workforce4–6. FC is created by randomly incorporating air voids into the cementitious matrix, resulting in a reduced density ranging from 400 to 1750 kg/m37. The presence of foam in the base mix is ascribed to a chemical response, leading to the formation of pores in the mortar slurry8,9. This decreases the weight of the concrete. The increased porosity significantly reduces the overall weight of the concrete10.

Supplementary Cementitious Materials (SCMs) are recognised in the construction industry for their ability to enhance the performance, durability, and sustainability of concrete and geopolymer composites. SCMs, which include industrial by-products, are employed to replace or enhance conventional Portland cement in concrete, offering advantages such as reduced carbon emissions, improved workability, and increased long-term strength. Fly ash and Ground Granulated Blast-furnace Slag (GGBFS) are among the most common supplemental cementitious materials, both of which have been extensively studied and included into concrete and geopolymer systems worldwide. Fly ash is a fine, powdery material produced from the combustion of coal in power production plants. This process often produces a by-product considered waste material8. Fly ash has become a prominent additional cementitious material in concrete production due to its ability to enhance workability, reduce heat of hydration, and improve concrete durability. Fly ash improves the long-term strength of concrete by facilitating a pozzolanic reaction with calcium hydroxide, leading to the creation of extra cementitious compounds. Furthermore, fly ash significantly reduces the probability of alkali-silica reactions (ASR), which can cause cracking in concrete exposed to certain aggregate types. Furthermore, its use as a supplementary cementitious material reduces the environmental impact of concrete production by lessening dependence on virgin cement, so advancing sustainability in the built environment. GGBFS is a widely utilised additional cementitious material produced by the rapid cooling of molten iron slag, a by-product of iron and steel manufacturing9. The rapid cooling process transforms the slag into a granular form, which, when finely ground, can function as a supplementary material in concrete. GGBFS enhances the mechanical properties of concrete by increasing its compressive strength, particularly at later ages, and improving its resistance to sulphate attacks and chloride-induced corrosion. The incorporation of GGBFS improves the impermeability of concrete, making it especially beneficial in adverse environments such as sea or industrial zones. Furthermore, the use of GGBFS reduces the overall carbon footprint of concrete production by lowering the demand for clinker, the most energy-intensive component of cement.

The incorporation of these traditional SCMs into geopolymer composites has been thoroughly documented in the literature. Recent research by Deng et al.10 in the study of PVA-fibre-reinforced slag- and fly ash-blended geopolymer composites examines the impact of fly ash and GGBFS on the mechanical properties and microstructure of geopolymer composites. The study highlights the significance of these materials in improving compressive strength, flexural strength, and fracture toughness, as well as their exceptional resistance to severe chemical environments. The study emphasises the synergistic advantages of integrating PVA fibres with slag and fly ash, resulting in a composite material with superior mechanical properties and enhanced microstructure. The emergence of advanced SCMs such as Tianqi Aluminosilicate (TAS) offers a great opportunity to enhance the sustainability and performance of concrete materials. TAS, an innovative aluminosilicate material, exhibits advantageous characteristics for its reactivity and ability to form geopolymer networks. When used with concrete, it can improve the material’s mechanical properties while concurrently fostering a more sustainable and environmentally friendly construction alternative. The examination of TAS and its compatibility with traditional SCMs like fly ash and GGBFS may enable the development of high-performance, low-carbon concrete materials that align with the growing demand for sustainable construction practices. By integrating novel SCMs such as TAS with traditional materials, scholars and industry professionals can enhance the performance of concrete and geopolymer composites while addressing pressing environmental challenges. As the construction industry progresses towards eco-friendly solutions, the exploration of innovative materials such as TAS will undoubtedly be crucial in shaping the future of sustainable building materials.

Exploring the use of Tianqi aluminosilicate (TAS) as a substitute for cement in FC opens promising opportunities for improving its mechanical, transport, and microstructural properties. Experimental studies have shown TAS to have the potential to improve the mechanical properties of concrete. For instance, study11 showed that TAS possessed the capacity to improve compressive strength of concrete with high particle packing and decreased porosity, and up to 43% improvement in bending strength that occurred when the TAS content in TAS-alumina composites was increased from 5 to 15 mass%. Another study revealed enhanced compressive strength in FC incorporating TAS. FC with a density of 1600 kg/m3 demonstrated increases in compressive strength of up to 8–10 MPa, demonstrating the potential of TAS to achieve high compressive strengths in FC12.

Flexural strength of concrete is also improved with TAS addition. Studies on mullite-TAS composites demonstrate that adding TAS reduces the thermal expansion coefficient while greatly enhancing flexural strength. As such, the improved performance is expected to fit FC development, thus providing increased resistance to bending stresses essential for structural applications. There was a significant increase in the flexural strength of composites containing 20% of TAS, which showed the material’s influence on concrete13. Study14 revealed up to 156 MPa flexural strength value by adding TAS to borosilicate glass composite. A related study15 showed that TAS improved both compressive and flexural strengths, making it an ideal reinforcement for FC where mechanical performance is critical15. Ibrahim et al.16 observed that polyolefin fibres slightly enhanced compressive strength by 4.3% and flexural strength by 9.3% in FC, demonstrating the noticeable impact of fibre reinforcement when combined with TAS. Moreover, TAS enhances the hardness and toughness of materials, which are crucial for resistance to wear and fracture. The use of TAS in aluminium titanate ceramics improved the hardness and toughness of the matrix, as evident in the dense and grain interlocking in the structures. These improvements should enhance FC by giving it better resistance to impact and mechanical performance. A factor like porosity of the matrix affects the mechanical properties materials significantly. TAS significantly reduces porosity by filling voids and enhancing sintering behaviour, resulting in denser materials with greater mechanical strength and improved durability17. In FC, reduced porosity can enhance both compressive and tensile strengths, making it more robust and reliable for construction applications.

In addition, TAS improves the thermal stability and mechanical properties of concrete. TAS is essential for materials subjected to heat cycles because it preserves structural integrity at high temperatures. By doing this, FC’s service life is increased, and its mechanical qualities are guaranteed to stay constant in a variety of environmental circumstances11. By reducing porosity and promoting the formation of strong, interlocking grain structures, TAS ensures that FC can reliably perform under various mechanical loads and environmental conditions. These improvements highlight the ability of TAS to support the advancement of long-lasting construction materials with outstanding reliability. Transport properties of FC (permeability, water absorption, and sorptivity) are important for determining durability and evaluating various environmental features. Adding TAS significantly enhances these properties. Studies have demonstrated that FC with a density of 1500 kg/m3, combined with polycarboxylate superplasticizers (SP), shows improved water absorption and sorptivity. The material’s overall durability is improved by reducing water absorption to about 2.5% by volume, which is achieved by better pore distribution and smaller pore diameters at the ideal SP concentration of 1.35%18. Viscosity and workability are two crucial rheological characteristics for the processing and use of FC. Incorporating TAS and superplasticizers notably improves these properties. Research indicates that adding polycarboxylate SP to FC enhances its flowability and stability. For example, increasing the SP content up to 1.35% improves the spreadability and stability of the FC mix, resulting in better mechanical performance and smaller, more uniformly distributed pores18. Additionally, activated foaming agents treated with microwave and ultrasonic methods have been shown to decrease pore size, thereby improving compressive strength and the stability of the foam structure19.

The presence of TAS has a substantial impact on the microstructural characteristics of FC, including the arrangement of pore sizes, phase structure, and density. Various studies have shown that TAS addition in concrete enhances the microstructure’s fineness, making it more homogeneous. TAS aids mullite composites reduces porosity and enhances microstructural integrity, resulting in improved mechanical properties. Specifically, TAS in mullite composites lowers the densification temperature and increases flexural strength due to the formation of a dense, interlocking grain structure13. Moreover, microstructural analysis of FC with varying densities (450–750 kg/m3) and silica fume content has shown that replacing cement with silica fume results in higher compressive strengths and more durable microstructures. SEM and XRD experiments demonstrate that silica fume undergoes a reaction with free calcium hydroxide in hydrated cement, resulting in the formation of calcium-silicate-hydrate (C–S–H). This compound is characterized by increased stiffness and durability compared to calcium hydroxide20.

Plenty of research has been done on TAS in dense concrete and composite materials, but not as much has been done on its use in FC. The main goal of research on TAS in dense concrete is to make its mechanical attributes better, for instance its compressive strength, durability, microstructural properties. However, FC systems include a lot of voids, which changes the matrix’s mechanical characteristics and how it behaves during hydration and cure4. There isn’t much extensive research on how TAS works in these kinds of porous systems, which makes it hard to comprehend how this material interacts with structures that have air in them.

In FC systems, having macro pores in the mix changes how quickly it hydrates and how it flows. Adding TAS may change the rate at which water is absorbed differently than adding regular dense concrete. In particular, not enough research has been done on how TAS interacts with the air bubbles in FC, which make it light11. Because TAS is less fluid, less workable, and less porous, it could behave very differently in FC blends than it does in more solid matrices. Because of this, it is very important that study be done to find out how TAS changes the fresh and hardened properties of FC, with a focus on how it interacts with the air-entrained matrix. One of the biggest problems with adding TAS to FC systems is that air-entrained concrete has a distinctive way of flowing. Adding air bubbles to the mix during the foaming process can modify the material’s consistency and flow qualities in a big way. It’s important for TAS and these air bubbles to interact because the solid particles in TAS could change the stability of the foam, which could cause the air cells to collapse or the foam structure to change.

Also, FC is less hydrated than dense concrete because it is porous8. This could make it harder for TAS to perform, such improve durability or thermal characteristics. TAS could also change the structure of the air voids, which are very important for FC’s ability to keep heat in and block sound. It is important to know how TAS works with the air-entrained matrix and whether it could make the foam stable to move forward with its use in FC. It is also important to investigate the complex interactions between the silica-alumina parts of TAS and the hydration products in the FC matrix16. Changes in the pore structure or the production of more gel phases could modify the mechanical and thermal properties of FC. More research is needed to find the right balance between strengthening effects and keeping FC’s lightweight qualities, especially when using varied amounts of TAS in the mix.

According to the review mentioned above, the addition of TAS to cement-based materials greatly improve their fresh and harden states properties. These improvements result in enhanced durability, workability, and mechanical performance, rendering cement-based materials more resilient and adaptable for a wide range of building applications. Therefore, this study investigated the possible application of TAS as a partial replacement for cement in FC production. The work is innovative because it looks at these relationships that have been ignored before. This study intends to help create new, lightweight building materials with better qualities by focussing on the unique problems and gaps in knowledge that come up when employing TAS on FC. Understanding how TAS affects the microstructure, hydration, and rheology of FC will help us make the best use of it as a crucial part of construction materials that are good for the environment.

Novelty of the study

In this study, TAS, a by-product of lithium extraction, is explored as a novel substitute for OPC in FC. While most studies focus on standard supplemental cementitious materials, this study assesses TAS’s overall effects on FC’s mechanical, transport, and microstructural characteristics. The innovative approach includes detailed analyses of various TAS proportions, highlighting the optimal replacement level for maximum performance enhancement. In addition, the study explored the effects of TAS on foam concrete performance, particularly regarding foam stability, porosity reduction, and overall durability improvement. This research’s repurposing of industrial waste not only advances the creation of high-performance building materials but also fits in with the growing demand for ecologically friendly and sustainable building methods.

TAS has demonstrated potential in improving the characteristics of diverse materials, such as dense concrete and ceramics. Nonetheless, its utilisation in FC has been restricted, and the methods by which it may enhance FC qualities are predominantly unexamined. This study seeks to address this gap by investigating the interaction between TAS and the air-entrained matrix of FC, enhancing its mechanical, thermal, and durability characteristics.

TAS functions due to its elevated alumina and silica content, which, when integrated into FC, facilitates the generation of supplementary hydration products, including calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) gel, so improving the bonding strength and longevity of the matrix. The incorporation of TAS into the porous matrix of FC may alter the pore distribution, hence enhancing thermal insulation and soundproofing characteristics. We assert that the distinctive chemical composition of TAS enhances the stability of air-entrained structures, mitigating the collapse of air spaces during the curing process, a prevalent challenge in lightweight concrete systems. These mechanisms are distinctive to the interaction between TAS and FC’s porous matrix, which markedly contrasts with solid concrete systems.

Aim and objectives

The aim was to evaluate the effectiveness of TAS as a sustainable partial OPC substitution in FC, focusing on enhancing its mechanical, transport, and microstructural properties. There were four objectives to be achieved to determine the optimal proportion of TAS that maximizes the compressive, flexural, and tensile strengths of FC, to assess the effect of TAS on the transport properties of FC, including permeability, water absorption, and sorptivity, to analyze the microstructural changes in FC with varying TAS content using Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) and to evaluate the overall performance and durability of FC incorporating TAS.

In this study, CEM1 OPC, sourced from DRN Technologies Sdn. Bhd. in Malaysia, was used as the main binder. The chemical compositions of OPC and TAS are shown in Table 2 that exhibit significant differences, which reflect their unique properties and potential applications in construction materials. The cement has a high concentration of CaO (59.91%). The high CaO content in cement is essential for creating the calcium silicate hydrates (C–S–H) during hydration, which provides strength and stability to concrete. Cement’s role is to act as a binder, which requires a substantial amount of calcium oxide to participate in the hydration reactions. However, CaO content is significantly lower in TAS (7.52%). TAS contains much lower calcium content as it is designed to interact with other compounds, such as lime or calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)2), to form supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) like calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H)22,23. SiO2 is more in TAS (53.98%) than in OPC (21.24%). TAS’s high silica content can improve the pozzolanic reaction in concrete, creating more C–S–H and increasing the material’s long-term strength and durability24. In contrast to OPC, which has a very low percentage of Al2O3 (5.82%), TAS has a considerable proportion (22.56%). Concrete’s early strength and resistance to sulphate attack can be improved by the calcium aluminate hydrates that can form because of TAS’s high alumina concentration22. Both minerals contain trace amounts of both Na2O and K2O. In contrast to TAS, which has 0.39% Na2O and 0.77% K2O, OPC has 0.16% Na2O and 0.49% K2O. Concrete may expand and crack because of the alkali-aggregate reaction influenced by these alkali oxides. To prevent such reactions, TAS’s somewhat greater K2O level must be controlled25. Besides, the MgO content in OPC is 3.68%, whereas in TAS it is 0.58%. The levels of MgO in both materials are quite small and are not likely to have a major impact, even if they can help produce expansive compounds like brucite in concrete22. The Fe2O3 level in TAS is 1.39%, whereas it is 2.78% in OPC. The lower iron content in TAS can influence the colour and may provide additional pozzolanic activity, contributing to the strength and durability of concrete22. L.O.I represents the amount of weight loss when the material is heated to a high temperature, indicating the presence of volatile compounds. TAS has a marginally greater L.O.I. of 5.97% than OPC, which has an L.O.I. of 3.03%. The breakdown of leftover organic compounds and carbonates during the heating process may be the cause of this increased L.O.I. in TAS23. The unique characteristics and possible applications in building of OPC and TAS are highlighted by their chemical compositions. TAS’s high SiO2 and Al2O3 levels imply that it may be used as a supplemental ingredient to enhance the strength and durability of concrete, whereas OPC’s high CaO and SO3 values make it perfect as a primary binder.

Mix design

The mix design for FC targeting a density of 1000 kg/m3 includes diverse proportions of TAS as a substitution for OPC. The mix uses a cement-to-sand ratio of 1:1.5 and a water-to-cement ratio of 0.46 to ensure proper workability and strength. The TAS was substituted for cement at levels: 0%, 10%, 20%, 30%, and 40%. Table 5 presents the details of the FC mix design, where each mix indication (FC0, FC10, FC20, FC30, and FC40) denotes a particular percentage of TAS substitution. For instance, FC0, the control mix with no TAS, contains 373.4 kg/m3 of OPC and 560.1 kg/m3 of sand. As the TAS substitution increases, the amount of OPC decreases proportionately while keeping the total binder content constant. For example, FC10 includes 37.34 kg/m3 of TAS and 336.1 kg/m3 of OPC, while FC40 has 149.4 kg/m3 of TAS and 224.1 kg/m3 of OPC. In order to measure the effect of TAS on the concrete properties, the sand, water, and foam quantities were kept constants for all mixes. Each mix maintains a consistent density of 1000 kg/m3 and a uniform sand content of 560.1 kg/m3. The water content is fixed at 171.8 kg/m3, ensuring a constant water-to-cement ratio across all mixes. The foam content is also kept steady at 30.66 kg/m3 to achieve the desired lightweight properties of the FC. This design makes it possible to compare the durability and mechanical characteristics of FC with variable cement replacement amounts by altering the TAS content. With the goal of improving the material’s performance and sustainability, this mix design technique methodically assesses the effects of TAS as a partial cement substitute in FC. Any observed differences in the FC’s properties are guaranteed to be caused by the different degrees of TAS replacement because sand, water, and foam are used consistently in all mixes.

FC preparation

A target density of 1000 kg/m3 was considered for the FC preparation, which entailed a careful process ensuring quality and consistency of the mix. This target density is achieved by carefully controlling the amount of stable foam introduced into the mortar slurry. A mix design ratio of 1:1.5 for cement and sand, and water/cement ratio of 0.46 was adopted. The influence of varying TAS amounts as an OPC substitute is examined in this study. A dry mixture of OPC, sand, and TAS is used to start the mixing process. Three minutes are spent completely mixing and combining the dry ingredients. This stage guarantees that the TAS, sand, and cement are dispersed equally throughout the mixture, which is essential for obtaining a homogenous blend. After combining the dry ingredients completely, water is added to the mixture. The blending of the mix continued for another 4 min, ensuring a uniform distribution of water and adequate hydration of all constituents. The workability of the mix was tested using a flow table. The test is used to determine the mix’s homogeneity and fluidity and helps to ensure mix is smoothly poured into the formwork. Stable foam is created using the Portafoam TM-2 machine, which employs the EABASSOC protein-based foaming agent. This protein foaming agent is known for producing stable and uniform bubbles, which contribute to the lightweight nature and insulating properties of the FC. The required quantity of foam is then added to the prepared mortar slurry. Precise control is maintained over the quantity of foam supplied in order to attain the desired density of 1000 kg/m3. This phase is vital as it directly influences the density and strength of the FC. After adding foam, a further five minutes of blending was done to ensure a perfect mix. This extended mixing period ensures that the foam is uniformly distributed throughout the mortar slurry and that there are no visible foam pockets. The goal is to achieve a consistent and homogeneous mix with stable air bubbles evenly dispersed. After that, the FC samples are left to cure in dampness till the test day. For the cement to completely hydrate and the concrete’s mechanical properties to fully develop, moisture curing is essential. This procedure aids in preserving the FC’s intended strength and longevity. In conclusion, a number of exact procedures are involved in the preparation of FC to guarantee the consistency and caliber of the finished product. The goal of the study is to create FC with the best density, workability, and mechanical qualities while examining the effects of TAS as a cement substitute by carefully regulating the ingredient amounts and the mixing procedure.

Experimental setup

Fresh-state properties

Assessing the workability, setting durations, and fresh density of FC is crucial for comprehending its performance during placement and its capacity to fulfil the demands of diverse building applications. These qualities affect the manageability, efficiency of the construction process, and the ultimate performance of the concrete. A variety of tests are utilised to evaluate these qualities, each offering significant insights into the properties of fresh concrete and their influence on later phases of the construction process.

The slump flow table test, performed in accordance with ASTM C230-9735, is frequently utilised to evaluate the workability and consistency of fresh FC. This test assesses the ease with which concrete may disperse on a level surface, indicating its fluidity and installation convenience. The process entails positioning a sample of fresh concrete into a conical form on a flow table. Upon the removal of the cone, the concrete is let to disperse, and the diameter of the dispersion is recorded. A bigger spread indicates enhanced workability of the concrete. This test is essential for assessing the concrete’s capacity to flow and occupy moulds or formwork without requiring excessive compaction or vibration, hence potentially lowering labour costs and enhancing efficiency in the construction process. The slump flow test indicates the concrete’s capacity for placement and compaction under its own weight, particularly in scenarios necessitating considerable fluidity, such as thin sections or intricate formwork. The test also offers insights into the likelihood of segregation or bleeding, which may jeopardise the final quality of the concrete. Engineers can evaluate the slump flow to ascertain the mix’s suitability for certain applications, guaranteeing optimal performance of the concrete during installation and subsequent curing.

The initial and final setting times of fresh FC are ascertained by the setting time test, performed in accordance with BS EN 196-336. The setting time is a crucial factor in concrete workability, as it determines the period during which fresh concrete stays malleable and can be shaped or positioned before it commences hardening. The first setting time is the moment when concrete shifts from a liquid to a semi-solid state, whereas the final setting time indicates when the concrete has completely hardened and is no longer malleable. Comprehending these durations is crucial for construction scheduling, as it aids in assessing the time allotted for placement, finishing, and modifications prior to the concrete becoming unmanageable. The setting times significantly influence the scheduling and coordination of building activities. If the setting durations are excessively brief, the concrete may cure prematurely, complicating the placement or finishing of the material. Conversely, protracted setup times may impede construction efforts, necessitating prolonged labour hours. Engineers can optimise the mix design by evaluating both the start and ultimate setting times to attain an ideal equilibrium between workability and durability, while also assuring optimal use of labour and resources.

The fresh density of FC is assessed in line with BS 12,350-637, offering insight into the material’s mass per unit volume. This test entails measuring a specified volume of fresh concrete to ascertain its density, a vital indicator of the material’s uniformity and efficacy. The fresh density significantly influences the ultimate characteristics of hardened FC, including its strength and durability. It also influences the general workability of the concrete mix and affects the convenience of transporting, placing, and finishing the material during construction. In FC, the fresh density is crucial since it affects the material’s thermal and acoustic insulation qualities, as well as its mechanical performance. A reduced fresh density generally signifies an increased amount of entrained air, perhaps enhancing insulating capabilities while diminishing strength. Conversely, an increased fresh density signifies a more compact concrete with more strength potential, although may diminish insulating characteristics. Consequently, it is essential to ascertain the fresh density to guarantee that the mixture satisfies the precise criteria for the planned application, balancing strength, workability, and other requisite attributes like as thermal and acoustic insulation.

Transport properties

To achieve a thorough comprehension of the transport qualities of concrete, multiple experimental techniques are utilised to assess different facets of its performance throughout time. These methods are meticulously selected to evaluate the material’s response to water absorption, air permeability, porosity, and chloride ion penetration, which are critical indications of durability and long-term stability in varying environmental circumstances.

Water absorption tests are conducted at 7, 14, and 28 days, following the protocols specified in BS 1881-12238. Cylindrical specimens having a diameter of 50 mm and a height of 100 mm are manufactured for this test. The process entails submerging the specimens in water and quantifying the overall water absorption. This test is essential for assessing the material’s moisture absorption capacity, which can considerably influence its long-term performance. Water absorption is crucial for concrete’s resilience to environmental conditions, including freeze–thaw cycles and chemical assaults, and serves as a reliable measure of the material’s overall longevity. The intervals of 7, 14, and 28 days offer insights into the progression of water absorption over varied curing durations, which is crucial for assessing the long-term performance of concrete subjected to diverse moisture circumstances.

The intrinsic air permeability of the concrete is evaluated according to the methodology established by Cabrera and Lynsdale39, utilising FC cylinders of 45 mm in diameter and 50 mm in height. This test assesses the permeability of air through concrete, which correlates directly with its pore structure. The air permeability is a critical measure of a material’s durability, as it indicates the ease with which moisture and gases can infiltrate the concrete, potentially resulting in reinforcement corrosion or other degrading forms. The air permeability test yields critical information regarding the connectivity and distribution of pores in concrete, influencing its mechanical qualities and resilience against harsh environmental factors.

Porosity measurements are performed at 7, 14, and 28 days utilising cylindrical specimens (50 mm in diameter and 50 mm in height), in accordance with the RILEM TC-14 CPC1140 standards. Porosity is an essential characteristic of concrete that profoundly influences its strength, durability, and permeability. This approach quantifies the total void area in the concrete, which is essential for assessing its resistance to the infiltration of deleterious elements such as water and chloride ions. Elevated porosity is frequently linked to diminished strength and heightened susceptibility to environmental degradation, especially in coastal or industrial settings. Monitoring porosity at various curing ages allows us to evaluate the temporal evolution of the pore structure and its influence on the concrete’s overall durability.

The Rapid Chloride Permeability Test (RCPT) is conducted in accordance with ASTM C120241 to assess the concrete’s resistance to chloride ion infiltration. This test used a FC disc with a diameter of 100 mm and a thickness of 50 mm. During the examination, an electric charge is transmitted through the specimen, and the quantity of charge that traverses is quantified. This indicates the permeability of the concrete to chloride ions. The penetration of chloride ions is a crucial determinant in evaluating the durability of concrete, especially in settings subjected to de-icing salts or maritime environments. The RCPT is a recognised technique for assessing concrete’s resistance to chloride-induced corrosion, a principal factor in the degradation of reinforcement within concrete structures. The test predicts the concrete’s performance in hostile settings and its possible service life by assessing permeability.

To guarantee the consistency and reliability of the test results, all transport property tests are performed on an average of three specimens, adhering to established standards for sample preparation and testing. This method reduces specimen variability and guarantees that the data accurately reflects the material’s overall qualities. The utilisation of several specimens facilitates a more comprehensive investigation of the concrete’s transport qualities, enhancing the reliability of the results’ interpretation.

Mechanical properties

Assessing compressive, flexural, and tensile strengths, in addition to the modulus of elasticity, is essential for ascertaining the comprehensive mechanical qualities and structural capability of concrete. These qualities are crucial for guaranteeing that concrete functions effectively in many applications and endures the varied loads it may face during use.

The compressive strength of concrete is assessed using 100 × 100 × 100 mm cube specimens, following BS 12,390-342. This test assesses the maximum load that concrete can withstand prior to failure, serving as a crucial indicator of its capacity to support axial loads. Compressive strength is a prominent test for concrete, as it directly correlates with the material’s structural efficacy. Elevated compressive strength is essential for concrete’s capacity to endure substantial loads without distortion or fracture. The test is performed at several curing ages, specifically 7, 14, and 28 days, to evaluate the progression of strength over time, facilitating an analysis of the concrete’s maturation and hardening under diverse environmental conditions. The outcomes of this test are essential for structural design, as they assist engineers in ascertaining the suitable mix and grade of concrete for construction applications.

Flexural strength is evaluated with prism specimens measuring 100 × 100 × 500 mm, in line with BS 12,390-543. This test evaluates concrete’s capacity to withstand bending strains, which is crucial for applications involving flexural loading, including beams, slabs, and pavements. The test entails exerting a load at the midpoint of the prism until failure, with the flexural strength determined by the maximum load and the specimen’s dimensions. This feature is essential for enabling concrete buildings to endure bending forces without fracturing, particularly in regions with unequal load distribution. The flexural strength test indicates the concrete’s performance under actual conditions, when bending and flexural forces frequently occur.

The splitting tensile strength is assessed using cylindrical specimens measuring 100 × 200 mm in accordance with BS 12,390-644. This test assesses the tensile strength of concrete, crucial for comprehending its performance under tensile loading circumstances. Tensile strength, as contrast to compressive strength, evaluates the material’s resistance to pressures that endeavour to elongate or separate it. This feature is crucial for concrete constructions experiencing tensile strains, such as bridge decks or pavements. The splitting tensile test entails exerting a compressive force along the cylinder’s diameter, resulting in longitudinal splitting. The outcome offers an assessment of the concrete’s capacity to withstand cracking from tensile stresses, which is essential for the structure’s durability and longevity.

The modulus of elasticity is evaluated using cylindrical specimens of 100 × 200 mm, in compliance with ASTM C46945. This attribute quantifies the stiffness of the concrete, reflecting its deformation under an applied load. The modulus of elasticity is a crucial quantity in structural design, since it indicates the concrete’s capacity to withstand deformation under stress. Concrete possessing a greater modulus of elasticity will exhibit increased stiffness, resulting in less deformation under load, which is crucial for structures necessitating high dimensional stability, such as skyscrapers or bridges. The test is applying a specified compressive load to the cylinder and measuring the resultant deformation, from which the modulus of elasticity is derived. This feature is essential for assessing the concrete’s performance under service loads, especially when it is integrated into a structural system comprising other materials like steel or composite elements.

Microstructural characteristics

Microstructural study is essential for comprehending the behaviour and performance of concrete at the microscopic scale. Analysing the material’s microstructure provides critical insights into how the composition and structural arrangement of its constituents influence the overall characteristics, durability, and long-term performance of the concrete. This study utilises scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to meticulously analyse the microstructure of concrete, providing a high-resolution perspective on the material’s pore architecture, constituent distribution, and any flaws. Pore size distribution measurements are conducted on FC to further examine the influence of pore features on the material’s transport capabilities and durability.

Microstructural study is performed via a Quanta FEG 650 microscope and an Oxford Instruments X-Max microscope, both featuring sophisticated imaging capabilities to capture intricate features of the concrete’s structure. Samples measuring 15 mm × 15 mm × 15 mm are meticulously prepared for SEM examination. The sample dimensions are selected to enable adequate representation of the area of interest, facilitating a thorough evaluation of the microstructure. The specimens are analysed at a magnification of 120x, allowing for adequate detail to discern critical elements such as pore structure, ingredient distribution, and any defects or fissures that may affect the concrete’s performance.

The SEM technique provides an effective means of obtaining high-resolution images of the interior structure of FC. At this magnification, the SEM can disclose vital information on pore size and distribution, which are crucial determinants of the material’s permeability, strength, and durability. Moreover, SEM offers insights into the distribution and bonding of several components, including aggregates, binders, and additives, which significantly influence the concrete’s overall performance under varying environmental circumstances. Analysing the microstructure allows us to detect potential defects, like vacancies, cracks, or inadequately bound particles, which could result in diminished performance or premature breakdown of the concrete under stress or in harsh settings.

The pore structure is a crucial element of the microstructure revealed using SEM. The pores in concrete determine its permeability, and their size, distribution, and connection directly affect the material’s resistance to water absorption, chloride intrusion, and chemical degradation. SEM examination facilitates the detection of several pore types, including capillary pores, air voids, and gel pores, which can yield insights into the hydration process and the efficacy of the binding phases within the concrete. A well-developed microstructure characterised by a more refined and less linked pore network generally signifies enhanced durability and strength.

The distribution of the elements is another essential characteristic noticed using SEM. The appropriate distribution of aggregates, cementitious substances, and additives enhances the consistency and durability of the concrete. SEM facilitates the detection of discrepancies in this distribution, such as irregular adhesion between the binder and aggregate particles, which may compromise the concrete’s overall efficacy. Furthermore, the investigation can elucidate the interactions of different additives or extra materials, such as fly ash, slag, or silica fume, with the cementitious matrix, yielding critical insights into their contributions to the enhancement of concrete qualities.

Pore size distribution studies of FC are performed using a mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP) equipment to examine the pore structure of the concrete in greater detail. This technique is extensively employed to evaluate the pore architecture of porous substances. The distribution of pore sizes is crucial in assessing the permeability, strength, and durability of FC, as the dimensions, connectedness, and arrangement of pores can profoundly influence its transport characteristics.

The instrument utilised for this study is the Autopore IV 9500 (Micromeritics), engineered for high-resolution assessment of pore dimensions from 3 nm to 360 µm. During this procedure, mercury is introduced into the concrete specimen under progressively increasing pressure, and the volume of mercury that permeates at each pressure increment is recorded. This data is utilised to compute the distribution of pore diameters within the specimen. The findings furnish comprehensive data regarding total pore volume, mean pore diameter, and pore size distribution. The pore size distribution elucidates the extent of connectivity among pores and the capacity for fluid transport inside the material, which directly influences the concrete’s resistance to water, chloride ions, and other deleterious substances. A precisely regulated pore size distribution is frequently linked to enhanced performance regarding strength, permeability, and durability.

Besides elucidating the advantageous features of the concrete’s microstructure, SEM is also proficient in detecting probable flaws or deficiencies. These may encompass the existence of fissures, voids, or regions of inadequate adhesion that could undermine the material’s strength and longevity. Improper hydration, insufficient curing, or incorrect mixing can result in the development of weak zones or air pockets, so diminishing the overall integrity of the concrete. Early identification of these defects can guide decisions on the optimisation of mix designs, curing techniques, and materials to enhance the overall performance of the concrete.

The SEM analysis is performed in compliance with ISO 16,70046 standards, guaranteeing that the techniques and outcomes align with globally acknowledged principles for microstructural characterisation. This standard establishes a framework for the preparation of concrete samples and the corresponding procedures for imaging and analysis, guaranteeing reliable and reproducible findings. By adhering to these guidelines, we can guarantee that the SEM analysis yields a rigorous and scientifically valid evaluation of the concrete’s microstructure, hence enhancing the overall quality and dependability of the research outcomes.

These results align with other studies that explored the use of alternative cementitious materials. For instance, research on using masonry residue and ground copper slag as partial cement replacements in self-compacting concrete has demonstrated similar trends in reduced slump flow with increased replacement levels47,48. In summary, incorporating TAS as a partial OPC replacement in FC affects workability, but the changes in slump flow remain within acceptable ranges for practical use. Adjustments to mix proportions or the use of superplasticizers might be necessary to achieve desired workability levels in mixes with higher TAS content.

Hardened density

This finding contrasts with other studies on alternative materials like ground waste clay brick and rice husk ash, which generally reduce concrete density due to their lower specific gravities compared to OPC49. In conclusion, Fig. 5 illustrates that increasing TAS content in FC leads to a rise in its hardened density. This intriguing result suggests that TAS uniquely influences the material properties of FC, meriting further investigation to fully understand the mechanisms involved and optimize the mix design for specific density and mechanical property requirements.

Setting time

Figure 6 illustrates how the setting time of various FC mixes is affected by replacing cement with TAS. The y-axis indicates the setting time in minutes, while the x-axis shows the percentage of TAS replacement, ranging from 0 to 40%. The initial and final setting times are shown for each mix. For the control mix (0% TAS replacement), the initial setting time is about 100 min, and the final setting time is approximately 175 min. As the TAS replacement level increases, both the initial and final setting times exhibit a noticeable trend. With a 10% TAS replacement, the initial setting time decreases slightly, while the final setting time increases. At 20% TAS replacement, the initial setting time remains relatively unchanged, but the final setting time continues to rise. This trend becomes more evident at 30% and 40% TAS replacement, with significant increases in both initial and final setting times. At 40% TAS replacement, the final setting time extends up to 200 min. The increased setting time with higher TAS content is due to the lower reactivity of TAS compared to OPC. As a by-product of lithium extraction, TAS contains high levels of silica and alumina, which react more slowly with water than the calcium silicates in OPC. This slower reaction rate delays hydration, leading to prolonged setting times24. These results are consistent with other studies exploring alternative cementitious materials. For instance, using fly ash and slag as partial replacements for OPC has also been shown to extend concrete’s setting time due to their slower hydration rates. In conclusion, Fig. 6 shows that increasing the TAS content in FC leads to longer setting times. This extended setting period can be beneficial for applications that require longer workability, allowing more time for mixing, transporting, and placing the concrete. However, it is essential to balance the extended setting times with the overall project schedule and requirements to ensure efficient construction processes.

The study noted that augmenting the TAS content in FC results in an elongation of the setting time. This delay may improve workability and provide more time for placement, but it also presents significant disadvantages in specific building scenarios. In bulk casting, extended setting durations might result in delayed hardening and heighten the danger of differential curing, which may compromise the final strength and durability of substantial pours. In warmer weather circumstances, extended setting durations might increase the danger of drying shrinkage or uneven curing, as the mixture may remain in a plastic state for longer, subjecting it to external influences. Conversely, in cold conditions, extended setting durations might be beneficial as they facilitate improved hydration and adhesion among the components, ensuring more thorough curing at reduced temperatures. Nonetheless, this may also postpone the construction timeline, resulting in prolonged curing durations and possible interruptions in the production.

To alleviate potential delays resulting from extended setting time, the incorporation of specific chemical activators, including calcium-based accelerators or alkali activators, can expedite the setting time of FC while maintaining the mix’s overall strength and durability. These activators can improve the hydration process, particularly in bulk casting at elevated temperatures where swift setting is essential. Additionally, by decreasing the water-to-binder ratio may facilitate the regulation of the setting time by diminishing the quantity of water accessible for hydration, hence abbreviating the curing duration. Nonetheless, this must be meticulously balanced to prevent undermining the workability and consistency of the mixture.

This leads to diminished strength and a decreased elastic modulus, seen at elevated replacement levels.

SEM assessment

The SEM images in Fig. 16 illustrate the microstructures of FC samples with varying percentages of TAS cement replacement: FC0 (control), FC10 (10% TAS), FC20 (20% TAS), FC30 (30% TAS), and FC40 (40% TAS). These images reveal significant variations in microstructural properties as the TAS content increases. The control sample (FC0) exhibits large and connected pores, CH (calcium hydroxide) crystals, and C–S–H gel. The large pore characteristic of FC contributes to its lightweight nature but compromising its mechanical strength and durability due to increased permeability. The presence of CH crystals indicates ongoing hydration processes, contributing to overall strength but also suggesting potential porosity issues. The C–S–H gel, the primary binding phase, is present but not uniformly distributed, resulting in a less dense and more porous structure.

SEM images of FC mixed with raw pumice.

As TAS replacement increases to 10% (FC10), significant microstructural improvements are observed. The SEM image shows smaller pores and micropores, CH crystals, C–S–H gel, and a denser phase, suggesting that 10% TAS helps fill larger voids, resulting in a denser concrete matrix. The well-distributed C–S–H gel indicates improved hydration and binding properties, crucial for concrete strength and durability. The FC20 sample exhibits further improvements, with a dense phase, small pores, and well-distributed C–S–H gel. Enhanced packing and better material compaction contribute to increased mechanical properties, aligning with research indicating that TAS optimizes FC by improving its microstructure and reducing porosity24. Supporting studies, such as those on palm oil fuel ash (POFA), show that similar additives enhance concrete properties by filling voids and improving hydration, leading to a denser and more durable matrix58.

However, at higher TAS replacement levels, such as 30% (FC30) and 40% (FC40), the SEM images reveal a decline in microstructural quality. The FC30 sample shows larger pores and increased connectivity, suggesting reduced matrix compactness and diminished mechanical properties. The FC40 sample highlights significant microstructural degradation, with large pores, cracks, and less uniformly distributed C–S–H gel, resulting in substantial porosity and reduced material integrity. These features align with findings that excessive TAS content can lead to increased porosity and reduced strength24. Research indicates that overuse of certain cement replacements, such as POFA, can lead to microstructural degradation and decreased mechanical properties, emphasizing the need to maintain optimal TAS replacement levels for enhanced durability in FC58.

Pore size distribution

Figure 17 illustrates that the incorporation of TAS into FC mixtures resulted in an alteration in the arrangement of pore sizes, characterised by an increase in the number of voids with diameters of 400 µm or less and a decrease in those with diameters above 400 µm. The amount of micro-pores with widths less than 400 µm significantly rose upon the introduction of TAS at concentrations up to 20%. The particle size of TAS employed in FC has been shown to be adequate for pore bridging. Nevertheless, increasing the quantity of TAS integrated into the FC augments both pore dimensions and distribution. In comparison to the control specimen (F0), TAS-based FC exhibited a higher pore frequency of less than 400 µm. The inclusion of TAS markedly reduced macro-pores to a diameter of under 500 nm. The C–S–H gel diminished the total porosity by occupying the voids. The admixture particles, because to their remarkable micro-aggregate action and extensive specific surface area, occupied the pores and diminished the overall porosity. The degree of secondary hydration diminished, but total porosity augmented following the incorporation of 20% TAS into the C–S–H gel, which inhibited its capacity to occupy more nano-pores; these findings are consistent with several studies59–61. The frequency was adversely impacted by an elevation in TAS percentage beyond 20%, resulting in a reduction of holes measuring 400 µm or less and an augmentation of pores ranging from 400 to 1000 nm or greater.

Conclusion

Incorporating Tianqi aluminosilicate (TAS) as a cement replacement in foamed concrete (FC) significantly improves its mechanical and transport properties. The optimal replacement level of TAS, around 20%, results in notable improvements in compressive strength, flexural strength, splitting tensile strength, and elastic modulus.
