Finding the Voices: Representation of Nurses in Dutch Newspaper Media Between 2019 and 2022
Authors: Jessica van Wijk, Fredericus H. J. van Loon, Fleur Hendrickx, Danielle Arets, Pieterbas C. B. Lalleman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo explore how nurses were represented in five Dutch newspapers between 2019 and 2022, with a focus on their visibility in policy and decision‐making.BackgroundThe media significantly shape public understanding of healthcare. Despite their key role, nurses are often underrepresented in media, especially in policy‐related coverage. This study addresses this gap in the Dutch context.MethodsDirected content analysis of 272 articles published between 2019 and 2022 across five high‐circulation Dutch newspapers. A coding instrument was developed from existing literature and applied using Atlas.ti. ENTREQ criteria were followed throughout.ResultsNurses were least often cited in articles about policy, finance, and innovation. Senior leadership sources, predominantly male, were cited most frequently, especially during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Nurses were more commonly cited in human‐interest, nursing identity and image, strike‐related, and ethical stories. In many articles, images either excluded nurses or portrayed them in ways that obscured their identity.DiscussionThe findings indicate that nurses’ voices are not effectively utilized, particularly in policy discussions. This aligns with previous studies showing the underrepresentation of nurses as expert sources, with a predominance of male higher management and medical professionals being cited more frequently.ConclusionThe imbalance in nurse representation, particularly in policy and decision‐making, highlights the need for systemic change.Implications for Nursing and Health PolicyMedia competency training is needed in nursing (under)graduate curricula and in nursing leadership development programs to effectively convey their expertise and work. A collaborative and systemic approach involving key stakeholders is advised to ensure that nurse voices are elevated, especially related to health policy.

Introduction

The way journalists select, produce, and distribute news stories is consequential for citizens’ understanding and perception of important issues (Lecheler and De Vreese 2019). By informing audiences about topics they may have little direct knowledge of, journalists help define the scope of public debate and provide a template to understand an issue or event, stressing some aspects and pushing others to the background (Happer and Philo 2013), also referred to as framing (D'angelo 2019). Media coverage also influences how policymakers prioritize healthcare‐related issues and the extent to which these topics enter public and political debate (Van Aelst and Walgrave 2016; Gill and Baker 2019). Within this context, journalists' choices in framing nurses in text and imagery shape public perception, opinion, and trust in the profession. These portrayals can reinforce, challenge, or erode common stereotypes, ultimately impacting how nurses are viewed and valued in society. The portrayal of the nursing profession, the inclusion of nurse voices and expertise in the media, and the extent to which nurses are heard through media channels have long been a topic of interest and concern (Sigma Theta Tau International 1997; González et al. 2023). For example, Mason et al. (2018a) analyzed a large sample of healthcare‐related articles and concluded that nurses were depicted in only 4% of all analyzed photos, noted as sources in 2% of the selected healthcare articles, and appeared in zero opinion pieces. Furthermore, nurses were never consulted as sources in articles covering health policy issues. This lack of representation is particularly concerning given nurses’ deep, unique understanding of healthcare systems and the increasing emphasis on their involvement in the development, rather than mere execution of, health policy (Anders 2021).

Background

In the Netherlands, nurses represent the largest segment of the healthcare workforce (56.2%) and are integral to all facets of healthcare. Of this workforce, 88.2% are women (BIG 2024). In 2018, 85% of the Dutch population expressed trust in nurses, which further increased to 92% in 2020 (Van der Hulst et al. 2023), underscoring the high level of trust the public has in nurses. Given nurses’ extensive involvement in healthcare and the trust they garner from the public, it would be logical that media outlets seek their insights and perspectives when reporting on healthcare‐related topics. However, various studies show that the utilization of nurses as primary and expert sources remains minimal, and their representation continues to be based on traditional, outdated stereotypes (Mason et al. 2018a; Summers and Broome 2019). Historically, the image of the nurse in the media has centered around themes such as the nurse as the angelic, even militant hero, a selfless helper, an underpaid extension of the physician, as a hands‐on white woman working an unskilled and marginalized job, the image of the naughty nurse depicted as both a ‘sex object’ and an unfortunate but popular Halloween costume (Bennett et al. 2020; Gill et al. 2019; Summers and Summers 2015).

While these portrayals have proven persistent over time, the onset of the global COVID‐19 pandemic offered a potential turning point in how nurses were represented in the media. An analysis of Twitter posts revealed a shift towards a positive sentiment, with 91.94% of tweets during the pandemic expressing positive views about nurses, compared with only 35.65% before (Şahan et al. 2022). In 2020, an analysis of English language publications mentioning nurses in news headlines revealed that while traditional nursing “tropes,” such as the angelic nurse, remain abundant, an increase in coverage during the COVID‐19 pandemic possibly “elevated the profession towards one with enhanced value and worth” (Bennett et al. 2020, p. 2754). The authors nevertheless warn of a temporary effect if nurse leadership falls short in maintaining the momentum of change towards a positive discourse, a stronger nurse voice, and increased influence in public policy. Other studies paint a more sobering picture. For instance, Rebolledo et al. (2021) conclude that during the COVID‐19 pandemic, nurses were used as a primary source in only 0.6% of television news broadcasts in Spain. In Canadian media, Gagnon and Perron (2020) found that nurse imagery continued to rely heavily on conventional images, particularly the heroic war metaphor. Chen et al. (2024) report that in two major Chinese newspapers, only 11.8% of healthcare‐related articles between 1949 and 2022 focused on nurses, compared with 67.7% centered on physicians. In South Africa, Joubert et al. (2022) observed a significant gender imbalance in expert source representation during the first six months of the pandemic, with men comprising 70% of those quoted.

In light of these patterns, scholars argue that nurses must actively engage with the media to counter stereotypes, communicate their contributions, and strengthen their role in shaping public opinion and influencing policy (Ten Hoeve et al. 2014; Cipriano 2022; Godsey and Hayes 2023). Proactive communication and increased media visibility are seen as essential in accurately informing the public about their unique role in healthcare (González‐Luis 2024). In the Netherlands, scholars also raise the issue of nurses’ voices and opinions being overlooked in the media and underscore the need to look deeper into media representation of nurses (Van Wijk et al. 2022). The question of who oversees the shaping and framing of nurses’ images and who, through media exposure, can assert influence on the political landscape is key and has not been investigated in‐depth in the Dutch context. A better understanding of the visibility of nurses in the media will serve as a foundation to continue research and efforts to increase nurses' voices and visibility in the media, especially regarding healthcare‐related policy matters. This study therefore addresses that gap by analyzing the representation of nurses in five major Dutch newspapers between 2019 and 2022.

Methods

Research Design

This study employed a qualitative research design, using content analysis as the primary methodological approach. Specifically, a directed content analysis was applied, following the model described by Hsieh and Shannon (2005). Existing literature on media representation of nurses was consulted to identify initial variables and categories, providing a deductive framework for analysis. The research involved both quantifying the frequency of themes, sources, and other variables, and examining the content of articles to identify commonalities in the portrayal of nurses. For transparency purposes, rigor, and to guide the research process, the ENTREQ checklist was used (Supplementary File S2) (Tong et al. 2012).

Sample Selection

Five prominent Dutch newspapers were chosen to ensure a comprehensive and representative dataset. Monthly circulation figures according to the National Multimedia Research Group (2022) were used to choose the five newspapers with the highest circulation. The selected newspapers included Het Algemeen Dagblad, De Telegraaf, De Volkskrant, Het NRC Handelsblad, and Trouw. Nexis Uni was used to search for articles published between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2022, using the following search string: nurs* (verpleegkund*) AND healthcare (zorg), for which the Dutch terms were used. Search limits included newspaper articles published in the Dutch language and articles containing 200 words or more. After removing duplicates, this process resulted in a total sample size of 2,613 articles. All articles were evenly divided among three researchers (J.W., F.H., and F.L.) who examined the assigned articles independently according to the following inclusion criteria: (1) the content of the article mentions or discusses nursing and/or the nursing profession, and (2) the content of the article is relevant to healthcare. Articles were classified as being relevant, potentially relevant, or not relevant. Initially, to ensure consistency, the three researchers classified a random sample of 60 articles jointly until sufficient consensus was reached, after which the researchers continued independently. Any article designated as potentially relevant was examined and given a final classification of relevant or not relevant by a fourth investigator (P.L.). Items containing an illustration were tagged with a yes or no code. Ultimately, a total of 1,168 articles met the inclusion criteria. A data extraction file was created in an Excel data sheet (Microsoft, Redmond, Washington, USA) to register article details and was stored on a secure research drive.

To facilitate analysis across different time periods, we categorized all articles into folders labeled “pre‐COVID,” “during COVID,” and “post‐COVID.” We defined the “pre‐COVID” period as the time from January 2019 to March 2020, the latter marking the onset of significant public life disruptions due to the coronavirus in the Netherlands. The “during COVID” period was designated as the height of the virus's impact, spanning from March 2020 to February 2021. The “post‐COVID” period was identified as March 2021 to December 2022. Although COVID‐19 remained a part of daily life during this period, the widespread availability of vaccinations led to the perception of the virus as less lethal. Consequently, widespread measures such as lockdowns and evening curfews were no longer implemented, and public life began to return to normalcy.

Coding Instrument Development and Analysis

Drawing on prior inquiries into media representation of nurses (Gagnon and Perron 2020; González et al. 2023; Mason et al. 2018a), initial codes applicable to this study were identified and bundled in a coding instrument. We used qualitative data management software Atlas.ti 24.0.0. (Atlas.ti 2023) to test the coding instrument with three researchers (J.W., F.H., and F.L.) in a joint pilot coding session on a randomly selected sample of 40 articles. Following an iterative process of testing and refinement, the final coding instrument (Supplementary File S1) enabled the collection of basic descriptive data, including article length, newspaper source, healthcare branch (e.g., mental health, hospital care, rehabilitation), and author. Further, the instrument facilitated thematic content coding and source identification. A randomly selected sample of 25% (n = 292) using Random Allocation Software version 2.0 (Saghaei 2004) was selected for data analysis. The articles were coded by the three trained researchers (J.W., F.H., and F.L.). After all articles were coded, a final consensus session was held, during which we removed 20 articles from the final sample due to irrelevancy, ensuring that all included articles strictly met the preset inclusion criteria. The final sample, therefore, consisted of 272 coded articles, comprising 24% of the total dataset. The distribution of the 272 coded articles over time was as follows: 60 articles published “before COVID‐19,” 103 articles “during COVID‐19,” and 109 articles in the “post‐COVID‐19 period.” Data analysis focused on describing quantitative patterns and trends in reporting, and an exploration of the qualitative content of the selected articles.

Trustworthiness

During the pilot coding session of 40 randomly selected articles, we resolved any emerging discrepancies, incorporated new codes, removed redundant codes, and consolidated overlapping codes. The newly developed coding instrument underwent a review process involving consultation with two researchers: P.L., an expert in nursing opinion‐leadership, and D.A., an expert in journalism. Their insights and feedback were incorporated until consensus was achieved regarding the refinement of the coding instrument. The researchers then individually coded the same randomly chosen 40 articles, which were merged into one Atlas.ti account in order to calculate intercoder reliability using Krippendorff's alpha. Any articles with an unacceptable level of agreement (Krippendorff's alpha > 0.8; Krippendorf and Bock 2009) were discussed until consensus was reached. This iterative process was repeated until an acceptable level of agreement was reached and all coders were sufficiently trained to continue coding individually.

Findings

Healthcare Branch

A total of seven distinctive healthcare branches were identified and included in the coding instrument. However, the majority of the articles (40.1%) did not focus on a specific branch of healthcare but addressed topics relevant to multiple branches or the healthcare sector in general. These articles were coded with an “overarching” branch code. Examples included multiple articles on a proposed legislation (Beroepen in Individuele Gezondheidszorg (BIG)) aimed at introducing a new level of professional differentiation among nurses, articles discussing the ramifications of political and financial decisions on all sectors of healthcare, articles discussing the pervasive issue of nursing shortages across various branches of care and potential strategies for mitigation, and articles addressing the challenges posed by the COVID‐19 pandemic for the nursing profession. Following this, a substantial proportion of the articles (37.4%) were related to hospital care. Articles concerning nursing home care accounted for 11.2%. Interestingly, of all the articles discussing nursing home care, only 15% were published before the COVID‐19 pandemic, highlighting a significant shift in focus on nursing homes, very likely as a result of the pandemic. The remaining branches of care (mental health, public health, rehabilitation, special needs care, and community health) collectively covered 11.2% of the articles.

Topics

The coding instrument included 20 unique topic codes, which are listed in Figure 1. After coding each article for relevant topics, a total of 749 topics were identified across the 272 newspaper articles. Few articles confined themselves to a single topic; instead, a combination of topic codes was often necessary to accurately capture the content of the article. Analysis of the distribution of topics across the predetermined time periods provided insights into the focus of newspaper coverage during each period and is illustrated in Figure 1. During, but especially after the COVID‐19 period, there was an uptick in articles focusing on “staff shortages,” “quality of care,” “workload,” and “human interest” stories. This shift reflects the heightened attention to the working conditions and emotional well‐being of healthcare workers during and after the peak of the pandemic. Interestingly, during the COVID‐19 pandemic time period, a decline was noted in articles focusing on the development of the nursing profession.

As most articles were coded with multiple topics and the most frequently used code was “COVID‐19,” we analyzed common co‐occurrences between this topic and others, see Figure 2. Looking closer at the content, articles that were coded with “COVID‐19” and “policy, politics, and decision‐making” often illustrated the challenges and failures encountered in implementing the newly introduced, and at times impractical, government guidelines. Other stories highlighted the creative approaches adopted to address staff shortages during the crisis within the existing policy framework. Furthermore, numerous articles detailed the acute shortages of testing equipment, especially in nursing homes, since guidelines for personal protective equipment and testing were predominantly directed towards hospitals.

Identifying Healthcare News Sources

We created 23 codes to identify and classify sources. A total of 828 cited sources across 272 newspaper articles were identified. The four codes most frequently, in order of frequency, used to describe a source were (1) Board of Director (BoD), Chief Executive Officer (CEO), president, director, (2) hospital/healthcare employee/other, (3) physician, and (4) nurse. The second code (hospital/healthcare employee/other) was rarely used as a code by itself but was utilized as an additional descriptor to aid classification of healthcare personnel in general. When a source had multiple professional designations, such as a physician with an academic appointment, their role was assigned based on the description as detailed in the article. Another example includes the mentioning of a director of a healthcare‐related institution whom the authors know to be a nurse, but the article does not mention the fact that the director is a nurse. Therefore, the general audience reading the article would only know the person is a director, and the source would be coded as “BoD, CEO, president, director.”

Nurses were used as sources in 84 articles and were therefore represented as a source in 30.9% of the total number of analyzed articles. Members of a BoD, CEOs, presidents, or directors of healthcare‐related institutions were cited in 121 articles, 44.5% of the total. Physicians were cited in 81 articles and were therefore represented in 29.8% of the total articles. Before the COVID‐19 pandemic (n = 60 articles), nurses were mentioned as sources in 21 articles (35%), members of a BoD, CEOs, directors, or presidents also in 21 (35%) articles, and physicians in 17 (28.3%). A total of 103 articles in the sample were classified as occurring during the pandemic, and we noted that the frequency of consulting individuals in senior managerial positions increased during this time. BoD, CEOs, directors, or presidents were cited in 53 articles (51.5%), physicians in 32 articles (31.1%), and nurses were consulted as sources for 30 articles (21.1%). In the post‐COVID period, BoD, CEOs, directors, or presidents continued to be consulted frequently in the 109 articles analyzed, appearing in 47 articles (43.1%), while nurses were used as a source in 33 articles (30.3%) and physicians in 32 articles (29.4%).

Sources and Gender

Of the 828 identified sources, 419 (50.6%) were male, 323 (39.0%) were female, and the gender of 86 sources (10.4%) could not be identified. The majority of the sources with an unknown gender were sources related to organizations such as the Ministry of Health, labor unions, nursing associations, and researchers affiliated with institutions, where the individual's name was not mentioned (34 out of 86, or 39.5%). Also, almost a quarter of the unidentified gender sources were spokespersons (21 out of 86, or 24.4%). Other sources whose gender could not be identified included references to “a surgeon,” “a nurse,” or instances where it was not possible to derive the gender from the name.

BoD, CEOs, presidents, or directors were identified as sources in 196 instances, physicians were identified 130 times, and nurses were identified 117 times. The majority of sources labeled as BoD, CEO, president, or director were male, making up about 70% of all mentioned sources. Of all the nurses who were identified as sources, 76.9% were female. Physicians, despite being a more gender‐balanced profession (58.7% female (BIG 2024)), were overrepresented by males: 63.8% against 30% females.

When looking at the 117 nurses identified in the dataset, six held a position as BoD, president, or director, accounting for 5.1% of the total nurses identified. Upon examining the sources that were coded both as a BoD, CEO, director, or president and as members of a nursing association (n = 25), a recurring pattern of names was noted. The female president of a BoD of a nursing association was cited as a source in 10 of the 25 instances. In one article, she was also identified as a nurse, and in another, she was identified as a nursing professor. Furthermore, a male president of the same organization was cited four times, and a female president for the branch of intensive care unit nurses within the same association was cited twice. The three aforementioned individuals together accounted for 16 of the 25 instances.

Nurses and Topics

Nurses were most frequently cited as sources in articles concerning “strikes,” “nursing identity and image,” “ethics,” and “human interest” topics. On the other hand, they were least often cited in articles addressing “policy, politics and decision‐making,” “business, finance and insurance,” and “technology, innovation, and digitalization of care.” Out of the 117 instances where nurses were cited, only 19.7% were in articles discussing “policy, politics, and decision‐making.” When nurses were consulted as sources for these topics, they frequently focused on their experiences executing policies rather than discussing or being part of the decision‐making process. For example, in one article, two male CEOs elaborated on the particulars and development of new COVID‐19 guidelines, whereas two nurses discussed the difficulties in implementing those guidelines: “It is not possible, washing someone from 1.5 meters away. Yeah, with a mop” and, “Look, the bathroom isn't even two by two; how will we keep 1.5 meters distance? I still had a mask at home that I brought from my last job as a nail tech” (Von Piekarts and Tieleman 2020, p. 2). We did find one article, authored by a female nurse educator, advocating for the direct inclusion of nurses on the Board of Directors and for structural changes to labor contracts to create more opportunities for nurse leadership positions (Cordfunke‐Krebbekx 2021). It is important to note that this article was an opinion piece authored and sent to the newspaper by the author herself rather than being the result of a journalist's consultation.

Human‐interest stories featured a nurse as a source in just over half of the instances (51.4%). Examples include a full‐page cover story about an Iranian woman who worked as a nurse in Iran but lacked the necessary language skills to find work in the Netherlands (Van Gestel 2021), a two‐page obituary honoring a male nurse who succumbed to cancer (Ploeger 2021), and a festive Christmas article featuring two female nurses who enjoy and look forward to working during the holiday season (Stavrianos 2022).

We found that some articles would have benefited from consulting a nurse. For example, an article published in June 2020 discussed negotiations regarding salary increases for nurses during the COVID‐19 pandemic and highlighted how this debate created a divide between political parties. Notably, no nurses were consulted as sources for the article. Instead, the article featured the opinions of several male politicians on the matter. Ultimately, the motion to increase salaries did not pass (Kieskamp 2020). The lack of nurse representation in the article is noteworthy, as their perspectives could have provided insight into the impact of policy decisions on the nursing profession and the broader implications on staff satisfaction and retention during the pandemic. Another article called for a “seat at the table” for more nursing personnel in crisis management teams but omitted nurse insights. Instead, the sources consulted were two male medical professors and a male member of a nursing home board, who did not have a background in nursing care (Nieber 2022).

Gender and Topic

The most frequently discussed topic, “COVID‐19,” was predominantly addressed by male sources, who accounted for 56.7% of the cited sources on this topic, while women were consulted as a source in 32.8% of all sources consulted for his topic. The often co‐occurring subject of “policy, politics, and decision‐making” was also more commonly discussed by male sources, who were cited in 50.6% of these articles, against 37.2% female. Overall, topics that we had identified as more commonly discussed by nurses tended to also be more female‐dominated. Notably, articles related to “human‐interest,” “nurse identity and nurse image,” and “strikes” featured females as sources in more than 50% of cases, see Figure 3. Females were consulted as sources in less than a third of the articles discussing matters surrounding “vaccination campaigns and testing,” “policy, politics, and decision‐making,” and “business, finances, and insurance.”

Images

Among the 272 articles analyzed, 124 included at least one image depicting a healthcare worker. In total, 222 images were coded, as some articles contained more than one relevant image. Each image was categorized as either “nurse” (n = 92), “not a nurse” (n = 54), or “unidentifiable healthcare worker” (n = 76). The latter category refers to individuals who were clearly healthcare workers, for example, due to a uniform or clinical context, but whose specific role was not clear in the image or byline. Across the 124 articles with images, 61 articles (22.4% of the full sample) featured at least one image coded as a nurse, 57 (21%) included only unidentifiable healthcare workers, and 27 (9.9%) featured images coded as not showing a nurse.

Centrality of nursing as a theme was coded for 266 of the 272 articles. Of these, nursing was the central focus in 119 articles, a secondary theme in 73, and mentioned in 74. In the 119 centrally themed articles, nurses appeared in images in 41 cases (34.5%). In articles where nursing was a secondary theme, nurses were pictured in 15 cases (20.5%), and in those where nursing was merely mentioned, this occurred in just four cases (5.4%). Notably, in 33 articles where nursing was a central theme and the article included at least one image, the image did not depict a nurse. These 33 articles represent 27.7% of the centrally themed nursing articles that included an image. For example, in “The hospital of the future is built around nurses” (Van der Geest 2022), which emphasized the rising influence of nurses in hospital design, the accompanying image showed the chairman of the board and the hospital's interior rather than a nurse. Visual anonymity was another common theme. Of the 127 individuals coded as nurses, 60 were unrecognizable, 33 of whom were obscured by protective clothing such as face masks. Still, a number of articles, particularly those related to COVID‐19 or personal stories, did portray nurses in a more individualized way, with names and portrait‐style images.

Discussion

This study aimed to explore the representation of nurses in Dutch newspapers between 2019 and 2022, using directed content analysis of 272 articles from five leading newspapers. While some studies suggest a positive shift in the image of nurses during and/or post‐COVID‐19 (Bennett et al. 2020; Şahan et al. 2022), our analysis did not find considerable differences in the topics where nurses were used as a source pre‐ and post‐COVID. This suggests that although their role might have been recognized publicly, it did not necessarily lead to greater inclusion in policy and decision‐making, which was a topic that saw increased media attention during and after the pandemic. These findings align with Verhoeven et al. (2024a), who concluded that Dutch nurses struggled with strategic positioning during the pandemic. Our analysis indicates that while nurses are mentioned in healthcare‐related articles, their voices are not always effectively utilized, especially in areas of policy. When cited, nurses were primarily cited giving information about the operational aspects of their profession, with little presence in broader discussions with societal or policy implications. This trend resonates with a recent study on nurse leadership during crisis, which suggests that, like journalists, executive hospital boards tend to frame nurses as more practical than strategic figures, ultimately jeopardizing their positioning and voice (Verhoeven et al. 2024b). Additionally, our findings align with earlier studies highlighting the underrepresentation of nurses as sources and experts in the media. Mason et al. (2018a) found that in their study, nurses were mentioned in only 13% of healthcare articles, with minimal coverage on health policy (4%). They also noted that nurses were never cited as sources in health policy discussions; instead, they mainly commented on the profession itself. Although a direct comparison between our study and theirs is limited due to differences in methodology and context, our findings reveal similar patterns and suggest this could be a wider issue. Therefore, efforts to explore media representation of nurses in other countries are recommended.

Our results highlight a predominance and entrenched hierarchy of male higher management and male medical professionals in media representation. In our study, the most frequently cited sources were members of Boards of Directors, CEOs, presidents, or directors, with almost 70% of these sources being male. The dominance of male leadership voices in media representation not only marginalizes (female) nurses but also limits diverse perspectives in healthcare decision‐making. To address this, healthcare institutions must proactively integrate nurses into governance structures and encourage media engagement. These findings align with a recent study on the feeble position of (female) nurse directors in the Netherlands (Martini et al. 2025) and an international integrative review highlighting the unequal inclusion of nurses on governing boards (Sundean et al. 2017). Both studies emphasize the socio‐historical undervaluation of female strategic expertise. Further, our results are consistent with findings of Joubert et al. (2022), who highlight the disproportional feature of male experts during the COVID‐19 pandemic. The authors emphasize that those being prominently featured affect who gets to influence policy and public opinion, and the authors advise institutions to support and incentivize female experts to engage with the media.

Intriguingly, the gender disparity among cited sources was quite pronounced for physicians. Although roughly 59% of all physicians in the Netherlands are female (BIG 2024), only 30% of the physicians cited in the analyzed articles were women. This finding is consistent with the annual Global Media Monitoring Project (2020), which reported that gender inequality in Dutch news media remains pervasive. The report, which, among other news outlets, analyzed six newspapers (including all five newspapers included in this study) concluded that when a nurse was featured in a story, 67% of those nurses were female, whereas when a physician was featured, this number dropped to 20%. Furthermore, when women were cited, it was more likely in the context of human‐interest stories rather than stories focused on professional expertise. They further report that during the COVID‐19 pandemic, only 19% of the stories analyzed were presented from a female perspective, and 26% of those cited as sources were female. Thus, the report also highlights the underrepresentation of women as sources during the pandemic, reinforcing the findings and significance of this study.

One of the strengths of our study is that it spans a four‐year period, covering both a time marked by a global health crisis and one without. During the COVID‐19 pandemic, we observed a notable increase in references to high‐level management figures. In their study, Van der Meer et al. (2017) explain this trend, noting that during crisis situations, journalists gravitate toward organizational sources who are deemed credible, knowledgeable, and familiar figures. As a result, other important but less familiar voices might be overlooked. This creates an imbalance in event coverage and reduces the representation of diverse perspectives. Mason et al. (2018b) further emphasize that biases against women and nurses can limit their representation in the media, despite the potential value they bring to healthcare stories. Journalists can lack an understanding of the nursing profession and struggle to locate and approach nurses as sources. For example, a recent study showed that journalist students in Spain continue to perceive the nursing profession as one of limited autonomy and being subordinate to physicians (Tíscar‐González et al. 2022). These issues highlight the need for further research into how healthcare journalists select and access sources during both crises and non‐crisis periods. Addressing this gap requires not only training journalists and journalism students to recognize and include nurses as valuable sources but also ensuring that nurses are more visible in leadership and decision‐making roles, trained in media competency skills, and supported in working closely with public relations offices of healthcare organizations (Mason and Ricciardi 2025). This effort is essential for demonstrating to the public, journalists, and policymakers that nurses' specialized knowledge is indispensable and should be a key part of broader healthcare discussions.

The scope of this study was limited to print media and did not include any exclusively online news sources. Although all the included newspapers publish the majority of their articles online, it is possible that some of the articles analyzed were only available in print. The 2024 Dutch Digital News Report highlights a decrease in news interest among younger readers, particularly those aged 18–34. The report also noted that this age group increasingly relies on social media platforms for news, with Instagram being the most important source. Additionally, platforms such as X and TikTok, as well as influencers and “regular people” without formal journalistic training, are recognized as important news sources (Lauf et al. 2024). This pivotal development has important implications for future research, which should include the dissemination of news on social media platforms. Furthermore, efforts to inform the younger public about developments in healthcare should increasingly focus on social media, while ensuring that nurses and the nursing profession are represented in a balanced manner across these platforms.

Conclusion

This study contributes to the growing body of research on the media representation of nurses by offering a detailed analysis of Dutch newspaper coverage between 2019 and 2022. Our findings provide evidence of the underrepresentation of nurses as sources in news related to policy and decision‐making, business and finance, and technology and innovation. In coverage of policy and decision‐making in particular, there was a marked reliance on male senior leadership as sources, especially during the COVID‐19 pandemic. By contrast, nurses were more likely to be included in stories focused on human‐interest topics, as well as in articles addressing nursing identity and image, strikes, and ethical issues. Several articles also missed the opportunity to include nurses as sources, even when the subject matter directly concerned their work. Many images depicted nurses in ways that made them unrecognizable. By actively integrating nurses into public discourse, equipping them with media competencies, and fostering stronger collaborations between journalists, policymakers, and healthcare leaders, we can ensure that nursing expertise is recognized as indispensable in shaping the future of healthcare.

Implications for Health and Nursing Policy

Given the pressing challenges ahead, such as staffing shortages, an aging population, and poor workforce retention, it is time to recognize nurses' agency and elevate the profession as one characterized by strong expertise and a relevant voice in policy and decision‐making. Promoting a positive image of nursing, increasing visibility in the media, and showcasing policymaking competencies require a concerted effort from multiple stakeholders. A collaborative and systemic approach to alter the nursing media landscape, image, and representation is warranted and involves, first and foremost, nurses, nursing directors, and nursing board members themselves (Mason and Ricciardi 2025). However, in order to be impactful, they need the full support of other board members, CEOs, physicians, public relations staff, policy‐ and decision makers, and journalists (González‐Luis 2024; Van Wijk et al. 2022). Educational curricula can contribute by equipping nurses with foundational knowledge of policymaking processes and encouraging active participation, for instance, through policy internships (Inayat et al. 2023). Additionally, fostering partnerships with journalism faculties will create opportunities for valuable media competency training. Such training has been identified as a key strategy empowering nurses to effectively engage with the media, assert their professional agency, and influence political decision‐making (Gagnon and Perron 2020; Mason et al. 2018a; Martín‐Fernández et al. 2025; Myers et al. 2022).
