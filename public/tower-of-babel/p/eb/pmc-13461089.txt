When crime signals cortex: frontotemporal dementia presenting as late-onset criminality and the case for neuroimaging in geriatric offenders
Authors: Rizwan Rafique, Syeda Tayyaba Batool, Syeda Malaika Batool, Joshua Ekouo
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

Late-life emergence of criminal or socially deviant behavior is often interpreted solely through legal or psychiatric lenses, yet mounting evidence suggests that, in some individuals, it reflects underlying neurodegeneration, particularly behavioral-variant frontotemporal dementia (bvFTD). Revised diagnostic criteria emphasize early personality change, disinhibition, apathy, and loss of empathy as core features, often preceding memory impairment[1]. Frontotemporal dementia (FTD) selectively affects frontal-insular-limbic circuits governing moral reasoning and impulse control[2]. Case series demonstrate that individuals with bvFTD may develop theft, sexual misconduct, or violation of social norms despite no prior criminal history[3]. Importantly, population-level analyses show criminal behavior is significantly more frequent in FTD than in Alzheimer’s disease, underscoring its disease-specific neurobehavioral signature[4]. Neurobiologically, degeneration of salience and executive networks disrupts emotional valuation and social restraint, explaining why such acts are frequently impulsive rather than premeditated[5]. Lesion-network mapping studies further reveal that focal damage to circuits linked with moral decision-making can produce criminal behaviors even without psychiatric illness[6]. Structural neuroimaging correlates abnormal conduct with atrophy in orbitofrontal and anterior temporal regions critical for behavioral regulation[7]. Despite this evidence, medico-legal systems rarely mandate neurological assessment for older adults presenting with first-time offenses. Failure to recognize neurodegenerative causes risks wrongful attribution of intent, inappropriate punishment, and missed opportunities for clinical care. From both ethical and public-health perspectives, geriatric-onset antisocial behavior should be treated as a neurological red flag requiring brain imaging and cognitive evaluation. As longevity increases worldwide, clinicians and legal authorities must collaborate to distinguish criminality from cortical disease. Recognizing when crime reflects neurodegeneration rather than volition is not merely diagnostic precision; it is justice informed by neuroscience.
