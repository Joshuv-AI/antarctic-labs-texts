Anion coordination chemistry of benzoates with cationic biguanide-cobalt(iii): from binding studies to biological evaluation
Authors: Bhupesh Kumar, Ritu Bala, Vinit Prakash, Jaya Verma, Simrandeep Kaur, Satwinderjeet Kaur, Amalija Golobič
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An anion-templated supramolecular strategy based on anion coordination-driven assemblies (ACDA) has been developed using a biguanide-cobalt(iii) complex as a functional building unit. In this approach, benzoate anions (ABA: p-aminobenzoate, NBA: p-nitrobenzoate, MBA: p-methylbenzoate and MEBA: p-methoxybenzoate) act as directing agents, promoting self-assembly with complex cation [Co(Hbig)3]3+ (Hbig: biguanide) via H-bonding and π–π stacking between adjacent para-substituted benzoates. The SCXRD studies also elucidate that benzoates exhibit varying numbers (up to 7) of H-bonding interactions with the complex cation as well as with lattice water. These interactions collectively facilitate the construction of ACDA and contribute to the overall stability of the supramolecular architecture. These architectures were also characterized by using spectroscopic techniques (UV-visible, FTIR and NMR), DLS and TGA. Anion coordination studies were performed to measure the binding affinities of benzoate anions towards the complex cation, signifying strong and selective anion coordination of NBA. Additionally, the ACDA were evaluated for biological activity (anticancer and antimicrobial), revealing promising potential as compared to their respective standard drugs.

Introduction

Anion coordination chemistry is a supramolecular discipline in which anions serve as central coordinating units, analogous to transition metals in classical coordination chemistry. Introduced by Jean-Marie Lehn in 1978, the field has advanced from basic anion recognition to the design of complex functional materials.1 Anions, like metals, display primary valency (charge balance) and secondary valency (coordination number), but their coordination is governed mainly by non-covalent interactions such as hydrogen bonding (–NH2, –OH etc.), π–π interactions, and halogen bonding rather than covalent bonds (see Scheme 1). Larger polyatomic anions require higher coordination numbers where monoatomic anions require lower coordination number.2 The ACDA form predictable supramolecular architectures (helicates, polyhedra, cages etc.) and underpin a rapidly growing field that links fundamental research with real world applications such as anion sensing & receptors,3–5 catalysis,6,7 therapeutic8–11 and environmental remediation12,13etc. In this article, para-substituted benzoate anions are selected because they can coordinate to the metal center either as ligands through coordination covalent bonds or as counter anions via non-covalent interactions. In benzoates, the oxygen atoms of the carboxylate group act as hydrogen bond acceptors with donors of complex cation, [Co(Hbig)3]3+ leading to 1D layers, 2D sheets and 3D supramolecular coordinated frameworks. Additionally, the planar aromatic rings of adjacent benzoate anions can align parallel to one another, enabling π–π stacking interactions that further enhance the stability and rigidity of the crystal structure. To effectively apply the anion coordination concept, the secondary coordination sphere selected for benzoates should possess the following potential: (a) high positive charge on the secondary coordination sphere (to promote strong electrostatic interactions), (b) the presence of hydrogen-donor groups (to enable extensive hydrogen-bonding interactions), and (c) an appropriate geometry of the secondary coordination sphere (to facilitate pre-organization & structural complementarity).14 In the literature, sporadic reports are available on anion binding for p-aminobenzoate,15–19p-nitrobenzoate,15,17–27p-methoxybenzoate,24,26,28p-methylbenzoate21–31 and none of them have been explored under the heading of ACDA. Moreover, previously studied anion binders for para-substituted benzoates exhibit several significant limitations, like lack of aqueous media, sophisticated synthetic procedures, not economical and generation of hazardous waste.32–34 To address these gaps, an ideal anion coordination sphere has been selected for benzoates which is based on biguanide-cobalt(iii) complex cation, [Co(Hbig)3]3+ and it meets all the essential requirements for supramolecular anion coordination assemblies. In addition, such assemblies may also serve as reducing the ionic character and increasing the lipophilicity of the supramolecular structures.35 This enhancement further facilitates effective penetration into microbial or cancerous cells, leading to the inhibition of their growth. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, only one report on SCXRD of tris(biguanide)cobalt(iii) chloride36 has been published, and our group is currently the only one actively working in this area of research.37–39 Therefore, this article reports synthesis, characterization, single crystal X-ray structure elucidation, aqueous anion coordination properties, anticancer and antimicrobial applications of tris(biguanide)cobalt(iii) cation containing environmentally and commercially important para-substituted benzoate anions (ABA, NBA, MEBA and MBA).

Experimental

Synthesis of tris(biguanide)cobalt(iii) p-aminobenzoate chloride 1.695 hydrate (1)

To synthesis tris(biguanide)cobalt(iii) p-aminobenzoate chloride 1.695 hydrate, an aqueous solution of p-aminobenzoate (0.491 g, 3.10 mmol) was prepared by adding p-aminobenzoic acid (0.425 g, 3.10 mmol) and NaOH (0.125 g, 3.10 mmol) in double distilled water. In another beaker, hot aqueous solution of [Co(Hbig)3]Cl3·H2O (0.5 g, 1.027 mmol) was prepared. Both aqueous solutions were mixed, stirred well and crystalline product appeared after about 3 days. The crystals were collected by gravimetric filtration, washed with ice-cold water and air-dried. Overall yield of product was 0.69 g (95.83%). The product was decomposed at 513 K. Anal. calc. for [Co(Hbig)3](p-aminobenzoate)2Cl·1.695H2O, (700.57 g mol−1): C, 34.29; H, 5.24; N, 33.97; O, 12.99; Co, 8.41%, found: C, 34.43; H, 5.22; N, 34.15; O, 12.61; Co, 8.47%.1H NMR (500 MHz, DMSO-d6) δ 7.59 (d, J = 8.5, 4H, C4), 6.89 (s, 12H, N2), 6.46 (d, J = 8.0, 4H, C5), 5.26 (s, 6H, N1), 4.87 (s, 4H, N6) and 13C NMR (126 MHz, DMSO-d6) δ 172.84 (C2), 154.77 (C1), 150.23 (C3), 131.10 (C4), 125.82 (C5), 112.87 (C6).

Results and discussion

Synthesis

With this expectation, the interaction of para-substituted benzoate anions with the tris(biguanide)cobalt(iii) cation may lead to the formation of ACDA, in which the benzoate anions serve not only as charge-balancing species but also as structure-directing units that promote supramolecular organization, as proposed (see Scheme 2). The sodium para-substituted benzoates (NaABA, NaNBA, NaMEBA and NaMBA) have been reacted with tris(biguanide)cobalt(iii) chloride monohydrate (SM) in 3 : 1 molar ratio as given in Scheme 2. The compositions of all products were initially determined by elemental analysis, which revealed that 1 and 4 were obtained in a 2 : 1 stoichiometric ratio, while 2 and 4 were obtained in a 3 : 1 with respect to their corresponding para-substituted benzoates and complex cation. The Co(iii) center is octahedrally coordinated by three biguanide ligands, rendering it kinetically inertness through strong Co–N bonds, while their twenty-one, N–H donors may enable extensive hydrogen interactions with para-substituted benzoate anions (see Scheme 2). Beyond hydrogen bonding, electrostatic attractions, aromatic π–π interactions and partial coordination linking neighboring cobalt complexes may further stabilize higher-order assemblies. The complexes exhibit different stoichiometric ratios (2 : 1 or 3 : 1) because the counter anions serve a dual function: maintaining charge neutrality and stabilizing the supramolecular crystal architecture. During crystallization, the system adopts the arrangement with the lowest overall lattice energy. The incorporation of counter anions can enhance packing efficiency by filling voids, reducing steric strain, and stabilizing channels or layered arrangements, thereby leading to different stoichiometries and improved crystal stability. Furthermore, the purity and composition of the complexes were confirmed by NMR spectroscopy, and these results were further supported by single-crystal X-ray structure determination.

Ultraviolet-visible absorption spectroscopy

The UV-visible spectra of 1–4 have been compared with SM, NaABA, NaNBA, NaMBA and NaMEBA. The absorption spectrum of Co(iii) low spin (d6), [Co(Hbig)3]Cl3·H2O shows two characteristic d–d absorption transitions at 477 (1A1g → 1T1g) and 353 nm (1A1g → 1T2g) along with one ligand to metal charge transfer (LMCT) transition at 270 nm.37 Moreover, sodium benzoates showed two characteristic absorption bands (NaABA (at 215 & 265 nm), NaNBA (at 217 & 273 nm), NaMBA (at 234 & 271 nm) and NaMEBA (at 247 & 280 nm)).16,23,40 In case of 1 three absorption bands were observed with the disappearance of 215 nm band of anion (see Table S1). LMCT band of SM (at 270 nm) merged with absorption bands of NaABA and single band appears at 265 nm, shows 5 nm blue shift of LMCT band (see Fig. S1). Complex 2 exhibits four absorption bands, with the band at 273 nm arising from the overlap of the 273 nm band of NaNBA and the LMCT band (270 nm) of SM. Complex 3 and 4 shows four absorption bands as given in Table S1. Moreover, the bands of NaMBA (271 nm) and NaMEBA (280 nm) were merged with the LMCT band of SM (270 nm), resulting in new bands at 265 nm and 280 nm, respectively. The blue (5 nm) and red shifts (10 nm) were observed in 3 and 4 respectively for LMCT band of SM. The shifting and mergers of absorptions bands in complexes indicate that anions are coordinated with spherically symmetric octahedral complex cations resulting in supramolecular architectures.

Nuclear magnetic resonance

The 1H NMR data for tris(biguanide)cobalt(iii) chloride monohydrate have been previously reported39 and compared with all the new complexes (1–4). The 1H-NMR spectra of 1–4, shows two signals at δ value in the ranges 6.99–7.07 (s, 12H, H–N2) and 4.93–5.26 ppm (s, 6H, H–N1) (numbering scheme of protons are given in Fig. S6–S9). A third signal overlaps with the broad water peak at δ value in the range 3.49–3.65 ppm (s, 3H, H–N3).45 The influence of para-substitution (–NH2, –NO2, –CH3 and –OCH3) on benzoate is clearly visualized due to shifting of δ values of aromatic protons (H–C4 and H–C5) with respect to benzoate anion.39 Among the four substituents, the strong electron donating (–NH2) and electron withdrawing (–NO2) groups exhibit the most prominent upfield and downfield shifts respectively for the aromatic protons (H–C4 and H–C5). The decreasing orders of δ values for H–C4 and H–C5 protons are 2 > 4 > 3 > 1 and 2 > 3 > 4 > 1 respectively (see Fig. S6–S9). Furthermore, the characteristic signals of para-substitutes (–N H2, –CH3 and –OCH3) are given in experimental section. 13C-NMR spectra have characteristics signals of carboxylate (C2) carbon at δ 172.84, 169.64, 172.04 and 171.75 ppm respectively for 1–4 (see Fig. S10–S13). Additionally, signal for –CN (C1) carbon was observed downfield from 153.51 ppm (for SM) to 154.77, 154.30, 154.57 and 154.78 ppm for 1–4 respectively. In 3 (–CH3) and 4 (–OCH3), carbon signal was observed at 21.39 and 55.55 ppm respectively. No additional peaks were observed in 1H and 13C-NMR spectra of all the complexes confirmed the purity. From the above data, it was observed that the δ values in the 1H and 13C NMR spectra showed downfield shift relative to those of the corresponding starting complex cation and anion. This phenomenon may be due to extensive hydrogen bonding between N–H groups of electronegative atoms of anions with cation resulting second sphere anion coordination moieties.

Single crystal X-ray structure of 1–4

Fig. 1, 2, 3 and 4 show ORTEP drawing of asymmetric units of the complexes with formulas [Co(Hbig)3](ABA)2Cl·1.695H2O (1), [Co(Hbig)3] (NBA)3·2H2O (2), [Co(Hbig)3](MBA)3·2H2O (3) and [Co(Hbig)3] (MEBA)3Cl·H2O (4), respectively. Crystal data, data collection and refinement parameters for 1–4 are given in Table S3. All four compounds represent anion-templated supramolecular architectures constructed via an ACDA approach, in which para-substituted benzoate anions act as directing agents around a conserved complex cation, [Co(Hbig)3]3+. In all four structures, charge-assisted anion recognition is expressed primarily through multiple N–H⋯O (carboxylate) contacts and water-mediated O–H⋯O bridges, while π–π interactions between adjacent benzoate rings further stabilize the packing. Crystallization water molecules are present in each assembly, and in two structures (1 and 4) chloride anions additionally participate as strong hydrogen-bond acceptors, introducing alternative crosslinking motifs without altering the integrity of the cationic module.

In all complexes, the cobalt(iii) central atom exhibits a distorted octahedral geometry defined by six nitrogen donors from three bidentate biguanide ligands, providing a structurally invariant building unit for ACDA. Coordination bond lengths and bond angles are displayed in Tables S4–S7. Representative Co–N distances fall within the expected range 1.894(2)–1.924(2) Å. Biguanide (Hbig) ligands are neutral molecules which are according to H atoms positions and according to the bond lengths in all four complexes in tautomeric form shown in Fig. S15. A complex cation [Co(Hbig)3]3+ embody a robust cationic scaffold that presents a dense array of hydrogen bond donors. This donor-rich surface drives anion templation by positioning benzoate anions through cooperative hydrogen bonding and depending on substituent, by enabling additional aromatic stacking contacts. The structural invariance of the cation confirms that supramolecular diversity originates from the anion identity and its secondary coordination sphere, not from changes in metal coordination geometry. All complexes form layered architecture. Complexes 2 and 3, which lack chlorine, adopt a layered structure consisting of alternating cation/water sheets and benzoate NBA or MBA sheets, respectively. Complex 1 and 4, which contain chloride anions, form a layered architecture composed of benzoate (ABA or MEBA)/water layers alternating with cation/chloride layers. In all four complexes alternating layers are bonded by numerous N–H⋯O hydrogen bonds, donated by N atoms from cations and accepted by carboxylate O atoms. Substituents of benzoate play different role in these architectures. The para-amino substituent of ABA in 1 introduces additional hydrogen-bond donor functionality. The para-nitro group of NBA in 2 enhances anion–π stabilising interactions. The para-methoxy group of MEBA in 4 acts as an additional acceptor (in addition to carboxylates) of the hydrogen bond (donated by the N–H groups from the cations), while para-methyl groups of MBA in 3 are involved neither in significant hydrogen bonding nor π-stabilising interactions.

The structure can be described as a stacking of layers composed of p-methoxybenzoate anions (MEBA) and water molecules, alternating with layers containing [Co(Hbig)3]3+ cations and chloride anions along the b-axis. Each chloride ion is surrounded by the N–H groups of cations and bonded to them via N–H⋯Cl intermolecular hydrogen bonds. N–H groups are donors also of hydrogen bonds to oxygen atoms of carboxylate groups of p-methoxybenzoate anions. Water molecules O1w are located in between the two types of layers. Each O1w molecule is an acceptor of N–H⋯O hydrogen bond given by N–H group of complex cation and a donor of two hydrogen bonds to O atoms from carboxylate group of two MEBA anions as can be seen also in Fig. 6. The geometric parameters of hydrogen bonds are given in Table S11. Crystal packing is stabilised also by π⋯π stacking interactions between aromatic MEBA anions, which is marked by pink colour in Fig. 5 and 6. The distance between the centroids labelled 1⋯4, 2⋯3, 9⋯11 and 10⋯12 is 3.838 Å and between 3⋯5, 4⋯6, 7⋯9 and 8⋯10 is 4.634 Å.

The crystal structure reveals that the molecular packing is governed by a combination of hydrogen bonding and π–π interactions, leading to a well-defined supramolecular architecture, composed of layers of para-substituted benzoate anions and layers containing complex cations and in 1 and 4 also chloride anions. Layers are stacked along a-axis in 1 and 3 and along b-axis in 2 and 4. All compounds contain also solvent water molecules. The intermolecular N–H⋯O and O–H⋯O hydrogen bonds, link substituted benzoate and chloride anions with [Co(Hbig)3]3+ cations and water molecules in 3D network. The H⋯A distances and D–H⋯A angles (Tables S8–S11) fall within typical ranges, indicating moderately strong interactions that contribute significantly to crystal stability. In addition to hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking interactions between aromatic rings are observed.

Distances involving ring centroids and nitro groups are in 2 in the range 3.565(2) and 3.764 (2) Å, while in other three compounds the distances between ring centroids are larger than 3.838(2) Å. On the other hand, the shortest N⋯O contact distance in N–H⋯O hydrogen bonds between cations and carboxylate group of anions is 2.799(4) Å in 2, while there are several hydrogen bonds of that type with shorter N⋯O contact distances in other three compounds. The shortest are 2.737(2), 2.682(2) and 2.687(3) Å in 1, 3 and 4, respectively. It can be concluded that electron donating – amino, methyl and methoxy substituents in (1, 3 and 4, respectively) strengthen hydrogen bonds which are accepted by carboxylate oxygen atoms. It seems that these substituents bring additional electron density to carboxylate oxygen atoms making them better H-bond acceptor. Contrary, electron withdrawing nitro group in 2 enhances anion-π stabilising interactions rather than strengthens hydrogen bonds.

The difference between the para-substituents is also that amino group is a potential donor of N–H⋯O hydrogen bonds, nitro and methoxy groups have N and O atoms, which are potential acceptors of hydrogen bonds, while methyl is not neither donor nor acceptor of classical hydrogen bonds. It turns out that p-aminobenzoate is a donor of weak N–H⋯O hydrogen bonds to water molecules, and oxygen atoms of nitro and methoxy groups are also acceptors of only very weak N–H⋯O hydrogen bonds. The shortest N⋯O contact distances involving para-substituents are 2.975(3), 3.055(4) and 3.075(3) Å in 1, 3 and 4, respectively. These hydrogen bonds are significantly less strong compared to those, accepted by oxygen atoms of negatively charged carboxylate group.

However, it seems that also the difference in the electronic influence of the para-substituent (electron withdrawing or electron donating nature) does not have a decisive impact on the final supramolecular organization, as supramolecular architecture is similar (layered) in all four compounds. This structural similarity is probably due to the similarity of the structural components: All four para-substituted benzoates are sterically quite similar, they have the same charge and all of them have carboxylate group which acts as a very suitable acceptors of N–H⋯O and O–H⋯O hydrogen bonds, which are charge assisted and consequently stronger, compared to hydrogen bonds which involve para-substituents amino, nitro, methyl or methoxy group.

The coordination numbers of all the anions in 1–4 are shown in Fig. 7. For all para-substituted benzoates, the N–H groups of Hbig and the O–H groups of the water molecules participate in ACDA formation. In 3, where the anion coordination number is 5, only the N–H groups of the complex cation contribute to ACDA formation with anion 1 (see Fig. 7). Unlike anions such as NO3− and PO43−, these assemblies do not adopt well-defined geometries.14 In 1, Cl− interacts with both the N–H groups of the cation and the O–H groups of the water molecules, whereas in 4 it interacts only with the N–H groups of Hbig and has a coordination number of 6. Overall, these results underscore the cooperative roles of the benzoate anions, metal center and ligand framework in controlling supramolecular architecture and properties.

Anion binding affinity and Job's plot

To investigate binding affinity (log β) of para-substituted benzoates (ABA, NBA, MBA and MEBA) towards complex cation, [Co(Hbig)3]3+, UV-visible titration experiments were performed as general procedure described in SI (see G1). In all titrations, complex cation and sodium para-substituted benzoates concentrations was 1 × 10−2 M and 1 × 10−4 M respectively. Absorption titration spectra of NaABA and [Co(Hbig)3]3+ reveals noticeable hyperchromic shift (see Fig. S16). The absorption band of NaABA (at 265 nm) merges with the LMCT band (270 nm) of complex cation forming a new single band at 265 nm and two d–d absorption bands had appeared at 353 and 477 nm. In NaNBA and [Co(Hbig)3]3+ titration, two types of shifts were observed, hyperchromic shift (in whole spectra) and blue shift (in LMCT and the anion band). The LMCT band (at 270 nm) of [Co(Hbig)3]3+ and anion band (at 273 nm) merged and produced a new band at 270 nm. Moreover, two d–d transitions appeared at 345 (with 8 nm blue shift) and 477 nm (see Fig. 8). In NaMBA and [Co(Hbig)3]3+ absorption titration, the NaMBA absorption band at 271 nm merges with the complex cation LMCT band at 270 nm, resulting in a new band at 265 nm with a 5 nm blue shift (see Fig. S17). Two additional d–d absorption bands emerge at 353 and 477 nm. In case of NaMEBA and [Co(Hbig)3]3+, LMCT band of the complex cation at 270 nm merges with the NaMEBA absorption band at 247 nm, forming a new band at 245 nm with a significant 25 nm blue shift (see Fig. S18). However other absorption bands show a hyperchromic shift, indicating increased absorption with emergence of d–d transitions absorption bands at 353 and 477 nm. Both hyperchromic and blue shifts in the absorption bands provide evidence for complex formation, indicating changes in the electronic properties of both the complex cation and the benzoate anions.

The binding affinities (log β) of para-benzoates were determined for complex cation and calculated using spectral fitting of absorption titration data in software Hypspec2014.48 The Hyperquad program was used through non-linear least-squares refinement. The experimental data were fitted using multispecies binding model to obtain the best-fit stability (binding) constants (log β values). The software analyses changes in spectral responses during titration and minimise the residual error between experimental and calculated data, providing reliable equilibrium constants and species distribution profiles. The obtained result indicates that all the anions (para-benzoates) demonstrated the binding affinity for complex cation in 1 : 1, 2 : 1 and 3 : 1, but maximum binding affinity of NBA with logβ value of 12.7404 ± 0.0300 in 3 : 1 stoichiometry as shown in Table 1. The maximum binding observed by NBA can be attributed due to the presence of extensive hydrogen bonding as it contains two additional acceptor sites (N and O atoms of nitro group). Both these atoms of the nitro substituent form more hydrogen bonds as compared to other substituents, which is further supported by the SCXRD studies of 2. However, the literature reports the binding affinities (log β) of various organic- and inorganic-based systems toward benzoate anions (ABA, NBA, MBA, and MEBA), with the maximum log β values for a 1 : 1 stoichiometric ratio being 6.5, 4.5, 4.2, and 5.2, respectively.15–30 In contrast, the present study demonstrates significantly higher log β values for anion coordination in an aqueous medium (see Table 1). This enhanced binding behaviour can be attributed to the strong noncovalent interactions between the anions and SM, promoted by the presence of twenty-one hydrogen-bonding sites (larger than the reported systems) and the tripositive charge of the complex cation. Moreover, the anion coordination process proceeds efficiently in the green solvent water, resulting in the formation of stable supramolecular ACDA.

The stoichiometric ratios of complexes 1–4 in aqueous medium were determined using Job's method of continuous variation by plotting corrected absorbance against mole fraction (for procedure, see G2). The Job's plots for NaABA, NaNBA, NaMBA, and NaMEBA showed corrected absorbance maxima at mole fractions of 0.65, 0.72, 0.73, and 0.68, respectively, indicating the formation of 1 : 2 complexes for NaABA and NaMEBA, and 1 : 3 complexes for NaNBA and NaMBA in aqueous medium (Fig. S19). These results are also consistent with the stoichiometries observed in the solid-state SXCRD studies of complexes 1–4.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) and stability studies

DLS studies confirmed the formation of supramolecular ACDAs in aqueous medium at a concentration of 10−4 M. The hydrodynamic diameters of 1–4 were found to be 396, 532, 452, and 450 nm, respectively (see Fig. S20(a)). The relatively large particle sizes observed for all complexes indicate the formation of extended supramolecular ACDAs than discrete molecular species in solution (as the size of a single molecule is typically in the range of 0.1 to 10 nm, depending on the type of molecule).49 This suggests that the complexes undergo significant intermolecular association in aqueous medium, leading to the generation of integrated, higher-order supramolecular architectures stabilized through non-covalent interactions.50–52 Further, zeta potential values (±10 mV), close to neutrality, indicate that the supramolecular ACDAs in 1–4 possess minimal electrostatic repulsion and only a weak net surface charge in solution.53–55 The structural integrity and stability of the assemblies are primarily governed by non-covalent interactions such as hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking and van der Waals forces (see Fig. S20(b)). These directional intermolecular interactions promote the organization of the complexes into extended supramolecular architectures in solution. Additionally, low electrophoretic mobility values (±1 µs cm V−1 s−1) further support the presence of weak overall charge transport in solution (see Fig. S20(c)), indicating that the supramolecular assemblies exhibit limited net charge movement. Such low mobility is consistent with tightly associated ion pairs and strongly interconnected supramolecular frameworks.56,57

The stability of SM and 1–4 in aqueous medium was evaluated over a period of 0–7 days and across a temperature range of 25–55 °C using UV-visible absorption spectroscopy. No appreciable changes in the characteristic absorption maxima (λmax) were observed with changes in either temperature (Fig. S21) or time (Fig. S22), indicating preservation of the electronic environment around the metal centre. These findings confirm that the supramolecular coordination framework of 1–4 remains stable under the studied conditions.

Biological properties

Antibacterial activity

Antibacterial activity investigations for various test compounds, including SM, NaABA, NaNBA, NaMBA and NaMEBA, 1–4 (see Fig. 9) were carried out as general procedure discussed in SI (see G3). The compounds were tested against six different bacterial strains (see Table S20), including Gram-positive (Bacillus subtilis and Staphylococcus epidermidis) and Gram-negative bacteria (Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Salmonella typhi and Escherichia coli). The data given in Table S20 represent that all sodium benzoates are inactive against all pathogens where SM showed significant antibacterial activity against all the pathogens except S. epidermidis. In addition to that all the complexes 1–4 show activity against all bacterial pathogens. The complexes 1, 3, and 4 showed the highest antibacterial activity against S. epidermidis, with zone of inhibition values of 29.60 ± 0.25, 31.30 ± 0.50 and 32.30 ± 0.25 respectively. However, 2 showed maximum activity against P. aeruginosa, with a zone of inhibition of 30.30 ± 0.25 mm.

Antifungal activity

Antifungal activity of SM, NaABA, NaNBA, NaMBA, NaMEBA and 1–4 were also investigated against three different fungal phytopathogens, F. oxysporum, C. herbarum and A. alternata (see Fig. 10 and Table S20) according to general procedure discussed in SI (see G3). No antifungal activity was observed by SM, NaABA, NaNBA, NaMBA, NaMEBA, 3 and 4 against all tested fungal phytopathogens. But 1 and 2 shows promising antifungal activity against F. oxysporum (26.60 ± 0.57) and C. herbarum (25.3 ± 0.33) respectively. The standard nystatin drug was also investigated against all three fungal pathogens and it was concluded that 1 and 2 shows better antifungal activity.

The data given in Table S20 represents that sodium para-substituted benzoates and SM are either inactive or very low antimicrobial activities against all the tested pathogens. As 1–4 form ACDA with the complex cation and anions, they effectively target and disrupt microbial proteins. The ACDA reduces the ionic character of 1–4 and increases lipophilicity (as confirmed in DLS studies), enabling these assemblies to penetrate microbial cell membranes more readily (aligning with the concept of enhanced cell permeability)35 and kill the microbes.

In addition, the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal/fungicidal concentration (MBC/MFC) assays of SM and 1–4 were also assessed against various Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacterial strains as well as fungal pathogens. Chloramphenicol and nystatin were employed as reference standard drugs for bacterial and fungal comparisons, respectively. The results indicated that 2 and 4 exhibited stronger antibacterial activity against the tested bacterial strains. B. subtilis was found to be highly susceptible, particularly to 3 and 4, with the lowest MIC and MBC values of 31.25 and 62.5 µg mL−1, respectively, which were higher than those of the standard drug. Meanwhile, 4 showed the highest activity against S. epidermidis, with MIC and MBC values of 15.6 and 31.25 µg mL−1, respectively, which was better than chloramphenicol under the tested conditions. On the other hand, moderate antibacterial activity was observed against K. pneumoniae, S. typhi, P. aeruginosa, and E. coli, as shown in Table S21. In the case of fungal pathogens, comparable MFC against C. herbarum was observed for 2, while 1 showed better activity against F. oxysporum with respect to standard drug.

Anticancer activity

The cytotoxic potential of the SM, NaABA, NaNBA, NaMBA, NaMEBA and 1–4 were determined using MTT assay in MCF-7, HeLa, HCT-15 cancer cell lines. The general procedure discussed in SI (see G4). The results indicated that the tested compounds exhibited higher sensitivity towards MCF-7 cells. Among all the tested compounds, 2 showed highest dose dependent growth inhibition potential with lowest GI50 concentration of 83.80 µM on MCF-7 cells. However, SM, 1, 3 and 4 showed cytotoxicity at 147, 281.50, >500 and 132.10 µM respectively. Furthermore, it was observed that the tested compounds showed minimal to negligible cytotoxic effects on HeLa and HCT-15 cell lines (see Table 2). The cytotoxic effects of 2 were also assessed on normal cell line (L-929) yielded no significant growth inhibitory effects up to the highest tested concentration i.e. 800 µM, indicative of very low toxicity towards normal cells. The selectivity index (SI) was determined to assess the selective antiproliferative effect of 2 on MCF-7 cells compared with normal cells (L-929). The calculated SI values for 2 were greater than 9.54, signifying that 2 exhibits strong selective cytotoxicity toward breast cancer (MCF-7) cells and literature suggests SI ≥ 10 is excellent for anticancer properties.58,59 Additionally, 5-florouracil (5-FU) used as reference drug showed cytotoxic effects at GI50 value of 219 µM against MCF-7 cell line. This implies that SM, 2 and 4 exhibits significant anticancer potential in comparison to reference compound 5-FU. Thamilarasan and J. F. Machado revealed that Co(iii) complexes exhibited anticancer activity only at comparatively high concentrations (∼100 µM). Morphological evidence of cellular apoptosis induction by 2 in MCF-7 cells

The highest cytotoxic effect was exhibited by the 2 against MCF-7 cell line with GI30: 24.02 µM, GI50: 83.80 µM and GI70: 292.27 µM values and negligible cytotoxicity towards normal cell line (L-929). This shows that it possesses selective antiproliferative effects than other tested compounds. A series of morphological investigations evidenced the cellular changes associated with its cytotoxicity induction. To uncover this, cell morphological assessments through phase contrast microscopy revealed the altered morphology of MCF-7 cells upon treatment of 2 characterized by cell shrinkage, membrane blebbing, loss of adherence and decrease in cell density in contrast to intact morphology of untreated (control) cells (Fig. 11(i)). In support of this, nuclear morphological alterations were detected by blue coloured fluorescent DAPI staining. On treatment with 2, the MCF-7 cells showed nuclear alterations characterized by nuclear condensation, nuclear fragmentation and formation of apoptotic bodies in comparison to untreated cells (Fig. 11(ii)). These observations were consistent with the apoptosis related cell death.65 Furthermore, the changes in mitochondrial membrane potential examined by rhodamine-123 staining demonstrated intact mitochondrial membrane potential characterized by evenly distributed fluorescence in untreated cells. The cells treated with 2 exhibited a marked decrease in fluorescence intensity indicative of loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) (Fig. 11(iii)). This MMP collapsing marked as a primary event in intrinsic apoptotic pathway.66 In addition to this, dual staining with acridine orange and ethidium bromide was also performed. Based on differential dye uptake, this distinguishes different cellular apoptosis stages i.e. the viable (live) cells, early apoptotic, late apoptotic and necrotic cells. The untreated cells show uniform green fluorescence indicating viable cells. The cells treated with 2 exhibits increase in apoptotic cell population marked by yellow fluorescent early apoptotic cells, orange fluorescent late apoptotic cells and red fluorescent cells necrotic cells (Fig. 11(iv)). These findings align with previous studies reporting the induction of apoptotic cell death by Ru(ii)–arene complexes in breast cancer cells.67 This indicates that treatment with 2 induces signs of apoptotic cell death in breast cancer (MCF-7) cells suggestive of its significant anticancer potential.

The DLS studies indicate that the supramolecular ACDAs remain intact in solution (390–540 nm), leading to a reduction in ionic character and an increase in lipophilicity, as evidenced by their low electrophoretic mobility and low zeta potential values in complexes 1–4. These intact supramolecular ACDAs may therefore penetrate cancer cell membranes more efficiently, consistent with the concept of enhanced cell permeability,35 thereby contributing to their cytotoxic activity.

Conclusions

In summary, a robust anion-templated supramolecular strategy based on ACDA has been developed using [Co(Hbig)3]3+, as a versatile cationic building unit. The para-substituted benzoate anions (ABA, NBA, MBA and MEBA) act as structure-directing agents, orchestrating hierarchical self-assembly through synergistic hydrogen bonding and π–π stacking interactions. Single-crystal X-ray diffraction analysis reveals that the benzoate anions engage in extensive supramolecular networking, exhibiting up to seven hydrogen bonding interactions with the complex cation and lattice water molecules, thereby stabilizing the 3D architectures. Comprehensive spectroscopic (UV-visible, FTIR and NMR), DLS and thermal (TGA) investigations corroborate the formation and integrity of the assemblies. Quantitative anion-binding studies demonstrate strong and selective coordination of para-substituted benzoates (maximum for NBA), underscoring the efficiency of the ACDA approach in directing supramolecular organization through predictable non-covalent interactions. Importantly, the functional relevance of these assemblies extends beyond structural design. The ACDA derived systems exhibit enhanced anticancer and antimicrobial activities compared to standard reference drugs, indicating that anion-directed organization can significantly modulate biological performance. Fluorescence microscopy studies (DAPI, rhodamine-123 and AO/EB staining) confirm apoptosis induction in MCF-7 cells, providing mechanistic insight into their cytotoxic action. These findings highlight the effectiveness of anion-directed coordination strategies for constructing functional metal-based supramolecular systems with combined structural and biological relevance.
