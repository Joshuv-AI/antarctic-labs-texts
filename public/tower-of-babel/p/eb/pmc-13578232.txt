Organizational dehumanization and career adaptability among clinical nurses: the mediating role of self-compassion and the moderating role of psychological safety
Authors: Mohammad Mahmoodzadeh, Hadi Mahmoudzadeh, Behrouz Dadkhah, Mohammad Kheradmandpour, Mehdi Mahmoudzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClinical nurses play a central role in healthcare delivery, yet the psychological demands of clinical settings may threaten their professional identity and adaptability. Organizational dehumanization can diminish nurses’ sense of value and motivation, while career adaptability enables them to manage occupational challenges effectively. In this context, self-compassion may serve as a protective psychological resource, and psychological safety may protect nurses’ self-compassion in the face of dehumanizing organizational experiences. This study aimed to investigate the association between organizational dehumanization and career adaptability among clinical nurses and to examine whether self-compassion mediates this association and psychological safety moderates the association between organizational dehumanization and self-compassion. This cross‑sectional study was conducted from June 2025 to March 2026. A total of 330 clinical nurses were recruited from five teaching hospitals in Ardabil, Iran, using stratified random sampling. Data were collected through standardized self‑report questionnaires, including the Organizational Dehumanization Scale, the Career Adapt‑Abilities Questionnaire, the Psychological Safety Scale, and the Self‑Compassion Scale. Data were analyzed using SPSS version. 26.0 and Hayes’ PROCESS v4.2, applying moderated mediation analysis with 5,000 bias‑corrected bootstrap resamples to examine direct and indirect effects. Organizational dehumanization was negatively associated with self-compassion (β = −0.248, p < .001) and career adaptability (total effect = −0.357, p < .001). Self-compassion partially mediated this relationship (indirect effect = −0.114, 95% CI [−0.167, −0.069]). Psychological safety moderated the indirect effect: the conditional indirect effect was significant at low (effect = −0.148, 95% CI [−0.214, −0.089]) and moderate levels of psychological safety, but was not significant at high levels, suggesting that psychological safety may buffer the negative influence of organizational dehumanization on career adaptability through self-compassion. Organizational dehumanization was negatively associated with nurses’ self-compassion and career adaptability. Self-compassion partially mediated this relationship, while psychological safety buffered the negative association between organizational dehumanization and self-compassion, thereby weakening the indirect pathway to career adaptability. Introduction

Respect for human dignity is considered a fundamental ethical principle within professional organizations and plays a central role in maintaining employees’ psychological well-being, occupational engagement, and sense of organizational justice. In healthcare systems, where interpersonal interactions and emotional labor are deeply embedded in daily practice, preserving employees’ sense of value and humanity becomes particularly important. However, employees may sometimes perceive that organizational priorities focus predominantly on productivity, efficiency, and performance outcomes while overlooking their emotional needs, individuality, and personal worth. Such experiences are increasingly conceptualized in organizational psychology as organizational dehumanization (OD), a phenomenon in which employees feel treated more as functional instruments than as human beings with emotions, autonomy, and intrinsic value1–4.

Recent evidence suggests that OD can negatively influence both psychological functioning and occupational outcomes. Employees who perceive OD environments often report higher levels of emotional exhaustion, detachment, psychological distress, reduced motivation, and weakened organizational attachment1,4. In clinical settings, nurses may be particularly vulnerable to these experiences due to persistent exposure to heavy workloads, staff shortages, emotionally demanding situations, limited organizational support, and rigid administrative structures. Over time, these conditions may gradually erode nurses’ psychological resources and be associated with reduced ability to adapt effectively to occupational challenges and evolving workplace demands.

One construct that may be substantially affected by such organizational experiences is career adaptability. Career adaptability reflects an individual’s capacity to manage professional transitions, respond effectively to workplace challenges, and maintain flexibility in changing occupational environments5,6. For nurses, adaptability is especially important because healthcare systems are characterized by continuous clinical complexity, technological change, emotional pressure, and unpredictable working conditions. Nurses with stronger adaptive capacities are generally better able to sustain professional competence, resilience, and long-term occupational functioning despite workplace stressors.

Although previous studies have extensively linked OD to outcomes such as burnout, depression, disengagement, and turnover intention, considerably less attention has been given to its potential relationship with career adaptability, particularly among clinical nurses. This gap in the literature is important because persistent experiences of dehumanization may be associated with lower nurses’ confidence, resilience, and psychological flexibility, all of which are essential for adaptive career functioning. Therefore, examining this relationship may deepen understanding of how negative organizational experiences influence nurses’ professional sustainability and occupational adjustment.

Self-compassion (SC) may help explain the psychological mechanism underlying this association. SC involves responding to personal difficulties, stress, or failure with kindness, emotional balance, and understanding rather than excessive self-criticism or emotional suppression7. Drawing on Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, OD may deplete nurses’ psychological resources by signaling that their emotional needs and personal worth are disregarded by the organization. This resource depletion may undermine nurses’ capacity to respond to themselves with compassion and understanding, thereby reducing their emotional regulation and resilience. Since career adaptability depends on sustained psychological resources such as confidence, curiosity, and coping flexibility, a reduction in SC may directly diminish nurses’ adaptive career capacities. Previous research supports this pathway, showing that SC is associated with greater emotional regulation, resilience, and occupational well-being8,9, while dehumanizing organizational experiences are linked to psychological resource depletion and reduced adaptive functioning.

In addition to individual psychological resources, organizational conditions may also shape how employees respond to adverse workplace experiences. Psychological safety (PS) refers to employees’ perception that the workplace is emotionally safe for expressing ideas, asking questions, and acknowledging mistakes without fear of embarrassment or punishment10. We propose that PS may moderate the relationship between OD and SC. In environments characterized by high psychological safety, employees may be less likely to internalize dehumanizing organizational signals as threats to their self-worth, because the interpersonal climate communicates acceptance, respect, and support. Conversely, when psychological safety is low, the harmful effects of OD on self-compassion may be amplified, as employees lack the protective interpersonal buffer that would otherwise sustain their psychological resources. Research consistently demonstrates that psychologically safe environments promote employee well-being, engagement, and resilience, whereas low psychological safety is associated with burnout and emotional strain11–13.

The present study aimed to investigate the relationship between OD and career adaptability among clinical nurses, and to examine the mediating role of SC and the moderating role of PS within this relationship. Theoretically, the study extends the emerging literature on OD by proposing and testing an integrated moderated mediation framework that contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of the psychological and organizational mechanisms underlying adaptive career functioning. Practically, the findings may assist healthcare managers and policymakers in designing interventions that promote self-compassion, strengthen psychological safety, and reduce dehumanizing organizational practices, thereby supporting nurses’ well-being, adaptability, and long-term professional sustainability.

Literature review

Theoretical framework

The present study draws on two complementary theoretical frameworks to explain the proposed relationships among organizational dehumanization, self-compassion, psychological safety, and career adaptability.

Conservation of Resources (COR) theory proposes that individuals strive to obtain, maintain, and protect valued psychological, social, and organizational resources14. Stressful workplace experiences that threaten or deplete these resources may impair employees’ well-being, adaptive functioning, and occupational outcomes. Within this framework, organizational dehumanization can be conceptualized as a resource-depleting experience that undermines nurses’ psychological resources, including self-compassion and career-related adaptive capacities. In contrast, psychological safety may function as a contextual resource that helps preserve individuals’ psychological resources in adverse work environments.

The Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) model complements COR theory by explaining how job demands and job resources jointly shape employees’ well-being and functioning14. Within this framework, organizational dehumanization can be viewed as a job demand that depletes psychological resources, whereas psychological safety represents a job resource that may buffer the negative effects of such demands. Together, these frameworks provide a comprehensive theoretical basis for explaining the proposed relationships and the moderated mediation model tested in this study.

Organizational dehumanization in healthcare settings

Organizational dehumanization (OD) refers to employees’ perceptions that they are treated as impersonal objects, functional tools, or replaceable resources rather than autonomous individuals with emotions and intrinsic human value3. According to Haslam’s framework, mechanistic dehumanization occurs when employees are valued primarily for productivity rather than personal well-being or individuality4,15.

In healthcare settings, nurses may be particularly vulnerable to OD due to heavy workloads, emotional labor, staffing shortages, and limited organizational support. Such experiences have been associated with emotional exhaustion, psychological distress, and reduced job satisfaction16–18. From a COR perspective, OD may deplete nurses’ valued psychological resources over time, weakening professional identity and adaptive functioning.

Career adaptability among clinical nurses

Career adaptability refers to individuals’ psychosocial capacity to cope with vocational tasks, transitions, and occupational adversities19. It includes concern, control, curiosity, and confidence6.

Higher career adaptability has been associated with greater job satisfaction, resilience, and work engagement among healthcare professionals20–22. However, consistent with JD-R model predictions, adverse organizational conditions may weaken adaptive career resources by reducing employees’ psychological well-being and motivation23–25. Accordingly, organizational dehumanization may be negatively associated with nurses’ career adaptability.

H1: Organizational dehumanization is negatively associated with career adaptability among clinical nurses.

The mediating role of self-compassion

Self-compassion (SC) involves self-kindness, common humanity, and mindfulness in times of difficulty7,26. In healthcare settings, SC has been associated with lower burnout and greater resilience27,28.

Drawing on COR theory, organizational dehumanization may deplete nurses’ psychological resources by signaling that their emotional needs and personal worth are disregarded by the organization. This resource depletion may undermine self-compassion, increasing vulnerability to self-criticism and emotional exhaustion. Since career adaptability depends on sustained psychological resources, reduced self-compassion may impair adaptive functioning.

H2: Organizational dehumanization is negatively associated with self-compassion among clinical nurses.

H3: Self-compassion is positively associated with career adaptability among clinical nurses.

H4: Self-compassion mediates the relationship between organizational dehumanization and career adaptability among clinical nurses.

The moderating role of psychological safety

Psychological safety (PS) refers to employees’ shared belief that the work environment is safe for interpersonal risk-taking, including expressing concerns and communicating openly without fear of negative consequences10. Psychologically safe environments have been associated with greater learning, collaboration, and well-being10,29.

Within the JD-R framework, PS may function as a key job resource that buffers the resource-depleting effects of OD. In psychologically safe environments, nurses may be less likely to internalize dehumanizing organizational signals as threats to their self-worth. Conversely, when PS is low, the harmful effects of OD on SC may be amplified. Consistent with COR theory, PS may therefore help preserve psychological resources under adverse conditions.

Empirical evidence supports this assumption in nursing contexts30. Recent nursing studies further support the relevance of organizational and psychological factors in nurses’ work-related outcomes. Abou Zeid et al. reported that organizational dehumanization was associated with work stress and work engagement among staff nurses, with work stress partially mediating the relationship between organizational dehumanization and work engagement31. In addition, El-Ashry et al. found that psychological safety played an important mediating role in the relationship between inclusive leadership and nurses’ implicit absenteeism, highlighting the importance of psychologically safe work environments in nursing contexts32. These findings provide further support for examining organizational dehumanization, psychological safety, and adaptive professional outcomes among clinical nurses.

H5: Psychological safety buffers the negative relationship between organizational dehumanization and self-compassion, such that the association is weaker when psychological safety is high.

Together, these hypotheses support a moderated mediation model in which self-compassion mediates the relationship between organizational dehumanization and career adaptability. In contrast, psychological safety moderates the first stage of this indirect effect (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Participants and setting

Data were collected from clinical nurses working in five teaching hospitals affiliated with Ardabil University of Medical Sciences in Ardabil, northwest Iran (Imam Khomeini, Fatemi, Alavi, Imam Reza, and Bouali hospitals) between June 2025 and March 2026. The study was designed and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies33.

Nurses with at least six months of clinical experience in direct patient-care roles were considered eligible for inclusion. Exclusion criteria were: (1) nurses in purely administrative positions without direct patient-care responsibilities; (2) nurses on any form of leave (sick leave, maternity leave, or annual leave) during the data collection period; (3) those unwilling or unable to complete the questionnaires; and (4) questionnaires with substantial missing or incomplete responses.

The target population consisted of 1,570 eligible clinical nurses employed across the five teaching hospitals affiliated with Ardabil University of Medical Sciences. The sample size was estimated using the finite population correction formula:

Using a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), a margin of error of 0.05, maximum variability (p = 0.5), and a finite population size of 1,570, the initial required sample size was calculated as 308.79, which was rounded to 309 participants. To compensate for an anticipated non-response rate of approximately 10%, the sample size was increased to 339 participants.

Proportional stratified random sampling was used to ensure representation proportional to each hospital’s size. Specifically, 122 nurses were selected from Imam Khomeini Hospital, 72 from Imam Reza Hospital, 67 from Fatemi Hospital, 58 from Bouali Hospital, and 20 from Alavi Hospital. Within each stratum, eligible nurses were randomly selected from official staff lists provided by each hospital’s nursing administration, using a random number sequence generated in SPSS. A total of 339 questionnaires were distributed. Nine questionnaires were excluded due to incomplete or invalid responses, leaving a final sample of 330 nurses. An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power version 3.1 (Linear Multiple Regression: Fixed Model, R2 deviation from zero). Assuming a medium effect size (f2 = 0.15), α = 0.05, and power (1−β) = 0.80, the analysis indicated that the required sample size was substantially smaller than the achieved sample size. Therefore, the final sample of 330 participants was considered adequate for the regression-based analyses conducted in this study, including the PROCESS moderated mediation model.

Data collection and recruitment

Data were collected between June 2025 and March 2026 across five teaching hospitals affiliated with Ardabil University of Medical Sciences. The first and second investigators visited each hospital and individually approached eligible nurses in their respective wards. Prior to distributing the questionnaires, the study’s aims, the voluntary nature of participation, anonymity of responses, and confidentiality procedures were explained verbally to each potential participant. Written informed consent was obtained from all nurses who agreed to take part.

Paper-based questionnaire packets were distributed to eligible nurses and completed in the nurses’ restrooms to ensure a quiet, private setting away from the clinical environment and supervisory presence. Participants were given two to three days to complete the questionnaires at their convenience, either during breaks or outside working hours. Completed questionnaires were returned directly to the principal investigator. Although data collection was coordinated with the educational supervisor and head nurse at each hospital to facilitate access to eligible staff, these individuals were not involved in the distribution or collection of questionnaires. They had no access to participants’ responses at any stage.

All questionnaires were anonymous; no identifying information was recorded on any form. Participants were explicitly informed that their responses would remain strictly confidential and would not be accessible to their supervisors or institutional management, and that withdrawal at any stage would carry no professional consequences. These measures were considered particularly important given the sensitive nature of the constructs under investigation, namely, organizational dehumanization and psychological safety, where concerns about workplace repercussions could plausibly influence candor in responses and introduce social desirability bias.

Measures

Demographic characteristics form

A socio-demographic form was used to assess participants’ characteristics, including age, work experience, gender, marital status, occupational position, employment status, hospital ward, and Monthly income. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± SD, while categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentage.

Organizational Dehumanization Scale (ODS)

Organizational dehumanization was measured using the Organizational Dehumanization Scale (ODS) developed by Caesens et al3.. The scale consists of 11 items assessing employees’ perceptions of being treated as objects or instruments within the organization. Responses are rated on a 7‑point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7), with higher scores indicating greater perceived organizational dehumanization3..

The Persian version of the scale was prepared using the forward–backward translation procedure in accordance with Beaton et al.’s guidelines34. Content validity was evaluated using the Lawshe method, yielding acceptable values for the Content Validity Ratio (CVR = 0.80) and the Content Validity Index (CVI = 0.91). In the original study, the scale demonstrated satisfactory reliability, and in the present study, internal consistency was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.89).

Career Adapt-Abilities Scale (CAAS)

Career adaptability was assessed using the Career Adapt‑Abilities Scale (CAAS), employing a version validated for use among clinical nurses by Liang et al.25. The instrument consists of 24 items across four dimensions: career concern, career control, career curiosity, and career confidence. Items are rated on a 5‑point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5), with higher scores indicating greater career adaptability. In the nurse validation study, the scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency, and confirmatory factor analysis supported an acceptable model fit25..

The Persian version of the scale was prepared using the forward–backward translation method. Content validity was assessed using the Lawshe method, yielding satisfactory values for the Content Validity Ratio (CVR = 0.84) and Content Validity Index (CVI = 0.94). In the present study, the internal consistency of the scale was satisfactory (Cronbach’s α =0.93).

Self-Compassion Scale (SCS)

Self‑compassion was measured using the Self‑Compassion Scale (SCS) developed by Neff (2003). The scale comprises 26 items across six dimensions: self‑kindness, self‑judgment, common humanity, isolation, mindfulness, and over‑identification. Items are rated on a 5‑point Likert scale ranging from rarely (1) to almost always (5). Negative subscales (self‑judgment, isolation, and over‑identification) were reverse‑scored prior to computing the overall self‑compassion score, with higher scores indicating greater self‑compassion. In the original validation study, the scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.92) and test‑retest reliability35.

The Persian version was prepared using the forward–backward translation method. Content validity was confirmed using the Lawshe method, yielding acceptable values for the Content Validity Ratio (CVR = 0.78) and the Content Validity Index (CVI = 0.91). In the present study, internal consistency was satisfactory (Cronbach’s α = 0.91).

Psychological Safety Scale (PSS)

Psychological safety was measured using the Psychological Safety in High-Fidelity Simulation Scale (PSHFSS), adapted for clinical nurses by Li et al. (2026),36. Although originally developed in a simulation context, the core constructs assessed by this scale, namely, the perception of safety to express thoughts and concerns without fear of retribution, are conceptually aligned with psychological safety as experienced in real clinical environments. The adaptation by Li et al. explicitly modified item wording to reflect everyday clinical practice rather than simulation scenarios, supporting its applicability to general nursing settings. The psychometric properties of the CV-PSHFSS were rigorously evaluated by Li et al. (2026) in a sample of 522 Chinese nursing students using both Classical Test Theory and Item Response Theory. Confirmatory factor analysis supported the original four-dimensional structure, yielding acceptable fit indices (CMIN/DF = 2.5, RMSEA = 0.054, GFI = 0.963, CFI = 0.982, IFI = 0.982). Internal consistency was excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.927, McDonald’s ω = 0.925), and test-retest reliability was strong (ICC = 0.925), supporting the scale’s suitability for use in clinical nursing populations.

The scale consists of 19 items assessing nurses’ perceptions of feeling safe to express their thoughts, concerns, and opinions without fear of retribution or embarrassment during clinical practice. Responses are rated on a 5-point Likert scale from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5), with higher scores indicating greater perceived psychological safety.

Content validity was evaluated using the Lawshe method, yielding acceptable values for the Content Validity Ratio (CVR = 0.80) and the Content Validity Index (CVI = 0.91). In the original study, the scale demonstrated satisfactory reliability, and in the present study, internal consistency was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.89).

Ethical consideration

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Ardabil University of Medical Sciences (Ethics Code: IR.ARUMS.REC.1404.127). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Full details of ethical procedures are provided in the Ethics Statement.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0) and Hayes’ PROCESS (version 4.2). Descriptive statistics (means and standard deviations) were computed for all variables. The normality of the data was assessed using skewness, kurtosis, and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, which indicated no serious violations of normality assumptions. The skewness and kurtosis values for all study variables are presented in the Supplementary Materials. Group differences across demographic variables were examined using independent-samples t-tests and one-way ANOVA, as appropriate. To assess common method bias, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted; however, it was used only as a preliminary diagnostic approach. Harman’s single-factor test indicated that a single factor explained 38.2% of the variance, suggesting no serious common method bias. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess associations among the study variables. Mediation and moderation analyses were conducted using PROCESS (Models 4 and 7), with indirect effects tested via bootstrapping (5,000 resamples) and 95% bias-corrected confidence intervals. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

Summary of main findings

The present study examined the relationships between organizational dehumanization, self-compassion, psychological safety, and career adaptability among clinical nurses. The findings indicated that higher perceived organizational dehumanization was associated with lower self-compassion and lower career adaptability. Self-compassion partially mediated the association between organizational dehumanization and career adaptability, suggesting that personal psychological resources may play a key role in explaining the link between organizational factors and adaptive work outcomes. In addition, psychological safety moderated the association between dehumanization and self-compassion, indicating that higher perceived psychological safety may buffer the negative relationship between organizational dehumanization and self-compassion. Overall, the results are consistent with the proposed conceptual model and highlight the potential importance of self-compassion and psychological safety in understanding nurses’ career adaptability in challenging clinical environments.

Comparison with previous studies

The negative association between organizational dehumanization and career adaptability observed in the present study is consistent with prior research that has directly examined the effects of adverse organizational behaviors on psychological resources and adaptive capacities among healthcare professionals11,37. These studies suggest that dehumanizing workplace experiences are associated with reduced psychological functioning and diminished work-related adaptability.

Indirect evidence from related research domains, such as workplace bullying, occupational stress, and employee burnout, further supports these findings by demonstrating that unfavorable organizational conditions are associated with reduced psychological resources and impaired adaptive functioning. Although these studies do not specifically examine organizational dehumanization, they provide converging evidence of the broader impact of negative workplace environments on employee adjustment.

The magnitude of the association observed in the present study within the Iranian nursing context may reflect context-specific amplifying factors that extend beyond those reported in Western or East Asian samples. Iranian nurses operate within a healthcare system characterized by chronic understaffing, high patient-to-nurse ratios, and limited material resources, partly due to long-standing structural and economic constraints. From a Conservation of Resources (COR) perspective, such resource-depleted environments may intensify the psychological impact of interpersonal dehumanization, as the loss of respect, recognition, and dignity may represent a more salient threat to already strained resource reservoirs38,39.

The partial mediation effect of self-compassion is consistent with prior research highlighting the role of internal psychological resources in explaining the relationship between organizational stressors and adaptive outcomes38,39. However, limited research has directly examined self-compassion as a mediating mechanism between organizational dehumanization and career adaptability, particularly in nursing populations. This highlights an important gap in the literature and underscores the novelty of the present study.

From a cultural perspective, the Iranian context adds layer of interpretation. In societies characterized by relatively high power distance and collectivistic values, self-compassion may be less culturally reinforced because norms emphasize self-sacrifice, duty, and deference to authority. As a result, variations in self-compassion may play a more critical role in explaining differences in adaptive functioning under stressful organizational conditions.

The moderating role of psychological safety is also consistent with prior research indicating that supportive organizational climates are associated with better psychological outcomes among healthcare workers11,40. In hierarchical healthcare systems, psychological safety may be particularly important, as it enables open communication, help-seeking, and emotional expression, thereby mitigating the negative effects of dehumanizing experiences.

Studies conducted in organizational contexts with stronger institutional support, flatter hierarchies, or more robust employee protection mechanisms have reported weaker or non-significant associations among similar variables26,38. These differences may be attributed to variations in organizational culture, access to resources, and coping mechanisms, further highlighting the context-dependent nature of these relationships.

The present findings are consistent with recent nursing research showing that organizational dehumanization is relevant to nurses’ occupational well-being and work-related functioning. For example, Abou Zeid et al. found that organizational dehumanization was related to nurses’ work stress and work engagement, suggesting that dehumanizing organizational experiences may be linked to less favorable professional outcomes among nurses31. The current study extends this line of evidence by focusing on career adaptability and identifying self-compassion as a potential psychological mechanism in the association between organizational dehumanization and adaptive career functioning.

Importantly, while previous research has examined organizational dehumanization, self-compassion, or career adaptability separately, few studies have integrated these constructs into a single theoretical model. The present study addresses this gap by testing a combined mediation–moderation framework among clinical nurses, thereby contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of how organizational and psychological factors jointly influence career adaptability39,41.

Overall, the convergence and divergence of findings across studies underscore the multifactorial and context-dependent nature of career adaptability in clinical nursing. These results highlight the importance of considering both individual psychological resources and organizational conditions when designing interventions to improve nurses’ adaptability and well-being in demanding clinical environments.

Potential mechanisms and theoretical explanation

The observed relationships among organizational dehumanization, self-compassion, psychological safety, and career adaptability can be interpreted through two complementary theoretical frameworks: Conservation of Resources (COR) theory42 and Career Construction Theory (CCT)41, supplemented by the Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) model40.

Advancing conservation of resources theory

COR theory posits that individuals are motivated to acquire, retain, and protect resources, and that resource loss is disproportionately more impactful than equivalent resource gain42. The present study advances COR theory in two important ways. First, it empirically demonstrates that organizational dehumanization functions as a primary resource loss stressor in clinical nursing: by stripping nurses of interpersonal recognition, autonomy, and a sense of valued identity, dehumanization initiates a loss spiral that depletes self-compassion, a critical personal resource, and subsequently undermines career adaptability.

This loss-caravan pathway (dehumanization → self-compassion → career adaptability) extends COR theory by identifying self-compassion as a specific internal resource that mediates the downstream effects of organizational-level resource loss on career-relevant outcomes38,39.

Second, and more importantly, the study advances COR theory by demonstrating that psychological safety operates as a contextual resource that interrupts this loss spiral at its origin. COR theory predicts that individuals with access to resource reservoirs are better protected against loss spirals; the present findings specify that psychologically safe environments constitute precisely such a reservoir in hierarchical healthcare contexts, buffering the erosion of self-compassion under dehumanizing conditions11,40. This moderated mediation model thus provides a more granular, context-sensitive test of COR theory than prior studies that have examined resource loss and gain in isolation.

Advancing career construction theory

Career Construction Theory conceptualizes career adaptability as a psychosocial resource comprising four dimensions: concern, control, curiosity, and confidence that individuals deploy to navigate vocational challenges and transitions41. The present study advances CCT in a previously underexplored direction by identifying organizational and psychological antecedents that constrain or facilitate the deployment of these adaptability resources in clinical nursing.

Specifically, CCT has primarily focused on how individuals actively construct their careers through narrative and adaptive responses, with comparatively less attention to how dehumanizing organizational environments structurally limit access to the internal states necessary for such construction. The present findings suggest that organizational dehumanization disrupts the self-referential psychological processes of self-kindness, common humanity, and mindful awareness that underpin self-compassion, thereby undermining the emotional foundation from which career concern, control, curiosity, and confidence emerge38,39. In this sense, the study extends CCT by demonstrating that career adaptability is not solely a function of individual agency but is also contingently shaped by organizational resource environments and their psychological consequences.

Furthermore, by showing that psychological safety moderates the dehumanization–self-compassion pathway, the study highlights the relational and contextual embeddedness of career construction processes, an aspect that CCT acknowledges theoretically, but that has received limited empirical attention in healthcare settings40,41.

Together, these theoretical extensions position the present study as a contribution to both resource-based and constructivist frameworks, offering an integrated account of how macro-level organizational conditions, meso-level contextual resources, and micro-level personal resources interact to shape nurses’ career adaptability in demanding clinical environments37,39,42.

Implications for nursing management, education, and practice

The findings of the present study may have several important implications for nursing management, continuing education, and clinical practice. Given that the study was conducted among clinical nurses, these implications should be interpreted as potential applications for supporting nurses in professional healthcare settings rather than as direct evidence of the effectiveness of specific interventions.

First, the observed mediating role of self-compassion suggests that individual-focused professional development programs may benefit from incorporating strategies to strengthen nurses’ internal psychological resources. Programs that include mindfulness-based activities, self-reflection exercises, and resilience-building strategies may help nurses develop greater self-compassion, which could support their ability to manage stress and maintain adaptability in demanding clinical environments38,39.

Second, the moderating role of psychological safety highlights the potential importance of supportive and respectful clinical environments. Continuing education and workplace-based training programs may emphasize team communication, peer support, constructive feedback, and conflict-resolution skills to foster psychological safety among nurses. In addition, clinical leaders and nurse managers may consider structured mentorship programs, simulation-based team training, and collaborative problem-solving activities to help nurses feel safe expressing concerns, learning from mistakes, and engaging in adaptive behaviors without fear of judgment or reprisal10,11,40.

Third, awareness of organizational dehumanization may be integrated into nursing management, education, and practice initiatives to help nurses recognize and respond to dehumanizing experiences in healthcare settings. Such initiatives may help nurses understand how organizational culture, leadership behaviors, workload pressures, and interpersonal communication affect professional well-being and career adaptability. At the organizational level, healthcare institutions may consider leadership training, workload management strategies, anti-dehumanization policies, respectful communication systems, and safe mechanisms for reporting concerns without fear of retaliation. These measures could help reduce dehumanizing practices and promote more supportive and ethically responsive work environments37,41.

Overall, the present findings suggest that both individual-level and organizational-level strategies may be relevant for promoting self-compassion, psychological safety, and career adaptability among clinical nurses. Rather than focusing solely on educational interventions, healthcare organizations should consider integrated approaches that combine professional development, supportive leadership, respectful workplace cultures, and psychologically safe clinical environments. Such efforts may contribute to nurses’ professional satisfaction, adaptive capacity, and long-term retention in challenging clinical contexts.

Cultural and contextual interpretation of the findings

The findings may also be interpreted within the broader cultural context of Iran. Drawing on Hofstede’s cultural dimensions framework, Iranian society is generally characterized by relatively high power distance and collectivistic tendencies. In such cultural contexts, hierarchical relationships and organizational authority may play a particularly important role in shaping employees’ workplace experiences and psychological responses. Consequently, experiences of organizational dehumanization may be perceived more intensely because employees often place considerable value on interpersonal respect, a sense of belonging, and recognition within organizational settings. Likewise, psychologically safe work environments may become especially important for preserving self-compassion and adaptive functioning, as they provide opportunities for supportive communication, mutual respect, and emotional security. Therefore, cultural characteristics may partially explain the observed relationships among organizational dehumanization, self-compassion, psychological safety, and career adaptability in the present study.

In addition to cultural considerations, the broader contextual environment during the data collection period may also be relevant for interpreting the findings. Data were collected between June 2025 and March 2026, a period during which Iranian healthcare organizations continued to operate under economic pressures, resource constraints, workforce challenges, and heightened geopolitical and security-related uncertainties in the region. Such conditions may have increased occupational stress and heightened nurses’ sensitivity to organizational experiences, thereby amplifying the importance of psychological resources and supportive workplace environments. Under these circumstances, perceptions of organizational dehumanization may have exerted a stronger influence on nurses’ self-compassion and career adaptability. At the same time, psychological safety may have become particularly valuable as a protective organizational resource. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted within this broader organizational and societal context.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the study relied exclusively on self-reported questionnaires, which may have increased the risk of social desirability bias and response distortion. This limitation may be particularly salient in collectivist cultural contexts such as Iran, where norms emphasizing social harmony, respect for authority, and conformity to organizational expectations may discourage full disclosure of negative workplace experiences. As a result, participants may have underreported sensitive perceptions such as organizational dehumanization or low psychological safety due to concerns about professional image, perceived disloyalty, or hierarchical workplace dynamics.

Although Harman’s single-factor test was conducted to assess common method bias, it has important limitations and may not fully detect common method variance. Since all variables were collected via self-report questionnaires at a single time point, common method bias cannot be ruled out. While procedural measures such as ensuring anonymity and confidentiality were implemented to reduce this bias, future research is encouraged to use more robust techniques, such as temporal separation of measurements, marker variables, or a common latent factor approach.

Second, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships among organizational dehumanization, self-compassion, psychological safety, and career adaptability. Therefore, longitudinal and prospective studies are recommended to clarify the temporal and causal relationships among these variables.

In addition, the study population was limited to nurses working in teaching hospitals affiliated with Ardabil University of Medical Sciences, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare settings or cultural contexts. Although proportional stratified random sampling was used to improve representativeness, the possibility of selection bias cannot be entirely ruled out.

Furthermore, potentially influential contextual and organizational variables, such as leadership style, organizational culture, workload, interpersonal climate, and social support, were not examined in the present study. These unmeasured factors may have influenced the observed relationships and should be incorporated in future research models to improve explanatory power.

Future research may benefit from mixed-method approaches and multi-center designs to enhance understanding and external validity. Additionally, future studies are recommended to employ CFA-based approaches (e.g., common latent factor models) to assess common method bias more rigorously.

Conclusion

The present study found that organizational dehumanization was negatively associated with self-compassion and career adaptability among clinical nurses. Nurses who perceived higher levels of organizational dehumanization reported lower self-compassion and reduced capacity to adapt to professional challenges and occupational demands. These findings suggest that dehumanizing workplace experiences may be linked to poorer psychological and professional functioning among nurses. The findings also indicated that self-compassion partially mediated the relationship between organizational dehumanization and career adaptability, suggesting that self-compassion may represent an important psychological resource in this association. In addition, psychological safety moderated the relationship between organizational dehumanization and self-compassion, suggesting that higher levels of psychological safety may weaken the negative association between them.

Overall, the findings highlight the importance of human-centered organizational cultures, psychologically safe clinical environments, and strategies that support self-compassion among clinical nurses. Healthcare organizations may benefit from efforts to reduce dehumanizing workplace practices and strengthen supportive professional environments. However, given the study’s cross-sectional design, causal conclusions cannot be drawn. Future longitudinal and multi-center studies are needed to confirm the temporal direction of these relationships and to examine additional organizational and contextual factors related to nurses’ career adaptability.
