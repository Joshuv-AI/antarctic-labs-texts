Molecular Mechanism of the SepF N‑Terminal Conformational Switch in Ring Assembly and Membrane Anchoring
Authors: Wenjing Liu, Chang Zhang, Mojie Duan, Meng Yao, Ziwei Chang, Jun Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Septum-forming protein (SepF) self-assembles into ring-shaped structures during bacterial cell division, acting as a bridge that anchors the Z-ring to the cell membrane and thereby facilitates membrane remodeling and constriction. In this study, we elucidate how salt ions regulate the ring assembly of SepF and its membrane anchoring using solid-state NMR (ssNMR) spectroscopy in combination with biochemical methods. We show that the N-terminal fragment of SepF (residues 1–12) is highly dynamic and disordered under salt-free conditions, which inhibits SepF ring assembly. In the presence of NaCl, this N-terminal fragment of SepF undergoes a conformational transition to a less flexible and more ordered state, enabling ring assembly and enhancing its membrane anchoring. In contrast, a SepF mutant that assembles into noncurved fibrils exhibits impaired membrane recruitment capability, highlighting the crucial role of assembly curvature in membrane remodeling. Based on these findings, we propose a mechanistic model in which the coordination of ring assembly and membrane anchoring is critical for membrane remodeling, with NaCl-dependent modulation of the N-terminal fragment playing a central role. Our work provides atomic-level insights into how SepF responds to environmental factors to regulate its conformation and assembly curvature, advancing our understanding of the functional regulation of supramolecular machinery in bacterial cell division.

Introduction

The cellular life cycle relies on the dynamic shaping of biological membranes. Key cellular processes, such as division, transport, and organelle formation, are all accompanied by complex changes in membrane morphology. While cellular lipid bilayers tend to remain flat in a natural state, specific membrane curvatures are formed by the action of the curved, self-assembling proteins. , For example, the ESCRT-III component Snf7 assembles into helically organized filaments that exhibit inverse curvature, driving membrane neck constriction during vesicle budding. , Similarly, the mechanochemical GTPase dynamin drives membrane fission by forming helical polymers that constrict and sever tubules upon GTP hydrolysis. , In bacteria, the membrane-protective protein PspA oligomerizes into ring-like structures that sense and stabilize the membrane under stress. , These examples illustrate that the properties of protein self-assembly, particularly their curvature, are closely linked to their ability to recognize, bind, and ultimately remodel membrane structures. Thus, elucidating how protein assembly is precisely regulated to produce functional curvature is crucial for comprehending its role in membrane remodeling.

SepF plays a key role in bacterial cell division by facilitating Z-ring formation. − In Bacillus subtilis, the absence of SepF leads to aberrant septum formation, and its codeletion with FtsA or EzrA results in bacterial lethality. , The SepF monomer consists of 151 amino acids and is divided into three functional regions: the N-terminal domain (residues 1–56, NTD) includes a membrane-targeting fragment (residues 1–12) and a linker region (residues 13–56), and the C-terminal core domain (residues 57–151, CTD) (Figure A). The CTD of SepF spontaneously assembles into ring-like structures that bind to and stabilize FtsZ protofilaments, whereas the NTD is responsible for interaction with the cell membrane, ultimately anchoring the Z-ring. − By anchoring treadmilling FtsZ protofilaments to the membrane and supporting their continuous reorganization around the division site, SepF promotes proper septum formation during cytokinesis (Figure A,B). Wenzel et al. confirmed that the diameter of spontaneously assembled SepF rings correlates with the thickness of the bacterial division septum. Chimera experiments, in which the conserved core domains of SepF from different species were exchanged, demonstrated that the diameter of the ring formed by the SepF core domain determines its own curvature, effectively acting as a molecular template dictating the nascent septum thickness. This directly links the assembly morphology of SepF to its core function in cell division (Figure B).

While previous studies have established that the NTD of SepF mediates membrane anchoring, and the CTD drives ring assembly, the integration of these functions to form correctly curved ring structures and achieve efficient membrane remodeling remains unknown. Particularly, the role of external environmental factors in regulating assembly and membrane anchoring is not well understood.

Determining the structure of large self-assembling macromolecules like SepF poses challenges for traditional methods such as X-ray crystallography due to their high molecular weight and crystallization difficulties. Solid-state NMR (ssNMR) offers unique advantages for studying large supramolecular assemblies as it is not limited by protein molecular size and can characterize both rigid and flexible regions. − This study employs ssNMR, truncation analysis, and other biochemical methods to elucidate how NaCl regulates the N-terminal conformation of SepF, thereby promoting self-assembly and enhancing membrane anchoring efficiency. Atomic-level structural dynamics from ssNMR indicate that under salt-free conditions, the N-terminal fragment of SepF is highly dynamic and disordered, inhibiting ring formation. NaCl induces a conformational transition, particularly in residues 1–12, stabilizing the 1–12 fragment to a more ordered state. These findings are supported by TEM imaging and interpreted using AlphaFold-predicted models. Density gradient centrifugation further shows that NaCl, especially at physiological concentrations, enhances the binding of SepF to phospholipids, reflecting its coordinated regulation of assembly and membrane anchoring. In contrast, the G137N mutant of SepF forms noncurved fibrils with minimal membrane interaction. Collectively, these results demonstrate that only correctly curved SepF rings can efficiently anchor the lipid membrane via their NTD and remodel the membrane. This work provides new insights into how environmental factors regulate protein assemblies, advancing our understanding of bacterial cell division.

Results

Full-Length SepF Assembles into Ring Structures Depending on the Presence of NaCl

Protein assemblies are influenced by their ionic environment. To study how this affects SepF membrane anchoring, we prepared mixtures of SepF and phospholipid vesicles without and with NaCl. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed distinct outcomes: near-physiological NaCl enabled SepF to form ring-vesicle complexes with liposomes (Figure C). In contrast, in the absence of salt, SepF assembled only into fragmented oligomers that could not organize into scaffolds for specific membrane anchoring. This clear, salt-dependent dichotomy led us to separate the process into two steps: ring assembly and membrane binding. Does NaCl regulate only ring assembly of SepF, the membrane anchoring, or does it coordinately control both steps (Figure D)?

As previously reported, full-length SepF (SepF-FL) assembles into ring-like structures in a buffer containing 75 mM NaCl, eluting at approximately 9 mL in size exclusion chromatography (SEC). This volume, close to the void volume of the column, indicates the formation of very large assemblies that far exceed the effective separation range (>600 kDa) of the column. To investigate the NaCl dependence of ring assembly, we performed biochemical and structural analyses of SepF–FL under varying NaCl concentrations. In the absence of NaCl, the SEC elution volume shifted to 11 mL. Based on the separation characteristics of the column (globular proteins: 10–600 kDa) and the monomer molecular weight (≈17.4 kDa), the apparent molecular weight was estimated to be in the range of 200–300 kDa, corresponding to approximately 11–17 subunits (Figure A). TEM images confirmed SEC results. As shown in Figure B, under salt-free conditions, SepF formed only curved fragments without defined closures, whereas it assembled into typical closed ring structures in the presence of 75 mM NaCl. Notably, unlike SepF-FL, the ring assembly of its the N-terminally truncated variant SepF57–151 did not require the presence of NaCl (Figure B).

NaCl Modulates the Ring Assembly of Full-Length SepF through Interactions with the N-Terminal 1–12 Fragment

Which region of SepF does NaCl influence during the assembly process (Figure C)? To address this question, we conducted ssNMR experiments, employing dipolar-coupling and J-coupling based methods to detect the rigid C-terminal domain (residues 57–151, CTD) and flexible N-terminal domain (residues 1–56, NTD) regions of the protein within the assemblies, respectively. The 2D NCA spectra, derived from dipolar-based cross-polarization (CP), capture signals from the rigid protein regions in the assembly. We compared the 2D NCA spectra of SepF–FL and its N-terminal truncated SepF57–151 under identical buffer conditions (Figure S1). The NCA signals of SepF57–151 assemblies, previously assigned, originate exclusively from the CTD region (residues 63–140). The superimposition revealed that the NCA signals of SepF–FL assemblies completely match the corresponding signals from SepF57–151, indicating that the NCA signals of SepF–FL assemblies stem solely from the CTD. Following this, we overlaid the NCA spectra of SepF–FL in 0 mM and 75 mM NaCl conditions (Figure D). The spectra were nearly identical, indicating that the core structure of the CTD itself was not altered by salt ions. Therefore, we concluded that NaCl regulation does not modify the CTD fold but rather acts through the flexible regions of the protein.

To pinpoint the exact region through which NaCl exerts its regulatory effect, we adopted a stepwise truncation strategy. Beginning with the minimal construct containing only CTD of SepF57–151, we incrementally reintroduced segments of the N-terminal region to generate a series of truncation variants: SepF39–151, SepF25–151, and SepF13–151 (Figure E). We then assessed their ability to assemble under NaCl-free conditions. TEM results revealed that all truncation variants could spontaneously form closed ring structures (Figure F). In contrast, the full-length protein with the intact NTD exhibited sensitivity to NaCl. These findings indicate that the presence of an inhibitory element within the N-terminal 1–56 residues, with the most N-terminal fragment (1–12) being a likely candidate for mediating this salt-sensitive inhibition.

To characterize the flexible regions of SepF assemblies that are invisible in NCA spectra, we employed J coupling-based experiments, such as 2D 1H–13C INEPT and 13C–13C TOBSY spectra. We first compared the spectra of SepF–FL assemblies under 0 mM and 75 mM NaCl conditions. The comparison revealed that the presence of NaCl, which promotes ring assembly formation, led to a significant attenuation or even disappearance of several signals in 2D 1H–13C INEPT spectra of the no-ring aggregate, accompanied by specific chemical shift perturbations (Figure A,B). These spectral variations indicated that NaCl alters the dynamics of a subset of residues. However, to completely assign these signals by 2D INEPT and TOBSY remains challenging due to insufficient sensitivity in the other 2D J-based 15N–13C spectra (i.e., hNCO) for connecting consecutive residues. Therefore, we designed a straightforward comparative strategy to identify signals originating from the N-terminal 1–12 fragment under comparable functional states.

The N-Terminal 1–12 Fragment Is Highly Dynamic in Non-ring SepF Aggregates

To mimic the no-ring aggregate state of SepF–FL formed without NaCl, we prepared a G109K–SepF13–151 sample, which forms a dimer rather than an assembly. Differences observed in the INEPT and TOBSY spectra between SepF–FL and G109K–SepF13–151 can be directly attributed to residues in the N-terminal 1–12 fragment (Figure D). This direct comparison allowed us to unambiguously identify several signals specific to the 1–12 fragment, including peaks for serine (Ser), lysine (Lys), methionine (Met), and phenylalanine (Phe) residues with characteristic chemical shifts (Figure C,E).

To confirm the reliability of this comparison and demonstrate that the G109K mutation does not alter the N-terminal conformation and dynamics, we conducted two control experiments. First, comparing the spectra of SepF–FL and G109K–SepF–FL (both under no-NaCl conditions) showed almost complete overlap, with the identified signals of the 1–12 fragment perfectly aligned (Figure S2). This indicates that the G109K mutation does not affect the intrinsic dynamics of the N-terminus. Second, the spectra of SepF13–151 and SepF13–151 G109K (both with no-NaCl conditions) were largely similar with slight differences (Figure S3). Notably, the key signals identified in Figure C are absent in both spectra, confirming the reliability of our comparative strategy and demonstrating that the G109K mutation does not affect the assignment of 1–12-specific residues. A schematic of the strategy is shown in Figure S4.

We also identified two additional sets of NaCl-attenuated signals, corresponding to two glutamate (Glu) and two alanine (Ala) residues (Figure S5). By analyzing how these signals behave in the spectra of various truncation variants (Figure S6) and integrating sequence data, we successfully localized them. The Glu signals were completely absent in the SepF25–151 truncation, suggesting they are associated with residues in the 13–24 region, which is glutamate-rich. The Ala signals were clearly present in the SepF13–151 spectrum but absent in SepF57–151. Since the only two alanines (A39, A44) in the N-terminal 1–56 sequence are located within the 39–56 fragment, they were attributed to the 39–56 region.

Through this systematic analysis, we assigned most NaCl-affected signals to specific sequence positions. These signals are predominantly concentrated in the N-terminal 1–12 fragment, including methionine (M1, M3), lysine (K4, K6, K8), serine (S2, S12), and phenylalanine (F10, F11) residues. The remaining signals locate in the 13–24 and 39–56 regions (Table S1). Secondary chemical shift analysis , of the assigned residues indicates that the N-terminal 1–12 fragment predominantly adopts a random coil conformation in the unassembled state (Figure F). One exception is noted for a serine residue near the C-terminal FF motif in the 1–12 fragment, which exhibits a distinct β-sheet propensity under no-salt conditions, likely due to its unique local hydrophobic environment. Importantly, upon NaCl-induced assembly, signals for these residues in the INEPT and TOBSY spectra disappear, providing direct evidence that this fragment transitions from a dynamic state to a more rigid state in the ring structure.

SepF–FL Ring Assemblies Shape Membranes by Interactions of Their N-Terminal 1–12 Fragment with the Membrane

The TEM image in Figure C suggests that the binding of SepF to lipids requires its assembly as a scaffold, a process that is dependent on NaCl. Previous studies have established that the N-terminal 1–12 residues of SepF serve as the core unit for its membrane-binding function. To examine the role of N-terminal 1–12 fragment in the membrane binding by ring assemblies, we investigated the interactions between preassembled SepF rings (with and without N-terminal 1–12 fragment) and liposomes using TEM. In the absence of ring assemblies, liposomes formed loose aggregates of varying sizes. When coincubated with SepF rings preassembled in 75 mM NaCl, liposomes were specifically recruited and encapsulated within the internal cavity of the protein rings, forming stable ring-vesicle complexes. In contrast, the SepF13–151 variant, which can form rings but lacks the N-terminal 1–12 fragment, completely lost this specific recruitment capability (Figure A). These results collectively indicate that functional membrane interaction by SepF requires its N-terminal membrane-binding module in the ring architecture.

We hypothesized that, in addition to promoting SepF ring assembly, NaCl may also enhance its binding to negatively charged lipid membranes. Using sucrose density gradient centrifugation, we compared the binding of SepF–FL assembly rings to liposomes formed by E. coli membrane extraction (containing PG/CL) at different NaCl concentrations (Figure B) (Table S2). SDS-PAGE analysis (Figure C) showed that the amount of SepF comigrating with liposomes to the top gradient fraction progressively increased as salt concentrations rose from 37.5 to 112.5 mM. This was corroborated by the decreasing amount of SepF in the bottom gradient fraction with increasing salt concentrations, both indicating enhanced membrane binding of SepF at a higher salt concentration. We note that the top-fraction band at 112.5 mM NaCl (Figure C, lane d) appears slightly fainter than that at 75 mM NaCl. This unusual band at 112.5 mM NaCl is likely due to incomplete recovery of the sample with a syringe during the process of fractionation.

Structural information on the N-terminal fragment during the conformational changes of SepF is essential for understanding its role in SepF assembly and membrane anchoring. Further analysis of the ssNMR spectra revealed that in the presence of NaCl, nearly all key peaks corresponding to the N-terminal residues 1–12 disappeared in the 1H–13C INEPT spectrum (Figure A,B), while the 15N–13C NCA spectrum remained unchanged (Figure D), thereby preventing direct extraction of secondary structural information on the N-terminal 1–12 fragment. We then turned to an AlphaFold2-predicted tetramer model (Figure D–F), which predicts that residues K4–F11 within the N-terminal fragment adopt an α-helical conformation in the assembled state. Taken together with the ssNMR results, the 1–12 fragment remains highly dynamic and disordered in the absence of NaCl, as evidenced by its strong INEPT signals (Figure A,B). Its motion occasionally brings it into the space between adjacent CTD subunits, sterically blocking the α–α assembly interface and preventing ring closure at that site. Because the fragment rarely occupies the assembly interface, most subunit contacts can still form, but ring closure is frequently interrupted. As a result, SepF assembles into curved oligomeric fragments rather than closed rings (Figure B). Conversely, in the presence of NaCl, the 1–12 fragment becomes more ordered and rigid, adopting a partial α-helical conformation. This stabilized fragment is positioned along the sides of the ring, thereby relieving the hindrance at the α-α assembly interface and residing near the core domain helices H1 (Y72–K83) and H2 (D98–A113) (Figure F).

To further characterize the conformational changes of the 1–12 fragment upon binding of the SepF ring to liposomes, we prepared a [13C, 15N]-labeled SepF ring assembly in the presence of liposomes (Table S3). The resulting 2D NCA spectrum was essentially identical to that obtained without liposomes (Figure S7A). In the 1H–13C INEPT spectrum, only a marked attenuation of residual serine signals was observed in the presence of liposomes, indirectly indicating rigidification of the 1–12 fragment, along with the appearance of several phospholipid signals (Figure S7B). However, these data did not provide the secondary structure information of the membrane-bound N-terminus.

We therefore employed molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to compare the interactions of the SepF N-terminal 1–12 fragment with phospholipids in the presence and absence of NaCl. As shown in Figures S8A–C, in the absence of NaCl, the 1–12 peptide adopted a loop structure near the membrane, whereas in the presence of NaCl, it formed a regular α-helix with high helicity from S2–F11. Moreover, the data in Figure S8D revealed that residues M1–N5 were inserted into the phospholipid membrane, demonstrating the importance of NaCl for membrane binding. Compared with the assembled state without liposomes, we inferred that the presence of the lipid membrane further rigidified the 1–12 fragment, and the formation of an amphipathic helix facilitated stable membrane anchoring through hydrophobic interactions.

Based on the cumulative evidence presented above, we constructed a three-dimensional schematic of the SepF ring–liposome complex (Figure G). Consistent with the finding that the N-terminal amphipathic helix of SepF preferentially targets positively curved membranes, the phospholipid vesicles engage the SepF ring at its inner, positively curved surface (Figure A,F). In this model, the SepF ring, with an outer diameter of 50 nm and an inner diameter of ∼40 nm, engages the lipid membrane via its inwardly arrayed N-terminal helices. The encapsulated liposome, with a thickness consistent with approximately 8 phospholipid bilayers, is compatible with the experimentally observed ring-vesicle complex architecture.

G137N SepF Fibrils Lacking Curvature Fail to Recruit Membrane

To investigate how the ring-shaped assembly of SepF influences its membrane-binding capacity, we compared the lipid-binding abilities of WT SepF ring assembly and the G137N SepF mutant, which forms straight fibrils, using density gradient centrifugation (Figure A). TEM imaging revealed that the G137N mutant self-assembled into long, unbranched, straight fibrils, consistent with the previous study. Under identical experimental conditions (75 mM NaCl), a substantial proportion of WT SepF migrated to the top sucrose gradient fraction due to its association with liposomes (Figure A, lane f) (Table S2). In contrast, G137N fibrils exhibited a very faint band in the top fraction (Figure A bottom, lane h), indicating that most fibrillar structures sedimented to the bottom of the tube and were unable to bind lipids effectively. When E. coli lipids were coincubated with G137N fibrils, vesicles were only sporadically observed and were primarily associated with fibril junctions (Figure B), suggesting weak and nonspecific interactions. As shown in Figure A, the membrane-contacting surface provided by straight fibrils is markedly smaller and discontinuous compared to the continuous interface formed by WT SepF rings.

These results demonstrate that membrane recruitment depends on the ring-shaped architecture of SepF. The inability of G137N fibrils to provide a continuous, curvature-matched scaffold severely compromises their membrane-binding capacity (Figure C). Collectively, these findings indicate that effective membrane remodeling by SepF requires both its N-terminal membrane-binding module and the correctly assembled, curved ring structure.

Discussion

This study revealed that the N-terminal 1–12 fragment of SepF acts as a molecular switch regulated by salt ions, with its conformational state governing the dual functions of SepF: ring assembly and membrane anchoring. , Under salt-free conditions, the 1–12 fragment is highly dynamic and disordered (Figure A,B), inhibiting the SepF ring assembly. In the presence of NaCl, this dynamic fragment becomes more ordered and rigid (Figure D–F), relieving the inhibition of ring closure. Additionally, NaCl promotes membrane binding of the 1–12 fragment (Figure C), which folds into a regular amphipathic α-helix capable of inserting into the lipid bilayer (Figure S8).

How does NaCl regulate the dual functions of SepF in membrane anchoring? Intramolecular electrostatic repulsion among residues K4, K6, and K8 maintains the 1–12 region in a highly flexible and disordered state, precluding the formation of stable secondary structure. This disorder causes the 1–12 fragment to undergo large-amplitude swinging motions (Figure ). When the 1–12 fragment occupies the α–α assembly interface, it sterically blocks subunit association at that site; otherwise, assembly proceeds unimpeded. Because only a small fraction of the fragments was at the interface, subunit contacts form at most available sites but are interrupted wherever the fragment intervenes, resulting in curved oligomeric segments rather than closed rings (Figure B). NaCl shields this electrostatic repulsion, leading to a conformation with reduced dynamics. , This conformational transition repositions the 1–12 fragment away from the assembly interface, enabling it to adopt an α-helical conformation docked adjacent to the CTD (Figure D), thus primed for subsequent membrane anchoring. At a near-physiological concentration (112.5 mM), NaCl optimally promotes folding of the 1–12 fragment into a stable amphipathic α-helix, as suggested by the salt-gradient membrane-binding assay (Figure C). Although NaCl may partially weaken electrostatic attraction between the positively charged 1–12 fragment and negatively charged lipid headgroups, the formation of an amphipathic helix enables stable anchoring through hydrophobic interactions rather than electrostatic adsorption alone. In the absence of NaCl, the 1–12 fragment remains a membrane-associated loop that is unable to insert (Figure S8), suggesting that electrostatic attraction alone may be insufficient for stable membrane anchoring without helix formation.

Why must SepF assemble into a ring structure for recruiting membrane? Insights from experiments with the G137N mutant clarify this necessity. Although the G137N mutant retains an intact N-terminal membrane-binding module, it assembles into linear fibrils, which severely impairs its ability to recruit membranes. This suggests that the SepF ring assembly is more than a passive scaffold; its specific curvature is crucial for creating an effective membrane-binding interface. The ring architecture spatially organizes multiple N-terminal modules into a curvature-matched array, enabling cooperative binding and providing a structure that physically complements the positive curvature of the division septum. Therefore, effective membrane anchoring by SepF relies on two indispensable elements: The driving force for membrane-anchoring provided by the N-terminal module and functional scaffold for the membrane curvature-matching provided by the C-terminal ring structure.

Regulating membrane binding and remodeling through N-terminal conformational dynamics of proteins is a conserved strategy from bacteria to eukaryotes. For instance, the N-terminal helix of MinE becomes exposed and inserted into the membrane only upon activation, while ESCRT-III protein Snf7 assembles into filaments with opposite curvature to drive membrane budding or scission. Although the direction of membrane curvatures shaped by these proteins may vary, their strategies align with that of SepF: Coupling self-assembly architecture with membrane-targeting modules to translate protein assembly curvature into the remodeling of specific membrane morphologies.

In the field of membrane curvature generation, previous research has elucidated the templating role of intrinsic protein structures, such as BAR domains, as well as the significant influence of lipid composition on membrane shaping. , However, how environmental physicochemical factors dynamically regulate protein conformation to coordinate its assembly and membrane binding remains poorly understood. This work reveals that physiological ionic conditions (NaCl) regulate SepF function through an ion effect: NaCl stabilizes the dynamic N-terminal conformation, triggering ring assembly, while simultaneously enhancing the interaction between the N-terminal fragment and the membrane. This establishes ions as active regulators that coordinate conformational switching and membrane anchoring, rather than merely a passive electrostatic shielding mechanism. Based on these findings, we propose a three-step model for NaCl-mediated SepF function (Figure ): In low salt conditions, the dynamic NTD 1–12 fragment inhibits assembly, corresponding to an inhibitory state. NaCl stabilizes the 1–12 fragment by shielding its charge, driving the system into an assembly state. Finally, in the presence of NaCl and membrane, the system reaches a membrane-binding state, where the preorganized 1–12 fragment folds and inserts into the membrane to form a stable ring–membrane complex. The key insight of this model is that ionic conditions precisely coordinate the regulation of the N-terminal module, converting environmental signals into the coupling of ordered assembly and efficient membrane anchoring, thus ensuring the accurate formation of the division machinery. This precisely ion-controlled, conversion between a disordered fragment and a functional ring-membrane complex in SepF offers inspiration for the design of novel stimulus-responsive biomaterials.

The corresponding microview (top) shows that intramolecular repulsion among K4, K6, and K8 (green circles with “+”) keeps the NTD 1–12 fragment (red) highly dynamic and disordered, which sterically interferes with the CTD (blue) assembly interface. Middle (assembly state): In the presence of NaCl, SepF assembles into curved ring structures. In the microview, NaCl screens the repulsion among positive charges (gray background), preorganizing the NTD 1–12 fragment into a more ordered helix conformation and relieving steric hindrance. Right (membrane-binding state): With NaCl and lipid membrane (pink), the 1–12 fragment folds into a complete amphipathic α-helix and associates with the membrane along the inner face of the ring. NaCl exerts a dual function, promoting both ring assembly and membrane binding (red dashed arrows).

Conclusion

In this work, we show that the N-terminal 1–12 fragment of SepF functions as a salt-regulated molecular switch governing both ring assembly and membrane anchoring. Under salt-free conditions, this fragment remains highly dynamic to inhibit assembly. In the presence of NaCl, ionic interactions stabilize the fragment, relieving the inhibitory effect and promoting ring assembly. Meanwhile, NaCl also facilitates the folding of the SepF N-terminal domain into an amphipathic α-helix, which enhances membrane binding. A SepF mutant that assembles into noncurved fibrils exhibited impaired membrane recruitment, further confirming that assembly curvature is essential for membrane remodeling. Based on these observations, we propose that NaCl-dependent modulation of the N-terminal fragment couples ring assembly with membrane anchoring, ensuring that curvature-matched scaffolds are assembled only under conditions favorable for membrane engagement. This work provides atomic-level insights into how ions modulate the conformation and assembly of a supramolecular machine during bacterial cell division.

Materials and Methods

Expression and Purification of SepF

The expression and purification protocol for Bacillus subtilis SepF (UniProt: O31728) was adapted from previously published methodologies. All truncated variants, including SepF13–151, SepF25–151, SepF39–151, and SepF57–151, were subcloned into the pET-21a plasmid and confirmed through DNA sequencing. The G109K-SepF and G109K-SepF13–151 mutants were generated using PCR-based site-directed mutagenesis. The amino acid sequences and physicochemical properties of all protein constructs used in this study are summarized in Table S4, as calculated using the ExPASy ProtParam tool. For protein expression, the wild-type SepF and G109K-SepF plasmids were transformed into E. coli C43­(DE3) and BL21­(DE3) cells, respectively. [15N, 13C] isotopically labeled samples were cultivated in M9 medium supplemented with1 g/L 15N-ammonium chloride and4 g/L 13C–glucose as nitrogen and carbon sources.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

Protein samples (10 μL) were applied onto 200-mesh Formvar/carbon-coated copper grids. After incubation for 3 min, excess solution was gently blotted away with filter paper, and the grids were washed twice with the appropriate buffer. Subsequently, 5 μL of 2% (w/v) uranyl acetate was added for staining. Images were captured using a JEM-1400 plus electron microscope operating at 120 kV.

Size Exclusion Chromatography (SEC)

Analytical size exclusion chromatography (SEC) was performed with 1 mg of purified target protein using a Superdex200 Increase 10/300 GL column, with a buffer flow rate of 0.5 mL/min at room temperature. Proteins eluted at various retention times were analyzed via 15% SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis.

Preparation of E. coli Liposomes

Total E. coli lipid extract powders were mixed and dissolved in methanol/chloroform at a molar ratio of 1:3, followed by rotary evaporation. The resulting lipids were resuspended in the appropriate experimental buffer at a concentration of 4 mg/mL and sonicated. The lipid suspension was subjected to 11 cycles of extrusion through a polycarbonate membrane with a pore size of 0.4 μm to achieve uniform liposome size.

Density Gradient Centrifugation

To assess the interaction between SepF and lipids, 1.2 mL of 4 mg/mL E. coli phospholipids was mixed with 1.2 mL of 0.5 mg/mL SepF at varying NaCl concentrations and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Solid sucrose was added to the SepF-lipid mixture to a final concentration of 30% (w/v). Following this, 2.4 mL of the mixture was layered at the bottom of a centrifuge tube, and 4 mL of 25% sucrose solution was gently added without disrupting the density gradient. This was followed by the sequential addition of 3 mL of 20%, 2 mL of 15%, and 2 mL of 10% sucrose solutions to fill the tube. Centrifugation was carried out at 291 K (18 °C) for 2.5 h at 111,200 g (25,000 rpm using a Beckman SW 40 Ti rotor) in a Beckman Optima XPN ultracentrifuge. Following centrifugation, 1 mL samples were collected from both the upper and lower layers for SDS-PAGE analysis.

ssNMR Experiments

ssNMR experiments were conducted on a 14.1 T (600 MHz 1H frequency) Varian VNMRS spectrometer equipped with a 3.2 mm 1H/13C/15N triple-resonance BIO-MAS probe. The experiments were performed at 273 K (dipolar-coupling) and 298K (J-coupling) with a magic angle spinning (MAS) rate of 11,111 Hz. Typical 90° pulse widths were approximately 4.5 μs for 1H, 5.8 μs for 13C, and 7.2 μs for 15N. A 65 kHz 1H decoupling power was applied during acquisition and indirect evolution periods. 2D 1H–13C INEPT (insensitive nuclei enhanced by polarization transfer) and 13C–13C TOBSY (total through-bond correlation spectroscopy) experiments were performed to detect highly dynamic moieties through J-coupling. The recoupling power for 13C–13C was three times the MAS rate, with the corresponding 90° pulse width set to 1/12 of the MAS rotation cycle, and a recoupling time of 7.5 ms. All ssNMR experiments conducted in this study are listed in Table S3. All collected NMR data were processed using NMRPipe software, with spectra analyzed using Sparky software.

Calculation of Secondary Chemical Shifts

Secondary chemical shifts are defined as the displacement values of spin nuclei in the protein influenced by their spatial environment. , This value is calculated as the difference between the observed chemical shift and the random coil chemical shift, as expressed in Equation Δδ=ΔδCA−ΔδCB=[ΔδCA(obs)−ΔδCA(rc)]−[ΔδCB(obs)−ΔδCB(rc)]

The secondary chemical shift is closely linked to the secondary structure of each residue, typically yielding positive values for α-helices and negative values for β-sheets.

Structure Prediction by AlphaFold2

AlphaFold2 software integrates deep learning with principles from physics and biology to predict the 3D structure of proteins based on their amino acid sequences. The amino acid sequence of SepF was submitted with the predicted four-polymer structure, using default settings. The resulting SepF model was utilized to analyze the final assembly mechanism.

Molecular Dynamics Simulation

The initial structure of the SepF N-terminal peptide (residues 1–12) was predicted and constructed using AlphaFold 3. The peptide was initially placed far from the POPC lipid bilayer, with a minimum distance of more than 10 Å between the heavy atoms of the peptide and lipids. Two simulation systems were established to evaluate the salt effect on the structural behavior of the SepF N-terminal peptide: one contained 0.12 M NaCl, and the other was salt free. The CHARMM36 force field was applied for the peptide and membrane lipids, and water molecules were described using the standard TIP3P model. Energy minimization of each system was carried out via the steepest descent method. Next, a 60 ps NVT equilibration was performed with the lipid membrane restrained, where the Langevin thermostat was used to maintain the system temperature at 300 K. This was followed by a 10 ns NPT equilibration under anisotropic pressure control using the Monte Carlo barostat. All bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained by the SHAKE algorithm. Finally, an 800 ns production simulation was run with a 2 fs integration time step, and atomic conformations were saved every 10 ps. The last 500 ns of the trajectory were used for subsequent distance and helicity analyses.
