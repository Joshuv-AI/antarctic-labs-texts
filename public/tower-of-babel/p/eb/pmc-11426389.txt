ABSTRACT
For 3000 years, psychedelics have been used in religious contexts to enhance spiritual thinking, well-being, and a sense of community. In the last few years, a renaissance in the use of psychedelic drugs for mental disorders has occurred in Western society; consequently, a pressing scientific need to elucidate the intricate mechanisms underlying their actions has arisen. Psychedelics mainly bind to serotonin (5-HT) receptors, particularly 5-HT2A receptors, but may also bind to other receptors. Unlike conventional psychotropic drugs used in psychiatry, psychedelics introduce a distinctive complexity. They not only engage in receptor activation, but also exert influence over specific neural circuits, thereby facilitating transformative cognitive experiences and fostering what many have identified as a spiritual contemplation or mystical experience. This comprehensive review describes clinical studies that have examined the propensity of psychedelics to enhance spiritual, mystical, and transcendent cognitive states. This multifaceted nature, encompassing diverse components and paradigms, necessitates careful consideration during the investigation of psychedelic mechanisms of action to avoid oversimplification. The present review endeavours to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the actions of 2 principal psychedelic substances, psilocybin and lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD), with a focus on monoamine and glutamate receptor mechanisms; molecular aspects, such as neuroplasticity and epigenetics; as well as the impact of psychedelics on brain circuits, including the default mode network and the cortico–striato–thalamo–cortical network. Given their distinctive and intricate mechanisms of action, psychedelics necessitate a novel conceptual framework in psychiatry, offering insight into the treatment of mental health disorders and facilitating the integration of the realms of brain, mind, and spirituality.

Introduction

Psychedelics have been used in religious ceremonies by various cultures throughout history. These substances, known for their ability to induce altered states of consciousness, have been employed as sacraments, aids to spiritual exploration, and catalysts for mystical experiences. The specific psychedelics used and the rituals surrounding their consumption vary across different religious traditions. For example, Native American Church ceremonies have mostly used peyote, Amazonian shamanism uses ayahuasca, while Central and South American indigenous traditions use psilocybin mushrooms.

In these cultures, psychedelics are employed as tools for spiritual exploration, allowing individuals to access heightened states of consciousness and encounter mystical experiences. These experiences can involve a sense of unity, transcendence of the self, and a feeling of interconnectedness with the universe or a divine presence, helping spiritual exploration and connection with the divine. The altered state of consciousness induced by psychedelics is seen as a means of achieving transcendence and deepening one’s spiritual understanding; for this reason, psychedelics have been called “entheogens,” from the ancient Greek meaning of “generating the experience of being in/with God.”1,2

Over the last few years, double-blind randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have demonstrated that 1 or 2 administrations of psilocybin, combined with psychotherapy, can treat major depression, and that this treatment may reduce heavy drinking among people with alcohol use disorder, compared with a chronic regimen of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs).3–7 More recently, lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD) was shown to reduce anxiety among patients with life-threatening conditions.8 Finally, the atypical psychedelic 3,4-methyl enedioxy methamphetamine (MDMA or ecstasy) was tested for the treatment of severe posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), with positive outcomes,9 even if not approved by the US Food and Drug Administration.10

Undoubtedly, these studies have inaugurated a novel trajectory in the field of mental health. This trajectory encompasses not only the introduction of innovative pharmacological medicines aimed at ameliorating psychiatric disorders, but also the exploration of their distinctive mechanisms, their mind-altering properties, and their propensity to induce states of transcendental contemplation and spiritual cognizance. This multifaceted functionality of psychedelics engenders a heightened complexity in comprehending their mechanisms of action.

These compounds function mainly as classical agonists, primarily targeting serotonin (5-HT) receptors, along with other monoaminergic receptors, generating molecular intracellular changes and epigenetic modifications. However, delving deeper, their influence extends to the restructuring of brain circuits, encompassing the default mode network (DMN) and cortico–striato–thalamo–cortical (CSTC) network. This circuit activation is probably responsible for phenomena such as ego dissolution and the perception of boundlessness, ultimately culminating in a convergence of effects that ostensibly facilitate the expansion of the spiritual faculties and access to heightened cognitive capacities inherent within the human brain. These psychedelic-induced effects help patients with mental disorders reach an enhanced comprehension of existential purpose, a profound reappraisal of human suffering, an exploration of traumatic imprints, an engagement with depressive symptoms, and a recalibration of negative cognitive ruminations often present during depressive episodes.

The goal of this review was to understand the mechanism of action of psychedelics by examining it at the receptorial and molecular level, as well as at the level of brain circuits, and by examining the effect of psychedelics on the spiritual mind. The review describes studies of the spiritual effects of psychedelics and theories of the mechanisms of action of these effects through the neurocircuits activated by psychedelics, as well as their mechanism of action on 5-HT receptors and other relevant neurotransmitters, with particular emphasis on the studies carried out in our laboratory.

Classification of psychedelics

There is still no consensus for the classification of psychedelics, and the definition of psychedelics is still based on a classification from the 1960s. Classic or serotoninergic psychedelic compounds are so called mainly because they interact with the serotoninergic system. These include the semisynthetic ergoline LSD; plant-derived tryptamines, such as psilocybin (the active ingredient found in magic mushrooms), N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT, the active ingredient in ayahuasca), 5-methoxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine (5-MeO-DMT), and lysergic acid amide (the active ingredient in morning glories); and phenethylamines, such as mescaline (the active ingredient in peyote and San Pedro cacti) and phenethylamine-based synthetic designer drugs.11 Serotoninergic psychedelics are mostly found in nature. Lysergic acid diethylamide is a chemical derivate from naturally occurring ergotamine; it can be created in a laboratory using the naturally occurring ergot alkaloid lysergic acid, which is contained in the rye parasite Claviceps purpurea (Figure 1).11,12

Besides this class, there are the dissociative anesthetics, including ketamine, phencyclidine, and the empathogens–entactogens, which include MDMA and 3,4-methylenedioxyamphetamine (MDA), synthesized by Merck in 1912, but never developed as a medicine.13

The enhancement of spirituality by psychedelics

Understanding the mechanisms of action of psychedelics demands a certain level of complexity. Psychedelics — unlike other drugs such as antidepressants, benzodiazepines, or stimulants — have the peculiar effect of enhancing spirituality, ego dissolution, and the mystical experience. The modern definition of spirituality and mystical experience is still a matter of debate but, here, following the Encyclopedia Britannica, spirituality is defined as the capacity for understanding fundamental moral, existential, or metaphysical questions, especially regarding the nature of the self (or soul or person), the meaning of life, and the nature of mind or consciousness.14 The mystical experience can be defined as the sense of unity with a divinity (coming from the old Greek μύω múō, meaning “to close,” “to conceal,” or “being silent”), also implicating a change in consciousness.15 Psilocybin, LSD, DMT, and entheogens have been reported to induce profound mystical experiences characterized by feelings of interconnectedness, unity, and transcendence, as well as a sense of being in the presence of something greater than oneself. These experiences often resemble spiritual or transcendent states described in religious ceremonies with mystical traditions. The ability of psychedelics to facilitate mystical experiences, foster a sense of connectedness, provide insight into the meaning of life, and engender a sense of community has been acknowledged not only by the indigenous populations of Central and South America, but also by ancient Greek and Roman civilizations, as well as early Christian groups.16

The first scientific exploration of the spiritual effects of psychedelics was conducted by the chemist who first synthesized LSD, Dr. Albert Hofmann. After synthesis, he embarked on a journey of profound self-experimentation. His personal encounter with LSD and his ensuing revelations are eloquently chronicled in his seminal work, LSD, My Problem Child.17 Within these pages, he articulated his transformative experience, recounting, “Waves of ineffable happiness flowed through my body. I had experienced the grace of God. But how could it be possible that it was radiating toward me, particularly out of this cheap lampshade? Then the inner voice answered: God is in everything.”17

Subsequently, Dr. Hofmann co-authored another notable work, The Plant of the Gods: Their Sacred, Healing, and Hallucinogenic Powers.18 As he continued to contribute to the realm of psychedelic research and exploration, he published his final literary endeavour before his passing at the age of 102 years, LSD and the Divine Scientist: The Final Thoughts and Reflections of Albert Hofmann.19 In this comprehensive treatise, he not only delved into the intricacies of LSD and its derivatives, but also expounded on their profound potential in aiding humanity’s use of entheogens for psychological healing and spiritual enlightenment.

Undoubtedly, the Marsh Chapel experiment (also known as the Good Friday experiment) stands as the first double-blind clinical study on the contemporary exploration into the connection between psychedelics, spirituality, and religion.20,21 In this study, students in theology from Boston University (where the Marsh Chapel is located) were enlisted as volunteers. These participants were then randomized into 3 distinct groups. Ten participants received psilocybin (30 mg) and 5 participants received a smaller dose of psilocybin (15 mg), while the other 15 participants, serving as the control, received nicotinic acid (300 mg). Notably, a significant majority of participants in the experimental cluster recounted profound spiritual experiences, including a sense of unity, transcendence of time and place, deeply felt positive mood, sacredness, ineffability, and persistent positive changes in attitude and behaviour toward themselves, others, and life. This experiment was the first empirical evidence supporting the concept that psychedelic substances could indeed facilitate religious experiences. Furthermore, in a long-term follow-up, a subset of these participants expressed that the encounter ranked as the most transformative episode in their lives.20,21

However, this pioneer study was biased by several drawbacks.22 To overcome the limitations of the Pahnke study, in 2006, Griffiths and colleagues23 conducted a seminal double-blind study on the potential benefits of psilocybin-assisted therapy, focusing on well-being and mystical experiences induced by psilocybin. The participants were hallucinogen-naïve adults with regular participation in religious or spiritual activities. Thirty volunteers received orally administered psilocybin (30 mg/70 kg) and methylphenidate hydrochloride (40 mg/70 kg) in a crossover fashion.

In this study, the mystical experience was evaluated with a 100-item questionnaire based on the classic descriptive work on mystical experiences and the psychology of religion by Stace,24 exploring 7 domains of mystical experiences, namely internal unity (pure awareness; a merging with ultimate reality); external unity (unity of all things; all things are alive; all is one); transcendence of time and space; ineffability and paradoxicality (claim of difficulty in describing the experience in words); sense of sacredness (awe); noetic quality (claim of intuitive knowledge of ultimate reality); and deeply felt positive mood (joy, peace, and love). The Spiritual Transcendence Scale was also used. This 24-item questionnaire assesses an individual’s effort to create a broad sense of personal meaning in their life and has been shown to demonstrate cross-cultural generality.25–27 A total score and 3 empirically derived factor scores (prayer fulfillment, universality, and connectedness) are calculated.

The study found that, a few hours after administration, psilocybin produced a range of acute perceptual changes, subjective experiences, and increased measures of mystical experience.23 Interestingly, 7 hours after administration, 22 (61%) of the total 36 volunteers had a “complete” mystical experience after psilocybin, while only 4 of 36 in the placebo group did so. Notably, 2 months after their psilocybin session, 79% of participants evaluated that their well-being or overall life satisfaction had moderately (50%) or significantly (29%) increased.23 This positive effect appeared to persist, with 64% of participants reporting the same outcome during the 14-month follow-up.28 In a subsequent study involving psilocybin, 94% of participants noted an enhancement in their well-being or life satisfaction in the 1-month evaluation.29 However, more comprehensive research is required to identify the specific factors that led to certain participants moderating their response to the treatment.

Enhancements in quality of life could hold even greater importance for people nearing the end of their lives. In parallel investigations, Ross and colleageues30 and Griffiths and colleagues31 found that 87% and 82% of patients with life-threatening cancer, respectively, reported elevated life satisfaction or increased well-being 6 months after undergoing psilocybin treatment. Many participants indicated that the experience acted as a catharsis, fostering a greater appreciation for life and aiding them in coming to terms with their own mortality. The distinct ability of psychedelic treatment to alleviate distress linked to impending death has been demonstrated across multiple studies. For instance, researchers found an amplified sense of continuity after death,29 greater acceptance of mortality,31 and a reduction in the fear of death, with 77% of participants reporting diminished fear in another study.32 These transformative changes likely contribute to the reduction in cancer-related feelings of demoralization and hopelessness documented in connection with psilocybin treatment.30

Notably, the profound spiritual encounters ensuing from the administration of psilocybin or LSD have manifested prominently within contemporary, expansive clinical trials of alcohol use disorder, depression, and anxiety. The subject of contention lies in discerning whether the spiritual encounters relayed by those exhibiting favourable responses stand as an indispensable predictive attribute regarding the progression of recovery and reactions to psychedelic interventions.

Participants in studies involving psilocybin for major depression, as well as for alcohol use disorders, have reported increased feelings of awe, reverence, and spiritual importance.3,33 These experiences may lead to positive changes in outlook, attitudes, and personal well-being; post-analyses of the studies have indicated that connectedness was directly linked to positive outcomes.34–36

Anecdotally, a few patients with alcohol use disorder reported having transformative spiritual experiences over the course of psilocybin-assisted psychotherapy in the Bogenschutz trial, which was likely a determining factor in the improvement of the disorder.33 Reported experiences took a variety of forms, including spiritual insights, beatific visions, and communion with the divine, with a deceased loved one, or with a holy figure. These spiritual experiences required the involvement of psychotherapists during the psilocybin-assisted psychotherapy. The spiritual content and material brought by the patients necessitated the intervention, integration, and interpretation of skilled psychotherapists.34 Participants in a parallel study from the same group also reported that the psilocybin treatment helped them process emotions related to painful past events and helped promote states of self-compassion and self-awareness, as well as feelings of interconnectedness.35

Similarly, in a post-analysis of a clinical trial of psilocybin and escitalopram,3 a significant 3-way interaction indicated significantly greater increases in connectedness for psilocybin responders, compared with escitalopram responders, whereas nonresponders did not increase in connectedness in either condition, indicating that the experience of connectedness, which is a feature of a spiritual experience, is essential for the recovery from major depression in a context of psilocybin-assisted psychotherapy.36

A study involving healthy volunteers found that scores on the Mystical Experience Questionnaire (MEQ) and the Persisting Effects Questionnaire (a questionnaire evaluating well-being) positively correlated among people tested with psilocybin, suggesting that the improved mental well-being produced by psilocybin was directly related to this mystical, transforming experience.37

Finally, in a trial on the use of LSD to treat anxiety in patients with life-threatening diseases and those near death, positive acute subjective drug effects and mystical-type experiences (measured with the MEQ30) were significantly correlated with long-term reductions in anxiety symptoms, indicating that acute positive effects of psychedelics may represent a predictor and biomarker for a positive response to the treatment.8 Similarly, acute mystical-type effects of LSD (200 μg) correlated with changes in well-being and life satisfaction among healthy participants.38

Recently, McCulloch and colleagues37 demonstrated that the mystical-type experience (measured by the MEQ) was qualitatively linked to positive persisting effects (measured with the Persisting Effects Questionnaire) and provided quantitative estimates of these effects in a population of healthy Danish volunteers. They also showed that the degree to which these experiences were described as positively emotionally expressed (MEQ item 17 “experience of ecstasy”) or as feelings of connectedness (MEQ item 28 “experience of unity with ultimate reality”) was robustly associated with lasting positive effects.

To summarize, the plethora of clinical studies indicated that the mystical experience — triggered immediately after the administration of psilocybin or LSD — correlated with long-term positive effects on quality of life and well-being (Figure 2).

Possible mechanism of spiritual enhancement

The mechanism underlying the enhancement of spiritual thinking by psychedelics is still unknown, but 5-HT2A receptor agonism plays a major role. This evidence emerged from brain imaging studies, with imaging performed immediately after the administration of psychedelics. A study by Preller and colleagues39 found that the subjective effects of LSD were fully blocked by ketanserin, a 5-HT2A receptor antagonist, including the experience of unity, blissful state, and insightfulness, while the spiritual experience was only partially and nonsignificantly blocked by ketanserin, suggesting that another mechanism may be responsible for this phenomenon. In addition, ketanserin inhibited the LSD-induced attribution of personal relevance to previously meaningless stimuli and modulated the processing of meaningful stimuli in cortical midline structures, in a functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) study.39

Similar results were previously obtained with psilocybin; ketanserin (high doses, 40 mg/kg) mostly blocked the effects of derealization and depersonalization associated with euphoria (OSE scale, measuring oceanic boundlessness). Illusions, hallucinations, and changed meaning (VUS scale, measuring visionary restructuralization) were blocked by ketanserin and risperidone (a mixed 5-HT2 and dopamine D2 receptor antagonist). Anxiogenic ego dissolution, thought disorder, and paranoia (AIA scale, measuring dread of ego dissolution) were blocked by ketanserin and risperidone, but were enhanced by haloperidol, a D2 receptor antagonist.40

In another study involving healthy volunteers, although a high (200 μg) dose of LSD did not further increase “good drug effects” or “drug liking” compared with a lower (100 μg) dose, it did further increase ratings of ego dissolution, oceanic boundlessness, and visionary restructuralization. Ketanserin markedly reduced the response to the high dose of LSD, especially for items linked to mystical effects rated by the MEQ43 — namely sacredness, internal and external unity, noetic quality, transcendence of time and space, and deeply felt positive mood — those rated by the MEQ30 — mystical experience, ineffability, positive mood, and transcendence of time and space — aesthetic experience, and items from the 5 Dimensions of Altered State of Consciousness Scale, including blissful state, spiritual experience, experience of unity, and changed meaning of perception.41 More recently, another study affirmed that the alterations of mind induced by LSD, including visual and acoustic alterations and ego dissolution, were blocked by ritanserine.42

Using resting-state arterial spin labelling, blood oxygen level–dependent functional connectivity, and magnetoencephalography measures, decreased connectivity between the parahippocampus and retrosplenial cortex correlated strongly with ratings of ego dissolution and altered meaning, implying the importance of this particular circuit for the maintenance of self or ego and its processing of meaning.43

Using [11C]Cimbi-36 positron emission tomography (PET) to assess 5-HT2A receptor binding, neocortex 5-HT2A receptor binding was statistically significantly negatively associated with duration of peak plateau of psychoactive effects from psilocybin and positively with time to return to normal waking consciousness.44 It was also significantly negatively associated with total MEQ score, indicating that 5-HT2A occupancy by psilocybin was directly related to consciousness alteration and the mystical experience. Brain 5-HT2A receptor binding predicted subjective temporal and mystical effects of psilocybin in healthy humans.44 Another study, using the same PET technique, found that change in mindfulness and 5-HT2A receptor binding correlated negatively, meaning that the tested ligand (i.e., psilocybin) bound the receptor and, thus, the [11C] Cimbi is less detectable.45

A recent case-report found that trazodone — a 5-HT2A receptor antagonist, as well as an H1 histamine and α-1 adrenergic receptor antagonist and SSRI — blocked the hallucinogenic effects of psilocybin but maintained the antidepressant effect.46

The role of alteration of brain circuits in the effects of psychedelics

The basis of the mind-altering properties of psychedelics and their capacity to trigger a mystical experience and reappraisal of past experiences is still unknown, but one of the possible hypotheses focuses on their capacity to modify the specific brain circuits that are implicated in the modulation of consciousness. The capacity of psychedelics to modulate specific circuitry is likely linked to their inherent capability to alter cognitive processes and lead one to reappraise negative events. This alteration encompasses the induction of hallucinations and dissociative states, with concurrent facilitation of an expansion of cognitive horizons. Expansion may pertain to an alternative apprehension of reality, the experience of suffering, self-perception, and overarching worldview, including the spiritual perception of existence.

This distinct capacity appears to be intricately connected to the potential of psychedelics to engage specific neural pathways within the brain through the activation of 5-HT2A receptors or other receptors. Although a debate persists concerning the principal neural circuits implicated in the cognitive expansion of psychedelics, multiple circuits may contribute to this phenomenon, rather than a singular circuit.

Until now, research on the circuits implicated in the mechanism of psychedelics has focused on the CSTC circuits, the DMN, the claustrum–cortex–claustrum circuit, and the cortex–amygdala circuit.

The cortico–striato–thalamo–cortical model

The CSTC network constitutes a complex circuitry of interconnected brain regions that assume pivotal roles in diverse cognitive, motor, and affective processes. This intricate architecture comprises bidirectional linkages that connect the cerebral cortex, striatum, and thalamus, and revert back to the cortex.47

In the CSTC network, the cerebral cortex is responsible for the reception and processing of sensory input, initiation of motor directives, and engagement in a range of cognitive functions, notably attention, decision-making, and planning. The striatum, which serves as the principal recipient structure within the basal ganglia, receives input from the cortex. It assumes a critical role in motor regulation, reward perception, habituation development, and procedural learning. The thalamus is a central relay hub in the brain that functions as an intermediary between sensory organs and the cerebral cortex. It acquires sensory signals from various body regions and transmits them to specific cortical areas. Within the framework of the CSTC network, the thalamus acts as a pivotal interface connecting the basal ganglia and the cortex. Importantly, a nucleus is directly involved in the CTSC network, namely the thalamic reticular nucleus (TRN), a small nucleus that envelops the thalamus. The TRN is a gatekeeper that filters the information going to the cortex; TRN cells that exhibit high levels of activity can almost completely inhibit thalamo–cortical relay neurons. When the thalamic gate is closed, no information is relayed to the cortex. On the contrary, when GABAergic neurons of the TRN themselves are inhibited, thalamo–cortical relay neurons are active and thalamo–cortical neurotransmission is allowed (i.e., the thalamic gate is open). Given its intrinsic pace-making properties, the TRN has been considered the brain rhythm generator.48

The CSTC model suggests that psychedelics alter information processing in the brain by stimulating 5-HT2A receptors located within cortico–striato–thalamic loops, resulting in a disruption of thalamic gating.49 Psychedelics may decrease connectivity between association cortices and the rest of the brain by decreasing thalamic filtering of internal and external stimuli; on the other hand, they may increase connectivity between the sensory cortex and the rest of the brain, ultimately increasing the sensory process and the capacity for re-appraisal of past experiences.50 In other words, under the influence of LSD or psilocybin, more information goes from the thalamus to the sensorial cortex and less information goes from the associative cortex to the striatum and thalamus.50 This model is supported by behavioural measures of impaired sensorimotor gating in humans after administration of psilocybin and LSD.51,52 In addition, the model agrees with neuroimaging measures of increased thalamo–cortical functional connectivity and synchronization of cortical sensory regions.50,53,54

A recent MRI study has confirmed that, compared with placebo, LSD increased the effective connectivity from the thalamus to specific unimodal cortices, increasing bottom–up and decreasing top–down information flow between the thalamus and cortices. Specifically, LSD increased the effective connectivity from the thalamus to both unimodal and transmodal cortices, indicating a breach in the hierarchical organization of ongoing brain activity.55

In our laboratory, studies involving rodents showed that LSD had a double effect on TRN neurons; it decreased spontaneous firing and burst-firing activity in 50% of the recorded neurons in a dose-dependent manner (5–160 μg/kg), starting at 10 μg/kg, while another population of neurons (50%) increased firing and burst-firing activity, starting at 40 μg/kg. This modulation was accompanied by an increase in firing and burst-firing activity of thalamocortical neurons in the mediodorsal thalamus. On the contrary, LSD excited infralimbic prefrontal cortex pyramidal neurons only at the highest dose tested (160 μg/kg).56 These data confirm the complex modulation of thalamic structures by psychedelics, pointing out their capacity to decrease some thalamo–cortical pathways while increasing others, as observed in brain imaging studies involving humans. More studies are needed to better identify the subtype of neurons implicated in this orchestrating activation (i.e., parvalbumin, GABAergic, or somatostatin neurons). By identifying the neuronal activation, consciousness and the application of psychedelics to pathologies related to thalamic–cortex dysrhythmia can be better understood.47

The activation of the CTSC circuit by psychedelic substances may constitute the core mechanism underlying their transformative effect on consciousness — including heightened sensory processing, pseudo-hallucinatory experiences, cognitive expansion, and depersonalization effects — as comprehensively discussed in the review by De Gregorio and colleagues.57 The impact of psychedelics on the CTSC circuit and consciousness may be the basis for opening up to a spiritual experience and, at the same time, to the transformative power over the elaboration of trauma and suffering experiences, as observed among patients with depression, PTSD and alcohol use disorder.3,4,7–9 However, additional research is needed to validate this hypothesis.

The DMN model

The DMN is a network of brain regions that are consistently active during restful wakefulness and are believed to play a role in internal mental processes, including self-referential thinking, daydreaming, and mind-wandering. It is a collection of interconnected brain regions that show synchronized activity and typically exhibit decreased activity during goal-directed tasks that require external attention.

The DMN primarily involves several key brain regions, including the medial prefrontal cortex, the posterior cingulate cortex — which includes the perigenual anterior cingulate cortex, an area that is mainly responsible for processing emotions and regulating the endocrine and autonomic responses to emotions — the precuneus, and the bilateral inferior parietal lobes. Other regions that are also part of the DMN include the hippocampus, the temporal lobes, and parts of the lateral parietal cortex. The DMN is involved in various cognitive processes such as self-reflection, introspection, autobiographical memory, social cognition, and theory of mind. It is thought to be important for constructing an individual’s sense of self and for monitoring the internal mental state; its dysfunction has been associated with various psychiatric and neurologic disorders.58 It is not surprising, then, that the main findings in functional neuroimaging experiments with LSD have related to changes in the functional connectivity of nodes in the DMN.43

Experimental evidence for this model hinges on the findings from neuroimaging of a disintegration of association networks, including the DMN.59 Employing fMRI, Stoliker and colleagues60 found that, under the peak effect of LSD (100 μg), inhibitory effective connectivity from the salience network to the DMN became excitatory, while inhibitory effective connectivity from the DMN to the dorsal attention network decreased, compared with placebo. Other research has suggested that psychedelics decrease functional connectivity within resting-state networks, while they increase functional connectivity across networks.61,62 Such bidirectional connectivity changes linked to the subjective effects of psychedelics, such as ego dissolution, as well as a reduction in self-referential awareness and self-world boundaries,63 which correlated with improvements in well-being 4 months later.43

However, although replication studies have also found that LSD decreased functional connectivity within nodes of the DMN,61 no reliable consensus has emerged on the changes that occur between nodes of the DMN and other areas of the brain, including the thalamus.64

The deactivation of the DMN and the relaxed beliefs under psychedelics (REBUS) model

The brain may become more flexible in processing sensory information and less constrained by pre-existing beliefs or expectations under the influence of psychedelics by reducing the ability of high-level cortical networks (e.g., the DMN) to control lower levels in the brain, specifically subcortical structures including the hippocampus, amygdala, and thalamus.

From these observations, Carhart-Harris and colleagues65 developed the REBUS model, suggesting that the effects of psychedelics on cognition and perception may be caused, in part, by a relaxation of precision weighting on beliefs. Psychedelics may enhance the bottom–up flow of sensory inputs, while reducing the precision of previous beliefs, expectations, and past experiences that normally constrain neural processing.65

The REBUS model is also strictly linked to the entropic brain hypothesis, which is based on the idea that the brain’s normal balance between order and randomness (entropy) is affected by psychedelics and the global effect is supposed to increase entropy in neural dynamics.66 In the baseline state of consciousness, the brain’s neural activity demonstrates a balance between order and disorder. This balance is crucial for various cognitive processes, including perception, thinking, and self-awareness, but when people consume psychedelic substances, their subjective experience is characterized by alterations in perception, emotion, and cognition owing to an increase in the entropy of brain activity. This means that the brain’s neural networks become less constrained (more relaxed) and more disordered during the psychedelic experience. This increase in entropy may result in a broadening of conscious experience, allowing for the emergence of novel perceptions, thoughts, and sensations.

Following this theory, the increase in entropy may generate ego dissolution (where the sense of self or ego temporarily dissolves) and contribute to the disruption of the usual boundaries and categories that define our self-concept. This, in turn, may facilitate mystical or profound experiences.

Carhart-Harris and colleagues66 also proposed that this altered state of consciousness may provide a window of plasticity in the brain that could facilitate therapeutic purposes, such as treating depression, anxiety, and PTSD. The hypothesis suggests that this altered state (greater entropy) may allow for new patterns of thinking and emotional processing.66 The REBUS theory remains to be confirmed by more solid empirical evidence, but it may also explain ego dissolution and the mystical state produced by psychedelics.

In support of these data, 5-HT signalling activation via the dorsal raphe pathways was discovered to be associated with increased DMN activity and decreased sensorimotor network activity, thus reinforcing that the mechanism of serotoninergic psychedelics is intimately linked to DMN activation.67 Moreover, in a large meta-analysis of brain imaging studies, self-related processing (i.e., when individuals think about themselves) was linked to the activation of the DMN. In particular, the perigenual anterior cingulate cortex showed more involvement in self-specific stimuli, compared with non–self-stimuli, and this activity overlapped with DMN activity during the resting state, thus distinguishing self-related processing from non–self-related conditions.68 It is logical to think that psychedelics, which impair the DMN, also trigger ego dissolution, with impairment of self-perception and an opening to transcendent consciousness.

Other promising circuits

The claustrum–cortical–claustral circuit revolves around the claustrum, a subcortical nucleus within the telencephalon that possesses the highest concentration of 5-HT2A receptors in the brain.69 This nucleus is characterized by an extensive and reciprocal connection with the cortex, including inputs from the claustrocortical glutamatergic pathway. Consequently, claustral neurons may play a pivotal role in the initial neural response to psychedelics.

In this model, it was suggested that the claustrum synchronizes standard cortical network states. Psychedelic drugs, however, interfere with 5-HT2A receptor–mediated network connections between the claustrum and the cortex, resulting in a weakening of these standard cortical networks during the effects of the drugs.70 For instance, acute administration of psilocybin was observed to reduce the amplitude of low-frequency oscillations in the claustrum. This coincided with a decrease in functional connectivity between the claustrum and the DMN and, simultaneously, enhanced connectivity between the right claustrum and the frontoparietal task control network. Notably, the left claustrum’s connectivity with the latter network was diminished as well.71

The claustrum–cortical–claustral network underscores the claustrum’s importance in regulating diverse states of cortical networks. However, the exact function of the claustrum, particularly its potential involvement in consciousness and psychopathology, remains enigmatic.72 Given the presence of both 5-HT2A and κ-opioid receptors — the main site of action of salvinorin A, a hallucinogen found in Salvia divinorum that is a potent κ-opioid (but not 5-HT2A) receptor agonist — the claustrum has been hypothesized to be the main site of action for substances that trigger transcendental thinking and consciousness-altering experiences.73 More studies are needed to better understand the function of the claustrum and its implication in the psychedelic effects of ego dissolution and spiritual thinking.

The prefrontal cortex–limbic system circuit

The primate lateral, medial, and orbital regions of the prefrontal cortex exhibit connections with limbic structures, which, in turn, establish reciprocal connections with the amygdala and brainstem components. These interconnected structures play a pivotal role in assessing threats and executing responsive processes that lead to physiologic arousal.

Notably, people under the influence of LSD were observed to have reduced neural responses within the left amygdala and the right medial frontal gyrus during exposure to fearful faces, in comparison to placebo. Given that the amygdala receives substantial 5-HT inputs from the dorsal raphe nucleus,74 this reduction in amygdala responsiveness could potentially be attributed to the acute inhibitory effects of LSD on 5-HT neuron firing.75

In a different context, microdoses of LSD were found to enhance amygdala connectivity with the right angular gyrus, the right middle frontal gyrus, and the left cerebellum. Conversely, connectivity with the left and right postcentral gyrus, as well as the superior temporal gyrus, decreased. Notably, the strength of connectivity between the amygdala and the middle frontal gyrus exhibited a positive correlation with positive mood after LSD administration.76 The impact of LSD on the amygdala–prefrontal cortex circuitry seems to be dependent on dose.

Studies in our laboratory have demonstrated that repeated LSD administration increases the synaptic strength of 5-HT2A and the α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazole propionate (AMPA) responses in the prefrontal cortex of mice. This effect is also parallelled by increased spinogenesis in this area. Moreover, the prefrontal cortex is necessary for the prosocial behaviour in mice induced by LSD,77 suggesting a role of this structure in the LSD-mediated behaviour.

Mechanisms of action of psychedelics on the 5-HT system

The main site of action of psychedelics remains the 5-HT system; 5-HT serves as the primary neurotransmitter associated with major depression and functions as the central mechanism through which psychedelics exert their effects. The capacity of psychedelic substances like LSD to bind with 5-HT receptors, especially the 5-HT2A and 5-HT1A receptors, has been linked to their effects.11

The dorsal raphe nucleus is the major source of 5-HT neurons in the brain,78 sending projections to cortical and limbic regions.79 The 5-HT neurons of the dorsal raphe are inhibited by 5-HT1A somatodendritic autoreceptors, but 5-HT1A receptors are also present at the post-synaptic level. On the contrary, 5-HT2A receptors are little expressed in the dorsal raphe nucleus74 but are abundantly expressed at the postsynaptic level (i.e., prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, thalamus, claustrum), as well as the locus coeruleus80 and ventral tegmental area.81

The 5-HT2A receptor is certainly the main site of action of psychedelics. Receptor activation engages specific neurons, producing the activation of brain circuits, therapeutic and mind-changing effects, and, ultimately, spiritual thinking, especially at higher doses.82 In support of the specific role of the 5-HT2A receptor in the evocation of spiritual and hallucinogenic experiences, a recent study by Warren and colleagues83 showed that a 5-HT1A-selective 5-MeO-DMT analogue had anxiolytic-like and antidepressant-like activity among socially defeated animals, without the hallucinogenic effects.

Hallucinogenic effects are likely triggered by activation of 5-HT2A receptors.84 The emergence of visual hallucinations induced by substances like LSD and psilocybin is attributed to the stimulation of 5-HT2A receptors on the postsynaptic membrane within layer V of the medial prefrontal cortex.85,86 Notably, overactivation of cortical 5-HT2A receptors alters the functionality of the CSTC circuit. This disruption leads to interference in the thalamic regulation of sensory and cognitive information, ultimately resulting in perceptual distortions such as hallucinations.87 Although the spiritual effect cannot be studied in animals, it is possible to study the cellular effects triggered by 5-HT2A receptor activation at the molecular, electrophysiological, and behavioural levels and to compare electrophysiological changes in specific areas with brain imaging and electroencephalography studies involving humans. Similarly, some behaviours observed in animals can be compared with human behaviours. However, since psychedelics act primarily in consciousness, experience reappraisal, and spiritual thinking, the study of psychedelics in animals remains a challenge and has intrinsic limitations. In rodents, hallucinogenic drugs produce a unique behaviour; the stimulation of 5-HT2A receptors leads to head twitches, a motor behaviour believed to correspond with hallucinations in humans.84 This effect is very strong, especially with LSD and psilocybin, but not with lisuride, a 5-HT2A receptor agonist that also acts as a D2 receptor agonist at the D2 autoreceptor level.88

In general, 5-HT2A agonists and psychedelics activate Gq-like G proteins to enhance the hydrolysis of phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate. This produces the mobilization of intracellular Ca2+ via the release of inositol trisphosphate and the activation of protein kinase C via diacylglycerol. Other signalling pathways at 5-HT2A receptors include activation of arrestin translocation and arachidonic acid release, as reviewed by Kwan and colleagues.89

Interestingly, Cornea-Hébert and colleagues74 reported that 5-HT2A receptors are predominantly cytoplasmic (intracellular), rather than associated with the plasma membrane, and not traceable in dendritic spines or axon terminals, concluding that they activate intracellular transduction pathways and intervene in the regulation of the expression of many genes, which suggests that their main function is to participate in retrograde signalling systems activated by 5-HT.74 The evidence that 5-HT2A receptors are mostly intracellular and that they mediate the plasticity-promoting properties of psychedelics was recently confirmed by Vargas and colleagues.90

Behavioural and neuronal effects of LSD: data from our laboratory

Even if many of the mind-altering and spiritual effects of psychedelics are impossible to study in rodents, the neuronal changes induced by psychedelics and some of their behavioural effects are translatable in animals by studying changes in single neurons and observing basic behavioural outcomes (i.e., anxiety, social, and antidepressant-like behaviours). Moreover, the changes in brain structures that modulate consciousness in humans (i.e., prefrontal cortex or reticular thalamus) are measurable in rodents, even if obvious limitations linked to the human brain’s complexity must be taken into account.

In our laboratory, rats were intravenously injected with low doses (5–20 μg/kg) of LSD, producing a decrease in spontaneous 5-HT firing activity, as previously reported by Aghajanian and colleagues.91 The decrease in 5-HT firing was prevented by a selective 5-HT2A antagonist (MDL100907),75 as well as by a 5-HT1A receptor antagonist (WAY-100635), suggesting that the 5-HT1A is also relevant for LSD function. The affinity of LSD for the 5-HT1A receptor was also reported in in vitro studies.92,93 Although most LSD research and hypotheses understandably centre around the serotoninergic neurotransmitter system, it is undeniable that LSD also has strong effects on the dopaminergic system, given its psychotic-like properties.57 Our laboratory’s in vivo studies support direct action of LSD on dopaminergic neurotransmission in the ventral tegmental area,75 but only at higher doses. Indeed, LSD (30–120 μg/kg) dose-dependently decreased dopamine firing activity in the ventral tegmental area, which was prevented by haloperidol, a D2 receptor antagonist, and by WAY-100635, a 5-HT1A receptor antagonist, as well as by the trace amine–associate receptor 1 (TAAR1) antagonist, N-(3-ethoxyphenyl)-4-(pyrrolidin-1-yl)-3-(trifluoromethyl)-benzamide (EPPTB). These results suggest that, at high doses, LSD strongly affects dopamine mesolimbic neuronal activity in a 5-HT–independent manner and with a pleiotropic mechanism of action involving 5-HT1A, D2, and TAAR1 receptors.75

This double effect of LSD at low doses (serotoninergic mechanism) and high doses (dopaminergic mechanism), could be the pharmacological basis of why, at low doses, LSD has some beneficial effects in elevating mood and well-being, as observed in studies with microdosing,94 while, at higher doses, LSD induces a hallucinogenic effect with mystical-like properties. However, more studies are necessary to correlate this dose–response effect of LSD in humans, and it cannot be ruled out that the hallucinogenic effects are mediated by both D2 and 5-HT2A receptors.

Remarkably, LSD shares a common effect with SSRIs and 5-HT1A receptors, a decrease in 5-HT firing activity, whereby they cause the release of 5-HT into the synaptic cleft through their blocking of the 5-HT transporter (Figure 3).95 As a result of this action, they subsequently activate 5-HT1A autoreceptors, leading to a reduction in firing rate.96 After several weeks of treatment, SSRIs desensitize 5-HT1A receptors, re-increasing 5-HT firing and acting as antidepressants or anxiolytics.97,98

The effects of LSD are strikingly similar to the mechanism of action of SSRIs in this respect (Figure 4). In fact, LSD decreases the firing and burst activity of 5-HT neurons in the dorsal raphe nucleus via 5-HT1A receptor activation, and this effect can be blocked by 5-HT1A antagonists.75 However after repeated treatment, 5-HT firing is increased because of desensitization of 5-HT1A autoreceptors.99 In stressed animals, 5-HT firing activity decreases over time,99,100 but a 7-day regimen with LSD (30 μg/kg/d) reverses this decrease by restoring the 5-HT firing rate. This long-term action of LSD is also correlated with its anti-anxiety and anti-stress properties, observed after 7 days of repeated treatment.99 Interestingly, RCTs have shown that LSD (200 μg) has anxiolytic effects that are detectable days and weeks after the treatment.8,101 Other studies on LSD and anxiety involving animals, healthy volunteers, and patients are reported in our review.102

Lysergic acid diethylamide is also known for its prosocial effects; it is considered an empathogen, similar to MDMA, evoking feelings of subjective well-being, happiness, closeness to others, openness, and trust.51 Our laboratory reported that repeated LSD administration promoted social behaviour, which encompasses a plethora of complex interactions among individuals, normally within the same species, that are usually beneficial to an individual or the whole group.77 Social behaviour was evaluated using 2 different paradigms, namely the direct social interaction and the 3-chamber tests. Interestingly, LSD not only promoted basic social behaviour between mice (direct social interaction time and sociability index in the 3-chamber test) but, remarkably, showed an effect in the social novelty index (curiosity for a novel mouse) of the 3-chamber test.77 The prosocial effect of LSD is mediated by the medial prefrontal cortex, given that the optogenetic inhibition of this area nullifies the prosocial effects.

This mechanism is likely mediated by potentiation of the synaptic responses of the AMPA and 5-HT2A receptors, but not N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) and 5-HT1A receptors in the medial prefrontal cortex. This intimate interaction between the 5-HT2A receptor and glutamatergic system induced by LSD has replicated previous evidence suggesting that the activation of postsynaptic cortical 5-HT2A increases extracellular glutamate release in the synaptic cleft, which is reversed by selective 5-HT2A antagonists40 and AMPA receptor antagonists.103 Electrophysiological studies have shown that antagonism of NMDA receptor subunit NR2B suppresses the glutamate release induced by LSD, suggesting an essential role for glutamate in the downstream effects of LSD.104 Functional metabotropic glutamate receptor 2 or 3 (mGluR2 or mGluR3) is also required, given that mGluR2 or mGluR3 antagonists block LSD-induced gene transcriptional changes.105

Since AMPA is linked to mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) phosphorylation and this mechanism has also been observed for the hallucinogenic antidepressant ketamine,106 we also investigated the potential effects of LSD on mTOR pathways and their implication in the prosocial effects of LSD. Indeed, repeated LSD exposure leads to an increased phosphorylation of protein kinase B and mTOR in the medial prefrontal cortex. Moreover, in conditional knockout mice lacking raptor (a structural component of mTOR complex 1) in excitatory glutamatergic neurons, the prosocial effects of LSD and the potentiation of 5-HT2A and AMPA receptors’ synaptic responses are nullified, demonstrating that LSD requires the integrity of mTOR complex 1 in excitatory neurons to promote social behaviour.77 The prosocial effects of LSD and other psychedelics in reinstating social reward learning in adulthood were recently observed by Nardou and colleagues,107 namely how these agents reinstate social reward learning in adulthood in parallel with metaplastic restoration of oxytocin-mediated long-term depression in the nucleus accumbens. Human exposure to LSD was reported to increase the social adaptation to opinions similar to one’s own through stimulation of 5-HT2A receptors, which increased neuronal activity in the medial prefrontal cortex in response to social feedback processing.108 These results indicate that LSD selectively enhances social behaviour by potentiating excitatory transmission in the medial prefrontal cortex through 5-HT2A and AMPA receptors and mTOR signalling.

In other preclinical studies, a single dose of LSD elicited antidepressant-like effects that were noticeable after 5 weeks in a rodent model of depression.109 Repeated LSD administration elicited antidepressant-like effects in a preclinical model without affecting controls, suggesting that, although the improvements elicited by LSD may be noticeable in disease states, they may not be so remarkable in physiologic states.110 More recently, it has been reported that the receptor for brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) tyrosine receptor kinase B (TrkB) mediated plasticity-related and antidepressant-like effects of LSD on neuronal networks and behaviour, even if the antidepressant effect on the forced swimming test was detectable only after repeated swimming sessions and 7 days after a single administration of LSD,111 providing in vitro validation of the similarity between LSD and some antidepressant drugs that also act through the TrkB receptor. Other effects of LSD in animals and humans are reported in detail in our recent review.11

LSD and other 5-HT and non–5-HT receptors

In addition, LSD possesses an affinity for other receptors in the 5-HT receptor family, especially the 5-HT2B receptor.112 It has been demonstrated to bind to the 5-HT2C receptor as a partial agonist, as it does 5-HT2A;113 the 5-HT2C receptor is implicated in the behavioural and cognitive effects of hallucinogenic drugs.114 Specifically, the effects of 2 hallucinogens on drug discrimination and head twitches were completely nullified by MDL100907, an exclusive 5-HT2A inhibitor, but were also weakened by SB242084, an exclusive 5-HT2C inhibitor.114 Interactions at the 5-HT2C receptor are believed to be accounted for by the absence of addictive qualities in psychedelic compounds and to partially mediate their anxiolytic and antidepressant effects.115–117

Agonist action has been described at the 5-HT1B, 5-HT1D, 5-HT1E, 5-HT6, and 5-HT7 receptors.112,118–122 Evidence for LSD interaction at the serotonin transporter (SERT) remains controversial. In fact, although an in vitro study reported no interaction with SERT,92 in vivo investigations reported decreased LSD effects in SERT-null mice,123,124 although these may be indirect effects (e.g., 5-HT1A autoreceptor sensitization) rather than mediated actions of LSD directly on SERT.

As previously mentioned, LSD has an effect on dopamine receptors (D2, D3, D4), especially at higher doses; activation is likely linked to psychotic effects and serious adverse effects.11

Psilocybin and 5-HT receptors

Few studies have explored psilocybin in animals. This may be because psilocybin is a prodrug that, in humans, is transformed into the active drug, psilocin. Given the different metabolism between rodents and humans, this may be a major limitation to exploring the effects of psilocybin in laboratory animals. Like LSD, psilocybin acutely decreases 5-HT firing in the dorsal raphe nucleus,125 confirmed also by studies in our laboratory. Serotoninergic and dopaminergic activity are mutually regulated. Indeed, the selective lesion of dopamine neurons decreases the spontaneous firing activity of 5-HT in dorsal raphe nucleus neurons by 60%, whereas the selective lesion of 5-HT neurons enhances the firing activity of dopamine neurons in the ventral tegmental area by 36%, indicating an inhibitory effect of the 5-HT input on dopamine neurons and an excitatory effect of dopamine on 5-HT neurons.126,127 The 5-HT–mediated regulation of striatal dopamine neurotransmission via serotoninergic afferents is involved in modulating reward, and serotoninergic antidepressants improve mood, cognition, and hedonic behaviour partially via 5-HT and dopamine interactions.128

Psychedelic-induced effects are thought to be modulated in part by 5-HT2A receptors located on the striatum, resulting in an increase in dopamine release.129 Psilocybin may exert its psychedelic effects mostly through 5-HT2A receptor agonism, occupying up to 72% of all human brain 5-HT2A receptors at clinically relevant doses.40,139–133 Psilocybin also interacts with the 5-HT1A receptor in vivo, without producing behavioural effects, and with 5-HT1D, 5-HT1E, 5-HT2B, 5-HT2C, 5-HT5, 5-HT6, and 5-HT7 receptors in vitro, although it is not known whether these interactions produce a clinically relevant effect.133–135 Using radioligand binding assays, psilocybin has been demonstrated to display a high affinity with 5-HT1A, 5-HT2A, and 5-HT2B in humans.136 Some authors have suggested that the 5-HT1A receptor mediates the deficits in attentional performance induced by psilocybin.137 Psilocybin also decreases 5-HT reuptake by inhibiting SERT,138 which may explain, in part, its antidepressant properties.

Given the difficulties of using a prodrug in rats, limited research has explored psilocybin in rodents. A single administration of psilocybin (1 mg/kg) or LSD (0.15 mg/kg) produced persistent antidepressant-like effects in rodents, detectable after 5 weeks.109 The same group found that the same single dose of psilocybin was able to reverse depression-like effects after 5 weeks among adult female rats subjected to adolescent chronic restraint stress, but without long-term alterations of mRNA related to synaptic density in brain areas relevant to depression. These results led the authors to hypothesize that the effect of psilocybin was not necessarily related to synaptic and neuroplasticity changes.139 Indeed, neuroplasticity is not a hallmark of psychedelics, but rather, this property is shared with SSRIs, antipsychotics, and substances of abuse.140 Psilocybin (1 mg/kg) showed fast-acting antidepressant-like properties in mice, displaying a significant restoration of their preference for sucrose solution and female urine 24–48 hours after psilocybin injection; the 5-HT2A receptor was not necessary for its therapeutic actions.141 More recently, use of the active metabolite psilocin (1.5, 2.0, and 4.0 mg/kg) was associated with significantly shorter immobility times in the forced swimming test and tail-suspension test, compared with vehicle-treated control mice, 24 hours after treatment. These effects were eliminated by pretreatment with volinanserin, a 5-HT2A antagonist. Surprisingly, the decreased immobility time in the forced swimming test in response to acute psilocin treatment was sustained for at least 3 weeks.142 In a recent study, psilocybin, 2,5-dimethoxy-4-iodoamphetamine (DOI), and a nonhallucinogenic 5-HT2A receptor agonist, lisuride, were tested in a chronic despair mouse model.143 A single injection of each drug in wild-type mice induced anxiolyticand antidepressant-like effects in the novelty-suppressed feeding, sucrose preference, and forced swimming tests, lasting up to 15 days. Interestingly, DOI and lisuride did not produce antidepressant-like effects in 5-HT2A receptor knockout (htr2a−/−) mice, while psilocybin was still effective and the 5-HT1A receptor and D1 or D2 receptor blockade did not affect the antidepressant-like effects of psilocybin in knockout mice. These data suggest that other receptors could contribute to the long-term antidepressant effects of psilocybin.143 This is consistent with the findings of Hesselgrave and colleagues,141 who found that ketanserin did not inhibit psilocybin’s antidepressant-like effects in a model of chronic stress.

Contrastingly, in a model of chronic corticosterone administration, Cameron and colleagues144 reported that the antidepressant-like effects of a single dose of psilocybin was abolished in htr2a−/− mice. These contradictory results may be owing to the difference in the depression model. Lisuride, but not DOI, also produced antidepressant-like effects, supporting the notion that a psychedelic experience might not be required for a therapeutic response to 5-HT2A receptor agonists.145,146

Influencing neuroplasticity: the role of psychedelics

Importantly, strong evidence suggests that the activation of 5-HT2A receptors by psychedelics may influence the expression and modulation of genes, which, in turn, may influence synaptic plasticity and induce long-term neurochemical changes.147–150 Martin and Nichols151 provide more details on the genetic changes induced by psychedelic drugs.

Psychedelics have the potential to stimulate neuroplasticity through various mechanisms, such as bolstering neural connections and promoting spinogenesis, which is the formation of new and persisting spines, representing novel synapses.99,152 This particular effect of psychedelics on neuronal plasticity has been linked to their effects on neuropsychiatric disorders; in the past, the same hypothesis was attributed to conventional antidepressants.153,154 It is possible that psychedelics share this neuroplasticity property with other classes of psychotropic drugs (including antidepressants, antipsychotics and mood stabilizers)155,156 without this necessarily being the essential and unique mechanism of action explaining the effects of psychedelics on mental health. Our research has revealed that stress conditions led to a decrease in spinogenesis within the mouse brain. However, a 7-day regimen of LSD not only increased spinogenesis in control mice but also appeared to restore the lost spines caused by stress.99 Similar observations have been observed by Ly and colleagues.152

Furthermore, it has been observed that LSD can elevate the expression of CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein β within the prefrontal cortex of mice.150 This specific transcription factor plays a pivotal role in synaptic scaling, a fundamental neuronal process governing synaptic functionality, as elucidated by Turrigiano.157 In alignment with the concept of neuroplasticity, LSD has demonstrated the capacity to augment the expression of c-fos, egr-1, and egr-2 in primary murine neuronal cultures.152,158,159

It is noteworthy that signalling through the sigma-1 receptor is associated with increased synaptic activity, neurogenesis, and attenuation of drug-induced cognitive impairments, as elucidated in studies by Moriguchi and colleagues160 and Romieu and colleagues.161 The indirect impact of LSD on this receptor’s function may contribute to the observed clinical enhancements following LSD administration.160,162,163 Furthermore, the interaction between the σ-1 receptor and D2 receptors could potentially contribute to the promotion of enhanced neurogenesis.164

A recent study conducted by our laboratory demonstrated that repeated LSD administration affected DNA methylation in genes related to neurotropic, neurotrophic, and neuroplasticity signalling.165 This effect extended to the protein levels of genes involved in these processes. Notably, proteomic analyses indicated that the most affected proteins were those related to receptor internalization and downregulation, suggesting a reorganization of receptors after psychedelic administration. This reorganization may be a fundamental mechanism mediating the effects of psychedelics and their capacity to rewire brain circuits. Indeed, by changing the expression of receptors and the communication between neurons, brain circuits may be reprogrammed in a more functional manner. Remarkably, repeated LSD administration also boosted signalling in the endocannabinoid system, which has also been involved in the regulation of neuroplasticity.166

Similarly, psilocybin’s effect on hippocampal neurogenesis has been shown to follow a biphasic pattern.167 Lower doses (0.1 mg/kg) showed a non–statistically significant trend toward increased neurogenesis, whereas higher doses (1–5 mg/kg) resulted in decreased neurogenesis 2 weeks after treatment.167 These changes in neuroplasticity were associated with improved fear extinction at lower doses, potentially mediated by psilocybin-induced enhancement of dopamine through 5-HT receptors, facilitating fear extinction learning.167,168

Recent research has also pointed out that psychedelics, like other antidepressants, may act on the BDNF receptor TrkB.111 More studies are needed to distinguish the differential activation of TrkB by psychedelics and SSRIs.

In synthesis, 5-HT2A and 5-HT1A receptors, along with other monoaminergic and glutamatergic receptors, are likely the main targets of psychedelics. The interaction of psychedelics with these receptors initiates an intracellular cascade that triggers neuroplasticity and epigenetic events, but also modulates specific neuronal circuits. The activation of these specific circuits may facilitate a mental expansion, allowing patients with trauma, stressor-related disorders, depression, or PTSD to better work through pain and suffering and move forward. The suffering may become meaningful and positively transformative. However, this hypothesis requires further research and validation.

Psychedelics and safety risk

Despite some evidence of physical and psychological safety, the current understanding of potential adverse psychiatric effects and abuse liability remains limited. However, there is evidence that both acute and chronic use of psychedelics can have negative effects on mental health and that some of these adverse effects are linked to the effects on spiritual thinking enhanced by psychedelics.

During sessions described by Johns Hopkins researchers, psilocybin resulted in increased heart rate and blood pressure (75% of participants), nausea and vomiting (15% of participants), headaches (30% of participants), and discomforts and stress (20% of participants). Among the reported psychological effects (30% of participants) were fear, panic, and anxiety; grief, sadness, and despair; insanity, isolation, existential loneliness, and sense of dying; and paranoia.30,31,169 With regard to the mystical effects of psychedelics, the post-session adverse effects were characterized as spiritual bypassing (meaning that the participant became hyper-spiritual and detached from reality), with a radical shift in worldview associated with ego inflation, overidealization, and a sense of grandiosity. Emotional destabilization and affect dysregulation were also observed.30,31

Psychedelics may induce hallucinogen-persisting perception disorder, which is the recurrence of drug-like effects long after the substance has worn out. Although the incidence of this condition is low and it seldom impairs life quality, it has been reported among people with a long-term history of psychedelic use or abuse, proportionally to the number of times the substance was ingested, although sometimes after 1 administration.170 Another serious concern with the use of psychedelics is the emergence of previously unknown traumatic experience; it can be difficult for the psychotherapist to discern if it is real or not.171 The emergence of novel unknown traumatic experiences, which are often difficult to treat, also opens several ethical questions about the use of psychedelics in clinical settings. These adverse effects strongly support that psychedelic therapy cannot be administered without psychedelic-assisted psychotherapy.171,172

Open questions

The realm of pharmacology pertaining to psychedelics and their application in the therapeutic domain of mental health heralds a novel avenue for ameliorating neuropsychiatric disorders. These substances exert their effects through a multifaceted interaction with various 5-HT receptors and different neuronal circuits, engendering positive outcomes in mental conditions characterized by anxiety, depression, and addiction. A concerted endeavour of ongoing research is also dedicated to the development of prototypes of optimal psychedelics engineered to circumvent the propensity for dissociation and hallucinogenic manifestations, while concurrently eliciting antidepressant and anxiolytic effects by stimulating single receptor subtypes.173

The research on psychedelics without spiritual and hallucinogenic effects is presently the approach that is deemed most worthy of pursuit, but it is still unknown if these serotoninergic drugs will represent a real novel treatment or if they will mimic other known serotoninergic compounds such as 5-HT1A agonists and partial agonists, 5-HT2A partial agonists, or SSRIs, which possess pharmacological properties but without the spiritual effect of psychedelics.146,174,175

It is still unknown if the real benefits of psychedelic drugs are linked to their capacity to modulate 5-HT2A receptors, thus triggering brain circuits by enabling spirituality, which can provoke a different perspective and reappraisal in patients. The anecdotal narratives originating from people and patients undergoing psilocybin or LSD experiences within clinical trials underscore a transformative and spiritually profound phenomenon. These accounts attest to the emergence of a newfound sense of purpose amidst existence, imbuing their depressive suffering and trauma with importance, and furnishing a heightened perspective on life, particularly in the context of terminal illnesses. This idiosyncratic encounter, incited by psychedelics, may constitute an integral facet of the curative trajectory of psychedelics in the field of mental health. The study of psychedelics urges a novel framework in psychiatry; patients under the influence of psychedelics must be studied by integrating the phenomenology of their personal subjective experience — their perception of self, space, and time — paralleled by objective measurements such as brain imaging techniques and electroencephalography.176

Reported experiences with psychedelics are in line with the growing body of research exploring the relationship between religion, spirituality, and mental health. Several studies have found a positive association between religious or spiritual beliefs and psychological well-being. People who identify as religious or spiritual often report higher levels of life satisfaction, happiness, and overall well-being. Some research suggests that religious involvement or spiritual practices may be associated with a lower risk of certain mental health conditions, such as depression, anxiety disorders, substance abuse, and suicidal behaviours, fostering resilience and adaptive coping strategies in the face of challenges. They may offer people a sense of hope, forgiveness, gratitude, and transcendence, which can aid in navigating difficult circumstances. Other studies, however, show that religiosity may increase neuroticism and guilt.177,178

The extent to which psychedelics contribute to enhanced well-being in the treatment of major depression, anxiety, or PTSD — whether through inducing altered cognitive states, activating monoaminergic receptors, or modulating brain circuits — remains a topic of ongoing debate in neuroscience and psychiatry. The efficacy of psychedelics may stem from a composite interplay of these factors, encompassing receptor-mediated circuit activation and the facilitation of neuroplasticity, thereby facilitating a transformative mental and psychological state. This state, in turn, can engender a cognitive reappraisal of maladaptive experiences, allowing for the creation of a novel, more positive insight into negative experiences.

Certain psychologists have additionally postulated that psychedelics may elicit intrinsic brain self-healing mechanisms, allowing the brain to fix abnormal neuronal circuits that are impaired in mental disorders, akin to healing observed in instances of inflammatory or infective pathologies.179

The pharmacological implications of psychedelics are forging an unprecedented trajectory in neuroscience. Psychedelics offer the possibility for re-evaluation of the construct of spirituality and neuroscience, compelling an expansive discourse between neuroscience, psychiatry, and related domains such as theology and philosophy.180,181 Furthermore, psychedelics underscore the conceptual interface linking psychopharmacology and consciousness changes linked to a different perception of suffering, trauma reappraisal, and the search for the meaning of suffering in conditions such as depression, trauma, and end-of life anxiety.

Conclusion

Psychedelics require a novel framework for neuroscience to dig into the relationship between the brain and the formation of self and consciousness, as well as the relationship between the brain and the sense of sacredness. Moreover, there remain questions of whether the sense of sacredness or spirituality may be related to mental health and recovery from mental disorders. Despite these numerous questions, psychedelic science opens a new chapter in the study of the link between the brain, mental health, and spirituality.
