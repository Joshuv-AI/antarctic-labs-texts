Effect of noise isolation with noise-cancelling earmuffs during laparoscopic surgery on acute postoperative pain: a clinical randomised controlled trial
Authors: Teng Wu, Hongwei Qi, Kangli Hui, Ying Hu, Miaomiao Xu, Jiejie Zhou, Manlin Duan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute postoperative pain is common in surgical patients, and intraoperative noise isolation has been shown to reduce pain for up to 24 h postoperatively. However, acute postoperative pain primarily occurs during the first three days after surgery, and it remains unclear whether intraoperative noise isolation affects the incidence of moderate-to-severe acute postoperative pain during this period. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate whether the use of noise-cancelling earmuffs during laparoscopic surgery reduces the incidence of moderate-to-severe acute postoperative pain.MethodsA single-center clinical randomised controlled trial was conducted in Jinling Hospital, Affiliated Hospital of Medical School, Nanjing University. The study included 86 patients aged 18–65 years who underwent elective laparoscopic surgery under general anaesthesia, had an ASA classification I-III, and received patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA). Participants were randomly assigned to either a noise isolation group or a control group. The noise isolation group wore noise-cancelling earmuffs to attenuate intraoperative noise from induction until the end of surgery, while the control group received conventional anaesthesia management without earmuffs. The primary outcome was the incidence of numeric rating scale (NRS, 0–10) pain scores ≥ 4 at 72 h postoperatively, measured using a 10-point numeric rating scale. Secondary outcomes included the incidence of NRS pain scores ≥ 4 at 24 h and 48 h postoperatively and the proportion of different tiers of NRS pain scores (mild [1–3], moderate [4–6] and severe [7–10]), daily frequency of additional rescue analgesic use over postoperative days 1–3, the maximum resting pain score and the maximum movement-evoked pain score at 24, 48, and 72 h postoperatively and total opioid consumption during the first 72 h postoperatively.ResultsThe noise isolation group had a significantly lower incidence of moderate-to-severe pain on the third postoperative day (7.1% vs. 25%, P < 0.05) compared with the control group. The incidences of moderate-to-severe pain on the first (33.3% vs. 56.8%, P < 0.05) and second (19.0% vs. 47.7%, P < 0.05) postoperative days were significantly lower in the noise isolation group compared to the control group. Noise isolation reduced the proportion of moderate pain (33.3% (14/42) vs. 56.8% (25/44), P = 0.003; 19% (8/42) vs. 40.9% (18/44), P = 0.027; 4.8% (2/42) vs. 22.7% (10/44), P = 0.016) on postoperative days 1 to 3 compared with the control group. Median additional rescue analgesic use was lower during the three postoperative days in the noise isolation group (0 [0–2] vs. 1.33 [0–2], P = 0.023). The median maximum resting pain scores in the noise isolation group were significantly lower in the postoperative 24 and 72 h periods (1 [0–2] vs. 2 [1–3], P < 0.05; 0 [0–1] vs. 1 [0–2], P < 0.05), as was the median maximum movement-evoked pain scores at 48 and 72 h postoperatively (3 [1.75–3] vs. 3 [2–4], P < 0.05; 2 [1.75–3] vs. 3 [2–3.75], P < 0.05). Opioid dosage (70 [61.6–74] vs. 73.2 [68–76.6], P < 0.05) and the number of PCIA pump button presses (0 [0–0] vs. 1 [0–2.75], P = 0.002) in the noise isolation group were significantly lower in the third postoperative day compared with the control group. Finally, sleep disturbances were less frequent in the noise-isolation group on the third postoperative day (19.0% vs. 40.9%, P < 0.05) compared to the control group.ConclusionIntraoperative noise isolation using noise-cancelling earmuffs reduces the incidence of moderate-to-severe pain and reduces pain severity distribution one to three days after laparoscopic surgery, and it also lowers both total opioid consumption and the need for additional rescue analgesia during the three days after surgery.Trial registrationRegistered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry ChiCTR2500097844 (Date: 26/02/2025).Keywords: Noise isolation, Postoperative pain, General anaesthesia, Laparoscopic surgery

Introduction

Over 300 million surgeries are performed worldwide each year [1], Yet inadequate postoperative pain management leaves approximately 48.7% of patients experiencing moderate-to-severe pain after surgery [2]. Insufficient analgesia increases the risk of surgical complications and adversely affects patient recovery and prognosis [3]. Opioids remain the primary analgesic approach, with a utilization rate of about 68% on the first postoperative day [4]. However, its adverse effects still make it limited, including nausea, vomiting, itching, sedation, respiratory depression and decreased bowel movements [5].

Consequently, researchers have explored multimodal analgesic strategies, such as nerve blocks, to improve postoperative pain control. As a result, professionals have begun to investigate multimodal analgesic modalities to treat postoperative pain, including nerve block therapy and other methods of nerve block or nerve block combined with opioids that can reduce opioid dosage and relieve acute postoperative pain. Even so, the adverse effects of this type of analgesia remain [6, 7]. Therefore, non-pharmacological analgesic modalities that are non-invasive, safe and effective with fewer adverse effects are gaining more attention, such as music therapy, virtual reality and exercise therapy [8–10].

Noise adversely affects both psychological and physiological function [11, 12]. There are multiple sources of noise in the operating theatre, including monitors, surgical instruments, and staff communications [13]. Previous studies have demonstrated that even under general anaesthesia, the primary and auditory cortices remain active, allowing the brain to process complex auditory stimuli [14–16]. Emerging evidence suggests that intraoperative auditory interventions (e.g., music or white noise) may reduce postoperative pain [17, 18]. Recent studies indicate that intraoperative noise isolation using earplugs or headphones can decrease postoperative pain and opioid requirements without increasing adverse events [19, 20]. However, these investigations were limited to the first postoperative day, whereas acute pain predominantly persists for three days after surgery [21].

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effect of intraoperative noise isolation on the incidence of moderate-to-severe acute pain within the first three postoperative days following laparoscopic surgery.

Methods

Design

This prospective clinical randomised controlled trial was conducted at Jinling Hospital, Affiliated Hospital of Medical School, Nanjing University, Nanjing, China. The study was approved by the Clinical Trial Ethics Committee and registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (No. ChiCTR2500097844). All patients signed a written informed consent on the day before surgery. The trial was conducted in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines.

Participants

Inclusion criteria: (1) Age of 18–65 years; (2) Scheduled for elective laparoscopic surgery under general anaesthesia; (3) ASA classification I-III; (4) Planned for postoperative PCIA. Exclusion criteria: (1) Hearing impairment; (2) History of psychiatric disorders or long-term use of psychotropic medications; (3) History of chronic pain or long-term use of analgesic medicines; (4) Planned for mechanical ventilation after surgery or still need for anesthesia; (5) Post-operative admission to the intensive care unit is required; (6) Patients who have participated or are participating in other trials; (7) Inability to complete postoperative assessments; (8) Previous surgery within 30 days; (9) Allergy to materials used in earmuffs; (10) Declined to provide informed consent.

Randomization and blinding

Eligible patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to the noise isolation group and the control group according to a computer-generated randomisation table. The groupings were placed in separate sealed envelopes, which were then handed in turn to an operating theatre nurse who was not involved in the study. When the patient entered the operating theatre, the envelope was opened by this nurse and the patient was allocated to the noise isolation and control groups. The noise isolation group was given specialised noise cancelling earmuffs (Peltor™ X5A, 3 M™, Poland) to wear, which were removed at the end of the operation. In order to maintain blinding, medical warm shawls were used to cover the ears of all patients after induction of general anaesthesia to ensure that patients, surgeons, nurses, anaesthetists and statisticians were blinded to the subgroups prior to final analysis.

Anaesthesia and postoperative analgesia management

Routine preoperative and biochemical tests were performed on all patients before surgery. Preoperative baseline data were collected from patients, and they were educated on how to score pain using NRS and visual analogue scale (VAS), as well as how to use the PCIA pump correctly.

Upon admission, intravenous access was opened, routine intraoperative monitoring was established, including finger pulse oximetry, electrocardiogram, and invasive blood pressure measurements, and the depth of anesthesia was monitored using sedline (Masimo, Mexico), a depth of anesthesia monitoring method indexed by the patient state index (PSI), to monitor the depth of anesthesia. Anaesthesia was induced sequentially with propofol (2.0–2.5 mg/kg), rocuronium bromide (0.6 mg/kg) and sufentanil (0.3 μg/kg), to facilitate the insertion of endotracheal tube, and the required depth of anaesthesia was maintained with intraoperative remifentanil (0.1–0.2 μg/kg/min), propofol (1–4 mg/kg/h), and 2% sevoflurane. Depth of anaesthesia (PSI: 25–50) was maintained with intraoperative loading doses of rocuronium bromide as required. Intraoperative doses of propofol, sufentanil, and remifentanil were recorded. The patient was transported to the post-anaesthesia care unit at the end of the operation. 100 mL of PCIA solution containing sufentanil (50 μg), oxycodone (20 mg), tropisetron (2 mg), betamethasone sodium phosphate (5.26 mg) and 0.9% saline was administered at a loading and patient control dose of 2 mL, with a background infusion rate of 2 mL/h, and a lockout duration of 15 min.

Outcome measure

Postoperative pain was assessed using the NRS and pain on leaving the post-anaesthesia care unit (PACU) was assessed using the VAS, with resting pain defined as the intensity experienced when lying quietly in bed and movement-evoked pain defined as the intensity when turning or coughing [20]. Sleep disturbances were evaluated using the NRS and the Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS). The VAS used a 10 cm horizontal line, with 0 representing no pain and 10 representing severe, unbearable pain. NRS sleep scores range from 0 to 10, where 0 indicates excellent or good sleep and 10 indicates inability to sleep through the night, with a total score of 6 or higher indicating a diagnosis of insomnia. AIS scores range from 0 to 24, with a total score of 6 or higher indicating a diagnosis of insomnia [22]. The primary Outcome was the incidence of a 72 h postoperative NRS pain score of ≥ 4 [23], where the NRS ranges from 0 (no pain) to 10, with scores 1–3 indicating mild pain and ≥ 4 representing moderate-to-severe pain. If the patient's maximum resting pain score is ≥ 4 or if there is a complaint of analgesia, the surgeon may administer flurbiprofen ester (50 mg/dose, intravenous drip) or parecoxib sodium (40 mg/dose, intravenous injection). Secondary outcomes included the incidence of NRS pain scores ≥ 4 at 24 and 48 h postoperatively and the proportion of different tiers of NRS pain scores, daily frequency of rescue analgesic use over postoperative days 1–3, maximum resting pain scores and maximum movement-evoked pain scores at 24, 48, and 72 h postoperatively, the total amount of opioids consumed during the three postoperative days, and VAS on leaving the PACU, the number of PCIA pump button presses during the 48 h postoperatively, and the incidence of sleep disturbances on the first and third postoperative days.

Previous studies established conversion factors: 50 μg of sufentanil is equivalent to 50 mg of morphine, 10 mg oxycodone equals 7 mg morphine [24], and 50 mg of flurbiprofen ester is equivalent to 4.4 mg of morphine [20]. Additional recorded parameters included extubation time, length of stay in the PACU, and incidence of complications (nausea, vomiting, dizziness, pruritus and urinary retention).

Sample size calculation

Based on Our preliminary observations, which revealed a 36% incidence of moderate-to-severe pain on postoperative day 3 in the control group compared to 6% in the noise isolation group, Sample size was calculated using PASS 15.0 (NCSS, USA) with a two-sided α of 0.05 and 90% power, yielding a required sample size of 74 patients. Accounting for a 20% dropout rate, we enrolled 94 patients. For statistical analysis, continuous variables were assessed for normal distribution using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analysed using an independent samples t-test. In contrast, non-normally distributed data were expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical data were presented as frequencies (n) and percentages (%) and analysed using the chi-square test. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS, version 27.0 (IBM SPSS), and P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

As shown in Fig. 2, the analysis of the primary outcome for both groups of patients undergoing laparoscopic surgery revealed that the noise isolation group exhibited a significantly lower incidence of moderate-to-severe pain on the third day (7.1% (3/42) vs. 25% (11/44), P = 0.025) compared with the control group.

Comparison of the incidence of moderate-to-severe pain at 24, 48, and 72 h postoperatively. CON: control; NI: noise isolation; PO: postoperative time point

Compared with the control group, the noise isolation group had a significantly lower incidence of moderate-to-severe pain on the first (33.3% (14/42) vs. 56.8% (25/44), P = 0.029) and second (19.0% (8/42) vs. 47.7% (21/44), P = 0.005) postoperative days (Fig. 2). Noise isolation increased the proportion of mild pain (66.7% (28/42) vs. 43.2% (19/44), P = 0.03; 81.0% (34/42) vs. 52.3% (23/44), P = 0.005; 92.8% (39/42) vs. 75% (33/44), P = 0.025) and reduced the proportion of moderate pain (33.3% (14/42) vs. 56.8% (25/44), P = 0.003; 19% (8/42) vs. 40.9% (18/44), P = 0.027; 4.8% (2/42) vs. 22.7% (10/44), P = 0.016) on postoperative days 1 to 3 compared with the control group, with no significant change in the incidence of severe pain (0 vs. 0; 0 vs. 6.8% (3/44), P = 0.242; 2.4% (1/42) vs. 2.3% (1/44), P = 1) (Fig. 3). The frequency of supplemental analgesia per person per day during the three postoperative days was significantly lower in the noise isolation group (0 [0–2] vs. 1.33 [0–2], P = 0.023) compared to the control group (Table 2). As shown in Fig. 4A, the median (IQR) maximum resting pain scores at 24 and 72 h postoperatively were significantly lower in the noise isolation group (1 [0–2] vs. 2 [1–3], P = 0.035; 0 [0–1] vs. 1 [0–2], P = 0.035), whereas the median maximum resting pain score at 48 h postoperatively (1 [0–2] vs. 1 [0–2], P = 0.384) did not differ. In terms of the maximum movement-evoked pain scores (Fig. 4B), there was no significant difference in the noise isolation group compared to the control group in terms of pain scores at 24 h postoperatively (3 [3, 4] vs. 4 [3, 4], P = 0.108), Yet median pain scores at 48 and 72 h postoperatively, respectively (3 [1.75–3] vs. 3 [2–4], P = 0.008; 2 [1.75–3] vs. 3 [2–3.75], P = 0.011) were significantly lower. As shown in Table 2, compared to the control group, the noise isolation group had substantially fewer opioid dosages (70 [61.6–74] vs. 73.2 [68–76.6], P = 0.025) and postoperative the number of PCIA pump button presses (0 [0–0] vs. 1 [0–2.75], P = 0.002) in the three days after surgery. Meanwhile, the noise isolation group showed a decrease in the incidence of sleep disturbances on the third postoperative day (19.0% (8/42) vs. 40.9% (18/44), P = 0.027) compared with the control group. Still, there was no difference in the incidence of sleep disturbances on the first postoperative day (50.0% (21/42) vs. 61.4% (27/44), P = 0.289). There was no significant difference in the preoperative AIS and NRS scores of the two groups (Table 1). There was no difference between the two groups in median (IQR) extubation time (30 [21.75–37.25] vs. 35 [24–41], P = 0.114) and median (IQR) length of stay in the PACU (38 [30–41] vs. 35 [30–41], P = 0.604). The noise isolation group did not improve median (IQR) VAS pain scores when patients left the PACU compared to the control group (2 (1–3) vs. 3 (2–3), P = 0.095), and we also did not observe a significant difference in the dosage of other analgesic medications between the two groups during the first 1–3 postoperative days (Table 2). Regarding adverse effects, as presented in Table 3, the noise isolation group demonstrated no increased incidence of delayed extubation, postoperative nausea and vomiting, dizziness, pruritus or urinary retention compared to the control group.

Discussion

This clinical randomised trial demonstrates that noise isolation is an effective and safe intervention for reducing acute postoperative pain following laparoscopic surgery. The intervention significantly decreased moderate pain proportions from postoperative days 1 to 3, with a progressive shift toward milder pain categories and no reported severe pain. These findings were corroborated by reduced supplemental analgesia requirements during this period. Noise isolation reduced patients' maximal resting pain on the first and third postoperative days, improved maximal movement-evoked pain on the second and third postoperative days, reduced postoperative opioid dosage, and lowered the incidence of sleep disturbances on the third postoperative day without increasing the incidence of adverse effects such as postoperative nausea and vomiting and dizziness. Noise isolation showed no significant effect on maximum resting pain scores on postoperative day 2. We hypothesize this may be attributable to increased bedside activity initiation among most patients at this postoperative stage. Similarly, the intervention did not reduce maximum movement-evoked pain scores on postoperative day 1, potentially reflecting limited patient mobility during this early recovery period.

Postoperative pain is common in patients undergoing abdominal surgery. Current evidence demonstrates that intraoperative noise reduction significantly lowers both maximum pain scores at multiple time points in the first 24 postoperative hours and opioid requirements after laparoscopic surgery [20]. There was no clinically significant difference in opioid consumption in the postoperative noise isolation group compared to the control group, although there was a statistically significant difference, which we believe may be related to, among other things, the patient's pain tolerance. Furthermore, postoperative noise isolation in hospital wards has also been associated with improved pain relief [25]. Since anxiety is a known predictor of postoperative pain, with highly anxious patients experiencing greater pain intensity [26], noise isolation may contribute to better intraoperative sedation, reduced postoperative anxiety, and improved patient satisfaction [27, 28]. Noise, defined as an unpleasant auditory stimulus, has been linked to increased cardiovascular risk [11, 12], anxiety-related disorders [29], and impaired concentration among surgical staff, posing potential safety hazards [30]. The average noise level in operating rooms during abdominal surgery exceeds the recommended maximum of 55 decibels [31]. Prior studies have identified intraoperative noise as an independent risk factor for postoperative pain [20]. While earplugs can reduce noise exposure [32], the earmuffs used in this study reduced the noise level to 37 dB, which is more in line with international standards for operating room noise. Our findings further support the effectiveness of intraoperative noise isolation in reducing moderate-to-severe pain and opioid consumption within the first three postoperative days.

While the exact analgesic mechanism of noise isolation requires further investigation, preserved auditory cortex activity during general anesthesia suggests environmental noise may be subconsciously processed, potentially modulating pain pathways [14–16, 33]. This suggests that the brain retains some capacity to interpret auditory stimuli during anaesthesia. Regarding the relationship between noise and postoperative pain, research has shown that patients exposed to lower noise levels exhibit reduced α, β, δ and θ wave amplitudes on intraoperative EEG compared to those in high-noise environments [19]. Additionally, noise levels exceeding 70 dB have been shown to induce mechanical nociceptive hypersensitivity in mice with plantar incisions, possibly due to noise-induced neuronal sensitisation and postoperative pain amplification [19]. In addition, Zhou et al. showed that a lower (5 dB) signal-to-noise ratio could produce analgesia in mice by affecting corticothalamic circuits [34], and You et al. found that noise isolation during surgery could reduce postoperative pain sensitivity based on pain pressure thresholds (PPTs) [20]. Combining the above descriptions, we hypothesise that noise isolation using professional noise-cancelling earmuffs can block the reception of exogenous noise by surgical patients, so that the noise is be received by the brain at a lower signal-to-noise ratio, reducing the processing of auditory information by the neurons in the brain, reducing pain sensitivity, and reducing postoperative pain. Moreover, the use of noise-reducing earmuffs is one of the low-cost, safe and effective ways of postoperative analgesia, and this measure did not increase the incidence of postoperative adverse reactions in patients.

Fu et al. demonstrated that the use of intraoperative noise-cancelling headphones reduced the incidence and severity of postoperative nausea and vomiting in patients undergoing laparoscopic gynaecological surgery under general anaesthesia and improved patient prognosis [35]. In contrast to previous reports, our study found no significant reduction in postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) incidence with intraoperative noise isolation. This null finding may reflect limited statistical power due to sample size constraints in assessing this secondary outcome. Additionally, while postoperative earplug use has been shown to alleviate sleep disturbances [32], Our findings demonstrate that intraoperative noise isolation specifically reduces the incidence of sleep disturbances on postoperative day 3, likely mediated by attenuated resting and movement-evoked pain, given the bidirectional relationship between pain and sleep disturbances [36]. There was no significant difference between the control group and the noise isolation group in the incidence of sleep disorders on the first night after surgery. Patients reported significantly worse sleep quality during the first postoperative night. This finding may be attributable to both residual anesthetic effects and environmental factors in the surgical ward, including elevated noise levels [37].

This study has the following limitations. Firstly, the study focused exclusively on laparoscopic procedures, which may limit the applicability of our findings to surgeries requiring specific positioning (e.g., orthopedic surgeries). The potential effects of intraoperative noise isolation on postoperative pain in such contexts remain unknown and warrant further investigation. Secondly, this trial used combined intravenous and inhalation anaesthesia, and future studies should assess the effect of noise isolation under different anaesthetic techniques on reducing postoperative pain. Thirdly, the study relied on subjective pain reporting without complementary hematological or inflammatory markers. Future research should examine whether noise isolation modulates objective physiological indicators of surgical stress and pain. Fourthly, this study was a single-centre, small sample size, randomised controlled trial, hence the need for a multi-centre study. Finally, intraoperative noise levels were not graded in this study, and the effect of different levels of noise on postoperative pain should be explored in the future.

Conclusion

In this clinically randomised trial, intraoperative noise isolation by wearing noise-cancelling earmuffs reduced the incidence of moderate-to-severe pain one to three days after laparoscopic surgery, decreased opioid dosage and the supplemental analgesia use per person per day for the first three days postoperatively. It lowered the incidence of sleep disturbances on the third postoperative day, all while maintaining a good safety profile.
