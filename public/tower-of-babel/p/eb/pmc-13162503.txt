Recurrent abdominal pain as a leading symptom for branchio-oto-renal syndrome: a case report
Authors: Alaa Senjab, Omar Al Ayoubi, Grace Tannous, Yaman Alsharef
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBranchio-oto-renal syndrome is a rare autosomal dominant disorder characterized by hearing impairment, branchial arch anomalies, preauricular pits, and renal malformations. Although recurrent abdominal pain is not a classic feature, it can arise owing to renal complications such as hydronephrosis.Case presentationAn 8-year-old Arab boy with a history of congenital sensorineural hearing loss presented to the emergency department with recurrent right flank pain radiating to the anterior abdomen occurring approximately every 2 weeks over 4 months. Previous treatments for suspected irritable bowel syndrome and enteritis were ineffective. Physical examination revealed a significant left preauricular pit and right costovertebral angle tenderness; no branchial fistula or external ear deformities were noted. Urinalysis showed moderate proteinuria, marked pyuria, and microscopic hematuria. Renal ultrasound revealed mild-to-moderate bilateral hydronephrosis, hypoechoic areas, mild pelvicalyceal dilatation, and early cystic changes, supporting the diagnosis of branchio-oto-renal syndrome. The patient was started on empirical intravenous antibiotics, and a urine culture was obtained; laboratory tests 48–72 hours after antibiotic initiation showed partial improvement in inflammatory markers and urinary sediment findings. However, serum creatinine remained mildly elevated (1.10 mg/dL), flank pain persisted, and renal ultrasound findings supported a congenital or obstructive renal anomaly. Further ear, nose, and throat and urologic evaluation were recommended; the patient was subsequently lost to follow-up.ConclusionIn children with congenital hearing loss, recurrent abdominal/flank pain should prompt renal evaluation and consideration of syndromic diagnoses such as branchio-oto-renal syndrome. Early systemic screening and genetic counseling are advised.

Background

Branchio-oto-renal (BOR) syndrome, also known as Melnick–Fraser syndrome, is a rare genetic disorder inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern [1, 2]. It has an estimated incidence of 1 in 40,000 to 1 in 70,000 individuals in the general population, but this rises to approximately 2% among children with profound hearing loss [2, 3].

BOR syndrome is most commonly associated with mutations in the EYA1 gene [3, 4]. According to Chang et al. (2004), the frequency of clinical manifestations in individuals with pathogenic EYA1 mutations included hearing loss (98.5%), preauricular pits (83.6%), branchial cleft anomalies (68.5%), renal anomalies (38.2%), and external ear anomalies (31.5%) [5, 6].

While the clinical presentation of BOR syndrome is highly variable, and may include atypical findings such as recurrent respiratory infections, persistent proteinuria, or cardiac anomalies [3, 7], recurrent abdominal pain is not classically described among the core features. However, it may occur as an indirect manifestation of underlying renal anomalies such as hydronephrosis, which has been described in several cases [8, 9].

We present a case of an 8-year-old Arab male patient with a history of congenital deafness, who presented with recurrent abdominal pain, leading to the clinical diagnosis of branchio-oto-renal syndrome.

Case presentation

We describe the case of an 8-year-old Arab male patient with a history of congenital hearing loss who presented to the emergency department with abdominal pain. The pain had been recurrent for 4 months, with a notable pattern of occurring approximately every 2 weeks. The pain was described as intermittent right flank pain radiating to the anterior abdomen. It was not colicky and not clearly related to fluid intake or micturition; however, it was intermittently associated with constipation and vomiting. There was no documented prior history of urinary retention or recurrent urinary tract infections reported by the caregivers.

The patient had been previously misdiagnosed with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) and enteritis and was treated with anti-flatulent agents and metronidazole, without symptomatic improvement. No laboratory or diagnostic investigations were performed during those prior outpatient evaluations, as management by other clinicians was empirical and symptom-based.

The patient was born to consanguineous parents (first cousins). Both parents were clinically healthy, with no reported history of hearing impairment, branchial anomalies, renal disease, or other features suggestive of branchio-oto-renal syndrome. No similar diagnoses were reported among other family members. However, no genetic testing or systematic clinical screening of first-degree relatives was performed owing to limited resources.

Hearing assessment using auditory brainstem response (ABR) testing performed at birth confirmed congenital sensorineural hearing loss. Despite this early diagnosis, no auditory rehabilitation was undertaken, and the patient did not receive any specialized audiologic follow-up. This lack of intervention may have contributed to delayed language development.

On presentation, the patient had a low-grade fever (37.8 °C) and was otherwise hemodynamically stable, with vital signs within normal limits for his age. Abdominal examination revealed right costovertebral angle tenderness. A left-sided preauricular pit was noted. No cervical (branchial) fistulae or external pinna deformities were identified. Laboratory investigations, including urinalysis, demonstrated moderate proteinuria (++), marked pyuria (++ white blood cells [WBCs]), microscopic hematuria (+ red blood cells [RBCs]), positive nitrites, and pus casts. Serum creatinine was 1.10 mg/dL and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) was 20 mg/dL. Baseline complete blood count (CBC) showed leukocytosis, and C-reactive protein (CRP) was elevated at ≈25–50 mg/L.

A renal ultrasound was subsequently performed, revealing mild-to-moderate bilateral hydronephrosis, hypoechoic areas, mild pelvicalyceal dilatation, and early cystic changes. The sonographic pattern—bilateral hydronephrosis with early cystic changes—favors an underlying congenital structural or obstructive renal anomaly. Owing to financial constraints and limited access to specialized services, confirmatory genetic testing (for example, EYA1 sequencing) and high-resolution temporal bone imaging (computed tomography [CT] or magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]) were not performed. Nevertheless, the combination of congenital sensorineural hearing loss, a preauricular pit, and structural renal anomalies meets the clinical diagnostic criteria for BOR syndrome and strongly supports the diagnosis.

The patient was started on empirical intravenous antibiotic therapy for presumed upper urinary tract infection (intravenous ceftriaxone 50 mg/kg once daily). A urine culture obtained prior to antibiotic initiation grew Escherichia coli. Follow-up laboratory tests 48–72 hours after starting antibiotics showed partial improvement, including a decrease in white blood cell count, a decline in C-reactive protein, and improvement in urine sediment. However, serum creatinine remained mildly elevated on repeat testing (1.10 mg/dL) with a borderline BUN (20 mg/dL), and the patient’s flank and abdominal pain persisted. These findings, together with the sonographic pattern of bilateral hydronephrosis with early cystic changes, support the likelihood of an underlying congenital or obstructive renal anomaly rather than solely acute kidney injury. The family was advised to complete the prescribed antibiotic course and to attend pediatric urology for further evaluation. Repeat renal ultrasound and consideration of voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG) or diuretic renography were recommended to assess for vesicoureteral reflux or ureteropelvic junction obstruction. An ear, nose, and throat (ENT) referral for audiologic assessment and potential temporal bone imaging was also arranged. Unfortunately, the patient did not return for scheduled outpatient follow-up. Discussion

Branchio-oto-renal (BOR) syndrome is most commonly associated with pathogenic variants in the EYA1 gene, first identified in 1997 and located on chromosome 8q13.3, accounting for approximately 40% of reported cases [10]. Although more than 80 distinct EYA1 mutations have been described, a clear genotype–phenotype correlation has not been established. Less frequently, mutations in SIX1, SIX5, and SALL1 have been implicated, while the underlying genetic cause remains unidentified in nearly half of affected individuals [10].

The classic clinical triad of BOR syndrome, as described by Chang et al. (2004) and reinforced by the systematic review by Biggs et al. (2022), includes hearing loss (reported in 98.5% of cases), preauricular pits (83.6%), and renal anomalies (38.2%) [6, 10, 11]. These three features are among the most consistently observed manifestations of the syndrome and serve as the cornerstone of its clinical recognition [6].

In the same study, Chang et al. (2004) proposed clinical diagnostic criteria for BOR syndrome, which are also commonly used to determine eligibility for EYA1 genetic testing; these include the presence of at least three major criteria, two major criteria with at least two minor features, or a single major criterion in the presence of an affected first-degree relative [6].

Major diagnostic criteria include: hearing loss (sensorineural, conductive, or mixed), branchial arch anomalies (such as branchial cleft cysts or fistulae), renal anomalies (including agenesis, dysplasia, hypoplasia, or hydronephrosis), preauricular pits, and external ear malformations [6].

Minor criteria encompass external ear anomalies, middle ear anomalies, inner ear malformations (for example, cochlear hypoplasia or enlarged vestibular aqueduct), preauricular tags, lacrimal duct stenosis, facial asymmetry, palatal anomalies, kidney stones, and growth retardation [6]. In this context, our patient met the clinical diagnostic criteria for BOR syndrome by fulfilling three major features—congenital sensorineural hearing loss, a preauricular pit, and structural renal anomalies—thereby strongly supporting the diagnosis. However, owing to resource limitations in a conflict-affected setting, genetic testing could not be performed, preventing confirmation of a pathogenic variant, definitive genotype–phenotype correlation, and provision of formal genetic counseling regarding familial recurrence risk.

The physical examination and renal ultrasound imaging played a crucial role in the initial detection of the preauricular pit and the underlying renal anomalies, respectively. Furthermore, congenital hearing loss can be a result of various underlying disorders causing other subtle deformities, thereby highlighting the importance of a comprehensive systemic evaluation at birth [12, 13].

Several conditions were considered in the differential diagnosis of this case. Alport syndrome is a hereditary nephropathy characterized by sensorineural hearing loss and hematuria but typically lacks preauricular pits or branchial anomalies [14]. Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) can present with bilateral renal cysts and microscopic hematuria, yet it is not associated with hearing loss or branchial defects and usually presents later in life [15]. Congenital cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection may cause sensorineural hearing loss and renal involvement but is usually accompanied by systemic features such as hepatosplenomegaly or neurodevelopmental delay [16]. The presence of the classic BOR triad—hearing loss, preauricular pit, and renal anomalies—makes BOR syndrome the most likely diagnosis in this case.

Regarding the auditory manifestations, our patient presented with congenital sensorineural hearing loss confirmed by auditory brainstem response (ABR) testing at birth. This aligns with the findings of Unzaki et al. (2018), who reported sensorineural hearing loss in 57.7% of their 101 Japanese patients, compared with 29.5% with mixed and 12.8% with conductive hearing loss [17]. While other cases, such as Cacciatori et al. (2022) in Italy, reported mixed hearing loss [18], the specific sensorineural nature in our patient underscores the phenotypic variability. Notably, the average age of BOR diagnosis in the Unzaki et al. study was 3 years, significantly earlier than our patient’s diagnosis at 8 years [17]. Despite the early identification of congenital hearing loss, the absence of auditory rehabilitation interventions or specialized audiologic follow-up likely contributed to delayed language development in our patient, emphasizing the critical need for timely and consistent multidisciplinary care in BOR syndrome.

Renal anomalies represent a major and often clinically significant component of BOR syndrome, with prevalence estimates ranging from 57% to 65% across reported cohorts. Chen et al. (1995) documented renal involvement in 65% of individuals with BOR syndrome, while Unzaki et al. (2018) observed it in 57.4% of their patient population [11, 17]. These anomalies encompass a broad morphological spectrum, including renal dysplasia, hypoplasia, agenesis (unilateral or bilateral), cyst formation, hydronephrosis, ureteral duplication, and vesicoureteral reflux (VUR). In our patient, renal ultrasound revealed bilateral hydronephrosis, mild pelvicalyceal dilatation, hypoechoic areas, and early cystic changes. These findings fall well within the documented phenotypic spectrum described by both Chen and Unzaki, solidifying the presumptive diagnosis.

However, a key distinguishing feature in our case lies in the mode of presentation of the renal involvement: While renal anomalies in the cited studies were part of the broader phenotype and not typically the presenting symptom, our patient presented primarily with recurrent abdominal pain. This persistent and often misdiagnosed symptom (initially as IBS or enteritis) prompted further investigation, which subsequently led to the identification of the underlying renal anomalies. This underscores a less commonly emphasized, yet clinically important, presentation pathway in BOR syndrome, where vague abdominal discomfort can serve as a crucial indicator of renal pathology.

Regarding functional renal involvement, Unzaki et al. (2018) reported that 38.6% of patients developed chronic kidney disease (CKD) and 12.9% progressed to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [17]. While our patient has not yet progressed to overt CKD, the mildly elevated serum creatinine (1.1 mg/dL) and borderline high blood urea nitrogen (BUN) (20 mg/dL) suggest early renal functional compromise. Furthermore, urinalysis revealed significant findings consistent with an upper urinary tract infection (UTI)—including marked pyuria, positive nitrites, positive pus casts, and microscopic hematuria—which likely exacerbated the renal pathology and contributed significantly to the patient’s symptomatology.

Management of hearing loss in BOR syndrome includes hearing aids, reported in 22 studies with variable success, with at least eight patients benefiting from hearing aids alone. Surgical interventions, reported in 28 studies, included tympanotomies, myringotomies with grommets, mastoidectomies, ossicular reconstructions, cholesteatoma removal, auricular surgery, stapedectomies, tympanoplasty, myringoplasty, and bone-anchored hearing aid (BAHA) insertion [10]. In our patient, although congenital hearing loss was present, management with hearing aids or surgical interventions was not provided, as the patient was subsequently lost to follow-up, preventing completion of audiologic and renal evaluations.

Limitations

This case report is limited by several factors, especially in the context of a conflict-affected and resource-constrained setting. Definitive genetic testing (for example, EYA1 sequencing) and high-resolution temporal bone imaging were not performed owing to limited resources, the high cost of these modalities, and lack of health insurance coverage. Higher-resolution ultrasound images were unavailable owing to equipment and archival limitations; however, the images provided are sufficient to demonstrate the key findings relevant to this case. Longitudinal renal follow-up was incomplete as the patient was lost to follow-up; his family resides in a region outside the control of the Syrian government, where access to specialized medical services is severely limited. Consequently, repeat imaging (for example, renal ultrasound [US], VCUG, or diuretic renography) and systematic clinical or genetic screening of first-degree relatives were not performed. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this report.

Conclusion

This case highlights the crucial diagnostic value of recurrent, nonspecific abdominal symptoms in uncovering renal manifestations of BOR syndrome. It also strongly reinforces the importance of systemic evaluation in newborns with congenital hearing loss to discover any associated abnormality. This is vital for early detection, appropriate management, and potentially, prevention of long-term complications.
