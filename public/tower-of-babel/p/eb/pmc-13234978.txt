Biphasic energy dependent effects of 650 nm diode laser photobiomodulation on orthodontic tooth movement and compression zone bone remodeling in vivo
Authors: Ke Zhang, Fei Liu, Yumei Zeng, Yaping Qi, Ying Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe dose-dependent associations between laser irradiation, orthodontic tooth movement, and alveolar bone remodeling remain unclear.ObjectiveThe present study aimed to investigate the differential photobiomodulation (PBM) effects of diode laser with low-level laser and high-level laser on orthodontic tooth movement (OTM) and alveolar bone remodeling on the compression zone.MethodologyA split-mouth design OTM model of rats was established. By varying the irradiation duration of a diode laser, wavelength of 650 nm, one accelerating OTM group with five different energy densities subgroups and one inhibiting OTM group with five different subgroups were obtained, respectively. The best accelerating/inhibiting efficacy of OTM was achieved by comparing the OTM distance of different subgroups with micro-CT. The osteoclastic level on the compression zone in the best accelerating/inhibiting subgroup was observed by Hematoxylin-eosin (HE) and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining, while the expression levels of receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand (RANKL), osteoprotegerin (OPG), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2), and osteocalcin (OCN) were individually explored by real-time PCR and immunohistochemical staining.ResultsThe maximum accelerating efficiency of OTM in the 6.7 J/cm2 subgroup was 36.3%, in which the lacunae and osteoclasts of the compression zone were increased on 7d, 14d (P < 0.05), and the expressions of RANKL, ALP, RUNX2, and OCN were upregulated and the expression of OPG was downregulated. The optimal inhibiting efficiency of OTM in the 106.7 J/cm² subgroup was 34.9%, in which the lacunae and osteoclasts of the compression zone were reduced on 7d, 14d (P < 0.05), and the expressions of RANKL, ALP, RUNX2, and OCN were downregulated and the expression of OPG was upregulated. Vertical alveolar bone height loss and orthodontic tooth loosening was observed in the subgroups with energy density over 123.3 J/cm² (P < 0.01).ConclusionsDifferential energy density laser exerted a biphasic effect on OTM which optimal parameters of 6.7 J/cm² could accelerate OTM of 36.3%, and 106.7 J/cm² could decelerate OTM of 34.9%, respectively. The osteoclast/osteoblast activity on the compression zone was promoted /inhibited.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08102-2.

Background

The long-term process of orthodontic treatment is a dominant driver of reducing the patients’ compliance and increasing risk of caries, tooth demineralization [1], periodontal disease, and root resorption [2]. Management to accelerate orthodontic tooth movement (OTM) and shorten the treatment duration while ensuring the efficacy of orthodontic treatment has become a hot topic among Orthodontists [3, 4]. Currently, drug injection [5], corticotomy [6], PAOO [7], ultrasonic vibration [8, 9], and extracorporeal shock wave therapy [10], etc. have been developed to accelerate OTM. Hence, physical approaches such as ultrasound vibration come with discomfort, and invasive methods (drug injection, corticotomy) make it unacceptable to patients. In 2000, Kawasaki [11] first introduced laser irradiation to accelerate OTM in rats, suggesting that low-level laser could accelerate OTM, which attracted widespread attention from orthodontists. Subsequently, numerous studies reported that low-level laser also appeared with several advantages of non-invasive, suppression of root resorption, and alleviation of orthodontic pain [4, 12, 13].

Low-level laser therapy (LLLT), actually referring to low energy density laser, also known as Photobiomodulation (PBM), has been widely used in the medical field. Studies [14] showed that the dose range of the low-level laser is 0.01–10 J/cm². In the field of orthodontics, a large number of cytological studies [15], animal experiments [16–18], and clinical trials [19–21] have confirmed that LLLT could accelerate OTM. However, the energy density of diode laser in present studies is scattered, (1–10 J/cm² in cytology studies; 2.5–1600 J/cm² in animal experiments [17, 18, 22–24]; 2.25–144 J/cm² in clinical study [20, 21, 25]. At the same time, Kim [26] reported diode laser with 0.83 J/cm² did not accelerate OTM. Several studies [18, 27–29] indicated that high-energy density laser (hereinafter referring as high-level laser, corresponding to low-level laser, but there is no consensus study on the range of high energy density) decelerates OTM, which has not been paid attention by orthodontists. This finding is consistent with Arndt-Schulz’s law proposed by German pharmacologist Hugo Schulz and physicist Rudolf Arndt in the late 19th century. In 1923, this law was further confirmed by Martius [14] “Weak stimuli slightly accelerate vital activity, stronger stimuli raise it further, but a peak is reached and even stronger stimuli suppress it, until a negative response is finally achieved.” However, the energy density of the present studies varies greatly, and there is no consensus on the optimal working parameters of laser modulating OTM. The efficiency of OTM is various under different working parameters. Can laser inhibition of tooth movement become a new method of anchorage control? At present, additional devices such as transpalatal arch, Nance arch, and implant anchorage are commonly used to enhance anchorage. Whether the efficacy of laser in inhibiting OTM can be verified and become a new technology for non-invasive anchorage control still needs further study.

We hypothesized that differential energy densities of diode laser can accelerate and decelerate OTM with different parameters in a rat model. To test our hypothesis, we used a multimodal approach including micro-CT, HE staining, TRAP staining, Real-time-PCR, and immunohistochemistry to explore the modulation of OTM and bone remodeling of alveolar bone on the compression zone.

Materials and methods

OTM models in rats

153 6-week-old male Wistar rats weighing 180 ± 15 g were purchased from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology (Beijing, China), raised and operated at the Animal Center of Xuanwu Hospital, Capital Medical University according to ARRIVE guidelines [30]. The animal experiment in this study was approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee of Capital Medical University (No. XW-20220511-1). Animals were housed two per cage under a 12-hour light/dark cycle, with ad libitum access to food and water. All animals were raised for 1 week before experiment began.

A split-mouth design OTM model of rats was established. All rats received intraperitoneal injections of pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg) before orthodontic force was applied. After anesthesia, a 0.2 mm stainless steel wire was placed around the neck of the bilateral maxillary first molar and upper incisor, then a 50 g nickel-titanium closed-coiling spring (TOMY, Japan) was used to connect the bilateral maxillary first molar and incisor and pull the first molar mesially as an orthodontic tooth. No animals were excluded from the experiment (Fig. 1A, B).

Groups and protocols of laser irradiation

Two parts were designed in this experiment as follow:

Part 1: Screening the optimal parameter to accelerate/inhibit OTM.

72 rats were randomly assigned to an Accelerating OTM group (n = 30), an Inhibiting OTM group (n = 30), and two corresponding control groups (n = 6 per control group). Sample size was determined using PASS 15.0 software based on pilot OTM data. In the pilot study, mean tooth OTM was 0.72 ± 0.05 mm on the laser side and 0.42 ± 0.09 mm on the control side. With a two-sided significance level of α = 0.05 and 80% power (1 − β = 0.80), a minimum of six animals per subgroup was required for the split-mouth design.

The Accelerating OTM group and the Inhibiting OTM group contain five different subgroups separately, which were obtained by varying the irradiation duration of a custom-made 650 nm semiconductor laser system (Beijing M.Y. Epoch Biotech Co., Ltd.) on the laser side of rats (Tables 1 and 2). The output power was calibrated using a power meter before each irradiation session. All animals were sacrificed on 14d after orthodontic force was applied, and orthodontic tooth movement was assessed using micro-CT. Intra- and inter-observer reliability for OTM measurements were assessed using ICC (> 0.85).

Groups and treatment

The irradiated gingiva and surrounding oral mucosa were examined at each session, and no laser-related mucosal lesions were observed. A preliminary experiment was performed to screen high-level laser parameters for inhibiting orthodontic tooth movement, testing energy densities of 30–90 J/cm² until inhibiting effect observed. The results were used solely for parameter selection and were not included in the statistical analysis.

Part 2: Alveolar bone remodeling in the compression zone by optimal parameter laser.

81 rats were assigned to control, accelerating, and inhibiting groups (n = 27 per group) and analyzed on 7d, 14d, 21d. HE, TRAP, and IHC analyses used six animals per group, while qPCR used three animals per group per time point, as determined by a priori power analysis. The laser irradiation doses for the accelerating and inhibiting groups were 6.7 J/cm² and 106.7 J/cm², respectively, as screened in Part I, and the irradiation protocol was the same as that described in Part I.

The efficiency of accelerating / inhibiting and alveolar bone height loss

All rats were sacrificed by overdose of anesthesia. The maxilla in part 1, containing incisors and molars, was removed and trimmed, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution at 4 ℃ for 24 h. Specimens in the fixing period were digitized by a micro-CT scanner (SkyScan1276, Bruker, Belgium). The images were reoriented in DataViewer software to align the axis of the occlusal plane of molars, measured the distance between the first and second molar as OTM distance, and calculated the efficiency of accelerating/inhibiting OTM.

The efficiency of accelerating/inhibiting = the difference in OTM distance between the control side and the laser side/OTM distance on the control side. Additionally, D1-D5 subgroups were designed to develop an evaluation of safety by measuring the alveolar bone height loss in the mesial side of the mesial root. Adverse events were monitored throughout the experiment. Tooth loosening was defined as markedly increased tooth mobility with impaired occlusion and extensive root exposure; animals showing severe tooth loosening or obvious distress were withdrawn from further laser treatment and managed according to institutional animal care guidelines. Orthodontic tooth loosening was qualitatively assessed by intraoral examination on 14d after force application and graded based on the direction of mobility (Grades I–III; criteria provided in the Supplementary Materials). Vertical alveolar bone height loss was quantitatively evaluated using micro-CT (Fig. 1C).

Histological analysis

Specimens of the maxilla in part 2 were decalcified in 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) at 25℃ for 6 weeks on a horizontal shaker. Additionally, specimens were washed with current water, dehydrated in graded alcohol baths, and cleared in xylene. Furthermore, specimens were embedded in paraffin and cut into slices of 5 μm by a microtome (Leica, RM2055, Germany) in a direction parallel to the long axis of the mesial root. The sections were dried at 60℃ for 24 h, deparaffinized, and hydrated in xylene and graded alcohol baths. The sections were then subjected to HE staining using a HE staining kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China) and TRAP staining using a TRAP staining kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China). The sections were scanned and transformed into images by a Digital Pathology slide Scanner (Leica, Aperio GT450, Germany). Periodontal fibers and lacunae of the compression zone were observed on the digital HE staining images. The number of TRAP-positive cells, considered osteoclasts on the digital TRAP staining images. The mesial cervical 1/2 region of the orthodontic tooth was considered as the compression zone because of the mesial tilting movement of the first molar observed by micro-CT.

Gene expression detection

The gene expressions of RANKL, OPG, ALP, OCN, and RUNX2 were tested. Messenger RNA (mRNA) was extracted with Trizol (TaKaRa, Shiga, Japan), reverse transcribed into cDNA by a PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix kit (RR036A, TaKaRa, Japan), and amplified by a SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ II kit(RR820A, TaKaRa, Japan). The primer sequences are shown in Table 3. Relative gene expression was detected by quantitative real-time PCR using a Roche Light Cycler® 480 Sequence Detection System (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) and calculated by the 2−ΔΔ Ct method. Each sample was assayed in triplicate.

Protein expression detection

The protein expressions of RANKL, OPG, and ALP were tested. For immunochemical staining, 3% hydrogen peroxidase was dropped on the sections for 15 min to inactivate endogenous peroxidase. Then the sections were soaked and heated in a 0.01 M sodium citrate solution to repair the antigen. The following primary antibodies were used: RANKL (Proteintech, China,1:250), OPG (ThermoFisher, the USA, 1:100), and ALP (ThermoFisher, the USA, 1:100). Specimens were incubated overnight at 4 ℃. A goat anti-rabbit IgG HRP pre-adsorbed secondary antibody (Abcam, the UK, 1:500) was applied for 30 min. Protein expression was calculated by OD value using the ImageJ 15.0 software. Identical regions of interest (ROIs; 1565 × 1565 pixels) were selected within the mesial cervical half of the orthodontic tooth root on the compression zone, and mean optical density (OD) was measured. Image acquisition settings were consistent, no additional thresholding was applied, and raw OD values were used without normalization.

All outcome assessments (micro-CT, histology, qPCR, and IHC optical density measurements) were performed in a blinded manner, with samples coded by an independent researcher until completion of data analysis.

Statistics analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 29.0 (IBM, USA). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For Part 1 (split-mouth design), comparisons between the laser side and the contralateral control side within the same animals were analyzed using two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). Bonferroni correction was applied for post hoc multiple comparisons. For Part 2, comparisons among experimental groups at each time point (7, 14, and 21 days) were conducted using one-way ANOVA, followed by Bonferroni post hoc tests when appropriate. All data met the assumptions for parametric analysis. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and a two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Orthodontic tooth movement measurements

For experiment 1, the OTM in the A2 and A3 subgroups laser sides showed significantly higher values than those on the control side (A2: 0.40 ± 0.09 mm vs. 0.30 ± 0.05 mm, A3: 0.42 ± 0.09 mm vs. 0.31 ± 0.04 mm). The accelerating efficiency was 34.8%, 36.3%, respectively. Thus, the optimal energy density for accelerating OTM was 6.7 J/cm². Similar increases were observed in subgroups A1(0.39 ± 0.08 mm vs. 0.29 ± 0.08 mm), A4 (0.44 ± 0.16 mm vs. 0.34 ± 0.13 mm), and A5 (0.45 ± 0.14 mm vs. 0.34 ± 0.11 mm) (all n = 6 per subgroup).

In contrast, the OTM in the D1-D5 subgroups laser side were significantly lower than those on the control side. The OTM values were 0.30 ± 0.09 mm vs. 0.42 ± 0.05 mm in D1, 0.28 ± 0.04 mm vs. 0.43 ± 0.05 mm in D2, 0.29 ± 0.07 mm vs. 0.46 ± 0.09 mm in D3, 0.28 ± 0.06 mm vs. 0.47 ± 0.08 mm in D4, and 0.29 ± 0.07 mm vs. 0.48 ± 0.04 mm in D5 (all n = 6). The inhibiting efficiency was 30.7%, 34.9%, 36.7%, 38.2%, and 37.2% respectively. In addition, the tooth mobility of the laser side was increased compared to the control side in D2-D5 as qualitatively observed Fig. 2A, B.

Alveolar bone height loss

There was no significant difference in the vertical bone loss of the mesial alveolar bone between the laser side and the control side in the subgroups D1 and D2 (P > 0.05). The alveolar bone height loss on the laser side in subgroups D3-D5 was significantly higher than that on the control side (P < 0.01) (Fig. 2C). Thus, the optimal energy density for inhibiting OTM was 106.7 J/cm².

Histological analysis via HE staining

Compared with the control side, the arrangement of periodontal ligament fibers of the compression zone on the laser side was more disordered than that in groups A and D on 7d, 14d, and 21d. The number of osteoclast lacunae of the laser compression zone on the laser side was higher than that on the control side in group A and lower in group D on 7d, 14d, and 21d. The histology observation on the control side of Group A and Group D was basically the same as Group C (Fig. 3).

For each sample, one sagittal section at a standardized anatomical level including the full length of the mesial root was selected. TRAP-positive cells located in the cervical half of the mesial root on the pressure side were counted across the entire region. The absolute number of TRAP-positive cells was recorded without additional normalization (Fig. 4). Six samples were analyzed per group (n = 6 sections).

Gene expression analysis

Compared with the control side, the expression levels of RANKL on the laser side were significantly increased in group A and decreased in group D on 7d, 14d, and 21d (P < 0.05); correspondingly, the expression levels of OPG on the laser side was significantly decreased in group A and increased in group D on 7d, 14d, and 21d (P < 0.05); the ratio of RANKL/OPG on the laser side was significantly increased in group A on 7d (P < 0.01). Post hoc comparisons showed a significant increase in the RANKL/OPG ratio on the laser-treated side on 7d under the accelerating condition, whereas no significant differences were observed at later time points. In contrast, the inhibitory condition exhibited a consistent downward trend in the RANKL/OPG ratio on the laser-treated side that did not reach statistical significance, likely due to limited sample size and inter-individual variability. In group A, the expression level of ALP on the laser side was significantly higher than that on the control side on 7d (P < 0.01); the expression of RUNX2 and OCN on the laser side was increased on 14d (P < 0.05). In group D, the relative expression of ALP on the laser side was significantly decreased than that on the control side on 7d (P < 0.01), and the expressions of ALP, RUNX2, and OCN on the laser side were decreased on 14d (P < 0.05). There was no significant difference in the relative expressions of ALP, RUNX2, and OCN between the control and laser side on 21d (P > 0.05) (Fig. 5).

Discussions

LLLT in accelerating OTM, following Kawasaki’s first report, is widely studied. Numerous studies [16–18, 24, 31, 32] have confirmed LLLT increases OTM, with significant heterogeneity in parameters (2.5–23 J/cm² in rats). Notably, in several studies which comparing the accelerating effect among different parameters [26, 27], we find that any higher level laser density with a scattered reported range and lacking rigorous experimental design not only fails to accelerate OTM, but decelerates it. This phenomenon might be consistent with Arndt-Schulz’s law “Weak stimuli slightly accelerate vital activity, stronger stimuli raise it further, but a peak is reached and even stronger stimuli suppress it, until a negative response is finally achieved.” This OTM inhibition effect by higher level laser could become a new method of controlling anchorage. In previous studies [16–18, 24, 31, 32], the energy density range of low-level laser exhibits heterogeneity and thus cannot fully conform to the LLLT window from 0.01 J/cm² to 10 J/cm² proposed by Arndt-Schulz’s law [14]. Therefore, we designed this experiment to explore the optimal energy densities.

In this research, we have embarked on an exploration to investigate whether laser with differential energy densities possess the capability to regulate OTM. We aim to identify the optimal laser parameters for accelerating or inhibiting OTM. The effect of a diode laser in modulating OTM is primarily related to its wavelength and parameters, including output power and energy densities. The biological effects of diode lasers on orthodontic tooth movement are closely related to wavelength and irradiation parameters. Previous studies have reported that red-light wavelengths around 630–660 nm exhibit greater osteogenic and bone remodeling potential than near-infrared wavelengths. Tani [33] reported that 635 nm laser plays a better role in bone generation than 808 nm and 435 nm, and Yang [24] found that 660 nm laser to promote bone remodeling better than 830 nm. Based on these findings, we selected 650 nm wavelength diode laser to compare the effect on OTM of different energy densities by a self-controlled study, and explore the level of bone remodeling on the compression zone by examining the histological changes of the periodontal tissue, the expression levels of bone remodeling-related genes and proteins after laser irradiation. Orthodontic force application induces responses and promotes bone resorption on the compression zone.

Low-level laser with Optimal Parameters Accelerates OTM

The accelerating effect of LLLT has been generally verified [16]. However, the optimal parameters of laser for modulating OTM and bone remodeling are less well established. In the present study, 3.3 J/cm², 5 J/cm², 6.7 J/cm², 8.3 J/cm² and 10 J/cm² was observed and 6.7 J/cm² has been verified the best efficiency of accelerating OTM of 36.3%, basically consistent with the result of other studies ranging from 19.3% to 31.6% [24, 31, 32]. Diode laser energy densities ranging from 5 to 23 J/cm² (such as 2015, Franzen [34], 23 J/cm²; 2019, Yang [24], 5 J/cm²; 2021, Guan [35], 10 J/cm²; 2024, Zhong [36], 8.8 J/cm²), had been proved to accelerate OTM, which is consistent with our results. We also conducted further observations on the changes in osteoclasts, genes and proteins after laser irradiation at 6.7 J/cm², confirming that the optimal laser parameters can enhance the efficacy of orthodontic tooth movement by accelerating bone remodeling. Notably, previous micro-CT–based evidence obtained using the same animal model has demonstrated that appropriately optimized photobiomodulation can accelerate orthodontic tooth movement without increasing root resorption, particularly in the tension zone [37].

In the present study, it was observed that the periodontal ligament of the compression zone on the laser side was narrower than that on the control side, and the number of bone lacunae and TRAP-positive cells on the surface of alveolar bone on the compression zone increased under the low-level laser irradiation. These results indicate that low-level laser may upregulate the activation of osteoclasts, which is similar to previous studies [38, 39]. Previous studies have shown that a variety of bone remodeling pathways could affect OTM [40–42]. Among them, the RANKL/RANK/OPG pathway [43–45] has been widely studied and verified to be related to orthodontic bone remodeling. ALP [46], RUNX2 [47] and OCN [48] are osteogenesis-related factors, representing early to late osteogenesis stages, which can be used to explore the effect of laser on osteogenesis. Given the effect on bone metabolism, exploring the differential effect of OTM may provide some value for clinical orthodontic treatment.

The results of real-time PCR and immunohistochemistry showed that low-level laser upregulated the expression of RANKL, ALP, RUNX2, and OCN, and reduced the expression of OPG, which is consistent with previous studies [24, 49]. However, the result is in contradiction with the finding that the laser could not modulate OPG expression during orthodontics [16]. Thus, we speculate that this effect may be caused by the difference in laser irradiation intervals between two studies. The ALP modulating effects were significant on 7d and 14d, while the RUNX2 and OCN were significant on 14d, which is consistent with existing reports [38, 50]. This temporal pattern may be related to ALP is considered to be an early marker of osteogenic differentiation, and the difference can be observed 7 days after orthodontic force loading in the rat model. RUNX2 is an osteoblast-specific transcription factor, which can activate and initiate the differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells into osteoblasts and regulate the maturation of osteoblasts. OCN is a specific product of osteoblast activity and is regarded as a marker of osteoblast maturation, so it is usually expressed at a later stage. The expressions of protein were delayed by one week compared to gene expression. Collectively, these findings suggest that low-level laser irradiation is associated with dynamic molecular changes related to bone remodeling processes.

High-level laser with Optimal Parameters Decelerates OTM

More importantly, a few studies have shown that high-level laser did not accelerate OTM, even decelerate it. In the present study, a 650 nm diode laser at 106.7 J/cm², administered every other day, resulted in fewer osteoclast lacunae on the irradiated side than controls at 7, 14, and 21 days via HE staining, indicating inhibited osteoclast activity. In 2006, Goulart [28] conducted a study using 780 nm diode lasers (70 mW, 25 J/cm²) on dogs resulting with an inhibiting efficacy of 45.7% on 21d, which is generally consistent with our findings of 34.9%. In 2008, Kim [26] found that 0.83 J/cm² neither accelerated nor inhibited OTM. The possible reason might be that the energy density used was relatively low, with laser irradiation applied only once daily during the first 7 days with a relatively short irradiation duration in the animal model, and thus failed to exert a substantial biological effect on periodontal tissues. In 2020, Karabel [18] used a 980 nm diode laser with total energies of 72 J and 54 J on two rat groups. Their results showed increased osteoclast activity in the 54 J group and decreased activity in the 72 J group, however, energy density values were not provided. However, In 2020, Marquezan [29] found that 830 nm diode laser with 6000 J/cm² energy density, applied daily for 7 days, increased osteoclasts. While our findings align with Karabel’s, direct comparison is hindered by unknown energy densities in their study. The discrepancy with Marquezan’s results may stem from substantial differences in applied laser energy densities. In these experiments, the energy densities employed by diode lasers vary significantly, and a consensus conclusion has yet to be reached.

In the pre-experiment of this study, energy densities of 30 J/cm², 60 J/cm² and 90 J/cm² were set up to explore the inhibiting OTM effect under laser irradiation, the inhibiting effect appeared when energy density reached 90 J/cm². Later, for the purpose of screening the optimal inhibiting parameter, the self-control design was keeping on to compare the inhibiting efficacy of several groups’ parameters (90 J/cm², 106.7 J/cm², 123.3 J/cm², 140.0 J/cm² and 156.7 J/cm²), and it was confirmed that the inhibiting OTM efficiency of 106.7 J/cm² -140.0 J/cm² laser reached 34.9%-38.2%. But the significantly greater degrees of tooth loosening compared to the control side occurred by laser irradiated when energy densities above 123.3 J/cm². Micro-CT revealed the vertical height loss of alveolar bone around the orthodontic tooth, suggesting that the use of excessively high-energy-lasers may pose safety risks. While exploring the inhibiting effects of high-level laser on OTM, an evaluation of the safety profile of high-level laser was also our focus.

We further observed the changes in osteoclasts, genes and proteins after energy density of 106.7 J/cm² laser irradiation, the results of real-time PCR and immunohistochemistry showed that downregulated the expression of RANKL, and increased the expression of OPG, ALP, RUNX2, and OCN after high-level laser irradiation. The gene modulating effects of ALP were significant on 7d and 14d, while the RUNX2 and OCN were significant on 14d. It has been verified that high-level laser irradiation is associated with inhibited alveolar bone remodeling and a concomitant deceleration of OTM. This inhibitory effect also has a time sequence. Considering both inhibiting efficacy and safety, the energy density of 106.7 J/cm² was determined as optimal for suppressing OTM. Notably, translational limitations exist between rodent models and humans due to differences in alveolar bone microarchitecture and remodeling dynamics, which may lead to exaggerated biological responses in rodents. Therefore, irradiation parameters effective in animal models cannot be directly translated to clinical orthodontic practice, highlighting the need for further clinical validation.

Unfortunately, a limitation of this study is the absence of observations regarding the gene and protein levels under higher energy density laser irradiation that causes orthodontic tooth loosening. Consequently, it remains inconclusive whether a further reduction in the level of bone remodeling is responsible for tooth loosening. Further in-depth research is warranted to address this gap.

Conclusions

We confirmed that the biphasic modulation of orthodontic tooth movement was achieved using a 650 nm diode laser operating at an output power of 30 mW in continuous-wave mode, with an energy density of 6.7 J/cm² for accelerating OTM and 106.7 J/cm² for inhibiting OTM. Differential energy laser may modulate bone remodeling by upregulating/downregulating bone remodeling-related genes and proteins. In-depth mechanism and the safety issues of high-level lasers still require further investigation.
