Efficacy of a high-protein food for special medical purposes in midlife and older adults at risk of malnutrition: a multicenter, randomized, active-controlled clinical study
Authors: Wenqiang Wang, Yingfei Diao, Keming Zhang, Zhongping Yang, Biao Liu, Wenhui Ye, Ruomei Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate the effect of an Intervention Food for Special Medical Purposes (FSMP) on the nutritional status and muscle function of middle-aged and older adults at risk of malnutrition.DesignA multicenter, randomized, active-controlled, parallel-group study.SettingTwo clinical centers located in China.Participants135 adults aged 45–80 years at risk of malnutrition (Mini Nutritional Assessment [MNA] score ≤ 23.5).Intervention and measurementsParticipants were randomized to one of three groups for a 12-week nutritional intervention: Intervention FSMP (one daily sachet of a novel high-protein FSMP plus dietetic counseling); Active Control (one daily sachet of an established FSMP plus dietetic counseling); and Control (dietetic counseling alone). The primary outcome was the change in MNA score. Secondary outcomes were body weight, handgrip strength, calf and mid-upper arm circumferences, and SARC-F score.ResultsLinear mixed-effects models showed significant group-by-time interactions for MNA score, body weight, BMI, handgrip strength, and calf circumference. At week 12, both FSMP groups demonstrated greater improvements in MNA score compared with Control (Intervention FSMP: adjusted difference 2.3, 95% CI 1.4–3.2; Active Control: adjusted difference 3.1, 95% CI 2.2–4.0; both P < 0.001). The between-FSMP comparison for MNA was not statistically significant (adjusted difference −0.8, 95% CI −1.7 to 0.1; P = 0.087). The Intervention FSMP group showed greater gains in body weight than Control (adjusted difference 1.2 kg, 95% CI 0.8–1.7; P < 0.001). Compared with Control, handgrip strength improved in both FSMP groups (adjusted differences 2.2–3.0 kg for left hand and 2.8 kg for right hand; all P < 0.001). Across groups, no significant differences were noted in adverse events and gastrointestinal tolerability.ConclusionTargeted 12-week nutritional intervention with a high-protein FSMP was associated with improvements in nutritional status and handgrip strength in adults at risk of malnutrition.

Introduction

Malnutrition is a major yet frequently under-recognized public health problem in middle-aged and older adults. It is associated with increased risks of frailty, functional decline, hospitalization, and mortality, and its prevalence is expected to rise as populations age [1]. Community-based epidemiological studies show that about 18%–20% of older adults are either malnourished or at risk of malnutrition, and this prevalence is even higher in hospital and long-term care settings [1,2]. In China, more than 25% of adults over 60 years of age are at risk of malnutrition, particularly those with chronic illnesses or undergoing rehabilitation [3].

Dietary optimization is an important component of the prevention and management of a wide range of health conditions [4]. When individuals are unable to meet their nutritional needs solely with food intake, nutritional supplements, designed as Food for Special Medical Purposes (FSMP), fill an essential gap [5]. These products may improve nutritional status, nutrient intake, and weight gain [6,7]. FSMP products in China are governed and regulated by the National Food Safety Standard of China, GB 29922, which is compatible with international standards of medical foods (EU, USA) for additional regulatory oversight [8].

Although clinical attention has traditionally focused on older adults, nutritional gaps and early muscle decline may begin in midlife, commonly defined as 45–65 years of age. Higher protein intake during midlife has been associated with better maintenance of physical function and lower risk of frailty in later life [9]. This stage also coincides with early anabolic resistance and hormonal shifts that may accelerate muscle loss [[10], [11], [12]]. Therefore, closing energy and protein gaps during midlife and early older age may represent an important strategy to delay or mitigate the progression toward frailty and sarcopenia [13].

The European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism and other international bodies recommend oral nutritional supplements (ONS) as adjuncts to dietetic counseling and medical therapy for adults at nutritional risk [14,15]. While the benefits of ONS in frail older adults are well documented, evidence in midlife adults and relatively stable outpatients at nutritional risk remains limited [9,16,17]. This gap is especially relevant for early nutritional intervention with high-protein FSMP in individuals who may have poor adherence to dietetic counseling alone.

We therefore conducted this multicenter, randomized, active-controlled trial to evaluate the effects of a novel high-protein FSMP on nutritional status and skeletal muscle function in adults aged 45–80 years at nutritional risk.

Materials and methods

Study design and participant recruitment

This two-center, randomized, age-stratified, parallel-group clinical study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the ethics committees of Tianjin Hospital (2020–YLS–124) and the First Teaching Hospital of Tianjin University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TYLL2020 [K]–055). The study was registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2000040169). Outpatients aged 45–80 years were eligible for inclusion if they were at risk of malnutrition, defined as a Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA) score ≤23.5, and had adequate oral intake capacity. Exclusion criteria included severe hepatic or renal dysfunction, dependence on parenteral nutrition, known allergies to study products, pregnancy or lactation, and participation in another clinical trial within the previous 3 months. After providing written informed consent, eligible participants were enrolled and assigned to one of three study arms: Intervention FSMP, Active Control, or Control.

Interventions

The study included three 12-week intervention arms: (i) The Intervention FSMP Group: Participants received one daily sachet (117.4 g of powder) of the locally manufactured, commercially available FSMP product (Xin Huo®, Inner Mongolia Yili Industrial Group Co., Ltd., China), providing 500 kcal and 20.1 g of protein as part of a complete nutritional formulation. (ii) Active Control Group: Participants received one daily sachet (116.2 g of powder) of the established FSMP product (Ensure®, Abbott Laboratories, USA), also providing 500 kcal/day with 18.5 g of protein. The products were matched primarily on daily energy provision rather than complete nutrient equivalence and were therefore not nutritionally identical, differing modestly in protein content, powder weight, and other formulation components, including taurine and dietary fiber where indicated in the available product information. Detailed product composition is provided in Supplementary Table S1. (iii) Control Group: Participants received dietetic counseling only. All three groups received the same dietetic counseling from a registered dietitian, aimed at achieving a total intake of ∼30 kcal/kg/day and 1.0–1.2 g protein/kg/day. Counseling focused on practical dietary strategies and was delivered at baseline and follow-up visits, with each consultation lasting approximately 30 min. Participants in the two FSMP groups were instructed to consume one sachet daily in two servings, taken between meals. No structured exercise program was prescribed as part of the study, and participants were asked to maintain their usual lifestyle and habitual physical activity throughout the 12-week intervention. Habitual physical activity was assessed using the Physical Activity Scale for the Elderly (PASE) [18] at baseline, week 4, and week 12.

Adherence assessment

To ensure uniform tracking of supplements taken during the 12-week intervention period, we reconciled participant logs with sachet counts and biweekly dietitian phone calls. Adherence was measured as the number of days supplements were consumed over 84 days. Participants were classified as having “good adherence” when they reached the 80% cut-off (67 days or more). The control group did not receive supplements and was therefore excluded from these adherence calculations.

Randomization and blinding

Participants were randomized to Intervention FSMP, Active Control, or Control using permuted blocks with stratification by age category (45–56, 57–68, and 69–80 years). Allocation was concealed using sealed, opaque envelopes and was revealed only after baseline assessments were completed.

Because the Control group did not receive a supplemental product, participant blinding was not feasible in this study. Investigators were aware of treatment allocation; however, outcome measurements were performed by assessors who remained blinded to group assignment throughout the 12-week follow-up to reduce the risk of measurement bias.

Outcomes

Assessment overview

To measure the impact of the interventions on nutritional status and muscle function, assessments were conducted at three different time points: baseline (week 0), week 4, and week 12. All assessments were performed by the same qualified researchers and conducted according to standardized SOPs to ensure uniformity and reliability of the data.

Primary outcome

The primary endpoint was the change in Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA) score between baseline and week 12. The main planned comparisons were Intervention FSMP versus Control and Active Control versus Control. The comparison between Intervention FSMP and Active Control was considered exploratory. Given the wide age range of enrolled adults, MNA was used as an indicator of overall nutritional status rather than as an age-specific diagnostic tool or a single-domain functional measure. Randomization was stratified by age category, and subgroup analyses were pre-specified to evaluate whether the intervention effects differed across age strata.

Secondary outcomes

The key secondary outcomes included body weight, body mass index (BMI), handgrip strength (HGS), fat-free mass (FFM), risk of sarcopenia (via the SARC-F questionnaire) [19], and calf and mid-upper arm circumferences (CC, MUAC). Standardized procedures were followed for each of these. Body weight and height were measured using calibrated electronic scales and stadiometers (under fasting conditions). FFM was assessed by bioelectrical impedance analysis using the InBody770 device. Measurements were performed according to the manufacturer’s standard procedures, including fasting assessment, prior bladder and bowel emptying, avoidance of recent vigorous exercise, a brief standing rest before testing, and standardized hand and foot positioning. The device uses a four-compartment body composition model, and whole-body fat-free mass was used as the primary body-composition outcome. Handgrip strength was measured with a calibrated handheld dynamometer; two trials were performed on each side, and the maximum value was used for analysis. SARC-F scores were obtained using the standard questionnaire. Primary and secondary efficacy outcomes were analyzed longitudinally using repeated-measures linear mixed-effects models; observed values and changes from baseline were summarized descriptively at each visit.

Safety monitoring

Throughout the study, adverse events (AEs) and serious adverse events (SAEs) were evaluated individually and categorized by cause and severity. Biochemical and hematological parameters were collected as part of safety monitoring, and abnormal findings were reviewed clinically when necessary. Because these variables are multifactorial and not specific indicators of nutritional efficacy, they were analyzed descriptively rather than treated as primary or key secondary efficacy outcomes in this short-term intervention.

Sample size calculation

The determination of the sample size was based on the change in the MNA score from baseline to week 12. As no previous three-arm trial with the same design was available, the sample size estimation was based on the primary comparison between one intervention group and the control group. According to previous randomized controlled evidence [20,21] and our preliminary observations in the target population, the expected mean difference in MNA score improvement was set at 2.8, with a pooled standard deviation (SD) of 3.4. Using PASS 15.0 software (NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, UT, USA), 32 participants per group were required to achieve 90% statistical power at a two-sided significance level of 0.05. Assuming a 20% dropout rate, the target sample size was increased to 40 participants per group. Because this was a three-arm parallel trial, the total planned sample size was 120 participants.

Statistical analysis

The analysis of efficacy was restricted to the modified intention-to-treat (mITT) population, which consisted of all randomly assigned participants with at least one post-baseline assessment. Participants with no post-baseline outcome data were excluded from this analysis set. For sensitivity analyses, efficacy outcomes were also evaluated in the per-protocol (PP) population. PP inclusion required evaluable week-12 outcome data and no major protocol deviations. In the two FSMP groups, major protocol deviations also included inadequate supplement adherence (<80% of planned intake). Because the control group did not receive supplementation, this adherence criterion was not applicable to that group. For the baseline characteristics, the summary was done for the intention-to-treat (ITT) population. Continuous variables are described as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR), based on the Shapiro-Wilk normality test results. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages.

The primary efficacy analysis for all outcomes used linear mixed-effects models for repeated measures in the mITT population. Post-baseline repeated measurements at week 4 and week 12 were modeled with fixed effects for treatment group, time, and the group-by-time interaction, with the baseline value of the corresponding outcome, age category (<65/≥65 years), and sex included as covariates. A participant-specific random intercept was included to account for within-participant correlation. The group-by-time interaction was used as the principal test of whether changes over time differed among study groups. Baseline, Week 4, and Week 12 values are presented as observed mean ± SD. Change at Week 12 represents the observed within-group change from baseline and is provided for descriptive purposes only. Model-estimated between-group differences in change from baseline at week 12, together with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), were derived from the estimated marginal means of the linear mixed-effects models.

For the two pre-specified primary comparisons for MNA score (Intervention FSMP vs Control and Active Control vs Control), Bonferroni adjustment was used to control the Type I error rate. The comparison between Intervention FSMP and Active Control was exploratory, and results are reported with effect estimates, 95% CIs, and descriptive P values. For secondary outcomes, P values were not adjusted for multiplicity and should be interpreted descriptively. The linear mixed-effects models used all available post-baseline data under the missing-at-random assumption; no imputation was performed for the primary repeated-measures analyses. Sensitivity analyses for primary and secondary outcomes were performed in the PP population using observed data. IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for all statistical analyses. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In terms of anthropometric outcomes, significant group-by-time interactions were observed for body weight and BMI (both P < 0.001). Compared with Control, both the Intervention FSMP group and the Active Control group showed greater increases in body weight (Intervention FSMP: adjusted difference 1.2 kg, 95% CI 0.8–1.7, P < 0.001; Active Control: adjusted difference 0.7 kg, 95% CI 0.3–1.2, P = 0.001) and BMI (Intervention FSMP: adjusted difference 0.5 kg/m², 95% CI 0.3 to 0.7, P < 0.001; Active Control: adjusted difference 0.3 kg/m², 95% CI 0.1 to 0.5, P = 0.001). The observed within-group changes from baseline to week 12 were 1.6 kg, 1.2 kg, and 0.4 kg for body weight, and 0.6 kg/m², 0.5 kg/m², and 0.1 kg/m2 for BMI, in the Intervention FSMP, Active Control, and Control groups, respectively. Exploratory comparisons between the two FSMP groups favored the Intervention FSMP group for body weight (adjusted difference 0.5 kg, 95% CI 0.2–1.0; P = 0.043) and BMI (adjusted difference 0.2 kg/m2, 95% CI 0.0 to 0.4; P = 0.047). No significant group-by-time interactions or between-group differences versus Control were observed for MUAC.

Dietary intake and physical activity

To provide context for changes in nutritional and functional outcomes, habitual dietary intake was assessed at baseline, week 4, and week 12 through three consecutive 24 -h dietary recalls (two weekdays and one weekend day) at each visit. As shown in Supplementary Table S2A, background dietary energy and macronutrient intake varied over time within each group, but no statistically significant between-group differences in change from baseline were observed at week 4 or week 12. Given the expected day-to-day variation in 24 -h dietary recall data, these results were interpreted as reflecting background dietary variability during follow-up.

When ONS supplementation was taken into account, total daily energy intake increased significantly in the two intervention groups compared with the control group at both week 4 and week 12 (Supplementary Table S2B). Total daily protein intake also increased numerically in both intervention groups after inclusion of ONS, although the between-group difference did not reach statistical significance. These supplementary analyses clarify the nutritional contribution of the intervention products beyond habitual background dietary intake.

PASE scores did not differ significantly among the three groups at baseline, week 4, or week 12 (Supplementary Table S3).

Adherence and per-protocol sensitivity analysis

Adherence to the assigned supplement was high in both intervention groups. In the Intervention FSMP group, the median number of intake days over the 12-week period was 84 days (IQR 83–84), and 88.9% of participants met the predefined adherence threshold (≥67 of 84 days). The Active Control group showed a similar pattern, with a median of 84 days (IQR 80.5–84) and 84.4% achieving ≥80% adherence. Adherence was assessed based on participant intake logs and sachet counts. The control group did not receive supplementation; therefore, adherence evaluation was not applicable. Per-protocol sensitivity analyses yielded results consistent with those of the primary mITT analysis, supporting the robustness of the main findings (Supplementary Table S4).

Safety and gastrointestinal tolerability

Adverse events, clinical laboratory parameters, and gastrointestinal symptoms were summarized descriptively. Hematology and serum chemistry values remained stable from baseline to week 12 across all three groups, with mean values largely within laboratory reference ranges and only a small and comparable proportion of out-of-range results at week 12 (Supplementary Tables S5 and S6). Week-4 assessments showed similar patterns, and no clinically meaningful safety concerns were identified during the intervention period.

Gastrointestinal symptoms, including bowel movement frequency, diarrhea, abdominal pain, and bloating, were comparable across groups at weeks 4 and 12. No statistically significant between-group differences were observed (all P > 0.05; Supplementary Table S7), and there was no indication of increased gastrointestinal intolerance with the Intervention FSMP compared with either the active control or control group.

Exploratory subgroup analysis by age

An exploratory subgroup analysis was conducted to describe estimated intervention effects across age categories (<65 and ≥65 years) (Supplementary Table S8). For MNA score, the point estimates favored both the Intervention FSMP and Active Control groups compared with the Control group in both age subgroups. The adjusted mean differences for the Intervention FSMP versus Control were 2.0 points among participants aged <65 years and 2.3 points among those aged ≥65 years; the corresponding estimates for the Active Control versus Control were 3.1 and 2.6 points, respectively.

For left handgrip strength, point estimates also generally favored the FSMP groups over the Control group. The adjusted mean differences for the Intervention FSMP versus Control were 2.9 kg in participants aged <65 years and 3.2 kg in those aged ≥65 years; the corresponding estimates for the Active Control versus Control were 1.8 and 2.4 kg, respectively. Confidence intervals were relatively wide for some estimates, particularly for handgrip strength, and these findings should be interpreted cautiously because the subgroup analyses were descriptive and exploratory.

Discussion

This study suggests that adding a high-protein FSMP-ONS to standard dietetic counseling was associated with improved nutritional status in middle-aged and older adults at risk of malnutrition. In the repeated-measures analysis, both FSMP groups showed greater longitudinal improvements in MNA score than counseling alone, with significant group-by-time effects supporting a more favorable trajectory of nutritional recovery over the 12-week intervention period. The Intervention product showed broadly comparable effects to the active control for the primary nutritional outcome, while objective gains in body weight and handgrip strength further corroborated the potential clinical benefit of this FSMP in outpatient care. Because no clear between-group differences were observed in background dietary intake during follow-up, whereas total daily energy and protein intake increased in both intervention groups when FSMP supplementation was taken into account, these benefits are unlikely to be explained by habitual diet alone.

Our findings are consistent with previous evidence indicating that high-protein oral nutritional supplementation can improve nutritional and functional outcomes in adults with nutritional risk [[22], [23], [24]]. A distinguishing feature of the present study was the relatively high level of protein intake achieved in the FSMP groups (>1.2 g/kg/day), which aligns with recommendations aimed at mitigating age-related anabolic resistance [20]. In addition, whereas many prior trials have relied solely on an unsupplemented control group [25], our study incorporated both an unsupplemented control and an active control, allowing an exploratory comparison with a globally established FSMP. Taken together, these findings suggest that targeted high-protein FSMP supplementation may help support nutritional status and muscle function in outpatient adults at risk of malnutrition.

Handgrip strength improved over time despite no statistically significant group-by-time difference in whole-body fat-free mass. Such a dissociation between functional and body-composition outcomes is not uncommon in short-term nutritional interventions, where improvements in muscle function may occur before detectable changes in whole-body muscle mass [26,27]. One possible explanation is that FSMP, particularly when enriched with high-quality protein, may support muscle protein synthesis and muscle quality, leading to early functional gains without measurable hypertrophy [[27], [28], [29]]. Habitual physical activity, assessed by PASE during follow-up, did not differ significantly between groups, making it less likely that the observed handgrip findings were driven by differences in physical activity, although residual confounding cannot be excluded. Moreover, the concomitant increases in BMI and calf circumference suggest that whole-body BIA may not have been sufficiently sensitive to detect subtle changes in muscle mass [30].

Subjective SARC-F scores showed no clear longitudinal between-group difference, which may reflect the lower sensitivity of this tool compared with dynamometry [31,32]. In contrast, handgrip strength, as an objective measure of muscle function, may be more responsive to early functional changes during nutritional intervention. In older adults, oral nutritional supplementation may help partially attenuate age-related anabolic resistance by providing additional energy and high-quality protein to support muscle protein synthesis [24,27]. This mechanism was not directly assessed in the present study. Although the present study was not intended to disentangle the independent effects of taurine and dietary fiber, variation in these formulation components may have influenced the observed outcomes. Taurine has been associated with muscle metabolism and may be relevant to sarcopenia-related muscle decline [33], whereas dietary fiber may support gastrointestinal function and nutrient utilization [34]. However, these factors should be interpreted as possible contributors only, rather than as direct explanations for the findings.

Another important element of this study was the inclusion of middle-aged adults (45–65 years), a population often absent from sarcopenia studies that focus on older adults [35,36]. Our findings indicated that nutritional interventions may have similar effects in this age group as in older populations. Exploratory subgroup analyses demonstrated functional improvements across all age categories, indicating that targeted nutritional supplementation may help mitigate anabolic resistance already emerging during midlife. These findings support the concept of early, preventive nutritional strategies aimed at preserving muscle reserves before overt functional decline becomes apparent [37].

The strengths of this study include its multicenter, randomized, and clinically controlled design, which allowed for a direct comparison between the Intervention FSMP and an internationally established reference product. The consistency of findings across both the modified intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses enhances confidence in the robustness of the observed treatment effects. The use of repeated-measures mixed-effects models also allowed the available longitudinal data to be incorporated while accounting for within-participant correlations over time. Furthermore, the 12-week follow-up period enabled the assessment of both short-term and intermediate nutritional and functional outcomes.

The study also has some limitations. First and foremost, there were randomization imbalances in several baseline nutritional and functional measures, including MNA score and BMI. These variables were addressed by including baseline outcome values as covariates in the linear mixed-effects models, but baseline heterogeneity is evident and results should be interpreted with caution. Regression to the mean cannot be excluded as a partial contributor to the observed between-group differences, despite statistical adjustment and the consistency of supportive analyses. In addition, MNA was used both to define eligibility and to assess the primary endpoint. Although it was selected to reflect overall nutritional status in this at-risk outpatient population, this overlap is not ideal and should be considered when interpreting the findings. The relatively wide age range of 45–80 years may have introduced additional clinical heterogeneity, since nutritional risk can have different causes and presentations in midlife versus older adults. Muscle mass was assessed using BIA rather than dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), which may limit the precision of fat-free mass estimation. BIA-derived measurements are sensitive to changes in hydration status, a factor that may fluctuate during nutritional interventions. Together with the relatively short intervention duration and the absence of a structured resistance exercise program, this may explain the lack of detectable changes in fat-free mass. Although outcome assessors were blinded, the open-label nature of the dietary intervention introduces a potential risk of performance bias; however, this concern is partly offset by the inclusion of objective endpoints such as handgrip strength. Finally, the predominance of female participants (74.1%) reflects typical recruitment patterns in community-based nutritional studies but may limit the generalizability of the findings to male populations.

This study emphasizes the significance of integrating high-protein FSMP into outpatient nutritional care as a strategy to improve nutritional status and muscle strength in middle-aged and older adults at risk of malnutrition. The observed effects of the Intervention FSMP were broadly comparable to those of the active control for the primary nutritional outcome and handgrip strength, supporting its potential clinical value as a locally produced formulation. It should be recognized that nutritional supplements should be used as an adjunct to a food-first approach and not as a replacement for food. These results demonstrate that combining nutritional supplementation with dietary counseling is effective in improving nutritional status and muscle function.

Confirming these findings should be the focus of future research, especially in conducting appropriately powered non-inferiority trials that make direct product comparisons. Moreover, to better understand the long-term persistence of these benefits and their effects on other outcomes, including quality of life, future research should include longer follow-up periods. Incorporating functional biomarkers in future research would help clarify the protein kinetics and other physiological mechanisms underlying the functional improvements observed in this population.

Conclusion

This study suggests that the Intervention FSMP-ONS was safe and was associated with improvements in nutritional status and handgrip strength when used as an adjunct to standard dietetic counseling in adults at risk of malnutrition. The repeated-measures mixed-effects analyses showed favorable group-by-time patterns for key nutritional and functional outcomes. However, given the baseline imbalance across study groups, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and confirmed in future adequately powered trials with better baseline balance. The Intervention FSMP showed broadly comparable observed effects and tolerability to those of an established international FSMP reference product, supporting its potential clinical utility as a locally manufactured option. Importantly, these findings suggest that targeted nutritional supplementation may be beneficial not only in older adults but also during midlife, a critical period for preserving functional capacity and supporting healthy aging.
