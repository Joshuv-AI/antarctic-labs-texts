Novel oral delivery system using modified inulin for enhanced liver cancer therapy
Authors: Sung-Ling Tang, Yu-Ju Lai, Wei-Cheng Lin, Meng-Yi Bai, Yu-Chi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide. Although advanced oral anticancer drugs, such as sorafenib and lenvatinib, are available for treating HCC, their effectiveness is limited by significant side effects and the requirement for optimal liver function, restricting their broader use. Furthermore, these drugs cannot be used in combination, leading to suboptimal treatment outcomes. This study introduces a novel therapeutic approach by repurposing two existing drugs, niclosamide and disulfiram, as a combination therapy to treat HCC. Despite their known anticancer properties, these drugs face challenges such as high lipophilicity and a significant first-pass effect, resulting in low bioavailability. To overcome this limitation, we used chemically modified inulin fibers as oral carriers to enhance drug absorption and protect the drugs from the acidic gastric environment. We compared the efficacy of this system across two HCC cell lines―HepG2 (from a Caucasian patient) and Huh6 (from an Asian patient)―to explore how genetic and ethnic variations impact sensitivity to the proposed therapeutic approach. Our findings indicate that the proposed inulin oral delivery approach improves drug efficacy both in vitro and in vivo. Moreover, our comparative study underscores the importance of considering ethnic and genetic diversity in designing effective cancer therapies. Overall, the proposed oral delivery system can potentially reduce the need for invasive procedures, lower infection risks, and minimize patient discomfort by avoiding the need for intravenous administration, while improving treatment outcomes.

1. Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common primary liver cancer and a leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally, with a five-year survival rate of only 18% [1–3]. Key risk factors include chronic hepatitis B and C infections, alcohol abuse, and the rising incidence of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [4]. Despite advancements in surgery, transplantation, and systemic therapies, recurrence and metastasis remain major challenges [5].

Niclosamide and disulfiram are existing drugs known for their anticancer properties. Niclosamide, an FDA-approved antihelmintic drug, has shown potential for cancer therapy by inhibiting key pathways such as the Wnt/β-catenin and nuclear factor (NF)–κB pathways, which are crucial for tumor growth and metastasis [6,7]. Originally used for tapeworm infections, niclosamide has demonstrated effectiveness in cancers such as colorectal, breast, and prostate cancer, with minimal toxicity to normal cells [8–10]. In HCC, niclosamide suppresses tumor migration and invasion by downregulating Twist-mediated CD10 expression. Disulfiram, originally approved for alcohol dependence, has gained recognition for its anticancer properties [11]. By disrupting key pathways such as the NF-κB and proteasome systems, disulfiram induces apoptosis and inhibits tumor growth [12,13]. When combined with copper, it enhances anticancer activity by generating reactive oxygen species and targeting cancer stem cells [14]. Preclinical studies have shown efficacy in breast, lung, and prostate cancer, with minimal toxicity to normal tissues [14]. Despite their known anticancer properties, niclosamide and disulfiram are both limited by a low bioavailability, arising from high lipophilicity and a significant first-pass effect. Thus, an effective drug delivery system is needed to improve the efficacy of these drugs.

Inulin, a natural polysaccharide derived from plants such as chicory, has gained attention as a versatile biomaterial for drug delivery due to its unique structure and biodegradability [15]. Its resistance to enzymatic breakdown in the upper gastrointestinal tract and selective degradation by colonic bacteria make it ideal for colon-targeted therapies [16,17]. Chemical modifications, such as acetylation, have expanded applications for inulin, improving its solubility and enabling the encapsulation of both hydrophobic and hydrophilic drugs [18]. Its biocompatibility, non-toxicity, and ability to form stable self-assembling structures make inulin a promising candidate for advanced drug delivery systems [19].

In this study, we apply modified inulin to enhance the bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy of a combination therapy using niclosamide and disulfiram as repurposed anticancer drugs in HCC. Specifically, we chemically modify inulin fibers as oral carriers to enhance drug absorption and to protect the drugs from the acidic gastric environment (Fig. 1). Moreover, we aimed to design a larger particle system capable of simultaneously encapsulating the drugs and providing embolization potential to hepatic tumor vasculature, similar to the clinically used HepaSphere Embolization Microspheres that offer chemoembolization for hepatocellular carcinoma. These advancements position inulin as a crucial scaffold for developing more effective and patient-friendly drug delivery solutions, with potential to enhance the efficacy of current treatments and improve outcomes for patients with HCC.

We compare the effects of our novel drug delivery system on two HCC cell lines, HepG2 and HuH6, representing different ethnic and genetic backgrounds. HepG2, from a Caucasian male, is widely used for its known drug metabolism, whereas HuH6, from a young Japanese male with hepatoblastoma, displays heightened sensitivity to mutagens. By utilizing these cell lines, we aim to determine how genetic and ethnic differences influence liver cancer drug sensitivity, enabling the development of more tailored and effective therapeutic strategies for diverse populations.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Materials

We procured inulin (from chicory), sodium acetate, acetic anhydride, niclosamide, and disulfiram from Sigma-Aldrich. Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM), a Pen Strep antibiotic solution (100 IU/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin), fetal bovine serum (FBS), and pH 7.4 phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were supplied by GIBCO BRL (Corning, NY, USA). We purchased glacial acetic acid from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). All other chemicals were of reagent or tissue-culture grade.

2.2. Methods

2.2.1. Synthesis of acetylated inulin (INAc)

To synthesize INAc, we first prepared a reaction mixture [20]. Sodium acetate (0.16 mmol) was dissolved in 20 mL of 0.129 M dimethylformamide, followed by the addition of 1.62 × 10−1 mmol of inulin. The mixture was heated to 40 °C using a water bath and stirred constantly until all solids were fully dissolved. Subsequently, we slowly added 55 mmol of acetic anhydride under constant stirring and maintained the reaction mixture at 40 °C for 24 h. Upon completion of the reaction, the mixture was transferred into 50-mL centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was decanted, and the precipitate was washed with acetone to remove any unreacted materials. We then dried the purified INAc under vacuum in an oven at a low temperature.

2.2.2. Characterization of INAc and substitution rate

We characterized the chemical composition of INAc using Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy [21].We placed small amounts of inulin and INAc on the FTIR sample holder and, after performing background subtraction, scanned the samples over a range of 400–4000 cm−1 [22]. Characteristic peaks corresponding to the functional groups were identified to confirm the acetylation of inulin. Additionally, we employed nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy to further characterize the structure of INAc. For this, we dissolved inulin, INAc, and acetic anhydride in deuterated dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and recorded 1H NMR spectra using an NMR spectrometer. 2.2.3. Electrospray and scanning electron microscopy

To optimize the electrospraying protocol, we varied the collection plate temperature (N2 (l), 0 °C, 10 °C, 20 °C), carrier concentration (9 wt%, 12 wt%, 15 wt%, 18 wt%), spray distance (3.5 cm, 7 cm, 10 cm, 13 cm, 16 cm), and solvent ratio (alcohol:- acetone; 1:1, 1:3, 3:1). We performed electrospraying under a 19-kV electric field and then freeze-dried the samples for 24 h.

To prepare samples for scanning electron microscopy (SEM), we collected a small amount of sample via copper tape; the samples were then transported to the Precious Instrument Center at Taiwan Tech (National Taiwan University of Science and Technology). Carbon tape was adhered to the specimen stage, and the copper tape with the sample was affixed onto the carbon tape with the sample side facing up [23]. We then placed the sample into a platinum sputter coater, where, after several vacuum cycles, sputtering was performed for 85 s. After sputtering, we mounted the sample on an SEM-specific stage and inserted the stage into the apparatus for SEM imaging. Particle sizes were measured from SEM images using ImageJ software.

2.2.4. In vitro cell viability

We conducted in vitro experiments using HepG2 and Huh6 HCC cell lines [24,25]. For the MTT assay, we seeded 20,000 cells in 200 μL of DMEM medium (DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% Pen Strep antibiotic solution) per well in a 96-well plate and incubated the cells at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 24 h. After incubation, we treated the cells with various concentrations of niclosamide, disulfiram, or INAc-encapsulated drugs. After 24 h, we removed the DMEM medium and added MTT reagent to each well. The cells were incubated for 4 h, formazan crystals were dissolved in DMSO, and the absorbance was measured at 570 nm via a plate reader.

2.2.5. In vivo assessment

To test the drug delivery system in vivo, we used male nude BALB/cAnN.Cg-Foxn1nu/CrlNarl mice (5 weeks old, average weight: 17.1 g) sourced from the National Laboratory Animal Center in Taipei, Taiwan [26,27]. All experimental procedures followed the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the National Defense Medical Center, with approval granted prior to the study (approval certificate no. IACUC-23-186). The mice were housed in groups of five animals per cage, with six groups for each cell line. We maintained the mice in a pathogen-free environment with a controlled temperature (25 °C ± 2 °C), a 12-h light/dark cycle, and a relative humidity of 60% ± 10%.

For the HCC model, we subcutaneously injected HepG2 or HuH6 cells into the right dorsal flank of the mice. HepG2 (6 × 106) or HuH6 (5 × 106) cells were suspended in 100 μL PBS prior to injection. We measured tumor growth three times per week by determining two bisecting diameters using a ruler, with tumor volume calculated according to the following formula: tumor volume (mm3) = ½ × width × length2. Once the tumors reached a volume of approximately 100 mm3, we randomly assigned the mice to one of six treatment groups: (1) PBS control, (2) niclosamide (1 μM), (3) disulfiram (1 nM), (4) niclosamide (1 μM) + disulfiram (1 nM), (5) INAc (15 wt%), or (6) niclosamide (1 μM) + disulfiram (1 nM) encapsulated in INAc (15 wt%).

Treatments began on day 18 for HepG2-bearing mice and day 14 for HuH6-bearing mice, following the appearance of palpable tumors. We administered the drugs via oral gavage every two days for a total of seven doses. Throughout the study, we monitored the body weight, food intake, and tumor size three times per week. At the end of the 14-day treatment period, the mice were sacrificed, and tumors, along with major organs, were collected for further examination.

2.2.6. Statistical analyses

Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). Specifically, Wilcoxon statistics, one-way analysis of variance, and the Student’s t test were used to assess the differences between the experimental groups. The data were considered significantly different when p < 0.05.

3. Results and discussion

3.1. Synthesis and characterization of INAc

We successfully synthesized INAc by acetylating inulin using acetic anhydride as the acetylating agent and sodium acetate as a catalyst. The reaction yielded a white precipitate, which was purified via centrifugation, acetone washing, and vacuum drying. Across three independent syntheses, the average yield was 0.98 g, with minimal variations in yield, demonstrating the reproducibility of the synthesis process. This consistency in yield highlights the robustness of our method, supporting INAc as a reliable candidate for further applications in drug delivery.

To confirm the chemical modification of inulin, we applied FTIR spectroscopy (Fig. 2a). The FTIR spectra of INAc showed the expected characteristic absorption bands. A strong absorption band at 1750 cm−1 confirmed the formation of ester carbonyl groups, whereas a prominent peak at 2900 cm−1 corresponded to methyl groups introduced during acetylation. We observed a shift in the hydroxyl group peak from 3500 cm−1 to 3300 cm−1, further indicating the acetylation of hydroxyl groups on the inulin backbone. These spectroscopic changes align with literature values, verifying the successful acetylation of inulin, which is a critical modification for enhancing its drug-carrying potential.

We further confirmed the degree of substitution (acetylation) of INAc using NMR spectroscopy (Fig. 2b). 1H NMR spectra revealed characteristic peaks corresponding to the methylene and methine protons of the fructose backbone, appearing at δ= 4.27–3.61 ppm and δ= 5.55–5.08 ppm, respectively. A distinct signal at δ= 2.03 ppm was attributed to the acetate groups. By integrating these peaks, we calculated the substitution rate of INAc as approximately 69.79%. This level of acetylation ensures that a substantial portion of the hydroxyl groups on the inulin molecule are acetylated, providing a balance between hydrophobic and hydrophilic properties, which is essential for encapsulating both hydrophobic and hydrophilic drugs.

These results clearly demonstrate that the acetylation of inulin not only improves its physicochemical properties but also enhances its suitability as a versatile drug delivery system. The high substitution rate and the confirmed structural modification through FTIR and NMR analyses suggest that INAc is well equipped to improve the bioavailability of hydrophobic drugs. Moreover, this modification opens up potential applications for INAc in targeting drug-resistant cancers and other challenging therapeutic areas in which advanced drug delivery platforms are essential. These findings set the stage for future exploration of INAc in the context of combination therapies, particularly for cancer treatments involving repurposed drugs.

3.2. Electrospray optimization

In this study, we applied electrospraying to produce INAc particles. To optimize this process, we varied the solvent ratio, collection plate temperature, INAc concentration, and electrospraying distance. The parameters were initially set at an alcohol:acetone solvent ratio of 1:1, a collection plate temperature of 0 °C, an INAc concentration of 915 wt%, and an electrospraying distance of 3.5 cm. We then varied each parameter individually to determine its influence on INAc particle formation.

3.3. Effect of solvent ratio on INAc particle morphology

The alcohol:acetone ratio used during electrospraying significantly affected the morphology of the INAc particles (Fig. 3a–c). When the ratio was 1:1, the particles formed were the most complete, with an average size of 2.53 μm in diameter. In contrast, a ratio of 1:3 resulted in notable particle rupture, with an average size of 1.9 μm. Increasing the alcohol content to a 3:1 ratio produced larger particles, averaging 2.93 μm; however, their morphology resembled small fruit pits, indicating incomplete particle formation. Therefore, an alcohol: acetone ratio of 1:1 proved to be the most optimal for maintaining particle integrity.

3.4. Effect of collection plate temperature on INAc particle formation

The collection plate temperature played a critical role in the morphology and size of INAc particles formed during electrospraying (Fig. 3d–g). At 20 °C, the formation of stable particles was hindered, as the particles either dissolved or failed to take the desired shape. However, at lower temperatures such as the temperature of liquid nitrogen (−196 °C) and 0 °C, the particle formation improved significantly. For 0 °C, we observed an average particle size of 2.53 μm, whereas an average size of 1.83 μm was obtained for −196 °C. For 10 °C, although there were some collapsed or dissolved particles, the average particle size was approximately 2.2 μm. These findings demonstrate that lower collection plate temperatures are optimal for forming stable INAc particles, with 0 °C being particularly favorable.

3.5. Effect of INAc concentration on particle formation

The concentration of INAc also had a significant impact on particle formation (Fig. 3h–k). At lower concentrations (9 wt% and 12 wt%), many particles ruptured, making them unsuitable for drug delivery applications. The average particle size was 1.713 μm at 9 wt% and increased to 2.20 μm for 12 wt%. However, higher concentrations (15 wt% and 18 wt%) produced stable, complete particles, with an average particle size of 2.53 μm for 15 wt% and 2.163 μm for 18 wt%. These results indicate that a concentration of 15–18 wt% is optimal for forming complete, stable INAc particles.

3.6. Effect of electrospraying distance on INAc particle formation

The electrospraying distance also influenced the particle size and stability (Fig. 3l–p), as previously reported [21]. For a distance of 3.5 or 7 cm, the formed particles had an average size of 2.529 or 2.7731 μm, respectively, with spherical morphology. As the distance increased, particle collapse became more prominent. We observed an average particle size of 2.375 μm for 10 cm and 2.978 μm for 13 cm. However, at 16 cm, the average size decreased to 1.687 μm, and the particles exhibited significant collapse. Therefore, we used a spraying distance of 3.5 cm in subsequent experiments.

3.7. Effect of solvent ratio on INAc + niclosamide + disulfiram particle morphology

When INAc was used to encapsulate niclosamide and disulfiram, the solvent ratio significantly affected particle formation (Fig. 4a–c). At a 1:1 alcohol:acetone ratio, the formed particles had an average size of 0.964 μm, with a complete and stable morphology. At a 1:3 ratio, partial dissolution occurred, and the average particle size increased to 1.519 μm. At a ratio of 3:1, severe particle dissolution occurred, making it difficult to obtain accurate particle size measurements. In the calculable portion, the average particle size was approximately 1.144 μm. These results confirm that the 1:1 solvent ratio is optimal for forming stable particles when encapsulating niclosamide and disulfiram.

3.8. Effect of collection plate temperature on INAc + niclosamide + disulfiram particle formation

The collection plate temperature had a profound impact on the formation of INAc encapsulating niclosamide and disulfiram (Fig. 4d–g). At the temperature of liquid nitrogen (−196 °C), particles failed to form, showing significant deviation from the INAc-only results. However, at 10 °C, particles formed with an average size of 1.757 μm; at 0 °C, the average particle size was approximately 1.506 μm. At 20 °C, particle formation was incomplete, with notable dissolution and failure to achieve the desired structure. The average size in the calculable portion was approximately 1.393 μm, indicating that low, but not ultra-low, temperatures are crucial for forming stable particles when encapsulating these drugs, with 0 °C and 10 °C providing the most favorable results.

3.9. Effect of INAc concentration on INAC + niclosamide + disulfiram particle formation

The concentration of INAc also played a critical role in the encapsulation of niclosamide and disulfiram (Fig. 4h, i). For a concentration of 15 wt%, the average particle size was approximately 0.964 μm. At 18 wt%, particle aggregation occurred, with particles clustering into larger complexes. The calculable average particle size at 18 wt% was 1.506 μm. These results suggest that while both 15 wt% and 18 wt% concentrations allow for effective drug encapsulation, 15 wt% is more suitable for forming distinct and stable particles, avoiding excessive agglomeration.

3.10. In vitro assessment of INAc and INAc + niclosamide + disulfiram particles

We assessed the cytotoxicity of the INAc particles, both drug-loaded and unloaded, using the MTT assay on HepG2 and Huh6 human HCC lines. The results demonstrated that the INAc particles alone exhibited minimal cytotoxicity, with cell viability remaining above 85% across all tested concentrations. However, when loaded with niclosamide and disulfiram, the INAc particles significantly reduced the cell viability in a dose-dependent manner. The combination of niclosamide and disulfiram delivered via INAc particles resulted in a greater reduction in cell viability compared with either drug alone, indicating a synergistic effect (Fig. 5).

3.11. In vivo assessment of INAc and INAc + niclosamide + disulfiram particles

3.11.1. Tumor growth and body weight

To assess the impact of the INAc particles in vivo, we used an HCC mouse model. For mice injected with HepG2 and Huh6 cells, Fig. 6 shows the changes in tumor volume and body weight observed throughout the experimental treatment period. As indicated in Fig. 6a,b, the body weight of the mice did not show any significant variation throughout the experimental process, confirming that the treatments did not induce systemic toxicity. Although a mild decrease in body weight was observed in some groups, particularly in the combination therapy group (Fig. 6a), this weight loss was transient and recovered by the end of the 14-day treatment period. Cachexia was unlikely to be the cause, as there were no signs of progressive wasting, abnormal behavior, or reduced food intake. In addition, histological analysis of major organs showed no evidence of systemic toxicity. We speculate that the temporary weight reduction may be related to stress or gastrointestinal discomfort caused by repeated oral gavage, rather than drug-induced cachexia. However, during the treatment period, the tumor volumes differed substantially between the two cell lines.

In the HepG2 model (Fig. 6c, see also Fig. 7), following 14 days of treatment, the control group had an average tumor volume of 1003.266 mm3. The group treated with 1 μM niclosamide had a lower average tumor volume of 627.1 mm3, whereas the group treated with 1 nM disulfiram had an average tumor volume of 609.902 mm3. The combination of 1 μM niclosamide and 1 nM disulfiram resulted in a tumor volume of 522.2 mm3. Treatment with 15 wt% INAc alone resulted in an average tumor volume of 778.5 mm3, whereas the combination of 15 wt% INAc with 1 μM niclosamide and 1 nM disulfiram led to the lowest average tumor volume of 347.112 mm3.

In the Huh6 model (Fig. 6d; see also Fig. 7), the control group similarly exhibited an average tumor volume of 1003.266 mm3 after 14 days of treatment. The group treated with 1 μM niclosamide showed a lower tumor volume of 604.074 mm3, while the group treated with 1 nM disulfiram exhibited an average tumor volume of 582.596 mm3. The combination of 1 μM niclosamide and 1 nM disulfiram resulted in a tumor volume of 481.088 mm3. Treatment with 15 wt% INAc alone led to a tumor volume of 718.962 mm3, and the combination of 15 wt% INAc with 1 μM niclosamide and 1 nM disulfiram resulted in the lowest average tumor volume of 232.114 mm3.

The results demonstrate that the combination of niclosamide and disulfiram significantly inhibited tumor growth compared with either drug alone. Furthermore, the INAc-based oral delivery system enhanced the efficacy of the combination therapy, leading to an even greater inhibition of tumor growth, especially in the Huh6 model, which showed a higher sensitivity to the treatment.

3.11.2. Histological assessment of tumors

Histological examination of tumor samples using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining provided additional insights into the effects of the treatments on cellular apoptosis and tumor morphology (Fig. 8) [28]. In the HepG2 model, the tissue sections from mice treated with INAc + niclosamide + disulfiram (Fig. 8f) showed significant signs of apoptosis compared with the control group (Fig. 8a), supporting the inhibited tumor growth observed in the treatment groups. In the Huh6 model, staining for the group treated with INAc + niclosamide + disulfiram (Fig. 8l) showed even more pronounced apoptosis in comparison to the control group (Fig. 8g), reflecting the greater inhibition of tumor growth observed in this model.

These histological findings confirm that the combination of niclosamide and disulfiram, delivered via the INAc carrier, effectively induces tumor cell apoptosis, inhibiting tumor growth. The consistency between the reduced tumor growth and apoptotic changes in both cell lines reinforces the potential of this combination therapy as a promising treatment strategy for HCC.

Given the significant improvement in tumor suppression observed in vivo, we infer that the oral delivery of niclosamide and disulfiram via INAc microparticles enhances drug bioavailability. As the particle size exceeds 1 μm, it is unlikely that INAc is absorbed directly into the bloodstream through enterocytes. Instead, we propose that the acetylated inulin matrix protects the encapsulated drugs from gastric degradation, enabling more intact drug to reach the intestinal lumen for absorption. Furthermore, the hydrophobic modification and particulate structure may promote both drug carrying, mucosal adhesion and sustained release, thereby prolonging the exposure window for absorption and minimizing first-pass metabolism. These mechanisms likely contribute to the improved therapeutic outcomes observed in the animal models.

3.11.3. Histological evaluation of major organs

To evaluate the systemic biocompatibility of INAc particles, we performed histological analysis of major organs collected from mice treated with INAc particles and INAc + niclosamide + disulfiram particles. As shown in Fig. S1 (https://doi.org/10.38212/2224-6614.3579), H&E staining of liver, kidney, heart, lung, and spleen tissues revealed no observable signs of inflammation, fibrosis, or necrosis in any treatment group. These results confirm that INAc particles and INAc + niclosamide + disulfiram particles are well tolerated and do not induce significantly detectable damage to vital organs as compared to the normal tissue.

4. Conclusions

In this study, we have demonstrated the effectiveness of niclosamide and disulfiram as a novel combination therapy for HCC, particularly when delivered via a chemically modified inulin (INAc) oral carrier [29]. Following treatment, we observed a significant inhibition of tumor growth in both HepG2 and Huh6 mouse models, with the latter showing higher sensitivity to the treatment. Histological analysis confirmed increased apoptosis in the treated tumors, further supporting the effectiveness of this combination therapy. These findings highlight the potential of repurposing niclosamide and disulfiram for HCC treatment [28], with INAc serving as an efficient oral delivery platform to enhance therapeutic outcomes.
