Genomic and metabolomics analysis: unlocking the biocontrol mechanism of Trichoderma virilente Z53 against anthracnose in Pleione yunnanensis
Authors: Jiabang Dong, Jili Chen, Yuzhou Feng, Xingxun Dai, You Li, Yining Di, Yu Liu, Tao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnthracnose caused by Colletotrichum spp. is harmful to orchidaceous plants including Pleione yunnanensis. The use of microorganisms for biocontrol is considered safe and effective. This study demonstrated that Trichoderma virilente Z53 not only exhibits strong antagonistic activity against 12 phytopathogenic fungal species but also promotes maize seed germination and seedling growth under in vitro conditions and effectively suppresses anthracnose disease in Pleione yunnanensis. Pot studies demonstrated that Z53, when used as a soil inoculant, significantly promoted plant height, root length, and fresh weight of maize, while markedly inhibiting the severity of leaf anthracnose caused by C. orchidophilum in P. yunnanensis. The Z53 genome spans 39.56 Mb and contains 10,521 coding genes. The draft genome analysis of Z53 revealed the genetic attributes for antagonistic properties, nitrogen assimilation, phosphate solubilization, siderophore production and production of volatile organic compounds. Gene clusters for antifungal metabolites trichoxide, Ascochlorin, and Clavaric acid, along with transcription factors such as C2H2 and bZIP, were also identified in the Z53 genome. Furthermore, the LC-MS/MS of crude cell pellet extract of Z53 identified 3,743 high-confidence secondary metabolites. These include antimicrobial compounds such as trichodermin, viridin, palmitic acid, isoprene, camphene, and 2-pentylfuran, as well as plant growth-promoting metabolites including indole-3-acetic acid, gibberellin A7, phosphoric acid, and L-tryptophan. Collectively, these findings establish T. virilente Z53 as a promising candidate for the development of biopesticides to control fungal pathogens and enhance plant defence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09040-5.Keywords: Pleione yunnanensis, Trichoderma virilente, Biocontrol, Genome analysis, Genes clusters, Bioactive substances

Introduction

Pleione yunnanensis (Rolfe) Rolfe is one of the basal botanical source plants of “Shancigu”, which is of significant medical value due to its pseudobulbs that exhibit efficacy in heat-clearing, detoxification, and anti-inflammatory applications [1]. Its bioactive polysaccharides also exhibit anti-breast cancer effects [2]. Nevertheless, plant pathogens significantly limit the cultivation of P. yunnanensis. Among these, anthracnose (leaf spot disease) is the most prevalent, leading to reduced yield and quality of P. yunnanensis and causing significant economic losses to the industry [3]. Colletotrichum spp. are the primary pathogens causing anthracnose in orchidaceous plants [4, 5]. The infection rate caused by the pathogen is extremely high, with May to September being the main disease season. Symptoms manifest as irregular yellowish-brown or black spots on leaf and stem surfaces, ultimately leading to leaf yellowing and plant wilting [6]. Currently, the control of anthracnose in P. yunnanensis remains primarily reliant on chemical pesticides. However, the long-term and extensive use of chemical pesticides not only leads to the development of resistance in pathogens but also kills non-target natural enemies, disrupts ecological balance, and threatens human health. Therefore, biocontrol methods based on microorganisms exhibit excellent potential for controlling plant pathogens because of their safe, eco-friendly, and highly efficient characteristics [7].

It has been suggested that the biocontrol microorganisms used to control plant pathogens include bacteria, actinomyces, and fungi such as Bacillus, Pseudomonas, Rhizobium, Trichoderma, Candida, and others [8]. These strains enhance host disease resistance and increase crop yields under both biotic and abiotic stresses [9–12]. Trichoderma spp. are highly adaptable, soil-dwelling, non-pathogenic saprophytic filamentous fungi that promote plant growth and enhance plant disease resistance [13]. It consists of species inhibiting plant pathogen growth directly (fungal parasitism, spatial and nutrient competition, production of antimicrobial compounds, etc.) or indirectly (inducing systemic plant resistance, enhancing root tolerance to stress, etc.) and thus represent prospective biocontrol applications [10, 14–17]. Trichoderma spp. can inhibit the growth of various pathogens by stimulating plant defense mechanisms and enhancing nutrient acquisition. For instance, T. harzianum and T. viridae significantly suppressed the growth of Fusarium solani [18], T. asperellum TC01 significantly inhibited the growth activity of C. gloeosporioides and promoted the growth of Camellia sinensis seedlings [15], T. gamsii suppressed the growth of Epicocum nigrum, Scytalidium lignicola, and Phoma herbarum, but showed no significant inhibitory effect on the growth of F. flocciferum [19]. Although molecular biology techniques have been used to identify and classify numerous Trichoderma spp. fungi, significant genomic variability exists among different species due to differences in their functional characteristics, phenotypes, and ecological distributions [20]. Therefore, dissecting the genome of Trichoderma strains is expected to provide key insights into their functional characteristics and modes of interaction with host organisms.

Whole-genome expression profiling represents a promising and powerful approach for dissecting the evolutionary trajectories of fungal species, identifying functionally relevant genomic regions, and uncovering genes with specialized biological roles [21]. Moreover, this strategy yields comprehensive genetic insights that facilitate the elucidation of biocontrol mechanisms [22]. Genome analysis of Paenibacillus polymyxa HK4 suggested the conserved nature of genomic determinants involved in plant interaction and competition in the rhizosphere as well as genes involved in phosphate solubilization, IAA production, hydrolytic enzymes, antimicrobial compounds, etc [21]. Furthermore, the complete genome sequence of P. polymyxa HY96-2 has revealed the molecular mechanisms underlying its biocontrol and plant growth-promoting activities [23]. Microbial metabolomics enables the qualitative and quantitative profiling of low-molecular-weight (< 1500 Da) metabolites, endogenous hormones, and microbial signaling molecules across the entire microbial life cycle or during specific physiological cycles [24]. The combined analysis of microbial genomics and metabolomics yields powerful insights into microbe-host interactions, disease mechanisms, and ecosystem functions.

In our preliminary study, the newly isolated strain Z53 was identified as T. virilente and exhibited remarkable antagonistic activity against C. orchidophilum. On this basis, the main objectives of the present study were: (i) to evaluate the in vitro inhibitory activity of strain Z53 against 12 phytopathogenic fungal isolates, (ii) to assess the capacity of strain Z53 to enhance plant growth and facilitate seed germination, (iii) to evaluate the in vivo antifungal efficacy of Z53 against C. orchidophilum, with a parallel comparison against commercially available chemical fungicides, and (iv) to dissect the biocontrol and plant growth-promoting potential mechanisms of Z53 by means of genome mining and metabolomics analyses.

Collectively, this study furnishes empirical evidence supporting the application of strain Z53 as a plant growth-promoting microorganism (PGPM) and biological control agent in agricultural production, thereby presenting a viable strategy for advancing sustainable agriculture.

Materials and methods

Phytopathogenic fungal isolates

A total of 12 pathogenic fungal strains were selected to conduct antagonism assays, and all these strains are deposited in the National-Local Joint Engineering Research Center for Southwest Biodiversity of Yunnan Agricultural University. Three strains of F. oxysporum were isolated from the root systems of Panax notoginseng afflicted with root rot, and designated as F. oxysporum A1, F. oxysporum A2, and F. oxysporum A3, respectively. F. solani was isolated from Saccharum officinarum leaves infected with dry rot. F. fujikuroi was isolated from Oryza sativa seedlings infected with rice blast. F. foetens and Pestalotiopsis pinicola were isolated from the roots of P. yunnanensis infected with root rot disease. C. orchidophilum was isolated from P. yunnanensis leaves infected with anthracnose. Botrytis deweyae was isolated from P. bulbocodioides leaves infected with gray mold. Didymella prosopidis and Leptosporella bambusae were isolated from P. pleionoides leaves infected with leaf spot disease. Alternaria alstroemeriae was isolated from P. grandiflora leaves infected with leaf blight. All strains were cultured on Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) medium, incubated at 28 ℃ for 7 d to reach the optimal growth phase, and then subjected to in vitro assays.

Pathogenic fungus-resistant strain

Z53, a disease-resistant strain utilized in the present study, was originally isolated by Feng et al. from the rhizosphere soil of healthy P. notoginseng plants and is currently deposited in the National-Local Joint Engineering Research Center for Southwest Biodiversity, Yunnan Agricultural University [25]. The internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region of strain Z53 was PCR-amplified with the universal primer pair ITS1/ITS4. Subsequently, BLAST analysis was conducted by aligning the amplified ITS sequence with those of T. virilente, T. aeroaquaticum, T. subeffusum, T. caerulescens, T. vadicola, T. pleuroti, T. rufobrunneum, T. longipile, T. orarium, T. lentiforme, T. vermifimicola, and C. garzense retrieved from the NCBI database, so as to accomplish accurate species delineation.

Antifungal experiments in vitro

Direct antagonistic inhibition of pathogenic fungi by fungus Z53

Dual-culture assay was employed to evaluate the inhibitory activity of strain Z53 against 12 pathogenic fungi on PDA plates [9]. Inocula of Z53 (5 mm diameter) and the pathogen were placed on opposite sides of 9 cm diameter PDA plates, with a spacing of 4 cm between them, and incubated at 28 ℃ for 5 d. The control plates were inoculated with the pathogen alone (5 mm diameter). The inhibitory activity of Z53 against the pathogenic fungi was calculated using the following formula: (1) Inhibition Rate (%) = (CD - TD) / CD × 100.

In this formula, CD denotes the mean colony diameter of the pathogenic fungi in the control group, whereas TD denotes the mean colony diameter of the pathogenic fungi in the Z53 treatment group.

Indirect antagonistic inhibition of pathogenic fungi by fungus Z53

To investigate the inhibitory effects of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) produced by Z53 on 12 pathogenic fungi, PDA was separately added to opposite sides of the separated plates. Subsequently, strain Z53 and the fungal pathogen were inoculated on opposite sides, respectively, with Petri dishes inoculated solely with the pathogen serving as controls [26]. The inhibitory activity of Z53’s VOCs against the pathogenic fungi was calculated using Formula (1).

Biochemical testing of strain Z53

Plant growth promoting activity

To evaluate the plant growth-promoting characteristics of strain Z53, the strain was inoculated onto Chrome azurol S (CAS) medium and incubated at 28 ℃ for 3 d. The halo surrounding the fungal growth area on the blue medium was observed, the appearance of an orange halo indicated the strain’s ability to produce siderophores [27]. The strain Z53 was inoculated onto Pikovaskaya agar medium (HiMedia) and incubated at 28 ℃ for 3 d to evaluate its phosphate-solubilizing ability [28]. The strain Z53 was inoculated onto potassium-solubilizing agar medium and incubated at 28 ℃ for 3 d to evaluate its potassium-solubilizing ability [29].

Protease activity

The inoculum with a diameter of 5 mm was inoculated onto caseinolytic agar medium plates containing 2% (w/v) skim milk as the substrate, and incubated at 28 ℃ for 5 d [30]. The proteolytic activity of microbial colonies was assessed by observing the formation of clear hydrolysis zones around the colonies. The proteolytic index was calculated using the following formula: (2) Hydrolysis Index = TD/CD.

In this formula, TD denotes the diameter of the transmission halo, and CD denotes the diameter of the fungal colony.

Amylase activity

The inoculum with a diameter of 5 mm was inoculated onto nutrient agar (NA) medium plates containing 1% (w/v) soluble starch, and incubated at 28 ℃ for 5 d [30]. After microbial growth, 3 mL of iodine solution was evenly distributed on the agar surface and allowed to interact with residual starch for 3 min to examine the formation of clear hydrolysis zones. The starch hydrolysis activity index was determined using formula (2).

Cellulase activity

The inoculum with a diameter of 5 mm was inoculated onto CMC medium plates containing carboxymethylcellulose as the sole carbon source and incubated at 28 ℃ for 5 days [30]. Cellulolytic activity was detected using 0.1% Congo red, followed by staining for 40 min and counterstaining with 1 M NaCl for 15 min. Hydrolysis activity was determined by measuring the diameter of the clear zone formed by cellulose degradation [31]. The cellulolytic index was determined using Formula (2).

In vivo inhibition of Z53 on C. orchidophilum

The P. yunnanensis used in this study were provided by the Kunming Institute of Botany, Chinese Academy of Sciences. They were healthy plants at the pseudobulb swelling stage, propagated from seeds via aseptic germination without any obvious damage, lesions or defects, and identified as Pleione yunnanensis (Rolfe) Rolfe by Professor Shibao Zhang from the Kunming Institute of Botany, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Prior to each experiment, the plants were surface-disinfected by soaking in 1% sodium hypochlorite solution for 5 min, rinsed twice with sterile distilled water, and then air-dried [32]. The following treatments were applied by spraying onto the plant leaves:

①T1 (Healthy control): Sterile distilled water.

①T1 (Healthy control): Sterile distilled water.

②T2 (Disease control): C. orchidophilum conidial suspension (1 × 108 spores/mL).

②T2 (Disease control): C. orchidophilum conidial suspension (1 × 108 spores/mL).

③T3 (Biocontrol Treatment): C. orchidophilum conidial suspension (1 × 108 spores/mL) + Z53 culture filtrate (1 × 108 spores/mL).

③T3 (Biocontrol Treatment): C. orchidophilum conidial suspension (1 × 108 spores/mL) + Z53 culture filtrate (1 × 108 spores/mL).

④T4 (Chemical Fungicide Treatment): C. orchidophilum conidial suspension (1 × 108 spores/mL) + chemical fungicide (1000 ppm).

④T4 (Chemical Fungicide Treatment): C. orchidophilum conidial suspension (1 × 108 spores/mL) + chemical fungicide (1000 ppm).

The culture filtrate was applied twice with a 7-day interval, followed by incubation at 25 ℃ in a growth chamber for 15 days. Disease severity was calculated using the following formula:

Infection Rate (%) = AL / AT × 100.

In this formula, AL denotes disease spots area of P. yunnanensis leaves infected with anthracnose, and AT denotes the average area of all P. yunnanensis leaves used in this experiment. Leaf area was measured using a LI-3000 C leaf area meter, and lesion area was determined using Image J.

Effects of Z53 treatment on the activities of defense related enzymes

The polyphenol oxidase (PPO), catalase (CAT), and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels in P. yunnanensis leaves from different treatments were determined. The activities of CAT, MDA, and PPO were all measured using plant CAT, MDA, and PPO activity assay kits. The kits were purchased from Suzhou Grace Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The analysis was performed using a multimode microplate reader (Spectra Max iD3).

Experimental verification of the plant growth promoting ability of strain Z53

Seed disinfection and germination assay

Given the poor germination of P. yunnanensis seeds resulting from lack of endosperm, and the intrinsically slow growth of the plants themselves, maize (Zea mays), a monocotyledonous model plant, was employed as a surrogate host. This approach aimed to investigate the effects of strain Z53 on seed germination and seedling growth, thereby evaluating its plant growth-promoting properties. Healthy maize seeds were obtained from the Seed Management Station of Kunming City, Yunnan Province, China. For fungal culturing, the seeds were first surface-disinfected with 95% ethanol for 1 min, followed by three washes with sterile distilled water. Strain Z53 was inoculated into 500 mL of Potato Dextrose Broth (PDB) medium and incubated at 28 ℃ with shaking at 200 rpm for 72 h. The culture was then centrifuged at 5000 rpm/min for 20 min at 4 ℃. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in sterile water to obtain a fungal suspension containing approximately 107 − 108 CFU/mL. The surface-disinfected corn seeds were soaked in equal volumes of the fungal suspension and sterile water for 1 h, respectively, air-dried, and then placed in sterile Petri dishes (n = 15) lined with moist Whatman filter paper for dark incubation at 25 ℃. Seeds soaked only in sterile water served as the control. The number of germinated seeds was counted after one week of incubation. Germination rate was calculated using the following formula: Germination Rate (%)=(Number of Germinated Seeds / Total Number of Seeds) ×100 [33].

Pot trials

For the pot experiment, garden soil (loam) was filled into pots with a diameter of 10 cm. Three maize seeds were sown per pot at a depth of 1 cm. The experimental group was watered weekly with a fungal suspension containing approximately 107 − 108 CFU/mL, while the control group was watered with sterile water. No organic or chemical fertilizers were used in this study. The pots were kept at room temperature and watered daily. Thirty days after sowing, the effects of Z53 on maize seedling were recorded in terms of parameters such as plant height, root length, and fresh weight. The experiment was set up with three replicates, and the average values from the three experiments were used for comparison between the experimental and control groups.

Genome sequencing and assembly

The total genomic DNA of Strain Z53 was extracted using the QIAGEN® Genomic DNA Kit. Whole-genome sequencing was conducted on a PacBio Revio sequencer at Wuhan Frasergen Bioinformatics Co., Ltd. Raw sequencing reads were processed using Fastp (v0.21.0) for quality filtering. Genome size and heterozygosity were estimated by k-mer-based analysis. The assembled genomic sequences and associated metadata are available under the NCBI BioProject accession number PRJNA1174370. HiFi reads were assembled into contigs using the Hifiasm (v0.19.5) assembler, and the assembly quality was evaluated in terms of completeness, continuity, consistency, and accuracy.

Genome annotation and comparative genomics analysis

Gene structure prediction was performed using PASA (v2.4.1), Exonerate (v2.4.0), and Augustus (v3.4.0). The predicted gene sets were integrated into a non-redundant, comprehensive gene set via MAKER (v3.01.03). Functional annotation of the predicted coding sequences was conducted by sequence alignment against the Pfam, NR, TrEMBL, Swiss-Prot, KOG, GO, and KEGG databases using multiple bioinformatic tools, including Diamond (v2.0.7), BLAST (v2.11.0+), InterProScan (v5.50-84.0), and HMMER (v3.1 b 2). The protein sequences encoded by the predicted genes were aligned to the Pathogen-Host Interactions Database (PHI) using the Blastp algorithm implemented in Diamond. Putative genes encoding carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) were classified with dbCAN2 based on the HMMER database [34]. Transcription factors (TFs) were annotated using Funannotate with reference to the Fungal Transcription Factor Database (FTFD). Biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) and their associated secondary metabolites were predicted using antiSMASH (v7.1.0) [35].

Single-copy orthologous gene families between strain Z53 and nine reference strains were identified using OrthoFinder (v2.5.4) [36]. Multiple sequence alignment of genes in the single-copy orthologous gene families was performed with MUSCLE (v3.8.31) [37], based on which a phylogenetic tree was constructed. Divergence times between species were estimated using r8s (v1.81) [38] and PAML (v4.10.0). CAFÉ (v4.2.1) was employed to analyze the expansion and contraction of gene families.

LC-MS/MS of Z53 cell-free extract

To characterize the cellular metabolites produced by strain Z53, ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis was conducted.

Strain Z53 was cultured in 500 mL PDB at 28 ℃ with shaking at 200 rpm for 5 d. Mycelia were harvested by centrifugation at 5000 rpm (4 ℃) for 30 min, and cellular metabolites were extracted using 20% acetonitrile-methanol (v/v). The extracted metabolites were subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis. The chromatographic system used consisted of an LC-30 A ultra-high-performance liquid chromatograph (Shimadzu, Japan) coupled with a TripleTOF 6600 + high-resolution mass spectrometer (SCIEX, USA). The LC parameters were set as follows: the chromatographic separation was performed on a Waters ACQUITY Premier HSS T3 Column (1.8 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm). The injection volume was 4 µL, the flow rate was maintained at 0.4 mL/min, and the column temperature was set at 40 ℃. Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water, and mobile phase B was 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. The gradient elution program for LC separation was as follows: 0–2 min, 5%–20% B; 2–5 min, 20%–60% B; 6–7.5 min, 99% B; 7.6–10 min, 5% B.

The MS parameters were set as follows: ion source gas 1 (50 psi), ion source gas 2 (60 psi), curtain gas (35 psi), and source temperature (550 ℃). The declustering potential (DP) was set to 60 V for the positive ion mode and − 60 V for the negative ion mode, while the ion spray voltage (IS) was 5000 V (positive mode) and − 4000 V (negative mode). The parameters for time-of-flight (TOF) MS scanning were configured as follows: mass range of 50–1000 Da and accumulation time of 200 ms. For product ion scanning, the parameters were set as follows: mass range of 25–1000 Da, accumulation time of 40 ms, and collision energy (CE) of 30 V (positive mode) and − 30 V (negative mode).

Statistical analysis

All in vitro experiments were carried out in two biologically independent trials, each containing three biological replicates. Data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to the datasets using GraphPad Prism (v10.1.2), with Tukey’s multiple comparison test implemented for post hoc pairwise comparisons.

Results

Identification and characterization of Z53

To elucidate the phylogenetic placement of strain Z53, its ITS sequence was subjected to multiple sequence alignment against those of eleven representative Trichoderma strains and one outgroup taxon. Phylogenetic inference based on the ITS sequences revealed that strain Z53 clustered together with T. virilente, T. aeroaquaticum, T. subeffusum, T. caerulescens, and T. vadicola in a well-supported monophyletic clade (Fig. 1a). The resultant phylogenetic tree indicated that strain Z53 occupied an independent subclade and shared a close phylogenetic relationship with T. virilente (BP = 87). Consequently, strain Z53 was taxonomically designated as T. virilente Z53 according to these phylogenetic affiliations.

Biochemical characterization of Strain Z53. a Phylogenetic tree of Z53 based on ITS sequence. b Hydrolysis halo of amylase, protease and cellulase. c Graphic presentation of hydrolysis index

Biochemical characterization revealed that T. virilente Z53 secreted a repertoire of hydrolytic enzymes, specifically, amylase (hydrolysis index = 1.1952 ± 0.1002), protease (hydrolysis index = 1.0340 ± 0.0209), and cellulase (hydrolysis index = 1.3294 ± 0.3262) displayed divergent catalytic activity profiles (Fig. 1b and c).

In vitro antifungal activity of T. virilente Z53

In vitro direct confrontation assays were conducted to evaluate the antagonistic efficacy of T. virilente Z53 against 12 phytopathogenic fungal isolates derived from various host plants. Following a 5-day co-incubation period, the pathogenic fungi showed a mean radial growth reduction of more than 60% relative to CK (Fig. 2a, Fig.S1a and Table S1). Notably, the inhibitory rate of Z53 against B. deweyae was the lowest at 62.30%, while the maximum growth inhibition (80.25%) was recorded for C. orchidophilum, demonstrating that Z53 could significantly suppress the mycelial growth of pathogenic fungi (Fig. 2a and Table S1). Furthermore, Z53 exerted substantial inhibitory activity (62.48%–75.68%) against all tested Fusarium strains, highlighting its great potential as a biocontrol candidate for the management of plant root rot diseases (Fig.S1a and Table S1). Collectively, these data clearly illustrate that T. virilente Z53 possesses potent antagonistic activity against a broad range of phytopathogenic fungi.

In vitro antagonistic effects of T. virilente Z53 on the growth of plant pathogenic fungi. a Direct confrontation and b indirect confrontation (VOC). CK denotes the untreated blank control group, whereas Z53 denotes the experimental group inoculated with Z53

Following a 5-day co-incubation period where the 12 phytopathogenic fungal isolates were exposed to VOCs emitted by T. virilente Z53, the mycelial growth inhibition rates of the pathogens spanned 58.33% to 79.45%, with C. orchidophilum being the most susceptible to VOC-mediated suppression (Fig. 2b, Fig.S1b and Table S1). These findings collectively illustrate that VOCs produced by Z53 serve as a key component of its biocontrol arsenal against phytopathogens.

In vivo inhibitory effect of T. virilenteZ53 against C. orchidophilum

After 15 days of inoculation with C. orchidophilum fungal suspension, extensive brown necrotic lesions were observed on P. yunnanensis leaves, with an average lesion area of 12.29 ± 2.81 cm² (Fig. 3a and b). Notably, leaves subjected to combined inoculation with T. virilente Z53 and C. orchidophilum showed a marked reduction in brown lesions, with the average lesion area reduced to 3.77 ± 0.62 cm² (Fig. 3b). Quantitative assessment of disease severity revealed that, compared to the control group (inoculated with C. orchidophilum alone), the co-treated group exhibited a significant suppression of disease severity (19.38 ± 2.73%). Importantly, this inhibitory effect was superior to that of the chemical fungicide treatment group (24.50 ± 5.30%) (Fig. 3c). Collectively, these findings confirm that T. virilente Z53 can effectively mitigate the progression of anthracnose on P. yunnanensis leaves induced by C. orchidophilum.

Following a 15-day incubation period, the MDA content in P. yunnanensis leaves was significantly elevated in the T2 treatment group relative to the T1 group. Notably, the MDA levels in the T3 and T4 groups were markedly reduced compared with the T2 group, with values approximating those in the T1 group (Fig. 3e). Meanwhile, the activities of CAT and PPO in P. yunnanensis leaves were significantly upregulated in the T2 group compared to the T1 group, however, the T3 and T4 groups exhibited even stronger activities of these two defensive enzymes than the T2 group (Fig. 3d and f). Collectively, these findings indicate that T. virilente Z53 can trigger the activation of key defensive enzymes involved in oxidative stress tolerance and phenolic compound metabolism, which in turn enhances the disease resistance of P. yunnanensis plants.

Plant growth-promoting activity of T. virilente Z53

In vitro plate-based assays confirmed that T. virilente Z53 exhibited significant phosphorus-solubilizing activity (both organic and inorganic phosphorus) and potassium-solubilizing capacity, which enabled the conversion of insoluble mineral potassium into bioavailable soluble potassium (Fig. 4a, b and c). Furthermore, the formation of orange halos around Z53 colonies on chrome azurol S (CAS) agar medium verified its ability to secrete siderophores—an important trait for iron acquisition and plant growth promotion (Fig. 4d).

The promoting effects of T. virilente Z53 on maize plants growth. a Dissolving organic phosphorus. b Dissolving inorganic phosphorus. c Dissolving potassium. d Secreting siderophores. e Seed germination rate (p = 0.0002). f Maize seeds germination. g Maize plants growth. h Plant fresh weight (p = 0.0002). i Root length (p = 0.0003). j Plant height (p = 0.0013). Mean values and standard deviations are based on 5 replicates

Maize seeds treated with T. virilente Z53 suspension exhibited a significantly higher germination rate compared to the untreated control (Fig. 4e and f), confirming the positive effect of Z53 on maize seed germination. Furthermore, maize plants cultivated in Z53-inoculated soil showed remarkably improved growth performance, with significant increases in plant height, root length, and fresh weight relative to the control group (Fig. 4g, h, i and j). Quantitative analysis revealed that the average plant height, root length, and fresh weight of Z53-inoculated maize were 31.5833 ± 0.7762 cm, 15.0733 ± 1.0033 cm, and 1.9761 ± 0.1548 g, respectively—representing substantial increases compared to the control group (24.9001 ± 1.2166 cm, 7.2633 ± 0.5707 cm, and 0.7749 ± 0.0178 g). Collectively, these findings indicate that T. virilente Z53 can significantly enhance maize seed germination and plant growth, which may be attributed to its nutrient-solubilizing (phosphorus, potassium) and siderophore-producing capabilities.

Genome sequencing and analysis of T. virilente Z53

Genome annotation of T. virilente Z53

To clarify the genomic features of T. virilente Z53 and decipher its potential biocontrol mechanisms, single-molecule real-time (SMRT) whole-genome sequencing was performed using a PacBio Revio sequencer. Raw sequencing reads were quality-filtered and de novo assembled to the contig level using Hifiasm (v0.19.6), resulting in 39 contigs. These contigs were subsequently anchored and ordered into 8 chromosomes, and a circular visualization of the genome was constructed using Circos (v0.69-9). The whole-genome of Z53 spans 39,560,945 bp with an average GC content of 48.81%, which is consistent with the GC content range (48–52%) of Trichoderma species. Genome annotation revealed 10,521 protein-coding sequences (CDSs), 214 transfer RNA (tRNA) genes, 490 ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes, and 23 small nuclear RNA (snRNA) genes (Fig. 5a and Table S2).

Genome map and annotation of T. virilente Z53. a Genome circle diagram of strain Z53. The five concentric rings from outer to inner represent: Chromosomes, Gene density, Repeats, ncRNA, and GC content. b CAZymes gene classification statistics. c PHI mutant phenotype statistical chart. d KOG annotation statistics

Functional classification of protein-coding genes was performed using the Gene Ontology (GO) database, which assigned 7,713 genes (73.71% of the total coding genes) to three main categories (Table S3 and Fig.S2b): biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). In the BP category, the most enriched terms were metabolic processes and cellular processes, reflecting the robust metabolic activity of T. virilente Z53. For the CC category, annotated genes were predominantly associated with cellular and membrane-related components, especially cellular constituents and membrane components—consistent with the secretion of extracellular enzymes (e.g., chitinases, amylases) and metabolites. In the MF category, catalytic activity and binding functions were the most abundant; the binding functions were mainly dominated by ATP binding, DNA binding, and metal ion binding, which are critical for enzyme catalysis and nutrient transport. Furthermore, 4,104 coding genes (39.01%) were annotated in the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database, which were assigned to 6 pathway categories (Table S3 and Fig.S2a). Genes involved in metabolism accounted for the largest proportion, with carbohydrate metabolism (376 genes) and amino acid metabolism (306 genes) ranking as the top two subcategories. Notably, 70 genes were involved in the biosynthesis of other secondary metabolites.

Functional annotation and classification of the genome were performed based on the Clusters of orthologous groups for eukaryotic complete genomes (KOG) database, resulting in the annotation of 5,604 genes in total (Table S3). Among these, 490 genes were annotated to be associated with the “Secondary metabolites biosynthesis, transport and catabolism” (Fig. 5d). This relatively high abundance indicates that Z53 possesses a genetic basis for synthesizing a variety of bioactive substances. In addition, 369 genes related to “Carbohydrate metabolism and transport” were identified, indicating that Z53 has robust basic metabolic activity and efficient nutrient utilization capacity, which is essential for its colonization and growth in the rhizosphere environment (Fig. 5d). Notably, 224 genes involved in “Inorganic ion transport and metabolism” were annotated, implying that Z53 may have the potential to regulate ion homeostasis in the environment under salt stress, thereby enhancing its environmental adaptability (Fig. 5d).

Annotation of specialized functions in the genome of T. virilente Z53

To explore the functional characteristics of Z53 related to nutrient utilization and pathogen-host interaction, its protein sequences were subjected to alignment with proteins in the CAZy and PHI databases. A total of 434 and 4,607 protein-related genes were predicted from the CAZy and PHI database alignments, respectively (Fig. 5b and c). Notably, the CAZyme-encoding genes identified were divided into six families: glycoside hydrolases (GHs, 240 members), auxiliary activities (AAs, 77 members), glycosyltransferases (GTs, 69 members), carbohydrate esterases (CEs, 24 members), carbohydrate-binding modules (CBMs, 16 members), and polysaccharide lyases (PLs, 8 members). Within the GH class, the four largest families were GH18 (44 genes), GH3 (16 genes), GH99 (10 genes), and GH2 (9 genes) (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Table 1). Notably, 7 genes in the GT1 family were predicted to participate in secondary metabolite glycosylation, a key step in the biosynthesis of bioactive compounds (Supplementary Table 1). PHI‑base annotation further identified 2,041 genes linked to reduced virulence, 263 to loss of pathogenicity, and 175 to lethal phenotypes. Furthermore, 35 genes encoding effectors (plant avirulence determinants) and 2 genes involved in enhanced antagonism were identified (Fig. 5c). These genes are closely related to Z53’s ability to inhibit pathogen virulence, interfere with pathogen-host interactions, and enhance its antagonistic activity against phytopathogens, further confirming the genetic basis of Z53’s excellent biocontrol potential.

Genome-wide annotation identified 398 putative transcription factor (TF)-encoding genes in T. virilente Z53, which accounted for 3.78% of the total predicted protein-coding genes in this strain (Table S4). Among these TFs, Zn-clus finger TFs (233 members) and C₂H₂-type zinc finger TFs (72 members) represented the two most abundant groups, accounting for 58.54% and 18.09% of the total predicted TFs, respectively (Fig. 6a). Other major TF families included bZIP (17 members), MYB (14 members), and C₃H (13 members) (Fig. 6a). These TFs, especially Zn-clus finger and C2H2-type zinc finger TFs, are closely involved in regulating the biosynthesis of antifungal metabolites and the response to plant-microbe interactions, providing an important regulatory basis for Z53’s biocontrol activity.

A total of 49 biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) were predicted in the genome sequence of T. virilente Z53 using antiSMASH analysis (Fig. 6b and Table S5). These BGCs were categorized into 8 classes: type I polyketide synthases (T1PKS, n = 13), terpenes (n = 9), nonribosomal peptide synthetases (NRPS, n = 8), NRPS-like fragments (n = 6), fungal ribosomally synthesized and post-translationally modified peptides (fungal-RiPP-like, n = 4), isocyanide-NRPs (n = 2), isocyanides (n = 1), and hybrid clusters (n = 6). Among these clusters, 6 BGCs exhibited high sequence similarity to those associated with known metabolites, namely choline (100%), clavaric acid (100%), metachelin (75%), fusaric acid (50%), ascochlorin (50%), and trichoxide (50%) (Fig S3). Notably, several BGCs showed extremely low homology with known clusters, implying that Z53 may have the potential to produce novel secondary metabolites.

Phylogenetic analysis

A robust phylogenetic tree encompassing ten fungal species was reconstructed using shared single-copy orthologous genes derived from genome-wide comparisons, offering a reliable framework for assessing interspecific genetic relatedness and evolutionary divergence patterns. Single-copy orthologous sequence-based phylogenetic clustering grouped T. asperellum, T. asperelloides, T. asperellum GCF020647865, T. asperellum CBS43397, and T. virilente Z53 into a well-supported monophyletic clade, indicating their close evolutionary affinity. Molecular clock analysis revealed that within the family Hypocreaceae, the Trichoderma spp. originated via an initial divergence event ~ 142.4 million years ago (MYA) (Fig. 6c). The most recent speciation event among the analyzed taxa was dated to approximately 5.0 MYA, while the split between T. virilente Z53 and the four congeneric species in its clade was estimated to have occurred ~ 130.7 MYA (Fig. 6c). Evolutionary dynamics analysis of gene families across the ten fungal species demonstrated that the frequency of gene family contraction exceeded that of expansion throughout their evolutionary trajectories. In comparison with Trichoderma spp., the outgroup taxa exhibited 669 gene family expansions and 207 gene family contractions. Within the Trichoderma spp., T. citrinoviride, T. lentiforme, and T. asperellum showed markedly intensified gene family contraction, with 952, 465, and 409 contracted families, respectively—these numbers were considerably higher than those observed in other Trichoderma spp (Fig. 6c). Notably, T. virilente Z53 experienced substantial evolutionary modifications across 475 gene families, with 137 families undergoing expansion and 338 families undergoing contraction (Fig. 6c). These genetic alterations may underpin its unique biocontrol and plant growth-promoting traits.

Genetic basis of plant growth promotion and biocontrol potential in T. virilente Z53

Preliminary genome-wide annotation of T. virilente Z53 uncovered a suite of genes responsible for the biosynthesis of metabolites that bolster plant defense responses, encompassing those encoding tyrosine-protein phosphatases, superoxide dismutases (SODs), catalases (CATs), mannosyltransferases, choline kinase, chitin synthases and dynein—key players in mitigating oxidative stress, reinforcing plant cell wall integrity and enhancing environmental adaptability (Table S6). The identification of glutamate-ammonia ligase (EC 6.3.1.2), glutamate synthase (EC 1.4.1.13), NAD-dependent glutamate dehydrogenase (EC 1.4.1.4), and glutamate dehydrogenase (EC 1.4.1.2) in Z53 provides genetic evidence that this strain can synthesize glutamic acid and glutamine, thereby enhancing nitrogen bioavailability and facilitating plant nitrogen uptake (Table S6). Concurrently, the presence of alkaline phosphatase-like protein (EC 3.1.3.1) and 3-phytase A (EC 3.1.3.8) genes endows Z53 with the ability to solubilize insoluble phosphate, which is critical for improving phosphorus acquisition by host plants (Table S6). Furthermore, phosphate transporters (pst2, pho13, pho88) and their regulatory genes (pho80, pho85) were identified in the Z53 genome (Table S6), implying that Z53 possesses the capacity to absorb environmental phosphate [21]. Notably, genes involved in the biosynthesis and transport of siderophores were also identified, including those encoding hydroxamate-type ferrichrome siderophore peptide, ferrioxamine B transporter, and siderochrome-iron transporter, which collectively enable Z53 to chelate and transport iron (Table 1).

Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), a naturally occurring auxin, is primarily synthesized via a tryptophan-dependent pathway. In the genome of T. virilente Z53, we identified open reading frames (ORFs) encoding enzymes involved in tryptophan biosynthesis, including indole-3-glycerol phosphate synthase (EC 4.1.1.18), anthranilate synthase component (EC 4.1.3.27), anthranilate phosphoribosyltransferase (EC 2.4.2.18), tryptophan synthase (EC 4.2.1.20), N-acetyltransferase (EC 2.3.1.254), and amidohydrolase (EC 3.5.2.3) (Table S6). Additionally, genes encoding aldehyde dehydrogenase (EC 1.2.1.3)—a key enzyme involved in IAA biosynthesis via the tryptamine (TAM) pathway—were also annotated [39]. However, the full complement of genes required for de novo IAA biosynthesis was not detected, suggesting that Z53 may rely on partial pathways or exogenous precursors for IAA production. Furthermore, genes encoding proteins involved in the biosynthesis of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) such as 2,3-butanediol, methanethiol, and isoprene, as well as genes encoding polyamine biosynthesis proteins, were also detected in the genome data of strain Z53. These include acetolactate synthase (ILV2), sulfate adenylyltransferase (MET3), methylthioribulose-1-phosphate dehydratase (MDE1), undecaprenyl diphosphate synthase (RER2), spermidine synthase (SPE3), and arginase (CAR1) (Table 1).

Analysis of secondary metabolites in T. virilente Z53

Untargeted metabolomics profiling of the fermentation broth of T. virilente Z53 identified 5,883 secondary metabolites. Following removal of low-confidence hits (match score ≤ 0.7), 3,743 metabolites remained (Supplementary Table 2). Among these, amino acids and its metabolites were the most prevalent, representing 32.03% of the total, followed by benzene and its substituted derivatives (14.66%), heterocyclic compounds (8.24%), and organic acids and their derivatives (7.01%). Screening of the identified metabolites revealed a substantial number of compounds with antimicrobial activity, including ethyl gallate, luteolin, trichodermin, caryophyllene oxide, viridin, palmitic acid, and isopalmitic acid. Additionally, various volatile antimicrobial compounds were detected in the secondary metabolome of T. virilente Z53, such as acetoin, isoprene, camphene, α-terpinene, 2-pentylfuran, 4-propylphenol, phenol, and phenylacetaldehyde. Concurrently, metabolites known to exhibit plant growth-promoting activity, including indole-3-acetic acid, indole-3-lactic acid, indole-3-glycol aldehyde, gibberellin A7, DL-glutamine, phosphoric acid, and L-tryptophan, were also identified in the T. virilente Z53 metabolome. Notably, pharmacologically active triterpenoid saponins, such as asiaticoside A, notoginsenoside R1, and ginsenosides, as well as steroidal saponins including neriifolin and dioscin, were detected in the metabolome of T. virilente Z53 (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

Antagonistic rhizosphere microorganisms have garnered extensive attention due to their multifaceted roles in inhibiting plant pathogens, enhancing plant growth, and synthesizing a diverse array of antifungal metabolites. Despite significant progress in their application as biocontrol agents, the intricate biocontrol mechanisms and genomic determinants that mediate host defense response regulation remain poorly elucidated, which limits the rational development and utilization of these microbial resources. This study systematically evaluated the antagonistic activity of T. virilente Z53 against a broad range of phytopathogenic fungi and, through an integrated genomics and metabolomics approach, elucidated the genetic underpinnings of its biocontrol potential. Phylogenetic analysis based on ITS sequences confirmed the taxonomic assignment of strain Z53 as T. virilente (Fig. 1a). Importantly, in vitro direct antagonism and indirect antagonism assays were performed using 12 plant pathogenic fungi, and the results demonstrated that T. virilente Z53 exerted potent antagonistic activity against all tested pathogens (inhibition rate > 55%) (Fig. 2, Fig.S1 and Table S1). Notably, the antagonistic efficiency against C. orchidophilum DC reached 80.25% in vitro. Collectively, these results indicate that T. virilente Z53 has excellent potent antimicrobial activity and may serve as a promising biocontrol agent.

Amylases secreted by PGPMs contribute to niche colonization by mediating nutrient acquisition on the plant surface and exerting competitive exclusion effects against phytopathogens [40]. Cellulases play multifaceted roles in biocontrol: they not only degrade fungal cell wall polysaccharides to release glucose fragments as signaling molecules, but also disrupt plant cell walls to induce the exudation of antimicrobial metabolites, thereby reinforcing plant defense responses against pathogenic invasion [41, 42]. Proteases are critical for antagonizing phytopathogens by degrading their structural proteins to suppress growth and reproduction [43, 44]. Furthermore, they participate in regulating plant stress-responsive signaling pathways and related gene expression, thereby enhancing plant stress tolerance [45]. Prediction of genes encoding CAZymes in strain Z53 identified a large number of genes encoding hydrolases (Fig. 5b). In vitro hydrolase activity assays confirmed that T. virilente Z53 is capable of producing proteases, amylases, and cellulases, indicating that part of the antifungal ability of Z53 may be derived from the hydrolases it produces (Fig. 1b and c). Additionally, amylases are involved in degrading plant cell wall components, softening the cell wall, and promoting plant water absorption and utilization [46]. Cellulases can decompose cellulose in plant cell walls to facilitate seed germination by softening the seed coat. Moreover, their degradation products increase available organic carbon sources in the soil, which further promotes root system growth and development [47]. Seed germination and pot experiments verified that Z53 can promote maize seed germination and seedling growth (Fig. 4), suggesting that hydrolases produced by Z53 may also serve as an important component of its growth-promoting capacity. However, since maize and P. yunnanensis are not congeneric plants, whether strain Z53 exerts growth-promoting effects on P. yunnanensis requires further validation through long-term pot and field experiments to confirm its practical application value in the cultivation of P. yunnanensis.

To resist pathogenic invasion, host plants activate their defense and antioxidant systems [48]. MDA is produced by plasma membrane peroxidation when plants are subjected to stress, and to a large extent reflects the degree of lipid peroxidation [49]. Previous investigations have demonstrated that PPO contributes to plant disease resistance by oxidizing phenolic compounds into quinones, which create a toxic microenvironment that inhibits pathogen colonization [50]. Meanwhile, increased CAT activity provides protection to the host, effectively preventing reactive oxygen species (ROS) damage to living organisms caused by pathogen infection [51–53]. In vivo antagonism assays, the groups treated with C. orchidophilum (T2, T3, T4) exhibited larger lesion areas and higher disease severity compared to the T1 group (Fig. 3a, b and c). Notably, the group treated with T. virilente Z53 (T3) showed smaller lesion areas and lower disease severity than the T2 and T4 groups. These results indicate that T. virilente Z53 can effectively promote overall plant health, thereby inhibiting disease progression. After 15 days of cultivation, the C. orchidophilum-inoculated groups (T2, T3, T4) showed significantly elevated levels of MDA, PPO, and CAT compared to the T1 group. Specifically, the PPO and CAT activities in the T3 and T4 groups were significantly higher than those in the T2 group, while the MDA content showed the opposite trend (Fig. 3d, e and f). The T1 group was neither infected with pathogens nor treated with Z53, reflecting the “normal” activity of these enzymes in P. yunnanensis plants. Thus, it can be inferred that at this stage, plants receiving T2, T3, and T4 treatments exhibited responses to microbial inoculants. The T3 group showed higher defense enzyme activities, suggesting that T. virilente Z53 may prime the host plant for long-term defense, enabling it to maintain a state of readiness against diseases. This priming effect is consistent with the mechanism of ISR mediated by PGPMs, wherein plants retain a moderate level of defense activation triggered by biocontrol agents, leading to enhanced responsiveness to future pathogen attacks [54, 55]. The elevated PPO and CAT activities coupled with reduced MDA content in Z53-treated plants further confirm that Z53 mitigates pathogen-induced oxidative stress and strengthens host defense capacity via ISR activation. Although T. virilente Z53 effectively suppresses the anthracnose pathogen of P. yunnanensis under controlled conditions, its biocontrol efficacy under field conditions may be compromised by environmental factors such as temperature fluctuations, competition from indigenous microorganisms, and ultraviolet radiation. Thus, the agricultural value of T. virilente Z53 warrants further validation through multi-site field trials spanning different seasons and growth stages of P. yunnanensis, as well as assessments of its integration with conventional disease management strategies.

T. virilente Z53 harbors a BGC associated with trichoxide production (Fig. 5b and Table S5), a trait that may contribute to reduced disease incidence and improved crop yield and quality. A homologous trichoxide BGC has also been identified in T. virens Gv29-8, and its products have been proven to exhibit significant antifungal activity against Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Candida albicans [56, 57]. Additionally, a gene cluster involved in ascochlorin biosynthesis was annotated in the T. virilente Z53 genome (Fig. 5b and Table S5). Ascochlorin, a naturally occurring sesquiterpenoid quinone alcohol, is recognized for its dual biological activities, including broad-spectrum antimicrobial effects and anti-inflammatory properties [58]. Consistent with this finding, a homologous ascochlorin BGC has been detected in the genome of Acremonium egyptiacum [59], and ascochlorin isolated from Stilbella fimetaria has shown potent antagonistic activity against Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and C. albicans [60]. T. virilente Z53 carries a BGC related to clavaric acid biosynthesis (Fig. 5b and Table S5). Clavaric acid can bind to β-lactamase, thereby enhancing the antibacterial efficacy of β-lactam antibiotics such as amoxicillin [61, 62]. Although these three classes of compounds were not detected in the secondary metabolome of Z53, untargeted metabolomics analysis revealed a wide range of antimicrobial compounds with potent antifungal activity, including trichodermin, viridin, isoprene, 2-pentylfuran, and ethyl gallate (Supplementary Table 2) [63–67]. These results collectively suggest that T. virilente Z53 likely inhibits the growth of C. orchidophilum primarily through the production of these antimicrobial compounds. Furthermore, T. virilente Z53 harbors a gene cluster associated with the biosynthesis of the siderophore Metachelin C (Table S5). Genome annotation of Z53 revealed that it carries genes encoding proteins such as siderochrome-iron transporter, ferrioxamine B transporter, and iron permease (Table 1). The presence of siderophores was also detected in the CAS agar plate assay, indicating that Z53 can produce siderophores to sequester iron from the surrounding environment, reduce the competitiveness of soil-borne pathogens, and promote host growth [68–70]. These results suggest that the siderophores produced by Z53 also play important roles in plant growth and stress resistance. Collectively, these results suggest that strain Z53 may inhibit phytopathogens and promote host plant growth by encoding and synthesizing multiple secondary metabolites through its BGCs. In future studies, the expression patterns of key genes within these biosynthetic gene clusters can be determined using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) to further clarify the association between the BGCs and the antagonistic activity of Z53, thereby deepening our understanding of the intrinsic mechanisms underlying its antifungal activity against phytopathogenic fungi.

Phylogenetic analysis revealed that T. virilente Z53 has an early genome divergence event, coupled with prominent gene family contraction and expansion (Fig. 6c). This evolutionary pattern indicates that Z53 likely colonized a unique ecological niche favorable for its survival and propagation at an early evolutionary stage, where it accumulated specific genetic variations and adaptive traits. These characteristics have endowed Z53 with the ability to maintain a relatively stable evolutionary trajectory, which is crucial for its adaptation to complex environmental conditions [71]. TFs are core regulatory components in microorganisms, and C2H2-type zinc finger TFs and bZIP TFs, in particular, have been documented to regulate fungal growth and development, secondary metabolite biosynthetic pathways, and the expression of genes associated with pathogen antagonism [72, 73]. The identification of 72 C2H2-type zinc finger TFs and 17 bZIP TFs in the Z53 genome suggests that these TFs may form a regulatory network to coordinate Z53’s growth and development (Fig. 6a), while also modulating its antagonistic responses against pathogenic fungi such as C. orchidophilum.

Rhizosphere microorganisms can engage in physical interactions with root surfaces through the formation of complex biofilms or microaggregates. Biofilm formation serves as a strategy to maintain beneficial relationships with hosts and enhances resistance to environmental stresses [21]. Consistent with this, genes regulating biofilm and cell wall formation were identified in the genome of T. virilente Z53 (Table S5). Specifically, choline-phosphate cytidylyltransferase PCT1 and phosphodiesterase PDE2 are involved in regulating the synthesis rate of cell walls and plasma membranes. Meanwhile, chitin synthases CHS2, CHS3, and CHS4 catalyze chitin biosynthesis, which enhances the tensile strength of the fungal cell wall [74]. These genes collectively support Z53’s ability to form stable biofilms and maintain cell wall integrity, laying a foundation for its beneficial interactions with host roots. Beyond structural regulation, Z53 harbors genes that bolster its antioxidant capacity and pathogen antagonism. GRE2 and ALO1, two conserved genes in PGPMs, mediate the induction of antioxidant enzymes, which scavenge excess ROS generated during host-microbe interactions or environmental stress, thus preserving plant health [75]. Phosphatase proteins play dual roles in maintaining Z53’s protein homeostasis and secreting bioactive antimicrobial metabolites, directly suppressing pathogen proliferation [75]. Furthermore, superoxide dismutase and catalase family proteins constitute a vital defense system, enhancing Z53’s self-resistance against pathogenic invasion and ensuring its sustained biocontrol efficacy in the rhizosphere.

Trichoderma spp. are capable of producing hundreds of volatile and non-volatile antagonistic secondary metabolites [22], such as non-ribosomal peptides, polyketides, and isoprene derivatives [76]. VOCs act as key signaling molecules that regulate plant physiological processes, including growth, stress response, nutrient uptake, and overall health. Consistent with this, genes involved in the biosynthesis of VOCs such as 2,3-butanediol, methanethiol, and isoprene were identified in the genome of T. virilente Z53 (Table 1). Correspondingly, a variety of VOCs with antimicrobial activity, including acetoin, isoprene, camphene, α-terpinene, 2-pentylfuran, and phenylacetaldehyde, were detected in the secondary metabolites of Z53, further supporting its biocontrol potential via volatile-mediated pathways (Supplementary Table 2). Triterpenoids produced by some PGPMs exhibit multiple beneficial effects, such as antagonizing pathogens, improving the host rhizosphere microbial community, and promoting host plant growth [77–79]. Similarly, this class of compounds was detected in the secondary metabolites of Z53, including centellasaponin A, ginsenoside Ro and notoginsenoside R1 (Supplementary Table 2). Among these, notoginsenoside R1 is a well-characterized triterpenoid saponin from P. notoginseng, with established antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, antithrombotic, and anticancer activities [80]. However, the yield of notoginsenosides is limited by the long growth cycle of P. notoginseng. Notably, notoginsenoside R1 was detected in the secondary metabolites of Z53, and seven genes belonging to the GT1 family were annotated in its genome (Supplementary Table 1), provides compelling evidence for Z53’s potential to synthesize this valuable compound. GT1 family enzymes are critical for the glycosylation step in triterpenoid saponin biosynthesis, a key modification that determines the bioactivity and stability of these metabolites. This suggests that Z53 could serve as a candidate for microbial cell factory development to produce notoginsenoside R1, while retaining its core biocontrol and plant growth-promoting traits.

Conclusion

The present study provides comprehensive genomic, metabolomic, biochemical, and physiological insights into the biocontrol potential of T. virilente Z53 against C. orchidophilum in P. yunnanensis. In vitro dual-culture and volatile-mediated antagonism assays collectively demonstrated that Z53 possesses potent antifungal activity against a broad range of phytopathogenic fungi. Consistently, pot experiments confirmed that Z53 can significantly reduce the severity of anthracnose caused by C. orchidophilum in P. yunnanensis. Furthermore, Z53 exhibited a prominent plant growth-promoting effect on maize, which collectively validates its effectiveness as both a biocontrol agent and a PGPM. The genome of T. virilente Z53 harbors a variety of BGCs responsible for producing bioactive metabolites, as well as genes associated with phosphate solubilization, nitrogen fixation, cell wall and plasma membrane formation, siderophore production, and host defense response priming. These genetic elements collectively lay the molecular foundation for Z53’s beneficial interactions with host plants and its adaptation to the rhizosphere environment. Furthermore, Z53 contains a large repertoire of CAZyme-encoding genes, which can produce multiple hydrolytic enzymes. These CAZymes endow Z53 with a competitive advantage over fungal pathogens by degrading pathogen cell walls, facilitating nutrient acquisition, and remodeling the rhizosphere microenvironment, thereby enhancing the host plant’s tolerance to biotic stresses. LC-MS data confirmed that Z53 produces a diverse array of antimicrobial compounds, including luteolin, trichodermin, viridin, isoprene, and 2-pentylfuran. These metabolites act synergistically with hydrolases to directly inhibit pathogenic fungi. Furthermore, Z53 produces a range of plant growth-promoting compounds, such as indole-3-acetic acid, gibberellin A7, phosphoric acid, and L-tryptophan, thereby enhancing plant growth. Collectively, the robust biocontrol activity of T. virilente Z53 may arise from the synergy of multiple complementary mechanisms: direct pathogen inhibition through the combined action of antimicrobial metabolites and hydrolytic enzymes, indirect host protection by triggering systemic immune responses, and the promotion of host plant growth.

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that T. virilente Z53 holds great promise as an ideal candidate strain for integrated disease management strategies in sustainable agriculture. Endowed with the combined traits of pathogen suppression, plant growth promotion, and defense response induction, Z53 provides a robust alternative to chemical fungicides for controlling C. orchidophilum and other phytopathogens.
