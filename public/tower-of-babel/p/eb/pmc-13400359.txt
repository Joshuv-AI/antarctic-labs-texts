When the heart is torn apart: Dual rupture after acute myocardial infarction
Authors: Soukaina Cherkaoui, Mohamed Sarsari, Houda Bachri, Jamila Zarzur, Mohamed Cherti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPost-infarction mechanical complications, including ventricular septal rupture and right ventricular free-wall rupture, are rare but fatal. Early recognition is critical to improve outcomes. A 95-year-old man with pulmonary fibrosis experienced chest pain three days before hospitalization. On admission, he had inferior STEMI with second-degree atrioventricular block, outside the reperfusion window. The next day, he developed recurrent chest pain and hemodynamic shock. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a muscular inferno-septal ventricular septal rupture (VSR) combined with right ventricular free-wall rupture (RVFWR). The coexistence of VSR and RVFWR is exceedingly rare and carries high mortality, highlighting the importance of early multimodality imaging and prompt diagnosis.Keywords: Ventricular septal rupture, Right ventricular free-wall rupture, Inferior myocardial infarction, Mechanical complication

Introduction

Mechanical complications after acute myocardial infarction (AMI) are uncommon but are major cause of early mortality, particularly in elderly patients. Ventricular septal rupture (VSR) occurs in approximately 0.2-0.3% of AMI cases, while right ventricular free-wall rupture (RVFWR) is even less frequent, at around 0.2% [1,2]. These complications are more likely to occur in older patients, those with first myocardial infarctions, delayed or absent reperfusion, and infarctions affecting the inferior or lateral walls [3]. Post-infarction VSR and right ventricular free wall rupture represent severe structural damage to the heart that led to rapid hemodynamic collapse. While isolated VSR or right ventricular free-wall rupture has been described, the combination of both is exceptionally rare, with only a few cases reported in the literature. We present such a case in a 95-year- old man, highlighting the clinical presentation, echocardiographic findings, and the importance of rapid diagnosis in patients at risk.

Case presentation

We report the case of a 95-year-old patient with no modifiable cardiovascular risk factors, and a history of pulmonary fibrosis under inhaled corticosteroids who presented to the emergency department with acute chest pain.

He reported several episodes of exertional angina over the week preceding his hospitalization, which had been neglected. Three days before admission, he experienced a prolonged episode of severe retrosternal pain radiating to the left arm, consistent with an acute coronary syndrome.

On admission, the patient was pain-free. Physical examination was unremarkable. Blood pressure was 124/70 mmHg and heart rate was within the normal range. Cardiovascular and pulmonary auscultation were normal, with no audible murmurs or signs of heart failure.

The electrocardiogram (ECG) showed ST-segment elevation in the inferior leads, a right bundle branch block (RBBB), and a first-degree atrioventricular (AV) block (Fig. 1). Laboratory testing revealed markedly elevated troponin and NT-probing levels, suggestive of myocardial injury, along with acute kidney injury (increased urea and creatinine) and signs of systemic inflammation (elevated C-reactive protein, D-dimers, and leukocytosis) (Table 1).

The following day, the patient developed sudden-onset severe chest pain accompanied by diaphoresis and profound hypotension (blood pressure 74/43 mmHg, heart rate at 105 beats/min). Clinical examination revealed cold extremities, delayed capillary refill, and the appearance of a new harsh holosystolic murmur at the left sternal border, suggestive of a mechanical complication.

A repeat ECG demonstrated further ST-segment elevation in the inferior and posterior leads (Fig. 2). Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed akinesia of the inferior and inferno-septal walls, consistent with a recent transmural infarction. A color Doppler study demonstrated a basal inferno-septal rupture with a high-velocity left-to-right shunt, consistent with a post-infarction ventricular septal rupture (VSR)(video1). Additionally, the right ventricle appeared non-dilated and hypokinetic, with discontinuity of the free wall near the acute margin, resulting in communication with the pericardial space with a circumferential pericardial effusion was present (Fig. 3, Fig. 4).

These findings indicated a combined rupture of the interventricular septum and right ventricular free wall explaining the patient’s rapid hemodynamic collapse and the cardiogenic and obstructive shock secondary to hemopericardium.

Despite rapid initiation of reanimation, and introducing vasopressor support and oxygen therapy, the patient’s hemodynamic status deteriorated rapidly, and he succumbed within the hour following the onset of shock.

Discussion

Post-infarction ventricular septal rupture (VSR) and right ventricular free-wall rupture (RVFWR) are uncommon yet catastrophic complications of acute myocardial infarction (AMI), occurring in approximately 0.2-0.3% and 0.2% of cases, respectively [1,2]. Their incidence has markedly declined with the widespread adoption of early reperfusion therapy, particularly primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) [1]. Known predisposing factors include advanced age, female sex, first myocardial infarction, absence or delay of reperfusion, and infarction involving the inferior or lateral walls, particularly when the basal septum and adjacent right ventricle are affected [3].

In the present case, additional factors such as pulmonary fibrosis and chronic inhaled corticosteroid therapy may have further increased myocardial fragility. Chronic pulmonary fibrosis raises pulmonary vascular resistance, leading to sustained right ventricular (RV) pressure overload, which heightens vulnerability to ischemic injury. Corticosteroids, even when inhaled, may impair collagen synthesis and delay myocardial healing, predisposing to rupture.

Because this patient presented three days after the onset of symptoms, he was outside the reperfusion window recommended by current ESC and AHA/ACC guidelines. Delayed presentation allows extensive transmural necrosis to evolve, compromising myocardial integrity and predisposing to mechanical failure.

In inferior infarction, the basal and mid inferno-septal region is particularly exposed to high systolic pressures and shear stress, making it prone to rupture. The thin wall of the right ventricle, when exposed to the same necrotic weakening and hemodynamic stress, may rupture concomitantly. These combined mechanisms explain the incidence of simultaneous VSR and RVFWR, which is exceptionally rare and almost invariably fatal [1,4].

From a pathophysiological perspective, myocardial rupture results from enzymatic degradation of the extracellular matrix and progressive thinning of the infarcted wall. This process typically peaks between the third and fifth days following infarction, when inflammatory infiltration and macrophage activity have cleared necrotic debris, leaving structurally weak tissue. The basal to mid inferno-septal region experiences substantial left-to-right pressure gradients, endorsing septal rupture, while the thinner right ventricular wall succumbs more readily to additional hemodynamic stress. The right bundle branch block observed in this patient likely reflected chronic right-sided strain due to pulmonary fibrosis rather than an acute ischemic conduction delay.

Clinically, patients with mechanical complications often present with abrupt chest pain, hypotension, and signs of cardiogenic shock. A new harsh holosystolic murmur may indicate VSR, but in cases of RV rupture, rapid pericardial tamponade may cause collapse before auscultatory findings can be detected [5].

Transthoracic echocardiography remains essential for diagnosis, providing immediate visualization of the rupture site, quantification of shunt flow, and detection of pericardial effusion.

Definitive management is surgical repair, which remains the only curative treatment. However, surgical mortality is extremely high in elderly patients and in those with delayed presentation or profound hemodynamic instability [6,7]. Timing of surgery is crucial: urgent repair is indicated in patients with persistent shock, but operating during the acute necrotic phase carries a substantial risk of recurrent rupture due to fragile tissue. Temporary mechanical circulatory support such as intra-aortic balloon pump counter pulsation or venom-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation may be used as a bridge to surgery, offering hemodynamic stabilization while awaiting tissue fibrosis and better surgical conditions [6,8]. In selected high-risk or inoperable patients, percutaneous closure may be considered, although experience remains limited and outcomes variable. The prognosis of combined VSR and RVFWR is exceedingly poor, with in-hospital mortality approaching 100% without surgical intervention [2,7].

Even with surgery, survival beyond the acute phase is rare, especially in the very elderly. This case emphasizes the importance of early recognition of infarction and timely reperfusion to prevent transmural necrosis and mechanical complications. It also highlights the need for rapid bedside echocardiography in any patient with post-infarction hemodynamic deterioration and the value of early multidisciplinary coordination between cardiology, imaging, and cardiothoracic surgery teams to optimize outcomes.

Conclusion

Mechanical complications after myocardial infarction, such as ventricular septal rupture and right ventricular free-wall rupture, are rare but often fatal. Elderly patients and those presenting late are particularly at risk. This case illustrates the need for prompt recognition, urgent echocardiographic evaluation, and early involvement of a multidisciplinary team. Identifying high-risk features, including advanced age, delayed presentation, inferior infarction, and factors affecting myocardial healing, is crucial for timely intervention and improving patient outcomes.
