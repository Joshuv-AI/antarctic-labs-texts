Anaemia in early childhood: prevalence, co-existence with undernutrition, and its correlates in urban slums of Belagavi
Authors: Deshna Oswal, N. S. Mahantashetti, Mubashir Angolkar, Mayank Singh, Shivani Haritay, Madan Godbole
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnaemia poses a significant barrier to a child’s growth and development, particularly in urban slums. Additionally, anaemia frequently co-exists with other forms of undernutrition, exacerbating health risks. The present analysis sought to determine the burden of anaemia, examine its co-existence with undernutrition, and identify its associated factors among children aged 9–36 months residing in the urban slums of Belagavi city.MethodsThis cross-sectional study analysed baseline data from a larger community-based study involving 137 mother-child pairs. Data on child, maternal, and household characteristics were gathered using a pre-structured questionnaire. Venous blood samples were analysed to determine haemoglobin levels, and anthropometric measurements were used to assess nutritional status. Descriptive statistics, univariate, and multivariable analyses were conducted to assess associations between potential risk factors, anaemia, and its co-existence with undernutrition.ResultsThe prevalence of anaemia among children residing in urban slums was 62%. Co-existence of anaemia with stunting, wasting, and underweight was observed among 26.8%, 6.5%, and 17.4%of the children, respectively. Maternal height ≥ 150 cm was associated with higher odds of anaemia (AOR 2.70; 95% CI 1.04–7.01); given the small short-stature subgroup (n = 29), this estimate is imprecise and should be interpreted cautiously. Additionally, low birth weight and lower maternal education were associated with the co-existence of anaemia and undernutrition.ConclusionsA high burden of anaemia was reported among children living in urban slums of Belagavi. The frequent co-occurrence of anaemia with stunting is concerning and underscores the need for future research. An unexpected, imprecise association between maternal height ≥ 150 cm and anaemia warrant cautious interpretation and confirmation in larger studies. Normal birth weight and higher maternal education were protective against the co-existence of anaemia and undernutrition.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26404-6.

Introduction

Early childhood represents a critical opportunity for shaping children’s physical, intellectual, and social development, laying the groundwork for their future [1]. However, anaemia—characterized by low haemoglobin concentration—pose a major barrier to achieving this potential. It disproportionately affects young children, impairing cognitive and physical development, compromises immunity, increases the susceptibility to infections and contributes to high child mortality. Its effects often persists in adulthood, reducing both productivity and overall quality of life [2–5].

Anaemia has diverse and multifactorial aetiology, including nutrient deficiencies, genetic disorders, and infections. Among these, Iron-Deficiency Anaemia (IDA) accounts for more than 50% of all cases [2, 6]. Despite longstanding interventions, anaemia continues to persist due to its complex pathophysiology and wide disparities in prevalence across regions [7–9].

Globally, approximately 40% of children under five are anaemic, with the highest burden reported in African and South-East Asia Region. In India, 67% of children aged 6–59 months are anaemic, highlighting a substantial public health challenge [2, 3, 10, 11]. Karnataka, a South western state of India reports an anaemia prevalence of 65.5%, among children, and urban settings are not exempt. In urban areas, 62.8% children are anaemic with those residing in urban slums being particularly vulnerable due to overcrowding, poor sanitation, and inadequate maternal education, and limited access to health care [12–14].

The situation is further exacerbated by the frequent coexistence of anaemia with other forms of undernutrition - such as wasting, stunting and underweight [15]. These conditions often share common causes like poor diet, frequent infections, and maternal malnutrition, creating a vicious cycle further undermining child health outcomes [13, 16, 17]. Yet, most of the existing research focus on anaemia and undernutrition in isolation, with limited studies exploring their co-occurrence, particularly in vulnerable urban slums.

Despite the high vulnerability of children in urban slums, there is a lack of literature assessing the burden of anaemia and its co-existence with undernutrition in these environments- especially in Belagavi, Karnataka. Therefore, the present analysis aims to examine the prevalence of anaemia, investigate its co-existence with anthropometric forms of undernutrition, and identify associated determinants among children aged 9–36 months in the urban slums of Belagavi, Karnataka. These findings aim to provide critical insights for informing targeted and integrated interventions to improve child related health outcomes.

Data and methods

This section details the cross-section analysis of a parent Randomized Control Trial CTRI Reg no: CTRI/2022/06/043002) conducted in Urban slums of Belagavi, Karnataka. It covers the study design and population, eligibility criteria, sample size determination, variables assessed, data collection and quality control procedure, and statistical method used.

Study design and population

The present study was a cross-sectional study conducted from March 2023 to August 2023 in Urban slums of Belagavi city, Karnataka, India. Belagavi city is a North-western part of Karnataka state located at 15° 52ʹ12ʺN and 74° 5ʹE. It has 49 slums, spread over 2.337 sq.km. The total slum population comprises of 11.67% of the total city’s population.

Eligibility criteria

Children aged 9–36 months who were residents of the slums and whose parents provided informed consent were included in the study. Children with a history of chronic illnesses such as genetic disorders, congenital heart disease, or severe malnutrition, or milk intolerance, were excluded.

Data collection tools and procedure

A structured and pre-tested questionnaire based on previous literature was utilized to collect required data. The questionnaire was divided into three sections, first part covered socio-demographic characteristic of the child, second part focused on dietary characteristics like type of diet consumed by the child, detailed dietary recall of previous 24 h and history of food consumed in past three days, along with a history regarding any infections in past three months. The last part of the questionnaire addressed data related to the mothers of study participants. The questionnaire was translated from English to local language (Kannada) and back translated to ensure accuracy. Field investigators were trained by the Principal Investigator to collect data from the questionnaire and measure anthropometric measurements. Data collection process involved interview of mothers, review of mother and child health card, anthropometric measurements, and haematologic examinations.

Anthropometric measurement

Standard procedure was used to collect anthropometric data; with the average of three replicate measurements taken as the final value. Height/Length was measured using a stadiometer/infantometer to the nearest of 0.1 cm, while weight was measured using Digital Seca scale to the nearest 0.1 kg. WHO Anthro software was used to calculate Z-scores.

Blood sample collection

With informed consent from the mother, venous blood samples were collected by a trained paediatric phlebotomist under aseptic conditions. Samples were transported in EDTA vacutainers under a cold chain to the diagnostic laboratory at KLE’s Dr. Prabhakar Kore Hospital and Medical Research Centre, Belagavi, where haemoglobin concentration was analysed using an automated haemoglobin analyser.

Data quality control

To ensure the quality of the data, the questionnaire was carefully designed, content was validated by experts (n = 7) in Paediatrics, Public health, and Research. It was then translated, and pre-tested in a pilot-study. Field investigators underwent a series of training sessions for 6 days to collect data. The principal investigator regularly checked the filled questionnaire for its completeness and consistency.

For haemoglobin estimation, manufacturer’s instructions and Standard Operating Procedure were strictly followed. Quality control measures included strict adherence to aseptic technique during blood collection, proper storage and handling of vacutainers. Standard protocols were followed in laboratory to ensure reliability.

Ethical consideration

The study protocol was approved by Institutional Ethics Committee, KLE Academy of Higher Education and Research, Belagavi (Ref no: KAHER/EC/21–22/250122001-G). Written Informed consent was obtained from all parents or caregivers. Confidentiality was maintained throughout the study. In alignment with ethical standards, appropriate actions were taken during data collection. Haemoglobin results were shared with caregivers immediately after testing, and mothers of children identified with moderate or severe anaemia were counselled and referred to the nearest Urban Primary Health Centre in accordance with IMNCI guidelines [21]. Counselling included advice on the consumption of iron-rich and vitamin C enhancing foods, the importance of regular deworming, and the use of footwear to prevent parasitic infections.

Statistical analysis

The collected data were entered into Microsoft Excel, coded, and exported to STATA version 17 for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics including mean, standard deviation, frequency, and proportions were computed to summarize the characteristics of the study population. We visually inspected the distribution of haemoglobin using a histogram with kernel-density overlay to assess data quality and outliers (Supplementary Figure). The primary outcome was anaemia (Hb < 11.0 g/dL, WHO) analysed as a binary variable; prevalence estimates are presented with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Co-occurrence was defined as anaemia plus at least one anthropometric deficit (stunting, wasting, or underweight) and coded as a binary variable (1 = co-occurs; 0 = otherwise). Furthermore, to assess the association, univariate logistic regression was performed. Characteristics of study participants (N = 137)

The mean HAZ score was − 1.6 ± 1.5, and that of WAZ and WHZ was − 1.3 ± 1.3 and − 0.6 ± 1.4, respectively. The mean BMI score was 0.4 ± 1.5 and haemoglobin concentration was 10.4 ± 1.3 g/dl.

Approximately 74% of children received exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months. The mean age of introduction of complementary feeding was 6.3 ± 2.6 months. At the time of data collection, the mean calorie intake was 579 ± 177Kcal, and the mean protein intake was 15.3 ± 5.7gm. A non-vegetarian diet was consumed by 81.7% of children.

Most (64.2%) of the children experienced diarrheal infection in past three months, and nearly half had a bout of respiratory tract infection. Clinical signs suggestive of Vitamin- D deficiency and anaemia were observed in 10.2% and 33.6% of children, respectively.

Maternal characteristics

The mean age of mothers was 26.6 ± 4.3 years, with 4.4% mothers being under 20 years of age. Majority of the mothers had education more than 8th standard (81%) while 4.4% were illiterate. The majority were housewife (92%), and of normal stature (78.8%). Regarding maternal BMI, 17.5% of mothers were underweight, and 36.5% were overweight/obese. Only 8% of mothers had birth spacing of less than 24 months, and 56.2% mothers had delivered via caesarean section. During pregnancy 68.5% of the mothers were anaemic, 37.2% mothers had fewer than four ANC visits, and 15.3% did not consume Iron Folic acid tablets.

Household characteristics

Hindu religion was most commonly reported (72.3%), followed by Muslim religion (24.8%), and others. About 48.2% of children belonged to families with more than five members.

Among feeding related characteristics, exclusive breast feeding for 6 months (63.4%) and those following a vegetarian (68.8%) or ovo-vegetarian (66.7%) diet showed a slightly higher prevalence of anaemia, but these differences were not statistically significant. Similarly children with signs of vitamin D deficiency (71.4%) and clinical signs of anaemia (65.2%) had a higher prevalence of anaemia compared to those without such signs, though not significant.

Regarding maternal characteristics, children of mothers ≤ 20 years (66.7%), of illiterate mothers (83.3%), of mothers working outside the home (72.7%) exhibited a higher prevalence of anaemia. Similarly other factors such as normal maternal BMI (68.2%), birth spacing of less than 24 months (72.7%), caesarean delivery (63.6%), maternal anaemia during pregnancy (62.9%), and more than four antenatal care (ANC) visits (67.4%) were associated with a higher anaemia prevalence, but were not statistically significant. Notably, maternal stature ≥ 150 cm was significantly associated with higher odds of anaemia in children (OR: 2.46, 95% CI: 1.07–5.67).

At the household level, no statistical difference in prevalence of anaemia was observed based on religion or size of family. However, a slightly higher prevalence was noted among children from Muslim households (64.7%) and smaller families (63.4%).

Discussion

Anaemia alongside under-nutrition is a lingering health problem among children, particularly those in urban slums settings. Growth of urban populace coupled with paucity of evidence on the burden of anaemia and its co-existence with undernutrition in these vulnerable population, demands for the current cross- sectional analysis. The findings highlight the significant burden of anaemia, its co-existence with undernutrition, and its intricate relationship with child, maternal, and household level factors in a population that is highly vulnerable due to poor living conditions and limited access to essential services.

Prevalence of anaemia

An important finding of this study was that the prevalence of anaemia was observed to be alarmingly high (62%), comparable with the prevalence reported in urban slums of Mumbai [23]. However, this prevalence is slightly lower than the state average for Karnataka. Notably, it aligns closely with district estimates for urban areas of Belagavi reported in 2019–2020. While this comparison does not specifically account for the burden in slums, but it underscores the persistent and severe burden of anaemia in urban setting, particularly among children residing in slums. According to WHO a prevalence of more than 40% is considered to be a severe public health problem [24]; therefore, anaemia represents a critical concern in this population.

Co-existence of anaemia with undernutrition

The co-existence of anaemia and stunting was observed in 26.8% of children consistent with findings from India and Ethiopia [16, 25, 26]. This dual burden reflects overlapping risk factors such as poor diet quality, low education level of mothers, poverty, food insecurity, and poor-sanitation [27]. The consistent overlap in disadvantaged populations indicates potential pathways warranting future investigation [26, 28].

Co-occurrence of anaemia with underweight was observed in 17.4% children and with wasting was 6.5%. A recent meta-analysis reported a pooled prevalence of anaemia-wasting co-existence at 5.44%, supporting our findings. There is scarcity of evidence looking at the co-existence of anaemia with wasting/underweight.

Determinants of anaemia

In the present study, multivariable regression analysis revealed that children of mothers with normal maternal height (≥ 150) had significantly higher risk of anaemia compared to those with shorter stature, an unexpected finding. The short stature subgroup was small (n = 29), and the confidence interval was wide (AOR 2.70; 95% CI 1.04–7.01), suggesting imprecision. Possible explanation includes residual confounding, differences in care or feeding practices, and selection bias. Although evidence linking maternal height to childhood anaemia is limited, prior work generally associates short maternal stature with adverse child nutritional outcomes [29]. The finding should therefore be interpreted with caution and explored in larger studies to clarify the pathways between maternal stature and childhood anaemia.

Several studies in India have identified common predictors of anaemia among children including younger age, male gender, lack of exclusive breast feeding, vegetarian diet, low maternal education, caste, region of residence, anaemia among mothers, advanced maternal age, and diarrheal infections [30–32].

Although not statistically significant, several associations observed were consistent with previous literature. Older children (24–36 months) had 59% lower odds of anaemia compared to younger children (9–11 months), reflecting breast milk being a poor source of iron and inadequate intake of iron in complementary feeding [33]. Similarly, children with normal birth weight, full-term birth, and mothers with higher education showed reduced anaemia risk. These trends reinforce the multifactorial nature of childhood anaemia, influenced by biological, perinatal, and socio-demographic factors.

Factors associated with co-existence of anaemia with undernutrition

Children with normal birth weight (≥ 2.5 kg) had significantly lower odds of concurrent anaemia with both stunting and underweight, consistent with existing evidence. Low birth weight (LBW) is a well-established risk factor for both undernutrition and anaemia, and is associated with a range of adverse health outcomes. Children born with LBW often experience inadequate fetal nutrition, reduced capacity to absorb and utilize nutrients efficiently, and an increased vulnerability to infections [25, 32]. Similarly, maternal education beyond 8th standard was significantly associated with lower odds of anaemia-stunting co-occurrence supporting the evidence that maternal education contributes to improved child care practices and better health outcomes [34, 35].

Although not statistically significant, several other trends observed in the present study—such as increased risk among children aged 12–23 months, male children, and children of anaemic mothers—are supported by previous research from low- and middle-income countries [17, 25, 26, 35, 36].

No significant associations were found for the co-occurrence of anaemia and wasting. This could reflect the acute nature of wasting and distinct causal pathways compared to stunting and underweight, highlighting the need of more research into this relationship.

Strengths and limitations

This community-based study provides valuable insights into the prevalence of anaemia, its co-existence with undernutrition, and associated factors among children residing in urban slums- a high-risk and under- researched population. It contributes to the limited body of evidence on the interrelationship between micronutrient deficiencies and anthropometric indicators of undernutrition in early childhood. A key strength of this study is the use of venous blood samples, which enhances the accuracy and reliability of haemoglobin estimation. The study took into account various factors that could be associated with anaemia or its co-occurrence with undernutrition.

However, several limitations warrants consideration. Its cross-sectional design limits the ability to infer causality, and the relatively small sample size may have reduced the statistical power to detect associations for some key variables. The higher caesarean section rate likely reflects delivery patterns in the study area and should be interpreted with caution. Less than half of the enrolled participants consented to provide blood sample for Haemoglobin estimation, which may have introduced potential selection bias. Data on social determinants such as participation in welfare schemes, social safety nets, or health insurance were not collected, limiting the assessment of structural influences on anaemia. In addition, the absence of detailed dietary intake data restricts a comprehensive understanding of the specific nutritional drivers of anaemia and undernutrition. Finally, the findings only be generalizable to the urban slum settings in India.

Conclusion

Anaemia was highly prevalent (62%) among children residing in urban slums of Belagavi city. The co-existence of anaemia with undernutrition, particularly stunting, is concerning and warrants attention. Maternal height was significantly associated with anaemia, normal birth weight and higher maternal education were associated with lower odds of the co-existence of anaemia and undernutrition. While findings should be read in light of the study’s limitations, they add to the limited evidence base and underscore the need for context-specific, integrated anaemia–nutrition strategies in urban slum settings.

Recommendation

Although only maternal height was significantly associated with anaemia in our study, trends in other variables and previous literature suggest multifactorial nature of anaemia. This underscores the need of targeted, context specific strategies tailored to urban slums population.

Future research should explore the shared possible nutritional, environmental, and biological pathways between anaemia and undernutrition. Understanding whether their co-existence demands integrated approach. Longitudinal studies incorporating detailed dietary assessments will be crucial to uncovering causal pathways and guiding the development of comprehensive, evidence-based strategies in prevention and management of anaemia and its overlap with undernutrition.
