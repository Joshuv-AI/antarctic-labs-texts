Less may be enough: a transplant-free future for low-risk relapsed Hodgkin lymphoma
Authors: Christopher J Forlenza, Sharon M Castellino
Published: 2025

Approximately 15% of patients with classical Hodgkin lymphoma (cHL), the most common cancer in adolescents, are at risk for relapse/refractory (R/R) disease after conventional chemotherapy with or without consolidative radiation therapy (RT). For decades these patients received reinduction with second-line chemotherapy and responders would be consolidated with high-dose chemotherapy (HDCT) and autologous stem cell transplant (ASCT). This approach in children and adolescents was supported by two randomized clinic trials in adult patients which demonstrated an advantage for HDCT/ASCT in comparison to conventional dose chemotherapy (1,2). Despite the absence of randomized data in pediatric patients, this approach was widely adopted and resulted in prolonged progression-free survival (PFS) in ~50% of patients (3,4). Results from the Checkmate 744 study titled “Transplant-Free Approach in Relapsed Hodgkin Lymphoma in Children, Adolescents, and Young Adults: A Nonrandomized Clinical Trial” (5) published in JAMA Oncology, challenge the standard approach to the management of R/R cHL using a risk-stratified, response-adapted approach with patients initially receiving nivolumab plus brentuximab vedotin (BV) and partial responders receiving BV and bendamustine, the study reports outcomes for a low-risk cohort, including complete metabolic response (CMR) rate prior to RT and 3-year event-free survival (EFS) in the context of a de-escalated transplant-free salvage.

The utilization of HDCT and ASCT has been associated with increased risks of late effects (6-9). These include subsequent malignant neoplasm, infections, chronic health conditions (mostly commonly cardiovascular and endocrine disease), and a nearly 10-fold increase in late mortality compared to the general population (6,7,9,10). Hopes to avoid or minimize late effects led to a growing body of literature indicating that HDCT/ASCT may be unnecessary in a subset of patients considered to be low-risk for a subsequent recurrence (11,12). The advent of novel agents, like the CD30 targeted antibody-drug conjugate, BV, and checkpoint inhibitors (CPIs) (nivolumab and pembrolizumab), into second-line therapy prior to ASCT has resulted in notable improvements with long-term PFS now approaching 80% (4,13-16).

While pediatric treatment for frontline cHL has employed risk-stratified, response-adapted approaches the concept of risk-adapted approaches to avoiding ASCT in R/R cHL had not been tested to date (17). In the Children’s Oncology Group (COG)-Euronet collaborative Checkmate 744 study, which was non-randomized phase 2 multicenter risk-stratified, response-adapted trial, Daw et al. investigated a transplant-free approach targeting a subset of low-risk patients that received second-line therapy with nivolumab in combination with BV followed by consolidation with involved site radiation therapy (ISRT) rather than HDCT/ASCT (5). Eligibility for the low-risk cohort were 5–30 years of age, no B symptoms, or extranodal disease and eligibility to receive RT in addition to meeting one of the following criteria: (I) stage IA/B, IIA/B, or IIIA at initial diagnosis with relapse >12 months from initial therapy or (II) stage IA/IIA with a relapse within 3 to 12 months from initial treatment having received ≤3 cycles of treatment and no prior RT. Trial patients received 4 cycles of nivolumab with BV followed by a repeat positron emission tomography (PET) assessment. Patients achieving a CMR, defined as a Deauville score ≤3, received 2 additional cycles of nivolumab with BV. Patients with suboptimal response received 2 additional cycles of BV with bendamustine. All patients with CMR after induction or intensification received ISRT at a dose of 30 to 30.6 Gy. Among 28 patients enrolled, 26/28 (93%) achieved a CMR at any point prior to RT and 3-year EFS and PFS is 87% [90% confidence interval (CI): 69.5–94.7%] and 95% (90% CI: 76.7–99.0%), respectively. Treatment was well tolerated with low rates of grade 3/4 treatment-related adverse events. Immune-mediated adverse events, which is a feared complication of CPI-based therapy were limited. This was the first study to utilize novel therapeutic approaches combining BV and CPI in a transplant-free salvage setting. In addition to minimizing the toxic impact of ASCT, this strategy avoids the traditional high-dose alkylator-based cytotoxic chemotherapies used in second-line therapy and supports further investigation of transplant-free approaches to relapsed cHL within the framework of a randomized trial.

Two additional trials evaluating the paradigm of transplant-free salvage were contemporaneously published alongside the results of Checkmate 744. Hoppe et al. reported the results of the embedded salvage arm of the COG study AHOD0431. Frontline therapy for low-risk cHL on this study consisted of 3 cycles of AV-PC (doxorubicin, vincristine, prednisone, and cyclophosphamide) +/− RT. Patients that experienced a low-stage (IA/IIA), non-bulky relapse following a complete response (CR) to upfront therapy received 2 cycles of IV (ifosfamide, vinorelbine) alternating with 2 cycles of DECA (dexamethasone, etoposide, cisplatin, cytarabine) followed by 21 Gy involved-field RT (IFRT). Thirty-two patients experienced a low-risk recurrence by protocol definition and 20 completed therapy on the salvage arm. The 5- and 8-year 2nd EFS was 85% (95% CI: 60.4–94.9%) and 78.5% (95% CI: 51.8–91.4%), respectively without ASCT (18). Euronet-PHL-R1 was a non-randomized, single-arm clinical trial that enrolled patients <18 years of age with first relapse of cHL and risk stratified patients by time to recurrence, initial therapy, and response. In this study, 2 cohorts of patients were considered low-risk and eligible for a transplant-free salvage: (I) late relapse, occurring >12 months after initial therapy and limited initial treatment (2 cycles) and (II) relapse >3 months after initial therapy in addition to a PET-negative response (Deauville 1–2) and ≥50% reduction in tumor volume on early response assessment after 2 salvage cycles. In total low-risk patients received 4 alternating cycles of IEP (ifosfamide, etoposide, prednisolone) and ABVD (doxorubicin, bleomycin, vinblastine, dacarbazine) followed by consolidative RT (20–30 Gy) to all involved nodes. Fifty-nine patients were classified as low-risk, with 41 receiving non-transplant based salvage and resulted in a 5-year PFS of 89.7% (95% CI: 80.7–99.8%) and OS of 97.4% (95% CI: 92.6–100%) (19).

These trials utilized similar parameters for risk stratification, conservatively considering initial staging, frontline treatment, and response to salvage chemotherapy (Table 1). While the second line therapies between studies varied, all patients received consolidative RT. It is important to recognize that each of these studies was relatively small and in the case of the Checkmate 744 and Euronet-PHL-R1 studies follow-up was short. Yet collectively, they provide a sound basis for challenging the standard approach for low-risk patients. Thankfully, preliminary efforts to address these critical questions are underway. Notably, Moskowitz et al. evaluated pembrolizumab in combination with gemcitabine, vinorelbine, and liposomal doxorubicin (GVD), followed by pembrolizumab maintenance, without RT, as an alternative to ASCT in CPI-naïve adults with R/R cHL of any stage (20). At a median follow-up of 23.4 months, the 2-year PFS was 51% (95% CI: 33–80%), with relapse-stage IV disease associated with a higher risk of progression. These findings underscore both the potential consequences of considering transplant-free approaches in patients with a more extensive disease burden and also raise the possibility that RT is a vital consolidative intervention in the absence of HDCT/ASCT. A successor study is randomizing patients with stage I–III disease to HDCT/ASCT or pembrolizumab maintenance with ISRT (NCT03618550). The impact of prolonged CPI inhibitor use, particularly in terms of cost and late effects in children, adolescents, and young adults, is an additional question of importance.

Rates of combined-modality therapy for patients with newly diagnosed cHL are on the decline. In recent decades, response-adapted approaches have been an area of extensive investigation; utilizing CT and PET-based response to guide the inclusion of RT (21-23). More recently, the incorporation of nivolumab and BV into frontline therapy resulted in excellent outcomes despite a minimal use of RT (24). As a result, an increasing proportion of patients are receiving second-line therapy without a prior history of RT. Although concerns remain about the late effects of radiation (10) modern techniques, such as proton therapy, intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT), and contemporary RT fields-have significantly advanced the ability to limit treatment areas, reduce exposure to risk organs, and minimize late effects (25-27) making RT an increasingly viable alternative to ASCT. Whether RT, maintenance therapy, or some combination of both provide adequate consolidation will be a necessary focus of future investigations.

The Checkmate 744 study, along with other recent trials, represent a meaningful shift in the treatment paradigm for relapsed cHL, challenging the long-standing reliance on HDCT and ASCT, particularly for low-risk patients. The integration of novel agents like BV and CPIs, along with more targeted use of RT, offers a promising path toward reducing long-term toxicity without compromising disease control. While studies provide compelling evidence that “less may be enough” for select patients, randomized trials are essential to define the boundaries of transplant-free approaches, identify which patients are most likely to benefit following failure in the setting of frontline immunotherapy, what methods of consolidation are suitable and ensure that evolving treatment paradigms are both safe and effective.
