Advancing the Frontiers of HfO2‐Based Ferroelectric Memories: Innovative Concepts from Materials to Applications
Authors: Zuopu Zhou, Lingqi Li, Yang Feng, Xiaolin Wang, Zijie Zheng, Leming Jiao, Haofei Zheng, Yufei Shi, Kah‐Wee Ang, Xiao Gong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe emergence of HfO2‐based ferroelectric materials has revolutionized ferroelectric memory technologies, which overcome several key challenges of conventional ferroelectric materials and exhibit outstanding performance, aligning with the surging demands from data‐centric computing. In this review, a comprehensive overview of recent developments in HfO2‐based ferroelectric memories is provided, spanning materials research, device engineering, integration strategies, and emerging applications. At the material level, advances in thickness and grain size scaling are discussed, as well as the investigation on polarization switching dynamics and cryogenic behavior. On the device front, progress is examined in ferroelectric field‐effect transistors (FeFETs), including alternative channel materials and gate stack designs, as well as novel device concepts beyond traditional FeFET architectures. Integration strategies to realize high‐density, high‐bandwidth memories, including the 3D stacking architectures and monolithic integration with logic transistors, are also reviewed. Finally, applications are explored that extend beyond conventional data storage. Through this multi‐faceted review, the aim is to identify both the technological opportunities and remaining challenges associated with HfO2‐based ferroelectric memories, with the goal of inspiring further innovations in this emerging field.

Introduction

The history of ferroelectric materials can be traced back to the 1920s when ferroelectricity was first observed in Rochelle salt.[ 1 , 2 ] However, due to fragility and water‐solubility, the very first generation of ferroelectric materials did not show much potential for applications. The distinctive material property regained research interest after the middle of the 20th century when the ABO3 perovskite‐based ferroelectric materials were developed, e.g., BaTiO3 and PbTiO3.[ 2 , 3 ] These stable materials with robust ferroelectricity fueled the explorations of various potential applications. Especially, the spontaneous electric polarization reversible under the external electric field and retainable without field makes the ferroelectric material a promising medium for non‐volatile data storage integrated with electronic systems. Several ferroelectric memory device paradigms were proposed and first demonstrated based on perovskite‐based ferroelectric materials, including ferroelectric random‐access memory (FeRAM), ferroelectric field‐effect transistor (FeFET), and ferroelectric tunnel junction (FTJ). Among them, FeRAM was the first commercialized ferroelectric memory. At the end of last century, companies including Ramtron, Texas Instruments, and Fujitsu launched their FeRAM products.[ 4 ] However, till the new century arrived, the market share of ferroelectric memory was still limited. In addition to the competitive pressure from the booming Flash memory technologies, which rapidly dominated the non‐volatile memory market, several intrinsic challenges associated with perovskite‐based ferroelectric memory have further reduced the competitiveness and hindered its market development. The introduction of perovskite materials raised concerns regarding their compatibility with modern complementary‐metal‐oxide‐semiconductor (CMOS) technology. Besides, the scalability of perovskite‐based devices failed to keep pace with the increasing demand for higher memory density. Furthermore, FeRAM suffers from destructive read operation, while the non‐destructive reading devices like FeFET remained elusive due to the short retention. This retention issue stems from a high depolarization field relative to the coercive field and significant charge trapping at the poor interfaces.[ 5 , 6 ]

The search for new ferroelectric materials is still on‐going. In 2011, the discovery of ferroelectricity in HfO2‐based materials redirected the trajectory of ferroelectric research.[ 4 , 7 ] HfO2‐based high‐k dielectric materials have already been widely adopted in modern CMOS technologies, enabling ferroelectric memory devices to leverage existing CMOS and mainstream memory fabrication infrastructure. Notable examples include FeFETs fabricated on advanced high‐k metal gate platforms and FeRAM developed based on 4F2 dynamic random‐access memory (DRAM) architectures.[ 8 , 9 , 10 ] Additionally, the robust ferroelectricity retained at a film thicknesses below 5 nm, combined with a high coercive field (approximately one order higher than that of perovskites) and superior interface quality when integrated with semiconductors (with trap concentrations at least one order lower than that of perovskites), supports effective thickness scaling in both metal‐ferroelectric‐metal (MFM) capacitors and metal‐ferroelectric‐semiconductor (MFS) gate stacks.[ 6 ] Such advantages enhance the compatibility of ferroelectric devices with advanced technology nodes, providing scalability in both density and operation voltages while maintaining reliable memory performance.

Theoretical study indicates that the various intrinsic superiorities of HfO2‐based ferroelectric materials originate from their unique fluorite structure. Different from the perovskite ferroelectrics, where strong dipole interactions cause polarization switching to propagate from nucleation, the HfO2‐based materials possess a structural characteristic that naturally forms spacers, effectively screening the elastic interactions among active ferroelectric dipoles. This structure stabilizes the localized dipole switching within half‐unit cell width (≈3 Å) and results in a large coercive field. Theoretical calculations further predict that the ferroelectric order in HfO2 can be sustained at sub‐nanometer lateral and perpendicular dimensions. These findings not only support the extreme scaling potential of HfO2‐based ferroelectric films in terms of both area and thickness but also demonstrate the possibility of stable multi‐level switching in highly scaled devices.[ 11 ]

The material breakthrough sparked a new wave of research in ferroelectric memories, further accelerated by the growing demand for data‐centric computing arising from rapid advances in artificial intelligence (AI). Over the past decade, the convergence of novel materials and emerging demands has inspired a series of innovations across multiple areas. The new class of ferroelectric materials exhibits a range of intriguing properties, which have prompted targeted material optimization strategies. At the device level, innovations include the integration of advanced semiconductor materials, the development of novel designs, and the exploration of new operation mechanisms that extend beyond FeRAM, FeFET, and FTJ. These advances have further enabled the investigation of novel integration strategies and broadened the scope of potential applications. Since entering the 2020s, research in this field has not only deepened—yielding considerable progress in understanding underlying mechanisms and refining existing optimization approaches—but also expanded. Efforts have been increasingly directed toward overcoming the inherent challenges, enabling new functionalities, and exploring emerging application opportunities beyond conventional memory use. Moreover, cross‐level co‐optimization has gained momentum, emphasizing bottom‐up and application‐specific designs, particularly for AI‐driven near‐memory and in‐memory computing.

Over the years, numerous studies have investigated HfO2‐based ferroelectric memories, with representative progresses summarized in several review articles. Schroeder et al. discussed the properties and origin of ferroelectricity in HfO2‐based materials, highlighting the physical factors influencing the phase formation and surveying their applications.[ 12 ] Shao et al. reviewed the ferroelectric HfO2 from the material preparation and physical mechanism to the memory devices and their critical challenges.[ 13 ] Liao et al. summarized the behavior and reliability characteristics of HfO2‐based ferroelectric thin films with a focus on their physical mechanisms and material origins.[ 14 ] Zagni et al. provided a comprehensive review of the reliability of HfO2‐based FeFETs, including the characteristics, physics, and evaluation for specific applications.[ 15 ] Lee et al. concentrated on the oxygen vacancies in HfO2, summarizing their formation and the influence on the materials and devices.[ 16 ] Park et al. introduced the research progresses on the physical down scaling of HfO2‐based ferroelectric films, showing the size effect of the films on both ferroelectric properties and device electrical performance.[ 17 ] The achievements reviewed in these articles provide the solid foundation of HfO2‐based ferroelectric memory technology. These experimental phenomenon, physical mechanisms, and theoretical frameworks have been supporting the constant exploration of new opportunities in this field. However, focusing on the most popular research directions, the existing reviews emphasize mainstream strategies on the challenges and conventional explanations on the mechanisms. The discussion of devices is largely confined to the three typical ferroelectric memories: FeRAM, FeFET, and FTJ. Some recent discoveries regarding physics and innovations in devices, integration, and applications may be missing.

In this review, we highlight the recent progress in HfO2‐based ferroelectric memories, with a focus on several emerging and innovative directions in material studies, device designs, integration strategies, and potential applications (Figure 1 ). By presenting these advances, we aim to provide a snapshot of the current frontiers in HfO2‐based ferroelectric memory research, illustrating the breadth of ongoing efforts, the opportunities on the horizon, and the challenges that remain.

Material Investigation and Optimization

Advancements in materials serve as a key driving force for the development of ferroelectric memory technologies. The superiorities offered by HfO2‐based ferroelectric materials have directly contributed to enhanced memory performance. However, to meet the escalating demands of future applications, ferroelectric materials must be investigated under more stringent criteria. High‐density integration requires not only thickness scaling to below 10 nm, but also lateral scaling of the film area to match the advanced technology nodes. The polarization switching should be assessed in nanosecond or even picosecond range to support high‐speed memory operations for use at higher levels of the memory hierarchy. Meanwhile, emerging application scenarios such as quantum information processing, space technology, and high‐performance computing are driving growing interest in material behaviors at cryogenic temperatures.

Numerous studies of HfO2‐based ferroelectric materials have been conducted to achieve a comprehensive understanding of their intrinsic material behavior as well as to develop tailored optimization strategies. Specifically, the polycrystalline nature of HfO2‐based films was investigated, which is critical to the uniformity of highly‐scaled devices and the polarization switching dynamics. Besides, the complex phase transition behavior of HfO2‐based materials was studied to support the aggressive thickness scaling under 5 nm and to evaluate the material cryogenic performance.

This chapter reviews recent advances and key insights in the thickness scaling, grain size scaling, polarization switching dynamics, and cryogenic behavior of HfO2‐based ferroelectric materials for high‐density, high‐speed memory and emerging application scenarios.

Thickness Scaling

Fully exploiting the low‐power and high‐density potential of HfO2‐based ferroelectric memories necessitates aggressive scaling of the active layer thickness. Numerous studies have examined the scalability of HfO2‐based ferroelectric films, revealing critical changes in ferroelectric properties as the thickness decreases below 5 nm.[ 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 ] Figure 2a‐i illustrates the polarization versus electric field (P‐E) hysteresis curves of MFM capacitors with Zr‐doped HfO2 (HZO) film thicknesses ranging from 10 to 2 nm, indicating that ultra‐thin HZO films usually exhibit a noticeable remnant polarization (P r) degradation, which poses challenges for their integration into advanced 3D structures. First‐principles calculations attribute this behavior to phase stability changes that are dependent on grain size, doping, and annealing temperature.[ 23 ] According to nucleation theory, crystallization initiates from the grains with the smallest sizes, favoring the phase with the lowest free energy relative to amorphous HfO2 (Figure 2a‐ii).[ 24 , 25 ] Grain size reduces during the thinning down of ferroelectric films, which promotes dominance of the tetragonal (t‐) phase during thermal processing and thereby degrades the ferroelectric response. Moreover, surface energy effects further stabilize the t‐phase when the HZO thickness falls below 5 nm.[ 12 ] The suppression of ferroelectricity in ultra‐thin HZO films arises from both the thermodynamic stabilization of the t‐phase, which is driven by reduced grain size and surface energy effects below 5 nm, and the emergence of pronounced leakage currents. The leakage current enhancement is primarily attributed to tunneling effects, defect‐induced conduction paths, and grain boundary‐mediated leakage.[ 26 , 27 ] This high leakage current adversely impacts ferroelectric memory performance by accelerating fatigue and increasing power consumption. Interestingly, as the HZO thickness scales down, an increase in breakdown field is observed.[ 28 ] Due to the dielectric breakdown mechanism primarily dominated by voltage,[ 28 , 29 ] thinner films experience lower absolute voltages under the same electric field compared to thicker counterparts, leading to reduced stress and improved endurance.[ 28 ] Moreover, due to the HZO thickness scales down, complete switching can be achieved at lower voltages in ultra‐thin HZO, thereby enhancing retention performance under the same operation voltage.[ 30 ]

Ultra‐thin HfO2‐based ferroelectric films have been achieved with several pioneering approaches. Cheema et al. demonstrated enhanced ferroelectricity in 1‐nm‐thick HZO films with significant oxygen polyhedral distortion and confinement strain.[ 18 ] Tian et al. directly characterized polarization switching in 3‐nm‐thick Y‐doped HfO2 films.[ 19 ] Chouprik et al. observed ferroelectric polarization in a 2.5‐nm‐thick HZO film integrated onto Si substrates,[ 20 ] while Gao et al. demonstrated ferroelectric hysteresis loops in HZO films as thin as 1.5 nm.[ 21 ] Nonetheless, these achievements exhibited modest polarization improvements under relatively high thermal budgets (>500 °C), with 2P r typically below 10 µC cm−2. Feng et al. proposed an innovative and low‐thermal‐budget (450 °C) approach based on oxygen‐vacancy‐assisted phase engineering, significantly enhancing polarization (2P r exceeding 40 µC cm−2) in 3 nm‐thick HZO films (Figure 2a‐iii).[ 22 ] This study also unveiled that in ultra‐thin HZO layers, the t‐phase is initially more stable due to its lower free energy compared to the orthorhombic (o‐) phase. However, as charged oxygen vacancy (Vo 2+) concentration increases beyond a critical threshold, the free energy difference between the t‐phase and o‐phase gradually diminishes, and eventually reverses, stabilizing o‐phase over the t‐phase (Figure 2a‐iv).

Grain Size Scaling

HfO2‐based ferroelectric thin films exhibit a polycrystalline microstructure when deposited by atomic layer deposition (ALD) or similar techniques. As shown in Figure 2b‐i, these films consist of grains with a broad size distribution (typically ranging from 10 to 70 nm) as well as variations in crystallographic orientation and phase composition. Characterization methods such as transmission Kikuchi diffraction (TKD) and grazing‐incidence X‐ray diffraction (GIXRD) have disclosed that the ferroelectric o‐phase (Pca21) preferentially forms in relatively smaller grains compared with m‐phase, whereas the non‐ferroelectric monoclinic (m‐) tends to appear when grain size exceeds ≈20 nm. This trend is consistent with thermodynamic predictions from density functional theory, which indicate that the o‐phase is energetically favored within an intermediate grain‐size regime due to a balance between surface and bulk energy, while the m‐phase becomes more stable as the grain size increases.[ 31 ]

This intrinsic polycrystalline nature introduces substantial challenges for device scaling and performance uniformity. In deeply scaled ferroelectric devices, the total number of grains per device active area is significantly reduced, amplifying statistical variation in switching behaviors among devices. The variation arising from stochastic single‐domain events and non‐uniform polarization narrows the sensing margin and increases the possibility of memory failure.[ 32 ] Furthermore, the random distribution of ferroelectric, anti‐ferroelectric, and non‐ferroelectric grains introduces both spatial and statistical inhomogeneities, which aggravate threshold voltage variation and degrade device reliability.[ 33 , 34 , 35 ] Particularly, when FeFET dimensions become comparable to grain size, risk of forming continuous leakage paths along the channel direction increases, which degrades the device performance and uniformity (Figure 2b‐ii).[ 36 ]

In addition, grain size reduction offers advantages for multi‐level memory and analog computing applications. Smaller grains allow finer modulation of polarization states, providing a greater number of achievable intermediate memory levels for high‐density data storage as well as more gradual and accurate weight updates for high‐precision analog synapses.[ 37 , 38 ]

These challenges and potential applications have motivated increasing interest in grain size engineering as a key strategy for minimizing device variation, stabilizing the desired ferroelectric phase, and improving the multi‐level memory characteristics. One effective approach involves optimizing the ALD cycle ratio of HfO2 and ZrO2 to tailor composition and crystallization behavior, which realized a reduction in average grain radius from 16.6 to 13 nm, along with an improved uniformity and P r.[ 39 ] Solid phase epitaxy (SPE) has also shown significant promise, enabling ≈30% grain size reduction and a 42% enhancement in P r under BEOL‐compatible processing conditions (Figure 2b‐iii).[ 40 , 41 ] Interlayer engineering, such as introducing thin Al2O3 or La2O3 layers, has also proven effective in interfering the crystal growth and further scaling of the grain size.[ 42 , 43 ]

Polarization Switching Dynamics

Today, advanced embedded DRAM (eDRAM) cell can be written within 5 ns, and embedded static random‐access memory (eSRAM) operates at sub‐nanosecond speed.[ 4 , 44 ] Thus, to compete with eDRAM or eSRAM at the high levels of memory hierarchy, ferroelectric memory is desired to achieve a comparable write speed. The polarization switching speed of the ferroelectric thin films determines the upper limit of ferroelectric memory write speed. However, directly probing polarization switching dynamics at nanosecond timescales is experimentally challenging, mainly due to the parasitics in the measurement setup. The associated RC delay effect distorts the applied voltage pulses and impedes the rapid polarization switching in ferroelectric capacitors, resulting in a longstanding underestimation of their switching speed.

To investigate the intrinsic polarization switching dynamics of HfO2‐based ferroelectric films, researchers have fabricated MFM ferroelectric capacitors with crossbar structure and reduced device area to control the RC effect. Coupled with a specialized measurement setup for ultrafast pulse generation and current sensing, this platform directly captured polarization changes at sub‐nanosecond timescales, demonstrating the potential of ferroelectric memory for high‐speed memory applications (Figure 2c‐i).[ 45 , 46 ]

In addition to the ultrafast characterization in sub‐nanosecond, understanding polarization evolution over a longer time range is equally important for low‐voltage memory operation and memory retention under depolarization field. This long‐term switching dynamic can be characterized by indirect measurements, such as plotting polarization change versus write pulse duration (ΔP‐t curves). Similar findings have been reported in various studies, showing a gradual and accumulative switching trend that typically initiates after 100 ns (Figure 2c‐ii).[ 47 , 48 , 49 ] This behavior contrasts with the observations from ultrafast measurements, where polarization change ceases before 100 ns without reaching its saturation value.

By optimizing the measurement setup, a recent study presents relatively accurate ΔP‐t characteristics from 10 ns to 1 ms, linking the observations in the ultrafast measurement and long‐term measurement.[ 50 , 51 ] In the results, a two‐phase polarization switching phenomenon can be observed (Figure 2c‐iii). By extensive measurements under varying temperatures and simulation verification, the phenomenon was explained by the electric‐field‐induced direct switching followed by the thermal‐activated accumulative switching, where the polycrystalline nature and asynchronized domain switching in the HfO2‐based ferroelectric films were identified as the root causes (Figure 2c‐iv).

Cryogenic Performance

HfO2‐based ferroelectric memories exhibit potential as cryogenic electronics.[ 52 , 53 , 54 , 55 , 56 ] Developing ferroelectric devices operating at cryogenic temperatures necessitates a thorough understanding of the material characteristics over wide temperature ranges. Literature reports indicate varied polarization‐temperature dependencies, as summarized in Figure 2d‐i. For instance, Wang et al. documented a reduction in P r for 20‐nm HZO films upon cooling, attributing it to suppressed domain‐wall mobility due to increased domain interface energy at lower temperatures.[ 52 ] Similarly, Adkins et al. reported a 24% reduction in polarization for 5.6‐nm HZO films when environmental temperature decreases from 280 to 33 K, primarily resulting from increased coercive fields and reduced defect mobility, which limits effective domain switching.[ 53 ] Notably, 5.6 and 6.3 nm HZO films exhibit divergent switching behaviors at cryogenic temperatures. This can be explained by the interplay between oxygen vacancy concentration and phase composition. At low temperatures, reduced vacancy mobility weakens oxygen‐vacancy‐assisted switching, particularly in films with high oxygen vacancy content.[ 56 ] Conversely, the t‐to‐o phase transition becomes more favorable thermodynamically as temperature drops, enhancing switching in films with a high t‐phase fraction but lower oxygen vacancy levels.[ 23 ] Thus, the observed differences stem from the varying dominance of these two mechanisms. Besides, Sun et al. identified domain‐wall pinning as a significant contributor to the P r reduction observed in 10‐nm HZO films at cryogenic conditions.[ 54 ]

However, some studies have reported enhanced polarization at lower temperatures, contrary to the above observations. Xing et al. discovered higher P r upon cooling and explained the phenomenon through first‐principles calculations. They found the metastable t‐phase becomes energetically less favorable at reduced temperatures, which lowers the kinetic barrier and facilitates a transition to the o‐phase.[ 55 ] Zhang et al. provided a more detailed investigation, differentiating polarization behavior by film thickness (Figure 2d‐ii). They showed that ultra‐thin films (3 and 5 nm) exhibited increased polarization at low temperatures due to a favorable t‐to‐o phase transition. In contrast, thicker films (7 and 10 nm) exhibit decreased polarization caused primarily by oxygen vacancy redistribution.[ 56 ] Based on the energetically favorable o‐phase at cryogenic temperatures, the study further introduced a novel kinetic barrier engineering (KBE) strategy. This approach led to a substantial 96% polarization enhancement in 3‐nm‐thick films.

Innovations in Device Designs

The pursuit of non‐volatile memory led to the development of three types of ferroelectric memories, i.e., FeRAM, FeFET, and FTJ, during the era of perovskite‐based ferroelectric materials. These typical device designs continue to play significant roles after HfO2‐based ferroelectric materials emerged. FeRAM is based on ferroelectric capacitors with relatively simple structure, where performance improvement is closely related to the material‐level optimizations. Besides, the destructive read operation of FeRAM inevitably limits its energy efficiency and operation speed. FTJ can be read non‐destructively but faces inherent challenges stemming from the difficulty in balancing the depolarization field, tunnel electroresistance ratio, and the magnitude of readout current. In comparison, FeFET decouples the write and read operations, demonstrating promising memory performance by leveraging HfO2‐based ferroelectric thin films. Therefore, over the past decade, significant efforts have been dedicated to optimizing FeFETs, where numerous innovations occurred. The advancements in alternative channel materials, e.g., oxide semiconductors and 2D materials, further unlock the new potential of FeFETs for high density 3D stacking and integration at back‐end‐of‐line (BEOL). In addition, various complex gate stack designs have dramatically enhanced the memory performance of FeFETs. On the other hand, researchers are continuing to explore new device designs with novel operation mechanisms to offer distinct advantages and functionalities, resulting in the emergence of several ferroelectric memory devices beyond FeFETs. In this chapter, innovations of FeFETs are introduced involving alternative channel materials and advanced gate stack designs. This is followed by an overview of several emerging ferroelectric memory devices beyond FeFET technology.

FeFET with Alternative Channel Materials

Silicon has long been the most important and widely used channel material in the semiconductor industry. Therefore, HfO2‐based FeFETs were initially developed based on silicon channel transistors to exploit the mature fabrication processes and ensure the compatibility with modern CMOS technology platforms.[ 8 , 9 , 57 , 58 , 59 ] In recent years, to fully unleash the potential of HfO2‐based ferroelectric memories in monolithic 3D integration, an increasing number of studies have focused on the incorporation of alternative channel materials to offer more integration flexibility at BEOL for FeFETs.

Oxide semiconductors include a diverse group of materials, e.g., IGZO, In2O3, ZnO, and IWO. Compared to silicon‐channel devices, oxide semiconductor channel FeFETs are free from a naturally formed, low‐quality interfacial layer at the ferroelectric/semiconductor interface, which avoids certain interface‐related reliability degradation mechanisms.[ 60 , 61 , 62 ] Besides this benefit, however, integration with oxide semiconductors also introduces challenges.

Precise thermal budget control is a critical consideration in the fabrication of oxide semiconductor FeFETs.[ 63 , 64 ] To avoid channel degradation during thermal processes, bottom‐gate structures are preferred for oxide semiconductor FeFETs. In this configuration, HfO2‐based ferroelectric films can be formed and activated before the deposition of channel materials to minimize the impact on the channel properties. To facilitate the activation of ferroelectricity, a capping layer replacement approach can be employed. In this activation process, a sacrificial metal layer (e.g., W and TiN) is deposited on top of ferroelectric layer right after its growth, forming a MFM structure along with the gate metal.[ 65 ] During annealing, the metal capping layer induces mechanical stress, which can effectively enhance ferroelectric phase formation.[ 66 , 67 ] After the activation, the sacrificial metal can be removed and replaced by the channel materials. In this way, good ferroelectricity comparable to that of MFM stack can be maintained. However, the removal of capping metal on ferroelectric film may induce defects and potential reliability issues. The thermal activation of ferroelectricity can also be conducted by post deposition annealing (PDA) without any capping layer. This method often leads to relatively weaker ferroelectricity due to the absence of capping effect. Introducing a thin Al2O3 interfacial layer has proven effective in enhancing the ferroelectricity activated by the PDA process.[ 68 ] In addition to the thermal process of ferroelectricity activation, many oxide semiconductor channel materials also require activation through annealing processes, where the temperatures are typically lower than that of HfO2‐based ferroelectric films. Similarly, these annealing conditions must be carefully optimized, as thermal process can also unintentionally degrade the ferroelectric properties of the underlying ferroelectric film.

Additionally, a weak‐erase issue has been identified as a major factor degrading memory window of oxide semiconductor FeFETs. It is noteworthy that, in n‐channel FeFETs, the erase operation is typically defined as applying a negative write voltage, which induces a positive shift in the threshold voltage. Conversely, the program operation involves applying a positive write voltage to induce a negative threshold voltage shift. This behavior contrasts with Flash memories (both floating‐gate flash and charge‐trap flash), where the threshold voltage shifts in the same direction as the applied write voltage. For effective ferroelectric switching during the program/erase operations, the channel in a FeFET is expected to provide both electrons and holes, whether through injection or generation, to induce a sufficient voltage drop across the ferroelectric layer. For example, in conventional silicon‐based n‐channel FeFETs, electrons are injected from heavily doped n‐type source/drain regions, while holes are either accumulated from the substrate in bulk devices (with body terminal connected) or generated via the gate‐induced drain leakage (GIDL) effect in floating body devices.[ 49 , 69 ] In contrast, oxide semiconductor FeFETs have neither bulk body regions nor efficient hole‐generation mechanisms. Moreover, oxide semiconductors usually feature wide bandgaps, which further limit the rate of band‐to‐band carrier generation even under a large erase voltage. As a result, polarization switching during the erase operation is often incomplete, making oxide semiconductor FeFETs difficult to achieve large memory windows.

Over the past decade, oxide semiconductor FeFETs have been demonstrated in a series of studies, where various approaches were proposed to reduce the impact of the weak‐erase effect for optimized performance.[ 65 , 68 , 70 , 71 , 72 , 73 , 74 , 75 , 76 ] It was shown that the weak erase can be effectively mitigated by scaling down the channel dimensions. In the case of In2O3‐based FeFETs illustrated in Figure 3a‐i, an ultrathin 1.5 nm In2O3 channel and a scaled channel length of 7 nm were employed, achieving a large memory window of 2.2 V.[ 68 ] The study further presented an increasing trend of memory window when the channel length is scaled down (Figure 3a‐ii). This improvement can be explained by the fringing electric fields near the source/drain stack, which facilitates polarization switching in the adjacent channel regions. Similar results and conclusions were reported for IWO‐based FeFETs (Figure 3a‐iii).[ 65 ] In addition, the double‐gate structure has also been demonstrated to enhance the erase operation in oxide semiconductor FeFETs (Figure 3a‐iv).[ 76 ] In this configuration, the channel region is not electrically floating, allowing the voltage to be more effectively distributed across the ferroelectric layer through electrostatic coupling. The reduction of the weak‐erase effect and the enhancement of memory window can also be achieved by tuning the capacitive coupling through Metal‐Ferroelectric‐Metal‐Insulator‐Semiconductor (MFMIS) structures and implementing the current modulation mechanism of ferroelectric‐modulated diode, which are detailed in Sections 3.2.1 and 3.3.1, respectively.

Another promising class of alternative channel materials is 2D semiconductors, such as MoS2, WS2, and WSe2. These materials maintain exceptional electrical conductivity even at sub‐1 nm thicknesses, showcasing their potential for continued gate length scaling in transistors.[ 77 ] The atomically flat, dangling‐bond‐free surfaces of the materials form pristine van der Waals interfaces with dielectrics, which favors the control of interfacial defect densities and charge trapping effect when integrating with HfO2‐based ferroelectric films.[ 78 , 79 ]

However, with the similar thin‐film transistor structure, the 2D‐channel FeFETs also face the weak‐erase issue. Besides, high temperatures and specific substrates are required to grow the single‐layer crystalline structure, posing challenges in the fabrication processes of FeFETs. Notably, growing 2D semiconductors directly on the HfO2‐based ferroelectric materials is difficult.[ 80 ] Thus, bottom‐gate 2D‐channel FeFETs rely on material transfer technologies, where contamination introduced during the transfer process remains a significant concern.[ 81 ] Conversely, due to the difficulty in the nucleation on the dangling‐bond‐free flat surface, ALD of uniform high‐quality HfO2‐based ferroelectric thin films on 2D semiconductors is also challenging for top‐gate FeFETs.[ 82 ] To address this issue, researchers have explored incorporating an interlayer by physical vapor deposition as the seed layer to realize top‐gate 2D‐channel FeFETs.[ 83 ]

Gate Stack Innovations of FeFETs

Metal‐ferroelectric‐insulator‐semiconductor (MFIS) structure is a basic gate stack structure of FeFETs, where the insulator can be unintentionally introduced during the fabrication processes (e.g., SiO2 interlayer in silicon channel FeFETs) or intentionally inserted for device optimization. This interlayer plays a crucial role in the investigation of FeFET performance. A thin interlayer suffers from high electric field stress, resulting in severe charge trapping effect and early dielectric breakdown. While increasing the interlayer thickness can lead to insufficient charge screening and high depolarization field, thereby degrading the memory window and retention of the devices. This trade‐off significantly narrows the design space for high‐performance FeFETs.[ 84 ] In order to further enhance the device performance, recent studies have implemented several innovative gate stack designs. For example, an MFMIS structure was proposed to decouple the constraints, and gate stacks with charge trapping layers were introduced to expand the memory window. In this section, these two variants in the design of FeFETs are discussed.

MFMIS Structure

MFMIS structure introduces a floating metal layer in the gate stack. This enables tunable capacitive coupling through adjusting the area ratio between the MFM and Metal‐Insulator‐Semiconductor (MIS) structures (AR = A MFM/A MIS). With a MFM capacitor area smaller than the active area of MIS (AR < 1), the memory window can be enlarged without increasing the voltage stress across the insulating layer. For alternative‐channel devices with thin‐film transistor structures, the MFMIS architecture exhibits additional benefits, offering a feasible solution for the thermal budget constraints and intrinsic weak‐erase problem. In an MFMIS FeFET with IGZO channel (Figure 3b‐i,ii), the transistor is stacked on an MFM capacitor.[ 85 , 86 , 87 ] The floating gate metal layer separates the ferroelectric material and transistor structure, protecting the ferroelectric layer from possible degrading effects (e.g., interdiffusion of oxygen vacancies) introduced by the oxide semiconductors during the thermal process. The significantly enhanced memory window can be observed in the I d‐V g characteristics of the fabricated device with small area ratios (Figure 3b‐iii). Besides, with mitigated weak‐erase effect, MFMIS structure eliminates the memory window dependence on source/drain fringing fields, thereby demonstrating a stable memory window across devices with different channel lengths (Figure 3b‐iv).

Intriguingly, the memory windows of MFMIS FeFETs can reach unexpectedly high values, which exceed twice the coercive voltage (Figure 3b‐v).[ 87 , 88 , 89 , 90 ] This unusually large memory window has been quantitatively interpreted through a quasi‐static model of the MFMIS structure.[ 91 , 92 ] In the configurations with small AR, the voltage drop across the ferroelectric layer is significantly enhanced. Under higher electric field, the leakage current through the layer increases exponentially, leading to many charges injected from gate electrode and trapped at the floating metal (Figure 3b‐vi). This effect contributes to the device memory window together with the ferroelectric polarization. It should be noted that, although the memory window is enlarged, the slow charge injection process lowers the write speed of the memory (Figure 3b‐vii) and the gradual charge loss degrades the retention performance.[ 89 , 93 ]

The MFMIS structure also provides flexibility in device design and fabrication due to the independent MFM and transistor structures. In recent work, a fin‐shaped gate structure for the transistor was demonstrated, which takes advantage of the 3D deposition capability of the oxide semiconductor channel material using ALD. This approach reduces the area footprint compared to conventional planar MFMIS FeFET.[ 94 ] Moreover, the work function of the floating metal layer can be engineered, offering an additional pathway to tailor device characteristics such as threshold voltage and switching behavior.[ 95 ]

Additionally, this structure further enables high‐performance 2D‐channel FeFETs. As shown in Figure 3c, Li et al. compared the bottom‐gate 2D‐channel FeFETs with MFIS stack and MFMIS stack, evidencing the prominent memory window enhancement introduced by the floating metal gate.[ 96 ] With a device structure drawn in Figure 3d‐i, Xiang et al. demonstrated a MoS2‐channel FeFET highlighting an ultra‐large memory window of ≈11.5 V and a high current on/off ratio exceeding 7 orders of magnitude (Figure 3d‐ii).[ 97 ] Furthermore, by utilizing the WSe2 channel, MFMIS FeFETs with ambipolar conduction behavior were realized to provide additional functionalities (Figure 3e).[ 98 ]

Stacks with Charge Trapping Layer

A series of recent innovations have focused on integrating charge trapping layers into the ferroelectric gate stacks. By utilizing the interplay of polarization switching and charge trapping, this hybrid configuration offers reduced write voltages compared to charge trapping flash memories, and a significantly larger memory window compared to standard FeFETs. However, this strategy also sacrifices the fast‐switching advantage of HfO2‐based ferroelectrics and raises concern about the data retention, as part of the memory behavior becomes governed by the slower and less stable charge trapping effect.

The charge‐trapping assisted ferroelectric gate stack shown in Figure 3f‐i features a ferroelectric layer with an inserted tunnel dielectric layer, which has been both experimentally demonstrated and theoretically modeled.[ 99 ] In this structure, the inserted dielectric layer serves as a charge tunneling barrier and introduces trap states at both two ferroelectric/dielectric interfaces. During write operations, polarization switching occurs concurrently with charge emission and capture at the two interfaces, which collectively contributes to the memory window of the device. Another example involves a charge trapping layer inserted beneath the gate electrode (Figure 3f‐ii), which was designed to introduce gate‐side charge injection and trapping effect.[ 100 ] In this configuration, the charge at the trapping layer induces a hysteresis direction that aligns with ferroelectric memory, thus leading to the enhancement in the memory window. Similar approaches were also applied in some other studies with different materials and positions of the charge trapping layers.[ 101 , 102 , 103 , 104 ]

Beyond FeFET

Concurrent with the advancements in FeFETs, researchers are actively investigating alternative device designs to address limitations of existing approaches, achieve superior performance, or realize new functionalities. At their core, these novel device designs fundamentally represent innovative readout mechanisms of the ferroelectric polarization states. For example, instead of reading the channel conduction current as in FeFETs, new approaches involve sensing the tunneling current of a Schottky diode or P‐N junction that modulated by a ferroelectric gate, as seen in the ferroelectric‐modulated diodes (FMDs) and ferroelectric tunnel field‐effect transistors (FeTFETs). Additionally, some recent studies have taken further steps by transferring memory readout from the current domain to the charge or optical domains, exemplified by ferroelectric capacitive memory (FCM) and ferroelectric photonic‐electronic memory (FPEM). These innovations expand the range of ferroelectric memory options, demonstrating strong potential for a variety of applications.

Ferroelectric‐Modulated Diode

To address the weak‐erase issue in FeFETs with oxide semiconductor channels, FMD was proposed.[ 105 , 106 , 107 ] This device shifts the current modulation region from the channel, as in conventional FeFETs, to the source region by forming a metal‐semiconductor Schottky diode. A typical FMD structure is illustrated in Figure 4a‐i. The device closely resembles a standard bottom‐gate FeFET with an oxide semiconductor channel but differs in that a high work function metal is used for the Schottky source contact, which controls the transistor's output current.

With a ferroelectric gate dielectric, the Schottky barrier at the source can be modulated by polarization, resulting in programmable tunneling current. In this way, the critical region for memory operation is located under the source contact, where the ferroelectric layer is sandwiched between the gate metal and the source metal. Consequently, the applied write voltages are delivered more effectively to the ferroelectric layer at this region, producing a higher electric field for write operations compared to the channel region (Figure 4a‐ii).

FMDs have been demonstrated using ZnO and IGZO channels.[ 105 , 106 , 107 ] Figure 4a‐iii provides an IGZO‐channel FMD with a Pt source contact.[ 106 ] Compared to a control device (FeFET with Ti source and drain), the FMD exhibits more than twice the memory window (Figure 4a–iv), experimentally confirming the advantages provided by this novel device design.

Ferroelectric Tunnel Field‐Effect Transistor

The tunnel field‐effect transistor (TFET) has a long history and has attracted considerable research interest due to its ability to achieve steep subthreshold swings below the 60 mV/decade limit of metal‐oxide‐semiconductor field‐effect transistors (MOSFETs). By incorporating HfO2‐based ferroelectric materials into the gate stack of TFETs, the tunnel current can be modulated by polarization, resulting in a FeTFET.[ 108 , 109 , 110 , 111 ]

Unlike MOSFETs, TFETs inherently exhibit ambipolar behavior. While this property is undesirable for achieving high power efficiency in logic applications—prompting the development of various methods to suppress ambipolar current, which increases fabrication complexity—it is not a limitation in memory applications. Thus, FeTFETs can be fabricated without the need for complex doping engineering or overlapping gate structures on the drain.[ 110 ] In fact, the ambipolar characteristic of FeTFETs can provide potential advantages for specific applications, such as their use in content‐addressable memory and XNOR operator.[ 108 , 109 ]

Figure 4b‐i illustrates the structure of FeTFET. The source and drain regions of the transistor are doped with p‐type and n‐type, respectively. Band‐to‐band tunneling occurs in the P‐I‐N junction under both positive and negative gate biases, resulting in ambipolar conduction behavior (Figure 4b‐ii).[ 110 ] By introducing ferroelectric layer into the gate stack, the polarization modulates the channel potential, thereby inducing hysteresis in the transfer characteristics. Figure 4b‐iii presents the measured hysteretic I d‐V g curve of a FeTFET fabricated based on a 14‐nm silicon FinFET platform.[ 108 , 109 ] FeTFETs have also been demonstrated using advanced III‐V vertical nanowire heterojunctions with negative transconductance to realize higher device density and operating frequency.[ 111 ]

Ferroelectric Capacitor Memory

FCMs (also referred to as FeCAPs, ferroelectric NvCAPs, and ferroelectric memcapacitors) replace the conventional current‐based readout methods by utilizing the programmable device capacitance to encode the stored data. The innovative charge‐domain readout scheme not only enables the high‐performance two‐terminal ferroelectric memory device with non‐destructive read property but also show advantages over the conventional resistive memories by bypassing several challenges of current‐domain operations.[ 112 , 113 ] First, the capacitive device eliminates the direct current path in the circuit, enabling lower power consumption with nearly zero static power. Second, the IR drop issue can be mitigated with higher device resistance compared to interconnects. Third, the FCM crossbar array can effectively circumvent the sneak path issue by implementing certain connection schemes. Fourth, reading under low DC biases results in negligible read disturbance, ensuring robust non‐destructive read operations.

FCMs can be realized with both MFM structures and MFS structures. As illustrated in Figure 4c, the capacitance modulation of a MFM FCM device can be realized by oxygen vacancies at the interface or asymmetric device structures (e.g., inserting an interfacial layer and using different metals as the electrodes).[ 114 , 115 , 116 , 117 , 118 , 119 ] The asymmetricity of the ferroelectricity‐induced hysteresis loop in the capacitance versus voltage (C‐V) characteristics leads to two different capacitance values at zero voltage bias, which can be sensed to determine the polarization state. The MFM FCMs maintain a compact device structure, offering excellent integration flexibility and low fabrication complexity similar to FeRAM. However, the capacitance ratio between the high capacitance state (HCS) and low capacitance state (LCS) is limited (typically lower than 1.30) due to the underlying capacitance modulation mechanism, which can further adversely affect the memory endurance and retention characteristics.

The capacitance ratio can be significantly expanded by an inversion‐type FCM based on MFS stack.[ 120 , 121 , 122 , 123 ] The device structure is drawn in Figure 4d‐i. In HCS, the dipoles of the ferroelectric layer attract the carriers to the silicon surface, forming an inversion layer connecting with the contact region and hence leading to a high capacitance. While in LCS, ferroelectric polarization lifts the surface potential, resulting in a depletion region at the P‐N junction and thereby a low capacitance. Figure 4d‐ii shows a C‐V curve measured in an inversion‐type FCM, where a capacitance ratio of over 200 can be observed.[ 112 ] It is noteworthy that this device structure and capacitive memory operation can be implemented in a standard FeFET by shorting the source and drain to form one terminal and using the gate as the other terminal.[ 124 ] This straightforward transition to FCM further makes FeFET a versatile device with transferable operation modes. Although MFS FCMs dramatically outperform MFM FCMs in terms of capacitance ratio, the introduction of semiconductors into the device structures limits the integration flexibility to some extent and potentially leads to higher operation voltages compared to MFM devices. Recent studies have demonstrated utilizing oxide semiconductors to form the MFS FCMs in order to provide BEOL‐compatibility and 3D integration capability.[ 125 , 126 ]

The novel capacitive reading scheme presents promising opportunities to overcome the limitations associated with conventional resistive memory technologies. Nonetheless, it also introduces new challenges. The capacitance‐based charge‐domain operation necessitates the redesign of peripheral circuits to achieve accurate and rapid capacitance sensing. Parasitic effects must be carefully considered in integration strategies to minimize access delay and enhance sensing margins. Additionally, there remains a lack of robust experimental demonstrations of FCM devices scaled to nanometer dimensions, leaving the scalability of these devices unverified. As a relatively new technology substantially diverging from conventional solutions, further studies across multiple levels are imperative to fully assess its potential in various applications.

Ferroelectric Photonic‐Electronic Memory

FPEMs extend ferroelectric memories into optical domain. As shown in Figure 4e‐i, a FPEM can be realized by incorporating the HfO2‐based ferroelectric film with silicon ring resonator.[ 127 , 128 , 129 ] In the structure, the ferroelectric dipoles are able to attract or repel the carriers in the silicon. The carrier concentration modulated by the ferroelectric polarization further determines the refractive index and propagation constant of the light, thereby leading to a programmable resonance peak shift of the micro‐ring. The measured transmission spectra of the ring resonator under different ferroelectric polarization states are plotted in Figure 4e‐ii, where a 6.6 dB extinction ratio can be observed. Integrated with photodiodes, the memory supports both electrical and optical signals for read and write operations.[ 128 ] This hybrid electronic‐photonic operation mode demonstrates potential to eliminate the electrical and optical signal conversion in the electronic‐photonic systems, promising to further improve the latency and power efficiency. This innovative device concept also offers a new technological pathway for programmable photonic switches used in photonic interconnects and data communication, as well as enabling artificial synapse devices for optical neuromorphic computing.

In addition to the integration with waveguides, HfO2‐based ferroelectric film has also been utilized to combine non‐volatile data storage with light sensing.[ 130 , 131 ] A ferroelectric optoelectronic memcapacitor was proposed based on MFS stack with lightly doped silicon (Figure 4f‐i).[ 130 ] The device is read by sensing the device capacitance, functioning as an accumulative‐type FCM. The optical‐dependent write operations are employed to achieve optical‐electronic fusion. In a dark environment, erasing the device to the LCS is challenging. When the positive erase voltage is applied at the top electrode, holes in the p‐type silicon will be repelled. Due to the relatively low doping concentration, a large depletion width is formed, which lowers the voltage drop across the ferroelectric layer and results in an insufficient electric field for the polarization switching. However, under light stimulation, photogenerated carriers are able to accumulate at the silicon surface and reduce the depletion width, thus facilitating the switching of ferroelectric polarization and hence the effective erase operation to LCS. This effect can be observed in the measured polarization switching loops with and without light exposure (Figure 4f‐ii).[ 130 ] With this interesting behavior, the device shows potential in in‐sensor computing or photoelectric in‐memory computing applications.

Integration Strategies

The inherent material advantages of HfO2‐based ferroelectrics, coupled with the semiconductor industry's shift toward 3D architectures for high‐density integration, are driving innovations in vertically stacked ferroelectric memories, which are further facilitated by the advances in BEOL‐compatible channel materials. On one hand, vertically stacking ferroelectric memories in multiple layers is a primary approach to increasing memory density and potentially reducing cost per bit, similar to the strategy used in 3D NAND Flash. On the other hand, ferroelectric memories can be vertically integrated with high‐performance CMOS logic circuits during BEOL processes, providing a potential monolithic integration solution to support higher bandwidth in a smaller form factor.

Beyond device‐level performance, integrating devices into arrays, circuits, and 3D structures requires evaluating memory performance at higher levels, which involves various additional critical factors such as device‐to‐device variation, disturbance issues, operation scheme, thermal budget, and parasitics. For instance, the read and write operations of memory can be delayed by the parasitic effects of interconnects, selectors, or peripheral circuits. Issues such as variation, disturbance, and sneak paths can all degrade the sensing margin. In the demonstration of FeFET on the 22 nm technology node, the sensing margin almost vanishes when assessing at array level, even though the memory window of single device can reach 1.5 V.[ 9 ] This makes multi‐level cell implementation nearly impossible.

In the development of integration strategies, cross‐level design‐technology co‐optimization can be effectively implemented. Liu et al. investigated the nonuniformity of HfO2‐based ferroelectric film caused by the different silicon substrate orientations and proposed a strategy employing the HfO2 seed layer to reduce the variation in the high‐density vertical integration with polysilicon.[ 132 ] Fu et al. quantitatively assessed the disturb characteristics at array level and combined device optimization with partial biased write scheme to improve the disturb immunity for selector‐free crossbar integration.[ 133 ]

The following sections of this chapter highlight several recent explorations and demonstrations in HfO2‐based ferroelectric memory integration strategies, including 3D stacked FeRAM, FeFET, and monolithic integrated devices for high‐density and high‐bandwidth memory applications.

3D Integration of FeRAM

FeRAM with ferroelectric capacitors and access transistors is one of the most extensively explored ferroelectric memories due to its advantages such as relatively simple structure, low operation voltage, and outstanding reliability.[ 10 , 43 , 134 , 135 , 136 ] As a milestone achievement, a 32 Gb dual‐layer 3D stacked 1T‐1C FeRAM was demonstrated by Micron with near‐DRAM performance metrics (Figure 5a).[ 10 ] The memory utilized the most advanced DRAM technologies, featuring 48 nm pitch trench capacitors with a 5.7 nm thick doped‐HfO2 layer and vertical channel polysilicon access transistors. The development of high‐density integration of FeRAM can closely follow the successful trajectory established by DRAM integration. Like the prevailing concept of 3D DRAM with multi‐layer stacked horizontal capacitors, 3D FeRAM can leverage analogous techniques to significantly boost memory capacity.[ 137 ] The two memory technologies share architectural similarities, enabling the direct application of mature DRAM fabrication strategies to FeRAM development. By adopting similar processes, such as high‐density pillar or trench capacitor, vertical or horizontal access transistor, and advanced patterning methods, FeRAM stands to benefit from the proven scalability and cost‐effectiveness of established DRAM technologies.

Beyond the 1T‐1C architecture, a series of studies have introduced a 2T‐nC structure for ferroelectric memory.[ 136 , 138 , 139 , 140 , 141 ] The configuration was designed for a quasi‐nondestructive readout technology, which enables multiple read cycles without requiring immediate write‐back, thereby significantly enhancing endurance and reliability of the memory. By allowing multiple ferroelectric capacitors to share the access and sensing transistors, 2T‐nC architecture exhibits superior scalability through 3D integration, offering an alternative integration strategy of MFM capacitors (Figure 5b).[ 138 ]

3D FeNAND

The success of 3D NAND Flash has established an integration paradigm for achieving ultra‐high memory density and continuous reduction in cost per bit, which is now guiding the integration of FeFETs toward vertical stacking architectures. The ferroelectric NAND memory (FeNAND) shares a similar structure with NAND Flash, allowing the adoption of various mature fabrication technologies. With the intrinsic advantages of FeFETs, this transition in storage mechanism is expected to enable lower operation voltages, faster program speed, and orders of improvement in endurance characteristics. For example, researchers from SK Hynix reported a 3D FeNAND, highlighting its potential as a strong candidate for the post‐NAND era (Figure 5c).[ 142 , 143 ]

Although high‐density 3D integration of FeFETs can be achieved by leveraging mature 3D NAND Flash technologies, the transition in operation schemes from NAND Flash to FeNAND still faces challenges. The positive write pulses at the gate terminal induce negative threshold voltage shift in FeFETs, which is opposite to the behavior of floating‐gate Flash or charge trapping Flash, and requires modifications in the operation schemes and associated peripheral circuits. In certain FeFET devices, this positive write voltage can be close to the threshold voltage, limiting the design margin for the pass voltage and thereby resulting in pass disturb issue during the reading of NAND.[ 144 , 145 ] Besides, the smaller memory windows of typical FeFETs compared to Flash may not be sufficient to support the stable operations as multi‐level memory cell. This bottleneck hinders FeNAND's ability to match the bit density and cost per bit of NAND Flash.

3D FeNOR

While 3D FeNAND connects ferroelectric memory cells in series, similar to conventional NAND flash, the FeNOR architecture arranges memory cells in a parallel configuration, akin to NOR flash. In a NOR array, each memory transistor directly connects the bit line (BL) to the source line (SL), and all cells on the same BL operate in parallel, each controlled by an individual word line (WL).

3D FeNOR was reported recently with BEOL‐compatible FeFETs utilizing ZnO channel.[ 146 ] In the design, the gates (WLs) of the MFMIS FeFETs run horizontally at each layer, while the BLs and SLs are implemented with two vertical metallic lines (Figure 5d‐i). Notably, their vertical side‐fin MFMIS structure, as shown in the TEM image (Figure 5d‐ii), not only reduces the cell‐to‐cell interference but also provides a small AR. Consequently, a ≈4 V memory window and over 106 on/off ratio of the memory cell were realized. Additionally, a horizontal FeNOR integration architecture was also proposed as another potential alternative design (Figure 5e).[ 88 , 89 ]

The FeNOR architecture presents distinct advantages and trade‐offs. By providing each cell with a direct current path to the bit line, NOR architecture allows cells to be read and programmed individually. The random‐access ability eliminates the need to bias an entire string of series‐connected cells as in NAND arrays, hence avoiding the pass disturb issue. However, both BL and SL must be individually connected, which increases the routing complexity compared to NAND arrays. It is noteworthy that FeNOR is particularly promising for in‐memory analog neural network acceleration. Because multiple parallel FeFETs contribute to the same BL, the architecture can naturally perform multiplication and accumulation (MAC) operations.[ 147 ]

Monolithic Integration

HfO2‐based ferroelectric memories further present potential for monolithic integration, enabling various applications beyond the standalone memories. In recent years, significant progress has been made in BEOL‐compatible ferroelectric devices as well as the monolithic 3D integration of logic and ferroelectric memory on the same die.

The examples include the abovementioned various BEOL‐compatible FeFETs based on advanced channel materials. These devices were designed for direct integration on top of existing CMOS or other functional layers without compromising the integrity of the underlying circuitry. Furthermore, the low‐temperature processing is also compatible with flexible electronics, where substrates demand ultra‐low thermal budgets.

Instead of integrating the entire memory structure at the BEOL, separated MFM capacitors at BEOL can work in conjunction with FEOL transistors to achieve memory functionality. Researchers in Fraunhofer IPMS demonstrated a MFMIS FeFET by connecting the MFM ferroelectric capacitor in the interconnect layer to the gate terminal of standard transistors.[ 148 ] The concept presents an easy approach to introduce ferroelectric memory to logic circuits without modifying the frontend CMOS processes.

Additionally, non‐volatile SRAM (NV‐SRAM) designs have emerged by integrating ferroelectric capacitors or transistors within SRAM cells. With power supply, NV‐SRAM can operate as a conventional 6T SRAM. During power‐down, the bistable latch state is “backed up” into the ferroelectric capacitors for non‐volatile long‐term storage. Upon power restoration, the state is quickly restored, providing instant‐on functionality. Therefore, this memory benefits from both the fast, reliable operation of SRAM and the non‐volatility of ferroelectric memory. The fabricated prototypes with HfO2‐based ferroelectrics demonstrated reliable store and recall operations with acceptable performance overhead, showing potential for the applications including normally off computing or intermittent computing powered by energy‐harvesting devices.[ 149 , 150 , 151 ] Furthermore, enabled by the oxide semiconductor transistors, the NV‐SRAM was also demonstrated with full BEOL compatibility (Figure 5f).[ 152 ] Thus, 3D stacking of the memory as multiple layers on top of FEOL logic circuits is also possible to overcome the density bottleneck and achieve high memory bandwidth.

Emerging Applications

Progress in materials, devices, and integration technologies collectively promotes the increasingly extensive exploration in applications. To align with the surging demand for computing power and the evolution of computing paradigms toward data‐centric architectures, significant research efforts have focused on various in‐memory computing applications. Similar to the other emerging non‐volatile memories, ferroelectric memories can be used to construct the MAC unit to accelerate the neural network related tasks. Numerous demonstrations have been reported on ferroelectric MAC accelerators for a variety of applications, where the high energy efficiency offered by ferroelectric memories outperforms other solutions.[ 97 , 147 , 153 ] In addition to these MAC‐based frameworks, the distinct properties of ferroelectric memories also inspire various innovative applications. This chapter selects several recent innovations on the applications of ferroelectric memories, including the spiking neural networks (SNNs), reservoir computing (RC), and logic‐in‐memory (LiM). It should be noted that the potential of ferroelectric memories is not limited to these directions. With on‐going exploration, new applications continue to emerge, such as ferroelectric‐based true random number generation, physical unclonable function, photonic computing, frequency doubler, and oscillator.

Spiking Neural Networks

SNNs capitalize on event‐driven processing, inherently promoting energy efficiency. A fundamental computational unit in SNNs is the leaky integrate‐and‐fire (LIF) neuron, which accumulates input signals over time (integration) and generates an output spike once a threshold is reached, followed by a decay (leak) of the internal potential if no spike occurs. This differs from the artificial synapses realized by various emerging non‐volatile memories, which are responsible for controlling the connectivity among neurons. In the hardware implementation, Zr‐rich HZO‐based antiferroelectric field‐effect transistor (AFeFET) demonstrates outstanding compatibility for artificial neuron. The inherent volatility of their polarization switching, characterized by the relaxation of the polarization state back to its original condition after the removal of the electric field, mirrors the reset behavior of LIF neurons. This polarization/depolarization‐driven volatility has been exploited in the realization of a LIF neuron without requiring external reset peripheral circuits, as shown in Figure 6a.[ 154 ]

The integration of these neurons with ferroelectric synapses further emphasized the potential of ferroelectric memories for energy‐efficient neuromorphic processors. This can be realized by AFeFET with the same MFMIS gate stack. In Figure 6b, Zheng et al. introduced non‐volatile synaptic features in MFMIS AFeFETs by leveraging work function engineering.[ 155 ] Gao et al. demonstrated by controlling the area ratio of the device, the retention of the device can be tuned from volatile to non‐volatile, thus offering both neuron and synapse functions in the same device platform.[ 156 ] Besides, the ambipolar WSe2 channel MFMIS FeFETs also provide similar reconfigurability.[ 98 ] As shown in Figure 6c, in WSe2 n‐FeFETs, removing the gate voltage preserves the polarization state due to the retention characteristics of HZO, thereby maintaining the program state for synapse function. In addition to neurons and synapses, encoding input signals into spikes is essential for the operation of SNNs. A recent study demonstrated a ferroelectric encoder that leverages the fast polarization switching of HfO2‐based ferroelectric material and the low‐power characteristics of MoS2 to encode continuous signals into temporal spikes.[ 157 ] As shown in Figure 6d, the encoder effectively converts analog inputs into discrete spikes through gate voltage modulation, enabling real‐time signal processing with enhanced efficiency. The encoder design incorporates a capacitor and comparator to facilitate time‐to‐first‐spike (TTFS) encoding, where the accumulated potential is compared with a predefined threshold (Figure 6e). The generated spike time is inversely proportional to the input intensity, demonstrating the exponential correlation between the spike time and input signal (Figure 6f).

Reservoir Computing

RC is a brain‐inspired computational framework derived from recurrent neural networks (RNNs), offering efficient processing of spatiotemporal data with reduced training complexity. Unlike conventional RNNs that require training all connections, RC fixes the internal reservoir layer and trains only the output layer, enabling low‐power implementation. The reservoir functions as a nonlinear dynamic system that transforms input signals into high‐dimensional states, capturing both current inputs and short‐term history. HfO2‐based FeFETs are well suited for RC systems, as their voltage‐dependent polarization dynamics produce tunable current responses that form diverse reservoir states.

A foundational approach to implementing HfO2‐based FeFETs in RC systems involves utilizing transient current responses to generate reservoir states. In such architectures, input signals are encoded as sequential voltage pulses, inducing transient polarization switching in the FeFETs. By sampling the resulting drain current response, a high‐dimensional state space can be constructed to map spatiotemporal patterns without the need for complex peripheral circuitry. Toprasertpong et al. demonstrated this scheme by employing a temporal mask function to modulate the gate voltage of FeFETs, thereby encoding input data into triangular pulse waveforms.[ 158 ] This method effectively leverages the volatile polarization states of FeFETs to emulate fading memory effects, achieving temporal information processing with minimal hardware overhead. In addition, Takagi et al. further tailored the FeFET‐based RC architecture for edge‐AI applications by optimizing the pulse encoding scheme and sampling strategy.[ 159 ]

Material engineering plays a pivotal role in optimizing RC performance. Li et al. demonstrated that by precisely controlling the O3 pulse duration during the ALD process, the oxygen vacancy concentration in HZO can be systematically modulated, resulting in rich reservoir states (Figure 6g).[ 96 ] This method leverages the back‐switching dynamics of HZO to induce transient memory effects, effectively enhancing state diversity and expanding the functional range of the reservoir. Zr‐rich AFeFETs have also been demonstrated as an excellent device choice for RC systems. Shi et al. further integrated both electrical and optical inputs into such a system to enable multimodal processing (Figure 6h).[ 160 ] The AFeFET structure exploits back‐switching kinetics to induce fading memory effects, achieving 95.4% recognition accuracy in spoken‐digit recognition tasks. By integrating a 2D channel, the reservoir state space is further expanded through light‐induced modulation, demonstrating the potential for multi‐terminal, multi‐modal RC architectures.

In addition to FeFET‐based architectures, ultrathin FTJs have emerged as an alternative RC platform, offering ultralow power consumption and robust temporal data processing capabilities. Yu et al. demonstrated a 3.5 nm HZO‐based FTJ RC system that leverages the tunneling electroresistance effect to generate reservoir states with high sensitivity and minimal energy dissipation.[ 161 ] By modulating the tunneling current under varying input pulse sequences, a diverse array of temporal states was achieved, effectively capturing transient data patterns while maintaining sub‐femtojoule power consumption.

Logic‐In‐Memory

Logic‐in‐Memory architectures integrate logic functions directly within memory cells, effectively reducing data transfer latency and power consumption. HfO2‐based FeFETs leverage their nonvolatile polarization states to encode logic operations, enabling multi‐bit processing with minimal circuit overhead. The multi‐level polarization states facilitate Boolean logic operations within a single device, significantly enhancing computational density and reducing transistor count compared to conventional CMOS logic. These states are achieved by applying different programming voltages to induce partial or full polarization switching in the HfO2 layer, resulting in distinct and stable threshold voltage levels that can represent multiple logic states.

Recent advancements have underscored the potential of FeFETs in LiM applications through reconfigurable logic structures. In a reconfigurable LiM architecture, HZO‐based FeFETs enable in situ logic computation with ultralow energy consumption, achieving less than 8 attojoules (aJ) per operation, which is a significant reduction compared to conventional CMOS gates.[ 162 ] As depicted in the device schematic (Figure 6i), a single FeFET can be dynamically reprogrammed to implement multiple Boolean logic functions, showcasing exceptional versatility in LiM systems. This reconfigurability not only reduces transistor count but also minimizes interconnect length, thereby mitigating data transfer latency and energy dissipation. Moreover, the multi‐gate configuration of HZO‐based FeFETs further enhances their logic processing capabilities. A three‐gate configuration was demonstrated to encode eight distinct input states, each mapped to a specific drain current output (Figure 6j). In this design, the three gates can be independently biased to binary levels, generating 8 possible input combinations. These combinations modulate the internal electric field across the ferroelectric layer, resulting in distinct polarization states and corresponding drain current responses. This approach effectively expands logic capacity while maintaining nonvolatile state retention, ensuring robust logic processing without continuous power input.[ 163 ]

Summary and Perspective

In this review, we provide a comprehensive overview of the recent progress in HfO2‐based ferroelectric memories, encompassing developments in fundamental materials research, device innovations, integration strategies, and emerging applications. From a materials perspective, the review delves into the thickness and grain size scaling, polarization switching dynamics, and cryogenic behavior. These new research focuses are crucial in determining the various key performance factors of ferroelectric memories, including the scalability, uniformity, operation speed, and potential in emerging application scenarios. In device innovation, the exploration of FeFETs involves alternative channel materials and complex gate stack designs, aiming to enhance integration density and device performance. Novel concepts beyond FeFETs, such as FMD, FeTFET, FCM, and FPEM, further suggest new pathways for ferroelectric memories with improved performance or enhanced functionality. Integration strategies focus on the potential of 3D architectures to achieve high‐density memory configurations. The monolithic integration of logic and memory further extends the applicability of ferroelectric memories, presenting avenues for efficient data‐centric computing architectures. Emerging applications leverage the unique properties of ferroelectric memories to support novel computing paradigms. These applications underscore the alignment of ferroelectric memories with future computing needs.

Despite the promising outlook, there are new challenges associated with these opportunities, some of which impede the scale‐up from laboratory demonstrations to industrial mass production. For instance, the polycrystalline nature of HfO2‐based ferroelectric materials often leads to variability in device performance especially at reduced dimensions, affecting the uniformity and sensing margins in high‐density memory arrays. Additionally, while non‐destructive readout schemes are under active investigation, current implementations fall short of replacing SRAM due to limitations in reliability, read/write speed, and operating voltage. Implementing ferroelectrics in 3D NAND architectures as a Flash alternative also remains complex, both from a process integration and performance standpoint. More broadly, the positioning of HfO2‐based ferroelectrics within the existing memory hierarchy is still being evaluated, which may affect their near‐term adoption.

In comparison, ferroelectric memories may offer greater promise in in‐memory/near‐memory computing systems, where their non‐volatility, energy efficiency, and process compatibility align well with architectural demands. The shift toward data‐centric computing underscores the need for cross‐layer co‐design—from materials and devices to systems and algorithms. Within this context, HfO2‐based ferroelectric memories could play a significant role in enabling more efficient and scalable hardware for future computing platforms.

In conclusion, the future of HfO2‐based ferroelectric memories hinges on overcoming various technological barriers through interdisciplinary collaboration. By advancing material science, refining device designs, enhancing integration techniques, and exploring novel applications, HfO2‐based ferroelectrics hold the promise of shaping the future of memory technologies as pivotal components in next‐generation computing platforms. The research progresses summarized in this review aim to present the current frontiers of HfO2‐based ferroelectric memories and inspires the continued innovation and exploration in this dynamic field.
