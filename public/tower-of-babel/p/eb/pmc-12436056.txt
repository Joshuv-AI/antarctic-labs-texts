Clinical Application of Lyon Consensus 2.0 in Asia: An Overview
Authors: Ming-Wun Wong, Chien-Lin Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The Lyon Consensus 2.0, published in 2023, provides an updated diagnostic framework for gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), emphasizing objective physiological testing and introducing the concept of “actionable GERD” to guide individualized therapy. This review evaluates the clinical applicability of this framework in Asia, where normative values require regional adaptation. Given limited access to physiological testing in primary care, an endoscopy-based strategy may be considered due to its greater availability, helping overcome diagnostic barriers. Key updates include recognizing Los Angeles grade B esophagitis as conclusive GERD evidence, adoption of prolonged wireless pH monitoring, and revised thresholds for pH-impedance studies, including mean nocturnal baseline impedance. High-resolution manometry and impedance remain important for evaluating refractory symptoms and regurgitation-predominant presentations. Challenges in Asian contexts include the limited predictive performance of Western-derived tools, such as the Lyon score, and the need for lower acid exposure thresholds. The Milan score, a novel manometry-based index, provides an assessment of anti-reflux barrier integrity and may guide selection of candidates for endoscopic anti-reflux therapies. The COuGH RefluX score, based on symptoms and risk factors, estimates GERD probability in patients with laryngeal complaints. Incorporating psychophysiological assessments, such as esophageal hypervigilance and symptom-related anxiety, can further enhance diagnostic precision and improve outcomes. Successful implementation of Lyon Consensus 2.0 in Asia will depend on contextual adaptation of diagnostic criteria and integration of accessible clinical and psychological tools.

INTRODUCTION

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) remains a prevalent and complex disorder requiring precise diagnostic criteria to optimize patient management. The Lyon Consensus, first published in 2018, provided a standardized framework for GERD diagnosis based on objective esophageal testing.1 However, advances in diagnostic techniques and a growing understanding of esophageal pathophysiology necessitated an update. The Lyon Consensus 2.0, published in 2023, refines the diagnostic criteria by introducing a stratified approach to test interpretation, categorizing findings as conclusive, inconclusive, supportive, or not supportive of GERD.2 This update aims to enhance diagnostic specificity and harmonize esophageal testing algorithms to distinguish GERD from disorders of gut-brain interaction and other esophageal conditions. A key aspect of Lyon Consensus 2.0 is the concept of “actionable GERD,” which integrates recent evidence to guide individualized treatment strategies. The revised framework acknowledges that troublesome symptoms alone are insufficient for diagnosis and underscores the importance of distinguishing typical versus atypical presentations in clinical decision-making. Furthermore, it incorporates prolonged wireless pH monitoring, updated impedance metrics, and establishes Los Angeles (LA) grade B esophagitis as conclusive evidence of GERD. The consensus also provides guidance on the diagnostic workup of refractory GERD, particularly when esophageal function tests are conducted on antisecretory therapy.

This review explores the clinical application of Lyon Consensus 2.0 in Asia, addressing regional considerations in GERD diagnosis and management. Given the diverse dietary habits, healthcare accessibility, and genetic predispositions across Asian populations, it is essential to evaluate the applicability of these updated criteria in an Asian clinical context.

MODERN DEFINITION OF GERD IN LYON CONSENSUS 2.0

1. Concept of “actionable GERD” and its clinical implications

The concept of “actionable GERD” introduced in the Lyon Consensus 2.0 defines clinical scenarios necessitating long-term acid suppression, escalation of medical therapy, or consideration of invasive procedures such as laparoscopic fundoplication, magnetic sphincter augmentation, endoscopic GERD therapies, or bariatric surgery. Because these interventions carry significant long-term consequences, the updated framework underscores that troublesome symptoms alone are not sufficient to justify such interventions and that objective evidence of reflux-related pathology must be obtained via endoscopic evaluation and/or esophageal physiologic testing. When both objective findings of reflux disease and symptoms consistent with GERD are present, a higher degree of diagnostic certainty supports more targeted therapeutic decisions.2

2. Differentiation between proven GERD and unproven GERD

A key element in the updated consensus is the distinction between proven and unproven GERD. In unproven GERD, where there has been no definitive evidence from endoscopy or ambulatory reflux monitoring, the recommendation is to test off therapy to confirm pathologic acid exposure or to rule out GERD altogether. In proven GERD, objective findings already establish the presence of reflux disease, and persistent symptoms despite therapy prompt evaluation for refractory GERD. This may involve function testing while on antisecretory therapy, helping physicians decide whether to escalate medical treatment or consider a procedural intervention such as laparoscopic fundoplication. By clarifying these scenarios, Lyon Consensus 2.0 provides clearer pathways to diagnosis and management.2

3. Relevance of symptom evaluation for the likelihood of GERD-related pathophysiology

Symptom assessment remains essential for clinical decision-making, but the Lyon Consensus 2.0 stresses that subjective symptom reports alone do not constitute a definitive GERD diagnosis. Although empiric antisecretory therapy can be considered for patients with typical GERD symptoms (heartburn, chest pain, and regurgitation), objective investigations become crucial in patients who fail to respond to proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), present atypical symptoms, or require confirmation prior to long-term therapy or interventional treatment.2 Two-thirds to three-quarters of patients with heartburn and approximately half of those with non-cardiac chest pain respond to short-term PPI therapy, yet a placebo effect ranging from 10% to 25% may confound interpretation.3 Furthermore, regurgitation—a classic GERD symptom—may persist despite PPI therapy, indicating the need for additional diagnostic scrutiny.4 Lyon Consensus 2.0 also refines the classification of GERD symptoms by their likelihood of being truly reflux-related. Typical symptoms (heartburn, regurgitation, and chest pain) exhibit stronger pathophysiological correlation and often guide straightforward initial management or confirmatory testing. Chronic cough and asthma have a weaker but plausible connection to reflux and thus might require objective testing to confirm GERD. Hoarseness, globus, nausea, and dyspepsia have an even lower correlation with reflux, and clinicians should explore non-GERD etiologies while considering targeted testing only if initial investigations suggest a reflux-driven pathogenesis.

For atypical or extraesophageal manifestations, Lyon Consensus 2.0 advises objective testing before initiating GERD-specific treatments to avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary PPI use.5,6 Continuing empiric therapy in patients with typical GERD symptoms who respond well may be justifiable, but clinicians must remain mindful of placebo effects.7,8 Before initiating long-term PPI therapy, objective evidence supporting a diagnosis of clinically actionable GERD is warranted. By underscoring robust clinical and pathophysiological evidence, the consensus ensures that GERD treatments reflect verified disease processes.2

1. Endoscopic criteria

The Lyon Consensus 2.0 recognizes LA grade B esophagitis as definitive evidence of GERD, given that LA grade A may appear in 5% to 7.5% of healthy individuals. In contrast, LA grades B, C, and D rarely occur in healthy subjects and are strongly associated with pathologically increased acid exposure.9-12 Research also shows that LA grade B esophagitis shares similar acid exposure time (AET) and PPI response patterns with LA grade C.9,13,14 Barrett’s esophagus, confirmed via biopsy, and peptic strictures likewise serve as conclusive markers of reflux-related disease, given their strong association with high acid burden and symptom resolution following GERD-targeted therapy.9,10,13,15 Because mucosal healing in patients on PPI therapy can approach 80%,16 the timing of endoscopy is critical: if esophagogastroduodenoscopy occurs after 8 weeks of PPIs, active esophagitis may no longer be visible, potentially leading to an underestimation of disease severity. Performing esophagogastroduodenoscopy before initiating acid suppression may, therefore, provide a more accurate assessment of GERD severity. Other endoscopic findings, such as hiatal hernia, may suggest increased reflux burden but are not independently diagnostic of GERD, and a normal endoscopic examination does not exclude the disease, particularly in those with non-erosive reflux. In Asian populations, lower prevalence rates of severe erosive esophagitis (LA grade C/D) and Barrett’s esophagus may reflect differences in genetics, dietary habits, and Helicobacter pylori infection rates. These regional factors underscore the importance of tailoring diagnostic strategies to local populations.

2. Ambulatory reflux monitoring

1) Prolonged wireless pH monitoring

The Lyon Consensus 2.0 places importance on prolonged wireless pH monitoring in evaluating unproven GERD, particularly for patients with normal endoscopic findings. Approximately 70% of individuals with GERD symptoms display no visible esophageal mucosal damage, making extended monitoring over 96 hours a valuable tool for capturing daily variations in acid exposure and improving both diagnostic accuracy and patient comfort.17-22 Newly established thresholds clarify test interpretation. GERD is confirmed if AET exceeds 6.0% on at least 2 days of monitoring, whereas an AET below 4.0% on all recorded days, accompanied by a negative reflux-symptom association, effectively rules out GERD. AET values between 4.0% and 6.0% remain inconclusive, necessitating clinical correlation. Patients whose AET is below 4.0% but exhibit a positive reflux-symptom association fulfill the criteria for reflux hypersensitivity.1

2) pH-impedance monitoring

Catheter-based pH-impedance monitoring remains an important diagnostic tool, particularly for assessing acidic, non-acidic, and gaseous reflux episodes. It is especially helpful in regurgitation-predominant GERD, belching syndromes, and pulmonary manifestations of reflux. In proven GERD patients who continue experiencing symptoms, ambulatory pH-impedance monitoring can distinguish refractory acid exposure from alternative explanations for persistent complaints, enabling more tailored management. Thresholds have been refined to clarify off-PPI and on-PPI results. Total AET above 6.0% off PPI therapy indicates GERD, while under 40 reflux episodes per day suggests the absence of pathological reflux.23,24 Reflux episode counts between 40 and 80 per day remain inconclusive, whereas more than 80 episodes per day support a GERD diagnosis. Patients with an AET above 4.0% and more than 80 reflux episodes per day despite optimal acid suppression fulfill criteria for actionable refractory GERD, potentially warranting advanced interventions.25-30

Baseline impedance measurement, or mean nocturnal baseline impedance (MNBI), has become a key adjunct marker. MNBI below 1,500 Ω suggests compromised mucosal integrity in GERD, whereas values above 2,500 Ω argue against pathological reflux. A multicenter study involving asymptomatic participants undergoing pH-impedance monitoring off PPIs identified 1,500 Ω at the 5th percentile, measured 3 cm and 5 cm above the lower esophageal sphincter, as an important cutoff.31

Regarding the inclusion of diagnostic parameters for refractory GERD in patients undergoing testing while on antisecretory therapy for proven GERD, a combination of AET exceeding 4% along with more than 80 reflux episodes per day on an optimized acid-suppressive regimen constitutes objective evidence of actionable refractory GERD. MNBI measurements can serve as supportive markers: values below 1,500 Ω suggest impaired mucosal integrity consistent with GERD, whereas readings above 2,500 Ω argue against the presence of pathological GERD.2

3) Considerations for Asia

Growing data suggest that acid exposure thresholds in Asian populations may be lower than those commonly used in Western cohorts. A meta-analysis of 19 Asian studies reported an upper normal AET limit of 3.2% (95% confidence interval, 2.7% to 3.9%) in asymptomatic subjects,32 which is notably less than the Western threshold of 6.0%. An AET above 4.0% has thus been proposed as an abnormal value for Asian patients.33 Although this lowered cutoff may more accurately reflect acid burden in Asian populations, its direct correlation with symptom generation still requires ongoing research to optimize region-specific diagnostic protocols.

4) High-resolution manometry

While high-resolution manometry (HRM) cannot diagnose GERD by itself, it offers supportive information by demonstrating abnormal esophagogastric junction (EGJ) morphology, weakened EGJ barrier function, and motility problems. These findings, categorized via the Chicago classification v4.0, often correlate with elevated AET and erosive esophagitis.34-36 HRM’s primary role in GERD assessment is ruling out achalasia or other major motility disorders when patients fail to respond to PPIs.37 Since motility disorders can mimic reflux symptoms, HRM is crucial for diagnostic clarity. A novel HRM-based metric, the Milan score, had been developed for the assessment of the likelihood of GERD. This score incorporates HRM parameters, including ineffective esophageal motility, EGJ contractile index, EGJ morphology type, and the presence of swallow-induced lower esophageal sphincter relaxation (Fig. 1).38 To validate this model, a supplemental cohort of patients undergoing HRM and pH monitoring was prospectively enrolled across 13 high-volume esophageal function laboratories worldwide. The findings suggest that the Milan score may serve as a valuable screening tool for risk stratification and severity assessment of GERD, enabling a more comprehensive pathophysiological evaluation of the anti-reflux barrier.38 However, while the Milan score represents a promising advancement, several limitations deserve critical consideration, particularly in Asian populations. Since the original validation predominantly involved Western cohorts, regional differences in esophageal motility, EGJ morphology, and normative HRM values may influence the diagnostic accuracy of this tool. There is a lack of physiological evidence that provides optimal cutoff thresholds tailored to Asian populations, and its ability to predict treatment outcomes in these populations remains unclear. Furthermore, its applicability in routine clinical settings, especially those with limited access to advanced diagnostic and motility modalities, has not been fully explored. Additional prospective studies are needed to refine clinical utility of the Milan score in Asian GERD patients, especially those presenting with regurgitation-dominant symptoms or inconclusive findings on conventional tests.38 While HRM findings alone do not provide a definitive diagnosis of GERD, integrating these results with esophageal function tests and endoscopic assessment can enhance diagnostic accuracy.

PRACTICAL CONSIDERATIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

1. Limitations of the Lyon score in Asian populations

Although the Lyon Consensus 2.0 provides a robust diagnostic framework, emerging evidence indicates that the Lyon score has reduced predictive value for treatment success in Asian populations. The Lyon score, developed to complement the updated consensus, assigns weighted values to findings from endoscopy and ambulatory reflux monitoring to confirm or exclude GERD.39 Despite effectively stratifying symptomatic patients into distinct GERD phenotypes in Western cohorts, it shows variable performance by geography and ethnicity.31 In a retrospective study comparing European and Asian groups, the Lyon score’s area under the curve for predicting therapeutic response was 0.908 (p<0.001) in European patients but significantly lower at 0.637 (p<0.001) in Asian cohorts. Factors such as different symptom profiles, diagnostic thresholds, and PPI responsiveness may explain these discrepancies. Asian patients often exhibit a higher prevalence of regurgitation-dominant presentations and may respond better to PPIs even in non-GERD contexts. Additionally, Asians are known to have lower normal AET values and higher baseline impedance levels.31 These distinctions underscore the need to refine diagnostic scoring systems or thresholds for Asian clinical practice. Future investigations should aim to validate region-specific approaches while maintaining alignment with global standards of GERD diagnosis.

2. Cost-effectiveness and accessibility of advanced diagnostic modalities

Laryngopharyngeal reflux (LPR) typifies extraesophageal manifestations of GERD, with symptoms ranging from chronic cough and hoarseness to globus, throat clearing, and postnasal drip.40 However, objective evidence of GERD is often lacking in these patients. Up to 60% of individuals labeled with LPR based on symptoms alone have normal ambulatory reflux monitoring, placing the accuracy of symptom-based LPR diagnosis into question.41 Consistent with these findings, clinical guidelines now advocate initial objective testing rather than empirical PPI therapy in patients with isolated LPR complaints who do not show typical GERD features.2,41 Nevertheless, limited access to specialized tests in primary care settings and the preference for noninvasive treatment approaches complicate guideline implementation. In many Asian healthcare systems, access to advanced esophageal function testing, such as HRM and wireless pH monitoring, remains limited, particularly beyond tertiary referral centers. Several barriers, including high procedural costs, lack of insurance reimbursement, and a shortage of trained motility specialists, hinder the widespread clinical adoption of these tools. Additionally, cultural perceptions and logistical burdens often reduce patient acceptance of invasive diagnostic procedures.33 To address these challenges, the COuGH RefluX score has been internationally validated as a clinical tool that estimates the likelihood of GERD in patients presenting with laryngeal symptoms.42 This model incorporates six clinical parameters—Cough, Overweight/obesity, Globus, Hiatal Hernia, Regurgitation, and male seX—and stratifies risk using thresholds typically between 2.5 and 5.0 (Fig. 2).42 According to the COuGH RefluX score, around 35% of patients fall into a very low-risk category for GERD, thus negating the need for additional diagnostic testing or PPI trials, and about 27% are considered very high risk, enabling earlier initiation of GERD-targeted therapies without further diagnostic steps.42 Application of the COuGH RefluX score in real-world populations, particularly for patients with chronic laryngeal symptoms but no esophagitis on endoscopy, has demonstrated its utility in guiding more personalized PPI therapy decisions.43 By integrating accessible clinical factors, the COuGH RefluX approach aligns with updated GERD management recommendations in settings where advanced diagnostic resources may be limited.

3. Management considerations beyond Lyon 2.0 actionable GERD, integrating esophageal hypersensitivity and hypervigilance into clinical decision-making

Although Lyon Consensus 2.0 offers a systematic diagnostic framework for GERD, it does not directly address the impact of esophageal hypervigilance and anxiety on symptom severity and treatment outcomes. Growing evidence suggests that symptom-specific anxiety and hypervigilance can correlate more strongly with GERD symptom perception than objective acid burden, highlighting the need for greater patient-centered care. The Esophageal Hypervigilance and Anxiety Scale (EHAS) is a validated instrument for assessing cognitive-affective responses in esophageal conditions.44 Multiple studies show that EHAS scores, rather than AET or endoscopic findings, are more predictive of symptom severity in GERD and LPR.45-47 Hypervigilance refers to an amplified sensitivity to potential threats in esophageal sensations, which can lead patients to overinterpret benign stimuli as harmful.48 This distortion may result in persistent or intensified symptoms even in the absence of measurable reflux events. Incorporating psychological assessments like EHAS can thus enhance the precision of treatment strategies. A multidisciplinary evaluation that includes both objective reflux measurements and cognitive-affective profiling may improve outcomes and patient satisfaction. Future updates to GERD guidelines could benefit from incorporating psychophysiological tools alongside conventional diagnostic measures to provide a more comprehensive and personalized management plan.

CONCLUSIONS

Lyon Consensus 2.0 refines and advances the approach to GERD diagnosis, underscoring the need for rigorous, objective evaluation to confirm the pathophysiological basis of symptoms. By emphasizing “actionable GERD,” the updated framework highlights scenarios in which medical or interventional therapies carry substantial long-term implications and therefore demand higher diagnostic certainty. Defining proven versus unproven GERD, designating LA grade B esophagitis as conclusive evidence, and introducing refined thresholds for prolonged wireless pH monitoring and pH-impedance studies help enhance diagnostic specificity. MNBI measurements, with cutoffs like 1,500 Ω, further support the classification of GERD severity. However, application in Asian settings warrants careful consideration due to differences in AET norms, MNBI values, and the lower prevalence of severe erosive esophagitis and Barrett’s esophagus. The Lyon score itself has shown variable predictive ability in Asian populations, suggesting the importance of local adaptations or alternative scoring systems. Moreover, this consensus does not directly address esophageal hypervigilance and anxiety, which can influence perceived symptom severity and treatment responses. Incorporating validated cognitive-affective scales such as EHAS and using clinical tools like COuGH RefluX for LPR allow a more patient-centered approach. Ultimately, the integrative paradigm outlined by Lyon 2.0, enriched by region-specific considerations and psychophysiological assessment, may offer a more individualized and effective strategy for managing GERD in Asia.
