The role of gut microbiota in hypertension-mediated organ damage (HMOD): a systematic review
Authors: Barbara Pala, Giulia Frank, Laura Pennazzi, Laura Di Renzo, Paola Gualtieri, Giuliano Tocci, Speranza Rubattu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHypertension (HTN), a multifactorial condition and major modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease, has been increasingly linked to gut microbiota (GM) alterations, encouraging investigation into its potential role in the progression of hypertension-mediated organ damage (HMOD). This systematic review aims to evaluate the role of GM in HTN pathophysiology and its contribution to HMOD, with the aim of identifying potential microbiota-related targets for personalized therapeutic strategies. The study, registered in PROSPERO and conducted according to PRISMA guidelines, involved a comprehensive search of five databases up to January 2025, selecting human and complementary animal studies investigating the relationship between GM, HTN, and HMOD. While few studies directly assessed HMOD, emerging evidence suggests a protective role of GM and its metabolites against cardiovascular, cerebral and renal injury. Heterogeneity in design, small sample sizes and a lack of standardized methodologies limited comparability therefore we did not perform a meta-analysis. In conclusion, GM dysbiosis and its metabolites are increasingly recognized as key providers to HMOD, offering encouraging targets for future preventive and therapeutic strategies. A substantial proportion of the available evidence originates from preclinical animal studies. While these findings provide valuable mechanistic insights, further longitudinal and interventional research in human populations is warranted to confirm their translational relevance.Graphical abstract Keywords: Gut microbiota, Hypertension, HMOD, Stroke, Animal model, Probiotics, Fecal microbiota transplantation

Introduction

As the most common modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD), Hypertension (HTN) remains a major global health concern. Despite widespread efforts to promote healthier lifestyles and significant medical advances in treating high Blood Pressure (BP), the number of people affected by HTN has doubled globally from 1990 to 2015 [1].

HTN is known as a multifactorial disease, in which various contributing factors can independently or collectively lead to a persistent increase in BP [2].

Recent clinical trial, such as the SPRINT study (Systolic Blood Pressure Intervention Trial), and the updated guidelines from the American Heart Association and the American College of Cardiology, lowering the threshold for defining high BP, indicate that efforts to reduce HTN will remain a key public health priority [3].

It has been repeatedly stated that the American College of Cardiology, the American Heart Association, and the European Society of Hypertension have collaboratively developed behavioral guidelines aimed at maintaining optimal BP, with a particular emphasis on non-pharmacological strategies [4].

Emerging evidence suggests that the Gut Microbiota (GM) contributes to the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying HTN [5]. Indeed, GM has emerged as a contributing factor in several chronic conditions, such as atherosclerosis, obesity, metabolic syndrome, and diabetes, all of which are well-established comorbidities or risk factors closely linked to HTN development [6].

Evidences from both human and animal studies [7–9] highlight a strong association between GM composition and HTN development. GM plays a fundamental role in host metabolic, immunological, and cardiovascular homeostasis [6].

Studies have consistently reported reduced microbial richness, diversity, and evenness in both a rat model (Spontaneously Hypertensive Rat, SHR) and individuals with elevated BP [10]. A reduced alpha diversity, particularly a lower Shannon index values in hypertensive subjects, was highlighted indicating less microbial richness and balance [11].

Several microbial genera appear to influence hypertensive phenotypes. In particular, a reduced abundance of Short-Chain Fatty Acid (SCFA)-producing bacteria and increased levels of pro-inflammatory taxa have been observed in patients with HTN, and are linked to elevated blood pressure and vascular dysfunction [11–14].

Further evidence for the involvement of GM in HTN pathogenesis comes from studies in germ-free (GF) mice, which developed elevated BP after receiving fecal transplants from hypertensive individuals, underscoring the transmissible nature of hypertensive phenotypes via the GM [15].

However, while the role of GM in the onset of HTN is increasingly accepted, less attention has been paid to its role in Hypertension-Mediated Organ Damage (HMOD) — a pivotal stage in the cardiovascular continuum, bridging risk factors and clinically manifest disease [16].

Based on the 2023 ESH Guidelines, our definition of HMOD encompasses functional impairments and structural abnormalities affecting both macrovascular and microvascular systems, as well as key target organs—including the brain, heart, kidneys, and retina—due to sustained elevations in a BP. Its presence correlates with heightened cardiovascular risk, particularly when organ damage is progressive or involves multiple systems. The growing accessibility and use of diagnostic imaging have significantly improved the detection of HMOD, even in individuals without clinical symptoms. Assessment of HMOD involves BOTH basic tests (e.g., ECG, renal function, and urinary albumin excretion) and advanced evaluations (e.g., cardiac imaging, measures of arterial stiffness, vascular ultrasound, retinal examination, cognitive screening, and brain imaging), reflecting the approaches suggested in the 2023 ESH guidelines [16].

This systematic review aims to evaluate the involvement of GM in the progression of HTN and its potential contribution to vascular (atherosclerosis, carotid plaques, arterial stiffness), cardiac (LVH), renal (CKD) and cerebral (stroke) injury.

This review aims to address a gap in the existing literature by focusing specifically on HMOD, rather than on hypertension alone, within the continuum of cardiovascular disease. Unlike previous reviews that primarily examined the association between GM and blood pressure, our work uniquely integrates both clinical and preclinical studies to explore the relationship between gut dysbiosis and target organ damage. Accurate assessment of HMOD is crucial for precise cardiovascular risk stratification and marks a clear turning point in primary prevention. Notably, HMOD may be reversible, as in the case of left ventricular hypertrophy, or irreversible, as with reduced glomerular filtration rate—where progression can only be slowed. In both scenarios, clear and precise quantification of HMOD is fundamental for prognostic evaluation. As life expectancy increases, the clinical priority is not only to prevent hypertension as a disease per se, but also to delay or mitigate its complications and organ-specific sequelae. These strategies could complement conventional antihypertensive therapies in attenuating cardiac, vascular, renal, and cerebral remodeling processes associated with hypertension.

Methods

Literature search

A comprehensive literature search was conducted using PubMed (MEDLINE), Scopus, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), the Evidence Database, and ClinicalTrials.gov, covering studies published until January 2025. Relevant publications were identified using a combination of controlled vocabulary and free-text terms related to GM and HTN.

Search terms included “hypertension - high blood pressure - stroke - cerebrovascular accident - ischemic stroke - hemorrhagic stroke - carotid plaques - arterial stiffness - pulse wave analysis - left ventricular hypertrophy - LVH - renal function decline - eGFR reduced - gut microbiota - gut microbiome - intestinal microbiota - fecal microbiota”.

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines [17], and the protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the registration number CRD420250656109. Although we initially set up our study as a meta-analysis and systematic review, we chose later not to conduct a meta-analysis due to the high heterogeneity of study designs, outcomes, and populations, as well as the predominantly descriptive nature of most results.

Study eligibility and selection

Initially, we aimed to include only human studies that met the following criteria: randomized controlled trials, case control or observational studies involving adults aged 18 or older, with or without HTN; studies that specifically evaluated HMOD or the HTN progression; and articles published in English with full-text availability.

We excluded duplicate records, studies involving irrelevant populations, those that did not clearly define BP as an outcome, those using alternative study designs, or those published in languages other than English. Furthermore, studies that did not report vascular, cardiac, or renal damage outcomes were also excluded.

However, due to the limited number of human studies directly linking GM with HMOD, we chose to broaden our inclusion criteria to incorporate relevant animal studies using murine models. These preclinical studies offered valuable mechanistic insights that complemented the human data and enriched the overall understanding of the GM–HTN relationship. In particular, we selected the stroke-prone spontaneously hypertensive rat (SHRSP) model which has proven instrumental in elucidating the role of GM in HTN and related organ damage [18].

The study selection process began with an initial screening of titles independently conducted by two reviewers. This was followed by a review of abstracts and full-text articles to assess their compliance with the predefined eligibility criteria. Final inclusion decisions were made through consensus between the two reviewers, and any disagreements were resolved by consultation with a third reviewer.

Screening and data extraction were managed using Excel and reviewed manually for consistency.

For each included study, we extracted data on study design, population characteristics, country, year, inclusion/exclusion criteria, key microbial findings (e.g., taxa abundance, SCFA levels, LipoPolySaccharides, LPS, presence), and specific HMOD endpoints such as CKD, LVH, or atherosclerotic plaques. The final database was built using Excel.

To assess the methodological quality and risk of bias in the included studies, we applied the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), a validated tool for evaluating non-randomized studies. Each study was independently scored across three domains: selection of participants, comparability of study groups, and ascertainment of outcomes. A score of 7 or higher (out of a maximum of 9) was considered indicative of low risk of bias [19].

Results

From the available literature, we initially identified 17 eligible studies. We ultimately selected nine case-control studies that specifically investigated the relationship between GM composition and HMOD-related endpoints.

To date, only one human study, conducted by Altemani et al. [20], has directly compared GM composition between hypertensive and normotensive groups with organ damage as a clear endpoint—albeit limited to a female population, reducing generalizability. Given the recognition of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy as acute manifestations of HMOD—and predictors of long-term cardiovascular and renal risk—we included this study [20] which found that women developing preeclampsia exhibited a significantly lower abundance of Coprococcus. This reduction was associated with diminished expression of butyrate-producing genes, and lower serum butyrate levels.

Another animal study, using SHRSP rats, investigated the role of GM in the development of renal–vascular HMOD, modeled as preeclampsia [21]. Alhasan et al. [21] reported elevated BP and proteinuria in the SHRSP group, alongside distinct GM features such as increased Prevotella and Bifidobacterium, and reduced Faecalibacterium and Blautia. Fecal SCFAs (acetate, propionate, valerate) were also increased.

The critical role of pregnancy—and especially lactation—in shaping the GM and thereby influencing the development of chronic diseases, including the prevention of HMOD, was underlined by Nelson et al. [22]. They used a cross-fostering approach to demonstrate that SHRSP pups nursed by WKY dams exhibited lower BP and preserved blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity compared to controls. These effects were linked to differences in GM composition, including an increase in Akkermansia in the WKY-associated GM, pointing toward GM-mediated modulation of cerebral HMOD.

We included in our review the work by Li and colleagues [15] as it demonstrated that dysbiosis of the GM is detectable across different stages of HTN—from normotension to pre-hypertension and overt hypertension - supporting the concept of a GM-driven progression along the HMOD, considered as cardiovascular disease continuum [16].

The authors identified distinct microbial and metabolic signatures associated with each stage, including reduced diversity and enrichment of pro-inflammatory genera such as Prevotella and Klebsiella. These microbial traits were transferable via fecal transplantation to GF mice, where they induced an increase in BP.

The only study explicitly addressing the HTN progression in relation to HMOD is the one by Avery and colleagues [2] using a GF mouse model. They demonstrated that microbial colonization status significantly influenced HMOD, particularly in the kidney. GF mice exhibited more severe renal injury in response to angiotensin II, along with reduced levels of anti-inflammatory SCFAs and increased Th17 polarization. Moreover, the kidney showed greater sensitivity to changes in microbial status than the heart, based on left ventricular (LV) mass assessments obtained via echocardiography and adjusted for tibia length (g/m).

To expand the spectrum of HMOD, we also included Karlsson and colleagues [23] whose study on patients with symptomatic atherosclerosis (a vascular form of HMOD) revealed altered GM profiles compared to healthy controls. In particular, Collinsella was enriched in patients, whereas Roseburia and Eubacterium, known butyrate producers, were more abundant in controls. Functional metagenomic analysis indicated increased expression of pro-inflammatory genes and reduced antioxidant pathways in patients.

Futhermore, we included the preclinical study by Robles-Vera and colleagues [24], which directly assessed HMOD—specifically LVH—in SHRs treated with probiotics (Lactobacillus fermentum, Bifidobacterium breve) and SCFAs (acetate, butyrate). Chronic administration of these agents significantly reduced BP and attenuated LVH, as evidenced by lower LV weight/tibia length ratio. Moreover, the treatment restored endothelial function, decreased oxidative stress, and rebalanced immune cell subsets, indicating a global protective effect on HMOD.

Additionally, Adnan et al. [25] transferred GM from SHR and SHRSP donors to normotensive WKY rats, resulting in increased BP and an altered Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio. The study highlighted, as a pro-hypertensive microbial signature, Desulfovibrio and Clostridium as elevated taxa, while Adlercreutzia was depleted.

Lastly, Bencivenni et al. [18] evaluated GM signatures in SHRSP rats fed a stroke-permissive high-salt diet. They found early and late dysbiosis patterns, with Desulfovibrio and Coriobacteriaceae enriched and SCFA-producing taxa (Faecalibacterium, Lachnospiraceae) depleted in rats that later developed stroke. According to the updated GRADE assessment (Table 3), the strongest evidence (moderate to high certainty) supports the role of GM manipulation—particularly through fecal microbiota transplantation, probiotic supplementation, and dietary modulation—in influencing HMOD. This is especially evident in preclinical models, where interventions demonstrated plausible effects on BP and target-organ protection (vascular, cardiac, cerebral). Moderate certainty evidence supports the contribution of GM dysbiosis to cerebrovascular damage and BBB dysfunction, as shown in cross-fostering models. The association between early-life GM alterations and renal dysfunction in pregnancy-related HMOD (such as preeclampsia) was rated as low to moderate, mainly due to moderate risk of bias and indirectness related to the specificity of the model. Conversely, findings based on observational data or limited sample sizes—such as bacterial depletion in atherosclerosis (Colinsella) or Coprococcus shifts in preeclampsia—received lower grades, reflecting greater uncertainty and less direct causality.

Discussion

The contribution of the GM to HMOD is an emerging area of research. Although current literature provides increasing evidence of GM dysbiosis in hypertensive subjects, according to our knowledge, only a limited number of studies have directly addressed the presence or prevention of target organ damage within this context [2]. Indeed, we opted not to conduct a meta-analysis.

This decision was based on substantial heterogeneity among the included studies, which varied considerably in their design (ranging from animal models to human cohorts and from observational to interventional approaches), the type of outcomes assessed (including cardiac, renal, cerebral, and vascular damage), and the populations involved (such as hypertensive vs. non-hypertensive subjects, pregnancy-related hypertension, or preclinical models). Furthermore, many studies were descriptive in nature and did not provide sufficiently disaggregated data on specific organ damage to allow for focused subgroup analyses [7–9, 26–31]. Several also lacked appropriate hypertensive control groups, limiting the ability to attribute observed damage specifically to elevated BP—for example, some stroke studies did not clearly distinguish hypertensive from non-hypertensive etiologies, complicating interpretation [32–34].

Although our systematic review included studies employing the SHRSP animal model, we excluded those that did not assess HMOD in key target organs such as the heart, kidneys, vasculature, or brain. Specifically, studies focusing primarily on hepatic or intestinal changes—such as those by Kanezawa et al. [35] and Santisteban et al. [36]—were not included in the final analysis.”

The Role of GM in HMOD

The Role of GM in HMOD

Multiple studies have shown that different bacterial species are involved in this process.

Beneficial taxa were consistently depleted in hypertensive states. These include butyrate-producing genera such as Coprococcus, Roseburia, and Eubacterium, which have anti-inflammatory properties and help maintain gut barrier integrity. Altemani et al. [20] reported a significant reduction in Coprococcus abundance and butyrate gene expression in women who later developed preeclampsia, a condition now increasingly recognized as a form of acute HMOD. Similarly, Karlsson and colleagues [23] identified a reduction of Roseburia and Eubacterium in patients with symptomatic carotid atherosclerosis, alongside an overrepresentation of Collinsella, a genus associated with increased systemic inflammation and metabolic disruption. Loss of SCFA-producing bacteria is frequently observed across different manifestations of HMOD, from cardiac and renal to vascular damage. Moreover, Alhasan et al. [21] demostrated that Blautia and Faecalibacterium were depleted and linked to renal impairment.

On the other side, Li et al. [16] linked an enrichment in Provetella and Klebsiella with HMOD. These findings are consistent with Alhasan et al. [21] which observed that Prevotella and Bifidobacterium were positively correlated with BP in preeclampsia-prone pregnancies. Furthermore, Bencivenni et al. [18] found both early and late reductions in SCFA-producing genera such as Faecalibacterium, Lachnospiraceae, and Lachnobacterium, and increases in Desulfovibrio, Coriobacteriaceae, and Streptococcaceae in salt-loaded SHRSP.

Moreover, as for stroke, although many studies did not evaluate correlations with clinical severity, microbial profiling consistently revealed upregulation of families such as Bacteroidaceae, Enterobacteriaceae, and Veillonellaceae, alongside genera like Escherichia/Shigella, Prevotella, and Streptococcus. Protective genera such as Eubacterium, Faecalibacterium, and Roseburia were found in reduced abundance compared to healthy individuals [21].

Meta-analyses focusing on CKD and stroke, both common hypertensive complications, further support the role of GM dysbiosis [37]. In CKD, a distinct enrichment of phyla such as Proteobacteria, and families including Streptococcaceae and Desulfovibrionaceae, has been observed, especially in end-stage disease. Conversely, beneficial taxa like Ruminococcaceae, Faecalibacterium, and Bifidobacterium tend to be depleted [37].

Taken together, these findings highlight a consistent association between GM dysbiosis and the progression of HMOD. While causality is yet to be established, modulation of the GM could represent a promising therapeutic avenue for preventing or mitigating end-organ complications in hypertensive patients.

2.Microbial Metabolites: Key Compounds Implicated in HMOD

Microbial Metabolites: Key Compounds Implicated in HMOD

Among the various GM-derived metabolites, SCFAs have consistently emerged as protective agents in cardiovascular health, particularly in the regulation of BP [38]. Their beneficial effects are largely attributed to vasodilatory and anti-inflammatory properties, mediated via specific G-protein-coupled receptors such as GPR41, GPR43, GPR109A, and Olfr78. While GPR41 activation is associated with hypotensive responses, Olfr78 has been implicated in hypertensive signaling [6, 39].

Evidence from animal studies further supports the protective role of SCFAs. In SHR, oral administration of probiotics or SCFAs prevented BP elevation and significantly reduced LVH, a hallmark of cardiac HMOD. These interventions also improved endothelial function, corrected the Th17/Treg imbalance, and attenuated vascular oxidative stress—key mechanisms involved in hypertensive organ injury [24].

The study by Avery et al. [2] reinforced this concept, showing that GF mice exposed to hypertensive stimuli developed more severe renal damage compared to colonized counterparts. The absence of SCFAs in GF animals was associated with increased Th17-mediated inflammation, emphasizing the immunomodulatory influence of microbial metabolites in modulating hypertensive renal injury.

Similarly, Alhasan et al. [21] reported elevated fecal concentrations of acetate, propionate, and valerate in preeclampsia-prone SHRSP suggesting that SCFA accumulation may reflect impaired absorption rather than increased synthesis, with potential implications for renal HMOD in hypertensive pregnancy.

In contrast to the protective effects of SCFAs, trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), a microbial metabolite derived from dietary precursors such as choline, phosphatidylcholine, L-carnitine, and betaine, has been strongly linked to adverse cardiovascular outcomes. Elevated TMAO levels have been associated with HTN [40], atherosclerosis, thrombotic risk [41] and multiple pro-atherogenic mechanisms [42–44].

These mechanisms include enhanced foam cell formation, impaired reverse cholesterol transport, endothelial dysfunction, and oxidative stress induction via NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Together with reduced endothelial progenitor cells, they contribute to plaque formation and vascular injury [5].

Interestingly, despite its recognized pathogenic role, TMAO did not exacerbate organ damage in the context of the Avery et al. model [2]. Although colonized mice had detectable TMAO levels, they exhibited less renal injury than GF mice, suggesting that beneficial microbial metabolites like SCFAs may counterbalance TMAO deleterious effects. This underscores the complexity of host–microbiota–metabolite interactions in HMOD and the need to evaluate metabolite profiles in a holistic manner.

Emerging evidence also connects elevated TMAO to cerebral small vessel disease and CKD, particularly in the presence of reduced SCFAs and an overrepresentation of bacteria capable of producing uremic toxins such as p-cresyl sulfate and indoxyl sulfate [45]. These compounds have been implicated in endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and vascular inflammation, and may represent additional mechanistic links between gut dysbiosis and HMOD that warrant further investigation.

Supporting this, Bencivenni et al. [18] found that SHRSP rats exhibited pro-inflammatory and endothelial-disrupting metabolites. These microbial shifts—characterized by depletion of butyrate-producing taxa and expansion of urease-, uricase-, and indole-producing bacteria—appear to promote inflammation and endothelial dysfunction, accelerating organ damage.

Lastly, although several studies have detected microbial DNA within atherosclerotic plaques [46], the plausible role of these bacteria in plaque formation remains debated. While microbial presence could reflect true pathogenic involvement in promoting inflammation and plaque instability, an alternative hypothesis is that these signatures merely represent residual, non-viable microbes without direct pathogenic roles [47].

3.Inflammation and HMOD

Inflammation and HMOD

One of the most consistent findings across the selected literature was the enrichment of pro-inflammatory bacterial genera such as Prevotella and Klebsiella in individuals with pre-hypertension and hypertension. Li et al. [15] associated these genera with elevated BP, and their presence correlated with inflammatory metabolic profiles and reduced microbial diversity.

These observations are echoed in preclinical studies in the SHRSP model. Alhasan et al. [21] identified enrichment of Prevotella and Parasutterella with a preeclampsia-like phenotype. Bencivenni et al. [18] similarly observed an increase in taxa associated with inflammatory signaling in high-salt fed SHRSP.

Another microbial component of interest is lipopolysaccharide (LPS), derived from the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria. Increased LPS-producing bacteria, such as Escherichia coli and Enterobacter cloacae, have been identified in individuals with metabolic disturbances and hypercholesterolemia. LPS is a potent pro-inflammatory trigger, leading to cytokine release and systemic inflammation, thereby can exacerbate multi-organ dysfunction [48, 49]. Moreover, it has to be underline that inflammation-linked HMOD is modifiable through maternal microbiota transfer, according to Nelson et al. [22].

Excessive production of pro-inflammatory cytokines contributes to elevated levels of circulating nitric oxide, thereby can trigger a widespread inflammatory response. This systemic inflammation may ultimately lead to multi-organ dysfunction, including impairment of cardiac, renal, hepatic, and pulmonary systems [5].

Within our selected studies, Robles-Vera et al. [24] demonstrated that probiotic or SCFA supplementation in SHR attenuated hypertension-induced vascular dysfunction by normalizing circulating LPS level and suppressing LPS–TLR4-mediated inflammatory signaling in the vasculature. These interventions were associated with reduced oxidative stress and improved endothelial function, highlighting the key role of LPS in hypertensive tissue injury. Similarly, Karlsson et al. [23] found an enrichment of genes related to LPS biosynthesis in patients with symptomatic atherosclerosis, suggesting a GM-derived contribution to systemic inflammation and vascular injury.

Interestingly, Bartolomaeus and colleagues reported that propionate supplementation reduced LPS-driven inflammation in hypertensive mice, resulting in improved BP control and attenuation of cardiac injury [50]. Moreover, Avery et al. [2] suggest the immunomodulatory effect of GM through the increase in Th17 polarization.

These findings support the hypothesis that modulating gut-derived LPS through GM-targeted interventions may serve as a promising strategy to prevent or mitigate HMOD.

To our knowledge, no previous review has directly compared the evidence linking SCFA, TMAO, and LPS with HMOD within a single analytical framework. 4.Potential Therapies: Probiotics and Fecal Microbiota Transplantation in HMOD

Potential Therapies: Probiotics and Fecal Microbiota Transplantation in HMOD

Recent findings have highlighted the therapeutic potential of modulating GM to prevent or attenuate HMOD. A significant reduction in Lactobacillus species has been correlated with elevated BP in both animal models and humans, underscoring the protective role of certain commensal bacteria in vascular homeostasis [51].

Beyond associations, Robles-Vera et al. [28] demonstrated that long-term administration of Lactobacillus fermentum and Bifidobacterium breve in SHR prevented BP elevation and reduced LVH. It also normalized vascular inflammation and restore Treg/Th17 immune balance—core mechanisms of HMOD. Similar results were reported by Alhasan et al. [21]. These effects were partially mediated through the restoration of SCFA-producing bacteria and a reduction in circulating endotoxins such as LPS.

Microbial modulation, through probiotics or SCFA supplementation, thus emerges as a promising therapeutic approach capable of acting upstream of traditional pharmacological targets. Supporting this, pharmacological and microbial interventions, such as Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG or telmisartan, have shown comparable benefits in mitigating dysbiosis-driven vascular dysfunction [6, 52]. These findings reinforce the idea that probiotic strategies—or even fecal microbiota transplantation—may be explored as adjunct therapies for the prevention and management of cardiovascular and renal complications in HTN. Importantly, Adnan et al. [25] demonstrated that fecal microbiota transplantation from hypertensive SHR rats to normotensive WKY rats led to a significant increase in BP in the recipients. This provided direct evidence that GM alterations alone can induce hypertensive phenotypes, underscoring the therapeutic potential of restoring eubiotic microbial communities.

Nevertheless, the findings reported by Avery and colleagues [2] also align with the recent position statement from the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) Working Group on Coronary Pathophysiology and Microcirculation. This position emphasizes the importance of using open-access microbiome datasets and standardized experimental protocols to enhance reproducibility and improve the translational value of hypertension research. The use of standardized guidelines, such as those proposed by Marques and colleagues [38], is strongly recommended to ensure methodological consistency in GM studies.

In line with these principles, Nelson et al. [22] employed a cross-fostering design to manipulate early-life microbiota exposure in SHRSP rats, demonstrating the translational utility of non-pharmacological, microbiota-based experimental strategies.

Moreover, future approaches should embrace the concept of personalized precision microbiota modulation, recognizing that individuals may differ in their baseline microbiota composition, metabolic capacity, and responsiveness to interventions (e.g., responders vs. non-responders to probiotics or dietary strategies). Tailoring interventions through patient stratification based on microbial and metabolic profiles could enhance therapeutic efficacy, optimize clinical outcomes, and reduce inter-individual variability.

5.Sex-related differences in study populations and outcomes

Sex-related differences in study populations and outcomes

It is important to underscore a critical gap in the current literature—namely, the lack of investigation into sex-specific responses in hypertensive GM research. Addressing this limitation is essential to ensure that future findings are more broadly applicable across sexes [53] Across the studies reviewed, the representation of sexes and the analysis of sex-specific effects were highly variable. The metagenomic study by Li et al. [15] included 196 Chinese adults (41 healthy controls, 56 pre-hypertensive, 99 hypertensive) with both sexes represented; however, although gender ratios differed among groups, no significant sex-specific differences in microbial composition were detected and no stratified analyses were performed. Robles-Vera et al. [24] employed an exclusively male SHRSP model, precluding any extrapolation to females. Similarly, Nelson et al. [22] used an exclusively male SHRSP model to investigate the impact of GM manipulation on blood–brain barrier integrity and neuroinflammation. While providing valuable mechanistic insights, the lack of inclusion of female animals limits the extrapolation of these findings to both sexes and precludes assessment of potential sex-specific responses to microbiota-targeted interventions. Moreover, Alhasan et al. [21] and Avery et al. [2] analyzed mixed-sex human cohorts without reporting sex-stratified outcomes, limiting the capacity to assess differential GM–HMOD associations.

In contrast, Karlsson et al. [23] specifically investigated pregnant women with and without preeclampsia, thus focusing entirely on a female physiological condition. Notably, distinct GM signatures were observed between preeclamptic and normotensive pregnancies, suggesting that pregnancy-associated hemodynamic and immunological changes may modulate microbial composition and metabolite profiles even within the same sex. However, these findings cannot be generalized to non-pregnant females or to males.

Other studies, including those by Bencivenni et al. [18], Adnan et al. [25], Altemani et al. [20], and Li et al. [15], included both sexes but did not consistently report or adjust for sex-related differences. Overall, the current evidence base is characterized by a paucity of studies designed to detect or interpret sex-related variability in GM composition, metabolite profiles, and their associations with HMOD phenotypes. Given the known influences of sex hormones, immune modulation, and cardiometabolic risk profiles on GM, future research should incorporate balanced recruitment, pre-specified sex-stratified analyses, and exploration of hormone-related modulatory effects to enable more precise, personalized interventions.

6.Knowledge gaps and research questions

Knowledge gaps and research questions

Several key areas require further investigation. First, future studies should aim to clarify the causal role of specific gut microbial taxa and their metabolites in the pathogenesis of HMOD, moving beyond correlative associations through well-designed mechanistic and longitudinal studies. Second, the potential for sex-specific differences in gut microbiota composition and function—and their influence on hypertensive organ damage—remains underexplored and warrants dedicated research, including stratified analyses and sex-based interventions. Third, there is a critical need for standardization of microbiome research protocols in the context of hypertension, including harmonized criteria for sample collection, sequencing methods, data analysis pipelines, and outcome definitions. Such standardization would improve reproducibility and comparability across studies, ultimately enabling more robust conclusions and clinical translation.

Limitations

Despite promising results, several limitations must be acknowledged. The main limitation is the predominance of preclinical animal studies among the available evidence. Although these studies are essential for elucidating underlying biological mechanisms, their findings may not fully translate to clinical outcomes in humans. Future research should prioritize well-designed prospective cohort studies and randomized controlled trials to validate these associations, assess causality, and evaluate the clinical efficacy of targeted GM modulation in preventing or mitigating HMOD. Most animal studies scored well on selection and comparability criteria but often lacked detailed reporting on outcome assessment and blinding procedures, limiting their translational strength. Human studies generally showed good methodological quality, particularly in terms of cohort selection and outcome ascertainment; however, some presented a moderate risk of bias due to small sample sizes, lack of adjustment for confounders, or short follow-up periods. Moreover, many studies relied on cross-sectional designs, lacked hypertensive control groups, or were conducted in small and homogeneous populations. Furthermore, most data derive from fecal samples, which may not fully represent mucosal or systemic microbial interactions. In preclinical studies, although models such as SHRSP offer high pathophysiological relevance for organ damage, their translational value is limited by species-specific GM composition and dietary dependency. Additional variability in animal housing, breeding, and microbial exposure further complicates reproducibility. Notably, few investigations evaluated microbial function directly (e.g., via metabolomics or transcriptomics), and SCFA or LPS levels were often inferred rather than measured. Nonetheless, the convergence of findings across human and animal studies reinforces the plausibility of a microbiota–hypertension–HMOD axis and highlights the need for longitudinal and interventional studies to establish causality and therapeutic potential.

Conclusions

Emerging evidence highlights a significant interplay between GM composition, its metabolic output, and the development of HMOD. Dysbiosis—marked by the depletion of beneficial taxa such as Roseburia, Eubacterium, Faecalibacterium, Blautia, and Adlercreutzia, and the expansion of potentially pro-inflammatory or endotoxin-producing genera including Streptococcus, Turicibacter, Escherichia/Shigella, Desulfovibrio, Clostridium, and Collinsella—appears consistently associated with cardiovascular, renal, and cerebrovascular complications in hypertensive individuals.

The role of microbial metabolites is pivotal in this context. SCFAs exert vasoprotective and anti-inflammatory effects, while elevated levels of TMAO have been linked to atherosclerosis, endothelial dysfunction, and renal impairment, partly through mechanisms involving oxidative stress, inflammation, and impaired lipid metabolism. Additionally, increased exposure to microbial endotoxins such as LPS may further exacerbate systemic inflammation, contributing to multi-organ damage.

Therapeutic modulation of the GM, through probiotics, prebiotics, or fecal microbiota transplantation, has shown beneficial effects in preclinical models. These include improved BP regulation, reduced vascular oxidative stress, and attenuation of LVH and renal damage—often mediated through the restoration of SCFA-producing taxa and suppression of inflammatory pathways. These interventions may offer a novel and adjunctive strategy for managing HMOD, particularly in the context of resistant hypertension, metabolic comorbidities, or hypertensive pregnancy syndromes.

Nevertheless, while associative and mechanistic data continue to grow, causality remains to be firmly established. Future longitudinal studies and controlled trials are essential to clarify whether targeting GM composition and function can effectively prevent or reverse HMOD in hypertensive patients. The integration of microbiome profiling into clinical practice may ultimately allow for more personalized and preventive approaches in CVD.

Moreover, future research should clarify whether targeted modulation of gut microbiota composition and function, for example through probiotics, prebiotics, or dietary strategies, can effectively prevent or reverse specific forms of hypertension-mediated organ damage in human populations. It should also elucidate the sex-specific and hormone-related determinants of microbiota–HMOD interactions to better inform personalized therapeutic strategies. Moreover, further studies are needed to understand how multiple microbial metabolites, including SCFAs, TMAO, and LPS, interact in vivo to influence the trajectory and severity of hypertensive organ damage.
