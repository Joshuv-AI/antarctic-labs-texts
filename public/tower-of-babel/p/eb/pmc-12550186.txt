Tailoring the formulation of wholemeal wheat flour: the selection and recombination of milling fractions to control acrylamide and deoxynivalenol contamination☆

Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Bakery products are staple foods that are produced throughout the world, and wheat (Triticum aestivum spp. aestivum) flour is generally their main ingredient. Most dietary guidelines recommend consuming whole grains over refined grains, because several beneficial compounds, such as dietary fibres, minerals, vitamins and phenolic compounds are concentrated in the outer grain layers, i.e. in the bran and aleurone layer (Žilić et al., 2012).

Despite the nutritional advantages of the use of wholegrain wheat flour in bakery products, it could increase the risks associated with the intake of several contaminants, whether generated by transformation processes, such as acrylamide (AA), or already naturally present in the raw material, such as mycotoxins. In particular, the AA content in wholegrain biscuits could increase by 65 to 89 %, compared to refined wheat flour (Žilić et al., 2020), and the concentration of mycotoxins in wheat by-products, such as bran, shorts and red dog, may be up to eight-fold higher than that of the whole kernel (Cheli et al., 2013). AA is a process-related compound that has been classified, by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC, 1994), as belonging to Group 2 A. The principal pathway for the formation of AA in food is through the reaction of free amino acid asparagine (ASN) with reducing sugars, as part of the Maillard reaction, under high temperature (usually >120 °C) and low moisture (Surdyk et al., 2004) conditions. As the content of reducing sugars in bakery products usually exceeds the level of ASN, this amino acid is the limiting factor in the formation of AA (Žilić et al., 2020).

Apart from AA, which is formed during thermal processing, deoxynivalenol (DON) is also considered one of the most important contaminants in wheat and its related bakery products. Mycotoxin DON is produced, along with its modified forms, during wheat cultivation by Fusarium species (EFSA, 2013). The consumption of food contaminated by DON is associated with acute, adverse, gastrointestinal effects, and the most exposed groups, as far as chronic exposure is concerned, are infants, toddlers and other children (EFSA, 2013). In this regard, the European Commission has issued Commission Regulation (EU) 2017/2158 which outlines mitigation measures and benchmark levels for the reduction of AA in bakery foods, with suggested values ranging from 50 μg kg−1 in bread to 350 μg kg−1 in biscuits. Effective mitigation measures are of relevant interest, and considering that the amount of acrylamide in 30 % of biscuits that are baked at 180 °C for 13 min exceeds the benchmark limit (Žilić et al., 2020), it is necessary to strictly control the formation of acrylamide in this product, thereby justifying the studies that have focused on AA mitigation strategies in biscuits.

Furthermore, as far as contamination by mycotoxins is concerned, Commission Regulation (EU) 2024/1022 established maximum levels of DON contamination in wheat grains (1000 μg kg−1), in the milling products of cereals (600 μg kg−1) and in bakery products (400 μg kg−1). However, AA and mycotoxins are almost unavoidable compounds, the former being formed through chemical reactions triggered by processing, starting from ASN, which accumulates during wheat ripening, and the latter being accumulated under natural field conditions and resulting difficult to degrade in post-harvest and transformation processes (Suman et al., 2019). Therefore, the natural presence of AA precursors or native mycotoxins in raw materials (grains and flours) before technological processes are applied is a key factor in their control in final bakery products, and in wholemeal ones in particular. As far as the types of flour with a different occurrences of outer grain layers are concerned, Italian legislation (Decreto del Presidente della Repubblica n. 187, 2001) only considers the ash content to classify cereal flours as wholemeal (ash content between 1.30 and1.70 %), brown (< 0.95 %), semi-white (< 0.80 %) or refined (white) (< 0.65 %).

The recombination of milling fractions (e.g. bran, shorts and red dog, which show a decreasing ash content in this order), derived from the outermost layers, with refined flour is the most common technical solution exploited at an industrial scale to obtain wholemeal flour in a roller milling (RM) process. Since the parameter used to define a four as wholemeal is the final ash content, Italian legislation does not distinguish between flour mixtures obtained from recombining all the fractions, and only refers to the original proportion of the kernel, according to the definition proposed by the Healthgrain consortium (Kamp et al., 2014) or to selected fractions (e.g. only bran without the germ). Furthermore, the legislation has not yet taken into account the application of different milling processes, as alternatives to RM, such as the traditional stone-milling (SM) technique. Among the other available milling techniques, debranning (D) is a by now well-established technology in durum wheat milling, but it is also of interest for some soft wheat applications. Debranning consists of an abrasive wheel progressively removing the outer layers of the kernel, from the outer layers to the inner ones. Thus, the debranned-fractions (DF) could be collected to separate the selected outermost layers, which contain most of the bioactive compounds from the other layers, concentrated especially in the aleurone, and could then be recombined with refined flour (Ciccoritti et al., 2017). Several studies have indicated a higher content of ASN in wholemeal flour than in refined flour (Fredriksson et al., 2004; Žilić et al., 2020) and DON (Cheli et al., 2013; Tibola et al., 2020), and they have highlighted an uneven distribution of both in the milling fractions. Furthermore, to the best of the authors' knowledge, no research has been carried out on controlling the risk of both AA and mycotoxins in wholegrain flour and in the derived baked products to maintain the health benefits of the outermost kernel layers by acting on the milling process and considering possible flour recombination and sieving.

The aim of the present study has been to identify milling solutions that would be able to reduce AA and DON contamination in baked biscuits made with wholemeal, brown and semi-white flour, by considering the distribution of the aforementioned compounds in milling fractions derived from different processes (RM, SM, D) applied to the same commercial batches of common wheat but with different combinations of flour mixtures. The hypothesis behind this study is that the results of the milling fractions characterisation, in terms of ash, ASN and DON, could be used to obtain predictive data useful to drive the recombination process, while ensuring compliance with the European legislation concerning AA and DON levels in the final product and, at the same time, maintaining the related nutritional benefits for consumers.

Materials and methods

Wheat samples

Two commercial batches of common wheat were provided and milled by an industrial mill (Molini Valente S.p.A, Felizzano, Italy) through different milling processes. The two batches were named according to the area of origin of the wheat; IT was used to indicate grain cultivated in the Po Plain in northern Italy, while EU was used to denote a mixture of wheat grown in France (47.5 %), Slovenia (29.5 %) and Hungary (23.0 %). Both batches were taken from wheat cultivated in the 2020–21 growing season, and they have been produced in accordance with the crop practices commonly applied in the considered cereal growing areas. In particular, according to the supply chain contract, the nitrogen fertilization was applied between the tillering and the stem elongation growth stage, based on the expected crop uptake, and a fungicide application was carried out at wheat flowering to minimize the possible contamination of mycotoxin in field.

The rheological properties of the refined flour from the two batches were characterised using a Chopin Alveograph (CHOPIN Technologies Alveolab®, KPM Analytics, Westbotough, U.S.A.) and a farinograph (FarinoGraph, Anton Paar GmbH, Graz, Austria). Both batches showed suitable rheological properties for biscuit making; the flour from the batch IT had a lower W (163 vs. 183 J 10−4, respectively) and stability (1.9 vs. 5.0 min (min)) than the batch EU, a slightly higher dough development time (1.4 vs 1.2 min), but the same values of P/L (0.63) and water absorption required to reach 500 BU on the farinograph (54 %).

Wheat kernels from both batches were carefully sampled and a representative homogeneous subsample (3 kg) was milled, using a laboratory centrifugal mill (Model ZM-100, Retsch, Haan, Germany), prior to analysis, to obtain wholemeal flour to use as a representative control of the initial kernels. Each batch was then subjected to RM and SM in an industrial plant, while a representative subsample of 10 kg of kernel was subjected to a D process using laboratory equipment. The milling and sampling procedures, more details of which are presented hereafter, were repeated three times to obtain three independent replicates of each sample.

Milling processes and sampling procedures

Roller milling (RM)

Overall, an amount of around 100 t of wheat grains was taken from the batch IT and 100 t from the batch EU, and milled using an industrial RM (LAM/CS, Ocrim S.p.A, Cremona, Italy) capable of processing 16 t hour−1 of wheat, together with flour purifiers (SDB 500, Ocrim S.p.A), obtaining at the end the refined flour, germ, bran, shorts and red dog. Samples were collected from the opening slits of the milling plant, according to the sampling method reported in European Commission Regulation (EC) No. 401/2006. A dynamic sampling procedure was set up in which each aggregate sample was the result of a careful blending of 40 incremental samples of 100 g each, collected over a period of one hour at regular intervals and replicated three times. The milling yield of each fraction was calculated and it resulted in 78.8 % for refined flour, 8.2 % for bran, 9.3 % for shorts, 3.6 % for red dog and 0.08 % for germ. The particle size of each milling fraction was measured using a set of six sieves (Retsch, Haan, Germany) with progressively smaller mesh sizes (μm): 2000, 1500, 1000, 710, 500, 250, and a final collector of particles <250 μm. A 50.0 ± 0.5 g sample was placed on the largest mesh sieve, and the sieves were mechanically shaken using a sieve shaker (AS 200, Retsch, Haan, Germany) for 20 min. After shaking, the material retained by each sieve was collected, weighed and expressed as g of particles per 100 g of sample for each particle size class. The particle size of each milling fraction is reported in Fig. S1 (A).

Stone milling (SM)

Aliquots of approximately 2 t of grains were taken from both the IT and EU batches and were milled using a stone mill (BioStoneMill, Ocrim S.p.a.) composed of two natural 1500 mm diameter and 200 mm thick stones, the upper of which was rotated at a speed of 110 rpm. A dynamic sampling procedure was set up in which each aggregate sample was the result of a careful blending of 40 incremental samples of 100 g each, collected over a period of 1 h at regular intervals and replicated three times. A further sifting process was performed using a single channel square plansifter (BMG 115, Ocrim S.p.a.) to collect brown flour with a particle size <250 μm. The milling yield for the wholemeal SM flour was estimated to be equal to 100 %, while the yield for the brown SM flour was 26.1 %. The particle size of each milling fraction was quantified, as described in paragraph 2.2.1 and is reported in Fig. S1 (A).

Debranning

Debranning (D) of the wheat grains, which involved removal of the outer layers by means of abrasion and friction, was performed at a laboratory scale using a TM-05C miller (Satake, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with an abrasive stone.

The kernels were initially pearled, starting from 5 kg of unprocessed grain samples, to remove 5 % of the original grain weight, according to the process reported by Giordano et al. (2019), and this resulted in a first debranning fraction (DF 0–5 w/w). The pearling process was repeated to remove a second, third, fourth, fifth, sixth, seventh and eighth fraction (DF 5–10, DF 10–15, DF 15–20, DF 20–25, DF 25–30, DF 30–35, DF 35–40 w/w, respectively). The residual 60 % of the starchy kernel (40–100 w/w) was also collected and milled in a laboratory centrifugal mill (ZM-100 model) equipped with a 1-mm sieve, so that the sum of the eight fractions and starchy kernel (40–100 w/w) represented the original lot. The D process was performed on both batches and repeated three times on each batch.

Milling fraction characterisation

Samples preparation

Prior to analyses for the quantification of the ASN and DON content, a representative aliquot of 100 g of each sampled fraction, collected from the RM, SM and D processes, was further ground to a fine powder (particle size <250 μm) with a Cyclotec 1093 sample mill (Foss, Padua, Italy) and stored for a maximum of four weeks at −25 °C. The remaining sampled fractions were stored, for at maximum of eight weeks at 4 °C, for the biscuit production process. The three replications of each fraction were analysed for the moisture, ash, ASN and DON content (the latter only for the batch IT), while the content of protein, fat, glucose and fructose was obtained from a merged representative sample of the three independent samples.

Proximal composition

The moisture content of the milling fractions collected from the RM, SM and D processes was determined by oven-drying at 105 °C for 24 h in order to express all the results on a dry weight (d.w.) basis.

Each sampled fraction was analysed for the content of ash (AOAC 923.03, Direct method), protein (AOAC 990.03, Dumas method, conversion factor: 5.7), fat (AOAC 2003.05, Soxhlet method), glucose and fructose (AOAC 985.09, Megazyme d-Fructose/d-Glucose test kit). The selected milling fractions for the flour mixtures formulation were analysed for their fibre content (AOAC 991.43, Megazyme Total Dietary Fibre assay kit) and starch amount (CEC, 72/199/EEC, Polarimetric method).

Free asparagine quantification

The ASN was quantified using a K-ASNAM L-Asparagine/l-Glutamine/Ammonia Assay Kit (Megazyme International Ltd., Ireland), according to the total deproteination and extraction process described by Lecart et al. (2018), albeit with some modifications to reduce the use of perchloric acid and using a more vigorous mixing, shortening the homogenization time. Reagents, in particular trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and potassium hydroxide (KOH), were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Canada Co (Oakville, Ontario, Canada). An aliquot of 20 mL of perchloric acid (1 mol L−1) was added to an aliquot of 10.00 ± 0.05 g of powdered sample and mixed using a homogenizer (Diax 600, Heidoplh scientific products GmbH, Schwabach, Germany) for 2 min for the extraction. Each sample solution was brought to pH 8 by adding 2 mol L−1 KOH and was then transferred to a gauged flask and adjusted, with deionized water to 100 mL. Each gauged flask was then refrigerated in an ice bath for 20 min to enhance precipitation, and the supernatant was subsequently filtered through Whatman® grade 1 filters (Brentford, the United Kingdom). The first five drops were discarded to avoid the presence of solid particles, and the filtrate was collected in an Eppendorf micro-tube (Eppendorf S.r.l., Milan, Italy).

The ASN quantification was performed following the kit assay protocol, provided by the manufacturer, albeit with halved volumes. A Cary 60 UV–Vis Agilent spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California, USA) was used to measure the UV absorbance of the sample extracts at λ 340 nm. The calculation described in the assay protocol allowed the concentration of L-asparagine to be determined and expressed as mg kg−1 d.w.

Deoxynivalenol quantification

DON analysis was performed as described in detail by Scarpino et al. (2019), on all the fractions sampled in the RM, SM and D processes for the batch IT. An aliquot of 5.00 ± 0.05 g was weighed for each milling fraction and then inserted into 50 mL centrifuge tubes and shaken at 300 rpm for 90 min (RS-LS 20 shaker model, Phoenix Instrument, Garbsen, Germany) with 20 mL of CH3CN/H2O/CH3COOH (79/20/1, V/V/V). The extract was filtered through Whatman® grade 1 filters and subjected to dilution with the same volume of diluting solution (CH3CN/H2O/CH3COOH 20/79/1, V/V/V). The diluted extract was vortexed and filtered through regenerated cellulose syringe filters (Phenex-RC, Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, the United States). After appropriate mixing, 20 μL of the diluted filtered extract was injected into the liquid chromatography, which was coupled with a tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) system. Each sample was analysed with both positive and negative polarity. The mobile phase consisted of H2O and CH3OH, both of which were acidified with 0.1 % V/V CH3COOH. A Gemini-NX C18, 100 × 2.0 mm inner diameter, 3 μm particle size 110 Å column, equipped with a C18 4 × 2 mm security guard cartridge (Phenomenex, Torrance, Califronia, U.S.A.), was used for this purpose. The DON content was expressed as μg kg−1 on a d.w. basis.

Formulation of the flour mixtures and biscuits and their characterisation

Flour mixture formulation

The compared flour mixtures and the relative percentages of each included milling fraction are reported in Table 1. The milling fractions were used with their original particle size, except for the bran included in the wholemeal bran and semi-white bran mixtures, which was micronised using a Cyclotec 1093 mill to obtain a powder with particle size <250 μm to reproduce the industrial practice of bran micronization aimed at limiting its impact on dough rheological properties (Hemdane et al., 2016). The ASN and DON contents, the proximal composition and the particle size of each flour mixture were calculated considering their concentration in each milling fraction and their percentage of inclusion in the mixture.

Biscuit production

The flour mixtures were then used to make biscuits in the bakery laboratory of Molini Valente S.p.A., Felizzano, Italy, considering a standard recipe for wholemeal biscuits. Briefly, the dough was obtained using the creaming method, which consists of the pre-mixing of high-oleic sunflower oil (80 g), sucrose (100 g), salt (1.6 g for biscuits made from wholemeal, brown and semi-white flour mixtures, and 1.3 g for biscuits made with refined flour) and vanillin (0.3 g) for 4 min at 285 rpm in a kneading machine equipped with a leaf-shaped beater (N50CE, Hobart GmbH, Offenburg, Germany) and gradually adding the water (80 g for biscuits made from wholemeal, brown and semi-white mixtures, and 67 g for biscuits made with refined flour) during the last 40 s. A flour mixture (300 g), ammonium bicarbonate (4.5 g) and sodium bicarbonate (2.3 g) were then added and kneading was continued for 3 min with the speed regulator set at 139 rpm. The obtained dough was rolled out with a dough sheeter (Easy, Tekno Stamap s.r.l., Grisignano di Zocco, Italy) to a thickness of 5.5 mm and cut, to obtain 40 raw biscuits, obtained using a circular stainless-steel 6 cm diameter pastry cup. The biscuits were then placed, maintaining the same arrangement, on a labelled stainless steel perforated baking tray. Two trays were placed together in the middle of an electric oven (Modul 4 T-40/60, Mondial Forni S.p.A., Verona, Italy), set at static mode at a temperature of 180 °C at the top and 165 °C at the bottom and baked for 27 min. After baking, the biscuits were removed from the oven, cooled on a grid at room temperature (25 °C) for 1 h and then packed in polyethylene bags and stored at a refrigerated temperature (4 °C) until analysis. Three separate dough production processes were carried out for each flour mixture, providing three replicates per mixture. Before the analysis, all the baked biscuits of each replication were ground using a Moulinex food processor (La Moulinette 800 W, Moulinex, Bagnolet, France) to obtain a homogeneous powder.

Moisture content

The moisture content of the baked biscuits was determined, as described in Section 2.3.2, to express the results on a d.w. basis.

Acrylamide quantification

The QuEChERS method was used for AA extraction. An aliquot of 2.5 g of powdered baked biscuits was weighed in a 50 mL polypropylene centrifuge tube, to which 5 mL of water (LC-MS grade) and 10 mL acetonitrile (LC-MS grade) were added, and the sample was vortexed for 1 min. Subsequently, 4.0 g of MgSO4 and 0.5 g NaCl were added and the sample was vortexed for a further 3 min. After centrifuging at 3000 rpm for 3 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was transferred into new 15 mL tubes, and 100 μL Carrez I (potassium ferrocyanide trihydrate) and 100 μL Carrez II (zinc acetate heptahydrate) were added. The tubes were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 3 min. Furthermore, 9.0 mL of the upper layer was pipetted out and collected in a glass balloon for solvent evaporation, which was conducted at Rotavapor (R-210, BÜCHI Labortechnik AG, Flawil, Switzerland). The solid residue was solubilised with 1 mL of water (LC-MS grade) and transferred to a 1.5 mL Eppendorf plastic tube, centrifuged at 14000 rpm for 5 min and transferred to closed vials.

AA analysis was carried out with a UHPLC system (Accela HPLC system, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, Massachusetts, U.S.A.) coupled with a diode array detector (Accela PDA detector, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) and an MS TSQ Quantum Access Max triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, San Jose, U.S.A.). A volume of 5.0 μL was injected and separated using a150 x 2.1 mm, 3 μm Luna® Omega Polar column (Phenomenex, Torrance, California, USA) at 35 °C. Formic acid in water (0.1 % V/V) and formic acid in methanol (0.1 % V/V) were used as the mobile phase at a flow rate of 250 μL /min. Atmospheric Pressure Chemical Ionisation (APCI) was used with positive polarity as the ionisation source. A running time of 20 min and a retention time for AA of 3.10 min were chosen. The AA/ASN ratio was calculated considering the quantified AA content in the biscuits and the calculated ASN content of the corresponding flour mixture formulation.

Deoxynivalenol quantification

The DON content in the baked biscuits was analysed according to the procedure reported in Section 2.3.4, considering the three independent biscuit productions for each flour mixture.

Statistical analysis

One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to compare the ash, protein, fat, glucose, fructose, ASN and DON content (the latter only for the batch IT) in the milling fractions obtained through different milling processes, considering the IT and EU wheat batches separately. In order to highlight the contribution of environmental conditions on ASN and proximate composition, a two-way ANOVA was carried out, with milling fractions and batches as fixed factor. One-way ANOVA was applied to compare the proximal composition of the flour mixtures, the estimated ASN content and DON content in the flour mixtures and raw dough (albeit only for DON), the accumulation of AA and DON in the baked biscuits and the colour of the biscuits. The data were tested for normality and homoscedasticity, and means were identified as significantly different (p-value ˂ 0.05) using the Ryan-Einot-Gabriel-Welsch F (REGW-F) statistical test. A regression analysis applying a simple linear model was carried out to describe the formation of AA in the derived baked biscuits as a function of the ASN content in flour mixtures. Model assumptions were checked through residual diagnostics.

A principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to investigate the influence of ASN, DON, ash and the considered nutrients on the compared flour mixtures. A PCA was also elaborated to investigate the influence of AA, AA/ASN, colour parameters (L*, a*, b*, hue, BI) and DON on the baked biscuits. For both flour mixtures and baked biscuits, Pearson's correlation matrices were calculated to assess the linear associations among the variables included in each PCA, providing additional insight into their interrelationships. Moreover, variable loadings were extracted and plotted to visualize the contribution of individual parameters to the principal components for each PCA. The data were first standardized by subtracting the means and dividing the results by the standard deviation of each variable.

SPSS for Windows statistical package, Version 28.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), was used for the ANOVA and linear regression analyses. RStudio, version 2023.12.1 + 402 (Posit Software, PBC, Boston, MA, USA) was used for the elaboration of the PCA and the corresponding biplots, plot of the loadings and plot of the Pearson's correlation matrices.

Results

Milling fraction characterisation

Free asparagine distribution

The ASN resulted to be higher in the fractions derived from the outermost layers of the kernel for the three studied milling processes consistently for the two batches (Fig. 1). Refined flour had the lowest value among all the fractions, and it was 70.6 % lower than the whole kernel. The highest ASN concentration in both batches was detected in the germ which, on average, resulted to be 12 times higher than the content of the whole kernel. ASN in bran was on average 21 % lower than in the shorts and red dog, but not significantly different from DFs 0–5 to 15–20 of the batch IT; however, in batch EU, bran showed a significantly higher ASN content compared to all DFs. The shorts and red dog showed higher ASN levels than the SM and DFs in both the batches IT and EU. The wholemeal SM and the brown SM in the batch IT did not differ significantly from each other or from the whole kernel, while the brown SM of the batch EU had 45 % less ASN than the whole kernel. A decreasing trend was observed for the various DFs from the outermost layers to the starchy kernel, with the exception of DF 0–5, which showed lower values than DF 5–10 and DF 10–15, but with significant differences only in batch EU. These DFs had 2.3 times higher ASN content than the whole kernel, but it was still lower than the bran, shorts and red dog. Two-way ANOVA revealed highly significant effects of batch, milling fraction and their interaction on ASN levels (p-value <0.001) (Table S1). The distribution of ASN in the different milling fractions considering the two batch together confirmed the results previously observed, with the germ that accumulated the highest ASN content. Among the RM fractions, bran confirmed to accumulate on average − 21 % of ASN than shorts and red dog. In SM, the brown flour showed about two times less ASN than wholemeal and in D, the decreasing trend from outermost to innermost DFs was confirmed. The average content of ASN in batch IT resulted +15 % higher than batch EU.

Results are expressed in mg kg−1 on dry weight basis. Each bar represents the average value of three independent replicates. Within each wheat batch, bars values with different letters are significantly different (p-value <0.001), according to the REGW-F test.

DON contamination

As expected, regardless of which milling process was adopted, the DON content was higher in the outermost layer fractions than in the ones richer in starchy compounds (Fig. 2). Furthermore, the distribution rate within the milling fraction was different from our previous data on ASN. Indeed, D showed the highest capacity to concentrate DON in specific milling fractions: DF 0–5 and DF 5–10 showed the highest DON contaminations, which were 6.8 and four times higher than the whole kernel, respectively, and significantly higher than RM and SM. DF 10–15 was not significantly different from bran, while the innermost DFs showed a lower DON contamination than the shorts and red dog. The DF 30–35 and 35–40 were the least contaminated fractions, with the DON content being 10 times lower than that of the whole kernel. Bran had a 2.9 times higher DON content than the whole kernel in the RM-fractions, followed by shorts (2.3 times higher) and red dog (two times higher). DON contamination further decreased in the refined flour and germ, and on average resulted in 2.2 times less DON than in the whole kernel. Since the wholemeal SM did not break down any of the kernel components, the DON contamination was not significantly different from that of the whole kernel, although it resulted to be reduced 1.9-fold in the brown SM obtained after sieving.

Flour mixtures and biscuits

Proximal composition of the flour mixtures

Selected milling fractions from batch IT have been used for the production of flour mixtures and preparation of biscuits. In Table S3 the proximal composition of the flour mixtures is reported.

As expected, the protein, fat and fibre content was higher in the mixtures with the highest percentages of ash, and the distribution was the opposite of that of the starch content. The wholemeal SM was characterised by −9 % less protein than the average of the other wholemeal mixtures, but was similar for the other analysed components, while the highest amount of protein was found in wholemeal DF 10–15, which, on the other hand, showed the lowest amount of fibre.

The average amount of fat decreased by −19 % from the wholemeal to the brown, semi-white and refined flour, while the fibre decreased by −37 %, −54 % and − 77 %, respectively, in the brown, semi-white and refined flour. The brown RM had +3 % higher protein content than the brown SM, while the brown SM had more fat (+15 %) and glucose (+28 %).

Particle size of the milling fractions and flour mixtures

The particle size distribution of the milling fractions selected for biscuit preparation is presented in Fig. S1A. The fractions that were richer in starchy kernel compounds overall resulted to be finer. As expected, this trend was clear in the RM-fractions, where a progressive reduction in particle size was observed from bran (89 % of the particles >1000 μm) to red dog (50 % of the particles <250 μm). Micronisation reduced the particle size of the bran and made it similar to red dog and brown SM. The wholemeal SM had a similar particle size distribution to the wholemeal control, but showed a higher occurrence of the intermediate particle size class (205–500 μm and 500–710 μm), while the brown SM was finer and more homogeneous. More than 20 % of the particles in the DFs were higher than 1000 μm.

As far as the biscuit making mixtures are concerned (Fig. S1B), the wholemeal SM exhibited the coarsest particle size distribution, which, however, was similar to the wholemeal control, with over 50 % of its particles >500 μm, while the wholemeal RM had 70 % of its particles under 250 μm. Similarly, the brown SM had coarser particles than the brown RM and the wholemeal mixtures, including DF and semi-white, red dog. The inclusion of bran in the wholemeal and semi-white mixtures resulted in a similar particle size distribution to that of the refined flour.

Free asparagine content in the flour mixtures and acrylamide contamination in the baked biscuits

The wholemeal mixtures had significantly higher ASN than the brown (on average − 33 %), semi-white (−45 %) and refined flour (−68 %) (Table 3). The wholemeal shorts showed the most abundant ASN concentration, which was +10 % higher than the wholemeal control, although not significantly different from the wholemeal RM. The lowest ASN concentration was detected in wholemeal bran and in the wholemeal mixtures with DF 5–10 and DF 10–15; these resulted in −28 %, −21 % and − 22 % lower values than the control, respectively. The brown RM, brown SM and semi-white red dog, did not differ from each other, and on average showed +51 % higher values than the refined flour, while the inclusion of micronised bran in the semi-white flour increased the ASN content by +30 %, compared to the refined one.

The different mixtures were used for the biscuit making process, and the levels of AA found in the baked biscuits are reported in Table 3. When the entire set of flour mixtures was included in the regression analysis (Fig. S2A), the relation resulted weaker, but still able to explain the 46 % of the observed variability.

Both the wholemeal SM and the control showed the highest AA contamination, with five times the content detected in the refined flour. The wholemeal RM and the wholemeal shorts showed a significantly increased content of AA, which was two times higher than the refined flour, while no other significant differences were detected in the refined flour or wholemeal bran, wholemeal DF 5–10, wholemeal DF 10–15, the brown and semi-white mixtures. Among the compared formulation, the AA content in wholemeal SM resulted close to exceed the EU benchmark of 350 μg kg−1 for biscuits (Commission Regulation (EU) 2017/2158), while all others remained below this limit for adult consumption. However, the wholemeal control, wholemeal SM, and wholemeal RM exceeded the benchmark for biscuits intended for infant (150 μg kg−1), with wholemeal RM exceeding it by 2.6 %, while the wholemeal control and wholemeal SM by an average of 2.4-times. The AA/ASN ratio was calculated to understand the differences in the reacting behaviour of ASN during the formation of AA, and it is reported in Table 3. The highest values were detected for the wholemeal SM, followed by the wholemeal control, while the lowest AA/ASN ratio was reported for wholemeal DF 5–10, which was significantly lower than the refined flour, but no different from the other wholemeal, brown and semi-white biscuits.

The colour parameters of the baked biscuits were also analysed, and they clearly resulted to be influenced by the quantity of the different milling fractions that had been included according to the ash content. The wholemeal control had the lowest L* and the highest a*, hue and BI. The inclusion of bran or shorts in the wholemeal mixtures resulted in higher L* and BI and lower a* values than SM or than the recombination with the DFs.

Apart from the refined flour, the lowest BI was recorded for the brown RM and the semi-white bran, the latter of which was 63 % lower than the wholemeal control, that also showed low values of a*.

Deoxynivalenol content in the flour mixtures

DON contamination was significantly higher in the wholemeal mixtures than in the brown, semi-white and refined ones (Table 4). On average, and compared to the refined flour, the DON content was +42 %, +5 % and + 15 % higher in the wholemeal, brown and semi-white flour mixtures, respectively. Notably, the addition of DF 5–10 and DF 10–15 resulted in the highest contamination, with the DON concentration, on average, being +25 % higher than in the wholemeal control. The DON levels in the wholemeal mixtures obtained from SM or from a recombination of the RM-fractions did not differ from each other or from the wholemeal control. In contrast, the brown SM, which recorded the lowest DON content, showed a reduction by −37 %, compared to the brown RM.

Deoxynivalenol content in baked biscuits

When moving from flour mixtures to the corresponding biscuits, the DON content of the mixtures was maintained in the raw dough and in the baked biscuits (Table 4). The highest contamination in the baked biscuits was reported for wholemeal DF 5–10 and 10–15, followed by the other wholemeal biscuits. The brown SM had the lowest content of mycotoxin in the baked biscuits, which also resulted to be −32 % lower than in the semi-white biscuits and − 21 % lower than in the refined biscuits. However, the DON content resulted under the limit established by Regulation (EU) 2023/915 set at 500 μg kg−1 in all the baked biscuits.

On average, a reduction by −9 % was observed in the DON content from the raw dough to the baked biscuits. The percentage of reduction after baking was greater in the wholemeal obtained by adding DFs and in all the mixtures that included RM-fractions, which did not differ from the wholemeal control, while a lower detoxification was observed in the wholemeal bran and wholemeal shorts (on average 7.7 %). The lowest reduction percentage was reported for the brown, semi-white and refined flours (6.5 %).

The PC2 resulted positively associated to glucose and negatively to DON and protein content. Notably, a strong positive correlation was observed between ASN and ash, as well as between DON and ash content, highlighting the importance of ash as a risk indicator in wholemeal flours. As expected, starch content was negatively correlated with ash, ASN, DON, and other nutrients, indicating that a higher proportion of starchy endosperm components is associated with lower levels of ASN and DON, due to their distinct distribution within the wheat kernel.

The weak correlation between ASN and DON, visible in Fig. S4, and suggested also by the angle of approximately 90° between the two vectors in Fig. 4, indicates that the mitigation of these two compounds requires specific and distinct management strategies. Similarly, a light association was present between ASN and the protein content of the milling fractions. The PCA biplot of flour mixtures (Fig. 4) showed that wholemeal bran, wholemeal DF 5–10, and wholemeal DF 10–15 clustered in the second quadrant, indicating similarities in their compositional profiles. These mixtures were clearly separated from the other RM wholemeal mixtures, wholemeal SM and wholemeal control, which grouped in the first quadrant and were more closely associated with ASN and reducing sugars. In contrast, the three mixtures located in the second quadrant were more strongly associated with DON and protein content, with wholemeal bran showing a comparatively lower level of DON. As visible in Fig. 4, the brown, semi-white and refined mixtures, being characterised by a lower ash content, are not related to the typical compounds of the outer kernel layers and are instead related more to the starch content, with an opposite trend to the DON and protein content. For the baked biscuits, PC1 accounted for 59.5 % of the total variability and PC2 for 19.9 %, explaining together 78.4 % of the variance (Fig. 4). The PCA results confirmed the separation between wholemeal biscuits, clustered on the right side of the plot, and brown, semi-white and refined biscuits, grouped on the left and associated to peculiarities in terms of L* and b* values. The analysis of the loadings (Fig. S3) highlighted that PC1 is mainly related to a*, BI and DON with a positive association, but also L* and b* with a negative trend, while PC2 is mainly positively associated to AA and AA/ASN and negatively to DON. The Pearson's correlation matrix (Fig. S4) confirmed that when low values of L* are observed, high values of a* and BI are expected, while the value of b* followed the same variations of L* as they resulted positive correlated. The colour parameters of the baked biscuits did not show a strong correlation with the AA/ASN ratio, however L* was negatively correlated with AA (r = −0.68), while BI resulted positively correlated (r = 0.68) with AA content. Values of L*, a*, b* and BI showed a strong correlation with the ash content (positive for a* and BI and negative for L* and b*). Within the wholemeal group, the SM and the wholemeal control remained positioned in the first quarter, driven by their particularly high AA contents and AA/ASN values. Conversely, the formulations including RM and D fractions were located in the second quarter. Among them, the wholemeal RM, wholemeal bran and shorts were further distinguished from DF 5–10 and DF 10–15 due to their lower DON content.

Discussion

Data collected from a comparison of a large set of mixtures, regarding AA and DON contamination in final baked biscuits obtained through the application of different milling processes to the same two wheat batches, have confirmed the higher sanitary risk associated with the use of wholemeal flours in baked products than in more refined ones. Furthermore, by quantifying the uneven distribution of these toxic compounds in the milling fractions and designing formulations that comply with the Italian legislation on the classification of wholemeal products, which is based on the ash content, this study has highlighted the importance of a correct selection and recombination of specific wheat milling fractions to mitigate the overall contamination of wholemeal bakery products.

Free asparagine and deoxynivalenol distribution in the milling fractions

The present study confirmed the higher potential for the formation of AA and contamination by DON in wholemeal baked biscuits, indeed the wholemeal control showed four times more AA and a 1.5-times more DON than refined flour. These results are related to the uneven ASN and DON accumulation within the wheat kernel: high ASN concentrations have been reported in wheat germ (Fredriksson et al., 2004) and in the aleurone layer (Shewry et al., 2009), while DON primarily accumulates in the outermost pericarp layers (Cheli et al., 2013; Sovrani et al., 2012; Tibola et al., 2020). Although environmental and agronomic factors could influence the overall ASN content in the whole kernel (Curtis et al., 2009; Curtis et al., 2016), our results showed that the distribution pattern of ASN in the different milling fractions presented few differences between different batches. Among the analysed techniques, RM was the only one capable of effectively isolating the ASN-rich germ, minimizing its spread across fractions. Only small residues of germ and aleurone were probably retained in the shorts and red dog, as indicated by their higher fat and ASN concentrations, while bran showed lower levels of both consistently for the two batches, as also reported by Fredriksson et al. (2004), who observed that shorts and red dog contained +6.9 and + 23.3 % more ASN than bran, respectively. In contrast, SM crushes all the anatomical regions of grain together, as the laboratory centrifugal mill used in this study to obtain the wholemeal control, thus limiting its potential to efficiently separate bran and germ from other grain constituents (Cappelli et al., 2020). This was reflected in the low milling yield of the brown SM and the compositional similarities between the wholemeal SM and brown SM due to their abrasive actions, which caused the mixture or dispersion of ASN-rich tissues in other kernel fractions, thus making the separation harder. Indeed, in our experiment, the DFs exhibited a clear but slightly decreasing trend in ASN from the outermost to the innermost fractions.

Regarding DON levels, our data confirm results reported by Zheng et al. (2014) with bran accumulated +80.5 % more DON than shorts, due to its higher proportion of outer layers, consistent with its greater ash content. The lower DON content observed in the brown SM rather than in the wholemeal SM suggests that sieving removed some of the most contaminated pericarp layers. D effectively concentrated DON in the outermost DF, preventing its spread into inner fractions, with DON decreasing by −41 % between DF 0–5 and DF 5–10 (Sovrani et al., 2012; Tibola et al., 2020). However, both DF 0–5 and 5–10 resulted in a higher DON content than the RM fractions (e.g. bran and shorts), because they mainly consisted of outer layers removed in sequence (Dexter & Sarkar, 2003). These findings support the use of D as a suitable strategy to remove pericarp fractions which are highly contaminated in common wheat, a pre-processing step before conventional RM (Tibola et al., 2020), or to carry out further D steps and select intermediate kernel layers, which are richer in bioactive compounds and micronutrients (Ciccoritti et al., 2017; Giordano et al., 2019), but have a breakdown in the content of DON and other toxic trace elements, such as heavy metals (Giordano & Blandino, 2018).

Wholemeal mixtures design and biscuits characteristics

Levels of ash, free asparagine and deoxynivalenol in flour mixtures

The possibility of defining a wholemeal flour on the basis of its ash content allows the different milling fractions to be recombined in mixtures that could be classified as “wholemeal” according to the current legislation, but also leads to differences related to their safety, and to their nutritional and technological characteristics. Thus, the smart mixing of the milling fractions could be a relevant practice to ensure the safety of final food products.

The highest concentration of minerals in the wheat kernel is located in the outermost layers of the grains, pericarp and aleurone (Žilić et al., 2012), thus the ash content decreases with the progressive removal of external layers through D (Sovrani et al., 2012). Our results, which were obtained by considering different milling strategies, confirmed a close correlation between the ash content and the ASN and DON in the milling fractions of both batches and in the flour mixtures obtained by batch IT, with a decreasing trend from wholemeal to semi-white and refined mixtures, as expected.

The highest ash content was found in RM fractions, particularly in bran, as also reported by Sarfaraz et al. (2017), whereas DF 0–5 and DF 10–15 presented lower values. Considering the ash content of the different fractions, for the formulation of wholemeal flour mixtures (1.6 % ash), the lowest percentage of inclusion was needed for the micronised bran (15.9 %), then for the shorts (21.4 %), DF 5–10 (24.6 %) and DF 10–15 (26.8 %). Consequently, limiting bran inclusion reduced the final ASN and DON concentrations, despite starting from a raw material with high contamination level. Indeed, the wholemeal bran presented similar ASN levels than brown RM but, despite the high DON contamination levels in the initial bran, the DON concentration in the mixture did not differ from other wholemeal mixtures that included less contaminated RM fractions, such as shorts or from wholemeal SM.

No significant differences in the ASN and DON content were observed between the wholemeal RM, wholemeal SM and wholemeal control; this suggested that the milling technique itself does not affect the ASN and DON accumulation when all the fractions of the kernel were maintained in the same proportions, as previously suggested by Albergamo et al. (2018).

Acrylamide formation and deoxynivalenol content in biscuits

In accordance with the ASN levels, the decreasing AA content from biscuits with 1.6 % ash to those with the refined flour confirmed that ASN is the main limiting factor for AA formation (Žilić et al., 2020) and the major risk for wholemeal formulation. Wholemeal SM biscuits showed the highest level of AA accumulation, that was close to the benchmark value set by Commission Regulation (EU) 2017/2158 for adult biscuits and nearly two-fold higher than the stricter limit established for baby food. In contrast, biscuits produced with wholemeal bran, shorts, and DFs (5–10 and 10–15) remained below both thresholds. Surprisingly, the wholemeal RM and the wholemeal shorts, despite having the highest ASN levels in the mixtures, formed two times less AA than the baked biscuits with wholemeal SM; moreover, despite a similar initial ASN content in the wholemeal SM and wholemeal RM, the biscuits made with the latter accumulated two times less AA than the former.

In our results, it was evident that the 1.6 % ash mixtures that included RM-fractions were better at mitigate the AA accumulation than the wholemeal control (reduction of 50 %). Although Schouten et al. (2023) reported the correlation between ASN and AA, our results suggest that additional compositional and technological factors can modulate the AA formation and may alter the AA/ASN ratio, as also observed by Capuano et al. (2009) in bread crisp model, Žilić et al. (2020) in biscuits and Covino et al. (2024) in pizza base. In particular, this last reported that factors such as fibre content, reducing sugars and damaged starch could impair the correlation between ASN and final AA levels. Moreover, in some cases, ASN can react without leading to a significant increase in the AA levels, since such intermediates as 3-aminopropionamide (3-APA) may accumulate instead of fully converting into AA (Žilić et al., 2020). Regarding the DON levels in baked biscuits, no significant differences were observed respect the content determined in the flour mixtures and in the raw dough, with major risk associated with wholemeal biscuits. Among them, the wholemeal DF 5–10 and wholemeal DF 10–15 accumulated the highest DON content, as a high percentage of inclusion of the DFs was required to reach the target ash content in the flour mixture.

Differences in particle size and nutritional composition

In order to interpretate some of the previous reported differences between the flour mixtures, it is possible to hypothesise that also the particle size, which is a consequence of the different mechanical forces applied during the milling, could influence the formation of AA and alter the AA/ASN ratio (Zhang et al., 2023).

Unlike SM and D, which do not efficiently separate fractions according to their particle size, RM can be used to obtain coarser fractions (bran and germ) and progressively finer ones (shorts, red dog and refined flour). In this study, micronised bran was used in the mixture design process.

In our study, the lack of a mixture that included unaltered bran (100 % of particles >500 μm) limited our conclusions on the effects of the particle size. Schouten et al. (2022) demonstrated that biscuits made with broken cotyledon bean flour accumulated more AA than those with unbroken cotyledon flour, probably because the ASN in the broken cotyledon flour was more available, and the finer cotyledon promoted faster water evaporation during baking, thereby promoting the Maillard reaction. By analogy, finer bran might present a larger available area and a more reactive ASN, and it might modify dough properties in ways that enhance AA formation. In our study, high AA/ASN ratios were observed in the wholemeal SM and wholemeal control: these mixtures contained crushed germ-derived tissues, which could increase their surface area and probably also ASN reactivity.

Moreover, the higher crude fibre content of these wholemeal formulations could contribute to a higher water binding and retention capacity (Hemdane et al., 2016; Jacobs et al., 2015), thus favouring the Maillard reaction and AA formation (Schouten et al., 2023). Another hypothesis that supports the differentiated content in AA in biscuits could be related to the specific content of pectic material in the layers of the caryopsis (endosperm and external layers), as first demonstrated by Chateigner-Boutin et al. (2014). Pectin could favour changes in the pH, and the selection of specific milling products, characterised by different amounts of pectin, could lead to a differentiated reduction of the pH in biscuit dough, thereby impacting the Maillard reaction (and AA) differently. In our study, the wholemeal control and the wholemeal SM showed significantly more browning, as indicated by the BI, while the biscuits made with RM − fractions were lighter, less red, yellow and brown than the other wholemeal baked biscuits. The PCA (Fig. 4) and Pearson's correlation matrix (Fig. S4) for baked biscuits highlighted a correlation between colour parameters and ash content. However, among biscuits with comparable ash content, differences in colour may be associated with variations in other flour components, which in turn could further account for the differences in AA levels, in addition to the factors previously discussed. Indeed, in the study of Prabhasankar and Haridas Rao (2001), SM had been associated with the generation of friction and physical forces that could result in reduction of protein content and increase in damaged starch. Indeed, in our study the wholemeal control and wholemeal SM were characterised by the lowest protein content among the compared wholemeal mixtures. Also, brown SM presented a lower protein amount than brown RM, with an increase in the amount of fat and glucose. So, the possible higher content of damaged starch could have promote the browning and the Maillard reactions (Li et al., 2023), with consequences on colour and AA levels.

The DON distribution did not appear to be correlated with the particle size in our study. However, Ríos et al. (2009) found that when DON contamination levels were high, this mycotoxin tended to accumulate more in the finest RM fractions, regardless of the inclusion of contaminated outer layers, thereby representing another factor that needs to be considered in the holistic management of the sanitary risk of wholegrain mixtures.

Comparative assessment of wholemeal mixtures

Considering the sanitary risks and nutrient composition together, the wholemeal bran, despite its lower protein content, showed several advantages. Since bran was rich in ash it could be included in lower percentages in the mixtures, contributing to limiting both the contamination by AA and DON in the baked biscuits. Moreover, this mixture also showed a reduction in the natural occurrence of glucose, which plays a key role in AA formation, especially in savoury snacks, where no other sugars are added to the recipe (Ferreira et al., 2024). The wholemeal bran also had a lower amount of fat, which is a favourable trait to enhance the shelf-life of flour (Lancelot et al., 2021). In our experiment, the inclusion of external DFs resulted in a high content of protein, without significantly increase in the risk of ASN accumulation and AA development. Indeed, DFS accumulated on average, three times less ASN than the bran, shorts, red dog and germ. However, since DON was highly concentrated in the pericarp, DF 0–5 and DF 5–10 showed mycotoxin levels that were twice those of the bran, shorts and red dog. This effect was further amplified by the lower ash content of the DFs, which required a higher inclusion rate during recombination to obtain wholemeal mixtures. Consequently, DFs could be used for wholemeal mixtures only when wheat batches have a low mycotoxin contamination level. On the other hand, D is of interest as pre-processing to remove the outer and most contaminated kernel layers before milling.

Despite being perceived as a traditional and high-quality technique, wholemeal SM showed a higher sanitary risk than the RM recombination, due to the higher AA formation risk. SM provided advantages for the reduction of DON contamination in brown flour. However, its limited milling yield, the lower protein content and the higher fat and glucose concentrations ruled out a net advantage of this flour over the other brown and semi-white mixtures.

Conclusions

Despite the favourable nutritional profile of wholemeal bakery products, the higher occurrence of natural and process contaminants concentrated in the outer kernel layers could limited their health benefits. This study demonstrated that the careful selection and recombination of milling fractions obtained by RM, SM and D is crucial to limit the ASN level, AA formation and DON accumulation. Overall, by adopting a holistic approach that considered both the milling process and the formulation of wholemeal flour, in relation to the main wheat contaminants, RM emerged as the most versatile technique for the separation of kernel components. This process produced well-distinguished fractions, as far as the ash content, ASN concentration, and particle size are concerned. Among the RM-fractions, bran resulted to be of particular interest because of its ash content, which allowed lower inclusion rates, thereby limiting the ASN and DON levels in the final mixture, but ensuring adequate fibre content, and limited quantity of fat. Future studies are required to verify the influence of the bran particle size on AA formation.

D proved to be an effective technique for isolating specific fractions which, on average, accumulated three times less ASN than the bran, shorts, red dog and germ. However, since DON was highly concentrated in the pericarp, DF 0–5 and DF 5–10 showed mycotoxin levels that were twice those of the bran, shorts and red dog. Consequently, D could be used for wholemeal formulations, provided that wheat batches exhibit low mycotoxin contamination levels. Alternatively, it may serve as a dry-fractionation technique to remove the outer and most contaminated kernel layers and subsequently discard them.

In conclusion, by adopting a holistic approach for the targeted recombination of specific milling fractions can represent an effective strategy to balance the nutritional quality and the mitigation of AA in wholemeal formulations.
