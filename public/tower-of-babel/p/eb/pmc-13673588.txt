Stakeholder experiences and expectations of transition of care services to reduce medication-related hospital readmission for cardiology patients
Authors: Shelley A Wilkinson, Centaine Snoswell, Liza Raggatt, Ian Coombes, Kelvin Robertson, Jared Miles, William Y S Wang, John J Atherton, Chariclia Paradissis, Sue Carson, Andrew Jones, Estelle Jensen, Michael Barras, Nazanin Falconer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTransition of care (ToC) for patients with cardiovascular diseases is a complex, high-risk period often leading to medication-related harm (MRH) and hospital readmissions. While specialised programs exist, a gap in individualised medication management services exist for specific cardiology patient populations. Pharmacist-led, interdisciplinary ToC services have demonstrated a positive impact on patient outcomes. The REducing hospital re-admission for high-risk CARDiology patients (RECARD) program aims to address this by co-designing, implementing, and evaluating a new pharmacist-led ToC service in post-acute myocardial infarction (AMI) or cardiac surgery patients. The current study focuses on understanding the experiences and expectations of patients and clinicians to inform the development of this service.MethodsThis qualitative study utilised semi-structured interviews and focus groups to gather data from patients and hospital and community clinicians aligned with three Queensland hospitals and surrounding primary and community care settings. Patients who had experienced an AMI or cardiac surgery in the past three months were interviewed via telephone or Microsoft TEAMS. Clinicians participated in focus groups or individual interviews. Data were audio-recorded, transcribed, and analysed using the six-phase thematic analysis method by Braun and Clarke, guided by Bradshaw's model of need to understand stakeholders’ expressed and comparative needs.ResultsData were obtained from 13 patient interviews and 40 clinicians, through seven focus groups and one interview. Three main themes, with associated subthemes, emerged from the data; Patient-level issues, System and process issues and Interprofessional collaboration. Patients described information overload, medication uncertainty, and anxiety at discharge, while clinicians identified delayed discharge summaries, fragmented communication with primary care, and service gaps - particularly impacting rural patients- as key risks for medication‑related harm.ConclusionsThe study's findings highlight the critical need for a patient-centred, pharmacist-led interdisciplinary ToC service that addresses patient knowledge deficits, psychosocial barriers, and the communication gaps between hospital and community care settings. The results from this study will inform the development of the RECARD program's "Adaptive ToC Pathway," ensuring it is tailored to meet the specific needs and expectations of both patients and clinicians, ultimately aiming to reduce MRH and hospital readmissions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06294-7.Keywords: Acute myocardial infarction, Cardiac surgery, Medication related harm, Transition of care

Background

Transition of care (ToC) for patients following acute type 1 myocardial infarction (AMI) or cardiac surgery is complex, often resulting in medication-related harm (MRH) and hospital readmissions [1, 2]. A 2021 scoping review of 37 studies reported that 16% of all 30-day hospital readmissions were medication related, of which, 40% were preventable [3]. A second scoping review found that polypharmacy, sub-optimal medication prescribing, and changes to medications were risk factors for hospital readmissions [4]. Cardiovascular post-intervention therapy is associated with the addition of multiple medications.

Whilst health programs exist for patients with heart failure and cardiac rehabilitation [5–7] and some evidence-based approaches exist to reducing risk of medication related harm at ToC for patients post AMI/Percutaneous Coronary Intervention (PCI/Coronary Artery Bypass Graft (CABG) [8–10], many of models of care are in a US context and gaps still exist in service delivery [1]. Patient movement from hospital to community care involves multiple health professionals, complex healthcare plans, and numerous medication changes, all of which can be challenging for them to navigate on their own. Furthermore, delays and gaps in communication have been noted [3, 11]. The Australian government and international health services have identified that ToC from hospital to primary care is a critical time that requires more effective management, including a tailored program [12, 13].

Ensuring a timely, effective and safe transition necessitates an interdisciplinary team and patient centred approach [1, 14–16]. Within this team, pharmacists play an integral role in ensuring medication safety and optimisation of care [17–20]. Pharmacists implementing and leading a ToC service have been shown to reduce readmissions, MRH, and improve quality of life in this cohort of patients [1, 9, 21–24]. REducing hospital re-admission for high-risk CARDiology patients (RECARD) is an Australian Government funded program of research that aims to co-design, implement and evaluate an individualised ToC service to reduce 30-day MRH readmission for patients who have recently had a type 1 MI and/or cardiac surgery [25]. The RECARD ToC model will be delivered as an “Adaptive ToC Pathway,” involving a collaborative team of pharmacists, nurse practitioners, and Indigenous health workers at three Queensland quaternary hospitals [26]. In the development of this model, we are considering and integrating the needs of patients and clinicians, system dynamics, and external influences with extant literature [1, 27–30].

This novel approach has combined needs assessment, behaviour change theories, implementation frameworks, and evaluation tools, which are aligned with the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [26]. The CFIR was chosen as a meta-framework for our theoretical approach to map the determinants influencing change within the complex health system [26]. Within this, and specifically relating to this study, we applied Bradshaw’s model of need to achieve a multidimensional understanding of stakeholders needs [31]. Expressed (listening to people) and Comparative (comparing populations) Needs were investigated in this study [31]. This approach is required as, despite this growing body of evidence, there remains a lack of stakeholder‑informed, context‑specific qualitative research exploring patient and clinician experiences and expectations to inform the co‑design of scalable transition of care models, particularly for post‑AMI and cardiac surgery patients who fall outside established heart failure or cardiac rehabilitation programs. This study aimed to investigate the experiences and expectations of patients post- type 1 MI and/or cardiac surgery discharged from one of the study sites and of clinicians currently providing care to this patient cohort. These findings will inform the development of a person-centered and locally appropriate pharmacist-led interdisciplinary TOC service.

Methods

Design and setting

This study used a qualitative approach to collect data from patients and clinicians involved in their care after a cardiac event (AMI / cardiac surgery). Semi-structured interviews were used for recently discharged hospital patients, and a combination of semi-structured interviews and focus groups with hospital and community clinicians.

Participant recruitment occurred across three health districts in Queensland. These included Townsville University Hospital (TUH) which is a regional site, and two metropolitan sites, Princess Alexandra Hospital (PAH) and Royal Brisbane & Women’s Hospital (RBWH). Primary health care providers and community pharmacists (CPs) from within these health districts were recruited. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Participants

Patients were eligible if they were aged ≥ 18, had an AMI or had undergone cardiac surgery such as CABG surgery, in the previous three months at one of the three hospital sites.

Clinicians were recruited across the spectrum of patient care after a cardiac event, from hospital admission through to primary care follow-up. Hospital-based clinicians included cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, residents, registrars, nurses, pharmacists, and Indigenous healthcare workers, while primary care clinicians included CPs and general practitioners (GPs). Clinicians were eligible to participate if they had been involved in the care of the above patient cohort in the last six months to ensure contemporary knowledge.

Consent and interview procedure

Patients were recruited to participate in one of two ways. Patients scanned a QR code from information flyers that linked to a Microsoft Forms page that collected their name, phone number, hospital at which they were admitted, and cardiac event. A RECARD study investigator then contacted them to discuss the project and answer any questions, provide the patient information and consent form and organised the return of the form. Alternatively, patients attending a follow-up cardiology outpatient clinic were approached by a study team member while in the waiting room. The team member explained the study and patients were invited to participate. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardian. Those patients who agreed to participate signed consent forms at that time and completed the QR code process. Consenting patients were contacted one to two weeks later to arrange and undertake the semi-structured interview. All participants were provided with a $AUD40 gift voucher for compensation of their time and sharing of their experiences, which was emailed to them at the completion of the interview.

Semi-structured interviews with patients were conducted via telephone or Microsoft Teams by two experienced qualitative researchers (SW (dietitian/implementation scientist) and CS (pharmacist/ researcher)) using an interview guide (Supplementary Table 1a). The main topics covered included patients’ experiences of their hospital stay and the weeks following discharge, including communication with clinicians, patients’ perceptions about changes to their medications before and after hospitalisation, any challenges they faced with their medications and patients’ views on follow up care at and after discharge. Demographic details such as age, sex, type of cardiac event, and date of cardiac event were collected from the patients prior to them receiving their gift card.

Hospital staff were recruited through advertisement within hospital departments and via snowballing. CPs and GPs were recruited through social media and email advertisement via professional organisations (e.g. Pharmaceutical Society of Australia) and snowballing. Clinicians were invited to participate in a semi-structured interview or a focus group, depending on their preference, availability, and the operation within their role (only focus groups were run). All clinicians went into the draw to win one of two $AUD100 gift vouchers. Informed consent was obtained from all participants who agreed to take part in either a semi structured interview (patients or clinicians) or a focus group (clinicians).

Focus groups were conducted by SW (dietitian/implementation scientist). Three focus groups were conducted in person at the metropolitan hospital sites, while the remaining four were conducted online for the regional site (two), the GPs (one), and CPs (one). Questions explored clinician experiences and expectations of ToC services, including medication management, and their views of proposed interventions to improve the transition of care of patients following AMI/cardiac surgery (see Supplementary Table 1b). The cardiac surgeons who were unable to attend the focus groups but wanted to participate were offered an individual semi-structured interview.

All interviews and focus groups were audio-recorded, and recordings were transcribed verbatim for analysis using automated transcription services through Microsoft Teams and/or a commercial transcription service. Transcripts were compared with audio recordings prior to analysis. Transcripts were not returned to patients or clinicians.

Data analysis

The overarching framework guiding the analysis was Bradshaw’s taxonomy, specifically, informing the ‘felt needs’ of the stakeholder [31]. This was applied as it enabled a structured, stakeholder‑centred assessment that distinguished between expert‑defined, perceived, expressed and comparative needs, making it particularly suited to a complex health care system involving multiple professional groups, service interfaces, and patient populations.

All transcripts were analysed separately using the six-phase thematic analysis method proposed by Braun and Clarke by two investigators (SW, CS). Briefly, Phase 1 involved becoming familiar with the data through listening to the audio and reading the transcripts, while taking notes. Phase 2 involved coding the data, and Phase 3 followed with theming the codes. Phase 4 involved a reviewing stage, whereby the themes generated were cross-checked against the codes and entire dataset. Phase 5 generated and defined names for themes and included illustrative text segments and Phase 6 was the production of this manuscript.

The final stage of this analysis was to generate evidence-based /evidence-informed interventions to enhance medication safety through mapping themes and subthemes to an implementation science framework [32]. Using the CFIR structure as outlined in Wilkinson et al. [26]. We classified themes and subthemes as barriers and/or enablers that existed in one of the six CFIR domains. These were then mapped and classified with the CFIR-ERIC matching tool and subsequently operationalised to actionable service changes.

All methods were carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethics approval was obtained from Metro North Health Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) B (Patients: HREC/2024/MNHB/103581; Clinicians: HREC/2023/MNHB/104151), both were ratified by the University of Queensland HREC (Patients: 2024/HE000970; Clinicians: 2024/HE000962). Site specific approval was granted by each site (Metro North, Metro South, Townsville University Hospital Research Governance Offices).

Reflexivity / positionality

The focus groups, interviews and thematic analysis was undertaken by two members of the team, SW and CS. Both were involved in their capacity as experienced researchers but did not have direct cardiac care experience at any of the study sites. As hospital trained allied health staff, both SW (dietitian) and CS (pharmacist) were able to understand the participants experiences, without the bias of having direct responsibility for patients receiving care after a cardiac event. However, both acknowledge that they have predetermined ideas about the appropriate way to communicate with and provide care to patients to achieve optimal outcomes within the hospital system, which may have influenced their data collection or analysis. They were also careful to let participants know that neither of them was employed at the study sites, and that their answers would not be relayed back to their hospital or clinical team.

Results

Participants

A total of 13 patients and 40 clinicians participated in the interviews and focus groups. The patient interviews were conducted March to May 2025; of the 13 patients, three were from the RBWH, seven from the PAH and three from TUH. The average interview length was 29 min (range: 14 to 90 min). The patient’s average age was 58.2 (range: 40 to 74 years) and 38.4% were female. The average length from heart event to interview was 197 days/6.5 month (Min = 24, Max = 1172 days/3.2 years).

Seven clinician focus groups were held (Oct 2024 to May 2025). Cardiologists were included from all sites, with Nurses and Pharmacists from two of the three sites (one metropolitan, one regional). Indigenous Health Workers attended at one site (Table 1). CPs and GPs were represented in one focus group each.

Theme 1: patient level issues

This theme explored the perceptions of factors and processes of patients from this cohort who ‘did well’ or not, and barriers and enablers to patients managing their transition from the hospital to community.

The first sub-theme, patient knowledge and beliefs encapsulated patient’s literacy, including health literacy, worry about their condition and/or the medication required, resistance and denial, as well as fluctuating motivation and long-term adherence to therapy.

“patient motivation, literacy and the desire to change outcomes, that’s the biggest problem we have” (Clinician Group 3)

“…fixed beliefs, and also taking a long time to convince someone in a busy clinical scenario is often a challenge” (Clinician Group5)

Patients described concern about the volume of new medications, especially for those who didn’t take medication prior to their events.

“It was me being overwhelmed with getting so much information” (Patient 2 )

“Scary actually, ‘cause you go from like taking 3 pills a day to up to 14 pills a day” (Patient 2)

It was noted by clinicians that patients display a broad understanding of medications and their cardiovascular conditions. For many, especially older adults and those in regional areas, limited health literacy was identified by their clinicians as a significant barrier to comprehending their treatment requirements and may also present as ‘understanding’ to ‘save face’ when being educated.

Clinician’s felt that patient ‘worry’ about their condition was thought to impair information intake during hospital admission, which exacerbated confusion on discharge.

“It’s the first conversation and they say, ‘yeah, but it’s the first time and it’s a lot of medication’ so they haven’t had any time to think about it” (Clinician Group 5)

It was thought by clinicians that this worry reduced adherence when patients were afraid of medications and prevented questions or information sharing by patients if they were nervous about the power differential with clinicians, especially specialists. While patients did describe feelings of fear and being overwhelmed in relation to information overload, as well as gratitude for clear empathetic communication, they also described positive interactions with health professionals, often nurses or pharmacists, for the time and input invested to make them comfortable with the new regimes.

“They [nurses] have more empathy than the doctors to be absolutely honest and they are - the patients often prefer them because there’s not this vertical separation.” (Clinician Group 3)

“… to have someone like her that was able to explain it in basic ‘bogan’ terms was awesome. Like, but having an, you know, like you’ve got all of these smart people coming in. All these doctors, nurses and everyone’s using these big scary words and then yeah, she (pharmacist) came in and just kind of made it all better…” (Patient 12)

Specific entrenched negative perspectives were thought to be held by patients about medications related to statins (cholesterol lowering drugs). These stemmed from misinformation from media or even community sources which were thought to impact adherence to this group of medications.

“it’s quite rare to have those side effects, but often you’re competing with one of their friends who had a complication” (Clinician Group 5)

It was also observed by some clinicians that some patients, especially when feeling well, denied the necessity of medication, reflecting a fatalistic attitude that can undermine long-term health and effective medication management.

“…then just stopping things - that they’ll start on the statin, they’ll do it for two months and then they’ll stop. They don’t quite get the point” (Clinician Group 1)

“I feel good doc, so I don’t need the drug.“(Clinician Group 1)

There were also sentiments about anxiety at the thought of transitioning out of hospital care, as patients said it felt like losing safety and support. Many expressed personal agency, with responding individuals recognising their role in recovery, while still acknowledging some experiences of communication breakdown. Family involvement and the use of accessible language emerged as crucial for understanding and coping with health challenges, especially for those with cognitive or emotional vulnerabilities.

“I was very nervous to leave hospital because the hospital was like my support blanket, and I felt safe there. If you know what I mean, like you just when you’re very sick, and you and they just charge you and go from home. I sort of had all these unknowns that I just had no idea about.” (Patient 3)

The second sub-theme was mediators and moderators of patient “success”. This encapsulated psychosocial issues (i.e. problems that arise from the interplay between their psychological state and their social environment, impacting their mental, emotional, and social well-being) impacting their ability to process information provided, and the impact of prescription complexity.

It was noted that the ‘discharge process’ often bombarded patients with information in a short period, leading to poor retention of important facts and subsequent confusion about how to manage their medication, especially when unwell. Frequent medication changes, whether due to hospital formularies, medication splitting/recombining, polypharmacy or switching between generic and brand medications, also resulted in confusion that was noted to potentially lead to non-adherence. This was compounded by (lack of) clinician time, with education sessions sometimes feeling rushed.

“Oh, the doctor, why do they talk so quick? I couldn’t understand them.” (Patient 2)

However, the way information was delivered, both format and frequency, as well as reinforcement from various members of the health care team enabled greater chance of patient “success”.

“every day [in hospital] there’s chances for education, and the less time you’re here, the less the education” (Clinician Group 5)

“it [written information] was good for my knowledge, but also I was able to like give it to my wife and that say she was like, knew what had happened” (Patient 2)

Further, psychosocial factors relating to their living situation (social/alone) or location (regional/remote) as well as financial constraints impacting the cost of medications were noted to force patients to ration or stop essential treatments and reducing the overall success of their transition from hospital to community. Patients appreciated having access to hospital pharmacists and written documentation to take to their CP (or GP), which enhanced their sense of control and understanding. However, some experienced a lack of follow-up after discharge, which left them feeling isolated and unsupported.

“My ‘list’ told me that I could ring [the pharmacist] with questions… I had their number” (Patient 10)

“The documentation they now give you compared to what is was 25 years ago is actually brilliant” (Patient 10)

“Having that information [provided was good] because when I left, I ultimately didn’t hear from the hospital and was very much on my own” (Patient 12)

Theme 2. System and process issues

This theme arose as clinicians and patients described the processes for navigating the health system, with a focus on its complexities related to ToC for patients. The first subtheme, hospital and ward systems, captured a very positive regard for the systems that exist.

Most clinicians and patients spoke of well organised processes that facilitate the patient hospital journey of admission to discharge. This included administration processes, planning and handovers, complementary education that was delivered and reinforced by different (cardiology ward) team members, as well as ways to prioritise high risk patients, including a traffic light dashboard (one site), the embedded, skilled and valued Indigenous health worker team, and key performance indicators set for timely discharge reports. Highly functional and evidence-based programs on offer for certain cohorts of patients (e.g. ‘Better Cardiac Care’ [6] and the ‘Heart Recovery Service’ [5]) were proudly recognised as gold-standard.

“Everything was, you know, went to plan and I walked out of there feeling on top of the world” (Patient 7)

“I got a lot of information, and I was so happy with the cardiac team, they did everything” (Patient 6)

Team cohesion and communication was recognised and valued. Patients particularly highlighted their appreciation for teamwork, communication, continuity of care, and emotional reassurance from staff. Patients described the care team as cohesive and supportive, with all levels of staff playing a vital role in making them feel understood and cared for. However, it was noted in focus groups that some of these processes get missed if staff are busy or overwhelmed, or on weekends or when patients do not qualify for programs offered.

Staff recognised that the comprehensive education and discharge process can be overwhelming for patients and at some times, condescending, particularly due to power differentials that exist, plus clinician frustration due to lack of time or patient comprehension. Staff shortages among nurses, pharmacists, and cardiac rehabilitation teams were regularly mentioned as resulting in less capacity for thorough patient education and medication management. Despite this, patients described the reassurance they received through repeated communication from staff, which helped them to process complex information and feel more secure.

“Sometimes you need to hear it a couple of time to really take it in and process it”. (Patient 2)

“[I had] quite a few conversations, which was good because sometimes it can go in one ear and out the other. But no, I had quite a few and they were willing to sit and listen each time”. (Patient 4)

The second subtheme, communication between hospital and community, reflected issues due to communication downfalls. Community clinicians noted delays in transferring key information such as discharge summaries to GPs, and to a lesser extent CPs.

“Often the patient will show up before the discharge summary” (Clinician -GP).

“They usually come in with the discharge summary as well, so we’ve got it electronically through email and then also physically” (Clinician – CP).

“It would be amazing if we had more community pharmacists being involved with the hospital process as well because that’s where it falls down.” (Clinician Group 2).

Clinicians also felt there was insufficient direct communication between hospital specialists and primary care professionals that creates a disconnect which can compromise care. Hospital-based clinicians were encouraged to better appreciate the practical challenges and constraints GPs faced in primary care settings and improvements on the presentation of discharge summaries to be more focused on the “what next” or the “if, then” format would improve their utility and overcome some concerns noted with GPs potential uncertainty and/or reluctance to change prescriptions post-discharge.

“I think communication with GPs is very one-sided.“(Clinician Group 4)

“They find it really hard to write recommendations for the GP because they - some of them have done a rotation through general practice, but others don’t really know what GPs do” (Clinician – GP)

It was also noted by hospital clinicians that many discharge summaries were immediately available and it was important for GPs to be (made) aware of what was available within the IT system (the ‘Viewer’ is the Queensland system). Although, GPs noted no notification system exists and that it was difficult to access due to security requirements.

“I am a big fan of the viewer, but only as a backup plan in case normal handover has failed.“(Clinician - GP)

CPs, while already noted to be key to dispensing medications and fielding patient questions, were suggested to potentially be even more effectively integrated into the overall care team.

Despite this, patients acknowledged that their hospital teams did their best to ensure that information was shared with their GP and CP, or that their health record was updated.

“They [the hospital] will e-mail my doctor, my GP, and my pharmacist.” (Patient 2)

Overall, the respondents described a need for stronger integration between hospital and primary care services, especially in rural areas, to ensure timely, informed, and supportive transitions in care.

The third subtheme, primary-care specific processes, detailed challenges faced in general practice and community pharmacy. GPs were hesitant to adjust hospital specialist-initiated treatments unless very clear instructions were provided.

“You do get feedback from some patients that GPs don’t want to change anything because they [the patient] have a cardiologist.” (Clinician Group 5)

“Some are very good with that, some are less hesitant or more hesitant, it's GP dependent.” (Clinician-GP)

They were often constrained by time in the consult, and all clinicians noted the problems that occurred when patients did not have a regular GP (in not seeking follow up). In CP, issues like medication stock availability existed, especially for regional sites. However, this was regularly circumvented through proactive and early contact from the hospital pharmacists. Itinerant or GP-less patients were noted to turn to their nearest CP for help with medication re-supply after their 30-day supply had finished.

“Pharmacy will have to order stuff in, people are waiting for a week for the next big truck to go past. I mean maybe they’re waiting for three days for their tablets to come out.“(Clinician Group 3)

“This is not an urban environment and that’s probably the biggest challenge, is that it’s rural, there’s a tyranny of distance and then there’s the complexity, a completely different patient cohort in terms of education, ability to understand, motivation and access to healthcare services, so it’s enormously complex.“(Clinician Group 3)

Patients described challenges in primary care access and coordination of post-discharge care, particularly in rural settings, for both GPs and CPs. Timely organisation of new webster packs was an example given by patients, as a positive when it was pre-arranged for discharge, and as a frustration when there was a delay and their tablets caused confusion. Patients in rural and remote locations also faced additional hurdles in accessing timely GP appointments, pharmacies, and specialist follow-up care, and even initial problems accessing timely care during their cardiac event. Two patients described their family’s decision to relocate since the cardiac events to ensure that they have access to experienced care locally.

“We’ve had to go to hospital. We don’t have rehab locally. We don’t have anybody we can talk to or anyone experienced whatsoever [in our rural town].” (Patient 9)

“It’s always a lack of knowledge here [in a rural town] and [lack of] care factor.” (Patient 9)

Theme 3. Interprofessional collaboration

This theme emphasised the recognised need for teamwork and shared responsibility across different healthcare disciplines. Clinicians discussed how essential it was for a variety of professionals to be involved in patient education and to support their transition from hospital. There was broad acknowledgment from both patients and all clinician groups that collaboration between cardiologists, pharmacists, nurses, cardiac rehabilitation staff, Indigenous health workers, and community clinicians (pharmacists and GPs) was critical for effective medication management and generally occurs very well, especially within hospitals. A focus on improved direct two-way communication between hospital and community settings, along with shared information systems, was identified as being vital for continuity of care.

The first sub-theme was defining and leveraging diverse professional expertise, highlighting the unique contributions of each healthcare role in medication management and the potential for better utilisation of their respective skills. Patients described an appreciation for the diverse professional expertise within the hospital care team and how it positively impacted patient experiences. They valued the collaborative nature of the teams, noting that hearing information multiple times from different professionals helped with understanding and emotional processing. It was recognised that each professional contributed uniquely, from specialists initiating changes and providing brief descriptions, pharmacists handling medication reconciliation and counselling, nurses providing bedside education, to GPs (and allied health) managing long-term care and cardiac rehabilitation offering lifestyle guidance. CPs’ roles could be increased given their potential to provide further education and counselling to patients, and troubleshoot simple medication issues

“Like, I know sometimes doctors can be a bit aloof because they have lots on their mind, you know, and lots of different patients to look after. But their support staff, like the nurses and [Indigenous] liaison officers and even the interns that were really good, you know?” (Patient 2)

“They worked as a really strong team” (Patient 10)

“So definitely everyone has their own important part in order to keep [them] ‘compliant’ and to help them to understand and comprehend what will be the further complications.” (Clinician Group 2)

Further, the value of pharmacists embedded within cardiology units was noted to have demonstrated positive impacts, with a suggestion for broader implementation of pharmacists at other sites. Benefits included best possible medication history (BPMH) on admission, planning for discharge from admission, medication-related counselling, in addition to well-connected and dedicated communication channel with primary care. It was also noted that this model would also be useful in primary care/GP surgeries.

The second sub-theme was recognising the potential opportunities for a ToC team. This sub-theme focussed on bridging the gap between hospital and primary care. This was seen to be essential to foster a better appreciation of each other’s (clinicians) roles and enhancing the integration of community-based professionals into the ToC model.

“When people are looking out for them they do well, and it’s the ones that fall through the cracks that don’t do well and don’t have people helping navigate.“(Clinician Group 2)

“They’re not getting any traction with the GP, and they don’t feel like they’re being heard by the GP. Yeah, so I think there probably needs to be a step between the two-week GP follow up.” (Clinician Group 2)

“I think you’d get more out of a home visit, to be honest, because you see what’s going on and how many buckets of medications they’ve got in various areas in their house.“(Clinician Group 4)

Patients who did experience any form of (informal) ToC support expressed a sense of gratitude and reliance, highlighting the important role these teams play in bridging the gap between hospital discharge and ongoing care. Patients appreciated the ToC team’s clear communication, availability for follow-up questions, and reassurance, which helped them feel prepared and supported during a vulnerable time. However, the contrast with the lack of contact from the hospital after discharge and the absence of local rehabilitation services in rural areas underscores ongoing challenges in continuity of care and healthcare access for these high-risk patients.

“They were very, very helpful with explaining things quite clearly in and letting me know that if I had any other questions not to hesitate to call [the hospital/ward team], but they prepared me very well.”(Patient 4)

“I couldn’t have done it without them [the hospital follow up team].” (Patient 7)

Mapping to an implementation science framework

Integrative solutions that emerged included patient-centred and transition of care communication strategies

The mapping and matching process is summarised below and in Fig. 1, plus outlined in detail in Supplementary Table 2a and 2b. At a patient level this included individualised and patient-centred care with a priority focus on high-risk patients. This would involve prioritising strategies to tailor communication and care to match to individual health literacy levels, beliefs, and cultural contexts. Strategies to enhance literacy may include tailored messaging and education through accessible educational materials, such as pictorial aids, ensuring culturally sensitivity through co-creation, and personalised messaging strategies. Special attention would be needed for patients on certain medications, such as heart failure drugs and anti-coagulants to enhance monitoring and adherence.

CP -Community Pharmacists; d/c – discharge; GP – general practitioner; HMR – home medication review; NRT – nicotine replacement therapy; RECARD - REducing hospital re-admission for high-risk CARDiology patients; ToC – transition of care

Interventions to improve ToC communication could include those pertaining to planning, role definition and delineation, and innovations. With respect to planning, recognising patients with risk factors from a prep-defined time (e.g. admission/first team meeting etc.) will allow systematic identification of who can “enter” and receive care according to the “Adaptive ToC pathway”. Patient flow along this pathway should also consider discharge (requirements) from the beginning of admission, allowing ample time to prepare community supports. Following discharge, early follow-up using a pre-determined methods (either phone call, telehealth, or home visit) in the initial post-discharge weeks can reinforce medication adherence and address concerns as they arise. This process should be supported by enhanced communication and information sharing via clear, timely discharge summaries, along with reliable and suitable channels between hospital, CPs and GPs. Focus areas regarding role definition and delineation include formalising and strengthening the role of CPs in the model of care as well as developing/negotiating concise, actionable guidelines for medication management between hospital and primary care, especially for GPs.

Capitalising on and further developing technology and digital solutions would facilitate the delivery of the ToC model. This may include development or better access to and use of shared records. Further, finding solutions to improve specialist follow-up for patients in regional settings is also critical area for further development.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the experiences and expectations of patients discharged following type 1 AMI and/or cardiac surgery and of clinicians involved in their care, to inform the development of a patient-centred and locally appropriate pharmacist-led interdisciplinary ToC service. Our major findings highlight critical patient-level issues such as variable health literacy, worry about their ability to understand and incorporate all changes required, and medication-related beliefs impacting adherence to therapies. We also identified significant system and process issues, including information overload during discharge and fragmented communication between hospital and community care. Conversely, positive perceptions of existing hospital systems and interprofessional collaboration within the hospital were described. Finally, the study underscored the importance of interprofessional collaboration between the hospital and community health services, advocating for enhanced two-way communication and better integration of diverse professional expertise, particularly from CP, into the ToC model.

These findings align with the broader evidence base regarding medication management and care transitions for cardiovascular patients [24, 33]. Patient-level challenges, such as health literacy deficits and medication non-adherence, are well-documented in the literature and remain significant barriers to optimal outcomes post AMI [34–39]. Our observations regarding patient anxiety and negative medication beliefs, particularly concerning statin drugs, align with previous research highlighting the influence of psychosocial factors and misinformation on adherence [40–42]. The identified issues with information overload during discharge are also consistent with studies demonstrating that patients often struggle to retain complex medical information provided at this critical time [43]. Furthermore, the communication gaps between hospital and primary care mirror widespread challenges in care coordination, often leading to fragmented care and potential medication discrepancies [44]. However, our findings provide a more nuanced understanding by acknowledging the strengths of existing hospital systems and the high regard for interprofessional collaboration within and across health care settings, which can serve as a foundation for future improvements. These include extending the interprofessional collaboration culture to span the hospital to primary care transition to bridge the gap that exists.

More specifically, our results highlight needs of this cohort of patients. Despite the positivity towards current services and staff-provided information they received during their care, patients expressed a desire for more comprehensive discharge information before and after leaving the hospital, including in/from their home. They discussed how a multidisciplinary approach enhanced their engagement in education during their admission, reinforce key health messages, and fostered a more holistic and reassuring care environment, and that extending this multidisciplinary care approach into sustained contact across their ToC has been highlighted as essential for addressing an unmet need. Patients and staff highlighted that while some of the digital systems for communication between hospitals and primary care are functioning in some locations, there are still insufficiencies in digital interoperability and communication between systems [11]. Patients recognised that they have a role in improving their transition experience by embracing a local pharmacy after discharge to facilitate communication and care continuity. Finally, rural and remote patients discussed their unique needs, reflecting on the importance of coordinated, patient-centred post-discharge communication to ensure safe and informed transitions, especially if their local primary care workforce was transient or unfamiliar with their condition. Designing ToC services that are information-rich, multidisciplinary, include good communication methods between hospitals and primary care, and support rural and remote patients are therefore essential to improve patient experience and by extension their care outcomes. These identified needs align with those documented in National policy documents [12].

The strengths of this study include its broad stakeholder engagement from multiple sites, providing rich insights from both patients and a diverse group of clinicians, and its focus on local, community context to inform a full service development. Despite the bias being introduced by the self-selection of our cohort (particularly the patients), the data have been collated to inform the framework of a ToC. Further, despite only wanting to include patients who had experienced a cardiac event in the past three months believing it would provide the best insight into the hospital experience, such rich and clear data came from patients outside this time period we chose to retain the learnings obtained.

To maintain a broad and relevant lens, these data will be combined with the Scoping Review results [1], within the proposed RECARD-CFIR framework [26], refined by a validated risk prediction statistical model. A separate qualitative study (Yarning) with Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander Peoples is also under way and Yarning results will also be drawn into the final model of care. This will be delivered as an ‘Adaptive Pathway’, allowing local tailoring at each site. The ToC Adaptive Pathways will be implemented and evaluated as per the study protocol [25].

Conclusion

Participants provided valuable insights into the complex landscape of patient experiences and clinician perspectives surrounding post-AMI and cardiac surgery care transitions. By identifying key patient and system-level challenges, alongside existing strengths in interprofessional collaboration, these findings lay a strong foundation for the co-design and implementation of a person-centred and locally appropriate interdisciplinary ToC service. The emphasis on tailored communication, enhanced interprofessional collaboration, and strategic use of technology will be vital in optimising medication management and improving patient outcomes in this vulnerable cohort.
