Implementing a remote self-screening programme for atrial fibrillation using digital health technology among community-dwellers aged 75 years and older: a qualitative evaluation
Authors: Kam Cheong Wong, Tu N Nguyen, Steven A Trankle, Tim Usherwood, Desi Quintans, Simone Marschner, Samual Turnbull, Anupama Balasuriya Indrawansa, Rose White, Mason Jenner Burns, Vishal Gopal, Richard I Lindley, Saurabh Kumar, Clara K Chow
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo examine the feasibility of implementing remote atrial fibrillation (AF) self-screening among older people supported by a remote central monitoring system.DesignProcess evaluation of the Mass AF randomised clinical trial (ACTRN12621000184875) with one-to-one semistructured interviews using interview guides underpinned by the Critical Realism approach and coded using the UK Medical Research Council Guidance of Process Evaluation Framework.Setting and participantsCommunity-dwelling people aged ≥75 years from both genders (ratio 1:1) and urban/rural (ratio 2:1) in Australia.InterventionsParticipants were provided handheld single-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) devices and trained to self-record ECGs once daily on weekdays for at least 6 months. A remote central team notified participants and general practitioners (GPs) of AF.Primary feasibility outcomesThe strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats (SWOT) analysis examined enablers (ie, strengths and opportunities) and barriers (ie, weaknesses and threats).ResultsOverall, 200 participants; 98.5% completed the 6-month programme, 96% reported being satisfied with screening and 48 were interviewed: mean age 79 years, 54% male and 71% urban. 11 GPs were interviewed: 55% female and 64% urban. Programme participants trusted the remote monitoring system that supported the screening programme and provided follow-up pathways where required. GPs saw opportunities to introduce this self-screening programme to at-risk patients and improve patients’ risk profiles. Programme participants reported that after being trained to use the device, they felt empowered to do self-screening and found it convenient. GPs saw empowerment could enhance the doctor–patient relationship. Programme participants and GPs valued screening in diagnosing AF that would otherwise be missed in usual care, but the uncertainty of effective screening duration could be a barrier.ConclusionsThis screening programme was feasible with the reinforcement of the underpinning enablers. Several implementation strategies were identified using SWOT analysis, including leveraging the opportunity for GPs to introduce this screening programme to at-risk patients.Trial registration numberACTRN12621000184875.

Introduction

There is increasing recommendation from guidelines that self-screening for atrial fibrillation (AF) in high-risk groups has utility in the early detection and treatment of AF,14 and leaders in the field are advocating the use of mobile health devices such as handheld single-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) devices as screening tools.5 Intervention AF screening studies using single-lead ECGs are gaining momentum.69 For example, the United Kingdom (UK) ‘Screening for AF with ECG to Reduce stroke’ study aimed to investigate the effectiveness of screening for AF versus usual care in people aged 70 years and older using handheld single-lead ECG devices (Zenicor). Participants were recruited from general practices, trained to use the device and asked to self-record a rhythm trace four times a day for 3 weeks, and the traces were remotely and centrally reviewed by two cardiologists.8 The researchers reported that the design of the intervention protocol and the implementation strategy (ie, the way the intervention is implemented, including the contextual enablers and barriers) could affect the outcomes.10 11 A deficiency in the design of intervention is defined as a weakness or limitation of the intervention protocol, while an implementation deficiency is caused by weaknesses or limitations of the implementation strategy.10 Examining the screening process to delineate the enablers of and barriers to implementation is crucial to help identify remedial actions and improve the intervention protocol and implementation strategy.10

We implemented a randomised clinical trial (RCT) of AF screening among community-dwelling people aged 75 years and older in Australia. The randomised control trial was called the Mass AF Screening Study. It was registered (ACTRN12621000184875), and the protocol was published.12 13 The intervention was as follows: we provided handheld single-lead ECG devices (AliveCor Kardia) to participants and trained them to use the devices to self-record ECGs once daily on weekdays in the community for 12 months (in the intervention group) and 6 months (in the control group). Participants’ ECGs were transmitted to a remote centralised monitoring system reviewed by trained personnel, who notified participants and their general practitioners (GPs) of AF diagnoses. The central team facilitated patients’ access to the necessary care and provided referral pathways to GPs who needed assistance. We aim to evaluate the feasibility of implementing the screening programme by examining enablers that include strengths (ie, screening programme attributes that help uptake of screening) and opportunities (ie, external factors that facilitate screening) and barriers that include weaknesses (ie, screening programme attributes that limit the uptake of screening) and threats (ie, external factors that impede screening) and addressing the enablers and barriers in view of implementing the AF screening programme interfacing with (ie, communicating, connecting and supporting) the existing healthcare system. The clinical outcomes of the RCT have been published.14

Methods

Design of the qualitative study: the predefined framework and interpretive lens

The feasibility of implementation encompassed participant experience and satisfaction with the screening programme, as well as the enablers of and barriers to implementation. In examining and explaining the feasibility, we applied the Critical Realism lens (ie, what works for whom, in what circumstances and how to enable the screening implementation).15 Through this lens, the context was taken into consideration. The context included the screening programme structure and participant circumstances. Context includes participants’ living environment, their ability to access and participate in the programme, their individual agency to act independently, their perceptions of screening value, and the structure and resources of the programme. The importance of context has been reported in other studies evaluating how intervention programmes work within the complexity of general practice and how context influences participant experiences with the programmes.16 17

The UK Medical Research Council Guidance of Process Evaluation18 was referenced to establish a priori framework that comprises three themes, that is, theme 1—implementation (what was implemented and how?), theme 2—mechanisms of impact (how did the intervention produce outcomes?) and theme 3—context (how did context affect implementation and outcomes?). This framework provided the structure into which interviews were transcribed and coded. The formulations of interview guides were informed by the Critical Realism approach, which allows the system (ie, programme structure and delivery mechanism) and perspectives of the programme participants and GPs to be examined. We explain how Critical Realism informed the formulations of the interview guides in our published qualitative methodology paper.19

Sampling and participants (programme participants and GPs)

A purposeful sampling approach20 was applied to invite participants and GPs from both genders (ratio 1:1) and urban/rural areas (ratio 2:1) to attend one-to-one semistructured interviews. The invited programme participants had used the ECG devices for at least 3 months. Programme participants were not remunerated for their participation. The purposively invited GPs comprised senior GPs who had participants enrolled in the study and junior GPs from the research networks. An $A50 gift card was given to GPs who attended the interview.

Data collection (interviews) and analysis approach

Interviews with programme participants explored their experiences of the screening programme, the training and support we provided, the process of self-recording ECGs and their perceptions of the overall values of the programme. GP interviews explored their perception of AF screening in the community in general and specifically for older people aged ≥75 years and their perceptions of this screening programme. The interview guides were published in the protocol,13 the original and revisions are shown in online supplemental tables 1–6, and the justifications for revisions were explained in our published methodology paper.19

One researcher (KCW) conducted all interviews and had no therapeutic relationship with any interviewees. He explained the screening programme and objectives of the interview and reassured participants that their responses in the interview would not affect any present or future therapeutic relationship. All interviews were one-off (no repeat interviews). KCW recorded field notes, including refinements of interview prompts to elicit an in-depth understanding of participants’ responses. The interviewees were offered the option of telephone or video interviews. The interviews were audio recorded after gaining verbal consent from programme participants and written consent from GPs. Interviews were transcribed using the Microsoft Word transcription application. KCW listened to the audio recordings and checked the accuracy of the transcripts. We asked participants if they would like to review the interview transcript and emailed it to them if requested.

The interviews were transcribed and inductively coded into the predefined framework progressively with consensus; KCW and SAT coded the same eight initial transcripts inductively. They convened to reach a consensus on the codes. KCW coded the remaining transcripts. The trends of novel codes (new information coded from interviews) and duplicate codes (repeated information between interviewees) were examined using cumulative frequency graphs and stacked column charts using our novel R-package.19 21 We examined the plateau of the novel interview codes and were satisfied with the richness and insights of the interviews in answering our research question about the feasibility of the intervention; hence, we stopped interviewing after the 48th interview with programme participants and 11th interview with GPs. The detailed approach, including descriptions of the path to the stopping point in recruiting participants, was published separately.19 NVivo (V.12; QSR International) software was used to organise and manage the qualitative data. Our reporting of qualitative data aligns with the Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative (COREQ) research guide.22

We analysed the enablers (ie, strengths and opportunities) and barriers (ie, weaknesses and threats) using the ‘strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats (SWOT)’ analytical method.23 The implementation and qualitative evaluation approach and methods are summarised in online supplemental figure 1.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in our research’s design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans.

Results

A total of 200 community-dwellers participated in the trial (mean age 79.0±3.4 years; 54.0% female; 72.5% urban), and 98.5% completed the 6-month programme. Of the expected 20 days per month, the overall monthly median number of days participants adhered to the protocol (ie, self-recorded ECGs) was 20 (IQR 17–22), resulting in a total of 31 980 ECGs transmitted to the central monitoring system. At the end of 12 months, participants were confident using the device (93%), reported it was easy to use (98%), found screening efficient (96%) and satisfied with the screening (96%). Detailed results, including the AF diagnosis rates, have been published elsewhere.14 Of the 200 participants, 48 were purposively invited; all consented and were interviewed from September 2021 to July 2022: age ranged from 75 years to 87 years, 26 (54%) males, 34 (71%) urban, 32 (67%) of the participants opted for a telephone interview and 16 (33%) for a video interview. All interviews were one-to-one except for two participants (P075 and P076) who opted for and were interviewed together. There was no observer (non-participant) in all interviews. The median interview duration was 22 min (IQR: 16.9–27.2 min).

We invited 11 GPs, and all of them consented and attended the interviews from December 2021 to March 2023: 6 (54%) females and 7 (64%) urban. The questions about GP clinical experience and working hours resulted in median categorical options of 10–19 years and 33–40 hours per week. All GPs opted for video interviews. The median interview duration was 32 min (IQR: 26–39). Transcripts of the interviews were emailed to three GPs who wanted to review them, and all of them agreed with the content of the transcripts.

Common views: programme participants and GPs

Some programme participants and GPs saw positive values in the screening programme because AF was diagnosed, which would otherwise be missed. They appreciated the communication and facilitation to access treatments. A participant reduced alcohol consumption after AF diagnosis.

Training, empowering, supporting and remotely monitoring participants’ ECGs enabled the implementation of this screening programme. Participants integrating self-screening into daily routines and introducing it to peers via social networks were the mechanism of creating positive impacts. The credibility of the screening programme and participants’ autonomy and convenience to participate were the contextual factors for deriving the positive impacts of the implementation. Programme participants’ barriers

Participants’ difficulty in self-recording ECGs caused by interferences from environmental noises and other electronic devices, anxiety about potential abnormal findings, and not receiving reassurance (that their ECGs were normal) were barriers to implementation. The barriers caused frustration and anxiety in some participants, which were the mechanism of creating negative impacts—dissatisfaction and non-adherence in some participants. Living environments with interferences, difficulty handling the handheld ECG devices with arthritic fingers and device costs (if not sponsored) were contextual barriers to the implementation. Discussion

Our findings indicate that remote AF self-screening is feasible among people aged 75 years and older who live independently in the community or have access to a support person at home, on the premises where there is effective training, communication and support by a central team. Both programme participants and GPs saw positive value in this screening programme because AF cases were diagnosed that would otherwise be missed in usual care. This programme demonstrated its efficacy in diagnosing AF and the feasibility of facilitating patients to access treatments, connecting GPs and patients to the necessary health services, and providing patients with the opportunity to improve modifiable risk factors (eg, reduce alcohol consumption). This programme promotes integrated care that can assist in reducing stroke risk.2429

Communication with participants to address their technical difficulties alleviated their fear of using technology and improved their confidence. The consistent support provided to participants included helping them set up phone reminders to self-record ECGs and checking on them when they did not transmit ECGs for three consecutive days. There is an opportunity to involve participants’ support personnel (eg, next of kin) to help and encourage participants to engage in the screening programme. The communication and support provided by a central coordinating team play an integral role in successfully implementing this screening programme. In a systematic review using the realist approach, Pearsons et al reviewed 39 studies that delivered integrated AF services and concluded that central coordinators are integral to successfully delivering an integrated AF approach.30

Through the SWOT analysis,23 we identified strategies to improve the implementation by explaining how to use the strengths and opportunities to improve the weaknesses and alleviate the threats, as illustrated below.

Anxiety about potential ECG abnormalities and lack of reassurance of normal ECGs could result in non-adherence to self-screening. This barrier can be addressed by modifying the protocol to include sending text messages to reassure participants of their normal ECGs at regular intervals (eg, weekly). Research has demonstrated that sending text messages using a remote central system improved patient adherence to medication31 and cardiovascular health outcomes.32 This text messaging platform can be adapted to send reassurance messages to participants in the screening programme. Participants with arthritic fingers had difficulty using handheld devices. Training these participants to handle the device by placing it on a stable table and placing two fingers of each hand on the device helped overcome the difficulty. However, these participants may need alternative monitoring methods, such as wearable devices that automatically record the heart rhythm.33 34 Our screening programme can be adapted for use with alternative screening devices. The costs of screening devices (if not sponsored) would be barriers to implementation. We optimised resource utilisation by recycling the ECG devices as participants completed the screening programme at different time points. Environmental interferences to ECG recording are threats that can be alleviated by educating participants, such as removing hearing aids, digital watches and electronic devices while recording ECGs and advising participants to find a quiet place (away from noise and vibration) while recording ECGs.

Competing priorities in performing various screenings in busy general practice settings are threats to promoting AF screening.35 36 Researchers evaluated the feasibility of AF self-screening kiosks at GPs’ waiting rooms using the same screening device as this study. They interviewed 6 GPs and 14 practice staff (ie, receptionists and practice managers). The GPs reported minimal disruption to their clinical workflow, but practice staff reported significant disruption to their administrative workflow because some patients required assistance to record ECGs.37 However, our remote centralised screening model alleviates the point-of-care screening burden at general practices because it would not significantly interrupt administrative and clinical workflow. GPs provide routine care to their patients as usual, but they will only respond to notifications from the remote monitoring team, manage the patients when AF is diagnosed and have access to the cardiologists in the screening team if necessary.

The lack of financial incentives to interpret ECGs is also a threat to AF screening in general practice. Simonson et al discussed the need for clinicians to be reimbursed for interpreting ECGs to make AF screening sustainable.38 This also applies to GPs in Australia who are not reimbursed for interpreting ECGs.39 This is a threat to implementing AF screening in time-poor and financially not-reimbursed GP settings. This remote centralised ECG monitoring supported by trained personnel to interpret ECGs reduce GPs’ burden in interpreting ECGs. The central monitoring team is supported by cardiologists, reducing GPs’ concerns about medicolegal liabilities in missing critical ECG abnormalities. Regarding GPs’ concerns about the lack of guidelines in effective intervals for AF screening, the eighth Atrial Fibrillation NETwork/European Heart Rhythm Association consensus conference40 suggested a systematic AF screening pathway and illustrated the role of patient-led screening, including the use of handheld ECG devices in the pathway and suggested repeating AF screening in 3–5 years intervals.41 However, emerging research could provide more evidence of the effective duration and interval of AF screening and inform updates on AF screening guidelines.

Strengths of this qualitative evaluation approach

The formulations of interview guides were underpinned by Critical Realism15 approach and the UK Medical Research Guidance of Process Evaluation,18 which provide a systematic and structured methodology to evaluate the feasibility of implementing the AF screening study. We also illustrated the refinement of interview questions and assessment of interview data sufficiency, which enhanced the rigour of the approach.19 Elaborating on enablers as strengths and opportunities, and barriers as weaknesses and threats, facilitates SWOT analysis, providing insights into leveraging enablers while addressing barriers.

Limitations of this qualitative evaluation approach

Fixing a predefined framework limited the categorisation of some participants’ observations. For example, some participants observed that it was easier to record ECGs (ie, it took them fewer attempts to record a rhythm trace successfully) in summer than in winter and after cleaning the contact points of the device with water-based liquid. These observations are kept in the online supplemental tables instead of in the main themes. The research protocol did not include interviewing the technicians who monitored ECGs and the research officers who trained participants to record ECGs. We gathered their feedback in regular team meetings. These were not captured in the qualitative evaluation.

Future directions

In Australia, GP health assessments for people aged ≥75 years are rebated by Medicare.42 This presented an opportunity to promote the community AF screening programme by approaching GPs and nurses to introduce the self-screening programme to the patients during health assessments. They can refer interested people to the study team, which will perform eligibility checks, enrolment and training of the participants. Integration of this into the health assessment workflow would potentially reduce GPs’ time to perform point-of-care AF screening and provide an opportunity for screening over time, which is more effective in detecting paroxysmal AF.7 The central support team facilitates patients’ access to treatments and provides GPs with a referral pathway, which are vital enablers.

Community venues, social events and participants’ perceptions of the credibility of our screening programme presented opportunities for promoting the screening programme to a larger population, including people living in rural and remote areas. The screening team can promote the credibility of the screening programme and recruit participants via various community venues (eg, pharmacies,43 podiatrists and community social events (eg, bike clubs and bush-walking groups). The promotion campaign can be widened to reach out to family members, support personnel for older people, and their social networks to raise their awareness of the importance of AF screening. The study team can train the support personnel to assist participants, which will improve participants’ connections with the monitoring team.

Conclusions

This study found that the AF screening programme is feasible in the context of community-dwelling people aged ≥75 years and can be implemented interfacing with (ie, communicating, connecting and supporting) the existing health systems. Clinicians can identify at-risk people and refer them to the screening programme. Through the SWOT analyses, we identified strategies to improve implementation and intervention protocol and to optimise resources to scale up this programme to reach a larger population, rural and remote communities, and people at risk of AF.
