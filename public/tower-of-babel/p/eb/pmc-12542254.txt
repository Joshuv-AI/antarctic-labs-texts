Assessing the reporting quality of educational interventions in trials on caregiver education for children with developmental disabilities using the GREET checklist: a meta-research study
Authors: Mersiha Mahmić-Kaknjo, Tea Andabaka, Martina Radić, Vesna Vučić, Valentina Košćak, Barbara Čaić, Milena Žuža Praštalo, Aleksandra Veselinović, Biljana Pokimica, Danijela Ristić Medić, Jelena Međaković, Edita Runjić, Tamara Prevendar, Antonia Jeličić Kadić, Antonija Hrkać, Antonia Čivljak, Marta Čivljak, Ivica Matić, Tatjana Zorčec, Livia Puljak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInadequate reporting of interventions in educational research hinders the identification and replication of effective teaching strategies. This study aimed to analyze the reporting of educational interventions in randomized controlled trials (RCTs) focused on educating parents and caregivers of children with developmental disabilities (CDD).MethodsThis was a meta-research study, conducted on the literature synthesized in a prior scoping review. We searched MEDLINE and Embase from inception to November 13, 2023. Eligible studies were RCTs on the education of CDD parents/caregivers. We evaluated reporting using the Guideline for Reporting Evidence-Based Practice Educational Interventions and Teaching (GREET) checklist and its explanatory paper. The primary outcome was median adherence to the GREET checklist, while secondary outcomes included adherence to individual checklist items. We analyzed discrepancies in ratings between the two raters and assessed improvements in reporting practices over time.ResultsWe analyzed 161 trials published from 1984 to 2023. Median adherence to the GREET checklist was 59% (10 out of 17 reported items), ranging from 29 to 82%. Adherence to individual GREET items varied from 2% (“Delivered as scheduled”) to 99% (“Intervention”, “Theory”, and “Educational Strategies”). The poorest adherence to the GREET checklist was found for items “Materials included”, “Adaptations”, “Incentives”, “Modifications”, and “Delivered as scheduled”. Discrepancies between two raters were noted in 565 (21%) of the 2,737 analyzed GREET items, with Cohen’s Kappa at 0.616. A modest improvement in reporting practices was observed in studies published from 2018 onward, compared to those published before 2018.ConclusionThe reporting of educational interventions in analyzed RCTs was suboptimal. To enhance study replicability, specific educational materials must be reported in detail or included as supplementary files to foster evidence-based practice. Some GREET checklist items are challenging to score and would benefit from more precise definitions. Addressing these gaps could improve the efficacy of educational programs for parents and caregivers of children with developmental disabilities and the usability of the GREET checklist.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12874-025-02677-0.Keywords: Evidence-Based practice, Reporting standards, Checklist, Reproducibility of results, Research design, Developmental disabilities, Educational interventions

Background

Inadequate reporting of interventions in educational research hinders the identification, evaluation, and implementation of effective teaching strategies and evidence-based practice (EBP). Clear and comprehensive reporting is essential for replicating studies and enabling educators to implement evidence-based interventions effectively. When interventions are poorly described, it becomes difficult for researchers and practitioners to discern which specific components contributed to the observed outcomes, thereby undermining the reliability, interpretability and applicability of the research findings [1].

The lack of detailed reporting can lead to implementation variability, as educators must interpret ambiguously described interventions differently, leading to inconsistent effectiveness of interventions across educational settings [2]. Furthermore, incomplete reporting may obscure the influence of contextual factors crucial for understanding how and why an intervention works, thereby constraining the interpretation and generalizability of findings across different populations, contexts, and settings [3].

Moreover, inadequate reporting can contribute to publication bias, as studies that fail to meet rigorous reporting standards may not be published, further skewing the available evidence base. This bias inhibits educators’ ability to make informed decisions based on a comprehensive understanding of the research landscape. It also complicates systematic reviews and meta-analyses, which rely on the quality of reporting to synthesize evidence effectively [4, 5].

Overall, addressing the inadequacies in reporting interventions in educational research is vital for enhancing the rigor and relevance of the field. Improved reporting standards can facilitate better replication, support informed decision-making among educators, and ultimately lead to more effective teaching strategies that enhance student learning outcomes.

Recently, we completed a systematic scoping review of educational interventions for parents and caregivers of children with developmental disabilities (CDD), assessed through randomized controlled trials (RCTs) (unpublished data). In that review, we aimed to analyze whether the trials were replicable by searching for descriptions of educational materials in RCTs. However, we found that most trials were non-replicable due to insufficient information about the tested interventions. As we were unable to identify any studies that explored the reporting of educational interventions in detail within this field, we recognized a significant research gap.

The only checklist available for assessing the reporting of educational interventions is the Guideline for Reporting Evidence-Based Practice Educational Interventions and Teaching (GREET) [6]. The GREET is a reporting framework designed to improve the transparency and replicability of educational interventions in healthcare research. It consists of items that ensure comprehensive descriptions of key aspects of educational interventions, including their theoretical framework, content, delivery methods, materials, adaptations, and evaluation strategies. By promoting standardized reporting, the GREET checklist facilitates the synthesis of evidence, enhances the reproducibility of interventions, and supports the advancement of evidence-based educational practices [6].

Thus, this study aimed to analyze the reporting of interventions in RCTs focused on the education of parents/caregivers of children with developmental disabilities using the GREET checklist.

Methods

Study design

This was a meta-research study, conducted as a follow-up study on research reports identified in a prior scoping review.

Protocol

The protocol for the systematic scoping review, in which we identified trials included in this analysis, was published prospectively in the Open Science Framework (link: https://osf.io/fz5sq). This analysis of intervention reporting was conducted as a follow-up study.

Study eligibility

We analyzed trials published in scholarly journals that were identified in the scoping systematic review of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) focused on education for parents and caregivers of CDD. Briefly, to conduct this scoping review, we searched MEDLINE and Embase from their inception to November 13, 2023, without any language restrictions. The search strategies are reported in Supplementary File 1. We exported and deduplicated search results using Covidence software (www.covidence.org). Covidence was also used for independent duplicate screening of titles, abstracts, and relevant full texts. All publications that were secondary analyses of studies already included in the extraction process were excluded from further analysis (n = 9 out of 170 manuscripts identified in the scoping review). These excluded publications (Supplementary file 2) included six secondary analyses/publications, one qualitative data analysis, one study protocol, and one study that was not focused on parent education and was thus out of scope. References to the included articles are provided in Supplementary File 3.

We analyzed 161 trials published between 1984 and 2023, all of which were papers written in the English language. The corresponding authors of two-thirds of the studies (95 out of 161) were from English-speaking countries (USA, UK and Australia).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the total median adherence to the GREET checklist, while the secondary outcomes included adherence to individual items within the GREET checklist. We analyzed discrepancies in ratings between the two raters and assessed improvements in reporting practices over time.

Checklist selection

The primary objective of this meta-research was to evaluate the completeness and transparency of reporting of EBP educational interventions. We therefore used the GREET guideline because it is a reporting guideline specifically developed for EBP educational interventions and is agnostic to study design, which suited our heterogeneous evidence base. In contrast, tools such as the What Works Clearinghouse (WWC) Standards [7], Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) checklist for RCTs [8], and JBI checklist for RCTs [9] are intended for critical appraisal of methodological quality/risk of bias (e.g., for RCTs and other primary designs) rather than for intervention reporting. Given our focus on reporting rather than internal validity or effect estimation, GREET was the most appropriate tool; WWC/CASP/JBI are acknowledged as complementary for future work centered on methodological quality.

Scope of analyses

Our prespecified objective was to evaluate the reporting completeness and replicability of educational interventions using the GREET checklist. We therefore did not extract or pool intervention effect estimates, conduct risk-of-bias assessments, or harmonize outcomes for meta-analysis. Given the heterogeneity of outcome constructs and measurement instruments across trials, quantitative aggregation of effectiveness lies outside the scope of this meta-research and would require a separate protocol.

Data synthesis and analysis

For the analysis of intervention reporting, we evaluated eligible studies using the GREET checklist with several modifications. The GREET checklist consists of 17 items [6]. GREET item 4 (“identify the EBP content,” i.e., the EBP steps targeted) was not scored because our objective was to assess reporting completeness and replicability, whereas item 4 chiefly addresses curricular scope (what content was taught). In addition, item 4 overlaps conceptually with other GREET domains we scored (e.g., learning objectives, materials, procedures), and including it would risk double-counting content-related information without improving the precision of our reporting assessment.

Additionally, we divided the 5th item into two separate assessments. The 5th item of the GREET checklist is: “5. MATERIALS: Describe the specific educational materials used in the educational intervention. Include materials provided to the learners and those used in the training of educational intervention providers“ [6].

The first part of the 5th item instructs to “describe” educational materials, which is a non-specific instruction that could result in a brief and uninformative description. The second part emphasizes “including” materials, which pertain to replicability. If the materials are not made accessible, likely, the intervention will not be replicable. Therefore, we evaluated the 5th item as items 5a and 5b. In item 5a, we assessed whether any description of educational materials was reported in the published article. In item 5b, we specifically evaluated replicability—whether educational materials were “included” and publicly available.

The reporting adequacy for each GREET checklist item was independently assessed by the two first authors using Microsoft Excel (Version 16.0) software (https://office.microsoft.com/excel). The two raters did not have prior experience with the GREET checklist. Assessments for each item were categorized as ”yes” (indicating clear and mostly comprehensive information), “no” (indicating no information provided), or “unclear” (indicating that some information was provided but was incomplete or imprecise).

Data extractors utilized the GREET Explanation and Elaboration document, published as Additional File 2 by Phillips et al., to guide their evaluations [6]. Two raters independently extracted data from the 161 included studies using the GREET checklist. We then compared two sets of completed data extraction forms and resolved any discrepancies via discussion. Raw data collected during the study were published on OSF (link: https://osf.io/huv7q/files/osfstorage).

Since the GREET checklist was published in 2016 [6], we used year 2018 as a cut-off for analyzing adherence to GREET before and after its publication, to allow some time for the checklist implementation after its publication.

We used descriptive statistics to summarize and present data as frequencies and percentages. Adherence to the GREET checklist was indicated by the median and interquartile range (IQR). The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare GREET adherence scores between trials published before and after 2018 (https://www.socscistatistics.com/tests/mannwhitney/default2.aspx). Inter-rater agreement was calculated using Cohen’s Kappa. We interpreted inter-rater agreement in line with Landis et al. [10]: Kappa < 0: no agreement, Kappa 0.00–0.20: slight agreement, Kappa 0.21–0.40: fair agreement, Kappa 0.41–0.60: moderate agreement, Kappa 0.61–0.80: substantial agreement, Kappa 0.81–1.00: almost perfect agreement. For these analyses, we used MedCalc Statistical Software version 23.1.3 (MedCalc Software bv, Ostend, Belgium; https://www.medcalc.org; 2023). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of included trials

Figure 1 shows the process of study selection. We analyzed 161 RCTs (Supplementary File 3). The included studies were published between 1984 and 2023, with more than two-thirds (N = 114; 70%) published in the last decade (from 2014 to 2023). The majority of trials originated from the United States, the United Kingdom, and Australia. Most of the trials (N = 160; 99%) were published in English, while one study was published in Spanish (0.6%). For the purpose of this research, one publication in Spanish was translated to English using Google translate, and uploaded into Covidence for further analysis.

Study selection flowchart

Interrater agreement

The first round of assessments yielded discrepancies in 21% of item ratings (n = 565/2737), which we reduced to 14% and 2% through successive consensus rounds, until complete agreement was reached. The mean number of discrepancies per trial was 3.5. Inter-rater agreement was substantial (κ = 0.616; 95% CI: 0.590–0.642). Highest disagreement rates were observed for items addressing “Environment”, “Materials included”, “Attendance”, and “Adaptations”. Detailed analysis revealed that ambiguity in item phrasing and variability in manuscript reporting contributed to inconsistency. Several GREET items were identified as candidates for future refinement.

The proportion of trials reporting individual items from the GREET checklist

The reporting of interventions showed notable variability across different items on the GREET checklist. Three items were reported in 99% of the trials: “Intervention”, “Theory”, and “Educational Strategies”. Additionally, “Learning objectives” and “Delivery” of the intervention were adequately reported in more than 95% of the trials. Adherence was somewhat lower, between 80% and 90%, for “Materials described”, “Schedule”, and “Time spent” of the intervention. The description of “Instructors” was reported in 68% of the trials (Table 1; Fig. 2).

The trials exhibited a low frequency of reporting on several key items. These included “Delivered as planned check” (53%), “Attendance” (40%), “Environment” (40%), “Materials included” (29%), “Adaptations” (25%), “Incentives” (17%), “Modifications” (7%), and “Delivered as scheduled” (2%) (Table 1; Fig. 2).

The following items significantly contributed to overall adherence to the GREET checklist: “Intervention”, “Theory”, “Educational strategies”, “Delivery”, “Learning objectives”, “Schedule”, “Time spent”, and “Materials described”. Conversely, the GREET items with the greatest potential for improvement include: “Materials included”, “Adaptations”, “Incentives”, “Modifications”, and whether the intervention was “Delivered as scheduled” (Fig. 3).

Primary outcome: median adherence to GREET

None of the trials fully reported all items on the GREET checklist. The average number of GREET items reported per trial (scored as “yes”) was 10.3, with a minimum of 5 items and a maximum of 14 items reported per trial. The average adherence score to the GREET checklist across studies was 61%, ranging from a minimum of 29% to a maximum of 82%. The median adherence to the GREET checklist was 59% per study, with an interquartile range (IQR) of 41–77%. This corresponds to a median of 10 GREET items reported per study (IQR: 7 to 13 items), with an interquartile deviation of 9%.

Changes in reporting practices before and after publication of the GREET checklist

A total of 79 trials were published before 2018, with a median adherence to the GREET checklist of 10 out of 17 items. For 82 trials published in 2018 and later, the median adherence was 11 out of 17 items (Fig. 4). A Mann-Whitney U test indicated no statistically significant difference before and after 2018 in median adherence to the GREET (p = 0.803).

Discussion

This reporting of educational interventions for caregivers of CDD was suboptimal, hindering the replicability of trials that tested those interventions. While certain elements, such as “Intervention”, “Theory”, and “Educational Strategies”, were consistently well reported, significant gaps remained in the documentation of key aspects of educational interventions, including “Materials Included”, ”Incentives”, and ”Delivered as scheduled”.

The specific educational materials utilized in the interventions were well described in terms of their type and general nature in 136 of the analyzed 161 papers (84%). However, only 46 (29%) articles provided sufficient detail on the “Materials included”, offering at least some indication, which has enabled assessors to identify the specific educational resources employed in the intervention. This information is crucial for facilitating the replication of these interventions.

The inability to replicate interventions tested in trials presents a critical issue in evidence-based practice, as it undermines the reliability, validity, and generalizability of research findings [11]. Replicability ensures that an intervention can be consistently implemented across different settings, populations, and researchers, which is essential for translating research into practice [12]. When studies lack sufficient detail in reporting intervention components, implementation strategies, or contextual factors, subsequent researchers and practitioners are unable to accurately reproduce the intervention accurately, thereby limiting its applicability and diminishing its impact [13].

Furthermore, non-replicable interventions contribute to research waste, as resources are expended on studies that cannot be verified or built upon [14]. This issue is particularly concerning in educational and clinical research, where incomplete reporting hinders systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and guideline development [15]. To enhance replicability, clear and comprehensive reporting frameworks, such as the GREET checklist for educational interventions [6], are essential to ensure that interventions are described with sufficient detail, including materials, delivery methods, and contextual adaptations, thereby facilitating reproducibility and improving the reliability of research findings.

It is essential to emphasize that generalizing educational materials is inherently challenging due to linguistic and cultural differences. We contend that educational research cannot be effectively replicated unless educational materials are either provided as supplementary resources or made publicly accessible through other means. However, that was very rare in the sample of trials we analyzed in this study.

The low frequency of reporting on critical items found in this study suggests a need for enhanced guidelines and adherence to existing checklists, such as the GREET. While the GREET checklist aims to standardize reporting, the low adherence to several items indicates a broader challenge in educational research. Even established guidelines like Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) have shown similar deficiencies in intervention descriptions. Adherence to guidelines could improve the reproducibility and applicability of educational interventions in practice [1, 16]. Conversely, some researchers argue that an excessive focus on checklist adherence may detract from the innovative aspects of intervention design, potentially stifling creativity in educational methodologies [17].

While this study highlights deficiencies in reporting, it is essential to recognize that some researchers may prioritize specific GREET items based on their relevance to the research question, which could justify selective reporting. This nuanced approach can sometimes yield more focused and relevant findings, albeit at the cost of comprehensiveness.

The analysis of GREET item reporting reveals significant variability in adherence across studies. The average number of GREET items reported was 10.3, with a median adherence score of 61%. This indicates that while some studies reported a substantial number of items, many fell short of fully complying with the GREET guidelines.

It is worth noting that the GREET checklist was published in September 2016, while the trials analyzed in this sample were published between 1984 and 2023. The data showed modest improvements in reporting practices for studies published in 2018 and later, indicating that awareness and adherence to guidelines such as GREET have increased over time. Despite these findings, overall adherence remains suboptimal, suggesting that further efforts are necessary to improve reporting standards and enhance compliance with reporting guidelines. This underlines the ongoing challenge of ensuring comprehensive reporting in research, as demonstrated by other studies where adherence to reporting checklists also exhibited significant variability [18, 19].

In the literature, we identified only one study that evaluated the reporting of a sample of trials against the GREET checklist, published by Bala et al. in 2024 [2]. That study analyzed 40 trials on evidence-based healthcare e-learning and concluded that the reporting was poor [2]. The reporting in their sample was poorer than that in our study. Specifically, only three items were adequately reported in at least 80% of the included studies in their analysis [2]. In contrast, our study found that eight items were reported in more than 80% of the trials. The median number of GREET items reported per study in their analysis was 8, with a third quartile of 9 (range: 4 to 14) [2]. In our study, the median number of GREET items reported per study was 10, with a range of 5 to 14 items.

Furthermore, only 7 out of 40 studies (17.5%) in Bala et al.’s sample reported at least 10 items, whereas our study found that 107 out of 161 studies (66%) reported at least 10 items [2]. Although the reporting appeared to be poorer in their sample than in ours, it is also possible that the assessors in Bala et al.’s study [2] applied stricter criteria due to the subjective nature of the assessments.

Of note, Bala et al. assessed 17 original items from the GREET checklist [2]. We revised the methodological approach by excluding the fourth item, which pertains to EBP content, and by splitting the fifth item into two separate categories. Our analysis of these two components indicated that educational materials were described in 84% of the trials; however, materials were included in only 29% of trials. This discrepancy is concerning as it hinders the replication of these studies. If materials are not included or made publicly available in 71% of trials, investigators who wish to replicate these studies may face significant barriers.

It is well-documented that authors are often reluctant to share research data upon request, frequently failing to respond to data inquiries [20, 21]. Future studies could explore the willingness of authors to provide study materials upon request.

In this study, we did not establish any cut-off points to categorize the adequacy of trial reporting based on predefined thresholds. Additionally, we did not assign points to our assessments, as reporting checklists were not designed or validated as tools for assessing reporting [22]. Moreover, there is no consensus regarding the threshold for various categories of reporting [23].

A study by Maticic et al. analyzed discrepancies between two authors naïve to the PRISMA-for-Abstracts checklist. They found that even after calibration, there were 9.3% discrepancies among the 2,928 analyzed checklist items, with a Cohen’s Kappa of 0.807, indicating almost perfect inter-rater agreement [24]. Their discrepancy rate was significantly lower than that observed in our study. This difference may be attributed to several factors. In their study, each item on the PRISMA-A checklist was scored as either “yes” or “no”, without an “unclear” category. The inclusion of a broader range of scoring options in our study most likely contributed to increased variability and disagreements in assessments. Moreover, the abstracts analyzed using the PRISMA-A checklist are concise, whereas full-text manuscripts evaluated by the GREET checklist often span dozens of pages. The smaller text volume being analyzed reduces the complexity of the rating process. Additionally, while the PRISMA-A checklist was published in 2013 [25], the GREET checklist was introduced more recently, in September 2016 [6], resulting in less time for familiarity and adoption.

We hope that these findings will raise awareness about the need to improve reporting of educational interventions for CDD. The need for transparent and complete descriptions of the interventions is threefold. Poorly described interventions bear consequences for researchers, practitioners, and caregivers - the end users. First, it becomes difficult to replicate and build upon existing interventions in RCTs, but also to adapt and apply interventions in different contexts. More precise descriptions of interventions in research articles would help practitioners choose effective intervention strategies for supporting parents and caregivers. Finally, they would provide more substantial evidence for policymakers and funders who prioritize well-documented studies when investing in caregiver programs.

This study had several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. As a retrospective analysis of published trials, our study relied on the completeness and accuracy of reporting within the included articles, which may not fully reflect the quality or execution of the interventions themselves.

Although the GREET checklist is a widely used tool for evaluating the reporting quality of educational interventions, it has inherent limitations. Certain items, such as those requiring a description of educational materials (item 5), may be subject to variability in interpretation, as indicated by the moderate inter-rater agreement in our study. The most probable reason may be the fact that GREET is a new instrument, used only in a few cases. Our study, to our best knowledge, was second use: ambiguity in item phrasing and variability in manuscript reporting contributed to inconsistency. The two raters assessing the adherence to the GREET checklist did not have prior experience with this tool, which could have influenced their evaluations. However, we attempted to mitigate this limitation by providing detailed instructions, utilizing the GREET Explanation and Elaboration document, and resolving discrepancies through discussion.

We selected 2018 as the cut-off year for analyzing adherence to the GREET checklist before and after its publication in 2016 for several methodological and practical reasons. While the GREET checklist was introduced in 2016, it takes time for new reporting guidelines to be disseminated, adopted, and consistently implemented in research practices. Given the typical lag between guideline publication and widespread uptake, we allowed for a transition period before assessing changes in adherence. By setting the 2018 cut-off, we aimed to capture studies that had a reasonable opportunity to integrate GREET recommendations into their reporting practices. However, this arbitrary selection does not account for variations in guideline adoption across different research communities, journals, and authors. Some trials published after 2018 may not have been influenced by the GREET checklist, while others published before 2018 may have adhered to similar principles of comprehensive reporting without explicitly referencing the checklist. Fourth, our study included only trials indexed in MEDLINE and Embase, which may have excluded relevant studies published in other databases or grey literature sources.

We did not score GREET item 4 (identification of EBP content) because our review focused on reporting completeness and replicability, whereas item 4 primarily indexes curricular coverage. Given conceptual overlap with other GREET domains (e.g., objectives, materials, procedures), including item 4 could inflate counts without sharpening the evaluation of reporting sufficiency. We acknowledge that this choice limits curricular mapping and recommend future work that specifically taxonomizes EBP content alongside reporting quality.

Of note, our use of GREET reflects the review’s focus on reporting completeness of educational interventions rather than on risk-of-bias appraisal. While frameworks such as WWC [7], CASP [8], and JBI [9] are well-suited to evaluate internal validity for specific study designs (e.g., RCTs), they address a different question from GREET. We did not conduct formal risk-of-bias assessments because we did not pool effects or test causal hypotheses.

Furthermore, in this study, we did not perform additional subgroup analyses for three methodological reasons. First, we had no pre-specified hypotheses to justify effect modification within particular subgroups; post hoc exploration would risk over-interpretation of chance findings. Second, many subgroups were sparsely populated, yielding low statistical power and unstable estimates that would not support reliable conclusions. Third, conducting multiple subgroup comparisons would inflate the familywise Type I error rate, increasing the likelihood of false-positive results.

While the included RCTs report a wide range of parent and child outcomes, our meta-research was designed to assess reporting quality and replicability, not to estimate effectiveness. Quantitative aggregation across trials would require preplanned extraction of effect sizes, risk-of-bias appraisal, and harmonization of non-commensurate outcome measures, which were not part of the present protocol.

Conclusion

The reporting of educational interventions in analyzed RCTs was suboptimal. To enhance study replicability, specific educational materials must be reported in detail or included as supplementary files, to foster evidence-based practice. Some GREET checklist items are challenging to score and would benefit from clearer definitions. Addressing these gaps could improve the efficacy of educational programs for parents and caregivers of children with developmental disabilities and the usability of the GREET checklist.
