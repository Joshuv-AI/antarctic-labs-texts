Impact of PAR2 on Tight Junctions and Esophageal Mucosal Inflammation in Rats with Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease
Authors: Mengdie Du, Luye Wang, Wenqian Duan, Wei Wang, Wenshang Fu, Yongshun Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundGastric content reflux activates protease‐activated receptor 2 (PAR2), which is a critical step in the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD). Claudins serve as the primary constituents of tight junctions (TJs). They are in charge of the barrier and polarity of epithelial cells, and the disruption of their function can lead to chronic inflammation. The present research is designed to investigate whether PAR2 influences TJs in the development of GERD.MethodsGERD models were established both in vivo and in vitro. Rabeprazole and/or GB‐88 (a PAR2 inhibitor) were administered. The expression of PAR2, Claudin‐1, and Claudin‐4, M1 macrophages, transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER), Interleukin‐8 (IL‐8), and Interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β) were measured. Furthermore, cells were treated with SLIGKV‐NH2 (a PAR2 agonist) and GB‐88 to verify the impact of PAR2 on TJs.ResultsRabeprazole improved pathological changes, decreased the concentration of IL‐8 and IL‐1β, and inhibited the accumulation of M1 macrophages in GERD rats. Rabeprazole inhibited the expression of PAR2 and enhanced Claudin‐1 and Claudin‐4 in vivo. GB‐88 declined PAR2 and the concentration of IL‐8 and IL‐1β, and increased Claudin‐1, Claudin‐4, and TEER in vitro. The combination of GB‐88 and rabeprazole was more effective in restoring Claudin‐4.ConclusionIn GERD, elevated PAR2 expression impaired esophageal mucosal barrier function by downregulating and inducing the redistribution of Claudin‐1 and Claudin‐4, while promoting M1 macrophage‐mediated secretion of IL‐8 and IL‐1β.

Introduction

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is a chronic condition caused by the reflux of gastric and duodenal contents into the esophagus. Its typical clinical manifestations include acid regurgitation and heartburn [1]. Globally, the number of GERD cases has shown a rapid upward trend annually [2]. According to prior research [3, 4], the pathogenesis of GERD is related to insufficient pressure of the lower esophageal sphincter, the presence of esophageal hiatal hernia, hypersensitivity of the esophagus, and mucosal injury, among others. Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) remain the first‐line treatment for GERD. Among the PPIs, rabeprazole demonstrates superior advantages, including excellent stability, rapid onset, and pronounced efficacy, which is widely used in the clinical management of GERD [5, 6]. As a known inhibitor of gastric acid secretion [7], rabeprazole functions as an anti‐inflammatory modulator by suppressing signaling pathways such as the NF‐κB pathway, the hypoxia‐inducible factor‐1α (HIF‐1α)/forkhead box protein M1 (FoxM1) pathway, and the toll‐like receptor 4 (TLR4) pathway [8, 9].

The esophageal epithelium acts as a critical protective barrier, preventing the penetration of noxious refluxate and thereby shielding subepithelial nociceptors from exposure [10]. TJs are responsible for partitioning the apical and basal cell areas, thereby creating cell polarity and enabling barrier function. This barrier is upheld between the cells of the esophageal mucosal epithelium through the action of bridging proteins, which consist of Claudins, Occludin, E‐cadherin, etc. Claudins make up the primary cytoskeletal proteins within TJs [11]. Claudin‐1 and Claudin‐4 are considered the main claudins in the esophageal epithelium [12]. Claudin‐1 expression in both the plasma membrane is increased around the erosion of both the spinous and granule layers, which is crucial to prevent paracellular small molecules from spreading through TJs in the mucosa [13, 14]. The presence of Claudin‐4 helps to reduce the paracellular permeability of ions and solutes, thereby maintaining the stability of the internal environment of tissues [15]. Overexpression of Claudin‐4 increases the number of the transepithelial electrical resistance and linker chains [16]. Bile salts and trypsin in duodenal reflux may undermine the barrier function, partly by destroying TJs [17]. Nonetheless, the mechanistic role of claudins in GERD has been scarcely elucidated and thus warrants in‐depth investigation.

Protease‐activated receptor 2 (PAR2) has a central role in producing immune‐mediated esophageal mucosal damage and triggering heartburn [18]. The expression of the PAR2 gene was considerably higher in the mucosa of GERD patients and mice [19, 20]. The activation of PAR2 can induce immune‐mediated inflammatory injury to the esophageal mucosa [21]. PAR2 stands as a plausible mediator of mucosal injury, though the specific mechanism between PAR2 and TJs is unclear.

Abundant macrophage infiltration was observed in the esophageal mucosa of GERD rats [22]. The substantial macrophage accumulation and subsequent release of inflammatory mediators constitute key factors exacerbating GERD symptoms. Early intervention to reduce cytokine production and activity may effectively delay squamous epithelial hyperplasia and Barrett's metaplasia [23]. Under stimulation by pro‐inflammatory type 1 helper T cell (Th1) cytokines and microbial factors, macrophages adopt the M1 phenotype characterized by pro‐inflammatory properties, Th1 immune responses, and anti‐tumor capabilities. However, whether M1 macrophage polarization contributes to GERD pathogenesis remains undetermined.

Materials and Methods

Establishment of GERD Rat

Eight‐week‐old male SD rats were obtained from Shanghai Slack Laboratory. All animal experiments were strictly performed in accordance with the project license (No. 2022017). All procedures complied with the ARRIVE guidelines. A total of 19 rats were randomly allocated into two cohorts: the sham group (n = 5) and the model group (n = 14). The sham group underwent a sham surgical procedure, while the model group received cardia myotomy combined with pyloric ligation. Unfortunately, four rats in the model group succumbed postoperatively. Subsequently, the remaining 10 rats in the model group were further randomized into the GERD group (n = 5) and the rabeprazole group (n = 5). Following surgery, the sham group was administered ddH2O via daily gavage, whereas the rabeprazole group received rabeprazole at a dose of 2.057 mg/kg by gavage for 14 consecutive days. After the 2‐week gavage period, the lower esophageal tissue (1.5 cm proximal to the esophagogastric junction) was obtained and longitudinally sectioned. A portion of the esophageal tissue was immediately preserved in liquid nitrogen for Western blotting (WB) analysis and enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA), and the remaining tissue was fixed in 4% formaldehyde for subsequent assays.

Cell Culture and Treatment

Human esophageal epithelial cells (HEECs) were obtained from Shanghai Yuanchuang Biology Co. Ltd. Trypsin was employed to establish a GERD model in HEECs. Specifically, HEECs were seeded into six‐well plates, and following overnight serum starvation, SLIGKV‐NH₂ (100 μM, HY‐P0283, MCE, USA) or GB‐88 (100 μM, HY‐120261, MCE, USA) was added 2 h. Cells were incubated for 6 h in trypsin‐supplemented medium (0.65 nM, pH = 6). Additionally, HEECs were subjected to treatment with 10% rabeprazole‐containing serum or control serum for a 24‐h duration.

TEER

HEECs (1 × 104 cells/insert) were seeded in Transwell inserts (apical side) with a pore size of 0.4 μm (TCS016024, JET, China). The cell medium was refreshed every 2 days. The cells were cultured for 7 days. TEER was quantified via a Millicell ERS‐2 system (Millipore, USA). Care should be taken to prevent the chopstick electrode from touching cells in the insert chamber. TEER was calculated by the following equation: TEERΩ/cm2=Rsample–Rblank×0.33cm2.

Histopathological Examination (HE)

Esophageal tissues were initially fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 36 h. The process of dehydration was carried out using graded ethanol. The tissues were immersed in xylene to eliminate ethanol. Once the tissues became transparent, they were embedded in paraffin. When it became tissue sections, they were first dewaxed in xylene. Hydration was performed with gradient ethanol. These sections were stained with hematoxylin–eosin. Following staining, the sections were re‐dehydrated using gradient ethanol and rendered transparent in xylene. They were then mounted with neutral gum, after which pathological changes were examined under a light microscope. Mucosal injury in slides was scored according to standardized damage score [24], which graded the degree of epithelial damage, submucosal edema, and submucosal inflammatory cells.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. For antigen retrieval, 0.01 M citric acid buffer was used. The sections were blocked with normal goat serum for 30 min. Primary and secondary antibodies were added to the sections successively, and incubation was carried out. Hematoxylin was then used for nuclear staining. The sections were mounted with neutral gum. The outcomes were examined under a microscope.

The IHC staining intensity was quantified using ImageJ software. The esophageal epithelial layer was strictly defined as the region of interest (ROI). The mean gray value within the ROI was measured and subsequently converted into optical density (OD) using the standard formula: OD = log10 (255/mean gray value). The OD value represented the relative protein expression levels.

TdT‐Mediated dUTP Nick‐End Labeling (TUNEL)

Paraffin‐embedded rat esophageal sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and permeabilized with Proteinase K for 20 min at 37°C. The sections were then incubated with the TUNEL reaction mixture (C1091, Beyotime, China), followed by Streptavidin‐HRP conjugation. Signals were visualized using a DAB substrate, and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin.

WB

Saline was injected beneath the mucosa of the esophageal tissue. The tissue scheduled for stripping was elevated and detached from the muscularis propria. With ophthalmic scissors, the epithelium was meticulously dissected from the muscularis propria and gathered for WB. The esophageal epithelium tissue or HEECs were lysed using radio immunoprecipitation assay buffer, and the supernatant was collected. Protein concentrations were quantified using Bicinchoninic Acid Kits. Subsequently, samples were separated via 10% or 12.5% denaturing polyacrylamide gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. After the blocking stage, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies specific to Claudin‐1 (1:500, ab180953, Abcam, USA), Claudin‐4 (1:1000, ab15098, Abcam, USA), PAR2 (1:1000, ab53156, Abcam, USA), and GAPDH (1:5000, D110016, sangon biotech, China). The next day, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibody (1:5000, A0208, Beyotime, China). Eventually, protein quantitative analysis was carried out with ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

The paraffin‐embedded sections were pretreated as previously described. After removing distilled water, mixed primary antibodies against cluster of differentiation 86 (CD86) (1:200, sc‐28347, Santa Cruz, USA) and cluster of differentiation 206 (CD206) (1:500, 18704‐1‐AP, Proteintech, USA) were applied and incubated at 37°C for 20 min. Following antibody removal and washing, secondary antibodies were added for 1 h incubation at room temperature. After repeated washing, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 10 min at room temperature. The slides were treated with autofluorescence quencher for 5 min, followed by a 10 min running water rinse. Conventional dehydration and clearing procedures were performed before mounting with neutral balsam. Microscopic examination was conducted for image acquisition and analysis.

ELISA

Samples and horseradish peroxidase were added to the enzyme‐labeled plates, followed by incubation at 36°C for 60 min. Chromogenic substrate was introduced to the plates, which were then placed in the dark at 37°C for 15 min. The reaction was stopped with a terminating solution. Finally, the OD was measured at 450 nm, and the concentrations of the samples were calculated. In this experimental process, Rat Interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β) ELISA Kit (ab100768, Abcam, USA), Human IL‐1β ELISA Kit (ab214025, Abcam, USA), Rat Interleukin‐8 (IL‐8) ELISA Kit (YJ32419, mlbio, China), and Human IL‐8 ELISA Kit (YJ336521, mlbio, China) were used.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 24.0 (IBM, USA) was employed to analyze the results. All the data were presented as means ± standard deviation (SD). Data analysis was conducted using one‐way ANOVA and Tukey's post hoc test for parametric data. p‐value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Rabeprazole was Effective in Improving Pathological Changes in the Esophagus of GERD

To explore the pathogenesis of GERD, the model was established through cardia myotomy combined with hemi‐pyloric ligation. The anesthetized rats were dissected along the midline of the abdomen to expose the lower esophagus and pylorus. From the esophagus‐gastric junction, a blunt dissection was performed approximately 1 cm upward. At the same time, the pylorus was hemi‐ligated with 4‐0 nylon thread (Figure 1A). We performed the procedure (Figure 1B) and finally obtained esophageal tissue on the 28th day. In the sham group, the mucosa of the lower esophageal tissue of rats was intact and the basal layer was flat (Figure 1C). The squamous epithelium was hyperplastic, the papillae of the lamina propria were prolonged and exceeded 3/4 of the mucosal layer with lymphocyte infiltration in GERD rats (Figure 1C). After rabeprazole administration, these pathological changes were notably ameliorated. Only a small infiltration of inflammatory cells was observed (Figure 1C). The histological damage score in the GERD group was 3.00 ± 0.71 more than that in the rabeprazole group (p < 0.001, Figure 1D). Rabeprazole is effective in improving pathological changes in GERD. In addition, the extent of apoptosis and proliferation was assessed using TUNEL and Ki67 staining, respectively. The percentage of Ki67 positive cells (p < 0.05, Figure 1E) and TUNEL positive cells (p < 0.05, Figure 1F) increased in GERD esophageal tissues compared to the sham group. The accumulation of TUNEL positive cells was alleviated with the treatment of rabeprazole (Figure 1F).

Claudin‐1 and Claudin‐4 were Decreased and PAR2 and M1‐Type Macrophages were Enhanced in GERD Rats

It is reported that PAR2 may be involved in the pathogenesis of GERD [25, 26]. In the esophageal lumen, the activation of PAR2 has been triggered by repeated weak acid or serine in refluxed material [27]. PAR2 mediated the inflammatory response. We further explored the role of PAR2 on TJs and inflammatory factors in GERD. The IHC staining images showed that OD of PAR2 was increased significantly in the GERD rats compared with the sham group (p < 0.01, Figure 2A). Meanwhile, increased cytoplasmic staining of PAR2 was observed (Figure 2A). Claudin‐1 and Claudin‐4 are the most abundantly expressed and prevent the diffusion of paracellular small molecules through TJs in the mucosa [28]. Claudin‐1 and Claudin‐4 were typically localized on the cell membrane in the sham group (Figure 2B,C). It was observed that the increased cytoplasmic staining and decreased membrane staining of Claudin‐1 and Claudin‐4 in the GERD group. WB demonstrated that the expression of PAR2 in the GERD rats was approximately twice as much as the sham group (Figure 2E). Compared with the sham group, Claudin‐1 (p < 0.001) and Claudin‐4 (p < 0.05) in the GERD group declined substantially (Figure 2E), which were consistent with IHC. The incorrect localization and decreased Claudin‐1 and Claudin‐4 are indicative of a structural breakdown in the TJs. The level of IL‐8 of the rat esophageal tissue in the GERD group was increased to 2093.96 ± 684.08 pg/mL compared to 591.96 ± 108.31 pg/mL in the sham group (Figure 2F). Compared with the GERD group, the level of IL‐8 in the rabeprazole group decreased significantly (p < 0.05). The concentration of IL‐1β in the GERD rat was roughly 2.02 times as much as it in the sham rat. And with the treatment of rabeprazole, the concentration of IL‐1β declined (p < 0.01). In addition, IF staining was performed to assess macrophage alterations in rat esophageal tissues. IF analysis revealed a significant increase in CD86‐positive cells in the GERD group compared with the sham group (p < 0.05, Figure 3B), indicating enhanced M1 macrophage infiltration. Rabeprazole treatment effectively attenuated this accumulation. The proportion of CD206‐positive cells in GERD rats was remarkably reduced to 2.52% ± 0.64% compared to the sham group, while treatment with rabeprazole increased to 21.78% ± 5.59% compared to the GERD rats (Figure 3C).

GB‐88 Decreased Claudin‐1 and Claudin‐4 In Vitro

It was reported that PAR2 downregulates the expression of TJs in some chronic inflammatory diseases, such as irritable bowel syndrome [29, 30, 31, 32]. To explore whether PAR2 regulates Claudin‐1 and Claudin‐4 in GERD, we added SLIGKV‐NH2 (a PAR2 agonist) and GB‐88 (a PAR2 inhibitor) to the cell culture system. The expression of PAR2 in the trypsin group was roughly twice as much as in the GB‐88 group (Figure 4A). GB‐88 was effective in inhibiting PAR2. In addition, Claudin‐1 in the GB‐88 group enhanced to 1.01 ± 0.06 compared to 0.59 ± 0.10 in the trypsin group (Figure 4A). HEECs pretreated with GB‐88 expressed more Claudin‐4 than the trypsin group (Figure 4A). IL‐1β in the GB‐88 group declined to 55.49 ± 23.17 pg/mL compared to 107.97 ± 10.26 pg/mL in the trypsin group (Figure 4B). ELISA indicated that IL‐8 in the GB‐88 group decreased significantly compared with the trypsin group (p < 0.01, Figure 4B).

The Combination of GB‐88 and Rabeprazole Inhibited Claudin‐4

To explore whether the combination of GB‐88 and rabeprazole was more effective, we categorized the cellular experiments into control group, GB‐88 group, rabeprazole group and drug combination group. It was indicated that the expression of PAR2 in the drug combination group was less than that of the GB‐88 group (p < 0.05, Figure 5A,B). There was no statistical difference between the rabeprazole group and the drug combination group in terms of the expression of Claudin‐1 (Figure 5A,B). The drug combination group expressed more Claudin‐4 compared to the GB‐88 group and the rabeprazole group (p < 0.05, Figure 5B). IL‐1β in the drug combination group declined to 46.04 ± 20.87 pg/mL compared to 70.33 ± 2.66 pg/mL in the GB‐88 group (Figure 5C). In addition, it was indicated that the concentration of IL‐8 in the rabeprazole (53.08 ± 30.40 pg/mL) group was more than that in the drug combination group (34.05 ± 13.88 pg/mL; Figure 5C). To evaluate the essential role of PAR2 in barrier function, TEER measurement was performed. GB‐88 group, the rabeprazole group and the drug combination group all exhibited significantly higher TEER than trypsin group (p < 0.001, Figure 5D). The combination therapy proved most effective in restoring TEER (96.80% ± 1.74%), whereas the GB‐88 group and the rabeprazole group reached 89.13% ± 2.30% and 89.83% ± 1.02% respectively (Figure 5D).

Discussion

GERD, a common digestive disorder, has a high prevalence in Western countries and is responsible for more than 50% of the direct health care costs related to digestive disorders in these regions [33]. Advanced GERD can potentially lead to dysphagia, hemorrhaging, and esophageal adenocarcinoma [34]. Reflux depends on an imbalance between aggressive and defensive forces. The esophageal mucosal barrier is an important part of the defensive forces. Despite the fact that 60% of GERD patients showed no endoscopically detectable erosion, there was a mucosal barrier defect, which may contribute to the pathogenesis of the symptoms [35]. Barrier dysfunction may manifest as dilated intercellular spaces (DIS), facilitating enhanced penetration of noxious stimuli to submucosal esophageal sensory neurons. Even physiological reflux loads can thereby trigger symptoms such as regurgitation and heartburn [36]. Esophageal mucosal barrier impairment represents one of the key pathophysiological factors in GERD development.

In the current investigation, the activation of PAR2 downregulated the expression of Claudin‐1 and Claudin‐4, while upregulating the secretion of IL‐8 and IL‐1β and promoting the accumulation of M1 macrophages. The combination of GB‐88 and rabeprazole may realize greater value in GERD through enhancing Claudin‐4. Therefore, we unveiled a previously unidentified mechanism linking PAR2 to esophageal barrier dysfunction, offering potential new therapeutic targets for the clinical management of GERD.

Protease‐activated receptors (PARs) are G‐protein‐coupled receptors. PAR2, a member of the PAR family, is expressed throughout the gastrointestinal tract. PAR2 is activated via N‐terminal truncation mediated by extracellular serine proteases, such as tryptase, kinin‐releasing enzyme, and coagulation factor VIIa. Upon cleavage, the newly exposed N‐terminal ligand binds to the receptor itself, thereby initiating a downstream signaling pathway. The findings in our experiments indicated trypsin activated PAR2 and released a large amount of IL‐8 and IL‐1β. PAR2 is upregulated in the esophagus of patients with GERD. Moreover, PAR2 is associated with histomorphological changes and the severity of symptoms [25]. It has been revealed that PAR2 activated the extracellular signal‐regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2) pathway and affected the cytoskeleton or promoted TJs phosphorylation to regulate epithelial permeability [37]. In addition, PAR2 activation induces M1 polarization and inflammation through the forkhead box O1 (FOXO1)‐dependent pathway leading to the upregulation of IL‐1β, IL‐6, monocyte chemotactic protein‐1 (MCP‐1), and tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α) [38]. This finding aligns with our results, suggesting that PAR2 may be involved in M1 macrophage polarization.

The levels of IL‐6, IL‐1β, and IL‐8 in GERD rats were significantly increased, and the impairment of the esophageal barrier was noticed [39]. Upon impairment of the esophageal epithelial barrier functions, squamous cells and sensory nerves are repeatedly exposed to the harmful refluxate, leading to the occurrence of symptoms. With the passage of time, the lamina propria and epithelium get thicker, basal cells experience hyperplasia, and papillae extend [40]. Esophageal squamous epithelial cells, upon exposure to acid and bile salts, secrete IL‐8 and IL‐1β, which serve to recruit neutrophils and macrophages [41]. The release of inflammatory cytokines by macrophages further destabilizes the epithelial barrier. And neutrophils secrete serine endopeptidase, which might play a part in activating PAR2 and further impairing the integrity of the epithelium [18]. Furthermore, IL‐1β independently modulates neurogenic esophageal muscle contractions, thereby elevating the likelihood of mucosal exposure and exacerbating inflammation [42]. In the present study, downregulation of PAR2 expression conferred protection to the esophageal mucosal barrier, with the combined administration of PAR2 inhibitors and rabeprazole yielding superior efficacy.

Under physiological conditions, the non‐keratinized stratified squamous epithelium of the esophagus constitutes a robust barrier that restricts ion diffusion, a function pivotal for safeguarding against reflux [43]. Esophagogastric junction contractile integral (EGJ‐CI) and mucosal integrity testing (MIT) are indicators of the barrier function. The cut‐off value of 44.16 in EGJ‐CI is used to predict pathological acid exposure, with a sensitivity of 46% and specificity of 42%; the sensitivity of MIT in identifying GERD was 76% and its specificity was 72% [44]. Perhaps, in the near future, the indexes related to esophageal barrier function will be widely applied in clinical practice. It has been suggested that the inhibition of gastric acid secretion may not be sufficient for symptomatic relief in GERD patients. PPIs‐refractory GERD accounts for approximately 30% of GERD. The impairment of the mucosal barrier function is one of its pathogenesis mechanisms [45]. The mucosal barrier offers intriguing therapeutic potential [46]. Rabeprazole can activate the Aryl hydrocarbon Receptor, which subsequently increases TEER and Claudin‐1, Claudin‐2, and Occludin [47]. Meanwhile, it modulates intestinal microbiota, thereby improving mucosal barrier function [48]. Compared with rabeprazole monotherapy, the combination of GB‐88 and rabeprazole significantly enhanced Claudin‐4 expression, whereas it had minimal impact on Claudin‐1. This may be related to the differences in the amino acid sequences of the C‐terminal domains. Claudin‐1 has a stronger interaction with scaffolding proteins (such as zonula occludens‐1, occludens‐2, occludens‐3), which makes its localization in TJs more stable [49]. Claudin‐1 is expressed in the basal and spinous layers, whereas Claudin‐4 exhibits more restricted localization, being exclusively detected in superficial epithelial cells. Claudin‐1 primarily maintains the high‐resistance barrier that protects mucosa against external insults, while Claudin‐4 preferentially regulates ion‐selective permeability to preserve electrolyte homeostasis [50, 51].

We acknowledge that the rat esophagus is keratinized, whereas the human esophagus is non‐keratinized. Thus, this model may not perfectly mirror human pathophysiology. In the future, experiments could be performed using 3D organotypic and organoid models to more accurately recapitulate the complexity of the human esophageal mucosa. Secondly, investigations utilizing PAR2‐knockout rats or the administration of GB‐88 in GERD rats are required to confirm that the changes in mucosal barrier and macrophages are directly mediated by PAR2 activation in vivo.

Conclusion

In GERD, the elevated expression of PAR2 led to a decrease in the expression and redistribution of Claudin‐1 and Claudin‐4. At the same time, it boosted M1 macrophage accumulation and the secretion of IL‐8 and IL‐1β. These alterations impaired the barrier function of the esophageal mucosa. Furthermore, the combination of GB‐88 and rabeprazole was more effective in restoring Claudin‐4.
