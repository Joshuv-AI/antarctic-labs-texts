Reply to: “NOTCH1: A Potential New Biomarker in the Era of Immunotherapy?” and “Caution in Interpreting NOTCH1 Mutation as a Predictive Biomarker of Tislelizumab Response in Esophageal Squamous Cell Carcinoma”
Authors: Zhihao Lu, Wenting Du, Xi Jiao, Yanni Wang, Zhang Zhang, Lin Shen
Published: 2025

We appreciate the interest of Aydemir et al1 and Lu et al2 in our manuscript reporting the biomarker analysis results from the RATIONALE-302 study,3 which identified NOTCH1 mutation (MUT) as a potential predictive biomarker for longer overall survival (OS) with tislelizumab versus chemotherapy and demonstrated that the presence of tumor-intrinsic NOTCH1 alterations facilitated a more immunologically activated tumor microenvironment in both patient samples and murine models. While acknowledging the merits of the study, the authors had several questions.

Aydemir et al raised concerns about prior radiotherapy treatment and the tissues collected in RATIONALE-302. To clarify, 86% of the tissues that were used to determine NOTCH1 status in the mutation profiling biomarker evaluable population (mutation BEP) were archival tissues and 47% were collected before radiotherapy. The percentages of archival tissues were balanced between NOTCH1 MUT versus wild-type (WT) subgroups (archival tissue WT, 85%; MUT, 89%; tissue collected before radiotherapy WT, 47%; MUT, 46%). We also investigated the percentages of patients between NOTCH1 MUT versus WT subgroups who experienced prior radiotherapy and found no difference (radiotherapy: WT, 64%; MUT, 63%). We therefore consider the risk of NOTCH1 mutations being affected by previous treatments to be low.

Lu et al had multiple questions. First, their statement regarding nonrandom tissue loss requires clarification. Tumor tissue samples obtained from patients in the RATIONALE-302 study were processed into 10 slides and tested sequentially for PD-L1 immunohistochemistry (3-9 slides, depending on the test repetition times), gene expression profiling (2-3 slides), and MUT profiling (2-4 slides). Consequently, many samples were exhausted after the first two assays, with only 48% of patients having sufficient tissue samples (slides) remaining for MUT profiling. A total of 41% of patients had evaluable MUT results, representing a success rate of 85% the data. Additionally, Table 1 in the manuscript shows that baseline characteristics and clinical efficacy were comparable across the intention-to-treat, PD-L1 BEP, gene expression profile (GEP) BEP, and mutation BEP populations, suggesting that patients with MUT data represented a random subset of the intention-to-treat population. We also performed principal component analysis on the GEPs. Samples with and without MUT data are evenly distributed, with no discernible clustering pattern. On the basis of the above points, we consider the missing tissue to be missing at random.

Lu et al also mentioned that tumors with loss-of-function NOTCH1 mutations had higher tumor mutational burden (TMB) and type-I-interferon signaling than NOTCH1-WT tumors and recommended a detailed baseline comparison of NOTCH1 MUT versus WT subgroups and a multivariable Cox model analysis. We investigated the clinicopathologic factors between NOTCH1 MUT and WT subgroups (Table 1). All clinicopathologic factors were balanced except age.

Additionally, the Data Supplement (Fig 2D) shows that there was no difference in the continuous PD-L1 tumor area positivity score between NOTCH1 MUT and WT subgroups (P = .54) and (Fig 2A) a higher TMB score in the NOTCH1 MUT subgroup compared with the WT subgroup (P = .0005). As mentioned in the Data Supplement Methods, TMB was calculated using an algorithm optimized for the assay.4 Therefore, for the multivariable analysis, we built a Cox model that includes age and continuous TMB as additional covariates, specified as follows:

Surv(OS,CENSOR) ∼ NOTCH1 + TREATMENT + NOTCH1*TREATMENT + AGE + TMB

After including age and TMB, the two-sided interaction P increased to .0458, compared with the original .0372, but it remains below the .05 threshold, thus continuing to support our initial conclusion.

Our study provides additional support for the predictive role of NOTCH1 mutations that was recapitulated in murine esophageal squamous cell carcinoma models (Fig 5A) and the Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center data set comprising mixed tumor types treated with PD-(L)1 monotherapies (Fig 2D). Finally, we acknowledge in the Discussion that the association between NOTCH1 mutations and improved clinical outcomes requires prospective validation in a randomized, controlled trial.
