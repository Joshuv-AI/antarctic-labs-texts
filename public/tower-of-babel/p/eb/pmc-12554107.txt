Six years of a clinical communication intervention in shared decision-making to promote documentation of goals of care for critically ill patients with a life-limiting illness☆
Authors: Georgia Peters, Sharyn Milnes, Nicholas Simpson, Olivia Gedye, Nima Kakho, Charlie Corke, Michael Bailey, Neil R Orford
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDescribe the association between the implementation of a shared decision-making (SDM) program and documentation of goals of care for critically ill patients with life-limiting illness (LLI).MethodsA prospective longitudinal cohort study was conducted from 1st January 2015 to 30th September 2020 in an Australian tertiary teaching hospital. Adult patients with LLI admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) were included. A SDM program consisting of communication training, a new goals of care form, and clinical support was implemented. The primary outcome was the proportion of patients with a documented SDM discussion. Secondary outcomes included patient treatment preferences and hospital utilisation parameters.ResultsA total of 1178 patients with LLI were admitted to the ICU during the study period and included in the study. Following the introduction of an SDM program, the proportion of patients with a documented SDM discussion increased from 22 % at baseline to a peak of 68 % at year five, then 60 % in year six of the study (adjusted odds ratio: 1.49, 95 % confidence interval: 1.38–1.60; p < 0.0001). Patients who had documented SDM were more likely to be older, female, frail, and have a prior advance care plan. SDM discussions resulted in higher rates of documented deterioration treatment preference plan (p < 0.0001), an increased ICU length of stay (3 vs. 2 days, p < 0.0001), referrals to palliative care services (p = 0.002), and a higher mortality rate. Time to death was significantly shorter in decedents with documented SDM compared to those without it (12 vs. 49 days, p < 0.0001).ConclusionThe implementation of a comprehensive clinical communication training program was associated with increased documentation of shared decision-making discussions for patients in ICU with LLI, which corresponded with changes in patient treatment preferences and healthcare utilisation by decedents. Further research is required to understand the impact of these conversations from the perspective of patients and their families.Keywords: Advance care planning, Advance directives, Communication, End-of-life, Patient-centred care, Person-centred care, Shared decision-making, Iintensive care

Key Messages

What is already known on this topic: Critically ill patients with life-limiting illness have a high likelihood of deteriorating in hospital and dying in the subsequent year.

What this study adds: A shared decision-making program consisting of communication training and longitudinal clinical support is associated with improved documentation of value-based goals of care discussions and treatment preference plans for patients with life-limiting illnesses.

How this study might affect research, practice, or policy: Because hospital utilisation did not significantly change when these discussions were held in intensive care, there may be a role to encourage these conversations in the ward setting before deterioration occurs. As time to death was markedly shorter in those who had shared decision-making conversations, there may be reason to trigger a referral to palliative care services for those with life-limiting illness who choose ward-level care or palliative care treatment pathways.

Introduction

Patients with life-limiting illness (LLI) admitted to acute hospitals are at high risk of deterioration and admission to intensive care.[1], [2], [3], [4] However, as these patients are also at increased risk of dying within the following year,5 it is important to ensure the care they receive during this time is consistent with their treatment preferences.

Shared decision-making (SDM) is one method of aligning healthcare interventions with patient goals, values, and preferences.6 Shared decision-making is considered adequate if it incorporates patient voice, concordant medical opinion, and a consensus decision for ongoing treatment. The patient must have the capacity to engage in this process, otherwise a surrogate decision-maker should be nominated. Shared decision-making aims to prioritise person-centred care, improve patient and family satisfaction with communication, and reduce the provision of non-beneficial care;7 it is a key component of the Australian National Safety and Quality Health Service Standards.8

However, SDM is not routinely practiced in Australian hospitals.9 The reasons for this include inadequate communication training,10 clinician discomfort with difficult conversations,11 perceived lack of time to have the conversation properly, and a lack of evidence describing the relationship between SDM and meaningful outcomes.6,12,13

iValidate (Identifying Values, Listening, and advising high-risk patients in acute care) is a communication skills and clinical support program for healthcare workers. It was designed to improve the rate of SDM discussions in hospitalised patients with LLI and has been implemented in a tertiary healthcare institution since 2015.14 This study aimed to describe the association of implementing this program on the prevalence of documented shared decision-making discussions and the clinical outcomes for patients with LLI who were admitted to a tertiary intensive care unit (ICU).

Methods

Population and setting

We performed a prospective longitudinal study in a tertiary regional hospital in South-West Victoria, Australia. Ethics approval from the Barwon Health Human Research Ethics Committee was obtained prior to commencement of the study.

Participants included in the study were adult patients with an LLI admitted to the ICU during the period from 1 January 2015 to 30 September 2020. Patients were identified through prospective daily auditing of the ICU electronic admission database by research coordinators. Patients with multiple hospital admissions only had the index admission included.

LLI indicators were adapted from the UK Gold Standards Framework prognostic indicators and the Supportive and Palliative Care Indicators Tool,15 and have been validated in our hospital population.5 In addition, patients were classified into illness trajectory groups of cancer, organ system failure, or frailty/dementia.16 Full details of the indicators and trajectories are provided in the Supporting Information (Supporting Table 1, Table 2 and Fig. 1).

Intervention

The iValidate program14 was designed to improve person-centred care for patients with LLI by offering clinical communication skills training and clinical support for healthcare workers. Full details of the training program are provided in the Supporting Text.

Part of the clinical support program involved updating the institutional Goals of Care (GoC) form from tick-box options to a combined tick-box and text-based document, which reinforced the steps of SDM (Supporting Fig. 2). The form prompts clinicians to identify and document the presence of a LLI, patient capacity and medical treatment decision-maker, and existing advance care directives. The open-text sections are used to describe and document patient values, goals and preferences, outline medical advice regarding appropriate treatment options, and document a consensus statement. Finally, a treatment preference plan in the event of clinical deterioration is recorded as a level of care concordant with this discussion.

From 2016, all ICU registrars completed the iValidate program as mandatory training at the commencement of their ICU term. In 2017, the GoC form was added to the ICU electronic record with an alert system that flagged patients with LLI and GoC status. Additional daily reminders of GoC status and clinical nudges were provided at multidisciplinary meetings and in clinical areas by iValidate champions.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the proportion of patients with a documented SDM discussion. This was defined as a completed GoC form that included patients’ values, goals and preferences, medical advice, a consensus decision, and a deterioration treatment plan.

The treatment preference plan was the decision recorded on the institutional GoC form. This was the maximal level of care to be implemented and was defined as intensive care (full ICU treatment including mechanical ventilation and advanced organ supports); high-dependency unit care (HDU: excludes cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and invasive ventilation but may include noninvasive ventilation, inotropes and/or vasopressors, or renal replacement therapy); ward-based care (active management with disease-specific therapies including antibiotics, supplemental oxygen, blood and component transfusion, and nutrition support); palliative care (comfort measures only); or not defined.

Secondary outcomes included the rate of completion of each component of the SDM process, the deterioration treatment preference plan, and clinical interventions and outcomes. Clinical parameters included ICU admission rate and interventions, hospital length of stay, hospital mortality, discharge destination, and 90-day outcomes including readmission rate, mortality, and days spent in hospital. Analysis of outcomes for the subgroup of patients with LLI who died by day 90 was also performed.

Data collection

Data collected included demographics, clinical information, admission diagnosis, referral source, GoC documentation, hospital outcome, and 90-day hospital readmission days and outcomes.

A pre-existing advance care plan (ACP) was defined as one that had been completed in the primary care setting or documentation of treatment limitations during a previous hospital admission. Assessment of documentation was performed by research coordinators and reviewed by two investigators (SM/NO).

Ninety-day outcomes (survival, unplanned readmission, and total days in hospital) were determined through examination of the hospital electronic database, which links to community-based services. The status of patients at day 90 was determined through identification of date of death or date of last healthcare system contact with the patient.

Statistical analysis

All data were initially assessed for normality. Group comparisons were performed using Chi-square tests for equal proportion, analysis of variance for continuously normally distributed data and Kruskal–Wallis tests otherwise, with results reported as frequency (%), mean (standard deviation), and median (interquartile range), respectively. Binomial outcomes were analysed using logistic regression, firstly treating study periods as categorical variables and then secondly as a continuous variable to assess a trend over time. Changes over time for continuous outcomes were determined using quantile regression. Multivariable sensitivity analysis was conducted by adjusting for age, gender, and illness trajectory. All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and a two-sided p-value of 0.05 was used to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

A total of 1178 patients with LLI were admitted to the ICU during the study period. Demographics, admission diagnosis, referral source, LLI trajectory and pre-hospital ACP are presented for patients overall and by study period in Table 1. Ninety percent of participants lived at home prior to admission. Frailty was the most common LLI trajectory, followed by organ failure and cancer. While there was variation in the distribution of demographics and LLI criteria over the course of the study, there was no sustained change from start to end, with the exception of a decrease in the proportion of patients admitted to ICU from a nursing home.

Documentation of SDM

The rate of SDM documentation increased throughout the study (Table 2). At baseline, only 22 % of patients with LLI had documented SDM discussions, which increased to a peak of 68 % at year five. The increase over time was statistically significant after logistic regression analysis (odds ratio for annual increase: 1.49; 95 % confidence interval (CI): 1.38–1.6; p < 0.0001).

Individual components of the SDM process are provided in Table 2. Documentation of each component—competence assessment, surrogate decision-maker identification, patient values, medical advice, and consensus—increased significantly from baseline as the intervention intensity increased. These findings remained significant after multivariable analysis for age, gender, and LLI trajectory.

Treatment preference in the event of deterioration

Identification of patient treatment preferences in the event of deterioration increased throughout the study, shown as a reduction of the ‘unspecified’ category from approximately 32 %–21 % (p = 0.006). There were no significant changes in documented preference for ICU, HDU, ward-based, or palliative care overall (Table 2).

Association between SDM and outcomes

Over the study period, there was a reduction in the proportion of patients with LLI who received mechanical ventilation (adjusted odds ratio for annual decline: 0.91 (0.85–0.99)), but no significant change in the use of renal replacement therapy, vasopressor support, or ICU length of stay (Table 3). There was no change in hospital length of stay or 90-day readmission rate for survivors. However, in decedents, the median hospital length of stay increased from 5 to 7 days, (adjusted annual change: 1.54, CI: -0.03 to 3.10, p = 0.05), and the number of days in hospital within 90 days of the index admission increased from 5 to 10 days (adjusted annual change: 1.61, CI: -0.01 to 3.23, p = 0.05).

The presence of documented SDM was not associated with any difference in the proportion of patients who requested intensive care treatment in the event of clinical deterioration. However, in patients with documented SDM, there was a redistribution of the preferred deterioration plan away from ‘unidentified’, to requesting HDU, ward-based or palliative care (p < 0.0001) (Table 4). Discussion

This study describes a significant and sustained improvement in the documentation of shared decision-making for patients with LLI admitted to ICU following the implementation of a comprehensive shared decision-making program.

Relationship to previous studies

Numerous strategies to improve SDM for patients with serious illness have been trialled with varying success. Previous research shows ‘low-touch’ interventions, such as providing clinicians with a one-page patient-specific summary to prompt discussion, were associated with an increase in documented GoC from 30 % to 35 %.17 Our study showed a sustained increase in documented SDM for over 60 % of high-risk patients—the highest rate of documented SDM described in an intensive care setting—following introduction of a longitudinal ‘high-touch’ intervention.

There are several possible reasons for this effect. First, iValidate teaches clinicians to recognise LLI using simple criteria that can be applied at the bedside. Second, multi-day clinical communication training with simulated patients is associated with improved skill acquisition.18 Third, nudges and feedback in the clinical environment encouraged longitudinal engagement in SDM by clinicians. Finally, introducing an electronic GoC form enhanced compliance with the complete SDM process.

Clinical factors associated with shared decision-making

The association of SDM with age, frailty, and non-cancer illness trajectory may reflect improved recognition of LLI as a result of the iValidate program, or increased recognition from different ward teams. The relationship between female sex and SDM is unexpected, and requires further examination.

Patients arriving from theatre were less likely to have documented GoC. This may be due to the barriers outlined in the Introduction or due to the perception that preadmission clinic may not be the appropriate setting for a GoC discussion. Strategies to identify high-risk elective surgical patients and flag them for GoC discussions have been tested,[19], [20], [21] and could be incorporated in our setting.

Patient-preferred deterioration plan

A major component of SDM in the context of patients with LLI is discussing the care to be offered in the event of deterioration and ensuring that it is aligned with the patient’s goals, values, and preferences. In this study, there was a clear difference in the preferred deterioration plan recorded for patients with SDM compared to those without it. In patients without SDM, almost half had no deterioration plan. In patients with SDM, this decreased to zero, with a corresponding increase in patients with palliative, ward, or HDU-level care as their preferred plan, and no difference in the proportion of patients wanting ICU-level care. This is important because patients without a deterioration plan are at risk of defaulting to ICU care when a sudden deterioration occurs, exposing them to potentially non-beneficial or unwanted intervention. Discussions that inform patients of their prognosis and educate them about the nature and consequences of interventions improve the perceived quality of communication,22,23 and may empower patients to choose less invasive treatments and prioritise quality of life over quantity if this is concordant with their wishes.

Healthcare utilisation and mortality outcomes

The SDM cohort had a longer ICU admission, had no difference in hospital length of stay, were more likely to be referred to palliative care services, had a higher mortality rate at all time points, and a shorter time to death than the non-SDM cohort. The reasons for these differences are unable to be clearly identified in a retrospective, longitudinal study design, but can be considered.

The SDM and non-SDM cohorts were demographically and clinically different, with older patients in the SDM cohort who were more likely to be female, more likely to have a prior ACP, less likely to be perioperative, and more likely to have frailty as an LLI. These groups have different survival trajectories, irrespective of SDM.

The increase in mortality and shorter time to death may be a result of demographic and clinical factors, selection bias, or active decision-making in patients engaged in SDM. Selection bias for SDM may occur if patients with advanced LLI or severe physiological derangement are more easily recognised by clinical staff; therefore, they are more likely to be flagged for SDM discussions. Alternatively, prior documentation of patient wishes may reduce uncertainty for patients, families, and healthcare workers when deteriorations occur and thus assist with transition to end-of-life care.

The lack of impact of SDM on hospital utilisation may reflect differences in patient cohort or an issue in timing of SDM. We have previously reported a significant decrease in 90-day hospital readmission when the same intervention was delivered to patients with LLI admitted to general medical wards.24 This raises the possibility that SDM is more effective at changing healthcare utilisation if it commences on the ward prior to deterioration.

Finally, the focus of the program is high-risk patients and end-of-life care, and despite the intensity of the intervention, a significant proportion of patients with LLI are not engaged in SDM. There is both regulatory and clinical demand to engage all patients in SDM and a coherent argument to aim for goal-concordant care.25 An organisation that aims to engage all patients in SDM, which establishes and assesses goal concordance, is likely to achieve higher levels of SDM.

Limitations and future research

The major strength of this study is the longitudinal tracking of a staged, increasing intensity intervention in a tertiary hospital, and is the largest prospective longitudinal study of shared decision-making in critically ill patients. A major limitation is the lack of assessment of the patient or surrogate decision-maker perspective, including their experience of the discussion, and whether their goals were met over time. It was not possible to elicit this perspective in this retrospective study. Also, without a control population, the exact nature of the relationship between this program and the outcomes we observed remains unknown. Third, as this was a single-centre study, the results may not be applicable in other institutions. Fourth, it is unclear which elements of the SDM process influence patient-centred and healthcare use outcomes.

Conclusion

The introduction of a clinical communication training and clinical support program was associated with a significant increase in the proportion of documented shared decision-making discussions in patients with LLI admitted to the intensive care unit. This corresponded with changes in patient treatment preferences and the amount of healthcare utilisation by decedents. Further research is required to characterise the patient experience of SDM conversations and to understand whether SDM achieves goal-concordant care or changes healthcare utilisation.
