Statistical Competence and Editorial Policies in Biomedical Publications
Authors: Olena Zimba, Aizhan Oralbek, Maidan Mukhamediyarov, Marlen Yessirkepov, Burhan Fatih Kocyigit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Misuse of statistical methods in biomedical research remains widespread, undermining scientific integrity and public health. Flawed analyses can lead to misleading clinical guidelines, unnecessary treatments, or concealment of true treatment effects. Statistical errors often result from a structural training gap that affects the research process from study design to reporting, rather than from individual incompetence. Authors should be able to select appropriate measures of central tendency based on the data distribution, apply parametric and nonparametric tests correctly, use regression analyses with attention to statistical power and collinearity, and interpret P values in the context of confidence intervals. The growing number of retractions in statistics and the continued citation of retracted articles highlight the limitations of current peer-review processes. Statistical Package for the Social Sciences is the most widely used statistical software in health sciences research, while R/RStudio is gaining increasing adoption due to its open-source nature, analytical flexibility, and capacity for fully reproducible and transparent analyses. Software selection can significantly influence analytical outcomes due to differences in default algorithms. Therefore, full disclosure of software versions and procedures is essential for reproducibility. Integrating artificial intelligence-based tools into statistical workflows introduces risks, including output hallucination, a lack of algorithmic transparency, and unresolved accountability. These tools should not be used without internationally accepted validation guidelines. At the publisher level, employing statistical editors, enforcing open data-sharing policies, and adopting contributor role taxonomies such as Contributor Roles Taxonomy are the most effective structural interventions. Enhancing the statistical quality of biomedical literature requires coordinated, sustained efforts from all stakeholders in scientific communication.

INTRODUCTION

Biomedical research necessitates the systematic collection, interpretation, and reporting of complex data concerning human health and disease processes.1 Statistical analysis, which is integral to this process, is essential for evaluating the significance of observational findings, quantifying differences and relationships between groups, generalizing obtained results, and establishing the reliability of research outcomes.23 Beyond serving as a mathematization tool, statistics provides an epistemological framework that upholds the integrity and reproducibility of scientific inference.4

Biomedical and healthcare research studies employing inappropriate statistical tests may ultimately result in misleading practice guidelines.5 As such, insufficient statistical rigor should be regarded not only as an individual author's error but as a systemic issue with gross implications for public health.6

The requirement for scholarly contributors, including authors, reviewers, and editors, to demonstrate essential knowledge and skills in statistical analyses is increasingly relevant. According to the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) criteria, scientific authorship entails intellectual contribution to the research and accountability for the entire work; delegating data analysis to external parties undermines this responsibility.7 Additionally, reliance on commercial regulatory services may introduce biases, such as selective reporting and P value manipulation. Researchers and authors should be skilled enough to evaluate their results and transparently report the limitations of their studies.8 Statistical literacy is also essential for reviewers to critically evaluate methodological choices, hypothesis testing, and effect sizes. Incompetence in this area undermines the quality control function of peer review.9 In their turn, editors share their responsibility for evaluating and accepting research reports with reproducible methods and details presented in line with globally acceptable standards.10 Accordingly, statistical competence should be regarded as a fundamental academic skill that underpins the responsibility of all stakeholders in scientific communications and credibility of the whole scientific process.

This article provides an overview of the appropriate use of statistical analyses in medical and healthcare publications and offers practical recommendations for authors, reviewers, and editors. The recommendations focus on three main areas: core statistical competencies for authors in designing, analyzing, and reporting biomedical research; journal and publisher policy recommendations to strengthen methodological oversight; and essential reporting items that must be included in methodology sections, such as disclosure of statistical software versions, specification of algorithms used, justification for test selection, and transparency about the use of artificial intelligence (AI)-based tools. These components provide a practical reference for all stakeholders aiming to improve the statistical integrity and reproducibility of biomedical research.

SEARCH STRATEGY

A systematic search was performed across PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, Scopus, and the Directory of Open Access Journals without a defined start date, including all articles published up to May 2026. The search utilized individual MeSH terms and selected Boolean AND combinations, with Biostatistics as the primary search term paired with Data Interpretation, Statistical; Peer Review, Research; Retraction of Publication; Reproducibility of Results; Software; Publishing; and Artificial Intelligence. Given the scope of this review, the search strategy was intentionally broad to ensure comprehensive coverage of the topic. Only articles published in English were included. No restrictions were placed on article type; original articles, reviews, editorials, and other publication formats were all eligible for inclusion. Additionally, reference lists of retrieved articles were manually screened to identify further relevant publications not captured by the initial search.

STATISTICAL ERRORS AS A DRIVER OF RETRACTIONS IN BIOMEDICAL LITERATURE

The rise in retraction rates over the past decade underscores persistent structural vulnerabilities in the publishing enterprise. In fact, retractions of medical publications have steadily increased over the 50-year period from 1975 to 2024.11 A systematic review of 43 studies demonstrated that 17% of retractions resulted from errors, particularly statistical errors.12

The increasing retractions due to statistical errors point to a systemic issue within biomedical research that extends beyond individual authors’ oversight. Traditional peer review may fail to identify statistical errors. Problematic methods and erroneous results appearing in publications may influence practice before retraction, thereby posing threats to public health.13 Furthermore, retraction notifications are frequently delayed, and retracted articles often continue to be mistakenly cited as valid outputs, perpetuating the risks in healthcare.14 Notably, analyses of retractions in AI-related publications demonstrate that data integrity issues and methodological errors, including statistical errors, are becoming increasingly complex, necessitating upgraded editorial oversight mechanisms.15

STATISTICAL TRAINING: KEY THEMES AND PRACTICAL COMPETENCIES

The inadequacy of statistical reporting in biomedical publications cannot be attributed solely to individual competence; it mainly results from an infrastructural training gap.1617 As most statistical errors arise during the early stages of the research process, specifically in design and analysis planning, acquiring fundamental statistical competencies is essential for ensuring the quality of research reporting.17 Video-sharing platforms such as YouTube and freely available online biostatistics courses may serve as indispensable tools for self-education. These resources require critical evaluation and careful selection by experts in the field.18

BASICS OF PARAMETER DISTRIBUTIONS AND APPROPRIATE MEASURES OF CENTRAL TENDENCY

For normally distributed parameters, means and standard deviations should be reported. For non-normally distributed parameters, medians and interquartile ranges are more appropriate. Minimum and maximum values should be reported to describe the range.19 The application of non-parametric tests in small-sample biomedical studies is also critical. When the sample size is insufficient and the assumption of normality is violated, the Mann-Whitney U test should replace the independent sample t-test. As sample sizes increase, certain parametric tests demonstrate greater robustness to violations of normality. Nevertheless, evaluating data distribution and verifying underlying assumptions remains critical when selecting statistical tests.20 In bivariate correlation analyses, Pearson’s correlation is appropriate for continuous variables with normal distributions, while Spearman’s correlation is preferable for data with non-normal distribution.

In logistic regression analysis, statistical power represents a significant conceptual challenge. At least 10 to 20 events are required for each independent variable included in the model; therefore, incorporating numerous variables in small samples substantially reduces statistical power and compromises the reliability of the results.21 Recent methodological evidence demonstrates that a fixed threshold is overly simplistic, as the minimum sample size required for regression models depends on the number of candidate predictor parameters, the outcome proportion, and the model’s anticipated predictive performance. Consequently, formal sample size calculations tailored to the specific context are recommended instead of relying on rigid rules of thumb.22 Collinearity in regression analysis is another critical concern. For instance, C-reactive protein and erythrocyte sedimentation rate are both biological markers of the acute-phase response. Including both variables in the same regression model introduces collinearity, or high correlation between independent variables, which significantly impairs the estimation of beta coefficients.23 It is essential to assess multicollinearity prior to model construction, for example by examining variance inflation factors or correlation matrices. In cases of substantial collinearity, selecting the most clinically relevant variable or constructing separate models are appropriate strategies.

The distinction between P values and confidence intervals (CIs) represents another important topic in statistical education. When 95% CIs are reported, the corresponding P value can be inferred from the CI. Consequently, some methodologists contend that reporting both measures is not necessary. Numerous reporting guidelines advocate reporting both, suggesting that CIs offer additional insights beyond the P value, particularly regarding effect size and the precision of estimates. Researchers are therefore encouraged to move beyond binary interpretations (significant/insignificant) based solely on the P value and to interpret findings alongside CIs within the relevant clinical context.24 Estimation thinking, encompassing effect sizes, CIs, and clinical relevance, should receive greater emphasis. P values should serve as supportive rather than primary measures for interpreting study findings.

CHOOSING STATISTICAL SOFTWARE

The accuracy and reproducibility of statistical analyses are influenced by the choice of statistical software. A bibliometric analysis of 10,596 articles published in PubMed found that the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) is the most widely used statistical software in health research (52.1%), followed by the Statistical Analysis System (SAS) (12.9%) and Stata (12.6%).25 Analysis of the relationship between study design and software preference shows that SPSS is predominantly used in observational (61.1%) and experimental (65.3%) studies, whereas Review Manager (43.7%) and Stata (38.3%) are more common in systematic reviews with meta-analyses.25

Some regional differences have been reported. For instance, in articles published in Peruvian medical journals between 2021 and 2022, SPSS (42.5%) and Stata (38%) were most commonly mentioned in the statistical analyses sections, while R was noted in only 3.2% of articles.26

SPSS is a widely accessible tool for novice researchers because its graphical user interface enables use without programming expertise. The software offers a wide range of functions, including descriptive statistics, logistic and linear regression, nonparametric tests, and reliability analyses.27 The use of scripting (syntax) is recommended to ensure full reproducibility of analyses, as menu-based analysis steps are not automatically documented by the software.28

R and RStudio, as open-source and freely available tools, are widely regarded as the gold standard for scientific transparency and reproducibility.29 The Comprehensive R Archive Network repository, which contains thousands of specialized packages, provides analytical flexibility across nearly all domains of biomedical research.29 Analyses conducted using R can be thoroughly documented, shared, and independently verified, positioning R as a central component of reproducible research practices.30 While the initial learning curve can be challenging, the RStudio interface and extensive online resources substantially ease adoption.

GraphPad Prism is widely utilized, especially in experimental medicine, pharmacology, and basic biomedical research, due to its intuitive interface and capacity to produce quality graphs. Although recent versions offer mixed models, survival analysis, and basic regression techniques, the software remains limited relative to SPSS and R for studies that require complex multivariate modeling and advanced statistical analyses.29

Stata holds a prominent position in epidemiology, public health, and health economics research, and is especially favored for panel data analysis, survival analysis, and complex sample designs. SAS remains one of the most widely used software packages in clinical trials and the pharmaceutical industry, particularly for U.S. Food and Drug Administration approval processes. Its use in research relies on programming requirements.31

A fundamental principle in software selection is that, regardless of the software chosen, a comprehensive and transparent report of all analysis steps—including software versions, package names, and applied algorithms—is essential for scientific reproducibility. At a minimum, reporting standards require specifying the statistical software used, including the software version, key packages or modules, principal analytical procedures, and, when possible, the availability of analysis code or syntax files. Merely stating the statistical method is inadequate, as different software packages may employ distinct default algorithms for the same analysis, which may substantially influence results. Providing detailed information about the software in the methods section enhances the reader's ability to evaluate the analysis and facilitates independent validation and replication. Furthermore, researchers should justify their software choices and, when feasible, share analysis code or syntax files via publicly accessible platforms, aligning with contemporary standards for research reporting transparency.

AI-BASED STATISTICAL PLATFORMS

A primary challenge in employing AI-based statistical analyses is the difficulty of validation and the associated risk of hallucinations. Specialized AI-assisted tools, such as Statcheck and GRIM-Test, can facilitate the identification of statistical reporting inconsistencies and help reviewers and editors detect methodological flaws; however, human oversight remains essential.32 When large language models (LLMs) generate statistical results based on training data, they can be driven by pattern-matching and prediction rather than genuine data analysis. This environment fosters information hallucinations, which may appear accurate but are actually unfounded or misleading in statistical interpretations.3334 Studies evaluating ChatGPT in biomedical contexts have demonstrated that the model may produce erroneous statistical results, and these errors are often difficult to detect using standard software outputs.35

A significant challenge associated with the use of AI analyses is the lack of transparency and reproducibility. In contrast to traditional statistical software, analyses based on LLMs often fail to document the specific algorithms applied, the underlying assumptions, and the procedures used to generate results. This lack of documentation prevents independent verification of analytical steps and hinders the replication of studies.36

Another significant issue relates to accountability and authorship. The ICMJE authorship criteria require intellectual contribution and accountability; attributing authorship to AI platforms is considered a fundamental ethical issue.37 Universal standards for reporting the extent of AI involvement in statistical analysis have not yet been established. This lack of clarity poses substantial risks to scientific integrity.

The inclusion of AI platforms in statistical analysis processes risks weakening authors’ capacity for in-depth understanding of their own data. Relying on AI outputs rather than directly evaluating data characteristics, normality assumptions, and the rationale for selecting appropriate tests may lead to methodological errors. For all these reasons, the routine use of AI-based statistical tools cannot be recommended without the development of internationally accepted guidelines for methodological standards, reporting requirements, and validation mechanisms.

PUBLISHER POLICIES

Enhancing the statistical quality of biomedical publishing extends beyond individual author responsibility, as journal policies and publisher strategies constitute critical structural factors. A comprehensive study of statistical review policies in leading biomedical journals found that, among respondents, only 23% conducted statistical review for all articles, while about one-third did so rarely or never.38 Several factors may contribute to this. First, there is a shortage of qualified statistical reviewers; biostatisticians are in high demand and often lack incentives to take on unpaid, time-consuming review tasks that receive little formal recognition. Second, adding a dedicated statistical review step extends editorial timelines and increases costs, potentially conflicting with the need for rapid publication. Third, many editorial boards lack members with sufficient statistical expertise to assess which manuscripts require specialized review or to select appropriate reviewers. Finally, smaller journals and those with limited resources may not be able to support a formal statistical review process. These structural and economic barriers, rather than a lack of awareness, likely explain the persistently low adoption of statistical peer review.

Hiring experts in biostatistics, epidemiology, and research methodology for review processes is the most effective approach to identifying correctable mistakes and errors in methods, violations of assumptions, and reporting deficiencies.39 Statistical review for studies involving complex multivariate models, survival analyses, or meta-analyses may significantly enhance the methodological reliability of biomedical literature.

Another key issue relates to adopting editorial policies of open data sharing. A comprehensive analysis of the editorial policies of 318 biomedical journals found that most have not yet established data sharing and analysis, and those that do differ in enforcement.40 Open data sharing strengthens reproducibility and enhances the value of biomedical science by enabling independent verification and facilitating secondary analyses.

Finally, ensuring transparent disclosure of scholarly contributors may add to accountability in research reporting. The Contributor Roles Taxonomy (CRediT) framework enables systematic identification of individuals responsible for statistical analysis through the categories “Formal Analysis” and “Methodology.”41 CRediT was introduced to resolve ambiguity in traditional authorship listings by defining fourteen distinct contributor roles. This framework allows each author’s specific contributions to be clearly documented. In terms of statistical integrity, this detail helps editors and readers determine whether those responsible for designing and conducting analyses have the necessary expertise, rather than relying on external commercial services without proper oversight. Explicitly assigning statistical roles also clarifies accountability for the integrity of the analysis, which aligns with the ICMJE requirement that authors are accountable for the entire work. Transparent role attribution further discourages honorary authorship and ensures fair recognition for biostatisticians, whose contributions have often been undervalued.42

In addition to core measures, publishers play a key role in ensuring consistent application of established reporting guidelines. Although CONSORT, STROBE, and PRISMA are widely recognized, their adoption varies considerably among journals. A study of five major reporting guidelines across 559 biomedical journals from 11 leading publishers found substantial differences in adherence requirements, with many journals recommending, rather than requiring, compliance. Journals that required adherence saw significantly higher citation rates, indicating that enforcement, rather than endorsement, leads to measurable improvements in publication quality.43

Formalizing statistical reporting requirements in author guidelines is equally important. Several journals now require authors to disclose the software version, the specific algorithms used, and the rationale for test selection. These elements are essential for reproducibility. Journals that include these requirements in their submission workflows produce more transparent and verifiable statistical methods sections.44 These requirements should also include pre-registration of studies and statistical analysis plans, especially for clinical trials and observational studies. Pre-registration is linked to reduced selective reporting and a lower risk of bias.

Overall, publishers should provide explicit guidance to authors on using up-to-date, validated versions of statistical software. CONCLUSION

The appropriate application of statistical analyses in medical and health research is essential for upholding scientific integrity, informing clinical decisions with reliable evidence, and ensuring the reproducibility of research findings. Statistical errors are usually preventable. These errors arise not only from individual author issues but also from infrastructural deficiencies in review processes.

Retractions due to statistical errors indicate the inadequacy of current quality control mechanisms. Challenges spanning research design, data analysis, statistical software selection, and reporting practices may lead to misleading practice guidelines, risky treatments, and adverse effects on public health. As AI-based statistical tools are rapidly integrated into biomedical research, related threats are becoming increasingly complex. Emerging methodological vulnerabilities include difficulties in validation, lack of transparency, and accountability concerns.

Within the framework of the proposed recommendations, enhancing the quality of statistical reporting requires that authors acquire fundamental statistical competencies, conduct transparent analyses using the latest versions of appropriate software packages, and adhere to available research reporting guidelines. Reviewers’ statistical competence is essential to the effective functioning of the peer-review process. Hiring statistical reviewers and enforcing data-sharing policy may further strengthen the methodological quality of biomedical publications.47

Overall, improving statistical reporting requires coordinated efforts of authors, reviewers, editors, and publishers. Authors should plan statistical analyses transparently, employ appropriate methodologies, and report findings in line with established guidelines. Reviewers are responsible for critically assessing the suitability and interpretation of statistical methods. Editors and publishers should promote statistical rigor by implementing dedicated methodological review, enforcing open-data policies, and establishing clear authorship accountability frameworks such as CRediT. Enhancing statistical competence and transparency at each stage will strengthen the reliability of scientific knowledge and its application in clinical practice.
