Differential prognostic value of glycemic dysregulation markers in critically ill individuals with ischemic stroke, with and without diabetes
Authors: Jing Feng, Chang He, Jianheng Gu, Hongyu Zhang, Hongbo San, Mengsi Zhan, Hongqian Fu, Jiahao Yao, Lang Li, Xuefeng Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlycemic dysregulation is common in critically ill patients with ischemic stroke and is strongly associated with adverse outcomes. However, the prognostic implications of hyperglycemia may differ substantially between patients with diabetes and without diabetes, reflecting divergent metabolic responses to acute stress. Traditional glucose metrics often fail to capture this complexity. Novel glycemic markers—hemoglobin glycation index (HGI), stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR), and glycemic variability (GV)—may better characterize both acute and chronic glycemic disturbances. Understanding whether these markers have differential prognostic value based on diabetes status is critical for advancing individualized glycemic management strategies in the ICU setting.MethodsThe cohort included 1,293 critically ill individuals with ischemic stroke, with a mean age of 68 ± 12 years, and 67% were male. Of these, 640 (49.5%) had diabetes and 653 (50.5%) did not. Three glycemic markers—HGI, SHR, and GV—were calculated from admission glucose and HbA1c values. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality at 30, 180, and 360 days. Multivariable Cox regression, Kaplan–Meier analysis, restricted cubic spline modeling, and time-dependent ROC curves were used to evaluate associations. To investigate potential heterogeneity, we subsequently performed subgroup analyses stratified by diabetes status, including recalibration of the HGI estimation models for patients with diabetes and without diabetes.ResultsAll three glycemic markers were independently associated with mortality, though their prognostic value varied by diabetes status. Among patients without diabetes, moderate HGI was associated with significantly lower 180-day (HR = 0.64, p = 0.049) and 360-day mortality (HR = 0.65, p = 0.023). SHR was a stronger predictor in patients without diabetes at 30 days (HR = 1.52, 95% CI: 1.11–2.08, p = 0.009), while GV was associated with increased 360-day mortality (HR = 1.50, p = 0.015). In contrast, for patients with diabetes, only SHR showed consistent association with 180-day mortality (HR = 1.46, p = 0.028).ConclusionHGI, SHR, and GV are independently associated with mortality in individuals with ischemic stroke who are critically ill, but their prognostic value differs by diabetes status. Patients without diabetes appear more vulnerable to glycemic stress, underscoring the need for diabetes-stratified approaches in glycemic risk assessment and ICU management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-01996-y.Keywords: Glycemic variability, Hemoglobin glycation index, Stress hyperglycemia ratio, Glucose metabolism, Ischemic stroke

Introduction

Ischemic stroke is a leading cause of mortality and long-term disability worldwide, with critically ill patients in the intensive care unit (ICU) facing particularly high risks of adverse outcomes despite aggressive management efforts [1–3]. Hyperglycemia is common in the acute phase of stroke and has been associated with larger infarct volumes, oxidative stress, blood–brain barrier disruption, and worsened neurological recovery [4–6].

However, traditional glucose metrics, such as fasting or admission glucose, often fail to reflect the complexity of glycemic disturbances in critically ill settings. The hemoglobin glycation index (HGI) reflects the deviation between observed and predicted HbA1c, thus capturing individual variation in long-term glycemic burden [7–9]. Clinically, HGI has been explored as a potential predictor of outcomes in patients with diabetes and cardiovascular diseases, although it is not yet widely adopted in ICU protocols. The stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR), defined as the ratio of acute to chronic glycemia, identifies stress-induced hyperglycemia more accurately than absolute glucose levels [10]. It has been incorporated into risk models for acute myocardial infarction and stroke, aiding early mortality prediction. Glycemic variability (GV), commonly calculated as the coefficient of variation of glucose measurements, quantifies short-term fluctuations and has been linked to adverse outcomes in various ICU populations [11, 12]. In clinical settings, high GV has been associated with greater risks of infection, prolonged ICU stay, and poor neurological recovery. Though these metrics are still primarily used in research, their clinical utility is increasingly recognized, and some centers have begun integrating them into individualized glucose management.

Recent studies have suggested that patients with diabetes and without diabetes may exhibit fundamentally different metabolic responses to glycemic dysregulation [13, 14]. Patients without diabetes may be more vulnerable to acute glucose toxicity, while those with diabetes may have adapted to higher baseline glucose levels. Yet, this potential heterogeneity has not been thoroughly examined in the context of individuals with ischemic stroke who are critically ill.

Therefore, in this study, we used the MIMIC-IV database to assess the associations of HGI, SHR, and GV with 30-, 180-, and 360-day all-cause mortality among ICU patients with ischemic stroke. Additionally, we conducted stratified analyses by diabetes status to explore whether the prognostic significance of these glycemic markers differs between patients with diabetes and without diabetes, aiming to inform more personalized glycemic risk stratification and ICU management.

Methods

Source

This study used data from MIMIC-IV v3.1, a publicly available, de-identified ICU database from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (2008–2022), developed by MIT’s Laboratory for Computational Physiology (https://physionet.org/content/mimiciv/1.0/) [15, 16]. All data are fully anonymized, with IRB approval and a waiver of informed consent [9]. The first author completed CITI training and was granted access (Certification No. 66611501). Additionally, as this study was based on retrospective, de-identified data from a public ICU database, it posed no risk to patient privacy or autonomy and complied with established ethical guidelines.

Research object and data collection

Inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (1) adult patients aged 18 years or older; (2) a confirmed diagnosis of ischemic stroke, identified using ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes; (3) at least three blood glucose measurements during the ICU stay; (4) availability of both admission blood glucose and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) records; (5) for patients with multiple ICU admissions, only the first ICU stay was considered; and availability of all-cause mortality data at 30, 180, and 360 days post-admission (Fig. 1). Structured Query Language (SQL) was used to extract relevant variables from the MIMIC-IV database. Collected data included demographic information, comorbidities, laboratory test results, medication use, mechanical ventilation status, and disease severity scores (Supplementary Table S1). Diabetes status was identified using ICD-9/ICD-10 diagnostic codes, including both type 1 and type 2 diabetes, and patients were categorized into two groups: with diabetes and without diabetes.

Flowchart of patient inclusion and exclusion from the MIMIC-IV database. Among 2,148 ischemic stroke patients screened, 0 were excluded for age < 18 years, 0 for ICU stays < 24 h, 855 for missing admission glucose or HbA1c data, leaving 1,293 patients in the final analysis

SHR, GV, HGI index calculation

The glucose value recorded within 24 h of ICU admission, without the requirement for fasting, was used as a surrogate for fasting plasma glucose (FPG). Although the MIMIC-IV database does not label glucose values as “fasting”, this early measurement was likely obtained before nutritional support and has been used as a surrogate for fasting glucose in prior studies [17, 18]. Subsequent glucose values were obtained via fingertip, serum, or whole blood samples. SHR was calculated as admission glucose divided by the estimated average glucose from HbA1c: SHR = glucose/(28.7 × HbA1c − 46.7) [19], with HbA1c in percent. GV was assessed by the coefficient of variation (CV), computed as (SD/Mean) × 100% of all glucose readings during ICU stay [20]. HGI was defined as the difference between observed and predicted HbA1c, based on a regression model within the study cohort: Predicted HbA1c = 5.908 + 0.094 × glucose (mmol/L) [11] (Supplementary Figure S1A). HGI estimates individual glycation deviation beyond baseline glycemia. To account for baseline differences in glucose metabolism, separate regression models were constructed to estimate predicted HbA1c values in patients with diabetes and without diabetes for HGI calculation during subgroup analysis.

Statistical analysis

Three glycemic variables—SHR, HGI, and GV—were categorized into tertiles based on their distribution for group comparisons and survival analyses. Optimal HGI cutoffs for 30-day mortality were identified using X-tile software, defining low (< −1.25%), medium (−1.25% to < 1.38%), and high (≥ 1.38%) groups (Supplementary Figure S2). Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± SD, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. ANOVA or Welch’s t-test was used for continuous data, depending on normality and variance equality; Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test was applied for categorical variables. Kaplan–Meier curves assessed 30-, 180-, and 360-day mortality across glycemic groups, with differences tested via log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate associations between glycemic markers and all-cause mortality. Three models were constructed: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for demographic covariates including age, sex; and Model 3 further adjusted for clinical severity (e.g., GCS, APS III), vital signs, laboratory parameters, comorbidities, and treatment factors. ROC curves compared the predictive performance of individual and combined markers. Restricted cubic spline models (three knots) explored nonlinear relationships, while threshold effect analysis identified potential inflection points. Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore whether the prognostic associations of glycemic markers varied by diabetes status. HGI values were recalculated within each subgroup using separate regression models. Forest plots were used to visualize hazard ratios and interaction effects. Sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness of these subgroup findings using different missing data strategies. All analyses were conducted using R software (v4.2.2), with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 1,293 ICU patients with ischemic stroke met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis. Baseline characteristics were then compared between survivors and non-survivors at 30 and 360 days. At 30 and 360 days, non-survivors were significantly older than survivors (p < 0.001). Non-survivors also exhibited higher white blood cell count, sodium, and creatinine, and were more likely to require mechanical ventilation, vasopressors, and insulin, with notable differences in medication use. Elevated HGI, SHR, and GV were associated with higher mortality. Variables with significant between-group differences were entered into multivariable models (Table 1).

K‒M survival analysis curves

Figure 2 shows Kaplan–Meier survival curves for HGI, SHR, and GV at 30, 180, and 360 days. For HGI, patients with moderate levels had the best survival throughout, suggesting a potential protective effect, while both low and high HGI groups showed reduced survival, indicating a U-shaped pattern. For SHR, the highest tertile (Q3) had consistently lowest survival, especially within 180 days, while Q1 and Q2 showed similar outcomes. For GV, group differences were minor at 30 days but became pronounced by 360 days, with higher GV associated with poorer long-term survival.

Solid lines indicate hazard ratios (HRs), and shaded areas represent 95% confidence intervals. HGI exhibited a U-shaped relationship with mortality, while SHR and GV showed a more linear increasing trend in risk.

The impact of HGI, SHR, and GV on short- and long-term mortality

Both categorical and continuous forms of HGI, SHR, and GV were assessed for 30-, 180-, and 360-day mortality (Supplementary Table S2,Table 2). Moderate HGI was consistently associated with lower mortality, with significant effects at 180 and 360 days. SHR in the highest tertile (Q3) was associated with increased mortality at 30 and 180 days, with a significant association at 180 days. For GV, the moderate group (Q2) showed higher 180- and 360-day mortality, with the 360-day association remaining significant. The highest GV group showed similar trends but lost significance, indicating a reverse U-shaped relationship. Continuous forms of HGI, SHR, and GV showed weaker associations.

Restricted cubic spline

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were used to assess nonlinear associations of HGI, SHR, and GV with mortality (Fig. 3). HGI showed a significant U-shaped relationship at 30, 180, and 360 days (p < 0.05), with moderate levels linked to the lowest risk. SHR demonstrated a largely linear, dose-dependent increase in mortality across all time points, with minor nonlinearity at 180 and 360 days (p < 0.05). GV was not associated with 30-day mortality, but higher values were linked to increased risk at 180 and 360 days, despite nonsignificant nonlinearity.

Threshold analyses identified SHR cutoffs at 0.801 (30-day HR = 1.75, p = 0.003) and 0.509 (180-day HR = 1.41, p = 0.003) (Supplementary Table S3). HGI values below –0.607 were associated with lower 180-day mortality (HR = 0.62, p = 0.044), though segmented modeling did not significantly outperform linear models. No threshold effect was found for GV.

Subgroup analyses

To assess consistency across populations, subgroup analyses were performed (Supplementary Figure S4). In patients without insulin therapy (N = 170), SHR and GV yielded unstable HRs (0.00, p < 0.001) for 30-day mortality due to complete outcome separation. However, HGI remained analyzable and showed a consistent inverse association with mortality, especially at 180 days. In patients without diabetes, HGI significantly predicted lower mortality (HR = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.50–0.95, p = 0.020), though no significant interaction was detected (p > 0.05), indicating general consistency across subgroups. SHR was positively associated with mortality across most subgroups, with the strongest effect at 30 days (HR = 1.52, 95% CI: 1.11–2.08, p = 0.009). This association was more pronounced in patients ≥ 65 years, where interaction reached significance (p = 0.024), suggesting age-modified prognostic relevance. At 180 and 360 days, the effect weakened, and interactions were nonsignificant. GV showed no significant association with mortality in any subgroup. Most HRs hovered around 1 with wide CIs, and no interaction was detected, indicating limited prognostic value across population strata.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses using multiple imputation strategies (random forest, KNN, MICE, and complete-case analysis; Supplementary Tables S4–S12) showed consistent results. SHR remained significantly associated with 180-day mortality across all methods. HGI’s moderate group consistently had the lowest 180- and 360-day mortality, supporting a U-shaped pattern. GV also showed a significant association with 360-day mortality in the moderate group. These associations were robust regardless of imputation strategy.

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the prognostic significance of three glycemic dysregulation markers—HGI, SHR, and GV—in individuals with ischemic stroke who are critically ill and revealed important heterogeneity based on diabetes status. While all three markers were independently associated with mortality, their predictive value varied notably between patients with diabetes and without diabetes. Specifically, HGI and GV demonstrated stronger associations with mortality in patients without diabetes, suggesting heightened vulnerability to acute glycemic stress in this subgroup. In contrast, SHR remained consistently predictive across both groups but showed the greatest short-term prognostic utility in patients with diabetes. These findings highlight the importance of considering underlying metabolic status when interpreting glycemic markers in critically ill stroke populations and provide new insights into stratified glucose management strategies in the ICU.

The differential prognostic value of glycemic markers between patients with diabetes and without diabetes may reflect distinct physiological responses to glycemic stress. Patients without diabetes typically lack chronic hyperglycemia-induced metabolic adaptations, making them more susceptible to acute glucose fluctuations and stress hyperglycemia. Sudden hyperglycemic surges in this group may provoke exaggerated inflammatory and oxidative stress responses, exacerbate endothelial dysfunction, and disrupt the blood–brain barrier—mechanisms strongly linked to worse stroke outcomes [4, 21–24]. In contrast, patients with diabetes often exhibit higher glycemic thresholds for stress activation and may have partially adapted vascular and immune systems, attenuating the immediate impact of glycemic excursions [25]. These biological differences may explain why HGI and GV were more predictive of mortality in patients without diabetes, while SHR retained prognostic utility across both groups.

HGI, SHR, and GV each represent distinct aspects of glycemic dysregulation and offer complementary prognostic insights. HGI primarily reflects chronic glycemic burden and individual deviation from expected glycation, potentially identifying patients with heightened vulnerability to long-term metabolic injury[7, 26, 27]. SHR captures acute stress hyperglycemia relative to baseline glucose levels, serving as a sensitive marker for acute illness severity and systemic stress [21, 22, 24, 28]. GV reflects short-term glycemic instability, which may trigger oscillating oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and immune dysregulation[28–31]. Our findings suggest that integrating these markers allows for a more comprehensive assessment of metabolic stress across different timescales and physiological mechanisms. The improved predictive accuracy observed when combining all three indicators further supports their synergistic value in mortality risk stratification among critically ill stroke patients.

Beyond prognostic value, these glycemic markers may offer actionable insights for frontline clinicians. Physicians can use HGI, SHR, and GV to identify high-risk patients early, prompting timely interventions such as tighter glucose control or closer hemodynamic monitoring. Nurses may incorporate these indices into routine assessments to support bedside decision-making and anticipate clinical deterioration. For interdisciplinary teams, stratifying patients by glycemic risk could help inform care planning, resource allocation, and discussions around prognosis in the ICU setting.

Collectively, our findings underscore the prognostic value of integrating HGI, SHR, and GV for mortality risk stratification in individuals with ischemic stroke who are critically ill. Notably, the heterogeneity in prognostic performance across patients with diabetes and without diabetes groups suggests that underlying metabolic status should be considered when interpreting glycemic markers and guiding glucose management strategies in the ICU. These results support a move toward stratified glycemic targets and monitoring thresholds tailored to individual glycometabolic profiles.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, it was based on retrospective data from a single tertiary ICU in the United States, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations or healthcare systems. Second, stroke-specific severity scores such as the NIH Stroke Scale (NIHSS) were not available in the MIMIC-IV database [25, 32]. Although general severity indicators like GCS and APS III were included in the models, unmeasured confounding may persist. Third, glucose values were collected intermittently rather than continuously, which may underestimate glycemic variability and affect the precision of GV calculation. Fourth, we relied on the first glucose measurement within 24 h of ICU admission to compute glycemic indices, but the fasting status of these samples was unknown, possibly introducing measurement variability. Fifth, patients with missing HbA1c or glucose values were excluded, which may have introduced selection bias and limited the external validity of our results. Lastly, while we adjusted for a wide range of clinical variables, residual bias from unrecorded factors (e.g., nutritional support, unmeasured treatments) cannot be entirely ruled out.

To address these limitations, future studies should employ prospective, multicenter designs and incorporate continuous glucose monitoring, validated stroke severity assessments, and more granular clinical data. Such efforts would help confirm the prognostic utility of HGI, SHR, and GV in broader ICU populations and support their integration into individualized glycemic management strategies.

Although this study utilized data from a U.S. ICU cohort, the findings may carry implications for other healthcare systems facing similar challenges, such as China, where the burden of ischemic stroke and diabetes continues to grow. Recent reports estimate that over 149 million adults in China had diabetes as of 2021 [33, 34], and the prevalence of stress hyperglycemia and undiagnosed diabetes remains high in critical care settings. Several studies from Chinese ICUs have examined stress hyperglycemia and glycemic variability in sepsis and cardiovascular populations [35], but their combined prognostic value in ischemic stroke remains underexplored [5]. Our findings may provide a useful foundation for future localized studies aiming to refine glycemic risk stratification and improve glucose management strategies in stroke patients within Chinese critical care contexts.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that HGI, SHR, and GV are independent predictors of mortality in critically ill patients with ischemic stroke, with their prognostic significance differing by diabetes status. HGI and GV showed stronger associations with mortality in patients without diabetes, while SHR remained predictive across both groups. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating individualized glycemic risk assessment into routine ICU practice. Integrating these markers may assist clinicians in identifying high-risk patients early, guiding glycemic control strategies, and informing multidisciplinary care decisions. Future prospective studies are needed to validate these findings across diverse populations and explore their utility in real-time clinical decision-making.
