Pressure surge and blade torque during shutdown of in-pipe drag-based turbines
Authors: Mohammad Karimipour Parashkooh, Nima Hasanzadeh, Peyman Sobhani, Amir F. Najafi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hydraulic transients in drag-based in-pipe turbines during sudden operational changes can induce pressure pulsations, water hammer, cavitation, and column separation in pipeline components located both upstream and downstream of the turbine. Additionally, the torque imposed on turbine blades is a critical concern, as frequent operational changes can accelerate blade fatigue and potentially lead to structural failure. This study integrates the Method of Characteristics (MOC) and Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) to model transient phenomena resulting from the abrupt stoppage of a hydrodynamic in-pipe turbine. The analysis evaluates how varying deceleration rates influence pressure surges, pressure pulsations, and torque amplification on blades across different blade counts. The results demonstrate that stoppage time significantly affects torque escalation and confirm that turbine blade count strongly influences the magnitude of pressure surges during transient events. Detailed scenario analyses further reveal that specific configurations exhibit greater susceptibility to extreme pressure fluctuations, thereby creating substantial operational and reliability challenges.

Introduction

With increasing global awareness of climate change and the urgent need to transition toward cleaner energy sources, alternative renewable energy technologies have been proposed over recent decades1,2. Among these emerging solutions, energy recovery from existing infrastructure, particularly water distribution networks, has attracted growing attention due to its ability to generate power without requiring major structural development. In this context, the use of in-pipe turbines represents an innovative and sustainable approach to harvesting energy from water distribution systems3. This method involves installing specialized turbines within pipelines to capture and convert excess hydraulic pressure from flowing water into electrical energy4. Traditionally, utilities manage this surplus pressure, which contributes to an increased risk of leakage, by using pressure-reducing valves (PRVs) that dissipate the excess energy as heat5.

While PRVs effectively regulate pressure and protect infrastructure, their operation results in significant energy dissipation. Recognizing this inefficiency has motivated researchers to explore technologies capable of converting this otherwise wasted hydraulic energy into usable electrical power. Many alternative approaches have been proposed to harness this form of energy, including the use of micro-generators in small-diameter pipelines to produce sufficient power for smart metering applications6, the incorporation of pumps operating as turbines (PATs) in municipal water pipelines7, and the deployment of propeller turbines8. Although each of these technologies offers distinct advantages, their applicability often depends on pipeline geometry, installation constraints, and flow characteristics.

Among the available solutions, lift- and drag-based vertical axis turbines (VATs) have attracted increasing attention in recent years due to their structural simplicity and ease of installation, as they do not require modification of the flow direction within the pipeline9. For example, LucidPipe™ has developed lift-based VATs designed for pipelines with diameters ranging from 12 to 60 inches and has reported rated power outputs between 14 and 60 kW10.

While commercial implementations demonstrate the practical feasibility of VAT technology, systematic academic investigations are essential to further optimize turbine performance and understand their operational behaviour under varying hydraulic conditions. Langroudi et al.11 conducted numerical and experimental investigations to evaluate the influence of various geometrical parameters of spherical lift-based VATs on the power coefficient and pressure coefficient, with the objective of optimizing turbine performance. Chen et al.12 examined several drag-based turbine designs equipped with deflectors that can be installed in a 4-inch pipeline and demonstrated that these turbines produce higher power output than comparable lift-based turbines. In a related study involving turbines of similar type and size, Hasanzadeh et al.13 identified seven key design variables and applied a multi-objective genetic algorithm to optimize turbine performance, achieving an efficiency exceeding 33% and a power output of 200 W. In a complementary investigation, the same research group analysed the impact of pipeline flow variability on the performance of the optimized turbine and proposed a dynamic deflector to maintain turbine performance under off-design operating conditions14.

Despite these advancements in improving turbine efficiency and power output, the integration of VATs into pressurized pipeline systems introduces additional hydraulic and structural challenges. Although drag-based VATs generate higher power output compared to lift-based designs, their runners and deflectors occupy a substantial portion of the pipeline, leaving only a small clearance of 3 mm between the rotating blades and the casing in the optimal design15. Consequently, changes in operating conditions can induce more severe hydraulic transients, making these turbines particularly susceptible to water hammer16. Analysing the transient hydraulic behaviour of in-pipe turbines is therefore critical for the safe and efficient design of pipeline systems17. Water hammer has long been a significant concern in hydropower systems and has caused several catastrophic failures, including the collapse of the Oigawa Hydroelectric Power Plant in 196018. Building upon this well-documented risk, the increasing integration of in-pipe turbines necessitates a deeper understanding of their behaviour under abnormal and transient operating conditions. Despite extensive research aimed at improving VAT efficiency and power output, studies investigating their behaviour under abnormal operating conditions, such as sudden stoppage or load rejection, remain limited.

Addressing these knowledge gaps requires accurate prediction of transient hydraulic behaviour within turbine and pipeline systems. The transient response of a pipeline system depends on both the rate of change of operating speed and the system configuration, due to acoustic coupling between the turbine and the piping, as well as pressure wave reflections and transmissions. The degree of unsteadiness induces specific physical responses, which may include compressibility effects19. Three-dimensional (3D) Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) has demonstrated considerable success in modeling turbomachinery devices under quasi-steady conditions. However, for transient simulations, accurate representation requires knowledge of appropriate boundary conditions, and the entire acoustic system of the turbomachine and the connected piping must be modelled. For example, Li et al.20 modelled a centrifugal pump along with its complete connected piping system to analyse transient flow during startup.

While fully resolved 3D simulations provide valuable insight into local flow physics, their application to large-scale pipeline networks remains computationally prohibitive. Such detailed modeling for larger systems imposes substantial computational costs and generates large volumes of data, much of which may be extraneous. In most sections of a piping system, flow is predominantly one-dimensional, with negligible variations in the other two dimensions. Therefore, one-dimensional analyses of transients and water hammer have been employed for decades, solving the continuity and momentum equations numerically using techniques such as the Method of Characteristics (MOC), finite difference, and finite element methods. The Method of Characteristics is widely used because it accurately simulates steep wave fronts, is relatively straightforward to implement, and is computationally efficient18. Nevertheless, multidimensional phenomena such as cavitation or vortex rope cannot be accurately captured with 1D solvers. Furthermore, flow inside hydraulic components such as turbomachines is inherently complex and multidimensional. Consequently, models representing these components, which serve as boundary conditions in MOC simulations, are limited, and for in-pipe VATs, no established models currently exist.

To overcome these modelling limitations, researchers have increasingly explored hybrid simulation frameworks that combine multidimensional flow resolution with system-level transient modelling. In a highly innovative study, Ruprecht and Helmrich21 analysed system oscillations in a hydropower plant caused by the draft tube using a coupled 1D/3D simulation. They modelled the penstock and turbine with 1D MOC and the draft tube with a 3D CFD solver, which allowed them to capture the complex multidimensional flow of the vortex rope in the draft tube while accounting for the response of the rest of the system. Ideally, by combining models that describe different sets of partial differential equations (PDEs) in separate domains, it is possible to achieve both accuracy and computational efficiency. Hérard and Hurisse22 investigated the coupling of two- and one-dimensional Euler equations by solving the Riemann problem at the interface and analysed the accuracy and stability of this approach. Deininger et al.23 evaluated two 1D/3D coupling strategies using compressible, density-based Euler methods for cavitating and non-cavitating flows. X. Zhang and Cheng24 applied two alternative coupling methods, partly overlapped coupling and adjacent coupling, to model water hammer-induced oscillations in a surge tank, and the results demonstrated accurate data transfer between 3 and 1D domains.

Numerous successful studies have reported the combination of MOC and CFD for centrifugal pumps operating under various transient conditions25–28. The primary distinction among these studies lies in the approach used to model the interface and transfer information between the two domains. Similarly, this coupled method has been applied to analyse load rejection processes in power plants equipped with Francis turbines, and the results were validated against pure 1D MOC simulations and experimental measurements29,30. Building upon these advances and addressing the absence of established transient models for in-pipe VATs, the present study aims to investigate hydraulic transients and water hammer caused by the sudden stoppage of drag-based VATs, with particular attention to the influence of turbine geometry and design on the maximum pressure induced in the system. The study also examines the resulting torque and stress on the turbine blades, which may contribute to blade fatigue and potential failure under frequent changes in operating conditions. To achieve a realistic representation of the pipeline system, MOC and CFD are coupled in this study. Beyond addressing VAT-specific challenges, this integrated framework establishes a versatile methodology for analysing transient hydraulic behaviour in various pipeline components, including flow meters and valves, which play a critical role in water hammer phenomena.

Problem description

The review of previous studies on in-pipe turbines indicates that most research has primarily focused on improving the hydraulic performance of these devices. For example, Hasanzadeh et al.13,15 addressed this objective through a combination of theoretical analyses, experimental investigations, and numerical simulations, ultimately resulting in an in-pipe turbine with an optimized geometry. Although hydraulic efficiency represents a fundamental performance criterion, the actual operation of in-pipe turbines is influenced by additional factors that arise under practical operating conditions. In particular, transient flow behaviour within the pipeline, together with the geometrical characteristics of the turbine, may introduce operational challenges that extend beyond steady-state performance. These challenges can potentially cause significant damage to the pipeline, the turbine, and associated upstream or downstream equipment. Among these operational concerns, the occurrence and severity of water hammer represent critical considerations in the design and operation of hydraulic devices. For drag-based in-pipe turbines, this issue is particularly important; however, despite its practical significance, it has not been thoroughly investigated.

To address this research gap, the present study examines the rapid stoppage of a drag-based vertical axis in-pipe turbine installed in a pipeline system supplied by a constant-level upstream reservoir with a head of H = 50 mH₂O, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Owing to the relatively high inlet pressure, the analysis assumes that transient pressure fluctuations remain above the water vapor pressure, thereby preventing cavitation or column separation within the system. The time scale of transient phenomena relative to the characteristic period of the pipeline, typically expressed as 2L/a, determines whether the system can be modelled using lumped or distributed parameters and whether liquid compressibility must be considered. In the present study, the rapid turbine stoppage combined with the relatively long upstream pipe section of 25 m in the one-dimensional region necessitates treating water as compressible and modelling the system as distributed. Simulation method

The simulation comprises a 3D CFD domain, used to model the turbine, deflector, and sections of pipe upstream and downstream sufficient to establish fully developed flow, coupled with a 1D MOC domain representing the upstream pipeline.

Three-dimensional solver

The geometrical model of the turbine and the upstream deflector was created using SolidWorks 2023 and subsequently imported into ICEM CFD 2023 for mesh generation. For the CFD analysis, ANSYS Fluent 23.1 was used to solve the unsteady Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes (URANS) equations in the CFD domain, while the MOC was implemented as a user-defined function (UDF) combined with an Execute Command written in Scheme within Fluent Cortex. At each time step, the CFD solver passes flow information to the MOC code, which calculates updated boundary condition variables and returns them to the CFD solver before advancing to the next time step.

Figure 3 presents the CFD domain and mesh. Exploiting the symmetry of the problem, only half of the actual domain was modelled, with a symmetry boundary condition applied in the x–z plane. The primary mesh in the rotating domain consisted of tetrahedral elements, while prism layers were applied near the walls to capture high-gradient regions and improve numerical accuracy. The interface between the rotating and stationary domains was handled using Fluent’s transient sliding mesh method. This method updates the relative mesh position at each time step, enabling dynamic interaction between the rotor and stator regions. A non-conformal mesh interface was employed, and flow variables were transferred through interpolation, ensuring conservation across the interface.Fig. 3Detailed view of the 3D CFD domain (generated using ANSYS Fluent 23.1).

Detailed view of the 3D CFD domain (generated using ANSYS Fluent 23.1).

Coupling interface

The concept of coupling MOC and CFD to exploit the strengths of each method presents a significant challenge: determining how to connect the two models so that the output of one serves as the input to the other. The first decision is the location of the interface between the two domains. To ensure fully developed flow, the 3D domain is extended to include a portion of the pipeline. On the 3D side, the interface consists of a surface containing multiple cell faces, each with data such as velocity components, density, and turbulence-model variables. On the 1D side, the interface is represented by a node storing only a single pressure and a single velocity value. Consequently, some form of averaging is required when transferring data from the 3D domain to the 1D domain. The interface values at each time step must satisfy the governing equations of both domains. Iterative26 and non-iterative24 schemes have been proposed to achieve this coupling.

In the iterative approach, the interface values are initialized using the results from the previous time step, and the pressure and velocity at the interface are repeatedly updated until the residuals of both sets of equations fall within an acceptable tolerance. This approach allows complete separation of the domains and provides greater flexibility in configuration design, but it is computationally expensive and can be less stable.

To reduce computational expense while maintaining sufficient accuracy, non-iterative coupling strategies have been developed based on simplifying assumptions. One such method is the Partly Overlapped Coupling technique proposed by Zhang and Cheng24, in which an additional sampling surface is introduced within the CFD domain to estimate the interface variables for the subsequent time step. This approach has demonstrated good accuracy, numerical stability, and computational efficiency in previous studies. For these reasons, it is adopted in the present work, both for the main simulations and for the validation cases.

Validation test of coupling methodology

Two validation tests were designed to assess the accuracy and consistency of the 1D–3D coupling methodology. The first test addresses a classic water hammer problem and is modelled using both the Method of Characteristics (MOC) and the coupled MOC–CFD approach. This comparison ensures that the hybrid method can reliably reproduce established 1D transient behaviour under benchmark conditions.

The second test complements the first by comparing the coupled method with a full CFD simulation of pressure wave propagation. While the governing equations are physically equivalent, their numerical treatment differs: the coupled approach integrates MOC with CFD using a finite volume formulation in the 3D domain, whereas the full CFD model applies a consistent finite volume formulation throughout the domain. By demonstrating strong agreement between the two approaches, this test confirms that the coupling interface does not introduce significant numerical artifacts and that the hybrid method maintains fidelity even when the entire system is resolved using CFD. Collectively, these tests validate the coupled methodology against both traditional 1D solvers and high-fidelity CFD simulations, addressing the two most relevant benchmarks in transient hydraulic modeling.

For reflected waves, both simulation approaches accurately captured wave timing and general waveform characteristics. However, reflections originating from the one-dimensional region in the coupled simulation displayed a more elastic response. The CFD only simulation produced smoother and more damped reflected waves. This difference is likely related to the equation of state implemented in the CFD solver, which produces a more gradual density and pressure relationship and therefore smooths reflected pressure waves. Overall, the hybrid model introduces slightly higher dissipation of propagating wave fronts, whereas the full CFD model tends to smooth pressure waves during reflection due to its density and pressure coupling.

Conclusion

In this study, the reliability of the coupled one-dimensional and three-dimensional modelling framework was first evaluated using two validation cases. In the first case, a classical water hammer problem generated by valve closure was simulated using both the Method of Characteristics (MOC) and the coupled MOC–CFD approach. The comparison revealed negligible differences between the two methods, particularly in predicting the peak pressure surge. These results demonstrate that the coupled method can serve as a reliable alternative to conventional MOC modelling in hydraulic systems where geometric complexity or flow characteristics necessitate detailed CFD analysis.

In the second validation case, the coupled method was assessed against a full CFD simulation of a unidirectional pressure wave. The predicted peak pressure differed by only approximately one percent, while the coupled framework also accurately captured the reflected pressure wave and reproduced the correct wave propagation speed. Following validation, steady-state simulations of the in-pipe turbine were conducted to establish baseline operating characteristics. The upstream pressure signal exhibited two dominant frequency components, namely the rotor–stator interaction frequency and its harmonics, as well as the system acoustic frequency, expressed as 2L/a, and its corresponding harmonics. In addition, the steady-state hydraulic head and blade torque were quantified to provide reference values for subsequent transient analyses.

Transient simulations were then performed for a five-bladed turbine subjected to three linear deceleration rates. The results indicated that, even under the most severe condition of instantaneous stoppage, the maximum pressure increase reached only approximately six percent of the steady-state pressure. In contrast, the torque applied to the turbine blades exhibited strong sensitivity to the deceleration rate, emphasizing its significance in fatigue assessment and structural reliability analysis. The generated pressure waves propagated both upstream and downstream in an oscillatory manner and gradually attenuated with distance from the turbine. Further analysis of blade count effects indicated that, even though higher blade counts have greater flow blockage and are therefore expected to produce higher peak upstream pressures, this is not always the case.

Overall, this study provides a detailed assessment of water hammer behaviour in vertical-axis in-pipe turbines and identifies the principal factors governing pressure surges and transient torque fluctuations. Future research should prioritize comprehensive experimental validation to confirm the numerical findings and enhance the practical implementation of in-pipe turbines. Additional work should characterize realistic deceleration profiles through controlled stoppage experiments.
