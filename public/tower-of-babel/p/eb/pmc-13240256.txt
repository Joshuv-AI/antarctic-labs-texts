From clinical trial to clinical experience: Lecanemab therapy in a real‐world case series
Authors: Michael Sonson, Golnaz Yadollahikales, Allison Flores, Ilana Lasner, Susan Lee, Zaldy Tan, Christina Quach, Sarah Kremen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe approval of anti‐amyloid therapies has expanded therapeutic options for patients with Alzheimer's disease (AD), but the pivotal trials that proved their efficacy restrict their applicability to a selected patient population. We describe a case series of seven patients with biomarker‐confirmed AD pathology evaluated in an academic memory clinic and treated with lecanemab whose characteristics would have disqualified them from trial participation. Patient histories and outcomes are presented, accompanied by description of the clinical team's reasoning and application of published Appropriate Use Recommendations for each case. Overall, lecanemab was largely well tolerated, with no unexpected safety concerns and amyloid‐related imaging abnormality rates comparable to trial data. Clinically, most patients remained stable over the treatment period, though some continued to decline. This series highlights the challenges and considerations in applying disease‐modifying therapies beyond trial populations, emphasizing the need for real‐world data to guide treatment in diverse AD presentations.

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer's disease (AD) causes severe debility, financial burden, and is expected to increase in prevalence with an estimated 131 million patients living with AD dementia by 2050. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 The approval of disease modifying therapies (DMTs)— lecanemab (Leqembi) in 2023 and donanemab (Kisunla) in 2024—marked a watershed moment in the management of AD after decades of trials failing to demonstrate efficacy. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Identifying candidates for these medications prompted new criteria for diagnosis of AD with emphasis on the biomarkers used to define the disease. 9

The inclusion criteria for the Phase 3 Clarity AD trial targeted a population with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or mild dementia due to canonical, amnestic AD. 5 , 6 , 7 The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)–approved prescribing information for lecanemab indicates use in patients with MCI or mild dementia due to AD. 10 Guidance beyond that is drawn from published appropriate use recommendations (AURs), which further clarify the definitions of MCI and mild dementia and allow for non‐amnestic clinical profiles. 11 Even with the AUR, a lack of treatment guidance for non‐canonical AD cases remains.

Approximately 6% to 13% of late‐onset AD cases and 25% to 30% of early‐onset AD cases present with non‐amnestic syndromes. The most common include posterior cortical atrophy (PCA), dysexecutive or frontal variant, and logopenic primary progressive aphasia (lvPPA). 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 The underlying amyloid pathology in non‐amnestic AD is similar to typical amnestic AD, though the staging of tau distribution is usually more advanced. 16 , 17 The degree to which lecanemab is effective in these cases remains unclear given their exclusion in the original Clarity AD trial. 6

Co‐pathology is common in AD, especially with advancing age, and includes cerebrovascular disease, dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), idiopathic Parkinsons's disease, and limbic predominant age‐related transactive response DNA binding protein 43 kDa encephalopathy (LATE). 18 , 19 Cerebrovascular disease is the second most common cause of dementia and is itself a risk factor for AD with rates of co‐pathology as high as 20%. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 DLB accounts for 10% of dementias and presents with AD co‐pathology in 70% of cases. 19 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 In all cases, AD co‐pathology causes a worse clinical outcome compared to those involving one pathology. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 25

While there are reasons to be optimistic about lecanemab's benefit in the broader AD population, there is an absence of data in non‐canonical AD presentations. This leaves clinicians in a difficult position, forced to either extrapolate from limited data or deny their patients DMT access. The Jona Goldrich Center for Alzheimer's and Memory Disorders at Cedars‐Sinai Medical Center (CSMC) functions as a tertiary referral center for neurobehavioral patients. An interdisciplinary clinical panel evaluates patients being considered for DMT, as has been previously described. 26 This panel decided to consider non‐canonical AD patients for DMT, drawing from the available evidence and clinical expertise. We present here seven patients initiated on lecanemab who fall outside the trial population, some of whom have completed the first 18 months of biweekly infusions. These vignettes are intended to document an early “lived experience” of using anti‐amyloid therapy in real‐world patients and to explore the efficacy, safety, and medical decision‐making in an academic clinical practice.

In accordance with institutional guidelines, this project qualified for an exempt determination and did not require formal institutional review board oversight or a waiver of consent.

CASE SERIES

Patient 1

Patient 1 was a 57‐year‐old right‐handed man with 16 years of education and family history of dementia in his mother, father, maternal uncle, and grandfather, who presented with 2 years of cognitive symptoms (Table 1). At age 55, the patient was working as a social worker but could not keep up with his workload and experienced progressive difficulty managing documents, multitasking, following instructions, and driving. By 57 he was dependent on family for his instrumental activities of daily living (iADLs). On general neurologic exam, the patient demonstrated oculomotor apraxia and simultanagnosia. Initial Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) was 21/30 with 1 point lost for orientation, 4 for attention, 2 for recall, 1 for language, and 1 for construction. The Clinical Dementia Rating Scale Global (CDR‐G) was 0.5, and the CDR Sum of Boxes (CDR‐SB) was 3. Neuropsychological examination demonstrated deficits in attention, visuospatial function (construction, pattern recognition), language (phonemic and category fluency), and memory (verbal and visual retrieval).

Patient 2

Patient 2 was a 59‐year‐old right‐handed Mandarin‐speaking man with 20 years of education without significant past medical or family history who presented for visuospatial and memory impairments. At onset 3 years earlier, he experienced difficulty driving within lanes, identifying objects on a table, and typing, impairing his ability to work as an engineer. He developed memory difficulties characterized by misplacing objects and word‐finding difficulty. Reading became more difficult with frequent skipping of lines. His sense of spatial orientation worsened, with inability to navigate within new environments such as an airport or a new city. Despite challenges, he continued working, using ride‐hailing services for transportation. General neurologic examination was notable for oculomotor apraxia and simultanagnosia. MoCA was 21/30 with 1 point each lost for object copy, forward digit span, fluency, delayed recall and orientation, and 2 points for clock drawing and repetition. CDR‐G was 0.5, and CDR‐SB was 2. Extended neurobehavioral examination (ENBE) demonstrated slowed processing speed, impaired executive function (sequencing, motor programming, working memory), memory (verbal and visual encoding and retrieval), and visuospatial function (construction and perception). Due to the language barrier, language could not be fully assessed but there was indication of mild anomia.

A brain MRI at age 60 noted mild microangiopathic white matter changes, right greater than left mild to moderate dorsolateral parietal atrophy, mild left dorsolateral prefrontal atrophy, and a 1 cm pituitary lesion (adenoma vs. Rathke's cleft cyst). Amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) scan was elevated. Genetic testing indicated APOE ε3/ε3 genotype. The patient was diagnosed with MCI with PCA syndrome due to underlying AD. He started lecanemab and at time of writing had completed 8 months without adverse events or ARIA. Functionally he remained largely stable, independent with iADLs with increased difficulty navigating large spaces such as airports and grocery stores. MMSE after 4.5 months was 26/30 with 1 point each lost for language, and construction and 2 points lost for attention. CDR‐G and CDR‐SB were 0.5 and 2, respectively. MRI at 6.5 months was negative for ARIA, and MoCA was 18/30 with some improvements compared to baseline (full points for digit span and orientation and 1 point for repetition) and some losses (2 points each lost for clock drawing and attention, 4 points lost on delayed recall) (Table 1).

Patient 3

Patient 3 was a 56‐year‐old right‐handed man with 18 years of education and family history notable for late‐onset dementia in his mother and unknown age in his maternal grandmother, who presented for memory changes 3 years prior. At age 53, the patient first noticed symptoms at work, described as gradually worsening confusion and mental sluggishness leading to retirement. He developed progressive dependence on others for managing money, using technology, and scheduling appointments over the next 3 years. Concurrently, he developed significant behavioral symptoms with anxiety, depression, and worsening apathy for which he was started on bupropion and amphetamine/dextroamphetamine. General neurological exam was unremarkable. MoCA was 20/30 with 1 point each lost for clock drawing, reverse digit span, and repetition; 2 for serial subtraction; and 5 for delayed recall. Neuropsychological examination was notable for reduced processing speed, deficits in memory (verbal and visual retrieval), semantic fluency, and executive function (working memory, processing speed, impaired executive control). Visuospatial functions and other language functions were preserved.

A brain MRI at age 57 was read as normal. Fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) PET showed decreased metabolism in the biparietal, left more than right frontal, and lateral and medial temporal lobes. CSF showed Aβ42 536 (L), total tau 540 (H), p‐tau181 62.1 (H), and p‐tau/Aβ ratio 0.114 (H). Genetic testing indicated APOE ε3/ε3 genotype. The patient was diagnosed with MCI due to dysexecutive variant of AD. He started lecanemab and MoCA after 13 months was 16/30 with 1 more point each lost for object copy, digit span, serial subtraction, and orientation. At the time of writing he had completed 13.5 months of DMT without adverse events or ARIA.

Patient 4

Patient 4 was a 69‐year‐old right‐handed woman with 16 years of education without significant family history, who presented for language difficulty starting 3 years prior. The patient's initial symptoms began with name‐finding difficulty, followed by difficulty recalling uncommon words, then common words with circumlocutions and occasional substitutions. Verbal and written communication were also affected. Symptoms progressed over 2 years with worsening executive function. Her driving ability declined, with difficulty following streetlights and inability to navigate beyond a small radius from her home. She had difficulty recalling plans or following instructions when cooking. Functionally she developed dependence on her family for all iADLs. General neurological exam was unremarkable. MoCA at age 69 was 19/30 with 1 point each lost for naming, serial 7, repetition, fluency, and abstraction; 2 lost for orientation; 3 lost for clock drawing; and 5 lost for delayed verbal recall. Neuropsychological examination demonstrated deficits in memory (verbal and visual retrieval), language (non‐fluent with hesitation, word‐finding pauses with circumlocution, repetition errors, confrontational naming), and executive/frontal function (impulsivity, perseveration, working memory).

A brain MRI at age 69 showed mild microvascular changes and mild left predominant hippocampal and temporoparietal atrophy. FDG PET demonstrated focal left temporal hypometabolism. CSF testing showed Aβ42 390 (L), total tau 355 (H), p‐tau181 45.1 (H), and p‐tau/Aβ ratio 0.116 (H). Genetic testing indicated APOE ε3/ε3 genotype. The patient was diagnosed with lvPPA and mild dementia due to AD. At age 70, she started lecanemab. Her ability to carry out tasks in an organized fashion and to complete multi‐step activities of daily living further declined. At age 71, after 18 months of lecanemab, her MoCA was 10/30 with 1 point each lost for object copy, clock drawing, and phonemic fluency; 3 each for naming and serial 7; 2 for repetition, abstraction, and orientation; and 5 for delayed verbal recall. A brain MRI at this time was read as concerning for subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) but repeat MRI was negative. With the patient approaching moderate dementia, further benefit from lecanemab was questionable and the family requested repeat amyloid PET imaging to guide decision making. The scan was negative and ultimately prompted lecanemab discontinuation.

Patient 5

Patient 5 was an 81‐year‐old right‐handed woman with 18 years of education and family history of mother with unspecified dementia in her 90s who presented for 2 years of memory changes. Symptoms began after a fall with head‐strike complicated by SAH at age 79. Over the next year, she began to repeat herself in conversations and forget past life events. At presentation, the patient complained of forgetfulness, and difficulty with name finding and navigation. Despite these deficits she remained independent with all iADLs and was still fully employed. General neurological exam was unremarkable. MMSE was 26/30 with 2 points each lost for orientation and recall. The neuropsychological examination demonstrated impaired visual memory, language (confrontational naming, phonemic and semantic fluency). She had above‐average scores in working and verbal memory, visuospatial construction, and executive function.

A brain MRI at age 81 was notable for temporoparietal and hippocampal atrophy and susceptibility‐weighted angiography (SWAN) demonstrated deposition of hemosiderin along the right frontal lobe and the body of the left temporal horn (Figure 1). This MRI was stable compared to the MRI at the time of her fall at age 79 with no progression or new areas of hemosiderin deposit. An amyloid PET scan was positive. Genetic testing showed APOE ε3/ε3 genotype. The patient was diagnosed with MCI secondary to AD and started lecanemab. On her fourth surveillance MRI after 6 months of treatment, a new 2 mm focus of hemosiderin in the right frontal lobe was seen, consistent with mild ARIA with hemosiderin (ARIA‐H). The patient was asymptomatic and was approved to continue DMT but decided to discontinue treatment due to treatment burden.

Patient 6

Patient 6 was a 64‐year‐old right‐handed man with 13 years of education, and past medical history notable for hypertension, human immunodeficiency virus complicated by Kaposi's sarcoma with undetectable viral load at time of presentation, migraine, and family history notable for mother with unspecified dementia at age 80. His memory concerns began after a stroke occurring 1 year prior that presented as “unusual behavior.” A non‐contrast computed tomography (CT) angiogram showed occlusion of the V3/V4 segment of the left vertebral and P2 segment of the right posterior cerebral arteries. Brain MRI showed an acute infarct involving bilateral cerebellar hemispheres and the medial right temporal lobe, as well as moderate medial frontal atrophy and subcortical white matter changes. After hospitalization, the patient had residual bilateral left superior quadrantanopia. Etiology of the stroke was unclear with differential including left vertebral artery dissection, vessel‐to‐vessel embolism, or cardioembolic cause. At age 64, he presented to the emergency room for imbalance and weakness and was found to have a left M3 middle cerebral artery aneurysm without active bleeding. Symptoms resolved during hospital admission and the patient returned to prior baseline. The aneurysm was subsequently coiled at age 65.

Between ages 63 and 65, the patient began to mix up appointments and transportation arrangements. Despite the use of calendar and phone reminders, he was dependent on family to manage his schedule. He repeated himself and had difficulty learning how to use new applications at work, though he had not received any complaints regarding performance. MoCA at age 64 was 23/30 with 1 point each lost for object copy, reverse digit span, and delayed recall, and 2 points each for serial 7s and repetition. ENBE demonstrated impaired attention, mild dysexecutive deficits in verbal and visual memory, impaired executive function (sequencing, slowed processing speed), and intact visuospatial and language functions. At 65, his medication adherence declined, but he was otherwise functioning independently.

At age 66, he reported functional improvement. General neurological examination in our clinic was notable for left superior quadrantanopia but otherwise unremarkable. His MoCA was 26/30 with 1 each for delayed recall and orientation and 2 points lost for serial 7s. A brain MRI at age 64 demonstrated mild right posterior hippocampal atrophy with some progression of mild white matter disease since age 63. Brain MRI at age 65 redemonstrated chronic right occipital infarct with cortical laminar necrosis and mild scattered white matter changes (Figure 2). CSF testing showed Aβ42 690 (L), total tau 345 (H), p‐tau181 43.2 (H), and p‐tau/Aβ ratio 0.063 (H). Genetic testing showed APOE ε3/ε4 genotype. The patient was diagnosed with MCI due to AD.

The patient started lecanemab and at time of writing had completed 2.5 months without ARIA on surveillance MRIs. Immediately after the first infusion, he experienced mild vertigo and lightheadedness, which resolved within one and 8 h, respectively. Two days after the first infusion, he had a moderate migraine headache lasting the entire day. He awoke the third day with a severe migraine headache, prompting an emergency room visit. Brain MRI did not demonstrate ARIA or other acute changes. The headache resolved with a migraine cocktail and did not recur with subsequent lecanemab infusions. He reported diarrheal illness for 3 days after the second infusion, which resolved spontaneously.

Patient 7

Patient 7 was a 71‐year‐old right‐handed man with 16 years of education, past medical history of obstructive sleep apnea, and no notable family history, who presented for memory changes that began 2 years prior. The patient was still working at the time of symptom onset and described difficulty with organization such as managing his schedule and appointments, and trouble with simple mental mathematics and medication management. Around the same time, he began to experience excessive movements in sleep and perceive illusions of people around his home at night, with eventual development of delusions of presence. His neurological examination was notable for left‐sided upper extremity bradykinesia, mild rigidity, and left greater than right postural and resting upper extremity tremor. MMSE was 27/30 with 3 points lost for recall. ENBE demonstrated deficits in encoding and retrieval memory, visuospatial function (construction and number orientation on clock drawing), language (phonemic and semantic fluency), and executive function (difficulty with abstractions, pattern extension). MoCA at age 72 was 19/29 (Mini Trails‐B omitted due to video visit technical limitations). One point each was lost for object copy, clock drawing, serial 7s, and fluency, and 4 points were lost for delayed recall.

Brain MRI at age 72 was notable for mild anterior medial frontal atrophy, mild periventricular white matter changes, and a chronic left sub‐insular infarct without evidence of microhemorrhage. FDG PET showed mild symmetric hypometabolism of the occipital lobes. A dopamine transporter scan demonstrated decreased tracer activity along the posterior margins of the bilateral putamen. CSF showed Aβ42 502 (L), normal total tau 231, p‐tau181 22.5 (H), and p‐tau/Aβ ratio 0.045 (H). SynTap α‐synuclein seed amplification assay was positive. Genetic testing showed APOE ε3/ε4 genotype. The patient was diagnosed with MCI secondary to Lewy body disease with AD co‐pathology.

The patient started lecanemab and at the time of writing had completed 11.5 months without adverse events or ARIA. MoCA after 11 months was 12/30 with 1 point each lost for repetition, phonemic fluency, object copy, trails, and abstraction; 2 points lost for orientation, digit span, and attention; 3 points lost for the clock draw; and 4 points lost on memory. His LBD features progressed with worsened parkinsonism, bradyphrenia, fluctuations, and visual hallucinations.

DISCUSSION

Overview

On July 6, 2023, lecanemab was formally approved by the FDA with label indication for use in patients with AD in MCI or mild dementia stage. 10 The label is broader in scope than the original trial or the AUR, and while the AUR provide significant clarity surrounding many aspects of lecanemab therapy, from patient selection to ARIA management, guidance on patients with prior complex medical conditions or less common cognitive syndromes is not detailed.

Our DMT review panel identified patients who met the pre‐requisite requirements to receive anti‐amyloid therapy according to cognitive score, disease stage, and amyloid status, but who also possessed exclusionary or lacked inclusionary characteristics, preventing them from receiving this treatment if strictly following the clinical trial criteria or the AUR. Our team felt that these patients should be considered. We carefully reviewed each case, focusing on the exclusionary condition and its potential effect on the risks and benefits of DMT. In some cases, our team members consulted with experienced DMT expert clinicians outside of our institution to glean insights on how we might proceed.

All patients considering DMT at our practice meet with providers at least three times before DMT starts. Our initial introduction to DMT occurs once enough data are available to begin the conversation (e.g., history, cognitive score, MRI). A follow‐up discussion happens after APOE and amyloid status are known. Once approved by the panel, a final discussion, which includes signing of an informed consent document, is reviewed and signed. This procedure was followed for all seven of these atypical patients with added emphasis on the uncertainty of risks and benefits for their particular cases, and additional verification that the patient and supportive care partner understood this information. Currently, only two of seven patients have completed 18 months of induction therapy, and six of seven have completed the first 6 months, when ARIA are most likely to occur.

Non‐amnestic presentations

The non‐canonical phenotypes of the first four patients—PCA, frontal‐variant AD, and lvPPA—would not have been included in the Clarity AD study as non‐amnestic syndromes did not meet the inclusion criteria for the trial. The AUR does allow such patients to be considered for anti‐amyloid treatments. 6 , 11 We concluded that the amyloid portion of their pathology would be largely similar to that of the target population and therefore they were considered eligible for treatment with lecanemab, even if the progression of tau was different than the majority amnestic population. 16 , 17

The larger challenge was that of staging. Progression and scoring on screening tests in this population can vary significantly from more typical AD presentations, with studies reporting more rapid progression and variation depending on the given syndrome. 13 , 27 This variation is often reflected by MMSE and MoCA scores below the acceptable cut‐off for DMT. Data suggest earlier treatment has greater potential benefit, necessitating diligent assessment to determine an accurate disease stage. 28 To account for this, we grounded our eligibility assessment in overall functional status. At the minimum, each patient in our cohort at the time of initiation was independent with all basic ADLs, reflective of mild stage dementia. In addition, the CDR, which combines cognitive ability and functional status, was utilized for some cases. An example is patient 1, whose MMSE was 21, but CDR was 0.5, illustrating this patient clearly fell within the MCI range. In addition, while ENBE or formal neuropsychological testing was administered to all our patients, a brief 2‐hour battery was specifically used for patients 1 and 4 to provide additional cognitive profile functional status data.

Over the course of therapy, all four non‐amnestic patients demonstrated decline on objective testing. Patients 1 through 3 remained functionally stable on ADLs/IADLs. Patient 4 had a much more progressive course. She was still in the mild dementia stage at lecanemab initiation, though she had experienced a sharp decline in ADLs in the 2 years prior to her initial presentation. Given her continued independence on some iADLs, there was hope that her decline in testing performance was disproportionately affected by language loss rather than a true global cognitive decline. However, it may be that her outcome was less favorable compared to the others in this group because she started therapy at a later disease stage.

Cases with cerebrovascular disease and prior cerebrovascular events

The phase 2 and 3 lecanemab trials and AUR allow for mild to moderate microvascular changes and for a lacunar infarct. Four of seven patients in our series had microvascular disease on MRI, and one had a sub‐insular infarct. Patients 5 and 6 had evidence of prior cerebrovascular events listed as exclusion criteria within the AUR. 11

Ruling out underlying cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) and previous hemorrhagic stroke became the primary factor in our assessment for DMT qualification. Both cases underwent a thorough review of their prior imaging, including evaluation for progression over time of microvascular damage and microbleeds on susceptibility weighted imaging (SWI). We also reviewed these cases for uncontrolled hypertension, as this is a risk factor for routine hemorrhagic stroke and ARIA‐H. 29 Our clinic does not offer lecanemab to patients with severe microvascular disease given concern that this may represent CAA according to the Boston 2.0 criteria. 30 The mechanism of ARIA‐H from DMT occurs from interaction between anti‐amyloid monoclonal antibodies and vascular amyloid aggregates as well as CAA‐derived amyloid fibrils. 31 , 32

The concern for patient 5 was the mechanism of the superficial siderosis: trauma or CAA. Hemosiderin from TBI results from tearing of vessels and formation of fibrin clots, with completion of bleed expansion usually within the first 72 hours after injury, 33 whereas hemosiderin from CAA is a result of accumulation of amyloid fibrils in vessel walls causing them to lose integrity and become leaky. 34 This patient's MRIs demonstrated stability of the hemosiderin deposits and lack of microbleeds since the TBI at age 79. We interpreted this as evidence of prior head injury and not suggestive of underlying CAA.

The risk for future bleeding on DMT after TBI is unknown. Looking to literature on traumatic intracranial hemorrhage for guidance, it is safe to implement oral anti‐coagulation within 72 hours if there is no change on imaging after 24 hours. 32 With this information and after consultation with experts at other academic centers, our team determined that anti‐amyloid therapy could be offered. As outlined in section 2.5, the patient demonstrated asymptomatic, mild ARIA‐H on routine surveillance MRI just after 6 months. Per guidelines for management of ARIA, 11 our team felt she could continue therapy with monthly monitoring MRIs until it was felt the ARIA‐H was stable.

Patient 6 had a known prior large PCA stroke. The first step in our assessment was determining that he had in fact experienced a stroke due to an ischemic event and not due to hemorrhage. Review of serial MRIs demonstrated stability for 3 years prior to presentation without evidence of new infarcts or hemosiderin deposits. His medical history was notable for hypertension at the time of his stroke, but it was better controlled in the year leading up to consideration for lecanemab infusion. Our team again consulted with external academic experts and determined that given his early stage of disease and ε3/ε3 status, he could be a candidate for DMT.

Neurodegenerative co‐pathology

Patient 7 would have been excluded by the criteria for Clarity AD and the AUR due to presence of Lewy body pathology accompanying AD. Despite this, our team decided the presence of confirmed AD CSF biomarkers, MCI stage, and APOE ε3/ε4 status made him eligible to receive DMT. Co‐pathology with AD is known to worsen outcomes for LBD patients. 24 , 25 In theory, slowing the progression of one of the neurodegenerative processes present in this patient might slow functional decline. With FDA approval, the ability to evaluate the efficacy of amyloid DMTs in patients with co‐pathology is now possible, and following these patients may help answer the question of whether treating the AD will be of benefit.

Outcomes and takeaways

Given the uncertainties surrounding treatment in non‐canonical AD patients, our chief concern was safety. While no definitive conclusions can be drawn from a small series, the incidence of ARIA within our cohort was not outside the range reported in the Clarity AD trial population. Patient 5 was the only one to demonstrate mild ARIA in our sample and we chose to follow AUR on how to proceed. The AUR suggest following a patient monthly after an ARIA event to monitor for progression until it is felt that the ARIA has stabilized. This patient was the most concerning from a safety perspective given prior hemosiderin deposit and the necessity to discern its initial origin. In such cases, it is important to thoroughly investigate all data to make an informed decision on whether to offer DMT. In addition, it may be necessary to increase the frequency of surveillance MRIs beyond the AUR, for example, monthly throughout the first 6 months.

Cognitive screening scores for AD patients with PPA or PCA may fall below the required score due to difficulties with language and visual spatial skills that significantly affect test‐taking abilities. Eligibility for DMT in these patients requires further assessment with emphasis on functional ability. CDR can be a helpful tool because it combines function and cognition, and the scores of 0.5 and 1.0 are understood to be equivalent to MCI and mild dementia, respectively. 35 Neuropsychological batteries are also useful to determine cognitive profile and functional status eligibility in cases in which the MMSE and MoCA may overestimate the extent of cognitive deficits.

In terms of clinical benefit, the limited number of cases makes it difficult for our team to determine whether our patients derived benefit from DMT. Most patients in the series declined on cognitive testing but remained functionally stable. However, it is notable that patients 4 and 7—who had PPA and LBD co‐pathology, respectively—have continued to demonstrate a steady trend of functional decline. The cause of their apparent lack of response is unclear. In patient 4's case there are two considerations, the first being the differences between amyloid and tau deposition in PPA as opposed to more typical AD, which has been noted to have disproportionate development of tau pathology, and the second being timing of treatment, which was started at the mild dementia stage. 16 , 17 This is consistent with the results of the Clarity AD trial, which demonstrated that the degree of functional impairment prior to infusion therapy initiation is inversely correlated with efficacy of treatment. 28 In patient 7's case, our suspicion for progression was highest for continued progression of the underlying Lewy body pathology.

CONCLUSIONS

While the availability of DMTs has dramatically changed treatment of AD, the determination of efficacy of these therapies rests upon canonical amnestic AD patients with MCI or mild dementia. Formal studies inclusive of a broad range of AD phenotypes and co‐pathologies of cerebrovascular and neurodegenerative conditions are rarely conducted due to the inherent challenge of statistical power and control of multiple variables. In the absence of broader inclusion in research, clinicians must rely on shared data and expertise to bridge the gap between official indications as provided by FDA, AUR, and real‐world practice in the new era of amyloid DMT. In the future, clinicians may have a clearer picture of DMT and their usage across AD patient phenotypes, as present‐day large collaborative efforts begin to amass this valuable information. 36
