The role of mesencephalic astrocyte-derived neurotrophic factor in digestive diseases
Authors: Shiyun Pu, Jiamin Yan, Tong Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMesencephalic astrocyte-derived neurotrophic factor (MANF) is an evolutionarily conserved endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-resident and secreted protein highly expressed in the digestive system. Emerging evidence has highlighted its pivotal roles in liver diseases, as well as in other digestive diseases including intestinal and pancreatic disorders. This review summarizes the expression, molecular mechanisms, and pathological roles of MANF in digestive diseases and evaluates its translational potential as a therapeutic target and biomarker.DiscussionMANF exerts effects primarily through ER stress regulation, antioxidative responses, and regulation of inflammatory signaling cascades. In liver diseases, MANF plays a context-dependent dual roles. It protects against alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD), metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), drug-induced liver injury (DILI), and ischemia/reperfusion injury, yet promotes immune tolerance in chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection and acts as an oncogenic driver in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC). In pancreatic disorders, MANF protects β-cells from ER stress-induced apoptosis in diabetes and mitigates acute pancreatitis. In intestinal diseases, it ameliorates inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC). The function of MANF are determined by cellular context, disease stage, and specific microenvironmental signals.ConclusionMANF emerges as a key regulator of digestive tract homeostasis. Targeting MANF may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for preventing and treating digestive diseases. Its dynamic expression patterns also suggest possible biomarker utility. Further studies are needed to optimize targeted interventions and validate its clinical translation.Keywords: Digestive diseases, intestine, liver, MANF, pancreas

Introduction

Mesencephalic astrocyte-derived neurotrophic factor (MANF), first identified in 2003 as a dopaminergic neurotrophic factor [1], is an 18-kDa endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-resident protein [2]. It is widely expressed in both the nervous system and non-neuronal tissues [3]. Notably, high levels of MANF mRNA and protein are detected in the digestive system of adult mice and developing embryos [4], suggesting tissue-specific functional importance. Beyond its established roles in neurodegenerative [5,6], metabolic [7,8], cardiovascular [9,10], renal [11,12], and retina diseases [13], as well as alcohol-related neurotoxicity [14], emerging evidence in humans and murine models has highlighted its critical contributions to the pathogenesis of digestive diseases, including hepatic, intestinal and pancreatic disorders. This review summarizes current understanding of MANF’s function in various digestive diseases, with a particular focus on its role in liver diseases, and evaluates its translational potential as a diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target.

Expression of MANF in digestive system

MANF is highly expressed throughout the digestive system. Its specific cellular localization varies across organs. In the liver, MANF is detected in hepatocytes and hepatic macrophages, with conflicting reports regarding its presence in hepatic stellate cells (HSC) [15,16]. Both human databases and experimental studies have confirmed substantial MANF expression in hepatic myeloid lineages [17,18]. Within the pancreas, MANF is localized to insulin-positive β-cells and acinar cells in both mice and humans [8], and is detected in ductal cells and δ cells, but not in α cells or pancreatic polypeptide cells [19–21]. Its expression in other pancreatic endocrine cells remains unconfirmed. In the intestine, MANF is observed in epithelial cells, the lamina propria, colonic crypts, and muscle layers [22,23], as well as in mucosal tissues of mice and humans [24]. It is notably enriched in pro-inflammatory macrophages in the lamina propria [24]. Evidence regarding its expression in other intestinal cell types remains limited.

Notably, hepatic MANF expression is dynamically regulated in response to dietary factors. High-salt diet upregulates MANF in mice [25], whereas essential oil supplementation suppresses its expression in the liver of lambs [26]. Dietary restriction elevates hepatic MANF in bovines, with levels reversing upon re-alimentation [27]. Switching from a high-fat to a standard diet increases MANF expression in rodents [28]. Although direct evidence for such dietary regulation in the pancreas and intestine is currently limited, the responsiveness of hepatic MANF underscores its potential role as a nutrient-sensitive regulator within digestive tract biology.

Role of MANF in liver diseases

Liver diseases rank as the 11th leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for over 2 million annual deaths [29]. This escalating health burden underscores the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies, particularly for challenging conditions like metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), where effective treatments remain elusive. Dysregulated hepatic and circulating MANF levels have been reported in different liver diseases, positioning MANF as a key mediator in disease progression. Recent studies have highlighted the multifaceted role of MANF in liver diseases.

MANF and alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD)

As the primary site of ethanol metabolism, the liver is highly susceptible to alcohol-induced injury. Pathophysiological processes in ALD involve complex interactions among ER stress, oxidative damage, inflammatory signaling, and autophagic dysfunction. Studies using ethanol-fed murine models and analysis of the GSE28619 dataset have consistently shown upregulation of hepatic MANF in both alcoholic hepatitis animal models and patients [30–34]. Chhetri et al. first reported that hepatocyte-specific MANF knockout exacerbated ALD by inducing ER stress through CYP2E1/oxidative stress pathways and enhancing inflammation via the iNOS/NF-κB signaling axis [30]. Subsequent studies have further confirmed these protective effects and uncovered additional mechanisms. Zhang et al. revealed that liver-specific MANF deletion aggravated ethanol-induced steatosis, associated with altered Stat3 phosphorylation and Atg5 transcription [33]. Xu et al. found that hepatocyte-specific MANF deletion deteriorated alcohol-induced liver steatosis by reducing ASS1 activity and AMPK phosphorylation [32]. Additionally, S100A16 deletion was found to ameliorate ALD by upregulating MANF [31]. Recently, myeloid-specific MANF deletion has been shown to exacerbate ethanol-induced liver injury, steatosis, neutrophil infiltration, and reactive oxygen species production by inhibiting the neutrophilic p38 MAPK/PU.1/miR-223 axis [34]. Collectively, MANF predominantly plays a protective role in ALD by regulating ER stress, metabolic homeostasis, and inflammation, thereby alleviating ethanol-induced liver injury. Its effects are cell-type-dependent. Both hepatocyte-derived and myeloid-derived MANF mediate these protective effects via distinct molecular mechanisms. Overall, MANF exhibits clear cytoprotective functions in ALD, positioning it as a potential promising therapeutic target.

MANF and MASLD

The MASLD spectrum includes isolated liver steatosis, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), fibrosis, and cirrhosis [35]. In vitro studies have shown differential MANF responses to lipotoxic stress. MANF protein levels were increased in primary hepatocytes treated with palmitic acid (PA) for 24 h [16], while HepG2 cells exposed to free fatty acids (FFAs) displayed biphasic expression (upregulation at 24 h, followed by downregulation at 48 h and 72 h) [36]. MANF mRNA and protein levels were found to be decreased in genetic and dietary murine models of MASLD [16,36]. Serum MANF levels were also lower in MASH patients [37]. Paradoxically, MANF expression was elevated in fibrotic livers [15,38,39], suggesting stage-specific regulation and a potential dual role in hepatoprotection and pro-fibrogenic responses.

The discrepancy between MANF expression in cellular models and liver tissues may be explained by stimulation complexity, temporal dynamics, and cellular heterogeneity. Cells are subjected to a single lipotoxic factor in vitro, whereas liver tissues are exposed to a synergistic pathological microenvironment involving lipid accumulation, persistent inflammation, oxidative stress, and immune cell infiltration, which collectively represses MANF expression in MASLD. The biphasic MANF expression in HepG2 cells reflects an acute lipotoxic response, while in vivo MASLD is a chronic progressive process that aligns with the late downregulation phase of MANF. Moreover, liver tissues consist of hepatocytes, macrophages, HSC and other cell types, unlike the hepatocyte-only focus of in vitro experiments. The differential MANF expression in MASLD and liver fibrosis may be attributable to two factors. First, MANF deficiency in MASH impairs lipid metabolism and promotes steatosis, while sustained ER stress in fibrosis triggers compensatory MANF upregulation. Second, hepatocyte-derived MANF dominates regulation of lipid homeostasis in the early steatosis stage, whereas macrophage-derived MANF mediated inflammation and HSC activation in the late fibrosis stage.

Functionally, MANF modulates hepatic lipid metabolism. MANF overexpression in HepG2 cells and murine liver decreased lipid accumulation by suppressing de novo lipogenesis and lipid uptake pathways [7,36]. Hypothalamic proopiomelanocortin neuron-specific MANF knockout and overexpression similarly affected hepatic lipid deposition, indicating a central nervous system-mediated regulatory axis [40]. In MASH and fibrosis models, MANF demonstrated pleiotropic protective effects. MANF knockout in hepatocytes exacerbated steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis, while MANF overexpression rescued these pathological phenotypes. Mechanistically, MANF suppressed lipid synthesis by downregulating Srebp1 and Lipin1 via the LXR pathway and dampened inflammation cascades by suppressing the NF-κB signaling pathway [16]. In CCl4-induced fibrosis, hepatocyte-specific MANF deficiency promoted high mobility group protein B1 (HMGB1) nucleus-to-cytoplasm translocation and accelerated mitochondrial dysfunction, resulting in HSC activation and hepatocyte apoptosis [39]. In hepatic macrophages, MANF competitively bound S100A8 to inhibit the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway, limiting pro-inflammatory macrophage differentiation and HSC activation [15]. Another study showed MANF ablation in mono-macrophages promoted CCl4-induced hepatic fibrosis [41], underscoring its cell-type-dependent antifibrotic effects. Additionally, peroxiredoxin 6 (PRDX6) bound to the C-terminus of MANF, promoting MANF secretion, and vice versa. The reciprocal interactions between MANF and PRDX6 suggested a novel axis for inhibiting HSC activation and liver fibrosis [42]. In addition, a vital role for MANF in age-related liver damage, fibrosis, and steatosis was also reported [37].

In summary, MANF shows a context-dependent dual role in the progression from MASH to liver fibrosis. Hepatocyte-derived MANF regulates lipid homeostasis, while macrophage-derived MANF dominates anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic effects. The contradictory expression pattern and functional shift are closely related to disease stage, involved cell types, and integration of local microenvironmental signals. Recognizing this complexity is crucial for translating MANF into a viable therapeutic target, which implies that future intervention strategies may need to precisely modulate the MANF pathway in a stage-specific and cell-targeted manner. Despite its significant potential, the clinical translation of MANF-based therapies requires further mechanistic validation and development of targeted delivery strategies.

MANF and viral hepatitis

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains a leading cause of chronic liver inflammation. MANF expression was elevated in HBV infection. Both hepatocytes and myeloid cells in HBV-positive mice showed elevated MANF mRNA levels [18]. Similarly, liver tissues from hepatitis B patients in phase IV exhibited significantly higher MANF levels than those from patients in phase I [43]. MANF levels in both liver and peripheral blood were gradually increased with fibrosis progression after chronic HBV infection [44,45]. Strong MANF immunoreactivity was also detected in liver tissues of patients with HBV infection and HCC [38].

Studies have demonstrated a regulatory role for myeloid-derived MANF in HBV pathogenesis. Myeloid-specific MANF knockout mice exhibited reduced serum hepatitis B surface antigen and envelope antigen. In HBV-positive myeloid-specific MANF knockout mice, hepatic hepatitis B core antigen protein and HBV RNA levels were lower, indicating enhanced viral clearance efficiency. Mechanistically, MANF deficiency enhanced HBV-induced immune response by inhibiting myeloid-derived suppressor cell expansion in the liver via the MAPK/IL-6/STAT3 signaling pathway. Furthermore, siMANF-based vaccination therapy cleared HBV in a murine model [18]. Thus, MANF exhibits a pathogenic role by promoting immune tolerance and viral persistence. Specifically, myeloid-derived MANF dampens antiviral immunity by promoting MDSC expansion. Therefore, targeting MANF represents a potential therapeutic strategy to enhance antiviral responses and promote HBV clearance.

MANF and carcinoma

Chronic liver disease typically progresses to cirrhosis and liver cancer. HCC accounts for the majority of primary liver cancer cases [46]. The expression of MANF in HCC remains contentious. Liu et al. found lower MANF levels in tumor tissues from both diethylnitrosamine (DEN)-induced HCC mice and HCC patients [47]. Conversely, another study reported higher MANF expression in HCC tissues by bioinformatics and experimental analyses [48]. M2-like tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) predominate in advanced HCC tissues and promote HCC progression. MANF levels were decreased in TAMs from HCC patients and DEN-induced mouse models [17]. The prognostic value of MANF is also debatable. MANF was identified as a hub gene in HCC by bioinformatics analysis. Further analysis showed MANF was an adverse prognostic biomarker for overall survival (OS) but not disease-free survival (DFS) [49]. He et al. linked high MANF expression to shorter DFS and higher recurrence risk, with no OS difference [48]. On the contrary, Liu et al. reported that patients in the MANF-high group had prolonged OS and relapse-free survival (RFS) [47]. Studies have explored the cell-type-specific mechanisms. Hepatocyte-specific MANF deletion accelerated DEN-induced HCC. MANF nuclear import and MANF-p65 interaction were enhanced, inhibiting the NF-κB/Snail signaling pathway and subsequently suppressing inflammation, hepatocyte epithelial mesenchymal transition (EMT), and HCC progression. In macrophages, MANF interacted with HSP70-1 and enhanced the HSF1-HSP70-1 interaction, reducing HSF1 nuclear translocation and downregulating HSP70-1 levels. As a result, TAMs were reprogrammed into the M1 phenotype, and neovascularization of HCC was suppressed. Myeloid-specific MANF knockout promoted tumor neovascularization by disrupting the HSP70-1/HSF1 interaction [17]. Additionally, MANF was regulated by METTL3-mediated m6A modification and sustained ER proteostasis, counteracting tumor development [50].

The seemingly conflicting findings on MANF’s role in HCC can be systematically explained by the following determinants. First, MANF exerts protective effects during early hepatocarcinogenesis, while in advanced HCC tumors, its expression in specific cellular compartments might support a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment. Second, hepatocyte-derived MANF inhibits EMT and inflammation, while TAM-derived MANF modulates angiogenesis and immune responses. Third, genetic mouse models show cell-type-specific mechanisms, whereas bioinformatics analyses of human patient cohorts capture cumulative effects of MANF from diverse cellular sources and disease stages. Fourth, the function of intracellular and secreted MANF may differentially influence tumor progression, but these two forms are rarely distinguished. The intracellular form may primarily manage cell-autonomous stress, while the secreted form could exert paracrine immunomodulatory effects. Finally, elevated MANF expression in late-stage HCC tumors may represent a compensatory response to severe ER stress rather than a direct driver of tumor aggressiveness. Moreover, divergent conclusions regarding MANF’s correlation with survival outcomes (DFS, OS, RFS) further stem from variations in tumor stage, etiology, treatment history, and statistical adjustments for prognostic covariates. Collectively, the prognostic value of MANF is strongly context-dependent. Future studies with stratified patient cohorts are needed to clarify its clinical relevance. Notably, MANF’s role in HCC is closely linked to tumor stage, cellular ecosystem, and the stress microenvironment.

Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) is the second most common primary liver cancer, accounting for up to 20% of all cases [51]. A recent study showed an oncogenic role of MANF in ICC pathogenesis. MANF mRNA and protein levels were significantly increased in the liver from sleeping beauty transposon (SBT) or thioacetamide (TAA)-induced ICC mouse models. In ICC patients, MANF expression was elevated in cancer tissues and serum, with high levels predicting poor prognosis [52]. Hepatocyte-specific MANF knock-in and knockout accelerated and decelerated ICC progression, respectively. MANF suppressed membrane translocation of CK19 by binding to Ser35 of CK19. Increased cytosolic CK19 interacted with NICD2 and triggered Notch signaling, thereby promoting transformation of hepatocytes into ICC cells.

In summary, MANF plays a multifaceted role in hepatocarcinogenesis, acting as either a tumor suppressor or promoter. The pathological outcome of MANF signaling is not intrinsic to the protein itself but is dictated by cellular and molecular context. HCC derives from hepatocytes, whereas ICC originates from cholangiocytes or transdifferentiated hepatocytes, which may result in the inconsistent expression and function of MANF in these two malignancies. The distinct components and inflammatory profiles of the tumor microenvironments also contribute to differential effects of MANF. Inter-study methodological variations, such as inconsistent detection approaches, may represent another source of discrepant conclusions regarding MANF’s function. Collectively, these findings position MANF as a candidate for therapeutic targeting, with cell type-specific strategies offering avenues for precision medicine, requiring additional mechanistic research and verification.

MANF and drug-induced liver injury (DILI)

DILI is a clinically significant adverse drug reaction with potentially severe complications. Rifampicin (RFP), a first-line antituberculosis medication with known hepatotoxicity, upregulated MANF expression in both HepG2 cells and mouse models [53,54]. MANF knockdown in HepG2 cells exacerbated RFP-induced unfolded protein response (UPR), increased cell apoptosis, and reduced proliferation [55]. Hepatic MANF alleviated RFP-induced cholestatic injury by enhancing Nrf2-mediated bile acid transport regulation [54]. The PERK-ATF4-CHOP axis has emerged as a critical pathway mediating DILI pathogenesis [56]. Hepatocyte-specific MANF knockout worsened RFP-induced liver injury by inducing pathological proliferation and apoptosis through the ATF4-CHOP signaling pathway [53].

APAP overdose leads to hepatocellular necrosis. MANF expression was increased in the livers of APAP-treated mice, primary mouse hepatocytes, and serum from both murine models and patients with APAP-induced liver injury. This upregulation was more pronounced in macrophages than in hepatocytes [57,58]. Hepatocyte-specific MANF knockout exacerbated APAP-induced hepatotoxicity by suppressing detoxification enzymes, promoting glutathione depletion, and enhancing apoptosis through activation of the PERK-eIF2α-ATF4-CHOP signaling pathway [59]. Conversely, MANF overexpression protected against APAP-induced hepatotoxicity [57]. However, Hou et al. reported a cell-type-dependent discrepancy. Hepatocyte-specific MANF deletion mice showed similar liver injury to wild-type (WT) mice. Myeloid-specific MANF knockout delayed liver repair after APAP treatment by reducing IL-10 production and phagocytosis via the p38 MAPK pathway, thereby inhibiting restorative polarization of macrophages [58]. Thus, these findings suggest distinct protective mechanisms of MANF in parenchymal and non-parenchymal hepatic compartments. Hepatocyte-derived MANF may primarily mitigate ER stress and apoptosis, whereas macrophage-derived MANF may regulate inflammatory responses. The reported differences in hepatocyte-derived MANF function may be explained by the following factors. First, methodological variations, including APAP dosage, administration routes, and endpoints measured, can lead to divergent experimental conclusions. Second, disease stage and injury severity likely differ. MANF may exert protective effects in mild-to-moderate APAP injury by resolving ER stress, but its protective capacity may be exhausted in severe overdose.

Collectively, MANF primarily serves a protective role in DILI. Its mechanisms are compartment-specific. Hepatocyte MANF alleviates injury mainly by modulating the UPR and apoptosis pathways, while myeloid MANF facilitates tissue repair by promoting restorative macrophage polarization and IL-10 production. The consistent upregulation of MANF across different drug-induced injury models (RFP and APAP) underscores its potential adaptive and protective function.

MANF and liver transplantation

Liver transplantation is the definitive therapeutic intervention for end-stage liver disease. Its outcomes are influenced by hepatic ischemia/reperfusion injury (IRI) and liver regeneration. IRI, a major contributor to early allograft dysfunction and graft failure, induced ER stress-mediated upregulation and secretion of MANF in both murine models and human patients [60,61]. Hepatocyte-specific MANF knockout aggravated ischemia/reperfusion-induced hepatocyte apoptosis and liver injury by activating the IRE1α/JNK/c-Jun/CHOP and PERK/ATF4/CHOP signaling pathways, whereas recombinant human MANF protein alleviated IRI [61]. Liver regeneration is crucial for prognosis after liver transplantation [62]. After 2/3 partial hepatectomy (PH), both mRNA and protein levels of MANF were upregulated in a time-dependent manner. MANF deletion attenuated proliferation and cell cycle progression of hepatocytes, delaying hepatic recovery after 2/3 PH. Mechanistically, MANF bound to the extracellular segment of LRP5 and promoted nuclear translocation of β-catenin, activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [63]. Taken together, MANF exerts protective effects at two critical stages in liver transplantation: IRI and regeneration. It mitigates IRI by suppressing ER stress-mediated apoptosis and promotes hepatocyte proliferation by activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Thus, targeted MANF delivery is expected to optimize graft viability and post-transplant recovery. Further preclinical and clinical investigation is needed.

In summary, accumulating evidence has underscored the critical and multifaceted role of MANF across the spectrum of liver diseases (Figure 1). Its expression is dynamically regulated throughout disease progression, highlighting its role as a candidate biomarker and a key mediator of hepatic pathophysiology. Its specificity and sensitivity as a diagnostic or prognostic biomarker still need to be validated in multicenter, large-sample clinical studies. The role of MANF is not simply protective or pathogenic but strictly context-dependent. This duality is determined by the disease type, disease stage, cellular origin, and molecular form. In ALD, DILI, IRI, and liver regeneration, MANF mainly exerts a cytoprotective effect. Its core mechanisms involve regulation of ER stress, apoptotic pathways, and inflammatory responses, thereby promoting hepatocyte survival and tissue repair. However, in chronic HBV infection, myeloid-derived MANF exhibits a pathogenic role by promoting immune tolerance. This functional divergence is also observed within individual disease progression. In MASLD, hepatocyte-derived MANF plays a protective role in the early steatotic stage by regulating lipid metabolism. However, in advanced fibrosis, MANF is compensatorily upregulated in response to sustained ER stress, with myeloid cell-derived MANF acting as the key mediator of anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic effects, yet carrying potential compensatory pro-fibrogenic risk. MANF is also an oncogenic factor in ICC, as hepatocyte-derived MANF promotes malignant transformation. In liver carcinoma, MANF functions differ drastically between HCC and ICC. HCC features a cell- and stage-dependent role for MANF, where hepatocyte-derived MANF acts as a tumor suppressor in early hepatocarcinogenesis and TAM-derived MANF in established tumors can promote angiogenesis and immune evasion. Conversely, MANF is an oncogenic driver in ICC, directly promoting malignant transdifferentiation via Notch signaling activation. Consequently, MANF is neither intrinsically ‘good’ nor ‘bad’. Its effect is an integrated result of cell-type-specific expression, disease-phase-specific signals, and balance between its intracellular and extracellular actions. Future translational strategies must move beyond simplistic enhancement or inhibition. Instead, researchers should aim to regulate specific molecular forms or downstream pathways at appropriate disease stage and in specific cell types to harness MANF’s therapeutic potential while avoiding unintended detrimental effects.

Role of MANF in pancreatic diseases

MANF and pancreatic endocrine diseases

MANF exerts regulatory effects on pancreatic endocrine and exocrine functions, with significant implications for diabetes, pancreatitis, and age-related pancreatic disorders. Research on MANF in pancreatic endocrine islets has expanded steadily. It played a crucial role in pancreatic β-cell survival, proliferation, and function. MANF expression was downregulated in β-cell aging models [65], while its expression and secretion were increased in human β-cells upon stimulation with IL-17 and TNF-α, which are critical contributors to β-cell apoptosis in type 1 diabetes (T1DM) [19]. MANF levels were also significantly increased in patients with hepatic lipid disorder (HLD) and type 2 diabetes (T2DM) and in cells subjected to ER stress induced by glucose, PA, and thapsigargin [66].

MANF deficiency in mice leaded to progressive postnatal insulin-deficient diabetes due to reduced β-cell proliferation and increased apoptosis caused by sustained ER stress [8,21]. Homozygous loss-of-function mutations in the MANF gene in humans resulted in childhood-onset syndromic diabetes with impaired β-cell function driven by elevated ER stress [67]. MANF held significant implications for various pancreatic diseases, particularly diabetes. In T1DM, AAV-mediated β-cell-specific MANF delivery in non-obese diabetic mice reduced the rate of diabetes development, and in vitro, MANF minimized insulitis and preserved insulin production [68]. In T2DM, serum MANF levels were higher in patients with newly diagnosed T2DM. Glucose, palmitate, and thapsigargin-induced ER stress upregulated MANF expression in β-cells in a concentration-dependent manner [66]. In Wolfram syndrome, MANF prevented ER stress-induced β-cell death and enhanced β-cell proliferation [69]. In β-cell aging, MANF was downregulated in H2O2-treated MIN6 cells and d-galactose-induced aging mice, accompanied by elevated insulin resistance and impaired glucose tolerance. Human recombinant MANF reversed these aging-related phenotypes [65]. Mechanistically, MANF regulated inflammatory signaling in cytokine-stressed human β-cells by suppressing NF-κB activation and Bcl10 [70] and promoted autophagy turnover to mitigate ER stress [66] (Figure 2A).

In summary, in pancreatic endocrine diseases such as diabetes, MANF exerts protective effects in β-cells, maintaining cell survival and function by alleviating ER stress, inhibiting apoptosis, and promoting autophagy. Its upregulation in different contexts such as T1DM, T2DM, and Wolfram syndrome often represents an adaptive stress response. Exogenous supplementation of MANF shows therapeutic potential.

MANF and pancreatic exocrine diseases

The function of MANF in pancreatic exocrine pathologies remains poorly understood. Chronic pancreatitis is a progressive inflammatory disease. It is invariably diagnosed at an advanced, irreversible stage, resulting in current treatments focusing on alleviating complications rather than targeting underlying disease processes. Therefore, novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies targeting early detection and intervention are required. Wu et al. demonstrated in mouse pancreatic acinar cells that alcohol exposure upregulated MANF expression. Exogenous MANF or adenovirus-mediated MANF overexpression ameliorated alcohol-induced injury and improved cell survival by inhibiting the IRE1α/caspase12/caspase3 apoptotic pathway (Figure 2B). Conversely, MANF knockdown with siRNA exacerbated damage, supporting its protective role in alcoholic pancreatitis via ER stress attenuation [71]. Consistently, another study showed that pancreatic-specific deficiency of MANF exacerbated severity of both caerulein- and alcohol-induced acute pancreatitis. MANF deficiency aggravated key pathological processes, including enhanced ER stress and UPR activation, increased acinar cell apoptosis, amplified inflammatory signaling, and heightened oxidative stress. Notably, these detrimental effects exhibited sexual dimorphism, with male mice showing more pronounced inflammatory and oxidative stress responses [72]. A cross-sectional study revealed cell-type-specific alterations in MANF expression in chronic pancreatitis [20]. Ductal MANF was significantly elevated in chronic alcoholic pancreatitis (CAP) compared to non-alcoholic cases and normal controls, and was positively correlated with fibrosis severity. Conversely, acinar MANF was reduced in CAP, suggesting a complex role of MANF in chronic pancreatitis pathogenesis.

Therefore, MANF inhibits ER stress-mediated acinar cell apoptosis and suppresses inflammatory and oxidative stress in acute alcoholic/caerulein-induced pancreatitis. In CAP, MANF exhibits cell-type-specific expression alterations that are closely linked to fibrosis severity. Additionally, MANF’s protective effects in acute pancreatitis show sexual dimorphism, with male mice exhibiting more severe pathological changes upon MANF deficiency, adding another layer of context dependence to its function in pancreatic exocrine disease. These findings establish MANF as a relevant factor in pancreatitis and highlight its therapeutic potential. However, mechanisms underlying MANF’s action in the exocrine pancreas remain incompletely elucidated. Further studies are warranted to evaluate MANF supplementation as a disease-modifying therapy and assess its utility as an early biomarker for pancreatic injury.

Role of MANF in intestinal diseases

The role of MANF in intestinal diseases has been less extensively investigated, with current research predominantly focusing on inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), including ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD). The prevalence and incidence of IBD are increasing worldwide. Currently, IBD treatment aims to control inflammation and symptoms, but a complete cure remains elusive and challenges persist.

Studies have demonstrated that MANF played a protective role in IBD (Figure 3). Wei et al. reported downregulated MANF mRNA levels and reduced MANF expression in colonic crypts and muscle layers of dextran sodium sulfate (DSS)-treated mice. They concluded that polysaccharide ameliorated DSS-induced colitis in mice by activating MANF [23], supporting the hypothesis that MANF is a critical mediator of intestinal mucosal protection and repair. In another study, Shen’s team observed elevated MANF levels in colonic mucosal tissues of DSS-treated mice. MANF was increased in inflammatory colonic macrophages from both UC patients and murine colitis models. Myeloid cell-specific MANF knockout deteriorated DSS-induced colitis, whereas recombinant human MANF alleviated it. Mechanistically, MANF restricted recruitment of inflammatory macrophages to colonic tissues and repaired intestinal injury by inhibiting the CHOP-BATF2 signaling pathway [24]. Furthermore, a colon-targeted MANF delivery system markedly alleviated colitis in mice [73], highlighting its therapeutic potential. Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) is an acute intestinal inflammatory disease that primarily affects premature infants. MANF was increased in intestinal macrophages of patients with NEC. In vitro, MANF promoted apoptosis of pro-inflammatory macrophages, preserved epithelial tight junctions, and inhibited intestinal epithelial cell apoptosis by downregulating the NF-κB pathway [22]. Overall, MANF is a pivotal regulator of intestinal inflammation. It inhibits pro-inflammatory macrophage responses, maintains epithelial barrier integrity, and promotes tissue repair. It is upregulated in inflamed intestinal tissues, particularly within macrophages. The protective effects, demonstrated through both systemic and targeted delivery, highlight its therapeutic potential for IBD and NEC.

Current research on MANF in intestinal diseases has several limitations. Existing studies focus only on IBD and NEC, and the role of MANF in other intestinal disorders remains unknown. Mechanisms underlying the effects of MANF on IBD and NEC remain to be fully elucidated. Clinical studies on MANF levels in patients with IBD and NEC are insufficient, and correlations between MANF expression and disease severity or treatment response have not yet been clarified. The colon-targeted MANF delivery system has shown therapeutic effects in mice, but its long-term safety and stability have not been evaluated. Therefore, further studies should explore the function and mechanism of MANF in various intestinal pathologies. More clinical studies are also required to validate its efficacy and safety in translational settings. In addition, research should focus on comprehensive evaluation of the MANF delivery system to facilitate its clinical translation.

Conclusion, challenges and prospects

This review systematically delineates the tissue- and cell-specific expression patterns and regulatory functions of MANF in digestive diseases. Structurally conserved and dynamically expressed across the liver, pancreas, and intestine (Table 1), MANF emerges as a key regulator of digestive tract homeostasis. In the liver, MANF protects against ALD, MASH, HCC, DILI, and IRI primarily through modulating ER stress, lipid metabolism, inflammation, fibrosis, and apoptosis, while it promotes immune tolerance in HBV infection and acts as an oncogenic factor in ICC. In the pancreas, MANF protects β-cells from apoptosis, counteracts acute pancreatitis by reducing ER stress-induced apoptosis and suppressing inflammation and oxidative stress, and shows cell-type-specific alterations in chronic alcoholic pancreatitis, which are correlated with fibrosis severity. In intestinal disorders, MANF mitigates IBD and NEC through regulation of macrophage recruitment and epithelial barrier repair (Table 2). Notably, MANF’s functional outcome is determined by cellular context (Figure 4).

Despite these biological functions, clinical translation of MANF faces several challenges. First, its contradictory roles—protective in some diseases such as ALD and MASLD but pathogenic in ICC and HBV infection—demand precise patient stratification and stage-specific intervention strategies. Second, the optimal therapeutic modality remains unresolved. Recombinant MANF protein acts rapidly, but has a short half-life in vivo. Repeated administration may induce immunogenicity. Gene-based therapies, such as AAV-mediated β-cell-specific MANF delivery, enable long-term transgene expression in target cells but carry risks of insertional mutagenesis and immune rejection, which need to be resolved through vector optimization and immune regulation strategies. Small-molecule modulators of MANF signaling are still in early stages of development. Third, selection of delivery strategies represents a critical bottleneck. Systemic administration of MANF may lead to side effects. Organ-targeted delivery systems (e.g. colon-targeted MANF delivery system for IBD) can improve local MANF concentration in target tissues and reduce systemic toxicity but require further optimization regarding delivery efficiency, stability and biocompatibility. Additionally, although circulating or tissue MANF levels show correlative fluctuations in multiple digestive diseases, their specificity, sensitivity, and prognostic value as clinical biomarkers have not been confirmed through large-scale, multicenter clinical studies.

Future research should focus on the following directions. First, clarify context-dependent mechanisms underlying MANF’s dual roles by integrating multi-omics approaches (e.g. single-cell transcriptomics, proteomics) and cell-type-specific modeling (e.g. conditional knockout mice, human organoids). Second, explore MANF functions in understudied digestive diseases to fully elucidate its roles in digestive physiology and pathology. Third, develop and optimize targeted delivery systems to enhance efficacy and minimize side effects. Finally, prospective human cohort studies are urgently needed to validate MANF as a reliable diagnostic and prognostic biomarker. Addressing these challenges will not only deepen mechanistic understanding of MANF in digestive diseases but also accelerate translation of basic research findings into precise diagnostic tools and targeted therapeutics, ultimately bridging the gap between laboratory discoveries and clinical applications.
