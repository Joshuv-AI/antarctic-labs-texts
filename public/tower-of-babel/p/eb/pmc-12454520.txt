Elranatamab Fixed Dosing: A Safe, Effective, and Convenient Dosing Approach
Authors: Mohamed Elmeliegy, Pooneh Soltantabar, Jennifer Hibma, Olivia Ashman, Diane Wang, Hoi-Kei Lon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBispecific T-cell engagers (TCEs) are a promising modality for cancer treatment, and evaluation of dosing strategies, including utilization of body weight–based versus fixed dosing, is essential to ensure optimal therapeutic outcomes. Elranatamab is a bispecific TCE that targets B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA) on multiple myeloma cells and CD on T cells. Elranatamab is approved for relapsed or refractory multiple myeloma (RRMM).ObjectiveEvaluate the impact of body weight on the pharmacokinetics (PK), safety, and efficacy of elranatamab.Patients and MethodsData from the phase 2 MagnetisMM-3 trial (NCT04649359) were used to evaluate the impact of body weight on the PK, safety, and efficacy of elranatamab. This trial comprised two cohorts: cohort A included patients who had not previously received BCMA-directed therapy and cohort B included patients who had received prior BCMA-directed therapies. All patients received a 76-mg fixed dose of subcutaneous elranatamab once weekly after a two-step priming dose regimen. Blood samples were collected from MagnetisMM-3 trial patients for PK analysis. This study analyzed the PK, efficacy, and safety of elranatamab across body weight quartiles.ResultsThe results demonstrated that when elranatamab was given at a fixed dose, the predose concentrations showed overlapping distributions with comparable medians, especially across the lowest three body weight quartiles, with a lower median for quartile 4, which was not considered clinically relevant. There were no clinically relevant differences in the safety profile between different body weight quartiles. With respect to efficacy, the overall response and complete response rates were comparable across body weight quartiles. A clinically meaningful objective response rate benefit with overlapping confidence intervals was observed across all four quartiles, consistent with the primary efficacy analysis. No trend was identified between body weight and progression-free survival and the duration of response on the basis of the Kaplan–Meier curves.ConclusionsConcerns with flat dosing include the potential for overdosing those with lower body weights and underdosing individuals with higher body weights. However, this study provides evidence that fixed dosing of elranatamab is effective and demonstrated a consistent and manageable safety profile across a broad range of body weights. There is no significant impact of body weight on the PK, safety, or efficacy of elranatamab. These findings support the approved fixed dosing of elranatamab in patients with RRMM.ClinicalTrials.gov IdentifierNCT04649359.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11523-025-01170-4.

Key Points

No clinically relevant differences were observed for elranatamab pharmacokinetics, safety, and efficacy when outcomes from the phase 2 MagnetisMM-3 trial were compared across body weight quartiles.These data verify the fixed dosing approach that is generally preferred for T-cell engagers and further support the approved fixed dosing regimen for elranatamab in patients with RRMM.

Introduction

Bispecific T-cell engagers (TCEs) are an emerging and promising modality for cancer treatment [1]. Blinatumomab, the first approved bispecific antibody (BsAb) construct, requires continuous intravenous infusion owing to its short half-life [2]. Over the past decade, newer, next-generation BsAbs have been developed with effective dosing strategies that further enhance patient convenience and compliance while also improving safety profiles. Examples of such dosing strategies include the implementation of step-up priming dosing regimens to mitigate cytokine release syndrome (CRS), subcutaneous administration, switching to less frequent doses after the initial cycles, and implementing limited treatment duration [3–5].

Elranatamab is a B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA)/CD3-directed BsAb TCE, which exerts its anti-myeloma effect via inducing cytotoxic T-cell–mediated killing of myeloma cells. It was approved in 2023 for patients with relapsed or refractory multiple myeloma (RRMM), receiving accelerated approval from the US Food and Drug Administration and a conditional marketing authorization from the European Medicines Agency. Elranatamab is also in development for other MM settings [6–10]. For its initial approval, elranatamab is administered at fixed step-up doses of 12 and 32 mg (on days 1 and 4, respectively), followed by 76 mg weekly (from day 8), with a reduced dosing frequency to once every 2 weeks after 24 weeks for responders. In the registrational phase 2 study (MagnetisMM-3), the dose interval transitioned to an every-4-weeks schedule in patients who received at least 24 weeks of treatment at the once-every-2-weeks schedule.

An important dosing consideration for TCEs is whether there is a need for body size–based dosing (based either on body weight or body surface area), as opposed to a fixed dose administration. The main goal from either dosing approach is to reduce interpatient variability in drug exposure. Body size–based dosing is common for cytotoxic small molecule chemotherapy (which is typically eliminated via hepatic metabolism or renal elimination) and was introduced for cancer chemotherapy several decades ago. Given the narrow therapeutic index for chemotherapeutic agents, body size–based dosing aims to account for the relationship between elimination and distribution processes and body size, thus reducing the potential for interpatient variability in pharmacokinetic (PK) exposure and the risk of overdosing patients with a lower body weight and underdosing patients with a higher body weight. This dosing strategy was based mainly on retrospective interspecies toxicity studies. However, the rationale for body weight-based dosing strategies has not been supported by retrospective PK studies as several studies have shown that body size–based dosing for chemotherapy does not result in reduced interpatient PK variability [11, 12].

For large biologics, elimination and distribution processes might not scale with body weight to the same extent as small molecules, as elimination occurs either through target-mediated elimination or mainly through the nonspecific FcRn-mediated proteolytic catabolism and intracellular degradation. The distribution of biologics (especially monoclonal antibodies [mAbs] and large BsAbs) is limited to plasma and extracellular fluids [11, 13]. Indeed, a simulation-based exercise of approved mAbs indicated that a fixed-dosing approach would result in reduced PK variability for most biologics, including mAbs and therapeutic proteins [14]. Finally, TCEs typically exhibit a relatively wide therapeutic window, such that minimal differences in PK exposure in patients with low versus high body weight are not expected to be clinically meaningful for either safety or efficacy.

As the development of TCEs continues to evolve, understanding the impact of body weight on the PK, safety, and efficacy of biologics is important to optimize their dosing regimens. Fixed dosing is the recommended dosing strategy when supported by PK and/or efficacy and safety data as it also offers several practical advantages, including ease of preparation and administration, reduced cost, and lower risk of dosing errors. Here, we evaluate the impact of body weight on the PK, safety, and efficacy of elranatamab using data from the phase 2 MagnetisMM-3 trial.

Methods

Study Designs and Patients

MagnetisMM-3 (NCT04649359) is a phase 2 trial of elranatamab in patients with RRMM refractory to a proteasome inhibitor, an immunomodulatory drug, and an anti-CD38 mAb. It comprised two cohorts: cohort A included patients who had not previously received BCMA-directed therapy and cohort B included patients who had received prior BCMA-directed therapies (excluding BCMA BsAbs). All patients received a 76-mg fixed dose of subcutaneous elranatamab once weekly, on a 28-day cycle. There was a two-step-up-dose priming regimen of 12 mg (day 1) and 32 mg (day 4) during the first week, with the exception of the first four patients enrolled in the study, who received a single priming dose of 44 mg on day 1 before a full treatment dose of 76 mg on day 8. Premedication with acetaminophen (650 mg or equivalent), diphenhydramine (25 mg or equivalent), and dexamethasone (20 mg or equivalent) was required before each step-up dose and before the first full dose of elranatamab.

Patients were assigned to one of four body weight quartiles on the basis of their baseline body weight according to the following classification:

Quartile 1 (Q1): those with a body weight that was equal to or less than the 25th percentile.

Quartile 1 (Q1): those with a body weight that was equal to or less than the 25th percentile.

Quartile 2 (Q2): those with a body weight that was more than the 25th percentile and less than or equal to the 50th percentile.

Quartile 2 (Q2): those with a body weight that was more than the 25th percentile and less than or equal to the 50th percentile.

Quartile 3 (Q3): those with a body weight that was more than the 50th percentile and less than or equal to the 75th percentile.

Quartile 3 (Q3): those with a body weight that was more than the 50th percentile and less than or equal to the 75th percentile.

Quartile 4 (Q4): those with a body weight that was more than the 75th percentile.

Quartile 4 (Q4): those with a body weight that was more than the 75th percentile.

All patients from both cohorts A and B (N = 187) were included in the PK analyses, as elranatamab exposure was similar between these cohorts. Since the safety profile of elranatamab in patients from cohorts A and B was similar, all patients (n = 183) who received the two-step-up-dose priming regimen (12 mg on day 1 and 32 mg on day 4) were included in the safety analysis; the four patients who received a single 44-mg priming dose were excluded. Given the different population in cohorts A and B, only cohort A patients (n = 123) were included in the efficacy analyses.

Pharmacokinetics Analyses by Body Weight Quartile

Blood samples were collected for PK analyses at the following time points: predose, during treatment (days 1, 4, 8, 15, and 22 of cycle 1; days 1 and 15 of cycle 2; and on day 1 of every third cycle thereafter), and at the end of treatment. Unbound (i.e., free) elranatamab concentrations were measured using a validated electrochemiluminescence method [15].

The distributions of predose concentrations at each cycle by baseline body weight quartile were evaluated via box and whisker plots. Pooled data from cohorts A and B were used in this analysis, given the similar PK between these cohorts. The analysis included trough samples collected after ≥ 2 consecutive planned 76-mg doses without dose modification.

Efficacy and Safety Analyses by Body Weight Quartile

The primary efficacy and safety results of the MagnetisMM-3 trial were previously reported [16]. The primary end point was confirmed by objective response rate (ORR), defined as partial response or better according to the International Myeloma Working Group criteria [17], as assessed by blinded independent central review. Secondary end points included complete response (CR) rate, defined as CR or better (≥ CR), duration of response (DOR), and progression-free survival (PFS). The impact of baseline body weight on efficacy was assessed via body weight quartile analyses of ORR and CR rates, DOR, and PFS. All efficacy analyses were conducted using cohort A data.

Adverse events (AEs) were graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (version 5.0), except for CRS and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), which were graded according to the criteria of the American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy [18]. Treatment-emergent AEs (TEAEs) were defined as any event occurring from the first dose of elranatamab through the minimum of 90 days after the last elranatamab dose or the start of new anticancer therapy. The safety analyses were also performed on the basis of pooled data from cohort A and B patients.

Results

Patients

A total of 187 patients (both cohorts A and B) were enrolled in the MagnetisMM-3 trial. The median (range) of baseline body weight in all 187 patients was 71.8 kg (36.5–159.6 kg), with 25th and 75th percentiles of 61.8 kg and 84.4 kg, respectively. The median (range) of baseline body weight in the 183 patients included in the safety analysis was 72 kg (36.5–159.6 kg), with 25th and 75th percentiles of 62 kg and 84.5 kg, respectively. In cohort A patients included in the efficacy analysis (n = 123), the median (range) baseline body weight was 72 kg (40.9–159.6 kg), with 25th and 75th percentiles of 62 kg and 82.1 kg, respectively.

The baseline characteristics and prior treatment summarized by baseline body weight quartile are presented in Table 1. In general, the baseline characteristics and prior treatment were balanced across body weight quartiles. However, the two lower quartiles had a higher proportion of patients who were female (59.6% and 68.8% for Q1 and Q2, respectively, versus 39.5% and 22.2% for Q3 and Q4, respectively). Q1 had a higher proportion of patients of Asian race (19.1% versus 2.2–9.3% for the other quartiles). In addition, Q1 included a higher proportion of patients with creatinine clearance ≤ 60 mL/min (55.3% versus 13.3–39.6% for the other quartiles). Finally, Q1 had a higher proportion of patients with ≥ 1 poor prognostic feature (76.6% versus 62.5–68.9% for the other quartiles).

Safety of Elranatamab by Baseline Body Weight Quartile

The safety analysis included data from 183 patients (both cohorts A and B) who received the two-step-up-dose priming regimen (12 mg on day 1 and 32 mg on day 4). An overview of TEAEs by body weight quartiles in the MagnetisMM-3 trial is presented in Table 2. There were no clinically relevant differences in the safety of elranatamab when patients were separated into body weight quartiles, including overall incidence of AEs, Grade 3/4 AEs, serious AEs, and discontinuations owing to AEs. The overall incidence of AEs was identical across quartiles (100%). The incidence of Grade 3 or 4 AEs was 63.8%, 68.8%, 60.5%, and 84.4% for Q1–Q4, respectively. The incidence of serious AEs was 72.3%, 77.1%, 81.4%, and 71.1% for Q1–Q4, respectively. Discontinuations owing to AEs occurred in 27.7%, 16.7%, 37.2%, 28.9% of patients for Q1–Q4, respectively. The incidence of AEs by System Organ Class and the most common any-grade, Grade 3 or 4, and serious AEs across body weight quartiles were also consistent with those reported in the overall population.

Efficacy of Elranatamab in Cohort A of MagnetisMM-3 Trial by Baseline Body Weight

The median (range) of follow-up since the initial dose was 17.58 months (0.23–36.83 months) for cohort A. A clinically meaningful ORR benefit was observed across the different body weight quartiles consistent with the primary efficacy analysis which showed confirmed ORR by BICR 61.0% (95% CI 51.8–69.6). The confirmed ORR by BICR for Q1, Q2, Q3, and Q4 was 53.1% (95% CI 34.7–70.9), 59.4% (95% CI 40.6–76.3), 62.1% (95% CI 42.3–79.3), and 70.0% (95% CI 50.6–85.3), respectively (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This work evaluated the impact of body weight on the PK, safety, and efficacy of elranatamab in the MagnetisMM-3 trial, in which elranatamab was administered as a fixed subcutaneous dose, regardless of body weight.

The data indicate comparable elranatamab Ctrough in patients across body weight quartiles with overlapping distributions, although the average Ctrough was lower in Q4. In a pooled population PK analysis, body weight was not a significant covariate on the clearance of elranatamab. Furthermore, simulations from the population PK model showed similar exposures in patients with extreme body weights (10th and 90th percentiles) when compared with the exposure for patients with median body weight. Therefore, the impact of body weight on elranatamab exposure is not considered clinically relevant, especially considering the similar safety and efficacy profiles across body weight quartiles [15].

The baseline characteristics and prior treatment were generally balanced across body weight quartiles. However, Q1 had a higher proportion of patients with ≥ 1 poor prognostic feature, 76.6% vs 62.5–68.9% for the other quartiles. The median baseline sBCMA was also higher in Q1 versus other cohorts. Efficacy was similar across body weight quartiles. A clinically meaningful ORR benefit with overlapping confidence intervals was observed across all four quartiles consistent with the primary efficacy analysis. The CR or better and VGPR or better rates were comparable, and there were no meaningful differences for PFS and DOR observed across body weight quartiles.

The overall safety profile, including the incidence of hematologic AEs, infections, and the most frequently reported AEs, was consistent across body weight quartiles. Detailed analyses of the impact of body weight on CRS have been previously presented [4]. Body weight was not a statistically significant covariate on any-grade or grade ≥ 2 CRS in a univariate logistic regression analysis from the first-in-human study (MagnetisMM-1; NCT03269136). In patients receiving the two-step-up-dose priming regimen in the MagnetisMM-3 trial, no significant trends on CRS incidence or severity were observed across different body weight quartiles.

Furthermore, multivariable analyses evaluated the effects of elranatamab exposure, body weight, and other clinically relevant covariates on efficacy (ORR, PFS, and DOR) and safety (neutropenia and infections) end points. Body weight was not a significant covariate for any of the efficacy or safety end points evaluated (Lon HK, et al. Target Oncol accepted; Soltantabar P, et al. PAGE 2024 Abstract 11081). These results highlight the lack of impact of body weight on the PK, safety, and efficacy of elranatamab and support the approved fixed-dosing approach of elranatamab.

Elranatamab is considered to have a wide therapeutic index, given the flat relationship between elranatamab dose/exposure and safety end points (neutropenia and infections [Soltantabar P, et al. PAGE 2024 Abstract 11081]), and the wide dose range at which efficacy was observed in the first-in-human study (215–1000 µg/kg [19]). The fixed dose of 76 mg once weekly, which is equivalent to the body weight-based dose of 1000 µg/kg, is the highest tested dose in the phase 1 MagnetisMM-1 trial and the recommended full treatment dosing regimen. This regimen achieves the highest free exposure at a given baseline soluble BCMA level in the dose range evaluated and results in a higher probability of achieving an objective response versus a lower dose, with no expected impact on safety.

These analyses and the results demonstrate that fixed dosing of elranatamab is effective and has a consistent and manageable safety profile across a broad range of body weights and that there is no significant impact of body weight on the PK, safety, or efficacy of elranatamab. There is no concern that patients with lower body weights are being overdosed or that patients with higher body weights are being underdosed.

As of February 2025, nine oncology BsAb TCEs are approved, and seven of these are administered as a fixed dose, regardless of the patient’s body weight (i.e., blinatumomab, elranatamab, epcoritamab, glofitamab, mosunetuzumab, tarlatamab, and tebentafusp) [2, 15, 20–24]. Body weight was not a significant covariate on drug clearance in the final population PK model for blinatumomab, elranatamab, and tebentafusp [2, 15, 20]. However, for epcoritamab, glofitamab, mosunetuzumab, and tarlatamab, body weight was included in the final population PK model as a significant covariate on clearance [21–24]. The only approved TCEs on the basis of body weight dosing are talquetamab and teclistamab [25, 26]. In the teclistamab population PK model, body weight was found to be an important factor affecting drug clearance [26–28]. Interestingly, where body weight–based dosing of teclistamab was implemented in the phase 2 MajesTEC-1 trial, subgroup analysis indicated a trend for lower ORR in patients with lower body weight. While this may suggest subtherapeutic dosing in patients with low body weight, the results might also be confounded by other covariates known to impact treatment outcome such as disease burden and presence of extramedullary disease and/or imbalances in the sample size in these subgroups. For talquetamab, body weight impacted total drug clearance and volume of distribution, however subgroup analysis showed similar ORR across body weight subgroups [25].

Conclusions

Given the findings from the current analysis for elranatamab and other approved BsAbs, the fixed dosing approach is generally preferred for TCEs, with body weight–based dosing adopted only when body weight is a significant covariate on PK or clinical outcomes. This recommendation of fixed dosing as a more practical efficient alternative simplifies the dosing regimen, reduces the risk of dosing errors and drug waste, and is consistent with learnings from a recent review article [5]. Ease of compounding and reduced waste will be critical factors in increasing access to BsAbs and their adoption in community settings.
