Sex-specific associations between levels of high-sensitivity C-reactive protein and severity of depression: retrospective cross-sectional analysis of inpatients in China
Authors: Bijun Chen, Jiangwen Dong, Wanjun Guo, Tao Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe aimed to clarify the controversial relationship between levels of high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) and severity of depression in men and women.MethodsMedical records were retrospectively analyzed for 1,236 inpatients at our medical center who were diagnosed with depression at discharge between January 2018 and August 2022. Depression severity was assessed during hospitalization using the 24-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale. Potential associations between severity scores and hs-CRP levels were explored using multivariate linear regression as well as smooth curve fitting to detect non-linear patterns.ResultsIn male patients, hs-CRP levels between 2.00 mg/L and 10.00 mg/L showed a non-linear association with depression severity overall (fully adjusted β = 1.69, 95% CI 0.65 to 2.72), as well as with severity of specific symptoms such as hopelessness, sluggishness, and cognitive disturbance. In female patients, hs-CRP levels showed a linear association with severity of cognitive disturbance (fully adjusted β = 0.07, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.12). These results remained significant after adjusting for age, body mass index, diabetes, hypertension, history of drinking, history of smoking, and estradiol levels.DiscussionLevels of hs-CRP show sex-specific associations with depression severity, particularly levels between 2.00 and 10.00 mg/L in men. These findings may help develop personalized anti-inflammatory treatments for depression, particularly for men with hs-CRP levels of 2.00–10.00 mg/L.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-024-06123-1.

Introduction

Depression is a widespread mental disorder characterized by a range of symptoms, including depressed mood, loss of interest, fatigue, feelings of worthlessness, guilt, suicidal thoughts or attempt, sleep disturbances, changes in weight or appetite, abnormal psychomotor change, and cognitive dysfunction [1]. Depression has been linked to aberrant inflammatory responses, reflected in elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines [2, 3] and of proteins involved in the acute phase of inflammatory responses [4]. One such protein is C-reactive protein, which hepatocytes secrete in response to pro-inflammatory cytokines during acute inflammation or infection [5]. According to the inflammation hypothesis of depression, stressors act via the serotonin system and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis to induce inflammatory responses that contribute to onset of depression [6, 7]. Consistent with this hypothesis, some studies have linked elevated CRP levels to greater risk of depression [8–10] and to development of more severe depressive symptoms [9, 11, 12].

Other studies, however, have failed to detect these associations [13, 14], and one potential explanation for this apparent discrepancy is confounding due to ethnicity. For example, a study in the US detected associations among Whites, but not Blacks or Hispanics [15]. Another potential explanation is confounding due to sex, but here, again, the literature is divergent: some studies have identified strong associations in men but not women [8, 16–18], while other studies have detected strong associations in women [19, 20]. These discrepancies in the literature may reflect ethnic and sex differences in immune responses [21]. Whatever the explanation, they highlight the need for rigorous analysis of potentially sex-specific differences in the associations between levels of C-reactive protein and depression severity.

Another reason why the literature has not consistently detected significant associations may be that it tends to assume that any associations, if they exist, can be detected using linear models [9, 12, 13, 19]. However, a comprehensive study involving 13,000 individuals revealed a non-linear correlation between C-reactive protein levels and the risk of depression. Specifically, an hs-CRP level of > 3.0 mg/L exhibited a higher probability of recurrent depression compared to an hs-CRP level of > 1.0 mg/L in men [17]. These considerations highlight the need for non-linear analysis in order to reliably assess associations of C-reactive protein levels and risk of depression or its severity.

In our study, we focused on cognitive disturbances as assessed by the 24-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD), which captures the cognitive symptoms associated with depression, characterized by negatively biased cognition. It is important to note that these cognitive disturbances are distinct from the cognitive impairments observed in conditions like mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or Alzheimer’s disease (AD), which involve more pervasive deficits in memory, language, and other cognitive domains.

Previous research has indeed explored the associations of C-reactive protein (CRP) levels with the severity of specific depressive symptoms, including fatigue, reduced appetite, sleep disturbances, low energy, and feeling depressed [22, 23]. Regarding cognitive symptoms, studies have found links between CRP levels and impairments in executive functions [24, 25]. Furthermore, elevated CRP levels have been associated with MCI in older adults [26]. However, these cognitive impairments differ from the negative cognitive biases often seen in depression [27]. Despite this body of work, there remains a gap in the literature regarding whether the association of CRP levels are with cognitive disturbances characterized specifically by negative cognition in individuals with depression. Our study aims to address this gap and contribute to better understanding of the potential relationships between inflammation and cognitive symptoms of depression.

In conclusion, to address these gaps in the literature, the present study retrospectively analyzed a cohort of men and women who were admitted to our hospital for depression and whose levels of C-reactive protein were assayed during that time. Using non-linear analysis, we explored potential associations of C-reactive protein levels with overall severity of depression and with severity of specific depression symptoms.

Methods

Patients

Medical records were retrospectively examined for inpatients diagnosed with depressive disorder at Hangzhou Seventh People’s Hospital between January 2018 and August 2022. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hangzhou Seventh People’s Hospital.

To be enrolled in the study, patients had to be diagnosed with a depressive episode according to one of the following codes from the International Classification of Diseases (10th edition): F32, F32.0, F32.1, F32.2, F32.3, F33, F33.0, F33.1, F33.2, F33.3, or F33.4. We examined only medical records related to the first admission of a patient during the recruitment period. Patients were excluded from the study if they (a) had been diagnosed with any other axis-I disorder (apart from anxiety disorders) or with a mental or behavioral disorder due to psychoactive substance use, or with a nervous system disease; (b) were younger than 18 or older than 60 years at admission; (c) were not Chinese citizens; (d) had a documented history of using nicotine or illegal substances; (e) had an overall score below 8 or an unknown overall score on the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD); (f) had a level of high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) > 100 mg/L in the presence of severe infection, or whose hs-CRP level was unknown; or (g) were assessed for hs-CRP and for HAMD score longer than one week apart.

Outcomes

Soon after admission, psychiatrists assessed all patients using the 24-item HAMD in order to assess overall severity of depression as well as the severity of specific depression symptoms according to the dimensions of anxiety/somatization, weight loss, cognitive disturbance, diurnal variation (type A or B), sluggishness, sleep disturbances, and feelings of hopelessness.

Assay of hs-CRP

Detection of C-reactive protein at concentrations below approximately 10 mg/L, termed “hs-CRP”, is widely used to detect infection, chronic mild inflammation and other chronic disorders [28]. Soon after admission, blood was sampled by antecubital venipuncture. Biometric samples were obtained using a standardized protocol. Serum samples were collected in SST containing clot activators and gel for separation. Whole blood samples were collected in EDTA anticoagulant tubes to prevent coagulation. Given the variability in clinical settings, the timing of blood collection relative to the participants’ diurnal rhythms and fasting status was not uniform. Some samples were collected in the morning, while others were not, and participants may or may not have fasted for 12 h prior to the blood draw. Upon receipt in the laboratory, serum samples were centrifuged within 2 h of arrival at 3600 RPM for 6 min to separate the serum from the clot and cellular components, after which the serum was immediately analyzed. Whole blood samples were processed and analyzed promptly upon arrival at the laboratory. In some cases, serum was assayed using a latex-enhanced immunoturbidimetric assay and an ADVIA chemistry XPT system (Siemens, Germany), which features a linear response range between 0.5 and 150 mg/L. In other cases, whole blood was assayed using the immunoturbidimetric assay and an Aristo automatic protein analyzer (Goldsite, China). The result was adjusted as serum CRP concentration for the sample, despite the assay kit measuring whole blood samples through its built-in program conversion (Linear range:0.90-380.0 mg/L). In either case, the detection sensitivity was sufficient to qualify as hs-CRP testing. The intra-assay coefficients of variability for both instruments are well below the threshold of 10%, ensuring that the measurements are consistent and precise. Furthermore, previous research has demonstrated compatibility between hs-CRP results obtained from the Goldsite protein analyzer and the Siemens specific protein analyzer [29]. This evidence supports the notion that the data from both instruments can be merged without significant loss of accuracy or reliability. Assays were performed in the hospital’s clinical laboratory according to standard operating procedures.

Statistical analysis

Categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous data were summarized using median (25th percentile, 75th percentile). All statistical analyses were performed in R 3.4.3 (www.r-project.org) or Empower 4.0 (X&Y Solutions, Boston, MA, USA), and significance was defined as p < 0.05.

First, associations of hs-CRP levels with overall severity of depression or severity of specific depression symptoms were explored using multivariate regression involving an unadjusted model, a “partially adjusted” model and a “fully adjusted” model. The fully adjusted model controlled for age, body mass index, history of drinking, history of smoking, hypertension, diabetes, estradiol level, and sex. The “partially adjusted” model controlled for all those variables except body mass index. Second, associations were also explored in different subgroups, including based on sex. Third, associations of hs-CRP levels with overall severity of depression or severity of specific depression symptoms were explored separately in the sexes using multivariate regression.

We applied piecewise linear regression models to examine the threshold effects of hs-CRP on overall severity of depression as well as severity of different depression symptoms using a smoothing function for the two sexes, while controlling for age, body mass index, history of drinking, history of smoking, hypertension, diabetes, and estradiol level. We determined the threshold (inflection) points based on the smooth curve plots and clinical experience.

Results

After screening just over 5,000 patients for enrollment, we narrowed down the sample to 1,236 (325 men) (Fig. 1; Table 1). The final sample showed a median age of 44 (30, 53) years, median body mass index of 21.72 (19.61, 23.76) kg/m2, median hs-CRP level of 0.60 (0.43, 1.49) mg/L, and median overall HAMD score of 22 (18, 26). Among the numerous clinicodemographic variables that we examined, only body mass index was significantly associated with hs-CRP level and overall HAMD score in univariate regression (Supplementary Table 1). Subgroup analysis showed that, based on multivariate regression, only sex exerted a significant apparent influence on the relationship between hs-CRP levels and overall severity of depression (Supplementary Table 2): the relationship was stronger in men (β = 0.51, 95% CI 0.02 to 1.00) than in women (β = 0.10, 95% CI -0.09 to 0.28).

We then controlled for age, diabetes, hypertension, body mass index, estradiol level, sex, history of drinking and history of smoking in multivariate regression. Among all patients, regardless of the number of confounders for which the model controlled, we did not detect significant association between hs-CRP levels and overall severity of depression (fully adjusted β = 0.28, 95% CI -0.15 to 0.17; Table 2). Two of the models detected a significant negative association of hs-CRP level with severity of weight loss, but this disappeared after controlling for body mass index. All models, in contrast, detected a significant positive association between hs-CRP levels and severity of sluggishness.

Among men, multivariate linear regression indicated a positive association between hs-CRP level and overall HAMD score, even after adjusting for all confounders (β = 0.58, 95% CI 0.10 to 1.05; Table 3). Among specific symptoms, hs-CRP level showed significant positive associations with cognitive disturbance (fully adjusted β = 0.15, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.30), hopelessness (fully adjusted β = 0.16, 95% CI 0.02 to 0.29) and sluggishness (fully adjusted β = 0.18, 95% CI 0.07 to 0.30). Piecewise regression detected non-linearity in the relationship between hs-CRP level and overall HAMD score (Fig. 2A): the relationship was directly proportional for hs-CRP levels between 2.00 and 10.00 mg/L, while no significant relationship was detected outside this hs-CRP range (β = 1.69, 95% CI 0.65 to 2.72). Similar non-linearity with inflection points at hs-CRP = 2.00 and 10.00 mg/L were observed for the specific symptoms of cognitive disturbance, hopelessness, and sluggishness (Fig. 2B-D).

Among women, multivariate linear regression gave substantially different results. No significant association was detected between hs-CRP level and overall HAMD score (adjusted β = 0.10, 95% CI -0.08 to 0.29). Nevertheless, hs-CRP level showed a significant positive association with cognitive disturbance (adjusted β = 0.07, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.12). Piecewise regression did not detect any non-linear associations between hs-CRP level and overall severity of depression or severity of individual symptoms.

Discussion

Our retrospective analysis involving methods to detect non-linear associations provides strong support for sex-specific relationships of hs-CRP levels with aspects of depression. We found that in men, hs-CRP levels were associated significantly with overall severity of depression as well as with severity of cognitive disturbance, hopelessness and sluggishness for hs-CRP levels between 2 and 10 mg/L. In women, levels were associated significantly not with overall severity of depression but with severity of cognitive disturbance, and these results were not restricted to a particular hs-CRP range.

Our research highlights the need for further studies to clarify why relationships between hs-CRP levels and depression severity differ between the sexes. One possible explanation is sex hormones. Variations in sex hormones, especially cyclical variations in ovarian hormones, are known to affect levels of C-reactive protein [30, 31]. In addition, levels of estradiol and male sex hormones can influence immune responses [21] We observed significant associations between hs-CRP levels and severity of depression even after controlling for estradiol levels, implying that estradiol alone cannot explain all the observed sex differences. Further research should explore the potential influence of progesterone and other hormones on the link between hs-CRP and depression.

Future studies should also examine more closely the potential influence of ethnicity on this link [15, 32]. Approximately 40% of variation in C-reactive protein levels among individuals may be hereditary [33]. While a previous study within a Chinese community population found no association between CRP and the incidence of depressive symptoms after controlling for relevant demographic variables [14], our findings align with research conducted in an Asian (Korean) population that observed an association between CRP and the prevalence of depression among men [16]. In contrast, a study among Caucasian patients with depression noted that CRP was associated with the severity of depressive symptoms in women [19]. Additionally, a study in an Australian population discovered an association between CRP and the onset of de novo major depressive disorder in women [20]. This suggests that caution is needed when generalizing our findings to populations in various geographical regions.

Our work suggests that the relationship between hs-CRP and severity of depression is non-linear, which may help explain why it was not detected in studies using linear models [13, 19]. The thresholds of 2 and 10 mg/L differ from those traditionally used to gauge cardiovascular disease risk [34], emphasizing the need to apply non-linear methods to determine hs-CRP levels associated with higher risk of depression or depression symptoms in men and women of different ethnic groups. Our findings indicate that there may be an inflammation-specific subtype of depression in men characterized by hs-CRP levels of 2–10 mg/L. This is in line with literature suggesting a link between hs-CRP levels > 5.00 mg/L and treatment-resistant depression [35]. At least among Chinese populations, men with depression and hs-CRP in the range of 2–10 mg/L may benefit from anti-inflammatory therapies.

Our analysis indicates a connection between heightened inflammation and cognitive impairment in individuals with depression, aligning with studies that have linked inflammation to various depressive symptoms [24, 25, 36]. It is important to clarify that the cognitive disturbances we refer to are those assessed by the 24-item HAMD, which assesses depressive symptoms that include cognitive elements such as self-guilt, suicidal thoughts, agitation, depersonalization/derealization, paranoid symptoms, and obsessive-compulsive symptoms [37]. These cognitive disturbances are not synonymous with the comprehensive cognitive functions evaluated through neurocognitive testing, which would encompass a broader range of cognitive domains, including memory, attention, and executive functions.

While our study did not find a direct relationship between anxiety/somatization and elevated hs-CRP levels, which contrasts with some previous findings [22, 23], we did observe an association between cognitive disturbances characterized by negative cognition and elevated hs-CRP levels. This association is in line with Beck’s cognitive model of depression, which posits that individuals with depression exhibit a negative cognitive bias that contributes to their emotional symptoms [27]. Furthermore, our findings extend previous research by showing that increased inflammation is linked not only to executive function impairments but also to cognitive distortions in both men and women with depression [24, 25]. This suggests that anti-inflammatory treatments could potentially benefit not only executive functions but also the cognitive biases associated with depression. However, it is crucial to note that the causality between inflammation and depressive symptoms, including cognitive disturbances, has yet to be definitively established. Further studies incorporating neurocognitive testing are needed to explore the intricate relationship between inflammation and the cognitive aspects of depression more thoroughly.

We detected a significant association between hs-CRP and severity of weight loss in our sample, which body mass index influenced. Thus, future work should explore how body composition may influence inflammation and thereby onset or aggravation of depression.

The findings from our study should be robust to confounding by ethnicity as a result of our ethnically homogeneous sample, and by numerous other factors as a result of our controlled multivariate analysis. The findings should be interpreted with caution due to several limitations. Firstly, hs-CRP measurements were derived from either serum or whole blood samples. While existing literature posits a comparability between these two sampling methods, there remains a possibility that minor discrepancies could affect the precision of hs-CRP levels assessments. Secondly, despite the hs-CRP assay and HAMD scoring being conducted within a one-week interval, the temporal proximity does not guarantee that the emotional symptoms evaluated are a consistent reflection of the immune status as indicated by hs-CRP levels. This temporal discrepancy may introduce either an overestimation or an underestimation of the observed association between hs-CRP levels and the severity of depressive symptoms. Additionally, the potential confounding effects of previous psychiatric or anti-inflammatory medications use cannot be disregarded, as detailed records for many patients were not accessible. These medications are known to modulate inflammatory biomarker levels [38], thereby potentially introducing variability into our results. Ultimately, the cross-sectional nature of our study allows the detection of associations, but not causality. Our findings should be verified and extended in longitudinal studies.

Conclusions

Our analysis of Chinese inpatients with depression suggests that hs-CRP levels of 2–10 mg/L in men with depression are associated with more severe overall disease, particularly symptoms of hopelessness, sluggishness, and cognitive disturbance. Among women with depression, we found a significant association between hs-CRP levels and more severe cognitive disturbance. These findings suggest that personalized treatments targeting inflammation may be effective against certain subtypes of depression.
