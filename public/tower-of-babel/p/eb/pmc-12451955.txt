Pharmacist-assessed medication adherence and quality of life in patients with epilepsy
Authors: Michael Petrides, Aliki Peletidi, Spyros Polyzois, Evangelia Nena, Theodoros Constantinidis, Christos Kontogiorgis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEpilepsy affects approximately 65 million individuals globally, with medication adherence a critical determinant of seizure control and quality of life (QoL). Community pharmacists, with their accessibility and expertise, are well positioned to support adherence and patient education. However, little is known about the interplay between adherence, readiness for change, and QoL in patients with epilepsy (PWE) in Cyprus. This study is the first comprehensive investigation in Cyprus, addressing this evidence gap and highlighting opportunities for pharmacist-led interventions.MethodsThis cross-sectional study (Sep 2022 – Oct 2023) was conducted at Limassol General Hospital. Greek-speaking adult PWE (N = 85) were assessed via semi-structured telephone interviews using validated tools: Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS-8), Medication Adherence Report Scale (MARS-5), Readiness for Change Ruler, and Quality of Life in Epilepsy Inventory (QOLIE-31). Ethical approval was obtained from the Cyprus National Bioethics Committee (EEBK EP2019.01.130). Statistical analyses included t-tests, ANOVA, and binary logistic regression using SPSS v28 (p < 0.05).ResultsHigh adherence was reported by 55.3% (MMAS-8) and 63.5% (MARS-5). Adherence type was significantly associated with QoL (Kruskal–Wallis H = 11.427, p = 0.010), with mixed non-adherence linked to poorer QoL (Bonferroni p = 0.014). The mean QOLIE-31 score was 75.3 (SD = 19.91), significantly higher than Greek (69.6; p = 0.010, Cohen’s D = 0.286) and U.S. (62.9, p < 0.001, Cohen’s D = 0.623) reference values. Employment (p = 0.009) was positively associated with QoL. Higher MARS-5 scores (≥4.8) and high/medium MMAS-8 scores (≥6) were significant predictors of better QoL (MARS-5: p = 0.003, OR = 4.826, 95% CI 1.738–13.401; MMAS-8: p = 0.004, OR = 7.125, 95% CI 1.899–26.729). Readiness for change was high (mean 9.48/10), largely driven by trust in physicians.ConclusionThis Cyprus-based study demonstrates strong associations between adherence, sociodemographic factors, and QoL in PWE. The novel adherence sub-classification provides valuable insights for personalised care. Community pharmacists can play a pivotal role in improving adherence, delivering patient-centred education, and enhancing epilepsy management via integrated multidisciplinary care.

Background

Epilepsy is recognised as a prevalent chronic noncommunicable neurological disorder, affecting approximately 65 million individuals worldwide (Thurman et al., 2011), across all age groups (World Health Organisation, 2024). The condition is marked by recurrent seizures, which are episodes of involuntary movement that either involve parts of the body (partial) or the entire body (generalised) (National Health System (NHS) UK, n.d.). These seizures are caused by abnormal electrical discharges in the brain and can vary significantly in severity and frequency. Despite the complexity of epilepsy, it is estimated that up to 70% of those affected could experience a seizure-free life with accurate diagnosis and appropriate treatment (Pan American Health Organisation (PAHO) / World Health Organisation (Americas Region), n.d.).

Globally, epilepsy poses a significant health burden, with a notable disparity in treatment availability between high-income and low – to middle-income countries (World Health Organisation, 2024). Epilepsy also carries significant socio-cultural challenges. In many parts of the world, individuals living with epilepsy face stigma (Sirven, 2015) and discrimination (Tellez-Zenteno et al., 2007), largely due to myths and misunderstandings that persist about the condition. The stigma can adversely affect their quality of life, leading to social isolation and discouraging individuals from seeking treatment. The risk of premature mortality for people with epilepsy is up to three times higher than the general population (Trinka et al., 2023), particularly in low – and middle-income countries, where potentially preventable causes of death like drowning and prolonged seizures are more prevalent.

Epilepsy presents significant challenges not only due to the recurrent nature of seizures but also because of its profound effects on patients’ quality of life (QoL) (Strzelczyk et al., 2023) and medication adherence (Babar, 2019; Donahue et al., 2024). Community pharmacists (CPs), with their accessibility and expertise, are in a prime position to address these challenges and play a crucial role in epilepsy management. To address these challenges, community pharmacists, given their accessibility, can play a crucial role in managing epilepsy.

Community pharmacists are uniquely positioned to improve medication adherence, a critical factor in preventing breakthrough seizures that can drastically affect patients’ quality of life. For example, in the United States, 90% of the population lives within a five-mile radius of a community pharmacy (Qato et al., 2017; Rice & LaPreze, 2023), and these pharmacies are often open beyond traditional business hours, making pharmacists highly accessible healthcare providers. In 2021, the availability of community pharmacies per 100,000 individuals ranged between 9 in Denmark and 97 in Greece, with an average value of 28 across OECD nations with comparable statistics (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), 2023). This density of pharmacies ensures that, in most countries, patients have relatively easy and convenient access to pharmaceutical care, which can facilitate medication adherence and timely interventions (Munaf & Mohammed, 2024). Pharmacists bring extensive knowledge of drug therapy, particularly antiseizure medications (ASMs), coupled with the capacity to monitor medication adherence, manage complex drug regimens, and provide patient education (Guignet et al., 2024). Despite their potential, pharmacists are rarely fully engaged in epilepsy care, primarily due to limited training in this area (Roth et al., 2016). They can offer strategies to overcome adherence barriers such as forgetfulness, side effects, and misunderstandings about medicinal products (Rice & LaPreze, 2023). By establishing medication reminders and simplifying dosing schedules, pharmacists can ensure that up to 70% of patients have the potential to lead seizure-free lives with consistent use of antiseizure medications (ASMs). Regular medication reviews and patient education also enable pharmacists to optimise treatment regimens, further minimising adverse effects and interactions (Koshy, 2012; Mackridge et al., 2025).

Beyond promoting adherence, pharmacists play an expanded role by educating patients and their families on various aspects of epilepsy (Ernawati et al., 2024). This includes not only recognising seizures but also providing guidance on first-aid measures, identifying potential triggers, and stressing the importance of following prescribed treatments. Addressing these educational needs allows pharmacists to help bridge existing gaps in patient and caregiver understanding, ensuring better disease management and safety (Eshiet et al., 2019; Moshé et al., 2015).

As integral members of healthcare teams, pharmacists deliver holistic care that addresses both the physical and psychological dimensions of epilepsy. Through individualised counselling, they equip patients with the knowledge and confidence to manage their condition effectively, while also helping to reduce the stigma and discrimination frequently linked to epilepsy (Jacoby et al., 2005).

Deeper integration of pharmacists into epilepsy care offers considerable advantages, especially in regions with limited access to specialised healthcare. Pharmacists serve as vital links between patients and healthcare systems, helping to reduce disparities by providing education, ongoing support, and accessible care (Daly et al., 2020). To maximise their impact, targeted training programmes are essential to equip pharmacists with the expertise required to manage epilepsy effectively. Strengthening their role not only enhances health outcomes but also addresses the diverse needs of individuals affected by epilepsy worldwide, ultimately improving both care quality and quality of life.

Prior to the 2019 reform, Cyprus and Ireland were the only EU member states without a comprehensive universal health coverage system (Petrou, 2019). The Cypriot healthcare sector was highly fragmented, comprising two parallel, segmented, and uncoordinated systems: a public sector providing free care to individuals meeting specific socioeconomic or medical criteria – such as impoverished citizens, public servants, and those with designated diseases – and a private sector financed predominantly through out-of-pocket payments or optional private health insurance. This inequitable arrangement was marked by inefficiencies; before the financial crisis, the absence of user charges in the public sector contributed to waste, while limited resources, bureaucratic structures, and inconvenient service hours reduced its capacity to meet patient needs. The 2013 economic crisis further strained the system, driving more patients to seek public care, exacerbating waiting lists, and in some cases forcing patients to resort to private providers at personal expense. Within this context, the public sector relied exclusively on centralised tender procedures for pharmaceutical procurement, a mechanism effective in reducing prices but inflexible in addressing individual patient needs.

In June 2019, the introduction of the National Health System (GeSY) marked the most significant reform in decades (Petrou, 2021), establishing universal coverage through a single-payer model administered by the Health Insurance Organisation, integrating public and private providers, and expanding benefits to include comprehensive pharmaceutical services. Procurement shifted from the tender-based approach to an open formulary model, underpinned by transparent, evidence-based criteria such as clinical efficacy, safety, therapeutic value, and cost-effectiveness. This transition created opportunities to better align medicine access with patient-centred care and long-term treatment sustainability.

Within this evolving governance and procurement framework, the present pilot study provides a foundation for developing targeted, evidence-informed interventions – particularly through the enhanced role of community pharmacists – to improve adherence, optimise epilepsy management, and ultimately strengthen health outcomes in Cyprus.

Methods

Study design and duration

This study is a quantitative cross-sectional pilot study conducted over thirteen months, from September 2022 to October 2023. It received ethical approval from the ‘Cyprus National Ethics Committee’ (EEBK EP2019.01.130), ensuring adherence to ethical guidelines governing research involving human participants. It also received approval from the ‘Scientific Committee for the Promotion of Research’ of the Ministry of Health of Cyprus (0528/2019, 5.34.01.7.6E) and from the ‘State Health Services Organisation’ [07.03.001(19)], as the data collection involved epileptic patients visiting the General Hospital of Limassol.

Data collection

Data were collected by a pharmacist/academic (MP) through telephone interviews. After ethical approval, the main researcher (MP) contacted the Assistant Director of Limassol’s General Hospital Neurology Department to invite participation in the study. Upon agreement, two neurologists informed patients about the study during their scheduled outpatient appointments. All patients attending the Neurology Department were initially briefed about the research. Individuals were eligible for inclusion, if they were: a) adults, b) diagnosed with epilepsy, c) receiving antiepileptic medication therapy and d) able to communicate in Greek. Eligible patients were provided with a Participant Information Sheet outlining the study’s purpose and procedures. Those who agreed to participate gave written informed consent. Subsequently, the researcher contacted the consenting participants to initiate the first phase of the study and arrange interviews to assess medication adherence, readiness for change and quality of life. Interviews were conducted over the phone and lasted approximately 20–30 minutes each.

Sample size justification

According to pharmaceutical utilisation data that were provided formally from Cyprus General Health System (GESY) to the main researcher (MP) upon request, approximately N = 3,460 individuals in Cyprus received antiepileptic medicines in 2020. A full-scale prevalence study in this finite population would require around n ≈ 346 participants for 95% confidence and a 5% margin of error (Raosoft calculation, assuming 50% response distribution) (Sample Size Calculator by Raosoft, Inc., 2004). As this was a pilot study aimed at assessing feasibility, recruitment processes, and data quality, a smaller sample was deemed appropriate in line with commonly cited methodological recommendations for pilot work. For quantitative studies, a sample of 30 participants per group (intervention and control) is often considered adequate to estimate parameters and assess feasibility before scaling up (Teresi et al., 2022).

The recruited N = 85 is consistent with, and exceeds, the median sample sizes reported in a recent methodological review of pilot and feasibility studies in UK between 2013–2020, which found a median of 30 participants per arm across 761 studies, with an interquartile range (IQR) 20–50 (Totton et al., 2023). The present sample provides preliminary data to identify operational issues, estimate variability, and inform the a priori sample size for the subsequent definitive study, thereby meeting the exploratory and feasibility objectives of the present work.

Instruments

The primary instruments used in data collection included the Morisky Medication Adherence Scale 8-Item (MMAS-8), the Readiness for Change Scale, the Medication Adherence Report Scale 5-Item (MARS-5), and the Quality Of Life In Epilepsy Inventory 31-Item (QOLIE-31). The validated Greek versions of the MMAS-8 and QOLIE-31 questionnaires were employed with formal authorisation from the respective copyright holders. The MARS-5 questionnaire was translated into Greek using a forward – backward translation methodology. The back-translated version was submitted to the original copyright holder, for expert appraisal. Following evaluation, approval of the Greek version was granted. Subsequently, pilot testing was undertaken with five individuals from the target population to assess item clarity and identify potential linguistic or cultural ambiguities; no such issues were observed. These pilot testing participants were not included in the final sample analysed in the study results.

Medication adherence

Medication adherence was evaluated using the 8-item Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS-8) (Berlowitz et al., 2017; Bress et al., 2017; Plakas et al., 2016). The official description of the MMAS-8 scoring system is the following: ‘The MMAS-8 scale consists of 8 items. Each of the first 7 items has 2 possible responses (yes/no), while the 8th item is answered with a 5-point Likert scale. The possible total medication adherence score ranges between 0 and 8, and the higher the score, the better the adherence level. A total score < 6 is considered low adherence, while a total score of ≥ 6 but < 8 indicates moderate adherence, and a score of 8 indicates high adherence’. (©MMAS 2006). In addition to overall adherence levels, the MMAS-8 scoring system also permits further differentiation of non-adherence into intentional (INA), unintentional (UNA), and mixed non-adherence types, enabling a more nuanced understanding of patient adherence behaviours.

In addition, the Medication Adherence Report Scale (MARS-5) was employed to assess self-reported non-adherence behaviours (©MARS 2018) (Chan et al., 2020; Horne & Weinman, 2002). The MARS-5 includes five items reflecting common non-adherent behaviours regarding medicinal products (‘I forget to take them’, ‘I alter the dose’, ‘I stop taking them for a while’, ‘I decide to miss out a dose’, ‘I take less than instructed’), each rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = always; 5 = never). Total scores range from 5 to 25, with higher scores indicating better adherence. An adjusted mean score (range 1–5) was also calculated by dividing the total score by the number of items, with higher values representing better adherence (5 = perfect adherence). According to MARS-5 scoring instructions, ‘The Adjusted Scale Score affords easy comparison between different versions of the MARS as each version is score 1–5 in the Adjusted Scale Score method’. Furthermore, the scoring instructions suggest dichotomising the scale ‘on the basis of the frequency distribution’, if ‘reported adherence is heavily positively skewed’.

Readiness for change

Readiness to change was assessed using the Readiness for Change Ruler (American Society on Aging and American Society of Consultant Pharmacists Foundation, n.d.; Zimmerman et al., 2000), which includes a 0–10 readiness ruler alongside three open-ended questions. This tool evaluates patients’ motivation and willingness to modify medication-taking behaviours or lifestyle habits.

Quality of life

Quality of life was measured using the Greek version of the Quality of Life in Epilepsy Inventory (QOLIE-31) (Piperidou et al., 2006). The QOLIE-31 consists of seven subscales assessing emotional well-being, social functioning, energy/fatigue, cognitive functioning, seizure worry, medication effects, and overall quality of life. Subscale scores range from 0 to 100. A total QOLIE-31 score is calculated as a weighted average of the subscale scores, with higher scores reflecting better quality of life (Vickrey et al., 1993).

Alongside these assessments, demographic data were collected to provide additional context for the analysis.

Participant inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants who met specific criteria were included in this study. Eligible participants were those over 18 years of age, diagnosed with epilepsy, currently on antiepileptic therapy, and Greek-speaking. The total number of epileptic patients that were identified from the hospital’s records were 151, based on the medical classification and diagnosis code (ICD-10) in their record. A total of 16 patients were excluded from the study due to various reasons that could affect the assessment process. These included communication issues, recent stroke occurrences, and conditions such as quadriplegia, brain paralysis, mental retardation, or Down syndrome.

Statistical analysis

The data gathered from the assessment tools were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 28). The analysis focused on evaluating medication adherence, readiness for change, and quality of life, utilising both descriptive and inferential statistics to provide a comprehensive understanding of the study results. This rigorous approach ensured that meaningful and reliable conclusions could be drawn about the role of community pharmacists in managing epilepsy, particularly in enhancing medication adherence and improving patients’ quality of life.

Results

Demographics

A total of 85 participants were involved in this study, which represents a response rate of 62.96% from the initial 135 individuals approached. Sample’s demographic information is summarised in Supplemental Table S1. Regarding gender distribution, 47 males (55.3%) and 38 females (44.7%) participated, and the average age was 51.54 ± 16.59. In terms of employment status, 34 participants (40.0%) were employed, whereas 51(60%) were retired, unemployed or engaged in housekeeping. In relation to marital status, 34 participants (40.0%) were single, whereas the rest 60% were either married, divorced or widowed.

With respect to medication regimen, 45 participants (57.7%) were receiving monotherapy, while 33 (42.3%) were on polytherapy. Regarding benzodiazepine use, 11 participants (14.1%) reported current use, whereas 67 (85.9%) reported no use. It should be noted that for both medication regimen and benzodiazepine use, data were available for 78 participants, as 7 individuals declined to disclose their medication information.

Adherence – MMAS-8

Medication adherence was assessed using the Morisky MMAS-8, where 47 participants (55.3%) had high adherence ( = 8), 26 participants (30.6%) had medium adherence (≥6 to <8), and 12 participants (14.1%) had low adherence (<6). Apart from the high/medium/low groups, the patients were also grouped in two separate groups (group A: high vs medium + low, group B: high + medium vs low), for assisting further statistical analysis. Non-adherent participants were further categorised into unintentional (UNA), intentional (INA), and mixed non-adherence groups based on their respective scores. More specifically, the 38 participants who were non-adherent (score < 8) were classified into unintentional non-adherence (N = 25, 29.4%), intentional non-adherence (N = 9, 10.6%), and mixed non-adherence (N = 4, 4.7%). Unintentional non-adherence was defined as having a higher UNA score than INA score, while intentional non-adherence was defined as having a higher INA score than UNA score. Mixed non-adherence was assigned when the UNA and INA scores were equal. The patients’ detailed adherence scores are summarised in Supplemental Table S2.

Adherence – MARS-5

Medication adherence was also assessed using the MARS-5 adjusted scale score, where 54 participants (63.5%) had perfect adherence ( = 5). As indicated by the distribution curve, the reported adherence was indeed heavily skewed; therefore, patients were categorised into three groups to facilitate further statistical analysis (Group A: ≥ 4 vs < 4, Group B: ≥ 4.8 vs < 4.8, Group C: 5 vs < 5). There are studies that have applied a 4.8 cut-off value (Kamble et al., 2012; Spetz et al., 2024), others that have used a 4.0 cut-off (Lin et al., 2016; Stone et al., 2021; Van De Steeg et al., 2009), and some that have applied the median value (Yang et al., 2013) of the MARS-5 data. The patients’ detailed adherence scores are summarised in Supplemental Table S3.

Readiness for change

The Readiness for Change Ruler demonstrated a high level of readiness among participants, with a mean score of 9.48 (SD = 1.45). A total of 72 respondents (84.7%) achieved the maximum score of 10, while 13 respondents (15.3%) scored between 0 and 9.

Qualitative analysis of participants who scored 10 revealed a striking uniformity in the underlying rationale for their readiness. In all cases, participants attributed their willingness to change to the doctor’s authority, expressing high levels of trust in the healthcare provider's recommendations. Representative statements included ‘If the doctor says so’, ‘I trust the doctor’, and ‘If the doctor says so and it is good for me’. These responses suggest that participants’ readiness was largely externally motivated, driven by deference to medical authority rather than autonomous engagement or active evaluation of the proposed change. This reflects a predominantly physician-centred model of decision-making, wherein trust in the provider plays a central role in facilitating readiness for change.

In contrast, participants with lower readiness scores expressed more nuanced perspectives, highlighting multiple cognitive, emotional, and experiential factors that influenced their hesitation. Some participants expressed contentment with their current medication regimen and daily routine, reducing the perceived need for change, as illustrated by statements such as ‘I am satisfied with my current medications and daily routine’. Others described prior negative experiences with medication changes that contributed to increased caution and reluctance. For instance, one participant explained, ‘In the past, when a switch to a generic medication was made in the hospital, I experienced side effects, making me generally cautious about changes’, while another stated, ‘I had a previous experience where the doctor changed my medication, which led to a problem’.

A recurrent theme was the desire for additional information and understanding before accepting any changes. This was reflected in comments such as ‘A more detailed discussion about the medications would help’ and ‘I need to understand the reasons behind any proposed change through detailed conversation’. Some participants reported generalised reluctance or anxiety toward making changes, even when recommended by a physician, as exemplified by statements like ‘I am hesitant with changes’, ‘I struggle to make dose adjustments, even if advised by the doctor’, and ‘I generally find it difficult to make changes’. Finally, a few participants indicated openness to change, if it were deemed necessary, as reflected in the statement ‘If necessary’.

The qualitative findings highlight two distinct readiness profiles within the study population. The majority expressed maximal readiness, primarily anchored in unconditional trust in the physician's authority. In contrast, a smaller subgroup expressed reservations shaped by previous experiences, emotional concerns, information gaps, and a desire for greater involvement in the decision-making process. These results underscore the importance of recognising variability in patient readiness for change. While physician recommendations remain a strong motivator, incorporating patient-centred communication, addressing prior experiences, and providing tailored information may be critical in facilitating readiness among those with residual hesitations. The findings further emphasise the need to balance professional authority with shared decision-making models to optimise patient engagement and adherence.

Quality of life

In the present study, Quality of Life (QoL) scores averaged 75.3 (SD = 19.91), with scores ranging from 18.1–99.4. To facilitate both statistical analyses and meaningful interpretation, participants were categorised into two groups using different QoL thresholds: Group A using a cut-off at 60 (QoL ≥ 60 vs <60), and Group B at 70 (QoL ≥ 70 vs <70). The selection of these cut-off points was informed by the need to enable comparisons with previously published international and regional data. The detailed quality of life (QoL) scores and subscale scores of the patients are provided in Supplemental Table S4, along with a comparison of the current findings to those of a previous Greek study (Piperidou et al., 2008) and a U.S. study (Devinsky et al., 1995) that contributed to the development of the QOLIE-89, QOLIE-31, and QOLIE-10 instruments.

To contextualise these findings, comparisons were made with QOLIE-31 scores reported in both global and regional studies. According to the global QOLIE-31 comparison review (Saadi et al., 2016), which synthesised data from 31 countries with a total sample size of 7,255 participants, the global mean QOLIE-31 score was 59.8 (SD = 8.0). Within the same study, the Greek population demonstrated a higher mean score of 69.6 (SD = 18.1), suggesting more favourable quality of life outcomes compared to the global average. In this context, the mean QoL score observed in the present study (75.3) exceeds both the global and Greek averages previously reported.

Several factors may contribute to these differences. Variations in healthcare delivery systems, access to specialised care, and differing perceptions of health-related quality of life may influence patient-reported outcomes across countries. The relatively higher scores in the present sample may reflect recent improvements in clinical management, better seizure control, higher levels of patient engagement, or specific characteristics of the study population, such as stable disease status or advantageous sociodemographic profiles.

The rationale for comparison with Greek QOLIE-31 data is further supported by linguistic, cultural and healthcare system similarities between Greece and Cyprus, which reduce variability related to cultural perceptions of health, reporting tendencies and healthcare access. Cultural factors are known to influence patients’ subjective assessment of quality of life, coping strategies, social functioning and perceived support. Thus, the Greek data offer a more appropriate and culturally relevant reference point compared to global data alone.

Moreover, the relevance of using Greek-language measures in the Cypriot context is reinforced by previous validation work. In a study validating the Greek version of the QOLIE-89 in Cyprus, patients evaluated the content validity, clarity and representativeness of the instrument (overall QoL score = 68.8, SD = 16.0) (Panayides et al., 2004). The majority (92.9%) endorsed the representativeness of the assessment, and significant correlations were observed between overall QoL scores and patient satisfaction. However, participants also identified several areas where content could be improved or better tailored to individual concerns, highlighting the importance of using culturally and linguistically adapted tools in evaluating health-related quality of life in local populations. These findings support the applicability and appropriateness of using validated Greek-language QoL instruments in the Cypriot epilepsy population while recognising the importance of individualised patient assessment.

The use of two separate cut-off points (60 and 70) for subgroup analyses allowed for further exploration of different levels of QoL impairment or preservation within the cohort, thereby facilitating more refined statistical comparisons with both regional and global benchmarks. When compared to the reference Greek study, independent samples t-test indicated that the overall QoL score of the current study’s patients with epilepsy (PWE) (M = 75.3, SD = 19.91) was significantly higher than the corresponding Greek QoL score (M = 69.6, SD = 18.09), t(84) = 2.452, p = 0.016, Cohen’s D = 0.286. In addition, significant differences were observed for the ‘Seizure worry’ (scale 1) and ‘Medication effects’ (scale 6) subscale scores, as presented in Supplemental Table S4.

Similarly, when compared to the reference U.S. study, the overall QoL score of the current study’s PWE (M = 75.3, SD = 19.91) was significantly higher than the U.S. QoL score (M = 62.9, SD = 16.31), t(84) = 5.739, p < 0.001, Cohen’s D = 0.623. Furthermore, significant differences were identified for the ‘Seizure worry’ (scale 1), ‘Energy/fatigue’ (scale 4), ‘Cognitive functioning’ (scale 5), ‘Medication effects’ (scale 6), and ‘Social functioning’ (scale 7) subscales, as shown in Supplemental Table S4.

Correlations

Correlations were examined between quality-of-life scores, adherence levels, demographic and clinical variables, including age, gender, education level, employment status, marital status, household living arrangements, number of children, age at epilepsy onset, duration of illness, treatment regimen (monotherapy vs polytherapy), and benzodiazepine use. Statistically significant associations identified through these analyses are presented in Supplemental Tables S5, S6, S7 and S8.

To examine whether quality of life scores differ among patients with different MMAS-8 adherence level (high, medium, low), a non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test was performed, since QoL scores weren’t normally distributed. The results showed no significant difference, H (2, N = 85) = 3.904, p = 0.142. The same test was performed to examine whether quality of life scores differ among patients with different MMAS-8 adherence type (adherent, unintentional non-adherence, intentional non-adherence, mixed non-adherence). The results showed that mean quality of life scores differ significantly between different adherence types, H (3, N = 85) = 11.427, p = 0.010. Post-hoc analysis using Bonferroni correction indicated which adherence types specifically are significant. The results revealed that mixed non-adherent patients had significantly lower QoL compared to intentional non-adherent patients (p = 0.014). There was no significant difference between the rest of the pairwise comparisons, as shown in Supplemental Table S5.

A statistically significant association between employment status and QoL overall score was observed (OR for employed versus unemployed/retired/housekeeping 4.839, 95% CI 1.479–15.827, p = 0.009), with employed PWE having a higher probability of reporting QoL overall scores higher than 70 than the rest.

Similarly, a weak but statistically significant association was observed between household living arrangements and the overall QoL score (OR for living alone versus living with spouse/parents/siblings 0.218, 95% CI 0.066–0.721, p = 0.009).

The key finding from this analysis was the correlation between medication adherence scores – assessed using both adherence tools – and overall QoL scores, applying both global and regional average cut-off values, as presented in Supplemental Table S6. In practical terms, patients with epilepsy who reported higher adherence scores demonstrated a higher likelihood of achieving QoL overall scores above both the global and regional average cut-off values.

For the MMAS-8 tool, this association was observed only in group B (high + medium vs low adherence) and solely for the regional average cut-off value (70). In contrast, for the MARS-5 tool, a significant association was identified across all groups for the regional average cut-off value (70), and specifically for group B (≥4.8 vs <4.8) when applying the global average cut-off value (60).

A statistically significant association between marital status and adherence was observed in assessments with both tools (MMAS-8: OR 2.922, 95% CI 1.163–7.340, p = 0.023) (MARS-5: OR 2.670, 95% CI 1.017–7.011, p = 0.046), with married PWE having a higher probability of reporting high adherence scores compared to single/divorced/widowed PWE, as presented in Supplemental Table S7.

Statistically significant associations between quality-of-life subscales scores, adherence levels, demographic and clinical variables are presented in Supplemental Table S8.

Discussion

This study represents the first comprehensive investigation conducted in Cyprus to explore the associations between demographic characteristics, medication adherence, readiness for change, and health-related quality of life (QoL) among patients with epilepsy (PWE). The findings contribute novel data to the regional literature while complementing and extending existing international evidence.

The sample demonstrated substantial variability in demographic and clinical characteristics, with a balanced gender distribution, a mean age of approximately 51 years, and representation across employment statuses. These characteristics are consistent with those reported in international studies of adult PWE populations, suggesting representativeness for patients receiving epilepsy care within the Cypriot healthcare system.

Most participants were managed with monotherapy, while benzodiazepine use was reported by a minority, reflecting current clinical practice guidelines that prioritise seizure control while minimising polypharmacy and sedative use. The relatively low prevalence of benzodiazepine use may reflect growing awareness of associated cognitive and dependency risks in chronic epilepsy management. A complete list of the antiepileptic medicinal products that are available in the Republic of Cyprus is presented in Supplemental Table S9. (Pharmaceutical Services – Ministry of Health, Republic of Cyprus, n.d.)

A key finding of this study was the high QoL scores observed in the Cypriot cohort (mean QOLIE-31 score: 75.3, SD = 19.91), exceeding both the global average (59.8) (Saadi et al., 2016) and previously reported Greek (69.6) and U.S. (62.9) reference values. These findings suggest favourable health-related quality of life outcomes in this population. Multiple factors may contribute to these differences, including advances in clinical management, improved seizure control, stable disease status, strong therapeutic alliances, and patient trust in healthcare providers. Given the linguistic, cultural, and healthcare system similarities between Cyprus and Greece, these findings also underscore the value of using regionally validated instruments for meaningful comparisons.

Several QoL subscales, including ‘Seizure worry’ and ‘Medication effects,’ demonstrated significant differences compared to both Greek and U.S. studies. Lower seizure-related anxiety and better reported medication tolerability may reflect individualised drug regimens, fewer breakthrough seizures, and effective physician-patient communication. These aspects are clinically meaningful, as seizure worry and medication side effects remain major contributors to impaired QoL in many PWE populations.

Importantly, this study identified robust associations between medication adherence and QoL. Both MMAS-8 and MARS-5 demonstrated that higher adherence levels correlated with higher QoL, particularly when applying regional cut-off values. This outcome emphasises medication adherence as a modifiable factor with direct implications for patient well-being and support the integration of routine adherence monitoring into standard clinical care. This finding agrees with previous studies (Eatock & Baker, 2007; Hu et al., 2020; Lin et al., 2016; Munaf & Mohammed, 2024; Shakir & Al-Asadi, 2012) in which medication adherence was significantly correlated with QoL.

A particularly novel contribution of this study lies in the categorisation of non-adherence into intentional, unintentional, and mixed types using the MMAS-8 scoring algorithm. To the best of available knowledge, no previous studies have performed such detailed sub-classification in epilepsy populations. Notably, mixed non-adherence was significantly associated with the poorest QoL outcomes, suggesting that overlapping cognitive, behavioural, and situational barriers may compound the negative effects of non-adherence. This novel insight highlights the clinical value of not only identifying the presence of non-adherence but also differentiating its underlying causes. Personalised adherence interventions targeting both intentional and unintentional factors may be more effective in improving both medication-taking behaviours and QoL outcomes.

Additionally, this analysis yielded many statistically significant associations across multiple demographic, clinical, and adherence-related variables. While this reflects the complex, multifactorial nature of QoL determinants in epilepsy, it should also be interpreted cautiously, given the potential for type I error when conducting multiple comparisons. Nevertheless, the consistency and strength of key associations – particularly regarding adherence, employment status, and social support – reinforce the robustness and clinical relevance of these findings.

Employment status, marital status, household living arrangements, education level, and treatment regimen also demonstrated independent associations with QoL and adherence. Employment consistently predicted higher QoL, likely reflecting the psychosocial benefits of financial security, daily structure, and social integration. This finding agrees with previous studies (Gu et al., 2016; Lee, 2005) in which employed adult PWE had higher QoL scores. Employment opportunities are influenced by a combination of clinical, psychological, and social factors (Wo et al., 2015). Interestingly, rates of unemployment and underemployment are significantly higher among individuals with epilepsy compared to the general population. Effective seizure management appears to be the key factor in securing and maintaining stable employment, highlighting the critical necessity of ongoing medical care and consistent follow-up for PwE (Narducci et al., 2025).

Marital status was statistically associated with high adherence scores, with both tools (MMAS-8 & MARS-5), with married PWE reporting high adherence scores compared to unmarried PWE. This finding is consistent with previous research on heart failure patients, indicating that unmarried individuals tend to have lower treatment adherence and are nearly twice as likely to experience adverse outcomes compared to those who are married (Wu et al., 2012), Furthermore, being married has been associated with significantly greater adherence to prescribed medication regimens than being single (Jarrah et al., 2023).

Furthermore, patients on monotherapy reported better QoL compared to those on polytherapy, consistent with literature indicating that treatment complexity may contribute to medication burden, cognitive complaints, and diminished well-being.

The readiness for change analysis demonstrated that most participants reported maximal readiness to implement treatment changes, primarily driven by strong trust in their healthcare providers. This finding reinforces the central role of physician-patient trust in treatment decision-making. However, a subgroup expressed hesitancy, often related to prior negative experiences (i.e. unpleasant side-effects experienced after medication treatment changes in the past), anxiety, or a desire for more detailed information prior to modifying therapy. These findings support the adoption of patient-centred communication strategies and shared decision-making models that incorporate both clinical expertise and individual patient preferences.

From a broader perspective, these findings align with international trends promoting holistic, multidisciplinary epilepsy care models that extend beyond seizure control alone. Epilepsy management increasingly encompasses the complex interplay of physical, psychological, cognitive, and social factors that shape patient experiences and outcomes.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The study relied on self-reported measures, which may introduce reporting or recall bias. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences, limiting conclusions about temporal relationships. The sample size, while adequate for the analyses performed, may restrict generalisability, and replication in larger, multicentred studies are warranted. Furthermore, although validated Greek-language instruments were used, additional psychometric evaluation of these tools in the Cypriot population may further enhance future research. Currently, the global integration of pharmacist-led interventions in epilepsy management is limited (Petrides et al., 2025). Although a few recent studies have investigated the potential involvement of pharmacists in this field, they underscore the significant role pharmacists can play in supporting medication adherence and improving patients’ quality of life. Ongoing research is necessary to thoroughly assess the clinical outcomes and cost-effectiveness of these interventions, with a focus on comprehensive disease management and patient education. Moreover, increasing both pharmacist and patient awareness of these services is critical to enhancing the overall quality of epilepsy care.

Future directions

The present findings establish a foundation for epilepsy research in Cyprus and suggest several directions for future investigation. Longitudinal studies incorporating objective adherence measures, such as electronic monitoring or pharmacy refill data, are recommended to validate and extend the current findings. Interventions targeting non-adherence profiles, alongside educational programmes supporting shared decision-making, should be explored. Broader examination of healthcare system structures, physician-patient communication, and psychosocial factors may further inform comprehensive patient-centred epilepsy care.

Conclusion

This study provides the first comprehensive evaluation of QoL, medication adherence, and readiness for change among patients with epilepsy in Cyprus. The findings demonstrate significant relationships between adherence behaviours, sociodemographic characteristics, and patient-reported outcomes, while emphasising the critical role of healthcare provider trust. The novel sub-classification of adherence behaviour represents a unique contribution to the field, offering new avenues for personalised intervention. These results contribute valuable regional data while aligning with global priorities that emphasise individualised, multidisciplinary, and patient-centred approaches to epilepsy care.
