Esketamine as an Adjuvant to Levobupivacaine in Thoracic Paravertebral Block for Thoracoscopic Lung Cancer Surgery: A Retrospective Propensity Score-Matched Cohort Study
Authors: Yibing Jing, Xiaoqin Jiang, Bo Hu, Yong Wang, Bingxin Song, Chao Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis retrospective, propensity score-matched cohort study evaluated postoperative analgesic, recovery, and safety outcomes associated with esketamine added to levobupivacaine for thoracic paravertebral block after thoracoscopic lung cancer surgery.MethodsWe analyzed patients undergoing thoracoscopic resection of non-small cell lung cancer between January 2020 and December 2024. Of 412 patients screened, 374 entered 1:1 propensity score matching. The esketamine group received 20 mL of 0.375% levobupivacaine with esketamine (0.5 mg/kg), whereas controls received levobupivacaine alone. The co-primary outcomes were 48-hour cumulative sufentanil consumption and postoperative pain intensity assessed by visual analog scale scores at 6, 24, and 48 hours. Secondary outcomes were time to first ambulation, chest tube duration, and hospital stay. Calendar-year-adjusted regression and a contemporaneous 2022–2024 analysis assessed sensitivity to temporal differences.ResultsAfter matching, 328 patients (164 per group) were analyzed. Mean 48-hour sufentanil consumption was 30.2% lower with esketamine (55.2 ± 11.9 μg vs 79.1 ± 12.8 μg; P < 0.001), and pain scores at rest and during coughing were lower at all assessed times (all P < 0.001). Recorded times to first ambulation, chest tube removal, and discharge were shorter (all P < 0.001). The estimated association with sufentanil consumption was materially unchanged after calendar-year adjustment (β = −24.2 μg; 95% CI, −27.1 to −21.2) and in the contemporaneous subset (β = −24.3 μg; 95% CI, −27.6 to −21.0). Psychomimetic events occurred in 14/164 esketamine recipients (8.5%) and no controls.ConclusionEsketamine added to levobupivacaine for thoracic paravertebral block was associated with lower postoperative opioid consumption and pain scores and shorter recorded times to recovery milestones. However, residual confounding cannot be excluded, and the 8.5% psychomimetic event rate does not support routine adoption of the 0.5 mg/kg regimen. Prospective randomized dose-finding trials are warranted.

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) remains one of the most common thoracic malignancies, and surgical resection offers the best chance of cure. The widespread adoption of video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) has reduced operative trauma considerably, yet postoperative pain continues to pose a major clinical problem. Thoracic incisions, chest tube placement, and pleural irritation all contribute to pain that, when undertreated, suppresses effective coughing and predisposes patients to pulmonary complications.1,2 Poorly controlled acute pain is also an independent risk factor for chronic post-surgical pain (CPSP).3 At the same time, heavy reliance on opioids carries its own costs: postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), ileus, and respiratory depression each run counter to the goals of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS).4

Ultrasound-guided thoracic paravertebral block (TPVB) has become an established component of multimodal analgesia in VATS.1,5 Published duration estimates vary with concentration, technique, and outcome definition; in a randomized TPVB study, 0.5% levobupivacaine provided a mean sensory-block duration of approximately 10 hours.6 This time-limited effect may not cover the full postoperative pain trajectory after VATS. To extend TPVB analgesia, various adjuvants have been studied, including dexamethasone, dexmedetomidine, betamethasone, and more recently, ketamine derivatives.7–10

Esketamine, the S-enantiomer of ketamine, is a potent non-competitive NMDA receptor antagonist that may attenuate central sensitization and opioid-induced hyperalgesia through a pharmacologic pathway distinct from the vasoconstrictive or anti-inflammatory actions proposed for some other adjuvants.11–13 However, whether perineural esketamine prolongs local blockade, and whether any observed effect is mediated locally or through systemic absorption, remain uncertain. Direct evidence for esketamine mixed with local anesthetic in peripheral nerve blocks remains limited.14 Separate studies have evaluated intravenous or systemic esketamine used alongside regional techniques or during thoracic surgery,15–17 but these interventions are distinct from perineural admixture. Direct perineural application in TPVB for lung cancer resection has received little attention.

Against this background, we conducted a retrospective, propensity score-matched cohort study to examine whether the use of esketamine with levobupivacaine in TPVB was associated with lower cumulative opioid consumption, lower postoperative pain scores, and shorter times to selected recovery milestones compared with levobupivacaine alone in patients undergoing VATS for NSCLC. Sensitivity analyses incorporating surgery calendar year and restricting the cohort to the overlapping treatment period were performed to evaluate the influence of temporal differences arising from the sequential introduction of esketamine into clinical practice.

Methods

Study Design and Ethical Approval

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at our hospital between January 2020 and December 2024. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of The Yixing Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital (Approval number: 2024-LW-073) and adhered strictly to the Declaration of Helsinki. Individual informed consent was waived due to the retrospective and anonymized nature of the study.

Patient grouping was based on the TPVB regimen documented in the anesthesia records. The addition of esketamine reflected evolving clinical practice at our institution: esketamine became available as an optional TPVB adjuvant from January 2022 onward, and its use was determined by the attending anesthesiologist based on clinical judgment. Before this period, levobupivacaine alone was the standard regimen. Because esketamine was optional rather than mandatory, control patients were drawn from 2020–2024, including patients operated during 2022–2024 who did not receive the adjuvant, whereas intervention patients were drawn from 2022–2024. To examine the sensitivity of the findings to this temporal difference, we compared annual distributions of key baseline covariates and performed a prespecified multivariable sensitivity analysis that included surgery calendar year as a continuous covariate, as described in Statistical Analysis.

Adult patients (18–75 years, ASA I–III) who underwent elective thoracoscopic radical resection of NSCLC under general anesthesia combined with ultrasound-guided TPVB were screened. Exclusion criteria included: (1) contraindications to regional anesthesia; (2) history of chronic opioid use; (3) severe hepatic or renal dysfunction (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2); (4) psychiatric disorders affecting pain or hallucination assessments; and (5) intraoperative conversion to open thoracotomy or re-operation within 48 hours. A detailed breakdown of the 38 excluded patients by reason is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Intervention Protocols

Ultrasound-guided TPVB was performed after induction of general anesthesia and positioning in the lateral decubitus position. The target level was selected according to the planned surgical incision and chest tube site. A linear 8–12 MHz probe was used for continuous ultrasound guidance. The product information for levobupivacaine hydrochloride injection (Jiangsu Hengrui Pharmaceuticals Co., Ltd., China) includes its use for nerve block or infiltration anesthesia in adults, with a maximum single dose of 150 mg. The control group received a single-shot TPVB with 20 mL of 0.375% levobupivacaine, corresponding to a total dose of 75 mg. The esketamine group received esketamine (0.5 mg/kg) mixed directly with levobupivacaine and injected into the thoracic paravertebral space, with the total injection volume maintained at 20 mL. The perineural esketamine–levobupivacaine regimen constituted off-label use and had undergone review by the hospital’s Pharmacy Administration and Therapeutics Committee before its clinical introduction. Following negative aspiration, the solution was injected with standard intraoperative monitoring in place. Standard resuscitation equipment and medications were immediately available in the operating room. Representative parasagittal ultrasound images before and after injection are shown in Figure 1. For patients with a BMI <30 kg/m2, the esketamine dose was calculated using actual body weight. For patients with a BMI ≥30 kg/m2, adjusted body weight (AdjBW) was used, calculated as AdjBW = IBW + 0.4 × (actual body weight − IBW). Ideal body weight (IBW) was calculated as 50 kg + 0.91 × [height (cm) − 152.4] for men and 45.5 kg + 0.91 × [height (cm) − 152.4] for women. The 0.5 mg/kg regimen reflected institutional practice, the pharmacologic rationale of NMDA receptor antagonism, and limited evidence for ketamine or esketamine as regional anesthetic adjuvants.11,14 Evidence from intravenous or systemic esketamine studies was considered separately because it does not directly establish the efficacy or neural safety of perineural administration.15–17

General anesthesia followed a common institutional framework. Anesthesia was induced with propofol, and rocuronium was used for neuromuscular blockade. Maintenance consisted of either intravenous anesthesia or combined intravenous–inhalational anesthesia. Remifentanil was administered by continuous infusion and individually titrated according to surgical stimulation. Postoperatively, all patients received a patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) pump for 48 hours. The PCA formulation contained sufentanil (2 μg/kg total dose) diluted with normal saline to a total volume of 100 mL, programmed with a background infusion of 0.5 mL/h, a bolus dose of 2 mL, and a 15-minute lockout interval.

Data Collection

The co-primary outcomes were 48-hour cumulative sufentanil consumption and postoperative pain intensity, assessed using Visual Analog Scale (VAS, 0–10) scores at 6, 24, and 48 hours after surgery, both at rest and during active coughing. Postoperative pain intensity was extracted from routine anesthesia follow-up records, in which pain was documented as VAS scores on a 0–10 scale. Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) assessments were not routinely recorded and were therefore unavailable for retrospective analysis. Secondary recovery outcomes included time to first ambulation, duration of chest tube retention, and total length of hospital stay. Postoperative adverse events—including postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), psychomimetic events, and respiratory depression—were extracted from nursing records, PACU records, and anesthesia follow-up sheets by a trained data abstractor blinded to the study hypothesis.

Psychomimetic events were captured as a composite endpoint encompassing two categories: (i) hallucinations documented with structured details regarding severity, duration, onset, and management; and (ii) brief behavioral disturbances, such as transient disorientation, recorded without a structured neuropsychiatric assessment. Nine hallucination cases were classified as CTCAE Grade 1 because they resolved spontaneously without pharmacological intervention. Structured severity grading was unavailable for the five behavioral disturbances. Routine anesthesia follow-up and ward records were also reviewed through hospital discharge for newly developed sensory disturbance, persistent numbness, motor weakness, and other adverse events.

Postoperative respiratory depression was identified from PACU records and defined as a respiratory rate <10 breaths/min and/or SpO2 <90% after extubation, accompanied by reduced responsiveness or requiring clinical intervention, including verbal or tactile stimulation, supplemental oxygen, or naloxone.

Statistical Analysis

Given the observational design, 1:1 nearest-neighbor propensity score matching (PSM) without replacement was employed to balance measured baseline characteristics. The eligible cohort contained 187 control patients and 187 esketamine-treated patients. Because a 1:2 matching ratio without replacement would have required 374 eligible controls to retain all treated patients, 1:1 matching was selected to retain the largest number of independent matched pairs while maintaining the prespecified caliper and covariate balance. A logistic regression model generated propensity scores based on age, sex, BMI, ASA class, tumor stage, smoking status, preoperative FEV1% predicted, and surgical duration, with a caliper set at 0.05. Post-matching covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD), with SMD <0.1 considered indicative of adequate balance.

Continuous variables were first tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using Student’s t-test; 48-hour cumulative sufentanil consumption satisfied normality (Shapiro–Wilk P = 0.185 and P = 0.064 in the control and esketamine groups, respectively) and is therefore reported as mean ± SD. As a sensitivity check, this outcome was also compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test, which yielded a directionally identical result (P < 0.001). Non-normally distributed variables, including ordinal VAS scores, are presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables are expressed as counts (%) and analyzed using Pearson’s χ2-test or Fisher’s exact test. Because two co-primary outcomes were specified, a Bonferroni-adjusted two-sided significance threshold of α = 0.025 was applied to each co-primary outcome. Multivariable linear regression was performed to evaluate the association between esketamine use and opioid consumption after matching. All eight covariates entered in the propensity score model were also included as adjustment variables in the outcome regression to further adjust for measured covariates. HC1 heteroskedasticity-consistent standard errors were used for all regression models.

Sensitivity analysis for temporal confounding: Because the treatment groups differed in their calendar-time distributions, a prespecified sensitivity analysis was conducted by adding surgery calendar year as a continuous covariate to the multivariable regression model. The stability of the esketamine coefficient after calendar-year adjustment was evaluated to assess whether the primary association was sensitive to a linear secular trend. Annual mean sufentanil consumption in the matched control group was also examined descriptively. As a complementary analysis to reduce temporal separation between groups, the cohort was restricted to patients operated during 2022–2024, when both treatment regimens were concurrently available, and the covariate-adjusted regression was re-estimated. A within-control comparison between the 2020–2021 and 2022–2024 periods was performed to examine whether the evaluated outcomes differed between these periods.

A two-tailed P < 0.05 indicated statistical significance, with Bonferroni adjustment (α = 0.025) applied to each co-primary outcome as noted above. The matched cohort of 164 per group provided 80% power to detect a between-group difference of approximately 4.1 μg in 48-hour sufentanil consumption (α = 0.025 two-tailed, assumed SD = 12 μg), substantially smaller than the effect size anticipated from prior literature. All analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Multivariable Regression and Sensitivity Analysis for Temporal Confounding

In the covariate-adjusted primary multivariable linear regression model (Table 4, Model 1), esketamine use was associated with lower 48-hour opioid consumption (β = −23.9, 95% CI: −26.6 to −21.3; P < 0.001). Adverse Events

Psychomimetic events occurred in 14/164 (8.5%) esketamine recipients and 0/164 controls (Fisher exact P < 0.001). These included nine hallucinations documented as CTCAE Grade 1, with a median duration of 15 minutes (range 5–30), and five brief behavioral disturbances, such as transient disorientation, for which structured severity grading was unavailable. All documented events were transient, and none required pharmacological treatment or psychiatric consultation. No newly developed sensory disturbance, persistent numbness, or motor weakness was documented through hospital discharge.

PONV occurred in 24.4% of control patients and 21.3% of esketamine-treated patients (P = 0.599). Respiratory depression was documented during PACU monitoring after extubation in 5/164 control patients (3.0%) and 4/164 esketamine-treated patients (2.4%; P = 1.000). Two control patients developed oxygen desaturation with unresponsiveness and received naloxone. The events documented in the esketamine group were transient and resolved promptly after verbal or tactile stimulation; none required naloxone.

Discussion

In this propensity score-matched cohort of 328 patients undergoing VATS for NSCLC, the use of 0.5 mg/kg esketamine with levobupivacaine in TPVB was associated with lower 48-hour sufentanil consumption, lower pain scores at rest and during coughing, and shorter recorded times to first ambulation, chest tube removal, and hospital discharge. Psychomimetic events occurred in 8.5% of esketamine recipients and were not documented in controls.

Previous studies seeking to extend TPVB analgesia have primarily evaluated dexmedetomidine, corticosteroids, and opioid adjuvants. Randomized studies reported longer analgesia or lower postoperative analgesic requirements with dexmedetomidine, dexamethasone, betamethasone, or sufentanil added to regional block solutions.7,8,10 A case series also examined dexmedetomidine combined with methylene blue.9 These findings support continued investigation of TPVB adjuvants, but they do not establish the efficacy or safety of perineural esketamine.

The 30.2% lower mean opioid consumption is consistent with the pharmacologic rationale for NMDA receptor antagonism, which may attenuate central sensitization and opioid-induced hyperalgesia.12,13,18,19 Zeng et al20 evaluated intravenous esketamine for rebound pain after TPVB regression, but systemic and perineural administration are distinct exposures. The mechanism of esketamine administered into the paravertebral space therefore remains uncertain. Systemic absorption may have contributed to both analgesic and psychomimetic effects, but plasma concentrations were not measured. Moreover, an association persisting for 48 hours does not demonstrate prolonged local blockade or a specific peripheral mechanism. Pharmacokinetic and route-comparison studies are required before attributing these findings to local rather than systemic exposure.

The association remained similar after adjustment for calendar year, restriction to the overlapping 2022–2024 period, and within-control comparisons across periods. These analyses partially reduce concern that a simple linear secular trend explains the observed association, but they cannot exclude nonlinear changes in perioperative practice, unmeasured co-interventions, residual confounding, or confounding by indication. Treatment selection remained at the discretion of the attending anesthesiologist.

Adequate control of movement-evoked pain is important after thoracic surgery because pain may impair coughing and mobilization.1,2 Patients in the esketamine group had shorter recorded times to first ambulation, chest tube removal, and hospital discharge. However, these secondary outcomes should not be interpreted as direct consequences of improved analgesia. They are also influenced by surgeon judgment, chest drainage and air leak, nursing mobilization practices, ward workflow, and discharge logistics. Although institutional criteria were used, including daily drainage <200 mL, absence of air leak, and radiological lung re-expansion for chest tube removal, residual clinician discretion and system-level influences remained.4,21,22

All documented respiratory events occurred in the PACU after extubation. Because 48-hour cumulative sufentanil consumption was measured largely after these events, it does not represent opioid exposure preceding the episodes. Two control patients received naloxone for oxygen desaturation with unresponsiveness, whereas the events in the esketamine group resolved with verbal or tactile stimulation. This difference in management reflected the documented severity of individual PACU events, but the small event counts and unavailable intraoperative opioid doses preclude causal attribution.

The 30.2% lower mean 48-hour sufentanil consumption must be weighed against psychomimetic events in 8.5% of esketamine recipients. These percentages describe different measures and cannot be compared as a direct numerical trade-off. Although the documented events were transient and required no pharmacological treatment, hallucinations and disorientation are clinically relevant after cancer surgery. Because treatment was not randomized and adverse events were retrospectively captured, these data cannot establish a favorable benefit–risk balance for the 0.5 mg/kg regimen. Routine use therefore cannot be recommended. Future prospective dose-finding studies should evaluate lower perineural doses, including 0.15–0.25 mg/kg, to determine whether potential analgesic and opioid-sparing benefits can be retained while reducing psychomimetic events, with systematic neuropsychiatric and neurologic safety assessment.

Limitations

This study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, its retrospective, single-institution design limits causal inference. Because esketamine use was determined by the attending anesthesiologist rather than assigned by protocol, confounding by indication cannot be excluded, and propensity score matching can adjust only for measured covariates. The groups also differed in their calendar-time distributions; although the year-adjusted and overlapping-period analyses partially reduce concern that a secular practice trend explains the association, they cannot exclude nonlinear or stepwise changes in perioperative care, clinician preferences, unrecorded co-interventions, or other residual confounding. The identity of the attending anesthesiologist was unavailable, precluding clustering analysis.

Second, patient-level intraoperative opioid doses could not be consistently extracted, because anesthesia documentation transitioned from handwritten to electronic records during the study period, and were therefore unavailable for adjustment. As intraoperative opioid exposure can influence both early postoperative pain and subsequent opioid consumption, residual confounding from differences in intraoperative opioid management cannot be excluded and may affect the magnitude of the observed effect.

Third, the secondary recovery outcomes—time to first ambulation, chest tube duration, and hospital stay—are influenced by clinician- and system-level decisions and cannot be attributed solely to differences in analgesia; the single-center design further limits generalizability to institutions with different patient populations, surgical volumes, or ERAS protocols.

Fourth, only a single esketamine dose (0.5 mg/kg) was studied, and drug-specific safety was assessed retrospectively. Neurologic safety relied on routine documentation through hospital discharge without standardized examinations or long-term follow-up, and plasma esketamine concentrations were not measured, so subclinical or delayed neurotoxicity cannot be excluded and route-specific pharmacokinetic interpretation is limited. Psychomimetic events were captured from clinical records rather than a validated dissociative-symptom instrument and some behavioral disturbances could not be uniformly graded, while episode-level PACU respiratory data were incomplete; together with the small number of events, this precluded reliable comparison of event severity, and chronic postsurgical pain was not assessed.

Prospective, multicenter, randomized controlled trials with systematic neurologic and neuropsychiatric assessment, route-specific pharmacokinetic evaluation, and longer follow-up are warranted.

Conclusion

In this propensity score-matched retrospective cohort study, perineural esketamine used with levobupivacaine in TPVB was associated with lower postoperative opioid consumption and pain scores and shorter recorded times to selected recovery milestones after VATS for NSCLC. The associations remained consistent across calendar-time sensitivity analyses, but residual confounding and confounding by indication remain possible. Psychomimetic events occurred in 8.5% of esketamine recipients, limiting the clinical acceptability of the 0.5 mg/kg regimen; these retrospective findings do not support its routine use. Prospective randomized trials should evaluate lower doses, such as 0.15–0.25 mg/kg, with systematic neuropsychiatric and neurologic safety assessment.
