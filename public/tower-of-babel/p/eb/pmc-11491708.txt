Protocol to characterize mouse dural mast cells by flow cytometry and immunofluorescence
Authors: Chih-Chung Jerry Lin, Nader Jaafar, Rudolph E. Tanzi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryMast cells, which constitute tissue-resident immune cells, are distributed in the dural meninges. Here, we provide procedural guidelines for investigating mouse dural mast cells using two techniques. First, we outline the procedures for dural tissue dissection, single-cell isolation, and subsequent surface staining for mast cell identification via flow cytometry. We then describe the techniques employed for whole dura tissue staining to visualize mast cells using confocal and slide scanning microscopy, followed by analysis using Nikon’s NIS-Elements Advanced Research software.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Lin et al.1

Before you begin

The dural layer of the meninges is suggested to play a significant role in regulating the brain’s environment through lymphatic drainage and by housing various immune cell populations.2 While there is considerable knowledge about meningeal macrophages, B cells, and T cells, our understanding of the functions of tissue-resident mast cells remains limited. Here, we provided flow cytometry and immunofluorescence protocols for researchers to investigate the characteristics of dural mast cells in laboratory mice.

Institutional permissions

Mice used in the protocol were housed in our specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility, maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle, and provided with unrestricted access to both food and water. All procedures outlined in this protocol were performed in compliance with approvals obtained from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Massachusetts General Hospital.

Expected outcomes

Flow cytometry analysis of dural mast cell

After acquiring dural cells by the flow cytometer, we use forward scatter area (FSC-A) by height (FSC-A) and side scatter area (SSC-A) by height (SSC-H) to exclude doublet events. Next, we use LIVE/DEAD stain and anti-CD45 antibody to gate viable immune cells. A series of lineage markers (TCRβ, Ly6G, CD115, and CD11b) are employed to remove T cells, neutrophils, monocytes, and macrophages. Finally, dural mast cells are identified as Kit and FcεRIα double positive cells. This population is absent in the Cpa3Cre/+ mice, which carry a targeted Cre recombinase insertion into the Cpa3 locus, resulting in mast cell deficiency4 (Figures 1B and 1C).

Immunofluorescence of dural mast cell

Dural mast cells express Kit on the cell surface and tryptase stored in the intracellular granules. After capturing dura images using a confocal microscope, one should visualize scattered mast cells double labeled by anti-Kit and anti-tryptase antibodies (Figure 2B, left). Many of these cells are located along the blood vessels and in the meningeal sinuses. Dural mast cells are absent in Cpa3Cre/+ mice as expected (Figure 2B, right).

Limitations

This protocol focuses on using flow cytometry and immunofluorescence to study mast cells residing in the mouse dural meninges. Please refer to Roussel-Queval et al. for a protocol to isolate other dural immune cells.5 The current protocol is not applicable for examining mast cells in other species.

The dura cell single-cell preparation only captures a portion of the mast cells present in the meninges, resulting in a limited cell yield for flow cytometry analysis. Additionally, the immunofluorescence protocol does not stain all dural mast cells residing in the sinuses due to the tissue’s thickness.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Tissue tearing during skull cap removal.

Potential solution

When removing the brain from the skull cap, make sure to not scrape the interior of the skull cap (steps 8–10; 36–38).

Problem 2

Tissue tearing or clumping when detaching it from skull cap.

Potential solution

When detaching the dural meninges from the skull cap, do not make any large movements with the forceps. Make sure to only make small movements (steps 8–11; 36–39).

Problem 3

Tissue getting stuck to the forceps.

Potential solution

Observe the forceps under the dissection microscope, making sure the forceps are sharp, with no hooks or dents (steps 11 and 39).

Problem 4

Low cell yield after dura digestion for flow cytometry.

Potential solution

•Use a P200 pipet to collect the cell suspension on the other side of the strainers to increase the yield (step 15).•When cell yield per mouse dura is less than 1×105 cells, combine suspended dural cells from multiple animals to increase the cell number.•We typically obtain a viability of 10–15% live CD45+ immune cells as determined by flow cytometry.
