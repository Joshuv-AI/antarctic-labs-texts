A student-community partnership to enhance cancer research training
Authors: Rebecca J Melillo, Christiane El Khoury, Amy L Shaver, Moriah Cunningham, Nathalia Benavides, Quezia Lacerda, Felix J Kim, Amy E Leader
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite the importance of community involvement in research, little formal training in community outreach and engagement (COE) is offered to cancer research trainees. A collaboration between the Office of COE and the Office of Cancer Research Training and Education Coordination (CRTEC) at the Sidney Kimmel Comprehensive Cancer Center at Jefferson led to the COE-CRTEC Trainee Working Group, a unique program in which trainees in cancer research each created a novel COE initiative.MethodsFour cancer research trainees were selected to serve as COE Program Liaisons (CPLs), each aligned with one of the four cancer center research programs. Each CPL developed, implemented, and evaluated a project that enhanced the bidirectional relationship between their research and the community. Trainees were provided a modest budget, support from the Office of COE, and a requirement to complete the project within one academic year.ResultsProjects included a cancer education seminar for older adults at a senior center, a prostate cancer education and screening event at a predominantly African American church, a video demonstrating a day in the life of a skin cancer researcher, and a podcast that featured SKCCC investigators answering research questions from community members.ConclusionStudents who would not typically be exposed to COE training gained experience developing, implementing, and evaluating community-based initiatives. Projects were diverse in topic and approach, reflecting the diversity of the trainees and the community. Allowing trainees, those who are the next generation of cancer researchers, to design community-based research may lead to more patient-centered research in the future.

Background

Community outreach and engagement in cancer research training

Engaging community members and cancer patients in the cancer research process helps ensure that scientific discoveries are inclusive, responsive, and trusted [1].Research proposals and grants, including those from Congressionally Mandated Medical Research Programs, the American Cancer Society (ACS), and the Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Institute (PCORI), are often reviewed with attention to community engagement. Additionally, the National Cancer Institute (NCI) Cancer Centers Support Grant guidelines require community outreach and engagement (COE) to span all aspects of a cancer center’s programming, including basic, clinical, translational, and population research [2].

Despite the emphasis on community and patient involvement in cancer research, formal training or experiential learning in COE is rarely incorporated into cancer research degree programs. Because of this, research trainees typically do not have the opportunity to interface with patients or community members affected by cancer nor understand ways that they may influence future research. Additionally, trainees often do not have experience translating their research projects and results to be easily understood by the public.

SKCCC COE – CRTEC trainee working group

The NCI-designated Sidney Kimmel Comprehensive Cancer Center (SKCCC) at Jefferson created a program in which trainees in cancer research programs had the opportunity to develop, implement, and evaluate a novel community outreach initiative. This program resulted from a collaboration between two NCI core components: the Offices of COE and the Cancer Research Training and Education Coordination (CRTEC) [2]. In addition to the valuable and unique experience provided to students, these novel initiatives allowed for the expansion of the bidirectional relationship between the SKCCC and its catchment or geographic service area. Selected trainees were designated as COE Program Liaisons (CPLs), with the expectation that they serve as liaisons to their respective research programs and oversee the COE project from conception to evaluation.

Methods

Trainee selection and program infrastructure

Trainee recruitment, led by CRTEC, aimed to select a diverse group of trainees across various dimensions, including race, ethnicity, and academic background. Recruitment started with advertisements within academic programs affiliated with the SKCCC at Thomas Jefferson University and Drexel University, including degree-granting programs in Biochemistry, Structural and Molecular Biology, Cell Biology and Regenerative Medicine, Genetics, Genomics and Cancer Biology, Immunology and Microbial Pathogenesis, Physician Scientist MD/PhD, Pharmacology and Physiology, and Population Health Sciences. Enrollment was open to any student receiving a graduate degree (Master’s, MD, or PhD) or in a formal mentorship program (post-doc, residents, fellows).

Recruiting materials were distributed to SKCCC leadership and faculty to share within their labs. Interested trainees were required to submit a Curriculum Vitae and cover letter to COE and CRTEC leadership. Trainees who were part of a research lab sought approval from their faculty mentor prior to program application. Interviews were conducted where trainees discussed novel ideas for COE projects for consideration and leadership shared program expectations. Additionally, candidates were evaluated on their passion for serving the community, previous experiences working or volunteering in a community setting, willingness to learn, ability to recruit other student volunteers, and communication skills. In selecting candidates, prior cancer research or cancer-related experience were not explicitly evaluated. This decision was strategic given that all candidates were affiliated with SKCCC’s training programs, it was assumed they had a foundational level of experience or interest in cancer research. Also, the primary focus of the program was to enhance community outreach across the cancer care spectrum. Advertising this prioritized candidates with robust community engagement passion over traditional research experience. Trainees were required to have at least one academic year remaining in their program and the ability to commit 1–2 hours per week to the initiative.

A weighted matrix was used to score and select four trainees from eight applicants. Each trainee was aligned with one of the cancer center’s four research programs: Cancer Risk and Control (CRiC), Immune Cell Regulation and Targeting (IRT), Molecular Oncology Regulation and Approaches (MORA), and Translational and Cellular Oncology (TaCO). A faculty mentor was assigned to each trainee to provide research guidance and ensure that the projects aligned with the aims of the program. Once trainees were selected, the Office of COE worked with research program leadership to identify faculty members for candidates unaffiliated with a faculty mentor. All CPLs received support from the Office of COE including project facilitation, guidance on community connections, and program evaluation. As this was a mentored, educational activity, trainees were not compensated for their work.

Project proposal conception and critical review

Each CPL was tasked to develop a COE project that enhanced the bidirectional relationship between their research and the communities in our cancer center’s catchment area. Additionally, the project was required to reflect one of the major research areas of focus of their research program and be achievable within one academic year. Based on the CPL’s project ideas, the Office of COE assisted in connecting the CPL’s with a community organization from existing community partnerships as needed. From the time of candidate selection, CPLs had four months to work with their mentor and the office of COE to develop and submit their proposal; including the project’s purpose and target population, project aims, methods, involved personnel, timeline, and expected impact on the SKCCC catchment area. Proposals underwent two rounds of review from the office of COE, where feedback was provided, and, ultimately, projects were approved for implementation to be completed within the academic year.

Ongoing CPL support

To ensure proper guidance for CPLs from conception to project completion, the office of COE organized monthly meetings to monitor progress and provide constructive feedback. These meetings were led by COE program staff and contained the CPL’s faculty advisor, the Associate Director for COE, and the Associate Director for CRTEC. Additional meetings between CPL and mentor, COE, or CRTEC staff were held as needed. Each CPL was provided with a $250 budget for project needs. In addition to the budget, CPLs were offered connections to various SKCCC resources such as the media department, marketing and communications department and were offered educational sessions on best practices for translating their scientific research to a lay audience.

Results

Our trainee group was diverse, comprising individuals aged 23 to 46 including African American, White, and Hispanic participants from four different departments and at different timepoints in their studies. Below, we highlight the development, implementation, and evaluation of the four projects designed by the first cohort of CPL trainees.

Aging gracefully: Research and practice to keep you healthy

Amy L. Shaver, PhD, PharmD, a geriatric oncology postdoctoral fellow, served as the CPL of the CRiC Program.

Background

Advancing age is one of the most important risk factors for cancer overall and for many individual cancer types [3]. As such, engaging elders in health education and the early detection of cancer is paramount. Aging Gracefully: Research and Practice to Keep You Healthy aimed to educate seniors about a broad range of cancer-relevant topics. The half-day seminar, conducted at a local senior center, featured speakers from the SKCCC, information about cancer and clinical trials, and an interactive integrative medicine session.

Methods

The seminar was developed in collaboration with one of the first senior centers in the country, situated in the city center of Philadelphia, PA, Philadelphia Senior Center. SKCCC subject matter experts provided three presentations that revolved around the needs of the senior adult population: exercise for older adults and those living with cancer; managing finances in older age, particularly if balancing the costs of an expensive disease like cancer; and understanding one’s medication and the risks of polypharmacy. An interactive demonstration of Tai Chi, gentle movements that are considered to be appropriate for seniors, closed the session. Ample time was allocated for attendees to ask questions and converse with the experts. SKCCC broadcast the program live via social media for those unable to attend in person and has since archived the taping on its website for indefinite viewing for all interested.

Role of COE

The Office of COE provided the connection to the senior center, which had previously expressed interest in cancer-related programming. The Office of COE also provided editorial assistance to ensure each scientific presentation was in lay language and facilitated dissemination of the event on social media.

Data collection & analysis

A brief five-question survey, developed and administered by the trainee, assessed the impact of the program. In addition, the content of attendees’ questions and their desires for future presentations were noted. A sign-up sheet was available for anyone to indicate their willingness to be involved in further discussions with a research team.

Findings

Twenty-two people attended the in-person seminar, and fifteen participated in the evaluation (Table 1). Attendees liked the format and would attend future seminars. Future topics of interest included more information on clinical trials, specifically, what clinical trials are, how they work, and how to get involved. Seniors wanted more information about medications (side effects and alternatives), preventive care, and nutrition. In polling the audience, 80% agreed they learned something new. In the four months after the seminar, more than 150 people viewed the seminar recording.

From my Bench to my Community / Desde mi Mesada a mi Comunidad

Nathalia Benavides, a PhD candidate in Immunology and Microbial Pathogenesis, served as the CPL for the IRT program.

Background

In the post-pandemic era of scientific misunderstanding and distrust of public health officials [4], educational efforts targeting local communities must consider communication mechanisms. Very little scientific education is available in Spanish, particularly in skin cancer research. Ethnic and racial minorities are at a significant risk of developing skin cancer due to systemic and structural racism, and there are broad gaps in education and community outreach [5]. Fortunately, researchers have made scientific advancements to characterize the role of various immune cells involved in the surveillance of and repair mechanisms in human skin [6]. Ensuring that this research is available and understandable to the general public, particularly those who speak Spanish, could reduce mistrust of research and a greater acceptance of scientific advancements.

Methods

The CPL aimed to educate the public, including Spanish-speaking communities, on the significance of basic science research in skin cancer by creating a two-minute educational video. The video was filmed in a research laboratory setting, and audio was recorded by the CPL. Medical Media Services edited video and sound at Thomas Jefferson University. The video included diagrams describing cancer progression and footage of a scientist performing experiments. Immediately after the video, there was a four-question survey inquiring about the effectiveness of the content and resources for additional information on skin cancer research and clinical trials. The video and the survey were available in English and Spanish and were disseminated to communities within the SKCCC catchment area through social media platforms like Facebook and Instagram.

Role of COE

The Office of COE provided guidance ensuring the video content was understandable for the public. Additionally, the Office of COE supported the dissemination of the video by sharing it with community partners and by including a QR code for people to view the video at a skin cancer screening event lead by the Mobile Cancer Screening Program, part of the Office of COE.

Data collection & analysis

Video views on each social media platform and survey responses were electronically recorded, collected, and analyzed.

Findings

Current findings include over 800 views on social media platforms (625 views on Instagram, 215 views on Facebook) and 17 survey responses using Qualtrics (4 Spanish and 13 English). Of the survey responses, three out of four questions received an almost 100% “yes” response, thus highlighting the positive receptivity of the content (Table 2). These results suggest that materials like these are effective for educating the community.

Impact/ Lessons learned

Bidirectional engagement between researchers and community members enhances collaboration and scientific discovery. Not only were we able to reach at-risk populations by presenting bilingual material, but, more importantly, the content of the video highlighted the importance of research for skin cancer therapeutics. Though this video reached over 800 people, the exact number who watched the entire video is unknown. Given the anonymous nature of social media view capture, it is unknown if views are unique or if users watched the video multiple times. Future efforts should work to understand the true impact of views, capture which platforms lend to the highest number of unique views, and capture whether or not the video increased trust in the science from the SKCCC. Through this effort and those like it, we hope to continue advancing education and promoting trust between scientists and community members, especially those most vulnerable.

A Church-Based initiative about prostate cancer research and screening

Moriah Cunningham, a PhD candidate in Biomedical Science Cell Biology & Regenerative Medicine, served as the CPL for the MORA program.

Background

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the second leading cause of cancer-related death in men in the United States [7]. African American men (AA) are twice as likely to develop and die from PCa than men of European ancestry (EA) [8]. The SKCCC Catchment Area Report 2022 suggests that one-third of men over the age of forty have never been screened for PCa [9]. This project aimed to provide free PCa screening, patient education, and insights about PCa research to the community most at risk for PCa.

Methods

The event was held at New Covenant Church of Philadelphia, a large, historically African American church in Philadelphia, PA. SKCCC experts presented general information about PCa, promising advances in PCa research, and new approaches to treatment. Speakers included a basic science researcher, a urologist, an epidemiologist, and five PCa patients. Substantial time was devoted to a question-and-answer period with the panelists and the audience. Free PCa screening for prostate-specific antigen (PSA) was offered on-site throughout the event.

Role of COE

The Office of COE assisted in scheduling, advertising, and promoting the event. Additionally, PCa screening was provided by the Mobile Cancer Screening Program at the SKCCC, part of the Office of COE.

Data collection & analysis

The trainee administered a survey to capture the following information: (1) understanding of PCa research, (2) uptake of PCa screening, and (3) suggestions on how to connect researchers and the community.

Findings

Forty-seven AA men attended the event. Of the attendees, 24 (51%) received PSA screening; five of the 24 men (21%) had an elevated PSA level (> 3 ng/mL) and were referred for follow-up care. Twenty-six of the attendees filled out anonymous surveys. All respondents affirmed that the event increased their understanding of PCa research and would return for future events (Table 3). Four men noted this was their first time getting screened for the disease. There was an option to provide an email address if interested in meeting with a researcher to learn more about cancer research; seven men shared their emails and were contacted for follow up with SKCCC researchers.

Impacts/Lessons learned

This event was well received by the community. Attendees provided constructive feedback for future events, including increasing event promotion, offering a virtual option, and inviting influential AA men in the community (sports players, state representatives, etc.) to the event. More importantly, this event created a bidirectional line of communication between researchers and the community. Men at a higher risk for PCa, as well as those eligible for PCa screening, were able to engage in direct conversations with experts in the field while scientists witnessed the community’s perspective and understanding of this disease.

“A growth mindset”: A podcast series to learn about cancer research

Quezia Lacerda, a PhD candidate in Biomedical Science, served as the TaCO CPL.

Background

It is well documented that limited health literacy is associated with adverse health outcomes, as patients have difficulty understanding their illness and navigating care [10, 11]. Similarly, cancer prevention, treatment, and research is often a complex and complicated topic. Thus, it is necessary to establish a platform dedicated to improving communication between community members and SKCCC researchers and clinicians, where patient questions about cancer and cancer research can be explained in a lay language format. Accordingly, this project aims to establish an ongoing podcast series for the community, where community members can have their cancer-related questions answered by SKCCC experts.

Methods

The CPL created a podcast channel, ‘A Growth Mindset’, to share relevant, accessible scientific information with the community. Community members were informally interviewed in various settings to determine what questions they had about cancer and cancer research. After written consent from the community members, their voices were recorded asking the questions. Snippets illustrating their most pressing concerns were included in the podcast for a direct representation of community member’s inquiries. Based on the questions, SKCCC scientists and clinicians were selected and invited for an informal “podcast” discussion answering the community members’ concerns. The initial episode and channel are housed on RSS.com, an audio-sharing platform that distributes to multiple streaming services and provides cross-platform analytics.

Role of COE

Community members were identified at a head and neck cancer (HNC) screening hosted by the Mobile Cancer Screening team. Additionally, the Office of COE provided guidance on the content of the podcast, ensuring the questions were answered in lay-friendly language.

Findings

An initial podcast episode was recorded, edited, and is ready for distribution. The initial episode is about human papilloma virus (HPV) and its relationship to HNC. The informal discussion included questions and answers between the community and the invited physician who explained more about the subject matter utilizing lay language. The episode will be disseminated to the community via social media outlets and shared on the public facing SKCCC website. A QR code was generated to facilitate public engagement further, offering a streamlined means for individuals to submit their queries and concerns to SKCCC researchers. These submissions will be used to contribute to shaping the content and identifying guest speakers featured in forthcoming podcast episodes.

Impact/Lessons learned

This project represents a thoughtful and practical response to the issue of limited health literacy within the SKCCC catchment area. By offering an engaging and informative platform for community engagement, the project has the potential to enhance health literacy, empower individuals, and foster a sense of collaboration between healthcare professionals and the public. While it is acknowledged that not all individuals have access to or listen to podcasts, this is one step toward ongoing, bidirectional communication between community members and investigators. In the interest of sustainability and alignment with the cancer center’s priorities, the CPL worked with SKCCC leadership and the marketing team to create a podcast channel that would exist long after the CPL’s term. Though establishing a novel social media platform was more intensive than originally understood, the podcast series is being continued by the 2024 CPL and the original podcast episode will be included.

Discussion

The COE-CRTEC Trainee Working Group provided cancer research trainees with an opportunity for exposure to community members’ perspectives and experience translating their science to be accessible to the average person. While the chosen projects varied greatly in both topic and approach, there was clear evidence of benefit to the community in each project. While one of the projects was slower to be disseminated to the community, the three projects that were disseminated were rated with high satisfaction and engagement from the community, with most attendees reporting that they learned something new or gained a better understanding of the cancer-related topic they discussed. There was also strong interest across the projects in attending a future session or learning more about cancer and cancer research. In the case of the PCa screening project, four gentlemen reported that this was their first-ever PCa screening. It is important to note that some bias is inherent given that there is selection bias for participants who are willing to attend or listen to cancer related information.

Exposing cancer research trainees in the pedagogy and process of COE is as important as ever as we continue to grapple with longstanding cancer disparities and clinical trials that fail to reach accrual goals. Vulnerable populations, such as racial and ethnic minority groups and those of low socioeconomic status, experience some of the most prominent health disparities [12]. Clinical trials, some of which are designed in ways that are burdensome or unappealing to patients, sometimes close because of accrual troubles; it is estimated that 20–40% of trials investigating new cancer medicines close due to low accrual [13]. Engaging trainees, the very people who are the next generation of cancer researchers, in learning from cancer patients and the community may lead to more patient-centered research and clinical trials in the future. Such patient-centered research holds the promise of attracting and retaining a more diverse group of patients, therefore enhancing the generalizability of the findings to a more diverse population [14]. As more diverse populations reap the benefits of advancements in cancer research and treatment, we should see progress in reducing cancer disparities.

We prioritized the selection of a diverse group of trainees not only to reflect the diverse community of our catchment area, but to increase and contribute to workforce diversity training. The CPLs all brought their unique culture and perspective to their projects that matched the culture of the community that they were partnering with. Research shows that while culturally tailored interventions face unique challenges, they often benefit from culturally respectful implementation strategies and strong community support [15]. Secondly, diversifying workforce development has been identified as a key area of focus for local cancer centers and national cancer organizations [16]. Although our cancer research trainees will most likely be leading the fight against cancer from a laboratory rather than from a clinic or bedside, bringing cultural diversity to research leads to new ways of thinking and new approaches to research, ultimately benefitting patients and their families.

As with any newly developed program, we encountered successes and challenges. Successes included strong support from cancer center leadership, who recognized the distinct contributions of COE and CRTEC to the cancer center’s mission. Senior investigators were willing to nominate their trainees to participate in the program, although some were concerned that this initiative could take time away from their laboratory research. To allay concerns, we supported each trainee in developing their project to keep them on track, use their time wisely, and optimize success. Though participation in this program was voluntary, trainees remained engaged and enthusiastic about their projects. Additionally, their contributions to this manuscript as co-authors is a well-received benefit of participation as each trainee builds their resume; a COE-based publication may set them apart from other candidates in the future job market, particularly those seeking employment in cancer centers. While the community members were involved in the design and implementation of the projects, they were not involved in the selection of trainees or their project topics. In the future, it would be recommended to involve community representatives in the selection and design process. Lastly, each trainee was confined to a $250 budget and an academic year timeline; the projects could have reached more people with a longer timeframe and increased funding. Nevertheless, two projects have spurred longer-term relationships with the cancer center, as we offered a second cancer educational session at the senior center in December 2023 and a second PCa screening event at the church in September 2023. As we learn from our experiences in the first year of developing and offering this program between COE and CRTEC, the SKCCC will continue to host this initiative and provide a novel opportunity for cancer research trainees, the cancer center, and the communities in our catchment area.
