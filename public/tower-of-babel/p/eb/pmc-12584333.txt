Did the legal regulation implemented after acts of violence in healthcare reduce violence?
Authors: Ali Avcı, Halil Mutlu, Muhammet Raşit Özer, Mustafa Korkut, Caner İşkorur, Ökkeş Zortuk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn Turkey, in May 2022, several legal regulations were introduced to prevent and reduce the increasing incidents of violence in healthcare. In this study, it was aimed to investigate whether legal regulation has an effect on reducing incidents of violence in healthcare.MethodsThis single-center retrospective observational study included data from healthcare workers who received a White Code alert in a tertiary hospital between 01.03.2021 and 01.09.2023. White Code cases were categorized into two groups: before and after the enactment of the legal regulation, known as the healthcare violence law, which was officially published in the Official Gazette.Results132 white code cases were presented in this study. Before the enactment of the healthcare violence law, the proportion of White Code cases in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) was 13.1%, whereas after the law, it was found to be 1.4%. Compared to the pre-legislation period, an increase in violence was observed across emergency departments, outpatient clinics, and inpatient wards [32 (52.5%) vs. 36 (50.7%), 20 (32.8%) vs. 29 (40.8%), and 1 (1.6%) vs. 5 (7%), respectively]. A statistically significant shift was noted in the distribution of White Code incident locations (p = 0.026). In the comparison of complainant and attacker genders, 75.75% of female attackers assaulted women, and the rate of women attacking other women was observed to be 2.375 times higher (p = 0.015). The mean age of the attackers who made verbal attacks was found to be higher and more significant than those who made physical attacks (37.42 ± 9.46 vs. 32.83 ± 7.52, p = 0.034 respectively).ConclusionsThe decrease in incidents of violence in the ICU suggests that the legal regulation and the White Code system may have a deterrent effect in such units. In conclusion, these results show that violent incidents have shifted from intensive care units to other areas such as emergency departments, outpatient clinics, and inpatient wards. The higher rate of female perpetrators attacking female victims highlights the need to address gender-based violence risks in healthcare services.

Introduction

According to the International Labour Organization (ILO), workplace violence is defined as actions that result in an individual being assaulted, threatened, injured, or humiliated while carrying out their professional duties, with the intention of direct violence by another person [1]. Violence in healthcare services is a serious problem worldwide, regardless of countries’ social, cultural, and economic conditions. Physical and verbal violence against healthcare workers is significantly increasing worldwide, and the World Medical Association has declared it a national emergency [2]. In addition to the physical and psychological effects on healthcare workers, workplace violence leads to decreased workforce motivation and disruption of the normal functioning of healthcare facilities, which also results in harm to patients [3, 4].

Violence against healthcare professionals is increasing day by day both in the world and in Turkey [5]. The “White Code” refers to a reporting mechanism established by the Ministry of Health to document incidents in which healthcare professionals are subjected to criminal acts during the course of providing medical care or due to their professional responsibilities. The system ensures the referral of such cases to judicial authorities and, upon the request of the victim, provides legal assistance through attorneys assigned by the Ministry.

In 2012, the “White Code” application was put into effect in order to prevent, respond quickly and record the increasing violence in health in Turkey. A helpline, “113,” was established for reporting and recording acts of violence [6]. However, after the COVID-19 pandemic, it was reported that incidents of violence in healthcare increased worldwide [7]. Moreover, different types of violence emerged during the COVID-19 period. In a study conducted by Hope Tiesman and colleagues, it was found that 22% of violent incidents during the COVID-19 pandemic involved perpetrators suspected of having COVID-19 engaging in violence by spitting or deliberately coughing [8]. In Turkey, in May 2022, several legal regulations were introduced to prevent and reduce the increasing incidents of violence in healthcare. Crimes committed against the healthcare worker were included in the scope of Article 100/3 of the Criminal Courts Law. A catalog crime refers to offenses for which there is strong suspicion based on concrete evidence, and those who commit these crimes are tried while in detention [9]. On May 27, 2022, the Official Gazette included “intentional injury crimes committed against personnel working in healthcare institutions and organizations during or due to their duties” within the scope of catalog crimes subject to detention [10].

In another study conducted by Idahosa ME and colleagues, when asked about the root cause of violence against healthcare workers, 72.5% of participants cited a lack of trust in the judiciary [11].

A review of the literature revealed that there are insufficient studies examining the impact of catalog crimes on White Code cases. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to investigate the effect of judicial proceedings following legal regulations on reducing incidents of violence in healthcare.

Methods

This single-center retrospective observational study was conducted in a tertiary care hospital and included White Code reports recorded between March 1, 2021, and September 1, 2023. In accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki study approval was obtained from the Karamanoğlu Mehmetbey University Medical Research Ethics Committee on 14.09.2023 with decision number 14. Inclusion criteria for the study were defined as cases aged > 18 years who had provided consent for the White Code incident. Cases without consent for the issued White Code, those who had approval but declined participation in the study, and those with incomplete data in the system were accepted as exclusion criteria. Sociodemographic and clinical data of both victims and perpetrators were extracted from the Ministry of Health’s White Code database and the hospital’s information management system. White Code was defined as an incident of violence against healthcare workers reported either via the national website (http://www.beyazkod.saglik.gov.tr) or by calling the dedicated helpline (“113”). These cases involve acts legally classified as crimes committed against healthcare personnel during the provision of services or in connection with their professional duties. Cases were categorized into two groups: those reported before the enactment of the healthcare violence law (Group 1: March 1, 2021 – June 1, 2022) and those reported after its enactment (Group 2: June 1, 2022 – September 1, 2023). In total, 156 White Code cases were reviewed. Fifteen cases were excluded due to the absence of informed consent forms, and nine cases were excluded because of incomplete data, such as missing information on the location of the incident or the identity of the reporting staff. At the end, 132 cases were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

The database created for this study was analyzed using SPSS version 27 (IBM), and GraphPad Prism 10 was used to generate the graphs. The collected data were classified, with categorical variables expressed as percentages and frequencies. A distribution analysis was conducted for numerical variables; data following a normal distribution were presented as mean ± SD, while non-normally distributed data were expressed as median and IQR. The chi-square test was used for categorical variable comparisons. The t-test and ANOVA were applied for numerical variable comparisons. A p-value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

In this study, 132 white code cases were analyzed. The relationship between incidents occurring before and after the implementation of the healthcare violence law was examined. Prior to the law, the proportion of incidents occurring in the ICU was 13.1%, which decreased to 1.4% after the law’s enactment [8 (13.1%) vs. 1 (1.4%), Relative Risk = 2,06 (95% CI: 1,27 − 2,67) p = p = 0,008, respectively]. However, White Code incidents in emergency departments, outpatient clinics, and inpatient wards increased following the legal regulation. Compared to the pre-legislation period, violence was found to have increased in emergency departments, outpatient clinics, and inpatient wards [32 (52.5%) vs. 36 (50.7%), 20 (32.8%) vs. 29 (40.8%), 1 (1.6%) vs. 5 (7%), respectively]. These results indicate that White Code incidents rose in certain units after the law, with more cases being observed. In conclusion, these findings show that violence shifted from intensive care units to other departments like Emergency, outpatient and inpatient. A statistically significant change was observed in the locations where White Code incidents occurred (p = 0.026). Although the number of women exposed to violence increased compared to the pre-legislation period, the difference was not statistically significant [30 (49.2%) vs. 45 (63.4%), p = 0.071, respectively]. Among professional groups, violence against physicians increased while it decreased among nurses compared to the pre-legislation period; however, no statistically significant difference was found [34 (55.7%) vs. 44 (62.0%), 14 (23%) vs. 13 (18.3%), p = 0.740, respectively]. Regarding the type of violence, verbal and physical violence increased compared to the pre-legislation period, but the difference was not statistically significant [56 (91.8%) vs. 64 (90.1%), 5 (8.2%) vs. 7 (9.9%), p = 0.492, respectively]. When examining the demographic characteristics of the perpetrators, there was an increase in the number of patients, patient relatives, female perpetrators, and the mean age of perpetrators compared to the pre-legislation period, but none reached statistical significance [21 (34.4%) vs. 29 (40.8%), 40 (65.6%) vs. 42 (59.2%), 11 (18%) vs. 22 (31%), 35.54 ± 8.62 vs. 38.25 ± 9.89, p = 0.282, p = 0.064, p = 0.054, respectively].No significant difference was found in the demographic characteristics of the complainants and perpetrators or in the follow-up of detainees before and after the law. In all White Code incidents, a gender-based comparison between complainants and perpetrators showed that female perpetrators attacked other females in 75.75% of cases, while the rate of attacks on males was 24.25%. In the comparison, the rate of women attacking women was observed to be 2.375 times higher (p = 0.015). Although male perpetrators were found to commit more violence against physicians, nurses, and allied health personnel compared to female perpetrators, the difference was not statistically significant [18 (54.55%) vs. 60 (60.6%), 9 (27.27%) vs. 18 (18.18%), 6 (18.18%) vs. 21 (12.12%), p = 0.531, respectively]. Regarding the type of violence, male perpetrators were found to engage more in verbal and physical violence than female perpetrators, but the difference was not statistically significant [32 (96.07%) vs. 88 (88.89%), 1 (3.03%) vs. 11 (11.11%), p = 0.293, respectively]. When comparing “White Code” cases by gender across emergency departments, outpatient clinics, intensive care units, and inpatient wards, male perpetrators were found to commit more violence than female perpetrators, yet no statistically significant difference was observed [17 (51.52%) vs. 51 (51.56%), 15 (45.46%) vs. 34 (34.34%), 0 vs. 9 (9.1%), 1 (3.03%) vs. 5 (5.6%), p = 0.257, respectively]. No association was found between the perpetrator’s gender and occupational group, incident location, or type of violence (Table 2). The mean age of the complainants who were verbally assaulted was 34.45 ± 8.41, and the mean age of those who were physically assaulted was 33.83 ± 7.57, with no significant difference between them (p = 0.404, Fig. 3a). While the mean age of the attackers who made verbal attacks was 37.42 ± 9.46, the mean age of the attackers who made physical attacks was 32.83 ± 7.52, and the mean age of those who made physical attacks was significantly lower than those who made verbal attacks (p = 0.034, Fig. 3b).

Discussion

This study is important because it is the first study to reveal its association with changes in white code cases after the legal regulation within the scope of catalog crimes for acts of violence in health in the Republic of Turkey. This study shows that following the new legislative regulation in Turkey, incidents of violence in healthcare settings appeared to shifted from intensive care units to other departments, including emergency services, outpatient clinics, and inpatient ward. In our study, violence in healthcare was less frequently reported in places with high security levels, such as intensive care, in the post-legal period. However, it has been found to increase in emergency department, outpatient clinic and inpatient services. This situation may be related to the lower level of security measures in these areas. On the other hand, the increased awareness of the legal regulation for violence in health by healthcare professionals may have caused more white code to be given. Therefore, studies on violence in healthcare have focused on the department where the work is performed. In a study conducted by Bhusal A. and colleagues in a tertiary hospital in Kathmandu, Nepal, it was found that employees working in intensive care units and surgical departments had a higher likelihood of being exposed to physical and verbal violence. The reasons for this include high patient flow, the severity of patients’ conditions, excessive workload, prolonged contact with patients and their relatives, and high stress levels among healthcare workers [12]. Similarly, in a study conducted by Halim Bin Ismail and colleagues in a hospital in Penang, it was shown that workplace violence was prevalent among nurses, particularly among young nurses and those working in the emergency department [13]. In another study by Otachi et al. examining violence data in the workplace, the experience of violence in the workplace varied according to the practice environment; the highest rates occurred in psychiatric (45.1%) and emergency (44.1%) services [14]. In a systematic review and Meta-analysis of the Prevalence, Characteristics and Associated Factors of Workplace Violence against Healthcare Professionals in India, emergency services have been shown to be one of the most common locations of workplace violence [15]. In the study of Johansen et al., titled Changes in the prevalence of workplace violence against doctors between 1993 and 2014 in all medical specialties in Norway, the psychiatric branch was found to be the department with the most violence [16]. In a study in which Sidise Temesgen et al. examining the relationship between the department in which nurses work and violence in healthcare, it was shown that violence was less prevalent in ICU and special units [17]. Similar to our study, while the significant decrease in the number of incidents in the ICU may be associated with the legal regulation, the increase in incidents seen in inpatient wards is thought-provoking, as violence is directed to different areas. This significant change in the places where the incidents are seen shows that legal regulations can only be effective in some areas. Comparable mechanisms to address workplace violence in healthcare also exist internationally. In the United States, the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) requires healthcare employers to implement workplace violence prevention programs, including risk assessments, reporting procedures, staff training, and administrative controls [18]. In certain states such as New Jersey, legislation specifically mandates hospitals to establish formal violence prevention policies and reporting mechanisms [19]. In the United Kingdom, the National Health Service (NHS) applies a zero tolerance policy toward aggression and violence against staff, complemented by the Violence Prevention and Reduction Standard, which obliges healthcare institutions to monitor, report, and systematically reduce workplace violence [20, 21]. While these systems differ in scope and implementation, the Turkish White Code system is distinctive in its centralized reporting and provision of immediate legal support to healthcare workers, which may contribute to its deterrent effect.

In a qualitative study conducted by Kevin Davey et al. on workplace violence among healthcare providers in emergency departments in India, verbal violence was shown to be the most common form of violence, similar to our study [22]. The fact that the mean age of the attackers was significantly higher than the victims in our study supports the relationship between tendency to violence against healthcare professionals and sociodemographic factors, which are frequently reported in literature.

When Daniela Acquadro Maran et al. examined the administrative records of 396 healthcare workers with violence notification forms in their study on gender differences in reporting violence in workplace, they found out that female healthcare workers saw violence as part of their work and that male healthcare workers were more prone to report violence [23].

When the relationship between gender and violence in healthcare was examined in the literature, Serpil Aytac et al. collected data from many health and non-health sectors in their study on the exposure of Turkish workers to violence in the workplace, and in this study, being a woman was found to be significantly associated with being a victim of violence in the workplace [24]. In our study, the fact that the rate of female aggressors attacking female victims (75.75%) was quite high and this relationship was found to be statistically significant (p = 0.015) shows that the dynamics of gender violence in the health sector should be examined in more depth. Similar to our findings in the study conducted by Altaf O. Assil and his colleagues, where the demographic characteristics of the attackers were also examined, the majority of the attackers were male [25]. The fact that the mean age of verbal aggressors is higher than that of physical aggressors (p = 0.034) indicates that the age factor of the aggressors may differ between the types of violence. One of the strengths of the research is the comparative examination of events that occurred before and after the implementation of the law on violence in health. The fact that the study covered 132 events increased the statistical reliability of the results in terms of sample size. Multidimensional analyzes such as age, gender, crime scene and type of violence increase the richness of the findings.

Limitations

Our study has a few limitations. Since it was based on retrospective data, we can’t be certain that the observed changes were directly caused by the legislation. To better understand these effects, future studies should ideally be prospective, multi-center, and include larger populations. Another limitation is that some subgroup analyses, such as physical violence perpetrated by female offenders or incidents occurring in intensive care units after the enactment of the law, included only a small number of cases, which may have limited the statistical power of these findings.

The fact that the data source is based only on a specific region or institution may restrict the generalizability of the results. Deficiencies in reporting incidents or differences in record keeping methods may result in the real dimensions of violence not being fully reflected. In a quantitative survey of healthcare providers in India conducted by Tania Ahluwalia et al., it was shown that 132 of 200 violence incidents were not reported. It is the idea that even if 24% of unreported incidents are reported, nothing will happen [26].

Another important limitation is the lack of integration with broader international standards. Unlike the OSHA requirements in the United States or the NHS Violence Prevention and Reduction Standard in the United Kingdom, this study was limited to a single-center analysis without nationwide policy comparison. This may restrict the extent to which the findings can be directly compared with international practices (18–21).

Conclusion

The observed decrease in violence incidents in ICUs may be associated with the legal regulation and the “White Code” practice rather than directly caused by them, and this association may reflect factors such as increased security or reporting behavior in such units. However, the increase in incidents in inpatient wards indicates that preventive mechanisms should focus more on these areas. The higher rate of female perpetrators attacking female victims highlights the need to address gender-based violence risks in healthcare services. Differences between verbal and physical violence emphasize the necessity of developing training programs for healthcare personnel that take this distinction into account. Conducting similar analyses in different regions or healthcare institutions could contribute to evaluating the overall impact of the law. Qualitative research can be conducted to further explore the relationship between types of violence and age factors. In particular, in-service training and risk management strategies should be developed to address the rising incidents of violence in inpatient wards. A comprehensive assessment of the long-term effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on violence in healthcare would be beneficial for formulating policies for such crisis situations.
