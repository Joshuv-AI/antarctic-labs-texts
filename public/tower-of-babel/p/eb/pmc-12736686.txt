Effectiveness of binaural beats in alleviating anxiety during periodontal flap surgery: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: T. K. Madhumitha, Viola Esther, P. L. Ravishankar, Kalaivani, Gayathri Kumar, Prem Blaise Rajula
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Dental anxiety is a prevalent concern, significantly impacting patient experience and treatment outcomes, particularly in periodontal flap surgery. Binaural beats, an auditory brainwave entrainment technique with demonstrated anxiolytic effects in medical contexts, have emerged as a promising nonpharmacological approach but remain underexplored in dental surgery. This study evaluates the efficacy of binaural beats in alleviating anxiety among patients undergoing periodontal flap surgery.Materials and Methods:Fifty chronic periodontitis patients requiring periodontal flap surgery with moderate-to-high preoperative anxiety scores were randomly allocated to a test group (binaural beat exposure) or a control group (no auditory intervention). The test group listened to binaural beats for 30 min (10 min preoperatively + 20 min intraoperatively) using stereo headphones. Anxiety was assessed using the Spielberger State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI), and physiological stress markers such as systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and heart rate (HR) were recorded in both groups at the preoperative and postoperative stages.Results:Binaural beat exposure significantly reduced anxiety and physiological stress markers (P < 0.0001). Mean STAI scores decreased from 49 ± 6.93 to 38.7 ± 9.46. SBP and DBP declined from 132.0 ± 7.79 to 124.6 ± 8.40 mmHg and from 87.2 ± 4.61 to 79.8 ± 6.91 mmHg. HR reduced from 93.4 ± 8.17 to 83.2 ± 7.15 bpm, whereas the control group demonstrated postoperative elevations in anxiety and stress markers.Conclusion:Binaural beats effectively reduced anxiety and physiological stress responses during periodontal flap surgery, offering a cost-effective, noninvasive alternative for anxiety management.

INTRODUCTION

Dental anxiety, ranked among the top five most prevalent forms of anxiety, is a subjective emotional state, often originating from unconscious reactions to vague threats.[1] It begins with subcortical brain arousal and manifests through various physiological responses, such as muscle tension and visceral disturbances. Dental anxiety is further divided into three types: association anxiety, triggered by specific stimuli like needles or drills; attribution anxiety, which stems from situational factors such as being in the dental chair; and appraisal anxiety, emerging from the recall of negative dental experiences.[2]

The invasive nature of periodontal flap surgery, which involves tissue manipulation and suturing, often intensifies anxiety. Despite the use of local anesthesia, the anticipation of discomfort can escalate anxiety. This heightened anxiety can intensify pain perception, hinder recovery, and impair wound healing due to increased sympathetic-adrenergic activity and elevated stress hormones that impact immune response.[3] Surgical anticipation profoundly triggers anxiety in dental settings, with 71% of patients awaiting periodontal treatment reporting fear and apprehension.[4] Shebl et al.[5] reported that preoperative anxiety worsens surgical outcomes by increasing intraoperative discomfort, postoperative pain, and recovery time and may precipitate bradycardia or syncope in cases of severe anxiety.[6]

Anxiety is a significant concern for both children and adults, with adults often hesitant to acknowledge their feelings. Tu et al.[7] reported that anxiety frequently results in the postponement or avoidance of periodontal flap surgery. Although several studies[38] have explored the impact of anxiety in periodontal surgeries, methods to alleviate anxiety remain underexplored. Conventionally, the focus of surgical success was on the technical expertise of surgeons and the efficacy of surgical techniques. However, contemporary practice recognizes that managing patients’ emotional state is equally vital for achieving optimal outcomes.[9]

Nonpharmacological interventions have recently emerged as promising alternatives for managing anxiety, particularly given the limitations of pharmacological methods such as sedatives and anesthesia, which, despite their effectiveness, can cause cognitive and physical side effects and are generally reserved for severe cases.[10] In light of the need to address the issue, this study explores the efficacy of binaural beats as an affordable, practical, and nonpharmacological method of anxiety reduction.

Binaural beats, originally identified by Dove in 1939 and subsequently advanced by Oster[11] in 1973, arise when two sinusoidal tones of constant intensity but different frequencies are played in each ear, resulting in the perception of a single tone through binaural integration within auditory pathways involving the olivary body, cochlear, and inferior colliculus nuclei. The resulting low-frequency modulation (<30 Hz) mirrors natural brainwave patterns and is categorized as delta (0.5–3.9 Hz), theta (4.0–7.9 Hz), alpha (8.0–11.9 Hz), and beta (12.0–29.9 Hz), each associated with distinct cognitive and behavioral responses.[1213] These beats synchronize neural oscillations through the process of brainwave entrainment,[14] thereby reducing anxiety, lowering stress, and enhancing mood, creativity, and pain relief by targeting specific brainwave frequencies. In this study, 6-Hz theta binaural beats were used to alleviate anxiety.

Binaural beats have shown potential for reducing anxiety in various medical procedures such as cystoscopy, cesarean sections, cataract surgery, and general anesthesia, as highlighted in studies from Ölçücü et al.,[15] Parodi et al.,[16] Yusim and Grigaitis,[17] Wiwatwongwana et al.,[18] and Padmanabhan et al.,[19] respectively. In dental practice, binaural beats have demonstrated promising outcomes in children,[20] yet their potential in adults undergoing periodontal surgery remains unexplored, despite growing interest in this approach. To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to assess this innovative, noninvasive intervention, seeking to explore its feasibility as an adjunctive tool for adults undergoing periodontal flap surgery. The study aims to investigate the efficacy of binaural beats in attenuating anxiety among patients undergoing periodontal flap surgery.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The randomized clinical trial was conducted from June to October 2024 in the department of periodontology after obtaining approval from the institutional ethical committee (XXX IEC-ST09241763). This study complied with the revised CONSORT guidelines [Figure 1]. Patients enrolled in this study provided authorization by signing an informed consent form, in alignment with the Helsinki Declaration (1975, revised 2013).

CONSORT flow chart

Initially, all patients underwent nonsurgical periodontal treatment along with oral hygiene instructions. Following a 4-week recovery period, patients who met the periodontal and radiographic criteria for flap surgery were chosen. The study enrolled 50 patients aged 18–65 years in good general health who reported for their first periodontal flap surgery appointment. Presurgical levels of anxiety were assessed by Spielberger’s State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) questionnaire [QUESTIONNAIRE], and those with moderate-to-high anxiety scores were included. STAI is considered the “gold standard” for evaluating preoperative anxiety, demonstrating robust reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.896) and validated through precise “state” and “trait” directives.[18] It includes self-report scales evaluated on a 4-point scale (not at all, somewhat, moderately so, and so very much) and classifies scores as “no or low anxiety” (20–37), “moderate anxiety” (38–44), and “high anxiety” (45–80).[21]

The study excluded individuals with a history of periodontal surgery, those on antianxiety medications, individuals with hearing impairments, participants with neurological or major psychiatric conditions, and pregnant women to avoid potential unknown effects.

Participants were randomly allocated to the control and test groups in a parallel design with a ratio of 1:1 through block randomization guided by a computer-generated sequence. An independent statistician generated the random allocation sequence, which was concealed through sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes until assignment. A clinical coordinator enrolled the participants, and a research assistant, uninvolved in outcome assessment, assigned interventions. To minimize measurement bias, the investigator who measured systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP), heart rate (HR), and STAI data at pre- and postoperative stages was blinded to the participants’ group allocation. The STAI score was designated as the primary outcome measure, with SBP, DBP, and HR as secondary outcome measures. The study used an automatic blood pressure monitor (OMRON Healthcare India Pvt. Ltd.) to assess SBP, DBP, and HR.

The test group received binaural beats, whereas the control group did not receive any auditory intervention. The binaural beats (200 Hz in the left ear and 206 Hz in the right ear) were generated using Brain Waves Binaural Beats software (Binaural Beats-Healing Sounds, Crafting Sound Apps, Turkey). Binaural beats were administered through stereo headphones at a volume level selected by the patient, ensuring it was adequate for them to still hear the surgeon’s communication. Once they were fully acclimated to the sounds and comfortable, binaural beats were administered for 10 min preoperatively, followed by the administration of local anesthesia, which continued for an additional 20 min during the intraoperative phase of flap surgery. This resulted in a total listening duration of 30 min, after which the audio was discontinued even if the surgery continued beyond that duration. The meta-analysis by Garcia-Argibay et al.[22] guided the selection of this protocol, revealing a robust link between binaural beat exposure duration and anxiolytic effectiveness, and recommending their application both preoperatively and intraoperatively for maximum benefit. A 10-min duration was proven to effectively reduce preoperative anxiety in dental settings, as demonstrated by Menziletoglu et al.,[23] Isik et al.,[24] and Salehabadi et al.[25] Based on these findings, this study incorporated a 10-min preoperative binaural beat session. However, for longer procedures like flap surgery, this alone was deemed insufficient. Therefore, an additional 20 min of binaural beats was administered intraoperatively in a dental setting, bringing the total exposure to 30 min. This is the first study to administer binaural beats intraoperatively in a dental setting. The decision to provide a total of 30 min of exposure was based on the findings by Ingendoh et al.,[13] which demonstrated that the effects of binaural beats persist for 30 min even after cessation. Given that the average duration of flap surgery is approximately 52 min per quadrant,[26] a 30-min exposure to binaural beats was considered sufficient to maintain a relaxed state throughout the procedure, enhancing its applicability in surgical settings.

Compliance with headphone use was continuously monitored by a dental assistant who was stationed throughout the procedure to help patients with operational guidance and to address any technical challenges or adverse reactions such as vertigo, seizures, or nausea. In case of adverse events or if patients felt discomfort, the perception of sound was planned to be withdrawn. However, no untoward effects from binaural beats were observed in this study. Following surgery, the headphones were removed, and the patients were requested to relax for 5 min, after which SBP, DBP, HR, and STAI scores were immediately assessed.

Sample size calculation

The sample size for the investigation was obtained using G*Power software, Heinrich-Heine-University, Düsseldorf, Germany, focusing on anticipated mean differences to ensure robust statistical outcomes. With calculations set at a 5% significance level and 80% statistical power and assuming a standard effect size of 0.8, the calculated sample size for the study was 50 participants.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using STATA software (version 17), StataCorp. LLC, College Station, Texas, USA. Categorized data were given as frequencies and percentages, whereas the continuous data were presented as the mean and standard deviation. The Shapiro–Wilk test was employed to assess statistical normality. The independent t-test was used to compare the means between the groups, the paired t-test for pre- and postmeasurements, and the Chi-square test for categorical variable associations. Significance was defined at P < 0.05.

RESULTS

Intergroup comparisons between the control and test groups were performed using an independent t-test, which showed no significant differences in demographic characteristics, preoperative SBP, DBP, HR, and STAI scores, whereas postoperative SBP, DBP, HR, and STAI scores demonstrated significant differences between the groups, as mentioned in Tables 1 and 2. A paired t-test was used to evaluate preoperative and postoperative parameters, such as SBP, DBP, HR, and anxiety scores within each group. In the control group, STAI scores, SBP, DBP, and HR showed significant postoperative elevations (P < 0.0001), as illustrated in Table 3, whereas the test group exhibited a significant postoperative reduction (P < 0.0001), as illustrated in Table 4. Since no comparable studies were found in the literature, a post hoc power analysis was conducted, which indicated a statistical power of 1.0000.

DISCUSSION

Binaural beats have regained neuroscientific prominence through advanced electroencephalography and magnetoencephalography studies. Kim et al.[27] demonstrated direct cortical engagement through altered neural synchronization during auditory processing. Systematic and scoping reviews by Ingendoh et al.[13] and Kobayashi et al.[28] confirm consistent modulation of brain oscillations, highlighting their potential as a neurophysiological tool for anxiety modulation and cognitive-emotional regulation.

Meta-analyses by Garcia-Argibay et al.[22] reveal that alpha and beta waves enhance vigilance, alertness, and creativity, whereas delta and theta waves promote relaxation and hypnosis. Jirakittayakorn and Wongsawat[29] demonstrated that exposure to a 6-Hz binaural beat induces theta activity, which is associated with focused attention, relaxation, and stress reduction, mimicking the effects of meditation. Accordingly, this study utilized binaural beats with frequencies of 200–206 Hz to create a 6-Hz theta wave, intended to alleviate anxiety and foster relaxation.

Xiong et al.[30] demonstrated that perioperative exposure to binaural beats significantly reduced anxiety, alleviated pain, and stabilized SBP and HR compared to nonbinaural audio. Similarly, Shamsi et al.[31] reported that binaural beats effectively reduced pain and associated anxiety in both acute and chronic conditions, suggesting that brainwave entrainment can modulate pain perception and emotional responses. These findings collectively reinforce the results of the present study, highlighting binaural beats as a promising nonpharmacological neuroacoustic approach for managing perioperative anxiety and discomfort.

Many studies have combined binaural beats with visual stimuli[3233] or soothing music to enhance their benefits. However, this study aimed to evaluate the standalone effectiveness of binaural beats. Pure binaural beats were used without musical accompaniment, as the irregular frequencies in music can disrupt the brain’s entrainment process.[30] In addition, this approach prioritized patient comfort and avoided interference from visual devices during flap surgery. Limited data exist on the impact of exclusive binaural beats administered both preoperatively and intraoperatively in adult dental surgical procedures. Comparisons were therefore drawn from studies on other medical procedures.

Ölçücü et al.[15] administered binaural beats to male patients undergoing cystoscopy and ureteral stent removal, reporting a marked reduction in STAI scores. This harmonizes with the outcomes of this study, which demonstrated a significant reduction in STAI scores in the test group.

Opartpunyasarn et al.[34] reported that binaural beats reduced anxiety levels during bronchoscopy procedures, as assessed using STAI questionnaires. In addition, physiological markers of anxiety, including SBP and DBP, significantly declined in the group exposed to the binaural beat audio. This aligns with the findings of the present study, which demonstrated a postoperative reduction in anxiety as well as in SBP and DBP in the test group. Loong et al.[35] reported that exposure to theta-frequency binaural beats during cataract surgery under topical anesthesia led to a significant decline in both intraoperative HR and anxiety levels. This finding echoes the results of the present study, which similarly demonstrated reductions in HR and anxiety following binaural beat exposure, suggesting a comparable autonomic and psychophysiological relaxation effect.

Dental anxiety is typically more intense than that experienced in other medical settings, primarily due to the distinctive auditory and tactile stimuli inherent to dental procedures such as drilling, ultrasonic scaling, and suction, which can heighten fear and discomfort. As supported by the meta-analysis of Kong et al.,[36] binaural beats not only act as an effective acoustic mask but also promote neurophysiological relaxation through brainwave entrainment. This dual mechanism of simultaneously reducing sensory stress and fostering internal calm makes binaural beats more advantageous for dental procedures than their use in medical procedures.

The findings of the present study, in conjunction with those of Shukla et al.,[37] emphasize that binaural beats therapy represents a clinically feasible, low-cost, and easily implementable adjunct for dental anxiety management. Furthermore, in line with evidence from Salehabadi et al.[25] and Qazi et al.,[38] it highlights the practicality of auditory-based interventions. Binaural beats reduce anxiety using simple, safe, and accessible technology such as smartphones and headphones, requiring no specialized equipment or training. Their affordability, acceptability, and scalability make them feasible in routine dental practice, even in resource-limited settings.

The present study lacked a long-term follow-up, thereby limiting the assessment of the sustained anxiolytic effects of binaural beats. The relatively small sample size, although statistically adequate, restricts the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. Moreover, anxiety was not analyzed according to age or sex subgroups, which could be explored in future research. It also lacked an additional comparison group with sham auditory stimuli (such as placebo sounds, white noise, or neutral music), which could have provided stronger validation of the specific superiority of binaural beats over other auditory relaxation methods.

CONCLUSION

Binaural beats serve as an effective adjunctive, nonpharmacological tool during periodontal flap surgery, providing a noninvasive and cost-effective approach to reduce patient anxiety and enhance overall surgical comfort. This method not only alleviates fear, encouraging patients to undergo surgery, but also supports improved postoperative recovery. Moreover, the administration of binaural beats is economical and feasible, making it a practical solution for managing anxiety in settings ranging from clinics to large hospitals. Incorporating binaural beats into periodontal treatment protocols addresses an often-overlooked but critical aspect of patient care, ultimately improving surgical experiences and outcomes.
