High rates of voluntary clinic attendance among community members screened with high cardiovascular disease risk scores in the rural and urban communities of Adama, Ethiopia
Authors: Kiya Kedir, Kufre Okop, Firaol Mesfin Ayele, Hailemichael Getachew, Melat Dereje, Belachew Hailu, Bezinash Geremew, Naomi Levitt, Rawleigh Howe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCommunity level screening, referral and care intervention for the rising burden of cardiovascular diseases (CVD) and its risk factors in sub-Saharan Africa has been advocated. However, very low completed referral rates among those identified at risk has been reported. The goal of the current study was to use a citizen science-based approach to identify and refer those at high CVD risk to local health clinics, assess the referral rates in each community, and explore reasons for non-attendance by urban or rural location.MethodsTwelve trained health extension workers (HEWs) screened 772 community members aged ≥ 35 year from 10 randomly selected health clinic catchment areas in an urban and a rural community in Adama district and Addis Ababa, Ethiopia with a mobile app-based on the Framingham 10-year CVD risk algorithm. HEWs also provided simple educational tools to support communicating CVD risk and counselling, and referral of at-risk persons for further care. Participants were followed up for four weeks after referral.ResultsThe proportions of participants with high (> 20%), moderate (10–20%), and pooled moderate and high risk (> 10%) were 6.0%, 12.6%, and 18.5% respectively. The most common risk factors identified included hypertension, diabetes mellitus (DM), and tobacco use. Of the 143 at risk participants identified, 124 were interviewed at 4-weeks follow-up (86.7%), and 80/124 (64.5%) voluntarily attended a local clinic for further assessment and management; rural at-risk participants (n = 42) had higher rates of follow-up (72.4%) compared with urban (57.6%) dwellers (p = 0.08). Those without prior hypertension or diabetes had lower rates of follow-up (57.1%) compared to those with at least one of these risk factors (74.1%, p = 0.05). The most common reasons for not attending clinic were inconvenience (63.4%), feeling fine (24.4%), and financial challenge (12.2%).ConclusionsWe observed high rates of voluntary clinic attendance among community members screened with moderate to high CVD risk scores in both rural and urban communities of Ethiopia. These findings imply that this novel approach may be useful for scaling up CVD risk screening in regions of Ethiopia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24557-4.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) has been increasing in Sub-Saharan Africa, and now is the leading cause of death among non-communicable diseases [1]. In Ethiopia, 11% of all deaths are estimated to be from CVD [1, 3, 4]. Multiple initiatives have been undertaken to reduce the prevalence of some risk factors through lifestyle modification programmes emphasizing proper diet, weight management, obesity control, and smoking cessation [5]. For individuals who already have a history of these and other risk factors such as diabetes or hypertension, the identification of patients with CVD risk is imperative for early identification, timely diagnoses, and long term management [1, 3, 4].

As a means to address this, screening at health care facilities has been advocated in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) and other low- and middle-income countries (LMIC). However, research studies have shown that prevalence of risk factors such as undiagnosed hypertension remain high [6], implying that additional screening methods, such as community level screening through door-to-door or community gatherings may be necessary, and have hence been encouraged [7]. Moreover, regardless of the site where screening occurs, uptake into care is variable. Indeed, studies among pilot CVD screening studies at the community level show uptakes anywhere from 10 to 65% [3, 6–13], and low referral rates limit effectiveness of other screening initiatives, notably cervical cancer [14, 15]. Multiple studies have cited many barriers to uptake [6, 8], but among these the lack of understanding of disease or risk in the community is commonly reported [8, 14, 16–19]. Because of cultural differences between western oriented health care systems and communities in LMIC, particularly in rural settings, there has been increasing emphasis on community “participatory research” aiming to improve communication of disease understanding and its risk to the community [20–23]. With the long-term goal of implementing health extension worker (HEW) mediated community level screening for CVD and risk factors with sustainable uptake into long term care, we recently employed citizen science participatory approaches [21, 24–26]. These studies explored community understanding of CVD and its risk factors and means to communicate risk to the community [21, 24–26]. In turn this facilitated the development of simple tools to be used during screening to support communicating CVD risk, provide adequate education and counselling, and referral of at-risk persons for further care in health clinics.

Thus, with this approach, the goals of the study were: 1) to determine the prevalence of 10-year CVD risk and underlying CVD risk factors in Addis Ababa, and the city and rural surroundings of Adama, Ethiopia, both to complement previous studies in this region [27] and provide a baseline care evaluation for future studies; and 2) to identify and refer individuals with high CVD risk to local health clinics, assess the referral rates in each community, and explore reasons for non-attendance according to urban or rural location.

Methods

Design

This study employed a cross-sectional survey design, leveraging a citizen science approach to screen, identify, refer to clinic care, and follow-up participants with absolute 10-year CVD risk scores greater than 10%.

Setting

The study was conducted in ten kebeles, the lowest administrative structure in Ethiopia. We included six kebeles from rural villages in the Adama district, two kebeles from Adama town and two others from Addis Ababa, the capital city and most urban area of Ethiopia. The Adama town is a regional town located about 100 km south-east of Addis Ababa while the Adama districts are found in the surrounding villages.

Study population and sampling

The study participants were sampled in a multi-stage approach. Initially, ten public health centres were purposively selected taking into account geographic and socioeconomic differences. This was based on the recommendations in preliminary stakeholder meetings with health clinic representatives, community and religious leaders and involved local non-governmental organizations. Two health clinics were selected from Addis Ababa, two in Adama town and six in Adama rural district. Of the six rural health clinics, two each were taken at increasing distances, 10 km, 25 km and 40 km, from Adama town. In urban areas, one health clinic was selected from an affluent area and one from an economically disadvantaged neighborhood. Once the health clinics were selected, we randomly identified a single kebele from among five kebeles in the catchment area. Finally, households in the selected kebeles were selected randomly; all adults within selected households who fulfilled the inclusion criteria were enrolled.

Sample size and assumptions

Initially, a sample size of 800 participants without prior risk factors was considered assuming a prevalence of 5% with a CVD risk score of > 20% [28] which would predict a minimum of 40 individuals identified with CVD risk to assess referral outcomes. Men and women aged 35–70 years who were present in the households and willing to participate were included. In our calculation we assumed that the multi-stage sampling would not have an appreciable effect on our sample size and did not cater for it in the sample size calculation.

Evaluation of CVD risk screening and referral

Prior to screening, HEWs working in the selected kebeles underwent training on topics such as research ethics, obtaining informed consent, common CVD risk factors, the use of mobile apps and anthropomorphic measurements. The one week-long training involved both didactic and practical sessions, was facilitated by an experienced research team, and included both pre- and post-training evaluations. Each HEW visited randomly selected households in the community and identified one or more eligible persons (adults aged 35–70 years) who consented to participate in the study. Participants were oriented with educational materials consisting of visual aids depicting CVD and its risks, and informed consent was obtained. Data were collected using a REDCap app deployed on tablet computers, and included age, gender, history of or current smoking, history of diabetes and hypertension as answered by the participants. Systolic (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure was taken three times (every 2–3 min) using an electronic blood pressure device (Omron); weight, height and waist circumference were also taken using a stadiometer (Crown, Tanzania) and recorded on the mobile app which subsequently calculated body mass index (BMI). Each individual’s risk score was calculated according to the risk equation [29] incorporated into the RedCap app. For women, the equation was [2.72*log Age] + [BMI] + [2.81* log SBP (if untreated hypertension)] + [0.62*smoking] + [0.78 * diabetes]. Smoking and diabetes histories were scored 1 for yes, and 0 for no. The coefficient for log SBP (treated hypertension) was 2.88. For men the coefficients for log Age, BMI, log SBP (if not treated hypertension), log SBP (if treated hypertension), smoking and diabetes, were, respectively, 3.11, 0.79, 1.86, 1.93, 0.71 and 0.53.

The HEWs informed the participants of their risk level. For those with a risk score > 10% (moderate and above risk) the HEWs provided counselling and a referral form depicting their risk and an appointment with a convenient health service acceptable to the participant. The research study team employed real-time assessment of data collection. Visualization of progress, preliminary analysis as well as queries were generated to address and correct any incompleteness or inconsistencies. Each HEW was requested to screen 6 to 8 persons per day. Research staff who participated in training the HEWs also provided support during fieldwork. One month after screening, the HEWs conducted a follow-up visit for individuals identified as having moderate and above CVD risk scores in order to assess whether or not they attended the referral clinic, and if not, reasons why not using a small, structured questionnaire. At risk individuals were evaluated at the health clinic by clinic staff as per national guidelines. Evaluation of clinic visits was done through questionnaires provided to the clinic staff and addressed new or confirmed diagnoses, and medications and counselling provided.

Data analysis

Mobile data from the survey (using RedCap platform) were imported into Stata software version 13 for data analysis. Standard descriptive and inferential statistics were undertaken. The primary outcome variable was CVD risk score, and the secondary outcome was clinic attendance (referral outcome) for those screened in each project location. For continuous variables with normal distributions, t-tests were used, while the Mann–Whitney U test were use to ascertain those with non-normal distributions. For categorical variables, we utilized Chi-square tests to ascertain their relationships. A 95% confidence interval was applied and two-sided p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. In addition, a binary logistic regression model was used to determine factors responsible for clinic attendance. The variables (i.e. Risk factors) identified by using univariate analysis in Table 3 and having p-values < 0.20 were further evaluated in a logistic regression model. Clinic attendance is the dependent (outcome) variable, and the indicated (risk) factors are the independent (predictor) variables.

Adjusted odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. Independent variables included in multivariate model were those exhibiting p-values of < 0.2 in tests of association with referral outcomes in univariate chi-square analysis.

Results

Prevalence of factors for CVD risk stratified by urban and rural regions

We selected both urban and rural sites for this study both because of our earlier results depicting differences in understanding of risk in these groups, and because of prior studies suggesting different prevalence of CVD risk factors in these regions. Table 1 depicts CVD risk factors among the 772 participants. There were 62.5% (483) of the participants from rural sites, 66.1% were women, and 25.5.% were aged 55 years and above. There was no significant differences in the age categories in the urban and rural settings. Among all study participants, 6.5% had a prior history of hypertension, and this was substantially higher in urban (14.7%) compared to the rural (1.7%) regions (p < 0.001). In addition, a substantial number of cases of new cases of hypertension in the study were identified at screening. The category of current hypertension (Table 1) represents the sum of those with a prior history and those newly diagnosed cases upon CVD risk screening, and this was substantially higher at 22.8% among total participants, compared with those with a known history (6.5%). Again, a significant difference was found in current hypertension between the urban (39.8%) and the rural (12.6%) groups (p < 0.001). None of those identified with hypertension required emergent referral. Prior history of diabetes was also higher in urban (8.7%) compared with rural regions (2.9%, p < 0.001). In contrast, smoking was more common in rural (6.0%) than urban settings (1.0%, p < 0.001). The percentages of participants with body mass index (BMI) > 25 was substantially higher in urban (60.6%) compared with rural (18.0%) regions (p < 0.001).

Ten-year cardiovascular risk, stratified by risk percentage and region

Participants were assessed for risk factors, the modified Framingham CVD risk calculation performed [28], and the results are depicted in Fig. 1. The prevalence of moderate CVD risk scores (10–20%) was significantly higher in urban (15.2%) compared to the rural (11.0%) dwellers (p < 0.05). Similarly, those with high CVD (at least 20%) risk were more prevalent in the urban (9.7%) than the rural (3.7%) group (p < 0.001). Further, comparing those above the 10% CVD risk level (the sum of the 10–20% and > 20% categories) indicated that 24.9% of urban dwellers had risk whereas 14.7% of the rural group were at risk (p < 0.001).

Prevalence of risk factors stratified by 10-year CVD risk categories and country regions

We also compared the individual risk factors among urban and rural participants, stratified by overall CVD risk. The percentage of participants with prior or current hypertension among the > 20% CVD risk category was 64.3% and 96.4%, respectively among the urban group. For the rural site, the proportions with prior and current hypertension were 27.8% and 61.1%, respectively (p < 0.001). While there were differences in prevalence of diabetes in the two region groups (Table 1), the percentage of participants with CVD risk who had a history of diabetes did not differ in a statistically significantly fashion between urban (53.6%) and rural (44.4%) groups. Smoking, which was more prevalent in the rural population (as seen in Table 1), was also more prevalent as a risk factor in the rural group; 27.8% had > 20% CVD risk in rural settings compared with 3.6% among urban participants. Of those with > 20% CVD risk, the percentages of participants with BMI > 25 was significantly higher among urban (71.4%) than rural (38.9%) groups (p < 0.001).

For the aforementioned risk factors, similar patterns were observed comparing the groups using the 10%−20% 10-year CVD risk category as with the high (> 20%) risk category. Gender and age were also different between urban and rural groups, but these were not always statistically significant. Thus, the percentage of those with high CVD risk who were female was higher in urban participants (32.1%) than rural dwellers (22.2%), but this was not statistically significant; however, in the 10%−20% risk category female proportions were significantly higher in urban (56.8%) than rural settings (22.6%, p < 0.001). Percentages of individuals aged > 55 years of age with high CVD risk was significantly lower in urban (78.6%) than rural (100%) regions (p < 0.05), but proportions of those with 10%−20% risk were nearly the same in urban (61.4%) and rural (69.8%) groups (p = 0.38). In summary, the overall proportions of at-risk individuals in urban settings were higher than that in rural regions; moreover, the pattern of risk factors differed. Relative to rural regions, those from urban sites were more likely to be overweight, younger and hypertensive, and less likely to be smokers and males.

Follow-up and attendance at health care clinics

Those individuals who were identified with at least a moderate 10-year CVD risk (i.e. CVD risk score > 10%) were followed up four weeks after referral. Of these, 124 of 143 (87%) were successively traced and questioned to assess whether they had voluntarily sought health care or not after referral, and if not, why not. As seen in Fig. 2, self-report clinic attendance was 64.5%. Moreover, apart from cited reasons by the participants themselves for lack of referral follow-up, we assessed whether any baseline risk factors would be associated with referral outcomes.

Proportions who attended clinic within four weeks of referrals. Urban, rural and total participant follow-up was 57.6%, 72.4% and 64.5%, respectively (P = 0.08)

A greater proportion of the referred rural participants attended compared to the urban participants 72.4% (42 of 58) vs. 57.6% (38 of 66) respectively, though this difference did not reach statistical significance. To explore other potential factors contributing to clinic follow-up we compared baseline CVD risk factors among clinic attendees and non-attendees. The rationale for this was that, given the importance of health education, it was conceivable that those a prior history of CVD or CVD risk factors might have, through previous interactions with health care personnel, more familiarity and understanding of the importance of follow-up. As shown in Table 3, none of these factors showed statistically significant associations with referral outcome in univariate analysis by chi-square testing. However, three factors, namely sex, prior hypertension, and smoking history exhibited p values of ≤ 0.2, justifying their inclusion in a multivariate model, whereby all independent factors are controlled for the effects of each other. As summarized in Table 4, prior history of hypertension (p = 0.009), as well as region (urban versus rural, p = 0.000), showed highly significant associations with referral outcome (i.e. clinic attendance). Specifically, those with a past history of hypertension were 3.84 times more likely than those without a history of hypertension to follow-up (Confidence interval, CI 1.40–10.51), and rural dwellers were 9.32 times more likely than urban inhabitants to follow-up (3.11–27.90). Those with a history of smoking also had higher odds ratios of follow-up (2.87) relative to non-smokers, but this was not statistically significant after adjustment for all other variables (p = 0.36).

Reasons for not attending health clinic for at risk participants

We were able to obtain information from 41 of 44 participants who chose not to attend a health clinic after having been notified of CVD risk. The most common reasons cited were time inconvenience (63.4%), followed by feeling fine (24.4%), and financial challenge (12.2%). No statistically significant differences were found between urban and rural sites. Interestingly, 7 of the 11 individuals who said they felt fine as a justification for lack of clinic attendance, had either a new or prior diagnosis of hypertension which is typically expected to be asymptomatic.

Clinic follow-up reports

We wished to provide additional information on the patients who reported that they followed up after referral. This was to confirm actual attendance, provide relevant confirmatory diagnostic information after being seen be a clinical health care provider, and determine follow-up actions by the health providers including medication and education provided. This served in part as a quality control for the initial screening event. We focused in particular on hypertension as this was one of the newly identified risk factors for those screened. Of the 80 individuals who reported attending clinic after referral we were able to confirm attendance of 74 of these through records of clinics to which they were originally referred. All patients referred had their blood pressure measured at the clinic. Of those 74 individuals, 39 had a known diagnosis of hypertension and a total of 40 had hypertension at clinic attendance, but there was not complete concordance between baseline and follow-up results, depicted in Table 5. Thus, among those without a prior diagnosis of hypertension, of the 10 who were found to be hypertensive at baseline screening 9 (90%) were also hypertensive at follow-up; however, of the 25 participants without a history of hypertension and normotensive at screening, 7 were subsequently observed to be hypertensive at clinic follow-up. Among the 11 who had a prior history of hypertension, but were normotensive at screening, 5 were hypertensive at clinic; of those 28 individuals with a prior hypertension history and hypertensive at screening, 19 were recorded to be hypertensive at clinic. Among the 74 subjects, 24 were tested for fasting blood sugar and another 14 for random blood sugar. There were a total of 19 diagnoses of diabetes among the 74 participants at follow-up; this included 12 previous cases and 7 newly diagnosed cases. Three patients were tested for cholesterol levels, resulting in one new diagnosis of dyslipidemia.

Prescriptions administered included 16 for blood pressure (nifedipine, enalopril and amplodipine), 3 for hypercholesterolemia (atorvastatin), and 5 for diabetes (metformin, insulin). All patients received some form of counselling: 67 were counselled on diet, 54 on regular physical exercise, 49 about substance abuse, 22 about drug adherence, and 45 about importance of regular clinic follow-up.

Discussion

Cardiovascular disease risk and the usefulness of screening programs and referral uptake is not well studied in African, and in particular Ethiopian settings. In the present study we have performed a novel pilot community level screening approach, with the long-term aim to not only improve screening for cardiovascular disease risk, but to increase sustained uptake of at-risk individuals into long term care. In brief the key findings, which will be discussed in more detail below, were : (1) We observed that 18% of those screened had a > 10% 10 year CVD risk in the target city of Adama, Ethiopia and its rural surroundings, and Addis Ababa; (2) There were differences between urban and rural sites in prevalence of risk factors; (3) We observed high overall rates of clinic attendance among referred participants with this program, and notably higher rates among rural compared with urban populations; (4) Among urban dwellers, the highest rates of referral were among those with a prior history of hypertension and/or diabetes; (5) The commonest reasons for lack of attendance included inconvenience, feeling well and financial constraints.

We observed overall 10-year high and moderate risks of 6.0% and 12.6% which are consistent with previous Ethiopian studies [2, 29], including WHO STEPS surveys [28]. Risks were higher in urban than in rural sites. In general, at-risk individuals from urban sites tended to be overweight, younger and hypertensive, and less likely to be smokers and males. Disparities in prevalence of CVD risk factors between urban and rural regions have been previously described in Ethiopia and other countries [2, 28, 30–37], and may relate to differences in diet, physical activities, stress and/or occupational or environmental risks [32]. It may also reflect fewer opportunities within rural clinics to identify or test for conditions such as diabetes and hypertension.

A key objective of this study was to assess the voluntary clinical attendance rate among participants identified with CVD risk who were subsequently referred for assessment and follow-up. While most prior studies have shown relatively low rates from 10 to 37% [8–12, 16], we observed here much higher average rate (64%), which was even higher among participants in rural settings. A notable distinction between this and previous studies was its orientation towards greater education and communication of CVD risk to community members during the screening process, identified as a possible limitation of previous studies [8], and supported by many studies emphasizing importance of education in compliance [15–18]. Thus, through a prior citizen scientist mediated study and focus group discussions with community members [21, 23–25], we explored community level understanding, misperceptions and knowledge gaps about CVDs and associated risk factors. This formed the basis of our easy to use and understand manual of visual aids minimizing the use of language and depicting CVDs and risk, and providing clearer message of risk to participants with elevated risk scores. It is tempting to infer that this approach contributed to the apparent success of this pilot, but additional studies will need to be done to explore this possibility further in a more definitive fashion.

Another important observation in this study which may shed light on factors important for referral uptake was our finding that rates among rural dwellers were higher (72%) than urban inhabitants (58%), and this was particularly apparent in rural sites far removed from the nearest city (unpublished observations). Though this may reflect cultural, behavioural, environmental, or educational differences, we suspect this may be related to the involved HEW. The initial HEW program in Ethiopia was designed towards rural sites, whereas the urban HEW program was implemented later [38, 39].

Some studies [39, 40] have implicated the perception within urban communities that urban HEW may have less qualifications, even though receiving the same training as rural counterparts. Thus, greater trust [41–43] in rural HEW may have contributed towards higher compliance to recommendations among CVDs at risk participants. Future studies will be required to explore this and other possibilities.

Notably, we observed significantly higher referral rates among participants who had a prior history of hypertension. We found only scattered reports in various disciplines who have noted findings consistent with this, including studies of increased referrals after lung cancer [44] or premarital screening [45] which were related to prior medical histories. Reasonable factors underlying our finding could include increased awareness, higher health literacy, personal experiences with and familiarity and comfort with the health system. There is evidence for these individual factors contributing to referral rates in other screening approaches [46]. While our finding in this study may appear predictable, it is apparently novel for CVD risk screening, and underlying reasons explaining the effect of prior hypertension should be further probed. For example, health literacy and familiarity with the system may also have predicted that diabetic patients would have had better referral outcomes, and this was clearly not the case in our study; conceivably such participants opted to delay follow-up in favor of previously scheduled appointments. In any case, further studies may potentially identify new underlying reasons or reinforce the importance of previously identified factors. Such studies could inform improved interventions impacting referral uptake in future initiatives.

Other issues were revealed in the follow-up questionnaire among participants who chose not to attend have been cited in previous studies [8]. These included time inconvenience, financial concerns, and the participants’ being asymptomatic. Given that most cases of hypertension are asymptomatic, the finding that some at risk individuals did not attend because they felt fine, even though they had hypertension, underscores the importance of education. Despite whatever gains we may have attained through an education-oriented community level screening approach, there is room for improvement.

Of those individuals who self-reported their clinic attendance, we were able to confirm their attendance through clinic records. Although nearly all new cases of hypertension were confirmed in follow-up, there were notable discrepancies among those either with or without a history of prior hypertension. It is well established that many factors can impact blood pressure readings, and lead to under or over-estimation upon initial readings [47], and discrepancies we observed are consistent with many previous studies [47–49]. Recent medication use or lack thereof among previously diagnosed cases may have contributed to those discrepancies. An important point however is that many new cases of hypertension and diabetes were found among those at risk individuals who reported for follow-up, as would be expected [50–52]. These clinical findings underscore both the value of screening as well as clinic follow-up.

Limitations

The study would have been improved with a larger sample size to more fully probe some of the factors involved in follow-up. During follow-up we used only a minimal questionnaire to evaluate outcomes and only after one month of initial screening. The study population was limited to the urban and rural areas of Adama, and urban Addis Ababa, and the study population may not have been entirely representative of these regions. For example, there was a preponderance of women recruited in the study, likely the result of the recruitment during weekday working hours. Additionally, selection of rural clinics at varying distances from Adama town may have resulted in incomplete representation of rural areas. Larger studies in more regions of Ethiopia, and with longer periods of follow-up in the future will be able to more fully assess the generalizability of these findings, the prevalences of risk factors, the short and longer-term impact of the screening in terms of patient integration into the health care system, and to better explore enablers and barriers to success using both quantitative and qualitative approaches.

Conclusion

We conducted a pilot CVD risk screening program in urban and rural sites in Adama, and urban Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, and observed high rates of voluntary health clinic follow-up rates, particularly among rural clinics. This may have been due to the emphasis given to understanding community level perceptions of CVD risk and the co-development of educational material. We also noted higher referral rates among those with a prior history of hypertension. Future larger studies need to be conducted to determine the relative importance of education, health literacy, HEW trust, and other factors in assurance of high follow-up rates and integration into the health care system.
