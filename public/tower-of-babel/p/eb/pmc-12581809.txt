End of the line: a kinetic ruler model for poly(A) tail termination
Authors: Anita H. Corbett
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this Outlook, Corbett discusses a study in this issue of Genes & Development by Gabs et al. that shows that competing reactions between the nuclear poly(A) binding protein 2 (NAB2) and the cleavage and polyadenylation complex (CPAC) kinetically control poly(A) tail length. Corbett explores the implications of this “kinetic ruler” model in advancing our understanding of post-transcriptional mRNA processing and gene regulation.

The length of the poly(A) tail added to mRNAs can modulate many steps in gene expression, including transcript stability, export, and translation. Despite the stochastic nature of untemplated poly(A) tail synthesis, poly(A) tails are typically of relatively uniform length within a given organism; that is, ∼60 adenosines (As) in budding yeast and ∼250 As in mammals. The nature of these poly(A) tails suggests a tightly regulated control mechanism that reflects interplay between the CPAC and poly(A) binding proteins (PAPBs).

In the present study, Gabs et al. (2025) explore the mechanistic contribution of the evolutionarily conserved zinc finger poly(A) RNA binding protein nuclear poly(A) binding protein 2 (Nab2) (Anderson et al. 1993) to poly(A) tail length control in budding yeast. Results of the studies, which combine elegant in vitro assays, biochemistry, and in vivo analysis, suggest a model in which Nab2 does not function as a physical ruler, as previously suggested for other PABPs like PABPN1 in mammals (Kühn et al. 2009), but as a kinetic ruler. Using high-resolution in vitro assays, they demonstrated that Nab2 binds to the poly(A) tail in a length-dependent manner, with increased binding affinity and subsequent Nab2 dimerization above a threshold of ∼25 adenosines. This dimerization of Nab2 terminates polyadenylation not by defining a fixed tail length but by outcompeting CPAC-mediated addition of nucleotides as the poly(A) tail elongates.

Crucially, poly(A) tail length is determined by the relative rates of two competing reactions: poly(A) polymerase-driven elongation and Nab2 binding. Higher Nab2 concentrations shift this balance, resulting in shorter tails. Conversely, longer tails form when levels of Nab2 are limiting or when Nab2 binding affinity for RNA is decreased. This dynamic interplay defines the concept of a “kinetic ruler,” an elegant mechanism where binding kinetics define poly(A) tail length.

The investigators built on prior biochemical and structural studies to dissect the molecular basis for Nab2 function in poly(A) tail length control (Marfatia et al. 2003; Kelly et al. 2010; Kuhlmann et al. 2014). Their results reveal that Nab2 CCCH zinc finger domains 5–7 are critical to detect poly(A) tail length and mediate Nab2 dimerization, both of which are essential to compete with the CPAC and terminate polyadenylation. Other domains, including ZnFs 1–4 and the RGG domain, enhance RNA binding and stabilization of the RNP complex but are not sufficient for termination.

A clever tool that Gabs et al. (2025) used to explore the functional consequences of Nab2 dimerization exploits engineered rapamycin-induced Nab2 dimerization that takes advantage of the rapamycin-dependent dimerization of FKB12 and FRB (Schifferer et al. 2017). The investigators used this engineered system of Nab2 fused to these domains to demonstrate that induced Nab2 dimer formation prematurely terminates polyadenylation, even in the absence of elongation of the poly(A) tail. This finding confirms that dimerization, rather than a discrete RNA length, is the functional switch for polyadenylation termination. The investigators also suggest that Nab2 autoregulation via an encoded A26 stretch within the 3′ UTR of the NAB2 transcript, which was defined earlier (Roth et al. 2005), confers a homeostatic feedback loop that ensures the robustness of poly(A) tail length control, buffering the system against variation in Nab2 activity, expression level, and/or subcellular localization.

At first glance, the kinetic ruler model proposed here contrasts the molecular ruler model proposed in mammals, where PABPN1 is proposed to physically measure the poly(A) tail (Kühn et al. 2009), contributing to the formation of a termination-competent complex. However, the finding here that a zinc finger module from ZC3H14, the human counterpart of Nab2, can partially complement Nab2 function in yeast suggests conserved functional principles across eukaryotes.

This study advances our mechanistic understanding of a key step in post-transcriptional gene regulation. Given that poly(A) tails are dynamically remodeled during mRNA export, translation, and decay, this kinetic ruler model introduces new possibilities to consider how nuclear RNA-binding proteins could influence the fate of mRNAs in the cytoplasm. Thus, this model provides a new way of thinking about a key step in mRNA biogenesis by providing evidence that poly(A) tail length is not statically determined by binding footprints but can be dynamically controlled by protein–RNA interaction kinetics. The kinetic ruler model defines a scenario in which dynamic rates of molecular interactions can encode critical regulatory information.

A number of interesting questions emerge based on the findings reported here. These questions for the future include considerations of how changes in the local Nab2 concentration and/or Nab2 binding affinity for RNA could be modulated under different cellular conditions to control poly(A) tail length. Both Nab2 subcellular localization and binding affinity have the potential to be regulated by post-translational modification(s) and/or cellular stress (Carmody et al. 2010; Heinrich et al. 2024), suggesting one potential mode of regulation. Beyond the budding yeast system, loss-of-function mutations in the human ZC3H14 gene are linked to an autosomal recessive form of nonsyndromic intellectual disability (Pak et al. 2011). Furthermore, studies of Nab2 in Drosophila reveal that Nab2 expression specifically in neurons is both necessary and sufficient to support viability and proper brain morphology (Pak et al. 2011). These observations raise the distinct possibility that, in more complex organisms, Nab2/ZC3H14-mediated control is critical for a subset of specific transcripts or is key in specialized cell types.

In summary, Gabs et al. (2025) offer a compelling, mechanistically grounded model of poly(A) tail length control that contributes new insight into our understanding of mRNA 3′ end processing. Their proposed kinetic ruler model, where the interplay of poly(A) tail elongation speed and RNA-binding kinetics dictates tail length, adds a new dimension to gene regulation and establishes a foundation for exploring similar regulatory phenomena in diverse biological contexts.
