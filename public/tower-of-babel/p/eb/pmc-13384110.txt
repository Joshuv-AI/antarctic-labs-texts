Peanut shell-derived nickel cobalt oxide@carbon and activated carbon for high-energy supercapacitor electrodes
Authors: Sokhna Dieng, Ndeye Maty Ndiaye, Ndeye Fatou Sylla, Bridget K. Mutuma, Balla Diop Ngom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The concept of employing green technology for energy applications is appealing and increasingly necessary as fossil fuels continue to deplete. This study investigates the use of sustainable carbon-based materials derived from peanut shell extracts and incorporated into a nickel cobalt oxide (NiCo2O4) matrix through a green synthesis route for high-performance supercapacitor applications. The resulting NiCo2O4@C composite combines the strengths of its components while mitigating their individual limitations. Synthesis parameters, particularly duration, were optimized to improve specific capacity and cycling stability, resulting in samples synthesized over different time frames. Structural and compositional analyses confirmed the successful formation of crystalline spinel NiCo2O4 integrated with defect-rich amorphous carbon, while morphological characterization revealed that the 4 hour synthesized NiCo2O4@C possessed a porous architecture with abundant cavities and uniformly distributed elements, contributing to its superior electrochemical performance. An asymmetric supercapacitor device assembled using NiCo2O4@C @4 h as the positive electrode and activated carbon (AC) derived from the residual biomass as the negative electrode, delivered a specific energy of 38 Wh kg−1 and a specific power of 712 W kg−1 at 1 A g−1. Notably, this approach enables 100% utilization of peanut shell biomass, offering a cost-effective and scalable solution for high-performance energy storage with minimal environmental impact.

Introduction

With the rapid expansion of hybrid electric vehicles and consumer electronics, the demand for efficient energy storage devices has become increasingly critical. Supercapacitors, which bridge the gap between conventional capacitors and batteries in terms of power and energy density, have emerged as one of the most promising next-generation energy storage technologies.1 The idea of developing them has attracted the attention of researchers worldwide due to their high-power density, long cycling lifetimes, and rapid charge/discharge capabilities.2–4 The choice of electrode materials plays a crucial role in optimizing supercapacitor performance.5 While commercial supercapacitors primarily rely on activated carbon-based electrodes, their relatively low specific capacitance limits their potential for advanced applications.6,7 Consequently, numerous studies have focused on developing new electrode materials that can offer higher efficiency and performance.8 Transition metal oxides and hydroxides, in particular, have gained significant attention due to their ability to undergo fast reversible redox reactions, leveraging multiple oxidation states to enhance charge storage capacity.9

Among these materials, spinel nickel cobaltite (NiCo2O4) stands out as a particularly promising candidate. It offers significantly higher electrical conductivity, at least two orders of magnitude greater than that of monometallic nickel or cobalt oxides (NiCo2O4 > NiO > Co3O4)10 along with high theoretical capacitance,11 cost-effectiveness,12 low toxicity,13 natural abundance, and environmental friendliness.14 Additionally, the redox behavior of both nickel and cobalt contributes to enhanced energy storage efficiency.15 However, despite these advantages, NiCo2O4 suffers from intrinsic conductivity limitations16 and a tendency to aggregate at the nanoscale, which significantly reduces accessible surface areas and blocks pseudocapacitive sites, ultimately compromising rate capability and cycle stability.17 To address these challenges, researchers have explored composite approaches by incorporating various carbon-based materials, such as graphene, carbon nanotubes, and hollow carbon spheres, to enhance electrical conductivity and particle dispersion.18–21 These carbon materials emerged as exceptional candidates for fabricating high-performance NiCo2O4-based composite electrodes.20,22,23 They offer high specific surface areas, excellent electrical conductivity, and outstanding chemical stability.8,13 Furthermore, they are abundant, cost-effective, and can be derived from biomass waste.25,26

Combining NiCo2O4 with carbon offers a promising approach to overcoming the limitations of each material, leading to enhanced electrochemical properties. NiCo2O4 provides high capacity but suffers from low electrical conductivity and agglomeration. Carbon helps prevent agglomeration and enhances conductivity, while also contributing additional surface area for ion diffusion. The composite structure facilitates fast electron transfer and deep penetration of electrolyte ions, improving cycling stability and energy density. Additionally, the synergy between these materials enhances redox activity and improves electrochemical reaction kinetics, making NiCo2O4@C a highly efficient electrode material for next-generation supercapacitors. For example, Xu et al. reported a NiCo2O4/graphene composite electrode with a specific capacitance of approximately 835 F g−1 at 1 A g−1, highlighting the beneficial role of graphene in improving conductivity and structural stability.20 Subsequent studies have shown that NiCo2O4/CNT, NiCo2O4/rGO, NiCo2O4/carbon nanofiber, and NiCo2O4/hollow carbon sphere composites further enhance charge-transfer kinetics, electrolyte accessibility, rate capability, and long-term cycling performance by creating interconnected conductive networks and buffering volume changes during repeated redox reactions.24

In this study, we developed a NiCo2O4@C composite using peanut shell extracts for supercapacitor electrodes. Peanut (Arachis hypogaea L., Fabaceae) is a leguminous plant native to Mexico, widely cultivated in tropical, subtropical, and temperate regions for its oil-rich seeds.27 The peanut shell, or hull, produced during the shelling process, accounts for approximately 20 to 32 wt% of the total weight.28 It has a netted exterior and resembles straw or wood chips in appearance (Fig. S1). Peanut shells are commonly used in the production of livestock feed flour, as a natural fertilizer additive, poultry bedding, mulch in poultry houses, and as fuel in industrial boilers. Despite these various applications, peanut shells are often considered agricultural waste or a byproduct of peanut processing. However, they contain numerous functional components that are non-toxic to humans and may be of significant interest for the green synthesis of nanoparticles.29,30 Moreover, peanut shells are rich in carbon-based compounds, such as lignin (27.0–33.0%), cellulose (34.7–45.0%), and hemicellulose (approximately 9.0%), making them a renewable energy source.31,32 In addition, peanut shells are composed of essential elements such as carbon (45.0–57.0%), hydrogen (5.0–7.0%), oxygen (35.0–47.0%), and nitrogen (1.0–2.0%).33,34 Their sustainable utilization not only adds value to this agricultural byproduct but also contributes to environmental protection.

We employed a one-step green synthesis method to incorporate biomass-derived carbon into a Nickel Cobalt Oxide (NiCo2O4) matrix, forming a hybrid composite. By varying the synthesis duration (2, 4, and 8 hours), we systematically investigated its effect on the structural evolution and electrochemical behavior of the composite, allowing the identification of optimal conditions that maximize specific capacity and stability.

Materials and methods

Preparation of the extract

The peanut shells were collected from the food market in Dakar, Senegal. They were first washed several times in tap water, then followed by de-ionized water to remove impurities and dust, then dried under direct sunlight for 3 days, and finally ground into powder.

The preparation of the solvent is described as follows: approximately 3 g of peanut shell powder were added to 150 mL of distilled water and macerated for 24 hours at room temperature to ensure maximum extraction of the bioactive compounds. The extract was filtered with Fisherbrand filter paper (GR111 185 mm × 25 µm) to remove residual solids. A clear, transparent yellowish extract (aqueous solution of peanut shell dye) was obtained (Scheme 1).

Illustration of the extraction step of peanut and sample preparations.

Sample preparations

All reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and directly used without further purification: nickel(ii) nitrate hexahydrate, Ni(NO3)2·6H2O, purity: ≥99%, cobalt(ii) sulfate heptahydrate, Co(SO4)·7H2O, purity: ≥99%, potassium hydroxide (KOH, min 85%). Polycrystalline nickel (Ni) foam (3D scaffold template with an areal density of 420 g m−2, electrical conductivity of ∼1.4 × 107 S m−1, mechanical strength of 50 MPa, and thickness of 1.6 mm) was used as material support and current collector.

About 1 g of cobalt(ii) sulfate heptahydrate (CoSO4·7H2O) and 0.5 g of nickel(ii) nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO3)2·6H2O) (Ni : Co = 1 : 2 molar ratio) were dissolved in 30 mL of the filtrate of the aqueous peanut shell dye. The solution was mixed using magnetic stirring at low speed for 2 h to ensure complete dissolution. The resulting solution turned light orange. It was then heated to 150 °C for 2 h in an electric oven, producing brown metal oxide nanopowders, which were kept for characterization. The same process was repeated at synthesis times of 4 h and 8 h to study the time effects on the nanopowder properties. The mass ratio (Ni : Co = 1 : 2), solution concentration, temperature (150 °C), and solvent volume (30 mL) were kept constant. Increasing the synthesis time resulted in darker nanopowders (Fig. S2).

The activated carbon was synthesized as reported elsewhere.35 About 5 g of the residue of the peanut shell was mixed with a few drops of deionized water (DI) to make a sludge. A compact rectangular block from the sludge mixture was placed in an oven at 80 °C to dry overnight. The dried compact block mixture was transferred into a horizontal tube furnace set at 600 °C (ramp rate of 5 °C min−1) for a period of 2 h in a 250 sccm flow of Argon gas. The black product obtained (referred to as carbonized peanut shell waste (CPSW)) was washed several times with DI and dried at 80 °C. A weighed amount of CPSW was mixed with KOH for the activation process at an elevated temperature. The moulded mixture blocks were subjected to pyrolysis at 850 °C using a 5 °C min−1 ramp rate for a 1 h period using 250 sccm of Argon gas flow. The resulting products were soaked in 3 M HCl and sonicated for 8 h to get rid of the excess unreacted KOH. Finally, the filtered solids materials were rinsed with DI until a neutral pH before drying at 80 °C for 12 h.

Material characterization techniques

The diversity of structural, morphological, and compositional properties of the prepared nanoparticles were evaluated using a series of analytical techniques such as X-ray diffraction (XRD), Raman spectroscopy (Raman), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) nitrogen adsorption–desorption analysis, energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), and derivative thermogravimetric (DTG).

The crystal structure as well as the size of the different samples of the nanopowders as prepared were studied using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, Bruker D8) with copper Kα radiation of wavelength λ = 1.5406 Å in an angular range of 2θ from 10° to 70°. Raman analysis was then carried out to verify the presence of the phases using a WITec confocal Raman microscope (WITec alpha 300 R, RAS+, Germany) operating at 532 nm. To better illustrate the effects of time on structure, agglomeration, and morphology, scanning electron microscopy images were recorded using an Ultra Plus 55 scanning electron microscope at an operating potential of 2 kV. The specific surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution were determined by Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) nitrogen adsorption–desorption analysis. Further analysis was done by EDS mapping to study the phase homogeneity and uniformity in the distribution of elements. The elemental composition as well as the oxidation states of the chemical elements of the NiCo2O4@C composite were obtained by XPS measurements. FTIR was employed to identify the functional groups and chemical bonding in the prepared samples. The FTIR spectra were recorded using a Bruker Tensor II spectrometer in the range of 4000–400 cm−1, with a resolution of 4 cm−1. TGA and DTG analyses were performed to investigate the thermal stability and composition of the NiCo2O4@C composites. Measurements were carried out using a NETZSCH TG 209 F1 Libra analyzer under a nitrogen atmosphere at a heating rate of 10 °C min−1 from room temperature to 800 °C.

Electrode preparation

Three-electrode preparation

All the working electrodes were prepared following the same procedure, which consists of mixing 80% of active material (NiCo2O4@C or activated carbon) composite and 10% of carbon black (to enhance electrical conductivity) using a mortar. Then, 10 wt% of polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) was added as a binder, along with a few drops of N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) to form a homogeneous suspension. Subsequently, the slurry was coated onto nickel foam (1 × 1 cm2), which served as a current collector, and dried in the oven at 60 °C for at least 12 hours to ensure complete evaporation of the NMP (Fig. S3).

The charge mass of the active material was acquired by measuring the electrode with a microbalance with an accuracy of 0.01 mg. Typically, each electrode material's active material loading masses were kept at approximately 3 mg cm−2 for consistency and reliable comparison. Under the same parameters, AC electrodes were prepared.

Two-electrode preparation

The schematic illustration of CR2032-type coin cells with NiCo2O4@C @4 h positive electrode and AC negative electrode is provided in Fig. S4. The preparation method for the two-electrode configuration was similar to that of the three-electrode system, with a few modifications. The slurry was coated onto a nickel foam sheet and was punched into circular discs with a diameter of 12 mm. A porous polypropylene membrane (Celgard, 18 mm diameter) was used as the separator. Each coin cell was assembled using a fixed volume of electrolyte (∼75 µL) to ensure consistency across all tests.

Electrochemical measurements

The electrochemical measurements of the different samples were carried out with a Bio-Logic VMP-300 potentiostat at room temperature, controlled by EC-lab® V1.40 software in a three-electrode (half-cell) setup and a two-electrode (full-cell) setup. A silver/silver chloride (Ag/AgCl) (3 M KCl) reference electrode and a platinum (Pt) plate as counter electrode were used with active material NiCo2O4@C and AC as working electrode to complete the test circuit in an aqueous solution of 6 M KOH. The electrochemical performance characteristics were evaluated by cyclic voltammetry (CV), galvanostatic charge/discharge cycling (GCD), electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), and stability (cycling) tests.

Results and discussion

Physicochemical properties

The XRD analysis of the three diffractograms (Fig. 1a) reveals similar patterns, with eight distinct diffraction peaks indicating the polycrystalline nature of the spinel NiCo2O4 and the amorphous nature of the carbon nanoparticles. All peaks are well indexed to the NiCo2O4 cubic phase (JCPDS card no. 20-0781).36 The prominent peaks at 22.8°, 28.6°, 36.4°, 39.0°, 45.4°, 54.3°, 65.3°, and 68.0° were ascribed to (111), (220), (311), (222), (400), (422), (511), and (440) planes of spinel NiCo2O4 respectively, which belong to the Fd3m space group system, a cubic crystal structure with face-centered.37 The narrow peak widths indicate a high degree of crystallinity, supporting the formation of the NiCo2O4@C composite. In contrast, no peaks corresponding to carbon are observed because of the amorphous nature of the biomass-derived carbon.38 However, Raman analysis (Fig. 1b) clearly confirms the presence of carbon through the characteristic D, G, and 2D bands at 1341.34, 1516.02, and 2444.0 cm−1, respectively.39 The G band is attributed to the in-plane stretching vibration of sp2-hybridized carbon in a graphite-like structure, while the D band indicates structural disorder associated with sp3 carbon or dopant heteroatoms.39 The intensity ratio between D and G bands (ID/IG) representing the degree of graphitization was 1.1, suggesting a high degree of defects within the material. The broad 2D band further supports the existence of amorphous carbon domains within the hybrid framework. For NiCo2O4@C @8 h, the Eg and A1g vibration modes of NiCo2O4 spinel are observed at 493.9 and 686.66 cm−1, respectively, aligning well with the literature.40 A distinct vibration band attributed to SO42− is observed at 1032.23 cm−1. The SO42− species, originating from the CoSO4·7H2O precursor, can enhance the electrochemical performance by improving ionic conductivity and facilitating rapid charge transfer at the electrode–electrolyte interface.41 At 4 hours, similar vibration modes appear at nearly identical frequencies, with Eg, A1g, and SO42− located at 477.7, 686.7, and 1056.6 cm−1. At 2 hours, the peaks for Eg, A1g, and SO42− are centered at 485.84, 638.69, and 1048.5 cm−1, respectively.

FTIR spectrum exhibited several characteristic vibrational features corresponding to the components of the hybrid material (Fig. 1c). The broad absorption band centered at ∼3220 cm−1 is attributed to O–H stretching vibrations from adsorbed water molecules or surface hydroxyl groups. However, prolonged time (8 h) may reduce surface functionalization. This trend is consistent with the SEM observations, where particle coalescence becomes more pronounced at extended synthesis durations.

As shown in Fig. 1d, all samples exhibit multistage weight losses, indicative of progressive decomposition events. The total weight loss decreases slightly with increasing synthesis duration: 2 h the final residual mass ∼26.36%, @4 h the final residual mass ∼25.80%, and @8 h, the final residual mass ∼25.16%. This suggests that prolonged synthesis time results in a more thermally stable material, likely due to better structural integration between NiCo2O4 and the carbon components. The initial weight loss observed below ∼150 °C in all samples is attributed to the evaporation of adsorbed water and volatile surface species. Subsequent weight losses occurring from ∼200 °C to 800 °C correspond to the decomposition of the organic components and partial degradation of the carbon matrix.48 The DTG curves in Fig. 1e provide further insight into the decomposition kinetics. All samples display several distinct degradation peaks, reflecting different stages of material breakdown. The major weight loss events shift to higher temperatures with longer reaction times, consistent with increased thermal stability due to enhanced interaction between components and more complete formation of the composite.

SEM images (Fig. 2a–c) reveal that the surface morphology of the NiCo2O4@C composites strongly depends on synthesis time. Initially, at 2 hours, the SEM images show that the sample is composed of quasi-spherical, agglomerated nanometric particles (Fig. 2a). These early-stage nanoparticles, while uniform in shape, tend to cluster, indicative of the initial phases of material synthesis. By the 4 h mark, the nanoparticles retained their quasi-spherical geometry but exhibited a greater degree of porosity and an increased number of cavities (Fig. 2b). The structural evolution could enhance the material's specific capacity, likely due to a larger accessible surface area that facilitates better electrochemical interactions and efficient electrolyte penetration essential for applications such as energy storage. At 8 hours, the morphology shifts significantly; the NiCo2O4@C sample developed a dense, almost smooth surface (Fig. 2c). The transformation suggests a reduction in porosity, potentially decreasing the effective surface area available for electrochemical reactions and possibly undermining the material's performance in electrochemical applications. These SEM observations underscore the critical role of time in material processing and its direct influence on the physical properties.

(a–c) SEM images at low (1 µm) and high magnification (200 nm) of the as-synthesized NiCo2O4@C @2 h, @4 h, and @8 h, (d) N2 adsorption–desorption isotherm NiCo2O4@C @2 h, @4 h, and @8 h, (d) NCO-C40, and (e) corresponding BJH pore size distribution curves.

All samples exhibit type IV adsorption–desorption isotherms with H3 hysteresis loops, characteristic of mesoporous materials formed by aggregates of nanoparticles (Fig. 2d). The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) specific surface area reaches 38.43 m2 g−1 for the 4 h sample, which is significantly higher than those of the 2 h (24.11 m2 g−1) and 8 h (14.00 m2 g−1) samples. The increase in surface area for the 4 h sample is attributed to its highly porous interconnected structure observed by SEM. In contrast, the lower surface area of the 8 h sample is due to particle coalescence and pore collapse during prolonged synthesis.

The Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) pore size distribution (Fig. 2e) further confirms that all samples possess predominantly mesoporous structures, with pore diameters mainly in the 3–10 nm range. Among them, the 4 h sample exhibits the highest pore volume and the most pronounced mesoporous distribution, indicating a greater density of accessible pores for electrolyte transport. In contrast, the 8 h sample shows a marked reduction in pore volume, consistent with its compact morphology and lower BET surface area. The combination of a high specific surface area and well-developed mesoporosity in the 4 h sample is expected to enhance ion diffusion, increase the number of electrochemically active sites, and ultimately improve its electrochemical performance.

Elemental mapping of the NiCo2O4@C @4 h sample (Fig. 3a) confirms homogeneous distribution of nickel (Ni), cobalt (Co), oxygen (O), carbon (C), and nitrogen (N) elements within the composite. The uniform dispersion of Ni and Co indicates successful incorporation of both metal species into the spinel framework. The presence of C originates from bio-organic compounds in the peanut shell extract, confirming the successful integration of carbon nanoparticles into the NiCo2O4 matrix. Additionally, S and N elements are detected, originating from the cobalt sulfate CoSO4·7H2O and nickel nitrate Ni(NO3)2·6H2O precursors used during synthesis. These elements are homogeneously dispersed and uniformly distributed throughout the composite, potentially enhancing its electrochemical properties. The data also indicate that the chemical composition remains consistent across different processing times (Fig. S7a), suggesting that time variation does not affect the elemental presence in the samples.

(a) Element distribution mapping images, distribution of individual elements of NiCo2O4@C @4 h including Ni, Co, O, C, N, and S, and (b) high-resolution XPS spectra of Ni 2p, Co 2p, O 1s, C 1s, N 1s, and S 1s of NiCo2O4@C @4 h.

The XPS spectra are identical and confirm the consistent presence of Ni, Co, O, C, N, and S across different synthesis times, consistent with the EDS results (Fig. S6a). Notably, carbon is the second most abundant element after oxygen, derived mainly from the extract, indicating a high level of composite purity with 100% of the intended elements present (Fig. S7b). High-resolution spectra for each element show similar profiles for various synthesis times, with nearly identical bond energies (Fig. 3b and S7b). For nickel (Ni), the Ni 2p spectra display two oxidation states, Ni2+ and Ni3+, and two shakeup satellites across distinct energy levels. For NiCo2O4@C, the Ni3+ 2p3/2 peak appears at energies of 856 eV, while the Ni2+ 2p3/2 peak is seen at 858 eV. The Ni3+ 2p1/2 and Ni2+ 2p1/2 peaks are recorded near 873.4 and 874.9 eV, respectively.42,43 Similarly, for cobalt (Co), the Co 2p spectra reveal two valence states, Co2+ and Co3+, with spin-orbital doublets as well as two shakeup satellites. The Co3+ 2p3/2 peak occurs around 780.8 eV, whereas the Co2+ 2p3/2 peak is found at 781.9 eV. Additional peaks relating to Co3+ and Co2+ in the 2p1/2 region are also recorded at 795.6 and 797.4 eV, respectively.44 Oxygen (O) 1s orbital spectra demonstrates the formation of a metal–oxygen (M–O) bond typical of spinel NiCo2O4 structure, with a peak at 529.9 eV, and another peak associated with the O–C bond around 532.1 eV.43 The C 1s spectrum exhibits three distinct peaks corresponding to CC, C–O, and CO bonds. The broad peak centered around 284.5 eV is attributed to sp2-hybridized carbon (CC),45 while the component near 286.6 eV is associated with sp3 carbon bonded to oxygen (C–O), and a third peak observed at approximately 288.2 eV corresponds to carbonyl groups (CO).46 N 1s orbital spectra identify four peaks (397.3, 399.7, 403.5, and 406.6 eV) corresponding to different nitrogen bonds, including pyridinic N and graphitic N, consistently displayed across the time points. S 2p orbital spectrum focuses on a peak for the SO42− ion (169.2 eV), confirming the presence of sulfur from the precursor material. These spectral findings support the effective synthesis of NiCo2O4@C and suggest potential enhancements in electrochemical activity due to the existence of cations Ni3+/Ni2+, Co3+/Co2+ in NiCo2O4@C composite, providing abundant active sites.23

Electrochemical properties

Electrodes with an active material mass loading of ∼3 mg cm−2 were evaluated to compare their electrochemical performance.

Cyclic voltammetry measurements were conducted at a scan rate of 50 mV s−1 within a potential range of 0.0 to 0.5 V (vs. Ag/AgCl), as depicted in Fig. 4a. All three electrodes displayed a pair of well-defined redox peaks corresponding to oxidation (anodic) and reduction (cathodic) processes, reflecting the faradaic nature of NiCo2O4, which is known to undergo multiple redox transitions.11 Specifically, the anodic peaks appeared at 0.38, 0.36, and 0.41 V, while the cathodic peaks were observed at 0.26, 0.25, and 0.27 V for the 2 h, 4 h, and 8 h electrodes, respectively. The electrodes @2 h and @8 h showed more shifted redox potentials compared to the @4 h electrode. Additionally, the 4 h sample exhibited a larger CV loop area and broader peaks, suggesting enhanced surface charge storage.49 These features highlight NiCo2O4@C @4 h as a promising electrode material with higher specific capacity. The nonlinear GCD profile, characterized by typical non-linear triangular symmetry in the charge and discharge cycles, supports the typical faradaic behavior previously observed in the cyclic voltammetry analysis. The pattern reveals highly reversible redox reactions within the material.17 From the GCD curves (Fig. 4b), it can be observed that NiCo2O4@C @4 h has a longer discharge time, confirming its much higher capacity, with a specific capacity value of 62.6 mAh g−1 (450.6 F g−1) at 0.5 A g−1 (Fig. 4c), followed by NiCo2O4@C @2 h with 60.4 mAh g−1 (435.15 F g−1) and NiCo2O4@C @8 h with the lowest capacity of 52.8 mAh g−1 (379.21 F g−1) and demonstrating superior electrochemical performance and supporting the CV results.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were conducted as shown in Fig. 4d. Among the samples, NiCo2O4@C @4 h exhibited the lowest equivalent series resistance (ESR), with a recorded value of 0.19 Ω (inset), compared to 0.33 Ω and 0.35 Ω for the 2 h and 8 h electrodes, respectively. This result indicates that NiCo2O4@C @4 h possesses lower internal resistance and significantly enhanced electrical conductivity.50 Furthermore, the electrode obtained at 4 h has higher specific capacities from 0.5 to 10 A g−1 (Fig. 4e). The specific capacity decreasing with increasing current density is mainly due to ion diffusion limitations.

At higher current densities, the rapid charge–discharge process limits electrolyte ion penetration into the electrode. In contrast, lower current densities allow sufficient time for ion diffusion throughout the active material, resulting in higher specific capacity.49 However, NiCo2O4@C @4 h retained more than half of its initial specific capacity at 10 A g−1 with a value of approximately 60%. This retention is significantly higher than that of the other samples, measured at 37% for 2 h and 51% for 8 h. After undergoing 7000 successive galvanostatic charge/discharge (GCD) cycles at a constant specific current density of 10 A g−1, as illustrated in Fig. 4f, the electrodes demonstrated excellent stability with significant capacity retention rates of 67.6%, 86.4%, and 84.1% for synthesis times of 2, 4, and 8 hours, respectively. These results not only confirm the high stability of the hybrid electrodes but also underscore the superior electrochemical performance of the NiCo2O4@C @4 h electrode, which exhibits a much higher specific capacity and better long-term stability compared to the other electrodes.

The inset GCD curves (first and last five cycles) confirm the stable potential profile over prolonged cycling, which shows that even after extended charge–discharge cycling, the curves remain undeformed and nearly symmetrical.

The electrochemical performance of the NiCo2O4@C synthesized at 4 hours can be explained by three key factors:

(1) The electrode features a structure composed of agglomerated nanoparticles that create more void spaces. These spaces are crucial as they provide an abundance of active sites. This structural characteristic facilitates rapid electron transfer and ion diffusion, which are essential during the faradaic redox processes involving the redox couples Ni2+/Ni3+ and Co2+/Co3+. (2) The electrode contains a higher percentage of nickel and cobalt, which are active materials that undergo rich redox reactions. This composition is beneficial because it promotes a more intense faradaic response. The rich redox chemistry of nickel and cobalt contributes to a higher capacity and more robust electrochemical activity, making the electrode more effective in energy storage applications. (3) Higher electrical conductivity in this electrode improves carrier transport, which is crucial for maintaining strong and consistent charge/discharge cycles. Enhanced conductivity ensures that electrical charges move efficiently through the material, which minimizes energy losses and improves the overall performance of the electrode during repeated cycling. These factors contribute significantly to the enhanced performance of the NiCo2O4@C @4 h electrode, making it an excellent candidate for applications requiring high-efficiency electrochemical energy storage. Conclusion

In this study, we successfully synthesized a high-performance NiCo2O4@C composite using a green, sustainable approach that utilized peanut shell waste as a carbon source. By optimizing the synthesis parameters, particularly the reaction duration, we achieved a nanostructured material with excellent electrochemical properties. The composite electrode exhibited a remarkable specific capacity and long-term cycling stability, retaining over 86% of its capacity after 7000 cycles at 10 A g−1. When assembled into an asymmetric supercapacitor device with activated carbon derived from the same biomass, the system delivered a high specific energy of 38 Wh kg−1 at a power density of 712 W kg−1. These results place the material among the top-performing NiCo2O4-based systems reported in the literature. This work not only demonstrates the electrochemical advantages of incorporating carbon into a NiCo2O4 matrix but also highlights the feasibility of turning agricultural waste into valuable energy storage materials. Notably, the approach enables 100% utilization of peanut shell biomass, as both fractions generated during processing were transformed into functional electrode materials: the aqueous extract was employed for the green synthesis of the NiCo2O4@C positive electrode, while the residual solid biomass was converted into activated carbon for the negative electrode. By eliminating the disposal of any biomass fraction, this strategy enhances the environmental and economic sustainability of the process and represents a promising route toward scalable and sustainable supercapacitor development.
