A study on the correlation among sleep quality in pregnant women, type 2 diabetes risk scores based on FINDRISC, and pregnancy-related anxiety
Authors: Shuang Liu, Jinyi Wang, Chengwen Ma, Tao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPregnancy-related anxiety is a common mental health issue during pregnancy, affecting at least 10% of expectant mothers. Although psychological and social factors are considered as one of the contributing factors for such situations, the potential association between the risk of type 2 diabetes and pregnancy-related anxiety has not been fully investigated. This study aims to explore the associations among sleep quality, type 2 diabetes risk scores (based on FINDRISC), and pregnancy-related anxiety in pregnant women.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted from March to December 2024 using convenience sampling to survey 469 pregnant women at a tertiary hospital in Jinzhou City. Data collection utilized demographic data questionnaires, the Pregnancy-Related Anxiety Questionnaire (PRAQ), the Finnish Diabetes Risk Score (FINDRISC), and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). The relationships among the variables were analyzed using SPSS 27.0 and AMOS software.ResultsThe mean ± SD scores for type 2 diabetes risk, sleep quality, and pregnancy-related anxiety were 9.047 ± 7.410, 7.719 ± 5.066, and 31.508 ± 8.170, respectively. Significant differences in pregnancy-related anxiety scores were observed across age groups, gestational weeks, parity, and history of adverse pregnancy and childbirth (all P < 0.05). Higher PSQI scores, indicating greater sleep disturbance, were positively associated with type 2 diabetes risk scores (r = 0.423, P < 0.01) and pregnancy-related anxiety (r = 0.340, P < 0.01). Sleep quality partially mediated the association between type 2 diabetes risk scores and pregnancy-related anxiety (indirect effect β = 0.103, 95% CI [0.057, 0.162]).ConclusionThese findings suggest that screening for type 2 diabetes risk, assessment of sleep quality, and monitoring of pregnancy-related anxiety may be integrated in clinical practice, as these factors may contribute to a comprehensive evaluation of the physical and mental health of pregnant women.Keywords: Anxiety, Sleep, Type 2 diabetes, Pregnant women

Introduction

During pregnancy, women often experience persistent fatigue and physical discomfort. Additionally, they frequently suffer from significant mood swings and mental health problems. The prevalence of psychiatric disorders during pregnancy is high, affecting approximately 20% of pregnant women. However, these disorders are still underestimated and underdiagnosed [1].

Pregnancy-related anxiety is a common psychological problem during pregnancy. Studies have shown that approximately 10% of pregnant women are affected by generalized anxiety disorder [2]. Anxiety is defined as an internal disorder or irrational fear without a clear objective cause, usually emerging when individuals encounter challenges, difficulties, or dangers [3]. Pregnancy-related anxiety has been associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes such as premature birth and low birth weight, and may also be linked to cognitive and behavioral difficulties in offspring, as well as long-term mental health problems [4]. However, compared with gestational depression, systematic research on gestational anxiety is still relatively weak, especially in terms of influencing factors and content dimensions. The exploration in this area is not yet sufficient [5].

The anxiety level of pregnant women may be influenced by various factors, among which high-risk pregnancy is a definite physiological risk factor [2]. High-risk pregnancy means that pregnant women face higher health risks during pregnancy, such as an increased probability of complications like gestational hypertension and gestational diabetes. These undoubtedly bring huge psychological pressure to pregnant women and significantly increase their anxiety levels. Additionally, physiological, environmental and social factors such as daily life stress and socioeconomic conditions, also play important roles [6].

It is worth noting that there is a significant bidirectional association between anxiety disorders and the risk of developing type 2 diabetes [7, 8]. This association is particularly significant for the special group of pregnant women. For the pregnant women themselves, anxiety disorders can greatly affect their physical and mental health. From a physiological perspective, the emotional stress triggered by anxiety activates the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal axis, leading to a continuous increase in cortisol secretion. Cortisol, as a stress hormone, remaining at a high level for a long time will interfere with the normal metabolic functions of the body, thereby inducing insulin resistance, reducing the sensitivity of body cells to insulin, preventing glucose from entering cells for utilization normally, and causing an increase in blood sugar levels, thereby increasing the risk of developing type 2 diabetes. Once a pregnant woman is diagnosed with type 2 diabetes, she needs to strictly control her blood sugar levels during pregnancy, which will bring additional psychological burdens to her and further aggravate her anxiety [9].

There is no uniform definition of sleep quality. Carskadon [10] defines sleep as a reversible perceptual behavioral state of detachment from and unresponsiveness to the environment, characterized by eye closure, behavioral quiescence, and perceptual detachment from the surrounding environment. Sleep is a key stage in neurophysiology, a necessary process for human physiological activity, and is also closely related to human psychological functioning. Many factors can affect sleep quality, such as stimulant use, stress, nutrition, and using electronic devices before bedtime [11]. During pregnancy, significant fluctuations in hormone levels trigger a series of physiological changes in a woman’s body. These changes may cause physical discomfort, which can disrupt normal sleep rhythms and lead to poorer sleep quality. During pregnancy, common sleep disorders for pregnant women include difficulty falling asleep, reduced sleep quality, shortened sleep duration, early awakening, frequent dreaming, and difficulty maintaining sleep. Some pregnant women may even develop obstructive sleep apnea-hypopnea syndrome and restless legs syndrome [12]. Sleep disorders can adversely affect both the mother and fetus. Existing research reveals a strong association between sleep problems and gestational hypertension and diabetes [13], and sleep duration of less than 7 h increases the risk of low birth weight infants [14]. Simultaneously, extensive research indicates that poor sleep quality is closely linked to anxiety levels in pregnant women [15]. The significant elevation of estrogen and progesterone levels during pregnancy may disrupt neurotransmitter balance, leading to alterations in sleep architecture and subsequently triggering anxiety.

It is evident that both the type 2 diabetes risk score and sleep quality are associated with anxiety levels during pregnancy. However, current research on the relationship among prenatal anxiety, sleep quality, and type 2 diabetes risk scores remains insufficient. Therefore, this study aims to explore the relationship between type 2 diabetes risk scores and prenatal anxiety while analyzing the mediating role of sleep quality. This investigation seeks to provide scientific evidence for developing targeted health care and prenatal psychological intervention strategies, thereby helping to reduce the incidence of prenatal anxiety and safeguard the physical and mental health of both mothers and infants.

This study is grounded in the biopsychosocial model [16]. This theory was proposed by Engel in 1977 and is currently considered the most effective theory for health promotion. The model suggests that an individual’s health status is the result of the interaction of biological, psychological, and social factors. The pathway relationships are shown in Fig. 1.

Methods

Study design, setting, and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted from March to December 2024, enrolling pregnant women undergoing routine prenatal checkups at a tertiary hospital in Jinzhou City, Liaoning Province. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) at least 18 years of age and have conceived naturally; (2) be mentally competent and able to complete the questionnaire; (3) voluntarily participate and sign an informed consent form. The exclusion criteria included: (1) women diagnosed with diabetes prior to pregnancy; (2) those with severe cardiovascular or respiratory diseases; (3) pregnant women with severe pregnancy complications; (4) those with incomplete medical records. According to Kendall’s sample calculation method [17], the sample size should be 5 to 10 times the number of variables. This study surveyed 30 items, including 12 demographic data questionnaires, 3 dimensions of the Pregnancy-Related Anxiety Scale, 8 items of the Finnish Diabetes Risk Score, and 7 dimensions of the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index. Considering a 20% sample loss, the minimum sample size required for this study was 360. Ultimately, 469 questionnaires meeting the inclusion criteria were collected.

Instruments

General information questionnaire

A general information questionnaire was used to investigate the demographic characteristics of pregnant women, including age, ethnicity, occupation, week of gestation, marital status, educational level, family per capita monthly income, parity, family history, personal history, history of adverse pregnancy and childbirth, and early pregnancy symptoms. The questionnaire underwent content validity testing prior to formal administration, yielding a content validity index (CVI) of 0.92. This indicates that the questionnaire possesses strong representativeness and relevance.

Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI)

The sleep quality of pregnant women was assessed using the Chinese version of the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). Information on sleep quality was collected using the PSQI, which was translated and introduced by Liu Xianchen et al. In 1996, it was an internationally used, validated, and reliable questionnaire [18] for self-reporting of sleep quality over a one-month period. It has been used to assess sleep quality in various populations, such as older adults [19], adolescents [20], and patients with various medical conditions [21]. The PSQI consists of 19 items, with a total of 18 items involved in scoring, covering seven dimensions: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction. Each dimension consists of one or more entries according to which 0ཞ3 is divided into 4 levels. The scale has a cumulative total score of 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating greater sleep disturbance. In our study, we calculated a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.886 for the PSQI.

Finnish Diabetes Risk Score (FINDRISC)

This study employed FINDRISC to assess the pre-pregnancy type 2 diabetes risk status of pregnant women. FINDRISC was developed by Lindström et al. [22] based on a 10-year cohort study initiated in 1987 for diabetes screening. It underwent external validation during the latter five years of the study and was subsequently refined by Saaristo [23]. Currently, the FINDRISC diabetes risk score incorporates age, BMI, waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), blood pressure, family history of diabetes, prior history of hyperglycemia, and lifestyle, dietary, and exercise habits. Scores range from 0 to 26 points. Participants were instructed to complete the questionnaire based on their pre-pregnancy physical condition (e.g., weight, waist circumference, exercise habits), aiming to obtain a baseline diabetes risk score. The IDF Diabetes Atlas (10th Edition) explicitly references FINDRISC as a type 2 diabetes risk assessment tool, using 15 points as the high-risk cutoff. In its original development and validation studies, the scale demonstrated high sensitivity (0.78–0.81) and specificity (0.76–0.77) for predicting type 2 diabetes onset within 10 years.

Pregnancy-Related Anxiety Questionnaire (PRAQ)

The Pregnancy-Related Anxiety Questionnaire (PRAQ) Chinese version was used to assess anxiety levels in pregnant women. The PRAQ was developed by Xiao Limin et al. and is applicable to Chinese pregnant women. The scale consists of 13 items in 3 dimensions: concern for self, concern for fetal health, and concern for delivery, and is based on a 4-point Likert scale, in which “1” means “no worry”, “2” means “occasional worry”, “3” means “frequent worries”, and “4” means “always worries”, and the score ranges from 13 to 52. A total score of < 24 was classified as no pregnancy-related anxiety; a total score of ≥ 24 was classified as pregnancy-related anxiety. In our study, we calculated a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.891 for the PRAQ.

Data collection

All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. Data were collected using a paper-based, face-to-face survey approach under strict confidentiality. Language clarifications were provided for items that participants found difficult to understand. For items that could not be self-administered, the researcher explained each item individually, the participant responded verbally, and the researcher recorded the corresponding response. The survey process took approximately 30 min per participant.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 27.0 and AMOS 28.0 software. Measurement data that followed a normal distribution were described by mean ± standard deviation (SD), and comparisons between groups were performed using t-tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Pearson correlation analysis was used to explore the associations between variables. A structural equation model (SEM) was established using AMOS software to test the mediating role of sleep quality between the type 2 diabetes risk score and pregnancy-related anxiety. Parameter estimation of the model was conducted using the maximum likelihood method.

Before modeling, normality tests were performed on all continuous variables (Shapiro-Wilk test), and multicollinearity was evaluated using the variance inflation factor (VIF). All VIF values were less than 5, indicating that the problem of multicollinearity was not severe. For the small amount of missing data (less than 2%) in the study, the complete information maximum likelihood method was used for processing. This method can effectively utilize all available information within the framework of the structural equation model. The significance of the mediating effect was tested using the Bootstrap method (repeated sampling 5000 times). All statistical tests were two-tailed, and differences were considered statistically significant if P < 0.05.

Results

General demographic information

Among the 469 pregnant women who met the criteria included in this study, 19.6% were under 25 years of age, 11.3% were unemployed by occupation, 19.8% were of ethnic groups other than Han Chinese, 34.5% were in early pregnancy, 86.8% were married, 21.5% were educated to bachelor’s degree or above, 61.8% were primigravida, and 21.5% had a family history. In addition, the incidences of personal history and adverse pregnancy and childbirth history were 37.1% and 33.5% respectively. Among those experiencing early pregnancy symptoms, 50.5% of the people were reported to have early pregnancy symptoms.

Most pregnant women experience pregnancy-related anxiety (79.7%), with 9.6% suffering from severe sleep disorders and 25.4% scoring high on the type 2 diabetes risk assessment (Table 1).

Correlation analysis of PSQI, PRAQ, and FINDRISC scores of pregnant women

Pearson correlation analysis revealed that type 2 diabetes risk scores (FINDRISC) were positively associated with greater sleep disturbance (higher PSQI scores; r = 0.423, P < 0.01). Furthermore, type 2 diabetes risk scores were positively associated with pregnancy-related anxiety (r = 0.428, P < 0.01), supporting the first research hypothesis that higher type 2 diabetes risk scores may be associated with increased levels of pregnancy-related anxiety. Greater sleep disturbance (higher PSQI scores) was also positively associated with pregnancy-related anxiety (r = 0.340, P < 0.01), supporting the second research hypothesis that poorer sleep quality may be linked to higher anxiety levels during pregnancy (Table 3).

Discussion

Sleep quality partially mediated the association between type 2 diabetes risk scores and pregnancy-related anxiety in pregnant women. This finding suggests that among Chinese pregnant women, improved sleep quality may be associated with reduced pregnancy-related anxiety, and this association may be more pronounced when considered alongside factors related to type 2 diabetes risk. These findings enhance our understanding of the multifactorial etiology of pregnancy-related anxiety by linking objective metabolic risk indicators with subjective sleep perceptions.

The results of this study indicate that anxiety is prevalent among pregnant women, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies [25]. Pregnancy-related anxiety may arise from multidimensional psychological concerns: fear of pain or unexpected complications during delivery may constitute an important source of anxiety; concern about possible fetal abnormalities may significantly contribute to the psychological burden on pregnant women; and worry about changes in physical appearance during and after pregnancy may also be associated with elevated anxiety levels [26]. The study found differences in anxiety levels among pregnant women of different age groups, with the older they were, the higher their levels of pregnancy-related anxiety. This is consistent with the findings of previous studies [27]. During the COVID-19 period, age was similarly associated with maternal anxiety and depression [28]. Several explanations may account for this pattern. First, older pregnant women face greater health risks, including an increased likelihood of pregnancy complications, which may contribute to concerns about their own safety and that of the fetus. Second, concerns about declining fertility or missing the optimal childbearing window may intensify with advancing age. Third, heightened societal and familial expectations regarding older-age pregnancies may impose additional psychological burdens. Anxiety levels also appeared to vary significantly across gestational stages. Late-pregnant women showed a more pronounced tendency toward anxiety compared with those in early and mid-pregnancy, consistent with a 2017 Brazilian study [5]. Additionally, primiparous women were more likely to experience anxiety than multiparous women [29], a finding further confirmed by Japanese scholars [30], and may stem from the fear of the unknown due to the lack of experience in childbirth, psychological stress triggered by the role transition and insufficient knowledge of parenting, and the mediating effect of the differences in the social support system on anxiety. However, some studies have also shown no difference in state anxiety and trait anxiety between primiparous and multiparous women [31]. Therefore, future exploration needs to be continued and deepened through longitudinal follow-up as well as intervention studies. Finally, maternal anxiety was significantly higher among those with a history of adverse maternal outcomes than those without a history of infertility, which is consistent with previous findings [32]. History of adverse pregnancy and childbirth, overdue labor, stillbirth, stillbirth, neonatal death, birth defects, and hyperemesis gravidarum. Pre-existing adverse experiences may trigger trauma, leading pregnant women to form negative beliefs about their fertility by doubting their fertility.

Consistent with previous studies, the risk score for type 2 diabetes is positively correlated with pregnancy-related anxiety [33, 34]. The elevated health risks associated with diabetes may contribute to concerns about maternal and fetal well-being, potentially intensifying anxiety during this physiologically demanding period. Therefore, in clinical practice and prenatal care, comprehensive assessment of diabetes risk, close monitoring of anxiety levels, and targeted psychological counseling and health management may be warranted to support the health of both mother and infant.

Greater sleep disturbance (higher PSQI scores) was positively associated with pregnancy-related anxiety. Multiple international studies have similarly demonstrated a significant association between anxiety and sleep quality [35], and this association has also been observed in specific populations, including adolescents, patients with advanced lung cancer, and those undergoing hemodialysis [36–38]. Declining sleep quality during pregnancy may be associated with depletion of emotional regulation capacity, potentially contributing to excessive worry regarding physical changes, fetal health, and birth outcomes. The underlying mechanism may be related to the influence of sleep on connectivity between limbic structures involved in emotional arousal and prefrontal regions responsible for emotional regulation, as well as the potential role of rapid eye movement (REM) sleep in processing emotional experiences [39].

Furthermore, the results of this study indicate that the quality of sleep in pregnant women has a mediating effect on the relationship between the risk score of type 2 diabetes and pregnancy-related anxiety. Within the biopsychosocial model framework, several pathways may account for this mediation. From a biological perspective, the elevated metabolic risk reflected by higher FINDRISC scores may be associated with dysregulation of the neuroendocrine system, potentially contributing to imbalances in stress hormone secretion (e.g., cortisol) that may interfere with normal metabolic functions. Good sleep quality may help regulate neuroendocrine rhythms and may contribute to restoring physiological balance, thereby potentially buffering the adverse biological effects associated with elevated diabetes risk. From a psychological perspective, concern about type 2 diabetes risk, as a psychological stressor, may contribute to negative cognition and emotional experiences, potentially associated with pregnancy-related anxiety. High-quality sleep may be associated with greater psychological stability, enhanced emotional regulation, and lower diabetes-related anxiety [40]. At the social level, diabetes risk scores may reflect not only metabolic status but also socioeconomic conditions, health literacy, and living environment. Low social support, high life stress, and unhealthy dietary and exercise habits may be associated with both higher diabetes risk scores and sleep disturbances [41]. Based on these findings, several clinical implications may be considered. Planned aerobic exercise may improve sleep quality in pregnant women [42].Therefore, during pregnancy, one can engage in aerobic exercises such as walking, swimming, and yoga [43]. In addition, European and American guidelines emphasize cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia [44] (CBT-I) as a first-line treatment for sleep and related problems, which can effectively treat insomnia. As it is more acceptable to pregnant women than other pharmacological acupuncture treatments, future studies should conduct high-quality randomized controlled trials to investigate interventions for sleep quality problems in pregnancy and apply proven intervention protocols to standard gynecological nursing practice.

However, this cross-sectional design makes it difficult to determine the causal relationship and direction between the variables. For instance, there may be a bidirectional relationship between sleep and anxiety [45]. Future research should conduct longitudinal tracking while controlling for specific confounding factors during pregnancy to clarify the temporal sequence and mechanism path between diabetes risk, sleep quality, and anxiety; at the same time, it is recommended to design intervention studies, such as sleep improvement programs based on cognitive behavioral therapy or regular exercise programs, to test their actual effectiveness in reducing diabetes-related anxiety, thereby providing more solid evidence for the connection between biological mechanisms and clinical interventions.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences, and all measurements relied on self-report instruments, which may have introduced recall bias. Second, the possibility of residual confounding factors cannot be excluded, and findings should be interpreted with caution. Third, the use of convenience sampling may limit the representativeness of the sample, and generalizability to other populations may be restricted. Future longitudinal studies are needed to more thoroughly examine the temporal sequence and potential associations among these relationships.

Conclusion

This study found a significant association between sleep quality in pregnant women, risk of type 2 diabetes, and pregnancy-related anxiety. Sleep quality partially mediated the relationship between type 2 diabetes risk and pregnancy-related anxiety. This suggests that sleep may be a key factor in understanding the link between metabolic risks and mental health during pregnancy. These findings underscore the importance of comprehensively assessing metabolic factors and sleep-related factors in prenatal care.
