Associations between dietary factors and serum uric acid levels in Korean women aged 19–65 years
Authors: Inji Lee, Sebin Choi, Hyunjung Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND/OBJECTIVESHyperuricemia is associated with an increased risk of chronic diseases such as hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and chronic kidney disease. This study investigated the dietary factors associated with the serum uric acid (SUA) levels in Korean women.SUBJECTS/METHODSThis study analyzed the data from the 2016–2017 Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, including 4,201 women aged 19–64 yrs. The dietary intake was assessed using a single 24-h dietary recall, and the SUA levels were categorized into quartiles. Multivariate linear regression analyses were conducted to examine the associations between the nutrient and food group intake and the SUA levels after adjusting for potential confounders.RESULTSHigher intakes of saturated fatty acids (SFA) and monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFA) were significantly associated with elevated SUA levels, whereas carbohydrate intake had a significant inverse association with the SUA levels. Dietary fiber intake was lower in the highest SUA quartile than in the lowest quartile, but dietary fiber was not independently associated with the SUA levels after a full adjustment. At the food group level, higher intakes of meat and high-purine seafood were positively associated with the SUA levels, whereas the grain intake showed an inverse association.CONCLUSIONHigher SUA levels may be associated with dietary patterns characterized by an increased intake of fats, particularly SFA and MUFA derived from animal-based foods, rather than the isolated effects of individual fatty acids. Carbohydrate- and grain-based dietary components may play a protective role in uric acid metabolism. Considering the link between elevated SUA and the chronic disease risk, dietary management focusing on overall dietary patterns rather than single nutrients may be an important preventive approach. Further prospective cohort studies will be needed to confirm the causal relationships between diet and the SUA levels in the general Korean population.

INTRODUCTION

Uric acid is the final product of purine metabolism and functions as an antioxidant under normal physiological conditions [12]. Hyperuricemia, defined as a serum uric acid (SUA) level ≥ 7.0 mg/dL in men and ≥ 6.0 mg/dL in women, is recognized as a precursor of gout and as an important risk factor and potential mediator for various cardiometabolic diseases, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, metabolic syndrome (MetS), and chronic kidney disease (CKD) [34]. In recent years, the prevalence of hyperuricemia has been increasing worldwide, exceeding 10% in many Asian countries such as China, Japan, and South Korea, and surpassing 20% in some Western countries, including the United States and Ireland [5].

In particular, elevated SUA levels in women have shown stronger associations with the risk of chronic diseases such as diabetes and MetS than in men. In South Korea, the prevalence of hyperuricemia among women has steadily increased from 2.2% in 2004 to 4.1% during 2008–2010, and 5.9% in 2016 [6].

Elevated SUA levels can result from increased production, decreased excretion, or both. Various factors, including a genetic predisposition, medical conditions, medication use, and dietary habits, influence the SUA levels [3]. In particular, dietary factors play a significant role in the development of hyperuricemia through purine intake and renal uric acid metabolism [7]. Previous studies have reported that a high consumption of alcohol, red meat, and seafood is associated with increased SUA levels [8]. More recently, certain foods such as soy products and fructose-containing foods are significantly associated with higher SUA levels, whereas some nutrients, including vitamin C, folate, calcium, and dietary fiber, are associated with lower SUA levels [89].

Although many studies have examined the relationship between dietary factors and SUA levels, most focused on individual nutrients or single food groups [101112131415]. Comprehensive analyses based on overall food group consumption are limited, and despite the potential differences from national dietary patterns and cultural backgrounds, research targeting the Korean population remains insufficient. In addition, previous evidence suggests that while genetic and physiological factors, such as muscle mass, sex hormones, and body mass index (BMI), as well as lifestyle behaviors like smoking and alcohol consumption, have a stronger impact on the SUA levels in men, one study reported that, in women, environmental factors, such as dietary and nutrient intake, are relatively more strongly reflected in changes in SUA levels [16].

Therefore, this study examined the associations between nutrient intake, food group consumption, and SUA levels among Korean adult women using data from the Seventh Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES VII). In particular, unlike previous studies, this study also included analyses of purine-related food groups to identify their direct relationship with the SUA levels.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

Data collection and source

This study examined the data from the KNHANES VII spanning 2016–2017. The KNHANES has been continuously conducted since 2007 and consists of three main components: a health interview, a health examination, and a nutrition survey.

The KNHANES VII was conducted in such a way that experienced investigators were required to report all food and beverages consumed the day before the survey through questionnaires based on memory. The nutrition survey, conducted by trained professionals using structured interviews, assessed the dietary behaviors, food intake frequency, and overall dietary intake. Anthropometric measurements, blood pressure assessments, and biochemical tests were performed at mobile examination centers. Further details regarding the study design and data collection methods can be found in previous publications [17].

Participant selection

The 2016–2017 dataset initially included 16,277 participants. Of these, 6,446 individuals who were not within the target age range of 19 to 64 yrs were excluded. Seven hundred and sixty-eight participants with missing SUA levels or incomplete nutritional data were also excluded to ensure data completeness. Participants with medical conditions that could influence the SUA levels, including cancer, hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and CKD requiring dialysis, were also excluded (n = 332). Participants who reported daily energy intakes below 500 kcal or above 5,000 kcal were also excluded (n = 1,507). After applying these criteria and also excluding male participants (n = 3,023), 4,201 participants were included in the final analysis.

Quartile grouping by SUA levels

The SUA levels were categorized into four quartile groups, from the lowest (Q1) to the highest (Q4).

This study included 4,201 participants. The quartile ranges and the number of participants in each group were as follows: Q1 (0.9–3.6 mg/dL, n = 976), Q2 (3.7–4.2 mg/dL, n = 1,131), Q3 (4.3–4.8 mg/dL, n = 1,043), and Q4 (4.9–8.9 mg/dL, n = 1,051).

Anthropometric and biochemical measurements

Anthropometric measurements were conducted using standardized protocols. The anthropometric variables included height, weight, BMI, and waist circumference. The participants’ height and weight were measured while wearing light clothing and without shoes. The BMI was calculated as the weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2). The waist circumference was measured at the midpoint between the iliac crest and the lower rib.

The blood pressure was measured three times after the participants had rested in a seated position for 10 min, and the average of these measurements was used for analysis. The blood samples were collected after at least 8 h of fasting. The SUA levels were measured using blood samples collected after at least 8 h of fasting, using the colorimetric method. The analysis was conducted using the same automated analyzer as used for other biochemical tests (Hitachi 7600; Hitachi Instruments Inc., Tokyo, Japan) to ensure precision.

Dietary evaluation

The KNHANES VII includes various nutrition-related components, allowing for a comprehensive understanding of participants’ dietary intake. This was assessed via a 24-h dietary recall (24HR) administered by trained interviewers.

The 24HR method provides detailed information on the types and amounts of foods consumed on the day before the survey, offering a near-real-time reflection of the participants’ dietary behavior. The collected dietary data were processed using the official food composition database maintained by the Korea National Institute of Health. The nutrient intake was calculated as the total daily intake per individual based on all reported foods and food groups.

In addition, the adequacy of nutrient intake was evaluated using the 2015 Dietary Reference Intakes for Koreans (KDRIs) established by the Ministry of Health and Welfare. The assessed nutrient components included the energy (kcal), carbohydrates, protein, fats, including saturated fatty acids (SFA), monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFA), and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA), as well as sugars, dietary fiber, and various vitamins.

Categorization of food groups with potential impact on the SUA levels

Foods associated with the SUA levels were categorized based on their nutritional characteristics and evidence from previous studies. High-purine foods included soybean products (soybeans, tofu, and soy milk), high-purine vegetables (e.g., broccoli, asparagus, spinach, peas, and dried mushrooms, excluding cauliflower), and high-purine seafood (fatty fish, including mackerel, saury, sardines, herring, Spanish mackerel, tuna, eel, salmon, smoked salmon, amberjack, and dried anchovies; shellfish, including dried mussels, crabs, lobsters, oysters, and dried shrimp; and seaweed, including dried shoulder and dried kelp) [18]. In addition, caffeinated drinks (coffee, green tea, and black tea), although negligible in purine content, were included as potential modifiers of the SUA levels.

Covariates

Variables including residential region, household income, education level, BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and physical activity were considered when assessing the sociodemographic and lifestyle covariates.

The residential region was divided into urban and rural areas based on administrative classification. The household income level was classified into three categories: ‘lowest to low,’ ‘middle,’ and ‘middle-high to high.’ The education level was categorized into three groups: ‘middle school or less,’ ‘high school graduate,’ and ‘college graduate or higher.’ The BMI was categorized into five groups based on World Health Organization criteria for Asian populations: underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–22.9 kg/m2), overweight (23.0–24.9 kg/m2), obese class I (25.0–29.9 kg/m2), and obese class II (≥ 30.0 kg/m2) [19]. The smoking status was classified into three categories: never smoked, past smoker, and current smoker [20]. Alcohol consumption was categorized based on reported frequency into three groups: ≤ 1 time per month, 2–4 times per month, and 2–4 times per week [21]. The physical activity level was assessed based on self-reported frequency and intensity of activity and was categorized into three groups: inactive, active, and health-enhancing [22].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using the PROC SURVEY procedures in SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA), based on data from the KNHANES VII. Because the survey used a stratified cluster sampling design rather than simple random sampling, complex survey weights, stratification variables, and primary sampling units were applied to ensure national representativeness.

The continuous variables were presented as the means ± SE using the PROC SURVEYMEANS procedure, while the categorical variables were reported as frequencies (n) and percentages (%) using PROC SURVEYFREQ. The differences between groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance for continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables. Bonferroni post-hoc tests were used to adjust for multiple comparisons. A two-tailed P-value of < 0.05 was considered significant.

The trends in nutrient and food group intake across SUA quartiles were evaluated using the P-value for the trend. The association between the SUA levels and dietary factors was analyzed using linear regression analysis. Model 1 included adjustments for age and total energy intake, while Model 2 added adjustments for the BMI, alcohol consumption, physical activity, education level, household income, smoking status, and residential area.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics according to SUA quartiles

Among the 4,201 participants, significant differences in demographic and lifestyle characteristics were observed across SUA quartiles (Table 1). The participants in Q4 were significantly younger than those in Q1 (41.5 ± 0.5 vs. 43.4 ± 0.5 yrs, P = 0.0003), and the proportion of rural residents was higher in Q4 (31.3%) than in Q1 (22.1%) (P = 0.0278). The prevalence of obese class II (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) increased progressively across quartiles, from 11.3% in Q1 to 44.9% in Q4 (P < 0.0001). The proportion of current smokers also increased from 11.9% in Q1 to 35.6% in Q4 (P = 0.0003), and the frequency of alcohol consumption was higher in Q4 than in Q1 (P < 0.0001). The physical activity level showed significant differences across quartiles (P = 0.0274), whereas the household income and education level did not differ significantly.

Anthropometric and biochemical parameters across SUA quartiles

The anthropometric and biochemical characteristics varied across SUA quartiles (Table 2). The body weight was higher in Q4 than in Q1 (61.5 ± 0.4 vs. 56.2 ± 0.4 kg, P < 0.0001), as were the waist circumference (80.5 ± 0.4 vs. 75.5 ± 0.4 cm, P < 0.0001) and BMI (24.5 ± 0.1 vs. 22.2 ± 0.1 kg/m2, P < 0.0001). The systolic blood pressure was 115.3 ± 0.5 mmHg in Q4 and 111.4 ± 0.5 mmHg in Q1, and the diastolic blood pressure was 75.9 ± 0.3 mmHg in Q4 and 72.5 ± 0.3 mmHg in Q1 (P < 0.0001 for both). The total cholesterol (202.8 ± 1.4 vs. 191.9 ± 1.4 mg/dL, P < 0.0001) and triglycerides (132.9 ± 3.5 vs. 95.5 ± 2.0 mg/dL, P < 0.0001) were all higher in Q4 than in Q1. The aspartic transaminase and alanine transaminase levels were also higher in Q4 (22.0 ± 0.3 and 21.1 ± 0.6 U/L) than in Q1 (19.2 ± 0.3 and 15.6 ± 0.3 U/L, P < 0.0001 for both). The creatinine levels differed significantly among the groups (P < 0.0001), but the mean values were similar across all groups (approximately 0.7 mg/dL), indicating minimal absolute differences. The estimated glomerular filtration rate was lower in Q4 (99.8 ± 0.4 mL/min/1.73 m2) than in Q1 (105.9 ± 0.3 mL/min/1.73 m2, P < 0.0001), and the high-sensitivity C-reactive protein was higher in Q4 (1.2 ± 0.0 mg/L) than in Q1 (0.8 ± 0.0 mg/L, P < 0.0001).

Nutrient intake across SUA quartiles

Table 3 lists the average daily nutrient intake across SUA quartiles. The carbohydrate intake was significantly lower in Q4 (259.0 ± 110.1 g) than in Q1 (276.5 ± 108.1 g, P < 0.0001). The SFA intake showed a significant increasing trend from Q1 to Q4 (P = 0.0041), and the MUFA intake showed a similar increasing trend (P = 0.0141). The dietary fiber intake was lower in Q4 (21.7 ± 12.8 g) than in Q1 (23.6 ± 14.0 g, P = 0.0178). No significant differences across quartiles were observed in the protein, calcium, phosphorus, iron, sodium, or vitamin C intake.

Food group intake across SUA quartiles

The food group intake varied across SUA quartiles (Table 4). The white rice intake tended to decrease from Q1 to Q4 (P = 0.0002). In contrast, intake of meat and processed meats increased across increasing SUA quartiles (P = 0.0017). Unsweetened beverage and alcohol intakes were progressively higher as the SUA levels increased (P = 0.0123 and P = 0.0109, respectively). In addition, caffeinated drink intake was higher in the upper SUA quartiles, showing an overall increasing pattern from Q1 to Q4 (P = 0.0080). In contrast, the intakes of whole grains, wheat/bread/noodles, potatoes, beans, nuts, vegetables, kimchi, mushrooms, fruits, eggs, fish/shellfish/seaweed, dairy products, soda or sugar-sweetened beverages, seasonings, oils, sugars, and processed foods were similar across quartiles. Among the high-purine food groups, the intake of soy products, high-purine vegetables, and high-purine seafood also did not differ significantly.

Multivariable associations of nutrient and food group intake with SUA levels

Fig. 1 presents the results of linear regression analyses assessing the associations between the nutrient intake and SUA levels. The carbohydrate intake was negatively associated with the SUA levels, with significant results in Model 1 (P = 0.009) and Model 2 (P = 0.002). By contrast, positive associations were observed for fat intake. SFA was significantly associated with higher SUA levels in Model 2 (P = 0.023), and MUFA intake showed a positive association in Model 2 (P = 0.007). Therefore, although the carbohydrate intake may be linked to lower SUA concentrations, higher intake of saturated and monounsaturated fats may contribute to increased SUA levels.

DISCUSSION

This study examined the associations between dietary factors and the SUA concentrations in Korean women using the data from the KNHANES VII. The findings indicate associations at both the nutrient and food group levels. In particular, the carbohydrate intake showed an inverse association with the SUA concentrations, whereas the SFA and MUFA intakes showed a positive association. At the food group level, grains, meat, and high-purine seafood showed differing relationships with the SUA levels.

At the nutrient level, higher carbohydrate intake showed a significant inverse trend with the SUA concentrations. This observation is consistent with previous studies reporting that dietary patterns characterized by higher intake of complex carbohydrates are often associated with lower SUA levels, potentially reflecting the differences in the metabolic profiles or accompanying dietary characteristics [2324]. In contrast, higher SFA and MUFA intakes were positively associated with the SUA levels. These relationships remained statistically significant in multivariate regression analyses after adjusting for age, energy intake, body mass index, and lifestyle-related covariates.

SFA and MUFA are dietary fatty acids that previous studies have examined in relation to various metabolic conditions, including insulin resistance [252627]. Despite this, evidence on the association between MUFA intake and SUA levels has been inconsistent, making it difficult to attribute the observed associations with the independent physiological effects of MUFA. In this context, the positive association between the MUFA intake and SUA observed in the present study may reflect the broader dietary patterns characterized by higher overall fat intake rather than a specific effect of MUFA itself. Regarding the dietary fiber intake, the analyses based on the SUA quartiles showed that fiber intake tended to be lower in the highest quartile than in the lowest quartile, with a significant difference between these groups. By contrast, linear regression analyses showed that the dietary fiber intake was not independently associated with the SUA concentrations. Therefore, dietary fiber intake may reflect broader dietary composition rather than directly determine the SUA levels. Specifically, lower fiber intake observed among the individuals with higher SUA levels may indicate reduced grain and carbohydrate consumption and a dietary pattern characterized by relatively higher fat intake.

Food group analyses further supported these patterns. The grain intake, including bread and noodles, showed an inverse association with the SUA concentrations, whereas the intakes of meat, eggs, and seafood were positively associated with the SUA levels. In particular, the intake of high-purine seafood remained positively associated with the SUA concentrations even after full multivariate adjustment, consistent with previous findings that seafood rich in hypoxanthine is often linked to higher SUA levels [282930]. These results suggest that the metabolic responses to purine-containing foods may differ according to the dietary source, highlighting heterogeneity between purines derived from plant-based foods and those from seafood.

Although unsweetened beverages and caffeinated drinks have shown increasing trends across SUA quartiles, linear regression analyses did not reveal significant associations. This lack of association may be related to the relatively low average intake of these beverages among the participants or to attenuation of their relationships after adjustment for other dietary and lifestyle factors.

This study has several strengths. First, it used nationally representative data from Korean adult women, enhancing the generalizability of the findings. Second, it comprehensively assessed the associations between the SUA levels and a wide range of nutrients, food groups, and high-purine foods. Third, the study effectively adjusted for potential confounders using multivariable regression models, improving the robustness of the observed associations.

Nevertheless, certain limitations should be acknowledged. Dietary intake in this study was assessed using a single 24HR from the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, which reflects food consumption on the previous day and may not adequately represent habitual or long-term dietary intake of individuals. Such day-to-day variability may have introduced random measurement error, potentially attenuating the true associations between dietary factors and health-related indicators. Moreover, causal inferences regarding the relationship between dietary factors and the SUA levels could not be established because of the cross-sectional study design.

Overall, these findings suggest that the SUA levels in Korean adult women are associated with purine-containing foods and the overall dietary patterns, including macronutrient composition and predominant food sources. Considering that elevated SUA levels have been linked to increased risks of hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, MetS, and CKD, dietary strategies aimed at improving the overall dietary quality may contribute to cardiometabolic risk reduction. Further large-scale prospective cohort studies are needed to clarify the temporal and causal relationships between dietary factors and SUA levels in the general Korean population.
