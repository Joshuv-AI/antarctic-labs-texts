Constructing medical student personas via social sensing data and entrustable professional activities (EPAs) framework: a multimodal thematic analysis approach
Authors: Chen Mengyu, Fan Dan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo construct precise learner personas for medical students by integrating social sensing data with the Entrustable Professional Activities (EPAs) framework, thereby informing targeted pedagogical strategies. The study aims to address the gap in integrating behavioral data with competency assessment for multidimensional learner characterization.MethodsPurposive sampling was employed to recruit 45 clinical interns (June–December 2024) based on data saturation principles. Data collection combined semi-structured interviews, social sensing technology (multimodal data from medical forums and clinical sensor arrays with AES-256 encryption for privacy protection), and EPA-based clinical observations. Thematic analysis via NVivo 12 Plus was used to extract features, with manual coding validating theoretical saturation after 45 interviews.ResultsA four-dimensional persona framework (learning attitude, clinical competence, social interaction, self-improvement awareness) was discovered, yielding five archetypes: Proactive Achievers (28.89%), Skilled Practitioners (20.00%), Social Collaborators (17.78%), Passive Followers (24.44%), and Ambiguous Improvers (8.89%). Intergroup differences in EPA-assessed clinical skills were significant (ANOVA, p < 0.05 for all dimensions).ConclusionThe multimodal framework enables data-driven learner characterization, providing evidence for personalized medical education. Future work will validate persona utility in educational interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07949-3.Keywords: Medical students, Learner personas, Social sensing, EPAs framework, Thematic analysis

Introduction

In medical education, competency-based frameworks like Entrustable Professional Activities (EPAs) have become central to evaluating clinical proficiency [1]. ten Cate O (2007) defined EPAs as “professional activities that can be entrusted to a trainee once sufficient competence is demonstrated,” forming the theoretical basis for competency-based assessment [2]. However, traditional EPA assessments often overlook dynamic learner behaviors, such as social interactions and emotional states in real-world settings. Recent work by Mador et al. (2021) identified distinct learner phenotypes in competency-based medical education (CBME), but these characterizations relied primarily on static performance metrics, missing opportunities to refine profiles through real-time behavioral data [3]. Social sensing technologies—defined as multimodal systems capturing digital traces (e.g., forum discussions, sensor-recorded clinical behaviors) with AI-driven analysis—offer a solution by enabling continuous monitoring of learner engagement [4, 5].

The integration of EPAs and social sensing addresses two key limitations: (1) EPAs lack granular data on learning processes [6], and (2) social sensing alone may not align with professional competency standards. By combining EPA-defined clinical benchmarks (e.g., diagnostic reasoning, patient communication) with social sensing-derived behavioral patterns, this study aims to construct multidimensional learner personas that bridge performance evaluation and contextualized behavior analysis.

Literature Gap: While prior studies have explored learner profiling [7, 8], none have systematically merged social sensing with EPAs to characterize how dynamic behaviors (e.g., online knowledge sharing, team collaboration) relate to EPA-assessed competencies. This gap hinders the development of personalized educational interventions that address both performance outcomes and learning process deficiencies.

Methods

Study design and sample

A mixed-methods design was employed, with purposive sampling used to recruit 45 fifth-year clinical interns (21–25 years old, mean = 23) from the Second Affiliated Hospital of Qiqihar Medical University (June–December 2024). Sampling ceased when theoretical saturation was reached (no new themes emerged after 42 interviews, verified by 3 additional interviews) [9]. Inclusion criteria: (1) active internship participation; (2) informed consent. Exclusions: (1) academic leave; (2) cognitive impairments affecting communication (justified by clinical practice requirements for safe patient care); (3) internship < 3 months.

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews

Trained interviewers (medical education researchers with ≥ 5 years experience) conducted 30–60-minute audio-recorded interviews covering three domains: personal attributes, learning platform usage, and clinical performance. Nonverbal behaviors were documented, and transcripts were verified with participants within 24 h. The interview guide (see Supplementary Material 1) was developed based on EPA competencies and social sensing literature.

Social sensing technology

Multimodal data were collected via

Digital traces were collected from the hospital-owned medical education platform (internal forum for clinical interns), which is managed by the institution’s Educational Administration Department. Data harvesting followed internal protocols, with PII removed via username anonymization and AES-256 encryption. As the platform is proprietary to the Second Affiliated Hospital of Qiqihar Medical University, no external platform authorization was required, and all data remained within the institution’s secure server infrastructure.

EPA-based assessments

Clinical instructors (≥ 10 years experience, certified in EPA evaluation) used ten Cate’s EPA framework [2] to assess interns across six core EPAs: history taking, physical examination, diagnostic reasoning, treatment planning, patient communication, and team collaboration. Weekly observations were recorded in structured checklists during rounds and simulated consultations. Inter-rater reliability for EPA assessments was excellent (Kappa coefficient = 0.82, p < 0.001). The EPA scoring in this study uses a 1–10 global rating scale (Supplementary Material 2) to assess overall performance in clinical scenarios, without mapping to specific milestones or entrustment decision levels (e.g., “supervision required” vs. “independent practice”) as recommended in ten Cate’s framework [2]. This simplified approach was adopted for feasibility in high-frequency internship assessments but Limits direct comparability with EPA programs using milestone-linked scales.The full EPA assessment rubric and checklist are provided in Supplementary Material 2.

Data analysis

Thematic analysis

NVivo 12 Plus was used to code interview transcripts, social media data, and EPA records following Colaizzi’s seven-step method [9]. Researchers iteratively identified phenomenological statements (e.g., “initiates independent literature review”) and grouped them into factual labels (e.g., “self-directed learning”). Semantically congruent labels were synthesized into four dimensions: learning attitude, clinical competence, social interaction, and self-improvement awareness. Data normality was confirmed via the Shapiro-Wilk test (all p > 0.05), and homogeneity of variances was verified by Levene’s test (all p > 0.05), justifying the use of ANOVA and Tukey post-hoc tests for statistical inference.

Persona construction

Manual feature extraction integrated thematic codes with EPA scores to define five archetypes. To derive these personas, k-means clustering with Euclidean distance was employed, with the optimal number of clusters (k = 5) determined by the elbow method and validated using a silhouette coefficient (0.71, indicating good cluster separation). For example, “Proactive Achievers” were characterized by high EPA scores in diagnostic reasoning, frequent online knowledge sharing, and self-reported goal-setting. Personas were validated by cross-referencing with clinical supervisors’ evaluations. K-means clustering with Euclidean distance was used to derive personas, with k = 5 determined via the elbow method and silhouette coefficient (0.71, indicating good cluster separation; values > 0.5 suggest reasonable clustering.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the institution’s Ethics Committee (Lun Jiao Review (2023) 02 No. 4). All participants provided written consent, and data collection from the internal medical education platform adhered to institutional guidelines for educational research. Since the platform is owned and managed by the hospital, no external data sharing or third-party authorization was required. Anonymized data were stored on HIPAA-compliant servers, with access restricted to research team members.

Four-dimensional persona framework

Thematic analysis identified a four-dimensional system: Learning Attitude, Clinical Competence, Social Interaction, and Self-Improvement Awareness. This framework was derived from 128 coded behavioral indicators, integrating EPA-assessed competencies (e.g., diagnostic reasoning) and social sensing data (e.g., forum engagement). Theoretical saturation was confirmed after 45 interviews, with no new themes emerging beyond the 42nd participant.

Five learner archetypes

Cluster analysis of dimensional scores yielded five distinct personas, each characterized by unique EPA performance profiles and behavioral patterns. Detailed comparisons, including full EPA scores across all six dimensions, are provided in Table 1 (expanded) and Supplementary Material 3 (Fig. 1).

EPA data integration in persona construction

EPA assessment data directly shaped the persona definitions, with each archetype demonstrating distinct patterns in competency attainment. Proactive Achievers consistently met or exceeded EPA benchmarks in diagnostic reasoning (9.2 ± 0.4) and treatment planning (8.9 ± 0.5), with all members of this group demonstrating independent case management capabilities. Skilled Practitioners, conversely, showed proficiency in EPA2 (Physical Examination, 8.8 ± 0.3), with strengths in operational sub-dimensions (e.g., standardized examination techniques) [10], but scored 15–20% lower in theoretical dimensions Like differential diagnosis, highlighting a gap between practical and conceptual competence. Ambiguous Improvers, the smallest subgroup, failed to meet basic EPA standards across all dimensions, with 75% requiring supervisor intervention during routine clinical tasks, indicating foundational deficits in multiple competency areas [11]. These findings underscore the utility of integrating EPA data into persona construction, as it enables precise identification of both strengths and performance gaps within learner groups [12].

Discussion

Heterogeneity in learner personas and EPA performance

The five identified personas—Proactive Achievers, Skilled Practitioners, Social Collaborators, Passive Followers, and Ambiguous Improvers—exhibit distinct patterns in EPA-assessed competencies and social sensing-derived behaviors. Proactive Achievers (28.89%) demonstrated superior performance in diagnostic reasoning (9.2 ± 0.4) and treatment planning (8.9 ± 0.5), aligning with Mador et al.’s (2021) description of “high-achieving phenotypes” in CBME. However, this study extends prior work by integrating real-time social sensing data (e.g.,3 + hours daily on learning platforms), revealing how online knowledge sharing correlates with clinical competence [13]. Conversely, Passive Followers (24.44%) showed consistent underperformance in basic EPAs (history taking: 5.5 ± 0.6), which may reflect gaps in self-directed learning—an identified limitation of traditional EPA assessments [14]. The small Ambiguous Improvers subgroup (8.89%) highlights the need for targeted interventions, as their low scores in all EPA dimensions (mean = 4.8 ± 0.5) suggest foundational skill deficits that require personalized support. Notably, the Ambiguous Improvers subgroup (n = 4) had limited statistical power, which may affect the generalizability of their low EPA scores (mean = 4.8 ± 0.5). This highlights the need for targeted sampling in future research to robustly characterize this learner type [15, 16].

Theoretical implications for EPA-Integrated profiling

This study bridges two key theories: (1) ten Cate’s EPA framework, which provides objective competency benchmarks, and (2) social sensing literature, which captures contextual learning behaviors. The integration addresses a critical gap: while EPAs evaluate “what learners can do,” social sensing reveals “how they do it” [17, 18]. For example, Skilled Practitioners (20.00%) achieved high procedural EPA scores (physical examination: 8.8 ± 0.3) but lacked theoretical engagement, a pattern undetected by traditional EPA reviews alone[19]. From a design thinking perspective, these personas function as “goal-directed archetypes,” enabling educators to anticipate learner needs. Social Collaborators (17.78%), for instance, excel in team communication EPAs (8.7 ± 0.4) but require structured knowledge consolidation to align with competency standards. This aligns with recent calls to merge EPA frameworks with learner-centered design [20–23]. Holmboe et al. (2017) noted that traditional EPA assessments often focus on summative outcomes, lacking granular data on learning processes—a gap addressed by integrating social sensing in this study [6]. Mador et al. (2021) identified learner phenotypes using static performance data, whereas our framework advances this by linking dynamic behaviors (via social sensing) to EPA outcomes [3].

Practical applications for medical education

The persona framework offers tangible educational strategies tailored to each learner type [24]. For Proactive Achievers, advanced EPA challenges such as complex case simulations are recommended to satisfy their self-directed learning tendencies, while Passive Followers may benefit from EPA-based gamification like badge systems for completing core EPAs to enhance engagement [25–27]. Ambiguous Improvers, meanwhile, would profit from mentorship tied to specific EPAs, such as weekly diagnostic reasoning workshops, to build foundational competencies [28]. These recommendations align with BMC Education’s focus on evidence-based interventions, as seen in integrating social sensing data into EPA feedback systems to enable real-time monitoring of learner engagement—an approach validated by Liu et al.’s (2025) work on social media profiling for academic support [12]. This integration not only enhances the personalization of medical education but also bridges behavioral analytics with competency assessment to foster targeted skill development [29–31].

Generalizability of the framework

The centralized platform in this study facilitated data collection, but many educational environments have fragmented systems. Adaptations include:

Multi-platform integration: Using API interfaces or standardized formats (e.g., xAPI) to aggregate data from LMS, clinical apps, and communication tools.

Alternative data sources: Supplementing with learning logs or instructor records if platform data are unavailable.

Simplified framework: Prioritizing EPA scores and key behavioral indicators (e.g., weekly study hours) in resource-limited settings.

Study limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged to contextualize the findings. The single-center sample (n = 45) may limit generalizability to diverse medical education systems, with the Ambiguous Improvers subgroup (n = 4, 8.89%) showed significant deficits in all EPA dimensions (mean = 4.8 ± 0.5), though this finding should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size [32]. The low sample size may lead to unstable statistical results, and future studies with larger samples (e.g., n ≥ 15) are needed to validate whether their performance gaps reflect true skill deficits or sampling variation. Additionally, the 6-month internship period constrains longitudinal analysis of EPA skill development over time. While six core EPAs were assessed to capture clinical competencies, future studies should incorporate all ten Cate-defined EPAs for comprehensive profiling. Finally, the personas have not yet been validated through educational interventions, which limits inferences about their practical utility in real-world training contexts [33, 34]. Recognizing these constraints highlights the need for multi-center, longitudinal research to enhance the framework’s applicability and validate its impact on medical education.The EPA assessment used a global 1–10 scale rather than entrustment decision levels (e.g., 5-level entrustment proposed by ten Cate, 2007), which may restrict generalizability to programs employing detailed milestone-based EPA evaluations. Future studies should integrate milestone mapping to enhance alignment with international EPA standards [35].

Future directions

Future research should prioritize several key directions to build upon these findings. Multi-center studies are essential to validate the generalizability of the identified personas across diverse medical education systems, while longitudinal designs will enable tracking of EPA performance trajectories throughout different internship stages. Additionally, developing integrated tools that merge EPA assessments with social sensing data could facilitate real-time learner feedback, enhancing the adaptability of educational interventions. Finally, testing persona-driven strategies—such as tailored EPA training modules—will be critical to measuring their tangible impact on educational outcomes and ensuring the framework’s practical utility in cultivating competent medical professionals. These steps will not only strengthen the scientific rigor of the approach but also bridge the gap between theoretical profiling and real-world educational application.

Conclusion

By merging social sensing data with EPA assessments, this study constructs robust learner personas that capture both competency outcomes and contextual behaviors. The framework addresses critical gaps in traditional EPA evaluations, offering a data-driven approach to personalized medical education. While validation in broader contexts is needed, these findings lay groundwork for integrating dynamic behavioral analytics into competency-based training systems [–].
