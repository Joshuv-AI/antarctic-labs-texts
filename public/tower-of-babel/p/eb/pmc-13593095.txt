Phenomenological study on decision fatigue among surrogate decision-makers for ICU patients
Authors: Yaoning Zhuang, Peipei Li, Ying Xu, Lisang Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Surrogate decision-makers often face multiple medical decisions under psychological and time pressures, leading to decision fatigue. Current research on decision fatigue in intensive care unit (ICU) surrogates focuses primarily on quantitative aspects, lacking an in-depth exploration of their experiences. This study aims to explore the experience of decision fatigue among surrogate decision-makers for ICU patients within the first 48 hours of ICU admission and to provide insights for clinical medical staff to implement decision support strategies and improve decision quality. This study employed purposive sampling, with sufficient samples being reached when no new themes emerged from the interviews, to select surrogate decision-makers of patients admitted to the ICU within 48 hours. Then, qualitative phenomenological research methods were employed for semi-structured interviews. Colaizzi method was used to analyze and refine the interview data. Ten surrogate decision-makers were included in this study. Surrogate decision-makers experienced decision fatigue during the early stages of ICU admission, characterized by 5 primary themes: emotional exhaustion, increased cognitive load, hesitation in decision-making, conflicting decisions, and inadequate support systems. Within the initial 48 hours in the ICU, surrogate decision-makers of patients may experience decision fatigue during the decision-making process. Medical staff should be aware of this phenomenon, take appropriate intervention measures, and provide decision-making support to enhance the quality of decisions.

1. Introduction

A surrogate decision-maker, also known as a healthcare proxy or agent, is typically the patient’s spouse, partner, adult child, or legal representative who can assume decision-making authority when the patient’s capacity to make informed medical decisions is compromised due to conditions that prevent effective communication of their wishes regarding medical choices.[1] Ideally, every adult should provide an unambiguous advance directive thoroughly discussed with their physician and communicated to all family members. However, only a small percentage of individuals have executed written directives, making surrogate decision-making a common practice in real-world scenarios.[2]

Patients in the intensive care unit (ICU) often face life-threatening situations that require prompt medical decisions.[3] The first 48 hours following ICU admission are clinically critical and complex, during which patients experience intricate and volatile conditions that render them unable to make decisions independently.[4] Consequently, surrogate decision-makers must make critical medical decisions on their behalf,[5] requiring them to assimilate large amounts of medical information under immense pressure and within a limited timeframe, increasing the risk of decision fatigue, a state characterized by diminished psychological resources and cognitive function due to the repetitive and high volume of decision-making demands.[6]

Decision fatigue refers to the phenomenon where surrogate decision-makers, under psychological stress, time pressure, and uncertainty about outcomes, make multiple medical decisions for patients. During this process, they consume a large amount of cognitive resources. As a result, their ability to make decisions and exercise control is impaired, leading to irrational decision-making behaviors such as impulsiveness or avoidance.[7] Decision fatigue not only compromises the quality of decisions made for patients but also endangers the physical and mental well-being of surrogate decision-makers.[8]

Previous studies have documented the high prevalence and multifactorial nature of decision fatigue. Internationally, research has primarily focused on developing assessment tools and targeted interventions, such as the Decision Fatigue Scale (DFS) by Hickman et al.[7] In China, emerging studies have begun to examine contributing factors among surrogate decision-makers, underscoring the urgent need for culturally tailored and innovative support strategies. However, despite increasing recognition and research on decision fatigue in China,[9] there remains a scarcity of comprehensive studies focusing on this issue within the context of ICU surrogate decision-making, particularly during the early stages of ICU admission.

This study aimed to explore the experiences of decision fatigue among ICU surrogate decision-makers during the initial admission period. Our purpose was to provide clinicians with essential insights to support the decision-making process and enhance the quality of critical care decisions.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Participants

This study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Studies to ensure transparency and replicability. The phenomenological Colaizzi method[10] was employed in this study. Phenomenology can deeply explore and understand the personal experiences of individuals in a specific context and allows for an in-depth investigation of lived experiences, revealing the essence of those experiences as perceived by the individuals themselves. To select patients treated in the ICU of a tertiary-level hospital for semi-structured interviews, we utilized a purposive sampling method. The sample size was determined by the theoretical saturation of surrogate decision-makers for ICU patients. Theoretical saturation refers to the point at which new interviews no longer yield new information or perspectives, indicating that the sample size is sufficient.[11] The inclusion criteria for patients were defined as individuals aged 18 years or older (considering the clinical applicability for ICU adults) and within 48 hours of their initial ICU admission. Conversely, patients who died or were transferred to another hospital during the initial admission were excluded. The inclusion criteria for surrogate decision-makers required them to be 18 years or older, the primary decision-maker for the patient’s treatment plan, capable of clear thinking and possessing good understanding and expression skills, and willing to participate in the study and provide relevant information. Exclusion criteria for surrogate decision-makers included having a mental illness or other conditions affecting decision-making ability and failure to complete the interview.

The study protocol was approved by the local Ethics Commission [approval no. 2022070-XZ01], and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

2.2. Data collection

The researchers collected data through face-to-face, semi-structured interviews in Mandarin Chinese. The researchers thoroughly reviewed relevant literature to formulate an interview outline. The outline included questions focused on the decision fatigue experienced by surrogate decision-makers, including the decision-making process, challenges faced, psychological and physical sensations, influencing factors, and available support systems. This outline was then tested through preinterviews with 2 research subjects, which allowed the researchers to refine and adjust the questions based on the feedback received, ultimately determining the final interview structure.[12–14]

The content of the interview outline focused on several critical areas: the typical thought processes and decision-making methods employed by participants when making treatment decisions for patients, with a detailed account of their decision-making process; the emotions and mental state experienced during the decision-making process; any difficulties or challenges encountered while making these decisions; any physical fatigue or discomfort felt when making decisions on behalf of family members, with detailed descriptions; other factors perceived to affect decision-making ability during the process; and the type of support and assistance desired during the decision-making process.

DFS was employed to assess the level of decision fatigue experienced by surrogate decision-makers during the initial admission phase of ICU patients.[7] The DFS is a validated scale designed to assess the degree of fatigue individuals encounter throughout the decision-making process, capturing various dimensions, including physical exhaustion, cognitive overload, and emotional fatigue. The scale consists of a series of questions designed to capture subjective experiences during decision-making, using a 4-point likert scale that ranges from “completely disagree” to “completely agree,” with scores ranging from 0 to 3. The total score is calculated by summing the individual item scores, where higher scores indicate greater levels of decision fatigue.

The interviews were conducted in the doctor’s duty room, chosen for its privacy and comfort. Prior to each session, researchers obtained informed consent from the participants and ensured that the interviews were recorded. Each interview lasted between 30 and 40 minutes, designed to thoroughly explore the participants’ experiences. This meticulous approach aimed to gather rich, detailed data to better understand the decision-making experiences of ICU surrogate decision-makers, ultimately contributing valuable insights to enhance decision-making support in critical care settings.

2.3. Data analysis

Two researchers (a Deputy Chief Nurse with a PhD in Nursing and a Bachelor-level Nursing Psychology graduate) independently coded the data. Coding was conducted using NVivo 14 Plus, following a double-blind independent coding – cross-validation process: dual-blind independent coding with strong inter-coder agreement (κ = 0.81); resolution of 28 discrepancies through a 3-tier consensus protocol: software flagging, anonymous review by a third methodology expert, and team-based contextual deliberation; and final thematic validation via respondent checking with 10 participants. Interviews were transcribed within 24 hours and further analyzed using Colaizzi 7-step method.[15] Each interview is conducted once, and the content of 1 or 2 interviews is analyzed. This rigorous approach began with thoroughly reading the transcripts to ensure comprehensive familiarity with the data. Meaningful statements were first identified and extracted from the transcripts. Recurring ideas were then coded to facilitate the aggregation of these codes into themes, from which detailed descriptions were derived to construct overarching themes. Finally, the extracted themes were returned to the participants for verification of their authenticity and accuracy. All procedures were performed in Mandarin Chinese.

3.1. Emotional exhaustion

3.1.1. Emotional stress

Surrogate decision-makers, faced with the fluctuating health conditions of patients, often experience negative emotional stress, such as anxiety, fear, and helplessness, during the decision-making process.[8] These intense emotions significantly impair their decision-making abilities, ultimately leading to decision fatigue. As expressed by participant T4, “When the doctor calls to ask for something, it is usually urgent, and we are terrified.” Similarly, participant T6 poignantly recounted, “We have not dared to sleep since last night, for fear that there will be problems inside (crying). We are always afraid and worried. We are afraid and nervous when the phone rings.” Such statements vividly illustrate the pervasive anxiety and relentless fear that besiege these individuals.

3.1.2. Numbness and fatigue

As the number of decisions increases during a patient’s ICU stay, surrogate decision-makers may experience decision fatigue, leading to a sense of numbness and physical and emotional exhaustion, which can negatively impact the quality of their decision-making. This condition can adversely affect the quality of their decisions. T1 articulated, “It is very tiring; he suffers, and so do we.” T4 echoed this sentiment: “Because my father is also thin, older, and in bad condition. But the doctor said that this time it was serious and he might not be able to recover on his own, so there was nothing we could do but proceed.” Similarly, T5 reflected, “Because it has been so long, my mom has been in the hospital for three months, so I am now a bit numb and just take it one step at a time.” These statements poignantly illustrate how the prolonged stress and relentless burden of caregiving erodes their emotional resilience, leading to a state of exhaustion that impairs their ability to make optimal decisions.

3.2. Increased cognitive load

3.2.1. Information overload

In the early stages of an ICU admission, the patient’s condition is often unstable and prone to rapid deterioration, requiring surrogate decision-makers to quickly comprehend and process a vast amount of complex medical information, intensifying the pressure to make decisions. As T7 expressed, “I am exhausted, not only physically, but also mentally. I feel the doctor has said a lot at once, and I am still confused.” Similarly, T10 remarked, “I find this kind of decision very difficult. Faced with this situation, I need to quickly understand and process complex medical information and then make a choice.” These statements underscore the formidable challenges faced by surrogates, who must rapidly assimilate intricate details under duress, resulting in significant cognitive and emotional strain. This strain can further complicate their ability to make informed decisions.

3.2.2. Time pressure

Most surrogate decision-makers report that the urgency of decision-making necessitates rapid choices within exceedingly short time frames. This time pressure often precludes thorough contemplation and discussion, exacerbating their significant decision-making burden. As T3 noted, “The situation was urgent, and there was no time for us to understand these things in depth.” Similarly, T10 remarked, “Time is tight, and the pressure is immense. There is no way to think slowly and calmly.” These reflections highlight the intense time constraints and heightened stress experienced by surrogates, which hinder their ability to engage in deliberate and reflective decision-making, ultimately compounding their overall pressure and anxiety.

3.2.3. Information acquisition limitations

Given the unique environment of the ICU, most surrogate decision-makers report that their understanding of the patient’s condition is primarily obtained through interactions with medical staff. However, the inherent busyness of medical personnel often precludes comprehensive communication and detailed exchanges. This limited access to crucial information exacerbates the psychological pressure on surrogates, potentially leading to decision fatigue. As T3 articulated, “We just came to communicate with the doctor at 11 o’clock. I will also ask for some of his current data reports. He said he would help me check and see how he is doing now, but we do not know his situation. The doctor is also very busy, so we dare not ask more.” Similarly, T6 expressed a desire for more transparency, stating, “I hope the medical staff can tell us the truth, that is, they can tell us in detail about her current condition changes and the effect of this treatment plan, so that we can better understand the situation.”

3.3. Decision-making dilemmas

3.3.1. Uncertainty about the consequences of medical decisions

Surrogate decision-makers often grapple with profound uncertainty when making patient decisions, a challenge exacerbated by their limited medical knowledge, the complexity of the patient’s condition, and the unpredictability of treatment outcomes and potential side effects. This uncertainty significantly undermines their confidence in decision-making. For instance, T1 shared, “Someone in my family had a tracheotomy before, and the patient died during the procedure, so I feel anxious when an elderly person undergoes a tracheotomy and intubation.” T2 said, “At that time, my mom and the other relatives were deeply concerned. They worried about what would happen to him once he was admitted – whether he would stay and what he might find out. We shared those same concerns.”

This personal history underscores the emotional burden and anxiety of making similar decisions. Similarly, T5 expressed, “I have many concerns. I am afraid the patient will die in the ICU or suffer other serious consequences, so it weighs heavily on me.” Anticipating worst-case scenarios adds another layer of stress. These reflections highlight the multifaceted pressures surrogates face as they navigate the daunting intersection of medical uncertainty, emotional trauma, and financial concerns.

3.3.2. Consideration of patient interests

Surrogate decision-makers are often profoundly troubled by considerations of the patient’s age, emotional well-being, and overall quality of life, further exacerbating the pressure of making critical decisions. T2 remarked, “He is 80 years old, and my grandfather is thin and small, and his health is not very good, with many problems; so I considered whether to do it later, because if I do not do it, his mouth will be open all the time, and he cannot eat.” This highlights the difficult balance between medical intervention and the patient’s physical state. T4 added, “At that time, I always thought that the old man would feel uncomfortable with the intubation, so I was still a little worried,” illustrating the emotional distress tied to invasive procedures. T5 articulated a long-term concern: “The main thing is to think long-term. Now, after CRRT is done, you stay in the ICU for a while, and you may get better for a while and then relapse, and then you have to go to the ICU again. It will be a vicious cycle. I do not know if this will be more torture for the patient. We have been troubled by this problem, so we do not know what is best for my mother.” These narratives underscore the complex interplay of medical, emotional, and ethical considerations that surrogates must navigate, amplifying their decision-making burden.

3.3.3. Economic pressure

Financial constraints directly impaired participants’ capacity to make and carry out care decisions. For some surrogate decision-makers, particularly those from families reliant on standard medical insurance, the exorbitant costs associated with ICU care are significant considerations in their decision-making process. T1 said, “Our family’s income primarily relies on those of us working outside the home. When he became ill, most of the outside work had to be put on hold.” T4 noted, “If we go to the ICU, the cost will be higher. We can still afford it in the short term, but in the long term, there will be a certain pressure.” This sentiment underscores the financial strain that extended ICU stays can impose on families. T6 further emphasized, “We have thrown all our money in; there is also financial pressure. A day here is... For a commoner, everyone can understand it, right?” Similarly, T10 expressed, “The cost of treatment in the ICU is expressive. Because my brother and we are not in a good financial situation, we cannot help much. The financial pressure is also very high.” These reflections highlight the profound impact of financial constraints on the decision-making process, adding a layer of complexity and stress for surrogate decision-makers already burdened by the critical health conditions of their loved ones.

3.4. Decision-making conflict

3.4.1. Conflict between patient wishes and surrogate decision-makers’ moral standards

Surrogate decision-makers will likely grapple with the influence of traditional Chinese filial piety and their moral principles throughout the decision-making process. They harbor a fervent desire for patients to continue treatment yet find themselves ensnared by the patients’ expressed wishes, thus confronting a profound dilemma. T4’s poignant testimony reflects this internal struggle: “When my father was hospitalized on a ventilator, he expressed how uncomfortable he was and that he wanted to go home. He did not want further treatment, but we thought that within our financial capacity, we should try to save him and let him live a little longer,” he said, visibly emotional. Similarly, T7 articulates the tug-of-war between practicality and morality: “can we try to save him? If he is saved, we will feel good, believing that we have done right by her and our conscience.” T9 also reported, “if we succeed in saving time, we feel at peace, knowing we have done right. However, we worry that we might fail or that even if we save time, the outcome might not be ideal, and she may have to endure a lot of suffering. We are concerned about this, so it is really tough to make a decision.” These testimonies underscore the intricate interplay of familial duty, personal ethics, and financial constraints, which significantly influence the decision-making journey of surrogate decision-makers in critical healthcare situations.

3.4.2. Conflict of opinions among family members

Within the decision-making process of specific surrogate decision-makers, conflicting perspectives among family members regarding the treatment plan for patients significantly influence their considerations. T1 illustrated this discordance: “When the elderly in the family heard that tracheotomy would make him unable to breathe, they did not want him to do it, but now it has to be done.” T4 recounted a similar scenario: “When I asked my mother this time, she said that if it is unnecessary to do the operation, then do not do it. The elderly believe that an operation will cause certain harm to the body, but now he cannot drain the ascites; I think we should actively listen to the doctor’s treatment.” These anecdotes underscore the complex dynamics within families, where varying perspectives on medical interventions challenge surrogate decision-makers to navigate divergent opinions while striving to make the best decisions for their loved ones.

3.4.3. Role conflict of substitute decision-makers

Surrogate decision-makers may juggle multiple roles simultaneously, significantly depleting their cognitive resources. T7 described the overwhelming strain of competing responsibilities, stating, “Rushing between my child’s school and the ICU after my mother’s critical diagnosis shattered me.” This highlights the sacrifices, including abandoning outside work, to meet the patient’s needs. T5 also described the exhaustive nature of the responsibilities of the surrogate: “Now I just sit here all day, taking care of her by myself, unable to do anything at home or work. But this is the only way.” These accounts reflect surrogates’ pressures as they strive to balance caregiving duties with the demands of maintaining their household and professional responsibilities.

3.5. Insufficient decision support system

3.5.1. Imperfect ICU support system

During the early stages of patient admission, the restrictive ICU visitation policies prevent surrogate decision-makers from contacting the patients directly. The lack of direct interaction with the patient and the unfamiliar ICU environment add significant emotional stress to the decision-making process. The inability to personally assess the patient’s latest condition and treatment progress exacerbates their decision fatigue. T3 highlighted this frustration, stating, “Then I want to learn more, just come out for an hour or two to talk or even watch the family members through video to see how they are sober, we will feel much more at ease.” Similarly, T8 expressed a desire for transparency: “We just hope to provide some ways for us to see what the family members are like inside so that we can know what the treatments are like and how effective they are.” These sentiments underscore the critical need for improved communication and transparency within ICU settings, as such measures could alleviate the emotional burden on surrogate decision-makers and enhance their ability to make informed decisions regarding patient care.

3.5.2. Lack of emotional support from the family

Surrogate decision-makers are profoundly eager for support from relatives and friends during the decision-making process, as such support can significantly alleviate the immense pressure they face. T1 expressed a deep-seated desire for familial backing, stating, “I hope I can get the support of my family, and when I make a decision here, my family will not say that the decision I made is wrong or something like that.” This highlights the emotional need for validation and understanding from loved ones. Similarly, T10 underscored the importance of a supportive network, articulating, “I hope I can get the support of my relatives and friends at this time. After all, I am under much pressure here, and they can support us when we face financial difficulties.” These reflections underscore the dual need for emotional reassurance and practical assistance from a support network as surrogate decision-makers navigate the daunting responsibilities and stressors of making critical healthcare decisions for their loved ones. This multifaceted support is crucial in mitigating the overwhelming burden and enhancing their capacity to make well-considered, informed decisions.

4. Discussion

In this study, we explored the experiences of decision fatigue among ICU surrogate decision-makers during the initial admission period. Decision fatigue refers to the decline in decision-making quality, prolonged reaction times, and increased discomfort due to the depletion of cognitive resources and the accumulation of psychological stress after making a series of decisions. Understanding the challenges faced by surrogate decision-makers in the ICU is essential for improving decision-making efficiency and outcomes.

4.1. Impact of personal emotional states on decision quality and decision fatigue

Emotional states such as anxiety and depression significantly affect decision quality. When individuals are in a negative emotional state, their ability to concentrate diminishes, and their capacity to process information is impaired. This reduces the accuracy of decisions and can contribute to increased decision fatigue. Our findings reveal that within the first 48 hours of a patient’s admission to the ICU, surrogate decision-makers experience profound negative emotions, including anxiety, fear, and helplessness. Both domestic and international studies have identified anxiety as the most prevalent psychological response among family members of ICU patients.[17] Research shows that anxiety levels among substitute decision-makers can peak at an alarming 97% during the early stages of a patient’s ICU stay.[18] Interviews reveal that this emotional exhaustion primarily stems from their acute awareness of the patient’s critical condition, which generates continuous fear, anxiety, and tension. Additionally, many substitute decision-makers for patients with chronic illnesses exhibit a numb attitude and manifest significant physical and mental fatigue throughout the decision-making process.[19] This fatigue is compounded by the depletion of self-control resources required to manage negative emotions, which leads to decision fatigue and a tendency to avoid decision-making responsibilities as the process progresses.[20] Rational emotive behavior therapy has been shown to alleviate anxiety, tension, and other related issues among substitute decision-makers.[21] Consequently, healthcare professionals should improve their understanding of decision fatigue and be able to recognize its signs in surrogate decision-makers. Timely assessment of decision fatigue is essential, as actively supporting and guiding surrogates can enhance the quality of their decision-making. In summary, personal emotional states significantly contribute to decision fatigue, and maintaining a positive emotional state can help alleviate its effects.

4.2. Role of poor decision-making environments in exacerbating decision fatigue

Adverse decision-making environments, such as information overload or time pressure, can exacerbate decision fatigue. Information overload makes it difficult for decision-makers to filter through large amounts of data to identify what is most relevant, leading to a more complex and inefficient process. Time pressure, meanwhile, forces decisions to be made hastily, without adequate reflection, increasing the likelihood of errors. During the initial stages of ICU admission, the severity and instability of the patient’s condition necessitate that substitute decision-makers quickly comprehend a substantial amount of complex medical information. The restrictive nature of information acquisition in the ICU environment further exacerbates this challenge, posing significant demands on their cognitive abilities. Research indicates that individuals experiencing high cognitive load often struggle with the cognitive capacity required to make sound health decisions.[22] This increased cognitive load can hinder substitute decision-makers from arriving at the most sensible and appropriate choices for the patient. Consequently, medical staff must consider enhancing the presentation of information by simplifying medical jargon and employing decision-support tools. Such measures can reduce the cognitive burden on substitute decision-makers, facilitating clearer and more informed decision-making processes. Improving the clarity and accessibility of medical information enables surrogate decision-makers to better navigate the complexities of the patient’s condition and treatment options, ultimately leading to more informed and well-considered healthcare decisions. In conclusion, the decision-making environment is a crucial factor influencing decision fatigue, and optimizing this environment can help reduce the risk of decision fatigue.

4.3. Impact of decisional indecision and conflict on decision-making difficulties, increasing the risk of decision fatigue

Decisional indecision and conflict significantly contribute to decision-making challenges, increasing the risk of decision fatigue. When faced with multiple similar or conflicting options, decision-makers often expend significant time and energy weighing the pros and cons. This struggle diminishes decision-making efficiency and can lead to psychological fatigue, further exacerbating decision fatigue. Surrogate decision-makers frequently experience hesitation and conflict when confronted with complex and uncertain medical scenarios. Their indecision often stems from a lack of medical knowledge and insufficient information, leaving them unsure whether their choices align with the patient’s wishes and best interests. Additionally, they must weigh considerations related to family dynamics and financial constraints. This phenomenon parallels the findings of foreign scholars.[8] Such decision-making hesitation and conflict can prevent timely treatment choices, potentially delaying optimal patient care and heightening the decision-making pressure on surrogates.[23] This issue is exacerbated by inadequate communication between medical staff and surrogate decision-makers, a lack of understanding regarding the patient’s family’s economic situation, and inconsistent moral standards and values among family members.[24] Studies suggest high-quality communication can enhance decision-making quality and reduce decision fatigue among surrogates.[7] Therefore, medical staff must establish robust communication mechanisms, engaging more effectively with surrogate decision-makers. Utilizing videos or decision support manuals can aid in comprehending treatment plans.[25] Moreover, increasing the medical insurance reimbursement ratio for major diseases could alleviate the financial burdens on ordinary families, thereby facilitating more informed and less pressured decision-making processes. In summary, decisional indecision and conflict are significant factors leading to decision fatigue, and enhancing decision-making skills and self-confidence can help mitigate this.

4.4. Role of effective decision support systems in alleviating decision fatigue

Effective decision support systems can assist surrogate decision-makers by helping them quickly identify critical information and make informed decisions, thereby reducing decision fatigue. In the ICU setting, surrogate decision-makers often face inadequate decision support, including insufficient medical knowledge, limited access to information resources, and a lack of emotional support. Research suggests that many surrogate decision-makers experience separation anxiety when patients are admitted to the ICU due to environmental and systemic pressures, which increases emotional distress and their need for support.[16] A well-structured visiting system not only mitigates decision fatigue among surrogates but also addresses patients’ emotional needs through surrogate involvement. Therefore, hospitals can leverage technological advancements such as the Internet to enhance the visiting system, which may provide a visual communication channel to help prevent and control environmental infections and meet the emotional needs of family members.[26] Such measures alleviate the emotional strain on surrogate decision-makers and facilitate their involvement in patient care, fostering a supportive environment conducive to well-informed decision-making and holistic patient care. In summary, effective decision support systems are crucial for alleviating decision fatigue and enhancing decision-makers’ ability and efficiency.

5. Limitations

This study is limited to participants with critical care respiratory illnesses in a single tertiary hospital, and only a few experienced moderate decision fatigue, limiting its generalizability. The conclusions drawn from this research reflect the perspectives of surrogate decision-makers for a specific subset of ICU patients and may not apply to the broader population. Therefore, it is primarily an in-depth exploratory study focused on individual cases.

5.1. Recommendations or implications for practice and further research

This study conducted semi-structured interviews with surrogate decision-makers of patients in the early stages of ICU admission, uncovering the presence of decision fatigue among these individuals. The primary contributors to decision fatigue included increased cognitive load and insufficient decision support systems. Surrogate decision-makers experienced emotional exhaustion, decision hesitation, and decision conflict during the decision-making process. Decision fatigue can reduce the quality of decision-making and adversely affect the physical and mental health of surrogate decision-makers, ultimately compromising the patients’ best interests. Therefore, healthcare professionals should continuously monitor and identify signs of decision fatigue in surrogate decision-makers of ICU patients, implement strategies to alleviate it, and enhance the quality of their decision-making.

6. Conclusion

This study revealed the status of decision fatigue during this critical period through semi-structured interviews with surrogate decision-makers of ICU patients within the first 48 hours. Decision fatigue manifests primarily through symptoms of emotional exhaustion, heightened cognitive load, decision hesitancy, conflict, and insufficient decision support systems. This phenomenon not only compromises the quality of decision-making but also has a detrimental impact on the physical and mental well-being of surrogate decision-makers. Consequently, there is a pressing need for medical staff to implement adequate support and intervention strategies to alleviate decision fatigue and enhance decision-making quality. However, it is imperative to acknowledge the study’s limitations, given its exclusive focus on cases of respiratory critical illness within a tertiary hospital setting. Therefore, the conclusions drawn from this study offer insights specific to surrogate decision-makers of certain ICU patients and cannot be generalized to the broader population. This study explores individual cases in depth, highlighting the complexities inherent in decision-making within the ICU context.
