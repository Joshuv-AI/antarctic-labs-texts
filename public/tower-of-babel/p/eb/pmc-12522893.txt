Efficacy and safety of Bifidobacterium combined with high-frequency repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation in the treatment of depression in adolescents: a preliminary randomised controlled trial
Authors: Jing-Jing Ding, Jun-Ke Zhang, Fei-Fei Zhao, Meng Chen, Zhi-Yuan Lin, Cong-Cong Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to explore the efficacy and safety of Bifidobacterium combined with high-frequency repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) in treating depression in adolescents.MethodsA total of 100 patients were selected and divided into an experimental group (n = 50) and a control group (n = 50) using a random number table. Patients in the experimental group were treated with Bifidobacterium and high-frequency rTMS, and those in the control group were treated with oral escitalopram oxalate. After 8 weeks of treatment, the Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HAMD-24) score, serum inflammatory factors, neuroendocrine indicators and microRNAs were determined in both groups. The single-blind principle was strictly followed throughout the study.ResultsThe HAMD-24 score after treatment significantly decreased in the experimental group compared with the control group, with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.05). The levels of serum tumour necrosis factor-α, interleukin (IL)-1β and IL-6 also decreased significantly in the experimental group compared with the control group (p < 0.05). Additionally, the elevated levels of dopamine, serotonin and cortisol, including dopamine, 5-hydroxytryptamine and cortisol, was more pronounced in the experimental group than in the control group. The serum levels of miR-16 and miR-195 also showed statistically significant differences (p < 0.05).ConclusionBifidobacterium combined with high-frequency rTMS is effective in the treatment of depression in adolescents with a favourable safety profile, providing references for the clinical treatment of this disease.Clinical trial number: ISRCTN16752763 (https://doi.org/10.1186/ISRCTN16752763), Date: 19/03/2025.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12991-025-00595-5.Keywords: Bifidobacterium, High-frequency transcranial magnetic stimulation, Depression in adolescents

Introduction

Depression is a common mood disorder with significant and persistent dejection as the major clinical feature [1]. As an umbrella term, there are several types of depression, including major depressive disorder (MDD), bipolar disorder, seasonal affective disorder and postpartum depression. In addition to a close correlation with suicide, depression is often associated with various chronic diseases, such as coronary artery disease, type II diabetes, angina pectoris, arthritis and stroke [2]. According to the World Health Organization, more than 320 million people worldwide are currently suffering from depression [3], and depression is expected to become the leading factor contributing to the global disease burden by 2030 [4].

Depression is a heterogeneous disease with highly variable disease duration and inconsistent response to treatment, and its pathogenesis is not yet fully established. The existing biological mechanisms of the aetiology of depression are mainly (1) stress and hyperfunction of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis and (2) dysregulated monoamine neurotransmitters. The pathogenesis of depression also involves an abnormal glutamatergic nervous system, decreased γ-aminobutyric acid neurotransmission, abnormal circadian rhythm, decreased neurosteroid synthesis and acetylcholine imbalance [1].

Antidepressants currently used for the clinical treatment of depression, mainly serotonin reuptake inhibitors, monoamine oxidase inhibitors and tricyclic antidepressants, exert their antidepressant effects by improving the homeostasis of the monoamine neurotransmitter system. These antidepressants are generally slow in onset, with low efficacy and high recurrence. The efficacy of these antidepressants is minimal in approximately one-third of patients; these diseases are known as treatment-resistant depression [5].

Clinical studies in recent years have revealed intestinal flora imbalance in patients with depression. Changes in the intestinal flora structure affect brain function through the microbiota–gut–brain axis [6], and supplementation of probiotics can also regulate intestinal dysfunction and improve the function of the central nervous system (CNS) [7]. Probiotics are made from normal flora in the human body or bacteria that can promote the growth of normal flora after being processed by modern pharmaceutical technology and mixed with corresponding excipients [8]. Although clinical studies suggest probiotics may benefit depressive symptoms through gut–brain axis modulation [9], recent meta-analyses reveal significant heterogeneity across trials. A 2023 systematic review found moderate-quality evidence for probiotic efficacy in depression (standardised mean difference = − 0.34) but highlighted crucial moderators: strain specificity (Bifidobacterium species showed superior effects vs Lactobacillus alone), treatment duration (> 8 weeks) and comorbid inflammation status [10]. Bifidobacterium is one of the important intestinal probiotics. Studies worldwide have found that Bifidobacterium can increase the level of monoamine neurotransmitters in the brain to exert its antidepressant effect [11, 12]. In addition, repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) is a safe, non-invasive neuromodulation therapy for MDD. It can stimulate neurons in a short period and affect the functional neural network in the stimulated site [13]. Since the approval of rTMS by the US Food and Drug Administration, this therapy has been widely used in the clinical treatment of depression worldwide [14]. Preliminary evidence suggests that rTMS improves cognitive function in patients with depression, including verbal memory [15, 16], attention [17] and executive function [18]. However, due to the variability in study designs and methodologies, as well as the limited assessment of cognitive function, the effect of rTMS on the cognitive function of patients with depression is not fully understood, and further exploration is needed.

Given the potential of both Bifidobacterium and rTMS in alleviating depressive symptoms through distinct yet potentially synergistic mechanisms, we hypothesise that combining these two interventions could offer a more effective and comprehensive treatment approach for depression in adolescents. Adolescence is a critical period for brain development and emotional regulation, and depression during this stage can have long-term impacts on mental health and cognitive development. Therefore, it is essential to explore novel and effective treatment strategies for depression in adolescents. The rationale for this combination lies in the potential for Bifidobacterium to modulate the gut–brain axis and enhance neuroplasticity, thereby augmenting the effects of rTMS on brain function. Furthermore, the anti-inflammatory properties of Bifidobacterium may counteract the potential inflammatory effects associated with depression, creating a more favourable environment for rTMS to exert its therapeutic effects [19]. On this basis, the present study was designed to investigate the efficacy and safety of Bifidobacterium combined with high-frequency rTMS in the treatment of depression in adolescents to provide references for the selection of treatment methods for depression.

Study participants and methods

Study participants

A total of 116 adolescent patients with depression admitted to the Wenzhou Seventh People’s Hospital between March 2022 and October 2022 were assessed for eligibility. Among them, 16 patients were excluded (10 did not meet the inclusion criteria, and 6 declined to participate). The remaining 100 patients were randomly assigned to an experimental group (n = 50) and a control group (n = 50) using a computer-generated random number table (see Fig. 1). To ensure allocation concealment, the random sequence was generated by an independent statistician who was not involved in participant recruitment or assignment. The allocation sequence was stored in sealed, opaque envelopes, which were opened only after the participant’s baseline data were collected. Block randomisation with a block size of 4 was employed to ensure balanced group sizes throughout the trial. This process ensured that each participant had an equal chance of being assigned to either group, thereby minimising selection bias. The sample size was calculated based on an expected effect size of 0.5 (Cohen’s d), which is commonly used in preliminary studies when specific prior data are lacking. This medium effect size is suitable for detecting clinically meaningful differences in depressive symptoms between groups. The calculation was performed using using G*Power software (version 3.1; supplier: Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany), with a statistical power of 0.8 and a significance level of 0.05, indicating a minimum of 64 participants (32 per group) were required. To account for potential dropouts, 100 participants were enrolled. No participants were lost to follow-up, and all 100 were included in the final analysis.

CONSORT flow diagram

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged 13–18 years; (2) met the diagnostic criteria for depression in the International Classification of Disease-10; (3) a total score of the Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HAMD-24) ≥ 20 in the initial assessment; and (4) right-handed. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a definite diagnosis of other mental disorders in the past; (2) a past or current history of manic episodes; (3) a past or current history of severe physical diseases; (4) used antidepressants, mood stabilisers, steroids, anti-inflammatory drugs, antibiotics or immunomodulators in the past 1 month; (5) used lactic acid bacteria products for ≥ 7 days in the past 1 month; (6) a history of alcohol or psychoactive substance abuse within the last 3 months; (7) contraindications for rTMS, including but not limited to intracranial metal foreign bodies, cardiac pacemakers and ear hearing aids; and (8) complicated with organic brain diseases, epileptic diseases or severe physical diseases. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Wenzhou Seventh People’s Hospital (Approval Number: EC-20210826–03, the approval date is August 26, 2021), and informed consent was obtained from all participants and guardians. All methods were performed following relevant guidelines and regulations.

The study was listed on the International Standard Randomised Controlled Trial Number (ISRCTN) registry with study registration number ISRCTN16752763 (10.1186/ISRCTN16752763). The study complied with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials clinical trial reporting guidelines (see Supplementary Material – Checklist) and has provided anonymised datasets (see Supplementary Material – Full dataset).

Randomisation and blinding

This study was a randomised controlled trial and employed a single-blind design (rater blind). To minimise bias and ensure the integrity of the blinding process, several measures were implemented – participants in different groups were assigned to separate wards whenever possible; the packaging of the two groups of drugs was removed and replaced with a standardised outer packaging; and patients in the control group were allowed to enter the rTMS treatment room, although they did not receive the treatment. However, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of a single-blind design, as the lack of blinding for participants may introduce bias in self-reported outcomes and expectations regarding treatment effects.

Treatment

Patients in the experimental group were treated with oral Bifidobacterium (0.07 g/capsule, approval number: Guo Yao Zhun Zi S10950032, Shanghai Sine Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.), 3 capsules, 3 times a day, for 8 consecutive weeks and high-frequency rTMS. In the rTMS treatment, the rTMS device with an ‘8’-shaped coil with a diameter of 70 mm was placed on the dorsolateral area of the left prefrontal lobe of the patient. The motor threshold was set to 90%. A 10-Hz pulse sequence was applied for 4 s, followed by a 20-s interval. Ten pulses per train were used, and the total stimulation time was 30 min a day, 4 times a week, for 8 consecutive weeks.

Patients in the control group were treated with oral escitalopram oxalate (manufacturer: Sichuan Kelun Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.; approval number: Guo Yao Zhun Zi H20080788) because it is a commonly used and well-established antidepressant in clinical practice, with extensive evidence supporting its efficacy in treating depression in adolescents. However, there is still a significant proportion of patients who do not respond adequately to escitalopram, highlighting the need for alternative or complementary treatments. The initial dose was 5 mg/day for 7 consecutive days. The dose was increased to 10 mg/day according to the tolerance of the patients for another 7 consecutive days and then to 20 mg/day according to the patient’s condition and tolerance for 8 weeks. At the same time, patients in the control group also entered the rTMS treatment room, equipped with ‘8’-shaped coils. However, they received sham rTMS treatment instead of active rTMS. The sham rTMS procedure mimicked the active rTMS treatment in terms of visual and auditory aspects but did not deliver significant magnetic stimulation. This was achieved by using a sham coil that visually and audibly resembled the active coil but did not produce effective magnetic pulses. This operation lasted 30 min a day, 4 times a week, for 8 weeks.

During the conduct of this trial, the trial protocol was not modified after its commencement. All procedures, including the treatment regimens for the control and experimental groups, the blinding methods and the duration of the study, were carried out strictly in accordance with the pre-established protocol.

Outcome measures

(1) Primary outcome: The HAMD-24 scores were evaluated before and after treatment. The HAMD scale was developed by Hamilton in 1960 [20]; the three versions of the HAMD available contain 17, 21 and 24 items, respectively, and the 24-item version was used in this study. Refer to the classification of Hamilton [20], final scores were interpreted as no depression (< 20), mild or moderate depression (≥ 20 and < 35) and severe depression (≥ 35). To ensure the accuracy and reliability of the HAMD-24 scoring, all raters underwent a standardised training programme, which included detailed training sessions on item scoring rules and practice rating sessions with experienced raters to calibrate scoring.

(2) Secondary outcomes: (i) The levels of serum inflammatory factors, neuroendocrine indicators and microRNAs (miRNAs) were measured before and after treatment. Briefly, approximately 5 ml of fasting venous blood was collected in the morning and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min, and the upper serum was collected. The levels of serum tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, dopamine (DA), 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) and cortisol (COR) were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay using an automatic biochemical analyser (Dxc800, Beckman, USA). The relative expression levels of serum miR-16 and miR-195 were determined by polymerase chain reaction. All measurements were strictly conducted by laboratory specialists following the instructions of the instruments and kits. The miRNA was measured as follows: 10 ml of fasting venous blood was drawn from the patients before and in the morning after treatment and centrifuged for 12 min (centrifugation radius 10 cm, rotation speed 3000 rpm), and the supernatant was removed and stored in a − 70 °C refrigerator for examination. Sample RNA was extracted according to the instructions in the RNA extraction kit, and total complementary DNA was obtained by reverse transcription processing. Primer 5.0 software was used to design primer sequences. Reaction system: 50 μl, prereaction at 95 °C for 2 min, unwinding at 95 °C for 5 s, annealing at 56 °C for 5 s and extension at 72 °C for 35 s. A total of 35 cycles were performed. The relative expression levels of miR-16 and miR-195 were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method; (ii) remission rate and incidence rate of adverse reactions. The remission rate was calculated based on the reduction rate of the HAMD scores (difference in scores before and after treatment / score before treatment × 100%). Patients were categorised as follows: ≥ 75% reduction was considered recovered, 50%–75% reduction was significantly effective, 25%–50% reduction was effective and < 25% reduction was ineffective. The remission rate was calculated as the sum of recovered, significantly effective and effective cases divided by the total number of cases. The incidence of adverse reactions was calculated by dividing the number of patients with adverse reactions by the total number of patients.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS version 26.0 statistical software. The normality of the data was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed measurement data, results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s), and comparisons between groups were performed using an independent samples t-test. For non-normally distributed data, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. Categorical data are presented as frequencies and percentages (n [%]), and between-group comparisons were conducted using the chi-squared (χ2) test. A significance level of α = 0.05 was used for all statistical tests, and all tests were two-tailed. Intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis was conducted using the “Last Observation Carry-over” (LOCF) method. Bonferroni correction addresses the issues of multiple comparisons and the risk of Type I errors. The comparison of Post-treatment HAMD between the two groups was conducted using univariate covariance analysis (ANCOVA) with gender, age and Pre-treatment HAMD as covariates.

Results

Comparison of general data between the two groups

A total of 100 patients were selected and divided into an experimental group and a control group using a random number table, with 50 patients in each group. The general data were compared between the two groups, and the results are presented in Supplementary Table 1. There were 22 male participants and 28 female participants, with an average age of 16.27 ± 1.54 years (range, 13–18 years), an average disease duration of 6.02 ± 1.32 months (range, 3–8 months) and an average length of education of 8.91 ± 1.21 years (range, 6–12 years) in the experimental group; and 23 male participants and 27 female participants, with an average age of 16.41 ± 1.61 years (range, 13–18 years), an average disease duration of 6.35 ± 1.46 months (range, 3–8 months) and an average length of education of 8.86 ± 1.19 years (range, 6–12 years) in the control group. The general data were comparable, with no significant differences observed between the two groups (p > 0.05).

Comparison of the Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression scores between the two groups

As shown in Supplementary Table 2, the HAMD scores of the experimental group and the control group were 29.97 ± 4.92 and 29.68 ± 4.83, respectively, before treatment, with no statistically significant difference (t = 0.297, p = 0.767); the HAMD scores in the experimental group (12.19 ± 4.61 vs 29.97 ± 4.92, t = 18.647, p < 0.001) and the control group (14.35 ± 4.57 vs 29.68 ± 4.83, t = 16.302, p < 0.001) were decreased after treatment compared with those before treatment, respectively, with a statistically significant difference; after treatment, the HAMD score of the experimental group was lower than that of the control group, and the difference was statistically significant (12.19 ± 4.61 vs 14.35 ± 4.57, F = 15.621, p < 0.001) (see Fig. 2).

Comparison of serum inflammatory factors between the two groups

There were no significant differences in the levels of serum TNF-α, IL-1β or IL-6 between the two groups before treatment (p > 0.05 for all comparisons). After treatment, the levels of serum inflammatory factors in both groups decreased, and the differences before and after treatment were statistically significant (p < 0.05 for all comparisons). Discussion

The prevalence of depression among children and adolescents in China has increased significantly in recent years. Suicide events frequently occur; the suicide tendency of this population is twice that of adults, with a high recurrence rate [21]. The development of depression in children and adolescents is closely related to social, biological and psychological factors, and the age of first episodes is getting lower. Depression in this population is characterised by long-term low mood, low self-esteem, delusion, pessimism, hallucination, anorexia and even suicidal tendencies or behaviours, which seriously impact the social interaction, life and health of children and adolescents. Current clinical studies primarily focus on the treatment of depression scientifically and effectively to alleviate the condition and reduce the disability rate.

In the present study, Bifidobacterium combined with high-frequency rTMS was used for depression in adolescents and compared with oral escitalopram oxalate. The conditions of patients in the experimental group and the control group were improved. After treatment, the HAMD score decreased significantly; the levels of serum inflammatory factors, including TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6, decreased, the levels of neuroendocrine indicators DA and 5-HT increased and that of COR decreased. Previous studies have shown that the onset of depression is primarily related to factors such as 5-HT, low norepinephrine function, dysregulated DA function in the mesolimbic system and decreased function of receptors [22–24]. Escitalopram oxalate is a commonly used 5-HT receptor inhibitor. It binds to the 5-HT receptor and subsequently affects the contact between 5-HT and the 5-HT receptor in the presynaptic membrane to directly reduce the uptake and increase the concentration of 5-HT in the synaptic space, which finally alleviates depression and anxiety. However, it is important to note that although the HAMD score differences between groups post-treatment were statistically significant, the clinical relevance of these differences should be interpreted with caution. The Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) for HAMD-24 is estimated to be 3–5 points. The observed difference of approximately 2 points may not meet this threshold, suggesting that the real-world clinical impact of the observed changes may be limited.

In the present study, Bifidobacterium combined with high-frequency rTMS effectively improved the levels of serum inflammatory factors and neuroendocrine indicators in the experimental group. One potential mechanism for this effect is that oral probiotics, such as Bifidobacterium, may modulate gut microbiota composition, leading to an improved gut–brain axis function [25]. Research has shown that Bifidobacterium can enhance the diversity and abundance of beneficial gut bacteria, which in turn can influence the production of neurotransmitters and neuroactive compounds [26]. For instance, Bifidobacterium may promote the synthesis of serotonin (5-HT) in the gut, which is crucial for mood regulation [27]. The reduction of inflammatory factors such as TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6 may reflect the regulation of the immune system, which in turn may positively affect the function of the central nervous system and alleviate depressive symptoms. Similarly, changes in neuroendocrine indicators enhanced neural plasticity and neural transmission, contributing to the observed clinical improvement. Furthermore, Bifidobacterium may influence the immune system by mediating immune cells to produce immunomodulators through various signalling pathways. This action can subsequently reduce the levels of pro-inflammatory factors, such as TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6, while also regulating COR secretion, thereby affecting CNS function. By enhancing the metabolism of DA and 5-HT, Bifidobacterium may inhibit synaptic changes, protect the plasticity of hypothalamic neurons and improve overall brain function, ultimately inhibiting the development of depression.

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation is a non-invasive, non-drug therapy widely used in clinical practice. During the procedure of rTMS, the magnetic stimulation coil is placed on the designated site of the scalp, usually the projection area of the prefrontal cortex. When the electric signal of the magnetic stimulator is discharged, a local magnetic field with a certain intensity is generated around the coil, and the magnetic field induces a current in the cerebral cortex. When the induced biological current exceeds the threshold of nerve cell excitability, nerve cells are activated to induce membrane depolarisation and trigger a series of physiological and biochemical reactions to relieve symptoms and treat diseases [28]. Although rTMS has shown promising results in treating various conditions, its potential impact on cognitive function remains an area of growing interest. Future studies could consider assessing cognitive function as an additional outcome measure to explore whether rTMS has beneficial effects in this domain, which may further support its therapeutic applications.

Clinical studies in recent years have found that intestinal flora dysregulation is involved in the development and progression of various mental disorders and affects the CNS and behaviour through pathways such as sustained immune response and neurotransmitter mediation [29]. In addition, gut microbiota products, such as cytokines, play an important role in the gut microbiota–brain interaction [30]. In addition, studies have revealed that gut microbiota is imbalanced in patients with MDD, the number of firmicutes is significantly higher than that of the general population and the number of probiotics in stool samples is significantly reduced [31–33]. In addition, changes in diet and living habits further lead to abnormal gut microflora [34]. Accordingly, conventional treatment methods, such as antidepressant drugs, alone are not effective in patients with depression. Therefore, the regulation of intestinal flora may serve as an adjuvant treatment for depression. Liu Daiyue et al. found that the improvement effect of antidepressants combined with intestinal flora regulators on clinical symptoms, adverse behaviours and cognitive function of patients with depression was better than that of antidepressants alone [35]. Bo Qiyu et al. showed that Bifidobacterium live bacteria combined with Prozac significantly alleviated the clinical symptoms and signs of patients with depression, and its efficacy was superior to that of Prozac alone in the treatment of this disease [36]. However, it is important to note that the shift in the gut microbiota may be transient and temporary. Some probiotic treatment trials did not find significant changes in gut microbiota composition after 4–8 weeks of intervention [10], suggesting that individuals may require a longer duration of treatment to achieve therapeutic effects. To advance this field, further larger-scale and longer-term studies are required.

Although the specific safety evidence of the combined use of probiotics and rTMS still needs further research, multiple studies have already supported the safety of using rTMS alone [37–39]. For instance, existing studies have shown that the application of rTMS in adolescents has demonstrated more significant clinical symptom improvements than in adults, and no safety or tolerability issues have been identified [39]. Furthermore, according to the “Expert Consensus on Clinical Application of Neuroregulation Techniques in Pediatrics” (2021) in China: rTMS should not be used for children under the age of 12. Therefore, the sample ages in this study were all above 12 years old. In terms of safety, no serious adverse reactions were reported in the two groups during the treatment. The treatment-related adverse reactions that the patients experienced, including headache, dizziness, hearing loss and other discomfort symptoms, were mild in severity and relieved within 1 day after the treatment. No patients discontinued treatment because of adverse reactions. There was no statistically significant difference in the incidence of adverse reactions between the two groups, indicating the favourable safety profile and tolerance of Bifidobacterium combined with high-frequency rTMS in the treatment of depression in adolescents.

There are several limitations in the present study. First, the relatively small sample size of 100 patients may introduce selection bias and limit the generalisability of the results. Second, the short observation period restricts our ability to assess long-term effects. Additionally, conducting the study at a single centre may limit the diversity of the sample and the applicability of the findings to broader populations. Furthermore, the control group did not receive a placebo treatment, which could have influenced outcomes due to the absence of a comparison for the placebo effect. Participants from different groups were assigned to different wards as much as possible, and the packaging of the two groups of drugs was standardized. Patients in the control group were allowed to enter the rTMS treatment room, even though they did not receive treatment. Despite all these efforts we have made, we admit that some participants may still have guessed their treatment allocation. Although our primary objective was to explore the combined effects of Bifidobacterium and rTMS, we acknowledge that this design introduces a bias that makes it challenging to discern whether the observed effects are due to the individual interventions or their interaction. Furthermore, although the inter-group differences in HAMD-24 scores were statistically significant, we acknowledged that the clinical relevance of this difference was uncertain as it did not reach the minimum clinically important difference of 3–5 points. Last, we did not control for diet and lifestyle factors that may affect gut microbiota and study outcomes. Future research with larger sample sizes, longer observation periods and multicentre designs is needed to validate our findings and provide more robust evidence regarding the efficacy of Bifidobacterium combined with high-frequency rTMS in treating depression.

Conclusion

Our research provides preliminary evidence suggesting that the combination of Bifidobacterium and high-frequency rTMS in the treatment of adolescent depression may have potential benefits. However, the study design and results do not conclusively demonstrate the superiority of this combined treatment over escitalopram. Although the observed improvements indicate potential benefits, further investigation in larger, more rigorously designed trials is necessary to confirm these findings and establish the clinical efficacy of this approach. It is important to note that the observed difference in HAMD scores between groups post-treatment was approximately 2 points, which may not reach the MCID for HAMD-24. This suggests that the real-world clinical impact of the observed changes may be limited. Despite these limitations, this study provides preliminary evidence that may inform future research and clinical decision-making for the treatment of depression in adolescents.
