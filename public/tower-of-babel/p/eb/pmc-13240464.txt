APOE‐associated disease risk and sex‐specific effects in Egyptian Alzheimer's disease: an exploratory study
Authors: Mai Othman, Eman Khedr, Shimaa Heikal, Mohamed H Yousef, Gharib Fawi, Sara El Farrash, Samer Salama, Muhammad Abdelshafi, Nesma G Elsheikh, Heba M Tawfik, Hany I Hassanin, Nourelhoda A Haridy, Gellan K Ahmed, Noha Abo Elftoh, Amna Ibrahim, Ahmed Qayaty, Eman M Ali, Agsha Atputhavadivel, Samir Shamma, Noha A Yousri, Mie Rizig, Mohamed Salama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONThe apolipoprotein E (APOE) gene is the strongest known genetic risk factor for late‐onset Alzheimer's disease (AD), particularly the ε4 allele. Evidence suggests that women carrying at least one APOE ε4 allele have a higher risk of developing AD compared to men. However, data addressing genetic risk and sex‐specific effects in Middle Eastern and North African populations remain limited.METHODSTo address this gap, we evaluated APOE allele frequencies, their association with AD risk, and their contribution to sex‐specific variability in a cohort of 63 clinically diagnosed AD patients and 201 cognitively healthy controls recruited across Egypt.RESULTSThe APOE ε3 allele was the most prevalent in both AD cases (83.3%) and controls (83.6%). The ε4 allele was more frequent among AD patients (12.7%) than controls (8.0%) (adjusted odds ratio [OR] = 1.09, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.46 to 2.49, p = 0.85), while the ε2 allele was less frequent in AD patients (4.0%) compared to controls (8.5%) (adjusted OR = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.24 to 2.36, p = 0.73). However, neither association reached statistical significance after Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction. Sex‐stratified analyses revealed no significant sex‐specific effects of APOE alleles.CONCLUSIONThis pilot study provides the first characterization of APOE genotype and allele frequencies in Egyptians with clinically confirmed AD. No statistically significant association was observed between APOE status and AD risk in either males or females. Larger, geographically representative studies are needed to further elucidate the role of APOE in AD susceptibility within the Egyptian population and to explore potential alternative genetic or environmental contributors.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, apolipoprotein E (APOE) alleles, APOE genotypes, dementia, Egyptian Dementia Network, risk factors

BACKGROUND

The APOE gene is a well‐established genetic determinant of dementia risk, particularly in late‐onset Alzheimer's disease (AD). 1 It encodes apolipoprotein E, a protein central to lipid metabolism, and exists in three primary allelic forms – ε2, ε3, and ε4 – each associated with distinct levels of disease risk. 2

The ε4 allele represents the strongest known genetic risk factor for AD, exhibiting a gene dose–dependent effect; individuals homozygous for ε4 may experience up to a 15‐fold increased risk. 2 In contrast, the ε2 allele is considered protective, reducing AD risk by approximately 50% and being associated with increased longevity. 2

The global distribution of APOE alleles varies substantially, contributing to population‐level differences in disease susceptibility. The ε3 allele, generally regarded as neutral, is the most prevalent worldwide (48% to 94%), with particularly high frequencies observed in Native American (72.0% to 91.1%) and European populations (64.0% to 89.8%). 3 , 4 The protective ε2 allele (0% to 38% globally) is least common in Native American populations (0.0% to 1.4%) and occurs more frequently in Asian populations (0.4% to 14.0%). 3 , 4 The ε4 allele, associated with increased AD risk, has a global frequency ranging from 3% to 41%, with the highest prevalence in African populations (14.3% to 40.7%) and lower frequencies in Europeans (6.8% to 31.0%), Asians (7.1% to 24.0%), and Native Americans (8.9% to 28.0%). 3 , 4 Interestingly, despite its relatively high prevalence across African populations, the effect of ε4 appears more variable and, in some contexts, attenuated compared to European populations. This variability is thought to reflect differences in genetic background, ancestry, and environmental exposures. 3 , 4

Despite this well‐established role of APOE in AD, its distribution and impact in Middle Eastern and North African populations, including Egyptians, remain poorly characterized. To date, only one study – limited by a small cohort size – has examined the role of APOE in AD among Egyptians. 5 This study aims to investigate the frequency of APOE variants and their association with AD risk in a well‐characterized, multicenter Egyptian cohort, while also exploring potential sex‐related differences in this relationship. The findings are expected to provide valuable insights to inform future research in this understudied population.

METHODS

Ethics statement

All participants or their legal guardians provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the American University in Cairo (IRB‐AUC 2024‐2025‐141).

Participant recruitment, diagnostic ascertainment, and clinical evaluation

This study was a multicenter, cross‐sectional case–control study. Participants, including patients and controls, were recruited through the Egyptian Dementia Network (EDN) registry 6 from 17 governorates (Sohag, Assiout, Assiut, Qena, Aswan, Minya, Beni Suef, Mansoura, Cairo, Giza, Tanta, Zagazig, Suez Canal, Alexandria, Benha, Damietta, Port Said). All participants self‐identified as native Egyptians for at least two generations.

Clinical assessments were conducted by qualified neurologists, geriatricians, or geriatric psychiatrists. AD diagnosis was established according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM‐5) 7 and the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10), 8 requiring evidence of progressive cognitive and functional decline over at least 6 months, with exclusion of delirium and primary psychiatric disorders.

To exclude alternative etiologies, a comprehensive medical history was obtained, and all participants underwent laboratory investigations (including liver and kidney function tests, complete blood count, glucose and lipid profiles, and thyroid function tests) as well as neuroimaging (magnetic resonance imaging [MRI] or computed tomography [CT]) to identify vascular, metabolic, or other systemic causes of cognitive impairment. Functional status was assessed using the Katz Activities of Daily Living (ADL) scale for basic self‐care and the Lawton–Brody Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) scale for more complex daily activities. Global cognitive function was evaluated using the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE). 9 , 10 , 11 Controls were cognitively healthy individuals without neurological symptoms.

Genetic analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral venous whole blood using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) according to the manufacturer's protocol.

APOE genetic variation was determined by genotyping two well‐characterized, non‐synonymous single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs): rs429358 (c.388T>C; p.Cys130Arg) and rs7412 (c.526C>T; p.Arg176Cys). The combination of alleles at these loci defines the APOE isoforms: ε2 (rs429358 T, rs7412 T), ε3 (rs429358 T, rs7412 C), and ε4 (rs429358 C, rs7412 C).

Genotyping was performed using a validated Kompetitive Allele‐Specific PCR (KASP™) assay (LGC Genomics, Hoddesdon, UK), a fluorescence‐based method enabling accurate bi‐allelic discrimination, as previously described. 12 , 13 Genotype clusters were visualized and assigned using SNP Viewer software (version 1.99; LGC Genomics).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2). 14

Cohort characteristics were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and counts with percentages for categorical variables. Genotype and allele frequencies were similarly reported.

Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) was assessed in the control group for both SNPs (rs429358 and rs7412) using the chi‐squared goodness‐of‐fit test.

Associations between APOE genotypes/alleles and AD phenotype were evaluated using Firth's penalized logistic regression, adjusting for age, sex, and geographic area with significance threshold of p < 0.05. Multiple testing across genotype models and stratified analyses was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (BH‐FDR) method with significance threshold of FDR‐p < 0.05. Exploratory sex‐stratified analyses were conducted using the same approach, adjusting for age and geographic area, to assess potential gender‐specific effects.

Given the age difference between patients and controls, a sensitivity analysis was performed by restricting the control group to individuals aged >60 years to improve comparability and reduce potential bias from younger controls. Results were compared with those from the full cohort to assess robustness.

A post hoc power analysis was conducted using the pwr package in R. Power was estimated using a two‐sided α of 0.05 and a two‐proportions z‐test.

RESULTS

Cohort description

A total of 264 participants were included (63 AD patients and 201 healthy controls). The AD group was older than controls (69 ± 7.6 vs 57 ± 7.9 years). Sex distribution was comparable between groups (51% male in AD vs 55% in controls). Among AD patients, the mean age at symptom onset was 66 ± 7.9 years. Early‐onset AD (<65 years) accounted for 40% (n = 25), while 60% (n = 38) had late‐onset AD. A positive family history of neurodegenerative disease was reported in 27% (n = 17) of patients. Cognitive function was significantly impaired in AD patients (MMSE: 15 ± 6.5) compared with controls (28 ± 1.7). Full demographic and clinical details are provided in Table 1.

APOE frequencies, association with disease status, and gender effects

KASP assay performed very well. Genotyping was successful for all samples, with clear clustering and no missing calls. Both SNPs were in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium in controls (rs429358: χ 2 = 0.489, p = 0.484; rs7412: χ 2 = 0.262, p = 0.609).

The ε3/ε3 genotype was the most common in both groups (AD: 71.4%; controls: 70.1%), followed by ε3/ε4, ε2/ε3, ε4/ε4, ε2/ε4, and ε2/ε2. At the allelic level, ε3 predominated in both groups (AD: 83.3%; controls: 83.6%), followed by ε4 (AD: 12.7%; controls: 8.0%) and ε2 (AD: 4.0%; controls: 8.5%). Power analysis

Post hoc power analysis, based on APOE ε4 frequencies of 13% in cases and 8% in controls, indicated that the study had approximately 25% power to detect an effect size corresponding to an OR of ∼1.7 at α = 0.05. To achieve 80% power under the same assumptions, an estimated 500 participants per group would be required in a balanced design. Therefore, this study is underpowered and should be considered exploratory and hypothesis‐generating, providing preliminary evidence to inform future adequately powered studies.

DISCUSSION

Egypt, the most populous country in the Middle East, has a population exceeding 110 million and is undergoing rapid demographic aging. 15 This transition is expected to lead to a dramatic increase in the burden of AD and other dementias. 16 In addition, Egypt has a complex genetic architecture shaped by millennia of admixture among European, African, Mediterranean, and Asian populations. 17 Understanding genetic contributors to AD, including factors influencing APOE‐related risk, is therefore critical for addressing this emerging public health challenge.

Previous studies in Egyptian populations linked APOE variants to a range of phenotypes (Table S2). The ε4 allele has been associated with an increased risk of type 2 diabetes mellitus and acute coronary syndrome, while also appearing to be protective against fibrosis in hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection. 18 , 19 , 20 In contrast, the ε2 allele has been linked to obesity, an increased risk of diabetic nephropathy, and lower warfarin dose requirements. 21 , 22 No associations have been reported between APOE variants and Parkinson's disease or HCV susceptibility. 23 , 24

However, evidence on the association between APOE and AD in Egypt remains limited. To date, only one small single‐center case–control study directly examined this relationship. Ramadan et al., in a study from Lower Egypt involving 53 AD patients aged ≥60 years and 100 age‐matched controls, reported a higher ε4 allele frequency in patients (28.3%) compared to controls (4%) (p = 0.001). 5 More recently, Elbeh et al., in a cohort of 50 AD patients, examined sleep quality and found that the ε3/ε4 genotype (p < 0.001) and ε4 allele were significantly more frequent among patients with poorer sleep quality (p = 0.002). 25

In the present study, we examined the association between APOE allelic and genotypic variants and AD risk in a multicenter Egyptian cohort of clinically well‐characterized AD cases (n = 63) and controls (n = 201) recruited from both Upper and Lower Egypt. To our knowledge, this represents the largest multicenter study of APOE and AD in Egypt to date. Importantly, the cohort includes both early‐onset (<65 years) and late‐onset (>65 years) AD, as well as patients with a family history of neurodegenerative disease.

Consistent with previous reports, the ε4 allele frequency was higher in AD patients (12.7%) than in controls (8%), while the ε2 allele was less frequent in AD patients (4%) compared to controls (8.5%), in line with its widely reported protective effect. However, these differences did not reach statistical significance after adjustment for age, sex, and geographic area, likely reflecting limited statistical power due to the relatively small sample size. Nevertheless, the observed trends are directionally concordant with findings from Ramadan et al. and with the broader international literature. 5

Comparison of allele frequencies in control subjects across Egyptian cohorts (Table 4) indicates broad consistency with European populations. The ε3 allele was the most prevalent in our cohort (83.3%), closely matching European estimates (∼77% to 83%). A crude meta‐analysis of ε4 allele frequencies in the Egyptian control group in our cohort and previously reported studies (Table 4) yields a pooled estimate of approximately 7% to 8% (range: 4% to 12.5%), aligning closely with European populations (6% to 14%). Similarly, ε2 frequencies (range: 8.5% to 25%) were within the range reported globally (0% to 38%). Overall, both allele and genotype distributions in this study are comparable to those observed in European cohorts. 3 , 4

To explore potential APOE sex‐specific effects, we conducted exploratory sex‐stratified analyses. Previous studies suggested that female ε4 carriers might have a higher risk of AD and earlier disease onset compared to males, particularly between the ages of 65 and 75. 26 Additionally, dementia prevalence in Egypt appears higher among women, 27 potentially reflecting longer life expectancy and sociocultural factors such as differences in education. 27 , 28 , 29 In our cohort, sex‐stratified analyses did not yield statistically significant associations; however, given the limited sample size, these findings should be considered exploratory and not indicative of true sex‐specific genetic effects.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the modest sample size limits statistical power to detect small to moderate genetic effects. Post hoc power calculations suggest that substantially larger cohorts (e.g., ≥500 participants in each group) would be required to robustly assess the association between APOE and AD risk in this cohort. Second, the overall control group was younger (mean age 57 ± 7.9 years) than the AD group (mean age 69 ± 7.6 years). Although age‐stratified analyses were performed, including an older control subgroup (mean age 67.8 ± 6.3 years), this reduced the effective sample size and may have impacted statistical power. Similarly, subgroup analyses – particularly sex‐stratified comparisons – were underpowered and should be interpreted with caution.

The principal findings of this study are that APOE allele frequencies in Egyptians are broadly comparable to those observed in European populations and that the direction of association between the ε4 allele and AD risk is consistent with global evidence, although not statistically significant in this cohort. This study should therefore be considered a hypothesis‐generating pilot, providing foundational data to inform future investigations of APOE and AD in Egypt. Larger, well‐powered studies – ideally including at least 1000 participants (e.g., ≥500 AD cases and ≥500 controls) – will be essential to more definitively assess APOE‐related risk in this population. Importantly, the robust clinical characterization of participants in this study provides a strong framework for future research.
