Comparative analysis of carotenoid accumulation during the flowering period in three Coreopsis tinctoria cultivars with different petal colors
Authors: Shuaijie Gao, Hong Jiang, Zhiyuan Li, Yong Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Coreopsis tinctoria Nutt., known for its vibrant flower colors, possesses significant ornamental and medicinal value, making it a highly promising medicinal flower variety. It is widely favored by consumers as a health tea, with flower color being a crucial quality factor. The growth indicators and pigment content during the flow-ering period were determined in three C. tinctoria cultivars exhibiting different petal colors: ‘JS’ (entirely golden yellow), ‘LS’ (golden yellow tops with reddish-brown bases), and ‘MS’ (entirely reddish-brown). Additionally, a comparative metabolomic analysis of carotenoid metabolites was conducted. The results showed that the three C. tinctoria cultivars exhibited distinct differences in flower phenotypes and growth characteristics. For example, plant height and stem diameter significantly differed among ‘JS’, ‘LS’, and ‘MS’ at various flowering stages. The total carotenoid content of ‘JS’, ‘LS’, and ‘MS’ was mainly concentrated in the early flowering stage (S2), full flowering stage (S3) and late flowering stage (S4), with significant differences observed. ‘LS’ and ‘JS’ reached their highest total carotenoid content in stages S3 and S4, respectively. Analysis revealed 58 carotenoid metabolites (seven carotenes and 51 xanthophylls) with abundances that varied significantly by flower color and developmental stage. Of these, 12 metabolites (two carotenes and 10 xanthophylls) were significantly enriched in the carotenoid pathway and exhibited notably high accumulation during the S2 and S3 stages, suggesting their important role in petal coloration variation. This study provides a theoretical basis for understanding the flower color mechanism of C. tinctoria and lays a foundation for the extraction and utilization of its active components.

Introduction

Coreopsis tinctoria Nutt. (C. tinctoria), commonly known as “Kunlun snow chrysanthemum” or “Alpine snow chrysanthemum,” is referred to as Gulqai in the Uyghur language. It is an annual, glabrous, and aromatic herbaceous plant belonging to the Asteraceae family (Chinese Academy of Sciences, 1979). The dried inflorescence of C. tinctoria was first recorded in Chinese Materia Medica and it is traditionally described as sweet in taste and neutral in nature (Shen et al., 2021). Pishan and Minfeng counties in Hotan, Xinjiang, are the primary production center of Xinjiang snow chrysanthemum, where it is commonly consumed as a tea (Yao et al., 2016; Xu, Chen & Chen, 2022). Modern pharmacological studies indicate that long-term consumption of the tea helps to regulate blood pressure, blood lipids, and blood sugar levels, which may help prevent cardiovascular diseases and diabetes. (Yang et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2024; Lan, Lin & Zheng, 2014). In December 2023, C. tinctoria was approved as a new food raw material in China and is now widely cultivated nationwide, showing great potential as a dual-purpose material for both food and medicine. Flower color is a key quality indicator of C. tinctoria and directly reflects its ornamental value. It exhibits a rich diversity in flower colors and phenotypes, such as solid yellow, bicolored, and solid red petals, along with ecotypes including flat-petaled, narrow-petaled, and double-petaled varieties, among other characteristics (He et al., 2025). Studies have shown that flavonoids, carotenoids, and alkaloids are the primary pigment compounds in plant flower coloration. Flavonoids and carotenoids, as important secondary metabolites, participate in regulating various physiological and biochemical processes in plants, including growth and development as well as responses to adverse environmental stresses (Yu et al., 2024a; Hao, Shi & Chen, 2022). However, current research on C. tinctoria has mainly focused on the extraction and identification of flavonoid components (Ren et al., 2019; Kang, Yang & Kang, 2018), with limited systematic studies on its phenotype. Previous studies have identified 158 flavonoid compounds in the flower buds and inflorescences of ‘LS’ and ‘JS’ at different flowering stages. ‘LS’ accumulated various anthocyanins during its flowering period, with cyanidin being the primary coloring substance. These findings lay the groundwork for understanding the coloration mechanism of C. tinctoria (Jiang et al., 2022). Carotenoids, which impart yellow, orange, and other colors to plants, serve as accessory pigments in the photosynthetic process. Recent studies on carotenoids have made significant progress in understanding their biosynthesis and nutritional improvement in plants such as pomegranate (Zhang et al., 2025a; Zhang et al., 2025b), poaceae crops (Tang et al., 2022), pepper (Zhang et al., 2024), oil palm (Zhang et al., 2025a; Zhang et al., 2025b), and hybrid lilies (Yu et al., 2024a; Yu et al., 2024b) through the application of high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS/MS) (Wang et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2024). However, relatively few studies have focused on the role of carotenoids in plant flower coloration (Fan et al., 2019; Xu, Liu & Xu, 2020). Despite the diverse flower colors and phenotypes observed in C. tinctoria, the potential role of carotenoids in their coloration and biological characteristics requires further investigation. In this study, a comparative analysis of carotenoids was conducted at different flowering stages in three cultivars of C. tinctoria, using both traditional biological methods and advanced UPLC-MS/MS technology. The objectives were to determine the carotenoid content and composition in C. tinctoria from Xinjiang, China, identify key differentially accumulated carotenoids, and lay a theoretical foundation for breeding new varieties and deep processing.

Materials & Methods

Plant materials

The experimental materials comprised seeds of three C. tinctoria cultivars from Xinjiang, China: ‘JS’ (yellow petals), ‘LS’ (petals yellow in the top and reddish-brown at the base), and ‘MS’ (reddish-brown petals). The trial was carried out between April and September 2024 at the Horticultural Science Practice Education Base of Xinjiang Agricultural University (E 87.35, N 43.95). In early April, plug seedling cultivation was initiated in a greenhouse using 50-cell trays, with 2–3 seeds sown per cell. At 14 days after emergence, one seedling was retained per cell. During the seedling stage, a diluted (1/2 to 1x) Hoagland nutrient solution was applied every 10 days. By late May, seedlings with 6–8 true leaves were transplanted to the field with a plant spacing of 40 cm × 60 cm. Uniform water and fertilizer management were maintained throughout the growth period. When the three cultivars of C. tinctoria (‘LS’, ‘JS’, and ‘MS’) reached the flowering stage, flower buds and fresh petals were collected at four different stages: the bud stage (S1), early flowering stage (S2), full flowering stage (S3), and late flowering stage (S4), with three biological replicates per stage. All samples were collected using sampling tubes, immediately submerged in liquid nitrogen for 20 min, and then transferred to a −80 °C freezer for long-term storage. Detailed sample labeling information is provided in Table 1.

Physiological index and growth determination

During stages S1, S2, S3, and S4 of the flowering period, 15 plants were randomly selected for measurement of plant height and stem diameter using a vernier caliper and a ruler. Total anthocyanin and total chlorophyll contents were measured using the method described by Xiao et al. (2025), which utilizes assay kits from Suzhou Geruisi Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Chemicals and instruments

Chemicals: methanol, acetonitrile, and ethanol (chromatographic grade; Merck, Germany); acetone (chromatographic grade; Sinopharm, Beijing, China); formic acid (chromatographic grade; Sigma-Aldrich); methyl tert-butyl ether, n-hexane (chromatographic grade; CNW, Düsseldorf, Germany); 2,6-di-tert-butyl-p-cresol (BHT, chromatographic grade; Aladdin, Shanghai, China); dichloromethane (chromatographic grade; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA); sodium chloride and potassium hydroxide (China). Standard compounds (>99%, BOC/Sigma-Aldrich dichloromethane, one mg/mL).

Instruments: liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) system (QTRAP 6500+ SCIEX); refrigerated centrifuge (5424R Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany); electronic balance (AS 60/220.R2, RADWAG, Poland); ball mill (MM 400 Retsch, Haan-Gruiten, Germany); centrifugal concentrator (CentriVap; Labconco); multi-tube vortex mixer (MIX-200; Jingxin, Shanghai, China); ultrasonic cleaner (KQ5200E; ShuMei, Kunshan, China).

Sample preparation and extraction

The sample extraction was performed according to the following procedure. Initially, the freeze-dried samples stored at ultra-low temperature were removed and ground into a fine powder using a grinding mill (MM 400; Retsch) at 30 Hz for 1 min. Subsequently, 50 mg of the homogenized powder was accurately weighed and extracted with 0.5 mL of an n-hexane/acetone/ethanol (1:1:1, v/v/v) solution containing 0.01% (w/v) butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT). The mixture was vortexed at ambient temperature for 20 min and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected after this centrifugation, and the extraction process was repeated once with the pellet. The resulting supernatants were combined following the second centrifugation step. Finally, the combined extract was concentrated under a gentle stream of nitrogen, reconstituted in 100 µL of dichloromethane, filtered through a 0.22 µm sterile membrane filter, and stored in amber vials prior to LC-MS/MS analysis.

Chromatography-mass spectrometry acquisition conditions

The analysis was performed using an ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) system (ExionLC™ AD; SCIEX) coupled with a QTRAP® 6500+ tandem mass spectrometer (SCIEX). Chromatographic separation was achieved on a YMC C30 column (3 µm, 100 mm × 2.0 mm i.d.) maintained at 28 °C. The mobile phase consisted of phase A (methanol/acetonitrile, 1:3, v/v, containing 0.01% BHT and 0.1% formic acid) and phase B (methyl tert-butyl ether with 0.01% BHT). A gradient elution program was applied at a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min as follows: 100% A from 0 to 3 min; a linear change to 30% A/70% B by 5 min; followed by a transition to 5% A/95% B at 9 min; the system was then returned to 100% A at 10 min and held until 11 min. The injection volume was 2 µL. Mass spectrometric detection was conducted with an Atmospheric Pressure Chemical Ionization (APCI) source operated at 350 °C. The curtain gas was set to 25 psi. Data acquisition was performed in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode, with the declustering potential and collision energy individually optimized for each target ion pair (Zhou et al., 2020).

Carotenoid identification and quantification

Qualitative analysis of mass spectrometry data was performed using the Metware Database (MWDB). Carotenoid quantification was carried out using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode in triple quadrupole mass spectrometry. After obtaining the mass spectrometry data of carotenoids from samples via MRM, the chromatographic peaks of all carotenoids were integrated, and peak integration correction was applied for the same compound across different samples.

Data evaluation

The mass spectrometry data were acquired and processed using Analyst 1.6.3 software. The analysis was performed in positive ionization mode using the Atmospheric Pressure Chemical Ionization (APCI) source. The Total Ion Chromatograms (TIC) and Extracted Ion Chromatograms (XIC) presented in Figs. S1 and S2 were obtained in this positive ion mode. The chromatographic peak area data was acquired corresponding to the quantitative signals of each concentration standard. Calibration curves were constructed for individual analytes by plotting standard concentrations on the x-axis against their respective chromatographic peak areas on the y-axis. The linear equations and correlation coefficients of the calibration curves for the target analytes are in Table S1 (Schweiggert et al., 2016; Qi et al., 2017). The integrated peak area was substituted into the standard curve equation for calculation, followed by further computation using the formula to determine the absolute carotenoid content in the samples. Data processing

All data are presented as the mean values from three biological replicates. Plant height and stem diameter are expressed as the mean ± standard error (SE). Other data were subjected to statistical analysis using SPSS software (version 22.0), with a significance level set at P < 0.05. The cluster heatmap was generated using R software (version 4.2.0; R Core Team, 2022).

Results

Changes in the pigment content and growth of three cultivars of C. tinctoria in flowering stages

As shown in Fig. 1A, the buds and inflorescences of three cultivars of C. tinctoria exhibit significant phenotypic differences, particularly in petal color. The plant heights of the three cultivars of C. tinctoria at the same stage exhibited significant differences. During stages S1 to S4, the ‘JS’ had the tallest plant height, followed by ‘LS’, while ’MS’ was the shortest. Specifically at stage S4, ‘JS’ reached the maximum height of 98.280 cm; ‘LS’ ranked second with 95.900 cm, and ‘MS’ was the shortest. Compared to ‘JS’ and ‘LS’, the height differences for ‘MS’ were 48.990 cm and 46.610 cm, respectively (Fig. 1B). And the stem diameters of the three cultivars of C. tinctoria at the same flowering stage exhibited significant differences. During stages S1-S4, the ‘JS’ had relatively thicker stems, ‘LS’ ranked second, while ‘MS’ had relatively thinner stems. Specifically at stage S4, the stem diameter of ‘JS’ was 8.440 mm, ‘LS’ ranked second with 6.710 mm, while ‘MS’ had relatively thinner stems at 4.880 mm compared to ’JS’ (Fig. 1C). Regarding total carotenoid content, significant differences were observed among the three cultivars of C. tinctoria at the same stages (S2-S4). At stage S3, ‘LS’ exhibited relatively higher total carotenoid content, while at stage S4, ‘JS’ showed the highest total carotenoid content (Fig. 1D). Regarding total chlorophyll content, at stage S1, ‘LS’, ‘JS’, and ‘MS’ all reached their highest levels, though no significant differences were found among them. During stages S2-S4, however, significant differences in total chlorophyll content emerged among ‘LS’, ‘JS’, and ‘MS’. Furthermore, at stage S4, total chlorophyll content was generally lower across all varieties, with ‘MS’ exhibiting the lowest content (Fig. 1E).

Sample quality control analysis

A mixed solution was used as the quality control (QC) sample. The stability of the instrument during the detection period was assessed by overlaying total ion chromatograms (TIC) of the same QC sample analyzed by mass spectrometry, as shown in Fig. S3. The coefficient of variation (CV) represents the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean of the original data, reflecting data dispersion. The empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF) was used to analyze the frequency of compounds with CV values below the reference threshold. As shown in Fig. 2, a higher proportion of compounds in QC samples with lower CV values indicates greater experimental data stability. More than 80% of the compounds in QC samples had CV values below 0.3, demonstrating the stability and reliability of the experimental data.

Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis

The orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) model was used to analyze metabolomic data to maximize differences between groups, making it an effective method for screening differential metabolites. As shown in Fig. 4, at the same flowering stage in three cultivars of C. tinctoria, the inter-group differences were significant, while samples within the same group were mostly clustered together, consistent with the results in Fig. 3. As illustrated in Fig. 5, the OPLS-DA model in this study evaluated differences between LC1_vs_JC1_vs_MC1 (R2X = 0.912, R2Y = 0.994, Q2 = 0.984, Fig. 5A), LC2_vs_JC2_vs_MC2 (R2X = 0.823, R2Y = 0.99, Q2 = 0.981, Fig. 5B), LC3_vs_JC3_vs_MC3 (R2X = 0.75, R2Y = 0.989, Q2 = 0.960, Fig. 5C), and LC4_vs_JC4_vs_MC4 (R2X = 0.831, R2Y = 0.996, Q2 = 0.982, Fig. 5D) by comparing the levels of carotenoid metabolites in paired samples. Q2 is an important parameter for evaluating the OPLS-DA model, where Q2 > 0.5 indicates a valid model, and Q2 > 0.9 signifies an excellent model. All control group models were valid, and the Q2 values for all groups exceeded 0.90, indicating that these models are stable and reliable for further screening of different carotenoid metabolites.

Screening and analysis of differential metabolites

Among them, the 10 carotenoids with the highest cumulative VIP values across the four flowering stages were α-cryptoxanthin (5.43), zeaxanthin-palmitate-stearate (5.40), violaxanthin dimyristate (5.31), (E/Z)-phytoene (5.26), neoxanthin (5.18), violaxanthin dioleate (5.14), α-carotene (4.02), violaxanthin laurate (3.87), violaxanthin palmitate (3.59), and violaxanthin (2.84). However, lutein dipalmitate (1.11) and β-cryptoxanthin myristate (1.15) among the lowest VIP values.

KEGG enrichment analysis of key differentially accumulated carotenoid metabolites

To explore the internal metabolic relationships among differentially accumulated carotenoid metabolites in three cultivars of C. tinctoria, KEGG functional annotation and pathway enrichment analyses were conducted. As shown in Fig. 8, the comparison groups LC1_vs_JC1_vs_MC1, LC2_vs_JC2_vs_MC2, LC3_vs_JC3_vs_MC3, and LC4_vs_JC4_vs_MC4 at different flowering stages showed that 8, 10, 9, and 10 differentially accumulated carotenoids, respectively, were significantly enriched in the metabolic pathways (ko01100), biosynthesis of secondary metabolites (ko01110), carotenoid biosynthesis (ko00906), biosynthesis of various plant secondary metabolites (ko00999), and biosynthesis of cofactors (ko01240) pathways. A total of 12 differentially accumulated carotenoid metabolites were significantly enriched at one or more flowering stages, including seven carotenes and five xanthophylls. Heatmap analysis of these 12 key metabolites clearly showed their distribution across different stages (Fig. 9). Metabolites such as phytofluene, β-carotene, violaxanthin, neoxanthin, and β-cryptoxanthin were significantly accumulated at the S1 (bud stage) in the three cultivars. Meanwhile, ɛ-carotene, γ-carotene, lycopene, and lutein exhibited significant changes during the S2–S3 stages. Additionally, (E/Z)-phytoene was significantly accumulated in ‘LS’ and ‘JS’ during the S2–S4 stages, while α-cryptoxanthin was significantly accumulated in ‘JS’ during the S2–S3 stages. These findings suggest that these differentially accumulated metabolites play crucial biological roles in the carotenoid metabolic pathways of the three C. tinctoria cultivars.

Discussion

C. tinctoria was introduced to China as an ornamental plant in the early 20th century and has since been widely cultivated. Xinjiang is a major production area for C. tinctoria, and those grown in the Hotan region exhibit distinct regional characteristics. Local residents sell dried flower heads of C. tinctoria as a specialty agricultural product (herbal tea) and also use them as a medicinal ingredient in Uighur medicine. Flower color is an important quality characteristic of C. tinctoria. The flowers are brightly colored, typically with ray florets exhibiting a reddish-brown base and a yellow apex (the ratio of reddish-brown to yellow areas ranges from 0 to 9/10) (Shen et al., 2021). In the locally cultivated C. tinctoria in Xinjiang, China, three distinct flower color types have been identified: those with a reddish-brown to yellow ratio of 2/3 to 3/4 (designated as ‘LS’), those with entirely yellow ray florets (designated as ‘JS’), and those with entirely red ray florets (designated as ‘MS’). Our previous study revealed that the petal coloration of C. tinctoria (‘JS’, ‘LS’, and ‘MS’) is directly associated with flavonoids, with cyanidin playing a key role in the reddish-brown regions of the petals (Jiang et al., 2022). Additionally, significant positive correlations were observed between total flavonoid content, total anthocyanin content, and plant height, indicating the direct influence of flavonoids on both flower color formation and plant growth (He et al., 2025). Carotenoids are widely occurring secondary metabolites and pigments in horticultural plants, playing an essential role in plant growth, development, and ornamental value (Zeng et al., 2022; Tian et al., 2022). This study investigated the relationship between carotenoids and flower color formation using ‘JS’, ‘LS’, and ‘MS’ as experimental materials. Significant differences in petal color, plant height, and stem diameter were observed among the three C. tinctoria cultivars during the flowering period (Figs. 1A–1C). Starting from the S2 stage, the total carotenoid content significantly increased in all three cultivars, with notable differences among them. The results demonstrated that although ‘JS’ exhibits lighter coloration, it accumulated the highest total carotenoid content by the late flowering stage (S4). Throughout the flowering period (S2-S4), the total carotenoid content in ‘MS’ remained lower than in both ‘JS’ and ‘LS’ (Fig. 1D). These findings are consistent with the results reported by Qing et al. (2022) in their study on two common zinnia cultivars, ‘Dreamland Red’ (DRE) and ‘Dreamland Yellow’ (DY). In this study, targeted carotenoid metabolomics identified a total of 58 carotenoid metabolites, with carotenes accounting for 12.07% and xanthophylls comprising 87.93%. Zhang et al. (2020) demonstrated that in the yellow petals of Tagetes standii, over 90% of carotenoids are xanthophylls and their precursors (β-carotene and ɛ-carotene). The findings of this study are consistent with Zhang et al.’s (2020) research on marigolds. The contents of 58 carotenoid metabolites showed significant variations across the four developmental stages, with distinct clustering patterns observed between flower buds (S1) and petals (S2-S4). Starting from the S2 stage, xanthophylls including lutein dipalmitate, lutein distearate, and lutein began to accumulate during the flowering period (S2-S4), reaching their highest concentrations in ‘LS’, ‘JS’, and ‘MS’ respectively. These findings suggest that these metabolites may differentially contribute to yellow coloration in the petals of ‘LS’, ‘JS’, and ‘MS’ cultivars. A study by Park et al. (2017) on carotenoids in six marigold cultivars revealed significant variation in specific carotenoid content among different cultivars, which is consistent with the findings of our research. The yellow, orange to orange-red coloration in plant petals is primarily attributed to the accumulation of β-carotene, lutein, and lycopene in certain species (Lu et al., 2022). This study identified 12 differentially accumulated carotenoid metabolites that were significantly enriched in the carotenoid biosynthesis pathway, including phytofluene, lycopene, and (E/Z)-phytoene. Among these, (E/Z)-phytoene accumulated significantly during the flowering period (S2-S4) in ‘LS’ and ‘JS’, but remained at low levels in ‘MS’. Lutein content peaked at the S2 stage in all three varieties (‘LS’, ‘JS’, and ‘MS’) and progressively decreased as flowering progressed. β-cryptoxanthin accumulation was highest in ‘JS’ during the S2-S3 stages, markedly exceeding levels in ‘LS’ and ‘MS’. These key differentially accumulated carotenoids may be directly involved in yellow coloration of the petals across the three C. tinctoria cultivars. Flower color formation is a complex process influenced by multiple factors (Liang et al., 2024). Building upon previous research on flavonoid pigments in ‘LS’, ‘JS’, and ‘MS’, it can be concluded that petal coloration in C. tinctoria results from the synergistic effects of flavonoids (particularly anthocyanins) and carotenoids. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying color regulation require further investigation.

Conclusion

This study conducted a preliminary investigation into the formation mechanism of floral pigment (carotenoid) differences among three cultivars of C. tinctoria. The results revealed significant variations in phenotypic traits (plant height and stem diameter) across different flowering stages in the three cultivars (‘JS’, ‘LS’, ‘MS’), suggesting a potential intrinsic relationship between their growth characteristics and flower color types. From the early to full flowering stages (S2–S4), significant differences were observed in the accumulation of total carotenoid content. Further targeted metabolomics analysis identified substantial changes in 10 key lutein derivatives, indicating their crucial biological role in petal color development during the flowering period. This study provides clear metabolic targets and critical time nodes for in-depth research on the flower color mechanism of C. tinctoria, and establishes a solid theoretical and practical foundation for metabolically guided, precision harvesting and processing techniques.
