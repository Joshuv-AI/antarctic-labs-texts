CrossFuse-XGBoost: accurate prediction of the maximum recommended daily dose through multi-feature fusion, cross-validation screening and extreme gradient boosting
Authors: Qiang Li, Yu He, Jianbo Pan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the drug development process, approximately 30% of failures are attributed to drug safety issues. In particular, the first-in-human (FIH) trial of a new drug represents one of the highest safety risks, and initial dose selection is crucial for ensuring safety in clinical trials. With traditional dose estimation methods, which extrapolate data from animals to humans, catastrophic events have occurred during Phase I clinical trials due to interspecies differences in compound sensitivity and unknown molecular mechanisms. To address this issue, this study proposes a CrossFuse-extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) method that can directly predict the maximum recommended daily dose of a compound based on existing human research data, providing a reference for FIH dose selection. This method not only integrates multiple features, including molecular representations, physicochemical properties and compound–protein interactions, but also improves feature selection based on cross-validation. The results demonstrate that the CrossFuse-XGBoost method not only improves prediction accuracy compared to that of existing local weighted methods [k-nearest neighbor (k-NN) and variable k-NN (v-NN)] but also solves the low prediction coverage issue of v-NN, achieving full coverage of the external validation set and enabling more reliable predictions. Furthermore, this study offers a high level of interpretability by identifying the importance of different features in model construction. The 241 features with the most significant impact on the maximum recommended daily dose were selected, providing references for optimizing the structure of new compounds and guiding experimental research. The datasets and source code are freely available at https://github.com/cqmu-lq/CrossFuse-XGBoost.Keywords: CrossFuse-XGBoost, multi-feature fusion, cross-validation screening, maximum recommended daily dose

INTRODUCTION

There are three major challenges in the development of new drugs: high costs, long development cycles and low success rates. A study conducted by Wouters et al. indicates that the median investment to introduce a new pharmaceutical product to the market is estimated to be $1.1 billion. The mean number of years of data per drug is 8.3 years [1, 2]. According to statistics, the attrition rate of candidate drugs during the new drug development process is as high as 96%, with drug safety–related attrition accounting for 30% of this rate [3]. Dosage is an important factor affecting drug safety, and many analyses suggest that daily dosage levels are important parameters in differentiating between safe and unsafe drugs [4]. The initiation of a new drug is the most risk-laden stage of a clinical trial. The choice of the initial dose in Phase I clinical trials is a pivotal factor that impacts the experiment [5]. The TGN1412 incident [6] that occurred in the UK in 2006 and the fatty acid amide hydrolase (FAAH) Phase I clinical trial incident in France in 2016 [7, 8] both resulted in catastrophic outcomes such as death and injuries. TGN1412 re-entered clinical trials under the new name TAB08 in 2011, with its dose reduced by 1000 times compared to that in the 2006 trial, and better tolerance was demonstrated. Currently, it has advanced into Phase II clinical trials [9].

Mishra et al. highlight the great importance of accurately calculating the dosage for first-in-human (FIH) clinical trials. They emphasize that past catastrophic failures in this area have led to a decline in the registration of Phase I clinical trials. The authors summarize various methodologies for estimating the FIH dosage [10]. For example, the no-observable-adverse-effect level (NOAEL) approach initially extrapolates the NOAELs determined in preclinical animal studies to the human equivalent dose (HED) using appropriate scaling factors. Subsequently, the HED is adjusted by safety factors to determine the maximum recommended daily dose (MRDD) for human subjects.

Despite the completion of a multitude of animal experiments before phase I clinical trials, the risk of drug use significantly increases when extrapolating to humans due to species differences that have not yet been fully understood. The fundamental assumption when estimating human FIH doses from animal doses is that a compound’s toxicity in animals is correlated with its toxicity in humans. In a study by Matt et al., using 326 compounds, it was found that the correlation coefficient (R) between the human MRDD and the respective rodent maximum tolerated dose (MTD) was only approximately 0.2, demonstrating that this correlation is far from ideal. This indicates considerable uncertainty in this method [11]. A post hoc study of TGN1412 found that the CD4+ effector memory cells of the cynomolgus monkeys used for preclinical toxicity validation lacked CD28 expression. This is an example of how unpredictable effects occur despite a 500-fold reduction in dose from animal experiments to FIH trials [12].

The MRDD is estimated based on human clinical trials as the upper limit of a drug’s daily dose. Contrera et al. noted that the uncertainties and errors associated with the MRDD derived from quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) modeling using human data will be smaller than those using current methods of extrapolation from animal data [13]. If we can predict the MRDD of a new compound using existing clinical research data, we can provide a reference for the selection of the starting dose in phase I clinical trials, reducing the number of animals used and the costs associated with preliminary toxicology studies and thereby shortening the research timeline.

In a literature search, we identified three QSAR studies that quantitatively predicted the MRDD. Two of these studies were from the Informatics and Computational Safety Analysis Staff at the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA). In the first study, Matthews et al. decomposed compounds into fragments ranging from 2 to 10 atoms. Statistical analysis was then used to identify fragments that might contribute to adverse reactions. Each fragment was assigned a value indicating its contribution to the MRDD, and a prediction model was constructed based on the contributions of these fragments. The results showed that the estimation error for the MRDD was less than ten times the experimental value [11]. In the second study, Contrera et al. employed the k-nearest neighbor (k-NN) algorithm. The average MRDD of the k-nearest neighboring compounds to the object to be predicted (based on the Tanimoto similarity) was used as the predicted value [13]. The third study was conducted by Ruifeng Liu et al. in 2012. They improved the k-NN method by dynamically adjusting the k value based on the Tanimoto similarity, proposing the variable k-NN (v-NN) method. Although the accuracy was increased to some extent, the applicability of the method is limited [14].

Although the MRDD can be predicted relatively well using these methods, the above QSAR methods still have some drawbacks. First, the decomposition of compounds is overly simplistic. Second, these methods do not consider the influence of molecular mechanisms, such as the drug’s target and transport proteins within the human body. Third, the methods use relatively simple QSAR models. To address these issues, we proposed a method that combines various feature data, namely, molecular fingerprints, molecular mechanisms and molecule–protein interactions, using an optimized extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) model [15] to predict a drug’s MRDD. This model demonstrates further improvement in terms of accuracy compared to that of the v-NN method. Our research can provide maximum dose references for clinical studies of new drugs, which will help reduce the risk of clinical trials and protect trial participants. Additionally, by analyzing the importance of these features in the modelling process, we explored the key factors influencing a drug’s MRDD. These findings can provide helpful references for preclinical research in new drug screening and development.

Dataset preparation

Training and external validation sets

The training set of human MRDD was obtained from the DSSTox (FDAMDD) FDA Maximum (Recommended) Daily Dose Database [16, 17]. This database was derived from 2114 drugs in the FDA/Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER) toxicology and Substance Registry Services (SRS) databases. Inorganic substances, high-molecular-weight polymers (>5000 daltons), fibers, salts, mixtures of organic chemicals and molecules with a molecular weight of <100 daltons, which are unsuitable for QSAR modeling, were excluded. Ultimately, a total of 1216 data entries were retained for further analysis. The data were determined from pharmaceutical clinical trials that employed an oral route of exposure and daily treatments, usually for 3–12 months. Some adult MRDDs reported as mg/day were converted to mg/kg-bw/day based upon an average adult weight of 60 kg.

The FDA group also assembled a set of 160 external validation compounds, which were not included in the DSSTox Database, gathered from public sources and FDA files [13]. From these, proprietary compounds with undisclosed structures and compounds not recorded in PubChem or DrugBank were excluded. This resulted in a total of 145 MRDD data points to serve as an external validation set.

Data inspection

Data inspection is the process of examining and validating the correctness and presence of outliers in data before conducting data analysis. In this study, the PubChem compound identifier (CID) was selected as the unique data identifier. During the uniqueness check, duplicate entries with a CID of 443 955 were identified and removed from the external validation set. For outlier detection, values exceeding three times the interquartile range (IQR) are considered extreme outliers [18]. The training set contained 141 such values, while the external validation set had 12. However, considering that these approved drugs have undergone experimental validation, it is not advisable to treat them as outliers. Supplementary Figure S1 available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/ shows the distributions of these data.

Dosage unit conversion for QSAR modeling

Oral MRDD values are typically expressed in mg/kg-bw/day, aiding comprehension for physicians, pharmacists and patients. However, in research, chemical reactions occur among atoms, ions or molecules and are subject to specific quantitative relationships. Therefore, in QSAR modeling, it is more appropriate to use molar mass to simulate the relationship between dosage and pharmacological response, typically adopting units of mmol/kg/day [14]. On the other hand, the repetition frequency and continuity of data affect the generalizability, stability, accuracy, interpretability and applicability of the model. When the labeled data have a low average number of replicates, it indicates that each unique combination of input variables has a higher chance of being represented in the dataset. This improves the regression model’s generalizability, as it can learn from a wider range of instances and capture the underlying patterns more effectively. Good continuity in the labelled data ensures a smoother relationship between the input variables and the output variable. In this dataset, if the units of the 1216 dosage data points are expressed in mg/kg, there would only be 181 unique entries after deduplication, with an average replicate count of 6.72 (Formula (1)). However, after conversion to mmol/kg-bw/day, the number of unique entries increased to 1193, with an average replicate count of 1.02. Therefore, the transformed data exhibit lower repetitiveness and better continuity.

Dose distribution transformation in QSAR modeling

Skewness is a statistical measure that quantifies the asymmetry of a probability distribution [19]. Figure 2A and B illustrates the distributions of the training and external validation sets, respectively, with skewness values of 15.43 and 6.03 (Formula (2)), respectively. Significant data skewness is observed. It is generally considered that a skewness above 3 means that the data are highly skewed. Such data tend to have a large mean squared error (MSE), which not only destabilizes the results predicted by the machine learning model, causing significant fluctuations, but can also lead to underfitting or overfitting, thereby decreasing the predictive accuracy [20]. Common methods to reduce data skewness include logarithmic transformation, Box–Cox transformation and data binning [21–23]. Box–Cox transformation approximates a normal distribution by estimating the optimal power parameter (lambda). However, for datasets with extreme skewness, this transformation may excessively compress or distort the data, compromising the accuracy of the analysis. Data binning is not suitable for datasets with a small amount of data because useful information may be lost after grouping, which would affect the model accuracy. Therefore, logarithmic transformation was chosen to reduce data skewness. After reduction, the standard errors of skewness were −0.30 and −0.16, respectively. As presented in Figure 2D–F, the skewness of the transformed data was significantly reduced. The distribution of each dataset is more uniform, and the degree of dispersion decreases.

Feature data selection

MRDD determination is the result of a series of complex drug interactions within the body [25]. Initially, physicochemical properties, such as the molecular weight, polar surface area and the number of hydrogen bond donors and acceptors, affect the absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion of a drug in the body. Second, certain key substructures known to cause toxicity, including aniline, 4-hydroxyaniline and thiazole, have been proven to be directly associated with toxicity [26, 27]. Furthermore, drugs exert their effects by affecting the synthesis, modification, activation or inhibition of proteins [28]. For instance, for drugs that exert their effects by binding to receptors on cells, their chemical reactivity, molecule shape and size and spatial arrangement are tailored to suit these receptors. Therefore, in this study, we chose to incorporate information on drug–protein interactions; physicochemical properties; and absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion and toxicity (ADMET), among other data types, to construct a QSAR model for predicting the MRDD.

Chemical–protein links from STITCH

Interactions between drugs and proteins constitute a significant part of biological processes in living organisms. The effects of a drug on an organism, including its clinical efficacy, are determined by its interaction with target proteins and the extent to which it disrupts the networks of protein–protein and protein–compound interactions [29, 30].

We collected proteins that interact with these drugs from the STITCH database [31]. The combined score between drugs and proteins signifies the affinity between them. In the process of constructing the MRDD model, we incorporated these protein features for two primary reasons. First, they can further enhance the robustness of the model. Second, based on the degree of influence these features have on the results, they could aid in identifying key proteins that impact a drug’s MRDD.

DrugBank data

Target proteins, enzymes, transporter proteins and carrier proteins are crucial components of a drug’s mechanism of action. For example, the target protein directly binds with drugs, influencing their distribution. DrugBank is an integrated database that combines bioinformatics and cheminformatics resources, offering detailed drug data, target information and mechanisms [32]. We extracted information related to the target proteins, enzymes, transporter proteins and carrier proteins interacting with the selected drugs from this comprehensive database.

ADMETlab data

ADMETlab 2.0 is an online web service designed for predicting the metabolic kinetics and toxicity features of compounds based on their chemical structures [33]. This platform is capable of calculating 88 feature parameters of compounds, including 17 physicochemical properties, 13 medicinal chemistry properties, 23 ADME properties, 27 toxicity endpoints and 8 toxicophore rules (encompassing 751 substructures). The models employed within ADMETlab 2.0 are based on a multitask graph attention framework that primarily consists of regression and classification tasks. For regression tasks, such as Caco-2 permeability and plasma protein binding, specific predictive values are provided. For classification tasks, such as P-glycoprotein (gp) inhibitors and human ether-a-go-go-related gene (hERG) blockers, the prediction results are rendered as probability values.

Molecular descriptors and fingerprints

Currently, there are numerous ways to represent molecules, including chemical formulas, SMILES, InChI, molfile, SDfile and others. Molecular fingerprints are an abstract representation of molecules; they encode molecules into bit strings and use fixed-size arrays to represent different sizes of molecules for computational input. There are four common types of molecular fingerprints: substructure key-based fingerprints, topological or path-based fingerprints, circular fingerprints and pharmacophore fingerprints [34]. No single chemical fingerprint can capture all the pivotal structures or properties of compounds (or ligand–target interactions) [35]. Each type of fingerprint has inevitable limitations in terms of describing the structural features of a molecule, and the combination of multiple fingerprints can compensate for the inadequacies of single fingerprint representation. In this study, after considering the features of various fingerprints and comparing their actual effects, a combination of four fingerprints was ultimately selected: CircularFingerprint, MACCSKeysFingerprint, PubChemFingerprint and RDKitDescriptors [36, 37].

Chemical–disease associations

The onset of disease results from the interactions of multiple molecular mechanisms. Drugs exert therapeutic effects by influencing these molecular mechanisms. For example, paclitaxel prevents the proliferation and spread of cancer cells by inhibiting microtubule assembly and cell division [38]. Therefore, the interaction information between compounds and diseases contains potential overall molecular mechanisms. Based on this, we selected chemical–disease association data from the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD) [39] as features to uncover the influence of potential molecular mechanisms on the MRDD.

C max and LD50

Pharmacokinetic studies primarily focus on the metabolism and elimination of drugs within the body, which significantly influence the drug dosage. Among the important parameters in pharmacokinetics are Cmax and LD50. Cmax is the highest concentration of the drug in the blood after administration, serving as a critical indicator of the drug’s rate and extent of absorption in a human body. Formula (3) elucidates that Cmax is directly proportional to the administered dose. LD50, on the other hand, refers to the dose under certain experimental conditions that results in mortality in 50% of the validation subjects. The LD50 value represents the degree of acute toxicity of a drug: the lower the LD50 value is, the higher the toxicity of the drug [40]. These two important features were extracted from the literature and structured in the Digital Drug Property Database (DDPD) [41]. We matched the Cmax and LD50 of some drugs as training features.

Feature engineering

Feature engineering is the process of enhancing the quality of raw data through methods such as transformation, selection and extraction [42]. The scope of feature engineering encompasses the reduction of unreasonable values, imputation of missing values and derivation of new features. The primary objective of this process is to construct a more meaningful and effective feature set. This assists machine learning algorithms in better identifying the patterns and rules within the data, thereby enhancing the predictive power of the models.

Missing values

Missing data are a common problem in machine learning [43]. Within the collected feature data, there are two null values and eighteen instances of infinity for the ADMET2.0-related feature ‘Flex’. Given the relative scarcity of missing data, we adopted the most prevalent methods for handling such instances. For null values, we employed mode imputation, while for infinite values, we substituted them with the maximum value. Considering the large number of missing values in the Cmax and LD50 data, simple imputation methods, such as mean or mode imputation, could introduce considerable bias. Expectation–maximization, while powerful, relies heavily on assumptions about the distribution of the data, which may not hold when the proportion of missing data is high. To provide more reasonable imputations, we evaluated several techniques, including hot-deck imputation, multiple imputation and machine learning–based methods [44]. We mainly considered machine learning imputation methods because most machine learning methods can capture the interdependencies between different types of features; Cmax and LD50 are correlated with physicochemical properties and other ADMET features. We performed 5-fold cross-validation on the non-missing data using the k-NN, support vector machine (SVM) and random forest algorithms. With these three algorithms, the average correlation coefficient (R) values for Cmax were 0.36, 0.51 and 0.65, respectively; for LD50, they were 0.22, 0.43 and 0.57, respectively. Based on these results, we ultimately chose the random forest algorithm to impute missing values.

Feature derivation

Feature derivation refers to the generation of new features from original data, with feature combination being a commonly employed method for such derivation. Feature combination often allows non-linear relationships between features and the dependent variable to be captured, thereby helping to reveal latent relationships within the dataset. Feature combination is not entirely reliant on data processing techniques. Instead, it integrates domain knowledge or practical experience to generate new features that carry domain-specific significance. Based on Formula (4) [4], elements such as Cmax and clearance (CL) can be extracted from the existing data. Their product is directly proportional to the dose. Therefore, we multiplied the Cmax and CL data to generate a new feature named cf1 (combination feature one). The bioavailability data from AdmetLab2.0 are a probability value falling within a range of 0 to 1. Consequently, we mapped these data on a 0–100% scale. Model tuning

During the training process, the model complexity and overfitting were controlled through parameter optimization. The main parameter results of this study were as follows: {‘n_estimators’: 1270, ‘subsample’: 0.62, ‘eta’: 0.22, ‘booster’: ‘gbtree’, ‘alpha’: 3, ‘lambda’: 0.7, ‘gamma’: 1.16}. Among them, n_estimators controls the number of subtrees. The more trees there are, the stronger the model’s learning capacity. However, this also increases the computational demand and the risk of overfitting. Subsample controls the proportion of sampling with replacement, which can make a single classifier more lightweight. Eta is the learning rate, with a larger eta speeding up the iterations but potentially preventing the model from converging optimally. Booster is used to select the type of weak estimator, as XGBoost includes more than gbtree models as weak estimators. Alpha and lambda are the coefficients of the L1 and L2 regularization terms, respectively. The larger the alpha and lambda values are, the heavier the penalty, effectively suppressing overfitting. Gamma is the minimum loss reduction required to make a further partition on a leaf node of the tree. The larger the gamma value is, the more conservative the algorithm and the fewer number of leaves on the tree. This lowers the complexity of the model [15, 47].

Improved feature screening based on cross-validation

In machine learning, dimensionality reduction can reduce the dimensions of data while retaining the primary features, thereby enhancing the generalizability of the model. The current dataset encompasses 27 187 features, contrasted with only 1216 labeled samples. The feature volume considerably exceeds the label counts, illustrating the ‘curse of dimensionality’ [48]. This scenario causes two major issues. First, it may lead to overfitting, rendering the model overly complex and incapable of generalizing to novel data. Second, the presence of noise and redundant information within the features increases the complexity of model training. During the training process, we observed that the mean accuracy on the training set during cross-validation consistently exceeded 0.9, while the mean accuracy on the validation set was 0.8304. In response to this, we employed multiple dimensionality reduction methods such as principal component analysis (PCA), analysis of variance (ANOVA), variance threshold and feature importance [49]. Meanwhile, we proposed a feature screening method based on feature frequency and importance (FFI) in cross-validation. The results are presented in Table 2. Among these, features sorted by frequency and importance exhibited the best performance, not only identifying the fewest number of features but also achieving the highest accuracy while preserving the interpretability of the features.

Evaluation results of dimensionality reduction methods

PCA is one of the most frequently employed techniques for dimensionality reduction [50]. In this study, we adopted two PCA-based reduction approaches. The first is global principal component analysis (GPCA), which directly applies dimensionality reduction to all features. Here, setting n_components to 0.9999 signifies that the selected features are expected to retain 99.99% of the original feature information. In this instance, only four features were obtained. The second approach is local principal component analysis (LPCA), which initially reduces the dimensions of each feature separately and then consolidates the results. In this study, we set n_components to 0.99 for each feature, ultimately yielding 2315 features. Although both methods reduced the feature data, they also significantly diminished the accuracy. This is attributable to PCA, which implements a linear transformation to map high-dimensional data to a low-dimensional space. This type of linear transformation must satisfy constraints such as orthogonality. As the number of features increases, the complexity of the data escalates exponentially, making it challenging to meet these constraints in a high-dimensional space [51, 52]. Additionally, a significant drawback of PCA is that it sacrifices the interpretability of the features.

The variance threshold operates by selecting features based on their individual variance [53]. This process first computes the variance of each feature within the dataset independently. Then, it eliminates the features that fail to meet a specified variance threshold. The principle of the variance threshold lies in recognizing the amount of information contained in each feature column; it is generally accepted that a lower variance signifies less information encapsulated within a feature. In extreme cases, a variance value of zero implies that all feature values are identical, equating to the absence of any valid information, necessitating swift identification and removal. In this study, we selected the top 1%, 5% and 25% of features with the highest variance. However, this approach failed to enhance the accuracy of the results.

ANOVA filters features by capturing the linear relationships between each feature and the labels [54]. In essence, it assesses the correlation between two columns and utilizes the P-value to determine if a significant correlation exists between a feature and label. In this study, P-values of 0.05 and 0.01 were used, and 6374 and 3607 features, respectively, were obtained, indicating the inability of ANOVA to effectively filter the features.

The feature importance method employs feature selection based on each feature’s importance score, where the importance score is derived from the information gain calculated based on the impact of each feature on the predicted value during the construction of tree models. In this study, due to the inclusion of a multitude of features, the complexity of the dataset increased, enhancing the randomness in the split point selection process of the classification and regression tree (CART). Thus, there was a certain degree of randomness in the computation of the feature importance scores. Therefore, we proposed a feature selection approach based on the frequency and importance of features across 40 cross-validation iterations, aiming to mitigate the impact of randomness.

During the training process, the model first conducts 40-fold cross-validation using the feature importance method. Then, the importance scores greater than zero (Supplementary Table S1 available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/) in each iteration of the 40-fold cross-validation were summed for each feature, and the occurrences of each feature were counted (Supplementary Table S2 available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/). The features were then sorted first by frequency and then by the sum of importance scores, yielding a total of 3439 features. Finally, the features were added one by one to the XGBoost model for 40-fold cross-validation training. When the number of features reached 241, the correlation coefficient peaked at 0.8388. Upon examining the model’s mean absolute error (MAE) and MSE, as depicted in Supplementary Figure S2C–E available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/, we observed that both types of error were at their minimum values when the number of features was 241. This method partially reduces the impact of model randomness on the results. Compared to the R-value obtained by the method that solely relies on feature importance for feature selection, the R-value obtained by the proposed method was improved by 0.0065.

Classification model

The XGBoost algorithm can be used for both classification and regression tasks [15], with differences in several aspects such as target variables, loss functions, sample weights and tree splitting criteria. The area under the curve (AUC) [55] was selected as the evaluation metric. Each tree in the construction process typically uses the Gini index (Formulas (8) and (9)) or information gain as the criterion for node splitting to determine the optimal splitting method. After the model training is completed, the feature importance score is calculated based on the contribution of each feature to the tree construction during the training process. In the feature importance screening stage, we selected a total of 241 features, including 130 structural features, 40 ADMET features, 35 compound–protein interaction features, 14 physicochemical properties, 12 disease features, 7 medicinal chemistry rules and 3 derived features, as shown in Table 3. In an analysis of the correlation between each of the top 30 weighted features and the MRDD, as shown in Figure 5A, the absolute value of the correlation between each individual feature and MRDD was below 0.5, with the highest being only 0.45. This indicates that the model captures not only simple linear relationships but also more complex non-linear relationships. The influence of dosage is a result of multifactorial interactions, which also explains why the PCA algorithm did not perform well in terms of dimensionality reduction. By analyzing the correlations among the top 30 features, as shown in Figure 5B, it can be observed that the correlations among these features are relatively low, demonstrating the effectiveness of feature selection. Among the top 30 features, only three pairs had correlations above 0.8: rdkit_29 and rdkit_122, rdkit_29 and rdkit_37 and rdkit_37 and rdkit_122. These features pertain to an identical molecular descriptor, and the descriptor algorithm is founded on an extensive collection of molecular structures, duly contemplating feature redundancy throughout the generation process. Consequently, deduplication was not executed. In both the regression and classification models, among the features with an importance count of 40, only one was non-repetitive. This indicates that the differences between the regression and classification models do not affect the selection of important features, further confirming the effectiveness of the 241 selected features, as shown in Supplementary Figure S4 available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/.

A further analysis was performed to explore biologically relevant effects on drug dosage for these features. Instead of limited quantity of features for 35 proteins, we selected the 879 proteins that affected the results by 40-fold cross-validation for functional enrichment analysis. Among the commonly used enrichment analysis methods, we chose GO [56], KEGG [57], Reactome [58] and WikiPathways [59] and used Metascape’s grouping function [60] to filter out results with high similarity. At a P-value threshold of 0.01, the top 30 GO (10 for each sub-ontology), 20 KEGG, 20 Reactome and 20 WikiPathways terms ranked by P-value are shown in Supplementary Figures S5–S8 available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/ [61], respectively. Among these results, some may relate to the tolerated drug dosage, such as ‘response to xenobiotic stimulus’, which typically involves enzymes (e.g. 9606.ENSP00000342007/CYP1A2 and 9606.ENSP00000337915/CYP3A4) in the liver that may affect drug metabolism [62]. ‘Circulatory system process’ could affect drug distribution and action in the body, thereby affecting the tolerated drug dosage [63]. Interestingly, hormone-related functions and pathways (and other signaling pathways, including nuclear receptor−related pathways) are enriched multiple times. In addition, the protein features listed in Supplementary Table S5 available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/ includes many proteins involved in the metabolism of hormone (e.g. 9606.ENSP00000356438/PTGS2) and the hormone response (e.g. 9606.ENSP00000306662/ADRA1B) [64]. It has been reported that certain small-molecule drugs that interact with proteins in the signaling pathways can show toxicity by disrupting the hormone response pathways [65, 66]. Moreover, certain genes like CYP3A4 involved in drug metabolism and disposition can also be regulated by xenobiotic-activated nuclear receptors [67]. We then performed protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis [60, 68] for the 35 proteins, the network graph is shown in Supplementary Figure S9A available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/. The molecular complex detection (MCODE) algorithm [69] identified a densely connected network neighborhoods, as shown in Supplementary Figure S9B available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/. The PPI analysis results indicate those proteins are involved in interaction networks. We also found that the selected features included the number of hydrogen bond donors, molecular weight and lipophilic partition coefficient, which are closely related to Lipinski’s rule [70]. Drugs that comply with Lipinski’s rule are generally more easily absorbed and distributed in the body, thus requiring a lower dosage.

Compound prediction with the external validation set

We further validated the effectiveness of the method with 145 external validation sets [13] and compared it with that of existing methods. Table 4 displays the comparative results of various methods. The QSAR model published by the FDA [13] predicted 99 out of 145 compounds, achieving a coverage rate of 68% and an R-value of 0.48. The v-NN method sets a Tanimoto distance threshold (TDT) of 0.45, which means that if no compound in the training set has a similarity greater than 0.45 to the compounds in the external validation set, v-NN will not predict the external validation compounds. Ultimately, the v-NN method produced 85 predicted results, with a correlation coefficient of 0.66 and a coverage rate of 59%. In contrast, the proposed method in this study is not restricted by the TDT. Thus, it achieved a 100% coverage rate. The 145 predicted results yielded a correlation coefficient of 0.63 (Figure 4B), slightly lower than that of the v-NN method but significantly higher than that of the k-NN method. After selecting a TDT of 0.45, the obtained correlation coefficient was 0.70, notably higher than the correlation coefficients of the v-NN and k-NN methods and the FDA’s QSAR model. The proposed method in this study demonstrates superior usability and accuracy. Moreover, it removes the need for the nearest neighbor search within the training set, thereby mitigating the influence of training set data on the results and addressing the limitation of the v-NN method when predicting compounds in the absence of similar compounds in the training set.

CONCLUSIONS

In this study, we introduced the CrossFuse-XGBoost approach, which significantly enhances the accuracy of MRDD prediction. First, during the feature selection phase, we fused various features such as molecular structure, drug–protein interactions and ADMET considerations to explore the potential molecular mechanisms. Furthermore, we enhanced the feature screening by combining the feature frequency with the importance scores during cross-validation. This refinement mitigates the impact of randomness in tree-based methods on feature selection, thereby improving the reliability of the screening outcomes. Our approach also achieved the optimal performance using the fewest features. Ultimately, we addressed the low coverage issue observed in existing v-NN methods, not only maintaining complete coverage of the test set but also achieving a 6.18% improvement in prediction accuracy.

Nonetheless, there are still some aspects of our approach that could be improved. The most salient issue is the scarcity of the dataset, which constrains the model’s capability. Employing a more extensive and comprehensive dataset could further increase the predictive power of our algorithm, narrowing the MRDD range, which is a priority because a broad MRDD range is more likely to result in adverse effects. Moreover, through screening, we identified 241 pivotal features that significantly affect MRDD; these features can be categorized into structural attributes, ADMET properties, drug–protein interactions and others. Future work will concentrate on a detailed analysis of these identified features, augmented by experimental validation, to pinpoint the key determinants of MRDD.
