Safety and efficacy of intrauterine lidocaine compared to warm isotonic sodium chloride solution for the control of pain during office hysteroscopy: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Amira Shoab, Amira H AbdelAziz, Hesham El-Shaer, Ahmed M Maged, Wael El-Sherbiny
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo compare the effectiveness of intrauterine lidocaine and warm isotonic sodium chloride solution as distension media for pain reduction during office hysteroscopy.MethodsThis triple-blind randomized controlled trial included 100 patients undergoing diagnostic office hysteroscopy. They were randomized into either the isotonic sodium chloride solution group, which underwent hysteroscopy using warmed isotonic sodium chloride solution as the distension medium, or the lidocaine group, which underwent hysteroscopy using 5 mL of 2% lidocaine diluted in warmed isotonic sodium chloride solution as the distension medium. The primary outcome was the pain visual analog scale score during and after the procedure.ResultsThe pain score reported throughout the procedure (during insertion, visualization and withdrawal) and 15 minutes after the procedure was significantly lower in women in the lignocaine group compared to the other group (1.9±1.0 vs. 3.1±0.8, 1.5±0.8 vs. 2.5±0.8, 1.7±0.8 vs. 2.8±0.7, and 1.1±0.2 vs. 1.7±0.8, respectively). Regarding complications, there were no differences between the two study groups in reported vaginal bleeding (1 vs. 2) or vasovagal reaction (0 vs. 1) with no reported cases of uterine perforation or creation of false tract. Regarding side effects, there were a significantly lower number of women who experienced lower abdominal pain, nausea and headache in the lignocaine group compared to others.ConclusionThe addition of lidocaine to isotonic sodium chloride solution as the distension medium during office hysteroscopy was associated with low pain scores during and after the procedure, short procedure time, reduced need for additional analgesia, high patient satisfaction, and low procedural difficulty scores.

Introduction

Hysteroscopy is the gold-standard diagnostic tool for assessing the uterine cavity because it allows direct visualization of the endometrium and can be used to diagnose and treat most intracavitary lesions [1].

Office hysteroscopy for the evaluation of patients with reproductive problems and abnormal uterine bleeding is now an established outpatient procedure that does not require anesthesia [2].

With current advancements in technology and instrumentation, most operative hysteroscopic procedures can be successfully performed in an outpatient setting, which is safe and more convenient for patients. The main challenges faced during outpatient procedures are pain [3] and anxiety [4].

During outpatient hysteroscopy, approximately one-third of patients report moderate to severe pain [5] and more than half of patients who experience failed hysteroscopy because of pain refuse to undergo the procedure again [6].

The main risk factors for pain development during hysteroscopy include instrument diameter, operator experience, distension medium, parity, absence of previous vaginal delivery, menopausal status, and procedure time [7].

Several pharmacological and nonpharmacological methods have been used to minimize pain and anxiety during hysteroscopy. These include analgesics, local anesthetics, antispasmodics, anti-inflammatory drugs, opioids, warming the distension medium, hypnosis, listening to music, and transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation [8].

The mechanism of action of local anesthetics is to block sodium channels in sensory neuron cell membranes, thereby altering neuronal depolarization and interfering with the transmission of pain signals to high centers [9].

The value of local anesthetics administered before uterine fluid distension has been evaluated in previous trials. In one randomized controlled trial, flushing the uterine cavity with 5 mL of 2% lidocaine 10 minutes before sonohysterography significantly reduced pain associated with office hysteroscopy [10].

In another randomized controlled trial, flushing the cervical canal and uterine cavity with 5 mL of 2% lidocaine diluted in 15 mL of isotonic sodium chloride solution 5 minutes before office hysteroscopy was associated with a significant reduction in pain [11].

This study aimed to compare the effectiveness of intrauterine lidocaine and warm isotonic sodium chloride solution as distension media for pain reduction during office hysteroscopy.

Materials and methods

This triple-blind randomized controlled trial was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki ethical standards between September 2024 and March 2025 at the hysteroscopy outpatient gynecology clinic at Kasr Alainy Maternity Hospital, after approval by the Kasr Alainy Ethical Committee (approval number: MS-195-2024).

All participants signed an informed written consent form after an explanation of the procedure, aims, and risks of the trial, as well as their right to withdraw from the study at any time. This trial was retrospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06946121).

All patients aged 18–60 years who were scheduled for office hysteroscopy were recruited for this study.

Exclusion criteria included contraindications to hysteroscopy, such as pregnancy, acute pelvic infection, genital herpes, severe vaginal bleeding, cervical stenosis, cervical malignancy, allergy to local anesthetics, need for operative hysteroscopy under general anesthesia, and previous cervical surgery. Patients with neurological or psychological conditions that could affect the perception of pain, those who had received analgesics within 48 hours before the procedure, those using drugs that may affect cervical consistency (including prostaglandins), and those who refused to sign the consent were also excluded.

The patients were randomized equally by simple randomization with a 1:1 allocation ratio into two groups using computer-generated random numbers. Before starting the procedure, an opaque closed envelope for each randomized patient was opened by a dedicated nurse who prepared the solution and delivered it to the operating physician. The nurses were not involved in any further part of the procedure.

The isotonic sodium chloride solution group included 50 patients who underwent hysteroscopy using warmed isotonic sodium chloride solution as the distension medium and the lidocaine group included 50 patients who underwent hysteroscopy using 5 mL of 2% lidocaine diluted in warmed isotonic sodium chloride solution as the distension medium.

The distension media were prepared and numbered by a third-party nurse who did not participate in hysteroscopy or data reporting. The surgeon, patients, and outcome assessor were blinded to group assignment.

All procedures were performed in the lithotomy position during the immediate postmenstrual period between cycle days 6 and 9. Patients were instructed to empty their bladders before hysteroscopy. The procedure was performed using a 30° 2.9-mm rigid hysteroscope with a 3.8-mm diagnostic sheath (LIGHT XA 180; TEKNO®, Tuttlingen, Germany) through a vaginoscopic approach. After cervical visualization and detection of the external cervical ostium, the hysteroscope was gently introduced into the uterine cavity. The maximum distension pressure was set at 70–80 mmHg using an automated hysteroscopic pump system (Hysteromat II; Karl Storz, Tuttlingen, Germany). The uterine cavity and tubal ostia were systematically visualized. All procedures were conducted by the same operator (A.S.), who had extensive experience with office hysteroscopy.

The primary outcome was the pain score reported by patients during and 15 minutes after the procedure, as assessed using the visual analogue scale (VAS).

The VAS is a tool used to measure subjective outcomes that cannot be assessed directly, typically psychometric outcomes. Patients mark their response along a continuous line from 0 to 10, where zero reflects no pain and 10 reflects maximum pain [12].

Secondary outcomes included the duration of the procedure, need for additional analgesia, need for dilatation, difficulty score (reported by the operator using the VAS), complications, side effects, and satisfaction score (reported by patients using the VAS).

The sample size was calculated based on an earlier study [13] that reported a clinical improvement in pain score of 15% (from 13.4 to 11.5) using a 20-cm scale. A sample size of 90 was needed to detect this difference, with a power of 80% and type I error of 0.05. The sample size was increased by 10% to account for potential dropout. Thus, 50 patients were included in each group.

IBM Statistical Package for Social Sciences Statistics software version 23 (IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA) was used to code, tabulate, and statistically analyze the data. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess normality in quantitative data, which were then described as mean±standard deviation and minimum-maximum range. An independent t-test was used to compare results. Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests were used to compare qualitative data expressed as numbers and percentages. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trails flow chart is shown in Fig. 1. There were no significant differences between the two study groups in age, body mass index, parity, indication for hysteroscopy, number of previous cesarean deliveries, number of previous curettages, number of previous hysteroscopies, or number of previous hormonal treatments (Table 1).

Two patients had small intramural fibroids less than 2 cm in diameter, and one patient had a small endometrial polyp measuring <1 cm.

The pain scores reported throughout the procedure (during insertion, visualization, and withdrawal) and 15 minutes after the procedure were significantly lower in patients in the lidocaine group than in those in the isotonic sodium chloride solution group (Table 2).

Patients in the lidocaine group had a significantly shorter procedure duration, less need for additional analgesia, and higher satisfaction scores than those of patients in the isotonic sodium chloride solution group (Table 2).

The difficulty scores reported by the operator were significantly lower in the lidocaine group than in the isotonic sodium chloride solution group (Table 2).

There was no significant difference between the two study groups in the need for dilatation or the number of failed procedures (Table 2).

Regarding complications, there were no differences between the two study groups in vaginal bleeding or vasovagal reactions, with no reported cases of uterine perforation or creation of a false tract (Table 3).

Regarding side effects, significantly fewer patients in the lidocaine group experienced lower abdominal pain, nausea, and headache than those in the isotonic sodium chloride solution group. There were no differences between the two study groups in reported vomiting, dizziness, or palpitations, and there were no reported cases of fever or shivering (Table 3).

Discussion

According to our findings, the addition of lidocaine to isotonic sodium chloride solution as the distension medium during office hysteroscopy was associated with low pain during and after the procedure, shorter procedure time, less need for additional analgesia, high patient satisfaction scores, and low difficulty scores reported by the operator, without an increase in complications or side effects. We also found that lidocaine use was associated with fewer patients experiencing low abdominal pain, nausea, and headache.

We chose lidocaine in our study because of its rapid onset of action during hysteroscopy and its short duration, which minimizes prolonged side effects.

The mechanism of action of intrauterine lidocaine flushing in reducing pain during office hysteroscopy involves several physiological and pharmacological processes. It exerts a local anesthetic effect on nerve endings by preventing the generation and conduction of pain signals through the endometrial and endocervical nerve endings. It reduces smooth muscle spasms of the uterine and cervical muscles, which are highly innervated by the autonomic nervous system [14]. Lidocaine also has mild anti-inflammatory and vasodilatory properties. Its efficacy is enhanced when combined with warm isotonic sodium chloride solution as the distension medium, as warmth further reduces uterine contractility and pain perception [15].

Barel et al. [16] conducted a randomized controlled trial in 2021 in 100 patients undergoing office hysteroscopy. Participants were randomized to the experimental group (10 mL of 2% lidocaine added to 1,000 mL of isotonic sodium chloride solution as the distension medium) or the comparator group (isotonic sodium chloride solution alone as the distension medium). They found a significant increase in postprocedural pain, as measured by the VAS, in the comparator group compared with the experimental group.

El-Ghazaly et al. [17] conducted a randomized controlled trial to assess the effectiveness of intrauterine levobupivacaine instillation in reducing pain in patients with abnormal uterine bleeding undergoing hysteroscopy. The trial included 120 patients scheduled for hysteroscopy with endometrial biopsy and randomized to either the levobupivacaine group (5 mL of 0.5% levobupivacaine intrauterine instillation at the end of the procedure) or control group (5 mL of 0.9% isotonic sodium chloride solution intrauterine instillation at the end of the procedure). They reported significantly lower VAS pain scores in the levobupivacaine group than in controls at 3 hours (1.31±1.02 vs. 1.62±0.76; P=0.01), 6 hours (0.81±1.24 vs. 1.53±0.88; P=0.004), and 9 hours (0.55±1.25 vs. 1.12±0.95; P=0.01) after the procedure. The need for additional analgesia was significantly lower in the levobupivacaine group than in the control group (P=0.005).

The main strengths of this study are its rigorous design, blinding of both participants and operators, and an appropriately calculated sample size. The main limitation was that all procedures were diagnostic and of short duration. Outcomes, such as pain, satisfaction, and difficulty, were evaluated subjectively using the VAS. Because this study was limited to diagnostic hysteroscopy, which is typically shorter and less invasive, pain perception may differ significantly during operative hysteroscopy. Therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to all office hysteroscopy contexts. Moreover, because none of the participating patients had large fibroids or polyps, caution should be exercised when applying these results to such populations.

Based on our findings, we recommend the addition of lidocaine to warm isotonic sodium chloride solution during office hysteroscopy to reduce pain and thereby lower the risk of procedure failure. A larger, multicenter, well-designed randomized controlled trial is needed to include patients with diverse characteristics, particularly those with expected difficulties, such as nulliparous and menopausal women, and to assess both diagnostic and operative hysteroscopic procedures.
