The Prognostic Significance of Radiographic Progression Without PSA Progression in Patients With Metastatic Hormone‐Sensitive Prostate Cancer Treated With First‐Line ARPI Therapy

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundRadiographic progression (RP) without prostate‐specific antigen (PSA) elevation has emerged as a clinically important pattern in the androgen receptor pathway inhibitors (ARPI) era, but its prevalence and prognostic significance in real‐world metastatic hormone‐sensitive prostate cancer (mHSPC) cohorts remain unclear.ObjectiveTo assess the prevalence and prognostic implications of RP alone progression in mHSPC patients treated with first‐line ARPIs.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 518 mHSPC patients treated with abiraterone acetate plus prednisolone, enzalutamide, or apalutamide. PSA progression was defined using PCWG3 criteria. Patients were categorized into PSA progression or RP alone progression groups, and clinical outcomes were compared.ResultsAmong 169 patients who developed castration‐resistant prostate cancer (CRPC), 34 (20.1%) experienced RP alone. RP alone patients had significantly worse overall survival compared to those with PSA progression (median 21 vs. 39 months, p = 0.007), despite exhibiting deeper biochemical responses—PSA nadir (0.055 vs. 0.86 ng/mL, p < 0.001) and a higher rate of PSA nadir < 0.2 ng/mL (61.8% vs. 27.4%, p < 0.001). The baseline characteristic differing between groups was CHAARTED volume classification; high volume disease was markedly more common in RP alone patients (94.1% vs. 77.8%). In univariate and multivariate analyses of the overall cohort, CHAARTED high volume disease was identified as an independent risk factor for RP alone (HR 10.3, 95% CI 1.20–89.3; p = 0.034).ConclusionRP alone progression occurred in approximately one‐fifth of patients and was associated with significantly poorer survival despite excellent PSA responses, underscoring the limitation of PSA‐based monitoring and supporting routine imaging for early detection of PSA‐incongruent disease progression in mHSPC.

Introduction

Prostate cancer is one of the most prevalent malignancies among men worldwide and remains a leading cause of cancer‐related mortality. For patients with metastatic hormone‐sensitive prostate cancer (mHSPC), androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) had long been the mainstay of treatment. In recent years, several landmark trials have demonstrated the survival benefit of combining ADT with androgen receptor pathway inhibitors (ARPIs), leading to a paradigm shift in the management of mHSPC. The LATITUDE trial showed that abiraterone acetate plus prednisolone (AA + P) significantly improved overall survival (OS) and radiographic progression‐free survival (rPFS) in high‐risk mHSPC patients compared to ADT alone [1]. Similarly, the ENZAMET and ARCHES trials demonstrated that the addition of enzalutamide (Enz) to ADT conferred survival benefits in both high‐ and low‐volume disease [2, 3]. The TITAN trial further confirmed the efficacy of apalutamide (Apa) in combination with ADT across diverse mHSPC populations, regardless of disease volume or prior treatment [4]. Prostate‐specific antigen (PSA) kinetics have historically been used as surrogate markers for treatment efficacy. However, the increasing use of ARPIs has revealed a novel clinical challenge ‐radiographic progression (RP) that occurs independently of PSA progression. This discordant progression pattern suggests that some tumors may evolve to become androgen receptor (AR) independent or neuroendocrine‐like [5], leading to disease progression that is not captured by PSA monitoring alone. In a recent secondary analysis of the TITAN trial, among patients who developed RP while receiving Apa, 52.2% experienced progression in the absence of PSA progression [6]. These patients were more likely to have visceral metastases and soft‐tissue progression and exhibited worse radiographic outcomes despite favorable PSA kinetics. Similar findings were reported in post hoc analyses of the ARCHES and CHAARTED trials [7, 8]. Although this discordance has gained increasing recognition, its clinical significance in real‐world cohorts of patients with mHSPC remains inadequately characterized. In particular, the impact of ARPI treatment on the prognosis of RP progression alone remains unclear. This multicenter retrospective study was conducted to evaluate the prevalence, prognostic implications, and treatment outcomes of RP alone progression in mHSPC patients receiving first‐line ARPIs. Our goal was to determine whether PSA‐based monitoring remains adequate in current practice and to identify patients who may benefit from more vigilant radiographic surveillance.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

Between 2011 and 2024, a total of 662 patients with mHSPC were treated in our multicenter cohort. Data were collected from five leading university hospitals and their affiliated hospitals, that is, Kindai University Hospital (Osaka, Japan), Jikei University School of Medicine (Tokyo, Japan), Osaka Medical and Pharmaceutical University (Osaka, Japan), Tokyo Medical University Hospital (Tokyo, Japan), Kawasaki Medical School (Okayama, Japan), Hamamatsu University School of Medicine (Shizuoka, Japan) and Fujita Health University Hospital (Aichi, Japan). Study participants were required to have a confirmed diagnosis of adenocarcinoma of the prostate and to have distant metastasis on bone, lymph node, or viscera. Of these, 518 mHSPC patients treated with ARPIs as first‐line treatment were included in this study. Patients who did not treated with ARPIs or had any missing clinicopathological/laboratory information were excluded from this study. The RP in each patient was evaluated by computed tomography (CT), which was typically performed every 3–6 months as part of routine follow‐up at each institution. New or enlarging soft‐tissue metastases (e.g., lymph nodes or visceral lesions) were evaluated on CT, whereas new bone lesions were assessed by either CT findings consistent with metastasis and/or confirmatory bone scintigraphy when clinically indicated. The PSA progression group includes patients with PSA progression, based on the Prostate Cancer Working Group 2 (PCWG2) criteria [9], both with and without concomitant RP. The RP alone group includes patients with RP without PSA progression.

Ethical Considerations

This study design was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Osaka Medical and Pharmaceutical University (IRB approved number: 2571‐7) and performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki [10].

Statistical Analysis

PSA progression was defined based on the PCWG2 criteria [9], which require a confirmed rise in PSA of ≥ 25% over the nadir and an absolute increase of at least 2 ng/mL, confirmed by a second measurement obtained at least 3 weeks later. Baseline clinical and pathological characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and compared using analysis of variance (ANOVA). Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages, and comparisons between groups were performed using two‐sided Fisher's exact tests. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from the initiation of first‐line ARPI therapy to death from any cause. Time to CRPC was defined as the duration from treatment initiation to radiographic or PSA progression during continuous ADT. OS from CRPC was calculated from the date of CRPC diagnosis to death from any cause. Survival analyses were conducted using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between survival curves were assessed using the log‐rank test. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using Cox proportional hazards regression models. Subgroup analyses were conducted to further explore the association between progression pattern (PSA progression vs. RP alone) and survival outcomes across different clinical strata, including type of ARPI used (abiraterone, Enz, Apa), baseline PSA level, metastatic burden (low vs. high volume), LATITUDE risk classification, and CHAARTED volume classification. Interaction terms were not formally tested due to limited statistical power. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and JMP Pro (version 18.2.1, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Among all 518 patients, 169 patients (32.6%) developed CRPC, including 135 (26.0%) with PSA progression and 34 (6.6%) with RP alone. All cases of RP were diagnosed based on these scheduled CT examinations rather than symptom‐driven imaging. By first‐line ARPI treatments, PSA progression occurred in 28.8% with AA + P, 17.5% with Enz, and 25.3% with Apa, while RP alone occurred in 7.4%, 5.2%, and 5.3%, respectively (Figure 1). Kaplan–Meier curves showed significantly shorter OS in patients with RP alone compared to PSA progression (median OS, 21 vs. 39 months; HR 0.50, 95% CI 0.26–0.96, p = 0.007; Figure 2A). Time to CRPC was also assessed. Contrary to expectations, the median time to CRPC was similar between RP alone and PSA progression (median time 12 vs. 11 months, HR 0.74, 95% CI 0.49–1.12, p = 0.100; Figure 2B). In contrast, OS after CRPC was significantly shorter in patients with RP alone (median 7 months) compared to those with PSA progression (19 months), although (HR 0.51, 95% CI 0.26–0.98, p = 0.007; Figure 2C). Consistent with previous reports [11], Kaplan–Meier curves showed significantly shorter OS in patients with combined PSA and RP (PSA + RP) compared with those with PSA progression alone (PSA alone) (median OS, 29 vs. 49 months; HR 0.57, 95% CI 0.33–0.98; p = 0.023). In contrast, no significant difference in OS was observed between the RP alone and the PSA + RP (median OS, 21 vs. 29 months; HR 1.36, 95% CI 0.73–2.53; p = 0.300) (Supporting Information: Figure S1).

To identify poor prognostic factors following progression to CRPC with RP alone, we further analyzed prognostic factors in patients with PSA progression (n = 135) and RP alone (n = 34), based on the result that post‐CRPC survival influence OS. Table 2 summarizes the baseline clinicopathologic characteristics of these 169 patients. The median age at primary diagnosis was 73 and 75 years, respectively (p = 0.357), and the distributions of age categories (< 75 vs. ≥ 75 years), Gleason pattern, and clinical T stage were comparable. Median baseline PSA was relatively lower in the RP alone group (180 vs. 307 ng/mL, p = 0.099). Similarly, no significant differences were found in Gleason pattern, clinical T stage, ECOG performance status, EOD score, or sites of metastasis. Histologically confirmed neuroendocrine prostate cancer was observed in only one patient in the RP alone group. In the PSA progression group, 14 patients (10.4%) received radiotherapy to the primary prostate lesion and 21 patients (15.6%) to metastatic sites during the disease course. In the RP alone group, 6 patients (17.7%) received radiotherapy to metastatic lesions, whereas no patients received radiotherapy to the primary tumor.

Notably, while the LATITUDE risk classification showed no significant difference, when patients were classified according to the CHAARTED volume criteria, the RP alone group had a significantly higher proportion of patients with high volume compared with those with low volume; in the PSA progression group, 30 patients (22.2%) had low volume and 105 patients (77.8%) had high volume, whereas in the RP alone group, 2 patients (5.9%) had low volume disease and 32 patients (94.1%) had high volume disease (p = 0.029).

To identify risk factors for RP alone, univariate and multivariate analyses were performed in the overall cohort of 518 patients (Table 3). In univariate analyses, a higher EOD score (≥ 2) and high‐volume disease according to the CHAARTED criteria were significantly associated with RP alone. In multivariate analysis, high volume disease remained an independent risk factor for RP alone (HR 10.3, 95% CI 1.20–89.3; p = 0.034), whereas other clinical variables, including age, Gleason pattern, clinical T stage, baseline PSA level, visceral metastases, and LATITUDE risk classification, were not independently associated with RP alone.

We further compared the patterns of metastatic spread between patients with RP alone and those with PSA alone progression and found no significant differences in metastatic sites (Table S1). Similarly, no significant difference in OS was observed according to the receipt of radiotherapy to either the primary tumor or metastatic lesions (Supporting Information: Figure S2).

These findings indicate that tumor volume, rather than risk factors such as a high Gleason Score, may be more relevant to RP alone progression. We had hypothesis that the treatment after CRPC may affect the prognosis, however, the proportion of taxane used after second‐line treatment after CRPC was 45.9% for PSA progression and 42.1% for RP alone, with no significance (p = 0.119; Table 2).

We next examined whether biochemical responses differed according to the pattern of progression (Table 4). Notably, patients who later developed RP alone exhibited deeper PSA suppression during initial ARPI therapy. The PSA nadir was significantly lower in the RP alone group compared with the PSA‐progression group (median 0.055 vs. 0.86 ng/mL, p < 0.001), and the proportion achieving a PSA nadir < 0.2 ng/mL was more than doubled (61.8% vs. 27.4%, p < 0.001). Despite these deeper PSA responses, the rate of PSA decline ≥ 90% was similarly high in both groups (94.1% vs. 88.9%, p = 0.529). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that PSA kinetics did not discriminate between patients who experienced PSA progression and those who developed RP, indicating that excellent biochemical responses do not preclude the emergence of PSA‐incongruent RP under ARPI therapy.

Discussion

In this retrospective multicenter study, we evaluated the prognostic significance of RP without concurrent PSA progression in patients with mHSPC treated with first‐line androgen receptor signaling inhibitors (ARPIs). Our findings demonstrate that RP alone patients had significantly shorter OS compared to those with PSA progression (median OS: 21 vs. 39 months, p = 0.007). Importantly, at the time of mHSPC diagnosis, differences between the two groups were observed only in the CHAARTED volume classification, suggesting that tumor volume may be relevant to the distinct disease biology of RP alone. Supplementary analyses further clarified the prognostic hierarchy among progression patterns. Patients with PSA + RP had significantly shorter survival than those with PSA alone, whereas survival did not significantly differ between RP alone and PSA + RP. These findings collectively indicate that RP—whether occurring alone or together with PSA progression—is consistently associated with poor prognosis, while PSA alone progression appears to confer comparatively more favorable outcomes.

Our observations are consistent with recent post hoc analyses of major randomized trials [6]. The discordant progression cases were more likely to present with visceral metastases, soft‐tissue disease, and neuroendocrine‐like features, all of which are associated with poor prognosis [6]. Similar patterns were also observed in the ARCHES and CHAARTED studies [7, 8], suggesting that RP alone is a reproducible and clinically meaningful phenomenon across various ARPI‐based regimens.

This discordance is likely rooted in tumor heterogeneity. Advanced prostate cancer consists of a complex mixture of AR‐dependent and AR‐independent subclones. Potent ARPI therapy may rapidly suppress PSA‐producing tumor populations that are dependent on androgen signaling, while inadvertently allowing androgen‐independent clones to expand. One well‐characterized escape mechanism is neuroendocrine differentiation, which is associated with low PSA levels, aggressive progression, and poor prognosis [5]. Emerging evidence implicates genomic alterations such as TP53, RB1, and PTEN loss in promoting lineage plasticity and resistance to AR‐targeted therapies [12]. Prior studies have suggested that alterations such as TP53 and RB1 loss have been implicated in neuroendocrine‐like transformation and may contribute to RP without PSA elevation [5]. Therefore, RP alone may reflect an evolutionary shift toward AR‐independence and PSA‐silent disease, necessitating alternate monitoring and treatment strategies. We believe that future studies incorporating molecular analyses will be valuable to address these important questions.

From a clinical perspective, these findings emphasize the limitations of PSA‐based monitoring, particularly in the era of potent ARPIs. Although PSA kinetics remain a useful surrogate for treatment response, they fail to capture all instances of disease progression. The Prostate Cancer Working Group 3 (PCWG3) recommends radiographic assessments every 8–12 weeks in clinical trials, especially in the presence of symptoms or PSA‐incongruent progression [13]. However, in real‐world settings, imaging is often delayed or omitted in asymptomatic patients with low PSA levels. Our data support incorporating routine imaging into follow‐up protocols for mHSPC patients, even when PSA responses appear favorable.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design and lack of standardized imaging intervals may have introduced bias in progression assessment. Second, radiographic criteria were not centrally reviewed, and imaging modalities differed across participating institutions. Nonetheless, the multicenter design and relatively large cohort strengthen the generalizability of our findings. Future prospective studies are needed to validate RP alone as a clinically meaningful subtype. Third, in the present dataset, genetic testing was not performed before disease progression because, under current Japanese insurance guidelines, genetic testing, including comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP), is not approved for patients with mHSPC in the first‐line setting. Consequently, the information on somatic or germline genetic alterations was not available for prediction of any progression. Integration of biomarker platforms, including circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), PSMA PET imaging, and biopsy‐based genomic profiling, may aid in early detection of patients at risk for discordant progression.

Conclusion

RP without PSA elevation is associated with worse survival despite favorable PSA kinetics in mHSPC patients receiving first‐line ARPI therapy. Our cohort showed tumor volume may be relevant to RP alone progression. These findings underscore the limitations of PSA‐based monitoring and highlight the need for routine imaging and further investigation into PSA‐incongruent disease biology.
