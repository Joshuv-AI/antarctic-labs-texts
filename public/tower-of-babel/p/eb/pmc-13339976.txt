Competition and Collaboration in the AI Race: Country‐Level Directional Evidence for Risk Monitoring and Policy
Authors: Pengyu Liu, Stavros K. Stavroglou, Saurabh Mishra, Epameinondas Katsikas, Athanasios A. Pantelous, Bilal M. Ayyub, James H. Lambert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTArtificial intelligence (AI) is reshaping national economies, yet country‐level AI–macro relationships remain poorly understood. Using annual data for the United States and China, 1980–2020, we develop a four‐layer triangulation framework—Pattern Causality, Granger causality, VECM‐based cointegration, and lead–lag correlation—to map directional associations between AI activity indicators and macro aggregates through weighted networks and heatmaps. Three patterns recur. First, nonlinear AI–macro dependence is moderate and mostly positive, making AI indicators useful monitoring signals rather than stand‐alone decision triggers. Second, publications and patents carry short‐run predictive content in Granger tests, making them candidate early‐warning indicators for macro surveillance. Third, cointegration places AI indicators mainly on the adjustment margin: Macro fundamentals condition long‐run AI–macro co‐movement more than AI indicators lead it. The international layer adds an important qualification. United States–China collaboration variables raise AI node centrality, especially for China, but a mechanical‐expansion null benchmark shows that most of this increase is expected from enlarging the network; beyond‐mechanical collaboration evidence concentrates in the cointegration layer. Overall, the US pathway is patent‐oriented and selective, whereas China's is denser and more collaboration‐intensive. The framework supports AI–macro risk monitoring and hypothesis generation, not structurally identified causal claims.

Introduction

Motivation and Research Gap

Artificial intelligence (AI) has expanded rapidly over recent decades. Advances in deep learning and the growing role of industry—via preferential access to substantial computing capacity, large‐scale data sets, and specialized human capital—have influenced research agendas and the diffusion of models (Ahmed et al. 2023; Bier et al. 2025). The scholarly record reflects this shift: AI‐engaged publications across disciplines increased by roughly 13‐fold between 1985 and 2022 (Duede et al. 2024), and medical AI publications rose from approximately 1600 in 2000 to more than 58,000 in 2022 (Shi et al. 2023). These indicators situate AI as a general‐purpose technology with broad scientific and technical ramifications.

The policy relevance of AI follows from its complementarities with data infrastructures, cloud computing, and next‐generation connectivity, and from applications in healthcare, e‐commerce, manufacturing, and security‐sensitive domains (Sun et al. 2025; Ali 2024). In parallel, AI features in geopolitical analyses of both hard‐power capabilities and soft‐power influence (Miailhe 2018). For risk analysis, central questions concern the extent to which AI‐related activity is associated with movements in macroeconomic aggregates and whether such associations differ across countries with distinct capability endowments and collaboration patterns.

Despite growing attention at the firm and sector levels (e.g., Cockburn et al., 2018), macro‐level evidence remains limited. The “productivity paradox” underscores this gap: Measured labor productivity growth has been weak even as AI capability advanced (Brynjolfsson et al. 2017). Moreover, frontier AI entails sizable fixed inputs in compute, data curation, and specialized labor, concentrating advanced capacity within a small set of economies. Against this background, we ask: (i) To what extent do indicators of AI activity co‐move with, or help predict, macroeconomic aggregates? (ii) Are these relationships similar across leading economies? and (iii) Does international research collaboration change the network position of AI indicators beyond what would be expected from mechanically adding new nodes? The US–China setting is instructive: While public discourse often emphasizes rivalry, bibliometric evidence indicates that joint US–China research tends to achieve higher citation impact than solo efforts (AlShebli et al. 2024). We treat collaboration as a measurable innovation asset, drawing on partnership‐metrics frameworks that quantify cross‐institutional and cross‐border ties (Linkov et al. 2020). Whether such collaboration aligns with macro‐level associations, and whether any apparent change exceeds mechanical network expansion, remains an open empirical question.

Identifying macroeconomic relationships involving AI is challenging because AI activity co‐evolves with other structural forces, can be jointly determined with growth, and frequently exhibits nonstationary dynamics that complicate linear modeling. To mitigate these issues, we adopt a triangulation strategy: a nonlinear Pattern Causality (PC) layer to detect directed, potentially nonlinear dependencies in multivariate time series (Stavroglou et al. 2019, 2020), complemented by Granger predictability (Granger 1969), cointegration/vector error‐correction model (VECM) analysis (Engle and Granger 1987), and a lead‐lag correlation layer. The data set comprises annual indicators for the United States and China over 1980–2020, including AI publications, AI patents, measures of international collaboration, and standard macroeconomic aggregates; transformations are aligned with each method's assumptions, and conservative multiple‐testing adjustments are implemented in the linear layers.

Related Work

Macro‐innovation and diffusion literature link R&D to growth and spillovers, but typically without isolating AI‐specific dynamics at the country level. Endogenous growth frameworks formalize innovation as a driver of long‐run growth (Romer 1990; Aghion and Howitt 1992), while semi‐endogenous models address scale effects (Jones 1995). Cross‐country evidence shows that domestic and trade‐partner R&D stocks raise TFP via international spillovers (Coe et al. 2009; Bayoumi et al. 1999). Diffusion studies document systematic lags and heterogeneity in technology adoption across countries, with implications for growth differentials (Comin and Hobijn 2004, 2010). This body of work links R&D, diffusion, and productivity, but it does not disentangle the institutional and technological mechanisms through which AI—as a general‐purpose technology with distinctive fixed inputs in compute, data curation, and specialized talent—maps into macroeconomic aggregates.

Recent empirical work increasingly centers on AI itself, with evidence richer at the micro than at the macro scale. Field and laboratory experiments report sizable productivity gains from AI tools (Brynjolfsson et al. 2025; Noy and Zhang 2023), reductions in translation frictions that expand trade (Brynjolfsson et al. 2019), sales improvements from customer‐facing chatbots (Luo et al. 2019), and enhanced learning outcomes in training contexts (Gaessler and Piezunka 2023). At broader scales, inference is fragile and sensitive to proxy choices. National or regional analyses report mixed and sometimes distributionally regressive associations: Regional AI innovation correlates with declines in labor's income share (Minniti et al. 2025), adoption gaps can widen inequality (Humlum and Vestergaard 2025), and vacancy‐ or task‐based measures indicate shifting job quality and composition (Acemoglu et al. 2022; Bonfiglioli et al. 2025). These studies underscore both the promise of AI and the challenges of aggregating micro impacts into macro conclusions.

An underexplored dimension is the international organization of AI research. Bibliometric analyses identify a collaboration premium: US–China co‐authored AI papers achieve higher citation impact than matched single‐country outputs, with the premium strengthening over time and across subfields (Klasa et al. 2020; AlShebli et al. 2024). This aligns with science‐of‐science evidence that atypical recombinations yield greater impact (Uzzi et al. 2013). Collaboration thus plausibly acts as a conduit for cross‐border knowledge spillovers (Breschi and Lissoni 2001). The durability and macro relevance of this premium are uncertain amid tightening techno‐geopolitical frictions: policy interventions have curtailed collaboration in adjacent domains (Jia et al. 2024), and recent portraits suggest a shift from convergence to divergence in US–China co‐authorship (Kitajima and Okamura 2025), even as selective cooperation persists (Li et al. 2024). For AI–macro questions, collaboration shocks and network re‐wiring imply that spillovers are contingent and time‐varying.

Four issues remain insufficiently addressed: (a) AI specificity at macro scale—classic R&D and diffusion results rarely isolate AI as a distinct GPT; (b) micro‐to‐macro mapping—strong micro‐level gains do not translate straightforwardly into national aggregates, and macro proxies are modeling‐sensitive; (c) the collaboration dimension—the extent to which international research collaboration, notably US–China, is reflected in macro‐level associations remains unresolved; and (d) methods under nonstationarity and potential bidirectionality—linear identification is challenging, motivating triangulation across complementary tools. To address these challenges, Section 2 introduces a harmonized US–China data set, and Section 3 details a four‐layer framework that triangulates nonlinear and linear evidence.

Contributions

This paper makes four main contributions to the study of AI and its macroeconomic implications, each stated as descriptive and risk‐monitoring oriented rather than structurally causal.

First, we advance macro–AI mapping by integrating linear and nonlinear methodologies. We develop a unified framework that combines PC with Granger causality, cointegration via VECM, and lead–lag correlation, allowing us to triangulate directional associations between AI indicators and macroeconomic aggregates in the United States and China from 1980 to 2020. PC captures nonlinear and state‐dependent dependence that may be less visible to traditional tools, while comparison with linear layers helps separate recurring patterns from method‐specific signals.

Second, we study cross‐border collaboration as a potential network channel, while explicitly testing the mechanical‐expansion concern. By incorporating US–China AI collaboration measures into the networks, we examine how joint research activity coincides with changes in centrality rankings and directional linkages. We then compare the observed centrality shifts with a mechanical‐inflation null benchmark, so that collaboration claims are tied to whether shifts exceed what would be expected from adding two generic nodes.

Third, we provide comparative evidence on asymmetries between the United States and China. The United States shows more selective AI–macro associations, most visibly around patent activity, whereas China's AI measures become more embedded when collaboration variables enter. Cointegration results further indicate that, in both countries, AI variables often adjust within long‐run equilibria rather than systematically leading them. This pattern is consistent with differences in innovation ecosystems, but we interpret it as comparative evidence on network structure rather than as evidence of structural economic effects.

Fourth, we emphasize methodological transparency for risk‐informed interpretation. We discuss the limits of PC under trending series, the risk of spurious links from monotonic data, the limited power of roughly 40 annual observations per country, and the possibility that new collaboration nodes mechanically inflate centrality. The result is a cautious account that confines inference to what the four‐layer design can credibly deliver—directional predictability, co‐movement timing, and network embeddedness—rather than extending to structural causal mechanisms or welfare counterfactuals.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the macroeconomic and AI indicators, detailing their sources, coverage (1980–2020), construction procedures, and caveats. Section 3 sets out the unified framework, including data preprocessing and the four methodological modules—PC, Granger causality, VECM cointegration, and lead–lag correlation—and explains how these are used to construct directed, weighted networks for comparative analysis. Section 4 presents the empirical results through weighted‐degree tables, heatmaps, and thresholded network plots, contrasting the United States and China with and without collaboration measures. Section 5 discusses the findings, distinguishes core from suggestive claims, and clarifies the interpretive boundaries of network topology. Section 6 concludes by summarizing the main insights and drawing monitoring‐oriented implications.

Data

We analyze 22 annual indicators for two large, AI‐leading economies—the United States and China. The data set comprises two groups: (i) 16 macroeconomic series from international statistical sources—the International Monetary Fund (IMF; World Economic Outlook, WEO, and International Financial Statistics, IFS), the World Bank's World Development Indicators (WDI) and the Organisation for Economic Co‐operation and Development (OECD), the International Labor Organization's ILOSTAT (ILOSTAT), and the United Nations' World Population Prospects (UN WPP)—and (ii) six AI‐related variables from the Microsoft Academic Graph (MAG).1 Because our AI measures are bibliometric and available only at annual frequency, all macroeconomic series are aggregated to annual frequency for temporal alignment. Early MAG coverage is sparse, with zero or near‐zero counts in the initial years (e.g., no recorded AI patent publications for China in 1965). As the field was nascent and these observations add limited information, we restrict the sample to 1980–2020—the longest contiguous, high‐quality span prior to MAG's retirement in 2021.

Macroeconomic variable selection follows principled criteria. We prioritize breadth over redundancy (avoiding, e.g., multiple GDP variants) in order to sample distinct dimensions of activity while respecting the limited number of annual observations. Indicators with extended missing sequences are excluded. Emphasis is placed on coverage consistency: We select series with complete or near‐complete data for both countries from 1980 to 2020 to support comparability and reliability. With the panel assembled and transformations aligned to each method's stationarity requirements, the analytical question turns to how directional structure can be extracted from a short, heterogeneous time series. Section 3 addresses this by mapping each research question to a dedicated estimation layer and aggregating the resulting pairwise evidence into directed, weighted networks.

Methodology

Design Principles and Overview

We aim to: (i) detect and quantify directional associations between AI activity and macroeconomic aggregates for the United States and China, (ii) assess how cross‐border collaboration variables change the network position of AI indicators, including whether those changes exceed mechanical expansion, and (iii) report the uncertainty around these links. This section maps each research question to the estimator best suited to address it and then aggregates the resulting pairwise evidence into time‐resolved, directed networks.

To limit single‐method bias, consistent with risk‐analytics practice for complex networks (Zhuang et al. 2022), we employ four complementary approaches, each illuminating a different aspect of the pathway from association to timing: Pattern Causality (PC) captures directional dependence in a model‐light, nonlinear setting, useful when effects may be asymmetric or not well represented by linear models. Granger causality (VAR) identifies short‐horizon predictive direction (“who predicts whom”) on stationarity‐consistent transformations. Cointegration (VECM) characterizes long‐run co‐movement and indicates which variable tends to adjust back toward equilibrium after shocks. Lead–lag correlation summarizes which series typically moves first over small leads and lags with minimal parametric structure.

Pattern Causality

We use the PC methodology of Stavroglou et al. (2019, 2020, 2021) to detect directed, potentially nonlinear dependence between pairs of annual series without imposing a parametric model. PC is designed for short samples and complex dynamics: It embeds each series into a low‐dimensional state space, compares local patterns across nearest neighbors, and asks whether patterns in X tend to precede and organize patterns in Y beyond Y’s own history.

Given two standardized annual series xt and yt, PC reconstructs a shadow attractor for each using delay vectors (Takens embedding): (1) Xt=xt,xt−τ,…,xt−(E−1)τ,Yt=yt,yt−τ,…,yt−(E−1)τ, with embedding dimension E and delay τ. For a reference time t, PC finds the k nearest neighbors of Yt in {Ys} (similar past conditions for Y), forms their small forward patterns (e.g., one‐step growth rates), and checks whether the corresponding neighborhood in {Xs} displays a consistent lead pattern that helps anticipate the pattern observed in Y at horizon h.3

For each neighbor index j of Yt, define a local pattern vector (return vector) over the embedded window, (2) sjY=yj(2)−yj(1)yj(1),…,yj(E)−yj(E−1)yj(E−1), where (3) Ytj=(yj(1),…,yj(E)).

Combine neighbors using distance weights (4) wjY=exp{−d(Yt,Ytj)}∑j′exp{−d(Yt,Ytj′)},d(·,·)Manhattan distance to obtain the signature (average local pattern) for Y at time t (5) PY(t)=∑j=1kwjYsjY.

Define PX→Y(t) analogously using the X‐neighbors matched to the same indices {tj} (i.e., the X patterns observed when Y was in similar local states).

Compare PX→Y(t) to the realized Y‐pattern at horizon h (or to PY(t), depending on implementation) via a similarity score; aggregate over t to form a success rate SRX→Y∈[0,1] that reflects how often the X‐neighborhood “gets the Y pattern right.” Repeat with roles reversed to obtain SRY→X. The asymmetry, (6) ΔSR=SRX→Y−SRY→X, summarizes direction. Following Stavroglou et al. (2019, 2020, 2021), we classify the sign of the association using the local alignment between the X‐implied pattern and the Y pattern (e.g., the sign of the inner product ⟨PX→Y(t),PY(t)⟩ averaged over t): positive (aligned), negative (anti‐aligned), or dark (nonlinear/ambiguous, mixed sign or weak magnitude).4

We record a directed edge X→Y if ΔSR exceeds a small margin δpc: (7) X→YifΔSR>δpc,wX→Ypc=max{0,ΔSR}(rescaled to[0,1]). Because PC is nonparametric and annual samples are short, we emphasize (i) stability of orientation across (E,τ,k) settings and (ii) block‐bootstrap null comparisons for ΔSR as robustness signals (rather than sole reliance on classical p‐values).

PC complements the linear layers by (i) allowing asymmetric, state‐dependent responses that may be poorly captured by VAR/VECM at annual frequency and (ii) using local patterns rather than global parameterizations, which is advantageous with limited observations and possible structural change. We nonetheless treat PC outputs as associational and rely on convergence with Granger, cointegration/VECM, and lead–lag layers for risk‐relevant interpretation. Because PC is computed on standardized levels, common trends can still induce apparent structure; we therefore reinforce interpretation with stability checks across (E,τ,k), block‐bootstrapped nulls, and a comparison with the stationarity‐consistent linear layers (see Appendix E and De Silva and Thesmar, 2024).

Operationally, the measure of “predictive precision” is the success rate: the frequency with which the X‐neighborhood correctly anticipates the local pattern of Y. Comparing SRX→Y with SRY→X yields the asymmetry ΔSR (orientation). The sign reflects average alignment between the X‐implied and realized Y patterns: positive (aligned), negative (anti‐aligned), or dark when alignment is weak or changes sign across neighborhoods (nonmonotone dependence).

Granger Causality (Short Run)

Complementing the nonlinear, pattern‐based evidence from Section 3.3, we implement a linear, forecast‐based check. Following Granger (1969), we ask: does one series improve the short‐horizon prediction of another after transforming to stationarity? Granger causality is interpreted here as predictive content at annual frequency.

Step 1: Preprocessing and stationarity. Let xt denote any macroeconomic or AI series observed annually. Occasional single missing values are linearly interpolated to preserve temporal coherence. Because the VAR is estimated on (weakly) stationary inputs, we difference once (8) Δxt=xt−xt−1, and standardize (9) zx,t=Δxt−μΔxσΔx, where μΔx and σΔx are computed within the estimation sample. Unit‐root diagnostics (ADF and PP) (Dickey and Fuller 1979; Phillips and Perron 1988) supporting xt∼I(1) and zx,t∼I(0) are reported in Appendix C, together with deterministic‐term choices.

Step 2: Forward‐expanding bivariate VAR(1). For each ordered pair (yt,xt) and each recursion length Tt≥L0=5, we estimate the symmetric bivariate VAR(1) on {1,…,t}: (10) yt=α0(t)+α1(t)yt−1+β1(t)xt−1+εy,t, (11) xt=γ0(t)+γ1(t)xt−1+δ1(t)yt−1+εx,t. Testing H0x→y:β1(t)=0 against H1x→y:β1(t)≠0 yields restricted and unrestricted sums of squared residuals, SSRR and SSRU, and the F‐statistic (12) Fx→y,t=(SSRR−SSRU)/1SSRU/(Tt−3)∼F1,Tt−3, with tail probability px→y,t. Reversing roles gives py→x,t. Collecting these over all pairs and t produces the panel {pi→j,t}i,j,t, which we use for time‐resolved visualizations (e.g., heatmaps).

Alongside the time‐resolved panel, we report the full‐sample test for each ordered pair using the same specification (VAR(1) on transformed series). Unless otherwise noted in figures/tables, significance thresholds are indicated in the captions. Lag‐length checks, residual diagnostics, and alternative transformations are documented in Appendix C.

This linear, short‐horizon layer is intentionally parsimonious given annual frequency and limited sample length. It serves as a complementary check to Section 3.3, where PC detects directional nonlinear associations, and Granger tests whether that direction also manifests as incremental linear predictability after differencing.

Having established the four layers individually, we now describe how their outputs are unified into a common network representation that supports like‐for‐like comparison across methods.

Cointegration and VECM (Long Run)

Sections 3.3 and 3.4 addressed nonlinear orientation and short‐horizon linear predictability. We now ask a complementary question: Do pairs of nonstationary series share a stable long‐run relation, and which variable adjusts when that relation is perturbed? Two I(1) series are cointegrated when some static linear combination is I(0), indicating an equilibrium link (Engle and Granger 1987). When cointegration holds, a vector error‐correction model (VECM) separates long‐run equilibrium adjustment from short‐run dynamics (Johansen 1988; 1991).

Step 1: Data preparation. Let xt denote any macroeconomic or AI indicator observed annually. Occasional single missing values are linearly interpolated to preserve timing; no differencing is applied at this stage. Because cointegration is scale‐invariant, we standardize levels within the expanding window (13) x∼t=xt−μxσx(xtin levels), with μx,σx computed over Tt={1,…,t}. Unit‐root evidence supporting I(1) behavior in levels (and the deterministic terms used) is reported in Appendix C.

Step 2: Forward‐expanding Engle–Granger test. For each ordered pair (yt,xt) and recursion length Tt=|Tt|≥L0=5, we apply the Engle–Granger two‐step procedure with a deterministic trend: (i) estimate the cointegrating regression (14) yt=a(t)+b(t)xt+ut, (ii) test the residuals ut for a unit root. The routine returns the residual‐based t statistic and right‐tail probability py,x,t. Reversing roles yields px,y,t. Collecting these over pairs and t gives the panel {pi→j,t}i,j,t used in time‐resolved displays (e.g., heatmaps); figure captions indicate the significance thresholds.

Step 3: Direction via VECM loadings (when cointegrated). When the residual test rejects a unit root, we estimate a rank 1 VECM with one lag in first differences, (15) Δyt=αyut−1+∑k=1p−1Γy,kΔyt−k+∑k=1p−1Φy,kΔxt−k+εy,t, (16) Δxt=αxut−1+∑k=1p−1Γx,kΔxt−k+∑k=1p−1Φx,kΔyt−k+εx,t, where ut−1 is from (14). The loading α measures the speed at which a variable corrects deviations from equilibrium. We characterize VECM direction by relative adjustment speed: The variable with the smaller absolute loading is labeled the slower adjusting side, and the other is labeled the faster adjusting side, (17) (slower adjusting,faster adjusting)=(y,x),|αy|<|αx|,(x,y),otherwise. This yields a direction classification for cointegrated pairs, complementary to (but distinct from) short‐run Granger orientation.

Unless otherwise noted, summary tables use the full‐sample Engle–Granger test and the corresponding VECM loadings when cointegration is detected; time‐resolved panels are for visualization. Lag caps, deterministic terms, residual diagnostics, and robustness checks are documented in Appendix C.

In sum, the cointegration/VECM layer isolates persistent co‐movements and identifies which variable tends to absorb long‐run imbalances. Together with Sections 3.3 and 3.4, it completes our triangulation across nonlinear association, short‐run predictability, and long‐run equilibrium adjustment at annual frequency.

Lead–Lag Correlation (Timing Layer)

The previous layers address nonlinear orientation (Section 3.3), short‐horizon linear predictability (Section 3.4), and long‐run adjustment (Section 3.5). As a simple timing check with minimal parametric structure, we compute lead–lag correlation to indicate which series tends to move first when two stationary series co‐move (Lo and MacKinlay 1990).

Step 1: Preprocessing and stationarity. Let xt be any macroeconomic or AI indicator observed annually. Occasional single missing values are linearly interpolated; no further imputation is applied. To obtain I(0) inputs, we difference once (18) Δxt=xt−xt−1, then standardize within the estimation window (19) zx,t=Δxt−μΔxσΔx, with μΔx and σΔx computed over Tt={1,…,t}. The same transformation is applied to all variables; unit‐root diagnostics appear in Appendix C.

Step 2: Lead–lag correlation for a single window. For each ordered pair (y,x) and a fixed lag L=1, define (20) rx→y(t,L)=corrzx,1:t−L,zy,1+L:t, (21) ry→x(t,L)=corrzy,1:t−L,zx,1+L:t. Following the script, we report a single signed coefficient (22) ρxy,t=−rx→y(t,L),|rx→y(t,L)|≥|ry→x(t,L)|,−ry→x(t,L),|rx→y(t,L)|<|ry→x(t,L)|. Hence ρxy,t>0 implies that x leads y; ρxy,t<0 implies that y leads x; and |ρxy,t|∈[0,1] measures the strength of the lead–lag correlation.

Step 3: Forward‐expanding recursion. Starting from the minimum window length L0=5, we iterate t=1,…,T and store, for each t, the N×N matrix ρt=[ρij,t]. The collection {ρt}t=L0T provides a time‐resolved map of lead–lag relations for visualization (e.g., heatmaps).

This timing layer is intentionally parsimonious and purely correlational. It complements Section 3.3 (nonlinear orientation), Section 3.4 (short‐run predictability), and Section 3.5 (long‐run adjustment) by indicating which variable tends to move first when co‐movement is present, aiding risk‐relevant interpretation without additional parametric assumptions.

For PC, the directed orientation comes from the success‐rate asymmetry; negative values are clipped to zero before optional linear rescaling to [0,1].

Empirical Findings

This section reports the empirical results in a risk‐aware, triangulated manner. We proceed in four steps: (i) We present rank‐based summaries of weighted degree centrality (out, in, and total) to establish the relative prominence of AI and macro nodes under each layer; (ii) we use heatmaps to place those ranks in full relational context‐localizing where interactions concentrate and how AI nodes align with macro blocks; (iii) we turn to thresholded network visualizations to trace pathways that persist as cutoffs tighten; and (iv) we triangulate across layers and representations, prioritizing structures that recur over single‐method signals.

Throughout, we emphasize relative movements over absolute connectivity, report breadth (cross‐pair, cross‐layer recurrence) and depth (survival at stricter thresholds), and distinguish PC positive/negative/dark patterns. Uncertainty is addressed via sensitivity to multiple cutoffs, cross‐method corroboration, and the mechanical‐expansion benchmark for collaboration effects reported in Appendix F. Cross‐method and cross‐country comparisons surface both recurring regularities and salient divergences; we interpret these patterns in light of the literature on innovation spillovers and growth (e.g., Acemoglu et al. 2016; Griliches 1979), while treating the networks as monitoring tools rather than estimates of structural economic effects. The presentation flags only those findings that replicate across layers and thresholds, with explicit figure and table anchors at point of use.

We report results both excluding (Table 3–Table 6) and including (Table 7–Table 10) collaboration measures to assess whether—and how—their inclusion repositions nodes within the network. Specifically, we track changes in (i) out‐degree (outgoing connectivity), (ii) in‐degree (incoming connectivity), and (iii) total degree (hub prominence). Because degree can rise mechanically as variables are added, we focus on within‐layer rank shifts (baseline vs. collaboration‐augmented) and on cross‐layer recurrences (PC, Granger, VECM, lead–lag). These comparisons help assess whether AI‐related indicators move toward outgoing‐connector or hub positions and whether macro aggregates change their incoming connectivity when collaboration enters the system.5

Baseline Patterns (No Collaboration)

Tables 3 to 6 report baseline (no collaboration) degree rankings for both countries: Tables 3 and 4 cover China, and Table 5 and 6 cover the United States. All edge weights are normalized to [0,1] for comparability across methods (see Section 3.7).

For China, the PC positive layer is anchored by macro fundamentals‐Population, GDP (PPP), and GDP (current USD)—at the top of the out‐degree rankings, while AI indicators sit lower in the AI panel (ranks 11–15 in Table 3). In PC negative, FDI (net) leads by out‐degree with AI patents entering as a secondary outgoing connector (rank 4 in the all‐variables panel of Table 3). In PC dark, AI conference papers and patents emerge as conduits (ranks 2 and 4, respectively), indicating oscillatory, nonmonotonic associations. The Granger layer shows denser short‐run predictability, with AI conference papers, patents, and journals placed high in out‐degree (AI panel, Table 3). By contrast, cointegration/VECM out‐degree is dominated by prices and scale aggregates (GDP deflator, Population, CPI, GDP (PPP) in Table 3), while the in‐degree side highlights AI journals and conference papers as comparatively stronger receivers (Table 4), consistent with adjustment rather than leadership in long‐run equilibria. Lead–lag correlations reinforce a limited leading role for AI: Patents appears among the top four by out‐degree, but the broadest leading timing correlations remain with core macro aggregates (Tables 3 and 4).

For the United States, macro variables again anchor the PC positive out‐degree (notably Population, CPI, and GDP), while AI indicators populate the upper tier of the AI panel without displacing the leading macro nodes (Table 5). PC negative is led by Unemployment (rather than capital‐account items, in contrast with China). The Granger layer exhibits strong short‐run predictive links involving AI: research publications ranks first in out‐degree in both the AI‐only and all‐variables lists, with patents and journals also high (Table 5). Cointegration out‐degree remains macro‐centric (GDP deflator, CPI, Population), with AI variables comparatively smaller (Table 5). In the lead–lag layer, AI patents is the strongest AI leader by out‐degree (rank 9 in the all‐variables panel, rank 1 among AI indicators in Table 5), while its in‐degree rank is more moderate (rank 7 overall), indicating broad leading timing correlations without being the most exposed receiver (Tables 5 and 6).

Taken together, the baseline suggests that AI variables in China have a limited transmitting role in PC positive and in long‐run cointegration, but they feature more prominently in short‐run predictability (Granger) and as receivers in equilibrium adjustment (cointegration/VECM in‐degree). In the United States, AI indicators—especially research publications and patents—occupy stronger transmitting positions in Granger and lead–lag, while long‐run leadership remains with prices and scale aggregates. These contrasts provide a reference point for Section 4.1.2, where collaboration measures are introduced to test whether joint research repositions AI and macro nodes within the system.

Adding Collaboration Variables

Introducing US–China collaboration variables shifts the prominence of AI indicators relative to the no‐collaboration baseline, with more pronounced descriptive changes in China than in the United States. For China (Tables 7 and 8), collaboration on publications and conference papers enters the upper tier of PC (positive and dark) out‐degree rankings, increasing AI‐related outgoing connectivity, though core macro drivers (GDP, population) remain dominant. PC negative links continue to be led by FDI with limited AI involvement. In the Granger layer, breadth expands materially: all four domestic AI variables and both collaboration measures move into the upper ranks for both out‐ and in‐degree, indicating denser short‐run predictive associations between AI and the macro block. However, the null benchmark in Appendix F indicates that the Granger increases do not exceed the mechanical‐expansion threshold, so we interpret this layer as suggestive rather than as beyond‐mechanical collaboration evidence. Long‐run structure is more supportive: In the cointegration/VECM layer, several domestic AI publication indicators show centrality shifts above the mechanical benchmark, while collaboration variables display high in‐degree but weak out‐degree, consistent with adjustment to macro fundamentals rather than a leading long‐run role. Lead–lag correlations add a modest change: Publication collaboration moves among the more synchronous movers, while GDP and trade retain the largest footprints.

For the United States (Tables 9 and 10), collaboration introduces more contained adjustments. PC positive/negative, Granger, and lead–lag rankings are broadly stable, while the PC dark layer shows the largest change: US–China conference paper collaboration rises to the top in both out‐ and in‐degree, indicating a nonlinear association absent in the baseline. In the cointegration/VECM layer, the same collaboration variable records the highest in‐degree, suggesting that US participation in joint AI activity tends to adjust to macro conditions. Patent publications also climb in in‐degree, but the overall centrality of US AI variables persists. As with China, the added nodes raise absolute connectivity; here too, interpretation rests on rank movements, stricter threshold survival, and the null benchmark rather than levels. In sum, collaboration is associated with broader AI linkages in China and targeted shifts in the US network, but only a subset of these shifts exceed the mechanical‐expansion benchmark.

For China (Figure 15), a 25% cutoff admits many AI→macro edges, but most are modest. Tightening to above 40% leaves three AI nodes—AI research publications, AI journal papers, and US–China publication collaborations—that maintain medium, positive links to GDP, gross savings, and gross national expenditure. A distinctive relation appears at this band: publication collaboration → education exceeds 55%, a pattern not mirrored by other AI indicators. At 75%, AI nodes drop out entirely and the remaining strong links are confined to the macro triad GDP, gross savings, and population. The United States (Figure 19) shows the same broad shape at 25% and 75%, but differs at the intermediate band (55%): alongside the Chinese trio, AI patent publications form several medium‐to‐strong links to GDP, GDP, PPP, CPI, and the GDP deflator. Thus, the US PC layer exhibits a wider AI footprint at mid‐range strengths, with patenting playing a larger network role than in China.

For China (Figure 16), AI nodes—especially the two collaboration indicators—remain central across all three p‐value cutoffs. Edges both to and from key macro aggregates persist even at p<0.01, indicating dense short‐run predictive associations between AI and the economy. In the United States (Figure 20), AI variables also rank prominently, but the graph is more segmented: macro nodes chiefly link to macro nodes and AI to AI, with fewer cross‐cluster arrows. Hence US AI indicators enter the predictive chain, yet their Granger‐predictive links to broad macro aggregates are narrower and more selective than in China.

In China (Figure 17), the three most central nodes at all significance tiers are AI‐related (AI journal papers, US–China conference paper collaborations, US–China publication collaborations). Nearly all arrows, however, point into these AI nodes from macro variables, consistent with the VECM loading‐based direction: AI indicators adjust to restore the long‐run equilibrium rather than drive it. The US network (Figure 21) is similarly segmented into AI and macro clusters with relatively few bridges; AI patent publications stand out as the most central node across cutoffs, yet most cointegrating links are again directed toward the patent node, placing it in a follower role despite its prominence.

For China (Figure 18), many AI–macro co‐movements appear at |ρ|>0.20 and >0.50, but only a few very tight pairs survive at |ρ|>0.80: the two collaboration nodes as a pair, the AI journal–AI publications duo, and a macro block linking GDP, gross savings, and population. The United States (Figure 22) is even more selective at |ρ|>0.80, with surviving edges limited to (i) the collaboration pair, (ii) exports–imports, and (iii) the macro triad GDP, GDP, PPP, expenditure. At the intermediate threshold, the US graph displays a visibly bipartite structure (macro on one side, AI on the other) bridged primarily by publication collaboration → GDP, echoing the separation seen in the Granger and cointegration panels.

Across methods, the pathway picture is consistent with the earlier rankings and heatmaps: nonlinear dependence (PC positive) is broad but moderate. Granger links are dense and short‐run and cointegration suggests that long‐run balance is restored mainly through adjustments in AI variables themselves. Further, the lead–lag co‐movement is of medium intensity with a small set of high‐synchrony pairs. The countries differ in how AI indicators are associated with macro variables: the US pathway is more selective and patent‐oriented; China's is denser, more collaboration‐intensive, and more tightly interwoven with the macro core.

Cross‐Method Triangulation

We reduce single‐method bias by requiring convergence across methods (PC, Granger causality, cointegration/VECM, and lead–lag), representations (degree‐centrality rankings, heatmaps, and thresholded networks), and countries (China and the United States). This triangulation privileges structures that repeat across lenses and down‐weights pairwise extremes; our claims are exploratory and policy‐informing rather than fully identified structural causality.

Three regularities emerge across layers and displays:

Nonlinear layer (PC). AI–macro dependence is visible, but it does not dominate the system. In the rankings (Section 4.1), AI nodes typically sit in the middle of the table rather than at the very top. In the heatmaps (Section 4.2), the AI block is lighter in the positive PC panel, signaling fewer strong nonlinear matches. In the thresholded PC networks (Section 4.3), AI edges thin out quickly as the cutoff rises. Taken together, these patterns indicate moderate, mostly positive, state‐dependent associations‐useful for context, but not evidence of pervasive nonlinear leadership. Appendix E also shows why PC is not treated as a stand‐alone layer: the top‐K edge Jaccard distribution is wide (0.23–0.88), with lower overlap mainly under parameter choices that strain the annual sample, so PC links receive interpretive weight only when they recur or are corroborated by other layers.

Short‐run layer (Granger). The predictive web is dense. AI outputs‐publications and patents‐repeatedly appear among the most central nodes in the rankings and remain connected in the networks even at p<0.01. This is consistent with broad directional predictability both from AI to macro variables and from macro variables to AI. The cross‐country contrast is stable: in China, AI and macro nodes are tightly interwoven; in the United States, links cluster within domains (macro–macro and AI–AI), and cross‐cluster arrows are fewer.

Equilibrium/co‐movement layer (cointegration and lead–lag). VECM direction places most adjustment loads on AI variables. In both countries, cointegrating arrows predominantly enter AI nodes, implying a follower role for AI when long‐run balance is restored. Lead‐lag heatmaps and networks contract at higher |ρ| thresholds to a small, stable subset of pairs‐within‐AI links and a few macro triads such as GDP‐savings‐population. Where cross‐cluster leadership survives, it is largely macro‐led.

Adding US–China collaboration variables increases the embeddedness and centrality of AI nodes in both countries, with a stronger descriptive effect in China and a more localized footprint in the United States. Because enlarging the variable set can raise overall connectivity mechanically, Appendix F compares observed AI‐node shifts with a mechanical‐expansion null. Only eight of 48 AI‐node shifts exceed the conservative ratio threshold ρ>2, and six of these are in the cointegration layer. We therefore treat collaboration evidence as strongest for long‐run co‐movement/adjustment and more suggestive in Granger, PC positive/negative, and most lead–lag layers. Table 11 summarizes the resulting distinction between core claims and suggestive readings after cross‐method triangulation and the mechancial‐expansion null checks.

The rankings (Section 4.1), full‐sample heatmaps (Section 4.2), and thresholded networks (Section 4.3) point to a common message: AI–macro associations appear in both countries, but their structure differs. In the United States, the footprint is more patent‐oriented and selective. In China, it is denser and more collaboration‐intensive. This triangulated view supports risk‐aware monitoring by highlighting structures that recur across methods while down‐weighting single‐test extremes and mechanically inflated centrality shifts.

Discussion

AI–Macro Associations Across Methods

The empirical patterns reported above are consistent and interpretable, but their policy relevance depends critically on what the four‐layer design can and cannot identify. The design maps directional predictability, long‐run co‐movement, temporal ordering, and nonlinear state‐dependent associations; it does not identify structural causal effects of AI on macroeconomic outcomes, nor does it estimate welfare consequences of collaboration or decoupling. These limits are not peripheral caveats—they shape every interpretive claim that follows. Our first contribution is methodological: integrating PC with Granger causality, VECM‐based cointegration, and lead–lag correlation to build a multi‐layered view of AI–macro linkages (Section 3–Section 4). Each layer asks a distinct empirical question.

Three regularities recur across countries, representations (centrality tables, heatmaps, thresholded networks), and methods. First, AI indicators enter moderate, mostly positive nonlinear associations with macro aggregates, visible across PC positive layers in both the United States and China; the moderate magnitude implies that AI indicators complement traditional macro variables in monitoring dashboards rather than replace them. Second, research publications and patents carry short‐run predictive content in Granger tests—most consistently in the United States where AI publications rank first in out‐degree in both the AI‐only and all‐variables panels—which is consistent with their use as candidate early‐warning indicators but not as structural drivers of growth. Third, cointegration and VECM evidence recurrently places AI variables on the adjustment margin rather than the leadership margin: In both countries, AI indicators tend to be the variables that correct deviations from long‐run equilibria, not the variables that shift the equilibria themselves. This pattern is robust to the inclusion of collaboration variables and survives stricter cointegration thresholds.

Taken together, the layers tell a coherent monitoring‐oriented story: AI indicator activity is embedded in the macro system, shows moderate predictive value at short horizons, and co‐moves with fundamentals at long horizons from an adjustment rather than a leadership position. This is the empirical foundation on which the monitoring workflow in Section 5.5 is built.

Collaboration and Mechanical Network Expansion

Our second contribution is to test whether collaboration‐related centrality changes exceed a mechanical network‐expansion benchmark rather than simply asserting that higher centrality after adding collaboration nodes reflects a substantive association. Adding any two nodes to an existing network of N increases the maximum possible weighted degree for every existing node by 2w¯0 (the mean baseline edge weight), irrespective of whether the new nodes carry substantive information. Without accounting for this mechanical effect, the inference that collaboration “matters” would be unfounded.

Appendix F addresses this concern directly. Across 6 approaches × 2 countries × 4 domestic AI indicators, only eight of 48 AI‐node shifts exceed the conservative beyond‐mechanical threshold ρi>2. Six of the eight are concentrated in the cointegration layer: three in China (AI research publications, AI journal papers, and AI conference papers, with ρi values of 3.61, 3.35, and 2.25, respectively) and three in the United States (AI patent publications, AI journal papers, and AI research publications, with ρi values of 2.26, 3.64, and 3.63). The remaining two are US PC dark for AI journal papers (ρi=2.04) and US lead–lag for AI conference papers (ρi=2.37).

The concentration of beyond‐mechanical shifts in the cointegration layer is substantively informative. It suggests that when US–China collaboration is present, the long‐run co‐movement structure of the AI–macro system is different in a way that exceeds generic network expansion, while the short‐run predictive architecture (Granger) and most nonlinear associations (PC) are not similarly differentiated. The Granger broadening that appeared visually striking in earlier analyses does not pass the null benchmark and is therefore classified as suggestive rather than core. Importantly, the direction of the cointegration finding is asymmetric: Collaboration variables display high in‐degree but weak out‐degree, indicating that they tend to adjust to macro fundamentals rather than lead them. This is consistent with a picture in which research collaboration is a feature of a well‐functioning macro–AI equilibrium rather than a driver of it—an important distinction for any policy reading.

Comparative Asymmetry Between the United States and China

The third contribution is comparative. The two countries share the broad pattern identified above—AI variables occupy the adjustment margin of long‐run co‐movement and carry some short‐run predictive content—but they differ in which indicators carry the most signal and in how collaboration reshapes the network.

In the United States, AI patent publications stand out. They rank among the top AI nodes by out‐degree in both Granger and lead–lag layers in the baseline network, they exhibit the highest in‐degree among AI variables in the cointegration layer (rank 1, weighted in‐degree 11.90), and they are among the three cointegration nodes whose centrality shifts exceed the mechanical benchmark when collaboration enters. This patent‐centric pattern is consistent with the US innovation ecosystem, in which patents constitute a legal and commercial bottleneck through which AI activity feeds into productive economic relationships (Bresnahan and Trajtenberg 1995).

In China, the dominant signal involves research publications, journal papers, and conference papers—not patents. In the baseline, Chinese AI variables are more peripheral in PC positive and long‐run cointegration layers than their US counterparts, but they gain substantially when US–China collaboration indicators enter: Three publication‐type indicators clear the null benchmark in the cointegration layer, and PC positive and dark out‐degree rankings see publication and conference collaboration variables rise to the upper tier. This collaboration‐intensive pattern is consistent with evidence on the citation premium for US–China co‐authored AI papers (AlShebli et al. 2024; Klasa et al. 2020) and with the relative weight of bibliometric output in China's AI development trajectory.

We stress that these patterns describe network structure under a specific data window (1980–2020) and two particular innovation proxies. They should not be read as evidence that patents cause macroeconomic performance in the United States, or that publication collaboration drives growth in China. The appropriate interpretation is that the macro‐embeddedness of AI activity depends on the institutional and innovative character of each economy, which argues for country‐specific indicator sets rather than a universal AI monitoring dashboard (Comin and Hobijn 2004; Brynjolfsson et al. 2017; Acemoglu and Restrepo 2020).

Methodological Transparency and Risk‐Aware Inference

The fourth contribution is transparency about what the evidence can and cannot support, and about the conditions under which findings deserve interpretive weight. The guiding principle is convergence: We highlight patterns visible across multiple methods and reinforced across representations, treating method‐specific signals as hypotheses rather than policy guidance (Section 4.4).

Four constraints bound the conclusions. First, annual data from 1980–2020 provide approximately 40 observations per country. This limits statistical power and makes the detection of short‐lived shocks or structural breaks unreliable. Results are most credible for persistent, macro‐scale co‐movements rather than for cyclical or policy‐driven dynamics. Second, PC is computed on standardized levels and does not provide a conventional p‐value; without cross‐layer corroboration, PC edges cannot be separated from apparent structure induced by common trends. Appendix E documents that the median Jaccard similarity of top‐K PC edge sets across hyperparameter configurations is 0.50 (IQR 0.23–0.88): A moderate backbone persists, but individual edges are sensitivity‐dependent, and the lower tail of the Jaccard distribution is a real small‐sample warning. Third, Granger and cointegration provide conventional significance metrics but rely on small bivariate systems and a large number of pairwise tests; individual p‐values should not be read in isolation. Fourth, adding collaboration nodes mechanically enlarges the network; Appendix F operationalizes this concern, but the ρi>2 threshold is a conservative diagnostic rather than a precise statistical boundary.

These constraints do not undermine the core findings; they calibrate them. Short‐run Granger predictive content in publications and patents, cointegration placing AI variables on the adjustment margin, and the concentration of beyond‐mechanical collaboration shifts in the long‐run layer are all consistent with multiple methods, survive stricter thresholds, and align with theoretical priors about general‐purpose technology diffusion (Bresnahan and Trajtenberg 1995; Comin and Hobijn 2004). That alignment between empirical patterns and theoretical priors—without claiming to test or confirm the theory—is the appropriate standard of evidence for a risk‐monitoring study of this scope.

Practical Implications for Risk Monitoring

The practical implication of this study is monitoring‐oriented. AI indicators can broaden traditional macro‐risk dashboards by providing complementary signals at short‐run (Granger) and long‐run (cointegration) horizons and by flagging whether collaboration‐related network structure departs from what mechanical expansion alone would predict. Neither signal is definitive in isolation; each is best used as a screening layer that triggers further analysis rather than as an automatic policy lever.

Operationally, we propose a three‐stage screening workflow. In the first stage, annual updates of the AI–macro panel are used to recompute Granger predictability of macro indicators from AI publications and patents. Persistent predictive content in this layer—checked alongside conventional macro predictors—would support elevating AI monitoring from supplementary to core status in the relevant country dashboard. In the second stage, VECM loading estimates are updated to assess whether AI variables remain on the adjustment margin or show signs of a changed adjustment role. Such a shift would represent a materially new pattern and would warrant escalation to model re‐estimation and sectoral cross‐check. In the third stage, collaboration‐related centrality shifts are screened against the null benchmark of Appendix F. A breach above ρi>2 in the cointegration layer—particularly for publication‐type indicators in China or patent indicators in the United States—would signal that the collaboration structure has changed beyond generic connectivity and may warrant closer scrutiny of how joint R&D relates to long‐run co‐movement.

A breach in more than one stage would trigger analyst review, model re‐estimation, and comparison with sectoral evidence on compute investment, labor market composition, AI‐intensive production shares, and strategic policy changes before any policy escalation. This sequential protocol prevents single‐indicator overreaction while maintaining sensitivity to system‐level shifts. It aligns the paper with risk‐analysis practice that combines early‐warning signals, network topology, systemic‐risk framing, and AI‐specific risk governance frameworks (Zhuang et al. 2022; Renn and Lucas 2022; Bier et al. 2025) rather than optimizing a single detection threshold.

The cross‐country asymmetry documented in Section 5.3 has a direct monitoring implication: A single shared AI indicator set is unlikely to be optimal for both the United States and China. Patent‐related indicators deserve prioritization in US monitoring; publication and collaboration indicators deserve prioritization in China. This suggests that international AI risk assessments should incorporate country‐specific indicator weights rather than applying uniform dashboards, a finding consistent with heterogeneous diffusion patterns documented in the GPT literature (Comin and Hobijn 2004; Brynjolfsson et al. 2017). In periods of intensifying techno‐geopolitical frictions—which can disrupt collaboration patterns abruptly (Jia et al. 2024; Kitajima and Okamura 2025)—the collaboration‐centrality benchmark in the third stage becomes especially valuable as an early indicator of network reconfiguration.

Conclusion

This paper examined whether and how AI indicators are associated with macroeconomic aggregates in the United States and China over 1980–2020, using a four‐layer framework that triangulates pattern causality, Granger causality, cointegration/VECM, and lead–lag correlation. Three core findings emerge from cross‐method triangulation and null‐network benchmarking, and two suggestive findings follow from method‐ or layer‐specific signals.

The first core finding is that AI indicators carry short‐run predictive content for macroeconomic aggregates in Granger tests, most consistently in the United States where AI research publications rank first in out‐degree connectivity in both AI‐specific and all‐variable panels. This supports using AI bibliometric indicators as candidate early‐warning variables in macro‐risk dashboards, not as structural drivers.

The second core finding is that AI indicators occupy the adjustment margin in long‐run cointegration relationships rather than the leadership margin. In both countries, AI variables tend to correct deviations from equilibrium while macroeconomic fundamentals—price indices, scale aggregates, trade flows—lead. This pattern is robust across threshold levels and is consistent with AI activity being shaped by the broader macro environment rather than reshaping it autonomously over the sample period.

The third core finding is that US–China research collaboration shifts AI‐node centrality beyond what mechanical network expansion predicts, but only in the cointegration layer. Of 48 possible AI‐node centrality shifts, only eight exceed the ρi>2 threshold, and six of these are in the long‐run co‐movement layer (three for China, three for the United States). The Granger and most PC collaboration shifts are consistent with mechanical expansion and are classified as suggestive. The directional interpretation is further qualified: Collaboration variables tend to adjust to macro conditions rather than lead them, indicating that research partnership is a feature of macro–AI equilibrium rather than its cause.

The two suggestive findings are: (a) dark PC links, which indicate nonlinear, nonmonotone associations that deserve monitoring as hypothesis‐generating signals but are not interpretable as policy mechanisms; and (b) the broader Granger connectivity observed when collaboration variables enter, which may reflect genuine system enrichment or mechanical graph expansion and requires future analysis with longer or higher frequency data to distinguish.

The comparative asymmetry between the United States and China deserves emphasis beyond its network description. The US pattern is patent‐centric and selective; China's is publication‐ and collaboration‐intensive. These differences are consistent with the two countries' distinct innovation ecosystems and suggest that country‐specific indicator sets are preferable to universal AI dashboards in international risk monitoring. The asymmetry becomes more consequential in periods of techno‐geopolitical friction, when collaboration structures can shift abruptly and the collaboration‐centrality benchmark becomes a leading rather than a lagging signal.

Three directions for future work follow directly from the study's limitations. First, richer data—higher frequency series, firm‐level or sector‐level AI adoption measures, or post‐2020 observations that capture the generative AI transition—would substantially improve power and allow structural break testing (Bai and Perron 2023) that is not feasible with the current panel. Second, the triangulation design could be extended to a broader set of countries, enabling panel cointegration methods that recover common long‐run trends while controlling for country heterogeneity. Third, the null‐network benchmark for collaboration is deliberately conservative; a more refined benchmark that models the graph‐generating process of AI collaboration networks—rather than drawing generic edge weights from the baseline distribution—would yield tighter inference about which centrality shifts are beyond‐mechanical.

All findings are interpreted as exploratory, risk‐aware evidence for monitoring and hypothesis generation. They provide directional predictability evidence, equilibrium co‐movement estimates, network embeddedness rankings, and a testable collaboration benchmark. They do not identify structural causal effects of AI on macroeconomic outcomes, estimate welfare counterfactuals, or provide a basis for treating any single AI indicator as a reliable policy lever on the strength of this evidence alone. Used within the three‐stage monitoring workflow described in Section 5.5, and updated as new data become available, the framework can serve as one component of an evidence‐based, risk‐informed approach to AI governance at the national level.
