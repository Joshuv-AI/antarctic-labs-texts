Unveiling sleep disturbances in KCNB1‐related disorders: Insights from a cohort of 78 individuals

INTRODUCTION

The KCNB1 gene (MIM# 600397) encodes an 858–amino acid protein forming the α‐subunit of the voltage‐gated potassium channel subfamily 2 (Kv2.1). 1 , 2 , 3 KCNB1 is broadly expressed, with highest levels in the brain, skeletal muscle, pancreas, and small intestine. 2 Pathogenic KCNB1 variants typically act through a loss‐of‐function mechanism, accounting for the broad clinical spectrum observed. 1

The phenotype spectrum includes global developmental delay, marked language impairment, behavioral disturbances, epilepsy, and additional comorbidities such as feeding difficulties and orthopedic or ophthalmologic abnormalities. 1 , 2 , 4 Affected individuals may present with developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (DEEs), characterized by frequent and often drug‐resistant seizures, or with developmental encephalopathy (DE) with drug‐responsive epilepsy or without seizures. 1 , 5

Sleep disturbances are well recognized and frequent in genetic epilepsies and developmental encephalopathies. 6 , 7 Evidence from sodium channelopathies highlights a complex bidirectional interaction between sleep and seizures. 8 , 9 In DEEs, recurrent epileptiform discharges and frequent seizures fragment sleep and disrupt its architecture, further lowering the seizure threshold and worsening seizure control. 6 , 7 , 10 Sleep also plays a pivotal role in synaptic plasticity and network remodeling, and individuals with severe neurodevelopmental delay frequently experience sleep disturbances arising from neurotransmitter imbalance, disrupted circadian regulation, and cortical–subcortical dysfunction. 7 , 11 , 12

To date, sleep disturbances in KCNB1‐related disorders remain largely unexplored, with only brief mentions in a few reports. 4 , 13 , 14 Early recognition and tailored management of sleep problems, given their broad implications, could markedly improve the quality of life of affected individuals and their families.

Here, we characterize sleep disturbances in individuals with KCNB1 variants, assessing their prevalence, features, and impact on daily functioning.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design, inclusion criteria, and data collection

We enrolled individuals with KCNB1‐related disorders through an international collaboration between the network of the French Reference Center for Rare Epilepsies and other centers, with additional support from the KCNB1 France Family Association (https://kcnb1‐france.org).

Participants carrying KCNB1 variants were included. For each subject, demographic and genetic data, developmental milestones, findings from physical examinations, detailed epilepsy history, electroencephalographic (EEG) recordings, and comorbidities were collected.

Patients were classified according to ILAE definitions 15 as having developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE) or developmental encephalopathy (DE). In patients meeting clinical criteria for DEE‐SWAS, formal neuropsychological assessments were performed as part of clinical care; however, detailed neuropsychological findings are beyond the scope of the present study and will be reported separately.

Seizure types and, when possible, epilepsy syndromes were defined according to ILAE classification. 15 , 16 , 17 Seizure frequency was categorized as yearly (<1/month), monthly (1–3/month), Weekly (≥1/week), or daily (≥1/day). Seizures were further clustered as frequent (daily to weekly), sporadic (monthly to yearly), or seizure freedom (seizure‐free for at least 1 year). We also specified if they were sleep‐related or non–sleep‐related. For each individual, the number of antiseizure medications (ASMs) was recorded as none, one ASM, or two or more ASMs.

To complete the assessment of sleep profiles, caregivers filled out two standardized questionnaires after providing anamnestic information: the Sleep Disturbance Scale for Children (SDSC) 18 and the Children's Sleep Habits Questionnaire (CSHQ, 22 items). 19 Although the SDSC is validated for individuals under 20 years of age, it was also administered to older participants with intellectual disability, consistent with previous literature. 20 SDSC was scored according to standardized procedures. Abnormal subscale scores were defined as T‐scores ≥70, corresponding to values above the 95th percentile, in line with the original validation of the instrument. Questionnaires were available in French, English, or Italian according to family fluency and were completed by caregivers; scoring was not blinded to clinical severity. Missing responses were systematically completed through telephone or telemedicine encounters, and all SDSC questionnaires were complete and included in the analysis.

We investigated associations between sleep disturbances and clinical variables, including motor function, behavioral features, and extra‐neurological comorbidities (orthopedic, gastrointestinal, cardiopulmonary, and ophthalmologic). Sleep profiles were also compared between individuals with DEE and DE. In addition, we analyzed the relationship between sleep disturbances and epilepsy characteristics—seizure type, semiology, frequency, sleep‐related occurrence, EEG features, and number of antiseizure medications (ASMs) administered. We finally explored potential associations between sleep disturbances and the protein domain affected by the identified KCNB1 variants.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or legal guardians, and the study was approved by our review board committee (NCT06380192).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported as frequencies for categorical variables and as means or medians for continuous variables, as appropriate. Group comparisons were performed using chi‐square or Fisher's exact tests. Age‐related trends in sleep disturbances were explored using locally weighted scatterplot smoothing (LOWESS) for descriptive purposes, and associations between age and abnormal SDSC subscale scores were tested using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient.

Associations between sleep disturbances and clinical or epilepsy‐related variables were assessed using univariate logistic regression models, with effect sizes expressed as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Predicted probabilities were derived from these univariate models. All tests were two‐tailed, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05 (or q < 0.05 after false discovery rate correction). Given the sample size, all analyses were considered exploratory, and multivariate models were not performed.

RESULTS

We enrolled 78 patients (42 males, 36 females), including 15/78 (19.2%) previously reported by Bar et al. 1 , 5 The mean age was 13.7 ± 9.3 years (median 11, IQR 9.07).

In silico prediction data were available for 65 individuals: 51/65 (78.5%) pathogenic, 11/65 (16.9%) likely pathogenic, and 3/65 (4.6%) variants of uncertain significance (VUS). All inherited variants were classified as pathogenic. Seventy‐four KCNB1 variants (74/78, 94.9%) occurred de novo, and four (4/78, 5.1%) were inherited from clinically unaffected parents with somatic mosaicism. Variant types included missense (69/78, 88.5%), truncating (8/78, 10.2%), and one in‐frame deletion (1/78, 1.3%).

Affected individuals presented with epilepsy, developmental delay, behavioral and psychiatric disorders, extra‐neurological comorbidities, and sleep disturbances.

Epilepsy

Epilepsy was present in 63 of 78 patients (80.7%). The mean age of onset was 2.9 ± 3.0 years (median 2, IQR 3.1). The most frequent seizures reported were tonic–clonic seizures (20/63, 31.7%), atypical absence seizures (11/63, 17.5%), tonic (10/63, 15.9%), and clonic seizures (10/63, 15.9%).

Sleep‐related seizures occurred in 31 of 63 patients (49.2%). Twenty‐three patients (23/63, 36.5%) were seizure‐free; 22/63 (34.9%) had frequent seizures; 13/63 (20.7%) had sporadic seizures; and for 5/63 (7.9%) patients, data on seizure frequency were not available.

Epilepsy syndrome was identified in 21 of 63 patients (33.3%), including infantile epileptic spasms syndrome (IESS, 10/21, 47.6%), Lennox–Gastaut syndrome (LGS, 5/21, 23.8%), childhood absence epilepsy (CAE, 3/21, 14.3%), and DEE with spike‐and‐wave activation during sleep (DEE‐SWAS, 3/21, 14.3%).

EEG data were available for 39 of 63 patients (61.9%). Awake EEGs showed slow background activity in 13 of 39 recordings (33.3%). Epileptiform discharges, observed in 25 of 39 individuals (64.1%), were multifocal (16/25, 64%), generalized (7/25, 28%), or focal (2/25, 8%). Photosensitivity during intermittent photic stimulation (IPS) was reported in 4 of 39 (10.2%), and hypsarrhythmia in 3 of 39 (7.7%). Sleep EEGs were recorded in 29 of 39 patients (74.3%); eighteen patients (18/29, 62.1%) had unremarkable sleep recording, four (4/29, 13.8%) showed absence of physiological sleep grapho‐elements, and seven (7/29, 24.1%) showed a spike‐and‐wave activation during sleep (SWAS) pattern, including three (3/7, 42.8%) clinically diagnosed with developmental and epileptic encephalopathy with spike–wave activation in sleep (DEE‐SWAS) (Figure 1).

Twelve patients (12/63, 19.0%) were not receiving ASMs, 21/63 (33.3%) were on monotherapy, and 30/63 (47.7%) were on polytherapy. The most used ASMs were valproate (18/51, 34.9%), lamotrigine (16/51, 31.7%), levetiracetam (13/51, 25.4%), cannabidiol (7/13, 14.3%), and clobazam (6/51, 12.7%).

Development, behavior, and comorbidities

Developmental delay was present in 75 of 78 patients (96.1%), including 57/75 (76%) with DEE and 18/75 (24%) with DE. Independent walking was achieved by 63 of 78 individuals (80.7%) aged ≥2 years, while 11/78 (14.1%) required assistance. Most participants aged ≥3 years showed expressive language impairment, with 26 of 61 (42.7%) being nonverbal. Behavioral disorders were reported in 34 of 78 patients (43.6%), autism spectrum disorder (ASD) in 45 of 78 (57.7%), and attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) in 38 of 78 (48.7%).

Extra‐neurological comorbidities were observed in 70 out of 78 patients (89.7%), including feeding problems (35/70, 50%), ophthalmologic disorders (14/70, 20%), orthopedic abnormalities (15/70, 21.4%), and cardiopulmonary involvement (6/70, 8.6%).

Sleep disturbances

Fifty‐three patients (53/78, 67.9%) exhibited sleep disturbances based on questionnaire scores and anamnestic data.

SDSC questionnaires

SDSC questionnaires were completed by all caregivers (78/78, 100%). Abnormal scores in at least one SDSC subscale were observed in 53 patients (53/78, 67.9%). Among them, 38/53 (71.7%) had disorders of initiating or maintaining sleep (DIMS), 25/53 (47.2%) showed disorders of excessive somnolence (DOES), 10/53 (18.9%) had sleep breathing disorders (SBD), 4/53 (7.5%) had sleep–wake transition disorders (SWTD), 2/53 (3.8%) had disorders of arousal (DA), and 1/53 (1.9%) had sleep hyperhidrosis (SHY). Among the 53 individuals with abnormal SDSC scores, 31 (58.5%) exhibited an isolated sleep disturbance affecting a single SDSC subscale, whereas 22 (41.5%) showed abnormalities in multiple subscales. In this last group, 17 (77.3%) had alterations in two subscales and 5 (22.7%) in three subscales.

Sleep disturbances were analyzed across three age groups: <5 years (7/53, 13.2%), 5–9 years (22/53, 41.5%), and >9 years (49/53, 92.4%). Among participants younger than 9 years, disorders of initiating and maintaining sleep (DIMS) predominated, whereas in older individuals, both DIMS and disorders of excessive somnolence (DOES) were frequent. The prevalence of DOES increase significantly with age (p = 0.011), being rare before 9 years and common thereafter (Figure 2A).

When age was considered as a continuous variable, distinct age‐related trends emerged across sleep disorder categories. Disorders of initiating and maintaining sleep (DIMS) remained the most frequent throughout the lifespan, decreasing from early childhood to adolescence and increasing again in adulthood. Contrarily, disorders of excessive somnolence (DOES) became progressively more frequent with advancing age, showing a significant positive correlation (p = 0.0007) (Figure 2B).

DOES were significantly more common in patients with orthopedic abnormalities (p = 0.016) and in those requiring assisted ambulation (p = 0.032) and showed a positive trend among nonverbal individuals. DIMS represented the most prevalent sleep disturbance across clinical subgroups, particularly among individuals with developmental encephalopathy (DE), ASD, ADHD, or spasticity (≈50%–60% within each subgroup). Increased DIMS frequencies were also observed in patients with assisted ambulation and feeding difficulties. The prevalence of SBD remained low (<20%), while DA and SWTD were rare (<10%) and showed no consistent association with any clinical feature (Figure 4A).

Within the epilepsy subgroup, sleep disturbances were significantly more common among patients receiving polytherapy (≥2 ASMs) (p = 0.037) and showed a positive, although nonsignificant, trend in those with frequent seizures (OR = 3.19, p = 0.09). No associations were observed for sleep‐related seizures, sporadic seizures, or seizure freedom. The likelihood of sleep disturbance was lowest in patients on monotherapy or with sporadic seizures and highest in those with frequent seizures receiving polytherapy (Figure 3C).

The interaction between seizure frequency and medication load demonstrated an additive effect: sporadic seizures/monotherapy ≈0.3; sporadic seizures/polytherapy ≈0.6; frequent seizures/monotherapy ≈0.45; and frequent seizures/polytherapy ≈0.9 (Figure 3D).

DIMS and DOES were the most prevalent sleep disturbances in the epilepsy cohort, whereas SBD, DA, SWTD, and SHY were uncommon. DIMS occurred in 55% of patients with frequent seizures and in 43% of those on polytherapy. Moderate rates were noted in patients with sleep‐related seizures (41%), while frequencies decreased in those with sporadic seizures (15%). Notably, insomnia‐like symptoms persisted even among seizure‐free individuals (57%). DOES was significantly more prevalent in patients on polytherapy (p = 0.0289) and showed a weaker, nonsignificant trend in individuals with frequent (45%) or sleep‐related seizures (47%). Rates were lower among those with sporadic seizures (31%) or seizure freedom (26%). SBD remained rare (<20% across subgroups), with only a slight increase among polytherapy patients (~20%). DA was infrequent but slightly higher in individuals with sleep‐related seizures (~12%), while SWTD and SHY were consistently <10% and showed no clear clinical associations (Figure 4B).

CSHQ questionnaires

The Children's Sleep Habits Questionnaire (CSHQ) was completed by 21 caregivers (26.9%). Thirteen caregivers (16.7%) did not complete the questionnaire, whereas in 44/78 cases (56.4%), the child's age fell outside the eligible range (4–10 years) for administration.

Abnormal scores in at least one subscale were identified in 13 patients (61.9%). The most frequently affected domain was Night Wakings/Sleep Anxiety (9/13, 69.2%), followed by Sleep Duration/Daytime Sleepiness (5/13, 38.5%), Bedtime Resistance/Sleep Onset Delay (4/13, 30.7%), and Sleep‐Disordered Breathing/Parasomnias (1/13, 7.7%).

Genetic and EEG associations with sleep disturbances

Sleep disturbances were observed among individuals carrying variants across different Kv2.1 domains. Sleep disturbances were more often noted among individuals harboring variants affecting transmembrane or pore‐forming regions, including S4, S6, and the pore‐forming domains. Variants located in S3 and S5 were less frequently associated with this phenotype. These observations are based on domain‐level frequencies across categories with variable and limited sample sizes (Figure S1).

The absence of physiological sleep grapho‐elements was associated with the presence of sleep disturbances, and 86% of patients exhibiting a SWAS pattern were affected (Figure 1). Although Fisher's exact tests did not reach statistical significance due to the small sample size, an association between disrupted sleep EEG architecture and clinical sleep disturbances was observed at a descriptive level.

DISCUSSION

This study highlights the high prevalence of sleep disturbances in KCNB1‐related disorders, a comorbidity that has received limited attention in previous studies. 4 , 13 , 14 These disturbances are well recognized and frequent in other genetic epilepsies and developmental encephalopathies, where they contribute substantially to neurobehavioral morbidity and negatively impact quality of life of the patient and his family. 10 , 11 , 21 , 22 , 23

The complex bidirectional relationship between sleep and epilepsy has long been acknowledged, particularly in individuals with frequent or drug‐resistant seizures. 7 , 11 , 24 Sleep modulates neuronal excitability and seizure threshold, while seizures and interictal epileptiform activity disrupt sleep architecture, creating a self‐perpetuating cycle. Seizures occurring during wakefulness and frequent nocturnal discharges can lead to sleep fragmentation, microarousals, reduced sleep efficiency, and alterations in stage distribution, including decreased slow‐wave (N3) and REM sleep. 7 , 12 , 25 , 26 , 27 Sleep‐related interictal discharges may act as internal triggers, producing excessive paroxysmal arousals and frequent transitions between sleep stages. 28 Moreover, antiseizure medications (ASMs) influence sleep–wake regulation. Depending on the agent and its pharmacologic profile, ASMs may cause daytime somnolence, insomnia, or changes in REM and slow‐wave proportions. 7 , 21 Polytherapy and the sedative effects of most ASMs may amplify sleep disruption, whereas poorly controlled seizures perpetuate cortical hyperexcitability.

Consistently, among patients with epilepsy, sleep disturbances were more frequently reported in individuals with frequent seizures or receiving polytherapy (≥2 ASMs). By contrast, patients on monotherapy or those who were seizure‐free generally showed more favorable sleep profiles. These associations should be interpreted with caution, as causality cannot be inferred. Rather, they likely reflect a convergence of markers of greater disease severity, including seizure burden, treatment complexity, and neurodevelopmental impairment, which are closely interrelated and all impact sleep quality. Together, these findings highlight the importance of integrated clinical approaches that consider seizure control and sleep disturbances within the broader context of epilepsy severity.

Sleep disturbances were more frequently observed in individuals with abnormal sleep EEG patterns, including sleep‐activated spike‐and‐wave (SWAS) discharges. However, sleep EEG recordings were available only for a subset of patients, EEG findings were heterogeneous, and systematic polysomnography was not performed. Consequently, these observations should be interpreted with caution. Nonetheless, the association between altered sleep EEG architecture and sleep disturbances is biologically plausible and consistent with prior reports describing fragmented and unstable NREM sleep as a marker of widespread network dysfunction in developmental and epileptic encephalopathies. 29 Physiologically, NREM sleep supports synaptic downscaling, a form of homeostatic plasticity that resets synaptic strength and metabolic demand. 29 , 30 Disruption of this mechanism, as seen in DEE‐SWAS, leads to cumulative impairments in synaptic normalization, which manifest as both sleep disruption and cognitive–behavioral regression. Notably, recovery of slow‐wave homeostasis following SWAS resolution has been associated with improved cognitive performance and behavioral outcomes, emphasizing the crucial role of sleep integrity in neurodevelopmental prognosis. 31

Sleep plays also a central role in the remodeling of neuronal networks, with REM sleep particularly involved in learning, emotional processing, and memory consolidation. 12 Individuals with severe neurodevelopmental impairment, regardless of epilepsy status, are especially vulnerable to sleep disturbances. This vulnerability reflects a convergence of mechanisms, including altered neurotransmitter signaling, circadian dysregulation, and impaired cortical–subcortical communication. 11 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 In our study, sleep disturbances were significantly more frequent in individuals with severe developmental impairment, nonverbal language, spasticity, assisted ambulation, feeding difficulties, and ASD. These findings suggest that network dysfunction underlying the motor, language, and behavioral phenotype of KCNB1‐related disorders also extends to sleep–wake regulation. Early recognition and targeted treatment of sleep problems might therefore provide meaningful improvements in daily functioning and overall quality of life for both patients and families.

Disorders of initiating or maintaining sleep (DIMS) were the most prevalent disturbance identified, consistent across both SDSC and CSHQ assessments and in line with previous reports in genetic DEEs. 6 , 10 DIMS predominated among patients with marked neurodevelopmental impairment, spasticity, feeding difficulties, ASD, or ADHD, as well as those with DEEs characterized by frequent seizures or polytherapy. Frequent nocturnal awakenings likely reflect disturbances of both sleep macro‐ and microarchitecture, as documented in sodium channelopathies including Dravet syndrome. 10 , 36 Poly‐graphic and acti‐graphic studies in these conditions have shown reduced sleep efficiency, prolonged latency, and increased transitions between stages, all contributing to sleep instability. 7 , 36

Disorders of excessive somnolence (DOES) represented the second most frequent sleep disturbance and were significantly associated with polytherapy, as well as showing higher rates in patients with frequent or sleep‐related seizures. Daytime sleepiness may stem from fragmented nocturnal sleep, sedative medication effects, or seizure‐related arousals that disrupt REM cycles. 6 , 10 In individuals on polytherapy, additive pharmacologic effects likely amplify this somnolence.

We found that the prevalence of disorders of excessive somnolence (DOES) increased with age, without parallel increase in seizure frequency or ASM exposure. This observation raises the possibility that daytime sleepiness in later disease stages could be influenced by age‐related changes in network organization, beyond direct epileptic or pharmacologic effects. Similar shifts toward non–seizure‐driven sleep–wake dysregulation have been proposed in other genetic DEEs, including Dravet syndrome. 37 Longitudinal studies will be required to clarify these mechanisms. Moreover, DOES were more common in patients with orthopedic abnormalities and in those requiring assisted ambulation. This association likely reflects a multifactorial contribution of sleep‐disrupting factors. Nocturnal pain, positioning‐related discomfort, abnormal posture, and common comorbidities such as gastroesophageal reflux, constipation, or recurrent coughing with possible pulmonary dysfunction may impair sleep quality, resulting in sleep fragmentation and daytime sleepiness in this patient group. 38 , 39 , 40

Disorders of arousal (DA) and sleep hyperhidrosis (SHY) were rare, consistent with observations in individuals with severe neurodevelopmental disorders. 6 , 41 Their low prevalence may reflect impaired cortical–subcortical integration and loss of stable N3 sleep, which normally supports parasomnias including DA. Persistent epileptiform discharges, reduced sleep efficiency, altered spindle activity, and diminished REM proportion may further dampen autonomic reactivity and arousal thresholds. 6 , 7 Collectively, these alterations provide a coherent pathophysiological explanation for the rarity of DA and SHY in this population. 23 , 37 , 42 , 43

Genetic studies have identified molecular pathways linking neurodevelopment, synaptic plasticity, and sleep regulation. Ion channels and neurotransmitter systems involved in neuronal excitability also modulate sleep–wake transitions, suggesting overlapping biological frameworks. 24 Evidence from sodium channelopathies such as SCN1A‐ and SCN8A‐related disorders supports this concept. 6 , 8 , 9 , 10 The Nav1.1 channel, encoded by SCN1A, is expressed predominantly in GABAergic neurons of the hypothalamus, thalamic reticular nucleus, and cortex, where its loss disrupts inhibitory control and sleep architecture. 8 , 10 Similarly, Nav1.6 dysfunction, resulting from SCN8A variants, alters the sleep–wake profile by reducing diurnal corticosterone levels and REM sleep. 6 , 9

Although sleep phenotypes in KCNB1‐related disorders have not been characterized, the widespread cortical and subcortical expression of the Kv2.1 channel and its central role in neuronal repolarization and network synchrony provide a compelling neurobiological rationale. 1 , 44 Kv2.1 loss of function alters action potential repolarization and disrupts synaptic homeostasis—processes fundamental to maintaining physiological sleep rhythms and arousal stability. 1 , 45 The convergence of these electrophysiological and molecular mechanisms likely contributes to the sleep abnormalities observed in our cohort.

LIMITATIONS

This study has some limitations. The assessment of sleep disorders was primarily based on two sleep questionnaires (SDSC and CSHQ), with a partial response rate for the CSHQ due to the narrow age range covered by this scale. Although these scores are validated, our results would have benefited from longitudinal data that allow exploring the modification of sleep disorder and from objective sleep measures, such as polysomnography, actigraphy, or other new friendly devices that are better tolerated by the vulnerable and disabled population. Moreover, we did not explore the use of sleep medications in our cohort. Given the exploratory nature of the analyses, potential confounding effects—particularly for the association between disorders of excessive somnolence and age—could not be fully disentangled from correlated factors such as developmental severity or antiseizure medication exposure.

CLINICAL RELEVANCE AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Sleep disturbances in KCNB1‐related disorders appear to arise from multiple, interacting factors—including seizure activity, medication effects, neurodevelopmental impairment, and intrinsic channel dysfunction. Comprehensive clinical and instrumental evaluation of sleep should be integrated into the care of affected individuals to optimize developmental outcomes, seizure control, and quality of life for both patients and caregivers. More efforts should be made to perform well‐conducted trials for sleep disorders and to consider sleep as an outcome measure in clinical trials of new therapies in this population.

Domains include the amino (NH2) and carboxyl (COOH) termini, transmembrane segments (S1–S6), linkers (S1–S2, S4–S5, S5–P, SF–S6), and the pore‐forming region (P, SF). Percentages indicate the proportion of patients within each domain subgroup.
