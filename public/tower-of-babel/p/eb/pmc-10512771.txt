Comparative analysis between the safety and efficacy of oral corticosteroids versus corticosteroids pulse therapies in IgA nephropathy
Authors: Yu Wang, Naya Huang, Yunuo Wang, Yu Jiang, Aikeda Yimamuyushan, Xi Xia, Li Fan, Jianwen Yu, Junbing He, Wei Chen, Qinghua Liu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study retrospectively compared the safety and efficacy of oral corticosteroid therapy (OCT) and corticosteroid pulse therapy (CPT) in the treatment of IgA nephropathy.MethodsOne ninety-two patients were diagnosed with IgA nephropathy and had an estimated glomerular filtration rate > 15mL/min/1.73m2 and 24-h urine protein level of 0.75-3.5g. Patients were divided into CPT and OCT groups according to the treatment protocol. The differences in the efficacy and safety between the two groups were assessed by logistic regression analysis and propensity score matching.ResultsSignificant differences at baseline, including 24-h urine protein level and eGFR, were observed between the two groups. Logistic regression analysis indicated that the remission rate increased significantly, while the incidences of total adverse events and infections decreased in CPT group compared with the OCT group after adjusting the potential confounding factors. Forty-seven pairs of subjects are matched by using propensity score matching with similar baseline data. The results indicate that the total remission rate and complete remission rate were significantly higher, while the incidences of total adverse events were lower (p = 0.008) in the CPT group than in the OCT group. The subgroup analysis showed that CPT group was more likely to achieve remission in patients with initial 24-h urine protein levels falling into the range of 2–3.5 g and Oxford Classification of S1 or C1/2 (p < 0.05).ConclusionAmong patients with IgA nephropathy and 24-h urine protein levels of 0.75–3.5g, CPT may be more effective than OCT in reducing urinary protein levels and improving renal function with a lower incidence of adverse events.Keywords: IgA nephropathy, corticosteroids, pulse therapy, efficacy

Introduction

IgA nephropathy (IgA nephropathy, IgAN) is a glomerular disease caused by abnormal deposition of immunoglobulin in the mesangial region of the glomerulus and is currently recognized as one of the most common and primary glomerular diseases in the world [1,2]. The most common diagnosis of IgA nephropathy is in adults between the ages of 20 and 40, with a variety of clinical presentations ranging from asymptomatic microscopic hematuria to rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis. Over the past decade, several GWAS in predominantly European and East Asian cohorts identified nearly 20 independent risk alleles for IgA nephropathy. The population distribution among these risk alleles matches known ethnic and geographic differences in susceptibility to IgAN, which has significant West–East prevalence differences, with the disease being most common in East Asia, less common in Europe, and likely rare in Africa [3,4]. In China, this disease accounts for approximately 30% to 40% of primary glomerular diseases [5].

Current data indicate that at least four factors are involved in the development of IgA nephropathy: the “multi-hit hypothesis”. Patients with IgAN have an increase in circulating IgA1 with galactose-deficient O-glycans in the hinge region (Gd-IgA1) (Hit1). The increased Gd-IgA1 leads to the synthesis of anti-glycan antibodies, both of which combine to form immune complexes that are deposited in the glomerular mesangium (Hits 2 and 3). These immune complexes stimulate mesangial cell proliferation, activate the complement pathway, and induce secretion of extracellular matrix, cytokines, and chemokines, which result in renal injury (Hit 4) [6].

Based on the pathogenesis of IgAN, many emerging treatment approaches and trials are ongoing, but there is currently no uniform treatment for IgA nephropathy. More than 100 clinical trials for IgAN were extracted on ClinicalTrials.gov. Most of these trials explored the treatment for primary IgAN confirmed by renal biopsy in adults, and immunosuppressive therapy was the most studied, followed by supportive care. Novel targeted therapeutic agents are also increasingly the focus of research, like targeting complement components, cytokines, and chemokines, or endothelin. However, the majority of trials had relatively small sample sizes and short observations [7].

Supportive treatment mode such as intensive blood pressure reduction (<130/80 mmHg), renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) blockade therapy is still a well-recognized treatment plan since the efficacy and safety of corticosteroids have always been and are still controversial. Two large, randomized control trials (RCTs) assessing the efficacy of corticosteroids have received attention in the past few years. One is the STOP-IgAN study [8,9] from Germany, which included IgAN patients with persistent urine protein > 0.75 g/d despite supportive care and demonstrated that although the immunosuppression group could reduce proteinuria, no significant rise in eGFR was noticed in the 3-year follow-up, and these patients experienced more life-threatening infections. Continued follow-up for 10 years showed the same results as before. The other is the TESTING study [10,11] from Peking University. The study indicated that corticosteroids could improve proteinuria and eGFR levels while also increasing the occurrence of serious adverse events, primarily infections, especially with the full-dose methylprednisolone protocol. In the 2021 KDIGO guidelines [12], maximum supportive treatment is recommended for the treatment of patients with IgA nephropathy, while 6 months of corticosteroid treatment can be an optional plan for patients who are at high risk of progressive CKD (proteinuria level > 1 g/d after 90 days of optimized treatment).

Corticosteroid treatments mainly include oral corticosteroid therapy and corticosteroid pulse therapy. However, there are few reports on the efficacy and safety of these two treatments. In this study, we sought to evaluate the efficacy and safety of corticosteroid pulse treatment on IgA nephropathy by using oral corticosteroids as a control group. These data may serve as a great reference for selecting an optimal treatment plan for IgAN patients.

Materials and methods

Patients

The patients diagnosed with IgA nephropathy between May 2013 and October 2020 at the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University were enrolled in this study by using the single-center retrospective study method. Inclusion criteria: 1) eGFR (simplified MDRD method)>15 mL/min/1.73m2; 2) 24h urine protein level >0.75 g and <3.5 g; 3) No corticosteroids or immunosuppressive therapy before renal biopsy; 4) Age ≥ 14 years; 5) Well-recorded clinical and pathological data with at least 3-months of follow-up data. Exclusion criteria: 1) Secondary IgA nephropathy, such as hepatitis B virus-related nephritis; 2) No complications with other diseases, such as diabetic nephropathy; 3) Pregnant and lactating women; 4) Along with some diagnoses which can cause rapid deterioration of renal function: such as acute interstitial nephritis and malignant hypertension; 5) Patients who have also used other immunosuppressive agents (such as mycophenolate mofetil) or only used some support treatment like RASBs after the renal biopsy. A flow chart of our study can be seen in Figure 1. All experiments on human subjects were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was approved by the Ethical Committee for Clinical Research and Animals of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (2016215), and informed consent was obtained from all subjects involved in the study.

Treatments

In the oral corticosteroids group (OS), an oral dose of 0.5–1 mg/kg prednisone was taken daily (maximum dose of 60 mg/d), and the dose was gradually reduced according to the patient’s treatment condition, tolerance, and individual differences. In the corticosteroids pulse group (SP), methylprednisolone (0.5 g/d) was given for 3 consecutive days plus oral prednisone (0.5 mg/kg) every other day at the beginning of the first, third, and fifth months. After 6 months, the dose of oral corticosteroids gradually decreased until the medication completely stopped.

Indicators

The demographic, clinical characteristics, and follow-up characteristics of all enrolled individuals were obtained from the electronic medical records systems of our institutions and telephone follow-up. The patient’s demographic data include age and gender. Clinical characteristics contain blood pressure, blood biochemical indicators like serum creatinine, 24h urine protein level, kidney Oxford pathological classification (MEST-C) [13], and RASB usage were collected. Follow-up characteristics collected some biochemical indicators like the 24-h urinary protein and serum creatinine level while adverse reactions during treatment were recorded. All adverse event data were obtained from the patient’s electronic medical records during the period of corticosteroid use, and the accuracy of the diagnosis was reconfirmed based on the patient’s clinical presentation and laboratory results. The end date of follow-up is set to 31 January 2021.

Definitions

The end point was defined as the first occurrence of a 40% decrease in eGFR, the development of ESRD (defined as eGFR ≤ 15 mL/min/1.73m2, undergoing maintenance dialysis or kidney transplantation), or death due to kidney disease [10].

The patient’s urine protein level and renal function at the time of the last follow-up visit were used to evaluate efficacy: Complete remission (CR): 24h urine protein level <0.4 g, stable renal function (eGFR declines <30% from the baseline); partial remission (PR): 24h urine protein level declines ≥50% and ≥0.4 g, stable renal function (eGFR declines < 30% from the baseline); invalid (no response, NR): not meet complete or partial remission criteria [14,15]. Total remission rate = (number of complete remission cases + number of partial remission cases)/total number of cases × 100%.

Statistical analysis

Normally distributed variables are presented as mean and standard deviation (SD) and compared using an independent or paired t-test, as appropriate Nonparametric continuous variables are presented as the median with the interquartile range presented as a median with interquartile range (25th and 75th percentile) and compared using an independent or paired Mann-Whitney U test as appropriate. Categorical variables are summarized using proportions and compared using the Pearson chi-squared test, continuity-corrected chi-square test, or Fisher exact test. Time-to-event data are described using Kaplan-Meier curves, and the between-group difference was compared using the log-rank test. At the same time, Logistic regression model was performed to assess the relationship between therapy and response.

Because this study was retrospective and the therapy choices were unstandardized, patients with and without therapy were likely to differ in risk factors of progression. To be able to compare outcomes in patients with corticosteroid pulse therapy with controls of similar risk, we used the propensity score matching (PSM). PSM is a statistical procedure that reduces bias by assembling a sample in which confounding factors are balanced between treatment groups, which offers methods to control for confounding by indication in observational studies [16,17]. The covariates in the model to estimate the propensity scores were as follows: Age, sex, creatinine, GFR, 24h urine protein level, mean arterial pressure, Pathological classification (MEST-C), the use of RASB drugs, and follow-up time. The caliper value setting is 0.2 and then PS values and PS weights were calculated. Nearest Neighbor Matching method is used with a 1:1 matching ratio.

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0, San Diego, California, USA) and SPSS (version 25.0, Chicago, USA). Differences were considered statistically significant when the two-sided p-value was less than 0.05.

Results

Baseline data comparison between the two groups

A total of 192 patients (95 males and 97 females) with a mean age of 33.26 years were enrolled in this study. The median follow-up time was 21 months. As presented in Table 1, no significant differences in BMI, hemoglobin, triglycerides, albumin, and Oxford pathological classification of M1, S1, and E1 were observed between the oral corticosteroid and corticosteroid pulse groups. Compared to the corticosteroid pulse group, the oral corticosteroid group had significantly higher age, mean arterial pressure, serum creatinine level, serum uric acid level, total cholesterol level, and 24 h urine protein level but lower initial eGFR and ratio of RASB drug use (all p < 0.05). The corticosteroid pulse group had a lower ratio of T1/2 (p = 0.001) but a higher ratio of C1/2 (p = 0.046) and a higher crescent proportion (p = 0.031), indicating more active lesions in the corticosteroid pulse group (Table 1).

The treatment efficacy and renal prognosis in the two groups

After treatment, the complete remission rate and total remission rate were significantly improved in the corticosteroid pulse group compared to the oral corticosteroid group (p < 0.05) (Table 2). When setting total remission as a dependent variable and adjusting for confounding factors, including age, sex, MAP, eGFR, 24-h urine protein level, Oxford pathological classification, and history of RASB drug usage, our results showed that the probability of achieving total remission after treatment was significantly increased in the corticosteroid pulse group compared to the oral corticosteroid group (OR = 2.509, 95% CI 1.171–5.377, p = 0.018) (Table 3).

The treatment efficacy and renal prognosis after propensity score matching in the two groups

To better compare the differences in treatment efficacy and renal prognosis between the two groups, 47 pairs of patients in total were matched by propensity score matching. After matching, the baseline data between the two groups showed no significant difference (p > 0.05) (Table 1), while the complete remission rate and total remission rate remained higher in the corticosteroid pulse group than in the oral corticosteroid group (p < 0.05) (Table 2).

We also observed that in terms of improving renal function, the levels of creatinine and eGFR at the initial stage of treatment were improved in the oral corticosteroid group but gradually deteriorated later. In the corticosteroid pulse group, creatinine and eGFR tended to decrease and increase, respectively, without significant differences. Moreover, both groups showed improved serum albumin levels and reduced proteinuria; however, corticosteroid pulse therapy seemed to be better at reducing proteinuria (Figure 3).

Comparative analysis of treatment safety between the two groups

No deaths were observed during the follow-up period. During treatment, the incidence of adverse events and infections in the oral corticosteroid group was higher than that in the corticosteroid pulse therapy group (84.1% vs. 51.4%, p < 0.001, and 48.9% vs. 32.9%, p = 0.043). After PSM, the incidence of overall adverse events in the oral corticosteroid group was also higher than that in the corticosteroid pulse group (86.7% vs. 57.8%, p = 0.008). There was no significant difference in the incidence of infection events between the two treatment groups (46.7% vs. 44.4%, p = 0.850), which might be attributed to the loss of follow-up and lack of adverse event data for some patients in the oral corticosteroid group (Table 5).

Discussion

IgA nephropathy is a chronic progressive disease that mainly manifests as hematuria and/or proteinuria and is accompanied by varying degrees of hypertension and impaired renal function. Approximately 20%–40% of patients progress to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) 10–20 years after diagnosis [19]. Therefore, timely diagnosis and early treatment are of great significance to the prognosis of IgA nephropathy.

Based on current trials [20], the efficacy of immunosuppression beyond corticosteroids such as cyclophosphamide, azathioprine, and cyclosporine is uncertain. As for MMF, the 2021 KDIGO guidelines [12] suggest that it may be considered as a steroid-sparing agent only in Chinese patients in whom corticosteroids are being considered. In addition, some potential new medicines have also gained attention in IgAN in the last few years, including Nefecon (a targeted-release formulation of oral budesonide [21,22]); complement activation pathway inhibitors (like OMS-721, LNP023, and ravulizumab); and the BAFF/APRIL dual inhibitor (Atacicept) [20,23–25].

However, corticosteroids are still commonly used in the treatment of IgA nephropathy, and it is not clear whether differences exist in IgAN prognosis with oral corticosteroids or corticosteroids pulse therapy. In this study, the corticosteroid pulse therapy we adopted was modified from the Pozzi protocol for IgA nephropathy [26] to reduce the immunosuppressive load. Specifically, in the Pozzi protocol, intravenous methylprednisolone 1 g per day for 3 consecutive days was given at the beginning of months 1, 3, and 5, plus oral prednisone at a dose of 0.5 mg/kg every other day in the remaining days. After 6 months, the drug was stopped directly. However, in our study, the dose of intravenous methylprednisolone was 0.5 g, and oral prednisone was gradually reduced after 6 months rather than stopped abruptly. The total amount of corticosteroids in the two protocols was similar.

Proteinuria was found to be an important risk factor for poor patient prognosis, which means that actively reducing proteinuria is of great significance to improve the outcome [27,28]. In this study, both before PSM and after PSM, the complete response rate and total response rate of patients in the corticosteroid pulse group were better than those in the oral corticosteroid group. In addition, corticosteroid pulse therapy can also significantly improve serum albumin levels and reduce proteinuria. With regard to reducing proteinuria, corticosteroid pulse therapy is superior to oral corticosteroid therapy, which was consistent with the Pozzi [26,29] and Tatematsu [30] cohort studies to some extent. The Pozzi study showed a significant reduction in proteinuria in 72 patients with no end-point event. The Tatematsu study found that cumulative PR and CR rates increased rapidly until 2 years (54.5% and 46.8% at 2 years) among IgAN patients treated with Pozzi’s regimen and then slowly but steadily increased up to 6 years (72.8 and 66.4% at 6 years).

In our study, we found that when comparing the renal function changes, creatinine was progressively decreased and eGFR was increased in the corticosteroid pulse group. In the oral corticosteroid group, creatinine and eGFR improved first but then subsequently deteriorated. Further subgroup analysis showed that the remission rate of patients in the corticosteroid pulse group was better than that in the oral corticosteroid group without statistical significance in spite of the initial stage of CKD, suggesting that the protective effect of corticosteroid pulse therapy on renal function may not be related to the basic renal function stage. However, due to the small sample size and short median follow-up time, the Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed no significant difference in the incidence of end events between the oral corticosteroid and corticosteroid pulse groups, which means that we cannot conclude that corticosteroid pulse therapy could improve the prognosis of the kidney disease. However, previous studies have reported that corticosteroid pulse therapy was more beneficial than oral corticosteroids [30–32]. Therefore, in the future, we may conduct multicenter cooperations to expand the sample quantity and extend the follow-up time to obtain more accurate conclusions.

To date, several lines of evidence have revealed various pathological variables involved in poor progression in IgAN, such as glomerulosclerosis, tubular atrophy lesions, mesangial hypercellularity, interstitial fibrosis, inflammatory cell infiltration degree, and vascular lesions [33–35]. Our further subgroup analysis showed corticosteroid pulse therapy to be more beneficial to patients with pathology classified as S1 or C1/2, which suggested that corticosteroid pulse therapy might be more appropriate for IgAN patients with segmental sclerosis and crescent formation to improve renal prognosis. Our data indicated a beneficial role of immunosuppressive therapy in IgAN patients with S1 lesions combined with podocyte hypertrophy and apical segmental sclerosis, which was to some degree in line with the Bellur SS cohort study [36].

It is known that corticosteroids cause a variety of adverse reactions, including infection, diabetes, hypertension, osteoporosis, and many other side effects [37]. Previous studies have reported various adverse events caused by corticosteroid therapy; for example, the TESTING[10] study had to stop its experiments because of severe infections. The corticosteroid used in this study as was methylprednisolone sodium succinate, which was administered intravenously to achieve a potent anti-inflammatory effect approximately 8 times that of cortisone. At the same time, the drug is recognized for its rapid effect, being less likely to cause water and sodium retention, and having fewer side effects. Therefore, only one patient treated with methylprednisolone was diagnosed with diabetes at the end of the experiment in the study performed by Pozzi et al. [26]. In our study, we also observed a significantly lower risk of overall adverse events, especially infections, in the corticosteroid pulse group than in the oral corticosteroid group.

The mechanism of corticosteroids may explain the discrepancies between the two groups. They exert their potent anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects primarily via genomic and nongenomic mechanisms. The classical genomic mechanism of corticosteroid action is mediated by the cytosolic glucocorticoid receptor (cGCR). This mechanism of corticosteroid action can be separated into two processes: transactivation and trans-repression. The Mannheim classification of rapid nongenomic mechanisms of corticosteroid action includes three major categories: nonspecific interactions of corticosteroids with cellular membranes; nongenomic effects mediated by the cGCR; and specific interactions with membrane-bound cGCR [38–40]. Oral corticosteroids exert their effects only through the genomic mechanism of glucocorticoids. Long-term use of glucocorticoids in the medium-to-high dose range can lead to serious side effects. In contrast, research pointed out that short-term intravenous administration of high corticosteroid doses can induce rapid clinical effects in some acutely exacerbated immune-mediated diseases, for which genomic and nongenomic mechanisms are both thought to be responsible. In addition, pulse therapy may be more effective in alleviating progression and has a relatively low rate of side effects [41,42].

Our study has several limitations. First, the baseline characteristics between the two groups in our study are inconsistent. Several reasons may explain this: our study was a retrospective single-center study; Doctors’ selection of pulse or oral corticosteroids may be empirical or based on preference. For instance, they may prioritize pulse therapy for patients with more active lesions, as shown in Table 1. In addition, the choice of the patients may also be inclined to oppose corticosteroid pulse, which may also contribute to the differences in the baseline. Nonetheless, we applied statistical techniques such as propensity score matching to reduce bias in order to assess the treatment differences between the two groups as much as possible. Second, although our study indicated the superiority of corticosteroid pulse over oral corticosteroid in reducing proteinuria, the long-term effects on renal function are not yet known due to the relatively short follow-up period, which is also a major limitation of our study due to the fact that IgAN is a slowly progressing disease. Third, when we collected information from patients, many of them were unable to remember minor or moderate adverse events that occurred during corticosteroid use, and relevant medical records were lost. In addition, the absence of some important follow-up indicators, such as creatinine, and the inability to obtain information about the cumulative dose and duration of corticosteroid use meant that some important data were missing in this study. Consequently, in order to provide more scientific evidence for the optimal corticosteroid therapy protocol for IgAN patients, additional large-scale, multi-center studies with extensive follow-up should be conducted.

Conclusion

In summary, for IgA nephropathy with a 24-h urine protein level of 0.75–3.5 g, compared with oral corticosteroid therapy, corticosteroid pulse therapy may be more effective in reducing proteinuria and improving renal function with a lower incidence of adverse events. However, a prospective cohort study with a large sample size is still required to further confirm our findings and explore the potential clinical applications of corticosteroid pulse therapy in IgA nephropathy.
