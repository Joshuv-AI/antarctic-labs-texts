Moral Courage and Ethical Decision‐Making Among Registered Psychiatric Nurses in Western Canada: A Cross‐Sectional Multiple‐Methods Study
Authors: Jordyn McBride, Kathryn Chachula
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMoral courage is a critical attribute for psychiatric and mental health nurses, who routinely navigate complex ethical dilemmas in mental health care settings. Despite its importance, limited research has explored moral courage and its relationship with ethical decision‐making confidence among Registered Psychiatric Nurses. The purpose of this study was to examine the relationship between moral courage, ethical decision‐making confidence, education level, and years of practice among Registered Psychiatric Nurses in western Canada. A cross‐sectional multiple‐methods design that adhered to the STROBE Checklist EQUATOR Network was used, involving 80 participants recruited within western Canada. Data was collected using the Nurses' Moral Courage Scale that utilised open‐ and close‐ended questions as well as the Ethical Decision‐Making Confidence scale. Statistical data was analysed using parametric and non‐parametric tests to assess group differences. Narrative data was analysed using thematic analysis. The findings revealed a significant positive relationship between moral courage and ethical decision‐making confidence. Ethical decision‐making confidence scores were higher among nurses with graduate‐level education compared to those with diplomas or baccalaureate degrees. However, no significant difference in moral courage scores was observed based on education level or years of practice, and no significant association was found between years of practice and moral courage. Qualitative findings revealed four major themes that included: (1) Advocating; (2) Patient Safety; (3) Reporting Co‐Workers; and (4) Standing up to Co‐Workers and Superiors. This study highlights the interconnectedness of moral courage and ethical decision‐making among psychiatric nurses, while revealing that years of practice alone do not predict moral courage.Keywords: ethical decision‐making, level of education, mental health nursing, mentors, moral development

Introduction

Registered Psychiatric Nurses (RPNs) represent a distinct nursing profession in western Canada who provide mental health, addictions, and neurodevelopmental care across the lifespan in a variety of settings (College of Registered Psychiatric Nurses of Manitoba [CRPNM] 2022). As a regulated profession with scopes of practice distinct from other nursing professions, RPNs in Canada contribute uniquely to mental health services, policy, and addressing complex psychosocial needs. Psychiatric and mental health nurses often face unique challenges in navigating ethical decision‐making and demonstrating moral courage (Ayaze and Akkuş 2023). The transition from novice to experienced nurse involves not only the acquisition of clinical skills but also the development of ethical competence and moral integrity (Barlem and Ramos 2014). However, the literature suggests that newly graduated psychiatric nurses may enter practice without fully demonstrating ethical competence, posing challenges to the quality of patient care as well as nurse retention (Pachkowski 2017).

Despite the ethical complexity inherent in psychiatric nursing, limited empirical research has examined how nurses develop the capacity to act ethically in challenging situations. Moral courage remains an underexplored construct within psychiatric nursing, despite its relevance to ethical decision‐making, advocacy, and professional accountability. The purpose of this study was to examine the relationships between moral courage, ethical decision‐making confidence, education level, and years of practice among RPNs practicing in western Canada and to explore the types of practice situations that nurses perceive as requiring moral courage.

Background

Psychiatric and mental health nursing is inherently ethically complex. Nurses working in these settings routinely encounter ethical dilemmas related to coercion, involuntary treatment, restricted patient autonomy, and competing obligations to patients, families, and institutions. Ethical challenges commonly reported in nursing practice include predicted difficulties related to protecting patients' rights, obtaining informed consent, managing staffing constraints, maintaining confidentiality, and responding to perceived unethical practices (Ulrich et al. 2010). Within mental health settings, these challenges are often intensified with the use of physical or chemical restraints, stigma associated with mental illness or substance use, and organizational pressures that may limit patient access to care (Salzmann‐Erikson 2018; Ventura et al. 2020). Engaging in ethical decision‐making within such environments requires more than technical competence; it demands moral competence grounded in ethical knowledge, critical thinking, and professional values (Hauhio et al. 2021; Pachkowski 2017).

One consequence of sustained exposure to ethical challenges is moral distress, a phenomenon first described by Jameton (1984) as the psychological discomfort experienced when one knows the right thing to do, but institutional constraints make it nearly impossible to pursue the right course of action. Jameton (1993) further differentiated moral distress from moral uncertainty and moral dilemmas, emphasising that moral distress arises when ethical clarity is present, but action is obstructed. In psychiatric and mental health nursing, moral courage is particularly salient due to the restrictions on patients' freedom, such as involuntary hospitalisation, seclusion, or mandated treatment (Ando and Kawano 2016b). Empirical research has consistently linked moral distress to negative outcomes including burnout, reduced job satisfaction, compromised well‐being, and increased intent to leave the profession (Ando and Kawano 2016a; Baele and Fontaine 2021).

The concept of moral courage has gained increasing attention within nursing scholarship. Moral courage is conceptually rooted in moral philosophy, where it is understood as the willingness to act according to ethical principles despite the risk of adverse personal or professional consequences (Murray 2010). Within nursing, moral courage has been increasingly recognised as a critical component of moral agency, particularly in contexts where nurses encounter ethical conflict, institutional constraints, or power asymmetries that inhibit ethical action (Musto et al. 2021). The concept gained prominence in nursing scholarship as researchers sought to better understand how nurses respond to moral distress as a catalyst for ethical action (Numminen et al. 2016, 2019). Moral courage is commonly defined as the nurse's ability to defend their professional ethical principles and to act according to them despite any anticipated or real adverse consequences (Numminen et al. 2019). Unlike moral sensitivity, which involves recognising ethical issues, or moral distress, which reflects the psychological discomfort that arises when ethical action is constrained, moral courage is fundamentally action oriented. It represents the behavioural enactment of ethical commitment in situations that include speaking up, reporting concerns, or challenging authority that may threaten professional relationships, credibility, or employment security. Moral courage is often conceptualised as a virtue that can be strengthened over time and is expressed through a commitment to protecting patients and upholding ethical standards (Hauhio et al. 2021).

In psychiatric and mental health nursing, moral courage is particularly relevant due to the ethical complexity of practice environments. Mental health nurses frequently navigate situations involving coercion, involuntary treatment, restricted patient autonomy, and hierarchical decision‐making structures that may limit their influence (Ventura et al. 2020). Demonstrating moral courage in these contexts often requires psychiatric nurses to advocate for vulnerable patients, question established practices, or challenge decisions made by colleagues or superiors. As such, moral courage is not only an individual virtue, but a relational and context‐dependent phenomenon shaped by organizational culture, power dynamics, and perceived support for ethical practice (Birkholz et al. 2022; Hauhio et al. 2021).

Despite growing conceptual clarity and the development of validated instruments to measure moral courage, empirical research examining moral courage within psychiatric nursing remains limited (Konings et al. 2021). Little is known about how moral courage relates to ethical decision‐making confidence, or whether it develops through professional experience or formal education alone. Addressing these gaps is essential for understanding how RPNs navigate ethical challenges within mental health settings and how ethical practice can be effectively supported across career stages and practice settings.

Methods

A literature review was conducted by completing a search of relevant articles from the Cumulated Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), Education Resources Information Centre (ERIC), and PsycINFO databases. Searches were performed using the following terms: ‘psych’, ‘nurs*’, ‘ethics’, ‘moral distress’, ‘mentor’, ‘model’, ‘ethical dilemma’, ‘moral courage’, ‘ethical courage’, ‘ethical decision making’, ‘psychiatric nursing’, ‘mental health nursing’, ‘novice nurse’, ‘ethical issues’, ‘training’, ‘ethical competence’, ‘clinical competence’, ‘mentorship’, ‘moral sensitivity’, and ‘newly qualified’. A total of 233 records were screened as reported in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systemic reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) diagram as per Page et al. (2021) (Figure 1). Search criteria (Table 1) included articles written in English, publication years between 2010 and 2023, while thesis dissertations and duplicate articles were excluded. Reports were also excluded if they were written in a language other than English (Reason 1) or were not relevant to the subject being researched (Reason 2). Gaps in the literature were identified to inform the study purpose and research design.

Research Design

The aim of this study was to gain a better understanding of the relationship between ethical decision‐making confidence and moral courage in a sample of RPNs practicing in western Canada. This cross‐sectional study adhered to the STROBE Checklist EQUATOR Network. Five research questions guided the study that included: (1) What is the reliability of the Ethical Decision‐Making Confidence Scale (EDMCS) and the Nurses' Moral Courage Scale (NMCS) in a sample of RPNs practicing in western Canada? (2) Are there differences in levels of ethical decision‐making; and (3) moral courage levels that are reported by newly graduated RPNs and RPNs with greater years of experience? (4) Are there differences in moral courage and ethical decision‐making levels between psychiatric nurses with a diploma, baccalaureate degree, or graduate‐level education? (5) What circumstances do RPNs report as requiring moral courage in their practice?

Data Collection

Following receipt of ethical approval from the Brandon University Research Ethics Committee (Ethics Certificate #23397), anonymous cross‐sectional data was collected between September 2024 and February 2025 using Survey Monkey from 80 RPNs practicing in the western Canadian provinces of Manitoba, Saskatchewan, Alberta, and British Columbia. An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power (Faul et al. 2007) to estimate the required sample size for independent samples t‐tests. Assuming a medium effect size (d = 0.5), a two‐tailed alpha level of 0.05, and power at 0.8, a total sample of 128 participants was estimated to be required. Although the full sample size did not reach this target, the obtained sample size (N = 80) was sufficient for exploratory analysis and allowed for meaningful comparison across experience groups of relatively comparable size (n = 15–23 per comparison group) to support more robust comparisons of means. Recruitment was completed through the College of Registered Psychiatric Nurses of Manitoba (CRPNM) and the College of Registered Psychiatric Nurses of Saskatchewan (CRPNS). The organizations sent an invitation to participate in the study by email through their private list serve of registrants. Additional recruitment methods included the Alberta Association of Nurses (AAN) monthly newsletter and sharing a recruitment poster on social media. Due to these recruitment methods, it was not possible to calculate a survey response rate.

Instruments and Research Variables

Nurses' Moral Courage Scale (NMCS)

The NMCS is comprised of 21 Likert scale items and three open‐ended questions which have been validated in four languages that measure nurses' self‐assessed moral courage (Ayaze and Akkuş 2023; Lee et al. 2022; Numminen et al. 2019, 2021). The three open‐ended questions invited participants to describe any professional development activities related to health care ethics, practice situations they perceived as requiring moral courage, and how they responded in those situations. The NMCS enabled the examination of associations between nurses' socio‐demographic factors and their moral courage across the four subscale dimensions that included: (1) Compassion and True Presence; (2) Moral Responsibility; (3) Moral Integrity; and (4) Commitment to Good Care. Higher scores indicate higher levels of moral courage across the dimensions. The Cronbach's alpha values of the four dimensions in the original NMCS (Numminen et al. 2019) were 0.81, 0.81, 0.82, and 0.73 respectively.

Ethical Decision‐Making Confidence (EDMC) Scale

Birkholz et al. (2022) developed the Ethical Decision‐Making Confidence (EDMC) scale which is comprised of 15 Likert scale items with two subscale factors. The first factor is focused on the development of foundational ethical knowledge, skills, and cognitive mastery. The second factor is based on applying knowledge by using correct ethical language and decision‐making frameworks in practice. Higher scores indicate higher levels of confidence for each dimension. The Cronbach alpha coefficients for both subscale dimensions range from 0.884–0.938 and 0.851–0.911 respectively (Birkholz et al. 2022).

Data Analysis

Data collected in Survey Monkey was exported to the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 28 for analysis. There were 122 respondents who accessed the survey; however, 42 were returned incomplete, resulting in 80 participants. Of those who did not complete the survey in its entirety, three respondents skipped the EDMC scale questionnaire, resulting in a sample size of 77 for this measure, and one respondent who did not complete the NMCS, resulting in a sample size of 79 for this measure. Cronbach's alpha co‐efficient was used to measure internal consistency of the scales, and a Pearson correlation matrix to assess the relationship between the subscale variables. Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) with alpha set to 0.05 was used when the Levene's Test for assumption of homogeneity was met, and the Kruskal–Wallis non‐parametric equivalent was used when the Levene's test revealed a significance value below 0.05 to compare the mean scores of the ethical decision‐making confidence and moral courage between the sub‐groups of interest. Braun and Clarke's (2006) thematic analysis was utilised to identify themes from the open‐ended survey questions (refer to Table 2 to view participants' demographic data).

Ethical Decision‐Making Levels

A one‐way ANOVA revealed there were no statistically significant differences in EDMC Scores for Factor 1 based on years of practice, F(3, 76) = 2.057, p = 0.113, n 2 = 0.08. While mean scores increased with years of practice, from 31.9 in the 0–5‐year group to 35.55 in the 21 or more years group, the difference did not reach statistical significance. Moral Courage Levels

Pearson correlation findings revealed that there was a weak, non‐significant association between moral courage and years of practice (r = 0.138, and p = 0.226). The non‐parametric Kruskal–Wallis test was used to assess differences in moral courage levels based on years of experience which did not show statistically significant differences, x 2(3, N = 79) = 3.272, p = 0.352. One‐way ANOVA testing for the NMCS subscales of Compassion and True Presence, F(3, 79) = 0.739, p = 0.989, n 2 = 0.002, Moral Responsibility, F(3, 79) = 1.029, p = 0.385, n 2 = 0.04, Moral Integrity, F(3, 79) = 1.268, p = 0.291, n 2 = 0.05, and Commitment to Good Care, F(3, 78) = 1.037, p = 0.381, n 2 = 0.04 were also evaluated, but showed no significant differences. This revealed that there was no significant difference in moral courage scores between new psychiatric nurses, those with 6–10 years, 11–20 years, and those with 21 or more years of experience for any NMCS data.

Moral Courage Qualitative Findings

Within the NMCS, RPNs were provided with an opportunity to respond to open‐ended questions where respondents could share a problem situation they encountered in their workplace that required moral courage and how they acted to solve the situation. Braun and Clarke's (2006) thematic analysis was completed independently by the researchers. Each identified and discussed initial codes to compare and corroborate common themes and findings, adding to the trustworthiness of the data analysis. No real names are used in the reporting of the qualitative findings. The following themes and subthemes were identified that included: (1) Advocating; (2) Patient Safety; (3) Reporting Co‐Workers; and (4) Standing Up to Co‐Workers and Superiors. Many of the themes were not mutually exclusive but were rather inter‐linked and connected to other themes.

Advocating

Predominant in the short answer responses was the theme of advocating. Standards of practice require that RPNs advocate for patients, challenge stigma and discrimination, coordinate care, and provide leadership (CRPNM 2019; McShane et al. 2024). This included advocating for clients, RPNs, members of the health care team, and for oneself. Many respondents found that often clients were not receiving adequate care or being mistreated. Olivia, with seven years of practice, stated that their ‘patient did not receive adequate care or proper diagnostic testing. Upon returning for my shift the following day I advocated for the patient to the charge nurse and EMO [Emergency Medical Officer]’. Respondents frequently identified scenarios where they were required to advocate for their patients. Andrew, with forty‐eight years of practice, illustrated that they ‘Successfully advocated to have resident gain full control of life (moved out of LTC [long‐term care]) despite MULTIPLE [sic] levels of resistance’. Brittney reflected on ‘Improper treatment of a patient by a psychiatrist. Having to advocate for different outcome due to the safety of the patient and family’. Respondents expressed that advocating for oneself often came at a great risk to themselves. As Taylor, with seven years of practice, reported, ‘I will not threaten my own safety, health, and well‐being because that impacts all patients’. Additionally, Helena demonstrated how RPNs were often advocating for co‐workers when they ‘called Union during Air [sic] conditioning malfunction to ensure that sick time from heat stroke was returned to sick bank’. These narratives highlighted examples of how RPNs are actively advocating within their roles, often navigating difficult interpersonal and organizational dynamics to uphold patient rights, professional integrity, and workplace safety.

Patient Safety

A second major theme pertained to patient safety that involved inappropriate use of restraints and patient mistreatment. Rosanne stated, ‘[I] Witnessed inappropriate use of restraints. Wrote up a report, provided direct education and addressed with unit leadership and security services’. Diane with eight years of practice reflected on ‘Witnessing mistreatment of a patient and having to report that person’. Respondents often reported how moral courage was required when observing the inappropriate use of restraints. Christine with twelve years of practice stated:

We had a patient that frequently required mobile restraints due to aggression. There was a lot of fear working with him, and staff would default to restraints and neglect building a therapeutic rapport. We worked with ethics and our security staff to come up with a care plan where everyone felt safe, the patient was getting holistic care, and there were clear progressive goals to reduce the use of restraints.

Additionally, Patricia with eighteen years of practice noted the stigma of substance use within mental health settings:

I have worked with many people in psychosis who also use drugs. It's very hard to get them admitted involuntary because all their symptoms get blamed on drug use and psychiatry don't want to ‘waste’ a bed on a drug user.

Collectively, these accounts underscored how moral courage is essential in safeguarding patient safety, particularly in ethically complex situations such as restraint use, patient mistreatment, and addressing stigmatising attitudes within mental health care settings.

Reporting Co‐Workers

Respondents were challenged with reporting co‐workers, including other non‐nursing members of the multidisciplinary team. Martin with four years of practice stated, ‘I worked with [a] co‐worker that was intoxicated at work and I had to make the difficult decision to inform the nurse in charge of the ward about the situation which led to a disciplinary action against the nurse’. Connie also discussed reporting a ‘doctor to my charge nurse and manager’. Carol with six years of practice stated:

Co nurses planned with other treatment team members to hold a patient down and shave their head to remove lice after failure [of] treatments. When I came on shift and realized this was the plan, I refused and got support from present co‐workers, management and medical doctors.

These responses illustrate the emotional and ethical challenges RPNs faced when reporting colleagues' unsafe or unethical behaviour. These accounts demonstrated their commitment to professional accountability and patient welfare despite potential interpersonal and professional consequences.

Standing Up to Co‐Workers and Superiors

The final theme was standing up to co‐workers and superiors. Respondents spoke about standing their ground and speaking out. Lorri with six years of practice stated:

The psychiatrist disagreed and management did not back us up so we truly had to advocate for all the geriatric patients' safety on our own multiple times until someone listened as we felt it was the right thing to do and our moral and ethical responsibility. Even if it meant going against a doctor and management's opinion.

It was often noted in the responses that RPNs were standing up and speaking out to management and superiors that included both medical doctors and psychiatrists. This was noted by Jamie, with six years of practice, who reported:

I stood my ground and insisted that keeping the patient in the ER for longer (since a psychiatry consult would entail many more hours of intrusive assessments) would be making us complicit in her boyfriend's abusive (and fraudulent) strategies to control her.

These themes showcased how RPNs demonstrated moral courage in their daily work.

Education Levels With Ethical Decision‐Making and Moral Courage

Exploration of the data showed the assumptions to assess Education Levels as per the EDMC and NMCS using ANOVA testing were met. A one‐way ANOVA was conducted to evaluate if there were significant differences in ethical decision‐making scores between psychiatric nurses with a diploma, baccalaureate degree, or graduate‐level education which revealed statistically significant results, F(2, 76) = 4.257, p = 0.018, n 2 = 0.10 between RPNs with a diploma or bachelor's degree in comparison to those with graduate‐level education (refer to Figure 3 to view the means plot). The same comparison revealed no significant differences in moral courage levels, F(2, 78) = 1.615, p = 0.206, n 2 = 0.04.

Discussion

Scale Reliability

Reliability analysis demonstrated excellent internal consistency for both the NMCS and the EDMC scale. The findings indicated that both instruments are reliable tools for measuring moral courage and ethical decision‐making confidence among RPNs in western Canada. The overall Cronbach's alpha for the NMCS in this study was 0.932, which aligns closely with previous research validating the scale in other cultural contexts. In the original Finnish sample, Numminen et al. (2021) reported a Cronbach's alpha of 0.93, while Belgian data reflected a slightly lower score at 0.91. Ayaze and Akkuş (2023) reported a Cronbach's alpha of 0.952 in Turkish nursing populations, confirming strong internal consistency of the NMCS across diverse jurisdictions.

The EDMC scale similarly demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a total Cronbach's alpha score of 0.941. Factor 1, which captures the development of foundational ethical knowledge, skills, and cognitive mastery, achieved a Cronbach's alpha of 0.922 in the study, while Factor 2 that captures the application of ethical language and decision‐making frameworks in clinical practice demonstrated a Cronbach's alpha of 0.891. These findings align closely with previous psychometric evaluations in the original study by Birkholz et al. (2022) with Cronbach's alpha scores that ranged from 0.851 to 0.938. The strong findings are particularly noteworthy given this study's unique focus on psychiatric nursing. The statistically significant positive relationships between the subscale variables of moral courage and ethical decision‐making reflect a reciprocal process whereby confidence in ethical reasoning reduces uncertainty and perceived risk, enabling nurses to act in accordance with their moral values when faced with ethically challenging situations. This relationship also suggests that initiatives aimed at strengthening nurses' ethical decision‐making confidence may also support the development and expression of moral courage to accept the personal or professional risks inherent in morally courageous action. Consistent with these findings, previous research has identified a positive association between moral courage and ethical decision‐making in nursing populations, with moral sensitivity mediating this relationship (Luo et al. 2023). Konings et al. (2021) noted the critical role of moral courage and emphasised the need for reliable, context‐sensitive instruments to evaluate ethical competence in nursing.

Ethical Decision‐Making

Findings from a one‐way ANOVA in the study revealed no statistically significant differences in EDMC Factor 1 scores across the four comparison groups related to years of practice. This suggests that while years of experience may play a role in shaping ethical decision‐making confidence, it does not exert a uniformly strong or consistent influence across all domains or ethical reasoning over time. These results are consistent with Mugerwa et al. (2022), who found that while clinical competence among newly qualified nurses was generally lower than that of their more experienced counterparts, certain aspects of competence, including ethical reasoning and confidence in decision‐making, did not differ significantly based solely on years of experience. Birkholz et al. (2022) emphasised that ethical decision‐making confidence is a complex construct influenced not only by years of experience, but also by contextual factors such as organizational culture, support systems, and leadership. These contextual supports may function as enabling conditions that allow ethical confidence to translate into moral courage by fostering psychological safety and legitimising ethical voice within clinical environments. The psychometric evaluation of the EDMC scale found variability in confidence scores not strictly tied to years of experience, suggesting that confidence in ethical decision‐making may develop through a combination of experience, years of practice, formal education, and institutional support.

The analysis of the EDMC Factor 2 scores revealed a statistically significant difference based on years of practice (p = 0.04), with mean scores rising from 17.5 in the 0–5 years of experience group to 21.55 in the 21+ years of experience group. Although the effect size was small (n 2 = 0.11), this finding indicates that for certain dimensions of ethical decision‐making confidence, experience may exert a more pronounced influence. Mugerwa et al. (2022) reported similar incremental improvements in clinical decision‐making and ethical reasoning confidence with increased experience. Birkholz et al. (2022) identified a positive relationship between leadership roles and ethical decision‐making confidence, which often correlated with years of professional experience. As RPNs advance in their careers, they are more likely to occupy leadership positions or take on complex ethical challenges, which may explain the observed increase in confidence levels with EDMC Factor 2.

Moral Courage Levels

Findings from the study revealed no statistically significant differences in moral courage scores across experience levels, in both the overall NMCS as well as each subscale. These findings suggest that moral courage may be a stable characteristic or value‐driven trait among RPNs. This outcome aligns with Numminen et al. (2019) in that moral courage is not solely a function of professional tenure or years of practice but may be equally present in novice and seasoned nurses alike. The weak, non‐significant Pearson correlation (r = 0.138, p = 0.226) observed between moral courage and years of practice further reinforces this perspective. This finding strengthens the position that moral courage may not be a linear, experience‐dependent trait. These findings suggest the need to support and foster moral courage development early in a nurse's career and ensuring supportive environments where ethical practice is encouraged, regardless of experience level.

Comparison of means through use of the non‐parametric Kruskal–Wallis test showed no significant difference in moral courage scores between RPNs with varying years of experience. Similarly, one‐way ANOVA results indicated no significant differences between the subscales (Compassion and True Presence, Moral Responsibility, Moral Integrity, Commitment to Good Care) based on years of practice. This suggests that specific dimensions of moral courage are not necessarily acquired or strengthened through accumulated years of experience alone. These findings echo the results of Hauhio et al. (2021), who also reported that moral courage levels did not systematically increase with years of experience. Their research suggested that while experience might expose nurses to ethically challenging situations, it does not inherently lead to higher levels of moral courage, implying that other personal, situational, or organizational factors may play a more pivotal role.

Narrative Themes

Advocating emerged as the most predominant theme, with RPNs describing frequent scenarios where they intervened to protect clients' rights, ensure appropriate care, and support both their peers and themselves. These findings are congruent with those of Hauhio et al. (2021), who identified advocacy as a fundamental expression of moral courage, particularly when nurses confront ethically challenging situations.

The theme of patient safety further illustrated the ethical tensions faced by RPNs, particularly concerning the use of restraints and patient mistreatment. Respondents' narratives highlighted situations requiring moral courage to challenge prevailing practices and advocate for patient‐centred, ethical care. This aligns with Mugerwa et al. (2022), who similarly found that nurses often encountered ethical dilemmas in care settings that required assertive decision‐making to protect vulnerable patients. The narratives also illustrated the stigmatisation of substance use within mental health care, an issue noted in prior research as requiring enhanced advocacy and ethical leadership from nursing professionals (Numminen et al. 2021).

The theme of reporting coworkers illuminated the difficult position RPNs occupy when confronting unsafe or unethical behaviour among colleagues. These narratives emphasised the emotional labour and potential consequences associated with upholding ethical standards in the workplace. Konings et al. (2021) reported that moral courage often manifests in the willingness to report wrongdoing despite personal risk, an observation supported by the current study findings.

Finally, standing up to co‐workers and superiors emphasised the hierarchical challenges within health care settings, where RPNs demonstrated moral courage by confronting physicians, psychiatrists, and management to advocate for patient safety and ethical care decisions. This theme reflects Birkholz et al.'s (2022) assertion that ethical decision‐making confidence is critical in navigating multifaceted interprofessional dynamics, nurse leaders in ethically complex situations, and asserting one's ethical obligations in the face of institutional resistance. In this way, ethical decision‐making confidence may serve as a foundation that supports morally courageous action in hierarchical and emotionally charged situations.

Education Levels With Ethical Decision‐Making and Moral Courage

RPNs who attained a master's degree had notably higher ethical decision‐making scores compared to those with a diploma or baccalaureate degree. Though the actual difference in the mean scores was modest, as indicated by a small effect size (n 2 = 0.10), the result suggested that higher levels of formal education may positively influence a nurse's ability to engage in ethical reasoning and decision‐making. Grady et al. (2008) noted that higher levels of formal education contribute to enhanced ethical competence by promoting exposure to ethics focused coursework, critical thinking exercises, and opportunities to engage with complex clinical and ethical dilemmas. Whitehead et al. (2014) found that nurses with advanced degrees reported greater involvement in ethical decision‐making and higher confidence addressing ethical conflicts within multidisciplinary teams. This pattern was attributed not only to educational content but also to the opportunities afforded by advanced practice roles that require frequent ethical deliberation.

In contrast, moral courage scores did not significantly differ based on education level. Despite higher mean NMCS scores among master's‐prepared RPNs, the differences did not reach statistical significance. These findings suggest that ethical decision‐making skills may be enhanced through education and experience while moral courage may be less influenced by formal education level alone. Similarly, Hauhio et al. (2021) found that while ethical education was associated with moral courage, this relationship was modest and often mediated by factors such as moral sensitivity, ethical climate, and the perceived supportiveness of the organization. Limitations and Future Research

The modest sample size limits generalizability. Use of convenience sampling and self‐reported data introduces response bias. Situational and organizational culture factors known to influence ethical behaviour in nursing practice were not directly measured and could be an area of future research. Additional future research should consider longitudinal designs, larger sample sizes, exploration of how emotional labour shapes nurses' moral courage, as well as the exploration of the dynamics between leadership and mental health nurses providing direct client care to deepen the understanding of these attributes.

Conclusion

This study examined moral courage and ethical decision‐making confidence among a sample of RPNs in western Canada. The findings demonstrated that both the NMCS and the EDMC scale possess excellent reliability when applied within an RPN population. This research contributes to a growing body of evidence supporting the importance of fostering ethical environments and leadership within mental health care settings immersed with ethical complexity. The study findings emphasise the need for ongoing education, organizational support, and policies that encourage moral courage and ethical decision‐making confidence among RPNs.

Relevance for Clinical Practice

Ethical decision‐making confidence among RPNs is positively influenced by experience and education, while moral courage is a more stable trait that is less impacted by time in practice or formal education alone (Numminen et al. 2016; Birkholz et al. 2022). In clinical practice, fostering ethical decision‐making and moral courage requires more individual effort and necessitates a supportive organizational culture. Nurse leaders play a critical role in creating environments that encourage ethical reflection, psychological safety, and non‐punitive responses to ethical concerns (Ulrich et al. 2010; Whitehead et al. 2014). Leaders should be trained to recognise ethical distress and proactively support staff navigating ethically complex situations (Deschenes et al. 2021). At the same time, leadership roles and professional experience do not sequester nurses from ethical challenges, as moral courage may be required both within leadership positions and in actions that question or confront authority. Mentorship programs and role models who offer guided reflection and support to RPNs can serve as a powerful strategy to develop both ethical decision‐making and moral courage for leaders and direct practice nurses (Block et al. 2005; Gularte‐Rinaldo et al. 2022).
