Pharmacokinetic‐Pharmacodynamic Model of Paclitaxel‐Induced Peripheral Neuropathy
Authors: Yuchen Sun, Manjunath P Pai, N Lynn Henry, Daniel L Hertz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChemotherapy‐induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN) is a common neurological toxicity of paclitaxel treatment in patients with cancer that can cause irreversible reductions in function and quality of life. CIPN risk is higher in patients with greater systemic paclitaxel exposure, including the maximum concentration at the end of infusion (Cmax). The objective of this study is to build a paclitaxel pharmacokinetic‐pharmacodynamic (PK‐PD) model of CIPN that could inform personalized dosing strategies. Data was obtained from the UMCC2014.002 clinical study of patients with breast cancer receiving weekly 1‐h infusion of 80 mg/m2 paclitaxel for 12 weeks. The PK model, built using paclitaxel Cmax and 16–24 h samples was combined with weekly a CIPN20 questionnaire to build the model. Model performance was explored by simulating CIPN resulting from a standard (80 mg/m2 × 12 weekly doses) and enhanced (100 mg/m2 × 6 + 80 mg/m2 × 6) dosing regimen and compared with the actual CIPN from patients receiving these regimens within the CALGB C9840 clinical trial. A two‐compartment PK model and a turnover PD model incorporating an effect compartment and threshold structure best described the data. The PK‐PD model adequately captures CIPN from patients in UMCC2014.002. Simulations recapitulate CIPN from standard (18% simulated vs. 21% actual) and enhanced (35% simulated vs. 30% actual) paclitaxel regimens from CALGB C9840. A novel PK‐PD model adequately describes CIPN from weekly paclitaxel. This CIPN PK‐PD model may be able to inform personalized dosing strategies that reduce CIPN and improve treatment outcomes in paclitaxel‐treated patients.Keywords: paclitaxel, peripheral neuropathy, personalized dosing, pharmacodynamic modeling, pharmacokinetics

Introduction

Paclitaxel is a naturally derived chemotherapy agent widely used to treat a variety of cancers, including breast cancer. Paclitaxel dose is determined by the patient's body surface area (BSA), and is usually administered weekly or every 2 or 3 weeks at the maximum tolerable dose with acceptable side effects [1, 2]. Neurological toxicity, referred to as chemotherapy‐induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN), is a debilitating adverse effect that occurs in up to 75% of patients [3]. Approximately 25% of patients [2, 4, 5] experience intolerable CIPN that requires a dose delay or reduction, as recommended by ASCO guidelines [6].

With no effective treatment for CIPN available, significant efforts have been made to develop personalized dosing strategies to avoid severe CIPN that can persist for years after treatment [7]. Systemic paclitaxel concentration is one of the most important predictors of CIPN, specifically the maximum concentration at the end of infusion (Cmax) [8] or the duration the systemic concentration remains above 0.05 μM (i.e., time above threshold, Tc>0.05) [9]. Based on these findings, therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM)‐based personalized paclitaxel dosing strategies have been demonstrated to reduce CIPN without reducing efficacy in prospective clinical trials [10, 11]. However, TDM‐based dosing is seldom used in clinical practice, in part due to the inconvenience of collecting a next‐day blood sample.

Understanding the relationship between paclitaxel exposure and CIPN development is critical to inform clinically feasible personalized dosing strategies. Pharmacokinetic‐pharmacodynamic (PK‐PD) modeling can be used to simulate the likelihood of patients developing CIPN and their anticipated response to personalized dosing strategies. PK‐PD models have been developed to understand the myelosuppression that results from several chemotherapy drugs including paclitaxel [12] and docetaxel [13, 14, 15]. Paclitaxel PK is well‐described by existing models [16]; however, there is a paucity of PD models linking paclitaxel PK to its resulting CIPN. Previously, Mehrotra et al. proposed a kinetic‐PD model that used paclitaxel dosing and CIPN data from the CALGB40101 clinical trial [17, 18] to propose a personalized dose delay or reduction strategy based on CIPN assessment through the third treatment cycle [19]. There have been no known efforts to test or translate this proposed strategy into clinical practice, perhaps due to reluctance to reduce doses and potentially jeopardize treatment effectiveness [20, 21, 22]. We recently proposed an alternative personalized dosing approach that may reduce CIPN while maintaining full dosing and efficacy by slowing the paclitaxel infusion rate [23]. Further investigation of this potential personalized dosing strategy requires a PK‐PD model that can assess the impact of varying infusion durations on CIPN development.

The objective of the study is to build a CIPN PK‐PD model using data from a cohort of patients with breast cancer receiving weekly paclitaxel treatment in whom paclitaxel PK and patient‐reported CIPN data were collected. Future work will explore whether this model can serve as a predictive tool to identify patients at high risk of CIPN and simulate the expected consequences of personalized dosing strategies to reduce CIPN and improve treatment outcomes.

Methods

Patients, Taxane Dosing and Pharmacokinetics, and Neuropathy Data Collection

The study population and data collection were described in detail in a previous publication [24]. Briefly, the observational study (NCT02338115) enrolled adult female patients with stage I–III or oligometastatic breast cancer who were scheduled to receive weekly 1‐h infusions of 80 mg/m2 paclitaxel for 12 weeks. Participants were recruited from the University of Michigan Rogel Cancer Center. Prior to enrollment, a written informed consent form was obtained from each participant. This study was approved by the University of Michigan IRBMED (HUM00086259) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

The administration of paclitaxel adhered to the standard protocol of the Rogel Cancer Center. Each patient's dose was determined based on their BSA, calculated from their actual weight and height at the time the treatment plan was established. The patients also received standard pre‐treatment medications and a 90‐min infusion for the first dose to monitor infusion reactions. For each patient, two blood samples were collected during the first infusion to quantify paclitaxel concentration in plasma. The first sample was collected during the final 10 min before the end of infusion, and the second sample was collected 16–26 h after the start of infusion. All samples were immediately placed on ice and plasma was isolated within 10 min of sample collection. Plasma samples were transferred to the University of Michigan College of Pharmacy Pharmacokinetics Core where paclitaxel concentration was quantified using a liquid chromatography/mass spectroscopy assay. Detailed paclitaxel dosing information for each patient including dose, infusion duration, and dates was retrospectively abstracted from MiChart, the electronic medical record used by the University of Michigan Rogel Cancer Center.

Neuropathy data were collected using the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) Quality of Life Questionnaire Chemotherapy‐Induced Peripheral Neuropathy (CIPN20) [25]. Patient responses were recorded on paper copies of the questionnaire the morning of their first infusion and weekly thereafter, whether or not the patient received an infusion that week. CIPN20 includes 20 questions that evaluate symptoms of sensory, motor, and autonomic neuropathy. Each symptom is rated on a scale from 1 (“not at all”) to 4 (“very much”). Since paclitaxel primarily causes sensory peripheral neuropathy but not ototoxicity, the 8‐item sensory subscale (CIPN8) score without the ototoxicity item was used in the analysis, similar to previous analyses of this cohort [23, 24]. CIPN8 was calculated by summing the scores for the eight questions related to sensory symptoms, resulting in a raw total score range of 8–32. The raw score was then adjusted by subtracting 8, as this indicates no symptoms across all questions, yielding a potential CIPN8 score range from 0 to 24 with higher scores indicating greater CIPN.

Pharmacokinetic Modeling

A population pharmacokinetic (PK) model was developed using Monolix Version 2023R1 (Antony, France: Lixoft SAS, 2023), following procedures similar to those described in a prior publication [23]. First, the dosing information for the first paclitaxel infusion (i.e., actual dose, infusion duration) and the paclitaxel plasma concentration data were prepared and formatted for analysis in Monolix. Both one‐ and two‐compartment model structures were tested to select the best base model. The initial estimates of the model were based on our previous publication [23]. Various error models including constant, proportional, and two combined (constant and proportional error following the same distribution and following two different distributions) were also tested to explain residual unexplained variability. Model performance was evaluated using the Akaike information criterion (AIC) as well as visual inspection of the diagnostic plots.

Pharmacokinetic Profile Simulation

The paclitaxel concentrations from the two samples collected during the first infusion and their actual dosing information for the entire treatment period (i.e., 12 weekly doses) were used to simulate the full PK profile for each individual for every dose received using Simulx Version 2023 (Antony, France: Lixoft SAS, 2023). Briefly, longitudinal dosing data for each individual were used as input to the PK model, and individual parameters determined in the aforementioned PK modeling process were incorporated to simulate their PK profiles throughout treatment.

Pharmacodynamic Modeling

The simulated PK profiles for each individual were used as input, while the observed CIPN8 scores from each week were used as the pharmacodynamic (PD) model output. Various PD model structures were explored using the non‐linear mixed effect modeling method in Monolix Version 2023 (Antony, France: Lixoft SAS, 2023). PD model development initially used a turnover structure, as proposed by Mehrotra et al. [17]. This model was supplemented with additional features of interest, including a requirement that the model reflected the association of higher maximum paclitaxel plasma concentration (Cmax) with more severe CIPN, consistent with previous findings from the same dataset [24], which enables this model to be used in future work to simulate CIPN from personalized dosing strategies that adjust infusion rate to alter Cmax [23]. This was achieved by introducing the structure of threshold value x between the central compartment of the paclitaxel PK model and the effect compartment.

Effect compartment and turnover models were explored to capture the delayed onset and gradual development of CIPN throughout treatment [26]. The final PD model selection was informed by AIC and visual inspection of diagnostic plots [17, 26]. Patients with a CIPN8 score > 8 (0–24 scale converted from raw CIPN8 score, equals > 33.3 in 0–100 scale) were categorized as experiencing clinically relevant CIPN, consistent with previous literature [19]. Using this threshold, the proportion of patients that were falsely predicted to have (type I error) or not have (type II error) CIPN at each weekly time point of CIPN20 assessment was calculated to evaluate the model's prediction accuracy.

External investigation of the final PK‐PD model was attempted using publicly available data from the CALGB C9840 clinical trial [2]. This phase III trial randomized patients with metastatic breast cancer to weekly or 3‐weekly paclitaxel dosing. The weekly dosing schedule administered paclitaxel 100 mg/m2 over 1 h for the first six doses and then 80 mg/m2 over 1 h for the remaining six weekly doses (enhanced dosing) to the first 116 participants. The protocol was then amended due to excess CIPN risk and the subsequent 232 participants received 80 mg/m2 throughout all 12 weekly doses (standard dosing). Although PK data were not available, the reported CIPN incidence for different dosing regimens was available to perform external model validation. The incidence of severe CIPN, defined as CIPN8 > 12 at any time during treatment, in our 60‐patient cohort receiving standard (80 mg/m2 × 12) and enhanced (100 mg/m2 × 6 + 80 mg/m2 × 6) dosing was simulated using our PK‐PD model. A CIPN8 threshold value of 12 (50 in a 0–100 scale) was used as a threshold for severe (i.e., grade 3+) CIPN, allowing for direct comparison with the actual rates of grade 3+ CIPN in patients receiving the standard and enhanced paclitaxel regimens reported in the C9840 trial, which did not report grade 2+ CIPN.

The model was also explored for its potential in predicting CIPN in patients receiving an every‐three‐week (Q3W) 175 mg/m2 paclitaxel regimen using the data from the every‐three‐week paclitaxel arm of the CALGB C9840 trial [2]. The dosing used for simulation was standardized, with all patients receiving a uniform 3‐h infusion every 3 weeks as administered in the CALGB C9840 trial [2]. Again, a CIPN8 threshold value of 8 and 12 was used as proxies for grade 2+ sensory CIPN and grade 3+ sensory CIPN, respectively, to compare the predicted vs. observed rates of grade 2+ and grade 3+ sensory CIPN.

Results

Patient Enrollment and Data Collection

Of the 65 patients enrolled in the clinical study, five patients were excluded due to patient withdrawal or protocol violations, yielding an analysis population of 60 patients with PK and CIPN data (Table 1). Their mean age was 52 years old and 93% self‐identified as white. The mean baseline CIPN8 (range 0–24) was 0.3 (1.25 on a 0–100 scale) and 83% had a baseline CIPN8 of 0. The mean cumulative doses of paclitaxel received were 11 (out of 12 planned) and 68% of patients completed all 12 doses. Fifty‐seven patients had available paclitaxel plasma concentration data from the end of infusion, and 59 patients had available concentration data from 18 to 26 h after the infusion.

The final PD model is a hybrid model including an effect compartment and turnover model, incorporating a threshold value in the effect compartment (Figure 2) with a combined error model. The classical Emax model in the turnover model structure was explored in the model development, but was not adopted due to poor model performance. The hybrid model structure of an effect compartment + turnover compartment was selected since it had better prediction performance than either model structure alone. The effect compartment is a separate kinetic compartment with different drug concentrations incorporated that serves as a structural component of the model to improve the alignment between central (plasma) concentrations and CIPN dynamics, enabling the model to capture the temporal characteristics of the drug‐effect relationship more accurately. However, using the effect compartment alone is not optimal since the CIPN will always increase, which is not consistent with the actual CIPN development (remains stable or decreases if there is a dose delay/reduction). As expected, simulations using this model indicate that patients will experience more severe CIPN from the enhanced vs. standard paclitaxel dosing regimens (Figure 5). The simulated CIPN score was capped by setting an upper bound of 24 such that any predicted value exceeding 24 was assigned a value of 24. This constraint does not impact the utility of the model, as in our case, CIPN scores above 8 are indicative of clinically significant neuropathy; excessively high predicted scores are still interpreted as CIPN‐positive after capping. The simulated grade 3+ CIPN incidence (defined as CIPN8 > 12) for this cohort of patients was similar to the actual grade 3+ CIPN incidence reported for patients from C9840 when comparing both the standard (18% simulated vs. 21% actual) and enhanced (35% simulated vs. 30% actual) paclitaxel dosing regimens [2].

The simulation of CIPN incidence using this model for Q3W paclitaxel 175 mg/m2 doses significantly overestimated the incidence of grade 2+ (predicted 62% vs. actual 21%) and grade 3+ (predicted 45% vs. actual 12%) sensory CIPN observed in C9840 [2].

Discussion

CIPN is a common paclitaxel toxicity that negatively affects patients' quality of life and limits treatment administration, potentially compromising treatment efficacy [21]. Significant efforts have been made to develop personalized paclitaxel dosing strategies to mitigate CIPN [11, 19, 23, 27], including those supported by paclitaxel PK models and CIPN PD models. Our PK‐PD model recapitulates the observed CIPN in a cohort of patients receiving standard weekly paclitaxel treatment and recapitulates the greater CIPN severity caused by a more intense dosing regimen, but significantly overestimates CIPN resulting from larger doses administered less frequently.

In the model development stage, multiple modeling strategies were tested, including modeling the CIPN using a logistic function and modeling CIPN as a categorical variable. However, those approaches have not been pursued since converting the CIPN8 scores to binary or categorical outcomes would lose information given the modest size of the dataset used to build the model. The final CIPN model is an extension of our previously published two‐compartment paclitaxel PK model and has similar population PK parameter estimates [23]. The PD component of the final model integrates an effect compartment and turnover structure, both of which are commonly used to model delayed PD responses [26]. The turnover model can accommodate many of the clinical characteristics seen with CIPN, including the delayed onset, cumulative increase with continued dosing, and resolution over time in the absence of treatment administration. The turnover model was also used in a previously published CIPN kinetics‐PD model from Sharma et al. [17]. However, using the turnover structure alone did not yield a stable model during model development, so an additional effect compartment was incorporated. The improvement of the model over the previous CIPN kinetic‐PD model was likely due to our inclusion of measured PK data, as a model built with only dosing data did not yield reliable CIPN predictions (data not shown).

The threshold paclitaxel concentration identified by our model that triggers the development of CIPN is 1735.75 ng/mL. Although the threshold we proposed here is not directly observable given the currently available data, this threshold is consistent with the previously reported probability of treatment disruption derived from the same dataset [24]. Compared to the previously reported paclitaxel threshold of 0.05 μM (equivalent to 42.7 ng/mL) used in time‐above‐threshold (Tc>0.05) analyses, the threshold concentration we identified is higher. However, a direct comparison is not appropriate as Tc>0.05 μM is not interpreted as a concentration above which CIPN necessarily occurs. Rather, Tc>0.05 is an overall systemic exposure metric that has been associated with an increased risk of peripheral neuropathy [28]. Incorporating this threshold in the effect compartment also ensures the model behaves as expected, with longer infusion durations leading to lower CIPN risks (data not shown).

External investigation using data from large clinical trial cohorts revealed that the final PKPD model could generally recapitulate the observed rates of severe CIPN from different doses administered weekly on the C9840 clinical trial [2], but did not perform well when applied to larger Q3W doses on the E1199 trial [29]. The model dramatically overestimated CIPN incidence from Q3W paclitaxel treatment, likely due to the much larger AUC of paclitaxel from larger Q3W doses that well surpassed the threshold concentration in all patients. CIPN symptom resolution between doses has been reported in the literature [30]; however, due to a lack of CIPN assessments between dosing intervals, our model can only capture symptom resolution for a subset of patients. Additionally, paclitaxel clearance decreases with higher doses and increases with longer infusion durations, resulting in lower clearance for the Q3W regimen compared to the weekly regimen [16], so there may be issues with applying both the PK and PD components of the weekly dosing model to Q3W dosing. Given these discrepancies, this model should not be directly applied to other paclitaxel regimens without tuning parameters, and perhaps altering the model structure, using data from patients treated with other regimens.

Several strategies to personalize paclitaxel dosing and optimize treatment outcomes have been investigated. Paclitaxel exposure varies significantly across patients [8] and two randomized trials have demonstrated that TDM‐based dose adjustment is beneficial in reducing CIPN, without evidence of reduced efficacy [10, 11]. However, paclitaxel TDM is rarely used in clinical practice due to a general lack of acceptance of TDM in oncology [31] and possible concerns from patients and physicians that reducing the paclitaxel dose would compromise efficacy [20]. Genetics‐guided paclitaxel dosing [32], another potential personalized treatment strategy, is not viable due to the lack of robust genetic predictors of paclitaxel PK [33]. Our PK‐PD model may be able to predict the clinical consequences of personalized paclitaxel dosing based on PK and early CIPN assessments. This may provide advantages over the current strategy of reducing or delaying doses after the patient has already developed intolerable CIPN symptoms [6]. Our model could be used to make personalized dosing decisions based on early CIPN assessments (e.g., week 1–3) to predict the patient's likelihood of future severe CIPN, and inform personalized dosing strategies including dose delays, decreases, or perhaps slowing the infusion rate [23]. We plan to use the model to simulate the resulting CIPN across various personalized treatment approaches, comparing them to the standard of care. Additionally, we will tune model parameters to recapitulate the actual CIPN across different paclitaxel regimens, including the currently investigated weekly dosing, every 2‐week or every 3‐week dosing, and the related compound docetaxel, using data from a large observational study SWOG S1714 [34].

There are limitations to our study that should be considered. There is a potential risk of overfitting when applying a complex model to a modestly sized cohort of 60 patients with only two paclitaxel measurements per patient. Specifically, the sparse PK sampling strategy may compromise the precision of individual PK parameter estimates and lead to shrinkage—both in individual predictions toward observed concentrations and in individual parameter estimates toward the population mean. Nevertheless, with the assumption that paclitaxel Cmax is the key driver of CIPN, the possible shrinkage of model prediction to observed Cmax is acceptable within the constraints of the available data. For the PD model, the shrinkage is calculated to be slightly negative (−0.08), indicating potential overfitting. The validity of our model was also assessed by comparing the estimated PK parameters to those reported in the literature using similar model structures. Direct comparison of our PK model with those developed using other datasets remains challenging, as the literature‐reported models employ the same compartment structure and model total paclitaxel concentrations is lacking. To our knowledge, only one published study reports a two‐compartment model based on total paclitaxel concentrations; however, there are differences in estimated parameters which are likely attributable to the lower paclitaxel dose and smaller patient population used in that study [35]. Nevertheless, the four parameters describing the two‐compartment PK model (Cl, Q, V1, and V2) were consistent with those derived from the two‐compartment PK model using a subset of the same data [23].

Another limitation of this study is the challenge of external model validation, given limited datasets with paclitaxel PK and differences in CIPN assessment between studies. However, our efforts to compare our model results with previous analyses using alternative approaches in this dataset [24], and comparing to CIPN reported from large clinical trials using different paclitaxel dosing regimens [2], provide some suggestion of the potential validity of our model for weekly paclitaxel dosing. Simulations suggest that the model may overestimate the CIPN for higher doses such as 175 mg/m2 Q3W, and should be restricted to the weekly doses used in model development. This may be due to differences in paclitaxel pharmacokinetic characteristics under different doses and infusion durations [16]. Without paclitaxel 175 mg/m2 Q3W PK data, we are unable to accurately predict exposure and develop a PKPD model for Q3W dosing. There are also limitations to the generalizability of our findings as our study was conducted only in females, who were 93% white, with breast cancer receiving a weekly paclitaxel regimen. Additional parameter tuning is needed to apply this model to other regimens and perhaps to other sexes, races, and/or tumor types. Finally, it is worth noting that our analysis focuses exclusively on sensory CIPN. Additional studies are needed to evaluate other domains of neuropathy, such as motor neuropathy, which in our cohort of paclitaxel‐treated patients appears to occur almost exclusively in patients with severe sensory neuropathy [36].

In conclusion, this PK‐PD model explains the CIPN resulting from standard weekly paclitaxel treatment. Ongoing work is simulating the expected consequences of various personalized dosing strategies, including dose reductions and delays, and an alternative approach of extending infusion duration, as well as using additional data to refine the current model for other dosing regimens. These simulations may support prospective clinical trials testing whether specific personalized dosing strategies effectively mitigate toxicities, retain efficacy, and can be used in clinical practice to reduce CIPN and improve outcomes in patients with cancer.
