The prevalence of clinically significant proximal femoral growth disturbance following open versus closed reduction in dysplastic hips performed after 6 months: A systematic review and meta-analysis of 150 studies
Authors: Tim Cheok, Aditya Boppana, Jake Christiansen, Alexander Beveridge, William Jordan, Rhys Martin, Zachary Van Hout, Jaideep Rawat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Late diagnosis of developmental hip dysplasia (DDH) may warrant either open or closed reduction of the hip. Proximal femoral growth disturbance (PFGD) is a possible complication following these procedures.Methods:A systematic search of PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, The Cochrane Library and OrthoSearch from the date of inception of each database through to the 2 February 2025 was performed. The primary outcome of interest was the prevalence of clinically significant PFGD following open or closed reduction after 6 months of age. This was defined as Kalamchi–MacEwen or Bucholz–Ogden Type 2 or above.Results:The prevalence of clinically significant PFGD in patients undergoing open reduction was 12.47% (95% Confidence interval (CI): 10.39–14.69), whereas the prevalence of clinically significant PFGD in patients undergoing closed reduction was 9.53% (95% CI: 6.91–12.46). Multivariate analysis suggested that mean age at time of reduction was associated with an increase in the prevalence of PFGD in patients undergoing closed reduction (p = 0.022).Conclusion:We described the prevalence of clinically significant PFGD following closed reduction and open reduction. In both open and closed reduction groups, we observed a high relative heterogeneity (I2), but acceptable absolute heterogeneity (τ2), and pooled prevalence estimates should be interpreted with caution. Further studies exploring the influence of disease severity and age on treatment method is required to allow reliable comparisons between open and closed reduction.PROSPERO Registration No.:CRD420251015622

Introduction

Developmental hip dysplasia (DDH) presents as a spectrum of disease, ranging from dysplasia to dislocation, affecting approximately 1% of live births. 1 Where this is identified early, treatment in the form of abduction bracing is efficacious in more than 90% of cases. 2 However, if left untreated, this may lead to ongoing dysplasia, dislocation, and early osteoarthritis of the hip. Screening for DDH has been widely adopted by most health jurisdictions, with a recent meta-analysis suggesting that universal screening reduces the risk of late diagnosis.3,4 Nevertheless, in patients where there was a delay in presentation, initiation of abduction bracing after 6 months is generally ineffective. Thus, surgical management in the form of a closed or open reduction may be recommended in this patient population, as well as in those where abduction bracing was unsuccessful.5–9

The term proximal femoral growth disturbance (PFGD) has recently been coined by Weinstein et al. 10 to better define radiographic changes around the femoral head in the context of DDH, encompassing findings of avascular necrosis. PFGD may be a devastating complication following treatment for DDH and have been hypothesised to be due to injury of the medial circumflex femoral artery. 11 PFGD also exists as a spectrum of disease, ranging from minor changes to the ossific nucleus, to complete effacement of the physis. While milder forms of PFGD are likely to spontaneously resolve, more severe forms can result in significant changes to the shape of the femoral head. 12 Various strategies have been described to reduce the risk of PFGD. These include avoiding excessive abduction when performing closed reduction and spica casting, the use of Lange’s second position cast following closed reduction, as well as the use of adductor tenotomy or femoral shortening osteotomy in cases where there is excessive tightness around the hip at the time of open reduction.13–19

The prevalence of clinically significant PFGD has been poorly described in literature. A previous study by Wang et al. 20 found a trend towards increasing odds of clinically significant PFGD in patients undergoing open reduction. However, this study had poor inclusion criteria and failed to account for various factors that could potentially affect the risk of PFGD, such as the use of preoperative traction, surgical approach for open reduction, as well as the use of concurrent pelvic and/ or femoral osteotomies. In our study, we aim to comprehensively summarise the evidence map of the reported prevalence of PFGD following open versus closed reduction in patients with DDH after 6 months of age, as well as to explore the influence of various factors that may modulate the risk of PFGD in this population.

Materials and methods

A systematic review was performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) recommendations. 21 The systematic review protocol was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (Registration Number: CRD420251015622).

Search strategy

We performed a systematic search of the literature across PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, The Cochrane Library and OrthoSearch from the date of inception of each database through to the 2 February 2025. The population of interest were patients with DDH aged 6 months or greater, but less than 10 years old. Comparisons were made between closed reduction versus open reduction. The primary outcome of interest was the prevalence of clinically significant PFGD, defined as Kalamchi–MacEwen or Bucholz–Ogden Type 2 or above (Figure 1).22,23 These thresholds were established based on previous studies demonstrating that Type 1 lesions are usually transient, whereas Type 2 lesions and above may lead to permanent deformity of the femoral head.22,24,25 Both classification systems were similar and widely applied, therefore selected as a basis for comparative analysis.

Top row showing the Kalamchi and MacEwin Classification – Grade 1: Changes isolated to the ossific nucleus; Grade 2: Lateral physeal damage resulting in coxa valga; Grade 3: Central physeal damage resulting in coxa breva; Grade 4: Total damage to head and physis resulting in deformity of the femoral head and neck.

Bottom row showing the Bucholz and Ogden Classification – Grade 1: Fragmentation of the ossific nucleus with slight widening of the neck; Grade 2: Lateral metaphyseal and physeal damage resulting in coxa valga; Grade 3: Entire metaphyseal damage resulting in coxa breva and trochanteric overgrowth; Grade 4: Medial metaphyseal and physeal damage resulting in coxa vara.

A literature search was conducted using the following search terms and Boolean operators (‘Open’ OR ‘Closed’) AND (‘Hip Dysplasia’ OR ‘Developmental Dysplasia’ OR ‘Congenital Hip Dislocation’ OR ‘DDH’ OR ‘CDH’) AND (‘Avascular Necrosis’ OR ‘AVN’ OR ‘Proximal Femur Growth Disturbance’ OR ‘PFGD’ OR ‘Osteonecrosis’ OR ‘Outcome’). The titles and abstracts were then screened by two reviewers (TC and ABo) for relevance and consideration and compiled into a provisional list. The provisional list was then assessed independently by the same two reviewers, each having read the full text for their potential inclusion. The two reviewers came to a consensus on the included articles. Where consensus could not be reached, it was achieved with a third reviewer (JC). The primary reviewers were both experienced orthopaedic registrars and the senior reviewer was a fellowship-trained consultant paediatric orthopaedic surgeon. We further explored several common factors that may influence the prevalence of PFGD. In patients undergoing open reduction, further analyses were performed to determine if the surgical approach (anterior vs. medial), presence of bony osteotomy, age at time of surgery, routine use of preoperative traction, study design, and publication year significantly affected the prevalence of PFGD. Similarly, in those undergoing closed reduction, we performed further analysis on the use of routine preoperative traction, routine adductor tenotomy, age at time of surgery, study design, and publication year on the prevalence of PFGD.

All published studies with sufficient extractable data were considered for inclusion. We only included patients with DDH who either received a closed or open reduction between the age of 6 months and 10 years. If the studies included patients outside of our age range, but there was sufficient information to extract cases which met our inclusion criteria, these studies were still included. We excluded studies describing surgical interventions in patients with other hip conditions, such as femoroacetabular impingement, Perthes disease, slipped capital femoral epiphysis and traumatic hip dislocations, studies using other criteria to measure PFGD apart from that described by Kalamchi–MacEwen or Bucholz–Ogden, as well as those with less than five patients in either group. Studies must exclude patients with teratological or neuromuscular hip dysplasia. Lastly, to reduce heterogeneity, we excluded studies on hip arthroscopy, arthroplasty, periacetabular osteotomy, closed reduction with gradual traction (e.g. Petit-Morel method), and closed reduction with pelvic/ femoral osteotomy. Studies were not excluded based upon their published language, study type, or geographic location.

Data collection and risk of bias assessment

Data extraction was performed by the first reviewer (TC) and validated by the second reviewer (ABo). The individual study characteristics and outcomes of interest were assessed. A risk of bias assessment, as described by Hoy et al. 26 was conducted for all included studies. Publication bias was assessed using inverse-variance funnel plots, with asymmetry confirmed using Egger’s test. Inverse variance funnel plots provide improved visualisation of publication bias where a large variation of sample size exists between studies. 27

Statistical analysis

Studies were split into ‘open reduction’ and ‘closed reduction’ groups and were analysed separately. All meta-analyses were performed using a random effects model due to anticipated heterogeneity between studies. The Freeman-Tukey double arcsine technique with Miller’s back-transformation was performed to estimate summary proportions, due to the likely rare occurrence of PFGD. 28 Sensitivity analysis using the ‘leave one out’ technique was used to ascertain whether the removal of any single study had substantial implications on the overall results. In the open reduction group, we performed exploratory analyses, univariate and multivariate meta-regression was used to compare the effect of age at time of reduction, surgical approach, presence and type of osteotomy, study design and publication year on prevalence. Both univariate and multivariate analyses were performed to analyse and quantify the complex relationships between factors that may influence the prevalence of PFGD. A separate exploratory analysis was used to assess the use of routine preoperative traction on the prevalence of clinically significant PFGD due to a lack of a valid comparator. Similarly in the closed reduction group, we performed exploratory analyses, as well as univariate and multivariate meta-regression to compare the effect of age at time of reduction, study design, and publication year on prevalence of PFGD. The use of routine preoperative traction and routine adductor tenotomy were again analysed as a separate exploratory due to a lack of a valid comparator. All analyses were performed Stata Version 18.0 (StataCorp, Texas, USA). The threshold for statistical significance was set at p < 0.050.

Open reduction

One hundred and twenty-eight studies17,29–155 with 165 datapoints described the prevalence of PFGD following open reduction in 9067 hips. Thirty-nine of our included studies (30.47%) were conducted in Türkiye and 110 studies (85.94%) had a retrospective design. A brief summary of these studies is included in Supplemental Table 1. The prevalence of clinically significant PFGD was 12.47% (95% Confidence Interval (CI): 10.39–14.69). Although the I2 value was 79.77%, the τ2 value of 0.12 was less than the pooled effect size, indicating that most data points were highly precise. Sensitivity analysis did not reveal any substantial difference in pooled results using the ‘leave-one-out’ technique (Supplemental Figure 1).

Six studies exclusively included patients who received a revision open reduction after a failed prior open reduction.34,55,73,95,96,108 Excluding these studies from our analysis, there was no substantial change in the overall prevalence of clinically significant PFGD (Prevalence = 11.86%, 95% CI: 9.86–14.00). Fourteen studies17,39,52,56,68,79,80,104,108,115,125,129,135,143 with 16 datapoints routinely used preoperative traction, of which 2 involved the use of skeletal traction.17,125 The prevalence of clinically significant PFGD in these patients was 18.87% (95% CI: 11.32–27.61). Similarly, although the I2 value was 83.86%, the τ2 value of 0.12 was less than the pooled effect size, indicating that most data points were highly precise.

Where pelvic osteotomy was performed, Salter Innominate or Pemberton’s osteotomy was most commonly employed. Univariate meta-regression analysis demonstrated that retrospective study designs were associated with a 7.38% increase in the prevalence of clinically significant PFGD as compared to prospective study designs. This was statistically significant (p = 0.011). Furthermore, the reported prevalence of clinically significant PFGD had reduced significantly in more contemporary literature (p = 0.010). A bubble plot illustrating this effect is shown in Figure 3. The patient’s age at reduction, surgical approach, as well as the presence and type of osteotomy performed did not significantly affect the prevalence of clinically significant PFGD on univariate analysis. Importantly, our multivariate regression analysis showed that none of our investigated covariates significantly affected the prevalence of clinically significant PFGD. Closed reduction

Twenty-nine studies48,52,103,113,115,116,136,156–177 with 32 datapoints described the prevalence of PFGD following closed reduction in 2605 hips. Ten of our included studies (34.48%) were conducted in China and 27 studies (93.10%) were retrospective in nature. The prevalence of clinically significant PFGD was 9.53% (95% CI: 6.91–12.46). Although the I2 value was 77.38%, the τ2 value of 0.04 was less than the pooled effect size, indicating that most data points were highly precise. Sensitivity analysis did not reveal any substantial difference in pooled results using the ‘leave-one-out’ technique (Supplemental Figure 2).

Eleven studies routinely used preoperative traction.52,115,157,160,163–167,171,172 Duration of traction varied between 1–3 weeks (Supplemental Table 2). The prevalence of clinically significant PFGD in these patients was 9.80% (95% CI: 6.58–13.51). There was no substantial heterogeneity between studies (I2 = 45.93%, τ2 = 0.01). Similarly, four studies routinely performed adductor tenotomies as part of the closed reduction. The prevalence of clinically significant PFGD in these patients was 14.65% (95% CI: 2.20–34.08). There was substantial heterogeneity between included studies (I2 = 90.66%, τ2 = 0.18).

Univariate meta-regression analysis demonstrated that increasing age at the time of closed reduction was associated with a statistically significant increase in the prevalence of clinically significant PFGD (p = 0.037). A bubble plot illustrating this effect is shown in Figure 4. However, neither study design nor publication year significantly affects the prevalence of clinically significant PFGD. Our multivariate regression analysis further demonstrated that for every month increase in patients’ age at closed reduction, the risk of clinically significant PFGD increased by 0.74%. This was statistically significant (p = 0.022). These findings are summarised in Table 2.

Risk of bias analysis

The median risk of bias score as described by Hoy et al. 26 was 9 out of 10 both for open reduction (range: 7–9) and closed reduction studies (range: 8–9). None of the studies selected their participants via census or random selection. Twelve studies were deemed not to be a true representation of our target population – five of these studies included only bilateral DDH,85,102,104,106,115,152 while another six studies described outcomes exclusively in patients undergoing revision open reduction.34,55,73,95,96,108 Thirty studies (20.00%) either had a relatively short follow-up period or, the follow-up period was unclear in their manuscript. Our risk of bias analysis is detailed in Supplemental Table 3. There was no significant publication bias for both open reduction (p = 0.222) and closed reduction (p = 0.205) studies. Discussion

Our analysis found a 12.47% prevalence of clinically significant PFGD in patients with DDH above the age of 6 months undergoing open reduction. The reported prevalence values were highly precise, contributing to a high I2 value, which may be attributed to the variation in patient population, surgical technique and postoperative management. When analysed based on the most common regions in which the study was conducted, the prevalence of clinically significant PFGD was 9.60% (95% CI: 6.99–12.50) for studies conducted in Türkiye, 18.01% (95% CI: 11.38–25.65) for those conducted in the United Kingdom, and 16.07% (95% CI: 10.19–22.80) for those conducted in the United States. Previous meta-analyses by Gardner et al. 178 and Novais et al. 179 have reported a prevalence of approximately 20%. Thus, our findings have shown that the reported prevalence had decreased significantly over time. This observation has been confirmed by our univariate meta-regression analysis, which showed that the reported prevalence of clinically significant PFGD has decreased in more contemporary literature. Our bubble plot analysis (Figure 2) further demonstrates this relationship. The lack of significance of the publication year on the prevalence of clinically significant PFGD on multivariate meta-regression analysis suggests that optimisation of patient factors and advancements in surgical technique could have accounted for the higher prevalence of clinically significant PFGD in older studies. In line with previous studies, we found that neither surgical approach nor the age at time of surgery had a significant effect on the prevalence of clinically significant PFGD.

Nevertheless, our finding that the presence and type of osteotomy also having no effect on the prevalence of clinically significant PFGD has yet to be described in the literature. Several high-quality studies comparing outcomes following various bony and/or soft tissue-only surgery in patients with DDH were identified in our systematic review. A case-control study by Carsi and Clarke 57 found that addition of pelvic osteotomy to anterior open reduction significantly lowered the rates of acetabular dysplasia without an increased risk of PFGD. Similarly, a randomised controlled trial by Duan et al. compared the outcomes following anterior open reduction and Salter osteotomy with those and a femoral shortening osteotomy. The study found no difference in clinical outcomes (MacKay’s criteria) and risk of PFGD. 70 A further retrospective cohort study by Spence et al. compared the radiographic outcomes following anterior open reduction in combination with femoral varus derotation osteotomy versus anterior open reduction in combination with Salter’s osteotomy. The authors also found that Salter’s osteotomy promoted better acetabular remodelling, however there was no difference in the risk of PFGD. 126 Therefore, we recommend the use of pelvic osteotomy as an adjunct to open reduction, as this has been shown to improve acetabular remodelling without an increase in risk of PFGD. Additionally, we report a higher prevalence of PFGD in patients undergoing preoperative traction. This difference is small and is currently of limited clinical significance. High quality comparative studies are required to clarify this relationship.

We found that the prevalence of clinically significant PFGD following closed reduction was 9.53% in patients with DDH above the age of 6 months at the time of reduction. This finding is somewhat similar to that reported in previous meta-analyses.20,180 When analysed based on the most common regions in which the study was conducted, the prevalence of clinically significant PFGD was 12.90% (95% CI: 11.28–14.61) for studies conducted in China, 6.60% (95% CI: 0.75–16.33) for those conducted in the United States, 1.40% (95% CI: 0.00–5.88) for those conducted in Türkiye and 5.74% (95% CI: 2.01–10.74) for those conducted in Japan. It is possible that regional variation may have played a role in overall prevalence; however, this must be interpreted with caution given the low number of studies. Unsurprisingly, there was no significant effect of the publication year on the prevalence of clinically significant PFGD. Of interest, both our univariate and multivariate meta-regression analysis suggested an increase in clinically significant PFGD with increasing age at closed reduction. This finding could suggest high failure rates associated with closed reductions in older patients, necessitating further open procedures, which carry a higher risk of PFGD than closed reduction alone, and thus needs to be interpreted with caution. The routine use of preoperative traction resulted in a similar prevalence of clinically significant PFGD to the general cohort. Furthermore, the effect of routine adductor tenotomy in conjunction with closed reduction on the risk of clinically significant PFGD cannot be precisely determined. This exploratory analysis only found a relatively small number of studies with high heterogeneity. Therefore, additional studies are required to further evaluate this widely used surgical adjunct.

Although our study comprehensively summarises the prevalence of clinically significant PFGD following open and closed reduction in patients with DDH above the age of 6 months, there are several limitations that needs to be addressed. Firstly, most of our included studies are retrospective in nature and may be subject to recall bias. Nevertheless, in both groups, the reported prevalence of PFGD was higher in retrospective designs, as opposed to prospective designs. This may be due to a longer follow-up period for retrospective studies. Secondly, the use of mean age in our meta-regression analysis to analyse the effect of age on prevalence of PFGD may risk introducing ecological fallacy. Similarly, disease severity may also contribute to PFGD risk. Ideally, an individual participant data meta-analysis is required to definitively address these relationships. Thirdly, the relative heterogeneity (I2) estimates for both open and closed reduction groups were high. Although absolute heterogeneity (τ2) indicated high precision of individual studies, it is possible that the pooling of the prevalence estimates may not be clinically meaningful. Fourthly, as suggested by Harris et al., 181 this is primarily a meta-analysis of non-comparative studies, and therefore pooled comparisons should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, despite the classifications being predictive of femoral head deformities, previous studies have found only weak to moderate interobserver reliability of the Kalamchi–MacEwen and Bucholz–Ogden classification systems.182,183 This may undermine the validity of pooled prevalence. Additionally, it is possible that the sequalae of PFGD may be less significant in younger patients due to remodelling potential. A more severe classification of PFGD may also not necessarily equate to functional morbidity, thus limiting clinical applicability of our findings. Lastly, we did not assess the severity of PFGD following closed or open reduction, as well as the prevalence specific to the type of pelvic and/or femoral osteotomy performed, all of which present an exciting opportunity for future research.

In conclusion, in patients with DDH older than 6 months of age, the prevalence of clinically significant PFGD in those undergoing open reduction was 12.74%, and the prevalence of clinically significant PFGD in those undergoing closed reduction was 9.53%. Direct comparisons between the two arms should be avoided, as this is a meta-analysis of non-comparative studies. 181 In both open and closed reduction groups, we observed a high relative heterogeneity (I2), but acceptable absolute heterogeneity (τ2), and pooled prevalence estimates should be interpreted with caution. The prevalence of clinically significant PFGD following open reduction is significantly lower in contemporary literature, likely due to advancements in surgical technique and improved patient selection. For those undergoing open reduction, the age at the time of surgery, surgical approach, as well as presence and type of bony surgery had no effect on the prevalence of clinically significant PFGD. Of interest, our meta-regression analysis showed that increasing age at the time of closed reduction may be associated with an increase in the risk of developing clinically significant PFGD. Further registry-based observational studies and randomised controlled trials with subsequent individual participant data meta-analysis are required to assess and monitor various treatment options in order to optimise care for this patient population.
