Nanophotosensitizers Based on Celecoxib-Conjugated Chitosan Using a Thioketal Linkage for the Reactive Oxygen Species-Mediated Delivery of Chlorin E6 and Treatment of Oral Cancer Cells
Authors: Inho Bae, Young-IL Jeong, Hee-Jung Kim, Byung-Hoon Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A celecoxib (CELE)-grafted chitosan copolymer was synthesized using a thioketal linkage for oxidative stress-mediated delivery of chlorin e6 (Ce6) and enhanced photodynamic therapy (PDT) against oral squamous carcinoma. CELE was conjugated to the amine groups of chitosan using thioketal dicarboxylic acid as a linker, resulting in the synthesis of a CELE-grafted chitosan (ChitoCELE) copolymer. The ChitoCELE copolymer was reconstituted in water, and Ce6 was subsequently incorporated to produce Ce6-incorporated ChitoCELE nanophotosensitizers. These nanophotosensitizers exhibited a spherical morphology with a diameter of less than 200 nm. Disintegration of the nanophotosensitizers was observed upon exposure to hydrogen peroxide, leading to an increased drug release rate. These results indicated that the nanophotosensitizers are sensitive to oxidative stress in vitro. The intracellular uptake of Ce6, production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and PDT-induced cytotoxicity were all higher following treatment with the nanophotosensitizers compared to treatment with free Ce6. Interestingly, CELE enhanced ROS production when combined with Ce6, indicating that CELE and ChitoCELE conjugates can amplify the PDT efficacy of Ce6-incorporated nanophotosensitizers. In an in vivo tumor xenograft model using KB oral squamous carcinoma cells, the nanophotosensitizers were preferentially delivered to solid tumors and efficiently suppressed tumor growth.

Introduction

Photodynamic therapy (PDT) is considered a safe regimen for cancer treatment because photosensitizers generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) only under exposure to light. These photosensitizers produce ROS when activated by light of a specific wavelength within the irradiated treatment field, while exhibiting no cytotoxicity in nonirradiated areas. PDT has been extensively investigated for the treatment of abnormal cells and various diseases affecting epithelial and mucosal tissues. − In particular, PDT serves as a valuable modality for both the diagnosis and treatment of oral squamous cell carcinoma, as it has demonstrated favorable clinical outcomes and can enhance patients’ quality of life. − Moreover, PDT is an ideal approach for the early detection and treatment of oral squamous carcinoma. While it can be used as a stand-alone therapy in the early stages, it can also serve as an effective adjuvant treatment in advanced stages. Weng et al. reported that PDT decreases circulating tumor cells, reduces the number of metastatic breast cancer cells, and suppresses lung and liver metastases. Furthermore, Kulichenko et al. demonstrated that chlorin e6 (Ce6)-mediated PDT can accurately detect the size and boundaries of tongue tumors, enabling the determination of tumor margins through changes in fluorescence signal intensity. However, the clinical benefits of PDT are often limited by several drawbacks related to photosensitizers. − For example, the lack of specificity of photosensitizers for disease sites and skin photosensitivity to bright light or sunlight are associated with side effects. − Due to the limited penetration depth of light in epithelial tissue or skin, typically less than 20 mm, photosensitizers must localize at the tumor site to effectively generate ROS. Consequently, the application of PDT is generally restricted to epithelial, mucosal, or skin cancers. These limitations are considered significant obstacles to the broader clinical utilization of photosensitizers.

Nanoscale carriers, such as nanoparticles, have been extensively developed due to their superior ability to target specific tissues or cells. − Nanoparticles are regarded as suitable vehicles for the site-specific delivery of bioactive molecules due to their small size, large surface area, and multifunctional properties. − Furthermore, the tumor microenvironment exhibits a higher redox potential than normal tissues, which can be exploited for tumor-targeted delivery using nanomedicines. Nanoparticles sensitive to oxidative conditions and acidic pH can specifically target pulmonary metastatic tumors and inhibit tumor metastasis. Accordingly, drug delivery systems that exploit oxidative stress have been proposed as ideal vehicles for tumor targeting, since oxidative stress levels are elevated in tumors compared to normal tissues. − In particular, ROS-responsive delivery systems utilizing nanoparticles are promising candidates for cancer PDT, as these nanoparticles can be engineered to degrade in response to ROS. , Furthermore, nanocarriers with dual sensitivity to both pH and ROS can efficiently deliver anticancer drugs and Ce6 to the tumor microenvironment, thereby suppressing the growth of hepatic carcinoma.

Celecoxib, a typical cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) inhibitor, is known to prevent oral squamous carcinoma. , Specifically, it significantly suppresses the lipopolysaccharide-induced proliferation of oral squamous carcinoma cells. Furthermore, celecoxib inhibits multidrug resistance induced by conventional anticancer drugs and can be used as an adjuvant therapy in combination with standard chemotherapy. Interestingly, celecoxib induces mitochondrial ROS production in a dose-dependent manner in cancer cells and enhances the sensitivity of patients with advanced metastatic cancers to anticancer drugs.

This study aimed to synthesize a celecoxib-grafted chitosan (ChitoCELE) copolymer using a thioketal linkage and to incorporate Ce6 into the ChitoCELE copolymer to develop nanophotosensitizers for ROS-sensitive delivery. We hypothesized that Ce6-loaded ChitoCELE nanophotosensitizers could facilitate stimulus-responsive drug targeting and improve PDT efficacy in oral squamous cell carcinoma. Accordingly, the PDT efficacy and biological activity of these nanophotosensitizers were evaluated using oral cancer cells and an animal tumor xenograft model.

Materials and Methods

Synthesis of ChitoCELE Copolymer

For ChitoCELE graft copolymer synthesis, a DMSO solution of thioketal dicarboxylic acid (ThdCOOH; 22.4 mg/5 mL) was reacted with 1-ethyl-3-(3-(dimethylamino)­propyl) carbodiimide (EDC, 19.2 mg)/N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS, 11.5 mg), followed by magnetic stirring for 3 h. Next, CELE (38.1 mg) was added, followed by stirring for 12 h to synthesize the CELE–ThdCOOH conjugates. EDC (19.2 mg) and NHS (11.5 mg) were then added to the solution, followed by 24 h of magnetic stirring.

Water-soluble chitosan (WSC) (360 mg) dissolved in a DMSO/H2O mixture (5 mL, 4/1­(v/v)) was added to a solution of CELE–ThdCOOH conjugates, followed by magnetic stirring for 24 h. The reactants were then dialyzed against distilled water using a dialysis membrane for 2 days. At 3 h intervals, distilled water was exchanged to remove organic solvents and byproducts. The resulting solution was lyophilized for 2 days to obtain the ChitoCELE graft copolymer. The yield was approximately >93% (w/w) and was determined as follows: yield (%, w/w) = [ChitoCELE weight]/[weight of (ThdCOOH + CELE + WSC)] × 100.

Fluorescence Spectrophotometry

To analyze the fluorescence properties of the nanophotosensitizers, a fluorescence spectrophotometer (excitation wavelength: 400 nm; Infinite M200 Pro Microplate Reader, Tecan Austria GmbH, Grödig, Austria) was used at 500 ∼ 800 nm. A Maestro 2 small animal imaging instrument was used to obtain fluorescence images of the animals (Cambridge Research and Instrumentation Inc., MA 01801, USA). To analyze the effect of oxidative stress, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4, 0.01 M) with H2O2 was used with the nanophotosensitizers.

Release of CELE from the nanophotosensitizer

The nanophotosensitizer solution (5 mL) was placed in a dialysis tube, as described previously. This was then placed in a Falcon tube with 45 mL of PBS (pH 7.4, 0.01 M). To test the ROS sensitivity of the nanophotosensitizers, a drug release study was performed in the presence of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). For oxidative stress, PBS was mixed with H2O2, and then, nanophotosensitizers were agitated for drug release studies (100 rpm, 37 °C). At predetermined time intervals, the collected PBS was used to measure the released Ce6 at 664 nm using a UV–vis spectrophotometer, and fresh PBS was then added.

Five milliliters of ChitoCELE nanoparticles, as empty nanoparticles, were placed in a dialysis tube to study the release of CELE from the nanoparticles. This was placed in a Falcon tube with 45 mL of PBS (pH 7.4, 0.01 M). H2O2 was then added to determine whether oxidative stress affects the drug release rate at 37 °C. The incubated media were replaced with fresh PBS at the scheduled times to measure the drug concentration. The absorbance of the harvested PBS was measured at 254 nm using a UV–vis spectrophotometer to determine the CELE concentration. Results of all experiments were calculated as the average ± standard deviation (S.D.) from three different experiments. Equal concentrations of chitosan were used for the blank tests.

Cell Culture

YD-38 human oral squamous and KB human oral epithelial cancer cells (Korean Cell Line Bank Co. (Seoul, Korea)) were cultured in a 5% CO2 incubator (37 °C) using RPMI1640 medium (supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum and 1% (v/v) antibiotics).

In vitro PDT Study

In 96-well plates, 1.8 × 104 YD-38 and/or KB cells were seeded and cultured overnight in a 5% CO2 incubator (37 °C). Ce6, ChitoCELE nanoparticles, or nanophotosensitizers in serum-free media were added to cancer cells (2 h). A stock solution of Ce6 in DMSO was diluted 100 times with serum-free RPMI1640 for Ce6 treatment (final DMSO content, 0.5% (v/v) in the final solution). Sterilized aqueous solutions of ChitoCELE nanoparticles or nanophotosensitizers were diluted in RPMI1640, followed by a 2 h exposure to cells. Then, serum/phenol red-free medium was added to cells washed with cold PBS for PDT (Expanded homogeneous beam, SH Systems, Gwangju, Korea; 664 nm, 2.0 J/cm2). Cells were placed in a CO2 incubator at 37 °C for 24 h. An MTT assay was used to evaluate the viability of cancer cells. Here, 30 μL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL in PBS) was used. After 4 h, the media were replaced with 100 μL DMSO, and then, absorbance (570 nm) was measured with an Infinite M200 pro microplate reader (Tecan, Mannedorf, Switzerland). During the PDT study, visible light was shielded to avoid interference from visible light. To determine the intrinsic toxicity (without PDT), cells were irradiated with light and cultured in the dark.

Intracellular Ce6 Concentrations

In 96-well plate, YD-38 or KB cells (1.8 × 104 cells/well) were exposed to Ce6, empty ChitoCELE nanoparticles, and/or nanophotosensitizers for 2 h. Cold PBS was used to wash the cells, and then, lysis buffer (GenDEPOT, Barker, TX, USA) (50 μL) was used to lyse the cells. The cellular Ce6 levels were measured using a microplate reader (excitation wavelength, 407 nm; emission wavelength, 664 nm).

Fluorescence Microscopy

For fluorescence microscopy observations of cells, a cover glass was placed in six-well plates, and YD-38 or KB cells (3 × 105) were seeded. Cells were exposed to Ce6, empty ChitoCELE nanoparticles, and/or nanophotosensitizers. Two hours later, cold PBS was used to wash the cells. The cells were fixed using Immunomount (Thermo Electron Co., Pittsburgh, PA, USA) and observed using a Nikon Eclipse 80i fluorescence microscope (Tokyo, Japan).

Cellular ROS Production

Cellular ROS production was measured using a fluorescent dye (DCFH-DA, 20 μM). To 96-well plates containing YD-38 or KB cells (1.8 × 104 cells/well), Ce6, Ce6 combined with CELE, ChitoCELE NP without Ce6, and/or Ce6-ChitoCELE nanophotosensitizers were added, and cells were cultured for 2 h with DCFH-DA. The supernatant was replaced with PBS to wash the cells. Then, 100 μL of fresh phenol red-free media was added to the cells, which were irradiated at 664 nm (2.0 J/cm2). For the analysis of ROS generation, the following conditions were used: excitation wavelength, 485 nm; emission wavelength, 535 nm (Infinite M200 Pro Microplate Reader).

In Vivo Study Using a Mouse Tumor Xenograft

For animal biodistribution fluorescence observations, 5-week-old mice (nude BALb/C mice (20 g) were used (Orient Bio Inc., Seongnam-si, Gyeonggi-do, Korea). Solid tumors were prepared via the subcutaneous injection of KB cells (1 × 106 cells) into the backs of animals. The animals were fed freely, and tumor development proceeded for 2 or 3 weeks until the tumor diameter was 4 ∼ 6 mm. Nanophotosensitizers (Ce6-based dose, 10 mg/kg; injection volume, 200 μL) were intravenously injected through the mouse tail vein. One day after drug administration, the whole body and organs were observed through fluorescence imaging (Maestro 2 imaging instrument). To observe the organs, avertin was used to anesthetize the mice, which were then euthanized.

To determine PDT efficacy against the animal tumor model, 1 × 106 KB cells were subcutaneously injected into the backs of mice, which were then freely fed until use. The solid tumors on the backs of the mice were allowed to grow to >4 mm in diameter, and then, free Ce6, a combination of free Ce6/free CELE, ChitoCELE nanoparticles, or Ce6-incorporated ChitoCELE nanophotosensitizers, was injected via the tail vein. The doses of Ce6 and CELE were 10 mg/kg (injection volume: 100 or 200 μL) in PBS (0.01 M, pH 7.4). Each treatment group consisted of five mice. Ten milliliters of ethanol/Cremophor EL mixtures (5 mL/5 mL) was used for Ce6, and this was diluted 10 times with PBS for intravenous injections. ChitoCELE nanoparticles or nanophotosensitizers were dissolved in water and filtered through a syringe filter for sterilization. The first day of drug injection was considered 0 days. Two days later, all mice were irradiated at 660 nm (10 J/cm2), and 5 days after the first administration, all mice were irradiated again. The diameters of the solid tumors were measured at intervals of 5 days, and the calculations were performed as follows: tumor volume (mm3) = length × width2/2.

Scientific care and ethical issues associated with animal experiments were monitored and reviewed by the Chosun University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (CIACUC). All animal experiments were carried out according to the guidelines of the CIACUC. The approval number for animal studies was CIACUC2023-A0029.

Statistical Analysis

Student’s t tests were employed to estimate the significance of the statistical analysis using the SigmaPlot program (Systat Software Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA).

Results

Synthesis of ChitoCELE Copolymer

To synthesize the CELE-grafted chitosan (ChitoCELE) copolymer, the carboxyl group of thioketal dicarboxylic acid (ThdCOOH) was first activated using EDC/NHS, and subsequently conjugated with celecoxib (CELE) to form the CELE–ThdCOOH intermediate. Subsequently, the remaining carboxyl group of the CELE–ThdCOOH conjugate was reactivated using the EDC/NHS system in Water-Soluble Chitosan (WSC) solution and subsequently conjugated to chitosan to yield the ChitoCELE graft copolymer (Figure A).

The labels a–f in the molecular structure denote the positions of protons whose signals are assigned to specific peaks in the NMR spectrum, and the numbers 1–6 indicate the protons of carbon 1–6 of glucose in chitosan.

Figure B shows that specific ThdCOOH peaks (a, b) were observed at 1.5 ∼ 3.5 ppm, while specific CELE peaks (c, d, e, f) were observed at 2.0 ∼ 2.5 ppm and 7.0 ∼ 8.0 ppm. The ChitoCELE graft copolymer thus exhibited specific peaks for both CELE and ThdCOOH. Furthermore, protons of glucosamine in chitosan were confirmed at 2.0 ∼ 5.0 ppm, indicating that CELE–ThdCOOH was successfully conjugated with chitosan. Subsequently, a CELE-grafted chitosan copolymer (ChitoCELE) was synthesized. The conjugation yield of CELE was higher than 93% (w/w).

In Figure B (a and b), the effects of oxidative stress on the physicochemical properties of the nanophotosensitizers were also investigated. Ce6 incorporation induced an increase in particle size compared to ChitoCELE nanoparticles. Interestingly, the addition of H2O2 significantly affected disintegration, and the changes in morphology were dependent on the concentration of H2O2. These results demonstrated that nanophotosensitizers can respond to ROS in vitro. The particle sizes and morphologies of the Ce6-incorporated ChitoCELE nanophotosensitizers were difficult to measure at >5 mM H2O2.

The in vitro release behaviors of CELE and Ce6 from Ce6-incorporated ChitoCELE are shown in Figure . Figure A shows that in the absence of H2O2, the release rate of CELE remained below 30% (w/w) even after 4 days. However, the addition of H2O2 accelerated the release rate of CELE from ChitoCELE nanoparticles in a dose-dependent manner, with higher H2O2 concentrations leading to increased drug release. ChitoCELE nanoparticles are ROS-sensitive and can specifically liberate CELE under oxidative stress conditions. Figure B shows a sustained release profile of Ce6 at a loading content of 8.9% (w/w), indicating delayed release behavior. Moreover, the introduction of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) significantly enhanced the Ce6 release rate in a concentration-dependent manner, with higher H2O2 levels resulting in increased release, as shown in Figure C. These findings suggest that the nanophotosensitizers exhibit oxidative stress-responsiveness, enabling selective Ce6 release under oxidative conditions. In addition, the fluorescence intensity of the nanophotosensitizers increased proportionally with H2O2 concentration, as depicted in Figure D.

In Vitro Cell Cytotoxicity of ChitoCELE Nanoparticles and Ce6-Incorporated Nanophotosensitizers

Figure A (a and b) shows the intrinsic cytotoxicity of the Ce6 and nanophotosensitizers against KB and YD-38 cells, respectively. The nanophotosensitizers exhibited minimal intrinsic cytotoxicity toward cancer cells. In the absence of photodynamic therapy (PDT), cell viability remained above 80% at concentrations of Ce6 up to 2 μg/mL. These findings indicate that the nanophotosensitizers possess low dark cytotoxicity against cancer cells. Figure B (a and b) presents the cytotoxic effects of free CELE and ChitoCELE on oral squamous cancer cells, respectively. As shown in both figures, the viability of KB and/or YD-38 cells decreased in a dose-dependent manner at CELE concentrations exceeding 10 μg/mL. In contrast, treatment with CELE at concentrations below 5 μg/mL exhibited minimal cytotoxicity toward cancer cells for both free Ce6 and Ce6-incorporated nanophotosensitizers. As a well-established anti-inflammatory agent, CELE may reduce the viability of oral squamous carcinoma cells through its pharmacological mechanisms. As shown in Figure C (a and b), the nanophotosensitizers enhanced intracellular Ce6 uptake in a concentration-dependent manner, similar to free Ce6, as the Ce6 concentration increased. In particular, nanophotosensitizers significantly increased intracellular Ce6 uptake compared to free Ce6.

Figure A demonstrates intracellular ROS generation in KB and YD-38 cells following treatment with varying concentrations of free Ce6, ChitoCELE NP, Ce6-loaded ChitoCELE NP, as well as treatment with free CELE (5 μg/mL) combined with different Ce6 concentrations. As shown in Figure A (a, c), free Ce6 induces intracellular ROS generation in a dose-dependent manner, and free Ce6 combined with free CELE leads to significantly higher ROS production compared to free CELE treatment alone in both KB (a) and YD-38 (c) cells. Notably, the combination of free CELE and free Ce6 is proposed to synergistically enhance ROS generation, as CELE may partially inhibit the cellular antioxidant defense or modulate specific signaling pathways, thereby sensitizing cells to Ce6-mediated oxidative stress.

In Figure A (b, d), intracellular ROS generation was markedly higher in both KB and YD-38 cells treated with Ce6-incorporated nanophotosensitizers compared to those treated with free Ce6 or free Ce6 in combination with free CELE. Here, the concentration of ChitoCELE NPs was determined based on the total concentration of the ChitoCELE NP copolymer, rather than the CELE content alone. In addition, the concentration of Ce6-ChitoCELE NP was determined based on the Ce6 content.

The PDT with nanophotosensitizers induced significant cancer cell death compared to that with the other treatments (Figure B­(a) and (b)). That is, nanophotosensitizer-based PDT significantly killed both KB and YD-38 cancer cells in a dose-dependent manner, and free Ce6-based PDT also decreased cell viability in a dose-dependent manner. Nanophotosensitizers thus exhibit excellent PDT efficacy. Furthermore, the phototoxicity of free Ce6 was also increased after a combination with free CELE was used to cotreat cells (i.e., the viability of cells decreased when free Ce6 was coadministered with free CELE, even though nanophotosensitizer phototoxicity was higher than that of free Ce6 or free Ce6/free CELE cotreatment) (Figure B­(c) and (d)). Cellular ROS production and phototoxic cell death can therefore be accelerated by its combination with CELE. The viability of both KB and YD-38 cells was not significantly affected by free CELE after a 2 h exposure, and viability was maintained at higher than 90% (Figure S1). All treatments were conducted at a CELE concentration of 5 μg/mL and a Ce6 concentration of 1 μg/mL.

KB cell apoptosis/necrosis was also studied (Figure and Figure S2). Both were found to increase following treatment with free Ce6, free Ce6/free CELE, and Ce6-incorporated ChitoCELE nanophotosensitizers. In particular, cancer cell necrosis and/or apoptosis were significantly increased when Ce6-incorporated ChitoCELE nanophotosensitizers were used. These results indicate that oral squamous carcinoma cell apoptosis and/or necrosis are accelerated by PDT based on nanophotosensitizers.

In Vivo KB Cell-Bearing Animal Tumor Xenograft Model and Anticancer Activity

KB tumor-bearing mice were used to evaluate the efficacy of the PDT (Figure ). To evaluate the tumor-delivery capacity, nanophotosensitizers were administered intravenously, and fluorescence images of the mice were observed (Figure A). The fluorescence of solid tumors was significantly higher than that of their normal counterparts, although liver fluorescence was also observed, indicating that nanophotosensitizers are superior for targeting tumors.

Figure B shows that free Ce, free Ce6/free CELE combinations, and nanophotosensitizers inhibited tumor growth. In particular, the nanophotosensitizers significantly suppressed tumor growth compared to that with free Ce or free Ce6/free CELE combinations. Interestingly, the combination of free Ce6 and free CELE was more efficient in inhibiting tumor growth than free Ce. Furthermore, ChitoCELE nanoparticles themselves also had inhibitory effects on tumor growth, although their efficacy was lower than that of the other treatments. Our results showed that the Ce6-incorporated ChitoCELE nanophotosensitizers were superior in terms of tumor targeting and PDT-mediated anticancer activity.

Discussion

As a representative COX-2 inhibitor, CELE has been reported to induce mitochondrial superoxide production in cancer cells, thereby disrupting the mitochondrial transmembrane potential through ROS-dependent apoptosis. These mechanisms lead to cancer cell death and, when combined with anticancer agents, exert synergistic effects against metastatic melanoma and breast cancer. Furthermore, in combination with chemotherapeutic agents, CELE disrupts mitochondrial redox homeostasis, thereby reducing ATP production and markedly enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of the drugs. Kim et al. reported that photodynamic therapy (PDT) using 5-aminolevulinic acid in combination with CELE suppressed the oxidative stress–induced expression of defensive proteins, such as HO-1, and synergistically reduced cancer cell viability. Similarly, Mao et al. observed a synergistic effect of CELE on the PDT response in cervical cancer cells and demonstrated that CELE-combined PDT improved therapeutic outcomes. Consistent with these findings, our results show that CELE enhanced intracellular ROS generation, induced apoptosis and necrosis, and decreased the viability of oral squamous carcinoma cells in vitro (Figures , , and S2). Moreover, CELE synergistically inhibited tumor growth in an in vivo KB tumor xenograft model (Figure ).

ChitoCELE nanoparticles also contributed to intracellular ROS generation and subsequent cell death, owing to the grafting of CELE onto chitosan via an ROS-sensitive thioketal linkage. Notably, the intrinsic ROS-producing capacity of CELE in biological systems may facilitate the disintegration of ChitoCELE nanoparticles. Incorporation of Ce6 into these nanoparticles to form nanophotosensitizers further amplified this effect, thereby enhancing ROS production, as demonstrated in Figures , , and . Moreover, the Ce6-ChitoCELE nanophotosensitizers efficiently delivered the photosensitizer into the intracellular compartment and markedly increased intracellular ROS levels, as shown in Figures and . This cascade of events accelerated apoptotic cell death via ROS generation and effectively suppressed tumor growth (Figures and ).

The tumor microenvironment exhibits distinct physiological characteristics − and typically comprises a variety of components, including malignant cells, adipocytes, immune cells, stromal cells, fibroblasts, blood vessels, tumor vasculature, lymphocytes, and dendritic cells, among others. , Due to this complexity, its metabolic activity is considerably higher than that of normal tissues. Characteristically, the tumor microenvironment is associated with elevated expression of diverse molecular receptors, acidic pH, and increased redox activity. − Paradoxically, these pathological features of the tumor microenvironment can be exploited as diagnostic biomarkers and/or as targets for therapeutic interventions. −

For example, Jung et al. reported that elevated oxidative stress caused by excessive ROS production in the tumor microenvironment enables both ROS imaging and drug delivery monitoring through nanoparticle-based magnetic resonance imaging. Furthermore, the acidic pH of the tumor microenvironment can be exploited for pH-sensitive peptide-based targeting of anticancer agents, which not only reduces the undesired side effects of chemotherapeutic drugs but also enhances their anticancer efficacy. Zhou et al. demonstrated that mRNA-loaded, ROS-responsive polymeric nanoparticles, when combined with indocyanine green-based photodynamic therapy (PDT), improved mRNA translocation efficiency and antitumor activity via ROS-induced nanoparticle disassembly.

Furthermore, nanoliposomes coencapsulating CELE and genistein have been shown to promote intracellular ROS generation, accompanied by a reduction in cellular glutathione levels, and to selectively induce apoptotic death in prostate cancer cells. Notably, CELE enhances the anticancer efficacy of conventional chemotherapeutic agents via the ROS-mediated mitochondrial pathway. In our study, oxidative stress within cancer cells accelerated the release of CELE; specifically, exposure to H2O2 triggered the disintegration of nanophotosensitizers, resulting in a drug release rate that increased in proportion to the H2O2 concentration (Figures and ). Compared with conventional photosensitizers such as Ce6, Ce6-incorporated ChitoCELE nanophotosensitizers markedly improved intracellular Ce6 delivery (Figure ). Consequently, Ce6-incorporated ChitoCELE nanophotosensitizers significantly inhibited tumor growth, and CELE alone also exerted tumor growth–inhibitory effects (Figure ). Therefore, ChitoCELE nanoparticles and their nanophotosensitizer formulations offer superior intracellular ROS generation and PDT-mediated anticancer activity.

Conclusions

A CELE-grafted chitosan (ChitoCELE) copolymer was synthesized via a thioketal linkage to enable stimuli-responsive Ce6 delivery, thereby inducing oxidative stress and enhancing PDT efficacy against oral squamous carcinoma cells. Transmission electron microscopy revealed that the resulting nanophotosensitizers possessed a spherical morphology with an average diameter of approximately 170 nm. Increasing the Ce6 loading led to a larger particle size and slower in vitro drug-release kinetics. In contrast, the addition of H2O2 induced nanophotosensitizer disintegration, increased particle size, and accelerated Ce6 release, confirming their oxidative stress–responsive behavior in vitro. Notably, nanophotosensitizers achieved the highest intracellular Ce6 uptake among all treatments tested. Intracellular ROS generation and phototoxic cell death induced by nanophotosensitizers were also significantly greater than those observed with other treatments. Interestingly, CELE alone increased intracellular ROS, and the combination of free Ce6 and free CELE further enhanced ROS production, indicating that nanophotosensitizers provide superior intracellular Ce6 delivery, ROS generation, and PDT performance. In vivo, in a KB cell–bearing tumor xenograft model, nanophotosensitizers were preferentially delivered to the tumor mass, resulting in effective tumor growth suppression.
