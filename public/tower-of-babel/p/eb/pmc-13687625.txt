Knowledge and resources gaps for adolescent and young adult (AYA) cancer patients: Results from survAYA, an Italian National Survey
Authors: Alberto Puccini, Sara Farinatti, Alexia Francesca Bertuzzi, Giacomo Giulio Baldi, Alice Indini, Paola Quarello, Elena Pagani Bagliacca, Maurizio Mascarin, Marco Maruzzo, Matteo Lambertini, Lorena Incorvaia, Giuseppe Maria Milano, Giulia Zucchetti, Assunta Tornesello, Marco Spinelli, Elisabetta Razzaboni, Teresa Perillo, Laura Marziali, Andrea Ferrari, Fedro Alessandro Peccatori, Angela Toss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:Adolescents and young adults (AYA; 15–39 years) represent a distinct oncology population with specific biological and psychosocial needs. Despite growing awareness, AYA patients often face fragmented care between pediatric and adult settings, resulting in diagnostic delays, limited clinical trial access, and inadequate psychosocial support. In 2021, the AIOM-AIEOP AYA Working Group was established in Italy to promote awareness and address these challenges.Methods:An anonymous, voluntary online survey was designed by the AIOM-AIEOP AYA Working Group and distributed during the AIOM National Congress (November 2024). Using iPads at a dedicated booth, oncologists and other healthcare professionals completed a questionnaire exploring knowledge, perceptions, and access to AYA-focused services. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics.Results:Ninety-seven professionals participated (median age 34 years; 75% female; 56% medical oncologists). Although 86% reported access to genetic counseling and 84% to fertility preservation services, only 18% had AYA-dedicated spaces and 21% employed trained AYA-specific staff. Forty-four percent felt competent in assessing hereditary cancer syndromes, and fewer than 25% were aware of AYA-focused research or trials. Psychological support was commonly available, whereas educational coaching and peer support groups were rare (5% each). Lifestyle counseling was frequent, but structured survivorship care and long-term follow-up were limited.Conclusions:This survey reveals substantial gaps in AYA oncology services in Italy, particularly regarding dedicated staff, infrastructure, and training. National coordination and implementation of standardized frameworks, such as through the AIOM-AIEOP network, are essential to ensure equitable, comprehensive care for AYA patients.

Introduction

Adolescents and young adults (AYA), defined as patients aged 15 to 39 years old, represent an often-overlooked category in oncology, distinct from both their adult and pediatric counterparts and characterized by unique biological, clinical, epidemiological and prognostic characteristics. 1

Prognostic differences represent only one of the elements defining AYA patients as a distinct category in oncology, as several aspects of clinical management also require specific consideration. From diagnosis to the complex process of treatment decision-making, AYA patients differ markedly from both pediatric and older adult populations. Diagnostic delays—often related to the rarity of certain malignancies and challenges in pathological assessment—together with limited access to clinical trials, may significantly affect outcomes.2-5 Moreover, treatment planning must carefully balance expected benefits with potential long-term toxicities, while addressing the unique psychosocial dimensions of this age group. Cancer is frequently diagnosed during a pivotal life stage, interrupting education, early career development, and family planning. Consequently, AYA patients face distinctive psychosocial challenges that are often unmet within traditional healthcare structures, which tend to be oriented toward either pediatric or adult populations.6-9

Healthcare professionals often demonstrate limited awareness of the unique biological characteristics and specific needs of AYA patients. Consequently, the scientific community increasingly advocates for holistic, multidisciplinary care models tailored to this population.10-12 Ongoing efforts aim to define core competencies for healthcare providers, promote AYA-centered frameworks of care, and foster greater awareness through the development of dedicated clinical and research programs. Beyond diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, a major concern remains the absence of structured survivorship care plans addressing the long-term physical, psychological, and social consequences of cancer and its treatment.13-15

In 2021, AIOM (Italian Association of Medical Oncology) and AIEOP (Italian Association of Pediatric Hematology and Oncology) joined forces and established the Italian AIOM-AIEOP Working Group dedicated to AYA. The AYA AIOM-AIEOP Intergroup has initiated a collaborative effort to enhance care for adolescent and young adult cancer patients in Italy. Drawing inspiration from the European ESMO–SIOPE Position Paper, 1 the Working Group aims to bridge the gap between pediatric and adult oncology through the creation of a synergistic national network. This initiative seeks to raise awareness, promote collaborative research, and strengthen partnerships with patient advocacy groups to better address the unique clinical and psychosocial needs of AYA patients. A major objective is to define the core requirements for AYA-designated centers and to develop a coordinated, nationwide framework capable of delivering comprehensive and age-appropriate care across Italy.16-21

During the AIOM National Congress held in November 2024, a dedicated survey was distributed to participating medical oncologists with the primary objective of assessing their knowledge, awareness, and perception of the availability and adequacy of services for AYA patients. This focus was chosen given the pivotal role of medical oncologists in managing cancer patients across disease stages and care settings. Evaluating their level of engagement and familiarity with AYA-specific issues is essential to identify existing gaps and inform future strategies aimed at enhancing the quality of care and the implementation of AYA-oriented programs within adult oncology services. Here, we present the results of this national survey and discuss key considerations and potential directions for the further development of AYA oncology in Italy.

Materials and methods

The study was designed as a descriptive, hypothesis-generating survey aimed at exploring current awareness and practices in AYA oncology across Italian centers.

The members of the AIOM-AIEOP AYA Working Group designed and conducted an anonymous, voluntary online survey based on a previous European initiative. 22 The survey was distributed during the 2024 National AIOM Congress, held in Rome in November 2024. Targeted at healthcare professionals involved in cancer care—including medical oncologists, radiation oncology specialists, nurses, and clinical research professionals—the survey aimed to capture a multidisciplinary perspective. Participants were invited to complete the questionnaire using iPads made available at a dedicated AYA booth within the congress venue, ensuring accessibility and encouraging broad participation.

The survey included questions covering a wide range of topics, from basic knowledge of AYA, oncology-related issues and the availability of dedicated facilities in Italian hospitals, to the daily management of AYA patients, survivorship care, and awareness of hereditary cancer syndromes and their management. The full survey with questions and responses both in Italian and in English are available in Online Supplementary Documents 1 and 2, respectively. The aim was to assess (1) the diffusion of AYA specific facilities inside Italian Oncology Institutes and (2) knowledge about AYA patients and their specific needs and characteristics among health professionals participating to the AIOM Annual Meeting.

Results

Participants’ demographics and knowledge

A total of 97 AIOM members completed the survey. The questionnaire was promoted among attendees of the AYA-dedicated session during the AIOM National Congress and visitors to the Fondazione AIOM booth (approximately 400 participants overall). Respondents included medical oncologists (56%), nurses (34%), radiation oncologists (2%), and psychologists (1%). The median age was 34 years (range 23–69), with a predominance of female participants (75%). Most respondents worked in Northern Italy (67%), primarily in research hospitals (45%) or university hospitals (32%) (Figure 1).

Geographic distribution of survey respondents: highlighting a clear north–south gradient in participation, with lower representation from hospitals in Central and Southern Italy.

Notably, 14% of respondents were unfamiliar with the AYA acronym, 28% were unaware of the age definition commonly adopted in the literature, and nearly half (48%) did not recognize the poorer prognostic outcomes associated with this patient group. Over a one-year period, 22% reported not having managed any AYA patients, whereas 4% treated more than 50 such cases annually.

Service availability across Italy

Most respondents reported the availability of fertility preservation (84%) and genetic counseling services (86%) within their institutions. However, several essential AYA-focused resources were lacking, including designated palliative care services (37%), dedicated physical spaces (18%), socio-educational programs (20%), trained AYA staff (21%), and tailored follow-up programs (72%) (Figure 2).

Availability of key AYA oncology–related services across Italian hospitals participating in the survey. The figure illustrates the proportion of respondents reporting the presence of specific services within their institutions.

Awareness of AYA-specific clinical or research protocols was limited: only 21% of respondents were aware of active studies addressing the clinical, epidemiological, or psychosocial aspects of AYA oncology.

Although psychological support was available in the majority of centers (94%), AYA-dedicated services and specifically trained personnel remained largely insufficient. Only a small minority of institutions (approximately 5%) reported the presence of educational tutors or peer support groups connecting young patients with shared experiences—despite the recognized importance of such initiatives in fostering adherence and psychological wellbeing. Furthermore, 90% of respondents indicated the absence of structured training programs on AYA patient management, highlighting a major gap in professional education. In terms of patient engagement, only 32% of centers actively collected feedback from AYA patients regarding their care experience, suggesting that the patient voice remains underrepresented in service evaluation and quality improvement processes.

Survivorship issues

When addressing practical aspects of survivorship and long-term follow-up, 69% of respondents reported routinely discussing fertility-related issues, 67% monitored adherence to national vaccination programs, and 58% actively promoted HPV vaccination. Only 11% had ever issued a “cancer-free” declaration for adoption purposes, underscoring the absence of standardized survivorship documentation procedures.

Despite these efforts, structured post-treatment care remains limited. After the standard oncologic follow-up period, only 24% of respondents referred patients to specialized services aimed at monitoring long-term adverse effects related to cancer or its treatment—such as cardiovascular risk assessment in AYA survivors previously exposed to cardiotoxic therapies.

Approximately 80% of respondents reported counseling AYA patients on healthy lifestyle habits following cancer diagnosis and treatment. Specifically, 70% routinely inquired about smoking habits at each visit, with 91% encouraging cessation and 71% recommending referral to smoking-cessation centers.

Regarding hereditary cancer syndromes, only 44% of respondents felt competent in collecting a comprehensive oncologic family history, revealing a further area in need of targeted education and training.

Discussion

Despite the increasing incidence of cancer diagnosis in the AYA population worldwide, 23 AYA patients remain in a “no man's land” and are often an overlooked demographic. From a practical perspective, this has clear consequences throughout the entire care pathway.

Our aim was to gain a clearer understanding of Italian healthcare professionals’ real-life knowledge and perceptions regarding the AYA population and the management of this distinctive subgroup of cancer patients. The survey explored four key domains: (1) general knowledge of AYA-specific issues, (2) availability of dedicated services within Italian hospitals, (3) approaches to daily clinical management from diagnosis to long-term follow-up, and (4) awareness and handling of hereditary cancer syndromes. Respondents participating in the AIOM National Congress identified multiple unmet needs across these domains, including the inconsistent definition of the AYA population in scientific literature, limited awareness of their specific clinical and psychosocial requirements, the widespread lack of dedicated services, and the absence of structured long-term programs for monitoring adverse events, vaccination adherence, and chronic treatment-related conditions.

Our results align with previous European and Asian surveys,22,24 which highlighted comparable challenges, including restricted access to AYA-specific services, suboptimal implementation of dedicated care models, and persistent inequities in the provision of AYA oncology care across different regions.

Although psychological support was reported in 94% of cases and fertility preservation and genetic counseling services were available in more than 80%, most institutions lacked an integrated, multidisciplinary framework including trained AYA staff and tailored social or educational initiatives. Less than one in four respondents reported access to such programs, underscoring a persistent gap in comprehensive care and patient-centered engagement.7,25

An oncological diagnosis in AYA patients disrupts the physiological process of growth and represents a break in the development of a pathway toward an adult life. In their pursuit of a sense of normalcy, psychosocial support is essential. Evidence highlights the importance of dedicated support groups, ideally hosted in spaces exclusively for AYA patients, separate from adult patients, and fostering connections with peers. 26 Regrettably, these optimal conditions have not yet been achieved in most Italian healthcare facilities.

In line with previous literature highlighting the limited enrollment of AYA patients in clinical trials, 27 our findings reflect a similarly low level of awareness among Italian healthcare professionals regarding ongoing AYA-specific studies. Fewer than one in four respondents reported being aware of any active research protocols or clinical trials addressing the clinical, epidemiological, or psychosocial aspects of the AYA population

This survey, promoted by the interdisciplinary AIOM–AIEOP Working Group, represents part of a broader European effort to address the challenges faced by adolescents and young adults (AYA) with cancer. While common minimum standards are warranted and advocated from different working groups and patient organizations, a fragmentation in both management and resources allocation does exist, being linked to awareness issues as well as to the inherent differences in European health care systems and consequently generating between-country discrepancies. 28 An example is represented by, but not limited to, the Network of Expertise on Adolescents and Young Adults, a specific work package within the framework of the Joint Action on Networks of Expertise (JANE), focused on establishing a European Network of Expertise on AYAs, to ensure that their distinct clinical, biological, and psychosocial needs are adequately recognized across the EU. These include a unique epidemiological profile, diagnostic delays, low clinical trial participation, and complex survivorship issues. The initiative is closely aligned with the activities of the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) and the European Society for Paediatric Oncology (SIOPE) AYA Working Group, aiming to enhance collaboration and harmonize care standards. In this context, our survey contributes a national perspective, highlighting current gaps and providing a foundation for the development of a coordinated Italian AYA oncology network.

This survey highlights substantial gaps in knowledge, training, and infrastructure for AYA oncology in Italy, particularly in non-academic centers or southern regions. While essential services such as genetic counseling and fertility preservation are widely available, access to dedicated AYA spaces, trained staff, and AYA-specific research remains limited.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The survey was conducted among a selected group of professionals attending the AIOM National Congress—individuals likely more aware and interested in AYA-related issues than the general oncology community. As such, the responses may reflect personal perspectives rather than a nationally representative picture of institutional resources. Moreover, the sample was geographically unbalanced, with a predominance of participants from northern regions, particularly from centers already active in AYA initiatives (Padua and Modena), thus introducing a North-centric bias. The relatively small sample size further limits the generalizability of the findings and reduces statistical strength. Additionally, information regarding the respondents’ tumor specialization was not collected, potentially overlooking areas such as hematologic or endocrine malignancies that are highly relevant in the AYA population. Another important limitation is the potential for self-reporting bias, as participants may have overestimated their adherence to recommended standards of care, such as the frequency of lifestyle counseling or smoking cessation advice, thus introducing a possible overrepresentation of best practices in the reported data.

Despite these limitations, this exploratory effort provides valuable preliminary insights and emphasizes the urgent need for broader, nationwide assessments. Future studies should involve a larger, multidisciplinary cohort and include diverse geographic and institutional settings to better define existing gaps and guide the development of standardized AYA oncology programs across Italy.

Conclusions

This descriptive, hypothesis-generating survey conducted among Italian healthcare professionals attending the AIOM 2024 National Congress provides a preliminary overview of the awareness and availability of AYA-specific services in Italy. Although the sample is not representative of the entire national healthcare system—given its limited size and the likely selection bias toward professionals already interested in AYA oncology—the findings suggest persistent gaps in knowledge, training, and dedicated resources for this population.

Rather than offering a definitive assessment, these results should be interpreted as an initial step toward understanding the current landscape of AYA oncology in Italy. They underscore the need for broader, multicenter initiatives aimed at collecting more comprehensive data and fostering greater institutional coordination. Such efforts could ultimately contribute to the development of a national framework for AYA-focused care, promoting equitable access to specialized services and improved long-term outcomes for this unique patient group.

The AYA AIOM-AIEOP Intergroup is committed to fostering inclusivity, connecting centers across Italy, and establishing shared criteria to improve care for adolescents and young adults with cancer. By creating a national network, we aim to streamline efforts, share best practices, and ensure consistent standards. Collaboration is key—both within Italy and across borders. We are actively engaging with other European and international AYA networks, such as JANE, to harmonize approaches and amplify our collective impact.29,30 Together, we are building a stronger, more unified voice for AYA oncology.
