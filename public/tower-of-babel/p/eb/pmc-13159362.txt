Towards a conceptualisation of collaborative decision-making within interprofessional collaborative practice: a concept analysis using scoping review methodology
Authors: Louise Beckingsale, Tim Wilkinson, Megan Anakin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCollaborative decision-making is a term used when patient care decisions are shared between health workers of different backgrounds. Yet, current definitions of collaborative decision-making do not consistently describe the actions involved in the decision-making process or how the term relates to similar terms. This situation makes learning, teaching, and researching this concept difficult. Therefore, this review aims to clarify the concept of collaborative decision-making within interprofessional collaborative practice.MethodsScoping review methodology was used to identify and select relevant literature. Four databases, were searched between Jan 2022 and December 2024 and included peer-reviewed articles that defined, described, or conceptualised collaborative decision-making or related terms. Extracted data were analysed with a concept analysis approach to develop a conceptualisation.ResultsThirty-seven articles were analysed. Collaborative decision-making was conceptualised as a dynamic and iterative social and cognitive process involving two or more participants from different backgrounds who prioritise their time to discuss and agree on the next steps in patient care. This process includes four core actions: (1) sharing profession specific knowledge and perspectives, (2) integrating knowledge and perspectives to create new and shared knowledge, (3) deliberating over options, and (4) negotiating differences to reach agreement.DiscussionThis conceptualisation clarifies collaborative decision-making as a unifying term for how decisions are shared within interprofessional collaborative practice. It shows that many related terms draw on a common conceptual base. It therefore offers educators a language and framework for making collaborative decision-making explicit to learners and provides researchers with greater conceptual clarity.ConclusionThis review produced a contemporary conceptualisation of collaborative decision-making for others to use when learning, teaching and researching this key component of interprofessional collaborative practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09085-y.Keywords: collaborative decision-making, interprofessional collaborative practice, concept analysis, scoping review, terminology

Background

Collaborative decision-making is a key component of interprofessional collaborative practice. Interprofessional collaborative practice in health care promotes safe and coordinated care, and ‘occurs when multiple health workers from different professional backgrounds provide comprehensive services by working with patients, their families, carers and communities to deliver the highest quality of care across settings’ [1]. Collaborative decision-making has been described as ‘a litmus test’ [2] or the greatest intensity of interprofessional collaborative practice [3]. These descriptions suggest that sharing information from different disciplinary perspectives to make joint decisions about patient care lies at the core of interprofessional collaborative practice.

Collaborative decision-making is suggested as a core competency to be learnt and assessed in health professions education training programmes globally [4]. A working definition for collaborative decision-making, for the purposes of this paper, is ‘the process by which interprofessional teams effectively work together in partnership with the patient/ family in the process of decision-making in chronic and/or complex care situations and come to agreements that enable optimal health outcomes to be achieved within available resources’ [5]. While this definition establishes scope and intent, it does not specify what constitutes the collaborative ‘process’. As a result, learners and researchers may struggle to identify what collaborative decision-making looks like in practice or how it can be observed, taught, or assessed.

Conceptual ambiguity is further compounded by the interchangeable use of collaborative decision-making and related terms in both education and research. ‘Group decision-making’ and collaborative decision-making are used to refer to interprofessional student groups being tasked to make ethical decisions [6]. ‘Interprofessional decision-making’ and collaborative decision-making are used when medical and pharmacy students are tasked with making medications decisions together [7]. ‘Collaborative clinical reasoning’ appears alongside collaborative decision-making when students are asked to develop integrated care plans [8]. ‘Collaborative problem solving’ is used in conjunction with collaborative decision-making when social work students engage with peers to develop communication and teamwork skills [9]. It is also unclear if collaborative decision-making involves the patient, as is seen in interprofessional shared decision-making [10] or if it a process that only involves health workers. The inconsistent use of these terms may create conceptual ambiguity for researchers and educators. This ambiguity may hinder efforts for teaching and learning collaborative decision-making because educators do not have clarity about its defining characteristics. Research efforts may be hampered by a lack of consensus and the diversity of terms used to describe the same phenomenon.

Further evidence of a lack of clarity about collaborative decision-making and its relationship to related concepts comes from literature review studies and educational frameworks. The term collaborative clinical reasoning was reviewed to determine how it was conceptualised and practised in multidisciplinary teams [11]. The term was found to be applied without consistent definitions or conceptualisations and the boundary between the term collaborative clinical reasoning and collaborative decision-making remains unclear. When interprofessional decision-making processes were examined in a scoping review, the authors concluded that a framework is needed to make visible the values that are common to each profession [12]. However, synonyms for ‘decision-making’ were not included in the search strategy, so the opportunity to address the ambiguity created by several similar terms used to represent decision-making was missed. Similarly, the revised 2023 Interprofessional Education Competencies (IPEC) framework does not include the term collaborative decision-making but instead uses the term ‘team decision-making’ [13]. Team decision-making is grouped together with ‘team-reasoning’ and ‘problem-solving’, however, only team reasoning is defined. For educators and researchers who use the IPEC framework, the term ‘team decision-making’ may be used for situations where decisions are shared among health professional students rather than the term ‘collaborative decision-making’ used elsewhere. Using these terms interchangeably, without explaining what they mean and how they relate to one another will hinder efforts to make explicit how collaborative decision-making is practised, learned, taught, and researched. Given the importance of developing competencies for interprofessional collaborative practice, greater consistency with the terms used to describe collaborative decision-making would help practitioners, students, educators, and researchers.

Therefore, to better understand the ambiguity that arises from the synonyms used for collaborative decision-making, this study aimed to clarify the concept of collaborative decision-making within interprofessional collaborative practice.

Methods

Two methods were used. First, scoping review methodology, guided by the Joanna Briggs Institute Scoping reviews guidelines [14] was used to systematically identify and select relevant literature, and extract data. Second, a concept analysis approach was used to analyse the extracted data and develop a conceptualisation [15]. The concept analysis offered a flexible method to validate and differentiate the abstract concept of collaborative decision-making from similar concepts to inform theory development and enhance communication.

Article identification

The search strategy was developed with advice from an academic librarian and involved four databases: CINHAL (Cumulative Index of Nursing and Allied Health), Medline, Scopus, and ERIC (Education Resources Information Center). These databases were chosen to capture a breadth of results. Searches were restricted to title, abstract, or keywords only for key terms due to the large volume of results when the search was widened to include all text. The search was limited to peer reviewed literature of any study design (e.g. primary research, quantitative, qualitative, scoping review, literature review, conceptual article) published between Jan 2022-Dec 2024. This date range captured current terminology and assumed that seminal research defining or conceptualising the search terms would be referenced. Grey literature was not searched because the interest was in research subjected to peer-review. All search strings contained three components. The first component contained search terms (“collaborative decision-making” OR… ) based on the terms included in the IPEC framework [13] and included team reasoning, team problem-solving, and team decision-making – and other synonyms that the authors had experienced in their reading of the interprofessional collaborative practice and decision-making literature. The research team chose not to include the terms interprofessional collaborative practice (or its synonyms) combined with decision-making (or its synonyms) because of the large volume of results that were captured but not related to the sharing of decisions. Because shared decision-making is the term commonly used to describe decision-making between a health professional and patient and/or family [16], it was excluded unless it was preceded by interprofessional. Interprofessional shared decision-making draws together the concepts of shared decision-making and collaborative clinical reasoning within an interprofessional team [17]. The second component (“interprofessional” OR…) contained terms to ensure the articles related to interactions between health workers from different backgrounds. It was acknowledged that multidisciplinary might also pick up intraprofessional interactions between medical disciplines, but this use of the term could be easily identified and relevant articles excluded. The third component (“patient” AND “health”) contained terms to ensure the articles were related to patient care or health service delivery. An example search string is available to view in Supplementary Table 1.

Article selection

Article selection involved a two-stage process facilitated by literature review software (rayyan.ai). Title and abstract screening were followed by a full text review. In stage one, the first author screened all titles and abstracts using the following criteria. The article had to (1) include a search term in the title, abstract, or keyword; (2) be related to interactions involving two or more health care workers from different backgrounds, and the provision of patient care or health service delivery, and (3) be published in English. An article was excluded if it consisted of an abstract only with no accompanying article such as a conference abstract. When there was uncertainly if an article should be included or excluded, it was retained for full text screening. The full text of each article was screened independently by two authors. The first author screened all articles and the second and third authors each screened half of the articles. In stage two, an article had to include at least one definition, description, or conceptualisation of a search term. Screening results were discussed until the research team reached agreement on the number of articles included for data extraction.

Data extraction

Criteria were developed to extract data from the literature. The research team pilot-tested and revised the data extraction criteria to ensure relevant information that addressed the aim was captured. Members of the research team worked independently to apply the criteria outlined in Table 1 to each article. The first author extracted data from all articles. The second and third authors each extracted data from half of the articles. The extracted data were summarised and are available to view in Supplementary Table 2.

• Descriptive: Articles that were descriptive by nature and did not include a research question, methods, or results section.

Data analysis and conceptualisation

Concept analysis was used to analyse the extracted data [15]. First, how each search term was defined, described, or conceptualised was outlined. Second, attributes used to describe collaborative decision-making were identified to distinguish it from the other search terms. The attributes of collaborative decision-making were thereby identified from the actions described in the definitions, descriptions, and/or conceptualisations of the term. The procedure of identifying defining attributes was repeated for each of the other search terms. Attributes for the other search terms were then compared to identify those in common with collaborative decision-making. Third, cases that were model, borderline, and related were constructed to illustrate the attributes of collaborative decision-making. A model case was constructed using all the defining attributes of collaborative decision-making to illustrate the actions that typify it in interprofessional collaborative practice. A borderline case was constructed using some of the defining attributes of collaborative decision-making to illustrate a process that is similar but not the same. A related case was constructed to illustrate an interprofessional interaction that did not encompass any of the defining attributes of collaborative decision-making to illustrate an example of health professionals and health workers from different backgrounds working in parallel [3]. Fourth, antecedents and consequences were identified to explain circumstances or events that must happen before collaborative decision-making can occur and the subsequent results of a decision-making process, respectively. Antecedents and consequences helped to position collaborative decision-making within a healthcare context. The antecedents were identified from the participants involved in the decision-making process and the conditions that are required for these participants to come together to make a decision. Consequences were identified by the outcomes of the collaborative decision-making process. The procedure of identifying antecedents and consequences was repeated for each of the search terms. Antecedents and consequences were then compared to identify those in common with collaborative decision-making. Fifth, empirical referents of collaborative decision-making were identified. Empirical referents are useful in practice because they can provide observable and measurable behaviours associated with the attributes of a concept. Empirical referents were identified by reviewing articles that described existing methods such as surveys or rating scales that operationalise collaborative decision-making through observable behaviours. These behaviours were then compared to the attributes of collaborative decision-making.

Results

Context

The setting of the articles, if they were stated, were predominantly educational (n = 13) or clinical team meetings (n = 9). The discipline of these clinical team meetings included: cardiology (n = 3), orthopaedics (n = 2), paediatrics (n = 2), intensive care (n = 1) and rehabilitation (n = 1). The remaining articles were situated in broader healthcare settings rather than specific clinical team meetings. The disciplines of these articles included health in general (n = 4), older persons’ health (n = 3), intensive care units (n = 2), primary health care (n = 2), oncology (n = 2), maternal health (n = 1), and paediatrics (n = 1).

Study designs

About one-half of articles had study designs that were either qualitative (n = 11) or hypothesis-driven (n = 8). The remainder were reviews (n = 6), descriptive (n = 5), mixed methods (n = 5), and other (n = 2). The two other studies were study protocols.

Aims

Over one-half of articles had aims that focused directly on collaborative decision-making or one of the other search terms; either to explore it qualitatively, test its effectiveness, or to evaluate a related training intervention (n = 21). The remaining 16 studies examined a search term indirectly, as a secondary aspect of interprofessional collaborative practice, teamwork, or communication processes.

Search terms

Seven of the fourteen search terms were identified in the articles: interprofessional shared decision-making (n = 13), collaborative decision-making (n = 8), team decision-making (n = 7), collaborative clinical reasoning (n = 5), collaborative problem solving (n = 4), group decision=making (n = 4), and interprofessional decision-making (n = 3). Importantly, more than one search term was identified in 10 articles. Nine articles included two of the search terms [7, 8, 19–25] and one article included three [6]. Other related terms and iterations of the search terms were identified and these included: multidisciplinary clinical decision-making [25] interprofessional clinical decision-making [12], collective clinical decision-making [23], and group medical decision-making [21].

Definitions, descriptions, and conceptualisations

Collaborative decision-making

Ten articles use the term collaborative decision-making [6–8, 22, 24–29]. One article defines collaborative decision-making as: ‘a two-way flow of information between parties, deliberation, followed by attainment of a joint decision’ [7]. This definition draws on a conceptual model of clinical decision-making in the intensive care unit [30]. The model presents four levels of decision-making, ranging from decisions made by individual professionals to decisions fully shared across professions. Collaborative decision-making represents the most integrative level, with attainment of a joint decision as an action differentiating it from other levels of decision-making. This model was used to examine how medical and pharmacy students collaborated to make decisions during an online interprofessional learning activity [7].

In the remaining nine articles, the meaning of collaborative decision-making is inferred by how it related to other ideas in the text. Collaborative decision-making is used in clinical contexts to describe a process where healthcare workers from different backgrounds come together to make patient care decisions [25, 26, 28, 29]. For example, it is described as ‘an open team discussion providing balanced speciality views, negotiating grey zones including divergent goals of care, and tailoring treatment that was individualised to the patient’ [29]. When the term collaborative decision-making is used in these contexts it appears to involve health care workers from various backgrounds; however, it was acknowledged that the patient and/or family should be involved, but very rarely happens [28].

Collaborative decision-making is used in education contexts to describe a process where students from different disciplines are asked to complete a task in an interprofessional learning activity [6, 8, 24]. For example, when used in simulated emergency planning scenarios it is described as ‘…shared decisions…[by]… reaching consensus…exchange and assessment of the compulsory information…’ [24].

Collaborative decision-making is also used in articles describing guidelines and tools. For example, chronic pain management clinical practice guideline recommended decision-making which includes ‘incorporating each specialist’s perspectives… [and] come to a consensus regarding modification to the care plan’ [27]. This statement appears under the subheading Shared decision-making and goal setting, leaving it up to the reader to decide if patients are assumed to be part of the process. When collaborative decision-making is used in a survey to assess observable behaviours demonstrated by nursing and medical students in an interprofessional training ward, survey items include: ‘Relevant nursing information present, Relevant medical information present, and Further procedure is planned by the team’ [22]. Collaborative decision-making is presented in conjunction with collaborative clinical reasoning in the tool as ‘Decision-making/ CCR [collaborative clinical reasoning]’ making it difficult to discern, which items pertain to which concept, or if they are viewed as synonymous or interrelated.

Across these articles, collaborative decision-making appears to be used interchangeably with collaborative clinical reasoning [8], group decision-making [6], interprofessional decision-making [7], and team decision-making [24, 25]. Collaborative decision-making also appears to be used in conjunction with collaborative clinical reasoning [6, 8]. The ways that collaborative decision-making is used, defined, and described in these articles suggest it is a dynamic process where different perspectives are brought together, options are deliberated, and negotiation occurs to agree on the best way forward.

Collaborative clinical reasoning

Five articles use the term collaborative clinical reasoning [6, 8, 11, 22, 31]. In a review article, collaborative clinical reasoning is defined as: ‘the process of reaching a shared mental model’ [11]. A conceptualisation of collaborative clinical reasoning is also described in this review article as ‘factors that may influence the performance of CCR [collaborative clinical reasoning]: (1) The initial distribution of information, (2) practitioners’ clinical experience in a team, (3) information exchange among members, and (4) individual retrieval and representation of the information that is shared by a team such as distribution of information or clinical experience’ [32] as cited in [11]. Collaborative clinical reasoning is also defined as: ‘collaboration of two or more health professions in developing a thinking framework regarding clinical problems and their management [31]. This definition is used to explore how collaborative clinical reasoning is used by interprofessional groups of students to create integrated care plans. The remaining three articles also use collaborative clinical reasoning in education contexts [6, 8, 22], however, as previously mentioned, collaborative clinical reasoning appears interchangeably or in conjunction with collaborative decision-making in these articles. Decision-making was also described as occurring after collaborative clinical reasoning in two articles [11, 31]. The ways that collaborative clinical reasoning is used and defined in these articles suggest that collaborative clinical reasoning is a process involving an individual’s own thinking/reasoning process followed by a team-based reasoning process to develop new knowledge and a shared understanding. This process may lead to individual or collaborative decision-making depending on the context.

Collaborative problem solving

Four articles use the term collaborative problem solving [33–36]. In a review article, collaborative problem solving is defined as ‘a process where cognitive and social skills of the TT [translational team] are used to integrate research findings and discipline-grounded interpretations into a cohesive model’ [33]. This definition is built on in the discussion when collaborative problem-solving is defined as ‘combines communication with cognitive problem-solving approaches from its team members to result in a shared interpretation or vision. These definitions draw on work from the field of psychological science [37]. Similarly, collaborative problem-solving is described as: ‘individual cognitive prerequisites and assigns the following key social skills to them: information sharing, negotiating, regulating, and maintaining communication’ [36]. This description is based on a conceptual model developed in an education setting [38]. In this model the term ‘negotiating’ is used to refer specifically to the team’s collaborative knowledge construction, which holds similarities to developing new and shared knowledge as described in collaborative clinical reasoning.

Group decision-making

Four articles use the term group decision-making [6, 19, 21, 23]. Group decision-making is not defined in any of these articles; however, it is used to describe a process occurring in team meetings to make collective decisions about patient care [19, 21]. For example, group decision-making’ is used to describe a process when ‘a group of clinicians with varied and relevant expertise…collectively review clinical information, discuss and formulate decisions on individual patient care…[and their] differing perspectives [allow] the emergence of new ideas, and co-construction of new knowledge’ [21]. Group decision-making processes are examined in cardiology team meetings using the de Groot Critically Reflective Diagnoses Protocol tool [19, 23]. This tool conceptualises group decision-making as a social and reflective process and includes a set of criteria to identify key behaviours that support the decision-making process [39]. Group decision-making appears to be used interchangeably with team decision-making [19, 21, 23] and collaborative decision-making [6]. The ways that group decision-making is used in these articles suggest it is a process used in clinical teams or student groups to reach shared decisions about patient care.

Interprofessional decision-making

Four articles use the term interprofessional decision-making [7, 12, 40, 41]. In a review article, interprofessional decision-making is defined as ‘various alternatives open to the group and their consequences’ and ‘a harmonization of different points of view to develop a common vision of the situation, which is necessary for the development of a shared plan of action’ [12]. When interprofessional decision-making is used to describe a decision-making process occurring in interprofessional discharge planning meetings involving a range of health care professionals it includes: ‘production and distribution of knowledge. citing opinions. joint decision-making. involving the epistemic contributions of different experts’ [41]. Interprofessional decision-making appears to be used interchangeably with collaborative decision-making in one article [7]. The ways that interprofessional decision-making is used in these articles suggest it involves a range of health professionals contributing profession specific expertise to collectively agree on the best way forward.

Interprofessional shared decision-making

Thirteen articles use the term interprofessional shared decision-making [20, 42–53]. Interprofessional shared decision-making is defined a process that:

‘…begins with a recognition of the equipoise, followed by information exchange, examination of patient values and preferences, evaluation of feasibility, determination of the preferred choice, and formulation of a compromise leading to the actual choice’ [43].

This definition is based on a conceptual model that brings together the concepts of shared decision-making and collaborative practice [10]. This conceptual model underpins definitions used in other articles [45, 52] and the model is referred to in a further seven articles [42, 46, 48–51, 53]. The use of interprofessional shared decision-making in these articles primarily centres around implementing and evaluating this conceptual model to improve health service delivery. This conceptual model also appears to underpin an interprofessional shared decision-making questionnaire developed for use in education and clinical settings [44].

In contrast to the conceptual model that represents interprofessional shared decision-making as process involving the patient, interprofessional shared decision-making is described as: ‘a collaborative process among [intensive care unit] clinicians that allows for team involvement in important clinical decisions, considering the best scientific evidence available and the combined expertise of clinicians involved, while incorporating the patient’s values, goals, and preferences’ [47]. This use of interprofessional shared decision-making appears to view decision-making as two separate processes; one process that occurs within the interprofessional team and the other between the patient and team. This term’s use is supported when characteristics of an interprofessional shared decision-making process in a neonatal intensive care setting are divided into ones that encourage interprofessional interactions and ones that describe the role of patients and families in the process [20]. Interprofessional shared decision-making is used interchangeably with team decision-making in this article. The ways that interprofessional shared decision-making is used in these papers suggests it is well conceptualised, although context appears to determine if the patient is a participant in the process or not.

Team decision-making

Seven articles use the term team decision-making [19–21, 23, 24, 54, 55]. Team decision-making is not defined in any of the articles; however, it is used to describe a decision-making process unfolding in team meetings to reach agreement on a way forward. For example, team decision-making is described as team members: ‘contributing multiple perspectives to help thoroughly assess intervention and support options’, and ‘providing their professional input…collaboratively… determin[ing]…a joint response’ [54]. Team decision-making is used interchangeably with collaborative decision-making [24, 25], group decision-making [19, 21, 23], and interprofessional shared decision-making [20]. How these articles use team decision-making suggest it is a team process where health professionals come together, often in team meetings, to share their profession specific knowledge and expertise, and through a process of deliberation and negotiation, come to an agreement on the next steps in patient care.

Defining attributes of collaborative decision-making

Four defining attributes were identified for collaborative decision-making: (1) Information sharing, which includes exchanging profession specific knowledge and perspectives in a back and forward manner to develop new understanding about a patient’s condition. (2) Integrating information, which requires bringing together profession specific knowledge and perspectives to create new and shared knowledge. (3) Deliberating over options, which is a process of weighing up the risks and benefits of various treatment or management strategies. (4) Negotiating differences, which includes coming to an agreement on the best way forward that all participants support. These four attributes were also identified for the terms ‘group decision-making’, ‘interprofessional decision-making’, ‘interprofessional shared decision-making’, and ‘team decision-making’. Across these terms, health workers from varied backgrounds contribute disciplinary expertise, construct new knowledge, discuss options and work toward consensus decisions regarding patient care. In contrast, only two attributes of collaborative decision-making – information sharing and integrating knowledge – were identified for ‘collaborative clinical reasoning’ and ‘collaborative problem solving’. These latter terms emphasise exchanging and integrating information to create new knowledge. Case examples

Examples for model, borderline, and related cases are shown in Table 3. In the model case, the nurse and the dietitian share their profession specific knowledge and integrate this knowledge through a back and forward discussion to create a new understanding of the patient’s condition and social situation. They then deliberate over approaches to provide nutrition care and come to an agreement on the best way forward for the patient and how they can coordinate care. In the borderline case, the nurse and the dietitian share and integrate information to reach a shared understanding of the patient condition and social situation, but do not make decisions about patient care together. However, the absence of deliberation and negotiation over the best way forward for the patient means that a collaborative decision is not made. In the related case, the nurse and dietitian transfer information to each other and this information is used in profession specific care but information is not shared in a back and forward manner and integrated to create new knowledge, nor are patient care options deliberated over or agreed upon. The related case illustrates an interprofessional interaction that is related to, but not the same as, collaborative decision-making.

Examples of Model, Borderline and Related Case to Illustrate the Attributes of Collaborative Decision-Making

Antecedents of collaborative decision-making

The antecedent of a collaborative decision-making process is two or more participants from various health backgrounds (in addition to the patient and/or family/carers when appropriate) who prioritise their time to discuss a patient’s treatment and/or management. The antecedents of collaborative decision-making are the same as the antecedents of interprofessional shared decision-making, because the patient and/or their family may or may not be participants in the process depending on the context (Table 2). In contrast, the use of the terms clinical reasoning, collaborative problem solving, group decision-making, interprofessional decision-making, and team decision-making indicate that they only require two or more participants from various health backgrounds.

Consequences of collaborative decision-making

The consequence of a collaborative decision-making process is agreement on the next steps in patient care. These next steps could relate to treatment or management and be outlined in an integrated care plan [8, 31] or in transfer of care such as in a discharge summary [28, 41]. The consequence of collaborative decision-making appears to be the same as for the consequence of group decision-making, interprofessional decision-making, interprofessional shared decision-making, and team decision-making (Table 2). However, this consequence differs from the consequence of collaborative clinical reasoning and collaborative problem solving because those terms indicate the development a shared understanding, sometimes referred to as a ‘shared mental model’. Depending on the context, these shared understandings may lead to onto collaborative decision-making or may lead to each clinician making their own profession specific decisions about a patient’s care.

Second, the VALUE template [48], developed for use in an intensive care setting, identifies behaviours that aid respectful communication and support interprofessional shared decision-making. Discussion

The concept of collaborative decision-making within interprofessional collaborative practice has been clarified with a proposed conceptualisation for use by educators and researchers. Collaborative decision-making can be conceptualised as:

A dynamic and iterative social and cognitive process involving a two or more participants from different backgrounds (including patients and/or family and/or carers when appropriate) who prioritise their time to discuss and agree on the next steps in patient care. This process involves four core actions: (1) sharing profession specific knowledge and perspectives, (2) integrating knowledge and perspectives to create new and shared knowledge, (3) deliberating over options, and (4) negotiating differences to reach agreement.

This conceptualisation builds on the broad definition of collaborative decision-making by Berger and colleagues [5] by emphasising its social and cognitive dimensions, and by identifying four core dynamic and iterative actions through which the process unfolds. These dimensions are illustrated through the identified empirical referents. The social dimension is reflected in how participants interact, communicate, and manage differences, while the cognitive dimension is reflected in how they reason, integrate knowledge, and evaluate options. This process is dynamic because participants continuously adapt their contributions in response to evolving information and each other’s perspectives, and iterative because the four core actions recur as understanding develops and decisions are progressively refined over time. By making these four core actions explicit, educators can help practitioners and learners to recognise opportunities for participating in collaborative decision-making and researchers can use a shared base for its study.

This conceptualisation can be used to underpins a rationale for how collaborative decision-making compares to other terms used to describe the sharing of decisions in interprofessional collaborative practice. This conceptualisation suggest collaborative decision-making can be viewed as a unifying term for the concept of when decisions are shared within interprofessional collaborative practice.

While collaborative decision-making could be considered a unifying term for the concept of sharing decisions in interprofessional collaborative practice, it does not suggest that those other terms lack value but rather they may be better suited to different contexts. For example, team decision-making may resonate more strongly in settings where a defined group of health professionals work together over time [56] or when decisions are made in team meetings [57]. Group decision-making may be the preferred term when the focus is on processes that are not tied to particular professional identities, such as when groups of students are tasked with making ethical decisions together [6]. Interprofessional decision-making may be more appropriate to use when only health care professionals are involved in the sharing of decisions, and the emphasis is on the diversity of clinical knowledge, such as in intensive care unit settings [58]. As such, sharing of decisions with the emphasis on diversity is commonly referred to as interprofessional clinical decision-making [12]. Whereas interprofessional shared decision-making, may be more appropriate for when the patient, or family members are present and an integral part of the decision-making process, as is described in the well conceptualised model of interprofessional shared decision-making [10]. By clarifying the specific use of terms related to collaborative decision-making, educators and researchers can chose the term that best fits their setting while still recognised the shared conceptual base they draw upon.

This conceptualisation relates collaborative decision-making closely to collaborative clinical reasoning and collaborative problem solving. For example, sometimes collaborative clinical reasoning will occur to create a shared mental model as was seen in the borderline cases and inform professions specific decision-making and practice [3], whereas sometimes it may lead to deliberating and negotiating over options and therefore be viewed as part of a collaborative decision-making process. This relationship is supported by evidence that collaborative decision-making is often used in conjunction with collaborative clinical reasoning [6, 8] suggesting they often happen in practice together. This finding is also supported in the clinical decision-making and clinical reasoning literature when clinical reasoning is described as a cognitive process that precedes decision-making [59] or when clinical decision-making is viewed as an umbrella concept that is multidimensional bringing together reasoning, problem-solving, and making choices about patient care [60]. Understanding this close relationship helps clarify how reasoning and decisioning processes interact in practice, supporting more informed teaching and research in interprofessional settings.

This conceptualisation offers educators a language and a framework for making collaborative decision-making more visible to students. By naming and defining the core actions, educators will be able to help students discern collaborative decision-making from other forms of clinical practice that may involve other health professionals but do not involve the sharing of decisions [3] and assist them in identifying conditions that enable or hinder each of the four core actions. A framework based on this conceptualisation may also empower educators and students to consider how they can move beyond observing collaborative decision-making from the periphery to being actively involved in the process. For example, educators may be able to identify different parts of the process and see where students can take a more central role. This shift from observing at the periphery to more central involvement, aligns with workplace learning theory, which emphasises that students develop competence by gradually engaging in authentic practice. Educators may also be able to sensitive students to the range of terms or synonyms used to refer to the sharing of decisions by emphasising the core actions.

For researchers, this conceptualisation provides an important step towards greater clarity about the terms and language used to describe the sharing of decisions in interprofessional collaborative practice and how their research fits in with the researchers of others [61]. This conceptualisation will also allow researchers to investigate collaborative decision-making specifically, whereas in the past it may have been conflated with broader concepts such as teamwork, interprofessional communication, or interprofessional collaborative practice [40, 62]. By defining its actions, researchers may be able to better understand how health professionals and health professional students engage with collaborative decision-making and what shapes their participation. This conceptualisation can also be used by others who are developing tools to support the assessment of collaborative decision-making [63], thus contributing to advancing the assessment of collaborative decision-making as a core competency [4].

Strengths, limitations and future directions

Combining concept analysis with a scoping review helped to overcome the limitations of each method, particularly as they pertain to concept development. Concept analysis provided a structured approach to analysing data aimed at concept clarification, whereas the scoping review provided a systematic and rigorous methodology to identify and select relevant articles. Some definitions, however, may not have been identified by the search because grey literature was omitted. Furthermore, because the interest was in current definitions and uses, the search was limited to January 2022-December 2024. This restriction may have omitted seminal articles defining collaborative decision-making or related terms. However, this omission was countered by reading and referring to the original references of definitions and conceptualisations cited in the selected literature. The proposed conceptualisation of collaborative decision-making is the output of one possible interpretation of the data. A strength of using this interpretive method is an acknowledgement of the cultural, contextual, and societal factors that contributes to the evolution of the conceptualisation [15] and use of collaborative decision-making. Consequently, this work builds a contemporary conceptualisation of collaborative decision-making that can be promoted among educators and researcher.

Potential future directions for this work includes using this conceptualisation of collaborative decision-making to examine how students are learning collaborative decision-making in interprofessional learning activities, how health professionals learn to work together in clinical practice, and how clinical educators might be able to support this learning. Further exploration of how collaborative decision-making plays out in different of types of interprofessional activities [56] or different types of teams [64] may yield insights about the impact of context on collaborative decision-making. For example, collaborative decision-making may look different in an intensive care unit team where team members are used to working with each other and decisions are made frequently and in person compared to when collaborative decision-making occurs between health professionals from different services who work in different locations and may have different priorities [65]. Further work is also needed to understand the role of social and material influences on decision-making processes [58]. For example, the role that monitoring devices or electronic health care records might have on how information is shared and integrated when making a collaborative decision about patient care.

Conclusions

Collaborative decision-making is a widely used term in interprofessional collaborative practice, yet it is often undefined or used inconsistently with other terms used to describe that decisions related to patient care are shared. A concept analysis approach using scoping review methodology produced a contemporary conceptualisation of collaborative decision-making. Four core actions are involved in this dynamic and iterative process of reaching agreement about the next steps in patient care. These core actions are shared among related terms drawing on a common conceptual base. This conceptualisation can be used by professionals to audit their interprofessional collaborative practices, by educators as they consider show to support students to learn collaborative decision-making, and by researchers to improve communication about their research.
