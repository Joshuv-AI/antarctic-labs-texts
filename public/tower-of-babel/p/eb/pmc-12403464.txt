Prevalence of cardiovascular disease risk factors in ethnic minority adults in plateau areas: a cross-sectional study in Yunnan Province
Authors: Wenlong Zhu, Nan Zhang, Yu Xia, Mingjing Tang, Lin Duo, Zhiling Luo, Xiangbin Pan, Da Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aim of this study was to assess the prevalence and clustering of cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk factors among ethnic minorities in plateau areas and their association with sociodemographic characteristics.MethodsFrom September 2023 to January 2024, data were collected among ethnic minorities in plateau areas of Yunnan Province through face-to-face interviews. Ordered logistic regression and restricted cubic splines were used to assess the association between sociodemographic characteristics and the risk of clustering of risk factors.ResultsA total of 2457 participants with a median age of 56.00 years were investigated. Insufficient vegetable and fruit intake (42.21%) was the most prevalent behavioral risk factor, followed by current smoking (26.62%). Hypertension (44.24%) was the most prevalent metabolic risk factor, followed by overweight/obesity (39.32%). Among participants, 44.36% had one behavioral risk factor, 21.69% had two, and 4.84% had three or more. The prevalence of participants having 1, 2, 3 or ≥ 4 metabolic risk factors was 29.55%, 21.86%, 15.83% and 5.54%, respectively. The clustering of behavioral risk factors was more prominent among participants aged 65–75, males, Tibetans, illiterates, and rural residents, while the risk of metabolic risk factor clustering was greater in older individuals, those with a high school education or higher, urban residents, and those with higher household incomes.ConclusionsCVD risk factors are highly prevalent and clustered among ethnic minorities in plateau areas of Yunnan Province, displaying heterogeneity across sociodemographic characteristics. Interventions targeting these risk factors may reduce the CVD burden of ethnic minorities in the plateau areas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-23864-0.

Background

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of death worldwide and a major contributor to reduced quality of life and a significant economic burden [1, 2]. The global age-standardized prevalence of CVD was estimated to be 7.24% in 2021, with a mortality rate of 0.25% [2]. The study by Chong et al. estimated a 90.0% increase in CVD prevalence and a 73.4% increase in crude mortality globally between 2025 and 2050 [3]. Many risk factors of CVD, including behavioral risk factors (smoking, alcohol intake, insufficient vegetable and fruit intake, etc.) and metabolic risk factors (diabetes, hypertension, obesity, hypercholesterolemia, etc.) are targetable for successful intervention and management, making most cases of premature CVD preventable [4, 5]. Therefore, early identification of individuals with risk factors of CVD and implementation of targeted interventions are essential to reduce preventable CVD mortality.

The prevalence of CVD in China continues to rise, with the number of existing CVD patients reaching 330 million in 2022, making CVD the leading cause of death in China [6]. The prevalence of CVD risk factors in China is also on the rise due to rapid economic growth and lifestyle changes [7, 8]. A nationwide, population-based screening project conducted between 2015 and 2019 revealed that the prevalence of hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, diabetes, overweight/obesity, current smoking, current alcohol intake, insufficient fruit intake, and insufficient vegetable intake among Chinese adults were 41.3%, 31.0%, 17.0%, 44.1%, 20.0%, 7.8%, 50.1%, and 17.5% respectively [9]. Compared with a single risk factor, a combination of risk factors significantly increases the incidence of cardiovascular disease [7]. A national cross-sectional survey revealed that among Chinese adults the prevalence of having ≥ 1, ≥ 2, and ≥ 3 risk factors of CVD was 70.3%, 40.3%, and 16.7% respectively [10].

Although extensive data have been accumulated from national-level CVD research, China's vast territory results in significant heterogeneity in population health status. The Qinghai-Xizang Plateau, the highest plateau in the world, is primarily situated in western China and is home to various ethnic minorities, including the Tibetans and Naxi people. Epidemiological surveys revealed an"altitude gradient effect"on the prevalence of CVD in high-altitude regions, with increasing altitude being associated with a heightened risk of coronary artery disease and myocardial ischemia [11, 12]. Previous studies have shown that the prevalence of CVD risk factors varies by region, altitude, ethnicity, sex, age, and education attainment [9, 10]. A meta-analysis indicated that, compared to those living in the plains, Tibetan adults residing on the Qinghai-Xizang Plateau have a higher prevalence of hypertension, yet a lower prevalence of diabetes [13]. Another study also suggested that dyslipidemia was more prevalent, and obesity and diabetes were less prevalent at higher altitudes [14]. However, few studies have analyzed the clustering of metabolic risk factors and the prevalence of behavioral risk factors among ethnic minority groups in plateau areas. Investigating and analyzing the risk factors for CVD in plateau regions could provide the data necessary to control the prevalence of CVD and its associated risk factors in these areas, thereby promoting health equity.

Yunnan Province is located on the Yunnan-Guizhou Plateau in southwestern China, as the transition zone from the Qinghai-Xizang Plateau to low altitude areas, where 25 ethnic minorities live. The unique geographical environment and cultural characteristics of the plateau areas of Yunnan Province significantly affect residents'cardiovascular health. The hypoxic environment causes an increase in red blood cells and thick blood, and the combination of a high-salt, high-fat diet and high-intensity labor promote the prevalence and clustering of cardiovascular diseases and their risk factors [15, 16]. In order to inform the development of targeted interventions to prevent CVD risk factors and reduce the burden of CVD among ethnic minorities, this study sought to determine the prevalence and clustering pattern of behavioral and metabolic risk factors for CVD among ethnic minorities in the plateau areas and to assess the sociodemographic factors associated with the clustering of behavioral and metabolic risk factors through a cross-sectional study conducted in Yunnan Province.

Methods

Study sites and sampling

This cross-sectional study was carried out between September 2023 and January 2024 in Yunnan Province, which has a mountainous plateau topography. Multi-stage stratified cluster sampling was used to select participants for the survey. All townships/streets at an altitude of 2500 m and above were included and divided into an urban group and a rural group. The simple random sampling method was used to select two townships/streets in the urban/rural group. Four villages/communities in each township/street were selected using the simple random sampling method. Sixteen villages/communities in Lijiang and Shangri-La were selected as the study areas, as these regions are populated and easily accessible, making it convenient to carry out the survey. Residents who had lived in the study areas for more than 6 months and were aged 35 years and above were included in the survey. Based on the sampling procedure described above and the prevalence of hypertension in Yunnan Province (38.5%) [17], we estimated that 2560 participants would be needed for the study. A total of 2560 participants took part in the study, of whom 84 did not pass quality control, and 19 were missing data on blood pressure, fasting blood glucose (FBG), or total cholesterol (TC). All participants completed a previously validated questionnaire (Li et al. [9]) in face-to-face interviews. The structured questionnaire included the following parts:sociodemographic: age, sex, ethnicity, education status, and annual per capita household income;behavior and diet: smoking status, alcohol consumption, vegetables and fruits consumption;comorbidities: time of diagnosis, medication, and family history of comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, and hypercholesterolemia.

This study assessed participants'smoking status in the past month (never smoked, smoked but not every day, or smoked every day). For alcohol consumption, this study assessed the frequency of alcohol consumption (no alcohol, alcohol consumption less than once a month, or at least once a month/week/daily), duration, type of alcohol, and amount of alcohol consumed per occasion. Participants were also asked how often they consumed fruit and vegetables in the past month (1–3 days per month, 1–3 days per week, 4–6 days per week, or daily). The height of the participants was measured by the BYHJ02 Height Scale (Zhongshan Beiya Electronics Co., Ltd, Guangdong, China), body weight was assessed with the InBody H20B Body Composition Analyzer (Biospace Medical Trading Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China). Blood pressure of participants was measured by Omron HBP-1300 Professional Portable Blood Pressure Monitors (Omron Healthcare Co., Ltd, Kyoto, Japan). Venous blood samples were taken from the participants after fasting for at least 8 h. FBG and TC were measured at the Beckman Coulter AU680 (Beckman Coulter, Inc., Brea, USA). Prior to use, all equipment was calibrated in accordance with the manufacturer's specifications.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fuwai Yunnan Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Grant number: 2023–026-01) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (6th revision, 2008), and electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Statistical analysis

The continuous variable age was presented as a median (25th percentile (P25), 75th percentile (P75)) and was compared using the Kruskal–Wallis rank sum test. Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages and compared using the chi-squared test or the Fisher exact test. The Bonferroni method was used to correct for the test level in the post hoc test. Clustering patterns of the behavioral and metabolic risk factors were presented as UpSet plots using the ComplexUpset package [24]. Because the clustering of behavioral and metabolic risk factors was ordered categorical variables, the association of sociodemographic factors with the presence of one or more risk factors in an individual was estimated as adjusted odds ratio (aOR) and 95% confidence interval (95% CI) and calculated using ordered logistic regression [25]. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) with three, four, and five knots were used to flexibly model the association between age and the presence of one or more risk factors. The RCS model with the smallest Akaike information criterion (AIC), i.e., the model with three nodes at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles, was selected (Table S1). The reference values (OR = 1) were set at 45 years for behavioral risk factors and 70 years for metabolic risk factors. RCS models were adjusted for sex, ethnicity, educational status, place of residence, and annual per capita household income.

All statistical analyses were performed using the IBM SPSS 26.0 statistical software and the R 4.4.1 software (https://www.r-project.org/). Statistical tests were two-sided, and a p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Factors with an aOR greater than 1 and a p value less than 0.05 were considered to play a significant role in promoting the clustering of risk factors.

Results

The cross-sectional study included 2457 participants aged 35 years and above with a median age of 56.00 (25th percentile (P25), 75th percentile (P75): 49.00, 63.00) years (Table 2). Among them, 43.02% were males, 27.43% were illiterate, 41.55% lived in rural areas, and 75.25% had an annual per capita household income of less than 21,000 yuan. Among all participants, 43.63% were Naxi minority, 42.49% were Tibetans, and only 13.88% were Han and other ethnic minorities. There were significant differences in the demographic characteristics of the three ethnic groups (p < 0.001). Prevalence of CVD risk factors

For behavioral risk factors, the prevalence of insufficient vegetable and fruit intake was the highest (42.21%), followed by current smoking (26.62%), insufficient sleep time (23.77%), and current alcohol intake (10.09%) (Fig. 2A). Except for current alcohol intake, there were significant differences in other behavioral risk factors between age groups (p < 0.05). The prevalence of insufficient vegetable and fruit intake and insufficient sleep time increased with age, and the prevalence of current smoking decreased with age (p for trend < 0.05, Table S2). Higher prevalence of current smoking (58.75% vs 2.36%, p < 0.001) and current alcohol intake (19.87% vs 2.71%, p < 0.001) but lower prevalence of insufficient sleep time (20.06% vs 26.57%, p < 0.001) among males compared to females. Notably, there were significant differences in the prevalence of behavioral risk factors among different ethnic groups (p < 0.001). Tibetans had the highest prevalence of insufficient fruit and vegetable intake (52.59%, p < 0.001) and insufficient sleep time (29.79%, p < 0.001), and had the lowest prevalence of current smoking (21.36%, p < 0.001) and current alcohol intake (6.23%, p < 0.001). Higher prevalence of current smoking and current alcohol intake was observed in participants with higher educational levels (p < 0.001). Rural residents exhibited higher prevalence of insufficient vegetable and fruit intake (55.43% vs 32.86%, p < 0.001), insufficient sleep time (27.03% vs 21.45%, p < 0.001), and current alcohol intake (12.93% vs 8.08%, p < 0.001), while urban residents had higher prevalence of current smoking (22.23% vs 29.74%, p < 0.001). Participants with higher annual per capita household income reported a higher prevalence of current smoking (25.58% vs 29.77%, p = 0.043) and a lower prevalence of insufficient sleep time (24.93% vs 20.23%, p = 0.018).Fig. 2Prevalence of behavioral (A) and metabolic (B) risk factors for cardiovascular disease among the population aged 35 years and above in the plateau areas of Yunnan Province

Prevalence of behavioral (A) and metabolic (B) risk factors for cardiovascular disease among the population aged 35 years and above in the plateau areas of Yunnan Province

For metabolic risk factors, the prevalence of hypertension was the highest (44.24%), followed by overweight/obesity (39.32%), central obesity (35.72%), hypercholesterolemia (17.11%), and diabetes (8.01%) (Fig. 2B). The prevalence of these metabolic risk factors showed an upward trend with increasing age (p for trend < 0.05, Table S3). Compared with females, males had a significantly higher prevalence of hypertension (48.91% vs 40.71%, p < 0.001) and diabetes (10.39% vs 6.19%, p < 0.001), and a significantly lower prevalence of central obesity (31.27% vs 39.08%, p < 0.001). Besides hypertension (p = 0.223) and diabetes (p = 0.620), there were significant differences in the prevalence of metabolic risk factors among different ethnic groups (p < 0.001). Tibetans had the highest prevalence of overweight/obesity (44.76%, p < 0.001) and central obesity (40.13%, p < 0.001), and the lowest prevalence of hypercholesterolemia (12.34%, p < 0.001). A higher prevalence of overweight/obesity (p < 0.001), hypercholesterolemia (p = 0.006), and diabetes (p = 0.005) was observed in participants with higher education attainments. The prevalence of overweight/obesity (34.19% vs 42.99%, p < 0.001), central obesity (29.36% vs 40.24%, p < 0.001), hypercholesterolemia (12.29% vs 20.53%, p < 0.001), and diabetes (5.24% vs 9.97%, p < 0.001) were significantly higher in urban residents than in rural residents. There was a higher prevalence of overweight/obesity (36.78% vs 41.02%, p < 0.001) and central obesity (33.93% vs 41.12%, p = 0.001) in participants with higher annual per capita household income.

Clustering of CVD risk factors

29.10% of participants had no behavioral risk factors for CVD (Fig. 3A1). The prevalence of having one, two, and three or more behavioral risk factors was 44.36%, 21.69%, and 4.84%, respectively. Among the participants with two behavioral risk factors, the prevalence of the combination of insufficient vegetable and fruit intake and insufficient sleep time was the highest, followed by the combination of insufficient intake of fruits and vegetables and current smoking. Among those with three behavioral risk factors, the combination of insufficient vegetable and fruit intake, current smoking, and current alcohol intake was the most common (Fig. 3A2). The prevalence of participants having 0, 1, 2, 3, or ≥ 4 metabolic risk factors was 27.23%, 29.55%, 21.86%, 15.83%, and 5.54%, respectively (Fig. 3B1). Discussion

This study assessed the prevalence of behavioral and metabolic risk factors for CVD among ethnic minority adults aged 35 years and above in the plateau areas. Compared with the results of a nationwide screening project [9], this study found a higher prevalence of current smoking (26.62% vs 20.0%), current alcohol intake (10.09% vs 7.8%), and hypertension (44.24% vs 41.3%), but a lower prevalence of overweight/obesity (39.32% vs 44.1%) and diabetes (8.01 vs 17.0%). Compared with a study conducted in the plain areas of Yunnan Province, the prevalence of smoking, central obesity, and hypercholesterolemia was higher in this study, and the prevalence of the other risk factors was similar [26]. Survey location, altitude, and population characteristics may explain these differences. Research has shown that the prevalence of overweight/obesity, diabetes, and metabolic syndrome is negatively associated with altitude [27–29]. The lower level of urbanization in high-altitude areas may contribute to a reduction in the incidence and prevalence of these diseases [29]. Additionally, the hypoxic environment at high altitudes may reduce the incidence of obesity and diabetes through mechanisms such as promoting negative energy balance, increasing insulin sensitivity, and reducing gut microbiota alpha diversity [29, 30]. High altitude may increase the prevalence of hypertension [15], resulting in a higher prevalence of hypertension in this study than the national level. Tibetans have a special local diet of tsampa, butter tea, beef, and mutton, which contains high salts and cholesterol [16, 31]. The diet of the Naxi minority is characterized by an excessive intake of fat and carbohydrates [32]. These unique lifestyles of ethnic minorities may also result in a higher prevalence of insufficient vegetable and fruit intake and hypertension. Nutrition knowledge and education should be strengthened to help ethnic minorities in plateau areas adopt good dietary habits, to further reduce the prevalence of CVD risk factors and the burden of CVD.

The prevalence of CVD risk factors varies among populations with different sociodemographic characteristics. The prevalence of current smoking, current alcohol intake, hypertension, and diabetes was higher among males, and the prevalence of central obesity and insufficient sleep time was higher among females. These may relate to gender differences in diet, lifestyle, occupation, and family responsibilities [10, 33]. For example, studies have shown that sleep deprivation in women is associated with an increased prevalence of central obesity [33, 34]. As smoking and alcohol intake were risk factors for hypertension and diabetes [35], high rates of these behaviors among males may contribute to the high prevalence of these conditions in this group. The results of this study showed that the prevalence of smoking and overweight/obesity was higher among those with higher levels of education and those with a high annual household income. Generally, individuals with higher levels of education tend to have higher incomes [36]. The high prevalence of smoking among highly educated and high-income individuals may be related to their experiencing greater work pressures. Additionally, those with higher levels of education and income had more opportunities to participate in social events, eat out more often, and intake more high-fat and energy-dense foods [37], which may partly explain the high prevalence of overweight/obesity in these groups. Overweight/obesity increases the risk of hypercholesterolemia and diabetes, leading to a higher prevalence of these diseases among highly educated individuals. In agreement with previous studies [4, 38], this study found a higher prevalence of behavioral risk factors and a lower prevalence of metabolic risk factors in rural areas. In addition to diet, differences in physical activity and economic level also influenced the differences in CVD risk factor prevalence between urban and rural areas [38, 39]. The convenience of transportation systems and sedentary work have led to a decline in the physical activity levels of urban dwellers. Furthermore, urban dwellers have easier access to processed foods and high-protein, high-fat diets [39]. These factors may result in a higher prevalence of metabolic diseases, such as overweight/obesity, hypercholesterolemia, and diabetes, among urban dwellers.

The study found that 26.53% of the population had two or more behavioral risk factors, with the cluster pattern being dominated by the combination of insufficient vegetables and fruit intake, current smoking, and insufficient sleep time; and the risk of behavioral risk factors clustering was higher among population aged 65–75 years, males, Tibetans, illiterates, and rural residents. There was no nonlinear relationship between age and behavioral risk factors clustering, but the risk of behavioral risk factors clustering increased with advancing age in the population aged 45 years and above. 43.23% of the population had two or more metabolic risk factors, with a cluster pattern dominated by the combination of overweight/obesity, central obesity, and hypertension. Participants with increasing age, high school education and above, in urban areas, and with higher household incomes had a higher risk of metabolic risk factors clustering. There was a nonlinear increasing relationship between the risk of metabolic risk factors clustering and age. These results might inform the priorities and target populations for the implementation of healthy lifestyle campaigns [40]. To reduce the prevalence and clustering of behavioral risk factors in ethnic minorities in the plateau areas, efforts should be intensified to promote healthy lifestyles among the population under 45 years. Promoting healthy lifestyles could be achieved through enhancing community-based nutritional education and implementing smoking cessation campaigns. Providing subsidies for fruits and vegetables could encourage people to consume more of these fresh products. Furthermore, measures such as restricting smoking in public places and increasing tobacco taxes could help to reduce smoking rates. It is essential to enhance the awareness of weight management among ethnic minorities in plateau areas and to encourage communities to provide basic fitness facilities and resources to reduce the prevalence of overweight/obesity. In plateau areas, amplifying metabolic diseases education, training primary-care clinicians, and allocating health resources contribute to improving the management of metabolic risk factors.

This study investigated the prevalence and clustering of behavioral and metabolic risk factors for CVD among ethnic minorities in the plateau areas, which may inform policy formulation by government agencies. However, this study has several limitations. First, this cross-sectional study assessed the prevalence of CVD risk factors, however, it poses challenges in analyzing causal relationships, such as those between behavioral and metabolic risk factors. Second, reporting bias and recall bias could not be avoided as the information gathered through the questionnaire was predominantly self-reported. Information related to lifestyle behaviors, such as smoking, alcohol intake, and sleep duration, may be susceptible to these biases, which might lead to an underestimation of prevalence rates. Third, due to considerations of survey feasibility, this study did not collect data on food consumption quantities, which may result in an underestimation of the prevalence of insufficient vegetable and fruit intake. It is necessary to conduct more detailed dietary surveys in future research to explore the dietary patterns of ethnic minorities in plateau regions. Last, the population under investigation in this study was mainly of Tibetan and Naxi individuals from the plateau areas in Yunnan Province, and the interpretation of the findings must take this limitation into account. Future research could include ethnic minorities at different altitudes, or Han Chinese populations residing in plateau areas, to further analyze the association between altitude and ethnicity on the prevalence and clustering of CVD risk factors.

Conclusions

In conclusion, ethnic minorities in the plateau areas of Yunnan Province had a higher prevalence of insufficient vegetable and fruit intake, current smoking, hypertension, and overweight/obesity, and a higher prevalence of clustering of these risk factors. The risk of behavioral risk factor clustering was higher for older age, males, Tibetans, illiterates, and rural residents; and the risk of metabolic risk factor clustering was higher for older age, urban areas, and higher household income. These findings emphasize the need to promote healthy lifestyles and dietary habits, as well as to strengthen the management of metabolic diseases among ethnic minorities in plateau areas, to reduce the prevalence of CVD risk factors and alleviate the burden of CVD.
