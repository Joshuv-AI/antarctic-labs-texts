ABSTRACT
IntroductionHypertension-mediated organ damage (HMOD) is well established in the heart, kidneys, and vasculature; however, early markers of cerebral involvement remain poorly defined.AimThis multicenter, cross-sectional Brazilian study investigated impaired intracranial compliance (ICC) across hypertension (HT) stages in adults without neurological disease or antihypertensive therapy, aiming to characterize early cerebral hemodynamic changes potentially related to hypertension-mediated brain organ damage.MethodsICC was assessed noninvasively from intracranial pressure (ICP) waveforms using the P2/P1 ratio and time-to-peak (TTP).ResultsA total of 252 participants were included (52.4% men, 87.7% < 65 years). Mean P2/P1 and TTP values were 1.32 and 0.25, respectively. Significant differences were found between prehypertension and stage 2 HT (p < 0.001) and between stage 1 and 2 (p < 0.05) for P2/P1, as well as between prehypertension and stage 2 for TTP (p < 0.05). Introduction

Why do we still lack a clinical biomarker of early brain damage in hypertension? Hypertension (HT) is the leading cause of mortality worldwide [1] and one of the primary contributors to deaths from cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases [2, 3]. In recent years, cumulative evidence has shown that chronically elevated blood pressure (BP) is associated with structural and functional changes in target organs, particularly the brain [4–6].

While HMOD is routinely evaluated in the heart, kidneys, and large arteries; early markers of cerebral involvement remain limited, despite the brain being a major target of long-term BP exposure. From a pathophysiological perspective, HMOD develops progressively with the magnitude and duration of HT, reinforcing the importance of identifying early, potentially reversible cerebral alterations [7]. Functional measures of cerebral hemodynamic adaptation, such as intracranial compliance (ICC), may therefore represent a promising approach to detect early brain involvement before irreversible injury occurs.

In addition to overt symptoms such as stroke, recent studies suggest that HT induces silent cerebrovascular changes representing the pathophysiological substrate of future stroke and long-term cognitive dysfunction and dementia [2, 8, 9]. These subclinical alterations may precede overt clinical manifestations by several years and, if identified, may provide opportunities for early detection and timely preventive intervention. However, the pathophysiological mechanisms by which elevated BP affects the brain are not yet fully understood. Autonomic imbalance, activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, loss of cerebral autoregulation (CA), and disruption of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) have been recently proposed as potential contributors [9, 10]. HT-induced changes in cerebral blood flow (CBF) may influence intracranial pressure (ICP), leading to reduced intracranial compliance (ICC) and potentially resulting in the development of intracranial hypertension (ICH). This may occur via increased arterial blood volume, vascular leakage, and cerebral edema, which can progressively reduce CBF, further aggravate edema, and create a vicious cycle of worsening cerebral dysfunction [11–14].

In this context, the identification of biomarkers capable of detecting early brain changes related to HT is clinically relevant. The investigation of this association has been facilitated by noninvasive methods analyzing ICP behavior. Based on the morphology of the ICC waveform, parameters such as the ratio between peaks P2 and P1 (P2/P1) and time-to-peak (TTP) have been used as indirect markers of ICC [15–18]. ICC reflects the ability of the cranial system to accommodate intracranial volume changes without significantly increasing ICP [19], being a critical indicator of the brain’s capacity to adapt to such changes. As a functional parameter, ICC may capture early disturbances in cerebral hemodynamic adaptation before the onset of overt neurological injury [4, 15]. However, ICC may be compromised in pathological conditions such as HT [20]. These parameters allow the evaluation of the efficiency of this compensatory mechanism and provide information on how BP may affect cerebral hemodynamics even in individuals without established neurological disorders.

Previous studies have demonstrated that hypertensive individuals may show altered ICC parameters, even in the absence of neurological disorders [21, 22]. Importantly, most of these studies evaluated patients with long-term HT, often under pharmacological treatment, limiting the understanding of when ICC alterations first emerge in the natural history of BP elevation. Although cerebral regulatory mechanisms may protect the brain in the early stages of HT [23–25], the degree to which these mechanisms remain effective and the point at which they begin to fail remain unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between different stages of HT and ICC parameters (P2/P1 and TTP) in adults without neurological disorders and not receiving antihypertensive medication, to characterize early cerebral hemodynamic changes potentially related to hypertension-mediated brain damage.

Methods

Study design

Cross-sectional, analytical, multicenter study, with ICC as the primary outcome in patients with different levels of elevated BP. The study was designed and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and followed Resolution no. 466/2012 of the Brazilian National Health Council. Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committees (REC) of the following institutions: Federal University of Goiás (approval no. 5.339.225 and 6.602.319); Pedro Ernesto University Hospital, University of Rio de Janeiro (UERJ), approval no. 7.526.174; University of Pernambuco (NICAP), approval no. 6.083.072; Irmandade da Santa Casa de Misericórdia de Porto Alegre (ISCMPA), approval no. 5.681.932; and Educational Foundation Jayme de Alta Vila/University Center, approval no. 5.549.745. All participants provided written informed consent before inclusion. This study was registered in the Brazilian Registry of Clinical Trials (RBR-5h33pjp) and in the Universal Trial Number (UTN U1111-1304-6626).

Population

Data collection was conducted between November 2022 and December 2024 at the following centers: Hypertension Unit of the Federal University of Goiás; CESMAC University Center of Hospital do Coração de Maceió; Rio de Janeiro State University; Hypertension and Preventive Cardiology Unit of Pernambuco; Hypertension and Cardiometabolic Disorders Service of Hospital São Francisco of Santa Casa de Porto Alegre; and Miocure Cardiologia Ltd., in Cruzeiro do Sul, Santa Catarina. The inclusion criteria were: individuals ≥ 18 years of age, classified as having prehypertension (systolic BP 130–139 mmHg and/or diastolic BP 85–89 mmHg) or hypertension (systolic BP ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic BP ≥ 90 mmHg), based on office BP measurements. The exclusion criteria included the use of antihypertensive medication; stage 3 HT (systolic BP ≥ 180 mmHg and/or diastolic BP ≥ 110 mmHg); pregnancy or postpartum status; diagnosis of ICH or hydrocephalus; or history of cardiovascular disease, defined as coronary artery disease (angina, previous myocardial infarction), heart failure, valvular heart disease, cardiac arrhythmias, obstructive peripheral artery disease, aortic aneurysm, previous stroke, or advanced chronic liver disease (Child-Pugh class B or C).

Procedures

All study procedures were conducted on the same day, following informed consent. Demographic and clinical data were collected using a self-reported questionnaire completed by participants, including age, sex, weight, height, family medical history, comorbidities (hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes), smoking status, physical activity level, and medication use.

Office BP measurement

BP was measured in the office using an automatic device (OMRON 1100) before ICC assessment. Measurements were taken with the participant seated, feet flat on the floor, arm supported at heart level, and palm facing upward. Participants were instructed to empty their bladder and to avoid moderate or vigorous physical activity, smoking, drinking alcohol, or consuming coffee for at least 30 min prior. BP was measured three times at 2-min intervals. The first measurement was discarded, and the mean of the last two was used for classification. Patients were included in the study immediately after the diagnosis of hypertension or prehypertension, prior to the initiation of lifestyle modifications or pharmacological treatment.

ICC evaluation

ICC was assessed non-invasively with the Brain4care® system (Brain4care, São Carlos, Brazil). Measurements were obtained in the supine position (0°) for 7 min, excluding the first and last minutes. The Brain4care® sensor, externally attached to the scalp, detects nanometric skull deformations that reflect the morphological characteristics of ICP waves associated with each cardiac cycle. This method does not require individual calibration and has been validated in comparison to invasive ICP monitoring [15–17]. Continuous waveforms were recorded and underwent digital filtering and preprocessing, including beat-to-beat segmentation and artifact rejection. Stable recording periods of at least 5 min were analyzed. From the averaged ICP waveforms, two validated parameters were computed using the proprietary Brain4care® algorithm: [1] the P2/P1 ratio, defined as the amplitude ratio between the P2 wave (associated with ICC and ICP) and the P1 wave (resulting from systolic CBF transmission); and [2] the time-to-peak (TTP, in seconds), defined as the normalized time from the onset of the waveform to the pulse peak (with all pulses rescaled to one second to eliminate heart rate influence). In contrast to invasive ICP monitoring, which defines ICH as sustained ICP > 20 mmHg for more than 5 min, the Brain4care® system defines ICH risk as P2/P1 ≥ 1.4 and/or TTP ≥ 0.3 s [18].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistical analyses were performed. Continuous variables were assumed to follow a normal distribution due to the sample size, in line with the central limit theorem. Outliers were retained. The data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with median and interquartile range (IQR) also reported. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) and t-tests, along with the Tukey’s and Bonferroni post hoc tests, were used to compare P2/P1 and TTP across different HT stages. Multiple linear regression models were used to analyze the outcomes P2/P1 and TTP and the explanatory variables age, sex, and HT stage. The Jonckheere-Terpstra test was used to assess increasing trends in P2/P1 and TTP parameters across HT stages. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The data were entered into the REDCap platform, and analyses were performed using the R software (version 4.3.2), including a graphical representation.

Discussion

This study evaluated the ICC waveform and compared the P2/P1 and TTP parameters across different HT stages in adults without neurological disorders and not receiving antihypertensive medication. The results demonstrated differences in these parameters across HT stages, suggesting that ICC becomes progressively compromised with increasing BP values. Taken together, these findings support the concept that alterations in ICC may reflect early cerebral hemodynamic disturbances associated with BP elevation. Additionally, female participants exhibited higher P2/P1 and TTP values across all stages of BP elevation, indicating a potentially greater susceptibility to early cerebral hemodynamics disorders.

Previous studies reported that P2/P1 values above 1.20 and TTP values above 0.25 reflect altered ICC in patients under neurological care [15, 17, 26, 27]. These thresholds, however, were derived from neurocritical populations, in which ICH is traditionally defined as sustained ICP >20 mmHg for more than 5 min. Until recently, this cut-off of 1.20 was interpreted as indicative of altered ICC but not as a definitive diagnostic marker. In 2024, the first study conducted in neurologically healthy individuals redefined these reference values, proposing P2/P1 ≥ 1.40 and TTP ≥ 0.30 s as criteria for ICH in non-neurocritical populations [18, 28]. Importantly, values above 1.20 are now regarded as an early warning sign of impaired ICC, possibly reflecting the initial exhaustion of compensatory mechanisms, and therefore may serve as a threshold for preventive approaches. Accordingly, values above 1.20 are now considered an early warning signal of impaired ICC, possibly reflecting the initial exhaustion of compensatory mechanisms rather than overt ICH. In addition, this study showed that for women aged ≥ 40 years the most appropriate cutoff for P2/P1 was also ≥ 1.40, suggesting sex-specific differences that are not yet fully understood.

In our sample, regardless of sex or HT stage, the mean P2/P1 was 1.32 and the mean TTP was 0.25 s, values that fall between those expected for healthy individuals and those reported in neurocritical patients. These results reinforce the hypothesis that BP elevation influences ICC through mechanisms distinct from acute neurological injury, but sufficient to induce measurable functional alterations. The association between elevated BP and changes in ICC was first described by Fernandes et al. in rats with renovascular hypertension and later confirmed by our group in patients with longstanding essential HT [21, 22, 29]. These studies based on the earlier cut-off of P2/P1 1.2 consistently demonstrated that a substantial proportion of hypertensive individuals already exhibit altered P2/P1 as also the TTP values, reinforcing the evidence that HT is associated with impaired ICC, even before the updated thresholds in healthy populations were established. Nevertheless, the HT stage at which these changes begin remains uncertain, highlighting the importance of investigating earlier phases of BP elevation.

Our findings provide new evidence that prehypertensive individuals, not yet receiving pharmacological treatment and with low cardiovascular risk, already exhibit changes in ICC parameters (P2/P1 = 1.26, TTP = 0.24). Importantly, these values fall within the range currently regarded as an alert zone for impaired ICC, reinforcing the concept of early functional cerebral involvement. There is a trend of progressive deterioration in these parameters with increasing BP, even among relatively young individuals. Furthermore, when analyzed independently of sex and age, the effects of BP on P2/P1 and TTP remained significant. These observations challenge the assumption that cerebral autoregulation and blood-brain barrier integrity provide complete protection against BP-related cerebral stress during the early stages of HT, suggesting that subtle hemodynamic alterations may precede overt neurological injury [23–25].

The early identification of ICC changes may represent a promising noninvasive biomarker for cerebrovascular risk stratification, particularly in the context of hypertension-mediated brain organ damage, given its role in the progression of cerebrovascular and neurodegenerative diseases [2, 6]. Previous studies have shown that women tend to present greater arterial stiffness, particularly after menopause [30–34], which may enhance the transmission of pressure pulses into the cerebral vascular bed [34, 35]. Our results align with this evidence, as women had significantly higher P2/P1 and TTP values across all HT stages. This is also consistent with the studies by Adderly et al. (2019), da Costa et al. (2023), Ocamoto et al. (2024), and Inuzuka et al. (2025), who also suggested that women may be more susceptible to early changes in cerebral hemodynamics, which potentially contributes to the higher prevalence of stroke and dementia observed in women [21, 22, 28, 31, 33, 36, 37]. Given that stroke remains the leading cause of death among women worldwide, these sex-specific differences in intracranial compliance may represent an under recognized mechanism contributing to long-term cerebrovascular vulnerability.

Multivariable linear regression analyses demonstrated that age and sex were the main determinants of both P2/P1 and TTP, while HT stage remained independently associated with these parameters, although with smaller effect sizes. These results suggest that BP contributes to ICC impairment beyond demographic influences, supporting the concept that BP-related cerebral hemodynamic alterations emerge early and may reflect functional brain involvement associated with HT rather than aging alone.

Proper BP control, even in its early stages, could significantly reduce the incidence of cerebrovascular events, cognitive decline, and dementia over time [2, 3, 8, 9]. A proposed mechanism for this protective effect is the preservation of CBF [37], which may reduce the impact on ICC. Nonetheless, further studies are necessary to evaluate how different classes of antihypertensive medications affect these ICP parameters, helping to identify more effective therapeutic strategies for prevention of neurological complications associated with HT.

Despite the significant contributions of this study, some limitations should be noted. As a multicenter study, data collection was conducted by different investigators. However, the team was previously trained on the standardized protocol criteria and data collection procedures. Additionally, due to the cross-sectional design, the study cannot establish causality between the analyzed parameters. The use of convenience sampling also introduces a potential selection bias. Future longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether early ICC alterations predict long-term cerebrovascular outcomes and cognitive decline.

In summary, our findings suggest that increasing BP values since the range of high-normal BP (prehypertension) to established hypertension stages 1 and 2 is associated with reduced ICC, indicating early impairment of cerebral hemodynamics in prehypertensive and hypertensive individuals. Furthermore, women exhibit higher P2/P1 and TTP values than men, regardless of age or HT stage. Taking together, these results support the hypothesis that intracranial compliance assessment may represent a functional marker of early hypertension-mediated brain organ damage.

Clinical Relevance

Early detection of cerebral hemodynamic alterations in hypertensive patients may be important for preventing long-term cerebrovascular complications. This study demonstrates that intracranial compliance deteriorates progressively with increasing BP levels, even in prehypertensive individuals without neurological disease. These findings suggest that noninvasive ICC monitoring using P2/P1 and TTP parameters may detect early functional hypertension-mediated brain damage, and higher P2/P1 and TTP values considered as early disease biomarkers. While ICC waveform analysis has been previously applied in neurological settings, its investigation in untreated hypertensive individuals broadens its cardiovascular relevance. The greater susceptibility observed in women may reflect sex-related vascular differences, possibly influenced by hormonal status. By including only untreated participants, we minimized pharmacological confounding and provided a more direct assessment of the relationship between BP and cerebral hemodynamics.
