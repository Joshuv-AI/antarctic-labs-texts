Gaps in the detection of drug-drug interactions between antipsychotic and cardiometabolic medications: a multisource analysis
Authors: Honghui Yao, Zixuan Peng, Yue Huang, Shuiyuan Xiao, Renrong Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndividuals with severe mental illness (SMI) are frequently prescribed both antipsychotic medications and cardiometabolic medications, placing them at increased risk of drug-drug interactions (DDIs). However, evidence guiding the identification and management of these interactions remains fragmented. To address this research gap, in this study, we systematically summarize potential DDIs between antipsychotic and cardiometabolic medications and evaluate the performance of commonly used online DDI checkers in identifying these interactions.MethodsA systematic review was conducted using PubMed, Embase, PsycINFO, and Web of Science to identify studies reporting DDIs between antipsychotic and cardiometabolic medications up to March 20, 2024. Disproportionality analysis was performed using data from the Canada Vigilance Adverse Reaction Online Database (1965–2024) and the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS, 2004–2024) to identify DDI signals. Four online DDI checkers—Drugs.com, Medscape, ddinter, and ANSM Thesaurus—were used to evaluate their ability to identify the observed interactions.ResultsAcross all sources, 1776 unique potential DDIs were identified. Clozapine was the most frequently implicated antipsychotic medication in a systematic review, often associated with musculoskeletal and connective tissue disorders. DDI signals associated with aripiprazole and quetiapine were also frequently observed. Except for nervous system disorders and cardiometabolic disorders, the adverse outcomes of DDIs involving aripiprazole or quetiapine were most commonly associated with musculoskeletal and connective tissue disorders and gastrointestinal disorders. Quetiapine interactions, especially with lipid-lowering agents such as simvastatin, were also commonly linked to musculoskeletal and connective tissue disorders. Notably, 45.4% of identified DDIs were not flagged by any of the four DDI checkers. Drugs.com detected the most interactions. Combinations of clozapine and metformin, ziprasidone and metformin, and risperidone and clonidine were consistently identified by at least three of the checkers.ConclusionsThis systematic review and disproportionality analysis identified potential DDIs between antipsychotic medications and cardiometabolic medications, many of which were not captured by commonly used DDI checkers. These findings underscore the need for clinicians to consult multiple sources and apply clinical judgment when prescribing these medications. Improved integration of pharmacovigilance data into DDI checkers may enhance the identification and prevention of harmful interactions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-025-04420-4.Keywords: Drug-drug interaction, Antipsychotic medication, Disproportionality analysis

Background

Severe mental illnesses (SMI), including schizophrenia, psychotic depression, and bipolar disorder [1], are associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular diseases and diabetes [1, 2]. Over the past decade, prescriptions for antipsychotic medications, primarily used to treat schizophrenia and other SMIs, have steadily increased [3]. Between 2008 and 2019, global antipsychotic medication consumption rose from 2.22 to 2.75 defined daily doses per 1000 people, marking an annual growth rate of 2.49% [4]. Although these medications are vital for managing acute psychiatric symptoms and preventing relapse [3], they may contribute to adverse cardiometabolic outcomes, such as metabolic syndrome and hypertension [5].

To mitigate these cardiometabolic risks, clinical guidelines recommend adjunctive treatment with cardiometabolic medications, particularly for diabetes prevention or weight management [6, 7]. Previous research has shown that individuals with SMIs are frequently prescribed medications for concurrent somatic conditions, particularly cardiovascular and antidiabetic medications [8]. However, prescribing multiple medications simultaneously increases the likelihood of drug-drug interactions (DDIs) [9]. Clinicians must, therefore, be aware of potential DDIs involving antipsychotic and cardiometabolic medications to reduce associated risks.

To support clinicians in making treatment decisions, recent reviews have summarized potential DDIs between antipsychotic medications and antihypertensive or antidiabetic medications [10, 11]. These reviews were limited by some issues, including inadequate quality assessments, narrow drug classification definitions, restricted data sources, and a predominant focus on explaining mechanisms. A comprehensive, updated synthesis should be done to incorporate a broader spectrum of cardiometabolic medications, such as cardiac therapies, peripheral vasodilators, and vasoprotective agents, as well as utilize diverse data sources, including the individual case safety reports dataset.

Computerized DDI checkers have been developed to assist clinicians in identifying potential interactions. Evidence suggests these tools can enhance clinical decision-making and reduce the risk of adverse events [12]. However, although one previous study attempted to evaluate the utility of DDI checkers for detecting interactions between antipsychotic and cardiometabolic medications, it was limited to comparing the total number of interactions identified by the tools and did not assess their performance against known or reported DDIs [13]. Thus, the accuracy and clinical relevance of DDI checkers in this context remain insufficiently evaluated.

This systematic review aims to provide an updated and comprehensive analysis of potential DDIs between antipsychotic and cardiometabolic medications. Additionally, this review evaluates the effectiveness of online DDI checkers in helping clinicians identify these potential DDIs.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted in three phases. First, we conducted a systematic review to identify studies reporting adverse outcomes associated with DDIs between antipsychotic and cardiometabolic medications in individuals with SMIs. Second, we identified DDI signals using two open-access drug surveillance databases: the Canada Vigilance Adverse Reaction Online Database (Canada Vigilance) and the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS). Third, we evaluated the ability of several online DDI checkers to identify potential interactions between antipsychotic and cardiometabolic medications identified in the first two steps.

Antipsychotic and cardiometabolic medications were defined according to the World Health Organization Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) Classification System. Antipsychotic medications included ATC code N05A, excluding lithium (N05AN01) [14]. Cardiometabolic medications included ATC codes C01–C10, B01, and A10 [15, 16].

Systematic review

We systematically searched MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, PsycINFO, and Web of Science databases up to March 20, 2024. Search terms included combinations of “antipsychotic medication,” “cardiometabolic medication,” “drug interactions,” specific antipsychotic medications, cardiometabolic medication subclasses, and names of commonly prescribed cardiometabolic medications. We included studies involving individuals receiving both antipsychotic and cardiometabolic medications, regardless of the indication for antipsychotic use. Where available, we extracted information on patient characteristics, including age, sex, comorbidities, underlying psychiatric diagnosis, and clinical setting. Adverse outcomes were defined as any clinical symptoms or events reported after the combined use of antipsychotic and cardiometabolic medications and suspected to be related to a DDI. These included both subjective symptoms and objective outcomes, as long as a temporal and causal link was suggested by the study authors. No language restrictions were applied. Reference lists of relevant articles and reviews were manually screened for additional eligible studies. The detailed search strategy is provided in Additional file 1: Table 1.

Studies included original research evaluating DDIs between antipsychotic medications and cardiometabolic medications in humans. Reviews and animal studies were excluded. There were no restrictions on study design, comparator groups, or specific outcomes. Two investigators (Z. P. and H. H.) independently screened titles, abstracts, and full-text articles, resolving discrepancies through discussion with the third investigator (H. Y.). Extracted data included study characteristics, involved medications, DDI mechanisms (pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic), and reported adverse events. Study quality was assessed independently by two reviewers (Z. P. and H. H.) using the Joanna Briggs Institute checklist [32]; disagreements were resolved by consulting a third reviewer. The systematic review adhered to the PRISMA statement for reporting systematic reviews and meta-analyses [33].

Identify the DDI signal from the drug surveillance dataset

We analyzed data from the Canada Vigilance Adverse Reaction Online Database (January 1, 1965, to May 31, 2024) and FAERS (January 1, 2004, to June 30, 2024) to identify DDI signals. These databases contain demographics, administrative details, drug information, Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) preferred terminology for adverse event coding, patient outcomes, reporting sources, treatment initiation, treatment end dates, and indications for use. Duplicate reports—defined as those with the same gender, age, reporting country, event date, start date, and drug indications—were removed. FAERS data were linked to the DiAna dictionary to convert drug names into active ingredients and corresponding ATC codes [34]. Records without identifiable active ingredients in FAERS were excluded.

We included the full dataset as the reference set to maintain study power and complement previous findings in a larger population, avoiding restrictions that could reduce power. All records within the study periods were included, regardless of the role assigned to antipsychotic medications and cardiometabolic medications by the reporter (e.g., primary suspect drug, secondary suspect drug, concomitant drug, or interacting drug). Additionally, a sensitivity analysis was performed by restricting the analysis to reports in which the antipsychotic or cardiometabolic medication was listed as a primary or secondary suspect drug, while all other reports served as the reference group. Outcomes were categorized according to the system organ class of the affected system, and specific adverse conditions were also considered in the analysis. Potential DDI signals were identified using three statistical methods: reporting odds ratio (ROR), proportional reporting ratio (PRR), and Bayesian confidence propagation neural network (BCPNN). A DDI signal was confirmed if it met thresholds in all three methods: ROR (95% confidence interval > 1, events ≥ 2), PRR (≥ 2, χ2 ≥ 4, events ≥ 3), and BCPNN (IC₀.₂₅ > 0) [35]. Data mining and statistical analyses were conducted using the pvad package in R version 4.3.1.

Drug interaction checkers

We utilized the following drug interaction checkers: DDInter, Drugs.com, Medscape, and the French National Drug Safety Institute (ANSM) Thesaurus (2009–2019). These tools are commonly used by clinicians and are open access. Web scraping was conducted up to April 2, 2025. We searched using a list of antipsychotic substances and their commonly used brand names to ensure high sensitivity in identifying relevant interactions. For each identified DDI involving an antipsychotic, we extracted the paired cardiometabolic medication directly and recorded the associated interaction severity according to each tool’s classification system. No manual mapping was required, as the interacting substances and severity levels were clearly provided by the platforms.

The DDInter includes 236,834 clinically relevant interactions among 1833 FDA-approved drugs, compiled from studies reporting drug effects in PubMed and categorized as major, moderate, minor, or unknown risk [36]. Drugs.com categorizes DDI into major (highly clinically significant; avoid combinations), moderate (moderately clinically significant, usually avoid combinations unless necessary), and minor (minimally clinically significant; minimize the risk) [9]. Medscape drug interaction checker assesses interacting drug ingredients, their effects, and clinical significance, classifying DDIs as serious (requiring alternative drugs), significant (requiring close monitoring), or minor [9]. The ANSM Thesaurus, which is maintained and updated annually, is considered the official source of DDI information for French clinicians, categorizing DDI into contraindicated, not recommended, precautions for use, and to be considered [37].

To ensure consistency across sources, DDI severity was harmonized into three levels: severe, moderate, and minor. We mapped classifications from Drug.com, Medscape, DDInter, and the French Thesaurus based on their original labels. For example, “Major,” “Contraindicated,” or “Avoid/Use Alternate” were grouped as severe; “Moderate,” “Modify Therapy,” or “Association Not Recommended” as moderate; and “Minor,” “Unknown,” or “Use with Caution” as minor. To assess consistency across tools, we conducted pairwise agreement analyses using Cohen’s kappa statistic, comparing severity categories after harmonization. To enhance the clinical relevance of our findings, we compiled a validation list of DDIs that were supported by multiple evidence sources. Specifically, we included DDIs that were identified either through disproportionality analysis or systematic review and were also flagged by at least three drug interaction checkers. For these validated interactions, we further assessed whether they were reported in the product summary information of the involved antipsychotic medications.

Results

Identified DDI from different resources

Systematic review results

From the 9404 studies initially identified, 472 duplicates were removed. After screening titles and abstracts, 36 studies underwent full-text evaluation, and among them, 15 studies met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1) [17, 21–29]. These studies included 49 unique drug-drug combinations associated with adverse outcomes (Table 1; Additional file 1:Table S2). One study was published in a language other than English [24]. Of the included studies, 13 were case reports [17–20, 22, 24–31], 1 was a case series [21], and 1 was an observational study [23]. Most studies reported interactions between antipsychotic medications and specific cardiometabolic medications, except for two, which showed cardiometabolic medication categories [22, 23]. The Joanna Briggs Institute quality scores indicated moderate to high methodological quality, with scores ranging from 5 to 8 for case reports (median: 7), scores of 5 for the case series, and 3 for the observational study. Patients in the included case reports and series ranged from 32 to 78 years, with both sexes represented. Schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and schizoaffective disorder were the most common psychiatric diagnoses. Hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, and atrial fibrillation were frequently reported comorbidities. Most DDIs occurred in inpatient or outpatient settings.

Flowchart of the systematic review

Most DDIs involved cardiac therapies, lipid-lowering agents, and beta-blockers, while interactions with antidiabetic medications were reported less frequently. The most commonly reported adverse events associated with these DDIs included musculoskeletal and connective tissue disorders, cardiac disorders, and eye disorders. Clozapine interacted with the broadest range of cardiometabolic medications, particularly cardiac therapies, beta-blockers, and antidiabetic medications. These combinations were most often associated with cardiac adverse events, followed by musculoskeletal and connective tissue disorders. Clozapine-related DDIs were the most frequently reported, particularly in combination with cardiac therapy, followed by quetiapine-related DDIs. Most cases involving quetiapine-related DDIs reported musculoskeletal adverse events, including muscle pain and elevated creatine phosphokinase (CPK), particularly with simvastatin. Similar events were also noted with the combination of quetiapine and atorvastatin.

Drug surveillance dataset

After excluding duplicates or those without substance information, 1,114,355 reports from Canada Vigilance and 15,165,019 reports from FAERS were included (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). In total, second-generation antipsychotics showed the highest use from 2004 to 2024, with third-generation agents surpassing first-generation antipsychotics after 2012 (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). Canada Vigilance included 13,109 reports using both antipsychotic medications and cardiometabolic medications, with a median patient age of 59.0 years (IQR: 46–71) and a female proportion of 55.1%. The majority of events were serious (83.2%), predominantly reported by market authorization holders and primarily occurred between 2015 and 2024 (52.3%) (Additional file 1: Table S3). In FAERS, 118,761 reports included antipsychotic medications and cardiometabolic medications, with a median patient age of 61.0 years (IQR: 48–73) and 56.2% female. Most FAERS reports originated from the USA (46.3%), were reported between 2019 and 2024 (56.0%), and frequently resulted in hospitalization (40.8%) (Additional file 1: Table S4).

We identified 8254 unique combinations of DDIs and adverse event categories, encompassing 1754 distinct antipsychotic–cardiometabolic medication pairs. Among cardiometabolic medications, cardiac therapies (307 pairs), antidiabetic medications (304 pairs), and ACEI/ARBs (213 pairs) were most frequently involved. First- and second-generation antipsychotics accounted for the majority of interactions (1422 pairs, 710 for first-generation antipsychotics and 712 for second-generation antipsychotics), particularly aripiprazole (138 pairs) and olanzapine (107 pairs). Sensitivity analyses yielded consistent findings; drug pairs highlighted in red were not retained in the sensitivity analyses. These two medications were most often associated with adverse events classified under cardiometabolic, nervous system, gastrointestinal, musculoskeletal, and connective tissue disorders. The most commonly reported adverse events overall included psychiatric disorders, metabolic and nutritional disturbances, and nervous system disorders (Fig. 2). The combination of quetiapine and prednisolone was linked to the widest range of adverse events, most often involving injury, poisoning, or procedural complications, gastrointestinal disorders, and musculoskeletal and connective tissue disorders (Additional file 2: Data S1). The DDI between quetiapine and ramipril, associated with injury, poisoning, or procedural complications, had the highest number of observations. This was followed by DDIs involving quetiapine with ibuprofen, associated with injury, poisoning, or procedural complications, and quetiapine with atorvastatin, associated with gastrointestinal disorders. Among the quetiapine-associated gastrointestinal DDIs, most were linked to self-limiting conditions such as fecaloma or discolored vomit; however, a small number were associated with more severe outcomes, including acute pancreatitis (Additional file 2: Data S2). At the disease-specific level, we identified 37,421 combinations involving antipsychotic medications, cardiometabolic medications, and diseases, covering 2489 distinct diseases. The most frequently reported events were nonspecific drug interactions, falls, and hypotension (Additional file 2: Data S2). Among the combinations with the highest number of observations, the interaction between quetiapine and hydrocortisone in relation to pericarditis was most frequently reported, followed by the combination of quetiapine and lisinopril, also associated with pericarditis. Additional file 1: Table S5 summarizes the five most frequently reported adverse events for each medication combination, excluding those related to nervous system and cardiometabolic disorders. Detailed disproportionality analysis results (ROR, PRR, IC) for these interactions are available in Additional file 2: Data S2.

Drug interaction checker analysis

Using four DDI databases—DDInter, Drugs.com, Medscape, and ANSM Thesaurus—we identified 7205 potential DDI pairs involving 39 antipsychotic medications. After deduplication, this resulted in 4876 unique interactions. The selection criteria and the number of interactions reported by each source are detailed in Additional file 1: Fig.S3 and Additional file 1: Table S6. Notably, no DDI pair was reported by all 4 sources, while 346 pairs were identified by at least 3. Drugs.com reported the largest number of interactions, followed by the ANSM Thesaurus. Several medications, including mesoridazine and promazine, were not listed in the ANSM Thesaurus, reflecting regional differences in drug availability and approval. Agreement between Drugs.com and DDInter was moderate (κ = 0.661), whereas lower concordance was observed between Drugs.com and Medscape (κ = 0.527). Agreement between Medscape and ddinter was weak (κ = 0.196) and minimal between the ANSM Thesaurus and any other source (all κ < 0.1) (Additional file 1: Table S7).

Most identified interactions were of moderate severity, predominantly involving first-generation antipsychotic medications combined with antihypertensive medications or cardiac therapies (Fig. 3). Severe outcomes were frequently associated with DDIs involving antipsychotic medications combined with cardiac therapies or beta-blockers.

In total, we identified 1779 unique potential DDIs or DDI signals from both the systematic review and disproportionality analysis. Of these, 25 were identified exclusively through the systematic review, 1734 from drug surveillance databases alone, and 20 were identified by both approaches (Additional file 2: Data S4). Notably, 45.4% of these potential DDIs were not reported by any of the four drug interaction checkers. The majority of these unreported interactions involved first-generation antipsychotic medications and cardiac therapies. Drugs.com reported the most DDI overall, particularly for combinations involving beta-blockers and ACEIs/ARBs with antipsychotics, and frequently classified these interactions as of major or moderate severity (Additional file 2: Data S4). In contrast, Medscape, DDInter, and ANSM Thesaurus demonstrated variable performance and more often identified interactions of moderate severity (Table 2). Four DDI pairs identified through both systematic review and disproportionality analysis were not found in any of the checkers. Severity classifications also varied across platforms, underscoring inconsistency in DDI detection. Interactions between clozapine and metformin, ziprasidone and metformin, and risperidone and clonidine were consistently highlighted by at least three checkers. In addition, the 107 validated DDI combinations and their corresponding product summary information for the involved antipsychotic medications are presented in Additional file 2: Data S5.

Discussion

This study identified 1776 unique potential DDIs between antipsychotic medications and cardiometabolic medications through a systematic review and disproportionality analysis. Both sources consistently highlighted interactions between first- and second-generation antipsychotics and cardiac therapies, while interactions between first- and second-generation antipsychotics and antidiabetic medications were notably more frequently identified by disproportionality analysis than previously reported in academic literature. The use of drug interaction checkers could help identify DDIs, especially those associated with severe or moderate outcomes.

The identified DDIs align with known pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic mechanisms. For instance, the effectiveness of oral anticoagulants may be potentiated by the inhibition of cytochrome P450 enzymes caused by antipsychotic medications [38]. Similarly, interactions between antipsychotic medications and antidiabetic medications involve both cytochrome P450 enzymes and, to a lesser degree, the P-glycoprotein transporter [10]. Animal studies have demonstrated that certain second-generation antipsychotic medications (asenapine and iloperidone) can reduce the activity of CYP enzymes responsible for metabolizing calcium channel blockers and ACEI/ARB, potentially increasing interaction risks [39]. Moreover, research has suggested that second-generation antipsychotic medications may impair the effectiveness of statins in patients with schizophrenia by altering lipid metabolism pathways [40]. Specifically, these medications may activate the ADMA-NOX-ROS pathway, reducing low-density lipoprotein receptor expression in liver cells and inhibiting endothelial nitric oxide synthase activity in blood vessels [40]. Given the increased risk of metabolic issues and cardiovascular disease among individuals receiving antipsychotic medications, clinicians should remain attentive to potential DDIs, especially in high-risk patient populations.

Previous studies on DDIs between antipsychotic and cardiometabolic medications have primarily focused on adverse outcomes related to cardiovascular disease, hepatotoxicity, central nervous system effects, and pancreatitis [10, 11]. However, adverse effects involving musculoskeletal, connective tissue, and gastrointestinal disorders found in this study have received comparatively limited attention. The mechanisms underlying musculoskeletal-related DDIs may involve several pathways. Antipsychotic medications have been associated with elevations in creatine kinase (CK), potentially due to dopaminergic effects in the striatum [41]. Co-administration with certain cardiometabolic medications, such as candesartan or bisoprolol, may reduce the metabolism of antipsychotics, thereby increasing their serum concentrations and exacerbating toxicity [42]. Additionally, some cardiometabolic medications—particularly beta-blockers—have themselves been linked to muscle damage, which may further compound the risk of musculoskeletal complications when combined with antipsychotic medications [43]. Gastrointestinal adverse effects may also be amplified through pharmacodynamic or pharmacokinetic interactions. Antipsychotics are known to affect gastrointestinal motility and function [44], and concurrent use of cardiometabolic medications may intensify these effects through overlapping or interacting mechanisms. From a clinical perspective, these findings underscore the importance of vigilant monitoring when managing antipsychotic–cardiometabolic medication combinations, particularly in patients with musculoskeletal, cardiovascular, or metabolic vulnerabilities. Based on the pharmacological mechanisms of the identified DDIs, key parameters for routine monitoring include ECG (QTc interval), creatine kinase and renal function, blood pressure, metabolic markers (glucose and lipid profiles), and bleeding risk. While monitoring frequency should be tailored to individual risk, baseline assessment and periodic follow-up every 3–6 months are advisable for high-risk combinations or vulnerable populations.

In recent years, drug interaction checkers have been increasingly integrated into clinical practice to help prevent potential DDIs and improve prescribing quality. Systems such as SFINX, developed in Sweden, exemplify efforts to provide healthcare professionals with reliable tools to support medication safety [45]. Evidence suggests that these computational tools can enhance clinical decision-making, particularly when users receive appropriate training [46]. Psychiatrists, in particular, may benefit from incorporating these tools as part of a broader strategy to reduce the risk of harmful drug interactions. In our study, drug interaction checkers were useful in identifying many potentially severe interactions. Among the tools evaluated—Medscape, Drugs.com, DDInter, and ANSM Thesaurus—Drugs.com identified the highest number of interactions from the potential DDIs derived from both the systematic review and disproportionality analysis, especially involving beta-blockers and ACEI/ARBs. This finding is consistent with prior studies showing that Drugs.com frequently identifies more DDIs compared to other checkers, likely due to its large, multi-sourced database and frequent updates [13]. However, the varied performance across tools highlights a significant limitation: reliance on a single drug interaction checker may result in missed interactions.

We also observed that several potential DDIs identified through disproportionality analysis and reviews were not reported in any drug interaction checkers. This gap likely reflects several factors: first, the lag in updating checker databases with emerging evidence, especially for newer or less commonly studied antipsychotic–cardiometabolic combinations; second, regional differences in drug availability, regulatory approval, and clinical use; and third, methodological variability in how DDIs are evaluated and classified across platforms. This underreporting poses a potential risk to patient safety, as unrecognized interactions may contribute to preventable adverse events. Additionally, inconsistencies in classifying interaction severity across tools further complicate clinical interpretation, which is consistent with previous studies [47]. The use of divergent classification systems and methodologies underscores a broader lack of consensus, making it difficult for clinicians to assess and act on potential DDI risks confidently. Given these limitations, clinicians are encouraged to use multiple drug interaction checkers with up-to-date literature and clinical judgment. A multifaceted approach to identifying and managing potential interactions is essential to ensure patient safety, particularly in complex treatment scenarios such as those involving antipsychotic medications and cardiometabolic medications.

Strengths and limitations

However, this study has several limitations. First, our systematic review may have missed studies from gray literature and emerging sources, such as conference proceedings and preprint websites. Besides, while we extracted and summarized the proposed mechanisms of DDIs as reported in the original sources, some of these mechanisms were supported by only a single reference or lacked a detailed pharmacological explanation. Second, while disproportionality analysis can detect DDI signals, it cannot alone provide conclusive evidence of a causal relationship between a drug and an adverse event due to the lack of exposure data, self-reported adverse events, and potential underreporting or selective reporting biases. Moreover, disproportionality analysis carries a risk of over-alerting, particularly for frequently co-prescribed medications. To mitigate this, we triangulated findings across multiple sources, including case reports and drug interaction checkers, and considered only those interactions that were identified in the review or disproportionality analysis and flagged by at least three DDI checkers. Third, we evaluated only four open-access drug interaction checkers, which may have led to the omission of some DDI-associated adverse drug reactions occurring in clinical practice or in commercial drug interaction checkers, although these four are widely used and accessible in specific countries or worldwide. Fourth, the lack of detailed clinical indications in interaction reports limits the precise interpretation of potential DDIs. Fifth, we required that DDI signals meet the threshold for three disproportionality methods (ROR, PRR, and IC), which may have led to under-detection of some potentially relevant interactions. However, this strict approach was chosen to improve robustness, reduce false positives, and is consistent with recommendations from previous studies. Finally, including all drug roles may increase false positives due to unrelated co-medications, but this was a deliberate choice to enhance sensitivity in this hypothesis-generating phase.

Conclusions

This study underscores the importance of recognizing significant DDIs between antipsychotic medications and cardiometabolic medications. Clinicians must practice cautious prescribing and rigorous monitoring. Drug interaction checkers can support clinical decision-making, but healthcare providers should utilize multiple sources and remain informed about emerging evidence. Enhancing awareness and proactive management are critical to optimizing outcomes for patients receiving antipsychotic medications.
