Biodistribution, radiodosimetry and pharmacokinetics of [18F]GSK1482160 for cerebral P2X7 receptor in living subjects
Authors: Lei Bi, Kun He, Lu Wang, Peizhen Ye, Yongshan Liu, Min Yang, Junjie Wei, Jie Ma, Huiyi Wei, Weiyan Zhou, Yifan Yuan, Liang Chen, Yihui Guan, Qi Yue, Guiting Li, Mingfang Wang, Fang Xie, Hongjun Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIncreasing evidence of the role of neuroinflammation in pathophysiology has highlighted the importance of P2X7 receptor (P2X7R)-targeted neurodegeneration diseases. The safety, biodistribution, pharmacokinetics and dosimetry of a promising F-18-labeled PET tracer [18F]GSK1482160 for imaging P2X7R were evaluated. The absorbed dose (AD) and effective dose (ED) of nonhuman primates (NHPs) and human adult volunteers were estimated. The total volume distribution (VT) was calculated via the 2-tissue compartment model (2TCM) and Logan plot (LoganAIF).ResultsThere were no adverse events after [18F]GSK1482160 dosing. Radioactivity accumulated predominantly in the hepatobiliary system, kidney, spleen, and brain. Organ dosimetry revealed the liver as the critical organ with a mean AD of 30.1 µGy/MBq, followed the adrenals at 22.8 µGy/MBq. The overall ED of the female and male human volunteers were 10.9 µSv/MBq and 8.88 µSv/MBq, respectively. [18F]GSK1482160 has the ability to cross the blood–brain barrier and is distributed throughout the whole brain with low region specificity. The 2TCM and LoganAIF models had good correlation and agreement, and the input functions of the image-derived and blood-sampling methods had good correlation but poor correspondence. The AD and ED produced by [18F]GSK1482160 were within typical levels for fluorine-18-labeled tracers.Conclusions[18F]GSK1482160 is a safe PET tracer with hepatorenal metabolism and it is up to 250 MBq (6.7 mCi) for human adults of [18F]GSK1482160 can be administered of a total effective dose of 2.73 mSv (0.273 rem) with the critical organ, the liver, receiving the maximum allowed exposure of 50 mSv (5 rem) in accordance with ICRP 103 recommendations. 2TCM and LoganAIF using a blood-sampling input function with blood metabolite correction are accurate methods for quantifying the tracer binding of P2X7R in the brain.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13550-026-01420-0.Keywords: P2X7R, Neuroinflammation, [18F]GSK1482160, Tracer kinetics, Dosimetry, Biodistribution

Background

Purinergic signaling pathways are mediated by purine nucleotides and nucleosides, including adenosine triphosphate (ATP), niacinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+), adenosine and other receptors widely expressed on immune cells [1]. In higher mammals, the nucleotide receptor family comprises two subfamilies, P2X and P2Y receptors [2]. In the P2X (P2X1-7) family, the EC50 of P2X7R combined with ATP is 100–780 µM, and the EC50 values of other P2 receptor subtypes are less than 30 µM [3]. Under homeostatic conditions, extracellular ATP concentrations are low due to the regulation of ATPases. Under conditions of cell destruction, hypoxia or death, intracellular ATP is released into the extracellular space; when the ATP concentration exceeds 500 µM, P2X7R is activated, which can lead to a proinflammatory state and immune regulation of innate and adaptive immune system cells [4, 5]. P2X7R is widely expressed throughout the body of mammals and is mainly distributed in cells of hematopoietic lineages and the central and peripheral nervous systems. Studies have shown that P2X7R is related to the progression of diseases associated with inflammatory, immune, neurologic, and musculoskeletal disorders, including multiple sclerosis, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, Huntington’s disease and rheumatoid arthritis [6–8]. P2X7R is expressed mainly in microglia and is expressed at lower levels in neurons, astrocytes, and oligodendrocytes [9]. There are studies exploring the effects of pharmacological blockade of P2X7R in neurologic disease, which indicate that P2X7R antagonists may be potential therapeutic drugs for neuroinflammatory diseases [10, 11].

A number of P2X7R antagonists for preclinical studies and clinical trials have been reported, such as A-740,003, AZ11657312, BBG, PPADS, PKT100, AZD9056, CE-224,535, GSK1482160, JNJ-54,175,446, JNJ-55,308,942, and EVT401 [12, 13]. The carbon-11 (half-life = 20.38 min)-labeled tracers used are [11C]JNJ54173717 [9, 14] (IC50 = 4.2 ± 0.01 nM, radiochemical yield (RCY) = 40–60%), [11C]SMW139 [15–18] (Kd = 4.6 ± 0.8 nM, RCY = 37 ± 9%) and [11C]GSK1482160 [19–21] (IC50 = 12.2 ± 2.5 nM, Kd = 1.15 ± 0.12 nM, RCY = 30–40%), [11C]JNJ54173717 and [11C]SMW139, which have been assessed for their characteristics in human. Considering the time stability for clinical applicability in the brain, a dynamic acquisition time greater than 90 min is more accurate with an acceptable deviation range [9, 17, 22], so many tracers labeled with fluorine-18 (half-life = 109.8 min) exist. The characteristics of [18F]JNJ64413739 (IC50 = 1 nM) have been assessed in human [22, 23]. [18F]FTTM (Kd = 25.32 ± 3.47 nM, RCY = 5–10%) has been used to identify vulnerable atherosclerotic plaques in temporal lobe epilepsy rodent models [24, 25]. [18F]PTTP (Kd = 12.376 ± 2.204 nM, RCY = 2.5–7%) has been used to distinguish lung tumors from inflammation in rodents [26, 27]. [18F]EFB (pIC50 = 5.74, RCY = 3–5%) has been used to evaluate the LPS rodent model of neuroinflammation [28]. [18F]4 A (IC50 = 7.86 nM, RCY = 9.86 ± 0.21%) has been used in LPS and Alzheimer’s disease rodent models [29]. [18F]GSK1482160 (IC50 = 2.12 ± 0.06 nM, RCY = 20–30%) has been used to quantify ankylosing spondylitis [30]. Compared with other reported fluorine-18-labeled tracers, [18F]GSK1482160 has better affinity and RCY and has a better ability to assess inflammation as well as neurodegenerative disease [31]. This is the first study to report the automated radiosynthesis of [18F]GSK1482160 and the first study to explore the safety, radiation dosimetry, biodistribution and radiopharmaceutical kinetics of [18F]GSK1482160 in human and nonhuman primates (NHPs) of rhesus monkey. [18F]GSK1482160 can pass through the blood‒brain barrier, which is also safe for translation into the clinic for P2X7R-targeted imaging.

Methods

Automated radio-synthesis

Fluorine-18 was prepared by the IBA 18/9 cyclotron (IBA Nirta® Liquid Target, Belgium) via the 18O(p, n)18F nuclear reaction on [18O]H2O with a 100 µA beam current and 20–35 bar target pressure at Guangdong Huixuan Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. The automated radiosynthesis of [18F]GSK1482160 was applied on FASTlabTM2 (GE HealthCare, United States). After [18F]KF was captured, the Sep-Pak Accel plus QMA plus light cartridge WAT023525 (Waters, United States) was eluted with 1.0 mL of Et4NHCO3 (Sigma Aldrich, Germany) solution to form [18F]Et4NF, which was dried at 125 °C for 5 min. For azeotropic removal of residual water, 1 mL of anhydrous acetonitrile (Thermo Fisher Scientific, United States) was added to the vial and evaporated to dryness under a stream of nitrogen at 120 °C. This azeotropic drying step was repeated once to ensure complete dryness of the precursor prior to the addition of the [18F]fluorination reagent. After [18F]Et4NF dried completely, 2 mg of bromo-difluoromethyl precursor (reported previously from our group [29–31]) dissolved in 0.5 mL of acetonitrile (Aladdin, China) was added to the reaction system, which was maintained at 120 °C for 15 min. A total of 4.5 mL of super pure water was added to the reaction bottle to dilute and mix, and then the mixture was transferred through a Sep-Pak C18 plus short cartridge (WAT020515; Waters, United States). After impurities on the C18 column were eluted with 10 mL of super pure water, the raw product on the C18 column was eluted into the product bottle with 2 mL of anhydrous ethanol (Xilong Scientific, China), which was further purified by semipreparative high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (IBA Synthera®, Belgium) with 35% acetonitrile (0.05% trifluoroacetic acid) at a 4 mL/min flow rate. Radioanalytical HPLC (Shimadzu LC-20AT, Japan) was used to conduct quality control by coinjecting [19F]GSK1482160 with 35% acetonitrile (0.05% trifluoroacetic acid) at a 1 mL/min flow rate. The good manufacturing practice (GMP) protocols followed Guangdong Huixuan Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd [32]. End of run post purification the specific activity was 32–176 GBq/µM ON A 2 CURIES total irradiation, after 75–90 min post irradiation synthetic time including purification with a non-decay-corrected radiochemical yield of 5–15% and dispensing vials for animals or patients injections. For quality control purposes and to ensure adherence to tracer kinetic principles, the maximum allowable injected mass dose of [18F]GSK1482160 per administration is set at 5.0 µg. This limit ensures < 5% receptor occupancy based on our blocking studies and maintains the validity of the tracer kinetic model.

In vivo study subjects and design

There were four NHPs (NO.1–4) with 0–1.5 h baseline brain dynamic positron emission tomography (PET), among which 5 min after the blocking agent [19F]GSK1482160 (1.6 mg/kg) was injected, NHP (NO.4) blocked brain dynamic PET. NHP (NO.4) performed multiple-time-point blood sample analyses. Three NHPs (NO.4–6) with 0–3 h baseline whole-body multiple-time-point PET scans were taken. Three human (NO. 1–3) underwent 0–1.5 h baseline brain dynamic PET, and two human (NO. 4–5) underwent 0–1.3 h baseline whole-body multiple-time-point PET. Dynamic PET scans were used to evaluate the pharmaceutical kinetics of the brain in human and NHPs, and whole-body multiple-time-point PET scans were used for dosimetry estimation. Six NHPs and five human volunteers underwent PET/CT imaging. For anatomical coregistration and attenuation correction, all subjects underwent a low-dose computed tomography (CT) scan as an integral component of each PET/CT imaging session. These CT scans were acquired immediately prior to the PET emission scan using the combined PET/CT scanner and were used for attenuation correction and anatomical localization of the PET data. The CT acquisition parameters were 120 kV, 30–50 mAs, and the associated radiation dose was included in the total effective dose calculations reported in Table 2. NHP (NO. 1–4) and human (NO. 1–3) underwent T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) without simultaneous scanning. The preclinical studies and clinical trials of this study were approved by the local ethics committee. The ethical approval number of the experiments for the NHP was LDACU20230301-02 at the First Affiliated Hospital of Jinan University. For the human volunteers, the trial registration number was KY022-075 at Huashan Hospital of Fudan University on February 22th, 2022. The detailed information is listed in Fig. 1 and Table 1. At the time of injection, the specific activity of [18F]GSK1482160 ranged from 32 to 176 GBq/µmol. The corresponding injected activity ranged from 167.42 ± 37.21 MBq (123.51–214.25 MBq) for NHPs and 251.07 ± 22.04 MBq (214.60–277.50 MBq) for humans, with the injected mass dose ranging from 0.5 to 2.5 µg. Based on our blocking study results and previously published methodology for estimating mass dose limits [31], this mass dose is well below the upper limit required to maintain tracer kinetic principles (i.e., < 5% receptor occupancy). All studies were conducted in compliance with institutional and regulatory radiation safety guidelines, which limit annual research-related exposure for healthy volunteers to 50 mSv (5 rem) in accordance with ICRP 103 recommendations.

PET imaging acquisition

For NHP, PET data were acquired with a Discovery PET/CT 690 (GE HealthCare, United States). The NHP was supine, and a stereotactic frame was used to fix the position of the head. PET data were collected with a slice thickness of 3.27 mm, a slice interval of 3.75 mm, a matrix size of 256 × 256 and a scan field of view (FOV) of 70 cm in 3D time-of-flight (TOF) mode. One FOV was used for the 1.5-h brain dynamic PET scan, and the data were reconstructed as frames × time (s): 6 × 20; 8 × 30; 4 × 60; 5 × 120; 5 × 240; 10 × 300. Five FOVs were used for 3-h whole-body multiple-time-point PET, and the acquisition time points were 3, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 32, 38, 45, 52, 58, 70, 82, 93, 105, 117, 142, 167, and 180 min. The PET data were reconstructed with corrections for attenuation, normalization, scatter and randomness.

For human, PET data were acquired with a PET/CT scanner Biograph 128 mCT (Siemens, Germany). For the head, the PET data were collected with an image matrix size of 256 × 256 × 148 and a voxel size of 1.59 × 1.59 × 1.50 mm3, and for the whole body, the image matrix size was 200 × 200 × 284 and the voxel size was 4.07 × 4.07 × 3.00 mm3. Brain dynamic PET was acquired with one FOV lasting 1.5 h from the time of the [18F]GSK1482160 injection, which was reconstructed as frames × time (s): 15 × 10; 5 × 30; 5 × 60; 10 × 300; 3 × 600. Whole-body multiple-time-point PET data were obtained in FlowMotion mode: (1) 0–50 min from the thigh to the head, with the first 10 passes at 2 m/min followed by 0.68 m/min; (2) 50–70 min scan of the brain (one FOV); and (3) 70–80 min scan of the whole body (5 FOV, 2 min/FOV). The PET data were reconstructed via the TruX algorithm with corrections for decay, normalization, dead time, photon attenuation, scatter and random coincidences.

PET data processing

For brain data analysis, the PMOD Neuro Tool (PNEURO) in the PMOD Biomedical Image Quantification System 4.205 (PMOD Technologies LLC, Switzerland) was used. The INIA19 atlas of rhesus macaques and the atlases of human were selected for volume of interest (VOI) creation. Brain dynamic PET was loaded as a functional image with reorientation to the standard orientation. T1-weighted MR images of the brain were loaded as anatomical images and segmented into gray matter, white matter and cerebrospinal fluid. The result of the segmentation was shown as a fusion of the tissue segment map with the MRI map, which was further matched with the PET image. After successful normalization, MRI mapping to the atlas space and PET mapping to the MR image were satisfactory, and through brain segmentation, the outline of each VOI was defined. By calculating VOIs, the time–activity curves (TACs) of the brain regions were obtained. The brain regions of the frontal cortex, occipital cortex, temporal cortex, caudate nucleus, putamen, thalamus, hippocampus and cerebellum were collected for further study. Whole-body PET and CT images were merged by Carimas 2.10 (Turku PET Center, Finland). According to the anatomical structure of the CT, each VOI was manually sketched to obtain the radioactive activity (MBq/mL, SUV) at the time of PET acquisition, from which the TAC of each VOI was obtained.

Collection of whole blood and plasma samples and radiometabolite analysis via HPLC

After [18F]GSK1482160 was injected, whole arterial blood samples of NHP were manually collected from the femoral artery at 0.3, 0.7, 1.0, 1.3, 1.7, 2.0, 2.3, 2.7, 3.0, 3.3, 3.7, 4.0, 5.0, 30.0, 60.0 and 90.0 min, and each sample at each time point was at least 1 mL. The plasma was separated from the red blood cells via centrifugation (10,000 rpm for 5 min), and the radioactivity of [18F]GSK1482160 in whole blood and plasma was determined via a gamma counter (2470–8020 WIZARD, Perkin Elmer) following a previously reported protocol [29–31]. The HPLC curves of [18F]GSK1482160 and the plasma samples (2, 5, 30 and 90 min) were mathematically integrated with the area under the curve (AUC) from 0 min to 20 min to obtain the total radioactivity. The parts of the parent ([18F]GSK1482160) and the metabolite were integrated under the corresponding peak times. The proportions of parents or metabolites were calculated as [AUC(parent or metabolite)/AUC(total)]×100%.

Arterial blood input function

For NHP, two kinds of input functions, the image-derived input function (IDIF) and the blood sampling input function (BSIF), were used for kinetic modeling. For IDIF generation, a small circular region of interest (ROI) consisting of 3–5 pixels (approximately 2–3 mm in diameter) was carefully placed in the center of the carotid artery on summed early-frame PET images (0–2 min post-injection), where vascular activity was maximal. To improve signal-to-noise ratio while minimizing partial volume effects from surrounding tissue, this ROI was placed on three consecutive axial slices where the carotid artery was clearly visualized, and the resulting time-activity curves were averaged. The IDIF was visually inspected for each subject to ensure acceptable quality, with clearly identifiable peak and exponential clearance phases. BSIF consists of plasma samples at different times counted by a gamma counter, which was attenuated and corrected to the injection time. Moreover, IDIF and BSIF were metabolite corrected in the blood.

Kinetics modeling methods for [18F]GSK1482160

The radiopharmaceutical kinetics of the NHP brain were explored at baseline and during blockage. Kinetic models of the 2-tissue compartment model (2TCM) and Logan plot (LoganAIF) were explored via IDIF following previously reported protocols [23, 33, 34] and BSIF, and each input function was processed with and without blood metabolite correction. The total volume distribution (VT) was calculated via the PMOD Kinetic Modeling Tool (PKIN), and the VT parametric image was obtained via the Pixelwise Kinetic Modeling Tool (PXMOD). In the blocking test, the receptor occupancy (RO) of each brain region was calculated, and the distribution volume of the non-displaceable (VND) reference binding compartment was obtained via saturation plots and occupancy plots. The radiopharmaceutical kinetics of the human brain were explored at baseline via 2TCM and LoganAIF with IDIF without blood metabolite correction. The relationship between the RNA expression of P2X7 and the distribution of [18F]GSK1482160 was analyzed via the Pearson correlation coefficient. P2X7 RNA expression data for each brain region were collected from the database of the Human Protein Atlas (https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000089041-P2RX7).

Safety, biodistribution and radiation dosimetry

Time activity curves (TACs) were imported into Origin software 2021 (OriginLab Corporation, United States) for integral column analysis, from which the peak concentration (Cmax, SUV), peak time (Tmax, h), and area under the curve (AUC, SUV ~ h) were obtained. The highest absorbed doses were observed in the organs involved in the metabolism and excretion of [18F]GSK1482160, including the liver (hepatobiliary clearance), kidneys (renal excretion), and urinary bladder (accumulation of excreted radioactivity). This distribution pattern is consistent with the known clearance pathways for this class of compounds and with the biodistribution observed in non-human primates. Since the organs in the metabolism and excretion of [18F]GSK1482160 follows first-order kinetics, the effective half-life (Teff0.5, h) of metabolism in each tissue can be obtained via exponential fitting. The uptake at 1 h (Ct(1 h), SUV) was used to evaluate the distribution of [18F]GSK1482160. The selection of source organs (adrenals, brain, esophagus, eyes, gallbladder wall, LLI, small intestine, stomach, ULI, rectum, heart wall, kidneys, liver, lungs, pancreas, prostate, salivary glands, red marrow, bone surfaces, spleen, testes, thymus, thyroid, and urinary bladder wall) was based on OLINDA/EXM 2.2 (HERMES Medical Solutions AB, Sweden) software. By integrating the TAC trapezoidal area of each source organ through Origin, the absolute area AUC (MBq ~ h) was obtained, which was then divided by the injected dose (A0, MBq) to calculate the disintegration. The number of disintegrations of each organ was entered into the window of set kinetics by using the International Commission on Radiological Protection (ICRP) (Pub 89) adult male and female models. Tissue-weighing factors in OLINDA/EXM were used. For the target organ absorbed dose (AD (Gy)), the radiation weighting factor of F-18 was equal to 1. The effective dose (ED (Sv)) of each species was calculated via OLINDA/EXM. Moreover, the AD and ED duration of 3 h were predicted by fitting the TACs of 3 h from 1.3 h.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) acquisition

For NHP, MR images were acquired for each animal on a 3.0 T scanner (SIGNA Architect 3.0 T; GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA) via a 16-channel flex coil. The 3D-T1 brain volume imaging (BRAVO) scan used the median sagittal plane as the scan baseline, and the sagittal plane was scanned via the 3D-T1 BRAVO sequence. Human high-resolution 3D MRI was performed on a 3T MRI scanner (Prisma 3.0T, Simens, Erlangen, Germany). For the acquisition of T1 MR images, we utilized a 3-dimensional magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo sequence with specific parameter settings. The repetition time was set to 3000 ms, the echo time to 2.56 ms, and the flip angle to 7°. To achieve high-quality images, we used an acquisition matrix of 320 × 320, providing an in-plane resolution of 0.8 mm × 0.8 mm. The acquired images had a slice thickness of 0.8 mm, and a total of 208 sagittal slices were acquired.

Statistical analysis

All the data are expressed as the mean value ± one standard deviation (SD), all the outliers were included in the analysis, and no data were excluded. An unpaired parametric t test with the same SD was used. The Akaike information criterion (AIC), Schwartz criterion (SC) was used to evaluate the goodness-of-fit between the original data and the fit of the 2TCM and LoganAIF. A lower AIC and SC represent better fit. Due to the small sample sizes (human: n = 2–3; non-human primate: n = 4), statistical inference was not performed. Data are presented using descriptive statistics only (mean ± SD, or range where appropriate). For comparisons between kinetic models (1TCM vs. 2TCM) and between species (human vs. NHP dosimetry), agreement was assessed descriptively using Bland-Altman plots, with the 95% limits of agreement calculated as mean difference ± 1.96 × standard deviation of the differences. Correlation plots were generated to visualize the relationship between measurements, but no formal correlation coefficients or p-values are reported, as these would be unreliable with the small sample sizes.

Results

Automated radiosynthesis of [18F]GSK1482160

In this study, [18F]GSK1482160 was prepared via an automatic synthesis module (Fig. 2a), and the conversion rate of fluorine-18 labeling was approximately 31% − 37% (Fig. 2b). The raw product was further separated and purified by radio semipreparative HPLC (Supplementary Information (SI) Figure S1, Table S1), and [18F]GSK1482160 was collected from 8.5 min to 12.0 min in 35% acetonitrile at 4 mL/min (Fig. 2c). The radio HPLC peak of [18F]GSK1482160 ranged from 12.5 min to 13.5 min in the presence of 35% acetonitrile at 1 mL/min, and the time delay between radioactivity and ultraviolet light was 0.043 ± 0.004 min (Fig. 2d). The radiochemical yield (%) was 12.37 ± 1.98 (decay-corrected end of synthesis) (n = 5), and the radiochemical purity (%) was 96.32 ± 1.57. Gamma spectrometry was performed 12 h after radiochemical labeling, which confirmed that the fluorine-18 nuclide was qualified. Quality control of [18F]GSK1482160 injection was carried out in SI Tables S2 and S3 and Figure S2, which revealed that the [18F]GSK1482160 product satisfied the GMP production requirements.

Blood metabolic stability and kinetic models of [18F]GSK1482160 in the NHP brain

The metabolic stability of [18F]GSK1482160 in the blood of NHP patients was tested (Fig. 3a). Parent [18F]GSK1482160 appeared from 14 min to 17 min, and the metabolite appeared from 10 min to 13 min. When the area under the line was integrated, the proportion of [18F]GSK1482160 without injection was 94% at 0 min. As [18F]GSK1482160 remained in vivo longer, it was gradually metabolized, with 84% at 2 min, 79% at 5 min, 53% at 3 min and 44% at 90 min (Fig. 3b). For kinetic modeling, the blood input function of IDIF was acquired by drawing a pixel VOI from the carotid artery via PET/CT images (Fig. 3c), and BSIF was acquired through manual blood sampling from the femoral artery. The IDIF and BSIF values with and without blood metabolite correction are listed in Fig. 3d and e. The perfusion and distribution of [18F]GSK1482160 in the blood of IDIF and BSIF patients were fast, with a peak uptake time (tmax) of 0.3–0.4 min. Meanwhile, the uptake of BSIF was lower than that of IDIF. The PET/MRI data and distribution of [18F]GSK1482160 in the NHP brain at baseline are shown in Fig. 3f and g. [18F]GSK1482160 was distributed throughout the whole brain with low region specificity, and the thalamus (VTIDIF(2TCM) = 1.37 ± 0.51, VTBSIF(2TCM) = 2.46) had greater uptake. The detailed qualifications of different regions at baseline are listed in SI Tables S4 and S5. The TAC of the thalamus and the fitting curves of 2TCM and LoganAIF calculated by IDIF and BSIF with or without blood metabolite correction are listed in Fig. 4a and b, which shows that the 2TCM had better agreement between the original data and methods. 2TCM with blood metabolites corrected was the most suitable method with the lowest AIC and SC for quantifying [18F]GSK1482160 in the brain (Fig. 4c − 4e and SI Table S6). VT calculated by 2TCM and LoganAIF was strongly correlated, with Pearson’s r = 0.99 and r2 = 0.98, and scattered Bland‒Altman plots with differences near 0 within the 95% confidential interval (Fig. 4f − 4k and SI Table S7). However, the VTs calculated via IDIF and BSIF had good correlations (metabolite-corrected group: Pearson’s r = 0.95, r2 = 0.90; no corrected group: Pearson’s r = 0.92, r2 = 0.84), and values deviating from 0 for both groups in the Bland‒Altman plot (Fig. 4l and q and SI Table S7). It was revealed that [18F]GSK1482160 could cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB), with low regional heterogeneity in the brains of NHP patients at baseline. 2TCM with blood metabolite correction was a better method for quantifying the kinetics of [18F]GSK1482160 in the brain.

Safety, biodistribution and radiation dosimetry

The whole-body PET/CT images at different times for human and NHPs are shown in Fig. 7a and b. In addition to having a low background level in muscle, [18F]GSK1482160 is distributed throughout the whole body and is metabolized through the kidneys and liver. The uptake and changes of TACs from humans were similar to those of NHPs, and from TACs (Fig. 7c − 7 h), [18F]GSK1482160 is mainly excreted the kidneys and liver (approximately 0.03–0.11 h) faster than did the brain (0.22–0.42 h). The metabolic organ [18F]GSK1482160 was absorbed earlier in the kidneys (0.03 ± 0.00 h for human, 0.04 ± 0.00 h for NHP) than in the liver (0.08 ± 0.01 h for human, 0.11 ± 0.05 h for NHP). The effective half-life of tissues for human was shorter than that of NHP, but for the brain, it was longer (3.38 ± 0.39 h) than that of NHP (1.54 ± 0.45 h). The detailed quantification is listed in SI Table S10.

The human liver had the largest AD, with 28.70 µGy/MBq for 3 h in females and 31.40 µGy/MBq for 3 h in males. The NHP kidneys had the largest AD, which was 47.53 ± 10.44 µGy/MBq for 3 h. The details are listed in Table 2 and SI Table S10. These data reflect effective dose for human adults. The dose-limiting organ was the liver with a mean dose across all participants of 30.1 µGy/MBq followed by the adrenals at 22.8 µGy/MBq. The mean ED of the females were 10.90 µSv/MBq, and the EDs of the males were 8.88 µSv/MBq. Taking the maximum 10.90 µSv/MBq as a reference and assuming that each patient needed to inject approximately 250 MBq (6.7 mCi) of [18F]GSK1482160 for PET scanning, the internal exposure dose in each PET scan was nearly only 2.73 mSv (0.273 rem) with the critical organ, the liver, receiving the maximum allowed exposure of 50 mSv (5 rem). Considering the damage caused by external CT irradiation and the annual limited exposure dose (10 mSv/year), patients can receive 3 [18F]GSK1482160 PET scans per year. The EDs of the published F-18 labeled probes are listed in SI Table S11. In conclusion, the AD and ED produced by [18F]GSK1482160 were within typical levels for fluorine-18-labeled tracers.

Discussion

This study reported the automated radiosynthesis of the P2X7R-targeting PET tracer [18F]GSK1482160 and evaluated its safety, radiation dosimetry, biodistribution and radiopharmaceutical kinetics in human and NHPs. [18F]GSK1482160 is distributed mainly in the kidney, liver and urinary bladder and is metabolized through the kidney and liver, similar to [11C]GSK1482160482160 [19–21]. For human livers, kidneys, adrenals, the small intestine, the pancreas and LLI have higher absorbed doses, which are within the safe limits of the ICRP [35]. The effective doses were 7.53 ± 1.20 µSv/MBq (for 1.3 h) and 9.89 ± 1.43 µSv/MBq (for 3.0 h), which were lower than those of [18F]FDG (18 µSv/MBq) [36], [18F]F-L-DOPA (21 µSv/MBq) [37] or other PET probes targeting P2X7R [18F]JNJ-64,413,739 (22 µSv/MBq) [22]. In conclusion, [18F]GSK1482160482160 satisfied the requirement to be a PET probe in terms of radiation dose safety.

Two methods of determining arterial blood input function were explored for kinetics: IDIF collected from the carotid artery through imaging and BSIF collected from the femoral artery through blood sampling. In the clearance stage, the uptake of IDIF in the blood was greater than that of BSIF, possibly because the radioactivity of IDIF originates from [18F]GSK1482160 in whole blood and the radioactivity of BSIF originates from the plasma. Research has shown that P2X7R is widely distributed in cells of hematopoietic lineages, such as macrophages, eosinophils, neutrophils, and T and B lymphocytes [38], possibly comparable to [18F]GSK1482160.

The total volume distribution (VT) for tracer kinetics calculated by 2TCM and LoganAIF with or without metabolite correction had a good correlation and concordance. The 2TCM with metabolite correction was the most suitable with the lowest AIC and SC. VT values calculated via IDIF and BSIF had good correlations but nonideal concordance. The reasons for the nonideal concordance caused by different input functions were nonartificial, such as the time difference in blood perfusion, the proportion of plasma in whole blood and the distributions of receptors in the blood. Owing to good correlation (metabolite corrected group: slope = 0.69, intercept = 0.33, Pearson’s r = 0.95, r2 = 0.91, no corrected group: slope = 0.59, intercept = 0.16, Pearson’s r = 0.92, r2 = 0.85), the results of IDIF could be corrected to BSIF. There was good agreement between the VT values of BSIF and corrected BSIF, and a scatter plot of the Bland‒Altman plot, with differences near 0 within the 95% confidential interval (SI Figure S3). When the clinical scan environment supports blood sampling conditions, BSIF is the most standard method. However, the method of IDIF is also feasible with good correlation; VT has the same distribution ratio as BSIF, and the DVR of IDIF is close to that of BSIF (SI Figure S4).

P2X7R has been extensively reported in neuroinflammation, and this study explored the radiopharmaceutical kinetics and target specificity of [18F]GSK1482160 in the brains of human and NHPs. In human subjects, [18F]GSK1482160 showed relatively uniform distribution throughout the brain, with similar VT values across all examined regions, indicating no substantial regional heterogeneity (Figure S5). Descriptive statistics for regional VT values are provided in SI Table S12. Due to the small sample size (n = 3), formal statistical comparisons between regions were not performed (SI Table S12 and Figure S5). Like [11C]GSK1482160, [18F]JNJ-64,413,739 and [11C]JNJ54173717, the putamen, thalamus and hippocampus had greater uptake of VT (2TCM), respectively. According to the information provided in the database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000089041-P2RX7), the VT (n = 3) of [18F]GSK1482160 and the RNA expression of P2X7R in human are listed in SI Table S13. In the correlation analysis of [18F]GSK1482160 and P2X7R, Pearson’s r equaled 0.82, which revealed that the distributions of [18F]GSK1482160 and the RNA expression of P2X7R were positively correlated. Therefore, [18F]GSK1482160 could be used to quantify the expression of P2X7R in vivo.

This study has several limitations: First, the human dosimetry estimates were derived from only two healthy participants. While these data provide initial safety estimates to support first-in-human use, they should be considered preliminary, and definitive human dosimetry will require evaluation in a larger cohort. Second, the kinetic modeling analysis in humans was limited to three healthy subjects. Although the results are consistent with the more extensive non-human primate data (n = 4), which included invasive arterial sampling and full compartmental modeling, the small human sample size precludes definitive conclusions about the optimal kinetic model or the quantitative accuracy of the derived parameters. Future studies with larger sample sizes, including both healthy volunteers and patient populations, are necessary to fully validate [18F]GSK1482160 as a quantitative PET tracer for cerebral P2X7R imaging. Also the NHP and human data were collected by different organizations and different equipment, and the PET data collection methods used for each species were slightly different. Although [19F]GSK1482160 was used in blocking experiment, it remained to show the selectivity as well as the saturable binding did not include substantial contributions from secondary targets. Although P2X7R has been extensively reported in neuroinflammation, it remains to further explore [18F]GSK1482160 is sensitive to neuroinflammation models. This study aimed to evaluate the safety, dosimetry and kinetics of [18F]GSK1482160 in a random normal population without considering subject genotyping and RNA single-strand conformation polymorphisms.

Conclusion

The results of this study revealed that the P2X7R-targeting PET tracer [18F]GSK1482160 was within the safe radiation dose range. Both the 2TCM and LoganAIF models can be used to quantify the radiopharmaceutical kinetics of [18F]GSK1482160 in the brain. IDIF and BSIF could be used as blood input functions, and the blood input function with arterial blood metabolite correction was a better method. In conclusion, [18F]GSK1482160 is a safe and promising PET tracer for translational research of neuroinflammation related diseases.
