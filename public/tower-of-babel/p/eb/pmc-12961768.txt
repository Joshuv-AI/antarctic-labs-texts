The critical gap in prevention: knowledge of periconception folic acid supplementation and its predictors among pregnant women in Ethiopia
Authors: Timsel Girma, Desalegn Tsegaw Hibstu, Befikadu Amare, Geracho Garolla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFolic acid deficiency is the most common pregnancy-related micronutrient deficiency, leading to fetal neural tube defects (NTDs). Periconceptional folic acid supplementation is highly effective in reducing NTDs by 72%-86%. However, awareness of this essential prevention method remains limited in lower-income countries, contributing to a high rate of preventable birth defects. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the knowledge and factors associated with periconceptional folic acid supplementation among pregnant women attending the Antenatal care clinic at Dilla University Teaching Hospital in 2024.MethodThis institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted at Dilla University Teaching Hospital from January to December 2024. A systematic random sampling technique was used to enroll 337 eligible pregnant mothers attending Antenatal Care. Bivariate and multiple logistic regression analyses were performed to identify associated factors. The strength of association was reported using the Adjusted Odds Ratio (AOR) with a Confidence Interval (CI), with p-values considered statistically significant.ResultOnly 8.9% (95% CI: 5.85%-11.96%) of pregnant mothers demonstrated good knowledge of periconceptional folic acid supplementation. Factors significantly associated with improved knowledge included higher education (AOR 5.13), current family planning use (AOR 4.06), having a planned pregnancy (AOR 3.59), being in the 26–30 age group (AOR 2.33), and employment status (AOR 1.63).ConclusionKnowledge of periconceptional folic acid supplementation is extremely poor (8.9%) among pregnant women in the study area. Factors like higher education, family planning use, and planned pregnancy are strong determinants of awareness. There is an urgent need to implement targeted health education programs integrated within Antenatal Care and Family Planning services to improve knowledge and reduce the incidence of preventable NTDs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40748-026-00254-x.Keywords: Antenatal care, Folic acid, Knowledge, Periconception, Pregnancy

Introduction

Optimal maternal nutrition, including carbohydrates, proteins, essential minerals, and multivitamins, is crucial for normal fetal development and long-term health, as the gestational period is a key factor in fetal development. Pregnant women are at higher risk of micronutrient deficiency due to the increased nutritional needs of both the mother and fetus. Folic acid deficiency is one of the most common micronutrient deficiencies in pregnant women [1–6].

Folic acid is a synthetic dietary supplement present in artificially enriched foods and pharmaceutical vitamins and is a fully oxidized monoglutamyl form of the water-soluble vitamin. The bioavailability of folate ranges from 50% for food to 100% for supplementation if taken on an empty stomach. It is key for the regulation of normal development of nerve cells, prevention of neural tube defects (NTDs) and other birth defects, erythropoiesis, and promotion of normal human growth and development. Additionally, to those benefits, intake of folic acid in the 1st trimester of pregnancy will increase cognitive performance; specifically, it improves vocabulary and verbal skills in children [7–11].

Since 1965, when the deficiency of folic acid and spinal bifida was detected, maternal supplementation with folic acid during the periconception period has significantly reduced the incidence of spinal bifida and other neural defects by 72–86% in both normal and high-risk pregnancies [12].

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that all women, 1 month from the moment they commence trying to conceive until 12 weeks of gestation, should take 400 µg a folic acid daily for NTD prevention [13, 14].

Recent studies and global health bodies affirm that routine gynecological visits and Family Planning (FP) appointments represent an ideal and often-missed opportunity to counsel women of reproductive age on adjusting their daily folic acid intake. This focus aligns with the World Health Organization’s (WHO) preconception care (PCC) interventional package, which explicitly includes nutritional supplementation, notably folic acid [15, 16].

Despite this clear international recommendation and the proven efficacy of the intervention, folic acid supplementation remains grossly insufficient in numerous countries, including Ethiopia. This failure is directly attributable to the critically low utilization of formal PCC services (less than 30%) and the resultant poor maternal knowledge regarding the critical need for supplementation [15–18]. Some studies have revealed that maternal conception of folic acid was significantly increased after improvement of maternal knowledge [16, 19]. The study sought to determine the current knowledge status of periconceptional folic acid supplementation and the socio-demographic and reproductive factors influencing this knowledge among pregnant women receiving ANC services at Dilla University Teaching Hospital in 2024.

Method and materials

Study setting

This cross-sectional, institution-based study took place at Dilla University Teaching Hospital (DUTH) between January and December 2024. DUTH, one of the largest teaching hospitals in the southern region of Ethiopia, is strategically located 360 km south of Addis Ababa on the major route toward Nairobi. It is situated approximately 411 km from the Ethiopia-Kenya border and serves the populations of the Gedeo Zone, West Guji Zone, and Sidama Region.

Ethical and registration details

The study obtained ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Pharma College. The research team adhered to the STROBE guidelines for observational studies and formally registered the study with the unique identifier researchregistry10401.

Selection of participants

All pregnant women who attended ANC clinic at DUTH during the study period and provided signed informed consent were included. Nevertheless, pregnant women were excluded if they had psychiatric disorders and communication barriers (speaking and reading problems).

Sample size and sampling techniques

The sample size was calculated using EPI Info based on the minimum detectable adjusted odds ratio(AOR), taken from the previous study done, which showed the history of spontaneous abortion is one of the predictors with an AOR of 12.64 [20]. Taking a 5% level of significance, a power of 80%, a 95% CI, and considering a 10% non-response rate, a total of 345 respondents were selected through a systematic random sampling method.

Data quality management and data collection method

The study questionnaires were adapted and adopted from existing literature. To ensure accuracy and consistency, they underwent a rigorous process: the questionnaires were first translated into Amharic and then back translated into English by language experts. Following this translation process, the instrument’s clarity was tested by pretesting it on 5% of the total sample size at Yirgachefe Primary Hospital before the main data collection began [20–22].

Following securing verbal and informed written consent from participants, the data was collected through face-to-face patient interviews. The questionnaires include socio-demographic and economic characteristics, obstetric characteristics, medical illnesses, and questions related to maternal knowledge of periconception folic acid supplementation. Data collection was conducted by three B.Sc. midwives under the supervision of one supervisor and the principal investigator.

Operational definition

Good knowledge: refer to women who respond more than average out of 12 knowledge-related questions about folic acid supplementation [21].

Poor knowledge: refer to women who respond less than average out of 12 knowledge-related questions about folic acid supplementation [21].

Data processing and analysis

After completing data collection, the data were manually checked for completeness, coded, and entered into Epi Info version 7. Subsequently, the data was transferred to SPSS version 25 for analysis. Categorical variables were summarized as numbers and percentages. To identify factors associated with pregnant women’s knowledge of periconception Folic acid consumption, a bivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to determine the association of each independent variable with the outcome variable. A crude odds ratio (COR) with 95% confidence intervals was obtained. We considered variables with a p-value less than or equal to 0.25 as candidates for the multivariable analysis. Consequently, those variables were analyzed using multivariable logistic regression. Furthermore, a multicollinearity test was performed using the variance inflation factor, and no multicollinearity was detected between variables (VIF > 10). The strength of the statistical association between the response and predictor variable was measured by adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals. P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Socio-demographic and socioeconomic factors

The study included 337 pregnant mothers who attended ANC at DUTH for analysis and achieved a high response rate (97.7%). Among 337 subjects analyzed, the majority were young, with 54.9% aged 18–25 years. The participants were predominantly married (98.2%), urban residents (83.7%), and largely composed of housewives (51.5%). One hundred thirty-three (39.5%) were from the Gedeo ethnicity, and 51.9% were Protestant. Educational background was modest, with 43.0% having completed primary school. (Table 1)

Knowledge about periconceptional folic acid supplementation

The results indicate that information exposure regarding periconceptional folic acid was low, with only 23.7% of the 337 participating mothers reporting that they had received information on the topic. Bivariate logistic regression identified that family planning, type of pregnancy, educational level, occupation, age, and gestational age had p ≤ 0.25 and were selected as candidates for multiple logistic regression analysis.

Discussion

The study found that only 8.9% of pregnant mothers who attended ANC clinic had adequate knowledge of periconceptional folic acid supplementation. This finding agrees with a study conducted by Mergia et al., which revealed that 7.5% of pregnant mothers who attend ANC in Hawassa town public health facility had good knowledge of periconception folic acid supplementation [20]. The consistent finding of extremely low knowledge (our and Mergia et al.) likely stems from several interconnected systemic issues common in lower-income settings, like a lack of preconception care [17].

While our findings align with those from Hawassa, the observed knowledge level (8.9%) is significantly lower than the 69% reported in a study conducted in Addis Ababa. This stark disparity can be primarily attributed to major differences in the socio-demographic characteristics of the study population. The Addis Ababa study had a vastly higher proportion of highly educated participants, with nearly half of their respondents being college and university graduates. In contrast, most of our participants had only completed primary school (49.3%). Our findings showed higher education to be the strongest predictor of knowledge; this difference in education level likely accounts for much of the observed knowledge gap. Additionally, their sample consisted almost entirely of urban residents (99.8%), who typically have better access to information (media, internet) and specialized healthcare facilities. While many of our participants were also urban (83.7%), the overall access to high-quality specialized health education and information flow is generally superior in the capital city compared to remote areas [21].

Other Studies also disagree and reported a higher magnitude than ours. The clarification for such a difference might be the poor utilization of preconception care services, Socio-economic, cultural differences of those populations from ours, and lack of uniformly accessible and nationally published reference guidelines for periconceptional folic acid supplementation contribute significantly to the observed variation [18, 23–25].

In our results, Maternal educational level, family planning, planned pregnancy, maternal age, and employment had statistically significant associations with their knowledge of periconception folic acid supplementation.

Our results demonstrate that educational attainment is the most potent predictor of knowledge, where attending college and higher levels of education increased the chance of a mother having good knowledge of periconceptional folic acid supplementation by 5.13 times. Consistent with our findings, Getaneh et al. disclosed that a higher level of education had a statistically significant association with women’s perception of folic acid supplementation knowledge. Similarly, other studies show that higher educational status enhances health literacy, improves the ability to seek out and understand complex health information (like the concept of preconception timing), and facilitates better communication with healthcare providers [26, 27].

Likewise, studies from China and Nepal also demonstrate that maternal educational attainment is a strong predictor of good maternal knowledge regarding periconception care, such as FA supplementation. This finding can be explained as mothers with a higher level of education have the capability and better chance of having information related to periconception care, including FA supplementation [28, 29].

Mothers utilizing FP demonstrated significantly better knowledge of periconception folic acid supplementation, with their odds of good knowledge being 4.06 times higher than those not using FP. This finding is consistent with research by Mergia et al. and Yitayal et al., which also noted that FP use improves periconceptional nutritional knowledge. This result also emphasizes that while FP services are a successful “touchpoint” for education, the current system may be failing to reach women who opt out of traditional family planning, a nuance that warrants further investigation [15, 20].

Our analysis indicates that pregnancy intention plays a pivotal role in knowledge levels, with mothers of planned pregnancies exhibiting nearly a fourfold increase in the odds of understanding folic acid requirements. These findings parallel those of researchers in the UK and Canada, reinforcing the notion that pregnancy planning is an independent driver of maternal awareness. Conversely, the significantly lower knowledge levels among women with unintentional pregnancies highlight a critical vulnerability, suggesting that health education often fails to reach those who are not actively preparing for conception [12, 23].

In contrast, a study from Korea reported that having a planned pregnancy doesn’t associate with maternal knowledge of preconception folic acid supplementation. However, this inconsistency is likely due to ceiling effects or population characteristics; most Korean respondents (> 90%) had planned their pregnancies, whereas our study observed a much higher rate of unintended pregnancies. Consequently, the distinct gap in knowledge we found between planners and non-planners may be more visible in contexts where unplanned pregnancy remains a prevalent public health challenge [30].

Maternal age was a robust predictor of folic acid knowledge in this study (AOR = 2.33), paralleling findings from Turkey and other scholarly works The disparity between those over 26 and those under 25 likely stems from increased maturity and a heightened likelihood of engaging with health services [21, 29]. As suggested by scholars in the field, older mothers may have greater exposure to reproductive health information, whereas younger women may require more focused outreach to bridge the knowledge gap regarding periconceptional supplementation [31].

In our study, we found that mothers who were employed were 1.63 times more likely to be aware of the importance of taking folic acid supplements during the periconception period. This finding was consistent with a study from China, which found that employed women were more knowledgeable than housewives [32].

Likewise, another study showed that housewives had a lower level of knowledge about periconceptional folic acid than employed women. This could be the fact that employed women have better access to social media and other sources of information [28].

Limitation: Although knowledge levels are influenced by many factors, this study was limited to examining individual-related factors. The limited sample size, in comparison to the number of variables, resulted in a slightly broader confidence interval for certain measurements.

Conclusions

Pregnant mothers with good knowledge of periconceptional folic acid supplementation were found to be limited. Factors such as maternal educational level, family planning, planned pregnancy, age between 26 and 30, and employment status were independent predictors of their good knowledge.

Recommendation

The Ministry of Health needs to integrate comprehensive counseling on folic acid supplementation (its benefits, required timing, and dosage) into FP, community outreach program and integrated youth friendly services. Especially, emphasis must be placed on the preconception period. Launch accessible and culturally sensitive mass media campaigns, radio, and social media to raise public awareness about the importance of folic acid before and during early pregnancy.

Researchers need to conduct qualitative studies to explore the barriers, beliefs, and cultural practices that specifically contribute to the low knowledge and poor uptake of periconceptional folic acid supplementation. Additionally, perform interventional studies to evaluate the effectiveness of the new integrated health education programs on improving both knowledge and the actual intake of folic acid among women in the region.
