Enantiocomplementary Bioreduction of Flexible Ring N‐(3‐Oxobutyl)Heterocycles Providing Enantiopure Chiral Fragments for Drug Discovery
Authors: Máté Gergő Honvári, Levente András Mócza, Bence Attila Kucsinka, Pál Csuka, Viktória Bódai, Diana Maria Scrob, László Poppe, Gábor Hornyánszky
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, the bioreduction of prochiral N‐(3‐oxobutyl)heterocycles comprising various (partially) saturated, flexible rings is explored using microbial whole‐cell ketoreductases such as wild‐type yeast strains including baker's yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) and Escherichia coli cells expressing two enantiocomplementary recombinant alcohol dehydrogenases. Initially, four wild‐type yeast strains are screened for ketoreductase activity on a series of nine flexible N‐heterocycles with prochiral carbonyl group in the N‐(3‐oxobutyl) sidechain. The yeast strains resulted in the corresponding (S)‐alcohols with a low to moderate conversions. Using recombinant alcohol dehydrogenase whole‐cell preparations as biocatalysts ((S)‐selective ADH from Rhodococcus aetherivorans (RaADH) and (R)‐selective ADH from Lactobacillus kefir (LkADH)) resulted in higher conversions in most cases, while maintaining the full enantiotopic selectivity. Usually, the preparative‐scale bioreductions showed comparable or even higher conversions than those observed in the small‐scale screening reactions, resulting in virtually enantiopure (S)‐ and (R)‐alcohols (ee > 99%), which are promising chiral fragments with a high degree of drug‐likeness. Docking studies confirmed the absolute configuration of the forming (S)‐ and (R)‐alcohols.Keywords: alcohol dehydrogenase, enantiocomplementary, ketoreductases, whole‐cell bioreduction, yeast

Introduction

Most active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) are sp2‐rich, rigid molecules. This phenomenon can be explained by taking a closer look at existing synthetic methodologies, particularly parallel syntheses and cross‐coupling reactions, frequently employed in drug discovery. Nonetheless, it is well established based on the relevant literature that compounds with greater saturation (“3D” molecules) are more likely to succeed at each stage of drug development, from discovery to registered drug.[ 1 ] Molecular shape diversity is essential for the comprehensive bioactivity of molecular libraries,[ 2 ] therefore, fragment‐based drug discovery (FBDD), which utilizes a diverse set of chemical scaffolds and functionalities to cover chemical space, is an effective method for identifying new chemical leads.[ 3 ] FBDD focuses on creating smaller (< 20 heavy atoms), diverse sets of simple molecules having weak interactions with targets of interest and likely adhere to a rule of three (rule of three: molecular weight < 300 Da, number of rotatable bonds < 3, and cLogP < 3).[ 4 , 5 , 6 – 7 ] Thus, FBDD can cover broad swaths of chemical space and allows the use of creative chemistry. FBDD has truly come of age, demonstrating its usefulness in many drug discovery programs, providing clinical candidates and multiple approved drugs.[ 8 ]

An excellent review assessed every FDA‐approved (United States Food and Drug Administration) small‐molecule API (1086 unique drugs) and reported on the top 25 most utilized nitrogen heterocycles found in their structure. The study concluded that 84% of the analyzed APIs (910 unique drugs) contain at least one nitrogen atom, while 59% (640 unique drugs) incorporate a nitrogen heterocycle.[ 9 ] Piperidine was the most abundant heterocycle, showing up in 72 unique APIs, for example, in the anticholinergic drug Flavoxate.[ 10 ] More specifically, the 4‐methylpiperidin moiety appeared in the anticoagulant argatroban,[ 11 ] while the 4‐benzylpiperidin and the ethyl piperidine‐4‐carboxylate moieties were present in the antihistamine fexofenadine,[ 12 ] and the antiperistaltic drug Diphenoxylate,[ 13 ] respectively. Piperazine was identified as the third most frequent nitrogen heterocycle, showing up in 59 of the analyzed APIs, for example, in the tyrosine kinase inhibitor Bosutinib, which contains the 4‐methylpiperazine moiety.[ 14 ] Pirrolidine was the fifth most abundant, appearing in 37 APIs, for example in the Janus kinase inhibitor Fedratinib.[ 15 ] Among the analyzed APIs, morpholine was the 17th most recurring heterocycle, being present in 12 unique APIs, for instance in the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) inhibitor Gefitinib.[ 16 ] Tetrahydroisoquinoline was identified as the 19th most abundant nitrogen heterocycle, occurring in 11 of the analyzed APIs, e.g., in the ACE inhibitor Quinapril.[ 17 ] Thiomorpholine wasn’t among the top 25 heterocycles, but it still showed up in one API, the antiparasitic drug Nifurtimox.[ 18 ]

Among the nine heterocyclic rings examined in the present study four are piperidine‐based [piperidine (4c), 4‐methylpiperidine (4d), 4‐benzylpiperidine (4e), and ethyl piperidine‐4‐carboxylate (4f)], while the remaining five [morpholine (4a), thiomorpholine (4b), pyrrolidine (4g), 4‐methylpiperazine (4h), and tetrahydroisoquinoline (4i)] also comprise a five‐ or six‐membered saturated ring, or a partially saturated, fuzed ring system (4i) (Scheme 1 ). These heterocycles contain multiple carbon atoms with sp3‐hybridization, which results in a nonplanar ("3D”) structure. From these heterocycles, after aza‐Michael reaction with methyl vinyl ketone (5), prochiral N‐(3‐oxobutyl)heterocycles could be prepared. Throughout the synthesis of pharmaceutically relevant molecules, an enantiotopic selective bioreduction step of such ketones could result in enantiopure secondary alcohols, which are suitable building blocks to incorporate these drug‐like, heterocyclic 3D fragments into the target structure.

Synthesis of flexible ring N‐(3‐oxobutyl)heterocycles 2a–i and their chemical and bioreduction to racemic and optically active alcohols (±)‐1a–i, (S)‐1a–i, or (R)1a–i, respectively.

Chiral compounds are important intermediates and building blocks in the synthesis of APIs, where enantiopurity is of utmost importance due to the strict regulatory requirements imposed by pharmaceutical authorities. Asymmetric secondary hydroxyl functionalities show up in numerous pharmaceuticals, for example, in the NK1 receptor antagonist Aprepitant,[ 19 ] in the beta blocker Propranolol,[ 20 ] or in the HMG‐CoA reductase inhibitor Atorvastatin.[ 21 ] Thus, converting the prochiral N‐(3‐oxobutyl)heterocycles into the corresponding enantiopure chiral alcohols results in promising chiral fragments with a high degree of drug‐likeness. For example, Fedrilate, a centrally acting cough suppressant which was patented as a mucolytic by Union Chimique Belge (UCB ), contains the 4‐morpholinobutan‐2‐ol moiety.[ 22 ] Furthermore, the same moiety can be found in an experimental molecule (compound 193), which showed an IC50 value of 1.2 μM on the Nagoya University‐Gastric Cancer‐3 (NUGC‐3) cell line during in vitro antitumor activity assays.[ 23 ]

Recently, the utilization of ketoreductases (KREDs, also known as alcohol dehydrogenases, ADHs) became a widely recognized practice in the commercial, industrial‐scale synthesis of enantiopure chiral alcohols.[ 24 , 25 – 26 ] In order to develop a synthetically viable, high‐conversion KRED‐catalyzed process, efficient cofactor regeneration is necessary, so that the expensive cofactor (NADH or NADPH) can be recycled.[ 27 , 28 ] Plenty of cofactor regeneration systems have been described so far, employing, e.g., 2‐propanol, glucose, or lactose as the cosubstrate for the reduction of the forming NAD+ or NADP+. Perhaps the most straightforward option is to utilize a whole‐cell system with inexpensive and easy‐to‐handle 2‐propanol as the cosubstrate, being converted to acetone by the same KRED, while the cofactor is regenerated. This method also ensures the easy scalability of bio‐reduction.

Reduction of ketones by whole‐cell biocatalysts is an efficient technology due to several reasons. Whole‐cell wild‐type yeast strains are useful biocatalysts in biosynthetic ketoreductions,[ 29 ] ensuring that the regeneration of the intrinsic cofactor (NADH or NADPH) happens in a highly efficient manner in their whole cells. KREDs are undoubtedly valuable biocatalysts, since various (S)‐ and (R)‐selective KREDs have seen industrial application during the synthesis of pharmaceuticals and other high‐value products.[ 25 , 30 , 31 ] For example, the alcohol dehydrogenase from Rhodococcus aetherivorans (RaADH) is an excellent biocatalyst, which accepts a wide range of ketones as substrates to produce (S)‐alcohols with high enantiopurity.[ 32 , 33 ] Another example is the alcohol dehydrogenase from Lactobacillus kefir (LkADH), which has established itself as a reliable and efficient biocatalyst with a broad substrate scope for the production of (R)‐alcohols.[ 34 , 35 ]

The objective of this study is to gain enantiopure chiral “3D” fragments with asymmetric alcohol functionalities [(S)‐1a–i or (R)‐1a–i]—that can be employed in FBDD—by enantiocomplementary bioreductions of ketones comprising flexible N‐heterocycles 2a‐i applying various biocatalysts with ketoreductase activity (Scheme 1).

This concept is surprisingly rarely exemplified in literature. Therefore, our study aimed to, 1) perform a preliminary assessment of the ketoreductase activity of numerous wild‐type yeast strains including baker's yeast on a set of prochiral N‐(3‐oxobutyl)heterocycles with (partially) saturated, flexible rings, 2) utilize enantiocomplementary alcohol dehydrogenase enzymes (RaADH and LkADH) to produce the (S)‐ and (R)‐enantiomers of the corresponding alcohols, and 3) perform bioreductions at preparative scale to isolate and characterize the enantiopure products.

These enantiopure (S)‐ or (R)‐3D heterocycles with asymmetric hydroxyl functions can be applied as members of chiral FBDD libraries and as potential building blocks for the preparation of APIs. Starting from the corresponding flexible heterocycles (HC), after aza‐Michael addition reaction, prochiral ketones are acquired. With the help of enantiocomplementary KREDs [(S)‐ or (R)‐KREDs], mediating enantiotopic selective reduction (ESR) reactions, both enantiomers of the flexible heterocyclic alcohol fragments can be prepared and utilized as potential building blocks in chiral FBDD libraries. Depending on the availability of reactive positions on the 3D heterocycle, the fragments may be extended only at the alcohol functions or can be utilized as central fragments.

Experimental Section

Chemicals

The (partially) saturated heterocycles, reagents, andsolvents were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), Merck KGaA (Darmstadt, Germany), and Molar Chemicals (Halásztelek, Hungary).

Whole‐Cell Alcohol Dehydrogenase Preparations

Lyophilized Yeast Whole‐Cell Preparations

The fermentation and lyophilization of the yeast strains were described earlier.[ 36 , 37 , 38 – 39 ] Further details about the yeast biocatalysts can be found in Section S1.3. of the Supporting Information.

Recombinant Whole‐Cell Alcohol Dehydrogenase Preparations

The recombinant KREDS used in our study [the (S)‐selective R. aetherivorans alcohol dehydrogenase (RaADH)[ 32 , 33 ] and the (R)‐selective L. kefir alcohol dehydrogenase (LkADH)[ 34 , 35 ]] were comprehensively investigated on various substrates, including optimization of the bioreductions. The codon‐optimized genes of LkADH (Uniprot ID: Q6WVP7) and RaADH (Uniprot ID: N1MBR5; later replaced by the A0AAT8XUI2 entry representing an M222K mutant compared to N1MBR5) were purchased from GenScript (Rijswijk, the Netherlands). The genes were placed into a pET19b vector with NdeI and BamHI restriction enzyme sites. The plasmids were transformed into the Escherichia coli (E. coli)BL21 strain with the heat shock method. The transformed cells were grown in 5 mL of luria bertani (LB) medium (tryptone, 50 mg; yeast extract, 25 mg; NaCl, 50 mg; pH = 7.0) supplemented with carbenicillin (50 µg mL−1) overnight at 37 °C at 180 rpm. For protein expression, 250 mL of autoinduction media (Na2HPO4, 1.5 g; NaH2PO4, 0.75 g; tryptone, 5 g; yeast extract, 1.25 g; NaCl, 1.25 g) and 10 mL of carbon supplementing solution (glycerol, 1.89 g; glucose, 125 mg; lactose, 500 mg) were inoculated with 1 mL of overnight culture. The cultures were shaken at 37 °C at 180 rpm for 16 h (reaching typically OD600≈1). The cells were separated with centrifugation at 4 °C at 2500 g for 20 min. After separation, the cells were resuspended in 10 mL of phosphate buffer (pH = 7.5, 50 mM) and centrifuged again with the previous settings. The cell pellet (typically ≈1.6 g wet weight) was resuspended in 10 mL of phosphate buffer (pH = 7.5, 50 mM) and used directly for the biocatalysis.

Analytical Methods

NMR spectra were recorded in deuterated solvents (CDCl3 in the case of free bases, DMSO in the case of hydrochloride salts and D2O in the case of piperazine dihydrochloride salt) on a Bruker DRX‐500 spectrometer operating at 500 MHz for 1H and 126 MHz for 13C. NMR signals were given in ppm on the δ scale. Infrared (IR) spectra were recorded on a Bruker ALPHA FT‐IR spectrometer (in ATR mode), and wavenumbers of bands were listed in cm−1. Optical rotations were measured on a Perkin‐Elmer 241 polarimeter at the 578 nm line of mercury and at the D‐line of sodium (589 nm). The polarimeter was calibrated with measurements of both enantiomers of menthol. Thin layer chromatography (TLC) was carried out on precoated TLC ALUGRAM Xtra SIL G/UV254 sheets (Macherey & Nagel); spots were visualized under UV light (254 or 365 nm) and/or with phosphomolybdic acid staining. Column chromatography was carried out with Geduran Si 60 (Merck) silica gel. Gas chromatography (GC) was used to determine the conversion and the enantiomeric excess of the forming alcohols (S)‐ or (R)‐1a–i in bioreductions from ketones 2a–i. GC analyses were performed with an Agilent 4890 GC equipped with flame ionization detector (FID) and Hydrodex β‐6TBDM column [25 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm film with heptakis‐(2,3‐di‐O‐methyl‐6‐O‐t‐butyldimethylsilyl)‐β‐cyclodextrin; Macherey & Nagel (Düren, Germany); H2 carrier gas (injector: 250 °C, detector: 250 °C, head pressure: 12 psi, split ratio: 50:1)] and an Agilent 5890 GC equipped with FID detector and HP‐5 column [30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm film with (5%‐phenyl)methylpolysiloxane; Agilent Technologies (Santa Clara, California, U.S.); H2 carrier gas (injector: 250 °C, detector: 250 °C, head pressure: 15 psi, split ratio: 50:1)]. The temperature programs and retention times are included in the Supporting Information (Table S2).

Determination of Conversion of Ketones 2a‐i in Bioreductions by GC

To determine the conversion of the bioreductions of ketones 2a‐i, samples (100 μL) were taken from the reaction mixtures. These samples were extracted with EtOAc (1000 μL), and the organic layer was then separated and dried over sodium sulfate. The decanted EtOAc extracts were analyzed directly. Since the alcohols 1a–i from the corresponding ketones 2a–i have the same number of carbon atoms, and very similar molar masses, the molar response factors of alcohols to ketones (GC equipped with FID detector) were assumed to be 1. As experiments in triplicates with the model compound 2a indicated a variation within 2%, further experiments were performed as single series.

Determination of Enantiomeric Excess of Alcohols (S)‐ or (R)‐1a–i in Bioreductions by GC

To determine the enantiomeric excess of alcohols (S)‐ or (R)‐1a–i forming in the bioreductions, freshly distilled acetyl chloride (10 μL) was added to the samples obtained as described in Section 3.3.1, and the acylation was conducted at room temperature for 1 min to give the desired acetylated alcohols. The excess of the acylating agent was quenched with water (10 μL, room temperature, one minute quenching time), the organic phase was dried over sodium sulfate and the decanted EtOAc‐extracts were analyzed directly.

Synthesis of N‐(3‐Oxobutyl)Heterocycles 2a‐i

To a cooled (−5 °C) solution of the corresponding (partially) saturated heterocycle (4a–i, 15 mmol) in dry diethyl ether (20 mL) was added a solution of methyl vinyl ketone (5, 19.5 mmol, 1.3 eq., in 10 mL dry diethyl ether) dropwise over ≈30 min. The resulting mixture was allowed to reach room temperature, then was stirred for another 30 min. Subsequently, 2 eq. of 20% HCl in EtOAc was added dropwise to the reaction mixture to precipitate the hydrochloride (2a–g, i) or dihydrochloride (2h) salt of these compounds. The precipitate was filtered, washed with diethyl ether (3 × 20 mL), and dried under vacuum to give the desired product. Characteristic data for the known N‐(3‐oxobutyl)heterocycles 2a, c–e, g–i are given in the S1.1. Section of, Supporting Information.

Synthesis of Racemic N‐(3‐Hydroxybutyl)Heterocycles (±)‐1a–i

A solution of the corresponding ketone (2a–i, 2 mmol) in MeOH (for 2a–e, g–i) or EtOH (for 2f) (5 mL) was cooled to −5 °C, then sodium borohydride (5 mmol, 2.5 eq. ((±)‐1a–g, i); 7 mmol, 3.5 eq. ((±)‐1h)) was added portionwise (over ≈30 min) to the above mentioned stirred solution. The resulting mixture was allowed to reach room temperature, then was stirred for another 30 min. The reaction mixture was evaporated in vacuum, diluted with dichloromethane (30 mL), and washed with water (3 × 30 mL). The organic phase was dried over sodium sulfate and concentrated in vacuum evaporator to give the desired product. Screening the Bioreduction of N‐(3‐Oxobutyl)‐Heterocycles 2a–i to (S)‐ or (R)‐N‐(3‐Hydroxybutyl)Heterocycles [(S)‐ or (R)‐1a–i] with Recombinant Whole‐Cell Alcohol Dehydrogenases

Recombinant E. coli cells expressing alcohol dehydrogenase (RaADH or LkADH) as a whole‐cell suspension (150 μL) were added to sodium phosphate buffer (760 μL, pH = 7.0, 64 mM). To the suspension were added the cosubstrate 2‐propanol (40 μL) and a solution of the ketone 2a–i (50 μL from a c = 1 M stock solution in the same phosphate buffer). This way the mixture had a final volume of 1000 μL, a cosubstrate concentration of 4 V/V% and a substrate concentration of 50 mM (7.1 to 12.3 mg mL−1 depending on the molar mass of the substrate). The resulting mixture was shaken at 700 rpm for 24 h at 30 °C. The sample preparation method was the same as above.

Bioreduction of N‐(3‐Oxobutyl)Heterocycles 2a–f, i to (S)‐N‐(3‐Hydroxybutyl)Heterocycles [(S)‐1a–f, i] at Preparative Scale

In a 20 mL vial, RaADH whole‐cell suspension (1.5 mL) was diluted with sodium phosphate buffer (7.6 mL, pH = 7.0, 64 mM). To the suspension were added the cosubstrate 2‐propanol (400 μL) and a solution of the ketone substrate (500 μL from a c = 1 M stock solution in the same phosphate buffer). This way the mixture had a final volume of 10.0 mL, a cosubstrate concentration of 4 V/V% and a substrate concentration of 50 mM (7.8 to 12.3 mg mL−1 depending on the molar mass of the substrate). The resulting mixture was shaken at 450 rpm for 24 h at 30 °C. After 24 h, the mixture was centrifuged for 10 min at ≈4000 g, 24 °C, and the supernatant was extracted with EtOAc (3 × 10 mL). The combined organic layers were dried over sodium sulfate and evaporated in vacuum. The crude residue was purified by column chromatography over silica gel (eluent: CH2Cl2/MeOH/NH4OH 10:1:0.2) to give the desired alcohols (S)‐1a–f,i (Table 2 , Entries 1,3,5–9).

Bioreduction of N‐(3‐Oxobutyl)Heterocycles (2a, b,i) to (R)‐N‐(3‐Hydroxybutyl)Heterocycles ((S)‐1a, b,i) at Preparative Scale

In a 20 mL vial, LkADH whole‐cell suspension (1.5 mL) was diluted with sodium phosphate buffer (7.6 mL, pH = 7.0, 64 mM). To the suspension were added the cosubstrate 2‐propanol (400 μL) and a solution of the ketone substrate (500 μL from a c = 1 M stock solution in the same phosphate buffer). This way the mixture had a final volume of 10.0 mL, a cosubstrate concentration of 4 V/V% and a substrate concentration of 50 mM (7.9 to 10.2 mg mL−1 depending on the molar mass of the substrate). The resulting mixture was shaken at 450 rpm for 24 h at 30 °C. After 24 h, the mixture was centrifuged for 10 min at ≈4000 g, 24 °C, and the supernatant was extracted with EtOAc (3 × 10 mL). The combined organic layers were dried over sodium sulfate and evaporated in a vacuum. The crude residue was purified by column chromatography over silica gel (eluent: CH2Cl2/MeOH/NH4OH 10:1:0.2) to give the desired alcohols (R)‐1a,b,i (Table 2, Entries 2,4,10).

Molecular Docking of N‐(3‐Oxobutyl)Heterocycles (2a–i) into the Active Sites of NADH/NADHP‐Containing Structures of ADHs

The possible reactive arrangements of the N‐(3‐oxobutyl)heterocycles 2a–i in the active sites of the (S)‐selective ADH from Rhodococcus aetherivorans (RaADH) and the (R)‐selective ADH from Lactobacillus kefir (LkADH) were investigated by molecular docking using Autodock VINA[ 42 , 43 ] implemented in PyMOL[ 44 ] by the DockingPie plugin.[ 45 ] The initial structures of the N‐(3‐oxobutyl)heterocycles 2a–i for docking were generated in HyperChem.[ 46 ] The crystal structures of the (S)‐selective alcohol dehydrogenase of Rhodococcus aetherivorans (RaADH)[ 47 ] and of the (R)‐selective alcohol dehydrogenase of Lactobacillus kefir (LkADH) containing methyl methacrylate [PDB ID: 7EJJ][ 48 ] were properly prepared as receptors for docking.

Preparing the RaADH and LkADH Structures for Docking

Due to the sequence refinements on the older N1MBR5 UniProt sequence (replaced by the A0AAT8XUI2 entry), the 3JV7 crystal structure should be regarded as the structure of a far from active site M222K mutant of the N1MBR5‐based RaADH. The active site surrounded by chain A in 3JV7, containing cofactor (NAD), an acetate ion (ACY), and (4S)‐2‐methyl‐2,4‐pentanediol (MPD) ligands [PDB ID: 3JV7][ 47 ] was used as docking site (Figure 1c). After importing receptor and ligand structures (2a–i) from PyMOL into the DockingPie, H‐atoms were added to the enzyme, the bonds and H‐atoms in the dihydropyridine moiety of NAD+ were edited by PyMOL's tools to correspond NADH, the ACY and MPD ligands were removed, and a grid was set [X = 48, Y = 48, Z = 48, grid space = 0.375; centered at the C‐atom of dihydropyridine bearing the hydride‐ion equivalent of NADH (X = 60.9, Y = 17.5, Z = 86.6), surrounded by residues of chain A of the tetrameric 3JV7].

Docking of Ketones 2a–i into the RaADH and LkADH Structures

Ten docking runs (exhaustiveness = 8, verbosity = 1, energy range = 3 kcal mol−1) were performed with all the ketones 2a–i, resulting in 10 poses for each run. Among the 100 resulting poses of each ketone 2a–i in the RaADH active site, the pose was considered the best reactive arrangement which had the best docking score (D.S.) among the docking poses exhibiting close vicinity (< 2.6 Å) of the pro‐R H‐atom of the dihydropyridine moiety of NADH to the carbonyl C‐atom and the carbonyl O‐atom to the Zn of chain A (< 2.2 Å) (details of the best reactive poses of ketones 2a–i within the RaADH structure can be found in Figure S71 and Table S3; see Section S4, Supporting Information).

In case of docking into the LkADH active site, the pose was considered the best reactive arrangement which had the best D.S. among the docking poses exhibiting close vicinity (< 2.6 Å) of the pro‐S H‐atom of the dihydropyridine moiety of NADPH to the carbonyl C‐atom and hydrogen bonding distance (< 3.1 Å) between the phenolic O‐atom of Y156 (chain A) and the carbonyl O‐atom in the substrate (details of the best reactive poses of ketones 2a–i within the LkADH structure can be found in Figure S72 and Table S4; see Section S4, Supporting Information).

Results and Discussion

In this study, various biocatalysts, including four yeast strains from the Witaria culture collection [Pichia carsonii (WY1), Lodderomyces elongisporus (WY2), Candida norvegica (WY4) and Candida parapsilosis (WY12)], commercially available baker's yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae, in both wet (BY) and dried (BYD) forms) and two recombinant, enantiocomplementary alcohol dehydrogenase enzymes ((S)‐selective ADH from Rhodococcus aetherivorans (RaADH) and (R)‐selective ADH from Lactobacillus kefir (LkADH)) were investigated in the bioreduction of a series of ketones with various (partially) saturated heterocyclic moieties {2a: 4‐morpholinobutan‐2‐one, 2b: 4‐thiomorpholinobutan‐2‐one, 2c: 4‐(piperidin‐1‐yl)butan‐2‐one, 2d: 4‐(4‐methylpiperidin‐1‐yl)butan‐2‐one, 2e: 4‐(4‐benzylpiperidin‐1‐yl)butan‐2‐one, 2f: ethyl 1‐(3‐oxobutyl)piperidine‐4‐carboxylate, 2g: 4‐(pyrrolidin‐1‐yl)butan‐2‐one, 2h: 4‐(4‐methylpiperazin‐1‐yl)butan‐2‐one, 2i: 4‐(3,4‐dihydroisoquinolin‐2(1H)‐yl)butan‐2‐one} (Scheme 1). The prochiral N‐(3‐oxobutyl)heterocycles with flexible rings (2a‐i) were prepared by aza‐Michael addition reaction of the corresponding (partially) saturated heterocycle (4a–i) and methyl vinyl ketone (5) (Scheme 1), resulting in good to excellent yields (82–94%). Racemic alcohol [(±)‐1a–i] and acetate [(±)‐3a–i] standards necessary as standards to develop the enantioselective GC measurements were prepared by sodium borohydride reduction (moderate to excellent yields (60–88%)) of the corresponding ketones 2a–i and acetylation of the alcohols with acetyl chloride (Scheme 1).

The chiral fragments with 3D heterocycle [(S)‐1a–i and (R)‐1a–i] may be extended only at the alcohol functions (1a–e, g–i; monofunctional HCs). Alcohol 1f, after hydrolysis of the ethyl ester, can be further functionalized and therefore can be considered as a bifunctional central fragment.

Screening the Bioreductions with Microbial Whole Cells with Ketoreductase Activity

Previously, a diverse selection of wild‐type yeast strains was tested in the bioreduction of various substrates in our research group.[ 36 – 39 ] In our previous study, several yeast strains were investigated for ketoreductase activity on a set of five prochiral ketones with (partially) saturated, flexible rings.[ 49 ] Additionally, the widely utilized baker's yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) was also tested, since it could be considered as a general whole‐cell biocatalyst for the bioreduction of prochiral ketones.[ 50 ]

First, in order to establish a comparative method and confirm experimental and detection procedures, lyophilized samples of the four wild‐type yeast strains from the Witaria culture collection and commercially available baker's yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) in both wet (BY) and dry (BYD) states were screened for ketoreductase activity on the target ketones 2a‐i (Sections S1.3., S1.4. and Table S1, Supporting Information).

The initial screening of the yeast strains was performed by TLC analysis of the test reactions for the presence of the corresponding alcohol product. The TLC‐based screen revealed that the four wild‐type yeast strains [Pichia carsonii (WY1), Lodderomyces elongisporus (WY2), Candida norvegica (WY4), and Candida parapsilosis (WY12)] exhibited ketoreductase activity toward the majority of the prochiral ketones (Table 1 ). Since the TLC‐based screening could not provide accurate conversion data and information about the selectivity of the screened bioreductions of various ketones with the selected yeast strains, the positive hits were further investigated by enantioselective GC methods. Thus, conversion of the ketones 2a–i and the enantiomeric composition of alcohol [(S)‐ or (R)‐1a–i] were determined by GC analysis of samples taken from the active screening reactions after 24 h (Table 1).

The enantiomeric composition of the alcohol products was determined only in cases of reaction mixtures with conversions exceeding ≈15% [except for substrate 2c with WY12 (Table 1, entry 15) and substrate 2e with BY (Table 1, entry 24)]. In the tests with yeast whole‐cells, the highest conversions in the 24 hr‐long reactions were observed with 4‐thiomorpholinobutan‐2‐one 2b as the substrate [c = 44%, 34%, and 31%, with L. elongisporus (WY2), C. parapsilosis (WY12), and P. carsonii (WY1), respectively]. The most active wild‐type yeast strain was C. parapsilosis (WY12), showing promising results with several substrates, e.g. with 4‐morpholinobutan‐2‐one (2a, 30% conversion, Table 1, entry 4), ethyl 1‐(3‐oxobutyl)piperidine‐4‐carboxylate (2f, 26% conversion, entry 31) and 4‐(3,4‐dihydroisoquinolin‐2(1H)‐yl)butan‐2‐one (2i, 31% conversion, entry 44). Irrespective of their activity, all wild‐type yeast strains that showed activity (WY1, WY2, WY4, WY12, and BY) proved to be highly enantioselective biocatalysts, resulting in essentially enantiopure (S)‐alcohols (S)‐1a‐f, i with enantiomeric excesses surpassing 99%.

Since with yeast strains only (S)‐alcohols could be produced (according to Prelog's rule)[ 51 ] the study was extended to recombinant ADHs as whole‐cell biocatalysts. The question may arise whether a reverse strategy could be utilized in this study, which would imply the following: performing genomic analysis of the yeast strains first, then conducting docking simulations using the encoded KREDs, and then verifying the results using recombinant whole‐cell biocatalysts. Regarding this possibility, one must consider that numerous carbonyl reductases might be encoded in the genome of each of the yeast strains examined here and genomic analysis alone is not necessarily informative about expression. For example, in a relevant study, 18 carbonyl reductases from baker's yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) were produced and investigated.[ 52 ] Concerning Candida parapsilosis (WY12), a total of six carbonyl reductases were reported.[ 53 , 54 , 55 – 56 ] This rationalizes why this approach was not selected for this study.

Rather, we selected for further studies two comprehensively examined enantiocomplementary ADHs [an (S)‐selective one from Rhodococcus aetherivorans (RaADH) and an (R)‐selective one from Lactobacillus kefir (LkADH), since they proved to be efficient in the bioreduction of a series of variously substituted 1‐(arylsulfanyl)propan‐2‐ones in batch mode[ 57 ]], aiming at the production of both enantiomers of the target alcohols (Table 1). Unlike the yeast strains, RaADH and LkADH accepted—at least to some extent—all the prochiral ketones 2a–i as substrates, converting them to their respective alcohol enantiomers. Even 4‐(4‐methylpiperazin‐1‐yl)butan‐2‐one 2h—which was not accepted as substrate by any of the investigated yeasts—could be reduced (c = 13% and 2% with RaADH and LkADH, respectively, Table 1, entries 40 and 41). These two recombinant ADHs exhibited the highest conversions with 4‐morpholinobutan‐2‐one 2a, 4‐thiomorpholinobutan‐2‐one 2b or 4‐(3,4‐dihydroisoquinolin‐2(1H)‐yl)butan‐2‐one 2i (in the range of ≈60–90%), enabling the production of both enantiomers of the corresponding chiral alcohols 1a, b,i at a preparative scale. Other ketones, such as 4‐(piperidin‐1‐yl)butan‐2‐one 2c, 4‐(4‐methylpiperidin‐1‐yl)butan‐2‐one 2d, 4‐(4‐benzylpiperidin‐1‐yl)butan‐2‐one 2e and ethyl 1‐(3‐oxobutyl)piperidine‐4‐carboxylate 2f could only be converted partially to the corresponding (S)‐alcohols (c= ≈40–60%) by RaADH, rendering only the (S)‐selective bioreductions feasible at preparative scale. Overall, the utilization of recombinant ADHs resulted in higher conversions than those observed in the yeast‐catalyzed reactions and allowed the production of both antipodes of the target alcohols, while maintaining their complete enantiopurity.

In summary, all the tested yeast strains and the recombinant ADH from R. aetherivorans (RaADH) exhibited the usual Prelog selectivity, leading to enantiopure (S)‐alcohols. The only biocatalyst with anti‐Prelog enantiopreference was the ADH from L. kefir (LkADH), leading to the formation of (R)‐alcohols. This agrees with the expectations, as the majority of ADHs follow Prelog's rule,[ 51 ] in which the NADH or NADPH cofactor delivers its hydride from the Re face of the substrate, producing (S)‐alcohols. Among the wild‐type yeast strains that exhibited ketoreductase activity, C. parapsilosis (WY12) proved to be the most active, while C. norvegica (WY4) was the least efficient, but still quite selective biocatalyst. Regrettably, neither the wet nor the dry form of baker's yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) proved to be an efficient biocatalyst for the reduction of ketones 2a–i, resulting in very low conversions with only the two bulkiest substrates after 24 h (c = 2% and 1% from 2e and 2i with BY; Table 1, entries 24 and 45, respectively).

Bioreductions with Enantiocomplementary ADHs as Recombinant Whole‐Cell Biocatalysts at Preparative Scale

Out of the nine ketones 2a–i investigated as substrates in the biotransformations, seven (2a‐f, i) could be converted to at least one of their enantiopure alcohol derivatives with moderate to excellent conversions. From this set of screening bioreductions, preparative scale biotransformations aiming at isolation and characterization of the enantiopure chiral alcohol products were performed in the cases of RaADH‐ and LkADH‐catalyzed bioreductions, resulting in conversions reaching at least 40% (Table 1, entries 5,6,11,12,16,22,26,32,47,48). Accordingly, the bioreduction of ketones 4‐(pyrrolidin‐1‐yl)butan‐2‐one 2g and 4‐(4‐methylpiperazin‐1‐yl)butan‐2‐one 2h with lower than 15% conversion was not investigated further.

The preparative scale bioreductions (tenfold scale‐up) were performed using E. coli whole‐cell preparations overexpressing ADHs from either R. aetherivorans [(S)‐selective RaADH, entries 1,3,5–9 in Table 2 ] or L. kefir [(R)‐selective LkADH, entries 2,4,10 in Table 2]. The bioreductions at larger scale showed comparable or in some cases higher conversions than those observed in the small‐scale screening reactions. The only exception was the bioreduction of 4‐(4‐benzylpiperidin‐1‐yl)butan‐2‐one 2e with RaADH (Table 2, entry 7), which resulted in only 15% conversion, one third of the small‐scale result (Table 1, entry 26). The moderate yields, lower than expected from the conversion values shown in Table 2, can be attributed to two issues: 1) the extensive emulsion formation and foaming that occurred during extraction, leading to challenging phase separation, and 2) the nonoptimized chromatographic separation resulting in mixed fractions of the forming alcohols and the corresponding unconverted ketones. Similarly to the screening reactions, all the isolated (S)‐ and (R)‐alcohol products were virtually enantiopure (ee > 99%), and the opposite enantiomers exhibited the same absolute values of opposite optical rotations.

Proving Absolute Configuration of the Products by Molecular Docking of N‐(3‐Oxobutyl)Heterocycles 2a–i into Active Sites of NAD(P)H‐Containing Structures of ADHs

Although most ADHs follow Prelog's rule,[ 51 ] which implies that the hydride of NAD(P)H attacks the carbonyl group of the ketone from the Re side, and accordingly, KREDs following Prelog's rule catalyze bioreductions (S)‐selectively, the experimental spatial structures of the two utilized ADHs provided the opportunity to determine the absolute configurations of the products of the current study based not only on analogies.

The crystal structures of the (S)‐selective alcohol dehydrogenase of Rhodococcus aetherivorans (RaADH) containing cofactor (NAD), an acetate ion (ACY) and (4S)‐2‐methyl‐2,4‐pentanediol (MPD) ligands [PDB ID: 3JV7][ 47 ] and of the (R)‐selective alcohol dehydrogenase of Lactobacillus kefir (LkADH) containing methyl methacrylate [PDB ID: 7EJJ][ 48 ]—prepared for calculations as detailed in Section 2.9.1.—were selected as starting structures to perform systematic docking of ketones 2a–i. The arrangement of the H‐atom transferring to the carbonyl group of the substrates determines the absolute configuration of the forming alcohols 1a–i.

Comparing the best reactive pose of 4‐morpholinobutan‐2‐one 2a (Figure 1a) with the ligand‐containing active site of wt LkADH (Figure 1c) revealed that the heterocyclic part of ketone 2a arranged in the same space of the active site as the MPD ligand, and the carbonyl moiety of the 3‐oxobutyl side‐chain of 2a adopted the same position as the ACY ligand in the starting structure. In the best reactive structure of ketone 2a (Figure 1a), the carbonyl O‐atom is anchored to the Zn of chain A (2.28 Å), rendering the Re face of the carbonyl C‐atom in close vicinity of the pro‐R H‐atom of the dihydropyridine moiety of NADH (2.67 Å). This arrangement defines the configuration of the forming alcohol 1a as (S).

Docking of all further N‐(3‐oxobutyl)heterocycles 2b–i in a similar way revealed that the pro‐R H‐atom of the dihydropyridine moiety of NADH attacks the carbonyl group of the substrates 2b–i from the Re face, thus proving the configuration of the forming alcohols (S)‐1b–i (see Fig. S71 and Table S3 in Section S4, Supporting Information).

Although crystal structures exist for LkADH [such as the ligand‐ and NADP‐free structure (PDB ID: 7P36)[ 58 ] or the NADP‐containing but closed structure (PDB ID: 4RF2)[ 59 ]] but none of them corresponds to the reacting state—containing NADP and substrate‐like ligand—of this (R)‐selective ketoreductase. Thus, the reactive structure of the LkADH triple mutant (F147L‐L153Q‐Y190P) containing NADP and methyl methacrylate (PDB ID: 7EJJ)[ 48 ] was modified back to wt LkADH and used for docking. The comparison of the reactive pose of 4‐morpholinobutan‐2‐one 2a (Figure 1b) with the ligand containing active site of wt LkADH (Figure 1d) indicated that the best reactive structure of ketone 2a docked in the same space of the active site as the methyl methacrylate ligand (Figure 1d), and the carbonyl moiety of the 3‐oxobutyl side‐chain of 2a was within H‐bonding distance of the O‐atom of the catalytically important Y156 (Figure 1b). This H‐bonding interaction between the phenolic O‐atom of Y156 and the carbonyl O‐atom (3.00 Å) oriented the Si face of the carbonyl C‐atom in close vicinity of the pro‐S H‐atom of the dihydropyridine moiety of NADPH (2.58 Å). This assembly determines the configuration of the forming alcohol 1a as (R). Correspondingly, docking of the other ketones 2b–i showed that the pro‐S H‐atom of the dihydropyridine moiety of NADPH attacks the carbonyl group from the Si face, thus proving the configuration of the forming alcohols (R)‐1b–i (see Fig. S72 and Table S4 in Section S4 of Supplementary Information).

Conclusions

In this study, aiming at the bioreduction of prochiral N‐(3‐oxobutyl)heterocycles with flexible rings 2a–i with the aid of enantiocomplementary alcohol dehydrogenase enzymes (RaADH and LkADH) to produce the (S)‐ and (R)‐enantiomers of the corresponding alcohols 1a–i, numerous novel compounds were prepared and characterized, including two ketones (4‐thiomorpholinobutan‐2‐one, 2b and ethyl 1‐(3‐oxobutyl)piperidine‐4‐carboxylate, 2f), one racemic alcohol (ethyl 1‐(3‐hydroxybutyl)piperidine‐4‐carboxylate, (±)‐1f), five (S)‐alcohols ((S)‐4‐thiomorpholinobutan‐2‐ol, (S)‐1b; (S)‐4‐(piperidin‐1‐yl)butan‐2‐ol, (S)‐1c; (S)‐4‐(4‐methylpiperidin‐1‐yl)butan‐2‐ol, (S)‐1d; (S)‐4‐(4‐benzylpiperidin‐1‐yl)butan‐2‐ol, (S)‐1e; and (S)‐ethyl 1‐(3‐hydroxybutyl)piperidine‐4‐carboxylate, (S)‐1f) and three (R)‐alcohols ((R)‐4‐morpholinobutan‐2‐ol, (R)‐1a; (R)‐4‐thiomorpholinobutan‐2‐ol, (R)‐1b; and (R)‐4‐(3,4‐dihydroisoquinolin‐2(1H)‐yl)butan‐2‐ol, (R)‐1i).

The preliminary screening of four wild‐type yeast strains and commercially available baker's yeast for the bioreduction of nine prochiral ketones with “3D” N‐heterocyclic moiety revealed access only to (S)‐alcohols (S)‐1a–i. Extending the biocatalyst scope with two recombinant ADHs as whole‐cell biocatalysts [(S)‐selective ADH from Rhodococcus aetherivorans (RaADH) and (R)‐selective ADH from Lactobacillus kefir (LkADH)] resulted in 1) on one hand higher conversions in the bioreductions with complete (S)‐selectivity using RaADH to provide alcohols (S)‐1a–i, and 2) on the other hand access to alcohols (R)‐1a–i using LkADH‐mediated bioreductions with complete (R)‐selectivity.

Molecular docking of the N‐(3‐oxobutyl)substituted heterocycles with flexible rings 2a–i could be successfully applied to prove the absolute configuration of all the forming alcohols (S)‐1a–i and (R)‐1a–i. The docking analysis can also aid future structure‐based mutagenesis to improve the ADH biocatalysts toward this class of substrates.

The synthetic usefulness of this approach could be demonstrated by the preparative scale production of seven (S)‐alcohols and three (R)‐alcohols, which are promising chiral fragments with a high degree of drug‐likeness in chiral libraries for FBDD campaigns. These promising results inspire us to generalize the possibility of using biocatalytic methods to access rich but unexplored libraries of 3D heterocycles with a wide selection of chiral sidechains for FBDD campaigns starting from the easy‐to‐prepare prochiral ketones.

In summary, the present study broadens the range of applicability of recombinant alcohol dehydrogenase whole‐cell preparations as biocatalysts to ketones involving flexible N‐heterocycles and establishes robust technology for producing chiral fragment library members with such 3D heterocyclic moieties.
