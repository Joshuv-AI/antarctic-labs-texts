Analysis of exercise behavior and influencing factors in patients with inflammatory bowel disease during remission and mild disease activity
Authors: Yuan Xue, Yajun Sun, Jiajia Wu, Bo Chen, Jing Wang, Bingqing Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and objectiveThis study aimed to investigate the distribution of exercise stages and exercise preference among patients with Inflammatory Bowel Disease in remission or with mild disease activity and explore their influencing factors.MethodsA survey was conducted among 282 patients with IBD in remission and mild disease activity, utilizing various scales and questionnaires, including a general information questionnaire, exercise preference questionnaire, Exercise Stages of Change Scale, Self-Efficacy for Exercise Scale, Exercise Benefits and Barriers Scale, and Exercise Social Support Scale.ResultsAmong IBD participants, 48 (17.0%) were in the pre-contemplation stage, 90 (31.9%) in the contemplation stage, 29 (10.3%) in the preparation stage, 29 (10.3%) in the action stage, and 86 (30.5%) in the maintenance stage. The most preferred option for participants is exercising in the evening in residential areas or parks, primarily through individual walks, three times a week, each lasting 30 to 60 min. The median score for exercise self-efficacy was 53.5 (IQR: 38–62); the median score for exercise benefits and barriers was 145.5 (IQR: 127.8–152), with perceived exercise benefits scoring 90 (IQR: 81–106) and perceived exercise barriers scoring 40 (IQR: 43–47); the median score for exercise social support was 12 (IQR: 9–14). Educational attainment, exercise self-efficacy, social support, and perceived benefits of exercise were important factors influencing the stage of exercise. (P < 0.05).ConclusionsThe findings indicate that IBD patients in remission or mild activity have suboptimal levels of regular exercise. Educational attainment significantly influences exercise behavior stages. To help IBD patients establish and maintain healthy exercise habits, strategies may focus on factors associated with exercise adherence, such as exercise self-efficacy, social support, and awareness of exercise benefits.Keywords: Inflammatory bowel disease, Exercise behavior, Exercise self-efficacy, Exercise benefits, Social support

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) develops mainly based on environmental and lifestyle changes, disturbances in intestinal microbial homeostasis, disturbances in intestinal mucosal immune function, and genetic susceptibility [1, 2], with a prolonged and recurrent course, and mainly includes Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC) [3]. Patients with inflammatory bowel disease suffer from gastrointestinal manifestations such as abdominal pain, diarrhea, and perianal lesions, as well as a wide range of symptoms such as anxiety, depression, fatigue, muscle sparing, pain, and a decline in the patients’ quality of life [4–6]. Although the clinical efficacy and prognosis of IBD patients have been greatly improved with the use of biologics and other medications, IBD is still incurable, and remission and active phases often alternate [7], so proper disease management and adjuvant therapy are particularly important to improve the overall health and quality of life of the patients, reduce the inflammatory response, and counteract the adverse effects of treatment.

As awareness of the health benefits of exercise continues to grow, particularly regarding its positive effects on musculoskeletal and cardiovascular systems [8], increasing attention is being paid to the impact of exercise on patients with IBD. A prospective cohort study by Jones et al. [9] found that higher levels of physical activity are significantly associated with a reduced risk of disease relapse in patients with IBD during remission. Similarly, Lo et al. [10] utilized Cox proportional hazards models for survival analysis, revealing that IBD patients with high levels of physical activity have lower mortality rates compared to those with low physical activity levels. Furthermore, a growing body of randomized controlled trials has confirmed that exercise interventions are both safe and beneficial for IBD patients. These interventions have been shown to positively influence gut microbiota, enhance bone density and muscle mass, improve cardiovascular function, and alleviate fatigue, negative emotions, and overall quality of life [11–14]. However, some studies have indicated that exercise lasting 2 h or more, particularly with a VO2max of 60% or higher, may induce physiological changes such as reduced perfusion, ischemia, altered gastrointestinal motility, and local or systemic inflammation, potentially leading to gastrointestinal syndrome and acute gastrointestinal disturbances [15]. Therefore, current evidence does not support the recommendation of long-term intense exercise for IBD patients. Despite the lack of authoritative exercise guidelines, clinical practice generally recommends regular physical activity for IBD patients in remission and those with mild disease activity, such as engaging in at least 150 min of moderate-intensity endurance exercise per week [14, 16, 17].

However, similar to many chronic diseases, the exercise participation rate among IBD patients is notably low. Compared to healthy individuals, IBD patients exhibit significantly lower levels of physical activity [18, 19]. Surveys indicate that 33.3% of IBD patients are physically inactive, with only 25% maintaining a regular exercise routine [20, 21]. This phenomenon may be influenced by various factors. In a survey of 859 patients with IBD, Tew [20] et al. found that 57% of participants walked as a form of exercise, but only 17% were categorized as ‘physically active’, and approximately 70% reported that abdominal/joint pain and fatigue limited their physical activity. The study also observed that among patients with CD, physical activity was associated with depressive symptoms and level of disease activity, while among patients with UC, physical activity was associated with depressive symptoms and age (both P < 0.05). Fagan [22] et al. surveyed 306 people with IBD and found that two-thirds of the participants felt that their illness limited the amount of exercise they could do, with fatigue, abdominal pain, and type of illness being common barriers to exercise, and that it is vital to increase physical activity in people with IBD to reduce both actual and perceived barriers. Cohen [23] et al. noted that the intestinal symptoms and extra-intestinal manifestations of IBD (e.g., muscle laxity, osteoporosis, fatigue) negatively affect patients’ ability to engage in exercise. Otto [24] et al. found that patients taking corticosteroids had lower exercise endurance than those who did not. Brevinge [25] et al. investigated 29 CD patients using a loop traversal method and questionnaire, showing that patients who underwent bowel surgery had reduced exercise tolerance, which decreased further with increased extent of bowel resection. Mack [26] et al. found that the type of disease significantly influenced the amount of leisure-time physical activity in patients with IBD, with CD patients being more likely to be physically less active than those with UC. Overall, the factors influencing physical activity/exercise in IBD patients were primarily related to the following: (1) demographic information, such as age; (2) disease-related characteristics, including type of disease, level of disease activity, intestinal resection, disease perception, and type of medication; and (3) psychological information, such as depression and perception of exercise disorders.

Considering that the existing exercise intervention trials for IBD patients mainly focus on those in the remission and mild activity phases of the disease [27, 28], the safety of exercise interventions for IBD patients in the moderate to severe activity phases remains to be further confirmed [29]. Current research consistently advocates for exercise in IBD patients during the remission and mild activity phases [30]. Therefore, it is necessary to focus on patients in remission or with mild disease activity, investigating their exercise behavior and analyzing the influencing factors. This demographic, characterized by relatively stable physiological conditions, emerges as a key target group for promoting sustainable exercise habits. Furthermore, while daily physical activity offers health benefits, structured, goal-oriented exercise—with its defined intensity and duration—demonstrates greater advantages in improving physical health, making it the preferred choice [31, 32]. Therefore, for IBD patients in the remission and mild activity phases, more attention should be paid to their exercise behavior rather than just physical activity. A database search reveals that there is currently a lack of research specifically addressing the exercise behavior and influencing factors of IBD patients in the remission and mild activity phases.

The Transtheoretical Model (TTM) posits that behavior change is closely related to self-efficacy, behavioral cognition, and social support factors [33]. Previous studies have shown that exercise self-efficacy, which is the application of self-efficacy in the context of physical exercise, is a significant predictor of physical activity behavior [34, 35]. Perceptions of the benefits of exercise can motivate patients to engage more actively in physical activity, while perceived barriers may lead to avoidance of exercise [35, 36]. Additionally, an individual’s behavior is influenced by their social and environmental context, with research indicating that social support has a significant impact on physical activity behavior [37, 38]. However, the specific effects of these factors on exercise behavior in IBD patients during remission and mild disease activity have yet to be fully validated.

Based on this background, this study aims to address the identified research gap by specifically focusing on IBD patients in remission or with mild activity. It seeks to comprehensively investigate their exercise behavior using the TTM framework and to quantitatively explore the influencing factors by integrating demographic, disease-related, and key theoretical psychological constructs (self-efficacy, benefits/barriers perception, and social support). Given that the effects of exercise are not sustained over the long term without continuous activity, it is crucial to focus on factors relevant to the action and maintenance stages of exercise [39]. This study plans to use the Stages of Change Questionnaire to investigate the factors affecting progression across different stages of exercise behavior in this specific IBD subgroup. The objective is to identify potentially modifiable factors that can promote and sustain exercise, thereby providing an empirical basis for developing targeted, stage-matched interventions. The findings are expected to provide novel insights for the clinical implementation of personalized, theory-informed exercise management strategies, encouraging more active and maintained participation in exercise among IBD patients in remission and with mild activity, thereby potentially enhancing their long-term health and quality of life.

Materials and methods

Participants

Recruitment for this study took place from March 2024 to February 2025 among patients visiting the Department of Gastroenterology at the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University, who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria for IBD patients. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Diagnosis of IBD based on the diagnostic criteria established by the Gastroenterology Branch of the Chinese Medical Association in 2023 [40], in a state of remission or with mild disease activity for over three months; (2) Adults aged 18 years or older; (3) Capable of communication and interaction; (4) Willingness to provide informed consent for participation. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients with mental disorders or cognitive impairments; (2) Concurrent severe chronic diseases (such as severe cardiovascular, neurological, renal, pulmonary diseases, etc.), tumors, et; (3) Individuals with impaired physical mobility; (4) Pregnant individuals.

Sample size determination

According to Kendall’s rough estimation method for sample size, the sample size should be 5–10 times the number of items on the scale [41]. The Exercise Benefits Perception Scale, with 29 items, has the highest number of items in this study. Therefore, the required sample size was 201–403 cases, calculated from an initial target of 145–290, considering a 20% non-response rate and 90% eligibility rate. Of 293 questionnaires distributed, 11 were excluded due to patient withdrawal or scheduled examinations. This yielded 282 valid responses with a 96.24% effective response rate.

Sociodemographic and clinical data collection

Participants who consented to participate in the survey were requested to complete a series of written questionnaires. Standard selection questions were used to gather basic demographic information, including age, gender, occupation, marital status, education level, weight, height (for calculating Body Mass Index [BMI]), disease type (CD, UC), disease duration, presence of other chronic illnesses, and current medication usage.

Disease activity in Crohn’s disease patients was assessed using the Harvey and Bradshaw criteria to determine the Crohn’s Disease Activity Index (CDAI) [42], while ulcerative colitis patients were evaluated using the modified Mayo scoring system to assess disease activity [43]. A CDAI score of < 150 indicates remission, while a score of ≥ 150 indicates active disease. Active disease is further classified as follows: 150–220 for mild, 221–450 for moderate, and >450 for severe. A modified Mayo score of ≤ 2 points with no subscore >1 defines clinical remission; scores of 3–5, 6–10, and 11–12 indicate mild, moderate, and severe disease activity, respectively.

Study questionnaires

Exercise stage change scale (ESC)

Developed by Marcus [44] et al. and translated by Yin [45] in Chinese, this scale categorizes exercise behavior into five stages based on regular exercise workouts: The Exercise Stages of Change Scale (ESC) categorizes patients’ exercise behavior into five stages based on their regular physical activity levels. Stage 1, the Precontemplation stage, indicates no current regular exercise and no plans to start within the next 6 months. Stage 2, the Contemplation stage, denotes no current regular exercise but plans to start within the next 6 months. Stage 3, the Preparation stage, signifies no current regular exercise but plans to start within the next 30 days. Stage 4, the Action stage, indicates current engagement in regular exercise for less than 6 months. Stage 5, the Maintenance stage, indicates current engagement in regular exercise for 6 months or more. Regular exercise is defined here as organized physical activity for the purpose of promoting health, at least three times a week for not less than 20 min each time, for a total of not less than 150 min per week. The intensity should elevate heart rate and breathing without being strenuous, and induce slight perspiration. The scale has demonstrated good reliability and construct validity. The sensitivity of this scale is 92.8%, and its specificity is 81.5%.

Self-efficacy for exercise scale (SEE)

Designed by Bandura [46], this scale measures individuals’ confidence in facing exercise challenges, consisting of 18 items. Each item is scored from 0 to 100, where 0 indicates no confidence at all, 50 indicates half confidence, and 100 indicates complete confidence. The ESE score is the sum of scores for each item divided by the total number of items. Higher scores indicate greater confidence in engaging in regular exercise. The Chinese version, developed by Wei [47], shows high internal consistency. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for this scale is 0.853.

Exercise benefits and barriers scale (EBBS)

Divided into Exercise Benefits (29 items) and Barriers (14 items) subscales, it assesses patients’ perceptions of exercise benefits and obstacles, aiding decision-making during behavior change [48]. Responses are scored on a 4-point Likert scale, with 4 indicating “strongly agree” and 1 indicating “strongly disagree”. Items on the Physical Activity Benefits Scale are positively scored, while items on the Physical Activity Barriers Scale are reverse-scored. The total scale ranges from a minimum score of 43 to a maximum score of 172, where higher scores indicate a more positive perception of physical activity and fewer perceived barriers. Tung’s [49] Chinese translation showed good reliability and validity for both the total and subscale scales. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this scale is 0.91.

Exercise social support scale (ESSS)

Developed by Sallis et al. [50], this scale utilizes 5 items to evaluate exercise support from family and friends, demonstrating strong reliability and validity. The Chinese version, translated by Yin [45], also exhibits robust internal consistency. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for this scale is 0.825.

Exercise preferences questionnaire

Designed by researchers based on a literature review, this questionnaire covers exercise forms, activities, locations, timing, duration, and frequency.

Ethical consideration

Participants provided written consent to participate in this study. The study received approval from the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University.

Statistical analysis

The database was established using Epidata 3.1 software with dual data entry. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 25.0 software. Categorical variables were described using frequency and composition ratios. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were represented as Median (P25, P75). Group comparisons for general survey data were conducted using t-tests, ANOVA, or non-parametric tests as appropriate. For statistically significant chi-square results, post-hoc analyses were conducted using adjusted residuals to identify which specific categories contributed to the significance. Effect sizes for categorical data were reported using Cramer’s V, with values interpreted as follows: 0.1 = small, 0.3 = medium, 0.5 = large. The Box-Tidwell method was used to test whether continuous independent variables had a linear relationship with the logit transformation of the dependent variable. Tolerance and the variance inflation factor (VIF) were used to assess multicollinearity among independent variables. If no multicollinearity was detected among the independent variables, ordinal logistic regression analysis was used for multivariate analysis; otherwise, non-parametric regression analysis or ridge regression was used. A significance level of P < 0.05 indicated statistical significance for differences. For the multivariate analysis, an ordinal logistic regression model was built to identify factors independently associated with the stages of exercise behavior. The dependent variable was the ordered categorical variable of exercise stage. The independent variables were selected based on a two-step rationale: (1) Variables showing a significant association (P < 0.05) in univariate analyses (Table 1) were considered as candidates; (2) Age, sex, disease type, and disease duration were included as pre-specified confounders due to their clinical relevance. The forward selection method (Likelihood Ratio) was used for model building. Multicollinearity was assessed using tolerance and the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) prior to analysis. Model fit was evaluated using the likelihood ratio test and goodness-of-fit test.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 282 participants who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria were included in the study. Of these, 205 (72.7%) were male and 77 (27.3%) were female. Among the participants, 203 (72.0%) had Crohn’s disease and 79 (28.0%) had ulcerative colitis. Only 41% were in the action or maintenance stage of exercise behavior. Univariate analysis results indicated significant differences in the distribution of exercise stages based on educational attainment, type of disease, and history of chronic disease (P < 0.05). As indicated by the post-hoc pairwise comparisons with adjusted standardized residuals, participants with a college education or above were significantly overrepresented in the action stage, while those with ulcerative colitis were significantly overrepresented in the pre-contemplation stage. Additionally, participants with comorbid chronic diseases were significantly overrepresented in the preparation stage. The effect size for educational attainment on exercise stage was small to moderate (Cramer’s V = 0.18). After testing for normality, it was found that the scores of exercise self-efficacy, exercise social support, and perceived exercise benefits/barriers were not normally distributed (p < 0.05). Hence, the median and interquartile range were used for representation. In this study, the median exercise self-efficacy score for participants was 53.5, with the 25th and 75th percentiles being 38 and 62, respectively. The lowest-scoring item was “I feel uncomfortable when exercising,” while the highest-scoring item was “During vacations.” The median exercise benefits/barriers perception score was 145.5, with the 25th and 75th percentiles being 127.8 and 152. The median exercise social support score was 12, with the 25th and 75th percentiles being 9 and 14, respectively. Independent variable assignment method

The interaction terms between continuous independent variables and their natural logarithmic values were included in the regression equation. Linear test results indicated a linear relationship between all continuous independent variables and the logit-transformed values of the dependent variable, with P-values ranging from 0.128 to 0.900. The tolerance values ranged from 0.460 to 0.979, and VIF values ranged from 1.021 to 2.167, indicating no significant multicollinearity among the introduced variables.

The model included pre-specified confounders (age, sex, disease type, disease duration) along with variables significant in univariate analyses (educational attainment, chronic disease history, exercise self-efficacy, exercise benefits perception, exercise social support). Attitudes and preferences to exercise

Participants in IBD with remission or mild disease activity show a preference for individual exercise, constituting 58.0% of the total. The favored exercise locations are predominantly residential areas or parks, followed by homes, accounting for 44.0% and 37.0% respectively. A majority of respondents (55.0%) indicated a preference for evening exercise (after 18:00), followed by the morning. Preferred exercise frequency and duration center around three times per week for 30 to 60 min each session. The next preference is exercising twice a week for less than 30 min per session. The most popular exercise is aerobic exercise, primarily walking, followed by jogging. Notably, the response rate for resistance exercises is low, being less than 6%. The main factors promoting exercise among participants include the perceived benefits of exercise on both body and mind, encouragement from family or friends, and guidance from healthcare professionals. The primary obstacles to exercise for participants are poor physical condition, followed by a lack of exercise time, unfavorable weather, and a sense of inertia. Discussion

Analysis of participants’ regular exercise behavioral stages and general influencing factors

Studies have shown that among patients with IBD in remission or with mild disease activity, a greater proportion are engaged in regular exercise behavior during the intention and maintenance stages. However, overall, 59.2% of patients are in the pre-action stages (pre-contemplation, contemplation, and preparation), compared to those in the post-action stages (action and maintenance). This result is different from the study by DeFilippis [51] et al., who were investigating the exercise habits of 250 patients with IBD and showed that 74% of the patients were found to exercise regularly. The discrepancy may be due to the stricter definition of regular exercise in this study, similar to the World Health Organization’s recommended exercise regimen [8], which requires at least three sessions of no less than 20 min of exercise per session and no less than 150 min of exercise per week to be considered to be in the action or maintenance phase. In contrast, the former study included those who exercised 1–2 times per week (accounting for 32.8%) as regular exercisers. In conclusion, this study demonstrated that the majority of participants did not meet the criteria for regular exercise, suggesting that exercise behaviors need to be further strengthened and promoted.

Some previous studies [22, 26] found that in patients with IBD, physical activity levels were correlated with disease activity, especially in patients with UC, who had a significant decrease in physical activity levels after diagnosis, which is different from the results of this study. The possible reason for this is that this study only included participants in remission and with mild disease activity, which is more stable, so the effect of disease activity or type on the stage of exercise behavior was not observed.

Notably, this study found that education level has a significant impact on the regular exercise behavior of patients with IBD in remission or with mild disease activity. Specifically, the higher the education level, the more likely patients are to maintain regular exercise. This phenomenon may be attributed to their ability to more effectively acquire and process health information and make scientific and rational decisions regarding disease management. This finding suggests that clinical healthcare providers should fully consider the educational background of IBD patients when developing exercise intervention plans, providing appropriate health education support to promote the establishment of regular exercise habits.

Furthermore, this study found that the majority of participants preferred a limited range of exercise types, primarily walking, which is consistent with previous research findings [20, 22]. Moderate-intensity exercise is considered a safe and beneficial option for IBD patients, demonstrating greater improvements in quality of life and emotional well-being compared to low-intensity exercise [52, 53]. However, the current participation rate in moderate-intensity exercise among patients is relatively low. Participants identified several barriers to regular exercise, including poor physical condition, unfavorable weather, laziness, lack of time, insufficient knowledge, lack of professional exercise guidance, and concerns about exacerbating their condition. Therefore, clinical practice needs to fully consider the specific characteristics of this population and provide increased attention. Healthcare providers should develop and guide individualized exercise plans based on the different stages of exercise behavior among participants, enriching the variety of exercises to maximize the benefits of physical activity.

Analysis of factors influencing the stages of regular exercise behavior in the TTM

Exercise self-efficacy

Exercise self-efficacy refers to an individual’s belief in their ability to overcome challenges and persist in exercise despite various obstacles [54]. It plays a crucial role in exercise behavior, particularly when faced with difficulties. The higher the level of exercise self-efficacy, the greater the likelihood that patients will initiate and maintain exercise routines, while the likelihood of reverting to unhealthy behaviors diminishes [55]. The results of this study reveal that the exercise self-efficacy level among IBD patients with remission and mild disease activity is scored at 53.5 (38, 62), indicating a relatively low exercise self-efficacy among them. Furthermore, the study also demonstrates that exercise self-efficacy levels significantly influence the distribution of different stages of exercise behaviors among patients. As patients’ exercise self-efficacy levels increase, they are more likely to be in higher stages of exercise behavior.

In addition, related research suggests that exercise self-efficacy serves as a robust predictor of exercise compliance and individual physical activity levels [56]. Consequently, in clinical practice, healthcare professionals may utilize behavior change theories (such as the COM-B model and intervention mapping) to strengthen health behavior motivation in patients at early stages of change. By assessing patients’ confidence levels in maintaining regular exercise when facing challenges, caregivers can identify barriers early, provide targeted training in problem-solving skills, and develop personalized exercise prescriptions. Additionally, peer support and regularly organized learning workshops can facilitate the sharing and exchange of successful exercise experiences among patients, thereby further enhancing their exercise self-efficacy.

Perception of exercise benefits

Patients’ perception of exercise benefits refers to their cognitive understanding of the positive outcomes associated with engaging in physical activity [57]. The findings of this study indicate that patients’ perception of exercise benefits significantly influences the distribution of different stages of exercise behavior. Patients with higher scores in perceiving exercise benefits are more likely to transition to higher stages of exercise behavior. Typically, the progression from one stage of behavior change to another is built upon a balance between the pros and cons of adopting healthy behaviors. When individuals perceive that the benefits of behavior change outweigh the drawbacks, they are more likely to transition to the next stage of behavior change.

However, the regression analysis results in this study have not been able to conclusively demonstrate the impact of patients’ perceived barriers to exercise on the distribution of different exercise behavior stages. This finding may be attributed to the specific characteristics of our study cohort. Since participants were exclusively in remission or with mild disease activity, the intensity of disease-specific barriers (e.g., severe pain, fatigue) was likely reduced, leading to a lower and more homogeneous perception of barriers overall. This limited variability may have constrained the statistical power to detect a significant effect. Furthermore, perceived barriers may not directly determine exercise stage but instead exert an indirect influence by undermining exercise self-efficacy—a strong and significant predictor in our model. The generic barriers scale used might also have failed to fully capture IBD-specific concerns (e.g., fear of flare-ups, toilet accessibility), further contributing to the non-significant result. Therefore, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence that barriers are irrelevant, but rather highlights the nuanced nature of behavior change in this clinically stable population, where the role of barriers may be attenuated or mediated through other factors.

This underscores the importance for healthcare providers to focus on factors associated with exercise behavior, such as patients’ awareness of exercise benefits, and emphasize the significance of exercise for IBD patients with remission or mild disease activity during health education and guidance related to exercise.

Exercise-related social support

The results of this study reveal that the total score of exercise-related social support among IBD patients is 12 (9, 14). Research indicates a positive correlation between the level of social support and behavior change, meaning that individuals who receive more comprehensive social support are more likely to undergo positive changes in their health behaviors. This study’s findings also underscore that exercise-related social support significantly influences the distribution of different stages of exercise behavior among IBD patients. Patients with higher scores in exercise-related social support are more likely to be situated in the higher stages of exercise behavior. Given the heightened prominence of psychological issues among IBD patients with remission or mild disease activity, who are prone to experience negative emotions such as anxiety and depression, their enthusiasm and adherence to engaging in exercise may decrease [29]. Consequently, cultivating a supportive exercise-oriented social environment holds significant potential for facilitating behavior transformation and development within this specific patient group.

Healthcare providers are advised to communicate with patients and their families about the vital importance of exercise-related social support. Social support acts as a buffer, helping individuals mobilize internal resources and share inner fears and suffering. It enables patients to feel cared for and valued within their families and communities, thereby strengthening their confidence to establish and maintain healthy behaviors. Therefore, healthcare providers should not only encourage family members to provide activity support but also plan and facilitate group activities to promote patient interaction and reduce feelings of loneliness. Simultaneously, providing easily accessible, low-cost exercise facilities and leveraging online platforms can compensate for weather and distance constraints as well as potential gaps in social support. Furthermore, identifying and assisting with technical and service access barriers improves the availability of exercise resources, facilitating behavioral change and development. Ultimately, fostering collaboration among healthcare institutions, communities, and families helps guide patients toward higher stages of exercise behavior.

Conclusion

Our research indicates that regular exercise behavior among IBD patients with mild disease activity or in remission requires further improvement. Factors such as educational level, exercise self-efficacy, perception of exercise benefits, and social support are key determinants influencing the distribution of patients across different stages of exercise behavior. For IBD patients in remission or with mild activity, healthcare management should primarily rely on outpatient care. Therefore, in future clinical practice, healthcare professionals need to consider these factors comprehensively and develop targeted, stage-specific exercise intervention strategies. Additionally, integrating the “Internet+” model to provide personalized exercise guidance and health education through online platforms and applications can help overcome geographic limitations, enabling patients to receive real-time feedback and support [58]. This approach may be associated with enhanced exercise self-efficacy and self-management capabilities, which in turn may encourage active participation in and adherence to regular exercise.

Furthermore, a comprehensive and systematic scientific exercise guideline for IBD patients is essential. Currently, there is no consensus on exercise plans for IBD patients. Clinical practitioners should focus on conducting randomized controlled trials to assess the specific effects of different exercise modalities on IBD patients, thereby providing a reliable basis for developing effective and scientifically-backed exercise guidelines.

Limitations

This cross-sectional study on exercise behavior and its influencing factors in patients with IBD in remission or with mild disease activity has several limitations. First, as a single-center study conducted at a tertiary hospital in Suzhou, the sample may not be fully representative of the broader IBD population. Geographic and healthcare resource specificities—particularly the region’s relatively high socioeconomic status and accessibility to medical services—could have led to an overestimation of variables such as educational level and exercise self-efficacy, thus introducing potential selection bias. Future research should use multi-centre recruitment. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of causal relationships between variables such as exercise self-efficacy and exercise behavior. Longitudinal studies with extended follow-up are needed to clarify the temporal dynamics of these factors. Third, the use of self-reported measures for assessing exercise behavior is susceptible to recall bias and social desirability bias, which may affect data accuracy. Future studies would benefit from incorporating objective assessment tools. Finally, although the total sample size was adequate for the primary analysis, it became limited when subgroup analyses were performed. Therefore, findings from subgroup comparisons and the multivariate regression model should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Further research with larger samples is warranted to validate these results and allow for robust within-group comparisons. Finally, the exercise preferences questionnaire used was self-developed for this study and has not been formally validated, which should be considered when interpreting the results.
