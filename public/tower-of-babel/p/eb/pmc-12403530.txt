Association between stress hyperglycemia and pneumonia in patients with stroke: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Xinyi Tu, Chenghao Shi, Jiajiang Jiang, Xiaodan Lu, Li Fan, Xiaoxiao Shi, Qian Li, Lizhu Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPneumonia is a frequent complication post-stroke and stress hyperglycemia (SH) is a physiological response to stroke. Although some studies have provided data on the impact of stress hyperglycemia on pneumonia incidence, no study to date has exclusively focused on investigating the direct relationship between stress hyperglycemia and pneumonia.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted across PubMed, EBSCOhost, Web of Science, Embase, CNKI, and Wanfang databases from inception to July 10th, 2024, to identify observational studies comparing the incidence of pneumonia between stroke patients with and without stress hyperglycemia. The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), and pooled ORs for each comparison were estimated using a random-effects model proposed by DerSimonian and Kacker. This systematic review is presented in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) reporting guidelines.ResultsTwelve studies were included eventually and the pooled incidence rate of pneumonia was 14.4%. The overall incidence of pneumonia in patients with stress hyperglycemia was significantly higher than in those without stress hyperglycemia (OR: 2.01; 95% CI: 1.72 to 2.34, P < 0.01).Significant heterogeneity was observed in the meta-analysis (P = 0.01, I²=54%). Meta-regression revealed that the effect size differed significantly across subgroups defined by SH metrics (blood glucose versus stress hyperglycemia ratio and blood glucose to HbA1c ratio, p < 0.01). These findings underscore important limitations, including measurement variability, significant heterogeneity, and potential residual confounding due to factors such as dysphagia severity, comorbidity burden, and variations in clinical management. Importantly, the overall quality of evidence was rated as low based on the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework, primarily due to the observational design of the included studies and inconsistency across results. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted cautiously and regarded as hypothesis-generating.ConclusionsThis systematic review and meta-analysis found that SH was significantly associated with post-stroke pneumonia, with patients exhibiting SH having twice the odds of pneumonia compared to those without.Prospective registrationThe protocol for this review is registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the registration number CRD42024563263.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-03901-9.Keywords: Stroke, Stress hyperglycemia, Pneumonia, Blood glucose, Glycated hemoglobin, Stress hyperglycemia ratio

Introduction

Stroke is the second primary cause of death and the third leading cause of disease burden around the world [1–3]. Pneumonia is the most frequent complication following the stroke [4]. A large systematic review [5] shows that the pooled frequency of pneumonia after stroke is 12.3% and the incidence of pneumonia is significantly higher among stroke patients undergoing mechanical ventilation, reaching 40% [6], which markedly affects their rehabilitation. Recently, some researchers found that patients with stress hyperglycemia are more likely to develop pneumonia [7–10]. Stress hyperglycemia (SH) is a neuroendocrine response to acute illness [11], typically manifesting as transient hyperglycemia that resolves following the alleviation of the acute condition [12]. The incidence of SH is 24% (21%−27%) in ischemic stroke [13] and 32.3% in hemorrhagic stroke [11], relating to mortality and impaired functionality after stroke [11, 14–16]. However, studies on SH in stroke patients have so far focused on outcomes such as mortality [17–19] and poor functional outcome [20–22], without giving sufficient attention to other early factors, such as pneumonia, that may influence mortality. And the potential link between SH and pneumonia development has yet to be conclusively demonstrated. Therefore, we undertook a systematic review and meta-analysis to explore the association between SH and pneumonia, aiming to provide a theoretical foundation for future studies.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA(Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) [23] reporting guidelines(Supplementary Table Ⅰ). The protocol for this review was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the registration number CRD42024563263.

Search strategy and selection criteria

An extensive literature search was performed in PubMed, EBSCOhost, Web of Science, Embase, CNKI and Wanfang databases without language restrictions from the inception to 10th July 2024. Studies published in languages other than English and Chinese were reviewed after being translated using translation software. We employed a retrieval method that integrates subject headings (MeSH) and relevant keywords, utilizing Boolean operators (AND) and (OR) to combine the terms. The search strategies were detailed in the registered protocol. Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) adult patients (age > 18 years) admitted to the hospital due to ischemic strokes(IS), intracerebral hemorrhage(ICH) and transient ischemic attack(TIA), diagnosed according to the World Health Organization criteria [24]; (2) blood glucose measurement obtained during the initial 72-hour post-admission or in the acute phase of stroke [25, 26]; (3) analyzed the proportion of pneumonia in patients with different blood glucose(BG) concentrations and the research data was available. Studies were excluded if: (1) the target population was pregnant or traumatized patients; (2) participants acquired pneumonia prior to hospital admission; (3) participants with febrile illness or active infection within 14 days prior to hospitalization; (4) systematic review and meta-analysis. (5) the study was unpublished or full text and detailed data of the study remained inaccessible despite multiple attempts to contact the author via email. The titles, abstracts, and subsequently, the full texts were independently screened for inclusion eligibility by two graduate students (Xinyi Tu and Chenghao Shi) serving as reviewers. Disputed studies underwent discussion by two reviewers to decide on inclusion. In cases of disagreement, a third researcher (Jiajiang Jiang) made the final decision. The reference lists of included articles were also screened for any potential studies. When multiple publications involved overlapping study populations, we selected the most recent or most comprehensive study.

Given the ongoing controversy surrounding the definition of SH and the varying standards used in previous studies to identify SH and non-SH, this review adopted a broad criterion. Specifically, this meta-analysis included studies that used solely BG levels to assess SH as well as those that considered the glycated hemoglobin(HbA1c), employing ratios of BG to HbA1c or the SHR to evaluate SH. Meanwhile, if the reference offered the OR value for pneumonia in patients with SH versus non-SH, we adopted the authors’ definition of SH. In cases where the OR values for pneumonia were not reported, we categorized patients into non-SH and SH groups based on the authors’ grouping, defining the lower half of blood glucose levels as non-SH and the higher half as SH. The metrics and cutoff values of each reference, along with information on whether patients with SH include those with diabetes, were recorded for subsequent subgroup analysis across different thresholds of SH. Pneumonia encompassed all types of pneumonia that occurred after a stroke, regardless of whether the patients underwent tracheal intubation or not [27].

Data extraction and quality assessment

The data independently extracted by the two researchers are as follows: first author, publication year, country, study design, sample size, sex ratio, setting, type of stroke, the mean or median of age, the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS), the primary and secondary outcomes, the time point of pneumonia assessment, metrics and thresholds of SH, the inclusion of diabetes or not and the number of individuals who developed pneumonia in participants with hyperglycemia versus normal BG. The NIHSS is a graded neurological assessment increasingly recommended for quantifying stroke severity in clinical settings. It evaluates various aspects, including speech, language cognition, inattention, visual field abnormalities, motor and sensory impairments, and ataxia [28]. And, the methodological quality of the included studies was assessed by two reviewers independently using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [29], a widely used tool for evaluating the quality of case-control and cohort studies [30]. Discrepancies between the two reviewers were resolved through discussion, and if necessary, with the involvement of a third reviewer (Jiajiang Jiang).

Outcome measures and data synthesis

The number of pneumonia cases and total sample size were extracted from twelve studies for both the SH and non-SH groups. The data extracted from studies were imported into Review Manager software (Revman, version 5.3.0). We used the calculated ORs and their corresponding 95% confidence interval (CIs) to determine the association between SH and pneumonia, presented visually using forest plots, and confirmed the stability of the results through sensitivity analysis. The results of meta-analysis and subgroup analyses were computed using the random-effects model proposed by DerSimonian and Kacker [31]. A P-value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. Moreover, the subgroup analyses aimed to determine whether the associations persist across different types of stroke, various settings, diverse NIHSS score levels, distinct time points of pneumonia onset, metrics of SH, the thresholds of SH and the inclusion of diabetes. Higher NIHSS scores were defined as a mean or median NIHSS score of 15 or higher, while the remaining studies were classified into the lower subgroup. Heterogeneity among studies was quantified using Cochran’s Q statistic (heterogeneity χ²) and the I² statistic. We interpreted I² values of 25%, 50%, and 75% as representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05 for the Q test [32, 33]. Moreover, meta-regression analysis was performed to identify factors influencing the strength of the association. Sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the stability of the meta-analysis results using the leave-one-out method and subgroup analysis stratified by methodological quality. This included sequential omission of individual studies and analyses based on varying quality levels, such as higher quality (NOS score ≥ 8) [34], lower quality (NOS score < 8), specific scores of NOS = 7, 8, and 9, as well as excluding studies with an NOS score of 7, 8 and 9. Publication bias was initially assessed using a funnel plot in RevMan, followed by Begg’s test and Egger’s test in Stata. If Egger’s test indicated the presence of publication bias (P < 0.05), the Trim and Fill method was utilized to adjust the study findings. Finally, the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework was applied to systematically evaluate the quality of evidence for the association between SH and the risk of pneumonia in stroke patients, using the GRADEPro GDT platform. The sensitivity analysis, meta-regression analysis and the Trim and Fill method were conducted in Stata 18.0.

Results

Selection process and characteristics of included studies

A total of 34,926 references were identified through the search strategy across six databases. After removing duplicates, 27,647 articles remained for title, abstract, and keywords screening. Subsequently, 1,084 studies were screened in full text. However, 23 articles were inaccessible for full-text review, and 1026 studies failed to meet the inclusion criteria. Ultimately, 35 studies remained. After excluding 22 articles due to a lack of detailed data and including one study identified through reference screening, 12 studies were included in the meta-analysis. The flow diagram of the selection process is shown in Fig. 1.

Flow chart of included articles

The characteristics of the included studies are presented in Table 1. The analysis encompassed 6 prospective studies and 6 retrospective studies. In calculating the pooled incidence rate of pneumonia, we excluded one cross-sectional study Elhefnawy et al., [58] and focused solely on the 11 cohort studies, which collectively enrolled 15,626 stroke patients. Among these, 2,244 cases of pneumonia were identified, yielding an overall incidence rate of 14.4%. In studies where the average age was available, the mean age of participants was 65.68 ± 1.23 years, and across all studies, the proportion of male participants was 59.4 ± 1.73%.

Quality assessment

The quality of the selected studies was acceptable. Eleven studies achieved NOS scores ranging from 7 to 9 points (Table 2), while one study scored 5 points [35]. Only five studies clearly demonstrated that pneumonia was absent before stroke onset. Additionally, two studies [10, 35] did not describe how pneumonia was assessed. Furthermore, four studies [8, 35–38] did not specify the timing of pneumonia assessment.

The association between SH and pneumonia

The forest plot in Fig. 2 illustrates the results of the meta-analysis. The pooled OR is 2.01 (95% CI: 1.72 to 2.34), indicating a significant positive association between SH and pneumonia. This result is statistically significant (p < 0.01). The individual study weights range from 2.7 to 13.6%, with You et al. [37] contributing the highest weight, thus exerting the greatest influence on the overall effect size. In terms of heterogeneity, the studies exhibit moderate variability, with an I² value of 54%. The Q test is statistically significant (Chi² = 23.99, df = 11, p = 0.01), suggesting there is some variability across studies.

Sensitivity analysis

Results of the sensitivity analysis using the leave-one-out method indicate that the estimates remain relatively stable with the overall effect size estimate hovering around 0.70 and none of the omitted studies cause a drastic change in the effect size or the confidence intervals. Moreover, sensitivity analysis revealed a significant association between SH and pneumonia across all NOS-based groupings (p < 0.01). Subgroup analysis by methodological quality showed that studies with NOS ≥ 8 exhibited lower heterogeneity (OR:2.06, 95% CI: 1.68–2.51, I² = 44.7%, p = 0.107) compared to those with NOS < 8 (OR:1.98, 95% CI: 1.58–2.48, I² = 55.0%, p = 0.049). Analyses for individual NOS scores (NOS = 7, 8, and 9) demonstrated consistent effect estimates for the association between SH and pneumonia, however, the NOS = 8 group had a confidence interval (95% CI: 0.389–1.074) that included 1, indicating potential instability. Furthermore, significant heterogeneity differences were identified across the four subgroups: NOS < 8(I2 = 55%, p = 0.049), NOS = 9(I2 = 66.2%, p = 0.031), exclusion of NOS = 7(I2 = 53.0%, p = 0.047), and exclusion of NOS = 8(I²= 62.4%, p = 0.004). The detailed results of the sensitivity analysis are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table Ⅱ.

The subgroup analyses of SH and pneumonia

The results of subgroup analyses are presented in Supplementary Fig. 2. The overall pooled effect size was 1.94 (95% CI: 1.83 to 2.05), representing a 0.07 reduction compared to the previous meta-analysis conducted without subgroup stratification. The overall heterogeneity is 44% (Chi²=122.83, df = 69, p < 0.01), lower than the previous meta-analysis. The OR varied across subgroups, with all effect sizes showing statistically significant differences(p < 0.01). In the subgroup of pneumonia occurring within the first 7 days of onset, the pooled OR is 2.06 (95% CI: 1.69 to 2.52), with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 44%, p = 0.11). For ischemic stroke, the OR is 1.72 (95% CI: 1.52–1.95), compared to the intracerebral hemorrhage subgroup, which has an OR of 1.85 (95% CI: 1.47–2.33). Further, in the analysis of study design, prospective studies showed a higher OR of 2.10 (95% CI: 1.67 to 2.63) with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 41%, p = 0.13), compared to retrospective studies, which had an OR of 1.93 (95% CI: 1.53 to 2.43) and higher heterogeneity (I² = 68%, p < 0.01). Regarding NIHSS scores, both lower and higher score subgroups demonstrated significant effects, with ORs of 2.06 (95% CI: 1.65 to 2.58) and 1.69 (95% CI: 1.37 to 2.09), respectively. Also, subgroup analyses stratified by different SH measurement metrics revealed differences in ORs. The association was particularly strong when SH was defined using the SHR or BG to HbA1c ratio(OR: 2.53, 95% CI: 2.14 to 2.98) compared to the metric of BG(OR: 1.76, 95% CI: 1.55 to 2.01). Notably, the grouping by glucose level thresholds showed that a glucose level ≥ 7.8 mmol/L was associated with an OR of 1.79 (95% CI: 1.50 to 2.14) with no heterogeneity (I² = 0%), while glucose levels < 7.8 mmol/L had a slightly higher OR of 1.82 (95% CI: 1.38 to 2.39) with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 57%, p = 0.07). Additionally, studies excluding patients with diabetes mellitus showed a higher effect size compared to the subgroup that included diabetic patients (2.32,95%CL:1.67–3.21 vs. 1.92,95%CL:1.61–2.30).

Meta-regression analysis

The results of meta-regression analysis are presented in Supplementary Table Ⅲ. The random-effects meta-regression model was fitted using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method. The regression coefficient represents the magnitude of a variable’s impact on the effect size (logOR), while the standard error reflects the precision of this estimate. The z-value, calculated by dividing the coefficient by its standard error, tests its significance, with a p-value less than 0.05 indicating statistical significance. A significant difference in odds ratios (ORs) was observed based on the metrics of SH, with a coefficient of 0.375 (95% CI: 0.174–0.575, p < 0.01). Other variables, such as study design, stroke type, NIHSS score levels, pneumonia within the first 7 days, setting, thresholds of SH, including diabetes or not and the diagnosis of pneumonia didn`t significantly influence the associations between SH and pneumonia (p > 0.05).

This study also utilized meta-regression to analyze the sources of heterogeneity(Supplementary Table Ⅲ). The percentage of heterogeneity (I²) indicates the extent of residual heterogeneity, with a p-value less than 0.05 indicating statistical significance, and the R-squared value (R-squared [%]) represents the proportion of total heterogeneity explained by the regression model. Among all the regression analyses conducted, those based on stroke types and thresholds of SH explained 73.53% and 93.70% of the heterogeneity, respectively, while the analysis focusing on the metrics of SH completely accounted for the heterogeneity(100%). After accounting for these factors, the remaining heterogeneity was reduced to zero in the three regression analyses.

Publication bias

The funnel plot of meta-analysis was used to analyze the publication bias, revealing a moderate balance(Supplementary Fig. 3). The funnel plot shows a slightly asymmetric shape, which might suggest a certain degree of publication bias. Furthermore, Egger’s test (p = 0.366) and Begg’s test (p = 0.150) reveal no statistically significant evidence of publication bias, as illustrated in Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5. Additionally, the Trim and Fill analysis identified several potentially missing studies on the left side of the funnel plot, suggesting some degree of publication bias(Supplementary Fig. 6). After adjustment, the pooled effect size of OR slightly decreased from 2.01 (95% CI: 1.72 to 2.34) to 1.95 (95% CI: 1.69 to 2.27), indicating that publication bias had minimal impact on the overall results.

The assessment of the grading of recommendations assessment, development and Evaluation(GRADE)

The evidence was initially rated as low certainty due to the observational study design. While the presence of a large effect size (OR: 2.01, 95% CI: 1.72–2.34) warranted upgrading the evidence to moderate certainty, substantial heterogeneity (I² = 54%) led to downgrading in the inconsistency domain. Additionally, no significant limitations were identified in other GRADE domains (risk of bias, indirectness, or imprecision). Consequently, the final certainty of evidence was adjudicated as low. The complete GRADE assessment is detailed in Supplementary Table Ⅳ.

Discussion

This study provides insights into the association between SH and pneumonia. Previous reviews have mostly focused on the influence of SH on mortality and poor functional outcomes [9, 39, 40], while a systematic analysis of its relationship with pneumonia remains lacking. This systematic review is the first comprehensive analysis of the impact of SH on pneumonia in stroke patients. In this review, the pooled incidence of pneumonia observed in this study is similar to that reported in previous research [5, 41]. The meta-analysis revealed a significant positive association between the presence of SH and pneumonia risk in stroke patients. The underlying mechanisms remain insufficiently studied. However, SH may be associated with phagocyte dysfunction [42, 43], resulting in suppressed immune function [44]. Additionally, it has been proposed [45] that SH induces an increase in pro-inflammatory mediators, such as tumor necrosis factor-α(TNF-α) and interleukin-6(IL-6) [46], thereby contributing to a higher incidence of pneumonia.

The meta-regression results indicate that the strength of the association varied significantly depending on the definition and metric used for SH (p < 0.01). In particular, studies employing the SHR or the BG-to-HbA1c ratio demonstrated significantly stronger associations compared to those using absolute BG levels. The positive coefficient (Coefficient = 0.375) suggests that SHR and the BG-to-HbA1c ratio are more sensitive indicators for identifying the risk of pneumonia. SHR, a novel index proposed by Roberts et al. in [47], is calculated as the ratio of admission BG to the estimated average glucose (AG), with AG derived using Nathan’s 2008 formula [48]: AG (mg/dL) = 28.7 × HbA1c (%) − 46.7. SHR have been shown in several studies [49–52] to better predict adverse outcomes in patients with SH compared to BG alone, primarily because they adjust for baseline BG levels. The observed heterogeneity in this meta-analysis may be largely attributable to the inconsistent definitions of SH across studies. Traditional reliance on single BG measurements fails to distinguish acute hyperglycemic stress from chronically elevated glucose levels, particularly in patients with diabetes [53]. In contrast, metrics like SHR and the BG-to-HbA1c ratio incorporate baseline glycemic control, offering a more individualized and pathophysiologically relevant assessment of stress hyperglycemia [54]. Therefore, future research should aim to adopt standardized definitions, with SHR emerging as a promising candidate to improve comparability across studies and enhance risk stratification in both diabetic and non-diabetic populations.

While we conducted subgroup analyses by stoke types, NIHSS scores, various settings and so on, other potentially significant confounders - including detailed stroke characteristics (lesion location, dysphagia severity), comorbidity burden (premorbid functional status, chronic lung disease), and variations in clinical management (antibiotic prophylaxis, glucose control protocols) - may not have been fully accounted for in the included studies. These unmeasured factors, inherent to observational designs, could partially explain the observed association between SH and pneumonia. Future prospective studies with more comprehensive characterization of potential confounders are needed to better elucidate this relationship.

Among the 12 included studies, the majority also analyzed the relationship between SH and both mortality and functional outcomes. Several studies [8, 10, 35–38, 55–57]indicate that SH is associated with an increased risk of mortality, significantly correlating with both in-hospital mortality and longer-term mortality at 3 or 12 months post-discharge. Additionally, Wang et al. [8] found that extremely low glucose levels were also linked to increased mortality. Furthermore, SH was also significantly linked to poorer functional outcomes, notably increasing the incidence of functional impairment at 3 months post-discharge [10, 35, 38, 56]. Importantly, this association persisted independently of in-hospital pneumonia [56, 58]. And according to the findings of Rinkel et al. [38], the association between ABG and poor outcome (mRS score 3–6) was J-shaped. Studies also suggest that SH may increase the likelihood of recurrent stroke [36] and additional infections [56], such as urinary tract infections.

The GRADE framework was applied to systematically evaluate the reliability of the meta-analysis results and identify factors that might influence the certainty of evidence. The GRADE assessment rated the evidence as low quality, primarily due to the observational nature of the included studies, the presence of residual confounding, and the moderate heterogeneity observed across studies (I² = 54%). These limitations preclude causal inference and necessitate cautious interpretation of the observed association between SH and pneumonia risk. Although our findings suggest a potentially important clinical relationship, the low certainty of the evidence means these results should be viewed as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive. It is essential that future studies, preferably with prospective and standardized designs, validate these findings using more rigorous methods and adjusted analyses.

From a clinical perspective, these findings raise awareness of the potential increased risk of post-stroke pneumonia associated with acute-phase stress hyperglycemia. However, they do not support immediate changes in clinical practice, particularly regarding intensive glucose control. Numerous clinical trials have shown that strict glycemic control has not consistently improved outcomes in stroke patients [59–61], and may in fact increase the risk of iatrogenic hypoglycemia, which itself is associated with worse neurological outcomes and increased mortality [62, 63]. Therefore, until higher-quality evidence becomes available, clinicians should avoid overly aggressive glucose-lowering strategies in the acute stroke setting, and instead consider individualized glycemic management that balances potential infection risk against the dangers of hypoglycemia. Future research should explore targeted thresholds or trajectories of glucose elevation that are predictive of infection risk without compromising patient safety.

The present meta-analysis has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, significant heterogeneity was observed across the included studies (I²=54%, P = 0.01), indicating substantial variability in effect sizes beyond random chance. Meta-regression analyses revealed that this heterogeneity primarily stemmed from differences in stroke type (IS vs. ICH; I2 < 0.01, p = 0.71), SH measurement metrics (blood glucose vs. SHR/GAR; I2 < 0.01, p = 0.38) and glucose thresholds (≥ 7.8 vs. <7.8, I2 < 0.01, P = 0.23). Subgroup analysis stratified by stroke types demonstrated complete homogeneity within both IS and ICH cohorts (I²=0% for each subgroup). Notably, this homogeneity was consistently observed following the exclusion of three studies [7, 10, 35] that included mixed stroke populations (both IS and ICH) or did not mention the specific stroke type. This finding suggests that the overall heterogeneity observed in the meta-analysis may primarily originate from the studies with combined stroke types.

Another significant source of heterogeneity is the inconsistent definition of SH, largely due to variations in measurement metrics and differing glucose thresholds used in the included studies. To address this, the review systematically classified the evaluation metrics based on two key methodological considerations: (1) whether they accounted for baseline glycemic status (HbA1c), which exhibits substantial variation between diabetic and non-diabetic populations, and (2) whether they employed the standardized glucose threshold of 7.8 mmol/L as defined by the American Diabetes Association (ADA) guidelines [64]. The analysis revealed that both the metrics and thresholds contributed to some of the observed heterogeneity. Although variations in SH definitions influenced subgroup-specific effect sizes, all pooled ORs exceeded 1 with statistical significance (p < 0.05), consistently supporting a positive association between SH and pneumonia. Nevertheless, standardizing SH diagnostic criteria remains imperative to ensure the reliability of future meta-analyses in this field. Among various metrics, the SHR has emerged as a promising tool, with several studies suggesting a best threshold around 0.95 [65, 66].

In this study, the definition and assessment timing of post-stroke pneumonia varied considerably across the included studies, potentially affecting the interpretation and comparability of results. Three studies [37, 57, 58] diagnosed pneumonia based on the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) criteria [67], providing a standardized clinical reference. In contrast, four studies [7, 35, 56, 68] explicitly excluded pneumonia present at admission, aiming to capture only hospital-acquired or post-stroke cases. Dziedzic et al. [55] clearly limited inclusion to hospital-acquired pneumonia, whereas Cai et al. [10] uniquely assessed pneumonia onset within 48 h after discharge, thereby excluding any in-hospital cases. Several other studies did not specify diagnostic criteria, introducing further definitional heterogeneity. Additionally, the timing of pneumonia assessment also varied, ranging from the first 48 h of admission to 48 h post-discharge. However, only Cai et al. [10] considered post-discharge events. Due to these discrepancies, our subgroup analysis focused on pneumonia occurring within the first 7 days of hospitalization, as this was the most consistently reported time frame. This limitation precluded direct comparisons between early-onset and delayed-onset pneumonia and may have led to underestimation of SH’s association with longer-term infection risk. Future studies should adopt standardized pneumonia definitions and assessment windows to improve cross-study comparability and clarify whether SH differentially affects short-term versus long-term pneumonia risk.

Additionally, some degree of publication bias was identified based on the funnel plot and the Trim and Fill analysis. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 6, the funnel plot of the Trim and Fill analysis displays a slight asymmetry, particularly on the left side, suggesting a potential absence of studies with lower effect sizes. However, the slight asymmetry didn`t reach a statistically significant level according to Egger’s test (p = 0.366) and Begg’s test (p = 0.150). To further evaluate its impact, missing studies (yellow dots) were imputed using the Trim and Fill method. The adjusted pooled OR was 1.95 (95% CI: 1.69–2.27), slightly lower than the original estimate (OR: 2.01; 95% CI: 1.72–2.34). Importantly, the positive association between SH and pneumonia remained statistically significant (p < 0.01), suggesting that the overall findings are robust despite the potential bias.

Conclusions

Results of this systematic review and meta-analysis indicated that SH was significantly associated with post-stroke pneumonia, suggesting a higher incidence of pneumonia among patients experiencing acute-phase SH. The strength of this association varied according to the SH metric used (BG versus SHR and BG to HbA1c ratio). However, our findings should be interpreted with caution, as they are hypothesis-generating rather than definitive for clinical practice. The quality of evidence was rated as low based on the GRADE evaluation. Importantly, the observational design of the included studies limits causal inference, and the presence of residual confounding—such as unmeasured differences in dysphagia severity, comorbidity burden, or clinical management strategies—cannot be excluded. These factors may have influenced pneumonia risk independently of SH, thereby affecting the validity of the observed associations. Moreover, heterogeneity in the definitions and timing of both SH and pneumonia likely introduced measurement bias. These limitations highlight the urgent need for future research to standardize diagnostic criteria for both SH and pneumonia, establish clinically relevant time windows for pneumonia surveillance post-stroke, and identify robust SH indicators. Future studies should also explore predictive models that adjust for confounding factors and help to better stratify pneumonia risk in stroke patients with SH.
