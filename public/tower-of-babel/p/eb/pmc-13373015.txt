Comparison of pediatric proximal femoral locking plates and dynamic hip screws in the fixation of pediatric intertrochanteric fractures, a biomechanical study
Authors: Jou-Hua Wang, Chien-An Shih, Chi-Hsiu Wang, Chia-Che Lee, Kuan-Wen Wu, Ting-Ming Wang, Ken N. Kuo, Chih-Kai Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Treatment options for pediatric intertrochanteric fractures range from conservative casting in young children to surgical fixation in older patients, most commonly using dynamic hip screws (DHS) or pediatric proximal femoral locking plates (PF-LCP). As there is limited biomechanical evidence favoring one method over another, this study aimed to compare the biomechanical stability of DHS and PF-LCP for the treatment of pediatric intertrochanteric fractures using a synthetic bone model. Sixteen synthetic composite femurs were allocated into two groups stabilized with either a DHS or a PF-LCP. To simulate Delbet type IV intertrochanteric fractures, a vertical osteotomy was created in each specimen. Biomechanical evaluation was carried out using a universal testing machine, assessing axial stiffness, vertical, horizontal, and total displacement, as well as ultimate load to failure under standardized loading conditions. Comparative statistical analyses were then performed to determine differences in biomechanical performance between the two fixation methods. Group P demonstrated significantly smaller vertical and total displacements (0.15 ± 0.15 mm and 0.41 ± 0.31 mm) compared with the DHS group (0.59 ± 0.42 mm and 0.75 ± 0.33 mm) (p = 0.018 and p = 0.045), whereas horizontal displacement showed no difference (p = 0.142). Axial stiffness and ultimate failure load were comparable between groups. All specimens failed at the level of the most distal screw; however, fracture sites occurred significantly more distal in the DHS group (89.7 ± 3.1 mm) than in the PF-LCP group (62.1 ± 2.3 mm, p < 0.001). In a synthetic bone model, PF-LCP group showed less vertical and total displacement than the DHS when fixing intertrochanteric fractures. This suggests that the locking plate may offer a stronger and safer option for treating pediatric intertrochanteric fractures.

Introduction

Proximal femur fractures are extremely uncommon, accounting for less than 1% of all pediatric fractures [1, 2]. The Delbet classification is widely applied, categorizing proximal femur fractures as follows: type I, transepiphyseal; type II, transcervical; type III, cervicotrochanteric; and type IV, intertrochanteric. Most of the available literature has concentrated on types I–III, with reported complications including nonunion and avascular necrosis of the femoral head. Type IV fractures generally carry the most favorable prognosis. The literature on fractures of the trochanteric region(Delbet type IV) of the femur in children is scarce, with most reports derived from geriatric populations. These fractures usually result from one of two mechanisms: high-energy trauma or, in the case of low-energy trauma, the presence of an underlying pathological bone condition [3].

Because of the rarity of pediatric intertrochanteric fractures, evidence-based management guidelines have not been established. In toddlers and in cases of non-displaced fractures, conservative approaches—such as casting or traction—remain viable treatment options. In older children and adolescents, however, surgical intervention is generally preferred, with commonly employed techniques including dynamic hip screws and plate fixation. With the advent of locking plate technology, its use has become widespread in the adult trauma population. A limited number of studies have reported the application of locking plates for pediatric proximal femur fractures, suggesting that this method may be a safe and effective option for children and adolescents [3–5]. Nonetheless, there is currently no consensus regarding the superiority of any specific fixation technique.

To overcome the limitations of previous studies, the present study compared the biomechanical performance of dynamic hip screws (DHS) and proximal femoral locking plates (PF-LCP) in the fixation of pediatric intertrochanteric fractures using a synthetic bone model. We hypothesized that the PF-LCP would demonstrate superior biomechanical stability compared with the DHS in this setting.

Materials and methods

Experimental specimens setup

This study utilized sixteen small, left-sided, fourth-generation synthetic composite femurs (model 3414; Sawbones, Vashon Island, WA, USA). Synthetic specimens were selected in place of cadaveric bones due to the inherent difficulties in procuring pediatric femoral specimens. To approximate adolescent anatomy, a small femoral model with a 9.5 mm intramedullary canal and an overall length of 375 mm was chosen, corresponding to the anatomical dimensions of an approximately 11-year-old patient [6]. Each specimen was sectioned into a 20 cm proximal segment and randomly assigned to one of two groups: the DHS group (Group D) or the PF-LCP group (Group P), with eight specimens per group. The anatomic axes of the femurs were aligned within 6-cm cylindrical plastic tubes, and the distal portions were secured using industrial concrete.

Before creating the intertrochanteric fracture, all specimens were pre-drilled to facilitate subsequent anatomic reduction. Proximal femoral osteotomies were then performed with a line saw machine to simulate a Delbet type IV fracture, using a vertically oriented cut angled at 70° from the horizontal. The fractures were anatomically reduced and stabilized with either a DHS or a PF-LCP, as described below. All fixations were carried out by two pediatric orthopedic surgeons under fluoroscopic guidance to ensure optimal implant length and positioning.

In Group D, the surgical procedure began with the insertion of 3.0 mm Kirschner wires under a 135° angled guide and fluoroscopic guidance. The K-wires were placed at the lower third of the femoral head on the anteroposterior (AP) view and centrally within the femoral head on the lateral view. A triple reamer was set to a length of 65 mm—the shortest lag screw available commercially—and reaming was performed down to the stop. The screw length was selected in accordance with the principle of physis preservation in pediatric fracture fixation. A lag screw was then inserted, followed by impaction of the DHS blade. The DHS plate was oriented along the femoral shaft, fixed to the shaft, and secured with four cortical screws. Finally, a compression screw was applied to achieve fragment compression (Fig. 1A, B).

In Group P, fixation was performed using a PF-LCP with 5.0 mm screws (5.0 mm Pediatric LCP Hip Plate 120˚, DePuy Synthes, West Chester, PA, USA). The procedure began by positioning the plate in a standardized location. A 5.0 mm cortical screw was first inserted into the diaphysis after drilling with a 3.5 mm drill bit. The remaining locking screws were then placed following drilling with a 4.3 mm drill bit. Three proximal locking screws, each 65 mm in length, were inserted into the femoral neck. As in Group D, screw length was determined according to the principle of physics preservation in pediatric fracture fixation (Fig. 1C, D).

Biomechanical testing protocol

Mechanical testing was carried out using a universal material testing machine (AG-X; Shimadzu Corp., Tokyo, Japan). Mechanical evaluation was carried out using a universal testing machine (AG-X; Shimadzu Corp., Tokyo, Japan). The distal end of each specimen was secured in a custom-designed metal fixture and mounted on the testing apparatus, following the methodology described in previous studies. A vertical load was applied to the center of the femoral head via a stainless-steel plate attached to a 5000 N load cell. Compressive force was delivered at a valgus angle of 7°, replicating the biomechanics of a normal bipedal stance. During setup, a leveling device was used to verify precise alignment of the valgus angle. (Fig. 2)

The biomechanical testing protocol was adapted from previously published methods [7]. Each specimen was first subjected to a 200 N preload at a crosshead speed of 2 mm/min. The load was then increased to 600 N at the same rate, and axial stiffness was determined from the slope of the linear elastic region of the load–displacement curve. Subsequently, cyclic loading between 100 and 1000 N was applied at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/s. Reference marks were placed on the proximal and distal fragments, and photographs were taken before and after cyclic loading to measure horizontal, vertical, and total displacement using open-source software NIH ImageJ (version 1.54p). Finally, a load-to-failure test was performed at a crosshead speed of 5 mm/min. Following catastrophic failure, both the ultimate failure load and the failure mode were recorded.

Statistical analysis

A priori sample size estimation was conducted using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7; Düsseldorf, Germany), informed by a pilot study of six specimens randomly allocated into two groups. The calculation assumed an α level of 0.05 and statistical power (1 – β) of 0.80. Based on the mean values and standard deviations of ultimate failure loads from the pilot study, an effect size of 1.72 was derived. Accordingly, a total of 14 specimens (seven per group) were determined to provide adequate statistical power.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics, version 22 (IBM SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive data for both groups are reported as means and standard deviations. Group comparisons for parametric variables were conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Axial stiffness in Group P (1098 ± 174 N/mm) was comparable to that in Group D (1241 ± 191 N/mm) (p = 0.191). After cyclic loading, vertical displacement (0.15 ± 0.15 mm) and total displacement (0.41 ± 0.31 mm) were significantly smaller in Group P compared with Group D (vertical displacement: 0.59 ± 0.42 mm; total displacement: 0.75 ± 0.33 mm) (p = 0.018 and p = 0.045, respectively). Horizontal displacement showed no significant difference between the groups (Group D: 0.36 ± 0.16 mm; Group P: 0.27 ± 0.39 mm; p = 0.142).

All specimens were ultimately loaded to failure. The ultimate failure load in Group P (2833 ± 270 N) was comparable to that in Group D (3103 ± 572 N) (p = 0.312). Failure modes were consistent across both groups, with all specimens sustaining fractures at the level of the most distal screw (Fig. 3). However, the fracture level, measured from the lesser trochanter, was significantly more distal in Group D (89.7 ± 3.1 mm) compared with Group P (62.1 ± 2.3 mm) (p < 0.001).

Discussion

The most important finding of the present study was that using PF-LCP to fix intertrochanteric fractures in this synthetic bone model resulted in smaller vertical and total displacements during cyclic loading compared with DHS, indicating that PF-LCP provides superior time-zero stability. Previous studies have shown that factors influencing the risk of complications include the stability of fixation [2, 8]. However, little is known about the most appropriate fixation method for treating pediatric intertrochanteric fractures. Hence, the present study evaluated the biomechanical properties of intertrochanteric fractures fixed with either PF-LCP or DHS, providing additional information for clinical reference.

Delbet type IV, or intertrochanteric fractures, extending from the greater to the lesser trochanter, have the lowest reported risk of avascular necrosis (AVN), with an incidence of about 12% [9]. Unlike intracapsular fractures, complications such as AVN or growth disturbances are rarely encountered in extracapsular trochanteric fractures, since the epiphysis is not involved. However, other complications have been described, including coxa vara deformity, leg-length discrepancy, and nonunion, which may result in functional impairment [10]. As a stable fixation construct for intertrochanteric fractures can reduce the risk of complications [2, 8], the findings of the present study suggest the use of PF-LCP, as it provides superior biomechanical properties.

The use of PF-LCP has been expanded to include fracture fixation of the proximal femur, although it was initially introduced to facilitate intertrochanteric and subtrochanteric osteotomies. A key contribution to its clinical development came from Slongo et al. of the Bernese group, who demonstrated that the device’s multiple screw angles, varied plate designs, and angular stability conferred by locking screws make it applicable and versatile for pediatric proximal femoral fixation [11]. However, shortly after their introduction, early adult studies reported complication rates as high as 38% and 70% in two separate series due to mechanical failure [12, 13]. Evidence regarding their effectiveness in pediatric fracture fixation remains scarce. Cope et al. conducted one of the largest retrospective studies to date, examining the use of PF-LCP specifically for fractures [14]. Fixation failure occurred in 17.4% of fractures fixed with PF-LCP but in none treated with other constructs (p < 0.05). Complications included screw breakage, bending, and loss of reduction, mainly in Delbet type II and III fractures. Importantly, no fixation failures were reported in Delbet type IV fractures, leaving uncertainty regarding the optimal implant for these injuries. The findings of the present study support the clinical observations by Cope et al. [14]., showing that PF-LCP provides superior biomechanical properties, potentially decreasing the risk of mechanical failure in Delbet type IV fractures.

The present study showed that group P exhibited significantly smaller vertical and total displacements compared with group D. This phenomenon may be related to the screw-plate locking mechanism in group P, which prevents micromotion of the fixation construct. However, it is worth noting that the screw-plate locking mechanism does not allow for compression across the fracture line. In contrast, the use of DHS can facilitate compression across the fracture line [15], leading to better bony contact. Therefore, caution should be exercised when choosing PF-LCP to treat this type of fracture, especially if the initial quality of reduction is suboptimal.

In addition to DHS and locking plate fixation, elastic stable intramedullary nailing (ESIN) has been described as a viable surgical option for pertrochanteric femoral fractures, particularly in children younger than six years of age. Kaiser et al. reported favorable outcomes using ESIN in this fracture pattern, highlighting its minimally invasive nature, suitability for young children, and the potential to avoid spica cast immobilization [16]. However, ESIN was not included in the present biomechanical comparison, which represents an area for future investigation.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a time-zero biomechanical analysis; therefore, the biological processes of fracture healing and the influence of preexisting bone pathology could not be assessed. Future clinical investigations are warranted to validate fixation strength and to evaluate the effectiveness of these methods in promoting fracture healing and addressing preexisting bone pathology. Second, synthetic composite femurs rather than cadaveric specimens were used for biomechanical testing. Although small synthetic models have not yet been formally validated against true pediatric bone, they were selected in this study to approximate pediatric anatomy because of the difficulty in obtaining pediatric cadaveric femurs. Third, intertrochanteric fractures were created using a saw-cut technique, which does not fully replicate unstable or complex fracture configurations seen in clinical practice. Finally, only axial loading was applied during mechanical testing, and torsional or multidirectional loading conditions were not evaluated.

Conclusion

In a synthetic composite bone model, the pediatric proximal femoral locking plate demonstrated less vertical and total displacement in the fixation of intertrochanteric fractures compared with the dynamic hip screw. These findings suggest that the pediatric proximal femoral locking plate may provide a safer and more robust fixation construct for the treatment of pediatric intertrochanteric fractures.
