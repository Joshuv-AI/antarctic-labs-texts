Cardiac tamponade as an initial atypical presentation of T-cell lymphoblastic lymphoma in a 17-year-old adolescent: a case report
Authors: Mahamadou Charfo Bacharou, Rim Belmalyani, Patrick Mulendele, Sadissou Gambobo, Abdel Nasser Drighil
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundT-cell lymphoblastic lymphoma is a type of non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma, which usually affects adolescents and young adults. The usual presentation is characterized by B symptoms and adenopathy associated with a mediastinal mass. Here we describe a rare case of T-cell lymphoblastic lymphoma in an adolescent, presenting as cardiac tamponade.Case presentationThe patient is a 17-year-old Moroccan adolescent with no medical history who presented with dyspnea. Echocardiography showed a large pericardial effusion, collapse of the right cavities, and noncompressible dilatation of the inferior vena cava. This tamponade picture required an emergency pericardiocentesis, and the thoracic computed tomography scan showed a mediastinal mass of 132 × 105 mm. Pleuropericardial biopsy made the diagnosis of certainty of T-cell lymphoblastic lymphoma. The complementary management consisted of a combination of chemotherapy.ConclusionLife-threatening emergency, cardiac tamponade is a rare presentation of T-cell lymphoblastic lymphoma. Management consisted of pericardiocentesis, complemented by combination chemotherapy.

Background

Pericardial effusion can result from infectious or autoimmune causes, post-radiation or chemotherapy, solid tumors, or hematological malignancies including lymphoma [1–3]. Lymphoma can, in the late stage of its evolution, lead to a pericardial effusion [1]. T-cell lymphoblastic lymphoma is a rare type of non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma, rarely causing pericardial tamponade and even more rarely presenting as an initial presentation [2]. Cardiac tamponade is a life-threatening emergency due to the sudden onset and/or extent of pericardial effusion, requiring emergency pericardiocentesis or pericardial drainage [3, 4].

Here we describe a rare case of T-cell lymphoblastic lymphoma, revealed by pericardial tamponade in a 17-year-old male adolescent. Pericardial tamponade required pericardiocentesis. Once stabilized and the diagnosis of lymphoma accepted, he was transferred to the clinical hematology department for chemotherapy.

Case presentation

A 17-year-old Moroccan adolescent, with no medical history, presented in the emergency room for chest pain evolving 10 days prior to a mild blunt chest trauma and which had worsened 4 days ago by the occurrence of dyspnea first at effort and then at rest. On examination the patient was polypneic at 30 cycles per minute, saturated at 98% ambient air. Blood pressure was 130/80 mmHg, tachycardia at 110 beats per minute, and had a muffled heart sound, jugular vein enlargement, and no crackling lung sounds. Examination revealed right pleural effusion; the rest of the physical examination was unremarkable. Electrocardiogram (ECG) registered sinus tachycardia 110 beats per minute with electrical alternans (Fig. 1). Transthoracic echocardiography revealed massive pericardial effusion, measured in subcostal view, 26 mm anteriorly and 23 mm posteriorly (Fig. 2), with systolic collapse of the right chambers and exaggerated transvalvular respiratory flow variation (30% in mitral valve and 50% in tricuspid valve). Dilated inferior vena cava was at 22 mm. In addition, a mediastinum cystic mass in contact with the heart extending to the superior vena cava was noted. In view of the cardiac tamponade, the patient underwent a pericardiocentesis with removal of 1500 ml of serohematic fluid. Chest computed tomography (CT) scan performed after pericardiocentesis showed a large anterior and middle mediastinal mass compressing the tracheobronchial axis and vascular elements with probable superior vena cava syndrome. It measured 132 × 105 mm and extended over 132 mm (Figs. 3 and 4). Irregular right pleural thickening of suspicious appearance was associated with right pleural effusion. Laboratory analysis revealed sodium level at 134 mmol/l; kidney (urea/creatinine), hepatic [aspartate aminotransferase/alanine aminotransferase (ASAT/ALAT), prothrombin time (PT)], and thyroid (TSHus) functions were normal. C-reactive protein (CRP) was 106 mg/L, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was 1485 IU/L, and complete blood count (CBC) levels were as follows: hemoglobin (Hb) = 12.5 g/dl, white blood count (WBC) = 10,180/mm3, and platelets (PLT) = 244,000. The serologies [human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) 1–2, hepatitis B and C, syphilis] were negative. The immunological assessment (anti-DNA antibodies, anti-nuclear antibodies, soluble anti-antigen antibodies) were negative, except for positive rheumatoid factor. Cytology of the pericardial fluid and transparietal biopsy of the mass were performed and histological analysis with morphological and immunohistochemical study demonstrated a T-lymphoblastic lymphoma. The morphological study of the specimen after staining with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) revealed striated and fibro-adipose muscle tissue infiltrated by a tumor proliferation of diffuse architecture, made up of small- to medium-sized monomorphic cells (Fig. 6). Immunohistochemistry showed diffuse expression of cluster of differentiation (CD)3 (Fig. 7), Terminal desoxynulceotidyl transferase (Tdt) (Fig. 8), CD10, and CD99 (Fig. 9) by the tumor cells. Absence of expression of CD20 by the tumor cells (Fig. 10). The proliferation index evaluated with Ki-67 was estimated at 95%. Discussion

Lymphoblastic lymphoma is a rare and aggressive form of non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma (NHL), occurring in adolescents or young adults with a male:female sex ratio of 3:1(1). T-cell lymphoblastic lymphoma accounts for about 90% of NHLs [1, 2]. Its incidence is estimated to be about 0.1–3 per 100,000 population per year [2, 3]. Although pericardial involvement due to malignancies has been described, it remains a rare and late manifestation of lymphomas and leukemias [4, 5]. Malignant pericardial effusion due to lymphoma is even more rare, with only a few cases having been reported [4–6]. Here we report a case of a 17-year-old adolescent with no medical history who presented with a lymphoma revealed by pericardial tamponade. Pericardial effusion may be due to direct tumor infiltration or related to venous–lymphatic compression or hematogenous dissemination. It may also be secondary to autoimmune disorders or to chemotherapy or radiotherapy used for the treatment of this malignancy [3, 4, 7].

The classic presentation of lymphoma is lymphadenopathy and B symptoms (fever, weight loss, night sweats), usually associated with a mediastinal mass that can be manifested by compressive signs [1, 3, 6, 7]. Dyspnea is often the main symptom leading to consultation, and may be related to compression of the superior vena cava by the mediastinal mass and pleural and/or pericardial effusion [1]. Pericardial localization of lymphoma is rare and even rarer in the form of cardiac tamponade [3, 6, 8, 9].

Pericardial involvement of non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma is a rare and usually late manifestation, approximately 20 months after diagnosis of the lymphoma [3, 8]. It accounts for 0.5% of cardiac involvement and only 1% of extracardiac involvement [4, 10].

Transthoracic echocardiography is the first-line modality for the diagnosis of tamponade, which will be confirmed by the right heart catheterization [3, 8]. Echocardiography allows for diagnosis of tamponade to be made by specifying the elements that have good sensitivity, of at least 90%, such as the appearance of a swinging heart, Right Atrial (RA) systolic collapse, early Right Ventricular (RV) collapse, and a dilated vena cava that cannot be modulated by breathing [8]. In our case, dyspnea associated with chest pain led to electrocardiogram being carried out. This showed sinus tachycardia with electrical alternans. The transthoracic echocardiography confirmed the diagnosis of large pericardial effusion and tamponade. The presence of a pericardial effusion is associated with a significant decrease in survival and the occurrence of major adverse events such as cardiac tamponade, cardiovascular collapse, and death [1, 4].

CT is one of the scans carried out as part of the etiological workup of any pericardial effusion. Its interest is to confirm or rule out a possible tumoral process [6]. Mediastinal mass is not uncommon in non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma; it is found in 50–75% of cases [1–3, 6, 7]. Thoracic CT scan carried out in our adolescent patient allowed for the highlighting of the compressive mediastinal mass. The diagnosis of lymphoblastic lymphoma was made by histological examination. In our case, the histology and immunohistochemical markers confirmed T-lymphoblastic lymphoma.

After pericardiocentesis, patient was no longer dyspneic and was started on chemotherapy treatment by the clinical hematology team for his lymphoma.

Conclusion

Pericardial tamponade is a life-threatening emergency, requiring rapid evacuation of fluid by pericardiocentesis or pericardial drainage. It is an extremely rare manifestation of T-cell lymphoblastic lymphoma. We report a case of lymphoblastic lymphoma with mediastinal mass presenting as cardiac tamponade in an adolescent.
