The effects of precision intervention based on user profile on the improvement of postpartum parenting behavior for rural‑to‑urban floating women: a study protocol for a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Yani Deng, Lu Liu, Ting Li, Lingling Huang, Qiyu Fang, Jiangtao Ouyang, Jia Li, Jiali Wu, Jing Chen, Xujuan Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRural-to-urban floating women often face great challenges in postpartum parenting, which negatively affects the well-being of these women and their infants. User profiles allow tailored support, making precision interventions possible. Consequently, a randomized controlled trial (RCT) protocol was designed to evaluate the effects of user profile–based precision intervention on improving postpartum parenting behaviors for rural-to-urban floating women.MethodsThis single-blind, three-arm multicenter RCT will enroll 360 mothers. The control group (n = 120) will receive standard postnatal care. The conventional intervention group (n = 120) will receive usual care plus access to a unified five-module internet-based support program (ISP) parenting platform. The innovative intervention group (n = 120) will receive usual care plus a precision intervention, in which the content of ISP is tailored to the participant’s assigned user profile group. Primary outcomes include maternal parenting behavior scores, maternal self-efficacy scores, and infant growth and development indicators. Secondary outcomes include postpartum depression scores, social support scores, and satisfaction with the intervention model. Outcomes will be assessed at baseline (T0), 6 months post-baseline (T1), 12 months post-baseline (immediately post-intervention, T2), and 6 months post-intervention (T3).DiscussionIf proven effective, this precision intervention will provide practical support for rural-to-urban floating women in China. As the first user profile-based precision intervention study targeting this population, it will offer evidence for clinical practice and could be applicable to immigrant mothers in other regions.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500098613). Introduction

Over the past 30 years, China’s rapid economic growth and accelerated urbanization have driven massive rural-to-urban migration, creating a group known as the “floating population” or “internal migrants“ [1, 2]. However, under the dual rural-urban household registration system (hukou), they are still officially classified as rural residents [1, 3]. In 2024, China’s floating population reached 380 million, of whom nearly half were women, primarily concentrated in megacities such as Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Shenzhen. Currently, about 65% of these female migrants are young women of childbearing age [3–6]. Although they have contributed significantly to economic growth, their access to healthcare services and social welfare remains far more limited than that of urban residents, due to the household registration system and constrained medical resources. As a result, these women face a central challenge: balancing their new maternal identity with the demands of infant care [7, 8].

Infancy is a period of rapid physical, psychological, and behavioral development [9]. As the “first responsible persons” for childcare, mothers’ parenting competence is closely linked to their children’s healthy development [7, 8, 10]. Many studies have shown that mothers’ lack of scientific parenting knowledge and skills during the postpartum period negatively affects the well‑being of both mothers and their children [11, 12]. However, most postpartum parenting research in China focused on city residents, with little attention to rural-to-urban floating women. Our preliminary study found that 75% of rural-to-urban floating women had suboptimal parenting care [13]. Moreover, the mental health of this group cannot be overlooked: approximately 54% of them experienced postpartum depression [13]. Two qualitative studies further revealed that compared to urban women, rural-to-urban floating women experience greater parenting stress, often feel “willing but unable to cope,” and struggle with parenting problems on their own. Thus, enhanced parenting support and focused attention for this group are critically needed [14, 15].

At present, traditional postpartum parenting interventions—both in China and abroad—are largely delivered offline and have shown some positive effects [16–23]. For example, one interventional study showed that Maternal Self-efficacy (MSE) was improved through face-to-face structured clinical interview (N = 83) [16]. Another study revealed that cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), based on the transtheoretical model (TTM), effectively improved postpartum psychological well-being, significantly enhancing maternal subjective well-being and parenting competence [17]. However, traditional face-to-face parenting interventions are often constrained by time, space, and cost, limiting their reach [24]. The widespread use of mobile internet, big data, and smart devices has accelerated the growth of mobile health (mHealth), leading to numerous online platforms for maternal and child care worldwide [24–27]. Nevertheless, a major service gap remains. Most existing platforms target wealthier families for profit and exclude rural migrant mothers [26–28]. Without appropriate and accessible support, these platforms fail to provide effective assistance for this population [29]. To address this gap, we developed an Internet-based Support Program (ISP) parenting platform specifically for rural-to-urban floating women in China [24]. In a randomized controlled trial (RCT) study, the ISP parenting platform significantly improved maternal self-efficacy scores at the end of the intervention (T0) and three months after the intervention (T1) (T0, mean difference = 6.66, 95%CI: 4.88–8.45, P < 0.001; T1, mean difference = 5.88, 95%CI: 4.08–7.67, P < 0.001) and decreased postpartum depression score (T0, mean difference = 3.05, 95%CI: 2.30–3.80, P < 0.001; T1, mean difference = 2.46, 95%CI: 1.82–3.09, P < 0.001) [8]. Although the ISP platform has demonstrated short-term benefits, its lack of precision leads to poor long-term user engagement. Furthermore, given the large and growing number of rural‑to‑urban floating women, routine uniform parenting interventions are too time‑consuming and labor‑intensive for widespread use. Therefore, precision interventions tailored to their diverse needs are urgently required to improve their parenting behaviors.

User profiling, a concept originating from marketing, refers to a data-driven archetypal model of target users constructed from authentic behavioral and demographic data [30]. Such profiles are typically algorithmically generated based on basic attributes (e.g., gender, education) and behavioral data [31–33]. User profiling has been widely studied and applied in fields such as personalized healthcare [34–36]. However, to date, user profiling has not yet been applied to postpartum parenting research, and no such profile exists for rural-to-urban floating women in particular. Given the limited medical resources and the growing needs of this population, effective support strategies are urgently needed. User profile–based precision interventions offer a promising approach. Therefore, we designed a randomized controlled trial (RCT) protocol to evaluate the effectiveness of a precision intervention based on user profiles in improving postpartum parenting behaviors among rural-to-urban floating women.

Method

Aim

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the effects of precision intervention based on user profile on the improvement of postpartum parenting behaviors for rural-to-urban floating women, including maternal parenting behavior, maternal self-efficacy, postpartum depression, and social support.

We hypothesize that, compared with the control group and unified intervention group at baseline (T0), 6 months post-baseline (T1), 12 months post-baseline (immediately post-intervention, T2), and 6 months post-intervention (T3), women in the precision intervention group will report statistically significant:

Design and recruitment

This research uses a single-blind, three-arm, multicenter, RCT design and followed the SPIRIT Statement and the applicable guidelines for standard clinical trial protocols. The completed SPIRIT checklist is provided as Additional file 1.

Study participants will be recruited from the maternity wards of four affiliated hospitals of X University. All admitted women will be screened for initial interest. Eligible women will then receive detailed information about the study’s purpose and procedures via information sheets, posters, and brochures, and will be assured that they may withdraw at any time without penalty. Participants

Women who meet the following inclusion criteria will be eligible for this study: aged 20–40 years; have resided in the study site for more than six months with no plan to leave within the next year; hold rural household registration (hukou); have delivered a healthy, full-term infant; are married and living with their husband; are able to read, speak, and write; own and can use a smartphone; have good online habits (e.g., regular internet use); are willing to obtain scientific parenting knowledge online; and voluntarily sign the informed consent form. Exclusion criteria include having a serious physical or mental illness, being unable to cooperate or refusing to participate, or having participated in other related parenting intervention studies within the past five years.

Randomization and blinding

Participants will be randomly assigned to three groups in a 1:1:1 ratio: a control group, a conventional intervention group, and an innovative intervention group. Randomization will be performed using the Research Randomizer tool. To conceal group allocation, sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes will be prepared and kept by independent staff. Outcome assessors and data analysts will be blinded to group assignment, but intervention providers will not be blinded due to the nature of the interventions.

Sample size

Based on pre-experimental data (means: 72.2, 68.8, 61.4; pooled SD: 11.6), using ANOVA with α = 0.05 and 80% power (β = 0.20), the minimum required sample size was 69 per group, accounting for pairwise comparisons. Considering a 40% attrition rate during follow‑up, we inflated the sample size to 120 per group. Thus, a total of 360 participants will be recruited and equally allocated (1:1:1) to the three groups.

Control group

Participants assigned to the control group will receive standard postnatal care, which includes in-hospital postpartum health education, postpartum home visits following hospital discharge, and the Maternal and Child Health Handbook (MCH Handbook). The Maternal and Child Health Handbook is a standardized maternal and child health record developed by a panel of experts organized by the National Health Commission (NHC) and has been promoted for nationwide use in China.

Conventional intervention group

In addition to postnatal usual care, participants allocated to this group will receive a one‑year unified intervention delivered through the ISP parenting platform. The theoretical framework of the ISP is based on Bandura’s self‑efficacy theory [38] and social exchange theory [39]. More details on the development of the ISP to promote parenting capability among Chinese mothers have been described in a previously published peer‑reviewed journal [40]. Unlike other commercially available parenting platforms, the ISP has been revised to incorporate several unique features tailored specifically to the characteristics of rural‑to‑urban floating women (see Table 3). The revised ISP retains its original five‑module structure (learning, communication, ask‑the‑expert, baby home, and reminder forums). However, each module has been redesigned from the ground up to address the specific characteristics of rural‑to‑urban floating women, including low educational level, low self‑confidence, high price sensitivity, lack of family support, and poor adherence. To meet these needs, the platform introduces tiered curricula, peer matching, free professional support, a curated knowledge base, and personalized reminders (Fig. 2 Content of the ISP for the conventional intervention group).

This tiered, standardized curriculum has been developed by parenting experts and researchers based on authoritative guidelines, expert consensus, and textbooks. Content is delivered in multiple formats, including text, images, audio, and video, which participants access via the ISP platform. According to learning difficulty, the curriculum is divided into three levels—beginner, intermediate, and advanced—enabling participants to progress step by step from basic to more advanced parenting knowledge and skills. To enhance intervention effects, each training session is accompanied by a learning outcome test. Assessments are designed to be engaging, using game‑based level passing, one‑on‑one or one‑to‑many competitive quizzes, and parenting video contests, with an emphasis on encouragement and rewards. Participants must complete and pass the test after each module before advancing to the next level.

Beginner level (low difficulty) curriculum covers transitioning to the maternal role; postpartum physical and mental self‑care; scientifically appropriate “Doing the month” (zuo yue zi); postpartum exercise guidance; breastfeeding, mixed feeding, and formula feeding; growth and development of infants aged 0–3 months; newborn massage; observation and home care of neonatal jaundice; and umbilical cord care and prevention of omphalitis. Each topic takes approximately 10 min to complete.

Intermediate level (moderate difficulty) curriculum covers maternal emotional management; benefits of exclusive breastfeeding; growth, development, and simple monitoring of infants aged 0–6 months; infant dental care; effective parent–child interaction and play; sources of infant security; daily infant care; vaccination precautions; introduction of complementary foods; and home safety assessment. Each topic takes approximately 15 min to complete.

Advanced level (high difficulty) curriculum covers the importance of happy parenting and emotional stability for mothers; application of multi‑sensory stimulation; maternal exercise and rehabilitation; growth, development, and detailed monitoring of infants aged 0–12 months; early education and parent‑child interaction; infant psychological development characteristics; warning signs requiring timely medical attention; prevention and home care of common childhood illnesses; prevention and home care of common accidental injuries; recognition and first aid for foreign body aspiration in the trachea; and infant cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR). Each topic takes approximately 20 min to complete.

After enrollment, participants are matched based on data similarity and assigned to peer support groups of 5–7 members each. Group discussions focus on maternal and infant care topics, encouraging experience sharing, mutual encouragement, and the formation of “mother circles” with similar backgrounds. This approach fosters resonance and a sense of belonging while expanding support beyond the family context.

Innovative intervention group

Participants in the innovative intervention group will receive standard postnatal care plus a one‑year precision intervention delivered through the ISP parenting platform. This study is the first to apply user profiling technology to postpartum parenting for rural‑to‑urban floating women, and to establish an innovative tripartite precision intervention model involving researchers, healthcare providers, and participants (see Fig. 3).

Second, cluster analysis is performed to categorize participants into distinct profile groups using data from baseline questionnaires and ISP platform usage (e.g., module access, task completion). Guided by the Fogg Behavior Model [41], which posits that behavior (B) occurs when ability (A), motivation (M), and a trigger (T) converge simultaneously (B = A + M + T), we classify participants based on their levels of parenting ability and motivation. This yields four user profile groups: high ability & high motivation, high ability & low motivation, low ability & low motivation, and low ability & high motivation. Accordingly, the number of clusters (k) is set to 4.

Precision intervention delivery

Based on the assigned user profile group, each participant receives a tailored intervention through the ISP platform. The intervention components and their intensity are adjusted to match the participant’s ability and motivation levels, thereby optimizing the “trigger” (the precision intervention itself) to promote effective parenting behaviors (See Table 5).

The modules are the same as those described for the conventional intervention group (see above), but with increased frequency and content tailored according to user profile labels (e.g., parenting needs, ability, motivation) for precise analysis, targeted delivery, and dynamic optimization.

Outcome measure

Primary outcome

Maternal parenting behavior scores: In this study, maternal parenting behaviors will be assessed using a structured observation method. Researchers will score mothers on a standardized parenting task scale (0-100) through either on-site or remote observation. The assessment includes four tasks: two mandatory tasks - cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and the Heimlich maneuver on an infant simulator - and two randomly selected tasks, one drawn from 13 basic daily care tasks and the other from 19 common infant illness care scenarios.

Maternal self-efficacy scores: The Self-efficacy in Infant Care Scale (SICS) will be used to measure maternal self-efficacy. It has 46 items across four dimensions. Each item is scored 0-100, with higher scores indicating greater confidence. The scale has good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.96; subscales range from 0.86 to 0.96) [42] .

Infant growth and development indicators: Medical staff will measure the infant’s length, weight, head circumference, chest circumference, etc., and plot growth curves. These measurements will be evaluated according to Chinese neonatal and infant growth standards. The infant’s left upper arm circumference will also be measured to assess nutritional status. An infantometer (measuring board) will be used to measure the infant’s recumbent length, accurate to 0.1 cm. An infant scale will be used to measure body weight, accurate to 10 g.

Secondary outcome

Postpartum depression scores: It will be assessed using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) [43]. The scale has 10 items, each scored 0–3. Total scores range from 0 to 30 based on the mother’s feelings over the past week. A score of 9 or higher suggests possible depression [44]. The Chinese version of the EPDS shows good reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.87) and validity (r = 0.79 with the Beck Depression Inventory) [45].

Social support scores: Postpartum social support will be assessed using the Postpartum Social Support Scale (PSSS). This 20-item Likert-scale self-report instrument yields a total score ranging from 0 to 60, with higher scores indicating a greater perceived level of social support [16]. The scale demonstrates sound psychometric properties, with an internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s α) of 0.89 and a content validity index of 0.90 [16].

Satisfaction with the intervention model: Measurements were conducted through qualitative interviews and self-made questionnaires.

Data collection and analysis

At baseline, we will collect demographic and clinical data from mothers in the maternity ward and ask them to complete three questionnaires (SICS, EPDS, PSSS). Follow-up assessments will be conducted at T1, T2, and T3. At each time point, the same questionnaires will be sent to mothers by WeChat or email to complete. To improve response, reminders will be sent by phone or WeChat one week before and after each follow-up.

All maternal data were analyzed using K-means cluster analysis (k = 4) to form four distinct user profiles based on the Fogg Behavior Model. All statistical analyses will be performed using SPSS 29.0. Baseline data will be reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), median with interquartile range (IQR), or frequency (%). Skewed variables will be transformed as appropriate. Missing data will be handled using the intention‑to‑treat (ITT) principle. Group comparisons at baseline will use one-way ANOVA for normally distributed continuous variables, the Kruskal-Wallis H test for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. For outcomes, we will use one‑way ANOVA if data are normally distributed; otherwise, Kruskal‑Wallis H test. If overall differences are found, post‑hoc comparisons will be performed using Tukey's HSD following ANOVA, or Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction following the Kruskal‑Wallis test. Furthermore, a Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) model will analyze changes over time (T0, T1, T2, T3), focusing on group-by-time interaction to evaluate intervention effects on parenting behavior, maternal self-efficacy, social support, and postpartum depression. All statistical tests will be two-tailed, with a significance level of 0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethical Committee of Medical School in Shenzhen University (approval number: PN-202400193), and registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500098613). All procedures will be conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.The ethical approval document is provided as Additional file 2.

The study poses no additional risks or harm and will not interfere with routine treatment. All participants will provide written informed consent and will be free to withdraw from the study at any time withoutpenalty. Data will be kept anonymously and confidentially.

Validity and reliability

This study adopts a single‑blind, three‑arm, multicenter RCT design with allocation concealment and blinding of outcome assessors and data analysts. The primary outcome—maternal parenting behavior—is assessed through structured observation rather than self‑report, thereby reducing social desirability bias. Two independent researchers will score the observations. Inter‑rater reliability will be assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) based on 20% of randomly selected observations.

The precision intervention is guided by theoretically derived user profiles based on the Fogg Behavior Model, with profile reassessment at six months to enable dynamic tailoring. All protocols are standardized, and researchers receive uniform training. The other main outcomes are measured using validated instruments (SICS, EPDS, PSSS) with good reliability and validity. The study follows CONSORT 2025 guidelines and is registered in a public clinical trial registry (ChiCTR2500098613). Together, these measures enhance internal, external, and construct validity.

Discussion

As the first RCT to evaluate a user profile-based precision intervention for improving parenting outcomes among Chinese rural-to-urban floating women, this study offers both methodological innovation and practical value. If proven effective, this precision intervention would replace the conventional one‑size‑fits‑all model with a data‑driven framework: mothers are stratified according to their parenting ability and motivation, enabling personalized content, intervention intensity, and support strategies. The ISP platform could also be adopted as a routine digital tool in community health centers and hospitals, where health professionals refer mothers to the platform after delivery to provide ongoing smartphone‑based support that enhances parenting skills, self‑efficacy, and mental health.

In addition, the intervention would help reduce disparities in parenting support between urban residents and floating women, thereby promoting health equity from early life by directly reaching underserved mothers and mitigating barriers such as low income, limited education, and lack of family support. The same methodology can inform the development of culturally adapted parenting interventions for other disadvantaged populations, including migrant mothers in other countries, low-income rural mothers, and ethnic minority groups.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, participants will be recruited from maternity wards of four urban hospitals in a single Chinese city, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future multicenter trials should include hospitals across different regions and levels (e.g., community or rural hospitals) to enhance external validity. Second, although the follow‑up period extends to 18 months (12 months of intervention plus 6 months post‑intervention), this may still be insufficient to evaluate long‑term sustainability of parenting behavior changes and child developmental outcomes. Longer follow‑up periods would provide more definitive evidence on enduring effects. Third, the intervention relies on internet access and digital literacy, which could inadvertently exclude women with limited technology skills, potentially introducing selection bias. Fourth, due to the nature of the behavioral intervention, it was not possible to blind the intervention providers or the participants themselves, which may introduce performance bias. However, outcome assessors and data analysts will be blinded to mitigate detection bias.
