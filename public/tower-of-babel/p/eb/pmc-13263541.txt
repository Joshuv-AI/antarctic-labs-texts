Radiotherapy and Cardiovascular Disease Survival Risk in Non‐Small Cell Lung Cancer: A Population‐Based Study From the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results Database
Authors: Li Liu, Chunhui Fan, Wenxin Ren, Mingyi Wen, Cunqi Li, Cuiping Hao, Yian Geng, Liping Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIt is well known that radiotherapy increases the cardiovascular risk of cancer patients, but there are few large‐sample studies with cardiovasular‐specific death as the endpoint in lung cancer patients.MethodsPatients aged ≥ 18 years diagnosed with non‐small cell lung cancer were retrospectively enrolled from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database spanning the years 2004 to 2021. Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression model was employed to assess prognostic factors influencing survival outcomes.ResultsA total of 89,569 patients met the eligibility criteria and were analyzed. Multivariate analysis did not find the effect of radiotherapy on CVD‐specific survival in the population with non‐small cell lung cancer (p = 0.58). Subgroup analysis was conducted on the queues before matching. Radiation therapy was found to be predictive of a higher risk of CVD‐specific survival in regional patients (HR, 1.68, 95% CI 1.1–2.59) and in those with stage III (HR, 2.16, 95% CI 1.26–3.69). We explored the relationship between radiotherapy and CVD‐specific survival at different follow‐up times after diagnosis, and found that radiotherapy was a risk factor for CVD‐specific survival in populations followed up for less than 2 years and 3–4 years.ConclusionOur analysis suggested that radiotherapy markedly amplified the risk of CVD‐specific survival in regional patients and those categorized as stage III. It is notable that in the patient cohort with a follow‐up period of less than 2 years, radiotherapy also became an independent risk factor for cardiovascular‐related survival in this group of patients.Keywords: cardiovascular disease; non‐small cell lung cancer; radiotherapy; the surveillance, epidemiology, and end results (SEER)

Introduction

Lung cancer and heart disease stand as the primary culprits, claiming the most lives among non‐communicable diseases globally [1]. These conditions share common etiologies, including cigarette smoking, increasing age, and socioeconomic deprivation. Approximately a quarter of people diagnosed with lung cancer are known to have concomitant cardiac disease [2, 3, 4]. The advancements in cancer treatment in recent years have been effective in prolonging the survival period of patients [5]. More and more lung cancer survivors may experience the side effects caused by cancer treatment. Therefore, cardiac complications and cardiogenic deaths related to cancer treatment cannot be ignored. Primary lung cancer causes significant medical and socio‐economic burdens [1]. Radiotherapy (RT) combined with systemic treatments such as chemotherapy, targeted therapy and immunotherapy offers the highest possibility of long‐term disease control for a large number of cancer patients who are not suitable for surgical treatment [6]. However, radiotherapy has clear cardiovascular risks, and it is crucial to minimize the risk of onset associated with this treatment. Some studies have confirmed that radiotherapy promotes cardiac events, but most articles take whether cardiac events occur as the endpoint, and few clinical studies use cardiovascular disease‐specific survival as the endpoint [7, 8]. Some scholars believe that subclinical cardiac insults are difficult to detect and diagnose clinically, yet they contribute to patient mortality. Consequently, some scholars suggest that all‐cause mortality may serve as a surrogate marker for radiation‐induced heart disease (RIHD) in large cohorts where relevant patient, tumor, and treatment data are available [9, 10].

Owing to the inherent challenges associated with evaluating such data prospectively, the utilization of high‐quality, population‐based databases is crucial in addressing these inquiries. Therefore, our aim is to investigate the relationship between radiotherapy and cardiovascular disease (CVD)‐specific survival in patients with non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), and to evaluate the prognostic factors of CVD‐specific survival in patients with NSCLC using the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. We focus on the short‐term and longterm effect of RT for CVD‐specific survival.

Materials and Methods

Data Resource

In the present retrospective investigation, we analyzed data sourced from the National Cancer Institute's SEER cancer registry program, a collaborative network of population‐based cancer registries. The SEER program constitutes a network of population‐based tumor registries that encompasses 28% of the United States population, including data on incidence, survival rates, and treatment modalitie [11]. The SEER program provides information on patient demographics, tumor characteristics, and initial treatment modalities (e. g., chemotherapy, radiotherapy, surgery). However, it does not include detailed information or data on subsequent therapeutic interventions. The SEER Stat software version 8.4.3 was employed to conduct the analytical procedures. Institutional Review Board approval was deemed unnecessary for this study, as it exclusively utilized de‐identified publicly available data.

Study Cohort

We identified the cohort from 17 registries released in November 2023 during the period from 2000 to 2021. This study included patients aged ≥ 18 years diagnosed with lung cancer during the period from 2004 to 2021 (International Classification of Cancer Diseases −3 [ICD‐O‐3] codes: [12] C34.0‐C34.3, C34.8, C34.9). Inclusion criteria: (1) Patients with positive histology; (2) Patients with primary cancer only or the first of two or more primaries; (3) Patients with lung cancer (ICD‐O‐3 codes: C34.0 to C34.3, C34.8, and C34.9); (4) Patients ≥ 18 years old; (5) NSCLC histological types: squamous (ICD‐O‐3 codes 8052, 8070–8076, 8078, 8083, and 8084), adenocarcinoma (8140, 8144, 8250, 8251, 8255, 8256, 8260, 8265, 8290, 8310, 8323, 8333, 8480, 8481, 8570, 8571, 8572, 8574, and 8576), and other carcinomas (8000, 8001, 8004, 8010, 8012–8014, 8020–8023, 8030, 8031–8033, 8042, 8046, 8050, 8082, 8123, 8150, 8200, 8230, 8240, 8244, 8246, 8247, 8249, 8252–8254, 8430, 8490, 8507, 8550, 8551, 8560, 8562, 8575, 8940, and 8980). Exclusion criteria: (1) Patients with a survival time of less than 1 month; (2) Cases with undocumented disease staging; (3) Individuals lacking mortality cause documentation; (4) Patients with unrecorded socioeconomic or geographic data; (5) Cases missing oncological treatment records. We adopted propensity score matching (PSM) to reduce the bias between the two groups. After matching, a total of 5704 patients were finally included, among which there were 2852 patients in the radiotherapy group and 2852 patients in the non‐radiotherapy group respectively. The covariates matched included age, gender, race, year of diagnosis, primary site, laterality, histology, marital status, stage, T and N classification, surgical procedure, chemotherapy, income, and residence. Survival analyses were conducted on the entire cohort and the matched cohort respectively. Multivariate analysis and subgroup analysis were conducted on the unmatched cohort. We chose the unmatched cohort for multivariate analysis instead of the matched ones because the matching process might eliminate the association between the matching covariates and the results and avoid the decrease in the effect size caused by the reduction in the sample size [13]. Overall survival(OS) is the primary endpoint and CVD‐specific survival is a pre‐specified exploratory endpoint. Cause of death was categorized by the International Classification of Diseases (ICD)‐9 code. CVD‐specific deaths include heart disease, hypertension, cerebrovascular disease, atherosclerosis, aortic aneurysm/dissection, and other diseases of arteries, arterioles, or capillaries [14]. The causes of death were determined from death certificates and confirmed by the patients' attending physicians, recorded by the health statistics of the National Cancer Institute [15]. The follow‐up time was determined by measuring the time from the initial date of cancer diagnosis to the occurrence of follow‐up failure, death, or the end of the study on December 31, 2021. Demographic and disease characteristics included age, gender, race, socioeconomic status (SES), marital status, urban or rural residence, geographic region, year of diagnosis, primary site of disease, tumor grade and stage, and information about the first course of cancer‐directed therapy. In the absence of individual‐level SES information, we utilized the median income of the census tract of residence at the time of diagnosis.

Statistical Analysis

The chi‐square test was used to analyze the categorical variables between the radiotherapy group and the non‐radiotherapy group. The Kaplan–Meier curve was used to compare OS, lung cancer‐specific survival rate and CVD‐specific survival rate, and the log‐rank test was used to determine the significant differences before and after PSM. Multivariate Cox regression analysis was conducted to clarify the key prognostic factors independently associated with CVD‐specific survival. Competing risk analysis was used to evaluate the impact of radiotherapy on CVD‐specific survival. Subgroup analyses were rigorously conducted, stratified by critical temporal parameters including diagnosis year and post‐diagnosis follow‐up duration. All statistical tests were bilateral and were statistically significant at p < 0.05. All analytical procedures were executed using the R statistical software package (http://www.R‐project.org, The R Foundation) and Free Statistics software version 1.9.2.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 89,569 patients met the eligibility criteria and were analyzed (Table 2). Among the cohort, 62.1% of patients were aged 65 years or older, with a predominant representation of White individuals (79.5%). Tumors originating in the upper lobe were observed in 60.3% of cases, while 58.0% of malignancies were localized to the right side. Histologically, adenocarcinoma comprised 49.6% of cases, followed by squamous cell carcinoma at 31.2%. Only 16.2% and 28% of the tumors were localized and regional stage, respectively, while 55.7% were distant stage. The American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) stage III constituted 29.9% of cases, while stage IV represented nearly half (46.8%) of the cohort. Notably, 89% of patients did not undergo surgical intervention, whereas chemotherapy was administered to 60.2% of the population. Demographically, over half (52.3%) of patients were married, and an overwhelming majority (82.6%) resided in county‐level regions.

Predictors of Radiation Therapy Use

Among the total patient population, 86,717 individuals (96.8%) received radiotherapy, whereas a mere 2852 (3.2%) forwent postoperative radiation therapy. In the multivariate logistic regression analysis, we found that age, race, marital status, income, year of diagnosis, stage, surgical method and chemotherapy were independent predictors of radiotherapy for patients (see Table 3). Compared with patients under 65 years old, lung cancer patients over 65 years old are 27% less likely to receive radiotherapy. Females, patients with a later diagnosis, Asian or Pacific Islanders, those at a regional stage, AJCC (II‐IV), N3, those from nonmetropolitan counties, and patients who had undergone surgery were more likely to receive radiation therapy. Conversely, black patients, married individuals, those with higher incomes, T3, T4 stages, and patients who had received chemotherapy were less likely to receive radiation therapy.

Survival Analysis for All Patients With NSCLC

In our study, the median follow‐up time was 12 months (range 1–215). The overall survival rates at 3 years and 5 years were 20.2% and 9.8% respectively. The ROC curve after propensity score adjustment shows an AUC of 0.8, indicating a good predictive effect. Whether in the overall cohort or in the post‐PSM cohort analysis, compared with patients who did not receive radiotherapy, patients who received radiotherapy had prolonged OS, and this was statistically significant (p < 0.0001, Figure 2A; p < 0.0001, Figure 2B). Lung cancer‐specific survival also improved significantly (p < 0.0001, Figure 2C; p < 0.0001, Figure 2D). In the pre‐PSM cohort, the CVD‐specific survival curves of the radiotherapy group and the non‐radiotherapy group intersected. This might be due to the small number of participants in the non‐radiotherapy group over the 3‐year follow‐up period (p = 0.035, Figure 2E). However, no statistically significant improvement in CVD‐specific survival due to radiotherapy was found after PSM (p = 0.26, Figure 2F).

Kaplan–Meier curves for all patients with and without postoperative radiation therapy: (A) OS before PSM; (B) OS after PSM; (C) Lung cancer‐specific survival before PSM; (D) Lung cancer‐specific survival after PSM; (E) CVD‐specific survival before PSM; (F) CVD‐specific survival after PSM.

Multivariate analysis of the pre‐matching cohort suggestd independent predictors for improving CVD‐specific survival, including being female (hazard ratio [HR] 0.67; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.62–0.72), a more recent diagnosis (HR, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.81–0.98; HR, 0.73; 95% CI, 0.65–0.81), Asian or Pacific Islander ethnicity (HR, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.74–1), right‐sided laterality (HR, 0.88; 95% CI, 0.82–0.94), undergoing surgery (HR, 0.59; 95% CI, 0.52–0.66), receiving chemotherapy (HR, 0.49; 95% CI, 0.45–0.54), and being married (HR, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.77–0.96). Predictors of worse CVD‐specific survival were a primary site in the lower lobe (HR, 1.16; 95% CI, 1.07–1.26), squamous cell carcinoma (HR, 1.27; 95% CI, 1.17–1.38), residence in nonmetropolitan counties (HR, 1.14; 95% CI, 1.02–1.28), N3 nodal involvement (HR, 1.17; 95% CI, 1.03–1.32), and older age (≥ 65 years) (HR, 2.03; 95% CI, 1.85–2.22). Radiation therapy was not an independent predictor of CVD‐specific survival (refer to Table 4).

Multivariate analysis of the unmatched cohort suggested independent predictors for improved OS to be female gender (HR 0.81; 95% CI, 0.8–0.82), radiation therapy (HR, 0.74; 95% CI, 0.71–0.77), more recent diagnosis (HR, 0.85; 95% CI, 0.84–0.87; HR, 0.64; 95% CI, 0.62–0.65), Black race (HR, 0.96; 95% CI, 0.94–0.99), and Asian or Pacific Islander ethnicity (HR, 0.83; 95% CI, 0.81–0.86), surgical intervention (HR, 0.56; 95% CI, 0.54–0.57), chemotherapy (HR, 0.57; 95% CI, 0.56–0.58), marriage (HR, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.9–0.94), higher income levels (HR, 0.93; 95% CI, 0.91–0.95; HR, 0.91; 95% CI, 0.88–0.93; HR, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.84–0.89), and T3 stage (HR, 0.97; 95% CI, 0.94–0.99). Predictors of worse OS were primary site in the lower lobe (HR, 1.09; 95% CI, 1.07–1.11), Squamous cell carcinoma (HR, 1.2; 95% CI, 1.18–1.22), regional (HR, 1.19; 95% CI, 1.14–1.25) and distant metastases (HR, 1.38; 95% CI, 1.31–1.46), higher AJCC stage, older age ≥ 65 years (HR, 1.22; 95% CI, 1.2–1.24), and residence in nonmetropolitan counties (HR, 1.04; 95% CI, 1.01–1.06) (Table 4).

We further explored the influence of various radiotherapy methods. The results showed that different radiotherapy methods did not have a statistically significant impact on CVD‐specific survival. Additionally, we found that all methods except radioisotope‐based treatment were associated with a significant improvement in the OS of patients (Table 4).

Discussion

The significant progress in lung cancer treatment methods and the subsequent prolongation of patients' survival have made monitoring the side effects and complications caused by lung cancer treatment increasingly important. However, current clinical guidelines often lack treatment recommendations for patients' previously existing comorbidities, which is a problem that must be comprehensively addressed in the constantly evolving treatment regimens. Notably, radiotherapy's biophysical effects exert dual consequences: while targeting malignant cells, collateral exposure inevitably impacts adjacent thoracic structures, significantly elevating risks of radiation‐induced cardiotoxicity. Conversely, emerging evidence reveals a bidirectional interplay, as preexisting cardiovascular pathologies may profoundly influence carcinogenesis patterns, disease progression trajectories, and therapeutic decision‐making frameworks [16, 17, 18]. Furthermore, cardiotoxicity is closely intertwined with shared risk factors for cardiovascular diseases (including smoking and hyperlipidemia) while damage inflicted on vital organs like the heart and lungs may paradoxically escalate cardiovascular mortality rates as patients survive longer. A growing body of clinical evidence underscores that lung cancer survivors undergoing radiotherapy confront an elevated risk of major adverse cardiac events (MACE), with studies revealing that the average cardiac radiation dose exhibits a dose‐dependent relationship with heightened MACE incidence [19]. However, some studies have shown (Guberina et al.) that the average radiation dose to the heart is not related to survival rate [20]. In this study, we did not find an association between radiotherapy and cardiovascular‐specific survival in the overall population of non‐small cell lung cancer. At present, there are no data on radiation dose‐volume parameters for cardiac substructures in the SEER database. We look forward to future studies that include these detailed data. However, subgroup analysis suggested that radiotherapy was a risk factor for CVD‐specific survival in patients with regional and AJCC stage III. The author speculates that the reason for this outcome might be because AJCC III or regional patients received higher radiation doses than patients with early‐stage lung cancer due to their later stage. However, compared with stage IV patients, due to factors such as the poorer general physical condition of stage IV patients, the radiation dose they receive is generally lower than that of stage III patients. Patients in stage III or regional have received larger radiation doses and thus have greater cardiotoxicity from radiation [21]. We need further research on the detailed radiation doses received by patients with tumors at different stages to further confirm this issue. Previous research results shows that increasing the radiation dose to the heart will increase adverse cardiac events [19]. Both the direct multivariate analysis and the competing risk model did not reveal any association between radiotherapy and CVD survival. However, in the subgroup analysis, we found that radiotherapy was associated with CVD‐specific survival in some populations, such as those in the III stage and regional areas. This suggests that only when the radiation dose to the heart reaches a certain level will it significantly affect CVD‐specific mortality. In most populations, the radiation dose is small, which may cause heart damage but does not affect CVD‐specific mortality. Detailed individual doses could have helped us determine the relationship between radiation doses and the specific mortality rate of cardiovascular diseases [22]. The comorbidities present at the diagnosis of non‐small cell lung cancer may be related to the treatment received and the mortality rate of heart disease [23, 24, 25]. However, there were no data on specific radiotherapy doses and patient comorbidities in the SEER database, so these data were not included in this analysis. However, the multivariate analysis of the matching cohort showed that right‐sided lung cancer was a favorable predictor of CVD survival compared to left‐sided lung cancer. It is speculated that this might be related to the fact that the heart is located on the left side, so the heart of the left‐sided lung cancer would receive a higher radiation dose.

We conducted a stratified analysis based on different years of diagnosis, and follow‐up time after diagnosis. The results suggested that radiotherapy was a risk factor for CVD‐specific survival only within 2 years and 3–4 years after diagnosis. This is consistent with the research findings that the number of hospitalizations for heart disease significantly increased after radiotherapy, and most hospitalizations for heart disease occurred in the first 2 years after radiotherapy [26, 27]. This might be due to the increase in the number of patients dying from CVD caused by acute or subacute cardiac injuries after radiotherapy. However, since conducting multiple subgroup comparisons would increase the risk of Type I errors, this study did not correct for multiple tests. Therefore, the research results are exploratory. Among patients with a survival time of more than 2 years, the number of cases that did not receive radiotherapy is too small, and larger‐scale studies are needed for analysis. Based on clinical experience, the authors believed that patients at different stages might receive different radiation treatment doses, and the impact of radiotherapy on CVD mortality might also vary. The damage to the heart caused by radiotherapy can be classified as acute damage and chronic damage. Therefore, the author chose to conduct subgroup analysis based on the follow‐up time. With the development of time, radiotherapy technology has made significant progress, and the damage to surrounding organs has also decreased. Therefore, subgroup analysis was conducted based on the year of diagnosis. After conducting subgroup analysis, the number of some subgroups was too small, which affected the statistical power and led to the possibility of false positive results.

However, no effect of radiotherapy on CVD‐specific survival was found in the overall population of non‐small cell lung cancer, which might be due to the following reasons:First of all, although the progress in treatment has reduced the short‐term mortality rate of lung cancer to some extent, it is still very high compared with other malignant tumors such as breast cancer and lymphoma [1]. In our study, the median follow‐up period was 12 months (ranging from 1 to 215 months), and the overall survival rates at 3 years and 5 years were 20.2% and 9.8%, respectively. Therefore, the number of patients who survive for more than 3 years after diagnosis is relatively small. The long‐term impact of radiotherapy on CVD‐specific survival may lead to bias in the results due to the small number of patients who survive for a long time. Furthermore, the high mortality rate of lung cancer leads to an underestimation of the risk of CVD‐related deaths, which also results in biased outcomes. It is well known that radiotherapy to the chest of patients with breast cancer or lymphoma can lead to RIHD many years later [21]. Lung cancer patients were older at the time of diagnosis than breast cancer patients (71 years versus 62 years) [28], and most patients were accompanied by multiple comorbidities. Furthermore, in radiotherapy for radical lung cancer, the radiation dose received by the heart is usually greater than that for breast cancer or lymphoma, especially in patients treated for stage III lung cancer [21]. Despite technological advancements, cardiotoxicity from chest radiotherapy is inevitable. Second, although death events are regarded as a reliable endpoint in oncology, for patients with non‐small cell lung cancer, it is an imperfect endpoint in RIHD. Because the heart plays a core role in the systemic response under acute medical conditions, studying the exact mechanism of death caused by cardiac dose becomes complex, which makes it difficult to accurately determine the cause of death in lung cancer patients [27]. The lack of sensitive cardiac monitoring methods after radiotherapy means that potential heart diseases may not be detected. It has been proposed that before the occurrence of acute medical stress factors such as sepsis or hemorrhage, event‐free (subclinical) cardiotoxicity does not have clinical significance, but emerges in these cases, hinders recovery and increases the risk of death due to concurrent medical problems [29, 30, 31]. Hospitalization for heart disease mainly occurs in patients with existing cardiovascular diseases, but more than 40% of the cases occur in patients without previous cardiovascular diseases [27]. This further highlights the increased risk of cardiac events in this group after receiving chest radiotherapy. Only 4.3% of the deaths in our research cohort were identified as mainly due to cardiac causes, which is much lower than the 25% mortality rate considered to be caused by cardiovascular diseases in the general population [32]. Certainly, the main reason for this outcome is the classification and treatment of diseases. However, we also have to consider whether there are any errors in the recorded causes of death for lung cancer patients. Some patients who died from cardiovascular causes were recorded as having died from tumors. As expected, most of the deaths were classified as cancer‐related, especially those who died at home. However, research shows that as many as one‐third of death certificates may be incorrect, and half of the autopsy results were not anticipated before death [33]. Some patients who died from CVD were recorded as having died from cancer, which might lead to false negative results in our study. However, since the causes of death in the SEER database cannot be further verified, we can only hope that future studies will place greater emphasis on the recording of patients' causes of death.

Lung cancer and its treatment methods make patients prone to cardiovascular diseases [34]. The interaction between radiotherapy and the hearts of lung cancer patients is a current research field, and ongoing studies are exploring the potential mechanisms. A recently published study found that there is an association between average cardiac radiation dose and cardiac events as well as all‐cause mortality in patients without previous cardiovascular diseases [35]. Future work in this aspect should take into account baseline cardiovascular diseases, heart‐specific hospitalizations and heart‐specific deaths. Studies suggest that we should increase suspicion of heart‐related deaths in those who have recently shown no signs of cancer progression, especially if the death is sudden and unexpected [27]. In this case, certified physicians must consider recording non‐cancer causes of death, taking into account the role of certified data in guiding future survival strategies for cancer patients.

The technological advancements in lung cancer radiotherapy, particularly the integration with treatment plans based on computed tomography (CT), have enhanced our ability to identify tumors and normal tissues. In the early 1990s, the combination of linear accelerators and CT imaging gave rise to three‐dimensional conformal radiotherapy [36, 37]. Improvements in these radiotherapy components have led to an increase in the ability to provide safe radiotherapy to the primary tumor. The toxicity of radiotherapy to the heart is also reduced, and we predict that the risk of CVD‐related deaths caused by radiotherapy will also decrease. Therefore, we conducted subgroup analyses of the data based on the diagnostic era, but unfortunately, no improvement in CVD‐specific mortality risk was found over the years. Of course, factors such as the accuracy of patient death cause records, whether cardiovascular diseases were present at the time of diagnosis, and other factors may affect the research results. We look forward to more precise diagnoses and comprehensive and clearly recorded studies with more detailed comorbidities.

Lung cancer has consistently ranked among the leading causes of death in Western populations [38]. For patients who are not suitable for surgery but may be cured, definitive RT combined with systemic therapy offers the greatest possibility of long‐term disease control. However, definite RT has cardiovascular risks, and it is crucial to reduce the side effects of this treatment. The cancer control rate of RT is also not ideal. Because 25%–40% of patients will experience local recurrence [39]. The results of the dose escalation test of RTOG‐0617 indicated that increasing the radiotherapy dose did not increase OS; instead, it led to a decrease in OS [22]. The higher mortality rate in this trial was associated with a higher dose of cardiac radiation. This finding is also supported by the recent Phase III LungART trial on postoperative pulmonary RT, in which although RT improved disease control, the overall survival rate did not increase due to the harmful cardiopulmonary effects [8]. In this study, radiotherapy improved the OS of patients in both the pre‐matching and post‐matching cohorts. However, we were unable to stratify based on the radiotherapy dose and investigate the relationship between the radiotherapy dose and OS. In the future, we hope that more studies will explore the relationship between the radiotherapy dose and radiotherapy method and the OS as well as the CVD‐specific survival rate of patients. Although in recent years, with people's increasing attention to the side effects of radiotherapy, radiotherapy technology has made great progress, its short‐term and long‐term effects are still significant and cannot be ignored. When lung cancer patients have concurrent cardiovascular diseases, they should be evaluated by cardiologists and radiotherapy should be carefully considered. We can also minimize the impact of radiotherapy on the heart by adjusting the radiation field and radiotherapy dose parameters [40]. The American Society of Clinical Oncology and the European Society of Cardiology have issued consensus and guidelines on the management of cardiotoxicity related to tumor treatment, and have proposed methods for monitoring and managing potential cardiotoxicity during tumor treatment, including risk stratification assessment and cardiotoxicity monitoring procedures [41, 42]. In view of the improvement in overall survival after radiotherapy for non‐small cell lung cancer and the increase in the occurrence of major clinical cardiac events, it is necessary for oncologists and cardiologists to jointly formulate strategies to reduce and manage cardiotoxicity.

Due to the large time span of this retrospective study, some confounding factors are difficult to avoid. First of all, the treatment and management of lung cancer and cardiovascular diseases have undergone significant changes over the past 20 years, so the research results may be biased. Secondly, targeted therapy and immunotherapy may lead to related hypertension, prolonged QT interval and myocarditis, etc. [43, 44]. Combined treatment may also aggravate adverse reactions. Thirdly, among the studied population, 96.8% (86,717 people) received radiotherapy, while only 3.2% (2852 people) did not. When conducting stratified analysis, the smaller number of people in the non‐radiotherapy group would also affect the reliability of the results. Only 3.2% of the patients in the cohort did not receive radiotherapy. There may be differences between the two groups in terms of stage, race, and the treatments already received. To reduce the impact of the imbalance in the population on the results, we conducted PSM to reduce the deviations between the two groups. Although PSM reduces the number of queues, the matched samples have better comparability. Moreover, the number of matched queues (n = 5704) is indeed smaller than the original data, but it is still quite large and still possesses sufficient statistical power. Fourthly, Residual confounding from unmeasured variables such as smoking, BMI, baseline cardiac medications and renal function. Indication bias due to differential radiotherapy uptake (lower utilization in patients ≥ 65 years and Black race). Fifthly, without baseline cardiac risk factors, attributing mortality differences to RT is problematic. For patients receiving combined treatment, their survival benefits have been confirmed, but it may increase the specific mortality rate of cardiovascular diseases. We still need studies that include detailed clinical data such as the radiation dose of patients, comorbidities present at the time of diagnosis, and more accurate causes of death. High‐quality prospective studies are needed to explore advanced radiotherapy techniques, such as magnetic resonance‐guided radiotherapy and proton beam therapy. These studies should include cardiac endpoints and biomarkers to understand the impact of cardiac protection strategies on the outcomes of lung cancer patients receiving thoracic radiotherapy.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our analysis suggested radiotherapy was associated with an increased risk of CVD‐specific mortality among regional patients and those classified as AJCC stage III. Furthermore, radiotherapy emerged as a substantial independent risk factor for cardiovascular‐related deaths within patient cohorts monitored for fewer than 2 years.
