Study on the Modes of Cell Death in Lung Cancer Epithelial Cells During Acute EBV Infection Using a Co‐Culture Model
Authors: Xinyue Zhang, Chenpeng Li, Shuilian Zhang, Wenqing Yan, Yu Chen, Danxia Lu, Zhi Xie, Pai Zhang, Xue Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivePulmonary lymphoepithelial carcinoma is an EBV‐associated malignancy; however, the pathogenesis of virus‐associated cancers remains incompletely elucidated. In particular, the early pathological changes and fate decisions of pulmonary epithelial cells following acute EBV infection have yet to be fully elucidated. This study aimed to investigate cell death modes of lung epithelial cells upon acute EBV infection.MethodsAn acute infection model was established by co‐culturing EBV‐positive Akata cells with lung cancer cells. Following infection, we evaluated morphological alterations and modes of cell death using immunofluorescence staining and confocal laser scanning microscopy.ResultsThe co‐culture system successfully established a 72‐h acute EBV infection in lung cancer cells, with ~10.3% of epithelial cells exhibiting EBV‐associated GFP fluorescence. Under these conditions, the majority of epithelial cells underwent cell death. More than half of the epithelial cells died by day 5 of co‐culture, and mortality progressively increased. At 72 h post‐infection, immunofluorescence analysis of pMLKL, cGSDMD, and cCASP3 revealed a significant upregulation of pMLKL protein expression (p < 0.001), whereas no significant differences were observed in cGSDMD (p > 0.05) or cCASP3 (p > 0.05) levels. ~15.1% of the remaining epithelial cells following B‐cell removal exhibited “cell‐in‐cell” structures. The internalized B cells underwent various forms of death, including apoptosis, pyroptosis, and necroptosis.ConclusionAcute EBV infection drives lung cancer epithelial cell death through pMLKL‐mediated necroptosis, without significant involvement of apoptosis or pyroptosis. Internalized B cells within “cell‐in‐cell” structures exhibit multiple death modalities. These findings provided novel insights into cellular fate decisions in EBV‐infected epithelium prior to latency.Keywords: apoptosis, EBV, necroptosis, primary pulmonary lymphoepithelial carcinoma, pyroptosis

Primary pulmonary lymphoepithelial carcinoma (PLEC) is a rare subtype of non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) characterized by distinctive histological features and is frequently associated with Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) infection. In the 2021 World Health Organization (WHO) classification of lung tumors, PLEC was reclassified as a subtype of primary lung squamous cell carcinoma (LSCC) and distinguished from pulmonary metastases of nasopharyngeal carcinoma [1, 2]. Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) is a ubiquitous gamma herpesvirus, with a global seroprevalence exceeding 90% in adults, and it establishes lifelong latent infection in B lymphocytes [3]. Although EBV latency typically remains asymptomatic in healthy individuals, the virus contributes significantly to the pathogenesis of several malignancies, including nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), lymphoepithelioma‐like carcinoma (LELC), Burkitt's lymphoma (BL), and certain types of gastric cancer [4, 5, 6], EBV‐associated malignancies account for approximately 1.5% of all cancer cases worldwide [7]. Recent studies have identified a prospective association between seropositivity for the viral capsid antigen (VCA)‐IgA antibody expressed during the EBV lytic phase and an increased risk of lung cancer, suggesting that EBV may indirectly contribute to the development and progression of select pulmonary tumors [6, 8]. The close relationship between PLEC and EBV infection is widely recognized [9, 10, 11]. Owing to its rarity, large‐scale randomized controlled trials to optimize treatment for PLEC have been limited; consequently, current therapeutic strategies are generally aligned with the standard regimens for LSCC.

The pathogenic mechanisms by which EBV contributes to PLEC development remain incompletely understood. The lack of reliable cellular and animal models for EBV infection of pulmonary epithelial cells has constrained investigations into early post‐infection cellular fate decisions, intracellular antiviral responses prior to latency establishment, and the process of malignant transformation following latent infection. Elucidating the dynamics of acute EBV infection in pulmonary epithelial cells and the ensuing cellular alterations is essential for understanding the early pathogenic events in PLEC.

Apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis constitute three major programmed cell death pathways. Caspases serve as key regulators of programmed cell death and can cleave gasdermin (GSDM) family proteins, leading to pore formation in the plasma membrane and subsequent pyroptosis. In human cells, GSDMD is cleaved by caspase‐1 and caspase‐4/5 to induce pyroptosis [12, 13]. Activation of the pro‐apoptotic BCL‐2 family proteins BAX and BAK increases mitochondrial outer membrane permeability, facilitating cytochrome c release and the assembly of the apoptosome—a heptameric complex involving APAF‐1—which activates executioner caspases‐3/7 to mediate apoptosis [14, 15]. Necroptosis, a caspase‐independent form of programmed necrosis, is driven by RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL. Phosphorylation of these proteins promotes plasma membrane pore formation, ultimately leading to cell lysis [16].

This study establishes an acute infection model by co‐culturing EBV‐positive Akata cells with lung cancer epithelial cells. The primary objective is to characterize the immediate phenotypic changes and modes of cell death in epithelial cells following acute EBV infection, thereby providing foundational insights into the mechanisms of inflammatory cell death and early cellular alterations preceding viral latency.

Materials and Methods

Main Reagents

4% paraformaldehyde fixative (Biosharp), Triton X‐100 (Biosharp), immunofluorescence staining blocking buffer (Beyotime), immunofluorescence staining primary antibody dilution buffer (Beyotime), immunofluorescence staining secondary antibody dilution buffer (Beyotime), phosphate‐buffered saline (Yeasen), p‐MLKL antibody (S358) (Abcam ab187091), cleaved Gasdermin D (Asp275) antibody (CST 36425S), cleaved Caspase‐3 (Asp175) antibody (CST 9664S), goat anti‐rabbit Alexa Fluor 647 secondary antibody (Invitrogen), DAPI (CST), Phalloidin‐Alexa Fluor 546 (Invitrogen), RPMI‐1640 medium (Gibco), fetal bovine serum (Gibco), Trypsin–EDTA (Gibco), 8‐well independent chamber slides (IBIDI), 75% ethanol (Ante), penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco).

Equipment

CO2 incubator (Thermo Fisher), confocal microscope (Nikon), centrifuge (Eppendorf), inverted fluorescence microscope (Nikon), shaker (Yooning).

Cell Culture

The lung cancer cell line A549 and Akata cells were obtained from the Cell Bank of Guangdong Provincial Key Laboratory of Translational Medicine in Lung Cancer, Guangdong Provincial People's Hospital. A549 cells were cultured in RPMI‐1640 complete medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution. Akata cells were maintained in RPMI‐1640 medium containing 5% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. All cells were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2. Upon reaching 80% confluence during the logarithmic growth phase, A549 cells were passaged. The cells were washed with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), detached using 0.25% trypsin–EDTA at 37°C for 3 min, and neutralized with serum‐containing medium. The cell suspension was transferred to 15 mL centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 800 rpm for 3 min at room temperature. The harvested cells were then seeded into chamber slides or six‐well plates for subsequent experiments.

Cell Model Construction

After A549 cell attachment, well‐activated Akata cells were shaken evenly in the culture flask, a portion of cells was counted, and Akata cells were diluted into 1 × 106 cells/mL with RPMI‐1640 medium containing 5% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution. After shaking evenly, Akata cells were added to A549 cells for co‐culture for 72 h.

Immunofluorescence

After co‐culture, the culture medium was gently aspirated from the chamber slides, and the cells were rinsed slowly with PBS once or twice. Fixation was performed by adding 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10 min, after which the PFA was removed and the cells were washed once or twice with PBS; 500 μL of 0.2% Triton X‐100 was added to each well for permeabilization at room temperature for 10 min. The Triton solution was then discarded, and the cells were washed three times with PBS, each for 5 min. Blocking was carried out using immunofluorescence staining blocking solution at room temperature for 10 min. After removing the blocking solution, 200 μL of primary antibody (diluted according to the manufacturer's instructions) was added to each well, and incubation was performed overnight at 4°C. The primary antibody was then removed, and the cells were washed three times with PBS at room temperature, each for 5 min. Following this, 200 μL of fluorescent secondary antibody (diluted 1:200 in secondary antibody dilution buffer) was added, and the slides were incubated at room temperature on a slow shaker in the dark for 2 h. After discarding the secondary antibody, the cells were washed three times with PBS on the shaker, each for 5 min. Next, 200 μL of phalloidin (prepared with 400× stock solution) was added to each well and incubated at room temperature on a slow shaker in the dark for 1 h. The phalloidin solution was then removed, and the wells were washed three times with PBS, each for 5 min. Finally, 150 μL of DAPI working solution (1 μg/mL) was added to each well and incubated at room temperature on a slow shaker in the dark for 5–8 min. After discarding the DAPI solution, the wells were washed three times with PBS, each for 5 min. An appropriate amount of PBS was added to each well, and the cells were imaged using a confocal microscope for documentation.

Analysis

All experimental data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. The statistical results were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD). When comparing two groups of samples, the independent sample t‐test (Independent sample t‐test) was used. When p < 0.05, the data had statistical significance. When p < 0.05, the symbol “*” was used; when p < 0.01, the symbol “**” was used; when p < 0.001, the symbol “***” was used.

Results

Establishment of Short‐Term Co‐Culture Model Between Akata and A549 and Morphological Changes of Cells

When co‐cultured with EBV‐positive Akata cells, A549 cells exhibited marked granulation and darkened appearance after 48 h, accompanied by the onset of cell shrinkage. The Akata cells displayed varying degrees of aggregation and attached to the A549 cells. At 72 h, A549 cells showed further shrinkage or inflammatory swelling, with slowed proliferation and the appearance of irregular, bubble‐like protrusions on the cell membrane. The Akata cells attached to A549 cells could be slightly agitated but did not readily detach. After an additional 48 h of culture in the absence of Akata cells, a marked reduction in number of A549 cells was observed, along with rupture of the cell membrane and leakage of cellular contents. Residual cellular debris was present between cells, and the remaining A549 cells assumed an elongated, filaments morphology, as shown in Figure 1.

Co‐Culture of Akata and A549 Leads to Necroptosis as the Predominant Mode of Death in Cancer Epithelial Cells

Immunofluorescence staining and confocal microscopy revealed that after 72 h of co‐culture with Akata cells, the expression level of pMLKL protein in A549 cells increased significantly (p < 0.001), with predominant localization on the A549 cell membrane. In Akata cells, pMLKL was distributed both intracellularly and on the cell membrane, as shown in Figure 2A,D. Compared with non‐co‐cultured controls, no significant difference was observed in the expression levels of cleaved Gasdermin D protein and cleaved Caspase 3 protein in A549 cells co‐cultured with Akata (p > 0.05). Positive signals for cleaved Gasdermin D protein and cleaved Caspase 3 protein were also detected in Akata cells, localized to the cell membrane or cytoplasm, as shown in Figure 2B,C,E,F. These findings suggest that necroptosis is the predominant mode of cell death in A549 cells following co‐culture with EBV‐positive Akata cells.

“Cell in cell” Structure Forms Through Phagocytosis in the Co‐Culture Model

During the investigation of cell death mechanisms by immunofluorescence, specialized phagocytic‐like interaction was observed between Akata cells and A549 lung cancer cells, as shown in Figure 3. EBV‐positive Akata cells expressing green fluorescent protein (GFP) appeared green and were frequently observed embedded within A549 cells. In residual epithelial cells remaining after B cells removal, the majority of A549 cells exhibiting this “phagocytic” phenotype contained a single internalized Akata cell. These internalized Akata cells underwent cell death within the vacuolar structures, with the representative images indicating pMLKL‐mediated necroptosis as the mode of death. The infected A549 cells displayed nuclear deformation and dispersed nucleoli. These observations clearly demonstrate the formation of a heterotypic “cell in cell” structure between Akata B lymphocytes and A549 lung cancer epithelial cells.

Yellow arrow indicates the engulfed Akata cells, most of which also underwent necroptosis and presented expression of pMLKL. Scale bar: 5 μm.

Mode of Death of Akata Cells During the Formation of the “cell in cell” Structure

Concomitant with the formation of “cell in cell” structure, we observed a marked increase in the fluorescence intensity of cell death‐related effector proteins within the internalized Akata cells, as shown in Figure 4. Specifically, the expression levels of pMLKL, cleaved Gasdermin D, and cleaved Caspase 3, which mediate necroptosis, pyroptosis, and apoptosis respectively, were all significantly increased. In some “cell in cell” structures, both cancer epithelial cells and Akata cells underwent necroptosis simultaneously, suggesting a severe innate inflammatory response. It can be seen that the fate of Akata cells after being “phagocytosed” by cancer epithelial cells during co‐culture is mostly cell death. Notably, in contrast to observations in A549 cells, the modes of death observed in internalized Akata cells encompassed necroptosis, apoptosis, and pyroptosis, pointing to a pronounced cellular inflammatory response.

Discussion

Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) is an enveloped γ‐type herpesvirus containing a linear double‐stranded DNA genome of approximately 172 K base pairs, encoding more than 80 open reading frames and non‐coding RNAs [17, 18]. EBV can infect different types of cells and has a high affinity for B cells and epithelial cells [19]. After EBV binds to receptors such as CD21 on B cells, it is further promoted to enter B cells under the action of gH/gL. In contrast, the infection process of EBV in epithelial cells is more complex, potentially involving multiple receptors such as EphA2, R9AP, and DSC2, as well as infection modes such as inter‐cellular virus transmission [20, 21, 22, 23].

Pulmonary lymphoepithelial carcinoma (PLEC), formerly known as pulmonary lymphoepithelioma‐like carcinoma, is a rare subtype of non‐small cell lung cancer etiologically associated with EBV infection. The specific mechanism of action of EBV in the occurrence and development of PLEC has not been fully elucidated. A comprehensive understanding of the early events following EBV infection—specifically, the alterations in pulmonary epithelial cell fate during acute infection, the cellular transitions preceding the establishment of viral latency, and the processes governing the development of stably infected latent cell lines—is crucial for advancing research into the carcinogenesis of EBV‐positive PLEC.

In this study, co‐culturing with EBV‐carrying Akata cells led to a specific and significant upregulation of pMLKL protein in A549 cells, suggesting that necroptosis mainly occurred in A549 cells after acute viral infection. There was no significant difference in the expression levels of cleaved Gasdermin D protein and cleaved Caspase 3 protein, indicating that apoptosis and pyroptosis hardly occurred in A549 cells during the 72‐h co‐culture period. In a study on the infection of epithelial cells by the severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS) virus, single‐cell and limited‐dilution analyses demonstrated that necroptosis was the main cell death event in virus‐infected cells, while uninfected bystander cells underwent apoptosis, and pyroptosis occurred later in the infection process [24]. In contrast, this study observed that necroptosis was the main death mode of cancer epithelial cells, while apoptosis of epithelial cells was not observed. It can be seen that the different infection models and conditions of EBV and SARS viruses may lead to different death modes of infected epithelial cells. Different death modes are associated with different intracellular antiviral mechanisms and will also result in different cell fate outcomes and biological effects. Inflammatory death modes may bring about bystander effects and local pro‐inflammatory responses.

In this experimental model, necroptosis was identified as the primary death modality of cancer epithelial cells following acute EBV infection, whereas pyroptosis and apoptosis were rarely observed. This finding suggests that acute EBV infection significantly promotes the inflammatory response and inflammatory death of epithelial cells, reflecting that epithelial cells in the early stage of acute EBV infection may undergo intense antiviral responses and mainly end up in death. In the model, if a portion of non‐dead cells are screened and continued to be cultured, it may contribute to the establishment of a relatively stable EBV latent‐infected lung cancer cell line, providing a stable cell model for subsequent studies on pulmonary lymphoepithelial carcinoma (related studies are currently in progress, and a preliminary model has been established and not reported hereby).

In this study, we found that Akata cells can be phagocytosed by epithelial cells to form a “cell‐in‐cell” structure. The “cell‐in‐cell” structure was discovered as early as the early 20th century and has only recently attracted wide attention due to its potential pathophysiological significance [25]. Akata cells are a B lymphoblastoid cell line capable of producing EBV. In studies of other tumor types, it has been reported that co‐culturing with Akata can effectively infect epithelial cells with EBV through an inter‐cellular infection mechanism [26]. In previous studies, co‐culturing Akata cells with epithelial nasopharyngeal cancer cells CNE‐2 in vitro can also form a “cell‐in‐cell” intracellular structure, and this unique cell structure can be easily observed in nasopharyngeal cancer specimens [25]. This is highly consistent with the results we observed in lung cancer cells, and we will conduct further research on PLEC in clinical specimens. Given the diversity of viral infection mechanisms, it is worth noting that previous studies have demonstrated that EBV infection requires cell‐to‐cell contact. In vitro experiments using indirect co‐culture or infection with cell‐free virus in the supernatant have not been shown to be feasible. Our subsequent work will further examine the feasibility of non‐contact modes of infection [27].

During co‐culture, the internalization of Akata cells by A549 cells to form cell‐in‐cell structures was frequently observed, occurring in approximately 15.1% of epithelial cells. The majority of these internalized B cells subsequently underwent cell death. Within these structures, Akata cells exhibited significantly elevated expression levels of the lethal effector proteins pMLKL, cGSDMD, and cCASP3, indicating the concurrent activation of necroptosis, apoptosis, and pyroptosis during the infection process. It can be seen that there are significant differences in the death modes of B cells and epithelial cells in the same system. The multiple death mechanisms of Akata B cells and their disease‐related effects need further study.

Several critical questions regarding PLEC clinical patients remain to be elucidated. These include the mechanisms by which cancerous epithelial cells persistently harbor EBV within tumor lesions, the potential presence of EBV‐carrying B cells within the primary tumor niche, and how the interaction between B cells and carcinoma cells influences epithelial cell fate and the immune characteristics of the tumor microenvironment. The results of this study provide certain reference value, and it is worth further exploring whether anti‐tumor effects can be promoted by activating the infection capacity of in situ B cells in PLEC tumors.

In summary, this study demonstrates that acute EBV infection, modeled by co‐culturing lung cancer epithelial cells with EBV‐positive Akata cells, specifically triggers pMLKL‐mediated necroptosis in the epithelial cells. These findings provide new insights into the fate determination of epithelial cells following primary EBV infection and the cellular events preceding the establishment of viral latency. While phagocytosis of B cells by cancer epithelial cells to form “cell‐in‐cell” structures is a common phenomenon, the mechanisms driving such syncytium formation and its implications for the immune microenvironment in clinical specimens require further investigation. The co‐culture model established herein not only offers a platform for generating EBV‐latent cell lines through repeated infection and screening, but also provides an experimental foundation for dissecting EBV infection modes and their oncogenic potential in pulmonary epithelium.
