Generating Broad-Spectrum Resistance to ALS-Inhibiting Herbicides in Rice by CRISPR/Cas9-Mediated NHEJ
Authors: Chao Ouyang, Xiongxia Jin, Huimin Zhao, Silan Chen, Guangmiao Zhao, Dan Li, Wei Liu, Xiuying He, Yongzhong Wu, Jing Yang, Baoguang An
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Herbicides are pivotal for modern agriculture, but challenges like weed resistance and crop rotation issues necessitate the development of herbicide-resistant genetic resources. This study focused on acetolactate synthase (ALS), a key enzyme targeted by numerous herbicides. Using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) and combining with whole-stage selection, we induced mutations in the OsALS gene of indica rice and identified novel in-frame mutations at the P171 and S627 sites, respectively. Among them, one mutation at the P171 site, the triple mutation P171T/R172G/M174L (ALS-TM) conferred broad-spectrum resistance to Imidazolinones Pyrimidinylthiobenzoates Sulfonylaminocarbonyltriazolinones and Sulfonylureas herbicides. Compared to wild-type (WT) rice, ALS-TM showed 1153-fold higher resistance to imazethapyr (IMT) than WT based on GR50 values (The herbicide dose causing a 50% reduction in growth), with minimal growth inhibition at 10-fold IMT treatment. Enzymatic assays revealed that ALS-TM maintained catalytic efficiency while reducing herbicide binding, which validated the resistance at the protein level. Field trials showed that ALS-TM mutant retained normal agronomic traits even after IMT spraying, indicating no yield penalty. Additionally, ALS mutations were validated as effective transgenic selection markers, enabling efficient rice transformation under different selection systems. These results demonstrated that ALS-TM could also serve as a reliable tool in basic research, facilitating the selection and identification of transgenic materials in laboratory studies. This study provided a robust method for generating herbicide-resistant rice germplasm and highlighted the potential of CRISPR-mediated NHEJ for creating novel resistant mutations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12284-025-00845-w.

Introduction

As an essential component of modern agriculture, herbicides are used to control weeds or other competing plants, which contributes to enhancing the efficiency and reducing the costs of global agricultural production (Parven et al. 2024). With the rapid rise in labor costs in China, the rice cultivation method is shifting from transplanting to direct-seeding. However, this transition brings about difficulties as weeds and weedy rice in direct-seeding fields are hard to control, which in turn impacts rice yield (Xu et al. 2019). Some herbicides with long half-lives would also affect subsequent crop rotation. These issues not only hinder the widespread adoption of rice direct-seeding technology but also pose significant challenges to the sustainability of rice production. Consequently, the herbicide-resistant genetic and germplasm resources are in urgent need.

By converting pyruvate into acetolactate, acetolactate synthase (ALS) is the key enzyme for the initial biosynthesis of three branched-chain amino acids, leucine, isoleucine, and valine. ALS is a unique enzyme in plants and microorganisms. ALS inhibitors are one of the most widely used herbicides, including five groups: Imidazolinones (IMI), Sulfonylureas (SU), Pyrimidinylthiobenzoates (PTB), Sulfonylaminocarbonyltriazolinones (SCT), and Triazolopyrimidines (TP) (Duggleby et al. 2008). Plants treated with these inhibitors stop growing, eventually wilting and dying. Most indica rice is tolerant to some SU and PTB herbicides, such as bensulfuron-methyl, pyrazosulfuron-ethyl and bispyribac-sodium (BS), while the japonica rice has weaker tolerance. Both subspecies are sensitive to all IMI and some SCT herbicides (Zhang et al. 2021). Since many SU herbicides have been gradually banned due to their long residual period, along with the evolution of resistance in weeds, it is necessary to broaden the resistance spectrum of rice.

Mutations in ALS could confer resistance to ALS-inhibiting herbicides. The first case of the ALS resistance mutation in the world was reported in Kochia scoparia (Saari et al. 1990). The P171T mutation of ALS (The amino acid number is standardized according to that of rice and hereinafter follows in this manner) rendered Kochia scoparia resistant to SU herbicides. As a result of the widespread use of ALS-inhibiting herbicides, many ALS mutations occurred mainly at the amino acid sites including A96, P171, D350, W548 and S627 or at sites close to them (Powles and Yu 2010; Shimizu et al. 2011), because these sites are present in the herbicide-binding region (McCourt et al. 2006). Another double mutation of ALS (G628E/V643M) conferred red rice resistance to imazethapyr (IMT) (Sales et al. 2017). Researchers also intentionally create ALS mutations in crops to achieve higher or broader herbicide resistance. The Clearfield rice, harboring the G628E mutation of ALS induced by EMS mutagenesis, was first commercialized in the USA in 2002 (Sudianto et al. 2013). The double mutation (W548L/S627I) of ALS was reported to endow rice with resistance to BS (Kawai et al. 2007a, b; Osakabe et al. 2005; Taniguchi et al. 2010). And this mutation made wheat, soybean and bamboo resistant to BS as well (Ogawa et al. 2008; Tougou et al. 2009; Ogita et al. 2012). While the single mutation of ALS (S627I) could be used to create the pyriminobac resistant Tall Fescue (Sato et al. 2013). The G95A-mutated ALS conferred highly specific resistance on rice to PTB herbicides (Okuzaki et al. 2007). Jietian rice varieties, carrying the W548M mutation of OsALS, showed resistance to a broad spectrum of ALS-inhibiting herbicides (Chen et al. 2021). After japonica rice cultivar Jinjing 818 being planted in soybean fields sprayed with IMI herbicide, A S627N-mutated ALS was found in it, which resulted in the resistance to imazethapyr (IMT) (Wang et al. 2018). A S627D and/or G152E mutated OsALS were induced by EMS in indica rice and displayed IMT resistance (Shoba et al. 2017).

In addition to the traditional mutagenesis, recently developed genome-editing technologies, especially the CRISPR/Cas technologies, exhibited great potential in accelerating germplasm improvement and crop breeding. TALEN-mediated homology-directed repair (HDR) successfully changed the specific DNA base in the endogenous ALS and produced the resistant W548L/S627I mutation (Li et al. 2016). Through CRISPR/Cas-mediated HDR, many teams also precisely replaced the endogenous ALS with the mutated donor sequences, generated the W548L single mutation or W548L/S627I double mutations and produced BS resistant rice (Endo et al. 2016; Sun et al. 2016; Butt et al. 2017; Ali et al. 2020; Li et al. 2019). Through CRISPR/Cas-mediated non-homologous end joining(NHEJ), a G628W-mutated OsALS was screened, which conferred a high level of tolerance to IMT and imazapic (IMP) (Wang et al. 2021). Base editing and prime editing also contributed to the herbicide-resistant ALS gene modification. A novel triple OsALS mutant (P171F/G628E/G629S) created by base editor displayed tolerance to five herbicides, including nicosulfuron (NSF), IMP, pyroxsulam, flucarbazone (FLZ) and BS (Zhang et al. 2020). P171F and R190H single OsALS mutants with BS tolerance were generated by base editing (Kuang et al. 2020). A S627G-mutated ALS was also obtained by base editing (Yan et al. 2021). The A96V mutation was introduced into OsALS through base editing and generated the imazamox (IMX) resistant rice (Shimatani et al. 2018). The W548L-OsALS mutation was also induced by prime editing (Butt et al. 2020).

In this study, we used CRISPR/Cas9-mediated NHEJ to induce mutations in the ALS gene in indica rice. In combination with the whole-stage selection pressure during tissue culture (selection, regeneration and rooting stage), we successfully obtained mutants resistant to different ALS-inhibiting herbicides, which were supported by several novel OsALS mutations. Our results offered an effective method to create gene mutations, which can provide reference for the development of herbicide-resistant germplasm resources in crops.

Results

CRISPR/Cas9 Targets Design and Vectors Construction

To develop rice mutants with enhanced tolerance to ALS-inhibiting herbicides, we designed two sgRNAs targeting to the P171 and S627 sites of OsALS on the reverse genomic DNA strands, respectively (Fig. 1A). Since the transformation of indica rice was relatively difficult, we firstly added a red fluorescent protein expression cassette (RFP) into the pYLCRISPR/Cas9Pubi-H vector. The red fluorescence facilitated the selection of positive callus. The sgRNAs were directly synthesized, ligated into the modified binary vector and driven by the OsU6a promoter, respectively (Fig. 1B). The constructs were designated as pCRGE-ALS171 and pCRGE-ALS627, respectively. After being verified by sequencing, the plasmids were introduced into rice calli via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation.

Whole Stage Selection Contributed to the Generation of Herbicides Resistant Mutants

In order to construct the ALS-inhibiting herbicides selection system, we performed whole stage selection tests including selection, regeneration, and rooting by adding BS or IMX into the media. During the selection stage, BS and IMX could inhibit the growth of non-transgenic calli at the concentration of 0.18 and 60 mg/L, respectively (Fig. 2A). Lower than that, there would be some non-transgenic calli arising (Fig. S1A, D). During the regeneration stage, the differentiation of non-transgenic calli into the shoots would be inhibited at the concentration of 0.068 mg/L for BS and IMX 0.1 mg/L for IMX, respectively (Fig. 2B and S1B, E). During the rooting stage, BS and IMX could inhibit the growth of non-transgenic shoots at the concentration of 1.36 and 0.1 mg/L, respectively (Fig. 2C and S1C, F). Then the Agrobacterium-mediated transformation was performed to introduce the binary vector pCRGE-ALS171 and pCRGE-ALS627 into rice (WT), separately. After generating the transgenic seedlings, herbicides were sprayed to screen for resistant lines (Fig. 2D–F).

Under the Hygromycin (HN) selection system, 1214 calli were screened for pCRGE-ALS171, yielding 74 transgenic lines, while 897 calli screened for pCRGE-ALS627 produced 65 transgenic lines. Similarly, under the BS selection system, 1004 calli for pCRGE-ALS171 resulted in 16 transgenic lines, compared to 670 calli for pCRGE-ALS627 that generated 29 transgenic lines. For the IMX selection system, 1015 calli screened for pCRGE-ALS171 yielded 20 transgenic lines, and 660 calli for pCRGE-ALS627 obtained 24 transgenic lines.The transformation efficiencies of the pCRGE-ALS171 and pCRGE-ALS627 vectors under the HN selection system were 6.10% and 6.35%, respectively, higher than those under BS (1.59%/4.33%) and IMX (1.97%/3.64%) selection systems (Table 1). This difference might reflect the stronger selection pressure exerted by the BS and IMX systems. Moreover, pCRGE-ALS627 vector exhibited higher herbicide survival rate than pCRGE-ALS171 vector under both BS and IMX selection (Table 1). Although more transgenic seedlings were generated under HN selection, few were resistant. For pCRGE-ALS171 vector, only 2 of 74 seedlings survived, with no ALS mutations detected. For pCRGE-ALS627 vector, 10 of 57 seedlings survived; with 5 heterozygous frameshift mutations, 1 heterozygous in-frame mutation and 1 biallelic mutations of ALS. In contrast, BS and IMX selection systems yielded significantly more resistant plants and mutations. Under BS selection, 9 of 16 pCRGE-ALS171 seedlings survived (5 with ALS mutations), and 14 of 29 pCRGE-ALS627 seedlings survived (9 with ALS mutations). Under IMX selection, 10 of 20 pCRGE-ALS171 seedlings survived (8 with ALS mutations), and 18 of 24 pCRGE-ALS627 seedlings survived (10 with ALS mutations) (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 2). Notably, the S627 site was close to the C-terminus of the ALS protein, where mutations might confer resistance without affecting enzyme activity (McCourt et al. 2006; Pang et al. 2002). Frameshift mutations at this site were therefore included as candidate resistant mutations. However, only in-frame mutations were counted for the P171 site. When excluding frameshift mutations, HN selection yielded far fewer in-frame mutations than the other two systems (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 2).

Novel ALS Mutations Conferred Broad Resistance to ALS-Inhibiting Herbicides

Sequencing analysis of the ALS gene in the resistant seedlings revealed 6 in-frame mutations at the P171 target site (Q169N/V170Q, P171I/R172G, P171T/R172G/M174L, P171M/R172V/R173E/M174L, V170A/P171N/R172T/R173S/M174L, and an LN insertion at P171) and 5 in-frame mutations at the S627 target site (deletion of G628, S627I, G628L, G628W, G628E, S627H/G628H/G629-) (Supplementary Table 2, Fig. 3). All P171-site mutations were novel variants while only G628L and S627H/G628H/G629- at the S627 site were novel variants. The P171T/R172G/M174L (designated as ALS-TM, Triple Mutation) mutation conferred the highest resistance to IMT, followed by P171I/R172G and P171LN insertion. The Q169N/V170Q (designated as ALS-DM, Double Mutation) mutation exhibited high resistance to BS, followed by V170A/P171N/R172T/R173S/M174L. The P171M/R172V/R173E/M174L mutation showed moderate resistance (data not shown). For the S627 site, the G628L and S627H/G628H/G629- mutation conferred moderate resistance to BS (data not shown) and all other mutations have been reported previously (Sudianto et al. 2013; Sato et al. 2013; Wang et al. 2021). We further characterized herbicide resistance of ALS-TM mutant in the T2 generation using the S627I mutant as a control. The ALS-TM mutant exhibited strong resistance to IMT and IMP, with no growth inhibition at 5× (The specific concentration of the herbicides used was provided in Supplementary Table 1) concentration and surviving at 10× concentration (Fig. 4). The ALS-TM mutant was also highly resistant to IMX, though slightly weaker than to IMT and IMP. The ALS-TM mutant also displayed strong resistance to FLZ, with growth largely unaffected at 4× concentration and some seedlings surviving even at 12×concentration. ALS-TM further enhanced BS resistance to 40× compared to WT, despite the latter already exhibiting considerable resistance. ALS-TM showed moderate resistance to NSF, showing slight growth inhibition at 2× concentration but survival at 4× concentration. Compared with the control ALS-S627I mutant, the ALS-TM mutant had significantly higher resistance to IMI, FLZ and NSF herbicides, while their resistance to BS was comparable (Fig. 4).

Characterization of the Novel OsALS Mutants

To investigate the newly identified ALS mutations, we characterized them at both the plant and protein levels. We used the previously generated ALS-W548L/S627I transgenic plants as a control in the following experiments, which displayed high resistance both to BS and IMT. Leaf samples from 2 to 3 leaf stage seedlings treated with 5×IMT were assayed for ALS activity. The ALS activity of WT plants was significantly inhibited after 12–48 h IMT spraying, whereas the ALS activity of ALS-TM mutant maintained or even increased. In ALS-W548L/S627I transgenic plants, ALS activity decreased slightly at the 12-hour treatment but gradually increased thereafter, though it was slightly lower than in ALS-TM mutants (Fig. 5A). Herbicide response assays also revealed obvious differences. WT seedlings were severely inhibited at 18.75 g a.i./ha IMT (0.2×) and died at 187.5 g a.i./ha (2×). Conversely, ALS-TM mutants and ALS-W548L/S627I transgenic plants exhibited high resistance. ALS-TM showed no growth inhibition at 1500 g a.i./ha (20×) and minimal mortality at 1875 g a.i./ha (25×) IMT, while ALS-W548L/S627I grew normally at 75 g a.i./ha (1×) and tolerated 750 g a.i./ha (10×) IMT with moderate inhibition (Fig. 5B and C). Compared to WT, ALS-TM and ALS-W548L/S627I displayed 1153.40-fold and 359.70-fold greater resistance in growth inhibition, and 102.49-fold and 45.26-fold higher resistance in mortality according to GR50 and LD50 values, respectively (Table 2). Notably, ALS-TM exhibited significantly higher resistance to IMT than the W548L/S627I transgenic plants.

To further characterize the ALS mutations, we expressed and purified the recombinant proteins in E. coli (Fig. S2) and the enzymatic activities and responses to different herbicides were analyzed. Enzymatic assays revealed that ALS-TM exhibited a similar Vmax to the WT, with a significantly increased Km but comparable overall catalytic efficiency. In contrast, ALS-DM showed a Vmax less than 10% of WT, which might contribute to the lethality of this mutant. Although ALS-W548L/S627I retained a Vmax close to that of the wild type, its Km was significantly increased by 22.78-fold, which might consequently lead to a marked reduction in its catalytic efficiency (Fig. 5D; Table 3). Herbicide response assays demonstrated that ALS-TM conferred high resistance to all three tested herbicides, whereas ALS-DM showed strong resistance to IMT, moderate resistance to FLZ, and no resistance to NSF. Based on IC50 values, ALS-TM exhibited 8,476.83-fold, 1,286.37-fold, and 585.28-fold higher resistance to IMT, FLZ, and NSF, respectively, compared to WT. Meanwhile, ALS-W548L/S627I also exhibited high resistance to IMT (4,208.94-fold) and high resistance to NSF (54.51-fold), but low resistance to FLZ (2.3-fold) (Fig. 5E–G; Table 3).

The Triple Mutant Suffered No Obvious Yield Penalty in the Field

To further evaluate the potential of the ALS-TM mutant for field applications, we compared its herbicide resistance and agronomic traits with WT under both herbicide-treated and untreated conditions. When 4 to 5-leaf seedlings were sprayed with 5× IMT in field trials, all WT plants died within 4 weeks, whereas ALS-TM mutants maintained normal growth with no visible phytotoxicity (Fig. 6A). Although the plant height of ALS-TM was slightly lower than that of WT (Fig. 6B), the panicle morphology (Fig. 6C), grain shape, or milling quality (Fig. 6D) were similar. Statistical analysis confirmed comparable performance between ALS-TM and WT in tiller number, grain number per panicle, panicle length, seed setting rate and 1000-grain weight, regardless of IMT treatment (Fig. 7). Notably, the consistent reduction in ALS-TM plant height aligned with observations in other gene-edited lines from our laboratory (data not shown). These results indicated that the ALS-TM mutation has minimal impact on key agronomic traits while conferring robust herbicide resistance, highlighting its promise for rice breeding programs.

The OsALS Triple/Double Mutation Genes Could be Used as Transgenic Selection Marker

In order to evaluate whether the ALS mutations could be used as transgenic selection marker, we constructed the binary expression vectors pCALSTM and pCALSDM, using pCALS-548L/S627I as a control, and then infected rice calli (RG237) through Agrobacterium-mediated transformation using different selection system, respectively. Transgenic seedlings could be obtained from all three selection systems for the three binary expression vectors. They all showed high transformation efficiencies when using the hygromycin-Hpt selection system, exceeding 20% (Table 4). Whereas the transformation efficiency of pCALS-W548L/S627I was much higher (24.18%) than that of pCALSTM (9.79%) and pCALSDM (3.66%) when using the BS selection system (Table 4). However, when using the IMX selection system, the transformation efficiency of pCALSTM (15.84%) and pCALSDM (12.76%) were much higher than that of the pCALS-W548L/S627I (7.62%) (Table 4). These vectors and selection methods provided alternative options for the rice genetic transformation. When treated these T0 transgenic seedlings with IMT and BS, we found that most pCALSTM transgenic lines were highly resistant to 5× IMT, while pCALSDM transgenic lines showed weaker resistance to IMT but survived. And pCALSTM, pCALSDM as well as pCALS-W548L/S627I transgenic lines exibited high resistance to BS (Fig. S3). These results further validated our previous findings on the herbicide resistance of ALS-TM and ALS-DM mutations.

Discussion

There have been numerous reports about the herbicide-resistant ALS mutations in plants emerging from weeds under continuous herbicide application or generated by physical, chemical or biological mutagenesis (Tranel and Wright 2017; Dong et al. 2021). In combination with the whole-stage selection during transformation, we utilized CRISPR/Cas9-mediated NHEJ to induce random mutations at the target sites of ALS gene in rice and successfully obtained mutants resistant to different ALS-inhibiting herbicides. Several novel ALS mutations at P171 and S627 sites were identified from these mutants, indicating the method is feasible and efficient.

The P171 site of ALS is a mutation hotspot and also the most reported mutation site. Currently, it has been reported that more than nine types of amino acids and over 90 cases of mutations have occurred in weeds, among which, the most frequently reported mutation was P171S (Tranel and Wright 2017). Most of these single P171 mutations caused plants to develop resistance to SU but not to IMI (Haughn et al. 1988; Yadav et al. 1986; Kawai et al. 2008). These results were consistent with the results from gene editing, where the P171F/L/S/A/Y single mutations showed tolerance to pyroxsulam, FLZ and BS, which belonged to the TP, SCT and PTB, respectively (Zhang et al. 2020; Kuang et al. 2020). Not only the single P171 mutations but also the double mutation, such as P171H/R172S, could not confer strong resistance to IMI (Kawai et al. 2008). The double mutation around the P171 site obtained in our study, Q169N/V170Q, conferred moderate resistance to IMI (Fig. S3). Although the previously reported triple mutation, P171F/G628E/G629S, exhibited tolerance to IMI (Zhang et al. 2020), this might be caused by the mutations at G628/G629 sites since the mutations around the S627 or G628 sites usually contributed to the IMI tolerance (Sudianto et al. 2013; Wang et al. 2018; Shoba et al. 2017; Tranel and Wright 2017). However, our novel triple mutation around the P171 site, OsALS-P171T/R172G/M174L, displayed high resistance not only to PTB, but also to IMI as well as SCT, indicating the resistant potential of the P171 site. Our results also demonstrated that multiplex mutation would exhibit a broader-spectrum resistance compared to single mutations, which could be applied in other sites or other target genes.

In addition to the resistance spectrum, the resistance strength also draws lots of attention. The OsALS-S627D mutant from EMS mutagenesis could survive at 0.57 g a.i./L IMT but the spray amount was not specified (Shoba et al. 2017). The transgenic rice with the OsALS-P171S showed tolerance to 300 µM bensulfuron methyl without any significant growth and yield penalties (Fartyal et al. 2018). The OsALS-A96V mutant could survive on medium supplemented with 0.07 g a.i./L IMX during the transformation (Shimatani et al. 2018). The OsALS-G628W mutant created by gene editing displayed normal growth under 70 g a.i./ha IMT (equivalent to the 0.93× concentration in our study, similarly hereinafter) and 240 g a.i./ha IMP (3.33×). Although the mutant could survive at 1400 g a.i./ha IMT (18.67×) and 2400 g a.i./ha IMP (33.33×), the growth were significantly inhibited (Wang et al. 2021). Most studies reported the W548L/S627I double mutation was related to BS as mentioned above, which endowed rice plants tolerant to 1 kg a.i./ha BS (33.33×) (Kawai et al. 2007a, b). We have tested its tolerance to IMI by transforming the OsALS gene harboring the double mutation into rice and the single copy transgenic plants could tolerate 375 g a.i./ha IMT (5×) (data not shown). The triple OsALS mutant (P171F/G628E/G629S) generated by base editor conferred japonica rice high tolerance to TP and PTB, which showed almost no influence on growth under the treatment of 108 g a.i./ha pyroxsulam and 403.2 g a.i./ha BS (13.44×). It could also tolerate a dose of up to 3024 g a.i./ha FLZ (100.8×), an herbicide belonging to SCT, although the growth was slightly inhibited when the dose above 378 g a.i./ha (12.6×). This mutant demonstrated a moderate tolerance to SU and IMI. It grew normally when treated with 80 g a.i./ha NSF (1.9×) and 108 g a.i./ha IMP (1.5×). When treated with 432 g a.i./ha of IMP (6×), the mutant showed slight growth inhibition. However, dosages higher than 160 g a.i./ha of NSF (3.81×) and 864 g a.i./ha of IMP (12×) would lead to significant growth inhibition (Zhang et al. 2020). Surprisingly, another single mutant, OsALS-W548M, exhibited broad-spectrum resistance to all five groups of tested herbicides. Except for a relatively weak resistance to some herbicides in the SU and PTB groups, it showed high resistance to IMI, TP, and SCT groups. Specifically, it could resist 864 g a.i./ha of IMP (12×), 360 g a.i./ha of IMX (7.66×), 600 g a.i./ha of IMT (8×), 270 g a.i./ha of BS (9×) and 252 g a.i./ha of FLZ (8.4×) (Chen et al. 2021). Our OsALS-P171T/R172G/M174L mutant also displayed broad-spectrum resistance, which differed slightly from that of the previous mutants. Our triple mutant demonstrated stronger resistance to IMT (10×), IMX (10×), IMP (5×) and BS (40×), moderate resistance to FLZ (4×)and relatively weaker resistance to SU (2×). In the field test, the mutant sprayed by 375 g a.i./ha of IMT (5×) exhibited performance comparable to the WT, with no yield penalty (Figs. 6 and 7).

As an important gene and germplasm resource, the ALS-TM mutation holds significant value for herbicide resistance in agricultural production. Its distinct characteristics enable it to contribute to crop tolerance against herbicides, thereby promoting sustainable and efficient farming practices. Furthermore, as a transgenic selection marker, the ALS-TM and ALS-DM have been successfully employed to generate transgenic plants resistant to IMT and BS with a moderate level of transformation efficiency (Fig. S3 and Table 4). These results demonstrated that ALS-TM could also serve as a reliable tool in basic research, facilitating the selection and identification of transgenic materials in laboratory studies.

CRISPR has significantly enhanced the efficiency and convenience of gene targeting. Approaches based on CRISPR encompass HDR and NHEJ, along with base editing and prime editing developed subsequently, all of which represented extremely useful gene-editing tools and techniques (Zhu et al. 2020). The CRISPR-mediated HDR, base editing and prime editing are capable of precisely editing or replacing the target genes and these methods are mainly based on known mutation types. Conversely, the CRISPR-mediated NHEJ method usually generates random mutations and may give rise to new amino acid variations, which would be particularly beneficial for generating novel mutations in the creation of herbicide-resistant gene resources. When combined with the whole-stage selection, these novel mutations can be efficiently screened and identified. In our study, over 21 types of DNA changes and 20 types of amino acid alterations took place. By combining with the whole-stage selection, we effectively obtained at least 11 resistant mutations, which clearly demonstrated the superiority of the CRISPR-mediated NHEJ method in the creation of novel mutations.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials and Growth Conditions

Rice plants (O. sativa L. ssp. indica Heguangsimiao (Wild Type, WT) and O. sativa L. ssp. tropical japonica RG237) were grown in a greenhouse with a 16/8-h light/dark photoperiod at 30/20°C or under field conditions at Haikou, Hainan province, China in 2021–2025.

Construction of CRISPR/Cas9 Vectors

In order to facilitate the transformation, we firstly amplified the RFP expression cassette sequence from the pC1301-35SN-RFP expression vector plasmid, which was preserved in our laboratory. Next, the pYLCRISPR/Cas9Pubi-H vector (Ma et al. 2015) was digested with Bsa I at two specific sites to replace the CcdB element and obtain a linearized vector. Finally, the RFP fragment was ligated into the linearized vector using the Gibson assemble method. For the convenience of subsequent vector construction, we also inserted the restriction enzyme sites of SnaB I, Pml I, Mlu I and Avr II (tacgtacacgtgacgcgtcctagg) between the RFP and Cas9 elements. Two targets against the OsALS genomic sequence (LOC_Os02g30630) corresponding to the P171 or S627 site were designed and the target sequences were (5′-CGATCATGCGGCGGGGGACCTGG-3′) and (5′-TCCTTGAATGCGCCCCCACTTGG-3′), respectively. The underlined bases represented the PAM sequence. The corresponding OsU6a promoter and sgRNAs were directly synthesized and ligated into the modified vector digested by the Pml I and Mlu I using the Gibson assemble method. After being verified by sequencing, the plasmids were transferred into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105 by electroporation for rice transformation.

Critical Concentration Tests and Agrobacterium-Mediated Transformation

The critical concentrations in the media at different stages of the tissue culture of WT were determined before the transformation. Critical concentration tests were conducted by adding varying concentrations of BS (0, 0.135, 0.18, and 0.27 mg/L) or IMX (0, 30, 50, 60 mg/L) to the induction, subculture, and selection media, respectively. These tests were carried out in the dark for a duration of 4 to 5 weeks to observe the emergence of new calli. For the critical concentration test of regeneration, positive selection calli were placed on a regeneration medium supplemented with BS (0, 0.023, 0.045, and 0.113 mg/L) or IMX (0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3,0.4 and 0.6 mg/L), respectively, in the light for 4–5 weeks. For the critical concentration test of rooting, the regenerated seedlings were placed on a rooting medium supplemented with BS (0, 0.068, 0.135, 0.18, 0.27, 0.45, and 1.36 mg/L) or IMX (0, 0.007, 0.01, 0.03, 0.05, 0.07 and 0.1 mg/L), respectively, in the light for 2–3 weeks. The critical concentration tests were performed at least twice.

After the determinations above, the Agrobacterium-mediated transformation was performed as previously described (Hiei et al. 1994; Toki et al. 2006) with modifications. Briefly, the sterilized seeds were placed on the MS induction medium for about two weeks, then the generated embryogenic calli were separated from the seeds. After being infected, the calli were placed on a selection medium supplemented with 0.18 mg/L BS, 60 mg/L IMX or 50 mg/L hygromycin (HN) for 4–5 weeks respectively. The positive calli were regenerated under 0.045 mg/L BS or 0.1 mg/L IMX selection pressure for 4–5 weeks, respectively. And the regenerated seedlings were placed on a rooting medium supplemented with 1.36 mg/L BS, 0.1 mg/L IMX or 20 mg/L HN, respectively. The transformation experiments were performed at least three times. A statistical analysis was conducted using a t-test.

Assay of Whole-Plant Tolerance to ALS-Inhibiting Herbicides

The transgenic T0 and wild type (WT) seedlings were planted in the greenhouse or field, which were foliar sprayed at two to three-leaf stage with BS (Japan Combine Chemical Industries Co., Ltd.), IMT (Shandong Xianda Agricultural Chemical Co., Ltd.), or IMP (BASF (China) Co., Ltd.) at the dose of 1500(50×), 750(10×) and 720(10×) g a.i./ha, respectively, using a agroatomizer to obtain the resistant lines. The transgenic T1-T2 of the triple mutants, ALS-S627I mutants and WT plants were used to determine the resistant level of different ALS-inhibiting herbicides. The spray concentrations of BS, IMP, IMT, IMX (Weifang Xinda Chemical Co., Ltd.), FLZ (Jinbo Pesticide Chemical Co., Ltd.) and NSF (Hebei Zhongbao Green Agriculture Technology Co., Ltd.) were (30×, 40×, 50× and 60×), (2×, 5×, 10× and 20×), (2×, 5×, 10× and 20×), (2×, 5×, 10× and 20×), (2×, 4×, 8× and 12×) and (2×, 4×, 8× and 12×), respectively. For the GR50 (herbicide dose causing 50% inhibition of plant growth) and LD50 (herbicide dose causing 50% plant mortality) assay, the T3 generation of the triple mutants and ALS-W548L/S627I transgenic plants were sprayed with IMT at the dose of (1×, 2.5×, 10×, 20×, 25×, 50×, 75× and 100×) while the WT plants were treated with lower doses (0.1×, 0.25×, 0.75×, 1×, 2.5× and 10×). The experiments were repeated at least twice, with 40 seedlings sampled each time, The GR50 and LD50 values were estimated via Dose-response inhibition analysis using the three-parameter non-linear regression in GraphPad Prism software (Yu et al. 2015; Cao et al. 2021).

The phenotypes were observed at 14 to 28 days after spraying. All the application volume was at the rate of 100 mL/m2. The 1× concentration recommended by the manufacturers and the corresponding spray concentrations of herbicides in these experiments were listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Identification of Mutation Sites in ALS Gene and Bivalent Vector Construction

The transgenic T0 plants were transplanted into the soil in the greenhouse after acclimatization and sprayed by different herbicides. Two to four weeks after the spraying, genomic DNA was extracted from young leaves of resistant plantlets using the CTAB method. The corresponding ALS gene fragments in each resistant plantlet were amplified by PCR using the primers ALSeditH F3(5′-AGATCCCACCGCAATATGCC-3′) and ALSeditH R3 (5′-ACAAACCTAGACAGCAGGAAGC-3′), ALSeditH F5(5′-TAAGAACCACCAGCGACACCAC-3′) and ALSeditH R5 (5′-CCGAGGACGCGAGGAAGAA-3′) designed around the S627 and P171 target sites, respectively and confirmed by DNA sequencing after being verified by agarose gel electrophoresis, respectively.

The OsALSP, OsubiT, ALS-TM (GenBank Accession Number, PV766925), and ALS-DM (GenBank Accession Number, PV766926) sequences were amplified from the genomes of the relative mutants, respectively. The ALS-W548L/S627I mutation sequence was previously synthesized in our laboratory. The OsALSP-ALS-TM-OsubiT, the OsALSP-ALS-DM-OsubiT and the OsALSP-ALS-W548L/S627I-OsubiT expression cassette were obtained through overlap PCR, respectively. Using the Gibson assembly method, the expression cassettes were then ligated into the plant binary vector pCAMBIA1300 digested by Sac I and Hind III. After sequencing verification, the correctly verified plasmids were named pCALSTM, pCALSDM and pCALS-W548L/S627I, respectively.

Prokaryotic Expression and Purification of Recombinant ALSs

In order to determine the enzyme characterization, the prokaryotic expression was performed. Each CDS was amplified by PCR using genomic DNAs extracted from the young leaves of ALS-TM, ALS-DM, ALS-W548L/S627I mutants and WT as the templates, respectively, with the gene-specific primers ALSF (5′-ttccaggggcccctgggatccATGGCTACGACCGCCGCG-3′) and ALSR (5′-gatgcggccgctcgagtcgacTTAATACACAGTCCTGCCATCACC-3′), which contained an additional BamH I site and Sal I site, respectively. The PCR fragments were ligated into plasmid pGEX-6P-1 (GE, United States), and the generated constructs, designated pGEX-ALS-TM, pGEX-ALS-DM, pGEX-ALS-W548L/S627I and pGEX-ALS-WT, respectively, were confirmed by sequencing. The constructs were used to generate fusion proteins with an N-terminal GST tag, to facilitate purification of the recombinant proteins.

One hundred milliliters cultures of Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) PLysS cells transformed with the constructs were grown in LB medium at 37℃ and 220 rpm in the presence of 100 mg mL−1 Ampicillin and 34 mg mL−1 Chloramphenicol until the cultures reached an OD600 of 0.5 to 0.8. The expression of recombinant ALSs was induced by addition of 0.1 mM IPTG, and the cultures were grown at 18℃ overnight. The cells were then harvested by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 1 min, washed once with 1 × PBS, and resuspended in the same buffer to obtain a 10–15% (v/v) cell suspension with 0.1% (v/v) Triton X-100. After lysis of the cells by sonication over ice, the extracts were centrifuged at 12,000 ×g and 4 °C for 20 min to remove debris. The supernatants were used for protein purification with GST-labeled protein purification kit (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Next, the highly purified recombinant ALS proteins were confirmed by SDS-PAGE. The purified proteins were used immediately for enzymatic activity measurements or stored in 50% glycerol at − 20 °C.

In Vitro ALS Activity Assay

The activities of ALSs were determined spectrophotometrically with a Multiskan GO microplate spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, Finland) according to methodology proposed by Yu et al. (2010), with modifications. For the enzyme activity test, the reaction mixture contained 50 mM K3PO4 buffer (pH 7.5), the substrates, sodium pyruvate, with a series of concentrations (See Supplementary Table 3), 1 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM TPP, 0.5 mM FAD, and the purified proteins from prokaryotic expression or crude protein extracts from rice (0.5 to 10 µg), in a final volume of 0.5 mL. The reaction mixtures were incubated for 60 min at 37 °C for the formation of acetolactate. The reaction was stopped by the addition of 50 µL of 3 M H2SO4 and incubated at 60 °C for 15 min to convert acetolactate to acetoin. Then 250 µL of creatine solution (0.5%, w/v) and 250µL of α-naphthol solution (5%, w/v) prepared in 2.5 N NaOH, were added and the mixture was incubated at 60 °C for another 15 min. After being centrifuged and cooled to room temperature, the enzyme activity was measured at OD530 nm by the standard curve of acetoin. The amount of acetoin was equal to that of acetolactate, which was the directly catalytic product of ALS. The protein concentration of the crude extract was measured by the BCA method. For the herbicide inhibition test, the concentration of sodium pyruvate was set at 100.0 mM. The technical grade NSF, FLZ and IMT (dissolved in DMSO) (Stanford Analycal Chemicals Inc., USA), were used at a series of final concentrations (See Supplementary Table 3), respectively.

To determine the activities of ALSs in planta, the 2-week-old seedlings of the WT, pCALS-W548L/S627I and ALS-TM mutant were sprayed with 5X IMT and the leaves were collected at 0, 12, 24 and 48 h after spraying. One gram of leaf tissues was grounded using liquid nitrogen. And 3 mL extraction buffer containing 100mM K3PO4 buffer solution (pH 7.5), 0.5 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM TPP, 10 µM FAD, 12 mM DTT and 10% (v/v) glycerol was added to the fine powder. Samples were agitated for 10 min at 4℃ and centrifuged (12,000 g for 15 min). Then the saturated ammonium sulfate was added to the supernatant in a ratio of 1:1. The mix solution was kept for 20 min at 4℃ with low-speed stirring. The protein was then precipitated at 12,000 xg for 5 min at 4℃ and redissolved in 300 µl of reaction buffer containing 50mM K3PO4 buffer (pH 7.5), 5 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM TPP, 0.5 mM FAD. And 20 µL 1.5 M sodium pyruvate was added into 280 µL of the enzyme reaction mixture and incubated at 37℃ for 1 h. The following protocols were the same as enzyme activity test above.

The I50 values (herbicide dose causing 50% inhibition of the ALS enzyme activity) were estimated via three-parameter non-linear regression analysis. Km (Michaelis constant) and Vmax (maximum velocity) values were calculated using non-linear regression analysis by fitting the data to the Michaelis–Menten equation. All of the parameters were calculated using at least two independent enzyme batches each consisting of at least triplicate determinations. Statistical analysis was conducted using t-test.

Agronomic Performances of Edited T-DNA-Free Lines

At maturity, important agronomic traits, including plant height, tiller number, panicle length, grain number per panicle, seed setting rate and 1000-grain weight were measured using at least 6 plants randomly selected from the edited line and WT with or without the IMT spraying. The 5×IMT was sprayed at 4 to 5-leaf stage. The plant height and the tiller number were measured at the ripening stage. The rest of the characteristics were measured before harvest. The data were collected from a single cropping season in Haikou. Statistical analysis was conducted using t-test between the edited lines and the wild type.
