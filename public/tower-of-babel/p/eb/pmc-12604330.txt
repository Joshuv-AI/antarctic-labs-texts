Esophageal obstruction by mediastinal seroma after laparoscopic repair of a giant hiatal hernia: a case report
Authors: Štěpán-Ota Schütz, Jaroslav Pažin, Tereza Husárová, Radek Pohnán
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHiatal hernia is increasingly prevalent in the Western population. Its incidence increases with age and morbid obesity and is higher in females. Symptomatic hiatal hernias, particularly grade IV, are associated with significant morbidity and severe symptoms, and often necessitate acute surgical intervention.Case presentationWe present a case of a 64-year-old Caucasian female patient with acute symptoms of a giant paraesophageal hernia containing the entire stomach, the proximal duodenum with D2 compression, and the pancreatic head. Background

A hiatal hernia (HH) is defined as the abnormal protrusion of the upper part of the stomach or other organs through the diaphragm’s hiatus [1]. A giant HH is characterized by the presence of at least 30% of the stomach in the chest cavity [2]. Factors such as age, female gender, and morbid obesity contribute to the increased incidence of giant HH in the Western population. Up to 95% of cases are sliding hernias, which are often asymptomatic or present symptoms similar to gastroesophageal reflux disease. The remaining 5% are paraesophageal hernias (PEH), classified clinically as either asymptomatic or symptomatic [1]. Symptomatic PEH are associated with higher complication rates of up to 27% and often require acute surgical intervention [3]. Grade IV giant HH, characterized by the presence of abdominal organs within the hernia sac are rare, and account for 0.3–15% of all HH [2].

Case presentation

We present a case of a 64-year-old Caucasian female patient with a body mass index of 39 kg/m2, who presented to the emergency department with acute upper abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting. The patient had a past medical history of arterial hypertension, hyperlipidemia, type 2 diabetes mellitus treated with oral antidiabetic agents, and obesity. There was no previous abdominal or thoracic surgery. Her family history was unremarkable, with no known gastrointestinal or oncological diseases. The patient was a nonsmoker, did not consume alcohol, and lived independently. She was fully employed prior to the acute presentation and reported no psychosocial stressors.

Computed tomography (CT) imaging showed a giant PEH containing the entire “upside-down” stomach, the proximal part of the duodenum with D2 stenosis, and the head of the pancreas (Fig. 1). She was admitted to the surgical ward, where a nasogastric tube was placed and bowel rest with intravenous crystalloid therapy was initiated. On the basis of these findings, the patient underwent a laparoscopic reduction of the hernia contents using a harmonic scalpel, followed by Nissen–Rossetti fundoplication with four Ethibond 2/0© sutures via the five-port technique. Hernia sac was left in the remaining cavity. The diaphragm defect was repaired using a semi-absorbable mesh, secured by an absorbable strap fixation device and V-lock© suture. A gastropexy to the diaphragm was performed. A Jackson–Pratt drain was placed in the remaining mediastinal cavity and a Penrose drain was positioned beneath the diaphragm. After the surgery, the patient was observed in the intensive care unit for 24 hours. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 6, tolerating a liquid diet and free of drains.

One month postoperatively, the patient returned to the emergency department with symptoms of esophageal obstruction, including odynophagia and upper abdominal pain. CT revealed food impaction in the distal esophagus, owing to compression of the esophagus by a newly formed seroma in the remaining cavity (Fig. 2). Glucagon was administered to relax smooth muscles, and impacted solids were cleared mechanically via gastroscopy. CT-guided percutaneous drainage was considered, but was not technically feasible. A conservative approach using a strict soft diet, analgesics, and 100 mg of hydrocortisone daily was initiated. The patient was discharged on day 4, tolerating a soft diet. During 12 months of follow-up, the patient tolerated solid foods without restrictions and exhibited no signs of dysphagia or odynophagia.

Discussion

Surgical repair of a giant HH and a grade IV hernia is a complex procedure requiring experienced laparoscopic surgeons. This procedure is associated with high morbidity rates of 14–18% in elective cases, which rises to 41% in emergency settings [4]. Seroma formation following HH reduction is a rare complication documented by few authors [3]. When combined with distal esophageal stenosis and solid food impaction, it requires an individualized clinical approach. The incidence and management of seroma formation after giant HH repair, particularly in combination with distal esophageal obstruction, remains poorly defined.

Some authors suggest excising the hernia sac to prevent seroma formation; however, this approach should only be taken by highly experienced surgeons [1, 2]. Conversely, the World Society of Emergency Surgery advises against sac excision in acute settings [5]. The robotic approach has recently gained popularity, with studies describing a lower complication rate (6.3%) compared with the laparoscopic approach (19.2%) [6]. Although reduction and excision of the hernia sac in giant and complex HH may prevent seroma formation, this approach carries the risk of major postoperative complications, such as pneumothorax. A robotic approach may facilitate safer hernia sac excision, potentially reducing postoperative complication rates. Currently, no guidelines exist for managing these rare complications, and existing guidelines for complex HH management are based on low-level evidence. Given the rarity of obstructing seroma formation after HH repair, large scale studies might be missing in the future; therefore, we believe that each case report detailing such complications provides valuable insights. Further prospective studies are necessary to better understand and define incidence rates, preventive measures, and treatment for this challenging complication.

Conclusion

Giant HH are complex to manage, with high risks of complications. While robotic-assisted repair offers potential benefits, further research is necessary to establish effective guidelines. Individualized treatment remains essential.
