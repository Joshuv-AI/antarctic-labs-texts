Fellow-led case-based curriculum to improve pediatric resident education in the neonatal intensive care unit
Authors: Eric A Raynal, Jennifer M Brady, Shelley R Ehrlich, Chunyan Liu, Beth Ann Johnson, Morgan E Hill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe objective of this study was to implement a novel, fellow-as-teacher curriculum for pediatric residents, built upon brief, case-based teaching in a level III neonatal intensive care unit (NICU).MethodsWe added a novel, case-based educational curriculum focused on key fetal and neonatal medicine topics, built around pediatrics board review questions. Fellows facilitated case discussions using these questions. Participating residents completed summative knowledge (NICU Rotation Test) and experience (Education Evaluation Survey) assessments pre- and post-implementation of this curriculum.ResultsEighty-three residents completed the NICU Rotation Test, 47 in the pre-curriculum group and 36 in the post-curriculum group. On the NICU Rotation Test, the median score did not differ between the pre- and post-curriculum groups. The post-curriculum Education Evaluation Surveys demonstrated significantly more positive responses than the pre-curriculum surveys.ConclusionsResidents reported improvement in the overall teaching during their rotation without interfering with patient care or being time intensive.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09106-w.Keywords: Neonatology, Case-based, Fellow-as-teacher, Resident

Background

To become a board-certified pediatrician, residents are required to gain critical knowledge in fetal and neonatal medicine. This core neonatal content is specified by the American Board of Pediatrics (ABP) [1]. However, with new Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) recommendations, residents are required to spend less time in the NICU. Specifically, NICU requirements decreased from a minimum of 8 weeks to a minimum of 4 weeks starting in the 2025–2026 academic year [1, 2]. Even with prior, more robust requirements, residents provided bag mask ventilation only 7.5 times on average, prompting concern that procedural competency may become unattainable because of these ACGME recommendations [3]. In fact, a qualitative study revealed limited clinical exposure to be a primary barrier to achieving competency [4]. This contraction in exposure creates a pressing challenge: how to efficiently and effectively prepare pediatrics residents with critical neonatal competencies within constrained rotations. Locally, we have seen deficits in pediatric in-training exam (ITE) scores across multiple domains related to fetal and neonatal medicine. Performance on the third-year ITE correlates best with performance on the American Board of Pediatrics certifying examination, supporting its use as one surrogate marker of board readiness [5]. Therefore, it is critical to maximize residents’ educational experiences during their reduced NICU exposure.

Utilizing fellows as teachers and promoting active learning are two strategies to optimize learning. In this study, we aimed to increase pediatric resident knowledge of key fetal and neonatal medicine topics through the development of a novel, case-based curriculum focused on content specified by the ABP and refined by expert opinion. We designed this curriculum using Kern’s six-step approach to curriculum development while grounding the instructional strategy in Kolb’s experiential learning theory (ELT) [6]. ELT posits that learning occurs through cycles of concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation. Case-based discussions in the NICU naturally provide the critical first step of ELT with concrete experiences, while guided fellow-led questions in the discussion period foster reflective observation. Residents then participate in concrete clinical experiences rounding in the NICU, providing them opportunities to apply their conceptual understanding to direct patient care.

Flashcards as an educational tool for case-based learning in the NICU have previously been shown to improve satisfaction, attendance, and overall educational experience [7]. Additional studies have demonstrated the benefits of resident-as-teacher curricula [8–14]. In work done by Backes et al., fellows can be seen as “very important” to resident education in the NICU [15]. In this study, fellows taught residents two to four times per month with each learning session lasting 45 to 60 min. This time commitment has been seen as a barrier to teaching as a fellow [16]. Additional barriers include frequent high acuity and resident workload in the NICU [17]. Various shorter teaching methods have been presented for use in medical education [18]. These approaches are supported by adult learning theory, emphasizing that adult learners focus best during shorter learning sessions [19]. One effective educational strategy may be a short window of protected teaching time immediately prior to rounds [20]. Our study, grounded in ELT, sought to demonstrate that adding short, fellow-led case-based sessions immediately prior to rounds paired with a learning object (i.e., a laminated flashcard) were superior to traditional educational methods alone in teaching critical fetal and neonatal medicine topics required for pediatric residents by the ABP and more effective in increasing pediatric resident satisfaction with education.

Methods

Study setting

We conducted this study in a 60-bed level III NICU staffed by neonatologists, NICU fellows, pediatric residents, and advanced practice providers. The residents were from a large academic pediatric and combined pediatric residency program, and their NICU rotation blocks were four weeks long. Prior to implementation of this curriculum, resident didactic education was provided by attending neonatologists, respiratory therapists, dieticians, pharmacists, occupational and speech therapists, advanced practice providers, and pediatric residents themselves in 30-minute sessions using standard PowerPoint lectures. These lectures served to educate but also orient residents during their time in the NICU and so needed to remain a part of their overall curriculum for the rotation. No scheduled formal teaching was conducted by fellows prior to this project. Fellows had varying training in teaching skills, which were not assessed as part of this study.

Curriculum development and implementation

Our curriculum development followed Kern’s six-step approach, including problem identification, targeted needs assessment, development of goals and objectives, identifying educational strategies, implementation, and evaluation. Problem identification was informed by national changes in NICU rotation requirements and local deficits in pediatric ITE performance. The curriculum’s goals and objectives were aligned with ABP-specified neonatal content and refined through expert neonatologist input. Educational strategies were selected to align with Kolb’s ELT, emphasizing brief, case-based discussions and immediate clinical application. Implementation and evaluation strategies are described later.

Our initial needs assessment surveyed 31 residents who rotated through the level III NICU prior to the study period and received didactic education through daily 30-minute PowerPoint lectures. In this assessment, residents reported a preference for simulation, bedside teaching, teaching during rounds, and case-based discussion, while only 6% (2/31) identified the current educational format, PowerPoint, as their preferred method of learning. Most residents (24/31, 77%) preferred teaching with a duration of 15 min or less. This discrepancy reflects an important educational gap: although we recognize the importance of learning efficiently, we have limited evidence-based, theory-driven approaches that recognize adult learning principles and accommodate the time pressures of the NICU environment and changing residency rotation requirements.

We then developed a novel, case-based educational curriculum based on 16 key topics in fetal and neonatal medicine as identified by expert opinion of neonatologists and outlined by the ABP. Questions were created or adapted from a well-recognized neonatology review textbook for the level of a pediatrics resident and used with permission of the editors [21]. We selected all questions prior to introduction of this curriculum and stored them in a single, interactive PowerPoint file [available for review upon request] on the fellow workstation in the NICU. In addition to the existing lecture-based educational curriculum, we built five-to-ten minutes for the fellow-led learning lesson into the resident rotation calendar immediately prior to the morning NICU huddle, four days weekly (Table 1). The neonatology fellow on service was responsible for leading these sessions. The fellows were given an overview of the curriculum during a fellow didactic session prior to curriculum implementation, but no additional pre-work was required of the fellows. Learning sessions were paired with double-sided laminated cards that covered high yield teaching points specific to the topic of the day (sample, Fig. 1; remainder available for review upon request). These laminated teaching cards served as a scaffold for abstract conceptualization, and immediate application of key concepts during rounds and patient care provided opportunities for active experimentation. We provided these cards to residents to be added to a single large O-ring to be easily carried and referenced throughout their rotation. Cards were prepared by NICU administrative staff.

Curriculum assessment

To assess the effectiveness of the educational curriculum, each resident voluntarily completed a summative knowledge assessment [available for review upon request], again, created or adapted from a well-recognized neonatology review textbook and used with permission of the editors [21]. To assess resident satisfaction with the novel curriculum, each resident voluntarily completed a summative experience assessment (Additional file 1). We included questions that assessed the relationship between the residents and neonatal fellows. These questions were created based on expert neonatologist input, pilot-tested with pediatric chief residents, and then revised based on feedback received. The assessments were administered on the last day of each resident rotation via REDCap [22, 23] and no identifying information was collected.

Data collection and analysis

Baseline data was collected for nine months prior to implementation of this curriculum, from May 2022-January 2023. The novel curriculum was launched in February 2023 and post-implementation data collection continued for eight months, through September 2023. Pre- and post-curriculum data were categorized into two distinct and independent groups.

Descriptive statistics summarized outcomes and demographic data between pre- and post-curriculum implementation groups. Frequencies and percentages were reported for categorical variables, and means, standard deviations, medians, interquartile ranges, minimums, and maximums for continuous variables. Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact tests (when the percentage of expected cell counts less than five was more than 20%) were used for associations between the curriculum and each categorical variable. Wilcoxon rank sum test was used for differences in medians of the continuous variables between pre- and post-curriculum implementation groups given the two groups were independent. All data analyses were conducted using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute).

Results

Over the study period, 8 unique NICU fellows provided teaching to 48 pediatric residents utilizing the case-based fellow-led curriculum.

In total, 83 residents completed the summative knowledge assessment (NICU Rotation Test). Of that total, 47 residents were in the pre-implementation group and 36 were in the post-implementation group (see Table 2 below). Of those 83, 79 residents (95%) completed the summative experience assessment (Additional file 1, Education Evaluation Survey). The final NICU Rotation Test total score did not differ between the pre- and post-implementation groups (median scores 10/16 and 9/16 in pre- and post-curriculum groups respectively, p = 0.28; Table 3). However, the post-implementation Education Evaluation Surveys demonstrated significant improvement in multiple areas (see Table 3 below). For example, overall teaching was reported as excellent in 50% in the post-implementation group versus 11% in the pre-implementation group (p < 0.0001). Similarly, we saw an improvement in those that strongly agreed that the morning didactics facilitated their learning from 7% to 27% (p = 0.04). Additionally, we saw an improvement in those that strongly agreed that the curriculum did not interfere with patient care, from 31% to 56% (p = 0.04). Nearly approaching statistical significance, respondents reported an improvement in the amount of time dedicated to teaching in the rotation (p = 0.0515), from too little to just right.

Discussion

Learning efficiency during residents’ rotations in the NICU is becoming more critical as overall exposure is decreasing. We created a novel curriculum to teach residents during their NICU rotation using fellow-led 5-to10-minute case-based learning sessions that were added alongside their traditional lectures, which continued throughout the study period. Having built-in shorter but high yield learning lessons will be crucial as residents’ time in the NICU decreases under new ACGME curriculum guidelines [2]. Additionally, these short lessons may be the critical moments that learners have for exposure to the field of neonatology, potentially influencing their career choices. Residents’ perceptions of teaching quality on their NICU rotation may impact these choices, and this curriculum improved those perceptions.

Although we did not demonstrate increased knowledge acquisition with the addition of this curriculum in our study sample, we did show that this curriculum did not negatively impact knowledge gained throughout the residents’ rotations, by comparing summative knowledge assessments before and after curriculum implementation. This finding may reflect limitations of the assessment tool, which was designed from fellowship-level questions. Additionally, the intervention was additive in an already dense learning environment and was designed with efficiency and experiential learning in mind. The residents’ perception was that with the addition of the novel case-based fellow-run curriculum, their NICU experience better facilitated their learning and covered topics more relevant to their education. From an ELT perspective, this curriculum supported rapid cycling through concrete experience, reflection, conceptualization, and application, which may explain why residents perceived improved educational quality despite no measurable improvement in summative knowledge scores. We intentionally developed this curriculum using ABP-specified NICU content, and therefore their perception aligns well with the curriculum content aims. The residents’ reported increase in overall teaching quality after implementation of this curriculum also demonstrates its value.

This curriculum could easily be implemented in a wide variety of NICUs. The simplicity of the structure requires little time for training or pre-work and can be confidently implemented by even a first-year NICU fellow. Although we implemented this curriculum as a fellow-led session, units with other staffing models could easily implement this with a different educational lead. It is our goal to make these cards widely accessible to interested residency training programs.

Limitations of this project include the learning object’s reproducibility. The laminated cards were prepared for groups of residents by a committed administrator. These cards could be prepared by an administrator, a rotation coordinator, or NICU volunteers. Additionally, the fellows facilitating the daily learning sessions may have variable comfort and skill with teaching. Providing additional instruction to fellows ahead of time could standardize teaching methods, although this would add time and complexity to the curriculum. Additionally, this may replicate existing fellows as teachers training efforts through Graduate Medical Education offices. We considered using ITE scores to track the impact of our curriculum, however, we could not identify which scores matched with the residents who rotated at the specific NICU where we implemented this curricular change (i.e., residents at this training program may rotate through different NICUs, each of which has a unique educational curriculum). We may not have seen improvement in the NICU Rotation Test scores because the questions were developed from questions targeting fellowship-level learners, perhaps limiting the amount of resident level improvement in knowledge able to be assessed. Finally, our evaluation strategies focused on learner-reported outcomes and may not accurately reflect objective measures of fellows’ teaching skills or their impact on resident performance in pediatric board examinations. Future strategies to overcome these limitations might be to integrate facilitator evaluations in the curriculum and to include higher-level learning outcomes data.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we demonstrated that the addition of a novel curriculum with short, case-based questions can be readily implemented in the NICU without compromising resident learning. Residents reported improved teaching quality and decreased impact on patient care by adding a fellow-led case-based curriculum to their standard lecture series. Future work may seek to collect feedback from fellows’ and residents’ experiences to make implementation decisions at other institutions and further improve upon this curriculum. As requirements for resident rotations in the NICU change nationally, this flexible curriculum could be implemented to maximize learning efficiency while promoting the role of fellows as teachers.
