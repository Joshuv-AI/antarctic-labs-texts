From genome to field, biocontrol efficacy of Bacillus subtilis strain M10 against Rhizoctonia solani in common bean
Authors: Tofick Barasa Wekesa, Grace Kennedy, Damaris Barminga, Alena Petra Wekesa, Oketch Fredrick Onyango, Martin Ng’ang’a Muigano, Ndinda Kavesu, Samuel Mwakisha Mwamburi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacillus species are widely exploited in sustainable agriculture due to their capacity to suppress soil-borne pathogens and promote plant growth. In this study, we investigated Bacillus subtilis strain M10, isolated from hypersaline soil of Lake Magadi, Kenya, which previously demonstrated antagonistic activity against Rhizoctonia solani. We combined whole-genome sequencing with field-based evaluation to elucidate the genetic basis of its biocontrol potential and assess its agronomic performance. The assembled genome comprises of 4,024,855 bp with a G + C content of 43.74% and a BUSCO completeness score of 99.5%. In silico genome analyses predicted 13 biosynthetic gene clusters associated with non-ribosomal peptides, terpenes, and ribosomally synthesized and post-translationally modified peptides, alongside 10 genomic islands, two prophage regions, and 108 strain-specific genes. Functional annotation further suggested the presence of genes potentially involved in rhizosphere colonization, plant-microbe interactions, environmental stress tolerance, and antimicrobial resistance, although these roles remain to be experimentally validated. Field trials conducted under dry and rainy seasons demonstrated that strain M10 significantly reduced root rot severity 20–28% lowered wilting incidence 20–37% and increased grain yield by more than 56–75% compared with untreated controls. Collectively, these results establish B. subtilis strain M10 as a safe and effective biocontrol agent with strong potential for sustainable management of R. solani in common bean production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54615-9.Keywords: Bacillus subtilis, Whole-genome sequencing, Biological control, Common bean, Rhizoctonia solani, Sustainable agricultureSubject terms: Biotechnology, Microbiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Leguminous crops (Fabaceae) play a critical role in global food security and sustainable agricultural systems by providing affordable sources of plant protein, dietary fibre, and essential micronutrients while improving soil fertility through biological nitrogen fixation1,2. Among these crops, common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris L.) is a major staple in sub-Saharan Africa, contributing substantially to household nutrition, income generation, and rural livelihoods3. Despite its socioeconomic importance, common bean productivity remains constrained by persistent biotic stresses, particularly soil-borne pathogens that cause severe yield losses and threaten the stability of production systems4.

One of the most destructive soil-borne pathogens affecting common bean is Rhizoctonia solani, a necrotrophic fungus responsible for damping-off, root rot, stem canker, and wilting. Infection by R. solani compromises root development, disrupts water and nutrient uptake, and ultimately reduces plant vigor and grain yield5–7. Significant losses attributed to R. solani have been reported across diverse agroecological zones, underscoring the need for effective and sustainable management strategies.

Conventional control of R. solani relies heavily on chemical fungicides. While effective in the short term, their extensive use has raised concerns regarding environmental contamination, non-target toxicity, human health risks, and the emergence of resistant pathogen populations8–10. These challenges have accelerated the search for alternative disease management approaches that are environmentally benign, economically viable, and compatible with sustainable agriculture11.

Biological control using beneficial microorganisms has emerged as a promising strategy for managing soil-borne diseases. In particular, bacteria belonging to the genus Bacillus have attracted considerable attention due to their ability to form endospores, survive under harsh environmental conditions, and produce a broad spectrum of bioactive compounds12. Numerous Bacillus subtilis strains have been shown to suppress fungal pathogens through multiple mechanisms, including antibiosis, competition for nutrients and niches, induction of systemic resistance, and direct promotion of plant growth13–15.

In a previous study, we isolated a Bacillus strain from hypersaline soil collected at Lake Magadi, Kenya, and demonstrated its strong antagonistic activity against R. solani under in vitro and greenhouse conditions16. The strain exhibited several plant growth-promoting and antifungal traits, including the production of protease, phosphatase, indole-3-acetic acid, and chitinase, and significantly reduced root mortality and pre-emergence wilting in P. vulgaris16–18. Based on 16 S rRNA gene sequencing, the isolate was initially classified as Bacillus velezensis. However, 16 S rRNA-based identification is limited by high sequence conservation, variable copy number, and intragenomic heterogeneity, particularly within closely related Bacillus species19. These limitations often lead to ambiguous taxonomic assignments and hinder accurate evaluation of biocontrol candidates20–22.

Whole-genome sequencing provides a robust framework for resolving taxonomic identity and predicting biosynthetic potential, and assessing safety-related traits such as toxin production, pathogenicity, and antimicrobial resistance23,24. This genome level characterization is increasingly important for the evaluation and potential commercialization of microbial biocontrol agents, particularly relevant for strains isolated from extreme environments, where unique selective pressures may shape novel functional traits.

In the present study, we performed whole-genome sequencing and comprehensive in silico analyses to characterize the genetic features of the Lake Magadi isolate and clarify its taxonomic identity. The genome was examined for biosynthetic gene clusters, genomic islands, stress tolerance mechanisms, and plant growth-promoting traits that may contribute to its antagonistic activity against R. solani. In parallel, field trials were conducted across dry and rainy seasons to evaluate the efficacy and stability of the strain under realistic agricultural conditions. By integrating genomic insights with field-based performance, this study aims to establish a robust foundation for the continued development, registration, and potential commercialization of this strain as a sustainable biocontrol agent for managing soil-borne diseases in common bean production.

Results

Genome features of Bacillus subtilis strain M10

The size of the assembled B. subtilis strain M10 genome summed up to 4,024,855 bp with a mean G + C content of 43.74% and a BUSCO completeness score of 99.5% (Supplementary Table S1). Genome annotation identified a total of 4,147 genes, including 4,061 protein-coding sequences (CDSs), representing 89.5% (3,602,400 bp) of the genome, with an average CDS length of 886.9 bp (Fig. 1). The genome contained four complete rRNA operons (16 S, 23 S, and 5 S), 77 tRNA genes, one tmRNA gene, and 74 pseudogenes.

Comparative analysis with three reference B. subtilis strains (BDSA1, MBI 600, and TY-1) revealed that strain M10 possesses a slightly smaller genome and fewer CDSs, while maintaining a comparable G + C content (Table 1). Strain M10 encoded more tRNA genes than BDSA1 but fewer rRNA and tmRNA genes than all three reference strains. The number of pseudogenes was lower in strain M10 compared with BDSA1 and TY-1.

Analysis of small RNAs (sRNAs) revealed diverse functional classes, including regulatory RNAs (27.0%), riboswitches (37.8%), ribosomal leader sequences (18.5%), housekeeping RNAs (13.5%), and protein-binding 6 S RNAs (2.7%) with proportions calculated based on total Rfam annotation hits identified across the whole genome (Supplementary Fig. S1). Repeat analysis identified 64 tandem repeats and 31 interspersed repeats (Supplementary Table S2). A single CRISPR array (106 bp; evidence level 1) was detected at genomic coordinates 8,543-8,648 (Supplementary Table S3). Pathogenicity screening classified strain M10 as non-pathogenic to humans (Supplementary Table S3).

Genomic islands and prophage regions

Ten genomic islands (GIs) were identified within the genome of strain M10, ranging from 4.1 to 43.3 kb in length (Table 2; Fig. 2). Eight GIs showed high sequence similarity (> 97%) to other B. subtilis strains, while two islands (GI6 and GI8) exhibited lower similarity (56% and 59%) to B. velezensis and B. subtilis strain LP, respectively. All GIs were predicted as integrated elements by multiple algorithms and collectively encoded 99 genes, with individual islands containing between 2 and 17 genes.

Functional annotation of GI-associated genes revealed the presence of genes associated with nutrient acquisition, putative antimicrobial related function, stress tolerance, and plant colonization (Supplementary Table S4). The two largest islands (GI9 and GI10), spanning 36.4 kb and 43.3 kb, respectively, contained genes annotated as being related to transport, metabolic adaptation, and stress response functions.

Prophage analysis identified four putative prophage regions in the genome (Supplementary Table S5). PHASTER detected one intact prophage (34.5 kb), one questionable region (49.6 kb), and two incomplete regions (15.8 kb and 14.1 kb). PHASTEST identified two intact prophages corresponding to the PHASTER-predicted intact and questionable regions and consolidated smaller regions into higher-confidence predictions (Supplementary Fig. S2). The prophages were assigned to the Brevibacterium Jimmer1/2, Osiris, Bacillus phi105, and SPβ lineages, with G + C contents ranging from 37% to 45%.

Phylogenetic placement and average nucleotide identity analysis

Multilocus sequence typing (MLST)-based phylogenetic reconstruction using maximum likelihood revealed that strain M10 clustered closely with B. subtilis strain H19, indicating strong genetic relatedness (Fig. 3a). Whole-genome phylogenomic analysis further supported this placement, with strain M10 forming a well-supported monophyletic clade with B. subtilis strains H19, KC14-1, and MBI 600 (Fig. 3b).

Average Nucleotide Identity based on MUMmer (ANIm) demonstrated high genomic similarity between strain M10 and other B. subtilis strains. The highest ANI values were observed with strains TY-1 (98.42%), BDSA1 (98.36%), MBI 600 (98.33%), and NIB353 (98.27%). In contrast, strain M10 exhibited lower ANI values with B. velezensis strain DMB07 (87.62%), supporting its assignment to the B. subtilis species complex (Fig. 3c).

KEGG pathway annotation assigned 2,563 genes across six major categories: metabolism, environmental information processing, genetic information processing, cellular processes, human diseases, and organismal systems (Fig. 4b). GO annotation showed enrichment of metabolic and cellular processes within the Biological Process category, catalytic activity and binding within Molecular Function, and intracellular components within Cellular Component (Fig. 4c). CAZy analysis identified 61 carbohydrate-active enzymes dominated by glycoside hydrolases and glycosyltransferases (Fig. 4d). TCDB annotation classified 1,009 transporter-related genes across eight transporter classes (Fig. 4e).

To enhance the depth of functional interpretation, we further explored genes associated with antibacterial activity. This analysis identified 12 genes linked to antibiotic biosynthesis (NRPS/PKS; pksB, bacA, bacB), bacteriocin/RiPP-related genes (ydeI, ykfA), and a cell wall-degrading enzyme (yoaJ), suggesting potential direct antibacterial mechanisms. In addition, numerous genes associated with efflux and resistance systems (mprF, bceA, bceB, norM, lmrA) were detected, indicating potential self-protection against antimicrobial compounds. These findings are further supported by KEGG annotations related to secondary metabolite biosynthesis and antimicrobial resistance pathways, collectively highlighting a genomic repertoire consistent with antibacterial activity and microbial competition (Supplementary Table S6).

Antibiotic resistance genes and virulence-associated factors

Resistome analysis identified multiple genes annotated as being associated with multidrug resistance, including efflux pumps (blt, bmr, lmrB, ykkC/D) and enzymatic resistance determinants (aadK, mphK), all detected with high sequence identity (> 98%) (Supplementary Table S7). Additional resistance mechanisms included the ABC-F ATPase vmlR and the membrane modification gene mprF.

Comparative analysis against the Virulence Factor Database identified homologues of genes annotated as virulence-associated based on sequence similarity (Table 3). These included cereulide synthetase A (cesA), hemolysin III (hlyIII), siderophore biosynthesis clusters (dhbA-F), and capsule biosynthesis genes (capA-D), as well as gene related to adhesion and motility, such as tufA, groEL/htpB, and flagellar components. However, no complete pathogenicity-associated operons or toxin gene clusters were identified. Furthermore, these annotations are based on sequence homology and are context dependent, and therefore do not constitute evidence of pathogenicity or virulence in strain M10.

Predicted secondary metabolite biosynthetic gene clusters

Genome mining using antiSMASH identified 13 putative biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) spanning 20.8–91.7 kb (Fig. 5; Table 4), including clusters predicted to encode non-ribosomal peptide synthetases, polyketide synthases, RiPPs, terpenes, and hybrid biosynthetic systems. Notably, several predicted clusters showed sequence similarity to well-characterized antimicrobial BGCs associated with the biosynthesis of compounds such as surfactin, fengycin, bacillibactin, bacilysin, subtilosin A, and sporulation killing factor (Supplementary Fig. S3), which are widely predicted to contribute to antifungal activity, bacterial competition and niche colonization. These findings suggest the predicted clusters represent core antimicrobial determinants with potential relevance, indicating biosynthetic potential rather than confirmed metabolite production.

Comparison with the MIBiG database further refined cluster annotation, revealing varying similarity scores to characterized biosynthetic pathways (Table 5). Pan-genome structure and strain-specific genes

Pan-genome analysis of strain M10 and seven reference Bacillus genomes was performed using seven publicly available Bacillus spp. reference complete genomes selected based on their close phylogenetic relationship and reported relevance in plant-associated and biocontrol contexts (Metadata), identifying a total of 12,236 genes. This includes 327 core genes shared among all strains (Fig. 7a). The accessory genome comprised 3,902 genes, while 8,007 genes were strain-specific. Bacillus subtilis KC14-1(104) had the lowest unique genes, followed by Strain M10 (108) while B. velezensis DMB07 (3796) had the highest number of unique genes (Fig. 7a). The function category of core genes includes cellular process (transport), metabolism, signal transduction, genetic information processing, translation and hypothetical protein (Supplementary Table S10). Additionally, the unique genes of strain M10 were putative to GTP cyclohydrolase, alkaline phosphatase synthesis, flagellin, maltose O-acetyltransferase, fructokinase, and response regulators (Supplementary Table S10). Presence–absence-based phylogenetic clustering revealed that strain M10 clusters closely with BDSA1, (Fig. 7b). Upset plot analysis further resolved gene-sharing patterns across strains, highlighting a conserved core genome alongside a substantial accessory and strain-specific gene pool. (Fig. 7c).

Predicted genes associated with plant growth promotion and stress tolerance

Genome analysis revealed genes annotated as being associated with nutrient acquisition, hormone biosynthesis, and stress tolerance. These included potassium transport systems (kdpABC, kup, trk/ktr), nitrogen metabolism genes, phosphate acquisition systems (PstSCAB-PhoU and PhoR/PhoB regulon), iron uptake systems, and siderophore biosynthesis clusters (Supplementary Table S11). Genes involved in tryptophan-dependent indole-3-acetic acid biosynthesis pathways were also identified (Fig. 8a, b). Stress response analysis revealed genes predicted to be involved in general stress response, oxidative stress defense, osmotic adaptation, and redox regulation (Supplementary Table S12). Field evaluation of biocontrol efficacy

Across two cropping seasons, application of strain M10 significantly reduced disease severity and improved yield parameters compared with pathogen-infected controls (Table 7; Fig. 9). During the dry season, disease severity in M10-treated plots was reduced to 28.14 ± 0.16%, while grain yield increased to 4,262.00 ± 3.18 kg ha− 1. Similar trends were observed during the rainy season, with disease severity reduced to 33.92 ± 1.40% and grain yield reaching 4,818.33 ± 11.89 kg ha− 1. Yield components, including pods per plant and seeds per pod, were consistently higher in M10-treated plots across both seasons.

Discussion

Members of the genus Bacillus are widely recognized for their ecological versatility and their capacity to establish beneficial interactions with plants, particularly through the suppression of soil-borne pathogens and enhancement of host resilience12. In this study, we combined whole-genome sequencing with field-based evaluation to elucidate the molecular basis underlying the biocontrol activity of strain M10 and to assess its performance under realistic agricultural conditions. By integrating genomic insights with phenotypic outcomes, our results provide a comprehensive framework supporting the development of strain M10 as a sustainable biocontrol agent against Rhizoctonia solani in Phaseolus vulgaris.

Initial identification of the isolate based on 16 S rRNA gene sequencing classified strain M10 as Bacillus velezensis16. However, whole-genome-based phylogenetic reconstruction and average nucleotide identity analysis clearly placed the strain within the B. subtilis species complex. This discrepancy highlights the limited discriminatory power of 16 S rRNA sequencing for resolving closely related Bacillus taxa, where high sequence conservation and intragenomic variability often obscure species boundaries19. Genome-level analyses therefore provide a more robust and reliable basis for taxonomic assignment and functional inference, particularly for candidate biocontrol strains intended for field application and regulatory approval21,25.

The genome of B. subtilis strain M10 exhibited high assembly quality and completeness, with genomic features consistent with previously reported B. subtilis strains26. The presence of a diverse array of genes involved in metabolism, transport, and stress response suggest ecological adaptability of the strain. Functional annotation revealed an abundance of carbohydrate-active enzymes, including glycoside hydrolases and glycosyltransferases, which are predicted to be involved in fungal cell wall degradation, nutrient acquisition, and rhizosphere competitiveness27,28. These genomic traits are consistent with earlier enzymatic assays demonstrating chitinase and phosphatase activity in strain M1016, thereby providing experimental support for genomic predictions.

Genomic island analysis further revealed that strain M10 harbors multiple horizontally acquired regions enriched in genes associated with nutrient cycling, biocontrol activity, stress tolerance, and plant colonization. Such islands are recognized as major drivers of bacterial adaptation, conferring selective advantages in complex environments such as the rhizosphere29,30. Genes encoding chitinases, xylanases, pectate lyases, and oxalate decarboxylases are predicted to contribute to fungal cell wall degradation and detoxification of plant-derived compounds, thereby enhancing competitive exclusion of R. solani31. Additionally, genes linked to biofilm formation, chemotaxis, and protein secretion suggest enhanced root colonization potential, a trait that is further supported by the observed field performance32.

Comparative analysis against the Virulence Factor Database identified homologues of genes classically associated with virulence in pathogenic bacteria, including siderophore biosynthesis, capsule formation, and adhesion-related proteins. In B. subtilis, however, these genetic elements are well documented to function primarily in environmental fitness, iron acquisition, and plant-associated lifestyles rather than in pathogenicity33. Importantly, strain M10 lacked complete toxin-encoding operons characteristic of pathogenic Bacillus species, supporting its biosafety. These findings reinforce the importance of contextual interpretation of virulence-associated genes in non-pathogenic, plant-associated bacteria and support the suitability of strain M10 for agricultural application.

Secondary metabolite biosynthetic gene cluster analysis predicted a rich repertoire of antimicrobial compounds, including non-ribosomal peptides and polyketides such as surfactin, fengycin, bacillibactin, bacilysin, and subtilosin A. These metabolites are well known for their antifungal activity, membrane-disrupting properties, and ability to induce systemic resistance in plants34,35. The identification of hybrid PKS/NRPS clusters associated with bacillaene further suggests the genetic capacity to inhibit competing microorganisms through interference with protein synthesis. Notably, several predicted clusters showed limited similarity to known entries in the MIBiG database, indicating the potential for novel secondary metabolites that require experimental validation36,37.

Beyond direct antagonism, strain M10 harbors extensive genetic machinery associated with plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) functions. Genes involved in phosphate solubilization, nitrogen metabolism, potassium transport, siderophore production, and indole-3-acetic acid biosynthesis collectively indicate potential for improved nutrient availability and hormonal modulation in host plants18. These genomic features corroborate previous biochemical assays demonstrating IAA production and phosphate solubilization by strain M1016 and provide a mechanistic explanation for the observed enhancement of yield components in the field.

Field trials conducted across contrasting seasonal conditions demonstrated that strain M10 significantly reduced disease severity and improved grain yield. The stability of biocontrol performance under both dry and rainy conditions suggests strong ecological fitness and rhizosphere competence, traits often linked to the sporulation capacity, stress tolerance, and biofilm-forming ability of Bacillus species18. The magnitude of disease suppression observed in this study is comparable to or exceeds that reported for other B. subtilis biocontrol strains evaluated against R. solani in different crops38–40. Importantly, yield enhancement extended beyond disease suppression, indicating that growth promotion and improved nutrient utilization contributed substantially to agronomic performance.

The concordance between genomic predictions, greenhouse assays reported previously, and field-level outcomes observed here provides strong evidence that the biocontrol activity of strain M10 is genetically encoded and environmentally robust. However, while genome analysis provides mechanistic hypotheses, only field and biochemical assays constitute direct validation of functional performance41. Collectively, these findings position B. subtilis strain M10 as a multifunctional biocontrol agent that operates through a combination of direct pathogen inhibition, enhancement of plant immunity, and promotion of host growth.

Despite the integrated genomic and field-based approach adopted in this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, field evaluations were conducted at a single experimental site; although efficacy was consistent across dry and rainy seasons, multi-location trials across different soil types and agroecological zones will be required to confirm the broader applicability and stability of strain M10. Second, functional insights into biocontrol and plant growth-promoting mechanisms were primarily inferred from genome annotation and in silico predictions. While these analyses provide strong evidence of biosynthetic and adaptive potential, complementary transcriptomic, proteomic, or metabolomic investigations under field conditions would be necessary to verify the expression and activity of key pathways. Finally, the interaction between strain M10 and native soil microbial communities was not explicitly assessed, and future studies incorporating microbiome-level analyses would help clarify its ecological impact and persistence following repeated field application.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive genomic and field-based evaluation of Bacillus subtilis strain M10 as a biocontrol agent against Rhizoctonia solani in common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris L.). Whole-genome sequencing supported the taxonomic placement of strain M10 within the B. subtilis species complex and identified a diverse genetic repertoire associated with antimicrobial production, nutrient acquisition, stress tolerance, and rhizosphere colonization. The presence of multiple biosynthetic gene clusters putatively involved in antifungal lipopeptides and siderophores, together with genomic islands linked to environmental adaptation, suggests the strain’s capacity to function effectively in complex soil ecosystems.

Importantly, genome-wide safety assessment did not identify toxin-associated operons characteristic of pathogenic Bacillus species, supporting the potential biosafety of strain M10 for agricultural use. Field trials conducted across contrasting seasonal conditions demonstrated consistent suppression of R. solani-induced disease and improvement in grain yield and yield components, indicating the ecological stability and agronomic relevance of the strain.

By integrating genomic insights with multi-season field validation, this study provides a strong foundation for the further development of B. subtilis strain M10 as a sustainable biocontrol candidate. Future work should focus on formulation optimization, large-scale field trials, transcriptomic profiling under field conditions, and comparative genomics with registered Bacillus-based products to further substantiate its function traits. Collectively, these efforts will contribute to environmentally resilient strategies for managing soil-borne diseases and improving crop productivity.

Materials and methods

Sample processing and genomic DNA extraction

Soil samples were collected from Lake Magadi, Kenya (2°S and 36° E), and transported to the Institute of Biotechnology Research, Jomo Kenyatta University of Agriculture and Technology (JKUAT), for microbial isolation. Pure bacterial cultures were established following a modified protocol described by Wekesa et al.16. Briefly, samples were serially diluted and plated on Nutrient Agar medium (HiMedia) composed of 10.0 gL− 1 peptone, 10.0 gL− 1 beef extract, 5.0 gL− 1 NaCl, 12.0 gL− 1 agar, supplemented with 0.01 mgL− 1 cycloheximide and 0.35% (w/v) NaCl to inhibit fungal growth. Detailed procedures for sampling, culture purification, and preliminary molecular identification are described in Wekesa et al.16.

For genomic DNA extraction, a single pure colony was inoculated into Luria-Bertani (LB) broth and incubated at 28 °C with shaking at 150 rpm for 48 h. An aliquot of the culture was transferred into fresh LB broth, adjusted to an optical density (OD₆₀₀) of 0.2, and incubated under the same conditions for an additional 24 h. Bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation and transferred into 2.0 mL microcentrifuge tubes (Eppendorf, Germany). Genomic DNA was extracted using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method as described by Ramyabharathi et al.42. DNA integrity was assessed by electrophoresis on a 1% (w/v) agarose gel, and DNA concentration was quantified using a Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). High-quality genomic DNA was submitted to Inqaba Biotech (South Africa) for library preparation and sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq platform (2 × 150 bp paired-end reads), following the manufacturer’s protocols.

Genome assembly, taxonomic classification, and annotation

Raw sequencing reads were quality-filtered using Fastp v.1.0.143 to remove adapter sequences and low-quality reads (Phred score < 25). The resulting high-quality reads were de novo assembled using the Read Assembly and Annotation Pipeline Tool (RAPT) v.0.5.5 with default parameters (https://github.com/ncbi/rapt). Genome completeness was assessed using BUSCO v6.0.0 under default settings, employing the bacillales_odb12 lineage dataset, which comprises 427 single-copy orthologs.

Gene prediction and genome annotation were performed using Prokka v.1.2.044. Non-coding RNAs, including tRNAs, rRNAs, tmRNAs, and small RNAs (sRNAs), were identified using the Rfam database v.1.1.4. Interspersed repetitive elements were detected using RepeatMasker v.4.1.2-p1, while tandem repeats were identified using Tandem Repeats Finder (TRF) v.4.10.0-rc.245. CRISPR loci were predicted using CRISPRCasFinder (https://crisprcas.i2bc.paris-saclay.fr; accessed 20 August 2025).

The assembled genome was screened for pathogenic potential using PathogenFinder 2.0 (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/PathogenFinder/; accessed 23 August 2025). Genomic islands were predicted using IslandViewer 4 (https://www.pathogenomics.sfu.ca/islandviewer; accessed 31 July 2025), while prophage regions were identified using both PHASTER (https://phaster.ca; accessed 20 August 2025) and Phastest v.1.0.1 (https://proksee.ca; accessed 20 July 2025).

To assess genomic relatedness, Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) analysis was conducted by comparing the assembled genome against publicly available Bacillus genomes retrieved from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov; accessed 28 July 2025). Pairwise ANI values were calculated using ANIm, ANIb, and Tetra methods via the web server, and results were visualized as clustered heatmaps in RStudio v.2025.05.1 + 513. Only validated Bacillus genomes exhibiting ≥ 94% similarity was retained for downstream analyses.

Multilocus sequence typing and whole-genome phylogenetic analysis

The assembled genome of strain M10 was deposited in the NCBI database (accessed 14 September 2025) and queried against the nucleotide collection (nr/nt) using BLASTn to identify closely related genomes. Fifteen complete genomes exhibiting high nucleotide similarity (> 99.79%) were selected for comparative analysis. Bacillus cereus strain NC7401 was included as an outgroup for phylogenetic rooting.

Housekeeping genes commonly used in multilocus sequence typing (MLST) were retrieved from the MLST database (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/MLST-2.0/; accessed 14 September 2025). For B. subtilis, the genes glpF, ilvD, pta, purH, pycA, rpoD, and tpiA were extracted, while the corresponding loci glp_3, gmk, ilv, pta, pur, pyc, and tpi were retrieved for B. cereus. Gene sequences were concatenated in the order pta-purH-rpoD-glpF-ilvD-pycA-tpiA to generate a multilocus dataset for each isolate. Alignments were performed using MAFFT v.7.52546 with the parameters “--globalpair --maxiterate 1000”. Phylogenetic trees were reconstructed using RAxML v.847 under the GTR+GAMMA model, with branch support evaluated using 1,000 bootstrap replicates.

To further refine taxonomic placement, whole-genome phylogenomic analysis was conducted using the Type (Strain) Genome Server (TYGS; https://tygs.dsmz.de; accessed 30 July 2025). Genome distances were estimated using the MASH algorithm48, and a maximum-likelihood phylogenomic tree was generated to resolve evolutionary relationships among strain M10 and reference Bacillus genomes.

Functional annotation, biosynthesis potential, and pan-genome analysis

Functional annotation of predicted coding sequences was performed using eggNOG-mapper (http://eggnog-mapper.embl.de; accessed 17 July 2025), assigning COG, KEGG49, GO, Pfam, and CAZy annotations. Transport-related functions were identified using the Transporter Classification Database (TCDB). Antimicrobial resistance genes were detected using Abricate v.1.0.150, while putative virulence-associated genes were identified by comparison against the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB)51.

Biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) associated with secondary metabolite production were predicted using antiSMASH v.8.0.2 (https://antismash.secondarymetabolites.org; accessed 18 August 2025), with ClusterBlast, ActiveSiteFinder, SubClusterBlast, and Pfam-based analyses enabled. Ribosomally synthesized and post-translationally modified peptides (RiPPs) and bacteriocins were identified using RiPPMiner and BAGEL 4 (http://bagel4.molgenrug.nl; accessed 2 October 2025). PRISM 452 was used to predict the putative chemical structures of selected secondary metabolites. Genome-wide functional categorization and identification of plant growth-promoting traits were further refined using the SEED (RAST) subsystem53.

Pan-genome analysis was conducted using strain M10 alongside seven publicly available Bacillus genomes (selected based potential use as biocontrol & PGPR, Country, Host and Source). GFF3 annotation files were analyzed using Roary v.3.13.054 with default parameters to identify orthologous gene clusters and generate a gene presence/absence matrix. Core, accessory, and strain-specific genes were defined, and a pan-genome-based phylogenetic tree was constructed using FastTree55. Gene distribution patterns were visualized using Phandango v.1.3.156.

Field evaluation of biocontrol efficacy

Field trials were conducted at Jomo Kenyatta University of Agriculture and Technology (JKUAT), Kenya (1°05’24.6"S 37°00’33.8"E), to evaluate the efficacy of Bacillus subtilis strain M10 against R. solani-induced root rot in Phaseolus vulgaris. The experimental plots (3 × 3 m) consisted of five rows with five plants per row, giving 25 plants per plot, arranged in a randomized complete block design (RCBD). The experiment included three treatments: T1 - strain M10 and R. solani (1.0 × 10¹² CFU mL− 1), T2 - untreated healthy control, and T3 - R. solani-infested control, each replicated five times (n = 5 Blocks per treatment).

Pathogen-inoculated plots were established by incorporating actively growing wheat bran cultures of R. solani into natural soil of 50 g m⁻² (dry weight equivalent), corresponding to an estimated inoculum density of ~ 1 × 10⁵ CFU g⁻¹ soil. Artificial inoculation was conducted to ensure uniform and adequate disease pressure across experimental plots, as natural field infestation of R. solani is often spatially heterogeneous and may not result in consistent infection levels necessary for reliable evaluation of biocontrol efficacy. Soil was thoroughly homogenized to ensure uniform distribution of inoculum, and plots were left for a 7-day equilibration period prior to planting to allow pathogen establishment57,58. Uniformity of infection was visually confirmed through preliminary symptom development before treatment application59.

Bacterial treatments were applied via soil drenching at a rate of 2 L ha− 1 using a cell suspension adjusted to 1.0 × 10¹² CFU mL⁻¹ through serial dilution and plate enumeration. Applications were carried out at 0, 2, 4, and 6 weeks after planting, corresponding to planting (seedling emergence), early vegetative (2–3 trifoliate leaves), mid-vegetative, and early flowering stage, respectively, based on preliminary optimization trials and reported effective field application regimes for Bacillus-based biocontrol agents.

Disease assessment was conducted by randomly selecting ten plants per plot and evaluating symptoms of yellowing, wilting, and root rot severity. Disease severity was scored using a standardized 0–5 scale (0 = no symptoms, 5 = plant death), and a disease severity index (DSI) was calculated for quantitative comparison among treatments60

Grain yield and yield-related parameters were recorded at harvest. The experiment was conducted over two independent cropping seasons (dry-June to October,2024 and rainy-March to May, 2024), with five replicates blocks per treatment per season, to ensure statistical reliability and to capture environmental variability under field conditions.
