Liquid Biopsy and Ocular Tumors: A Review
Authors: Celine Chaaya, Andrea Sarkis, Georges El Haddad, Ernest Diab, Alexandre Schakal
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To explore the various applications of liquid biopsy (LB) in ophthalmology and provide a comprehensive discussion on its utility in the field.Methods:A thorough exploration of the literature was conducted, and a total of 47 articles were included.Results:In RB, where conventional tumor biopsies carry risks of spreading the tumor beyond the eye, LB, particularly from the aqueous humor, emerges as a promising alternative. It provides a secure means of detecting cfDNA. In addition, LB is effective in uveal melanoma, particularly through digital droplet PCR, in mutation detection and predicting disease progression. Moreover, vitreous LB proves highly effective in diagnosing vitreoretinal lymphoma, exhibiting greater sensitivity than traditional cytopathological methods. This noninvasive approach not only enhances diagnostic accuracy but also holds potential for guiding biologically targeted therapies, showcasing the ongoing advancements in LB technology poised to redefine and revolutionize the field.Conclusion:These findings offer valuable insights for personalized treatment strategies in ocular malignancies.

INTRODUCTION

Liquid biopsy (LB) has emerged as a promising tool in the field of oncology. The use of LB using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) has significantly expanded in recent years due to the demand for less intrusive methods for diagnosis, prognosis, and disease monitoring of cancer.1 LB in ophthalmology involves the analysis of ocular-specific genetic material, such as cell-free DNA (cfDNA), circulating tumor cells (CTC), and microRNAs (miRNAs), found in the aqueous humor (AH) or vitreous humor (VH) of patients.23 In addition, research highlights the significance of various biomarkers, including circulating tumor RNA (cRNA), miRNAs, tumor-derived exosomes (TREs), and tumor-educated platelets (TEPs), in LB applications.

As research develops, incorporation of LB into standard clinical practice could enhance patient outcome and result in more accurate and effective ocular health care. This narrative review aims to comprehensively discuss the different applications of LB in ophthalmology.

METHODS

We conducted a search of all English-language literature on LB published in electronic databases following the Preferred Reporting System for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis guidelines. In addition, a review of past articles on the use of LB in medicine was carried out. We conducted a search in the PubMed database using the following keywords and MeSH terms: “liquid biopsy” and “eye neoplasms” and “ocular tumors” and “ophthalmology”. A reviewer (CC) selected studies published between January 2000 and March 2024. Following the removal of duplicate entries, an initial screening of titles and abstracts was conducted and full texts of articles deemed potentially relevant were subsequently reviewed for eligibility. Articles focusing on the application of LB in ophthalmology were selected. Types of articles included were randomized trials, cohort studies, case–control studies, cross-sectional studies, and case series. Case reports, conference abstracts, and letters to the editor were excluded from the review. A total of 47 articles were included in this narrative review [Figure 1].

RESULTS

Liquid biopsy in retinoblastoma

Retinoblastoma (RB) is a type of pediatric cancer that develops in a child’s developing retina. It is the most frequent intraocular tumor in children, affecting between 1/15 and 20,000 live births every year.4 Cancerous tumors can develop in either or both eyes. The RB1 tumor suppressor gene, which controls the cell cycle, is bi-allelic lost in most cases. The RB1 gene’s bi-allelic inactivation causes unilateral tumor in 60% of patients and manifests as somatic loss in the retina. However, the original RB1 mutation occurs in the germline in 40% of patients, who then experience a second “hit” in the retina with tumors affecting both eyes. Finally, a small percentage (2%) of tumors appear to be caused by v-myc avian myelocytomatosis viral oncogene neuroblastoma derived homolog (MYCN) amplification without RB1 gene alterations.5 Without treatment, RB is fatal, and even with treatment, if the cancer does not respond to the therapy, there is a substantial risk of losing an eye.67

There was an acceleration of advancements in conservative management of RB over the past 20 years with the introduction of systemic chemotherapy and intra-arterial injections. A new conservative approach is currently taking place with LB.8 It is not a novel idea in the world of oncology to use an LB to track malignant disease. In fact, the proportion of sampled cfDNA that comes from tumors, known as the tumor fraction (TFx), has proven to be a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker.9 Effectively, tumor biopsy for RB is not performed due to the potential risk of extraocular tumor spread. A technique to assess epigenetic tumor patterns in vivo would be particularly significant given the key role epigenetics plays in this malignancy.10

In patients’ plasma, cfDNA from intraocular RB which comes directly from the tumor cells can be detected and quantified. The idea of a blood plasma LB could provide the patient with several benefits over an AH sample.11 However, no correlation has been noted so far.12 In a concept study by Kothari et al., it was showed that in individuals with severe unilateral RB and no detectable germline mutation, plasma cfDNA analysis has the potential to identify somatic RB1 mutations. Effectively, they discovered somatic RB1 mutations in 8 of 10 cfDNA samples and 10 of 13 RB1 mutations.13

These findings can be used for the diagnosis and management of RB; however, new studies are emerging on the detection of cfDNA from AH.14 The clear fluid in front of the eye, known as AH, is an especially rich source of tumor-derived cfDNA for RB that could be used for this matter.15 Studies suggest that for the safe and effective identification of chromosomal alterations in RB, AH from affected eyes has proved to be more secure and superior to blood harvesting.16 Berry et al. demonstrated that AH can be extracted from RB eyes and that genomic analyses of the extract were correlated to tumor mutation profile.17

AH extraction is a minimally invasive approach that can be conducted during intravitreal injection of chemotherapy. This technique comes with a negligible risk of bleeding, infection, cataract, or iris trauma. The preliminary work by Berry et al. demonstrated that AH is a higher-yield source of cell-free tumor DNA than blood via whole genome sequencing.12 In the study conducted by Li et al., DNA methylation profiling of paired AH cfDNA and primary RB tumors was performed, and the results were compared to known RB tumor DNA methylation profiles. There was a high concordance between the methylation profiles of AH cfDNA and primary tumors. These results could mean that AH profiling is a potential mechanism to evaluate the methylation status of the RB.10 It was also demonstrated that the DNA from the AH may be utilized to identify the tumor-specific RB1 mutation even in the absence of tumor tissue which could be useful in mutation pattern analysis and detection of mosaicism.18 The investigation of RB somatic copy number changes (SCNAs) in all eyes, even those that are actively undergoing therapy or even treatment-naive, has been made easier by the AH LB for RB.19 For instance, the presence of chromosome 6p gain with an amplitude of ≥1.5 to the median or focal MYCN amplification is a significant prognostic molecular signature that corresponds with a tenfold risk of loss of intraocular tumor control requiring enucleation.1720 Moreover, gains on 1q, 2p, and 6p and losses on 13q and 16q are believed to contribute to RB tumor progression and growth alongside bi-allelic RB1 inactivation.21 AH biopsy is being used for the simultaneous identification of RB1 mutations and SCNAs for RB as previous studies were conducted on tissue analysis from enucleated eyes.22 A recent application of this new technique was done by Im in Los Angeles. A total of 14 AH samples from 14 non-RB eyes in 12 children and 128 RB AH samples from 62 RB eyes in 55 children were studied. Different types of nucleic acid and protein concentration were measured. They proved that using quantification techniques, AH is effectively a high-yield source of numerous circulating analytes (dsDNA, ssDNA, miRNA, and protein), whose amounts can be assessed at diagnosis and throughout treatment.23 Although different biomarkers can be used in the application of LB and can be quantified (dsDNA, ssDNA, miRNA, and proteins), using Qubit fluorescence assays, one study demonstrated that RNA was not reliably quantifiable.23

Research has also shown that long noncoding RNAs may serve as potential biomarkers for detecting RB at various stages by targeting multiple miRNAs and transcription factors that are crucial in either stimulating or suppressing RB.24

Research on LB in RB may help understand the different mechanisms that lead to treatment resistance thus enabling the identification of potential therapeutic targets.16 Gudiseva et al. acknowledge that these developments in LB are a component of a future strategy for improved RB diagnosis and treatment.25 The application of both LB and noninvasive prenatal diagnosis offers a wealth of new possibilities to RB patients.11 However, although it is contained in the same ocular space as the tumor, the AH develops in a different compartment.26 It is important to note that the amount of biofluid (and thus the amount of DNA) that can be extracted from the eye is the limit of the AH-based platform.26

Liquid biopsy in uveal melanoma

Uveal melanoma (UM) is the most common primary intraocular tumor in adults and the most common cause of death because of an intraocular disease. Even with appropriate treatment, almost 50% of patients develop metastasis. It is thus important to make an early diagnosis and accurately predict the metastatic risk of a patient. Currently, tumor biopsies are required to assess the metastatic risk of UM. However, this method is invasive and comes with a lot of potential complications. It is thus important to develop less invasive strategies that can accurately predict the metastatic risk of UM. In addition, biomarkers for the early detection and progression of this tumor, in particular molecular indicators, are currently lacking in clinical practice.27

Blood

Bustamante et al. conducted an experimental study to test the efficacy of LB in the peripheral blood in diagnosing UM and monitoring its progression. For this, they set up an animal model consisting of rabbits that had UM cell lines injected into their suprachoroidal space in the right eye. First of all, the authors decided to test the efficacy of digital droplet PCR (ddPCR) at detecting small quantities of mutated DNA copies of the main driver mutations genes in UM. They were able to detect mutated DNA for different mutations. LB testing returned positive on average 24 days after the beginning of the study, while clinical examination detected tumors more than a week later at 31.4 days postinoculation. Moreover, ddPCR could detect multiple UM driver mutations as the authors had injected different UM cell lines (each with a different driver mutation) in each rabbit group, but the LB successfully identified the mutated ctDNA (GNAQ and GNA11) in both rabbit groups. In addition, there was a strong correlation between ctDNA levels in the peripheral blood and tumor size (r = 0.6, P < 0.001). The same study was then conducted on patients with UM and a control group. The authors had similar conclusions with UM patients showing the presence of mutated ctDNA (GNAQ/11, PLCβ4, and CYSLTR2) whereas none of the control patients had those mutated ctDNAs. There was also a strong correlation between the American Joint Commission for Cancer (AJCC) classification of UM and the percentage fractional abundance (which refers to the portion of the mutant allele frequencies over the wild type) (r = 0.69, P = 0.008). These data suggest that ctDNA can bring valuable information for the diagnosis of UM and its progression.2829

Herrspiegel et al. screened over 80 serum proteins and compared their expression levels between metastatic and nonmetastatic UM patients. They found that leptin and osteopontin had different expression levels between metastatic and nonmetastatic patients and met the minimum requirements for a prognostically useful test (lower bound of area under the curve 95% confidence interval >0.5). They then developed a classification to assess the risk of metastasis based on the serum expression levels of these two proteins. The metastatic risk was considered low if leptin concentration levels were high whereas osteopontin concentration was low, intermediate if either leptin concentration was low (<5 ng/ml [men] and <15 ng/ml [women]) or osteopontin concentration was high (>5 ng/ml), and high if the patient presented both a low leptin and a high osteopontin concentration. Further analysis found that patients who were classified as having a low, intermediate, and high risk of metastasis had a median time to metastasis of 6.1, 4.4, and 1.4 years, respectively. Patients with a low serum leptin concentration and a high osteopontin had a shorter time to metastasis (P = 0.046 and P = 0.048, respectively). Further analysis found that patients in a higher metastatic risk category had both a shorter metastasis-free survival (P = 0.007) and a shorter overall survival (P = 0.018).30

Another study investigated the potential role of biomarkers in determining the stage of tumor extension in patients with ultraviolet (UV). Researchers conducted exome isolation from the blood of subjects. Findings indicated that hsa-miR-144-5p and hsa-miR-191-5p were promising biomarkers, enabling differentiation between patients with localized and advanced UV.27

Aqueous humor

Conventionally, tumor biopsy was conducted to assess the metastatic risk of UM. However, this technique is quite invasive and puts the patient at risk of retinal detachment and tumor dissemination. Peng et al. recently conducted a study to evaluate the potential of AH LB in assessing the risk of metastasis in UM. The authors recruited 20 patients with UM, of which 15 gene expression profile 1 (GEP1) and 5 GEP2 and extracted small samples of 0.1 mL of AH via corneal paracentesis. They then analyzed the expression levels of 1469 proteins in the AH and tried to correlate the protein expressions with the GEP classification. Principal component analysis grouped the patients in 2 clusters, with one cluster containing 91.67% of GEP1 patients and the other 80% of GEP2 patients. The authors found that of the initial 1469 proteins, 45 had significantly different expression levels between GEP1 and GEP2 patients and were thus selected for further analysis. The same experiment was repeated but this time using only the 45 differentially expressed proteins. This method resulted in the formation of 2 clusters, with 100% of GEP2 patients being grouped in cluster A and 75% of GEP1 patients in cluster B. The authors also noted that two GEP1 patients were in the middle between the two clusters which suggests that these might be patients who are in a transitional phase from a benign to a more advanced disease.31

Vitreous humor

Velez et al. conducted an experimental study in which they tried to assess the presence of potential biomarkers in the VH that could predict the metastatic risk of UM. For this experiment, eight patients with UM were recruited and underwent vitreous biopsies. Seventy-seven proteins were found to be differentially expressed between the patients with UM and the control group. However, when the authors tried to compare GEP1 and GEP2 patients with UM, they found that 46 proteins were differentially expressed between the two groups. To validate these findings, the authors repeated this experiment on an independent cohort, this time including 11 UM patients and 11 controls. Protein expression levels were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay and compared between the two groups. Two proteins had significantly higher expression levels in UM patients compared to controls, while c-Myc showed higher expression levels in GEP2 patients compared to GEP1 (P = 0.009). Met/hepatocyte growth factor receptor was elevated in stages 3 and 4 of the AJCC classification of UM compared with classes 1 and 2 (P = 0.046). These data suggest that vitreous biopsies can bring valuable information in the diagnosis and classification of UM and assess its metastatic risk.32

Liquid biopsy in vitreoretinal lymphoma

Vitreoretinal lymphoma (VRL) is a rare type of ocular tumor. Conventionally, the diagnosis of VRL has been challenging, mainly because its clinical manifestations are very similar to uveitis. The main diagnostic tool remains the cytopathological examination, but its detection rate for VRL remains poor. We hereby summarize the latest findings regarding the diagnosis and prognostic evaluation of VRL.

Gu et al. conducted a study in which they evaluated the diagnostic potential of vitreous LB in the diagnosis of VRL. For this, they recruited 23 patients with VRL and 25 patients with uveitis and performed vitreous biopsies on them. For each patient, two samples of vitreous fluid were extracted, of which one was sent for cytopathological examination and the other for cfDNA sequencing. cfDNA sequencing revealed that the most common mutations in VRL patients were myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (MYD88) (78%), ETS variant transcription factor 6 (ETV6) (48%), proto-oncogene serine/threonine-protein kinase (48%), B-cell translocation gene 2 (30%), and interferon regulatory factor 4 (7 eyes, 30%). A logistic regression based on the mutations of MYD88 and ETV6 proved highly effective in diagnosing VRL with a sensitivity of 91.3% and a specificity of 95%. Meanwhile, the sensitivity and specificity of cytopathology were 82.6% and 100%, respectively. Moreover, the authors evaluated the prognostic significance of several gene mutations. For each gene, the patients were divided between those who had the wild type and those who had the mutant type. Log-rank tests were then performed to test the statistical significance of a difference in the progression-free survival between the two groups. The P values of the log-rank tests for MYD88, ETV6, and immunoglobulin-associated beta (CD79B) are 0.589, 0.066, and 0.061, respectively. These results indicate that LB can be effective tool in the diagnosis and prognosis of VRL.33

In addition, Cani et al. performed next-generation sequencing on LB extracted from four VRL patients. They found MYD88-L265 mutation and a MYD88-S243N mutation. They also found a copy number loss in cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2A (CDKN2A) which is a tumor suppressor in all four patients. The authors hypothesized that these findings would lead to new biologically targeted therapies for VRL.34

Other ocular tumors

Although the use of LB has been mostly reported in RB and UV, its application in other ocular malignancies including conjunctival melanoma, conjunctival lymphoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and sebaceous carcinoma is expanding. LB has yet to be specifically investigated in the context of conjunctival melanoma and squamous cell carcinoma. However, it is reasonable to suggest that methodologies developed for cutaneous melanoma might be applicable to conjunctival cases.35

Choroidal metastases represent the most common intraocular malignancies in adults, despite being asymptomatic for most cases. They are most frequently associated with breast and lung cancers. The improvement in survival rates for these cancers has led to an increased incidence of detected choroidal metastases. Typically, these metastases are diagnosed at advanced stages, limiting the use of LB. Nevertheless, it may still be beneficial in specific scenarios, such as when the primary malignancy is unknown, when obtaining a biopsy from the primary tumor proves difficult.36

DISCUSSION

Recent advancements in medical research have paved the way for innovative diagnostic approaches in health care. One such promising development is the integration of LB in ophthalmology, presenting a paradigm shift in standard clinical practice. The use of LB in hematologic and solid tumors has proven to be reliable and accurate, notably in the detection of tumor markers. LB may also reveal intratumor heterogeneity and clonal evolution in response to treatment or help predict tumor relapse.1 Despite plasma being the primary source of genetic material for tumor genomic profiling, LB is being researched in several biologic fluids.1 This innovative technique can also be used for diagnosing and monitoring various ocular diseases. It allows the recognition of genetic mutations, chromosomal abnormalities, and epigenetic changes linked to ocular pathologies such as RB, age-related macular degeneration, and UM. Moreover, its noninvasive nature offers a convenient alternative to traditional biopsy methods, minimizing risks and discomfort associated with invasive procedures.2

LB plays a crucial role in molecular diagnosis by identifying various biomarkers. These include CTC, cfDNA, cRNA, miRNA, and TREs.37 Each of these biomarkers offers unique insights into tumor biology and can assist in monitoring disease progression and response to therapy, making LB a valuable tool in oncology.

The literature suggests that miRNAs play a crucial role in cancer by upregulating various oncogenes. In the context of UM, miRNAs have emerged as valuable biomarkers for both diagnosis and prognosis, offering insights into the progression of the disease and potential therapeutic targets.38394041424344

Research has shown that individuals with metastatic UM exhibit higher concentrations of total exosomes in their venous blood compared to healthy individuals.4045 This observation suggests that exosomes may be significant in indicating the biological properties and metastatic tendencies of UM. In addition to UM, exosomes were shown to be related to other nonmalignant ocular diseases.46 While no specific panels of TREs linked to UM have been established yet, this area of research is rapidly evolving, with ongoing studies aimed at uncovering such biomarkers. In addition, TEPs are gaining considerable attention for their potential as an accessible LB method for early cancer detection. These platelets carry information about the tumor microenvironment, which can provide valuable insights into the presence of cancer at its earliest stages.47 Although no reports have been documented regarding ophthalmologic tumors, their application in colorectal cancer and breast and pulmonary tumors have been described.4849

This article provides a comprehensive exploration of the application of LB in three ocular malignancies – RB, UM, and VRL and the potential application in different ocular tumors. In RB, where traditional tumor biopsies pose risks of extraocular tumor spread, LB, particularly from AH, emerges as a promising alternative, offering a secure method for detecting cfDNA and insights into the methylation status.12 The article supports the efficacy of LB in UM, specifically through digital droplet PCR, for detecting mutations and predicting disease progression. It highlights the potential of LB in assessing metastatic risk in both uveal and VH.25 Notably, vitreous LB proves highly effective in diagnosing VRL, demonstrating a higher sensitivity and thereby surpassing the limitations of traditional cytopathological methods.29

These advancements offer valuable insights for personalized treatment strategies in ocular malignancies. LB allows for the noninvasive detection of genetic and molecular changes associated with different malignancies. The incorporation of LB into standard clinical practice in ophthalmology represents a transformative step toward more precise, patient-centered ocular health care. LB techniques offer a wealth of benefits that can revolutionize the diagnosis, monitoring, and treatment of ocular malignancies.

Furthermore, evaluating the feasibility of integrating LB into routine screening protocols for high-risk populations and assessing its cost-effectiveness compared to traditional methods could potentially enable earlier detection of ocular tumors and improve prognosis.

The studies reviewed demonstrate the potential of LB in ocular oncology; however, there are several limitations that must be considered. A primary limitation is the heterogeneity across studies, including variability in study design, sample types, and biomarker detection methods, which limits the generalizability of findings. In addition, translating biomarkers from research into clinical practice poses substantial challenges. The absence of standardized protocols, inconsistencies in sensitivity and specificity, and concerns regarding cost and accessibility must be addressed to enable wider clinical adoption.

Future research should focus on large-scale, standardized studies to validate promising biomarkers in ocular oncology and explore strategies to integrate LB technologies into routine clinical practice effectively. Overall, this noninvasive approach not only enhances diagnostic accuracy but also holds promise for guiding biologically targeted therapies, emphasizing the ongoing progress of LB technology to redefine the field, but future research is necessary to further guide its use in clinical practice.

In conclusion, LB has proven its efficacy in the management of diverse ocular tumors such as RB, UM, and VRL. Integrating noninvasive techniques such as LB into routine clinical practice has the potential to improve patient outcomes significantly, fostering a more precise and effective approach to ocular health care.
