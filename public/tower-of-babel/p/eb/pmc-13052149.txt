Artificial Intelligence-Driven Segmentation of Three Oral Diseases: Enabling Precision Diagnosis and Decision Support
Authors: Baowen Cheng, Meijuan Xiang, Zhuofan Zhao, Xiaodong Zhang, Yu-feng Wang, Shuyun Ge, Fang Zhao, Meng Li, Haiyan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe advancement of AI-assisted oral disease diagnosis critically relies on the availability of annotated, high-quality datasets and resilient deep learning architectures. Nevertheless, progress in this domain has been impeded by the scarcity of publicly accessible data and inadequate benchmarking of existing models. To address these limitations, we introduced a meticulously curated dataset encompassing three prevalent oral mucosal diseases and undertook a thorough assessment of five deep learning models, focusing on their performance in concurrent lesion segmentation and classification.MethodsThis study involved the compilation of a dataset comprising 808 high-resolution clinical images, encompassing three distinct oral mucosal pathologies: oral lichen planus, oral leukoplakia, and oral benign ulcers. Data collections were facilitated through clinical practices, and the images were subsequently annotated by three oral medicine specialists, who provided both diagnostic labels and pixel-level segmentation masks. To ascertain the dataset's utility and efficacy, five sophisticated deep learning architectures were benchmarked for their capacity to perform simultaneous segmentation and classification tasks.ResultsOur benchmark evaluation indicated robust model performance on the proposed dataset. For the task of lesion segmentation, the optimal model achieved a mean Dice coefficient of 0.768 ± 0.031. Concurrently, an accuracy of 0.940 ± 0.013 was attained for disease classification. These findings collectively affirmed the dataset's substantial suitability for the development and rigorous assessment of AI models within authentic diagnostic contexts.ConclusionThe proposed dataset presented a standardised benchmark to facilitate the advancement of AI development in dental applications. Combined with rigorous deep learning validation, we provided a reproducible framework for precise lesion localisation and accurate disease identification.Clinical RelevanceThe proposed integrated dataset-model pipeline facilitates the development of dependable AI tools capable of aiding clinicians in prompt diagnosis, mitigating diagnostic inconsistencies, and augmenting decision-making processes within standard oral health care practices.

Introduction

The Global Oral Health Status Report highlighted a substantial worldwide health challenge: approximately 3.5 billion individuals, constituting nearly half of the global population, were affected by oral diseases,1 where timely detection was paramount for efficacious therapeutic outcomes. Alarmingly, evolving lifestyle dynamics have contributed to a disconcerting epidemiological shift, manifesting in the increasingly premature onset of oral pathologies.2 Current diagnostic methodologies are encumbered by their dependence on manual clinical evaluation, a time-consuming procedure that engenders substantial impediments within health care provision. Given the restricted patient throughput achievable by dental practitioners, a considerable number of cases remained unaddressed owing to deferred or inaccurate diagnoses. This intrinsic systemic constraint accentuated an urgent demand for innovative interventions: the establishment of precise, scalable automated diagnostic systems constituted not merely an avenue for technological advancement but an imperative for global public health to ameliorate this diagnostic deficit and foster equitable access to prompt oral health care.

Deep learning has witnessed remarkable progress, profoundly enhancing the diagnostic capacities of AI-based systems and thereby facilitating expedited and more accurate disease detection in medicine.3, 4, 5, 6 Within the field of dentistry, AI-driven automated diagnosis processes were predominantly centred on two principal tasks: disease classification7 and lesion localisation.8 The first involved identifying the type of oral diseases, while the second entailed segmenting affected regions in medical images. The second task was inherently more intricate and challenging in its implementation relative to disease classification. Recent advancements in deep learning have resulted in the proliferation of highly effective semantic segmentation models, including U-Net,9 Deeplabv3+10 and SegNet,11 which have demonstrated robust performance across various domains. Nevertheless, the operational efficacy of these AI models was critically contingent upon the quality, diversity, and scale of the input data.12 Although numerous public datasets have been curated to facilitate AI research in dentistry, encompassing conditions such as dental caries,13 periodontal disease,14 and oral lesions,15, 16, 17 they often present limitations such as limited disease coverage, inadequate sample sizes, or dependence on non-standard imaging modalities (eg, spectral imaging), thereby constraining their broader utility in routine clinical practice.

Here, we introduced a high-quality dataset of oral diseases, including three types of oral mucosal lesions: oral lichen planus (OLP), oral leukoplakia (OLK) and oral benign ulcers (OBU), which comprised aphthous and traumatic ulcers. OLP is recognised as a potentially malignant disorder, with a reported malignant transformation risk of 1.2%.18 OLK, an established precancerous lesion, exhibits a malignant transformation rate ranging from 1.1% and 40.8%.19 OBU is not considered precancerous and typically does not pose a risk of malignant transformation.20 Accurate diagnosis of these conditions is critical for effective clinical management, cancer risk monitoring, and improving patient quality of life. Each image in the dataset was reviewed and labelled by three experienced oral medicine specialists, providing two labels: disease type and pixel-wise lesion annotation. To advance AI-based methods for oral disease detection, five state-of-the-art (SOTA) segmentation models—U-Net,9 Deeplabv3+,10 SegNet,11 FCN,21 Attention U-Net22—were benchmarked for pixel-wise segmentation. This work aims to provide a standardised, real-world-ready resource to support the development and validation of robust AI systems for integrated classification and localisation in oral health care.

Methods

Dataset establishment

A total of 808 images were collected from 350 patients (Figure 1A, age range: 4 months-79 years; male-to-female ratio: 7:11; all of Chinese ethnicity) over an 11-year period (2005-2025) at the Shanghai Municipal Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine Affiliated Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine and Shanghai Ninth People's Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. Patients had received clinical diagnoses of either OLP, OLK or OBU, which were subsequently confirmed via comprehensive medical examinations, including biopsies. Photographic documentation was performed using a Canon EOS 7D Mark II with EF-S 15-85MM F/3.5-5.6 or Honor Magic 6 Pro smartphone during standard clinical appointments, yielding 1 to 6 images per patient based on the distribution of lesions (Figure 2A). Image annotation

Annotations were performed by three board-certified oral medicine specialists utilising LabelMe v5.2.123 (Figure 1B). A semi-automated, AI-assisted active learning methodology was implemented, commencing with manual segmentations that trained a U-Net model. This model subsequently generated preliminary masks for images in the dataset, which were then reviewed and refined by the specialists. Discrepancies were resolved through a consensus voting mechanism among all three specialists. Each image received dual labels: disease category and pixel-level lesion segmentation. The final annotations, comprising a disease category label and a pixel-level segmentation mask, underwent validation via this iterative, quality-controlled process (Figure 1C), as further depicted in Figure 2B. The annotation process involved two iterative rounds (Figure 3), employing 385 and 604 images for training, respectively. Of the total annotated images, 468 images were fully manually annotated, while 340 images were generated via AI-assisted segmentation followed by specialist review, thereby demonstrating a significant enhancement in annotation efficiency attributed to AI integration.Fig. 3Detailed annotation iterative process. Models

The primary evaluation criteria for a dataset designed for developing and deploying deep learning algorithms derive from its versatility and efficacy across diverse algorithms. Therefore, to evaluate the proposed dataset, comprehensive experiments were performed utilising five SOTA deep learning models for image segmentation: U-Net, Deeplabv3+, SegNet, FCN, and Attention U-Net. These models were judiciously chosen to exemplify a variety of network design principles, including encoder-decoder architectures, Atrous Spatial Pyramid Pooling, and attention mechanisms. The detailed model architectures are provided in Table S1 of Appendix A.

Data preprocessing and argumentation

Given the high native resolution of the clinical images (3008 × 2000 pixels), which poses computational inefficiencies for deep learning training, all inputs were uniformly resized to 640 × 448 pixels. Pixel intensities were subsequently normalised to the range [0, 1] via division by 255, in accordance with standard preprocessing practices for convolutional neural networks. No device-specific normalisation or additional preprocessing was applied to images captured by different devices. For data argumentation, we employed three strategies: sliding window cropping, rotation, and brightness adjustment. Specifically, in the sliding window approach, fixed-size windows with a consistent stride were slid across the original image to extract subimages. For each sub-image, we computed the proportion of lesion pixels and retained only those exceeding a predefined threshold to avoid introducing excessive background-dominated samples. The thresholds were set per disease type: 0.5 for OLP and OLK, and 0.09 for OBU. For rotation augmentation, each image was randomly assigned one of three options: 45° rotation, 135° rotation, or no rotation according to a uniform probability. Similarly, brightness augmentation was applied with equal random probability among three choices: darken, brighten, or no change. All augmentation operations were implemented using OpenCV.

Training details

All experiments were conducted using PyTorch 2.5.2 (with CUDA 11.8 support) on a computing platform equipped with NVIDIA A100 GPUs and AMD EPYC 7763 64-core CPUs.

The model training process involved a two-stage approach designed to optimise both segmentation and classification performance (Figure 4). First, a multimask output training strategy was implemented, where the number of output masks corresponded directly to the number of disease categories (OLP, OLK and OBU). This methodology facilitated the simultaneous execution of lesion segmentation and disease classification, given that each mask was specifically engineered to delineate pathological regions associated with its respective disease class. Subsequently, the dataset was randomly divided into training and validation sets at an 8:2 ratio at the image level. This division maintained the original class distribution through stratified sampling, thereby ensuring an impartial evaluation process.Fig. 4Model training and validation procedure.Fig 4 dummy alt text

Model training and validation procedure.

Models were trained for 1000 epochs with a batch size of 80, using the Adam optimiser (initial lr=0.002 with cosine annealing decay) (detailed in Appendix B). Our composite loss function integrated a novel BCE-log (Jaccard) term for segmentation accuracy, which effectively balanced pixel-wise precision with regional overlap,24 alongside a standard cross-entropy term for classification performance. To accelerate training speed while preserving stability, mixed-precision (FP16) arithmetic combined with gradient clipping (max norm = 1.0) was employed. This comprehensive strategy achieved optimal convergence and efficiently leveraged NVIDIA A100 GPU resources, as evidenced by the consistent improvement in validation metrics across all evaluation epochs. To ensure statistical robustness and assess sensitivity to initialisation, we conducted 10 independent training runs, each with a distinct random seed (seeds 1 through 10).

Results

Dataset of three oral diseases

The proposed dataset25 includes 808 clinical images from 350 patients, comprising 293 cases of OLP, 288 of OLK, and 227 of OBU. Patient ages ranged from 4 months to 79 years, with a male-to-female ratio of 7:11. These three groups were well-balanced in sample size but exhibited distinct characteristics regarding lesion morphology and anatomical distribution. The target-region ratios were 8.0% for OLP, 7.8% for OLK, and 1.1% for OBU. This result indicated that OBU lesions consistently demonstrated a significantly smaller lesion-to-image ratio compared to OLP and OLK, a characteristic consistent with their typically smaller and more focal morphology. Each patient within the dataset received a diagnosis of only one of the three specified conditions and presented with 1 to 6 affected lesion sites. Analysis of classification results

The classification results in Table 4 reveal not only high accuracy across all models (>92.6%) but also the limited impact of architectural variations on diagnostic discrimination among visually similar oral lesions. The narrow performance range (overall accuracy: 0.926-0.94; per-class accuracy: 0.911-0.944) suggests that the dataset’s expert annotations and standardised imaging provide sufficient discriminative signal for diverse architectures to learn disease-specific features effectively. U-Net achieved the highest overall accuracy (0.940 ± 0.013), with consistent performance across OLP (0.931), OLK (0.934), and OBU (0.944). However, the differences between models were marginal. FCN, despite its simplicity, reached 0.928 overall accuracy, and DeepLabv3+ performed comparably on OBU (0.943). These results imply that, for oral mucosal lesion classification, data quality and clear diagnostic boundaries may matter more than architectural novelty: even classical models like FCN achieve clinically relevant accuracy when trained on a well-annotated, multiclass dataset. The primary challenge appears to stem not from interclass confusion, but from inherent intraclass morphological variability.

Discussion

A comprehensive evaluation of five state-of-the-art deep learning models on our expert-annotated dataset demonstrates that high-quality clinical data can effectively support both accurate lesion segmentation and robust disease classification, which are core capabilities for AI-assisted oral diagnosis.26 The consistently strong performance across diverse architectures not only reflects high diagnostic precision but also highlights practical advantages over prior oral AI systems, which often depend on controlled imaging or specialised equipment. Critically, our models achieve this under real-world clinical constraints: images were acquired using digital cameras or smartphones in routine outpatient settings, without colour calibration or complex preprocessing. Notably, our results are comparable to or exceed those reported in existing oral AI research.13,15, 16, 17 In cases of similar performance, our work offers distinct advantages in dataset scale and disease coverage, encompassing three clinically relevant conditions, where two disorders may exhibit subtypes with differing malignant potentials.

In this context, AI tools can play complementary roles across the diagnostic pathway. In primary or outpatient settings, they can serve as efficient screening assistants by rapidly analysing clinical photographs to flag suspicious lesions for specialist review. At advanced care stages, they function as decision-support systems that aid in differential diagnosis and support risk stratification through quantitative analysis of lesion characteristics. The core clinical value of our approach thus lies in improving early detection, reducing diagnostic ambiguity, and enabling objective, image-based risk assessment, all of which are made possible by the tight coupling of precise spatial delineation and categorical classification.

This synergy is further reflected in task-dependent model performance: SegNet achieved the best segmentation results, likely due to its index-based pooling and unpooling mechanism, which preserves fine boundary details essential for capturing irregular OLP borders or speckled OLK margins. In contrast, U-Net demonstrated the highest classification accuracy, attributed to its effective integration of multiscale contextual information through skip connections. This divergence underscores a broader principle in medical AI: success depends less on architectural novelty and more on alignment with specific clinical tasks and diagnostic logic, a perspective increasingly emphasised in the medical imaging literature.27, 28, 29 From a clinical perspective, robust model performance enables reliable early lesion detection with minimal false positives or missed diagnoses. The close match between predicted and ground-truth masks confirms spatial fidelity, supporting applications in monitoring and treatment planning.

Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations. First, the dataset is retrospective and drawn from two hospitals in Shanghai over a 20-year period, which may limit its generalizability to other populations, ethnicities, or imaging protocols. Second, although all annotations were established through consensus among three board-certified oral medicine specialists, interobserver agreement was not formally quantified using standardised reliability metrics. Third, the analysis was conducted at the disease level, without further stratification into clinically relevant subtypes such as erosive vs reticular OLP or homogeneous vs nonhomogeneous OLK, which are known to differ in malignant potential. This subtype-level granularity was beyond the scope of the current work but represents an essential direction for future research aimed at risk-stratified diagnosis and prognosis.

Future work will focus on prospective validation, multi-centre collaboration using heterogeneous imaging devices, reader studies to assess diagnostic impact in real-world clinical settings, and the integration of clinical subtype stratification into both annotation protocols and model design. Linking imaging phenotypes to longitudinal patient outcomes will further enable data-driven malignancy risk prediction, transforming our framework from a diagnostic aid into a tool for precision monitoring and early intervention, which are essential steps toward trustworthy, real-world deployment.

In summary, our standardised, clinically grounded dataset bridges AI research and oral health care by enabling simultaneous lesion localisation and classification. It addresses the critical shortage of benchmark resources in dental AI30 and aligns with community calls for rigorously curated, transparent, and ethically governed datasets.31,32 By embodying the International Dental Federation’s principles for AI adoption, this work represents a foundational step toward trustworthy, generalizable, and human-centred AI in oral medicine.

Conclusion

This study presented a high-quality, clinically annotated dataset enabling precise segmentation and classification of three oral mucosal diseases. Exhibiting strong model performance and practical applicability, it functioned as a robust benchmark for advancing AI tools that promote early, equitable, and accurate dental diagnostics. The dataset fulfilled a pressing gap in dental AI by supplying extensive data on diverse oral health conditions, thereby supporting research in domains such as 3D reconstruction, image translation, and segmentation.
