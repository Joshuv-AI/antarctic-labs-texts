Utility of a coronary artery disease polygenic risk score in a low-risk primary prevention cohort
Authors: John H Bertot, Giorgio EM Melloni, James Pirruccello, Frederick K Kamanu, Yi-Pin Lai, Patrick T Ellinor, Marc S Sabatine, Christian T Ruff, Nicholas A Marston
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo evaluate whether a coronary artery disease (CAD) polygenic risk score (PRS) identifies clinically low-risk individuals at elevated atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk.MethodsWe conducted a longitudinal cohort study within the UK Biobank including adults aged 40–74 years, free of ASCVD and diabetes, not on lipid-lowering therapy, and with baseline LDL <190 mg/dL. Participants were enrolled from 2006 to 2010 and followed through 2021. Ten-year ASCVD risk was estimated using pooled cohort equations. A CAD PRS derived from 2.3 million genome-wide variants was categorized as top 1%, 5%, 20%, 50%, and bottom 50%. Lipoprotein(a) and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein were similarly stratified. The primary endpoint was a composite of myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, or cardiovascular death. Cox proportional hazards models estimated adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) for 10-year ASCVD events, and net reclassification improvement (NRI) was assessed at 5% and 7.5% risk thresholds.ResultsAmong 266,917 low-risk individuals (median age 54; 38% male), ASCVD risk increased substantially across higher PRS strata. Compared with the bottom 50%, individuals in the top 50% had more than double the risk (aHR 2.48), the top 20% had over triple the risk (aHR 3.71), and the top 5% and 1% had 6-fold (aHR 6.31) and 9-fold (aHR 9.73) higher risks, respectively (all p < 0.0001). Adding PRS improved model discrimination (AUC 0.76 vs 0.72) and reclassified 12% of low-risk individuals upward—over tenfold more than Lp(a) or hs-CRP.ConclusionIn primary prevention, a CAD PRS identifies individuals with markedly higher ASCVD risk despite low clinical risk estimates and may merit consideration among ASCVD risk enhancers.Keywords: ASCVD, CAD, Polygenic Risk Score, Primary Prevention, UK Biobank

Lay Summary

This study of 266,917 UK Biobank participants not on lipid lowering therapy without a history of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease demonstrates that incorporating a genetic risk score for coronary artery disease can reveal substantial, otherwise unrecognized cardiovascular risk in individuals who appear low-risk by traditional clinical assessment with pooled cohort equations. Specifically, participants with high genetic risk scores had two- to nine-times higher chances of heart attack or stroke compared with those with low genetic risk, even when their clinical risk appeared low. Moreover, the addition of genetic risk via coronary artery disease polygenic risk score identified far more individuals who might benefit from preventive therapy than other widely available biomarkers such as inflammation (hs-CRP) or lipoprotein(a).

Introduction

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) accounts for 19 million deaths globally each year [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. A recent analysis demonstrated that approximately one half of individuals diagnosed with premature coronary artery disease (CAD) did not meet guideline recommendations for a preventive statin prior to their first event [3]. Previous studies, including data from the YOUNG-MI registry, also found that a majority of adults presenting with premature myocardial infarction (MI) would not have met guideline-based treatment thresholds for prior statin initiation [4,5] These data highlight the deficiencies of current prevention strategies and the need for novel methods of identifying those at greatest risk for ASCVD.

Clinical practice guidelines previously did not carry a recommendation for statin initiation in low ASCVD risk individuals [6]. Updates to the most recent guidelines now provide a class IIa recommendation for statin initiation in adults at borderline risk (3% to <5% 10-year ASCVD risk by PREVENT equations) and a Class I recommendation for those at intermediate risk (5% to <10%), with treatment decisions guided by shared decision-making and individual risk profile. Individuals with a low ASCVD risk score (<3% 10-year ASCVD risk) do not carry a guideline recommendation for statin initiation in the absence of markedly elevated LDL-C (>160–189 mg/dL) or ≥10% 30 year PREVENT ASCVD risk estimate[7] Polygenic risk scores (PRS) can offer additional insight into an individual’s ASCVD risk that is not captured by conventional risk estimates or risk enhancers and are now recognized as risk enhancing factors though remain without a class or recommendation or level of evidence [7]. Prior works have demonstrated that incorporation of a CAD PRS to the AHA/ACC ASCVD PCE risk calculator significantly improves prediction of cardiovascular events independent of conventional clinical risk factors, especially among those with borderline or intermediate- ASCVD risk [8,9]. However, the utility of the PRS and other risk enhancing factors amongst clinically low risk individuals requires further investigation. In this analysis, we sought to evaluate whether a CAD PRS can identify low-risk individuals with higher than anticipated ASCVD risk in which statin therapy should be considered and compare its utility to well-established, guideline-acknowledged risk enhancers hs-CRP and Lp(a).

Methods

Study design and population

We performed a longitudinal cohort study utilizing the UK Biobank that included participants aged 40–74 years old without ASCVD or diabetes not receiving lipid lowering therapies and with a baseline LDL cholesterol (LDL-C) under 190 mg/dL. Enrollment was from January 1, 2006, to June 1, 2010, with follow up until March 2021. ASCVD was defined as: MI, stroke, or CV death as determined by hospital admission records, primary care physician documents and death certificates. Median follow-up ± IQR was 11.2 years (10.5 – 11.9)

End points and follow-up

The study end point was an ASCVD composite, defined as myocardial infarction (MI), ischemic stroke, or CV death in accordance with the most recent guideline statement [10]. To determine each diagnosis, we utilized the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision (ICD-10), code I21 and its subcodes I210-I214 and I219 to define MI, codes I62 and I63 to define ischemic stroke, and CV death was defined as any death with ICD-10 code I00 to I99. Participants were followed for 10 years to compare with the predicted risk from the ASCVD PCE risk score.

ASCVD risk score

All participants with available baseline data in the UK Biobank had a 10-year ASCVD risk calculated using the PCEs [11]. This risk score incorporates age, sex, race, smoking status, history of diabetes, total cholesterol, low density lipoprotein (LDL), high density lipoprotein (HDL), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and use of antihypertensive therapies and aspirin. Recalibration was adopted to match UK Biobank disease burden by sex and racial composition. Participants with an estimated 10-year ASCVD risk <5% were deemed clinically low-risk and included in this analysis.

Genetic risk score

The CAD PRS is a LDpred-based, multi ancestry, 2.3 million genome-wide single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) derived from the summary statistics of the Coronary Artery Disease Genome-Wide Replication and Meta-analysis in addition to the Coronary Artery Disease Genetics Consortium [12]. Each individual SNP dosage was multiplied by the PRS weights and summed overall to generate a raw score. We derived genetic ancestry from the similarity of the UKBB principal components (PCs) to the participants of the 1000 genome project v3 (1KG). The PRS was standardized in each of the 5 main 1KG populations and restandardized overall, and participants were then analyzed into genetic risk categories by percentile: bottom 50%, top 50%, top 20%, top 5%, and top 1%. Supplemental Methods 1). We performed a separate sensitivity analysis utilizing an earlier 6 million SNP PRS derived without participants from the UK Biobank [13].

Biomarkers

In addition to the clinical parameters needed to calculate PCE ASCVD risk estimates, we utilized baseline serum Lp(a) (Randox Bioscience, UK on an AU5800 platform) and hs-CRP (measured by immunoturbidimetric high-sensitivity analysis on a Beckman Coulter AU5800) data for participants [14]. The distribution of values across both biomarker assays was ascertained and participants were classified as having Lp(a) and hs-CRP in the top 50%, top 20%, top 5% and top 1%. (Supplemental Methods 2)

Statistical analysis

10-year ASCVD PCE risk score was used to classify participants as clinically low-risk. The low-risk group was stratified by genetic risk to determine whether the CAD PRS could identify clusters of participants with an estimated 10-year ASCVD risk higher than expected. We identified individuals in the following PRS categories: top 50%, 20%, 5%, and 1% PRS categories within this low-risk cohort who had an observed 10-year risk that would result in different recommendations for statin initiation based on AHA/ACC Guidelines. Splines were derived from a Cox proportional hazard model using a natural spline smoother with 3 knots at quantiles 0.1, 0.5 and 0.9. A reclassification analysis was performed, and the net reclassification improvement (NRI) index was calculated in all participants. (Supplemental Methods 3)

Furthermore, we utilized a Cox proportional hazards model to calculate hazard ratios (HR) for ASCVD events as a function of CAD PRS percentiles. The HR was reported by percentile of PRS distribution utilizing the bottom 50% as the referent group. The models were adjusted for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, SBP, LDL-c, HDL-c, genetic ancestry (by principal components 1–5), and type of array used for genotyping. This model satisfied the proportional hazards assumptions for the variable of interest (standardized PRS and PRS divided into percentile).

A sensitivity analysis was also performed utilizing non-overlapping PRS strata with the bottom 50%, 50–80%, 80–90% and top 10% groups. ROC curves and AUC values were derived at 10 years to assess the relative utility of the CAD PRS added to a base survival model that included PCE predicted risk. Brier Score contrasts were applied to derive p values. Using the same models, the Net Reclassification Index (NRI) was calculated using two clinically meaningful cutoffs, 5% and 7.5% ASCVD risk at 10 years. The percentage of correctly up classified individuals, those that are correctly reclassified to a risk over 5% or 7.5% (NRI+), and incorrectly up classified individuals, those that incorrectly reclassified upwards (NRI-) are reported alongside. NRI was also stratified by PCE groups 0–1%, 1–2%, 2–3%, 3–4%, 4–5% and the number needed to genotype (NNG) to reclassify individuals to a higher risk category was calculated as 1/(NRI over 5%) of a model containing CAD PRS and PCE vs PCE alone. A NNG sensitivity analysis was performed using the PREVENT equation and PREVENT risk groups of 0–1%, 1–2% and 2–3%. Splines were replicated using the PREVENT equation and ASCVD risk estimates. P values are 2-sided with a .05 cutoff for significance. Statistical analyses were conducted using R, version 4.4.2 (R Project for Statistical Computing) and the preventr R package.

Results

A total of 266,917 participants had a 10-year ASCVD PCE Risk Score <5%, consistent with low-clinical risk. Compared with participants in the bottom 50% CAD PRS, participants in the top 20%, 5% and 1% of CAD PRS had increasingly higher proportions of hypertension and levels of LDL-c, Lp(a), and hs-CRP. Despite these differences, the mean PCE predicted risk (SD) was not meaningfully different across increasing CAD PRS categories, ranging from 1.7% (± 1.3) for the bottom 50% up to 1.8% (± 1.3) at the top 1%. CAD PRS and ASCVD event rate

Whereas individuals in the bottom 50% of PRS had a 0.9% incidence of ASCVD events over 10 years, individuals in the top 50% had a more than 2-fold increased risk of an ASCVD events at 2.2% (adjusted HR (aHR) of 2.48 (95% CI, 2.31–2.65)). Those in the top 20% of CAD PRS had a cumulative event rate of 3.2% representing a nearly 4-fold increased risk (aHR of 3.71, 95% CI, 3.43–4.00), and those in the top 5% and top 1% CAD PRS had more than 6-fold (aHRs of 6.31, 95% CI, 5.71–6.96) and nearly 10-fold increased risk (aHR 9.73, 95% CI, 8.33–11.36), respectively with absolute event rates of 5.2% and 7.2% at 10 years (Fig. 1A/1B).

Integration of CAD PRS with ASCVD risk score

The ASCVD risk for each CAD PRS risk category was assessed as a function of the predicted 10-year ASCVD Risk by PCE. (Fig. 2A) When CAD PRS stratification was introduced, a significant gradient of risk was identified across the entire range of the low-ASCVD risk (0–5%). Most notably, the gradient of risk was such that each increase in CAD PRS category resulted in a lower ASCVD risk threshold needed to cross into a higher risk category of borderline (5%−7.5%) or intermediate risk (>7.5%). For participants in the top 50% of CAD PRS, this occurred when the PCE predicted risk was 3.75% or higher. In the top 20% of CAD PRS, this threshold was crossed when the PCE risk was approximately 2.5% or above. This trend continued for the top 5% and top 1% of CAD PRS, with individuals crossing over into borderline risk categories when the PCE predicted risk was approximately 1.7% and 1.2%, respectively. Furthermore, the top 20%, 5%, and 1% of CAD PRS crossed the intermediate risk threshold when PCE predicted risk was approximately 4%, 2.5%, and 2%, respectively. (Fig. 2A) As a result, CAD PRS reclassified 50% of low-risk individuals when the 10-year risk was 4–5%, 20% of patients when 10-year risk was 3–4%, and 5% of patients when risk was 2–3%. (Supplemental Table S3) CAD PRS reclassified less than 1% of patients with a very low ASCVD risk (0–2%). A similar gradient of risk reclassification was observed in the sensitivity analysis of non-overlapping PRS percentile strata and demonstrated with an earlier PRS developed without those individuals from the UK Biobank (Supplemental Figure S2 and Supplemental Figure S3). Further, in a sensitivity analysis utilizing the 10-year PREVENT ASCVD risk estimator with low, borderline, intermediate and high-risk thresholds defined by the most recent guidelines (Supplemental Figure S4) a similar gradient of risk reclassification was observed.

The utility of the CAD PRS was then evaluated in terms of prediction accuracy using ROC curves and AUC values at 10 years and a formal net reclassification analysis was conducted to measure the percentage of individuals correctly and incorrectly up classified to risk levels carrying statin recommendation (5% and 7.5% risk thresholds). The addition of the CAD PRS as a continuous variable increased the AUC of the base model (PCE ASCVD) from 0.72 to 0.76 (p < 0.0001) (Table 2, Fig. 3) and exhibited improved performance compared to a CAD PRS only model (Supplemental Figure S5). The integration of the CAD PRS to the clinical base prediction model resulted in an NRI at 10 years over the 7.5% and 5% mark of 11% and 12% respectively, with minimal incorrect up classification (1–2% for both cutoffs, Table 2). The genetic risk threshold with the highest AUC (0.75) was obtained at the top 20% percentile, with 10% and 8% correctly reclassified over the 7.5% and 5% risk thresholds (Supplemental Table S4). Moreover, the NNG analysis utilizing patients from each PCE predicted low risk percentile category demonstrated increasing yield of genetic testing as subjects approached borderline risk category such that the NNG was 28 for individuals in the 1–2% PCE predicted risk category versus 4 for those in the 4–5% PCE risk category (Supplemental Figure S6). In a sensitivity analysis utilizing PREVENT predicted risk and low risk percentile categories, the NNG was 250 in the 0–1% predicted risk category, and decreased to 7 and 4 in the 1–2% and 2–3% predicted risk categories, respectively (Supplemental Figure S7).

Integration of hs-CRP and Lp(a) with ASCVD risk score

The same series of analyses were repeated with both hs-CRP and Lp(a). Using the same percentile cutoffs for hs-CRP, we observed event rates consistent with borderline risk classification (5–7.5%) for the top 50%, 20%, and 5% when PCE predicted risk was approximately 3.5% or above. The top 1% of hs-CRP crossed this threshold at a PCE risk closer to 3%, and was the only group to achieve risk levels in the intermediate range (≥7.5%), but only when PCE risk was at 5%. (Fig. 2B) The addition of hs-CRP to the base model increased the AUC of the base model by less than 0.1 (AUC 0.723 to 0.726 for hs-CRP). (Table 2, Fig. 3) The integration of hs-CRP to the clinical base prediction model resulted in an NRI over the 7.5% and 5% mark of 0.5% and 0.6% respectively. (Table 2) Model performance and NRI did not vary significantly with hs-CRP percentile category. (Supplemental Table S5)

In analysis of Lp(a), there was essentially no reclassification of low-risk patients with ASCVD PCE Risk Score <4%. (Fig. 2C) The addition of Lp(a) did not increase the AUC when added to the base model (AUC = 0.723 to 0.723 with Lp(a)). (Table 2, Fig. 3) The integration of Lp(a) to the clinical base prediction model resulted in an NRI over the 7.5% and 5% mark of 0.3% and 0.1% respectively. Discussion

In this analysis of a primary prevention cohort at clinically low risk of ASCVD, we observed three key findings. First, CAD PRS allows for significant risk stratification among individuals deemed to be at low clinical risk of ASCVD, such that a subgroup of these participants were reclassified into an ASCVD risk range in which statins would typically be considered. Second, CAD PRS can be integrated into the ASCVD PCE risk score with a much higher level of precision by using the exact 10-year risk estimate and more narrow CAD PRS categories. Third, the CAD PRS provides better risk prediction and reclassification in this low-risk cohort than the commonly used risk enhancers Lp(a) and hs-CRP.

Prior work by Aragam et al. demonstrated that ASCVD PCE risk estimation does not adequately distinguish between individuals at high and low polygenic risk, and that a high genetic risk score (i.e. top 20%) was associated with an approximately 2-fold increased odds for ASCVD compared to those with low genetic risk [15]. Work by Khera et al. has shown that high polygenic risk for CAD can confer marked ASCVD risk on par with monogenic risk seen in conditions such as familial hyperlipidemia (FH), especially at the extremes of CAD PRS [8,[15], [16], [17], [18]]. Our findings build on these prior studies, as we observed that the PCE ASCVD risk score was nearly identical across PRS percentiles. These findings suggest that the CAD PRS captures risk independent of the PCE and PREVENT risk estimates.

Our study also extends these CAD PRS observations to the lowest risk cohort based on conventional PCE estimated risk, a group that is not considered for preventive statin therapy. Current guidelines recommend statin initiation to treat dyslipidemia among patients with familial hyperlipidemia (FH), irrespective of ASCVD risk score [19,20], a recommendation based on the significantly elevated risk for those with FH compared to non-FH counterparts [[21], [22], [23]]. Based on the mounting data demonstrating the similar strength of risk prediction with CAD PRS compared to FH, strong consideration should be given to the contribution of PRS in ASCVD and efforts to mitigate this risk furthered.

Moreover, how best to integrate CAD PRS into clinical risk scores is another distinct aspect of this analysis. Prior studies, have used categorical binning for both ASCVD risk and CAD PRS [15,[24], [25], [26], [27], [28]]. Unique to our methodology is the utilization of an integrated 10-year ASCVD and CAD PRS risk model that allows for a higher level of precision by using the exact PCE predicted ASCVD risk on a continuous level and narrower CAD PRS categories. For instance, in our integrated model, an individual with a PCE predicted risk of 4% with a CAD PRS in the top 5% would expect an approximately 8% 10-year ASCVD event rate. As a result, this individual may be considered intermediate risk for ASCVD, thus potentially resulting in markedly different clinical decision making. Importantly, this precise, integrated approach also demonstrated significant utility in mutually exclusive PRS strata suggesting an increasing gradient of risk even below the most extreme PRS percentiles.

The yield of CAD PRS testing also increases the closer an individual’s PCE is to the 5% threshold as demonstrated in the NNG analysis with a NNG of 4 for individuals in the PCE predicted 4–5% risk range or PREVENT predicted 2–3% range. For those in the PCE predicted 3–4% group or PREVENT 1–2% risk, there was a NNG of 5 or 7, respectively, demonstrating the ability of CAD PRS testing to provide clinically meaningful upclassification in risk assessment. Therefore, one might consider CAD PRS testing if the PCE score is in the 3–5% range or for individuals in PREVENT 2–3% range, especially in the presence of other risk enhancing factors.

Furthermore, we noted significantly greater reclassification to higher ASCVD risk categories compared to conventional biomarkers Lp(a) and hs-CRP. When Lp(a) and hs-CRP are tested in this cohort it may impact the decision to pursue more aggressive primary prevention with statin initiation despite a lower ASCVD risk profile [29]. This strategy has been proven for hs-CRP in a clinical trial, and recent data suggest that a novel biomarker risk assessment approach measuring hs-CRP, Lp(a) and LDL cholesterol approximates long term cardiovascular risk with precision beyond conventional 10-year estimates [30,31]. Our findings add to the growing body of literature supporting the recognition of CAD PRS as a guideline acknowledged risk-enhancing factor.

The AHA recently published its first scientific statement on the utility of PRSs and concluded that these novel risk scores may be utilized in select clinical scenarios where they could potentially impact management [9]. The CAD PRS has been at the forefront of clinical application, and its role in guiding statin initiation in primary prevention is a frequently touted use case. Our work extends this potential application to low-risk individuals with improved precision. With prior evidence demonstrating enhanced predictive utility of PRS at younger ages, and now to a broader category of low risk patients, polygenic evaluation seems to have greatest utility earlier in the disease course [26]. Further, subgroup analyses of randomized trials have found that a high CAD PRS is also associated with greater benefit from lipid lowering therapy, both in primary and secondary populations [[32], [33], [34]]. Taken together, the CAD PRS meets the AHA Scientific Statement’s requirements for efficacy through its effective risk prediction, ability to guide management, and even predict therapeutic benefit from therapy.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, the UK Biobank cohort was included in the over 1-million participant CAD GWAS that developed the score which increases the risk of overfitting and overestimation of risk estimates. However, the subjects included in our analysis comprise approximately 2% of the total cases used and we performed a sensitivity analysis developed without these UK Biobank individuals which demonstrated continued predictive utility of genetic risk. Second, the analyses were primarily conducted based on the earlier PCEs from the 2018 AHA/ACC guidelines and have subsequently been replaced by the PREVENT risk estimators as per the most recent 2026 AHA/ACC Guidelines for the management of dyslipidemia. However, we conducted sensitivity analyses utilizing PREVENT risk estimation where appropriate with similar predictive utility demonstrated. Third, it is possible that individuals in whom a higher Lp(a) and hs-CRP are likely to impact ASCVD incidence may be excluded from our population since we identified only those participants at lowest clinical risk without established ASCVD. Lastly, given that the UK Biobank only included participants 40–74, we are unable to evaluate the CAD PRS performance in younger populations.

Conclusions

Among individuals at low risk for ASCVD, a CAD PRS identifies those at greater risk for cardiovascular events than predicted by traditional risk estimates and reclassifies a significant proportion of individuals to a level of ASCVD risk that warrants consideration for statin therapy.
