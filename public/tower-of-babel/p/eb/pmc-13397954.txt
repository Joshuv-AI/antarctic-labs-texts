Feasibility assessment of integrated oral health care pathways designed for pediatric and adult patients referred to School of Dentistry
Authors: Talieh Mahmoud Hashemi, Mohammad Reza Khami, Samaneh Razeghi, Reza Yazdani, Armin Shirvani, Mahdia Gholami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionTransitioning from a treatment-oriented model to a prevention-focused approach is essential for improving oral health outcomes. This study aimed to assess the feasibility and implementation outcomes of Integrated Oral Health Care Pathways (IOHCPs) for pediatric and adult patients referred to a dental school setting.MethodsA four-month mixed-methods feasibility study was conducted at the School of Dentistry, Tehran University of Medical Sciences. IOHCPs were implemented for pediatric and adult patients using risk-based preventive care, oral health education, and structured follow-up. Participants were allocated into three groups (control, routine care, and IOHCP intervention). The study followed the Donabedian framework (structure, process, and outcomes). Clinical outcomes (OHI-S, DMFT, and bleeding on probing [BOP]) and behavioral indicators were assessed. Process indicators were evaluated between routine care and IOHCP groups. Semi-structured interviews were conducted to assess structural feasibility. Statistical analyses included trend tests, chi-square tests, and independent t-tests, with a significance level of 0.05.ResultsA total of 780 patients (420 adults and 360 children) were included. OHI-S showed a significant improvement across groups in both adults (2.75 to 1.88) and children (2.29 to 1.25; p < 0.001). Behavioral indicators demonstrated favorable trends in the IOHCP group, including increased tooth brushing frequency and higher preventive care uptake (p < 0.001). Process indicators showed marked improvement in the delivery of preventive services in the IOHCP group compared to routine care (p < 0.001). DMFT increased in adults over time, reflecting cumulative disease experience, while no significant change was observed in children. Differences in BOP between routine care and IOHCP groups were not statistically significant.ConclusionThe implementation of Integrated Oral Health Care Pathways in a dental educational setting was feasible and acceptable. The pathways improved preventive care delivery processes and were associated with favorable oral hygiene behaviors. However, changes in clinical disease indicators were limited within the short study period. Further long-term studies are needed to evaluate sustainability and clinical impact.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08622-x.

Introduction

Oral health is an essential component of overall health and well-being, influencing nutrition, communication, and quality of life. Despite major advances in dental science, oral diseases such as dental caries and periodontal diseases remain among the most prevalent chronic conditions worldwide, largely due to their strong association with behavioral and lifestyle factors as well as limited access to preventive services [1, 2]. Traditionally, dental care systems have been predominantly treatment-oriented, focusing on managing established disease rather than preventing its occurrence. This approach often results in repeated restorative interventions, high treatment costs, and limited long-term improvement in population oral health. In contrast, contemporary health systems increasingly emphasize integrated, prevention-oriented models of care that prioritize early risk assessment, health education, and continuous preventive management [3, 4].

Integrated care pathways have been widely used in medicine to standardize care delivery, improve coordination among providers, and ensure evidence-based decision-making. These structured frameworks define the sequence and timing of interventions for specific patient groups, aiming to optimize outcomes and improve efficiency of care delivery. In dentistry, however, the application of structured care pathways remains limited, particularly in educational settings where preventive and therapeutic services are often delivered separately [5]. These pathways aim to define the most appropriate course of care for patients, including those with special needs, based on the best available evidence and expert consensus. They provide a standardized framework that enables timely clinical decision-making while ensuring consistency in documentation, monitoring, and continuous quality improvement. Although clinical care pathways have a longer history of application in medical settings, their demonstrated benefits in improving service delivery and patient experience have increasingly supported their adoption in dentistry. In dental care, pathways enhance transparency, guide clinicians through essential preventive and therapeutic steps, and align both patients and providers toward clearly defined outcomes [6, 7].

To address this gap, Integrated Oral Health Care Pathways (IOHCPs) were developed for pediatric and adult patients referred to the School of Dentistry, Tehran University of Medical Sciences. These pathways were designed as structured, evidence-based frameworks incorporating risk assessment, individualized oral health education, behavioral counseling, and professional preventive interventions. Unlike traditional treatment-based models, IOHCPs emphasize prevention and continuity of care and are implemented by dental students under the direct supervision of clinical faculty as part of routine clinical training [8]. Because the successful implementation of preventive principles requires early professional socialization, these pathways were designed to be delivered by dental students within clinical departments. Based on the faculty’s needs, resources, and organizational structure, four IOHCPs were developed for adults and one pathway for children aged six years and older. The pathways incorporated risk assessment, oral health education, self-care promotion, and professional preventive interventions. Their development was led by an interdisciplinary team and grounded in evidence-based guidelines. Detailed descriptions of the pathway design process have been published elsewhere [9, 10].

Given the importance of evaluating whether such a system-level intervention can be implemented in real clinical and educational settings, a pilot feasibility study was conducted. The evaluation was guided by the Donabedian framework, assessing structure, process, and outcomes of care [11]. Accordingly, the present study aimed to evaluate the feasibility and implementation outcomes of IOHCPs in a dental educational setting using a mixed-methods design.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a quasi-experimental controlled feasibility design with repeated cross-sectional sampling and a mixed-methods approach. Reporting was guided by the STROBE statement for observational components and best practice recommendations for mixed-methods research. The study was conducted over a four-month pilot period in 2021–2022 at the School of Dentistry, Tehran University of Medical Sciences (TUMS) [8].

Setting and development of Integrated Oral Health Care Pathways (IOHCPs)

The Integrated Oral Health Care Pathways (IOHCPs) were developed through an interdisciplinary process based on evidence-based guidelines and expert consensus. The pathways incorporated caries and periodontal disease risk assessment, individualized oral health education, behavioral counseling, and preventive clinical interventions.

Unlike conventional treatment-based dental care, IOHCPs structured preventive and educational interventions as mandatory components of routine care delivery. The pathways were embedded into clinical workflows and implemented as part of student clinical training under direct faculty supervision. The IOHCP included four core components: (1) risk assessment using standardized criteria, (2) individualized oral health education, (3) behavioral counseling, and (4) professional preventive interventions (e.g., fluoride application, scaling where needed) [8].

Participants and grouping

The study population included pediatric and adult patients referred to the School of Dentistry.

Participants were allocated into three non-random groups based on timing of exposure to IOHCP implementation:Group 1 (pre-intervention control): patients who had not yet received care under faculty servicesGroup 2 (routine care group): patients receiving standard dental care before IOHCP implementationGroup 3 (intervention group): patients receiving care after implementation of IOHCPs

Data collection procedure

Data was collected in repeated waves at two-week intervals.

For Groups 1 and 2 (pre-implementation phase), four rounds of simple random sampling were conducted. In each round, 35 adults and 30 children were selected per group. Overall, 140 adults and 120 children were included per group.

For Group 3 (post-implementation phase), data were collected using the same repeated cross-sectional sampling strategy over four waves, including 140 adults and 120 children.

This repeated sampling approach was used to reduce temporal bias and capture variability in clinical implementation.

Qualitative data analysis

All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s six-step framework.

Two researchers independently coded the transcripts. Initial codes were compared and refined through discussion. Themes were developed through iterative grouping of codes. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was achieved [20].

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed using SPSS version 24.

Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

Continuous variables were analyzed using one-way ANOVA.

Categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square tests.

Trend analysis across groups was performed using the Cochran–Armitage trend test.

Multivariable regression models were used to adjust for potential confounders including age, sex, and baseline oral health status.

A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics of participants were comparable across the three study groups. No statistically significant differences were observed in age or sex distribution between Group 1, Group 2, and Group 3 (p > 0.05), indicating acceptable baseline comparability and supporting internal validity of the comparisons (Table 1).

Clinical outcomes

Adults

A statistically significant trend was observed in Oral Hygiene Index–Simplified (OHI-S) across the three groups, with progressive improvement from Group 1 to Group 3. Mean OHI-S decreased from 2.75 ± 1.55 in Group 1 to 2.47 ± 1.56 in Group 2 and 1.88 ± 0.98 in Group 3 (p-trend = 0.002).

Bleeding on probing (BOP) showed a decreasing trend across groups; however, the difference between Group 2 and Group 3 was not statistically significant after intergroup comparison (p = 0.125).

For DMFT, an increasing trend was observed across groups (Group 1: 10.45 ± 4.47; Group 2: 11.09 ± 4.52; Group 3: 12.36 ± 4.15; p-trend = 0.007). This increase reflects the cumulative nature of the index rather than short-term change in disease occurrence (Table 2).

Children

In children, OHI-S demonstrated a significant improvement across the three groups, decreasing from 2.29 ± 1.04 in Group 1 to 1.84 ± 0.74 in Group 2 and 1.25 ± 0.72 in Group 3 (p-trend = 0.002).

DMFT did not show a statistically significant difference across groups (p = 0.58), indicating no measurable short-term change in caries experience during the study period (Table 2).

Behavioral outcomes

Adults

Behavioral outcomes showed significant positive trends across the three groups.

The proportion of participants reporting tooth brushing at least twice daily increased from 15.5% in Group 1 to 15.9% in Group 2 and 30.1% in Group 3 (p-trend < 0.001).

Regular flossing increased in Group 3 compared with Groups 1 and 2 (p < 0.001). Frequency of sugary snack consumption showed a decreasing trend across groups (p < 0.001).

Dental check-up attendance increased from 0% in Groups 1 and 2 to 5.5% in Group 3 (p = 0.002). All participants in all groups reported use of fluoride toothpaste (Table 3).

Children

Similar behavioral improvements were observed in children.

The proportion brushing at least twice daily increased from 1.4% in Group 1 to 2.9% in Group 2 and 18.5% in Group 3 (p-trend = 0.003).

Flossing frequency improved significantly in Group 3 compared with earlier groups (p < 0.001). A reduction in frequent sugary snack consumption was also observed (p = 0.002).

Dental check-up attendance reached 100% in Group 3 (p < 0.001). All children used fluoride toothpaste across all groups (Table 4).

Process indicators

Implementation fidelity indicators demonstrated marked improvement following IOHCP introduction.

In adults, the proportion receiving oral hygiene instruction increased from 8.8% in Group 2 to 64.8% in Group 3 (p < 0.001). Similar improvements were observed for flossing instruction (2.7% vs. 61.6%), preventive counseling (8.8% vs. 64.8%), and scheduling of follow-up visits (0% vs. 64%) (Table 5).

Discussion

Comparison with previous studies

The findings showed a positive impact of the designed care pathways and related interventions on the oral health of pediatric and adult patients, both in terms of clinical (e.g. OHI-S) and behavioral indicators, which is consistent with evidence from previous studies that structural oral health education and preventive programs significantly improve oral and gingival health. For instance, a recent randomized controlled trial among elementary school students showed that three months after a theory-based oral health educational intervention, the proportion of those brushing at least twice daily and flossing frequency increased, gum bleeding decreased, and OHI-S and plaque indices improved significantly [21].

Likewise, community-based and school-based interventions have been reported to reduce dental plaque and improve gingival health in preadolescents. In a community trial in Tehran on 9-year-old children, when parents were involved in supervised tooth brushing and oral hygiene monitoring, the likelihood of achieving healthy gingiva significantly increased compared to control groups [22].

Our findings for behavioral change — increased tooth brushing and flossing, higher check-up attendance, reduced sugary snack consumption — also echo results of meta-analyses summarizing school-based oral health interventions in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Such interventions yield significant reductions in plaque scores and improvements in oral hygiene behaviors [23].

In contrast, clinical outcome measures showed more limited changes. While OHI-S improved significantly across groups, DMFT did not show reduction and instead demonstrated an increasing trend among adults. This finding should be interpreted in light of the cumulative nature of DMFT, which reflects lifetime disease experience rather than short-term changes. Therefore, meaningful changes in this index would require longer follow-up periods and sustained preventive exposure. Similarly, although bleeding on probing decreased across groups, the difference between the routine care and IOHCP groups was not statistically significant, suggesting that periodontal improvements may require longer intervention duration or more intensive behavioral reinforcement [24, 25].

Moreover — as in our context — many school- and community-based interventions successfully improved oral hygiene indices (e.g. OHI-S, plaque) and hygiene behaviors without substantial changes in caries levels in the short to medium term [26].

Overall, the pattern observed in our study — significant improvements in hygiene behavior and gingival health, but limited change in caries indices among children — is congruent with the broader literature. This suggests that while structured, evidence-based care pathways are effective in improving preventive behaviors and gingival health, reducing caries experience may require longer follow-up, repeated preventive measures (e.g. fluoride, sealants), and possibly broader community-level interventions [27].

The marked improvement in process indicators following implementation of the integrated oral health care pathways highlights the importance of structured care pathways in reshaping service delivery in dental educational settings. Preventive processes such as oral hygiene instruction, tooth brushing and flossing education, preventive counseling, and follow-up scheduling were substantially more frequent among patients exposed to the care pathways compared with those receiving routine care. These findings are in line with Harris et al., who reported that introducing care pathway commissioning in primary dental care led to measurable improvements in preventive practices and standardization of care processes rather than clinical outcomes alone [28]. Similarly, Alshammari et al. emphasized that successful integration of preventive care into routine dental practice depends on deliberate changes in clinical workflows, with patient education and preventive counseling positioned as core components of care delivery [29]. Collectively, these studies support the notion that systematic improvements in care processes are central to the effectiveness of prevention-oriented oral health interventions.

Strengths

Trend sampling at different intervals was the strength of our sampling. To evaluate process indicators, we did not simply check patients’ paper records but also asked patients through questionnaires to ensure that the process was being carried out for them. Such project that encompasses most preventive processes of the faculty was implemented for the first time. One of our strengths was having a control group. To assess the quality of care pathways, we considered three types of outcomes, including process, infrastructure, and outcome indicators, based on the Donabedian model, which is a health service evaluation model.

This pilot was conducted under the supervision of clinical professors, and having a supervisor to conduct the implementation was one of our strengths for evaluation. And for the first time, clinical departments were encouraged to collaborate on preventive measures in the faculty, which was a milestone in the integration of prevention and treatment and the beginning of extensive interdisciplinary cooperation in the faculty. According to the results of this project, the enthusiasm of patients for free preventive dental care can be a promising point for pursuing preventive treatments for society.

Limitation

Based on follow-ups conducted by the study implementation team, an older version of an electronic health record (EHR) system was found to exist at the dental school. However, reactivating and utilizing this system would have required substantial revision, updating, and the development of technological infrastructure. Despite repeated efforts by the research team to pursue this issue with the relevant authorities at the faculty, implementation was not feasible within the study period due to financial constraints and a shortage of specialized human resources. Consequently, despite the well-established benefits of electronic patient record systems in facilitating the implementation of care pathways and improving the efficiency of patient follow-up, the designed care pathways were ultimately implemented using paper-based records.

Accordingly, one of the major limitations of this study was the reliance on paper-based records instead of an electronic system, which made data recording and analysis more challenging. For example, maintaining all care pathway forms—each of which was re-evaluated at every follow-up visit and included repeated documentation of clinical indices—required substantial physical storage space and increased the risk of document loss. This limitation has also been identified in previous studies as a major barrier to the development and sustainability of care pathways. Prior research has emphasized the need to utilize electronic health technologies, such as online education platforms, electronic health records, and patient web portals, to facilitate the implementation of care pathways and improve continuity of care [30].

Furthermore, the insufficient time allocated in the dental student curriculum for the full implementation of preventive processes, which led to a decrease in motivation to provide this type of care in clinical departments, highlights the need for curriculum reforms, including the allocation of teaching topics or academic units, or grading for preventive activities.

Another major challenge in implementing the care pathways was the lack of an integrated prevention-and-treatment mindset among clinical faculty members and students. Some faculty members in restorative dentistry and periodontology departments declined to supervise the implementation of care pathways due to faculty shortages and heavy educational workloads. As a result, residents from the Oral Health Department were required to assume supervisory roles. This situation indicates that the implementation of care pathways and systemic changes within the dental school may face resistance from department heads and faculty members. Therefore, if these pathways are to be adopted as routine practice, support from higher-level authorities—such as the dean or the faculty educational council—is essential to formally mandate their implementation across departments.

Based on these findings, changes in faculty policies appear necessary to encourage clinical faculty to actively integrate preventive approaches into routine patient care. It is anticipated that increasing awareness among clinical faculty regarding the importance of integrating prevention and treatment may lead to greater motivation and collaboration across clinical departments. Additionally, the lack of student motivation and attention to preventive care during treatment represents an important issue that warrants further consideration. Findings from the semi-structured interviews with the interdisciplinary team suggest that enforcing preventive activities through academic grading and close faculty supervision may help address this challenge and gradually improve students’ attitudes toward prevention; however, further studies are needed to confirm this assumption.

It should also be noted that a more precise evaluation of clinical indices, such as DMFT, requires a longer follow-up period than the four-month pilot phase of this study. The limited duration of the project did not allow for longer-term implementation of care pathways. Similarly, due to the short duration of the pilot feasibility study, comprehensive patient follow-up and in-depth assessment of long-term implementation challenges were not possible. More detailed and robust evaluations would require studies with extended follow-up periods.

Conclusion

The findings indicate that implementing integrated oral-health–promotion care pathways in a dental educational setting (the School of Dentistry, TUMS) was feasible, effective, and acceptable to patients and, to some extent, faculty members. These pathways can serve as a practical model for strengthening prevention-oriented approaches, improving oral-health literacy, and enhancing the quality of care in other dental schools.
