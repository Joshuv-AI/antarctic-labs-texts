Comparative performance of large language models and Drugs.com versus Lexicomp for antiseizure medication drug–drug interactions: A cross-sectional study with iterative prompting analysis
Authors: Maazuddin Mohammed, Mohammed Amer Khan, Vaibhav Chaudhary, Hamzeh Alsaleh, Syeda Bushra Fatima, Biplab Pal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAntiseizure medications (ASMs) are frequently co-prescribed and are associated with a high risk of clinically significant drug–drug interactions (DDIs). Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used for clinical queries, yet their performance in detecting ASM-related DDIs compared with established drug interaction databases remains uncertain.MethodsA cross-sectional comparative study evaluated 186 ASM–comedication pairs (126 classified as major/moderate by Lexicomp) using ChatGPT-4, DeepSeek-V3, and Drugs.com, with Lexicomp as the reference standard. Interactions were assessed for presence, severity, mechanism, and management recommendations. Major and moderate interactions were classified as clinically relevant for performance analysis. A post hoc iterative prompting analysis was conducted exclusively on false positive cases to assess improvements in specificity. Performance metrics included sensitivity, specificity, predictive values, and overall accuracy. Two clinical pharmacists independently assessed outputs for accuracy, clarity, and completeness, with adjudication by a third pharmacist.ResultsDrugs.com demonstrated the best overall performance, with sensitivity 0.870, specificity 0.629, and accuracy 0.709. ChatGPT showed high sensitivity (0.842) but low specificity (0.358), reflecting frequent over prediction of interactions. Low specificity reflected frequent overclassification of non-interacting or minor pairs as clinically relevant, potentially leading to alert fatigue, unnecessary monitoring or therapeutic modification. DeepSeek achieved the highest sensitivity (0.877) but the lowest specificity (0.200) and the greatest number of false positives. Iterative prompting substantially improved specificity for ChatGPT (0.77) and DeepSeek (0.42), correcting many false positive classifications.ConclusionChatGPT and DeepSeek provided broad interaction overviews but demonstrated limited specificity under single-prompt conditions. Structured, evidence-constrained prompting improved specificity in false positive cases within this dataset. Drugs.com showed the most balanced performance compared with zero-shot LLM outputs. At present, LLMs may serve as supplementary explanatory tools, whereas structured drug interaction databases remain more reliable for primary DDI screening.

Introduction

Managing complex diseases often requires the simultaneous use of multiple medications, but this practice significantly increases the risk of pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic drug-drug interactions (DDIs).1 These interactions can lead to serious adverse events or drug toxicity, posing a substantial threat to patient health2 and are exacerbated by factors such as age, comorbidities, and metabolic or genetic variations.3 Many anti-seizure medications (ASMs) have a narrow therapeutic index and are either substrates, inducers, or inhibitors of key drug-metabolizing enzymes, including CYP and UGT pathways. As a result, clinically relevant interactions may lead to toxicity, therapeutic failure, or seizure breakthrough. Given the chronic use of ASMs and frequent polypharmacy in patients with epilepsy and comorbid conditions, accurate identification and classification of DDIs is essential for safe clinical management.4, 5

Using software such as Lexicomp is the most effective way to identify DDIs. This is crucial because the large number of interacting pairs makes it a challenging task for any pharmacist.1 Large-language models (LLMs), such as ChatGPT and DeepSeek, which were launched in 2022 by OpenAI and by a Chinese company in 2025, respectively, have shown significant promise as tools for improving healthcare delivery.6, 7, 8 Within the field of clinical pharmacy, the performance of LLMs has been partially explored by testing for identifying drug-herb interactions and performing a national pharmacist examination.3, 9 This study specifically evaluates ChatGPT-4, which is the leading, freely available LLM, DeepSeek-V3, which represents an emerging high-performance model, and Drugs.com, a free database with the highest accuracy rate for detecting DDIs among free resources.10 We utilized Lexicomp,11 a subscription-based DDI screening tool, as the reference standard because of its availability and superior accuracy scores compared with other available software.12

One limitation of LLMs is that they do not have access to private and paid databases or sources, and if they do, legal issues might arise, such as conflicts of interest, copyright, and data security.13 Additionally, the sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of these LLMs in identifying DDIs are still unclear, and there is considerable ambiguity regarding the capacity of these tools to predict or detect potential DDIs.13 Specifically, the sensitivity of a detection tool refers to its ability to correctly identify a true, clinically important interaction (i.e., minimizing false negatives), while specificity refers to its ability to correctly ignore the interaction pairs that are clinically unimportant (i.e., minimizing false positives).12 Low sensitivity may lead to missed clinically significant interactions and potential adverse outcomes, whereas low specificity may result in overestimation of interactions, causing unnecessary concern, monitoring, or changes in therapy. Although prior studies have evaluated LLM performance in predicting DDIs, limited comparative evidence exists specifically for antiseizure medication interactions using standardized severity classification and iterative prompting strategies. The primary objective of this study was to compare the performance of ChatGPT-4, DeepSeek-V3, and Drugs.com with Lexicomp in identifying clinically relevant DDIs for commonly prescribed ASMs. A secondary objective was to assess whether a structured, evidence-constrained iterative prompting strategy could reduce false positive classifications and improve specificity. We hypothesized that LLMs would demonstrate high sensitivity but lower specificity compared with structured databases, and that iterative prompting would improve specificity in false positive cases.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a comparative analysis to evaluate the effectiveness of ChatGPT-4, DeepSeek-V3, and Drugs.com in identifying, characterizing, and managing DDIs for commonly prescribed ASMs. The study employed a cross-sectional design with blinded evaluation of responses across all platforms. Outputs from ChatGPT, DeepSeek, and Drugs.com were extracted, deidentified, and assigned anonymous codes (A, B, C, D) before being evaluated by the clinical pharmacists. Raters were therefore blinded to the source of each response. Following the primary comparative analysis, we conducted an exploratory iterative prompting analysis to examine whether refined, specificity-focused prompting could improve AI model performance, particularly for reducing false positive (FP) classifications. Data collection procedure

ChatGPT-4 was accessed through the OpenAI web interface (chat.openai.com) using two free accounts, with the model label ‘ChatGPT-4’ as displayed at the time of data collection. DeepSeek-V3 was accessed via the official DeepSeek web interface. For both tools, default settings were used; no temperature or other hyperparameters were manually modified, and web-browsing or plug-ins were not enabled. ChatGPT version 4 was used, considering its higher sensitivity and specificity than ChatGPT version 3.5.13 Two free accounts were created on ChatGPT-4 to collect data. Since the free version of ChatGPT imposes a maximum question limit within a 24-h period, drug interaction screening was conducted between February 17 and March 16, 2025. DeepSeek was free to use with unlimited prompts; therefore, data from it were collected between May 12 and May 14, 2025. Although data collection occurred in separate calendar windows, all queries for that tool were completed in a continuous period using the same model version and interface, minimizing within-tool drift. However, potential temporal model updates cannot be fully excluded and are acknowledged as a limitation. A new conversation was initiated for each drug interaction query to avoid interference from previous interactions. Each drug interaction pair was investigated using a standard prompt, and responses were recorded verbatim for subsequent analysis. All prompt strings used for the primary and iterative querying process are provided in Appendix A, along with representative example outputs to facilitate reproducibility. The standard primary prompt was as follows:

“Find and explain the reasoning behind the drug interaction between drug A and drug B. If a clinically significant interaction is detected, indicate whether treatment should continue as is, continue with monitoring (specifying what to monitor), or be modified (provide details of modification), and classify the severity of the interaction. Provide a clear answer.”

Each ASM was paired with co-prescribed medications to form test cases, which were queried in ChatGPT, DeepSeek, Drugs.com, and Lexicomp. Information on the interaction, severity classification, and recommended management was recorded verbatim. In cases where ChatGPT produced a rating consisting of two categories (e.g., mild to moderate), a lower DDI rating was considered. This conservative approach of assigning the lower severity when a range was given was adopted to avoid overestimating the LLM's tendency to flag interactions as more severe. Additionally, for computing sensitivity, specificity, and related performance metrics, clinically relevant interactions were combined as major and moderate, whereas minor and no interaction were grouped as not clinically relevant, consistent with previous comparative evaluations of DDI tools.12, 19

Definition of clinically relevant drug interaction

For this analysis, DDI severity was defined strictly according to the categorical ratings provided by Lexicomp. For each ASM-comedication pair, Lexicomp was used as the reference standard to classify the interaction as major (including contraindicated), moderate, minor, or no interaction, based on its internal evaluation of clinical consequences and recommended management (e.g., need for therapy modification, close monitoring, or routine use without special precautions).11

To compare tools, we mapped their severity labels to Lexicomp's categories as follows: (1) Lexicomp and Drugs.com labels of ‘major/contraindicated’ were mapped to major, ‘moderate’ to moderate, ‘minor’ to minor, and ‘no interaction’ to no interaction; (2) LLM descriptions containing explicit terms such as ‘major’, or ‘contraindicated’ were coded as major, those describing ‘moderate’ or ‘clinically significant’ interactions without contraindication as moderate, and those labeled, ‘minor’ or ‘not clinically significant’ as minor and ‘no clinically significant interaction’ as no interaction, respectively.

Evaluation metrics

The true positive (TP), true negative (TN), false positive (FP), and false negative (FN) values of ChatGPT, DeepSeek, and Drugs.com were determined. A drug interaction classified as a major/moderate interaction in Lexicomp was considered a TP if it was also identified as a major/moderate interaction in the LLMs and Drugs.com and an FN if it was classified as a minor/no interaction in the LLMs and Drugs.com. Conversely, a minor interaction identified in Lexicomp was considered a TN if it was also classified as a minor/no interaction in the LLMs and Drugs.com and an FP if it was identified as a major/moderate interaction in the LLMs and Drugs.com.

Sensitivity was defined as the ability of the database to accurately identify major or moderate drug interactions, whereas specificity referred to its ability to disregard minor interactions. The positive predictive value (PPV) represented the likelihood that an interaction identified by the database was significant, whereas the negative predictive value (NPV) indicated the probability that interactions not identified by the database were insignificant. The sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV were calculated using the following formulas12, 19:Accuracy=TP+TN/TP+FP+TN+FNSensitivity=TP/TP+FNSpecificity=TN/FP+TNPPV=TP/TP+FPNPV=TN/TN+FN

95% confidence intervals for sensitivity and specificity were calculated using the Wilson score method for binomial proportions, as it is suitable for smaller proportions. Data from all three sources were carefully documented in a structured format, including the drug pair (ASM and co-prescribed drug), presence or absence of a DDI, severity classification (mild, moderate, severe), mechanism of interaction (if provided), and management recommendations. Two experienced clinical pharmacists, each with more than 5 years of experience, independently rated the responses from ChatGPT, DeepSeek, and Drugs.com for accuracy, clarity, and completeness, with Lexicomp used as the reference standard. Ratings were given on a 5-point Likert scale, where a score of 5 represented “completely correct” (entirely accurate, with no errors or omissions), and a score of 1 represented “completely incorrect” (entirely inaccurate, with no relevant or correct information). Intermediate scores (2–4) reflected increasing degrees of partial correctness, clarity, or completeness. The responses were blinded and assessed for (a) accuracy, (b) clarity, and (c) completeness of the mechanism of interaction and recommended management. Discrepancies of ≥1 point between raters or any disagreement on whether a response was clinically acceptable triggered adjudication by a third clinical pharmacist specialized in neurology, whose rating was considered final for that pair. In addition to matching Lexicomp, raters were instructed to consider the clinical appropriateness of the recommended management (e.g., monitoring vs dose adjustment) in their accuracy and completeness scores, using Lexicomp as the primary reference but allowing expert judgment when LLMs proposed reasonable alternative wording or equivalent strategies.

For the primary analysis, TN and FP were defined for all ASM-comedication pairs using Lexicomp as the reference standard, allowing computation of specificity and NPV. In the iterative prompting sub-analysis, only FP pairs were re-queried; therefore, negative instances were not systematically re-evaluated, and sensitivity and NPV were not recalculated for that subset.

Exploratory iterative prompting analysis

To address the high rate of false positives observed in the primary analysis, we conducted an exploratory post-hoc analysis. This analysis examined whether a three-step iterative prompting approach could improve model specificity.

Iterative prompting protocol

Each interaction pair identified as a false positive in the primary analysis was re-queried using a three-step iterative prompting sequence developed by prompt engineering methods described by Liu et al.20

Step 1: Specificity-focused prompt.

“You are a clinical pharmacist. For the drug pair [ASM name] and [comedication name], list only clinically relevant interaction that is supported by clinical data, established pharmacokinetic pathways (i.e., documented CYP enzyme induction/inhibition), or authoritative interaction databases (e.g., Lexicomp, Micromedex, FDA drug labels). For each interaction identified, provide: (a) mechanism; (b) direction and magnitude of effect; (c) clinical consequences; (d) recommended management. If no clinically relevant interaction is documented, state clearly: ‘No clinically relevant interaction identified based on current evidence.’ Avoid theoretical class effects, speculative mechanisms not supported by literature, or extrapolations beyond documented pathways.”

Step 2: Iterative refinement and evidence justification.

If the model identified any interaction in Step 1, a second query was issued:

“Review your previous answer. For identified interaction, explicitly state the type of supporting evidence (source of your information). If you are uncertain about the evidence strength, reclassify the pair as ‘possible interaction—low certainty’ or ‘no clinically relevant interaction identified’ rather than asserting a definite interaction. Provide your final classification: ‘no interaction’, ‘minor’, ‘moderate’, or ‘major’, with one sentence of justification.”

Step 3: After the second step, if the model gave inaccurate explanation, then a third query was asked:

“Give the original case references or database excerpts.”

Because LLMs are known to generate fabricated references, all cited sources were treated as non-authoritative; instead, we used this step qualitatively to observe whether requiring source citation prompted the models to withdraw unsupported claims or to reclassify speculative interactions as ‘possible’ or ‘no clinically relevant interaction’. Responses citing non-existent or incorrectly attributed references were noted as FP during the evaluation.

Model parameters

All iterative re-testing used the same model versions, interfaces, and default settings as the primary analysis to isolate the effect of prompt refinement. Each drug pair-prompt combination was run once (no multiple sampling) to maintain comparability with the primary single-prompt design.

Evaluation of iterative responses

Responses at each step were recorded verbatim and compared to Lexicomp's classification. False positive corrections were categorized by: (a) mechanism clarification; (b) source validation; (c) severity reclassification; or (d) change from interaction-present to no-interaction conclusion.

For the iterative analysis, we recalculated specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and accuracy using the same formulas as the primary study to enable direct comparison.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the frequency and types of DDIs identified by each tool. Interrater reliability between the two primary pharmacists was assessed via Cohen's kappa - κ (quadratic weights) in RStudio (version 4.5.1) before adjudication. The third adjudicator's rating was used only for the final analytic dataset and not for kappa estimation.

Ethical approval

The study involves neither human nor animal subjects nor patient-specific or identifiable data. All the data were obtained from published literature and clinical tools. Therefore, ethical approval was not needed. All the responses were used strictly for research purposes and not for clinical decision-making. The study followed standard ethical research practices.

Results

A total of 186 drug pairs were evaluated. According to Lexicomp, 105 pairs were classified as clinically relevant (moderate, major, or contraindicated interactions) and 81 as not clinically relevant (minor or no interactions). Comparative performance across ChatGPT-4, DeepSeek-V3, and Drugs.com demonstrated marked variability in sensitivity and specificity. Drugs.com demonstrated the most balanced sensitivity (0.790) and specificity (0.629) among the evaluated tools; this balance resulted in the highest accuracy (0.720). ChatGPT also demonstrated high sensitivity (0.876), indicating reliability in detecting true interactions. However, its lower specificity (0.395) reflected a tendency to overprediction, leading to more false positives. Discussion

The emergence of LLMs such as ChatGPT and DeepSeek has introduced new opportunities in clinical decision support systems, particularly for identifying and managing DDIs. This study systematically evaluated the performance of these LLM-based tools in detecting DDIs involving ASMs. Specifically, the outputs of ChatGPT-4, DeepSeek V3, and Drugs.com were compared against Lexicomp, which was used as the reference standard for identifying and classifying potential DDIs involving ASMs. Drugs.com achieved the most balanced performance, with high sensitivity and the highest specificity and overall accuracy among the tested tools. This finding is consistent with observations in previous work showing that traditional drug databases generally perform better at avoiding false positives than AI-based systems do.8, 21. In contrast, both LLMs showed high sensitivity but substantially lower specificity, primarily due to overprediction of moderate interactions and underrecognition of minor or no interactions. This finding supports earlier evidence indicating that LLMs tend to generate false positives by flagging non-interacting drug pairs as potential risks.13 An exploratory iterative prompting strategy, applied to false-positive pairs, improved LLM specificity and positive predictive value, indicating that more constrained, evidence-focused prompts can partially mitigate overestimation.

Analysis of the interaction severity classification revealed further discrepancies. DeepSeek overidentified moderate interactions (148 cases) and underreported minor interactions (10 cases), showing a pattern of overestimation. This result aligns with findings from Lima and Bukola et al.,8, 22 where AI tools failed to consistently replicate the severity gradings provided by structured reference databases. In contrast, Drugs.com is closely aligned with Lexicomp in identifying major interactions, reinforcing its potential reliability as a supportive clinical tool. The high false-positive rates observed for ChatGPT and DeepSeek have important clinical implications. Overestimation of moderate interactions may contribute to alert fatigue if LLM-generated warnings are integrated into clinical workflows. Excessive flagging of low-risk or theoretical interactions could lead to unnecessary monitoring, unwarranted therapy changes, or reluctance to prescribe effective combinations, thereby increasing treatment complexity and patient anxiety without clear benefit.

Examination of the LLM-generated responses revealed several recurring error patterns within our dataset. First, class effect overgeneralization was observed where valproic acid-SSRI combinations were uniformly labeled as ‘moderate’ interactions due to presumed additive bleeding risk. However, closer inspection showed this risk to be clinically documented primarily for fluoxetine, while interactions with sertraline and fluvoxamine were pharmacodynamically minor or theoretical. Second, inaccurate mechanism attribution accounted for a substantial portion of false positives. For instance, metronidazole was erroneously claimed to inhibit valproic acid glucuronidation via UGT inhibition, an interaction not supported by clinical evidence. Similarly, phenytoin metabolism was misattributed to CYP2D6 rather than the correct pathways (CYP2C9 and CYP2C19). These mechanistic errors mirror findings from Aksoyalp et al.,23 who reported that ChatGPT frequently misclassified CYP enzyme properties when assessing DDI. Third, severity overestimation was common, particularly for phenytoin-containing combinations, where reliance on CYP2C9 metabolism was overstated while alternative pathways (CYP2C19, CYP2C8) were overlooked. Finally, Inaccurate or incomplete citation practices were observed, with both models referencing secondary databases without primary literature verification. For the valproic acid-estrogen combination, Lexicomp classifies it as a moderate interaction reducing valproate concentrations, while Micromedex characterizes it as “potentially clinically significant.” Such discrepancies advocate the need for expert validation of LLMs generated pharmacological claims.

These errors reflect broader concerns about the reliability of LLM training data and the potential for misinformation if used without expert supervision.13 Radha Krishnan et al.3 further reported low sensitivity (0.24) for ChatGPT in real-world clinical environments, emphasizing that while LLMs can supplement decision-making, they are not yet suitable for independent use in patient care.

Lexicomp demonstrated near-perfect interrater agreement (κ = 0.946), reflecting its structured and standardized format. Among the evaluated tools, DeepSeek showed the highest agreement for accuracy (κ = 0.729), followed by Drugs.com (κ = 0.549) and ChatGPT (κ = 0.501). Completeness showed substantial agreement across tools (κ = 0.702–0.725), whereas clarity was lowest for ChatGPT (κ = 0.364). This aligns with prior findings that ChatGPT may provide factually correct responses but variable interpretability.2 In contrast, Bukola et al.22 reported strong clarity and completeness but weaker mechanism and recommendation accuracy. Similar variability across reference standards has been noted by Bischof et al.,24 and performance inconsistency has been attributed to the probabilistic architecture of LLMs.13

Despite these limitations, LLMs demonstrate some significant strengths. In this study, ChatGPT received the highest mean clarity score from the human evaluators (4.68 ± 0.49) and consistently produced structured and comprehensible responses. These results are supported by comparative studies of AI and clinical decision support systems,24, 25. in which AI tools often excel at simplifying complex medical information and presenting it in a format accessible to both clinicians and patients.

In addition to pharmacokinetic interactions, pharmacodynamic interactions are also common among ASMs, particularly those acting on sodium channels. These interactions occur at the drugs' sites of action and may lead to synergistic or antagonistic effects without necessarily altering serum concentrations. Such effects are often difficult to measure and are primarily identified through clinical observation rather than measurable pharmacological parameters. Some combinations, such as lamotrigine with valproic acid, show synergistic therapeutic benefits but may require dose adjustments to minimize adverse effects. Conversely, combinations like lamotrigine with carbamazepine or oxcarbazepine can produce antagonistic effects or increase the risk of adverse reactions.4

The exploratory iterative prompting analysis demonstrated that specificity improved when prompts required explicit evidence justification and discouraged speculative mechanisms. Within the subset of false positive cases, many previously overestimated interactions were reclassified appropriately. However, this analysis was restricted to false positives and therefore reflects post hoc refinement rather than full-dataset re-evaluation. Additionally, multistep prompting may be less feasible in routine clinical workflows, where time constraints favor single-query screening.

From an implementation perspective, these findings indicate a cautious, role-specific use of LLMs in clinical practice. LLMs may be most appropriate for generating patient-friendly explanations of confirmed interactions, supporting education and shared decision-making. In contrast, primary screening for DDIs and final classification of severity should remain anchored in structured tools such as Lexicomp or Drugs.com, which demonstrated higher specificity and more stable severity grading in our dataset. Institutions considering LLM integration should therefore design workflows in which LLMs supplement, rather than replace, established decision support systems. Future research should focus on developing clinically validated models by linking LLMs with established drug databases, conducting real-world simulation studies, and implementing ethical and regulatory precautions to ensure their effective, safe, and responsible use.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the analysis was limited to antiseizure medications and a predefined set of commonly co-prescribed drugs, which may restrict generalizability to other drug classes or the complexity of real-world polypharmacy. Second, ChatGPT-4 and DeepSeek-V3 were evaluated during different calendar periods; although identical query structures and default settings were used, potential background model updates cannot be excluded. Third, a standardized single-prompt approach was used in the primary analysis to ensure consistency, which may not reflect real-world querying behavior. Although iterative prompting improved specificity, this analysis was restricted to false positive cases and represents post hoc refinement rather than full re-evaluation. Fourth, major and moderate interactions were combined for binary performance analysis, which may obscure differences in severity misclassification. Finally, pharmacist ratings involved some degree of subjective interpretation despite good interrater agreement, and the study evaluated simulated queries rather than real-world clinical workflows or patient outcomes.

Conclusions

ChatGPT-4 and DeepSeek-V3 demonstrated high sensitivity but limited specificity in identifying clinically relevant DDIs for commonly prescribed ASMs under single-prompt conditions. Overclassification of moderate interactions was common, potentially reducing reliability for primary screening. Drugs.com showed the most balanced performance relative to Lexicomp in this dataset. Iterative, evidence-constrained prompting improved specificity within the false positive subset, indicating that structured query design influences LLM output. However, these improvements were observed in a post hoc subset analysis and may not fully translate to routine clinical use. At present, LLMs may serve as supportive tools for explanation and educational purposes, whereas structured drug interaction databases remain more appropriate for primary clinical decision-making. Continued evaluation, structured integration with validated databases, and longitudinal performance assessment are necessary before broader clinical adoption.
