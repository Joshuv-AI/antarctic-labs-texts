Response to Letter to the Editor: ‘NALIRIFOX versus nab-paclitaxel and gemcitabine in older patients with treatment-naive metastatic pancreatic cancer: a subgroup analysis of the pivotal NAPOLI 3 trial’
Authors: T Macarulla, R Pazo Cid, MS Womack, A Cubillo Gracian, A Zervoudakis, H Hatoum, V Chung, A Patel, AS Paulson, S Pant, EM O’Reilly, RA Hubner, E Van Cutsem, T Bekaii-Saab, L Zhang, J Li, H Chen, F Maxwell, ZA Wainberg, D Melisi
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

We read with interest the Letter to the Editor in the current issue from Kahl et al., regarding the article ‘NALIRIFOX versus nab-paclitaxel and gemcitabine in older patients with treatment-naive metastatic pancreatic cancer: a subgroup analysis of the pivotal NAPOLI 3 trial’.1 We thank Kahl et al. for their careful commentary on our analysis. While we agree with several of their points, we continue to believe that, for patients with metastatic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (mPDAC) with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status score of 0-1 enrolled in the trial, our evidence supports the conclusion that NALIRIFOX improved survival versus gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel (Gem + NabP) irrespective of age.1,2

We would like to make the following points in response to the observations made in the letter.

•Kahl et al. note that the overall survival (OS) benefit of NALIRIFOX versus Gem + NabP was not statistically significant in either the <70- or ≥70-year age groups on which the article focused. Our intention when designing the post hoc analysis was to evaluate the consistency of the risk–benefit profile of NALIRIFOX versus Gem + NabP across age groups, rather than to test prespecified statistical hypotheses. No α score was assigned to the analysis owing to a lack of control for multiplicity, and P values were not reported. We also note that patient numbers in age-stratified subgroups were small relative to the overall trial population. Nevertheless, no clear differences in OS were observed between subgroups among NALIRIFOX-treated patients and this remained true for all age groups assessed. Hazard ratios for the comparison between NALIRIFOX and Gem + NabP were consistent across age groups, with numerical benefit demonstrated for NALIRIFOX, and were comparable with the overall trial population, in which a statistically significant OS benefit for NALIRIFOX was seen (hazard ratio 0.83, 95% confidence interval 0.70-0.99, P = 0.036).2 Similar patterns were observed for progression-free survival (PFS).

•Kahl et al. also note that the trial population was not fully representative of typically older and frailer patients with mPDAC in the real world. They also state that a single age cut-off is not appropriate for geriatric screening. We agree with them on both of these points and, as described in the article, argue that treatment should be individualized based on holistic patient data rather than simple chronologic age. Our results support the use of NALIRIFOX in selected fit older patients. Our trial design was broadly pragmatic, but we encourage the conduct of prospective studies tailored to geriatric populations across treatments. We remain committed to such studies, including those conducted using real-world data.3

•Kahl et al. argue that OS may not be the most suitable trial endpoint for geriatric patients. We note that regulatory considerations necessitate the use of clinical rather than patient-reported primary endpoints. OS was chosen as an appropriate primary endpoint of NAPOLI 3 overall, with PFS and objective response rate as secondary endpoints, and we have therefore reported these outcomes in age-stratified subgroups. Further to the primary analysis, we have also reported an assessment of health-related quality of life (HRQoL) in the overall population, which demonstrated that time to deterioration in HRQoL was longer with NALIRIFOX than with Gem + NabP.4

•Kahl et al. mention a meta-analysis of phase 3 trials, comparing NALIRIFOX, FOLFIRINOX, and Gem + NabP for the first-line treatment of mPDAC.5 Contrary to the statement in their letter, this analysis demonstrated significantly shorter OS for Gem + NabP than for NALIRIFOX, although no difference was seen between NALIRIFOX and FOLFIRINOX.5

•Kahl et al. state that the cost of NALIRIFOX is increased relative to FOLFIRINOX and Gem + NabP. However, the costs of these treatments are also impacted by toxicity management, including hospitalizations due to hematological adverse events.6,7

•Finally, Kahl et al. call for a head-to-head trial comparing NALIRIFOX with FOLFIRINOX. The ability to do this is constrained by regulatory, ethical, and patient enrollment considerations, particularly as FOLFIRINOX does not have Food and Drug Administration or European Medicines Agency approval for the treatment of mPDAC. NAPOLI 3 attempted to answer the question of whether triplet or doublet therapy is superior as a first-line treatment option. The comparison between NALIRIFOX and FOLFIRINOX may be best made through real-world studies; however, the lack of consistent and standardized data capture across institutions also makes this challenging. Thus, data are currently limited to single-institution or small multicenter studies. However, we note that indirect real-world comparisons, including an external control arm study and a multicenter retrospective analysis of NALIRIFOX treatment data, suggest that NALIRIFOX offers an OS benefit relative to FOLFIRINOX in both trial and real-world settings.8,9 To facilitate the conduct of real-world studies across oncology, we encourage the consistent collection of standardized efficacy, safety, and patient-reported outcome data between centers.

We thank Kahl et al. again for their comments but continue to conclude that, for the patients enrolled in the trial, age does not appear to impact the survival outcomes of NALIRIFOX treatment.1 We welcome further assessment of NALIRIFOX in real-world populations, including in elderly patients.
