Investigating the relationship between monocyte to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio and carotid artery plaque in cerebral infarction patients under different blood pressure and glucose metabolic conditions
Authors: Shencheng Luo, Kun Ma, Ting Liu, Jiawen Dong, Lin Zhu, Sitong Shen, Bernhard Kolberg, Jing Li, Jiangwei Shi, Junhua Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aims to explore the connection between the Monocyte to High-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol Ratio (MHR) and the formation of carotid artery plaque (CAP) in individuals who have experienced a cerebral infarction (CI). Additionally, it examines the relationship between CAP and varying blood pressure and glucose metabolism stratifications.MethodThe investigation involved 10,627 individuals with cerebral infarction. Participants were categorized based on their blood pressure levels and glucose metabolism profiles, and then sorted into tertiles according to their MHRs. Logistic regression analysis was used to assess the relationship between MHR and carotid plaque in CI patients, adjusting for potential confounders and applying FDR correction. Discrimination performance was evaluated using ROC curves, NRI, and IDI. Restricted cubic splines and threshold analysis were employed to explore dose-response relationships and identify inflection points.ResultMHR demonstrated a significant positive association with carotid artery plaque (CAP) in patients with cerebral infarction. For each one-standard deviation increase in standardized MHR, the adjusted OR was 1.154 (95% CI: 1.079–1.235, P < 0.001). The strongest association was found in the T3 group (MHR > 0.552) (OR = 1.392, P < 0.001), which remained significant after FDR correction. Subgroup analysis indicated that this association was significant in individuals with prehypertension (OR = 1.169, P = 0.020), hypertension (OR = 1.180, P < 0.001), prediabetes (OR = 1.158, P < 0.05), and diabetes (OR = 1.137, P < 0.05). After FDR adjustment, the association remained robust in the hypertension group (FDR-adjusted P = 0.004) and the prediabetes group (FDR-adjusted P = 0.030). MHR displayed a nonlinear association with CAP (P for nonlinear = 0.04) and a threshold effect, with an inflection point at 0.89 (OR = 2.47, P < 0.001 when MHR < 0.89). Incorporating MHR improved the model’s AUC to 0.746 (P = 0.049), with NRI = 0.095 and IDI = 0.002 (both P < 0.001).ConclusionIn patients with cerebral infarction, MHR is significantly associated with carotid plaque. This association remains robust in hypertensive and prediabetic patients after FDR adjustment. Furthermore, MHR exhibits a nonlinear relationship with carotid plaque and a threshold effect at an inflection point of 0.89, significantly enhancing plaque discrimination performance.

Introduction

Cerebral infarction accounts for 65.3% of all stroke cases and is a leading cause of disability [1]. A key cause of cerebral infarction is the rupture of carotid artery plaques (CAPs) followed by secondary thrombosis. Therefore, the intrinsic characteristics and progression of CAPs are critical factors in cerebral infarction risk [2, 3]. Inflammation and lipid accumulation are the primary pathological features of atherosclerosis. These two processes interact closely, driving the vascular injury repair response and consequently promoting plaque formation and progression [4–6]. Monocytes (MO) serve as a significant source of proinflammatory factors in atherosclerosis. Their relationship with plaques is primarily expressed through inflammatory responses, cell migration and differentiation, and the regulation of chemokines and adhesion molecules, all of which contribute to plaque stability [7–10]. HDL-C helps suppress CAP advancement by lowering lipid and inflammatory levels, consequently improving atherosclerotic plaque durability [11]. The monocyte-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (MHR) is a recently proposed comprehensive indicator that reflects the dynamic status of inflammation and lipid levels. It serves as a marker for low-grade metabolic inflammation and is valued for its ease of measurement and strong clinical relevance [12, 13]. Studies have demonstrated that elevated MHRs are associated with a higher presence of atherosclerosis. Additionally, these ratios may act as a prognostic marker for disease progression in patients with type 2 diabetes and acute intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) [14–16]. MHR, a novel biomarker indicative of atherosclerosis, has been extensively researched in the cardiovascular field. Increased levels of MHR have been shown to be associated with poor prognosis in patients experiencing ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and to indicate heightened cardiovascular risk in individuals with chronic kidney disease [17, 18]. Recent research on the utility of MHR in the field of cerebral infarction has revealed important findings. For instance, it has been found that elevated MHR levels are independently associated with stroke severity [19], long-term adverse functional outcomes, and all-cause mortality in patients with cerebral infarction [20], and its elevation is also independently associated with the presence of carotid artery disease in stroke patients [21]. However, the majority of previous investigations focused on predicting outcomes during the acute phase of cerebral infarction (such as severity, prognosis, and complications), while the value of MHR in stroke onset and recurrence, and its association with carotid plaque in cerebral infarction patients, remain under-explored. Furthermore, research indicates that blood pressure, a traditional factor associated with stroke, may induce the development of atherosclerosis via hemodynamic alterations [22]. Similarly, abnormal blood glucose levels can promote the formation of chronic vascular lesions [23]. Considering these research limitations and the potential role of clinical factors, this study analyzes the association between MHR and carotid plaque in cerebral infarction patients, further exploring this relationship in stratified populations based on blood pressure and glucose metabolism status.

Study population and methods

Research participants

This research involved 68,982 hospitalized patients with cerebral infarction at the First Teaching Hospital of Tianjin University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, covering the period from January 1, 2013, to May 1, 2023. Rigorous participant selection protocols were implemented to ensure valid and reliable research findings. The patient selection procedure is illustrated in the flowchart presented in Fig. 1 (Fig. 1). This study received approval from the Ethics Committee of the First Teaching Hospital of Tianjin University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Approval No. TYLL2020[K] 057) and was registered with the China Clinical Trial Registry (Registration No. ChiCTR2100045415).

Exclusion criteria

Individuals with the following conditions are excluded: ① concurrent cerebral haemorrhaging, brain abscesses, or space-occupying lesions within the brain; ② a history of stroke or imaging that indicates past strokes; ③ concurrent infectious conditions, malignant growths, or significant liver or kidney dysfunction; ④ those undergoing IV thrombolytic therapy or endovascular thrombectomy during critical stages of stroke; ⑤ cases lacking comprehensive carotid ultrasound data, incomplete lipid levels, or presenting inconsistencies or outliers that defy physiological reason; ⑥ and individuals currently taking any medication aimed at lowering cholesterol levels.

Data collection

Ultrasound assessment of CAP

Seasoned ultrasound technicians perform blind assessments using colour Doppler in B-mode, specifically examining the common and internal carotid arteries, as well as their junction. Carotid intima-media thickness (CIMT) is calculated by averaging the bilateral common carotid artery IMT measurements taken 10 mm proximal and distal to the bulb. The maximum value from four sites is recorded with a CIMT of ≥ 1 mm indicating thickening [25]. According to the Mannheim criteria [26], CAP is defined as a lesion with ≥ 0.5 mm intimal protrusion into the lumen, exceeding 50% of the surrounding intima-media thickness (IMT), or a thickness of ≥ 1.5 mm. Simultaneously record the echogenic characteristics of plaques: Hypoechoic plaques have echo intensity lower than that of the intima layer. Isoechoic plaques display an echo intensity that is relatively consistent with the intima layer. Hyperechoic plaques exhibit an echo intensity equal to or higher than that of the adventitia layer. Mixed-echo plaques show heterogeneous echoes, with at least 20% of the area exhibiting this characteristic [27]. Strict quality control is implemented throughout the process to ensure consistency in image acquisition and analysis, with all results reviewed by certified personnel.

Clinical and laboratory assessments

This study involved professional medical personnel who collected participant-related information, which included age, gender, NIHSS score, smoking status, alcohol consumption, current medical history, and past medical history. Blood pressure measurements were obtained using an automatic sphygmomanometer (Yuwell Medical Devices & Supplies Co., Ltd., Jiangsu Province, China). Patients were categorized into distinct blood pressure subgroups according to systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) criteria: normal blood pressure (SBP < 120 mmHg and DBP < 80 mmHg); prehypertension (120 mmHg ≤ SBP < 140 mmHg and/or 80 mmHg ≤ DBP < 90 mmHg); and hypertension (SBP ≥ 140 mmHg and/or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg) [28]. Patients were stratified into different glycemic states based on glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c): HbA1c < 5.7% indicates non-diabetes, 5.7% ≤ HbA1c ≤ 6.4% indicates prediabetes, and HbA1c ≥ 6.5% indicates diabetes [29]. Stroke severity at admission was graded according to the NIHSS score: 0–4 (mild), 5–15 (moderate), 16–20 (moderate to severe), > 20 (severe) [30]. Participants were grouped by MHR tertiles: T1 (< 0.375), T2 (0.375–0.552), T3 (> 0.552) [31]. Automatic analyzers (Hitachi, Ltd., Chiyoda, Tokyo, Japan) measured several indicators, including MO, TC, TG, HDL-C, LDL-C, and HbA1c. Blood samples for these measurements were collected within 24 h of patient admission and before the start of systemic anti-inflammatory or intensive lipid-lowering therapy.

Quality control

During data collection, we adhered strictly to the established inclusion and exclusion criteria. We screened eligible patient data using an intelligent data management platform and created an Excel database. To ensure data quality and maintain blinding, two professionals cross-checked the original medical records against the entries in the system. Data entry personnel conducted multiple verifications and self-checks, which were followed by a final review from two additional independent researchers. This multi-tiered validation process ensured the accuracy and completeness of the data throughout.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 27.0 and R software (version 4.5.2). Categorical variables, such as gender, were presented as percentages (%) and evaluated using χ² tests. Non-normally distributed continuous variables (such as age, blood pressure, etc.) were expressed as median (interquartile range), and the Kruskal-Wallis H test was applied. To assess the association between the ratio of MHR and the occurrence of CAP, we employed binary logistic regression to determine the odds ratio and its 95% confidence interval. The median value across MHR tertiles was treated as a continuous variable when calculating the P-trend, while controlling for potential confounders such as sex, age, smoking habits, alcohol intake, and various lipid measurements. Further stratified analysis was conducted based on blood pressure and glucose metabolism status. After applying multivariable adjustments and performing stratified analysis, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was used to evaluate the statistical robustness of the association between MHR and carotid plaque amid multiple comparisons. Predictive performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, which measured the area under the curve (AUC), optimal cutoff values, and Youden’s index. Changes in model specificity following threshold adjustments were analyzed, and the DeLong test was utilized to compare statistical differences in model AUC before and after incorporating MHR. The incremental predictive value was clarified through net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) indices. Additionally, restricted cubic splines (RCS) were employed to fit the nonlinear dose-response relationship between MHR and CAP, further investigating its threshold effects. All statistical tests adhered to a two-sided P < 0.05 significance threshold.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Among 10,627 study subjects, the median age was 64.0 years, with an interquartile range of 57.0 to 72.0 years. Stratifying by MHR tertiles revealed that the T3 group (MHR > 0.552)had a higher proportions of males, smokers, and drinkers compared to the T1 group (MHR < 0.375) (all P < 0.05). Participants in the T3 cohort exhibited significantly elevated levels of HbA1c, Glu, LYM, NE, MO, PLT, and Hb, along with substantially reduced HDL-C concentrations, all showing statistical significance at P < 0.001. Although the T3 group had lower systolic blood pressure readings, they presented higher NIHSS scores. Additionally, this group experienced a greater overall incidence of CAPs and displayed more hypoechoic and mixed-echoic plaque formations compared to the T1 group, with all differences being highly significant (P < 0.001). In contrast, the prevalence of hyperechoic plaques was similar between the two groups, showing no statistically significant variation (P = 0.738). Finally, there were no significant differences in intima-media thickening or the prevalence of coronary heart disease when comparing all three study populations (P > 0.05) (Table 1).

Relationship between MHR and CAP

Relationship between MHR and CAP in the overall study population

Two logistic regression models were employed to evaluate the relationship between MHR and carotid artery plaque (CAP). In unadjusted Model 1, standardized MHR (per 1-standard deviation increase) demonstrated a significant positive association with the presence of carotid artery plaque (OR = 1.182, 95% CI: 1.124–1.243, P < 0.001). After further adjustments for potential confounders in Model 2, this positive association remained statistically significant (OR = 1.154, 95% CI: 1.079–1.235, P < 0.001). This indicates that in this study population, each one-standard-deviation increase in MHR was associated with a 15.4% higher odds of detecting carotid plaque. In both unadjusted and adjusted models, the T3 group (MHR > 0.552) exhibited the highest OR values for carotid plaque among the three categories, with values of 1.460 and 1.392, respectively. When MHR was categorized, the trend P values for the association between MHR and carotid plaque in both unadjusted and adjusted models remained consistent with those observed when MHR was treated as a continuous variable (all P < 0.001). Furthermore, after adjusting for false discovery rate (FDR), these associations remained statistically significant (FDR-adjusted P values: 0.001, 0.032, 0.001, and 0.001), reinforcing the robustness of our findings (Table 2).

Relationship between MHR and CAP in patients with different blood pressure statuses

Subgroup analysis stratified by blood pressure indicated that, in the unadjusted model, standardized MHR (per 1 standard deviation increase) was significantly positively associated with the development of carotid plaque in both the prehypertension and hypertension subgroups (both P < 0.05), while no significant association was observed in the normotensive subgroup. After adjusting for confounding variables, this association remained significant in the prehypertension (OR: 1.169, 95% CI: 1.025–1.334; P = 0.020) and hypertension (OR: 1.180, 95% CI: 1.086–1.282; P < 0.001) subgroups.

Further analysis, which treated MHR as a categorical variable (tertiles), demonstrated that in the unadjusted model, both prehypertensive and hypertensive patients in the T3 exhibited significantly higher odds of carotid plaque development. However, following adjustment for confounding factors, only the association in the hypertensive T3 group remained statistically significant (OR = 1.447, P < 0.001).

After applying false discovery rate (FDR) correction, the association between MHR (as a continuous variable) and carotid plaque, as well as the association between the T3 category and carotid plaque, retained statistical significance solely in the hypertension group (FDR-adjusted P values = 0.004, 0.004), whereas the corresponding associations in the prehypertension group did not reach statistical significance after adjustment. Interaction analysis revealed no statistically significant interaction between MHR and blood pressure status with respect to carotid plaque (P = 0.264) (Table 4).

Discriminatory performance of MHR for CAP

To clarify the discriminatory value of MHR for CAP, this study constructed a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and analyzed its incremental performance. Traditional clinical risk factors (age, gender, smoking, alcohol consumption, total cholesterol, triglycerides, platelet count, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, hemoglobin) yielded an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.745 (95% CI: 0.734–0.756) with an optimal cutoff value of 0.760 and a Youden index of 0.371, indicating moderate discriminatory ability for carotid plaque. The model fit was significantly improved after adding MHR to the traditional risk factors (likelihood ratio test χ² = 24.42, df = 1, P < 0.001). This addition increased the model AUC to 0.746 (95% CI: 0.735–0.757), with a statistically significant difference (DeLong test, P = 0.049). After incorporating MHR, the optimal cutoff point was adjusted to 0.776 (Youden index 0.370), with specificity improving from 0.667 in the traditional model to 0.704, demonstrating that MHR enhances disease classification accuracy. The model’s Net Receiver Operating Characteristic Index (NRI) reached 0.095 (95% CI: 0.050–0.139, P < 0.001), and the integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) was 0.002 (95% CI: 0.001–0.003, P < 0.001), indicating that MHR significantly enhances the model’s classification and discrimination capabilities for carotid plaques (Tables 6 and 7).

Nonlinear Relationship Between MHR and CAP

Using restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis, we examined the nonlinear dose-response relationship between MHR and CAP, adjusting for confounding factors such as age, gender, smoking, alcohol consumption, total cholesterol, triglycerides, platelet count, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, and hemoglobin. The analysis revealed a significant nonlinear dose-response relationship between MHR and CAP (P for overall trend < 0.001, P for nonlinearity = 0.04), as illustrated in Fig. 2 of the RCS plot.

Discussion

Association between MHR and CAP in patients with cerebral infarction

This study identified a significant positive association between MHR and carotid plaque in the overall population of patients with cerebral infarction. Further stratified analysis revealed that this association was particularly pronounced in individuals with hypertension and prediabetes. When MHR was integrated into the baseline model that included traditional risk factors, the model’s AUC exhibited a statistically significant marginal improvement (DeLong test P < 0.05). Furthermore, the NRI and IDI results indicated that MHR substantially enhances the model’s classification and discrimination capabilities. The modest increase in AUC may be attributed to the baseline model already incorporating key atherosclerosis-related factors. Nonetheless, this small yet significant improvement implies that MHR has additional value in refining clinical assessments. Restricted cubic spline and threshold analyses uncovered a nonlinear relationship between MHR and CAP, with an inflection point at 0.89. MHR values below 0.89 represent the sensitive range for this association, providing a reference for clinically precise assessments of carotid plaque in cerebral infarction patients. This range also reflects the phased changes in inflammation-lipid imbalance within this population. Interaction analysis revealed no significant interactions between MHR and either blood pressure status or glucose metabolism status concerning carotid plaque association (all P > 0.05). This suggests that blood pressure and glucose metabolism do not exert synergistic modifying effects on the relationship between MHR and carotid plaque. Therefore, the stratified analysis in this study is exploratory and descriptive, aiming to illustrate the association between MHR and carotid plaque across different blood pressure and glucose metabolism states, rather than comparing differences in association strength between strata.

From a mechanism perspective, the relationship between monocytes and atherosclerotic plaques is primarily reflected in inflammatory responses, chemotaxis and migration, as well as plaque stability [7–10]. During atherosclerotic inflammation, monocytes migrate to damaged areas and differentiate into macrophages. These macrophages then phagocytose lipids and convert into foam cells, which contribute to the formation of the plaque core. This process is tightly regulated by chemokines, particularly the CCL2-CCR2 axis, which primarily drives monocyte recruitment to the plaque. Additionally, receptors such as CCR1 and CCR5 play a synergistic role in this recruitment process [8, 9, 32]. Monocytes engage with endothelial cells through adhesion molecules (including VCAM-1 and VLA-4) to accomplish transendothelial migration [9]. Macrophages derived from monocytes secrete matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which degrade the extracellular matrix and compromise plaque stability [10]. Conversely, HDL-C stabilizes plaques by suppressing the migration of macrophages derived from monocytes. It also facilitates cholesterol efflux, which reduces lipid accumulation in macrophages, and exerts anti-inflammatory actions [33, 34]. (Mechanism diagram shown in Fig. 3). Therefore, as a composite inflammatory marker derived from traditional peripheral blood cell counts, MHR illustrates the dynamic balance between inflammation and lipid metabolism. It integrates the opposing effects of monocytes which are pro-inflammatory and promote plaque progression, and HDL, which is anti-inflammatory and stabilizes plaques. This integration offers a novel perspective for the clinical assessment of atherosclerosis.

Data from recent investigations further show that MHR is strongly associated with multiple metabolic and cardiovascular diseases [35]. The findings of this study are consistent with those from previous research not only as a composite indicator of inflammation and lipid metabolism, but also an indicator of atherosclerotic manifestations. Shuang Liu et al. confirmed its positive association with carotid plaque in the general population [13]. However, this study focused on stroke patients and used plaque as the observation metric, thereby better demonstrating MHR’s value in assessing stroke onset. Specific data analysis in this study further supports these associations. Baseline analysis revealed that the T3 group (MHR > 0.552) had a significantly higher incidence of overall carotid plaques and a greater proportion of hypoechoic and mixed-echo plaques compared to the T1 group (MHR < 0.375). No differences were noted between the groups for hyperechoic plaques. Previous studies have shown that hypoechoic and mixed-echo plaques, which are characterized by large lipid cores and thin fibrous caps, are more susceptible to rupture and detachment, making them high-risk factors for cerebral infarction. In contrast, hyperechoic plaques tend to be more stable [36, 37]. The increased proportion of unstable plaques in the T3 group suggests a significant association with the occurrence of cerebral infarction. Elevated MHR levels may be linked to the formation of these unstable plaques, potentially increasing the possibility of cerebral infarction. Binary logistic regression analysis indicated that MHR is independently associated with carotid plaque. After adjusting for various confounding factors, the highest tertile group (T3) consistently showed the highest odds of carotid plaque occurrence, with odds ratios (OR) of 1.460 (95% CI: 1.306–1.633) and 1.392 (95% CI: 1.199–1.615). These findings further support a significant association between elevated MHR levels and an increased likelihood of carotid plaque formation.

Association characteristics between MHR and CAP in cerebral infarction patients with different blood pressure statuses

Hypertension initiates and drives the progression of atherosclerosis through various pathways, including abnormal substance transport, hemodynamic changes, and inflammatory reactions. In particular, abnormalities in substance transport, such as altered vascular permeability and porosity, accelerate the infiltration and retention of lipoproteins, which in turn promotes the formation of foam cells [38]. Hemodynamic changes, such as increased tensile stress on the vascular wall, can exacerbate vascular injury [39]; Simultaneously, hypertension upregulates the expression of monocyte chemotactic protein-1 (MCP-1), promoting the accumulation of monocytes and macrophages in plaques [40], and pre-activates peripheral blood mononuclear cells, causing them to secrete large amounts of IL-1β in response to angiotensin II stimulation, and to secrete significant quantities of pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-1β and TNF-α in response to stimuli like lipopolysaccharide [41]. The processes mentioned above collectively affect vascular structure and function, contributing not only to the progression of atherosclerotic plaques but also to the potential rupture of these plaques, which can lead to clinical events such as cerebral infarction.

In light of these mechanisms, we further demonstrated, through blood pressure-stratified analysis, that in the unadjusted model, the standardized MHR was positively associated with carotid plaque in the prehypertension and hypertension subgroups (both P < 0.05), but no significant association was detected in the normotensive subgroup. Following adjusting for confounding factors, this association remained significant in the prehypertension (OR = 1.169, 95% CI 1.025–1.334; P = 0.020) and hypertension (OR = 1.180, 95% CI 1.086–1.282; P < 0.001) subgroups. Further stratification of MHR by tertiles revealed a significant association with carotid plaque only in the T3 group of hypertensive patients after adjustment (OR = 1.447, P < 0.001). In addition, after FDR adjustment, the association between MHR (continuous variable) and its T3 categories with carotid plaque remained statistically significant only in the hypertension group (FDR-adjusted P = 0.004). This finding supports previous research; a multicenter study across 31 provinces in China confirmed that hypertension is an independent risk factor for all stages of carotid atherosclerosis [42]; Yaqing Zhou et al. further pointed out that in the health examination cohort, MHR showed a significant positive association with hypertension [43]. The relationship between lipid metabolism and cardiovascular disease in hypertensive populations is complex. Previous studies have identified a U-shaped association between HDL-C levels and cardiovascular events in hypertensive patients, indicating that both excessively low and excessively high HDL-C levels may be associated with an increased likelihood of such events [44]. This suggests that using a single lipid indicator for disease assessment may have limitations. Based on this, the present study utilized the composite indicator MHR as a starting point and discovered a significant positive association between MHR and the presence of carotid plaque in patients with cerebral infarction. This association was particularly pronounced in the hypertension subgroup. These findings not only suggest that MHR may serve as a sensitive indicator for assessing carotid plaque status in patients with hypertension-related cerebral infarction but also offer a new perspective on managing this patient population through the lens of inflammation-lipid interactions.

Association characteristics between MHR and carotid plaques in cerebral infarction patients with different glucose metabolic states

This study performed a stratified analysis based on glucose metabolism status, revealing that, after adjusting for confounding factors, MHR was significantly associated with carotid plaque only in the prediabetes (OR = 1.158, 95% CI 1.026–1.307) and diabetes (OR = 1.137, 95% CI 1.027–1.258) subgroups, while no statistical significance was found in the non-diabetes subgroup. When MHR was categorized by tertiles, the strongest association occurred in the T3 group of prediabetes, which remained significant after FDR adjustment (FDR-adjusted P = 0.030). Several studies have investigated the clinical significance of the MHR across various glucose metabolic states. Notably, Cairong Ruan et al. found that this ratio demonstrates a nonlinear positive association with the risk of prediabetes [45]; Research by Jiawei Chen et al. indicates that the MHR serves as a practical indicator for assessing the early presence and progression of carotid atherosclerosis in patients with T2DM [46]. The findings from the aforementioned studies are consistent with the results of this research, further validating the value of MHR in assessing glucose metabolism abnormalities and vascular lesions.

Impaired glucose metabolism can trigger inflammation and lipid metabolism issues, marked by elevated monocyte levels and reduced HDL-C levels [45, 47, 48]. Elevated MHR indicates an inflammatory-lipid imbalance, reflecting atherosclerosis progression. The characteristics vary across glucose metabolism abnormality stages, with a corresponding association between MHR and carotid plaque. Research by Noah Moruzzi et al. shows that prolonged hyperglycemia leads endothelial cells to downregulate mitochondrial respiration, shift to glycolytic metabolism, and decrease the ATP/ADP ratio [49]. Prediabetes, marked by blood glucose fluctuations, induces oxidative stress and inflammatory responses (e.g., NLRP3 inflammasome activation) [50, 51], resulting in earlier vascular endothelial dysfunction. This explains the stronger association between MHR and carotid plaque in the prediabetes subgroup.

Advantages and constraints

The robustness of our research is rooted in the strong data coherence achieved through a comprehensive, single-center retrospective study design. This approach effectively minimized potential bias by eliminating discrepancies in measurement methods and standards that can occur across different medical institutions. Additionally, the relatively large sample size, combined with rigorous inclusion and exclusion criteria, further reduced substantial bias. We took into account possible confounders that could affect the association between MHR levels and the development of carotid artery plaque. Analyzing a population with cerebral infarction also adds valuable evidence for the importance of monitoring and regulating MHR within this specific group.

However, our study is not without several limitations. First, as a cross-sectional investigation, data were collected at a single time point, thereby permitting the establishment of an association between the two variables rather than a definitive causal relationship. Prospective studies are required to further validate these findings. Second, the reliance on single-center data implies that the baseline characteristics of the study subjects (such as reasons for consultation and demographic background) were relatively homogeneous, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the results to some extent. Finally, to elucidate the independent association between MHR and carotid plaque, we excluded the potential influence of hypoglycemic and antihypertensive medication interventions on the natural course of blood glucose and blood pressure. While this methodological choice helps to concentrate on the role of MHR itself, it also constrains the applicability of our conclusions to populations undergoing medication. Future studies should systematically integrate medication use to more comprehensively evaluate its significance in clinical practice.

Conclusion

This study identified a significant association between MHR and carotid plaque. Given its low detection cost and ease of measurement, this indicator may serve as a potential biomarker for the early diagnosis and prevention of cerebral infarction in routine health screening and clinical practice. We recommend that individuals with elevated MHR levels, particularly those with hypertension or prediabetes, receive particular attention during routine screening to effectively facilitate secondary prevention of cerebral infarction.
