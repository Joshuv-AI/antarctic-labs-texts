Global, regional, and national burden of asthma from 1990 to 2021: analysis of data from global disease burden in 2021
Authors: Cong Hu, Shuxiong Nong, Yixin Chen, Lin Zhang, Yongfeng Chen, Chenang Liu, Ping Li, Chilin Liao, Meng Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAsthma is one of the diseases that pose a serious threat to human health globally. The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study from 1990 to 2021 conducted a comprehensive assessment of the prevalence, incidence, mortality, and Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALY) of asthma. This study particularly focuses on the integration of prevalence data with other indicators and carries out stratified analyses at the global, regional, and national levels based on gender, age, and Socio-Demographic Index (SDI).MethodsThe data for this study is drawn from the 2021 Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study, which provides comprehensive data on diseases, injuries, and risk factors. This study aims to explore the temporal trends of asthma and assess the dynamics of age-standardized prevalence rates (ASPR), incidence rates (ASIR), death rates (ASDR), and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) for asthma, with the goal achieved by estimating the annual percentage change (EAPC) over the study period. The study conducted a detailed stratification based on gender, 20 age groups, 21 GBD regions, 204 countries and territories, and 5 SDI quintiles.ResultsIn 2021, asthma continued to impose a substantial burden on the global health landscape, with a total of 260479186.9 (227209547.5-297967236.9) cases and an ASPR of 3340.1 (2905.2-3832.2 per 100,000 people. The ASIR was 37864175.4 (31381241-46919993.7), translating to 516.7 (425.4-646.1) per 100,000 people. In 2021, the number of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) attributed to asthma was 21422859.9 (16956877.7-26887091.7), and the ASDR was 264.6 (208.3-333.4) per 100,000 people. Analyzing regional disparities, it was observed that High SDI regions had the highest ASPR, and ASIR, while Low SDI regions had the lowest ASDR and Age-standardized DALY rates. Geographically, Kazakstan and Pakistan stood out with the high ASPR, and Papua New Guinea stood out with the highest ASDR, and Age-standardized DALY rates. Asthma primarily affects minors, with no significant difference between males and females. High BMI has emerged as the most important risk factor for asthma-related DALYs globally.ConclusionThis pattern indicates a complex relationship between socio-economic development and health outcomes. Regions with higher Socio-Demographic Index (SDI), which typically represent more developed areas, tend to have a higher prevalence of asthma but lower mortality rates and disease burden. These patterns highlight the necessity for low-income regions to learn from the targeted public health measures of high-income areas in developing asthma management plans, in order to address the inequalities in asthma outcomes.Keywords: Asthma, GBD, DALY, Incidence rate, Prevalence

Introduction

Asthma is a chronic inflammatory condition that fluctuates in severity and duration, marked by respiratory symptoms including shortness of breath, wheezing, coughing, and a feeling of tightness in the chest [1, 2]. Even with advancements in the realms of prevention, diagnosis, and therapeutic strategies, asthma remains a considerable health burden for populations across the globe, with significant gaps among various regions and socio-economic groups [3, 4]. According to the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA), the burden of premature death and reduced quality of life due to asthma remains substantial. Asthma has imposed a significant economic burden on the global economy, primarily reflected in medical expenses and productivity losses [3, 4]. For example, in China, the economic burden of asthma includes direct medical costs, indirect costs (such as productivity losses due to absenteeism), and hospitalization fees [5]. Studies have shown that the average annual direct medical costs for asthma patients range from $348 to $1,187, while indirect costs range from $7 to $1,195. In the United States, the total cost of asthma to society was estimated at $56 billion in 2007, which included $50.1 billion in medical expenses, $3.8 billion in productivity losses due to absenteeism and missed school days, and $2.1 billion in losses due to premature death [6]. Globally, the burden of asthma varies significantly across regions and populations, with low- and middle-income countries facing particularly severe shortages of diagnostic and treatment resources, further exacerbating the problem [7].

Asthma incidence and disease burden vary significantly across different age groups [2–4, 8]. For instance, children and adolescents are high-incidence populations for asthma, with etiology closely related to genetic factors and early environmental exposures such as viral infections and allergen contact [9]. In contrast, asthma in the elderly is more likely associated with chronic inflammation and comorbidities like chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and cardiovascular diseases [10]. hormonal changes during special physiological stages such as menstrual cycles, pregnancy, and menopause in females can affect asthma onset and symptom presentation [11, 12]. Differences in SDI levels reflect significant disparities among regions in terms of medical resources, public health infrastructure, and lifestyle, which have a profound impact on asthma incidence and disease burden [13]. Despite the significant threat that asthma poses to global health, research data on asthma remain relatively scarce. The complexity of asthma’s etiology, the diversity of its symptoms, and the significant heterogeneity observed across different regions and populations all contribute to the challenges in data collection and integration [8]. These factors limit the comprehensive assessment of the global burden of asthma. However, accurately assessing the global burden of asthma is crucial for developing effective public health policies, optimizing resource allocation, and improving patient outcomes. Therefore, this study aims to fill this research gap by analyzing the GBD 2021 data. It seeks to explore the global trends in asthma prevalence, its economic burden, and major risk factors, thereby providing a scientific basis for future research and policy-making. The existing body of data on the global implications of asthma is insufficient, and there is a pressing need for an in-depth analysis utilizing the most recent GBD 2021 statistics to obtain a clearer picture of the global burden of asthma [8]. Such comprehensive and current data are essential for crafting effective health policies and designing targeted intervention strategies.

Methods

Data source and population

The GBD 2021 study provides an in-depth assessment of health loss linked to 371 diseases, injuries, and conditions, alongside 88 risk factors across 204 countries and territories. This analysis employs the latest epidemiological data and standardized methodologies. Specific data regarding asthma were gathered from systematic literature reviews, survey data, longitudinal studies, and other sources, all of which were integrated into the Disease-Model Bayesian Meta-Regression (DisMoD-MR) tool (version 2.1). This tool, which utilizes the Bayesian Priors Regularisation and Trimming (MR-BRT) model, amalgamates all available morbidity and mortality data with epidemiological and spatial linkages to produce accurate estimates of disease burden. Since the study used publicly accessible, de-identified data from the GBD study, no ethical approval was necessary. The GBD study adheres strictly to ethical guidelines in data collection and analysis to safeguard human subjects. The data utilized in this study were sourced from the GBD 2021 dataset, accessed via the Global Health Data Exchange query tool (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/). The data were categorized by region, gender, country/territory, and risk factors.Using the Cause of Death Ensemble Model (CODEm) framework, mortality rates are calculated. Data is carefully adjusted before analysis to guarantee accuracy, and CODEm uses a variety of models to predict mortality more precisely. To provide an assessment of asthma incidence, prevalence, and mortality, this technique takes into account the variations in research design and methodologies across diverse data sources. Yearly Life Loss (YLL), which measures the effect of premature mortality, and Years Lived with Disability (YLD), which reflects the burden of life caused by asthma, are the two factors we take into account when calculating the DALYs attributable to asthma. The SDI, which takes into account factors including per capita income, education level, and birth rate, is used to gauge how developed a nation or area is. Greater socioeconomic development is indicated by a greater SDI, which ranges from 0 to 1. It is also known to be correlated with rates of illness incidence and death. This study examines the relationship between the prevalence of asthma and socioeconomic advancement by classifying nations and areas into five SDI categories, ranging from low to high [14, 15]. This study typically consider age groups every 5 years, obtain various types of data (including prevalence, incidence, mortality, and DALYs), and standardize data using age-standardization techniques to ensure comparability across different populations. Sensitivity analysis is an important method used to assess the robustness of research results to assumptions, data sources, model selection, or other key factors. When using tools such as DisMod-MR, model assumptions (such as the natural history of the disease, epidemiological parameters, etc.) may influence the results. By altering these assumptions, the stability of the results can be observed. Comorbidities and environmental factors have significant impacts on the global distribution and disease burden of asthma. Environmental factors such as smoking, occupational exposure, and air pollution are important risk sources for asthma, while comorbidities like cardiovascular diseases, obesity, and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) further increase the difficulty of asthma control. There are significant differences in asthma burden across different regions and populations, which are closely related to socioeconomic status, medical resources, and environmental exposure.

Study design

Flow chart of studies via GBD databases was shown in S1. The study also looked at how different risk variables affected the burden of asthma in addition to the main indicators of mortality, morbidity, incidence rate, and DALYs. According to the GBD 2021 research, it contained information on fatalities and DALYs associated with asthma caused by the four main risk factors: smoking, excessive alcohol use, obesity, and inactivity. The study included additional regional stratification to highlight regional differences in the impact of these factors. In order to measure the impact of these risk factors, sophisticated techniques were used, such as spatiotemporal Gaussian process regression and dissmod-mr2.1, which allow us to simulate the exposure distribution of each risk factor across various demographic groups and geographical areas. Based on epidemiological studies, a theoretical minimal risk exposure level (TMREL) for each component was calculated. These estimates are translated into meaningful health outcomes that reveal how changing these risk factors may affect asthma outcomes across different populations. This comprehensive approach not only highlighted the direct burden of asthma but also clarified the significance of modifiable risk factors, providing a more profound understanding of the disease’s impact and potential treatment options.

EAPC and BAPC model

The estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) is used for statistical analysis to evaluate the trend of age-standardized rates (ASR) for the incidence, mortality, DALYs, and prevalence of asthma. With a.i. representing the age-specific rate for each group, wi representing the number of individuals in the standard population that belong to each age group, and A representing the number of age groups, the formula for calculating the ASR per 100,000 people is ASR = ∑(a.i. × wi)/∑wi × 100,000. The regression model used to construct EAPCs explains the trend of ASR over a certain time period: Y is the natural logarithm of ASR, X is the year, α is the intercept, and Y = α + βX + e, e is the error term, while β is the slope or trend. EAPC, or annual percentage change, is computed as 100 × (exp(β) – 1). The ASR is regarded as steady if neither requirement is satisfied. The association between SDI and the age-standardized incidence of asthma is assessed using Spearman’s correlation. To predict future changes in the burden of asthma, we use the Bayesian Age-Period-Cohort (BAPC) model in this work, which includes an integrated nested Laplace approximation. In comparison to alternative prediction techniques, this model has proven to have better coverage and accuracy. Following known procedures from other studies, the computations are carried out using the R-package BAPC. The statistical program R (version 4.4.1) is used for all analyses and visualizations.

Result

According to the GBD study, there was a considerable decrease in the age-standardized incidence rate, mortality, and DALYs of asthma from 1990 to 2021. The results also emphasized the persistent differences between geographic regions and the quintiles of the Social Demographic Index (SDI).

In 2021, asthma continued to impose a substantial burden on the global health landscape, with a total of 260479186.9 (227209547.5-297967236.9) cases and an ASPR of 3340.1 (2905.2-3832.2 per 100,000 people. The ASIR was 37864175.4 (31381241-46919993.7), translating to 516.7 (425.4-646.1) per 100,000 people. In 2021, the number of DALYs attributed to asthma was 21422859.9 (16956877.7-26887091.7), and the ASDR was 264.6 (208.3-333.4) per 100,000 people. Analyzing regional disparities, it was observed that high SDI regions had the highest ASPR, and ASIR, while low SDI regions had the lowest ASDR and Age-standardized DALY rates. Geographically, Kazakstan and Pakistan stood out with the high ASPR, and Papua New Guinea stood out with the highest ASDR, and Age-standardized DALY rates. Asthma primarily affects minors, with no significant difference between males and females (Table 1.; Fig. 1).

The prevalence of asthma varied significantly by area worldwide and is strongly correlated with SDI levels. There were notable variations in ASPR, with low-medium SDI areas showing the lowest incidence and high SDI areas showing the greatest prevalence (Table 1.; Fig. 1). The time trend of ASPR revealed different patterns in different regions. SDI levels indicated different stages of epidemiological transformation. The high SDI region showed an initial decrease followed by stable fluctuations, while a stable decline was observed in other SDI regions (Table 1.; Fig. 1). With an EAPC of −2.65 (−2.75 to −2.54), Eastern Europe exhibited the greatest reduction, suggesting that the prevalence of asthma is declining there (Table 1.). ASIR and ASDR also highlighted these geographical variations. Whereas the ASIR and ASDR were lowest in High-medium and High SDI regions, the ASIR reported was highest in Low SDI locations. Age-standardized DALY rates, which measure the illness burden, highlight these geographical variations even more. The high SDI region has the least amount of strain, whereas the low SDI zone has the most. All SDI regions showed a stable falling over the last 30 years (Table 1.; Fig. 1). Among all countries, Kazakstan and Pakistan stood out with the high ASPR, Papua New Guinea and some African countries stood out with the highest ASDR, and Age-standardized DALY rates (Fig. 2). whereas The Federal Republic of Nigeria, Mali, and Chad have the most increases in ASPR. The results of this investigation underscored the persistent worldwide prevalence of asthma, especially in locations with Low SDI, underscoring the necessity of focused treatments in these areas. Additionally, the study found that the age distribution of asthma incidence has changed, with minors bearing a disproportionately high burden (Fig. 3).

The findings of our study showed that the greatest ASPR is found in High-income North America, where it is 9583.3 (8169.5-11493.3). Caribbean is next in line, with 7638.5 (6722.2-8563.4) (Table 1.). A high incidence rate of 1193.8 (1000.9–1446) per 100,000 was also seen in the Caribbean. South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa have a high death of asthma worldwide. This might result from a confluence of variables, including a lack of knowledge about the risk factors for asthma, and inadequate access to high-quality healthcare. According to the research, High-income North America ranks among the regions with the highest age-standardized incidence rates (ASIR) for asthma globally. (Table 1.). With an EAPC of −2.04 (−2.31 to −1.77), High-income Asia Pacific ASIR had the most falling. In contrast, High-income North America, which has a high income, saw the largest rise in EAPC, with a value of 0.65 (0.35 to 0.95) (Table 1.). However, the prevalence of asthma was falling with age between 1990 and 2021, particularly among those aged 75 to 79. The incidence rate of asthma is much greater in older people than in younger people in all SDI regions (Fig. 4). In the 70–74 age range, the ASPR ratio is comparable for males and females; however, as age rises, males continuously exceed females in the same age range. These results demonstrated the heterogeneity and complexity of the epidemiology of asthma among nations (Fig. 5). There may be notable variations in risk factors, healthcare systems, and preventative measures among nations, as seen by the broad incidence rate and sharp rise in prevalence. We found a strong correlation between the most severely impacted nations when we analyzed the 2021 ASDR and DALYs data for asthma. Using the GBD 2021datebase, the study classified the DALYs associated with the main risk factors for asthma: High body-mass index, Occupational asthmagens, Smoking, Nitrogen dioxide pollution. The data was then further stratified by location. High body-mass index is the primary cause of disability-adjusted life expectancy from asthma worldwide in 2021. Each SDI area has a different contribution from these risk variables to DALYs. The worldwide burden of asthma was predicted to fluctuate significantly between 2021 and 2040, with varying patterns observed across assessment techniques. The rising global prevalence and incidence of asthma among both genders indicate potential improvements in asthma management. It is predicted that the age-standardized rate of deaths and DALYs for asthma would remain relatively falling. For all four measures, the male incidence rate is consistently higher than the female incidence rate, although the gender gap is expected to narrow with time, especially for prevalence (Fig. 6).

Discussion

The results of the global asthma burden between 1990 and 2021 have shown a complex web of regional variations and patterns. It was observed that High SDI regions had the highest ASPR, and ASIR, while Low SDI regions had the lowest ASDR and Age-standardized DALY rates. Our research findings indicate that in some countries, the age-standardized prevalence of asthma is high, yet the age-standardized mortality rate is relatively low, a pattern predominantly observed in high-income countries. This is largely attributed to the effective management of asthma medications and standard care in these high-income nations. Consequently, despite the rise in asthma prevalence due to unavoidable risk factors such as urbanization, the mortality rate remains low. In contrast, in some low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), although the age-standardized prevalence of asthma is lower, the age-standardized mortality rate is higher. For these countries, accessing effective, available, and affordable medications presents a significant challenge. For instance, in certain countries, long-term standards for medical care have not been established, and there is a scarcity of outstanding doctors and specialized organizations, resulting in generally poor medical care for asthma patients. Therefore, in these LMICs, there are still many unnecessary deaths due to asthma [8, 16, 17].

These risk factors are more common in nations that are experiencing economic growth and adopting fast foods, which raises the incidence of asthma and other non-communicable illnesses [18–20]. Minors have the highest ASPR, ASIR, ASDR, and DALY. An analysis of risk factors indicates that the global burden of asthma is substantially affected by High body-mass index, Occupational asthmagens, Smoking, Nitrogen dioxide pollution [21–24]. The analysis indicates that asthma has the highest incidence in children [25, 26]. A complex interaction of several causes may be reflected in this trend, such as implementing tobacco control measures, making dietary adjustments, and enhancing strategies to prevent exposure to Occupational asthmagens, as these asthmagens significantly contribute to the burden of asthma. The onset of childhood asthma is influenced by a variety of factors during the early stages of life, including viral infections, air pollution, genetic susceptibility, obesity, and immune abnormalities [27, 28]. Based on the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021, compared to 2019, the decline in prevalence and mortality rates of asthma in older adults is primarily attributed to socio-economic factors. These factors affect air quality, tobacco use, awareness of early screening, and early diagnosis of asthma, as well as the use of treatment medications [18–29]. The reduction in asthma prevalence and mortality rates is evident. However, due to the lack of data on medication use in individual countries in the GBD, it is not possible to estimate the impact of medication use on the disease burden across different countries [30–32]. Further research is needed to assess this burden. Additionally, previous studies have found that changes in policy and socioeconomic factors may also influence disease rates [18, 28, 29]. Regarding risk factors, body mass index (BMI) is the most critical for disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) (Fig. 7). In 2021, the age-standardized mortality rate attributed to asthma was more significantly affected by high BMI than by smoking. When analyzing the percentage of asthma DALYs caused by smoking, our results also indicate that smoking has a substantial impact on asthma DALYs. Decisions on tobacco control and dietary adjustments, as well as improvements in measures to prevent occupational asthma, are crucial, as occupational asthma also has a significant impact on DALYs. These risk variables’ effects varied by geography and SDI quintiles, underscoring the necessity of focused preventative tactics that take into account the unique risk factor profiles of various demographics [23–26]. African countries indeed face numerous challenges in addressing asthma, but the nature of these challenges is not yet fully understood. These challenges may include both the severity of the asthma burden and the insufficient capacity to cope with this burden. According to relevant studies, the asthma burden in sub-Saharan Africa is relatively complex. Despite a relatively low incidence rate, the mortality rate is quite high. This is similar to the overall situation in low Socio-Demographic Index (SDI) countries, which often lack sufficient medical facilities and protections, leading to inadequate diagnosis and treatment of asthma. Compared with high-SDI countries, low-SDI countries (including many African nations) face more difficulties in asthma management. High-SDI countries usually have better medical resources and higher drug accessibility, which helps to reduce the mortality risk of asthma. However, due to limited medical resources, asthma patients in low-SDI countries may not be able to receive timely and effective treatment, which further exacerbates the disease burden. In addition, environmental factors (such as air pollution and occupational exposure) and changes in lifestyle (such as an increase in obesity rates) also have some impact on the incidence and disease burden of asthma. Therefore, the challenges that African countries face in dealing with asthma may include both the severity of the disease burden and the insufficient capacity to cope with it. The commonalities of these challenges with other low-SDI countries lie in the lack of medical resources and inadequate diagnosis and treatment. However, the specific manifestations may vary due to regional differences.

To tackle the risk factors and decrease the global incidence of asthma, a multimodal strategy is required. Strategies in high-income nations should include providing better dietary alternatives [18, 27, 28]. Tobacco control efforts, such as raising tobacco costs, enacting complete smoke-free regulations, and enhancing smoking cessation programs, might be strengthened in low- and middle-income nations. Air pollution may be addressed by boosting sustainable energy, improving public transit, and tightening emission regulations [29–31]. The growth in obesity may be halted by creating culturally relevant dietary standards and encouraging traditional, healthier eating habits [32–35]. In all situations, including asthma evaluation into primary care visits and emphasizing the early detection and treatment of modifiable risk factors are potential additional ways to lessen the burden of asthma [8, 17, 36–41]. A high BMI is one of the main contributors to asthma-related disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) globally. Occupational asthmagens include gases, fumes, dust, organic dust, dampness and mold, cold conditions, and other chemicals. These occupational exposures are closely related to the exacerbation and onset of asthma. Smoking is a known risk factor for asthma and is closely associated with asthma incidence and mortality. Smoking can lead to increased airway inflammation and reduced lung function, thereby increasing the frequency and severity of asthma attacks. NO₂ is a common air pollutant primarily from vehicle exhaust and industrial emissions.

The GBD study chose high BMI, occupational asthmagens, smoking, and NO₂ exposure as the main risk factors for asthma because these factors have a significant impact on asthma incidence, mortality, and DALYs losses globally. The selection of these factors is based on extensive epidemiological studies and data analysis, aiming to more comprehensively assess the global burden of asthma and its attributable risks [42]. It is important to take into account the limitations of this study when evaluating the findings. First, in different nations and areas, the quality and accessibility of data sources may have an impact on the estimation’s accuracy. An underestimation of the actual burden of asthma may result from underreporting of cases and a lack of trustworthy epidemiological data in several low- and middle-income nations. Second, the estimation may be rather unclear due to the GBD methodology’s reliance on a number of assumptions and modeling approaches. It does not provide insights into the burden of asthma in clinical settings due to the lack of data on emergency department visits, hospital admissions, and asthma medication usage. Our study only considers the most well-known risk factors for asthma and does not account for potential factors such as solid fuel and particulate matter pollution from households and workplaces. The estimated asthma-related deaths do not distinguish between different chronic respiratory disease types, leading to underestimation. While sophisticated statistical techniques are used in GBD research to overcome these uncertainties, the findings should be regarded as the trustful estimates that are currently available.

In conclusion, there are still large differences in the absolute burden of asthma between nations, regions, and SDI areas, even if age-standardized rates have decreased. In order to meet the unique demands of various populations, especially in low- and middle-income nations, the research findings highlight the necessity of focused preventive and treatment initiatives. the main findings of the study and implications for public health policies should be more explicitly highlighted. For example, it can be emphasized that there is a need to strengthen asthma prevention and management measures globally, especially in low- and middle-income countries. Specific policy recommendations should be proposed, such as enhancing asthma surveillance systems, improving the accessibility and quality of medical resources, and conducting public health education activities targeting major risk factors.
