In Silico analysis of concentration and spatial distribution effects of magnetoelectric nanoparticles on peripheral nerve activation
Authors: Valentina Galletta, Marta Bonato, Marta Parazzini, Emma Chiaramello, Serena Fiocchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The delivery of selective and specific neural stimulation is of paramount importance to enable effective interactions with the nervous system while reducing potential side effects. Neural interfaces play a key role in the communication between an external device and nervous tissue, and technological advances have been made in recent decades to optimize this interaction and thus improve the quality of life of patients. However, several challenges remain to be overcome, such as the trade-off between selectivity and invasiveness or the modulation of a specific stimulation that could mimic natural axonal spiking. In this context, emerging magnetoelectric nanoparticles (MENPs) could be designed as a possible solution to current limitations, allowing minimally invasive, highly selective, wireless and more natural neural stimulation. Among the variables that influence the interaction of MENPs with neural tissue, both the concentration and the distribution of nanoparticles play a key role, but appropriate studies and quantification of their effects are still lacking. Our study aims to fill this gap, moving an important step toward more realistic and informative modeling of the MENPs–nerve interaction. In addition, the great variability in the anatomy of peripheral nerves was considered by evaluating the stimulation capability of the nanoparticles while interacting with both myelinated and unmyelinated axons. A holistic computational framework was used, ranging from multiphysics characterization of the ME effect of a single nanoparticle to modeling the effects of the nanoparticle concentration and stochastic distribution to assess axonal electrical responses. Owing to their improved performance, nanorod-shaped MENPs (NRs) were selected as stimulation sources, and multiple nanoparticles were assumed to be randomly distributed in a small volume within the tissue. The results demonstrate the strong influence of both concentration and distribution on the stimulation capability of nanoparticles, with the stimulation capability increasing as the nanoparticle concentration increases. Specific axonal excitability strongly influences axonal responses, with the stimulation capability of MENPs varying greatly between myelinated and unmyelinated axons. This study makes a significant contribution to the foundational knowledge required for applying MENPs in peripheral nerve stimulation and provides quantitative evidence regarding how nanoparticle concentration and distribution influence MENP–nerve interactions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s42234-025-00187-4.

Background

Electrical stimulation of the nervous system is an effective treatment for a number of cognitive disorders and motor function diseases, such as epilepsy, pain relief and motor function control (Badi et al. 2021; Bhadra and Peckham 1997; Fitchett et al. 2021). In recent years, the integration of electrical stimulation into neuroprosthetic devices has paved the way for innovative medical treatments and technologies aimed at restoring motor and sensory system functions in impaired patients (Bavishi et al. 2019). Various applications target the peripheral nervous system (PNS), where electrical neurostimulation can be used both to generate movement and to provide sensory feedback by triggering somatic sensations, as in motor and somatosensory neuroprostheses (Ding et al. 2024; Donati and Valle 2024; Prochazka 2018).

The delivery of highly selective and minimally invasive electrical stimulation to the PNS is highly important to ensure effective communication between the external device and the neuronal response, improving patient mobility and quality of life. However, the current state-of-the-art neural interfaces, which include surface, extraneural, and intrafascicular electrodes, suffer from a trade-off between selectivity and invasiveness (del Valle and Navarro 2013; Navarro et al. 2005).

Recent progress in research has led to biocompatible nanomaterial technologies that enable unprecedented modulation of neuronal activity (Karim et al. 2024; Ranke et al. 2024), thus overcoming the limitations of traditional neural interfaces and improving overall neuroprosthetic performance and tolerability.

In this context, the coexistence of the nanometric scale and unique magnetoelectric (ME) effect, i.e., bidirectional coupling between magnetic and electric fields, in magnetoelectric nanoparticles (MENPs) has attracted particular interest, with the potential to develop a new approach for minimally invasive, wireless and highly selective interactions with nervous tissue. The ME effect can be intrinsic when considering single-phase multiferroic materials or arise from the interface of two different phases, as in the case of core‒shell structures. MENPs are indeed composite materials with a core‒shell structure consisting of a magnetostrictive core coated with a biocompatible piezoelectric shell (Kopyl et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2020). Since the coupling between the core and shell is bidirectional and linear, it is possible to distinguish between the direct ME effect, where magnetic control governs electrical polarization, and the converse ME effect, where electric control governs the magnetic phase. Specifically, for the direct ME effect, an external magnetic field causes the magnetostrictive core to experience nonzero strain. This strain is mechanically transmitted to the piezoelectric shell, which converts it into an electrical charge distribution (Eerenstein et al. 2006; Fiebig 2005; Kopyl et al. 2021). This specific effect has already been exploited in previous in vitro and in vivo studies, demonstrating the ability of MENPs to selectively modulate and stimulate neural activity in both central and peripheral locations (Guduru et al. 2015; Kozielski et al. 2021; Nguyen et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2022). Once MENPs are delivered to the desired target site, they must be properly tuned by applying an appropriate external magnetic field, eliciting an electric field that will interact with the nervous system (Pardo and Khizroev 2022).

As a first estimation of the stimulation potential of MENPs, the magnitude of the two-phase interaction, i.e., the performance of MENPs, is considered and quantified by the direct ME coefficient αE=dE/dH [V/cm⋅Oe], where higher-order ME interaction terms are ignored (Eerenstein et al. 2006; Fiebig 2005; Kopyl et al. 2021). Various manufacturing variables affect the performance of MENPs (Lawes and Srinivasan 2011), with the current state of the art being the core-shell cobalt ferrite (CFO) and barium titanate (BTO) MENPs, recognized for their good in vitro and in vivo biocompatibility and their demonstrated efficacy in previous experimental studies (Kaushik et al. 2016; Nair et al. 2013; Nguyen et al. 2021; Pardo et al. 2021). Among the other manufacturing characteristics, geometric anisotropy was found to improve performance, with an increase in the ME coefficients assessed by computational methods for nanorod-shaped MENPs (Marrella et al. 2023).

Despite significant progress in predicting the ability of MENPs to stimulate nervous tissue, we are still far from achieving a realistic representation of an actual experimental design, particularly in vivo. For example, the influence of the distribution and concentration of MENPs on the electrical output has not been comprehensively addressed. This is crucial because the interaction between nanostructures and nervous tissue is significantly affected not only by the strength and timing of the electric potential but also by its spatial arrangement. Given the size relationship between MENPs and neuronal excitable sites, quantifying the likelihood of modulating or stimulating neural responses becomes essential. In this context, computational methods represent an essential approach to study the potentialities of PNS stimulation by MENPs, allowing a detailed assessment of the electrical quantities involved and a deeper understanding of the mechanisms of complex physical phenomena. A previous study characterized the interaction of a single MENP or multiple MENPs with variable concentrations, modeled as agglomerates, with a single neuronal fiber (Galletta et al. 2024). The ability of MENPs to stimulate the PNS was confirmed, and a first quantification of the influence of the orientation, distance and concentration of MENPs on their stimulation ability was performed.

In this study, we move toward a more realistic modeling of the stimulation exerted by MENPs on the PNS, with the aim of more specifically quantifying the influence of the concentration and distribution of MENPs on their stimulatory capacity. A comprehensive theoretical framework was adopted, ranging from the design of the direct ME effect of the nanoparticles themselves to the quantification of the effect of multiple particles with different concentrations on the electric field distribution in human neural tissue and the associated neuronal response. Owing to their promising ME coefficient, nanorod-shaped MENPs (NRs) are considered as stimulation sources. The influence of NR concentration on the ability to elicit neural activity was assessed under the assumption that NRs were randomly distributed in a small volume within the neural tissue. Given the great variability in the anatomy of autonomic and somatic peripheral nerves, which are composed of a mixture of motor and sensory neurons with profoundly different excitability characteristics, evaluating the effects of such variables on different neuronal fibers becomes a strategic factor for better understanding and optimizing the stimulatory capacity of nanoparticles. For this reason, such an investigation was carried out using biophysical computational models of both myelinated and unmyelinated peripheral fibers. Artificial multiplication factors (MFs), defined as the ratio of the external voltage needed to activate an action potential in a neuronal fiber and the voltage induced by MENPs distributed in the tissue, were used to indirectly assess the feasibility of axonal stimulation.

Our study provides a detailed characterization of the effects of nanoparticle concentration on their stimulation ability while also realistically accounting for the influence of their random spatial distribution. The results of this study provide fundamental information for the targeted application of MENPs in peripheral nerve stimulation, making significant contributions to understanding the actual nerve‒MENPs interaction.

Methods

The influence of the NR concentration and stochastic distribution on their stimulation capability was characterized via a computational approach consisting of three main steps, as shown in Fig. 1.

First, the direct ME behavior of a single CFO-BTO nanorod-shaped ME was characterized via a multiphysics approach (Fig. 1a) to estimate the maximum amplitude of the electric potential (V) distribution on the external surface of a single NR. The effect of stimulation from multiple NRs on the surrounding biological tissue was subsequently investigated (Fig. 1b). The NRs were assumed to be distributed in a small volume within a fascicle of fibers, and the effects of three different weight/volume (w/v) concentrations, i.e., 0.1% w/v, 1% w/v and 10% w/v, were analyzed in terms of the electric field distribution (E) within the nerve tissue. To account for the random distribution of particles, a set of 50 randomly obtained NR configurations were simulated for each concentration. Finally, a hybrid modeling approach (Romeni et al. 2020), which integrates the electric potential distribution within nerve tissue with detailed neuronal dynamics models (Fig. 1c), was used. The responses of eight different peripheral axons, each defined by a specific fiber geometry and corresponding neuronal dynamics, were investigated.

Modeling the direct ME effect of a single nanorod

Using the same approach as in previous in silico studies (Chiaramello et al. 2022; Fiocchi et al. 2022; Marrella et al. 2023), the direct ME effect of a single nanoparticle was characterized via a finite element method-based simulation tool, COMSOL Multiphysics 6.2.

The ME nanostructure was modeled with a CFO core coated with a BTO shell surrounded by biological tissue mimicking the properties of the nerve material (with a relative permeability of µr=1 and electrical conductivity of σ = 0.35 S/m). The entire domain was enclosed within a cylindrical infinite element layer to simulate open boundaries. The material properties of the ME nanostructures are listed in Table S1. Owing to the improvement in ME performance afforded by geometric anisotropy (Galletta et al. 2025; Marrella et al. 2023), a nanorod-shaped nanostructure (NR) was chosen. The geometric dimensions of the NR were as follows: a 5-nm shell thickness, a 25-nm core radius, and a 200-nm height, in line with dimensions reported in the literature (Si et al. 2013).

To induce the direct ME effect, i.e., to magnetize the nanoparticle, the NR was stimulated with an external uniform magnetic field directed along its main axis (Fig. 1a). The magnetic field amplitude at which the NR response was evaluated was chosen on the basis of simulation results analyzing the evolution of the ME coupling coefficient with increasing external bias magnetic field amplitude: a maximum NR performance was found at approximately one hundred kA/m. Thus, we evaluated the NR response at an external DC magnetic field amplitude of 100 kA/m.

For modeling, magnetostriction and piezoelectric effect multiphysics couplings were used along with electrostatic, solid mechanics and magnetic field modules. A magnetic field boundary condition was applied to all the rectangular faces of the domain to simulate a uniform DC magnetic field, while the response of the NR was estimated by fully coupling the magnetostrictive and piezoelectric domains. For more details on the equations describing the multiphysics approach, see (Fiocchi et al. 2022).

Modeling of the NR distributions: NR concentration variation

The influence of NR concentration on neuronal activity excitation was investigated by randomly positioning NRs in a small volume of nerve tissue with dimensions equal to 1 μm× 1 μm × 20 μm (see Fig. 1b). Assuming that the NRs were stimulated by an external sinusoidal magnetic field of 100 Hz with an amplitude of 100 kA/m, each NR was approximated by a dipole aligned with the externally applied magnetic field, as in previous studies (Betal et al. 2016; Galletta et al. 2024). Geometrically, each NR was modeled as a cylinder with a height and radius equal to 210 and 30 nm, respectively, which is consistent with ‘Modeling the direct ME effect of a single nanorod’. Dipole-like electrical equivalent modeling distinguished two conductive surfaces (hpole = 80 nm) separated by an insulating layer (V = 0, hins = 50 nm). Using Dirichlet boundary conditions, an electric potential of ± 11.5 mV was assigned to the two conductive surfaces (see Fig. 1b), corresponding to the maximum amplitude of V obtained for a 100 kA/m magnetic field over the surfaces of the NR, as estimated in ‘Modeling the direct ME effect of a single nanorod’. The insulating layer was made of techotane material (σ = 0 S/m).

Three different NR concentrations of 0.1%, 1% and 10% weight/volume (w/v) were chosen, corresponding to 5, 50 and 480 nanoparticles, respectively, distributed in the small volume of tissue. The choice was based on the concentrations used in previous in vitro and in vivo studies, which were below the threshold of dose toxicity (Guduru et al. 2015; Kaushik et al. 2016; Kozielski et al. 2021; Nguyen et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2022). For each concentration, 50 different random spatial configurations of NRs were simulated to capture the variability in the nanoparticle distribution. To avoid Dirichlet boundary problems, a minimum distance of 0.04 μm was assumed between nanoparticles in all directions. The median values of the interparticle distance were 7.3 μm, 6.1 μm, and 5.1 μm for w/v of 0.1%, 1%, and 10%, respectively. In all the simulations, the NRs were assumed to align in the same direction, i.e., in the direction of the external magnetic field, which was assumed to be perpendicular to the volume height.

Estimation of the electric field distribution

The small volume enclosing the NRs was assumed to be located within a fascicle of fibers, which was modeled as a parallelepiped measuring 12 μm× 12 μm × 80 μm with dielectric properties of nerve tissue at the NR stimulation frequency (Hasgall et al. 2022). Within the fascicle, we modeled a single neural fiber as a linear spline of variable length aligned with the major axis of the biological tissue (Fig. 1b). The fiber was attached to one of the external surfaces of the volume containing the randomly distributed NRs, resulting in a total length of 20 μm being directly exposed to NR stimulation.

The ohmic quasi-static approximation was used to estimate the distribution of the electric field in the computational domain. A hexahedral, nonuniform mesh up to one-tenth the size of the NR was used to discretize the computational domain, and fine grid increments were used to accurately discretize the volume containing the NRs. Modeling the neural fiber response

Biophysical computational models of both myelinated and unmyelinated fibers were considered. Eight different neuronal models were studied independently (Fig. 1c) and assigned to the geometric linear spline representing the peripheral fiber (i.e., red dotted linear spline in Fig. 1b), whose total length varied depending on the neuronal model, as detailed below.

Both smaller and larger myelinated fibers were represented by the MOTOR (MRG) model (McIntyre et al. 2002, 2004), which consists of a double-cable structure that realistically simulates the biophysical properties of human myelinated axons. Diameters of 1 and 2 μm were selected for small-diameter fibers (i.e., small MRG fibers), and diameters of 5.7, 7.3, 8.7, and 10.0 μm were selected for large-diameter fibers (i.e., MRG fibers). The fiber length varied geometrically from a minimum of 0.9 mm to a maximum of 2.3 mm depending on the fiber diameter. To ensure consistent comparisons, the position of a single Ranvier’s node was kept fixed across simulations at approximately half the height of the volume enclosing the distributed NRs. Additionally, since peripheral nerves are primarily composed of unmyelinated axons (Pelot et al. 2021), two dynamics of unmyelinated C-type fibers were considered: Schild (Schild and Kunze 1997) and Tigerholm (Tigerholm et al. 2014), which were used to model unmyelinated peripheral axons that target the autonomic and somatic nervous systems, respectively. A diameter of 1.0 μm was selected for the two models, which is in the range of unmyelinated fiber diameters (Ochoa and Mair 1969; Sharma and Thomas 1974). We chose a fiber length of 80 μm and a segment length of 0.2 μm as a compromise between physiological realism and computational load. To simulate the electrical stimulation performed by the NRs, a sinusoidal stimulus with a normalized amplitude and a frequency of 100 Hz was superimposed on the V dipolar distribution at its maximum amplitude over the surfaces of the NRs, as modeled in ‘Modeling of the NR distributions: NR concentration variation’. This modeling approach relied on the linear approximation of the ME coefficient, according to which the time dependence of the electric field is linearly dependent on the magnetic field behavior in time (αE=dE/dH). The stimulus duration was 50 ms, and the total amplitude of the electrical stimulus exerted by the NRs was as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\phi\:}\left(\text{t}\right)=\text{V}\bullet\:\text{M}\text{F}\bullet\:\text{h}\left(\text{t}\right),$$\end{document}

where h(t) is the sinusoidal stimulus with a normalized amplitude, V is the initial electric potential distribution characterized as defined in ‘Modeling of the NR distributions: NR concentration variation’, and MF is the multiplication factor, a dimensionless number used to scale V and estimate the minimum pulse amplitude needed to elicit an action potential in the neural fiber. In other words, the MF assessed how many times the electric potential exerted by NRs must be amplified to elicit a neuronal response.

For each of the described NR concentrations and random distributions, successive simulations were run to assess the individual responses of the different peripheral fibers. In each simulation, the MF was determined iteratively for each fiber: the considered neuron was stimulated with a series of pulses of increasing intensity, i.e., the MF value, until the generation of a spike. A spike was detected when the membrane potential of the neuron depolarized below a threshold value, which was assumed equal to 60 mV.

Data analysis

To characterize the influence of the NR concentration when the electric potential on the surface of the NRs is at its maximum amplitude, the E field distributions within the small volume enclosing the NRs, on its external surface facing the nerve fiber, and along the length of the fiber attached to the volume were statistically analyzed. The percentage of fiber length experiencing electrical potential values above two thresholds, i.e., 50% (T50%) and 70% (T70%) of the maximum value of V along the fiber length, was quantified. The variability of the results across the 50 random configurations obtained at each concentration level was quantified as an evaluation of the influence of the stochastic distribution of the NRs on the E field distribution generated in nerve tissue.

To estimate the probability of eliciting a neuronal response while accounting for the different neuronal dynamics, the MFs obtained for the 50 random configurations at fixed concentrations were fitted with the highest log-likelihood probability distribution functions. The probability of obtaining a MF equal to or less than 1 or 3 and a third threshold named the ideal threshold (IT) were evaluated. Specifically, the IT was defined as two times the MF value required to evoke an action potential when the fiber is stimulated with a single NR placed in close proximity to an active site, i.e., a Ranvier’s node for myelinated fibers and a segment for unmyelinated fibers. Results

NR direct ME effect: NR surface electric potential distribution

Figure 2 shows the coupling between the magnetostrictive core and the piezoelectric shell when the NR is exposed to a magnetic field of amplitude equal to 100 kA/m aligned along its main axis. Figure 2a shows the core magnetization, which reaches values above 120 kA/m and causes deformation of the core. The electric potential distribution observed on the surface of the NR, as shown in Fig. 2b, is determined by the mechanical contact of the shell with the magnetostrictive core and the intrinsic material properties of the shell itself. Our results confirm the dipole-like electric potential distribution, which reaches minimum and maximum values of ± 11.5 mV and a resulting ME coefficient on the order of 1 V/cm⋅Oe.

As expected, the median and percentiles of the electric field distribution in the tissue increase with increasing NR concentration. For example, the 1st percentiles of the electric field values perceived inside the volume and averaged over the 50 random configurations increase from 1.0 to 1.8⋅103 V/m from w/v of 0.1% (i.e., when 5 MENPs are placed into the volume of interest) to 10% (i.e., when 480 MENPs are placed into the volume of interest), with the corresponding 95th percentiles increasing from 1.6⋅103 to 1.3⋅105 V/m. Furthermore, the variability decreases with increasing NR concentration, indicating a significant influence of the specific NR position on the electric field distribution in the tissue. For example, the coefficients of variability of the 1st percentiles of the electric field values perceived inside the volume decrease from 91.0% to 4.8% from w/v values of 0.1% to 10%, respectively, with the corresponding coefficients of variability of the 95th percentiles decreasing from 11.3% to 0.3%. As expected, a different trend is shown for both the minimum and maximum electric field values, which are not shown here but remain at approximately the same order of magnitude at varying concentrations. The minimum electric field distributions lie between 0 and 35 V/m for all the considered concentrations, with distributions shifting reasonably toward 0 V/m at lower concentrations. The distributions of the maximum electric field values show a similar trend, with the maximum values averaged over the 50 configurations for each of the concentrations considered being between 7.9⋅105 and 1.1⋅106 V/m. Our results show slightly higher maximum electric field values at w/v of 10%, which can be attributed mainly to the influence of the relative positions of the NRs and the summation effect of the electric field. The corresponding coefficients of variability decrease at a fixed concentration: greater variability is shown for minimum electric field values, whereas less variability is shown for maximum electric field values. For example, the coefficients of variability decrease from 93.1% to 4.3% when the minimum and maximum electric field values at a w/v of 0.1% are considered.

Analysis of neural fiber response

Figure 5 shows the descriptive statistics of the MF values for each of the neural fibers considered at different NR concentrations. The results show a similar trend for each peripheral fiber separately: the required MF values decrease as the NR concentration increases. In addition, at varying NR concentrations, smaller myelinated fibers, i.e., small MRG of 1.0 μm and 2.0 μm, require MF values that are more than twice as high as those of larger myelinated fibers, i.e., MRG fibers with diameters of 5.7 μm to 10.0 μm. The median values of MF at w/v 0.1% are 9.9⋅10 and 3.7⋅102 for the small MRG 1.0 μm and MRG 5.7 μm, with corresponding medians of 5.3 and 2.0 at w/v 10%, respectively. A different trend was shown when myelinated and unmyelinated fibers were compared. At a w/v of 0.1%, smaller MFs are required for unmyelinated fibers than for myelinated fibers. At a w/v of 1%, the MFs are comparable. At a w/v of 10%, higher MFs are required for unmyelinated fibers than for myelinated fibers. For example, the medians of the MF distributions at w/v 0.1% are 89.0 and 4.3⋅102 for the unmyelinated Schild 1.0 μm and MRG 10.0 μm, respectively, with corresponding medians of 8.3 and 2.3 at w/v 10%. A more detailed representation of the populations of MFs is shown in Fig. S1, which illustrates three of the different neuronal dynamics considered, namely, the small MRG 1.0 μm, the MRG 5.7 μm and the unmyelinated Tigerholm 1.0 μm. Histograms of MFs and their probability density functions are shown for each of the three fibers and for each of the NR concentrations evaluated. For readability, the range of each histogram varies according to the specific range of MFs. As the concentration of NRs varies, the theoretical distribution used to fit the data varies, as reported in Table S2. To visually assess the likelihood of fit, QQ plots comparing the distribution of MFs with the corresponding theoretical distribution fit for each of the three specific fibers are shown in Fig. S2. Discussion

The increasing demand for novel electrical stimulation and modulation techniques within the peripheral nervous system has highlighted the potential of magnetoelectric nanoparticles as a promising approach. MENPs offer the potential for wireless stimulation of specific fascicles while ensuring minimal invasiveness. Computational approaches are indispensable tools for evaluating the stimulation efficacy of this groundbreaking technology. They are essential for quantifying both the electrical output of ME nanoparticles and the consequential response of the surrounding nervous tissue. To offer a proper estimation of the interaction between ME nanoparticles and peripheral nervous tissue and to provide a realistic quantification of their stimulation capability, this study provides a step ahead toward a more realistic modeling approach. Through a holistic framework, we started from multiphysics modeling of the ME effect of a single nanostructure, with the shape of a core‒shell nanorod, moving to modeling the effect of multiple NRs with variable concentrations and stochastic distributions enclosed in a small volume of tissue inside one peripheral nerve fascicle, and ending with an assessment of the electrical response of different peripheral neuronal fibers. Given the large variability in peripheral nerve anatomy, neuronal dynamics models of both myelinated and unmyelinated fibers were used. All the presented models were based on validated models and well-established approaches, i.e., the model presented in (Fiocchi et al. 2022) to characterize the direct ME effect of a single nanoparticle and the hybrid approach (Romeni et al. 2020) to estimate the electrical response of peripheral axons.

A multiphysics approach is essential for characterizing the ME coupling of a single nanoparticle because of the interfacing of the two phases. The simulation of the behavior of a single NR exposed to an optimized bias magnetic field of 100 kA/m allowed for the quantification of its polarization changes and, consequently, a 22.5 mV electric potential difference in its shell (Fig. 2). The corresponding direct ME coefficient is approximately equal to 1 V/cm⋅Oe, which is consistent with the ranges describing the direct ME effect reported in the literature for nanorod-shaped MENPs (Basov et al. 2017; Oh et al. 2010). In addition to the increase in ME performance, the geometric anisotropy of the nanoparticle facilitates access to the neuronal membrane (Zhang et al. 2024), which is crucial for the interaction of such technology with the neural fibers. However, future studies can focus on further optimizing the ME performance according to the needs of specific applications.

Following this initial assessment, the influence of the concentration of nanoparticles was evaluated by assuming that the nanoparticles were distributed in a small volume. The choice of such a small volume mimics the presence of an artificial flexible material doped with NRs whose dielectric properties are very similar to those of the surrounding biological tissue. This follows the hypothesis described in (Shoffstall and Capadona 2018; Woods et al. 2020) and assumes the presence of a small structure, similar to (Suarato et al. 2025). The dimensions of the volume encompassing the MENPs are below the physiological range of fascicle diameters reported for mammalian peripheral nerves (Gustafson et al. 2009; Stewart 2003; Sunderland and Bradley 1952), thus allowing for the insertion of this small volume inside a fascicle of fibers.

Reducing the distance between the electrode and the target neural tissue dramatically improves the selectivity of neural stimulation while enhancing the exploitation of the somatotopic organization of peripheral nerves, allowing preferential recruitment of neural fibers innervating a specific body area or with a specific function (Badia et al. 2010; Hallin 1990; Navarro et al. 2005; Prodanov et al. 2007). With actual electrodes, the minimum distance from nerve fibers can be achieved either by extraneural electrodes, which surround the epineurium of peripheral nerves, or by intraneural electrodes, which penetrate the perineurium of individual fascicles. However, this closer proximity is achieved at the cost of increased invasiveness and potential nerve damage. Some examples of extraneural electrodes are cylindrical nerve cuffs, which contact large groups of fibers and thus provide limited selectivity while preserving tissue integrity, and flat interface nerve electrodes (FINEs), which increase the surface area in contact with nerve tissue, thereby increasing selectivity at the expense of nerve injury resulting from the compressive force of the reshaping process (Loeb and Peck 1996; Ordonez et al. 2012). For intraneural electrodes, such as intrafascicular electrodes (LIFEs) (Lago et al. 2007), Utah slanted electrode arrays (USEAs) (Thomas et al. 2022) or transverse interfascicular multichannel electrodes (TIME) (Boretius et al. 2012), the trade-off between selectivity and invasiveness is more balanced, but the risk of fascicular damage is high, which is strictly related to electrode implantation and mechanical stability, where slight movements of the electrode position could lead to disruption of the surrounding nervous tissue. The nanometric scale of MENPs dramatically increases the proximity of the stimulation source to the nerve fibers, increasing stimulation selectivity while limiting the risk of nerve tissue damage.

A direct consequence of such a dramatic increase in stimulation selectivity is the possibility of achieving asynchronous activation within a population of cells forming peripheral nerve tissue. Such an activation pattern could mimic the natural asynchronous and nonuniform activation of peripheral fibers, in contrast to the synchronous activation of a population of cells caused by traditional stimulation electrodes, which can lead to potential side effects such as unnatural sensory perceptions or muscle co-contraction (Kiernan et al. 1997; Polasek et al. 2007; Raspopovic et al. 2014).

For electrodes, potential side effects could be mitigated by correct placement of the electrode stimulation contacts to minimize the distance between the stimulation site and the target tissue (Tan et al. 2014); for nanoparticles, control of the nanoparticle concentration is a key factor in this regard.

The investigation of the influence of the concentration of MENPs on the volume of interest revealed that the greater the number of MENPs, the greater the probability of eliciting an action potential, regardless of the type of fiber, myelinated or unmyelinated. In our study, we investigated nanoparticle concentrations ranging from 0.1% to 10% by weight/volume (w/v). A w/v of 10% may appear high compared with the concentration values reported in previous in vivo and in vitro studies (Guduru et al. 2015; Kaushik et al. 2016; Kozielski et al. 2021; Nguyen et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2022). However, the concentration is evaluated in relation to the small volume in which the MENPs are assumed to be distributed rather than considering the entire body volume, resulting in a significantly lower concentration value when this value is rescaled. Moreover, if we consider the number of MENPs in a small volume at a w/v of 10%, i.e., 480 nanoparticles, the value is well below the dose–toxicity threshold, which is on the order of billions (Guduru et al. 2015; Kozielski et al. 2021). Thus, the concentrations used in our analysis are biocompatible. In addition to dosage, the spatial distribution of nanoparticles is also important, and our study allowed realistic quantification of the influence of this variable. Indeed, the literature indicates that several nanoparticles are required for a single nerve fiber to achieve effective stimulation and that direct physical contact with the dielectric membrane of the neuronal fiber is needed to fully benefit from their strong electric field (Guduru et al. 2015; Zhang et al. 2022). Owing to the rapid decay of the electric field distribution of a single MENP, the position between different nanoparticles is important for achieving summation effects and thus higher stimulation levels. Therefore, the configuration of the MENPs becomes another key factor in increasing their effectiveness in stimulation, being affected by both the relative position between the nanoparticles and their location relative to the active sites of the nerve fibers. Our results reflect all these observations and provide a fundamental and detailed quantification of what could realistically occur. Specifically, Figs. 3 and 4 show and quantify the distribution of the electric field within the volume enclosing the distributed NRs and on the outer surface facing the nerve fiber, respectively. As expected, owing to the rapid decay of the electric field generated by the nanoparticles, lower electrical stimulation is sensed on the surface of the small volume rather than within the volume. Thus, when comparing Figs. 3 and 4, the results show that the perceived electric field distribution is reasonably greater within the small volume than on its external surface at a fixed NR concentration, with the corresponding variability of the results increasing from within the volume to its outer surface. However, when considering each scenario individually, i.e., Figs. 3 and 4 separately, the probability of NRs being distributed close to each other increases with increasing NR concentration. This results in an increased electric field perceived by the nerve and a contemporary decrease in its variability. Similarly, the electric potential along the fiber length in contact with the NR-doped volume exhibits a similar trend (Table 2): as the NR concentration increases, so does the likelihood of their random distribution near the neuronal membrane. This results in a higher maximum electrical potential and a greater percentage of fiber length experiencing a high electrical potential value. Thus, our results highlight how at higher concentrations, NRs are more likely to localize near the active sites of neuronal fibers, thereby increasing their stimulation capacity.

Highly nonlinear neuronal dynamics are well known to be strongly influenced by specific neuronal biophysics as well as the delivered electrical signal. In this study, multiplication factors were used to indirectly assess neuron excitability by quantifying how much the electrical stimulation exerted by the nanoparticles needs to be amplified to elicit a neuronal response. These dimensionless numbers, which are linearly correlated with both the ME coefficient and the external bias magnetic field, provide a quick and direct characterization of the interaction between MENPs and nerves. However, they do not clearly indicate how these physical variables need to be adjusted to enhance stimulation. Therefore, future studies are needed to optimize these variables to physically increase the electric potential amplitude exerted by the MENPs by the multiplication factor as we advance the engineering of this technology. The evaluation of the electrical responses of the peripheral axons (Fig. 5) reveals a decrease in the amplitude of the multiplication factor and its variability as the NR concentration increases. This occurs regardless of neuronal dynamics, thus reflecting the strong influence of nanoparticle concentration on stimulation capability. When different types of neuronal biophysics are compared, our results confirm that small-diameter myelinated fibers require higher gain coefficients than large-diameter myelinated fibers, regardless of the NR concentration (Figs. 5 and 6). This contradicts the size principle of natural motor neuron recruitment (Henneman et al., 1965). As expected, a significant difference is observed when myelinated and unmyelinated axons are compared because of differences in axonal excitability (Figs. 5 and 6). At low concentrations, there is a greater probability of having a NR near an active site for unmyelinated fibers than for myelinated fibers because the former are excitable everywhere, whereas the latter are only excitable at Ranvier’s nodes. This results in lower multiplication factor amplitudes and less variability for unmyelinated fibers than for myelinated fibers (Fig. 5). As the NR concentration increases, the probability of a NR being near an active site also increases for myelinated fibers. This increased probability, coupled with the greater excitability of myelinated fibers than unmyelinated fibers, yields higher gain coefficients for unmyelinated fibers than for myelinated fibers, which is consistent with the literature (Lubba et al. 2019). With respect to the delivered stimulus, to match the slow dynamics of neurons (Pardo and Khizroev 2022), it was assumed that the NRs elicit low-frequency stimulation of the surrounding neural tissue, specifically at a frequency of 100 Hz, which resulted in a range of frequencies used in previous in vivo and in vitro studies (Guduru et al. 2015; Kim et al. 2025; Kozielski et al. 2021; Nguyen et al. 2021). However, establishing an appropriate encoding strategy that is tailored to a specific application is highly important. Examples include improving the usability and acceptance of artificial limbs, simulating natural tactile sensations that enable proper control of the prosthesis (Formento et al. 2020; Tan et al. 2014; Valle et al. 2018, 2024), or treating specific pathological conditions such as epilepsy or depression through vagal nerve stimulation or chronic pain treatment (Austelle et al. 2022; González et al. 2019; Kaye et al. 2021). Such encoding strategy modulation can be achieved by properly tuning the magnitude and frequency of the externally applied bias magnetic field (Kozielski et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2022), and future studies are needed to properly modulate such stimulation parameters.

Finally, compared with our initial study (Galletta et al. 2024), the current work significantly advances the modeling of interactions between MENPs and peripheral nerves. Specifically, we more precisely quantified the electrical quantities by modeling MENPs with random positions and different concentrations within a small tissue volume rather than treating them as agglomerates. Additionally, rather than focusing on characterizing the response of a single peripheral fiber, we conducted an initial characterization of the activation patterns of both myelinated and unmyelinated fibers within the peripheral nerve.

Limitations

In our study, we assumed that the nanoparticles were distributed within a small, parallelepiped-shaped volume. This limited our analysis of the stimulation capability of MENPs to a small volume of tissue and a short length of peripheral fibers that were exposed to nanoparticle stimulation. For example, only one Ranvier node was exposed to MENP stimulation in myelinated fibers. While this assumption improved characterization of the MENPs’ stimulation capability and allowed us to investigate important variables, such as nanoparticle distribution and concentration, future studies are needed to develop increasingly realistic models of nerve-nanoparticle interactions and provide more accurate information for experimental setups.

Conclusions

This study focused on modeling the electrical stimulation of peripheral nerve tissue by novel magnetoelectric nanoparticles, addressing for the first time the influence of nanoparticle concentration and distribution while considering the wide variability in peripheral nerve anatomy. As an assumption in our modeling, the nanoparticles were assumed distributed within a small portion of nerve tissue. Our results revealed that both the concentration and the configuration of the nanoparticles play key roles in the definition of electrical contact with peripheral nerves, influencing coverage for a specific motor task or sensation. An increasing stimulation capacity is observed with increasing nanoparticle concentration, and the variability at a fixed concentration is caused by the stochastic displacement of the nanoparticles. In addition, the importance of optimizing the modulation of stimulation parameters, which could be targeted to specific peripheral fibers, was highlighted as a direct consequence of the strong influence of axonal excitability.

Overall, our results highlight the potential of MENPs to provide wireless, minimally invasive, selective and more natural mimicking neural stimulation. The nanometric scale and the modulation of all the variables involved could allow the design of personalized neural interfaces, which could be tailored according to the patient anatomy as well as the specific application. However, future studies are needed to explore more realistic settings and optimize the interaction between nanoparticles and nerve tissue. Our work represents a first effective contribution in this direction.
