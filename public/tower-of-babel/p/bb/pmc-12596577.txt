Particle Therapy in the Multimodal Treatment for Locally Advanced Malignancies of the Nasal Cavity and Paranasal Sinus—Single Institute Experience
Authors: Fatima Frosan Sheikhzadeh, Gertrud Schmich, Niklas Recknagel, Edgar Smalec, Tasneim Abdelrahman Mohamed, Vicky Soborun, Kerem Tuna Tas, Philipp Lishewski, Klemens Zink, Khaled Elsayad, Fabian Eberle, Hilke Vorwerk, Thomas Held, Boris A Stuck, Sebastian Adeberg, Ahmed Gawish
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeParticle therapy with protons or carbon ions is a promising method for treating locally advanced nasal cavity and paranasal sinus carcinomas (NPSC). This study evaluates the clinical outcomes and toxicities of patients treated with carbon ion radiation therapy with a boost (CIRT-B) at our institution.Methods and MaterialsPatients with NPSC who received combined treatment with CIRT-B and intensity modulated radiation therapy were considered. Local control (LC) and survival rates were estimated using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and proportional hazards models.ResultsBetween 2016 and 2023, a total of 66 patients were included in the analysis. Of these, 53 patients (80%) received primary radiation therapy as their first-line treatment, while 13 patients (20%) underwent salvage reirradiation for recurrent disease. The median total dose administered in the upfront radiation therapy group was 74 Gy (range, 70-78 Gy relative biological effectiveness), whereas the median total dose in the salvage radiation therapy group was 45 Gy (range, 39-51 Gy relative biological effectiveness). The median duration of LC was 21 months in both the upfront and salvage groups. The overall median LC across all patients was 14 months. The 2-year LC rate was 73% for the upfront radiation therapy group and 52% for the salvage radiation therapy group, with a statistically significant difference (P = .038). In terms of early toxicities, there were no grade 4 adverse events reported.ConclusionsCIRT-B combined with photon radiation therapy is an effective and safe treatment for advanced NPSC in both primary and salvage settings.

Introduction

Locally advanced nasal cavity and paranasal sinus carcinoma (NPSC) are rare cancers, representing less than 5% of all head and neck tumors, with an incidence of about 0.55 cases per 100,000 people per year.1,2 They comprise a diverse histologic group with various cell types. This is supported by the source that discusses the different types of sinonasal carcinomas, highlighting their heterogeneity and the various cell types involved in these malignancies.3 Lymph node metastases in sinonasal tumors negatively impact prognosis and necessitate adjustments in treatment to enhance survival rates.4 Early detection and surgical removal of affected lymph nodes are crucial for successful treatment outcomes.5

Modern photon therapy techniques like intensity modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) achieve improved tumor control in sinonasal cancer compared to conventional radiation therapy (RT) by sparing healthy tissue while simultaneously enhancing patient survival rates and quality-of-life.6 IMRT typically achieves 2-year local control (LC) rates of 75% to 85% and 5-year disease-free survival of 70% to 80% for advanced NPSC.6,7 However, particle therapy delivers equivalent therapeutic doses while providing superior sparing of surrounding healthy tissues through the Bragg peak phenomenon. This unique physical property allows precise energy deposition at the target depth with minimal exit dose, offering a significant advantage over conventional photon RT,8 potentially reducing toxicity and improving outcomes for patients with sinonasal malignancies. Carbon ion radiation therapy (CIRT) shows promising results in treating locally advanced malignant sinonasal tumors.5,9, 10, 11 Recurrences in the treatment of sinonasal malignancies have a negative influence on prognosis, and early detection, as well as aggressive therapy, are essential to improve patient survival.12 CIRT might offer an effective and safe treatment option for patients with locally recurrent nasopharyngeal carcinoma who have not responded to conventional treatments.13,14 Similarly, proton therapy (PT) is a promising treatment option for patients with tumors of the nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses. It is advantageous for reirradiation and treating locally advanced tumors.15

In this study, we aim to investigate the clinical outcomes and toxicities in patients with NPSC treated with particle therapy combined with IMRT.

Methods and Materials

This analysis investigated patients treated with particle therapy between 2016 and 2023. Sixty-six patients were included in the analysis. The primary endpoints were LC, progression-free survival (PFS), and overall survival (OS). Secondary endpoints included early toxicity and organ preservation. Survival was evaluated using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and proportional hazards models.

Patients underwent computed tomography (CT) simulation using a dedicated long shoulder mask for motion reduction with a slice thickness of 3 mm. The visible tumor was defined as the volume of the contrast-enhanced primary tumor (gross tumor volume; GTV-A) observed in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and other imaging modalities. Pathologic lymph nodes were contoured as a separate GTV (GTV-LN). The clinical target volume for CIRT boost (CTV-B) was defined by an isotropic 5-mm expansion of the GTV-B and manually adjusted to exclude sensitive organs. The CTV for photon irradiation (CTV-E) encompassed the extended target volume, including CTV-B, typical tumor spread pathways, and in advanced stages of lymph node involvement, elective lymph node regions (facial, buccal IX, Ib, II, III). The clinical target volume for simultaneous integrated boost (CTV-SIB) was generated through an isotropic expansion of 5 to 7 mm from the GTV-SIB. Subsequently, the planning target volume (PTV) was derived by applying a uniform 3-mm margin expansion to each corresponding CTV.

CIRT was performed using carbon ions (C12) with an active raster scanning technique.16 Two to 4 noncoplanar treatment beams were employed, with daily image guidance using orthogonal X-rays and weekly CT-based recalculations. Photon RT was delivered with 50 Gy to PTV-P and PTV-E (single dose 2 Gy), five times weekly using a Varian TrueBeam linear accelerator (with motorized multileaf collimator, 0.5 cm leaf width) at the Department of Radiotherapy and Radiation Oncology, with daily cone beam CT-guided positioning. The prescribed dose was normalized to the median dose of the target volume, ensuring PTV coverage within 95% to 107% of the isodose.

The particle beam dose was calculated as a photon-equivalent dose in Gray (Gy), accounting for the relative biological effectiveness (RBE) determined by existing radiobiological studies. RBE values were set at 1.1 for protons and between 2 and 3.7 for carbon ions, depending on the linear energy transfer (LET) of carbon ions.17

Patients with advanced tumors, positive lymph nodes, and subtotal resection status (Table 1) received a CIRT boost of 24 Gy (RBE) (single dose 3 Gy [RBE]) to PTV-B in 8 fractions, along with weekly chemotherapy. In the definitive setting, CIRT was administered to the extended tumor region (CTV-P) with up to 45 Gy (RBE) (single dose 3 Gy [RBE]), followed by a boost to the residual tumor (GTV-B) with 15 Gy (RBE) (single dose 3 Gy [RBE]), resulting in a total dose of 60 Gy (RBE) in 20 fractions. For salvage reirradiation with carbon ions, 39 to 51 Gy (RBE) was delivered in 13 to 17 fractions (single dose 3 Gy [RBE]). The GTV-R was defined based on contrast-enhanced MRI-T1 and received an anatomically adapted safety margin of 3 to 5 mm (CTV-R). Previous treatment plans were fused with the current target volume, considering the cumulative biologically effective dose for organs at risk.

Study design and population selection

This retrospective analysis examined CIRT outcomes across different treatment scenarios within our institutional experience. While primary RT and salvage reirradiation represent distinct clinical paradigms, both populations were included to comprehensively evaluate CIRT applications in sinonasal malignancies. This approach reflects real-world clinical practice where CIRT is used across the treatment spectrum. Separate subgroup analyses were performed to account for differences between treatment scenarios, acknowledging the inherent limitations of combining these populations.

Treatment selection criteria

IMRT at a dose of 50 Gy combined with a CIRT boost of 24 Gy RBE has been used for advanced tumors (T3-4) that had incomplete resection, positive or close surgical margins, lymph node involvement, or in patients with high-risk histologies (N = 46). In contrast, primary CIRT alone, delivering 60 Gy RBE over 20 fractions, has been applied to patients with unresectable tumors that are near critical organs (N = 7). Additionally, salvage CIRT, with doses ranging from 39 to 51 Gy RBE, has been administered for local recurrence following prior RT (N = 13).

CIRT, because of its high LET and RBE ranging from 1.1 to 4.0, enables substantial hypofractionation.10,11 Standard CIRT protocols typically employ 2–3 Gy (RBE) per fraction. The National Institute of Radiological Sciences has established a regimen of 57.6 Gy (RBE) delivered in 16 fractions (3.6 Gy per fraction) for head and neck tumors.11

Conventional photon-based IMRT typically delivers 66 to 68 Gy for definitive treatment.6 However, the conventional dose levels of ≤70 Gy often yield poor LC in advanced sinonasal tumors.8 In contrast, the present study uses 74 Gy (RBE), representing both a physical and biological dose escalation. Combining a higher total dose and larger fraction size results in a significantly increased biologically effective dose compared to conventional 2 Gy fractionation.10,11

Follow-up examinations were conducted quarterly during the first 3 years after particle therapy and at 6-month intervals thereafter. Routine follow-up included physical examinations, endoscopy, diagnostic imaging (CT and/or MRI), and blood tests. Acute and late toxicities were assessed using the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (AEs), version 4.03.

To further evaluate treatment-related toxicities, we used individual logistic regression models to assess the influence of tumor recurrence, previous surgeries and RTs, age, RT-modality, sex, and the cumulative RT dose on the occurrence of AEs ≥ III according to National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for AEs criteria. Multivariate analysis models models were not employed because of the small sample size and low total number of AEs ≥ III.

Statistical analysis

OS was calculated from the start of RT to death or the last follow-up. PFS was measured from therapy initiation to disease progression or death, with patients alive and disease-free at the last follow-up censored. LC was defined from the start of therapy to local recurrence. Continuous variables were presented as medians with ranges (minimum to maximum), and categorical variables were reported as frequencies with proportions. Group comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. Survival curves for OS, PFS, and LC were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, with the log-rank test applied for univariate analysis of factors such as age, sex, primary tumor location, surgical indication, primary GTV (GTV-primary), radiation type, and EQD10/2. All statistical tests were 2-sided, with a P value < .05 considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (Version 23; IBM).

Results

Patient demographics and tumor characteristics

Our analysis included 66 sinonasal tumor patients (Table 1). The median age was 64 years (range, 37-87). The median follow-up period was 25 months. Males constituted 62% of the cohort, while females represented 38%. The most frequent tumor location was the nasal cavity (32%), followed by the maxillary sinus (21%). Most patients presented with advanced disease, with 68% (n = 45) classified as T4 stage and 68% (n = 45) showing evidence of bone infiltration. Only 15% of patients had nodal involvement. Table 2.

Treatment modalities

Curative resection was performed in 44% of patients (Table 1). The radiation treatment cohort comprised 53 patients (80%) receiving their first course of RT and 13 patients (20%) undergoing reirradiation after primary RT. The most common protocol combined IMRT and CIRT (C12) in 67% of patients. Among these, 40 patients received 24 Gy (RBE) C12 in 8 fractions and 50 Gy photon radiation. Exclusive C12 therapy (60 Gy [RBE] in 20 fractions) was used in 7 cases. Lymphatic drainage pathways were irradiated in 22 patients (33%).

Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed significant prognostic factors. T4 tumors showed inferior LC compared to T1-3 (P = .029), and bone invasion was associated with worse PFS (P = .047).

Survival analysis

The median follow-up was 25 months. For the whole cohort, the 1-year OS rate was 85% (95% CI, 77.2%-94%), the 2-year OS rate was 71% (95% CI, 60.1%-84%), and the 3-year OS rate was 66% (95% CI, 53%-81%). Patients receiving primary RT (n = 53) demonstrated superior outcomes. At the 1-year, 2-year, and 3-year follow-up intervals, this group demonstrated promising PFS and LC outcomes, reflective of the efficacy of the treatment protocol. PFS rates were 79%, 76%, and 70%, respectively, underscoring the durability of disease control over time (Fig. 1). The group who underwent RT of elective lymph nodes showed a better PFS than those who did not receive it; rates of 3-year PFS were 76% vs 66%, respectively. Similarly, LC rates were consistently high, with 80%, 78% and 73% achieved at 1, 2, and 3 years, respectively. These findings highlight the robustness of upfront RT strategies, particularly in advanced sinonasal tumors, where local recurrence and progression remain significant clinical challenges (Fig. 1). The Kaplan-Meier curve in Figure 2 demonstrated no significant difference in progression in the field between the patients who underwent definitive RT and the patients who underwent resection with residual tumors.

Patients receiving primary RT demonstrated superior median OS (not reached vs 18.5 months, P = .032), PFS (24 months vs 14 months, P = .041), and LC (21 vs 14 months, P = .038) compared to those undergoing salvage reirradiation. Local recurrence was the most common failure (20%, n = 13), followed by distant metastases (13%, n = 9) and regional recurrence (9%, n = 6).

In the case of salvage RT, the overall model was statistically significant (Omnibus test: χ² = 4.281 P = .039), indicating that the predictors contributed to the explanation of the outcome variable. The identified risk factors for poorer outcomes in salvage RT included prior surgery (OR = 2.41, 95% CI, 1.18-4.93, P = .016), higher T-stage at recurrence (T3-4 vs. T1-2, OR = 1.87, 95% CI, 1.09-3.21, P = .024), and early relapses (within <12 months, OR = 1.64, 95% CI, 1.05-2.58, P = .031). However, the model's goodness-of-fit measures suggested only modest explanatory power (Cox and Snell R² = 0.063, Nagelkerke R² = 0.084).

However, when examining the individual predictor, the reirradiation did not influence the development of higher-grade AEs (P = .056). All other possible predictors could not establish statistically significant models when evaluated with the Omnibus test and were therefore abandoned. It is possible that a larger study population could have sufficed to show significant predictors of AEs ≥ III, especially in the case of RT in recurrent disease.

Side effects

One patient discontinued treatment after 30 Gy. All patients experienced grade 1 and 2 toxicities. During RT, 23% required hospitalization for mucosal side effects, 32% required feeding tubes (PEG=Percutaneous Endoscopic Gastrostomy), and weight loss was documented in 15% of patients (Table 2). Acute grade 3 toxicities included mucositis (41%), xerostomia (37%), dermatitis (21%), and dysphagia (65%). At 3 months, grade 3 toxicities decreased to xerostomia (30%), dysphagia (0%), and mucositis (5%). No grade 4 toxicities occurred.

Univariate Cox regression identified several independent prognostic factors (Table 3). Salvage reirradiation was consistently associated with inferior outcomes across all endpoints: LC (HR = 2.4, 95% CI, 1.2-4.8, P = .038), PFS (HR = 2.3, P = .041), and OS (HR = 2.5, 95% CI, 1.3-4.9, P = .032) compared to primary treatment. Advanced T4 classification showed an increased risk for local failure (HR = 2.2, P = .029), disease progression (HR = 2.5, P = .018), and death (HR = 2.3, P = .025). Mucosal melanoma demonstrated the most adverse prognosis across all survival endpoints compared to squamous cell carcinoma.

Discussion

The findings from this study reinforce the efficacy and safety of state-of-the-art particle beam RT (PBRT), particularly CIRT, as a viable option for sinonasal tumors. The high LC and PFS rates observed align with the increasing evidence that PBRT offers superior outcomes for complex sinonasal malignancies because of its unique physical and radiobiological advantages.

The observed survival outcomes are consistent with prior studies reporting excellent LC and survivals with PBRT for sinonasal malignancies.18,9 Patel et al19 and other meta-analyses highlight PBRT’s advantage in achieving better locoregional control and OS when compared with photon-based approaches, such as IMRT, particularly in anatomically complex regions.20 The superiority of PBRT is attributed to its ability to deliver highly conformal doses with a steep fall-off, minimizing exposure to surrounding critical structures.10,21 CIRT's high LET and RBE also enhance cell-killing efficacy, particularly in radioresistant histologies such as adenoid cystic carcinoma (ACC) and melanoma.19, 22

The superior dose distribution of CIRT, with its characteristic Bragg peak enabling precise target coverage while sparing critical structures like optic nerves and brainstem, was crucial for treatment success.10,21

Dagan et al9 evaluated the outcomes of 84 patients with sinonasal cancer treated with PT between 2007 and 2013. Most (87%) received adjuvant PBRT following surgery, and 13% underwent definitive PBRT. Advanced disease (T3/T4) was present in 94% of patients, and common histologies included olfactory neuroblastoma (27%), SCC (squamous cell carcinoma) (26%), and ACC (17%). The median dose of PT was 73.8 Gy (RBE), and most patients (75%) received concurrent chemotherapy, primarily low-dose weekly cisplatin. At 3 years, LC, neck control, disease-free survival, OS, and cause-specific survival rates were 83%, 94%, 63%, 68%, and 70%, respectively. gross total resection (GTR), followed by PT, achieved the highest LC (90%), while patients with gross disease at the time of PT had significantly lower LC (59%). High-grade histology was associated with worse outcomes and increased risk of distant metastases.

Our retrospective study and the prospective IMRT-HIT-SNT trial both demonstrate that combined photon-CIRT is effective and safe for sinonasal malignancies, with acceptable toxicity profiles and promising tumor control rates.23 Our results should be interpreted in the context of recent prospective and large-scale retrospective studies evaluating particle therapy for sinonasal malignancies. The prospective phase 2 study HIT-SNT demonstrated promising outcomes with combined photon-CIRT in a cohort of 80 patients, reporting 2-year LC rates of 81% and OS of 85%.23 These findings align closely with our observed 2-year LC rate of 73% and OS of 77%, despite differences in study design and patient selection.

A key feature of our cohort was the high proportion of advanced-stage patients, with 68% presenting with T4-stage tumors. Our analysis revealed no significant difference in LC between definitive and postsurgical RT for residual disease. This finding suggests that primary particle RT may be a valid treatment option in cases where only incomplete resection (R1/R2) is achievable. However, our study cannot address whether complete surgical resection (R0) would provide superior outcomes compared to primary RT, because our cohort did not evaluate this comparison.

Complementary evidence supporting the long-term efficacy of the combined modality comes from Akbaba et al,24 who reported outcomes in 227 patients with sinonasal ACC. Their impressive 5-year LC rate of 85% and OS of 78% demonstrate the durability of the treatment response, especially for ACC histology. Variations in outcomes between their study and ours may be attributed to our more heterogeneous histologic cohort, where differences in radiosensitivity likely influenced treatment response.

Treatment protocols across these studies show both important similarities and differences. Our study used a median total dose of 74 Gy (RBE), comparable to the standardized doses in the HIT-SNT protocol,23 although we observed greater variation in fractionation schemes. The carbon ion boost technique remained consistent across all studies, though the prospective nature of HIT-SNT allowed for more standardized delivery parameters. Our integration of IMRT aligns with current strategies to optimize dose distribution, which is supported by both comparative studies. The toxicity profile observed in our study aligns well with previous reports. Acute grade 3 toxicities, including mucositis (55%), xerostomia (44%), and dysphagia (41%), were comparable to those reported in both the HIT-SNT study and the Akbaba series.23,24 The absence of grade 4 toxicities across studies validates the favorable safety profile of particle therapy in this challenging anatomic region. Patient selection criteria represent an important point of differentiation between the studies. The prospective nature of the HIT-SNT study enabled stricter inclusion criteria,23 while our cohort included a higher proportion of salvage cases (20% vs. 0% in HIT-SNT). The Akbaba series focused exclusively on ACC histology,24 offering valuable insights into this specific subgroup, while our study included multiple histologies, providing broader applicability but potentially diluting histology-specific results.

A recent study from Florida demonstrated effective long-term disease control and acceptable toxicity profiles in a cohort of 143 patients with predominantly advanced-stage and high-grade disease. Patients achieving GTR before RT had significantly better LC (87%) compared to those with gross disease (57%). This finding differs from our observation and highlights the potential benefit of complete tumor resection when achievable. However, it is important to note that in our cohort, we primarily compared definitive RT to postsurgical RT for residual disease (incomplete resection), rather than to patients with GTR. The Florida study underscores the importance of achieving GTR for optimal outcomes while balancing the risks of severe toxicity.9

This is similar to the findings by Dagan et al, who reported better LC rates (90%) with GTR. This suggests that the high biological effectiveness of carbon ion therapy may help overcome the traditional prognostic disadvantage of positive surgical margins.

Reirradiation poses unique challenges because of increased risks of toxicity and decreased efficacy. Patients in this study undergoing reirradiation exhibited significantly poorer survival outcomes (median OS: 18 months) than those receiving primary RT. Fan et al15 and Koto et al18 reported similar inferior outcomes for reirradiation, with reduced OS and PFS rates.15,18 However, salvage reirradiation using CIRT remains feasible for selected patients, particularly those with limited locoregional recurrences.15,13 Novel fractionation schedules and integration with systemic therapies could further enhance the role of reirradiation.

PBRT demonstrated a favorable safety profile in this study, with no grade 4 acute toxicities and limited late toxicities.14 This finding corroborates earlier reports from Mizoe et al11 and Jensen et al,10 who noted significantly reduced rates of severe toxicities with PBRT compared to IMRT. The Bragg peak effect of PBRT ensures precise dose delivery, which is crucial in sinonasal tumors near critical organs such as the optic nerve, brainstem, and cranial nerves.25,26 Late toxicities, such as osteonecrosis and vision impairment observed in this study, were rare but highlighted the need for cautious dose planning in advanced and recurrent diseases. Duprez et al20 and van de Water et al21 emphasize the importance of meticulous patient selection and advanced treatment planning, particularly for tumors with orbital or skull base invasion. Our findings suggest that further refinement of planning techniques, including pencil beam scanning technology, may mitigate toxicity risks.

This study reinforces the need for multimodal treatment approaches for sinonasal malignancies, particularly those with advanced-stage or high-risk features. Surgery remains the cornerstone of treatment for resectable cases; however, achieving GTR is often challenging because of the region’s anatomic complexity. Adjuvant PBRT is an effective option to complement surgical resection and improve outcomes in cases with close or positive margins.18,27

A large cohort by Weixu Hu et al14 evaluated the safety and efficacy of PBRT in treating sinonasal malignancies at the Shanghai Proton and Heavy Ion Center. A cohort of 111 patients with nonmetastatic sinonasal malignancy received either definitive or salvage PBRT, using proton RT, CIRT, or a combination. The majority of patients (86%) presented with advanced disease (T3/4), and outcomes were assessed over a median follow-up of 20 months.

The failure patterns observed in this study, with local recurrences being the most common, mirror those reported in prior studies.5,18 This underscores the importance of achieving robust LC with PBRT, particularly in T4 tumors and those with bone infiltration. While distant metastases were less frequent, their occurrence highlights the need for integrated systemic therapy approaches, particularly in high-risk histologies like melanoma and sarcoma.15

Future research should focus on prospective trials comparing PBRT with advanced photon-based therapies, such as adaptive RT and IMRT, to delineate its relative benefits further.7 Efforts to explore novel systemic therapies, including immunotherapy, combined with PBRT may also improve outcomes, particularly in radioresistant and metastatic cases. Additionally, international collaboration will be essential to develop standardized protocols and address the rarity of sinonasal malignancies through multicenter trials.18,27

Similar to our approach, ongoing prospective trials evaluate particle therapy's role in head and neck cancers. The ACCO (Adenoid Cystic Carcinoma and Carbon Ion Only Irradiation) study is a particularly relevant example, because it investigates the efficacy of CIRT for adenoid cystic carcinomas of the head and neck region. This randomized phase 2 trial compares carbon ion therapy alone with a bimodal treatment approach (carbon ions plus photons), similar to the treatment strategy employed in our study. While focusing specifically on adenoid cystic carcinomas, which comprised 21.2% of our patient cohort, the ACCO trial’s endpoints of LC, PFS, and quality-of-life align with our outcome measures. The results of this trial will provide valuable comparative data to validate our findings regarding the efficacy of combined modality treatment approaches in radioresistant head and neck malignancies.28

Our analysis has several important limitations. First, this retrospective study design introduces selection bias and limits systematic AE assessment. Second, the heterogeneous patient population with diverse histologies, stages, and treatment approaches may confound outcome analyses. Third, the median follow-up of 25 months is insufficient for mature LC and late toxicity evaluation. Fourth, combining primary and salvage populations while providing comprehensive experience represents different treatment paradigms with distinct therapeutic goals. Fifth, the small salvage group (n = 13) limits statistical power for robust subgroup analyses. Finally, the absence of standardized quality-of-life assessments represents a significant limitation. Future prospective studies with extended follow-up periods and larger patient cohorts must validate these findings and establish definitive treatment guidelines. Comparative effectiveness research comparing CIRT directly with modern photon techniques in randomized controlled trials would provide the most substantial evidence for treatment selection in this challenging patient population.

Conclusions

CIRT combined with photon RT is an effective and safe treatment for advanced NPSC in both primary and salvage settings. This approach shows manageable toxicity levels and offers a viable option for locally advanced sinonasal cancers, with promising outcomes in various clinical scenarios.
