Examining the thoughts and experiences of repeatedly revised knee arthroplasty patients—a qualitative study
Authors: Julie Heegaard, Christina Sadolin Damhus, Anders Odgaard, Michael Mørk Petersen, Alexandra Brandt Ryborg Jønsson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKnee arthroplasty is a common surgical treatment aimed at improving function and reducing pain. While generally successful, some patients experience complications or dissatisfaction, leading to a sequence of revision surgeries. These revisions are less successful than primary surgeries and carry risks. This study explores the impact on daily life of knee revisions for pain and poor knee function, patient motivations, and experiences, aiming to inform better decision-making.MethodsA qualitative approach was used to investigate the experiences and thoughts of multiply revised knee patients with pain and poor function as dominating indications. We conducted 12 semi-structured single interviews with patients from three tertiary referral centres. Patients who had undergone two or more knee revisions, were cognitively competent and able to communicate in Danish were considered for inclusion. Patients who were revised because of infection or fracture were excluded from interviews.ResultsThe patient conversations highlighted the daily struggle to achieve what each of the patients identified as a normal existence. The study identified one main theme: striving for a normal life and two sub-themes: (1) the desire for an active life and (2) the hope for improvement. The themes highlight the patients’ wishes and thoughts in the period following surgery, what they find particularly challenging, and the underlying reasons for their willingness to undergo another revision. The results emphasize how patients’ strong wish for a physically and socially active life free from pain, combined with their high hopes for improvement and faith in the biomedical model, drives a willingness to undergo additional surgeries despite previous suboptimal outcomes.ConclusionThe study finds that patients’ desire for a normal life and hope for improvement influence their decision to undergo revisions despite the potential risks. The pain impacting their daily lives and a strong trust in biomedical solutions leads them to consider further surgeries. The results of the study contribute to a better understanding of this patient group and can be used for informed decision-making regarding revision surgery, ensuring that the offered surgery aligns with the patients’ needs.Trial registrationOur article does not report on health care intervention on human participants Clinical trial number: not applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09111-y.

Background

Knee arthroplasty is a common and generally successful procedure for individuals with severe knee degeneration, aiming to improve mobility, reduce pain, and enhance quality of life [1]. The operation is generally considered successful, and with an aging population, it is assumed that more and more patients will undergo this type of surgery [2–5]. Unfortunately, not all patients achieve the desired results. Some face well-defined complications like aseptic implant loosening, periprosthetic infection, instability, and periprosthetic fracture most often leading to revision surgery [2, 6, 7]. Others (around 10%) report persistent pain and poor knee function or dissatisfaction (10–20%) with uncertain or ill-defined causes [7, 8]. When these issues arise, patients often seek the surgeons’ advise regarding revision of the knee arthroplasty.

Even after the most thorough diagnostic investigations, there is often some uncertainty except for the most obvious diagnoses, and the patient may well be offered a revision if symptoms are sufficiently severe. A revision procedure may hence be suggested even when it is generally accepted that results after a revision procedure based on an uncertain indication are worse than compared to revisions with well-defined indications [5, 9, 10].

While many benefit from a revision procedure, others fail to achieve the expected improvements, and dissatisfaction rates after revision knee arthroplasty are higher, at approximately 30% [5, 11, 12]. Consequently, the diagnostic process may start again, and the first revision may only be the first of a series of subsequent revisions [12, 13]. This defines the multiply revised knee. The exact cause for each revision in a sequence of revisions may be either well- or ill-defined, but we suspect that there will most often be diagnostic elements that cannot clearly be defined.

With an aging population, the number of patients undergoing knee arthroplasty is expected to rise [2–5], and According to the Danish registry of knee arthroplasties an increasing incidence of revisions has also been projected [3, 4]. Performing multiple knee arthroplasty revisions poses risks for the patient and is costly for the healthcare system [4, 12, 14]. It is therefore important to avoid unnecessary surgeries. However, preventing multiple revisions is challenging without understanding what truly motivates patients to undergo repeated surgeries. While qualitative studies are becoming more common in healthcare research to explore complex questions and patient perspectives on satisfaction and pain, there is a knowledge gap in understanding patients who undergo multiple knee arthroplasty revisions [15–23].

We aim to shed light on multiply revised knee arthroplasty patients’ views by focusing on the following research questions using qualitative methods: (1) How do multiple knee arthroplasty revisions affect patients’ daily lives and independence? (2) What factors influence patients’ motivation to undergo multiple knee arthroplasty revisions? (3) What are the experiences of patients who have undergoneknee arthroplasty revisions?

Method

The study used a qualitative approach to investigate the experiences and thoughts of patients who had undergone multiple knee arthroplasty revisions. Comprising a junior medical doctor, two orthopaedic surgeons, a postdoctoral researcher in public health sciences, and a medical anthropologist , the research team ensured clinical relevance and methodological rigor throughout the study design, data collection, and analysis. All included patients had been seen and assessed by a senior orthopaedic surgeon specialised in knee arthroplasty surgery. We aimed to interview patients who had undergone two or more knee arthroplasty revisions due to pain, poor function, or other knee-related issues. Patients who were revised because of infection or periprosthetic fracture were not interviewed. This decision was based on our understanding that patients undergoing surgery due to life- or limb-threatening infection or a fracture do not face the same choice regarding whether to undergo revision surgery or not. Patients needed to be cognitively competent and able to communicate in Danish. All patients meeting these inclusion criteria were eligible for interviews, regardless of whether they were offered an additional revision after their consultation in the clinic or not. This was due to the study’s aim of exploring patients’ already lived experiences with previous revisions, rather than following them prospectively after another revision.

In the period from 24.10.2023 to 16.01.2024, ten patients were recruited for interviews interviews at Rigshospitalet – Copenhagen University Hospital . Additionally, one patient from Odense University Hospital and one from Aarhus University Hospital were contacted and interviewed between 20.02.2024 and 28.02.2024. During the interviews, patients were asked to complete questionnaires with demographic data (Table 1).

Patient demographics

An interview guide was specifically developed for the study and subsequently assessed and revised by the first author and two additional researchers (CSD and ABRJ) experienced in conducting qualitative interviews (see appendix). An English-language version of the final interview guide is available as a supplementary file. The guide was designed to include open-ended questions, ensuring that patients were not restricted in their responses. The guide included questions related to patients’ experiences before and after their surgeries, as well as inquiries about their daily lives and current well-being. We specifically asked patients to reflect on their past medical history and thoughts related to undergoing multiple revision surgeries. In total, twelve semi-structured interviews were conducted. Of the twelve, eight were conducted face-to-face, and four were conducted by telephone, based on patients’ preferences. The physical interviews were conducted immediately after the out-patient consultation in a private, undisturbed conversation room. The telephone interviews were scheduled at a time convenient for the patient, and the first author (JH) conducted the interviews. The first author had not been involved in the patients’ medical care or prior hospital experiences. The interviews were recorded and subsequently transcribed verbatim and reviewed by the first author.

Data analysis

All interviews were analysed using a qualitative thematic text analysis with an abductive approach [24]. The abductive approach ensured a flexible and open approach to the data, which was necessary to identify and understand the complex experiences and emotions of patients who had undergone multiple knee revisions.

The software NVivo was used for code categorization. The transcriptions were thoroughly reviewed to gain a deeper understanding of the data and identify initial themes. Subsequently, the data were coded by identifying recurring ‘units’ that could provide answers to research questions or were otherwise seen as significant. These units were then assigned codes. Similar or repetitive codes were grouped into subcategories. From these, relevant themes were identified that could address research questions and provide insights into other important perspectives discovered during the interviews. These themes represent central elements and aspects of patients’ experiences that were considered relevant for better understanding patients’ thoughts and motivation for undergoing repeated surgeries. The coded transcriptions were subsequently reviewed and reevaluated to confirm the identified themes and find relevant quotations.

The data analysis was conducted by the first author, with each step of the analysis process discussed between JH and ABRJ. During the analysis, researchers were mindful of their own biases and their potential impact on the analysis. Emphasis was therefore placed on continuously reflecting on the data and critically considering how the researchers’ own preconceptions might have influenced the results.

Ethics

The project was evaluated and approved by the Danish research ethics committee (record number: F-23061083). Informed consent to participate was obtained from all of the study participants. All participants signed a consent form and were informed that participation was voluntary, they would remain anonymous, and they could withdraw from the study at any time.

Results

We interviewed twelve patients with a mean age of 66 (range: 53–76) years, 5 males and 7 females, having undergone on average 4 (range: 2–5) knee revision arthroplasties and the last operations performed 14 months (range: 1–67) prior to the interview. An overview of individual patient demographics, number of revisions, and time from last revision to interview is provided in Table 1. The patient conversations highlighted the daily struggle to achieve what each of the patients identified as a normal existence. This is expressed through the main theme: ‘striving for a normal life’. Further, ‘striving for a normal life’ was divided into two themes, each highlighting patients’ experiences related to their condition: (1) the desire for an active life and (2) the hope for improvement (Fig. 1).

The active life

During the interviews, it became clear that patients aspired to lead an ‘active life’. For many, their active lifestyle was a central part of their identity and quality of life. Of course, the perception of what constitutes an active life can vary from person to person, but several aspects were consistent.

Patients shared how their lives and daily routines had been affected by their knee arthroplasty revisions, resulting in several limitations in their day-to-day activities that, for many, led to significant frustrations:“Of course there were some frustrations. It’s so frustrating to go through it all, and you think thoughts like, can you keep your job? Will this get better, and what will happen?” (Patient no. 2).

“Of course there were some frustrations. It’s so frustrating to go through it all, and you think thoughts like, can you keep your job? Will this get better, and what will happen?” (Patient no. 2).

Most patients reported struggling with pain, which significantly impacted their daily lives. The pain hindered their ability to independently manage everyday tasks, and some patients expressed the need for assistance from a partner or other family members when the pain became too much. They described difficulties in performing the same number of daily chores as before receiving a knee arthroplasty. Most could still manage cleaning and cooking (although some needed aids), but they experienced greater fatigue, took longer to complete tasks, and required more frequent breaks:“Now, everything takes much longer time. (…) The pain also means that if my sister calls and asks if we can meet tomorrow, I might say no, I can’t handle it. Whereas before, I would have said yes, of course.” (Patient no. 4).

“Now, everything takes much longer time. (…) The pain also means that if my sister calls and asks if we can meet tomorrow, I might say no, I can’t handle it. Whereas before, I would have said yes, of course.” (Patient no. 4).

The physical limitations also had a significant impact on patients’ social lives. This affected several patients to a particularly high degree, as they could no longer participate in everyday activities such as shopping with a daughter or going for a walk in the forest with family:“Well, you see, I can’t participate in, for example, when we go to our summer house and go for a walk there, I have to stay at home. So, it has come to the point where I’ve bought an electric scooter so I can be a little involved, but if we’re going somewhere to see something, well, that doesn’t work. There are many things I have to say no to. I simply can’t.” (Patient no. 2).

They described losing part of their ability to engage with the world around them and expressed a desire not to be isolated at home. (…) So many things have been limited in terms of what I could do, and it makes you feel lonely.” (Patient no. 7).

Seven patients mentioned that during their course, they had lost some of their work capacity and one explained that he had been able to keep his position, but with changes to his daily work tasks. While some patients had to change positions, others had to leave the job market altogether. These patients emphasized what a significant loss it had been not to continue their jobs. Some mentioned the financial consequences this had caused, but interestingly, it was the day-to-day social interactions with colleagues that patients expressed a particularly strong longing for. The participants pointed out how work provides the daily social circle that is so crucial for many:“I couldn’t stand not doing what I was really happy with and what I was good at. Hope for improvement

Patients approached their primary surgery with the expectation that their symptoms would cease, and their lives would improve:

“I had the expectation that everything would be fine.” (Patient no. 8).

When this subsequently didn’t prove to be the case, patients sought new doctors in the hope of reaching a stage where they could achieve pain relief and return to their normal lives:“You always hope it will get better each time, right? That it will be like it used to be.” (Patient no. 1).

“You always hope it will get better each time, right? That it will be like it used to be.” (Patient no. 1).

This idea of improvement was associated with patients’ strong trust in doctors and surgical procedures. Patients have big faith in the capabilities of modern medicine, and some believe that another operation will enable them to regain their previous function and quality of life. It seemed that some patients had high expectations of what could be achieved with another operation and trusted that it would improve their problems:“Interviewer: Can you remember what you thought about having to undergo reoperation?Patient: Well, I thought it was great. This time I’ll be lucky.” (Patient no. 7).

Patient: Well, I thought it was great. This time I’ll be lucky.” (Patient no. 7).

Many patients expressed an underlying belief that there must be something physically wrong with their knee that a new operation could correct. Several mentioned that their decision and desire to undergo further surgery were often driven by a feeling that their situation could not get any worse than what it already was and that yet another operation couldn’t possibly worsen their pain or function:“Yes, we’ve been positive all the way through. And, well, it can’t get worse. I just thought, well, it’s a choice between the plague and cholera. So, it was just like, ‘Well, if you can fix it and I can walk normally, then go for it.’.” (Patient no. 5).

They expressed that their situation was so unbearable that any change would be an improvement. This desire underlines the patients’ desperation for improved quality of life, despite the known risks associated with major surgery. And as our material show, despite these known risks, the patients’ desperation remains an overruling factor, and several patients don’t give much thought to the risks of revision surgery when deciding on yet another operation:“Well, there’s always a risk with surgery, whether it’s a major or minor operation, so yes, I’m familiar with that. But it wasn’t something I spent much energy thinking about. (…) Because my everyday life wasn’t particularly good, so…” (Patient no. 5).

Discussion

Our results show that the participating patients have a great wish for returning to a familiar life in which their physical and social activities are not limited by their knees. Their hope for recovery and for a normal life served as a driving force for undergoing another yet operation despite the known risks associated with the procedure. Many patients report that they cannot imagine how they can attain an acceptable everyday life without the help of another surgery and therefore tend to seek out such medical intervention. A major reason patients perceive their usual life as unattainable is due to the limitations caused by their chronic pain. This aligns well with what has previously been reported in studies concerning postoperative dissatisfaction among knee arthroplasty patients [2, 6, 18, 25–27]. In one qualitative study of primary knee arthroplasty patients, it was reported that half of the patients expressed difficulty adjusting to the pain, resulting in pain related distress, frustration and depression [21]. Likewise, a systematic review by Kahlenberg et al. argue that pain is one of the most significant factors for patient dissatisfaction after surgery [26].

The majority of patients in our study describe interpreting the pain as an indication of an underlying physical issue within the knee. In a qualitative study, Mahdi et al. similarly describe how post-surgical knee arthroplasty patients try to find a physical explanation for their pain speculating on issues like malpositioned implant, ligament damage, fracture, infection, thrombosis, or loosening. It can therefore be difficult for patients to understand why doctors cannot ‘cure’ their knee or alleviate their pain, which may be a contributing factor to patients holding on to the hope of improvement [28]. As our results show some patients consequently continue to seek doctors for surgery and accept it if offered, despite the risks of complications and possible worsening of their pain [29]. This hope for improvement that was expressed by the participating patients is likewise described in the qualitative study of post-surgical knee arthroplasty patients by Nyvang et al. [19]. They explain that while some of the participating patients began to accept that they might have to live with a painful knee others empathized how they were holding onto the hope that the knee could somehow improve as our results also show.

In our study the patients express that the chronic pain results in difficulties in returning to what they experience as their normal life. This loss of their active and social life is described as a difficult challenge and a reason for wishing to undergo additional surgery. These results resemble findings from other qualitative studies [20, 22, 23, 30]. Nicolson et al. explains how revised knee patients struggle with their personal and social identity after surgery [30]. In the study patients express that they are no longer able to be the person they used to be prior to their surgeries and likewise talk about their los of identity and social exclusion, leading to great frustration. Mahdi et al. similarly describe how participants from their study describe an inability to perform their normal daily and valued activities, leading to great disappointment and resulting in a feeling of limited independence [20].

Our study also offers new insights in showing that the majority of patients express perceptions that align with a biomedical understanding of good health being equal to absence of disease [28]. However, a few of the interviewed patients view their health and pain more as a balance between multiple factors, with well-being at the centre, resembling the arguments used by the bio-psycho-social model [31–35]. This has also been discussed by Jeffery et al. showing that variations in expressed acceptance of pain were not related to patients postoperative WOMAC pain score suggesting that psychological factors may play a role [21]. Some of the patients from our study explain how they have learned to live with their pain because of developed coping mechanisms to use in their everyday lives. The same is found by Jeffery et al. In their study some patients find it easier to cope with the pain compared to others. The patients from the study described that they had come to accept that the pain was a part of their life and that they had become accustomed. This aligns with findings from Meints et al., who showed that differences in how patients cope with their pain are associated with the reported intensity of the pain and even associated with the patients’ physical function [33]. They explain that patients who adopt an active coping strategy, i.e. taking personal control of their pain, tend to experience positive outcomes. In contrast, they argue that passive coping, where control is relinquished to others, such as the healthcare system, has been associated with increased pain. As a result, patients who actively manage their pain are more likely to adapt to it and may be less inclined to pursue repeated surgeries. Likewise, Edwards et al., showed that higher levels of self-efficacy are associated with lower pain intensity and physical limitations [36]. They argue that a sense of control can influence how patients perceive their own ability to manage and control their pain. Similarly , it has been suggested that higher self-efficacy can lead to greater overall post-surgical satisfaction in knee arthroplasty patients [23]. In our study, some patients mention that they are able to manage daily challenges by knowing what they can and cannot do because of their pain, providing such a sense of control. In contrast, individuals with low self-efficacy may feel more helpless regarding their pain and experience a loss of control. According to Edwards et al., this can lead to a higher dependency on more passive treatments, such as additional surgeries, similar to what we observe in our study [36, 37].

Unfortunately, post-surgical chronic pain has been described as a complex condition that can be difficult to treat [38, 39]. Therefore, if there is a chance of alleviating patients of their chronic knee pain with additional surgery or other medical intervention one should of course strive to help the patient by offering this. There are many known reasons for why a patient might need a revision surgery including aseptic loosening, infection, pain, stiffness or instability [2, 3, 5, 12]. However, indications for revision knee surgery can be difficult to determine, and it is not always clear which patients will benefit [40]. There is an understanding amongst orthopaedic surgeons that a revision surgery should not be recommended to patients with pain of an unknown origin. This is empathised by Sabah et al. who found that patients who underwent knee arthroplasty revisions for stiffness or unexplained pain had some of the poorest postoperative joint function and generally worse outcomes [10]. Likewise, Arndt et al. have found that patients undergoing knee arthroplasty revision for the indication of unexplained pain had worse results on PROMs and were less satisfied [9]. Therefore, multiple revision surgeries may not be the solution for complex chronic pain conditions. There is a need for thorough research into these surgical indications in order to more confidently assess which patients would benefit from revision surgery. This could lead to clearer classifications of the indications for revisions. Additionally, for those patients who are considered unlikely to benefit from another surgery further research into postoperative chronic pain and the clinical effect of multiple revisions on patients’ complex pain conditions would be of great value.

We should aim to identify the patients whose indications for revision have been shown to result in good outcomes. With this knowledge, the surgeon can more confidently align expectations with the patient and thereby improving the patient’s understanding of what is medically possible.

For those patients where the classification of indications does not provide a basis for expecting improvement from another operation, there is a need for alignment of surgical expectations. Patients should be informed that chronic pain can arise from a combination of physical and psychological factors and that a multidisciplinary approach may be the most effective course of treatment. However, care must be taken in communicating this knowledge. The clinician must not prematurely assume that these patients are aware of this complex understanding of pain from the bio-psycho-social model. This assumption may lead to the patient feeling neglected or as if their pain is not being taken seriously by medical professionals, which has previously been shown to be an issue for this group of patients [30].

Limitations

It is not the purpose of qualitative studies to generalize findings to the broader population. Consequently, the patients in this study may not fully represent all individuals within this patient group. However, qualitative interviews have allowed us to gain a deeper understanding of patients’ experiences and perspectives, an insight that would not have been accessible through traditional questionnaire surveys. We believe that the findings from such qualitative studies can provide a valuable foundation for future quantitative investigations of these phenomena.

It could have been relevant to include patients from more in an attempt to increase the diversity among the patients. However, it is relevant to mention that Rigshospitalet is a tertiary referral centre of the most complicated knee arthroplasty cases, including revisions. It is considered the largest centre in Denmark for this patient group, receiving patients from all socio-economic backgrounds and demographics.

During the analysis, the themes encountered by the first author were considered, and the author reflected on whether these were truly the patients’ intended meaning, or if it was the author who, due to their medical knowledge, was interpreting the subject differently. Bias was hereby attempted to be avoided by the first author by consciously and continuously reflecting on any potential presumptions that could have influenced the analysis. Additionally, the first author maintained close contact with the data throughout the process and reviewed the transcriptions multiple times to ensure that themes and patterns were not misunderstood or overinterpreted.

There are patients with a clear reason for failure of their primary knee arthroplasty, such as technical errors, where revision surgery is expected to result in good outcomes. However, some patients remain dissatisfied without a clear cause. For these patients, conditions like regional pain syndrome or other unknown pain aetiologies may be involved, leading to less predictable results after revision. This makes it difficult to generalise outcomes across all revision patients. In this study, all patients were assessed by a knee arthroplasty surgeon. However, no formal multidisciplinary assessment, such as involvement of a pain team or psychological evaluation, was conducted. We acknowledge that knowledge about the presence of pain syndromes or similar conditions would have strengthened the study; however, this was not the primary aim of our qualitative design. Similarly, while this study provides valuable insight into patients’ subjective experiences following multiple revision surgeries, it did not include a formal assessment of psychological health. Some patients expressed emotional distress that may reflect symptoms of depression. However, since no standardized screening tools were used, we cannot draw clinical conclusions in this regard. We do acknowledge that psychological factors such as depression developed pre- or postsurgical may be of relevance and importance when discussing the experiences of this patient group and to possibly differentiate between them. It is important to emphasise that despite some of our patients expressing sign of emotional distress many patients report high levels of satisfaction following revision surgery [11].

Several of the patients in this study were seen in the outpatient clinic specifically because they had been referred due to ongoing knee-related problems and were being assessed by a surgeon to determine whether further treatment was needed. This may have introduced a selection bias, as patients who had experienced good outcomes after their revision surgery were less likely to attend follow-up outpatient consultations, and therefore did not have the opportunity to be approached and included by our research team. However, the aim of this study was to explore the experiences of patients who had undergone multiple revision procedures, and it must be assumed that some of the reasons for repeated surgeries were linked to unsatisfactory outcomes — in contrast to those who did not require multiple revisions.

As seen in Table 1 the patients had undergone several revision surgeries. These had often been performed over a period of many years and across different hospitals or regions. Consequently, several had more than one surgical indication over time, and for some revision procedures, clinical notes were unavailable. Based on the available patient data from medical records and interviews, it is clear that none of the patients underwent revision surgery solely due to life- or limb-threatening indications. Furthermore, the available data showed that all patients had undergone at least one revision for a known and remediable cause, such as aseptic loosening or other mechanical complications. However, we cannot exclude that some patients who have undergone multiple revisions were revised at times due to a known and remediable cause and at other times because of an unknown aetiology like unexplained pain. While this made it unfeasible to categorize patients according to a single, clearly defined revision indication, we would have liked to do so, as it likely would have strengthened our study. However, we risked not providing an accurate representation when complete information about revision indication history was not available for all patients. Moreover, this was not the aim of our qualitative design, which focused on exploring patients’ overall lived experiences with multiple revision knee arthroplasties. Despite this limitation, we believe our results remain clinically relevant in guiding future patient-centered care. However, future research should aim to explore and compare patient experiences and outcomes across different surgical indications, as the underlying cause of revision may significantly influence both expectations and satisfaction.

Conclusion

The study has identified key factors driving patients’ decision to undergo multiple knee revisions, despite the risk of complications. Patients expressed that their desire to “strive for a normal life” and their “hope for improvement” were central factors in their decision to continue with revisions. The chronic pain and its impact on their daily lives and social interactions were recognised as such significant challenges that they could not dismiss the possibility of another surgery. This supports the patients’ strong trust in biomedical solutions, such as surgery, as a means of reducing their pain. They continue to believe that their problem is caused by a mechanical or biological problem that can be remedied. The results of the study contribute to a better understanding of this patient group, their experiences, and their motivation to continue with these complex operations. Hopefully, the results can be used for informed decision-making for both the patient and the surgeon when discussing and deciding on revision surgery, ensuring that the offered surgery aligns with the patients’ needs.
