Smoking paradox in coronary function and structure of acute ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction patients treated with primary percutaneous coronary intervention
Authors: Shanghong Zhang, Ziqiang Lin, Bingyan Yu, Jieliang Liu, Junguo Jin, Guang Li, Haojian Dong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Smoking paradox has generated inconsistent findings concerning the clinical prognosis of acute ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) patients, while providing limited insights into coronary anatomy and function which are crucial prognostic factors. Therefore, this study aimed to further investigate the existence of smoking paradox in coronary anatomy and function.MethodsThis study divided STEMI patients into smokers and non-smokers. Quantitative coronary angiography, angiography‑derived microcirculatory resistance (AMR) and quantitative flow ratio (QFR) were utilized to analyze coronary anatomy and function. These parameters were compared using multivariable analysis and propensity score matching. The clinical outcomes were evaluated using Kaplan-Meier curve and Cox regression.ResultsThe study included 1258 patients, with 730 in non-smoker group and 528 in smoker group. Smokers were significantly younger, predominantly male, and had fewer comorbidities. Without adjusting for confounders, smokers exhibited larger lumen diameter [2.03(1.45–2.57) vs. 1.90(1.37–2.49), P = 0.033] and lower AMR [244(212–288) vs. 260(218–301), P = 0.006]. After matching and multivariate adjustment, smokers exhibited inversely smaller lumen diameter [1.97(1.38–2.50) vs. 2.15(1.63–2.60), P = 0.002] and higher incidence of coronary microvascular dysfunction [233(53.9%) vs. 190(43.6%), P = 0.002], but showed similar AMR and clinical outcomes compared to non-smokers. There was no difference in QFR between two groups.ConclusionSmoking among STEMI patients undergoing pPCI was associated with smaller lumen diameter and higher occurrence of coronary microvascular dysfunction, although it had no further impact on clinical prognosis. The smoking paradox observed in coronary anatomy or function may be explained by younger age, gender, and lower prevalence of comorbidities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-024-04093-6.

Introduction

Smoking is a significant risk factor for cardiovascular diseases and has been proven to be associated with a higher mortality rate in ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) patients treated with primary percutaneous coronary intervention (pPCI) [1–4]. However, early research reported contradictory findings, suggesting that cigarette smoking was related to improved clinical outcomes in young patients with acute myocardial infarction [5, 6]. The smoking paradox, which is not yet fully understood, may be attributed to factors such as a younger age of onset, fewer risk factors, the potential enhancing effect of tobacco derivatives on antiplatelet drugs, and other potential mechanisms [3, 4, 7–9]. Furthermore, previous studies have primarily focus on clinical endpoints, such as new congestive heart failure, short-term survival, and mortality rate [4, 5, 10]. There is little research analyzing the smoking paradox from a more nuanced perspective of coronary anatomy and function, which are crucial pathophysiologic factors for prognosis. Quantitative flow ratio (QFR) as a novel approach for fast and non-invasive computation of fractional flow reserve, had been proved to be a significant predictor for adverse events in acute coronary syndrome cohort [11]. In the setting of STEMI, post-pPCI QFR in culprit vessels measured 7 days after onset could predict 2-year adverse events [12]. On the other hand, angiographic microvascular resistance (AMR) derived solely from a single angiographic view as a feasible computational alternative to pressure wire-based microvascular resistance, has been proven to have good diagnostic accuracy in assessing coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) [13]. The increasing post-PCI AMR was also correlated with vessel-oriented composite outcomes [14].

Thus, we use more accurate angiography and functional examinations based on quantitative coronary angiography (QCA), QFR and AMR to investigate the relationship among smoking, coronary structure, and functional outcomes. Our aim is to evaluate the existence of the smoking paradox and identify possible reasons for this phenomenon.

Methods

Study population and data collection

This retrospective observation study is a subgroup analysis of a multicenter, open-label, prospective cohort study titled “Outcomes in STEMI patients with high thrombus burden treated by deferred vs immediate stent implantation in primary percutaneous coronary intervention: a prospective cohort study” (registered at www.chictr.org.cn, ChiCTR1800019923). The larger study was conducted in four cardiovascular centers in South China. Patients were enrolled based on the following exclusion criteria: (1) patients with left-main culprit lesions; (2) patients treated with intravenous thrombolysis before PCI; (3) patients with known rheumatic diseases. All data were collected from patients’ electronic medical records and the authors conducted telephone follow-up as well.

Definition and laboratory examination

STEMI was defined as the presence of chest pain symptoms along with at least one of the following two criteria: (1) typical signs of ST-segment elevation in two contiguous leads on the electrocardiogram; (2) typical signs of elevated cardiac biomarkers, including troponin, creatine kinase, and creatine kinase-myocardial band with a proportional increase [15]. The smoking status was categorized into two groups: Smokers and Non-smokers, based on whether the total number of cigarettes smoked currently exceeds 100 [16]. Coronary no-reflow phenomenon was defined as a thrombolysis in myocardial infarction (TIMI) flow grade < 3 after stent implantation [17]. CMD was defined as AMR>250 mmHg·s/m [18]. Intracoronary thrombus integral was from 0 to 5 according to TIMI Thrombus Grade [19]. Coronary function was primarily assessed using TIMI grade, QFR, AMR and CMD, while coronary anatomy was evaluated based on distal reference diameter, proximal reference diameter, minimum lumen diameter (MLD), lesion length, and percentage diameter stenosis. The clinical outcomes of concern included all-cause death, overall cardiac events, and heart failure.

Hypertension was diagnosed based on the following criteria outlined by the European Society of Cardiology guideline: (1) systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg; (2) home pressure monitoring average of ≥ 135/85 mmHg for two measurements at least 3 days apart [20]. Diabetes and peripheral arterial disease were diagnosed according to medical history. Total triglyceride, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, hemoglobin and creatinine were detected via colorimetry or immunoturbidimetry. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was measured by qualified cardiac sonographers during the period of hospitalization following the pPCI.

Percutaneous coronary intervention.

All patients underwent PCI via radial or femoral approach with regular antiplatelet therapy with aspirin and P2Y12 antagonists (clopidogrel or ticagrelor). The pPCI was performed within 12 h from the onset of symptoms, or after 12 h in the 75 presences of persistent ischemia, hemodynamic instability, or life-threatening arrhythmias. The choice of drug-eluting stenting or bare-metal stent was at the operator’s judgment based on clinically necessary without preference. Severe stenosis was defined as the presence of stenosis ≥ 50% in the left main coronary artery or ≥ 70% in the left anterior descending branch, left circumflex branch, or right coronary artery. The vessel lesion was classified as single or multiple depending on whether the stenosis is present only in the culprit vessel or in combination with the other vessels.

QCA and QFR analysis

The angiography image data were analyzed by two experienced observers from the Angiography Laboratory of Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital using the QAngio XA 7.2 software (Medis Medical Imaging Systems, Leiden, the Netherlands) for QCA analysis in culprit vessels.

Coronary physiology was assessed at the Core catheter lab (Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital, Guangzhou, China) using the Murray law-based quantitative flow ratio calculation on a single coronary angiography image run. This was done using recently developed software (AngioPlus Core, Pulse Medical Imaging Technology, Shanghai, China) [21]. QFR was computed with 3D/QFR software using two approaches: (1) assuming a fixed blood flow (fQFR); (2) considering the flow velocity estimated by the time needed for the contrast agent to fill the segment, which is known as contrast-flow QFR (cQFR). The calculating steps were performed by the software automatically as follows: (1) outlining the examined epicardial coronary artery during contrast injection and determining the velocity of the contrast flow; (2) choosing the analysis frame with a clear lumen outline at the stenotic segment as the key frame; (3) outlining the lumen contour of the vessel and its side branches with diameters of 1.0 mm or more on the key frame; (4) reconstructing the reference diameter function with a stepwise reduction across bifurcations in accordance with Murray’s fractal law; (5) modeling hyperemic flow velocity and calculating the pressure drop. The delineation of lumen contours on the interrogated vessels and their side branches was performed automatically by AI using a convolutional neural network based on the U-Net architecture. Additionally, AMR was computed based on the formula reported in a previous study [18]. Based on the pressure drop, the distal coronary pressure is obtained, while AMR is computed as distal coronary pressure divided by the hyperemic flow velocity. QCA, QFR, and AMR were measured before stent implantation but after thrombus aspiration and balloon predilatation, if the patient underwent these procedures, and again at the end of PCI. ΔQFR was defined as the value of cQFR-fQFR.

Statistical analysis

Statistical data analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics Software 26.0 (IBM SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA). Quantitative data with skew distribution was presented as median (quartile range), while qualitative data was expressed as frequency (per centum). The Mann-Whitney U test, Chi-square test were employed to determine significant differences between groups for skewed distribution data and categorical variables, respectively. Fisher’s exact test was used when the expected frequency in the variable is less than 5. Rank data were analyzed using Wilcoxon rank sum test. The association among smoking status, QCA and QFR was assessed through linear regression. Kaplan–Meier curve, Log-rank test and Cox regression analyses were utilized to evaluate clinical outcomes. Considering the baseline differences presented in this study and the results of previous studies [3, 4], the following variables were adjusted in regression models: age (as continuous variables), gender, hypertension, diabetes, LAD versus non-LAD infarct artery, final TIMI flow grade 0/1 versus 2/3, Killip class 1 vs. 2/3/4. Propensity score matching was used to screen out the smoker group and the non-smoker group with insignificant baseline differences such as gender, age, and comorbidities, so as to make a more reasonable comparison between the two groups. At this point, if there is still a difference in outcome between the two groups, the difference can be entirely attributed to experimental factors. Subgroup analyses was performed according to significant differences in the baseline variates: age, gender, LAD infarct artery, hypertension, diabetes, IABP used, and LVEF ≥ 40%. P value < 0.05 (two-sided) was considered statistically significant in all analyses.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 1258 patients were included in this study, with 528 classifieds as smokers and 730 as non-smokers (Figure S1). Smokers were significantly younger than non-smokers by at least 6 years on average [58(51–65) vs. 64(54–74), P < 0.001] and a higher proportion of males [519(98.3%) vs. 536(73.4%), P < 0.001]. Additionally, smokers had a lower prevalence of hypertension [228(43.2%) vs. 371(50.8%), P = 0.007] and diabetes [72(13.6%) vs. 141(19.3%), P = 0.008]. The levels of total triglyceride, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, creatinine, location of culprit vessel, post-stent TIMI grade, and intracoronary thrombus burden did not show significant differences between the two groups (Table 1). There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of gender, age, hypertension, diabetes, total triglyceride, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol after propensity score matching, except for the higher creatinine and lower LVEF in smokers (Table S1).

(Table S2). Smoking [OR 1.441(95%CI 1.125, 1.846), P = 0.004] was considered as a risk factor for CMD, along with age over 60 [OR 1.453(95%CI 1.135, 1.861), P = 0.003). Clinical outcomes

Smokers initially had a lower unadjusted rate of all-cause death [15(3.10%) vs. 54(8.1%), P = 0.001], cardiac events [47(9.8%) vs. 95(14.4%), P = 0.026], and heart failure [24(3.9%) vs. 8(1.7%), P = 0.040] during a mean follow-up period of 840 days compared to non-smokers. However, after multivariable adjustment, no significant association was found between smoking and clinical outcomes (Fig. 1). Smoking was found to significantly promoted all-cause death [HR 2.613(95%CI 1.475, 4.630), P = 0.001], heart failure [HR 2.265(95%CI 1.018, 5.042), P = 0.045] and overall cardiac events [HR 1.484(95%CI 1.046, 2.104), P = 0.027] without considering confounding factors. When adjusting for baseline differences in turn, age and gender were able to eliminate the effect of smoking on heart failure or overall cardiac events, while only age had an impact on all cause death (Table 4).

Discussion

This study revealed that smokers undergoing pPCI for STEMI show similar QFR and AMR compared to non-smokers, suggesting no significant differences in coronary physiology. However, smokers had a smaller lumen diameter and a higher incidence of CMD when accounting for the notable differences in age and presenting characteristics. Meanwhile, smoking status did not impact the clinical prognosis of these patients.

Consistent with previous studies, the smoking STEMI patients in this study are notably younger compared to non-smokers [4, 22, 23]. A younger age of onset typically indicates fewer underlying comorbidities or additional risk factors such as atherosclerosis. One hypothesis suggests that cigarette smoking primarily promotes thrombosis by inducing vascular endothelial dysfunction, leading to increases platelet activation and aggregation, elevated fibrinogen levels, and enhanced thrombin generation. However, smoking may impair fibrin crosslinking rather than directly increase the vulnerability of atherosclerotic plaque [24–26]. The absence of a significant difference in blood lipid levels among STEMI patients with different smoking statuses in this study also partly supports the notion that smoking primarily contributes to thrombosis rather than atherosclerosis. Another notable difference observed after propensity score matching between the two groups is the significantly higher level of creatinine in smokers. Previous studies have highlighted smoking as a risk factor for cardio-renal events and its association with the earlier progression of renal insufficiency and myocardial infarction [27]. This finding aligns with the established effect of smoking in accelerating the onset and progression of cardio-renal atherosclerosis [28, 29].

In contrast to previous studies, the main objective of this analysis is to investigate the impact of smoking on the structure and function of culprit vessel. Previous research has suggested that smokers tend to have larger lumen diameters, which can be attributed to the vasodilator properties of carbon monoxide produced by cigarette combustion [30]. Carbon monoxide is more resistant to oxidative stress than nitric oxide in human body [30]. Another possible explanation for structure divergence is that younger individuals generally exhibit better vascular structure, less lipid deposition and atherosclerotic plaque, which aligns with the similar baseline characteristics observed in most studies focused on smoking paradox [4, 23, 31]. The hypothesis is partially supported by the finding that the MLD is lager before adjusting but smaller than that of non-smokers after PSM in this study. Moreover, previous meta-analyses have shown that hypertension followed by male gender, diabetes, and age, predicts coronary artery calcification [32]. Therefore. the younger age of onset and fewer comorbidities among smokers in this study also partly explain why coronary artery structure or function was better in this group before PSM. However, despite the potential positive effects of smoking on clopidogrel conversion [33], which leads to more efficient platelet inhibition and reduced distal microembolization of thrombus material, our results indicate that smokers are more prone to CMD. This suggests that the beneficial effect of smoking on clopidogrel may be counterbalanced by the negative effects of smoking itself, such as increased thrombus burden and enhanced free radical-mediated oxidative stress [34, 35]. Additionally, cigarettes smoking has been associated with prothrombotic effects, including increased platelet reactivity, reduced endogenous fibrinolysis, and lipid peroxidation [35]. Therefore, the overall detrimental impact of smoking on coronary structure and function should not be overlooked.

On the other hand, smoking has the tendency to promote vasoconstriction and vasospastic angina by increasing the sympathetic tone, enhancing oxidative stress and inflammation to impair endothelial function, and reduce NO bioavailability [36]. The decreased production of NO also increases collagen deposition, reduces angiogenesis and collateral development, and promotes the conversion of endothelial cells into mesenchymal cells, leading to microvascular rarefaction [37, 38]. Microvascular inflammation and reduced NO availability directly promote proliferation of fibroblasts, which increase the production of fibronectin and collagen leading to cardiac remodeling following myocardial infarction, pressure overload, and myocarditis [39, 40]. CMD characterized by persistent changes in microvascular tone, are also associated with the development of structural abnormalities, which include luminal narrowing, due to inward remodeling of intramyocardial arterioles, rarefaction of microvessels, and microembolization after atherosclerotic plaque rupture or during coronary intervention [41]. Both arteriolar remodeling and decrease in capillary density contribute to increased microvascular resistance and reduced coronary flow reserve [42–45].

Our study suggests that the initial perception of better prognosis among smokers may be an illusion caused by confounding factors, which is in accordance with some previous research [46, 47]. Many researchers agree that the substantial age difference is likely the major confounding factor, as smokers exhibit a similar risk of death or re-hospitalization as non-smokers after adjusting for age and correcting for other risk cardiovascular factors has negligible effects on the association between smoking and mortality [4, 47]. However, the conclusions regarding the prognostic effects of smoking remain contradictory. Early studies indicate no difference in efficacy or the likelihood of irreversible myocardial injury after pPCI among patients with different smoking statuses [10]. Some studies even reported better short-term prognosis in smoking patients who developed STEMI [5]. On the other hand, recent research has declared that smoking is still associated with significantly increased mortality or risk of cardiac events compared to non-smokers, even after adjusting for baseline characteristics [3, 48]. Furthermore, most of conclusions suggesting that smoking is beneficial for epicardial myocardial perfusion or clinical prognosis are based on thrombolytic studies [49–52]. Pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamics studies have demonstrated that certain components of tobacco activate cytochrome P450 1A2 and 2B6 enzymes, promoting hepatic biotransformation of clopidogrel to its active metabolite [33]. This possibly explains why smoking patients derive greater benefits from thrombolytic and anti-plate therapy [53, 54], while non-smokers do not experience a change in mortality after clopidogrel treatment [55]. In this analysis, the favorable unadjusted outcomes among smokers can be fully explained by their lower age and fewer cardiovascular risk factors compared to non-smokers. Therefore, our findings challenge the concept of smoker’s paradox regarding better prognosis after pPCI..

It is crucial to emphasize that smoking is a well-established risk factor for the occurrence and progression of coronary artery disease. Although our study suggests that smoking may not have a significant impact on clinical prognosis, it is important to recognize that smoking is strongly associated with the development of STEMI at a younger age and with fewer underlying risk factors. This association clearly demonstrates the detrimental role of cigarettes in cardiovascular disease and highlights the need for targeted smoking cessation programs, especially aimed at younger populations. And it could promote the practice that using smoking status as an important factor in risk assessment for STEMI, particularly for patients presenting with few other risk factors. Furthermore, our findings indicate that smoking negatively affects both coronary lumen and function, providing evidence against the existence of smoking paradox. This can help dispel misconceptions, reinforce the importance of smoking cessation, and take more aggressive therapeutic strategies and follow-up care to address the adverse effects in improving overall cardiovascular health. Therefore, the overall concepts of smoking paradox should not be misunderstood as a neutral or beneficial effect of smoking. Many studies have acknowledged that the detrimental effects of smoking on long term prognosis for STEMI patients are primarily offset by age. Smoking cessation remains the most critical public health measure to reduce the burden of cardiovascular disease, which should be vigorously advocated.

Limitation

Limited by its retrospective design, accurate quantification of pack-years of smoking exposure was not feasible. However, we categorized smoking as a binary factor to differentiate individuals who have been exposed to tobacco from those who have never been exposed. This approach allows us to demonstrate a correlation between smoking and coronary structure and function. Moreover, our findings suggest the need for further prospective research to assess the causal relationship and accurately quantify the impact of cumulative smoking exposure in pack-years on these outcomes. Additionally, it is important to note that our study is a post hoc subgroup analysis. Although we performed propensity score matching to adjust for several confounders, we cannot exclude the potential contribution of other unaccounted moderators or residual confounding to the observed results. Nevertheless, considering the characteristics and primary objective of our cohort, we believe that these factors are unlikely to have critically influenced our results.

Conclusion

The present study observed that smoking among STEMI patients undergoing pPCI was associated with a smaller MLD and a higher occurrence of CMD, although it had no further impact on clinical prognosis. The smoking paradox observed in coronary anatomy or function may be explained by factors such as younger age, gender, and a lower prevalence of comorbidities
