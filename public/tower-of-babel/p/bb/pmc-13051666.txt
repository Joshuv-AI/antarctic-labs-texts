Neurosarcoidosis mimicking multiple meningiomas: illustrative case
Authors: Hiroki Suetsugu, Kyoichi Tomoto, Kentaro Watanabe, Wataru Uegami, Ryo Ishikawa, Ryouto Wachi, Michihiro Tanaka, Yuichi Murayama, Toshihide Tanaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDAmong intracranial extra-axial lesions, meningioma and solitary fibrous tumor are common differential diagnoses, whereas neurosarcoidosis is rare. Because its manifestations are diverse and nonspecific, establishing a preoperative diagnosis is often difficult. The authors report a case of neurosarcoidosis presenting as multiple intracranial extra-axial lesions and review its clinical and surgical features.OBSERVATIONSA 64-year-old man with no significant medical history presented with blurred vision in his left eye. Sarcoidosis is a systemic granulomatous disease characterized by noncaseating epithelioid cell granulomas that can affect various organs, including the lungs, lymph nodes, heart, eyes, skin, skeletal muscle, and nervous system.1 Neurosarcoidosis, in which the nervous system is involved, occurs in approximately 5%–7% of patients with sarcoidosis.1 Isolated neurosarcoidosis, in which lesions are confined to the CNS without systemic involvement, is extremely rare, accounting for only about 1% of all cases.2

The clinical manifestations of neurosarcoidosis depend on the location of granulomatous lesions and are generally nonspecific.3 Reported symptoms include headache, seizures, visual disturbances, and facial nerve palsy,4 as well as psychiatric symptoms and partial paralysis in some cases.5,6 Gadolinium-enhanced MRI is considered the gold standard for the radiological assessment of neurosarcoidosis7 and demonstrates variable imaging findings depending on the affected sites. Other diagnostic tools, such as serum angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) measurement and positron emission tomography (PET), may also be useful;2,8 however, diagnosis remains challenging due to the disease’s heterogeneous and nonspecific presentation.

Therefore, histopathological evaluation remains essential for definitive diagnosis.9 Here, we report a rare case of isolated neurosarcoidosis that was preoperatively suspected to be radiation-induced multiple meningiomas and was ultimately diagnosed by postoperative histopathological examination following craniotomy and tumor resection.

Illustrative Case

History of Present Illness

A 64-year-old man with no significant medical history presented with blurred vision in his left eye. He had no relevant family history, but his occupational history included substantial radiation exposure during the preceding 3 years. One month before admission, he noticed progressive visual disturbance, and ophthalmological evaluation revealed a central scotoma in the left eye. Gadolinium-enhanced MRI of the brain demonstrated seven uniformly enhancing, intracranial extra-axial lesions, including one located along the left sphenoid ridge (Fig. 1A and B). The lesions appeared hypointense on diffusion-weighted imaging, isointense on T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), and hypointense on T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) (Fig. 1C–E). Based on the imaging findings and his history of radiation exposure, radiation-induced multiple meningiomas were initially suspected. To preserve visual function and obtain a definitive diagnosis, a left frontotemporal craniotomy with resection of the sphenoid ridge lesion was planned. Intraoperative Findings

The operation was performed under general anesthesia. Using a pterional approach, we performed an extradural anterior clinoidectomy, followed by dural opening. On opening the sylvian fissure, a dura-based tumor was identified along the left sphenoid ridge. After coagulating and dividing the feeding vessels from the dura, internal decompression of the tumor was initiated.

The tumor appeared yellowish-white and granular, grossly resembling a meningioma. It was extremely firm and strongly adherent to the surrounding structures; therefore, piecemeal resection was carried out using both ultrasonic aspiration and sharp dissection (Fig. 2A). Multiple pial feeders were identified and coagulated (Fig. 2B). The internal carotid artery (ICA), optic nerve, and oculomotor nerve were carefully identified and preserved during tumor removal (Fig. 2C). Because of dense adhesion between the tumor and the oculomotor nerve, a small portion of the tumor was intentionally left in place, as well as a part of the posterior tumor margin (Video 1). Intraoperative frozen-section analysis was consistent with meningioma, showing no evidence of malignancy (Fig. 3A). Histological Analysis

Intraoperative frozen-section analysis revealed proliferation of cells with abundant cytoplasm in a background of collagen fibers, consistent with a diagnosis of meningioma (Fig. 3A). However, subsequent examination of formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded sections demonstrated the presence of numerous multinucleated giant cells that were not evident in the frozen sections. The lesion exhibited a characteristic pattern of nonnecrotizing granulomatous, accompanied by collagen fiber deposition and lymphocytic infiltration, which is a typical presentation of sarcoidosis (Fig. 3B and C).

Postoperative Course

Based on the pathological diagnosis of neurosarcoidosis, high-dose steroid pulse therapy with methylprednisolone (1 g/day) was initiated on postoperative day (POD) 18 and continued for a total of 6 days. This was followed by oral prednisolone, starting at 40 mg/day and gradually tapered thereafter. Gadolinium-enhanced MRI performed on POD 27 demonstrated a reduction in lesion size (Fig. 4A–D), indicating responsiveness to steroid therapy. On POD 37, methotrexate (4 mg/day) was added to the treatment regimen in combination with prednisolone. Postoperatively, the patient exhibited diplopia and ptosis due to left oculomotor nerve palsy. At 9 months postoperatively, both radiological improvement and gradual recovery of ocular motility were observed, although mild residual symptoms persisted. Discussion

Observations

Neurosarcoidosis is a rare manifestation among intracranial extra-axial lesions. Within this entity, isolated neurosarcoidosis confined to the CNS accounts for approximately 1% of all cases.2 Neurosarcoidosis can affect any component of the nervous system, including the brain and spinal cord parenchyma, nerve roots, leptomeninges, dura mater, and even adjacent osseous structures.3

Although some reports have described leptomeningeal involvement as the most typical presentation,3 others have reported dura-based lesions as the predominant manifestation.6

Dural neurosarcoidosis typically presents on MRI as a localized dural mass or diffuse dural thickening with homogeneous gadolinium enhancement and low signal intensity on T2WI.3,10 The main differential diagnoses include meningioma, solitary fibrous tumor, dural metastasis,11 IgG4-related disease,12 hypertrophic pachymeningitis,13 lymphoma, idiopathic intracranial hypertension, and granulomatosis with polyangiitis.14 Marangoni et al.8 reported that gallium scintigraphy has high sensitivity for detecting systemic sarcoidosis, whereas fluorodeoxyglucose PET/CT can serve as an alternative modality, as it frequently demonstrates uptake in extrapulmonary lesions such as those in the CNS.15,16 Elevated ACE levels in the CSF have also been reported in some cases;2,17 however, this finding is nonspecific and can be observed in conditions such as multiple sclerosis, Guillain-Barré syndrome, Behçet’s disease, brain tumors, and neurodegenerative disorders.9,18

In the present case, the lesion demonstrated homogeneous gadolinium enhancement and low T2 signal intensity, but no elevation of serum or cerebrospinal ACE levels was observed postoperatively. Although a radiation-induced meningioma was initially suspected, a broader range of differential diagnoses, including inflammatory diseases such as neurosarcoidosis, should have been considered preoperatively. Based on a review of previously reported cases of neurosarcoidosis mimicking meningioma (Table 1),5,7,9,10,19–32 there appears to be a female predominance (6 men, 17 women), with an age range of 24–79 years (median 45 years). Clinical manifestations varied depending on the lesion location and included headache, seizures, and cranial nerve deficits such as visual disturbance, oculomotor or trigeminal neuropathy, vestibulocochlear symptoms, and higher cortical dysfunctions such as aphasia. In 19 of 23 cases (83%), the lesions were supratentorial, while the remaining 4 occurred in the cerebellopontine angle. On MR T2WI, hypointensity was reported in 10 of 15 cases (67%), including the present case, suggesting that this finding may serve as an important radiological clue for neurosarcoidosis. Regarding surgical management, the extent of resection varied: biopsy in 4 of 20 cases (20%), partial resection in 10 (50%), and total resection in 6 (30%). Intraoperative findings were commonly described as whitish, granular, firm lesions with strong adhesion to surrounding structures. Steroid therapy proved effective in 17 of 18 cases (94%), except for 1 case reported by Wilson et al.10 In our case, the tumor was yellowish-white, granular, firm, and strongly adherent to adjacent tissues, consistent with previous reports. Sandhu et al.25 reported a case of convexity meningioma in which intraoperative frozen-section analysis revealed granulomatous inflammation, prompting conversion from tumor resection to biopsy. In our case, a preoperative diagnosis of meningioma led to tumor resection, and the intraoperative frozen diagnosis supported this impression. However, postoperative histopathological analysis revealed neurosarcoidosis. In addition, the adhesion to the optic and oculomotor nerves was firm, and although careful dissection was performed to preserve them, oculomotor nerve palsy remained postoperatively. As the definitive diagnosis of neurosarcoidosis relies on histopathological confirmation, biopsy or resection is essential. However, if intraoperative evaluation raises suspicion for steroid-responsive disease, conversion from tumor resection to biopsy may be a reasonable strategy to minimize surgical invasiveness and complications, as suggested in prior reports. In cases involving fragile neurovascular structures, such as the oculomotor nerve, careful surgical planning is particularly warranted. In summary, this case highlights the importance of considering steroid-responsive diseases such as neurosarcoidosis in the preoperative and intraoperative differential diagnosis. Anticipating such possibilities allows surgeons to develop tailored operative strategies, minimizing surgical morbidity while optimizing patient outcomes.

Lessons

We reported a rare case in which a patient initially diagnosed with multiple meningiomas underwent craniotomy and tumor resection and was subsequently diagnosed with neurosarcoidosis. Isolated neurosarcoidosis is extremely rare, and as shown in previous reports, its preoperative diagnosis remains challenging. In this case, findings such as low signal intensity on T2WI and intraoperative appearance were consistent with previously reported cases and may serve as useful clues for differentiation. However, histopathological confirmation remains essential. For dural lesions such as this, considering inflammatory diseases that may respond to postoperative steroid therapy is important, as an appropriate pre- and intraoperative differential diagnosis may help minimize surgical invasiveness and complications.
