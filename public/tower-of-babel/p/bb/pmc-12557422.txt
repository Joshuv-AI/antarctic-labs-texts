Is α1-antitrypsin deficiency a cardiovascular risk factor?
Authors: María Torres-Durán, Esmeralda García-Rodríguez, Laura Villar-Aguilar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Extractα1-Antitrypsin deficiency (α1-ATD) is a rare genetic condition caused by mutations in the SERPINA1 gene, whose predominant clinical manifestation in adults is the development of early-onset pulmonary emphysema in individuals with a history of tobacco smoke exposure [1]. In adult patients with α1-ATD, obesity and exposure to environmental factors, such as alcohol consumption or certain medications, may modulate the development of liver disease [2]. It is also associated with various forms of liver disease, which may arise during childhood or adulthood. In adult patients with α1-ATD, obesity and exposure to environmental factors, such as alcohol consumption or certain medications, may modulate the development of liver disease [2]. Although most research has focused on these clinical manifestations, other less frequent presentations – such as anti-nuclear cytoplasmic antibody-positive vasculitis or panniculitis – have also been described [3].

From a physiological perspective, the principal and most extensively studied function of α1-antitrypsin (α1-AT) is the inhibition of neutrophil elastase activity. However, various studies have shown that α1-AT can also effectively neutralise other serine proteases, accounting for >90% of the antiprotease capacity of human serum. In addition, other less well-known properties of α1-AT have been described [4], including its anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects [5], its protective role against oxidative stress, its contribution to connective tissue repair, and its antimicrobial properties [6], as summarised in figure 1.

Emerging scientific evidence suggests, however, that the clinical approach to α1-ATD should be broadened beyond its classical respiratory and hepatic manifestations. Several recent studies indicate a potential association between α1-ATD and significantly increased cardiovascular risk, which has traditionally been underestimated in clinical practice. This association may not be attributable solely to factors shared with COPD, such as smoking or systemic inflammation, but could also involve intrinsic mechanisms related to α1-ATD itself. Among these are uncontrolled neutrophil proteolytic activity, degradation of elastic fibres in blood vessels and the myocardium, and persistent low-grade systemic inflammation. In this context, a possible direct link has been proposed between α1-ATD and various forms of cardiovascular disease (CVD), including atherosclerosis, heart failure and atrial fibrillation.

The study by Winther et al. [7] published in this issue of ERJ Open Research explores the relationship between α1-ATD and atrial fibrillation through the analysis of two large Danish cohorts with long-term follow-up. The findings are significant: individuals with α1-ATD exhibited a 1.5–2-fold increased risk of hospitalisation due to atrial fibrillation, greater use of digoxin (hazard ratio (HR) 2.11, 95% CI 1.42–3.14) and higher mortality in which atrial fibrillation was a contributing cause (HR 2.60, 95% CI 1.65–4.11). This excess risk persisted even after adjustment for cardiovascular risk factors and comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes and COPD. The authors further investigated the underlying mechanisms using mediation analysis, demonstrating that both heart failure and systemic inflammation – measured by high-sensitivity C-reactive protein – contributed partially (19.5% and 4.4%, respectively) to the association between α1-ATD and atrial fibrillation. These findings support the involvement of structural cardiac remodelling and chronic low-grade systemic inflammation as potential pathophysiological mechanisms.

These compelling findings add to the previous work by the same research group, which identified a significantly increased risk of hospitalisation due to heart failure and heart failure-specific mortality in individuals with AATD, compared with matched controls without α1-ATD from the same two Danish population-based cohorts [8]. In that study, patients with α1-ATD exhibited an adjusted HR of 2.64 (95% CI 2.25–3.10) and 1.77 (95% CI 1.14–2.74) for hospitalisation, and 2.28 (95% CI 1.57–3.32) and 3.35 (95% CI 1.04–10.74) for heart failure mortality, in the respective cohorts. This risk was even more pronounced among individuals without known cardiovascular comorbidities, further supporting the hypothesis that AATD may act independently as a primary risk factor for myocardial dysfunction.

Consistent with this, Ellis et al. [9] assessed the prevalence of CVD and the incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) in a combined cohort of nearly 3500 patients with COPD and severe α1-ATD, drawn from the USA (AlphaNet) and the UK (Birmingham AATD Registry). At baseline, 14.7% of patients had a history of CVD, and those who had experienced frequent exacerbations in the preceding year showed a higher prevalence of pre-existing CVD and a significantly increased risk of MACEs, including myocardial infarction, heart failure and stroke. Even in the absence of prior CVD, frequent exacerbators demonstrated an elevated risk of MACEs: HR 2.12 (95% CI 1.38–3.23).

Taken together, these three studies, summarised in table 1, present α1-ATD as a condition associated with a significant cardiovascular risk.

Equally noteworthy is the study by Sapey et al. [10], in which clinical, radiological and biochemical assessments were used to evaluate the risk of CVD in patients with COPD and severe α1-ATD not receiving augmentation therapy, compared with patients with COPD without α1-ATD and healthy controls. The authors analysed arterial stiffness by measuring aortic pulse wave velocity (aPWV) [11] and applied the QRISK2 cardiovascular risk prediction score [12] across the three groups: α1-ATD, COPD without α1-ATD and healthy controls. While aPWV was higher in patients with α1-ATD (after adjustment for age and smoking), QRISK2 systematically underestimated their risk.

The most relevant finding of the study was that standard clinical tools for cardiovascular risk prediction, such as QRISK2, significantly underestimated risk in the α1-ATD population, as determined by aPWV – a validated marker of arterial stiffness and predictor of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. ∼45% of patients with α1-ATD demonstrated a mismatch between low QRISK2 scores and elevated aPWV values, and this subgroup exhibited a higher incidence of cardiovascular events during follow-up. This suggests that conventional cardiovascular risk prediction algorithms may be inadequate for patients with severe α1-ATD. Furthermore, aPWV correlated strongly with plasma levels of Aα-Val541, a biomarker of proteinase 3 activity, providing a plausible link between neutrophilic inflammation and systemic vascular remodelling.

From a biological perspective, the plausibility of the association between α1-ATD and CVD is supported by several mechanisms. One hypothesis centres on the unopposed activity of neutrophil elastase and other serine proteases due to dysfunctional α1-AT. These enzymes are not only involved in alveolar destruction but are also thought to affect cardiovascular tissues. The connective tissues of the subendocardium and blood vessels are rich in elastin, and their chronic proteolytic degradation may compromise the structural integrity of these organs. As demonstrated by Winther et al. [8], the increased risk of heart failure in patients with α1-ATD – even in the absence of known predisposing conditions such as ischaemic heart disease or hypertension – suggests a direct effect of elastase activity on cardiac structure and function.

Furthermore, systemic inflammation may act synergistically with the protease–antiprotease imbalance. α1-AT has well-documented anti-inflammatory properties, and its deficiency may exacerbate systemic inflammatory responses. Proinflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress are well-known drivers of both atherogenesis [13] and arrhythmogenesis [14], providing an additional potential link between α1-ATD and CVD.

It should be emphasised that these associations do not appear to be explained solely by pulmonary damage. Indeed, the authors adjusted their models for lung function (forced expiratory volume in 1 s/forced vital capacity) and smoking, as well as for bronchodilator treatments (including β-agonists and theophylline), without any weakening of the strength of the association. This suggests that α1-ATD may exert a distinct and independent cardiovascular effect.

Progress in understanding the cardiovascular risk associated with α1-ATD will largely depend on the systematic collection and prospective analysis of clinical data. In this context, patient registries represent a strategic asset. A prominent example is the European Alpha-1 Research Collaboration (EARCO) registry [15], a collaborative infrastructure designed to collect longitudinal and standardised data from patients with α1-ATD across different countries. EARCO enables the characterisation of the clinical course of α1-ATD patients, their comorbidities and the long-term effects of various therapeutic interventions [16, 17].

In parallel, the development of new artificial intelligence and machine learning tools applied to these large datasets opens up novel analytical possibilities. These technologies will enable the identification of nonlinear relationships and complex data structures beyond the reach of traditional analytical methods, facilitate the construction of predictive risk models, and generate new hypotheses regarding pathophysiological mechanisms [18]. The integration of patient registries with these advanced analytical tools will support the implementation of personalised medicine in α1-ATD, potentially transforming how we stratify, monitor and treat patients with α1-ATD, including in relation to cardiovascular risk stratification and management [19], and ultimately enabling the development of more individualised and effective interventions.
