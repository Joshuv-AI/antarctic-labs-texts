Effects of “temporal preparation” on neural processing of physical and affective material attributes
Authors: Kenta Nakajima, Takehiro Nagai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
It remains unclear whether different material attributes, such as glossiness and aesthetics, rely on different stages of visual processing. This study investigated how “temporal preparation,” defined here as participants’ expectation of stimulus duration, modulates the neural dynamics of material perception, thereby helping reveal the underlying processing stages. Electroencephalography (EEG) was recorded while participants viewed images of various objects and made judgments of either glossiness or aesthetic value. Temporal preparation was manipulated by changing the ratio of short (100 ms) to long (1000 ms) stimulus presentations within each session. This manipulation was intended to alter participants’ expectations of stimulus duration without directly constraining the actual presentation duration. Decoding analyses were used to classify binary perceptual judgments from the EEG signals. The results showed that expectations of shorter durations enhanced decoding accuracy for glossiness, whereas expectations of longer stimulus durations tended to enhance decoding accuracy for aesthetics. These findings suggest that temporal preparation influences neural representations differently depending on the material attribute. This approach provides a useful methodological framework for dissociating perceptual strategies and offers new insights into the temporal dynamics of different material impressions.

Introduction

Humans are able to rapidly extract various types of information from a scene, such as color, shape, and the number of objects. Recent research on perception in natural scenes has increasingly focused on the mechanisms underlying material impressions, that is, how the brain infers material properties from visual input (Fleming, Wiebel, & Gegenfurtner, 2013; Fleming, 2014). In vision science, material perception can be understood as the process by which the brain estimates the physical properties and surface conditions of objects from retinal images. In addition, material perception has been discussed not only in terms of individual attributes such as glossiness, but also in relation to material classes, where observers categorize objects (e.g., metal, wood, or fabric) based on visual cues (Fleming et al., 2013).

Material impressions comprise multiple attributes, each serving a distinct perceptual role. Even when limited to the visual modality, material impressions include various attributes such as glossiness and transparency. Previous studies on material impressions have reported that certain material impressions (e.g., glossiness) are associated with particular material classes (Nagai et al., 2015), suggesting the importance of material impressions in object recognition. Furthermore, Fleming (2014) summarized that the visual cues involved in material impressions vary substantially across different attributes; for example, glossiness depends on highlights and object shape, whereas transparency relies on object contours and layered structures. These findings suggest that different types of material impressions involve distinct characteristics and processing mechanisms.

An important question is how these diverse attributes of material impressions are interrelated in their underlying perceptual processes. Both hierarchical and parallel accounts have been proposed for the cognitive mechanisms underlying material impressions. For example, a hierarchical relationship has been suggested based on ratings of material impressions for texture stimuli (Liu, Lughofer, & Zeng, 2015; Okamoto, Wakamatsu, Nakauchi, Kwon, & Sakamoto, 2020). In the study by Liu et al. (2015), participants rated multiple texture images on several material impressions. The evaluated dimensions, such as “artificial–natural” and “like–dislike,” were categorized into three hierarchical groups based on their characteristics. They further developed a feed-forward model to predict material impressions from image features. The results suggested a hierarchical structure in which lower-level material impressions served as the basis for higher-level ones. This hierarchical view is also consistent with neurophysiological perspectives, which propose that material perception is supported by successive stages of visual processing along the ventral visual pathway (Komatsu & Goda, 2018). Consistent with the findings of Liu et al., research on material attributes often assumes a hierarchical processing structure in which lower-level material impressions are derived from image features and, in turn, give rise to higher-level impressions through successive stages of processing.

In contrast, parallel relationships between glossiness and material class recognition have also been proposed. In the studies by Schmid, Barla, and Doerschner (2023), participants rated the perceived glossiness and judged material classes of various computer-generated (CG) object images. To clarify the direct relationship between glossiness and material class recognition, they performed linear regression analyses using glossiness ratings as predictors and material class ratings as dependent variables. The results showed that differences in material class recognition could not be explained by glossiness ratings alone, indicating that glossiness perception is not a direct predictor of material class. Moreover, they found that each material class correlated with distinct low-level image features. Because material class recognition was associated with distinct low-level image features that differed from those predicting glossiness, material class could not be derived from glossiness. This pattern raises the possibility that glossiness and material class recognition do not form a hierarchical sequence but instead arise in parallel from separate sets of visual features. These findings raise broader questions about which visual information and processing stages underlie different material impressions, and about how strongly each type of impression depends on visual processing.

Two psychophysical approaches have been proposed to clarify the processing stages underlying material impressions. The first approach infers the processing stage by examining which image features underlie material impressions, an approach adopted in numerous studies (Anderson, 2011; Fleming & Bülthoff, 2005; Motoyoshi, Nishida, Sharan, & Adelson, 2007; Sharan, Li, Motoyoshi, Nishida, & Adelson, 2008; Sharan, Rosenholtz, & Adelson, 2009). In this approach, the relationship between material impressions and specific image features is analyzed, and the depth of processing is inferred from the complexity or number of image features required to explain the perception. For example, some material impressions can be explained by relatively simple statistical image features, whereas others require more complex or higher-order feature interactions. Indeed, Sharan et al. (2009) investigated the visual features contributing to material category judgments using a large image set of objects. They found that roughness and regularity could be explained by relatively simple statistical features. In contrast, material impressions such as glossiness and transparency required more complex image features involving interactions between reflectance properties and object shape. Thus, the relationship between image features and material impressions has been considered a useful basis for estimating the depth of material impression processing. However, most of these studies have been limited to showing correlations between participants’ responses and image features, without demonstrating whether participants’ material impressions directly depend on these features.

As a second approach, some studies have examined the depth of processing based on the temporal characteristics of material impressions (Cant & Goodale, 2007; Hansen & Hansen, 1988; Nagai et al., 2015; Rousselet, Macé, & Fabre-Thorpe, 2003; Schyns & Oliva, 1999; Sharan et al., 2009; Sharan, Rosenholtz, & Adelson, 2014; Wiebel, Valsecchi, & Gegenfurtner, 2013). For example, Wiebel et al. (2013) analyzed participants’ responses to material and object classes in object images while manipulating stimulus presentation time to compare the temporal characteristics of material and object recognition. In this study, images of materials such as wood and stone, as well as objects such as humans and animals, were presented for less than 67 ms. The results showed that material recognition required longer processing time than object recognition. Furthermore, by identifying the minimum presentation time at which classification performance exceeded chance level, the temporal characteristics of material recognition were revealed.

However, these previous methods provide limited insight into the processing stages and temporal characteristics of various material impressions. First, studies on the temporal characteristics of material impression processing have primarily relied on responses under brief, experimenter-controlled presentation durations or on reaction times. Such approaches offer limited observation time and present stimuli under unnatural viewing conditions, which may not fully reflect actual material impressions. Moreover, psychophysical experiments depend on participants’ subjective reports, making it difficult to clarify the underlying neural processing objectively. If these processes could be measured more objectively and linked to subjective experience, a more robust understanding of the processing of material impressions could be achieved.

Electroencephalography (EEG) has been widely used to analyze the temporal characteristics of neural information processing. Many studies have used EEG to investigate the temporal characteristics of processing different visual features such as color and faces (Hajonides, Nobre, van Ede, & Stokes, 2021; Li, Zhang, Liu, & Luo, 2022). For example, Hajonides et al. (2021) presented participants with Gabor stimuli varying in color and orientation while recording EEG. They then performed linear discriminant analysis on the EEG data to decode color and orientation. The results showed that decoding accuracy was higher when the EEG from the right hemisphere was used. This effect appeared in a relatively early time window of approximately 150–350 ms for stimuli presented in the left visual field. This indicates a hemispheric asymmetry in the neural representation of visual information, suggesting the involvement of spatially organized, contralateral representations in the early visual cortex.

In EEG signals, components related to perceptual and cognitive processing are enhanced depending on task demands (Bocincova & Johnson, 2019; Noah et al., 2020; Smith & Smith, 2019; Yip et al., 2022). Bocincova and Johnson (2019) recorded EEG while participants viewed Gabor stimuli varying in color and orientation and were instructed to memorize either color or orientation. A support vector machine (SVM) was then used to decode color and orientation from the EEG signals. The results showed that decoding accuracy for both color and orientation exceeded chance levels regardless of the task, indicating that these low-level visual features can be decoded from EEG. Furthermore, when comparing the temporal dynamics of decoding accuracy, features relevant to the task remained above chance levels for more than 1000 ms longer than those of task-irrelevant features. Yip, Cheung, Ngan, Wong, and Wong (2022) also examined the temporal windows during which task-dependent differences in decoding accuracy emerge. In their study, participants performed shape or texture judgments while viewing CG images that varied in shape and texture, and EEG signals were recorded. Decoding results showed higher decoding accuracy for task-relevant than task-irrelevant features in a late time window starting around 400 ms after stimulus onset. These findings demonstrate that EEG decoding accuracy is enhanced for task-relevant features, helping elucidate the temporal characteristics of perceptual processing.

Previous studies have also examined the relationship between material perception and EEG. For example, Wiebel, Valsecchi, and Gegenfurtner (2014) showed that different material categories, such as stone and wood, can be discriminated from event-related potential (ERP) signals as early as around 100 ms after stimulus onset. However, because this approach relies on differences between image categories, the observed effects may primarily reflect low-level visual differences between images rather than the temporal dynamics of material impressions. Similarly, Orima, Wakita, and Motoyoshi (2025) demonstrated that surface properties such as glossiness can be discriminated from visually evoked potentials at latencies of around 200 ms. Although these findings indicate that certain material-related features are decodable from EEG signals, they do not necessarily capture how different types of material impressions evolve over time as subjective perceptual constructs. Therefore, it remains unclear how different material impressions unfold temporally in the brain under controlled conditions, and whether their temporal characteristics differ across attributes when low-level image differences and explicit categorical distinctions are minimized. The present study addresses this gap by focusing on the temporal dynamics of material impressions (e.g., glossiness and aesthetics) using EEG decoding under controlled stimulus and task conditions.

In this study, we aim to explore the temporal characteristics of neural processing of material impressions using EEG. A simple comparison of EEG signals across different material impressions often reveals only subtle differences in informative time windows, on the order of several tens of milliseconds, which are difficult to capture with statistical stability. Therefore, a mere comparison of temporal windows may not be sufficient to probe the stages of material impression processing. To address this limitation, we introduce the concept of “temporal preparation” as a new approach to examine the stages of information processing involved in material perception under relatively naturalistic viewing conditions. In this context, temporal preparation refers to the participants’ expectation of stimulus duration. Thus temporal preparation can be regarded as a task-related expectation about how long the stimulus will be available for processing. This approach allows us to bias processing toward particular stages without substantially constraining viewing duration. This idea is grounded in findings that visual attention and temporal expectation can modulate the temporal characteristics of EEG signals. For example, Hubbard, Kikumoto, and Mayr (2019) demonstrated that expectations about facial stimuli can alter the temporal dynamics of EEG responses during face perception. Similarly, studies by Mares et al. (2024) and Jin, Nobre, and van Ede (2020) reported that temporal expectation of pre-stimulus blanks and attentional shifts influence EEG decoding accuracy. Based on these findings, we examine the effects of temporal preparation on EEG responses to reveal distinct processing stages involved in different material attributes.

The present study focuses on glossiness and aesthetics as two representative yet qualitatively distinct attributes of material impressions. Glossiness has been extensively studied in vision science and is known to depend on relatively well-defined visual cues such as specular highlights, luminance contrast, and the interaction between reflectance properties and object shape (Fleming, 2014; Komatsu & Goda, 2018; Motoyoshi et al., 2007). Although these cues involve higher-order image statistics, glossiness perception is often considered to rely on relatively direct mappings from visual features to perceptual judgments and has been successfully predicted from image-based features in previous studies. In contrast, aesthetics reflects a more complex evaluative process that integrates multiple sources of information, including perceptual features, prior knowledge, and subjective preferences (Chatterjee & Vartanian, 2014; Leder, Belke, Oeberst, & Augustin, 2004; Liu et al., 2015). Neurocognitive models of aesthetic processing propose multiple stages, ranging from early perceptual analysis to later cognitive and affective evaluation, suggesting that aesthetic judgments involve more extended and integrative processing than basic material attributes.

Based on these distinctions, glossiness and aesthetics provide a useful contrast for examining differences in temporal processing dynamics within material perception. Although glossiness is closely tied to specific visual cues and may be more strongly influenced by earlier perceptual processing, aesthetics is expected to involve additional higher-level processing stages beyond initial feature extraction. In the present experiment, temporal preparation was manipulated implicitly by varying the distribution of short and long stimulus presentations within each session, thereby biasing participants’ expectations about how long a stimulus would remain available for processing. To isolate the effect of this expectation from physical stimulus duration, EEG analyses were restricted to the long trials that were common to both temporal-preparation conditions. Combining EEG analysis with this manipulation allowed us to examine whether different material attributes rely on different stages of neural processing under relatively natural viewing conditions.

Purpose

The present study aimed to clarify the temporal characteristics of neural processing underlying different material attributes by manipulating the attribute being judged (glossiness vs. aesthetics) and temporal preparation. Temporal preparation was intended to bias the stage of visual processing on which participants relied, without altering the actual presentation duration. Based on the assumption that glossiness relies more on low-level visual features, whereas aesthetics relies on more complex ones, we hypothesized that expectations of shorter durations would enhance decoding accuracy for glossiness, whereas expectations of longer durations would enhance decoding accuracy for aesthetics. EEG decoding analysis was used to test this hypothesis. The results showed that decoding accuracy for glossiness was higher under the expectation of short stimulus durations, whereas decoding accuracy for aesthetics tended to be higher under the expectation of long stimulus durations. A two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) further revealed a significant interaction between temporal preparation and attribute, indicating distinct temporal processing characteristics for glossiness and aesthetics.

Methods

Experimental design

In this study, participants’ temporal preparation was manipulated by varying the proportion of trials with short (100 ms) and long (1000 ms) presentation durations within each session. The rapid-preparation condition consisted of a 1:1 mix of short and long trials and was intended to bias expectations toward shorter durations, whereas the slow-preparation condition consisted only of long trials and was intended to bias expectations toward longer durations. These two conditions are collectively referred to as the temporal preparation conditions. Because long trials were included in both session types, only long trials were used for EEG analyses. The temporal preparation manipulation is illustrated schematically in Figure 1. This design allowed us to isolate and examine the effects of temporal preparation itself, independently of differences in the stimulus images.

Participants

Twenty-six undergraduate and graduate students from the Institute of Science Tokyo (Tokyo, Japan) participated (21 men, five women; age range: 19–34 years). An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power assuming a medium effect size (f = 0.25), α = .05, and power = 0.80 for a 2 × 2 repeated-measures ANOVA. The analysis indicated that at least 24 participants were required. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and passed the Ishihara color vision test. The experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee for Research Involving Human Subjects of the Institute of Science Tokyo (approval number: 2023341). Prior to the experiment, all participants received a full explanation of the procedures and provided written informed consent.

Stimuli

The visual stimuli consisted of object photographs selected from publicly available online image databases. Specifically, 320 images depicting objects with different shapes, materials, and lighting conditions were selected from online image databases, including the Flickr Material Database (Sharan, Liu, Rosenholtz, & Adelson, 2014b), the Open Affective Standardized Image Set (OASIS) (Kurdi, Lozano, & Banaji, 2017), and the Geneva Affective Picture Database (GAPED) (Dan-Glauser & Scherer, 2011). Each image was resized to 512 × 384 pixels so that the entire object fit within the frame, subtending a visual angle of 10 × 7.5 degrees. Some example images are shown in Figure 2, and all images are presented in Appendix A (Figure A1). These images are referred to as the test stimulus set. In addition, 640 images that were not used for EEG analysis were prepared as the support stimulus set. Stimulus images were selected based on glossiness and aesthetics ratings obtained from six independent participants who did not take part in the main experiment; we chose images spanning a wide range of each attribute while minimizing the correlation between the two ratings.

Schematic illustration of the temporal preparation manipulation. Temporal preparation was manipulated at the session level by varying the proportion of short (100 ms) and long (1000 ms) stimulus presentations. In the rapid-preparation condition, short and long trials were intermixed, whereas in the slow-preparation condition only long trials were presented. EEG analyses were restricted to the long trials common to both conditions, allowing differences in neural response to be attributed to the expectation induced by the session-wise duration distribution rather than to the physical duration of the analyzed stimuli.

Procedure and settings

The experiment was conducted in a darkroom covered with blackout curtains. The exterior of the darkroom was further covered with an electromagnetic shielding sheet to reduce external electromagnetic interference. Inside the darkroom, a liquid crystal display for stimulus presentation (ColorEdge CG248; Eizo Corporation, Hakusan, Ishikawa, Japan), a numeric keypad, and a chinrest were set up. The display resolution was 3840 × 2160 pixels with a refresh rate of 60 Hz. The display was hardware-calibrated and set to sRGB mode (D65 white point).

Luminance and chromaticity characteristics were measured using a colorimeter (ColorCAL2; Cambridge Research Systems Ltd., Rochester, UK) to confirm that the display properties matched the calibration settings. The experiment was controlled using a PC (OptiPlex SFF-7010; CPU: Intel Core i5-13600; Dell, Inc., Round Rock, TX, USA). The experimental program, developed in MATLAB R2020a with Psychtoolbox-3 (Brainard, 1997; Kleiner, Brainard, & Pelli, 2007) on Ubuntu 18.04 LTS, controlled stimulus presentation and participant response collection. During the experiment, participants used a chinrest to maintain a viewing distance of 90 cm. Participants responded using the numeric keypad.

At the beginning of each session, the experimenter verbally instructed participants to judge either glossiness or aesthetics for all trials in that session. Within each session, trials were either all long or a 1:1 mixture of short and long trials. Participants were informed in advance that stimuli could be presented with different durations. However, they were not given any explicit instructions to adopt particular strategies or to adjust their responses based on stimulus duration or trial proportions. Moreover, participants were not informed about the proportion of short and long trials in each session. Thus any differences in temporal preparation across conditions were expected to arise solely from the distribution of stimulus durations experienced repeatedly within a session. In other words, temporal preparation was assumed to be shaped implicitly through repeated exposure to the trial distributions rather than through explicit instructions.

As described above, each trial had either a long or a short duration. The stimulus sequence is illustrated in Figures 3a and 3b for long and short trials, respectively. In each trial, a gray background (luminance 59.6 cd/m², chromaticity (x, y) = (0.301, 0.319)) was presented, followed by a single stimulus image at the center of the screen for either 100 ms (short trial) or 1000 ms (long trial). A black fixation cross was displayed at the center of the screen, and participants were instructed to maintain their gaze on this fixation point and minimize blinking during the trial. For long trials, stimuli were selected from the test stimulus set, whereas for short trials, stimuli were selected from the support stimulus set. The separation of stimulus sets between short and long trials was intended to prevent participants from memorizing the test stimuli. Additionally, for short trials, three colored masks generated from the support stimulus set (Greene & Oliva, 2009) were randomly selected and presented sequentially for 50 ms each to suppress afterimages. The masks were synthesized using Portilla–Simoncelli (PS) texture statistics (Portilla & Simoncelli, 2000). Following stimulus presentation, the background was shown again for 1750 ms in short trials and for 1000 ms in long trials, so that the interval from stimulus onset to the response prompt was 2000 ms in both conditions. After this, the prompt “Please indicate the strength of the target attribute” appeared, and participants made a binary judgment regarding the strength of the assigned attribute for the stimulus image, pressing the “4” key for weak and the “6” key for strong. Here, “strength” referred to either perceived glossiness (high or low) or perceived aesthetics (high or low), depending on the attribute. Participants were instructed to use a consistent personal criterion and to base each response solely on the current image, without reference to previous trials. After each response, the next trial began after a 1200–1400 ms background interval. Each session consisted of eight blocks of 40 trials each, for a total of 320 trials.

Trial sequences for (a) long trials, (b) short trials, and (c) an overview of the experimental design.

Each participant completed a total of six experimental sessions, covering the two material attributes: glossiness and aesthetics. For each attribute, participants completed one slow-preparation session and two rapid-preparation sessions. In the slow preparation session, all stimuli from the test stimulus set were presented once. In the rapid-preparation sessions, half of the test stimuli were presented in one session and the remaining half in the other session, each stimulus presented once. The order of material attributes and the sequence of temporal preparation conditions were randomized across participants. Additionally, the presentation order of stimulus images within each session was also randomized for each participant. Across all six sessions, participants completed a total of 1920 trials (320 trials × 6 sessions). The entire experiment lasted approximately three hours, with all sessions completed by each participant in a single day.

EEG measurement

EEG was recorded using a 32-channel active-electrode system (actiCAP 32; Brain Products, Gilching, Germany) and an AC amplifier (BrainAmp 32; Brain Products) connected to a laptop (MSI Modern-14-B11MOU-617JP, CPU: Intel Core i7-1185G7). Nineteen electrodes were positioned according to the international 10-20 system, and the remaining electrodes were placed midway between these standard positions. The reference electrode was located at FCz, and the ground electrode was placed above the glabella. EEG signals were sampled at 1000 Hz, and electrode impedance was maintained below 5 kΩ. A photodiode was attached to the stimulus display to record the exact onset timing of stimulus presentation in the EEG recording software.

EEG preprocessing

The recorded EEG data were preprocessed as follows. EEG analyses, including preprocessing, were performed using the MATLAB toolboxes EEGLAB (Delorme & Makeig, 2004) and ERPLAB (Lopez-Calderon & Luck, 2014). First, a Butterworth high-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 0.1 Hz was applied to remove slow drifts, and a second-order Butterworth low-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 80 Hz was applied to remove high-frequency noise. Next, the data were re-referenced to the average of all electrodes. The EEG data were then epoched from 1000 ms before stimulus onset to 2000 ms after stimulus onset, resulting in epochs of 3000 ms in total.

Artifact rejection was performed in the following order, based solely on voltage fluctuations without applying independent component analysis (ICA)-based component removal. First, trials in which the frontopolar electrodes (Fp1 and Fp2) exceeded ±120 µV between 500 ms before and 1000 ms after stimulus onset were considered to contain blinks and were excluded from further analysis. Second, for each participant, any electrode exhibiting voltage fluctuations exceeding ±80 µV in more than 10% of trials was considered noisy and removed from further analysis without interpolation or replacement. Finally, trials containing voltage fluctuations exceeding ±80 µV at any electrode other than Fp1 and Fp2 were also excluded as noisy trials. On average, 16.0 trials per condition were rejected, and 0.52 electrodes were removed per participant. Because only a small number of electrodes were excluded, we consider this procedure to have had minimal impact on the overall results.

Analysis

Behavioral data analysis

For each combination of attribute and temporal-preparation condition, the number of “strong” responses was summed across participants for each test stimulus. First, within each temporal preparation condition, we computed the Pearson correlation (across stimuli) between the numbers of “strong” responses for glossiness and aesthetics. Next, for each attribute, we computed the correlation coefficient between the response numbers across temporal preparation conditions.

This analysis aimed to examine how participants’ response patterns varied across attribute and temporal-preparation conditions. Note that each participant provided only one judgment per test stimulus in each condition. Thus the analysis was based on the responses aggregated across all participants.

ERP

The analysis of event-related potentials (ERPs) examined whether neural responses differed between stimulus groups associated with weak versus strong material impressions for each attribute and temporal preparation condition. ERPs are components of the EEG that occur in response to internal or external events. In this study, ERPs were obtained by averaging EEG signals across trials per participant within each stimulus group. Because single-trial EEG reflects a mixture of neural processes, stimulus-related activity is difficult to isolate. By averaging across trials within the same stimulus group, the brain activity associated with that stimulus group can be extracted. ERPs reflect neural processes related to perception and cognition and are widely used as indicators of these processing stages.

The analysis was conducted using the preprocessed EEG data described above. Based on participants’ responses to each stimulus image, trials were classified into two groups. In this experiment, each participant made a judgment for each test stimulus only once under each attribute condition and temporal preparation condition. Therefore, for each attribute and temporal preparation condition, stimulus images were assigned to the strong or weak group based on the majority response across all participants: a “strong group” (images judged as strong by more than half of the participants) and a “weak group” (images judged as weak by more than half of the participants). As a result, the number of images included in each group was as follows: in the glossiness–rapid preparation condition, 156 images were classified as strong and 164 as weak; in the glossiness–slow preparation condition, 158 images were classified as strong and 162 as weak; in the aesthetics–rapid preparation condition, 163 images were classified as strong and 157 as weak; and in the aesthetics–slow preparation condition, 158 images were classified as strong and 162 as weak.

ERPs were calculated by averaging the EEG across all trials for each stimulus group and temporal-preparation condition per participant, time-locked to the stimulus onset. A 500 ms pre-stimulus period was used as the baseline, and baseline correction was performed by subtracting the mean voltage during this period from each trial. Differences between the ERPs of the stimulus groups were then computed, and statistical analyses were conducted based on these differences.

For statistical testing, the tmax permutation test (Blair & Karniski, 1993) was used. Specifically, paired-samples t-tests across participants were conducted at each time point on the ERP differences between stimulus groups. Then, for 2500 permutation samples, the condition labels of each trial were randomly reassigned to the two groups, and the same paired-samples t-test was conducted for each permutation. For each permutation, the maximum t-statistic (tmax) was extracted, resulting in a distribution of tmax values across all permutations. This distribution was used as the null distribution, and the critical values corresponding to the upper and lower 2.5% of this null distribution were used as the significance thresholds for a two-tailed test. If the observed t-statistic from the original data exceeded these thresholds, it was considered statistically significant. This procedure allowed the detection of significant differences between conditions at each time point and electrode while controlling for multiple comparisons.

Support vector machine

This analysis examined whether differences in temporal preparation and judged attribute could be detected in multivariate EEG patterns, and whether differences in decoding accuracy reflected differences in the temporal characteristics of material-impression processing. This analysis examined EEG representations associated with three experimental factors: attribute condition, temporal-preparation condition, and response strength. To examine these neural representations, an SVM was trained separately for each factor to decode the two corresponding conditions (e.g., glossiness vs. aesthetics, rapid vs. slow, strong vs. weak). For each decoding, the relevant EEG trials were divided into two groups (e.g., glossiness vs. aesthetics for attribute, rapid vs. slow for temporal preparation, and strong vs. weak for response strength), which were then used to train and evaluate the classifier.

For attribute decoding, trials in which participants judged glossiness or aesthetics were pooled within each temporal preparation condition and classified according to the attribute being judged, regardless of the behavioral response. For temporal preparation decoding, trials within each attribute condition were divided into two groups (rapid vs. slow) and classified accordingly. For response strength decoding, EEG data were classified according to whether the stimulus was judged as “strong” or “weak”. The label for each stimulus was the same as in the ERP analysis. Although this procedure ignores individual differences in category boundaries, it allowed us to increase the signal-to-noise ratio by treating each test stimulus as having a stable perceived strength across participants.

For the SVM analysis, a 2000-ms window was extracted for each trial, spanning from 500 ms before stimulus onset to 1500 ms after stimulus onset. This window was analyzed using a sliding window approach with a width of 50 ms (50 time points at 1 ms intervals) and a step size of 10 ms. The EEG data within each time window (number of electrodes × 50 time points) were reshaped into a one-dimensional feature vector before decoding. To avoid bias in decoding accuracy because of unequal trial numbers between groups, underbagging was applied. Although the difference in the number of trials between groups was relatively small, this procedure was implemented to eliminate any remaining concern that slight imbalances in trial counts could influence decoding performance. In underbagging, the larger group was randomly downsampled 400 times to match the number of trials in the smaller group. The SVM decoding was performed in each iteration, and the mean accuracy across all iterations was used as the final decoding accuracy to ensure balanced trial numbers and stable estimation. For each iteration, a linear SVM was used to decode the class labels, and accuracy was evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation at the trial level per participant. During each cross-validation fold, features were standardized within the training data, and the same parameters were applied to the test data to prevent information leakage. The SVM learned to classify the two groups based on the training data, and the proportion of correctly predicted trials in the test data was taken as the decoding accuracy. The SVM analysis was conducted separately for each participant.

Additionally, since SVM decoding was performed repeatedly, the regularization strength hyperparameter was fixed at the reciprocal of the number of training samples, which is MATLAB's default setting. Other settings included using the dual stochastic gradient descent method to minimize the objective function, L1 (Lasso) regularization, and setting the optimization termination criterion to an absolute gradient tolerance of 10−8. Finally, decoding accuracies for each time window were averaged across participants. It should be noted that in the current paradigm, the EEG signals may include overlapping activity related to both perceptual processing and motor preparation, as manual responses were not temporally separated from perceptual judgments.

Statistical testing of the SVM decoding accuracy was conducted using a permutation test. The purpose of this test was to determine whether the decoding accuracy exceeded chance level (0.5). In this analysis, the EEG data for each trial were randomly assigned to two groups within each participant, and the same decoding procedure was performed. This procedure was repeated 400 times, and the significance of the observed decoding accuracy was evaluated on the basis of its percentile in the null distribution. Specifically, for each permutation sample, the decoding accuracy was averaged across participants to form an empirical null distribution from 400 permutation samples. The p-value was computed as the proportion of permutation samples whose mean decoding accuracy across participants exceeded the observed mean decoding accuracy.

Because tests were conducted across multiple time points, a multiple comparisons problem arose. To address this, a 1% false discovery rate (FDR) control using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was applied. Specifically, the p-values from the permutation tests at each time point were sorted in ascending order. Each p-value was multiplied by the total number of time points and divided by its rank to obtain the corrected p-value. Time points with corrected p-values less than 0.01 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Behavioral data

Figure 4 shows the relationship between the numbers of “strong” responses across all participants under slow-preparation and rapid-preparation conditions for (a) glossiness and (b) aesthetics. For both glossiness and aesthetics, participants’ responses were highly consistent across the two temporal-preparation conditions, indicating very high correlations (Glossiness: r = 0.980, p < 0.001; Aesthetics: r = 0.968, p < 0.001, Pearson's correlation coefficient). Thus, although temporal preparation was manipulated at the session level, participants’ overt response patterns were highly stable across the rapid and slow conditions.

Relationship between the numbers of “strong” responses in the slow-preparation and rapid-preparation conditions for (a) glossiness and (b) aesthetics. The horizontal axis indicates “strong” response numbers under the slow-preparation condition, and the vertical axis indicates response numbers under the rapid-preparation condition. Each circle represents one or more stimuli located at the same coordinate, and the size of each circle reflects the number of stimuli (i.e., frequency) corresponding to that response combination.

Additionally, Figure 5 shows the relationship between the numbers of “strong” responses for glossiness and aesthetics within the (a) rapid-preparation and (b) slow-preparation conditions. The correlations between the attributes were low (slow-preparation: r = 0.285, p < 0.001; rapid-preparation: r = 0.296, p < 0.001), indicating that participants used different criteria when judging glossiness and aesthetics, even for the same stimuli. We next examined whether these differences were reflected in the EEG signals.

Figure 9 shows the grand-average decoding accuracy between “strong” and “weak” stimuli, averaged across participants, based on EEG data for the glossiness (a) and aesthetics (b) attributes. For glossiness, decoding accuracy was significantly above chance from 60 ms to 1000 ms under both temporal preparation conditions, and it gradually increased after stimulus onset, peaking around 500 ms. For aesthetics, accuracy was significantly above chance during the 60–700 ms interval in the slow-preparation condition only. In contrast, in the rapid-preparation condition, above-chance accuracy was observed only around 500 ms and appeared intermittently. When comparing the temporal preparation conditions, decoding accuracy tended to be higher in the slow-preparation condition than in the rapid-preparation condition during the 0–500 ms period.

Based on the results of the linear SVM analyses, the effect of temporal preparation on the decoding accuracy appeared to differ between glossiness and aesthetics. To statistically evaluate the relationship between material impressions and temporal preparation, a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted. The 200–300 ms time window was selected a priori based on previous literature. Prior ERP studies have shown that object-level processing and category-related discrimination emerge approximately 200–300 ms after stimulus onset (e.g., Schendan & Maher, 2009; Grill-Spector & Kanwisher, 2005). In addition, studies of material perception have reported that properties such as glossiness can be discriminated from EEG signals from approximately 200 ms after stimulus onset (e.g., Orima et al., 2025). Based on these findings, we expected material-related processing to emerge within this latency range, and the 200–300 ms window was selected in advance. Inspection of the ERP waveforms in the present study revealed a prominent positive peak at occipital electrodes beginning at approximately 190 ms, which was broadly consistent with, although not perfectly aligned with, this predefined interval. Although the time window was selected in advance, it did not constitute a strictly independent a priori choice. Therefore, we conducted an additional sensitivity analysis to examine whether the results depended on this specific interval. This analysis was intended to assess the robustness of the findings with respect to the choice of time window.

Decoding accuracy was first averaged within 50-ms windows centered on each target time point. A 50-ms moving window (10-ms step) was applied across the 200–300 ms interval, yielding 10 window-based accuracy values per condition for each participant. These values were then averaged to obtain a single decoding-accuracy measure for each condition, as shown in Figure 10. This measure was submitted to a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with temporal preparation and attribute condition as within-subject factors, with each participant contributing one observation to each cell. The normality of residuals was assessed using the Lilliefors test, which indicated no significant deviation from normality (p = 0.50 > 0.05). Mauchly's test of sphericity was performed, yielding p = 0.202 > 0.05, indicating that the sphericity assumption was not violated. The results revealed a significant main effect of attribute condition (F(1, 25) = 11.55, p = 0.002, ηp2=0.318) and a significant interaction between temporal preparation and attribute condition (F(1,25)=6.60,p=0.017,ηp2=0.208). To confirm that these effects were not dependent on the exact choice of the analysis window, additional sensitivity analyses were conducted using time windows shifted by ±20 ms (i.e., 180–280 ms and 220–320 ms). Both shifted windows yielded significant main effects of attribute condition and significant interactions between temporal preparation and attribute condition (180–280 ms: main effect, F(1, 25) = 4.47, p = 0.04, ηp2=0.152; interaction, F(1,25)=6.54,p=0.01,ηp2=0.207; 220–320 ms: main effect, F(1, 25) = 16.46, p < 0.001, ηp2=0.397; interaction, F(1,25)=6.92,p=0.01,ηp2=0.217), indicating that the observed effects were robust to small variations in the selected time window. The significant interaction indicates that temporal preparation affects glossiness and aesthetics differently. To further investigate this interaction, post hoc simple main effect analyses were conducted by examining the effect of temporal preparation separately within each attribute condition. Pairwise comparisons between the rapid- and slow-preparation conditions were then performed separately for glossiness and aesthetics, with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. These analyses revealed that for the glossiness condition, decoding accuracy was significantly different between temporal preparation conditions (p = 0.037). In contrast, no significant difference was observed for the aesthetics condition (p = 0.20). Specifically, decoding accuracy was higher for glossiness under rapid than slow preparation, whereas aesthetics showed no significant difference between preparation conditions.

Mean decoding accuracy in the 200–300 ms time window for each condition. The two bars on the left correspond to glossiness, and the two bars on the right correspond to aesthetics. Within each attribute, results are shown separately for the rapid and slow temporal preparation conditions. Error bars represent ±2 standard deviations across participants.

To address potential concerns about the ANOVA assumptions, we also analyzed the data using a linear mixed-effects model with temporal preparation and attribute condition as fixed effects and participant as a random effect. The results likewise revealed a significant interaction between temporal preparation and attribute condition (F(1, 100) = 7.87, p = 0.006, f2 = 0.100), confirming that the differential influence of temporal preparation on glossiness and aesthetics decoding accuracy was robust across analytical approaches. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen's f2 based on the change in the coefficient of determination (R2) between the full and reduced models. The marginal R2 and conditional R2 for the full model were 0.17 and 0.30, respectively. These results suggest that temporal preparation does not uniformly modulate decoding performance, but instead exerts differential effects depending on the material attributes being decoded.

Note that, although decoding accuracy in the present study remained below 55%, this level of performance is comparable to that reported in previous EEG decoding studies. For example, prior work has shown that even for relatively low-level visual features such as color and orientation, decoding accuracies typically range around 55%–65% (Bocincova & Johnson, 2019). Similarly, studies on object and feature decoding using EEG have reported modest but reliable above-chance accuracies within a similar range (Grootswagers et al., 2017; Yip et al., 2022). These relatively low accuracy levels are generally attributed to the low signal-to-noise ratio and high variability inherent in EEG signals.

Therefore, the decoding performance observed in the present study should be interpreted as reflecting subtle but reliable differences in neural representations rather than strong classification performance. In this sense, the results provide evidence for systematic modulation of neural processing by temporal preparation, even though the absolute decoding accuracy remains modest.

Exploratory analysis 2: Decoding analysis based on chroma

To investigate how temporal preparation affects neural information processing, we conducted an additional analysis in which low-level visual features were decoded. Specifically, we aimed to examine whether the influence of temporal preparation was limited to attribute-relevant visual features or extended to attribute-independent features. Here, we focused on chroma as a low-level visual feature because color information is expected to be less relevant for glossiness judgments but more relevant for aesthetic judgments. If temporal preparation selectively influences attribute-relevant neural processing, temporal preparation should have little or no effect on decoding saturation from EEG recoded during glossiness judgments, but should modulate chroma decoding from EEG recoded during aesthetic judgments.

We grouped the stimuli based on their chroma levels as follows. The chroma of each stimulus image was defined by first converting the sRGB values of all pixels into the CIELAB color space with a white point of D65 chromaticity and then calculating the square root of the sum of squares of the a* and b* values for each pixel. Subsequently, the mean chroma across all pixels in each image was computed. Finally, the stimuli were divided into two groups based on this mean chroma: a “high-chroma” group and a “low-chroma” group. These two groups served as the decoding labels in this analysis.

The results are shown in Figure 13. This figure presents the decoding accuracy of stimulus chroma based on EEG recorded during glossiness judgments in panel (a) and aesthetic judgments in panel (b). The decoding accuracy based on EEG recorded during glossiness judgments was intermittently above chance up to approximately 700 ms, showing peaks around 50–100 ms for both temporal preparation conditions. This early peak is consistent with the nature of chroma as a low-level visual feature. However, little difference was observed between the two temporal preparation conditions.

The decoding accuracy based on EEG recorded during aesthetic judgments was significantly above chance from 0 to 1000 ms in the rapid-preparation condition. In contrast, in the slow-preparation condition, decoding accuracy was significantly above chance only from 0 to 250 ms. To test whether the early temporal processing of chroma differs depending on attribute relevance, a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with temporal preparation and attribute condition as factors was conducted for chroma decoding accuracy. Because chroma reflects a relatively low-level visual feature, we first examined an early time window (100–150 ms), which was selected in advance to capture early sensory processing. Prior studies have shown that basic visual properties such as color and orientation can be decoded from EEG signals as early as approximately 100 ms after stimulus onset, reflecting early stages of visual processing (e.g., Hajonides et al., 2021; Orima et al., 2025). In addition, this interval approximately corresponded to the latency of peak discrimination accuracy observed in the decoding time course. Because this choice was not strictly independent of the observed data, we conducted sensitivity analyses using slightly shifted time windows (±10 ms) to assess the robustness of the findings. Before conducting the ANOVA, we assessed whether its assumptions were satisfied. The normality of residuals was assessed using the Lilliefors test, which indicated no significant deviation from normality (p = 0.26 > 0.05). Mauchly's test of sphericity was performed, yielding p = 0.336 > 0.05, indicating that the sphericity assumption was not violated. In the 100–150 ms interval, a significant main effect of attribute condition was found (F(1,25)=5.29,p=0.030,ηp2=0.174), whereas the interaction between temporal preparation and attribute condition was not significant. Sensitivity analyses using adjacent time windows (90–140 ms and 110–160 ms) yielded the same pattern of results (90-140 ms: main effect, F(1,25)=3.95,p=0.05,ηp2=0.136; 110-160 ms: main effect, F(1,25)=5.81,p=0.02,ηp2=0.189), suggesting that the main effect of attribute condition was consistent across small variations in the selected time window.

For comparison with the main analysis, we also examined the 200–300 ms time window. In this interval, neither a main effect nor an interaction was observed. Thus, although chroma could be decoded in an early time range, the present analysis did not provide evidence that temporal preparation differentially modulated chroma decoding as a function of the attribute being judged in either the 100–150 ms or the 200–300 ms window. However, because this analysis was exploratory and the time window was not fully defined a priori, these results should be interpreted cautiously.

Discussion

The present study aimed to examine how temporal preparation modulates neural processing underlying material impression judgments by comparing EEG decoding accuracy between glossiness and aesthetics. Specifically, we investigated whether temporal preparation selectively biases processing toward earlier or later stages of visual processing for these two material attributes. The results showed that decoding accuracy for glossiness was higher when short stimulus durations were expected, whereas decoding accuracy for aesthetics tended to be higher when long stimulus durations were expected. Furthermore, a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA conducted in the 200–300 ms interval revealed a significant interaction in decoding accuracy between temporal preparation and attribute condition. Post hoc comparisons further showed that decoding accuracy differed significantly between temporal preparation conditions for glossiness, whereas the corresponding difference for aesthetics was not significant. This interaction indicates that the effect of temporal preparation on decoding accuracy differed between glossiness and aesthetics. Specifically, the findings are consistent with the hypothesis that rapid preparation enhances glossiness-related processing.

In addition, the results of the present study suggest that the two attributes examined, glossiness and aesthetics, may rely on neural processing with distinct temporal characteristics. For glossiness, higher decoding accuracy was observed under the rapid-preparation condition than under the slow-preparation condition. This result is consistent with the idea that the processing of glossiness relies primarily on relatively low-level visual cues, such as local luminance gradients and specular highlights in the visual input (Marlow, Kim, & Anderson, 2012; Sawayama & Nishida, 2018). In contrast, aesthetics tended to show higher decoding accuracy under the slow-preparation condition than under the rapid-preparation condition, though this tendency was not statistically significant. This suggests that higher-level, more time-consuming processing may be involved in judgments of aesthetics. However, because the simple comparison between temporal preparation conditions was not significant for aesthetics, this interpretation should be regarded as tentative. These findings align with previous research, which suggests that glossiness relies on relatively simple image features (Morimoto et al., 2026; Sawayama & Nishida, 2018; Wiebel, Toscani, & Gegenfurtner, 2015) extracted early in visual processing, whereas aesthetics and other complex material evaluations require the integration of multiple sources of information (Liu et al., 2015).

Effect of temporal preparation on neural activity

The additional analyses provide further insight into how temporal preparation influenced neural processing of material impressions. In the electrode-based decoding analysis, the interaction between temporal preparation and attribute condition was observed when decoding was performed using occipital electrodes. Because occipital activity is closely related to visual processing of the stimulus, this result suggests that temporal preparation influenced neural representations associated with visual analysis during material-impression judgments.

The chroma analysis further helped to characterize the nature of this effect. Because chroma reflects a low-level visual feature, this analysis allowed us to examine whether temporal preparation broadly modulated early visual processing irrespective of the judged material attribute. However, no interaction between temporal preparation and attribute condition was observed for chroma in either the 100–150 ms or the 200–300 ms time window. This result suggests that the main interaction observed for material impressions is not readily explained solely by a uniform modulation of low-level visual-feature processing. Rather, temporal preparation may have influenced stages of processing in which visual information is differentially used depending on the material attribute being judged.

Taken together, these findings suggest that temporal preparation may influence stages of processing in which visual information is selectively used for material-impression judgments, rather than affecting low-level visual coding in a feature-general manner. In other words, temporal preparation may modulate how visual information is weighted or used depending on the material attribute being judged. However, the present analyses do not specify which particular visual features or computational processes were altered by temporal preparation. Clarifying this issue will require future studies that directly manipulate candidate image features and examine how such manipulations interact with temporal preparation.

Methodological significance

The temporal characteristics of neural processing underlying material impressions have been an important topic in studies of material perception, as described in the Introduction. Previous studies on temporal characteristics often manipulated presentation duration (Nagai et al., 2015; Schyns & Oliva, 1999; Sharan et al., 2009; Wiebel et al., 2013) or measured reaction times (Cant & Goodale, 2007; Hansen & Hansen, 1988; Nagai et al., 2015; Sharan et al., 2009; Sharan et al., 2014a), which may have limited participants’ ability to fully observe the stimuli. Under such conditions, it becomes difficult to distinguish between the direct effects of restricted information from the stimulus and the indirect effects resulting from changes in participants’ judgment strategies caused by differences in available information. For example, the strategies used under a short presentation duration may differ from those under a longer presentation. In contrast, in the present study, we manipulated only participants’ temporal preparation, without limiting the actual presentation duration, thereby primarily modulating their judgment strategies. This methodological approach has the advantage of maintaining a constant amount of available stimulus information while clearly isolating the effects of strategy differences.

Furthermore, manipulating temporal preparation serves as a useful tool for probing differences in neural processing stages across material impressions, as discussed in the Introduction. The rapid-preparation condition is likely to bias processing toward lower-level visual analysis, which in turn may enhance glossiness perception. In this way, manipulating temporal preparation provides insights that cannot be obtained simply by analyzing correlations between visual features and material perception or by manipulating presentation duration. Specifically, the temporal-preparation manipulation helps identify which visual features of the stimulus participants prioritize and which stages of visual processing they rely on, offering insights into processing strategies that are difficult to capture by varying presentation duration alone or by measuring reaction times.

Indeed, our decoding analysis revealed an interaction between material impressions (glossiness vs. aesthetics) and temporal preparation. These results suggest that manipulating temporal preparation provides a useful way to probe the temporal hierarchy of material perception without confounding stimulus exposure time.

Limitations of the study and future directions

A limitation of the present study is that the observed effects of temporal preparation were evident only at the neural level, as revealed by EEG decoding, whereas no clear differences were detected in behavioral measures. One possible reason is that the stimuli used in this study were designed to elicit easily distinguishable judgments for glossiness and aesthetics, which may have reduced the sensitivity of participants’ responses to variations in temporal preparation. Future studies should therefore use experimental designs that are more sensitive to behavioral changes, for example by using stimuli with more ambiguous material properties or by adopting tasks that require finer perceptual discrimination. Such approaches would allow researchers to test whether temporal preparation can influence perceptual decisions or response patterns at the behavioral level. Importantly, obtaining converging behavioral evidence would make it possible to directly link the neural modulations observed in EEG decoding to changes in perception or decision-making, thereby clarifying whether the temporal preparation effects identified in the present study have functional consequences for material impressions.

Finally, the present findings suggest that temporal preparation may serve as a useful tool for probing the temporal characteristics of material perception. Future studies could apply this approach to other material attributes beyond glossiness and aesthetics, such as transparency, roughness, or softness, to examine how temporal preparation differentially influences their processing. By systematically extending this method to a wider range of material properties, it may be possible to map the temporal profiles of diverse material impressions and clarify how different levels of processing contribute to their formation. Such work would help establish temporal preparation as a general methodological framework for investigating the temporal dynamics of material perception.

Conclusions

In this study, temporal preparation influenced decoding accuracy for material-impression judgments: the rapid-preparation condition biased expectations toward short stimulus durations, whereas the slow-preparation condition biased expectations toward long stimulus durations. These results suggest that temporal preparation modulates the neural processing of material impressions. Furthermore, the influence of temporal preparation differed between glossiness and aesthetics, with a significant interaction observed between temporal preparation and material impressions. Specifically, decoding accuracy for glossiness was higher under the rapid-preparation condition, whereas decoding accuracy for aesthetics tended to be higher under the slow-preparation condition. These results suggest that temporal preparation interacts with the material attribute being judged and can be used to probe the temporal characteristics of attribute-specific processing. That is, glossiness appears to rely more on early visual cues that are processed rapidly, whereas aesthetics may involve more extended and integrative processing. Therefore, this study provides novel insights into the temporal dynamics of material impressions by leveraging temporal preparation as a methodological tool.
