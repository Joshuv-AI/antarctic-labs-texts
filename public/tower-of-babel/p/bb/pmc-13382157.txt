Tumor‐type–specific expression of TTF‐1 in primary central nervous system tumors classified by the 2021 WHO criteria
Authors: Lu Xu, Hong‐Jiu Ren, Jian Gu, Bing‐Wen Shan, Tong Ren, Chu‐Hang Liu, Chun‐Xian Cai, Xiao‐Yu Zhang, Juan‐Han Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThyroid transcription factor‐1 (TTF‐1) is a widely used immunohistochemical marker for tumors of thyroid and pulmonary origin; however, its expression profile and diagnostic relevance in primary central nervous system (CNS) tumors remain incompletely characterized, particularly following the revisions in the 2021 WHO Classification of CNS Tumors. We systematically evaluated TTF‐1 expression in a cohort of 214 primary CNS tumors diagnosed between 2021 and 2024 at The First Affiliated Hospital of China Medical University and classified according to current WHO criteria. Immunohistochemistry for TTF‐1 was performed, and expression patterns were correlated with histological tumor type, molecular classification, and WHO grade. Statistical analyses were conducted to compare expression frequencies across tumor subtypes. TTF‐1 showed tumor‐specific expression patterns. It was highly sensitive in central neurocytomas (89.47%) and fully specific for subependymal giant cell astrocytoma (SEGA) among circumscribed gliomas. Focal TTF‐1 positivity was sporadically detected in high‐grade diffuse gliomas, without significant links to molecular alterations, anatomical sites, or other common immunohistochemical indicators. TTF‐1 was consistently negative in multiple newly classified and rare CNS tumors, whereas partial intracranial mesenchymal tumors with FET–CREB fusions presented TTF‐1 positivity. In conclusion, TTF‐1 serves as a reliable sensitive marker for central neurocytomas and a highly specific marker for SEGA. Given its occasional positivity in high‐grade gliomas and certain intracranial mesenchymal tumors, pathologists should interpret TTF‐1 results prudently to avoid misdiagnosing primary CNS lesions as metastatic tumors. TTF‐1 is therefore a valuable auxiliary biomarker for difficult differential diagnosis of CNS tumors.

Introduction

In routine diagnostic practice, overlapping expression of thyroid transcription factor‐1 (TTF‐1) has been observed in both primary and metastatic central nervous system (CNS) tumors. In addition, variability in sensitivity among different antibody clones further complicates interpretation, posing significant challenges for differential diagnosis. Clarifying the expression profile of TTF‐1 across diverse CNS tumor entities is therefore essential for optimizing its clinical application.

TTF‐1 is a member of the NKX2 homeodomain transcription factor family and plays a pivotal role in early embryonic development and morphogenesis of the thyroid, lung, and diencephalon. Although TTF‐1 mRNA can be detected at low levels in a wide range of adult tissues, protein expression is largely restricted to thyroid follicular cells and respiratory epithelium, including type II pneumocytes and bronchiolar Clara cells [1, 2]. In diagnostic pathology, TTF‐1 is widely used as an immunohistochemical marker of pulmonary and thyroid origin and serves as a valuable tool for determining the primary site of metastatic tumors [3]. Commonly used TTF‐1 antibody clones include SP141, SPT24, and 8G7G3/1. Comparative studies, particularly those comparing SPT24 and 8G7G3/1, have demonstrated that SPT24 exhibits significantly higher sensitivity [4, 5]. While 8G7G3/1 is often recommended for diagnosing primary lung adenocarcinoma because of its specificity, its lower sensitivity limits its broader diagnostic utility. No single clone is universally optimal, and the higher sensitivity of SPT24 may provide additional value in selected tumor contexts [6]. Previous studies have reported TTF‐1 expression in a subset of primary CNS tumors, including neurohypophyseal tumors of the sellar region, such as granular cell tumor and pituicytoma, as well as tumors of the third ventricle, including chordoid glioma and subependymal giant cell astrocytoma [7, 8]. TTF‐1 positivity has also been described in glioblastoma and central neurocytoma; however, reported results have been inconsistent, likely reflecting differences in antibody clones and methodological approaches across studies [7, 9, 10, 11, 12]. Importantly, metastatic CNS tumors – particularly those originating from the lung or thyroid – may also express TTF‐1, resulting in diagnostic overlap with primary CNS neoplasms and further increasing diagnostic complexity.

The 2021 fifth edition of the WHO Classification of Tumors of the Central Nervous System introduced substantial revisions by integrating molecular parameters into tumor classification and grading, leading to the addition and redefinition of multiple tumor entities, such as polymorphous low‐grade neuroepithelial tumor of the young and FET–CREB fusion‐positive intracranial mesenchymal tumor [13]. To date, TTF‐1 expression in these newly defined or reclassified entities has not been systematically investigated.

In this study, we analyzed a large cohort of primary CNS tumors classified according to the latest WHO criteria to systematically assess the expression profile of the nuclear transcription factor TTF‐1 using the SPT24 antibody clone across diverse histological types and molecular subgroups. We further explored correlations between TTF‐1 expression and key glioma‐associated biomarkers, including GFAP, OLIG2, p53, and Ki67. By integrating critical molecular features such as IDH mutation status and 1p/19q codeletion, this retrospective analysis employed multidimensional statistical approaches to comprehensively evaluate associations between TTF‐1 expression and clinical characteristics, histopathological classification, WHO grade, immunohistochemical markers, and molecular alterations, with the aim of clarifying its potential pathophysiological relevance and diagnostic utility in CNS tumor pathology.

Materials and methods

Patient selection and ethics statement

Formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) tissue samples were obtained from patients who underwent surgical resection or biopsy for primary CNS tumors at The First Affiliated Hospital of China Medical University between 2021 and 2024. A total of 214 cases were included. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of China Medical University [approval no. AF‐SOP‐07‐1.2‐01; review no. (2025) 337].

Tumor classification

All cases were reviewed and classified according to the 2021 fifth edition of the WHO Classification of Tumors of the Central Nervous System. Integrated diagnoses were established based on histomorphology, immunohistochemistry, and molecular findings where applicable. Tumors were categorized into adult‐type diffuse gliomas, pediatric‐type diffuse gliomas, circumscribed astrocytic gliomas, glioneuronal and neuronal tumors, ependymal tumors, choroid plexus tumors, and other CNS tumor entities (Table 1).

Tumor classification

IDH mutation status was assessed in parallel via two approaches: immunohistochemistry with an IDH1 R132H‐specific antibody (clone MX031, MXB Biotech, Fuzhou, Fujian, PR China), and PCR‐based sequencing covering IDH1 and IDH2 hotspot mutations. All molecular and immunohistochemical analyses were performed at the Department of Pathology, The First Affiliated Hospital of China Medical University, Shenyang, Liaoning, PR China.

Immunohistochemistry

TTF‐1 immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical staining for TTF‐1 was performed on FFPE tissue sections using a ready‐to‐use mouse monoclonal antibody (clone SPT24, MXB Biotech, Fuzhou, Fujian, PR China; ready‐to‐use). Sections (4 μm thick) were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the sections in EDTA buffer (pH 9.0, MXB Biotech) for 20 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubation with hydrogen peroxide at room temperature for 10 min. After rinsing with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), sections were incubated with the primary antibody for 60 min at room temperature, followed by incubation with a polymer‐based secondary antibody (MXB Biotech) for 15 min. Immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine (DAB, MXB Biotech) as the chromogen. Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted.

Evaluation of TTF‐1 immunostaining

TTF‐1 expression was independently evaluated by two or more experienced pathologists blinded to clinical data. Nuclear staining in tumor cells was regarded as positive. Immunohistochemical staining was semi‐quantified according to the proportion of positive tumor cells and staining intensity (Table 2). Based on previously established criteria [9], a case was classified as positive if the combined score (calculated as the product of the proportion score and the intensity score) was ≥1 and negative if the combined score was <1. In cases of discrepancy, a consensus was reached by joint review.

Other immunohistochemical markers

This study utilized retrospectively assembled diagnostic immunohistochemical data, including Ki‐67 (rabbit monoclonal antibody, clone 30‐9, Ventana Medical Systems, Tucson, AZ, USA; ready‐to‐use), GFAP (mouse monoclonal antibody, clone MX047, MXB Biotech; ready‐to‐use), OLIG2 (rabbit monoclonal antibody, clone MXR048, MXB Biotech; ready‐to‐use), and p53 (mouse monoclonal antibody, clone DO‐7, Ventana Medical Systems; ready‐to‐use), derived from original pathology archives.

The Ki‐67 labeling index was systematically re‐evaluated across the entire cohort to ensure consistency in comparative analyses. Data availability for GFAP, OLIG2, and p53 was variable, reflecting their guideline‐driven and selective application in routine diagnostic practice; these established markers were included to explore their potential association with TTF‐1 expression in this translational analysis.

Additional immunohistochemical stains performed according to contemporary WHO diagnostic criteria included ATRX (mouse monoclonal antibody, clone MX071, MXB Biotech; ready‐to‐use) and H3K27M (rabbit monoclonal antibody, clone RM192, MXB Biotech; ready‐to‐use). These markers were documented in diagnostic archives but were not primary endpoints of this study.

Molecular analysis

Molecular analyses were performed on retrospectively archived tissue samples in the hospital's molecular pathology laboratory. PCR‐based assays were applied using an ABI 7500 Fast Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) to detect MGMT promoter methylation, TERT promoter mutations, IDH mutations, and BRAF V600E mutation, while fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) was performed on a Nikon 90i fluorescence microscope (Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) to identify other molecular alterations, including 1p/19q codeletion, EGFR amplification, and CDKN2A/B homozygous deletion. All PCR kits and FISH probes used in this study were commercially supplied by Genetron Health (Beijing, PR China). All experiments were performed in‐house following standard protocols and the manufacturer's instructions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Associations between TTF‐1 expression and clinicopathological variables, including age, sex, tumor location, tumor subtype, and immunohistochemical markers (GFAP, OLIG2, p53, and Ki67), were evaluated using Pearson's chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

TTF‐1 expression in diffuse gliomas

Adult‐type diffuse gliomas

These TTF‐1‐positive glioblastomas demonstrated marked cytological atypia, multinucleated giant tumor cells, necrosis, and microvascular proliferation on hematoxylin and eosin staining (Figure 1A–C), with nuclear positivity for TTF‐1 confirmed by immunohistochemistry (Figure 1D). The remaining glioblastomas were negative for TTF‐1 (Figure 2A). In astrocytoma, IDH‐mutant, TTF‐1 positivity was observed in 6.25% (1/16) of cases. Expression was detected in one WHO grade 4 astrocytoma, whereas all WHO grade 2 and grade 3 astrocytomas were negative (Figure 2B,C). No significant difference in TTF‐1 expression was observed between glioblastomas and astrocytomas (p = 0.6).

Discussion

Thyroid transcription factor‐1 (TTF‐1) is a 38‐kDa nuclear DNA‐binding protein belonging to the NKX2 homeobox family and functions as a tissue‐restricted transcriptional regulator. In adult tissues, TTF‐1 expression is largely confined to endoderm‐derived thyroid follicular epithelium, pulmonary alveolar type II cells, and bronchiolar Clara cells, as well as selected regions of the brain [7, 14]. Within the central nervous system, TTF‐1 expression exhibits a highly restricted spatial distribution during development, particularly involving the ventral hypothalamus, the floor of the third ventricle, and the medial ganglionic eminence (MGE) [15]. Notably, the MGE represents a transient embryonic structure that regresses within the first year of life [16], providing a developmental explanation for the absence of TTF‐1‐expressing cells in corresponding regions of the adult brain.

From a technical perspective, several antibody clones targeting TTF‐1 have been applied in diagnostic immunohistochemistry, among which SPT24 and 8G7G3/1 are the most widely used. Comparative studies have demonstrated that the SPT24 clone exhibits higher sensitivity for TTF‐1 detection, particularly in pulmonary neoplasms [4, 5]. Given its reported superior binding affinity and diagnostic performance, the present study employed the TTF‐1 (SPT24) clone to systematically evaluate TTF‐1 expression across a large cohort of primary CNS tumors.

Importantly, the definition of TTF‐1 immunopositivity varies substantially across published studies. While some investigations have relied solely on the percentage of positive tumor cells using relatively high cut‐off values [17, 18], others – particularly those focusing on CNS tumors – have adopted combined scoring systems incorporating both staining extent and intensity, with lower positivity thresholds [9]. In line with the latter approach, and to ensure comparability with existing CNS‐focused literature, we applied a semi‐quantitative scoring system integrating both parameters and defined positivity as a total score ≥1.

In high‐grade diffuse gliomas, previous studies have reported low but reproducible rates of TTF‐1 positivity, particularly in WHO grade 4 astrocytic tumors [9, 19]. However, these studies did not distinguish between glioblastoma, IDH‐wildtype, and astrocytoma, IDH‐mutant, WHO grade 4. By stratifying cases according to the current WHO classification, we demonstrated that TTF‐1 expression occurs in both entities, with comparable positivity rates. Notably, TTF‐1‐positive WHO grade 4 diffuse gliomas in our cohort consistently exhibited pronounced anaplastic features, including endothelial proliferation, pseudopalisading necrosis, increased mitotic activity, and tumor giant cells. These findings are concordant with earlier observations and suggest that TTF‐1 expression in high‐grade gliomas may be associated with tumor dedifferentiation rather than lineage specificity [9]. Nevertheless, given the limited number of positive cases, this association should be interpreted with caution. The potential relationship between TTF‐1 expression and tumor location remains controversial. While Drew et al reported a predilection for frontal lobe localization in TTF‐1‐positive high‐grade gliomas [20], our data did not demonstrate a statistically significant association between TTF‐1 expression and anatomical site.

Similarly, although an inverse correlation between TTF‐1 and p53 expression has been described in pulmonary adenocarcinoma [21], no such relationship was identified in diffuse gliomas in the present study. These discrepancies may reflect intrinsic biological differences between central nervous system and pulmonary tumors, as well as inherent limitations related to cohort size and retrospective study design. It should be noted that p53 immunohistochemistry serves only as a routine pathological surrogate for TP53 pathway alteration but cannot precisely define actual TP53 mutation status [22]. Regrettably, due to the limitations of retrospective sample collection and available experimental conditions, further TP53 gene sequencing validation could not be performed in the present cohort. Beyond p53, we further analyzed the association of TTF‐1 expression with major molecular alterations in glioblastoma, including IDH1/2 mutation, EGFR amplification, TERT promoter mutation, 1p/19q codeletion, CDKN2A/B homozygous deletion, BRAF V600E mutation, and MGMT promoter methylation; none of these molecular markers showed a significant correlation with TTF‐1 expression (all p > 0.05). Specifically, expanding the cohort size and adopting next‐generation sequencing to characterize molecular profiles in a genetically defined population will be essential to further clarify these relationships. Importantly, because TTF‐1 is routinely used to identify pulmonary origin in metastatic brain tumors, awareness of its potential expression in primary gliomas is essential to avoid diagnostic misinterpretation, particularly in small biopsy specimens.

Central neurocytoma represents one of the CNS tumor entities with the highest frequency of TTF‐1 expression. Consistent with and extending previous reports [7, 23], we observed a high positivity rate in central neurocytomas, while no TTF‐1 expression was detected in extraventricular neurocytomas in our cohort. This finding supports the hypothesis that these tumors may originate from TTF‐1–positive progenitor cells located within the subventricular zone [24, 25]. From a practical diagnostic perspective, the marked difference in TTF‐1 expression between central neurocytomas and morphologically overlapping entities – such as oligodendroglioma and ependymoma – highlights the utility of TTF‐1 as an adjunct immunohistochemical marker [26, 27, 28]. When interpreted in conjunction with established markers, including GFAP, OLIG2, and NeuN, TTF‐1 may substantially improve diagnostic accuracy in challenging cases. Notably, the number of extraventricular neurocytoma cases in this study was limited; therefore, a definitive expression pattern cannot be concluded, and further investigation with larger samples is warranted.

The strong and uniform expression of TTF‐1 in all SEGAs (6/6), contrasting with its complete absence in other circumscribed astrocytic gliomas, such as pilocytic astrocytoma and pleomorphic xanthoastrocytoma (0/19), highlights a distinctive immunophenotypic profile for this entity. To evaluate TTF‐1 as a diagnostic marker for SEGA among circumscribed gliomas, we compared SEGAs (6/6) with all other circumscribed gliomas (0/19); revealing a statistically significant difference (p < 0.0001), which underscores the absolute specificity within this morphologic group. Furthermore, a comparison between SEGAs (100%, 6/6) and adult‐type diffuse gliomas (9.52%, 4/42) also demonstrated a statistically significant difference (p < 0.0001). This pattern not only reinforces the proposed origin of SEGAs from TTF‐1‐positive progenitor cells of the subventricular zone but also establishes TTF‐1 immunohistochemistry as a practical adjunct tool for distinguishing SEGA from its morphological mimics in diagnostically challenging cases.

In contrast, TTF‐1 expression was consistently absent in pediatric diffuse midline glioma, astroblastoma, MN1‐altered [29], desmoplastic infantile ganglioglioma, and dysplastic cerebellar gangliocytoma. The anatomical distribution of these tumors does not overlap with CNS regions known to express TTF‐1 during development, which may partially account for the lack of immunoreactivity. In addition, we report for the first time the absence of TTF‐1 expression in polymorphous low‐grade neuroepithelial tumor of the young, further expanding the immunophenotypic characterization of this recently defined entity. Interestingly, TTF‐1 expression was identified in a subset of FET–CREB fusion‐positive intracranial mesenchymal tumors, a finding not previously reported. Given that TTF‐1 expression has generally been absent in extracranial mesenchymal tumors driven by similar gene fusions [30, 31, 32], the biological basis of this observation remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

In summary, this study clarifies the diagnostic utility of TTF‐1 in CNS tumors. In the context of intraventricular and neuronal tumors, TTF‐1 serves as a highly sensitive marker for central neurocytomas, providing a reliable adjunct to distinguish them from oligodendroglioma and ependymoma. Among circumscribed gliomas, it demonstrates near‐absolute specificity for subependymal giant cell astrocytoma (SEGA), distinguishing it from other morphologically overlapping low‐grade gliomas. Furthermore, our data expand the expression profile by documenting TTF‐1 negativity in several rare entities, including polymorphous low‐grade neuroepithelial tumor of the young (PLNTY), astroblastoma, desmoplastic infantile ganglioglioma, and dysplastic gangliocytoma of the cerebellum. While TTF‐1 expression can be observed in glioblastoma, its presence must be interpreted with caution when differentiating high‐grade glioma from metastatic carcinoma. These findings collectively reinforce the role of TTF‐1 as a valuable adjunct in specific diagnostic scenarios. Future multicenter studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate these observations and refine its application in routine diagnostic algorithms.
