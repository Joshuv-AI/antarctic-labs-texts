GULP1 enhances GLUT4 translocation by counteracting ACAP1–ARF6 inhibition
Authors: Xin Wen, Jin Mei, Yidan Jiang, Meiyu Qian, Juan Wang, Shibo Xu, Yurui Su, Lili Xu, Qin Liu, Mengyuan Zhao, Huizi Zhang, Jingzhou Wang, Cuizhe Wang, Jun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe molecular underpinnings of obesity-induced insulin resistance, a key driver of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), remain incompletely understood, limiting targeted therapies. While our prior work revealed that miR‑548ab impairs glucose homeostasis by downregulating GULP1, the precise mechanism by which GULP1 regulates insulin sensitivity, particularly through the GLUT4 trafficking machinery, is unknown. This study aimed to elucidate the novel function and molecular basis of GULP1 in controlling insulin-stimulated GLUT4 translocation and systemic glucose metabolism.MethodsBioinformatics analysis predicted interactions between GULP1 and GLUT4 vesicle regulators (ACAP1, ARF6). In vitro and in vivo models were used to assess GULP1’s effects on glucose uptake and GLUT4 translocation. Insulin resistance was evaluated via glucose tolerance tests and insulin sensitivity assays. Mechanistic studies investigated how GULP1 promotes insulin-mediated GLUT4 translocation to the plasma membrane by alleviating ACAP1-induced inhibition of ARF6 activity.ResultsGULP1 significantly enhanced glucose uptake in adipocytes and muscle cells by promoting GLUT4 translocation to the plasma membrane. In obese mice, GULP1 overexpression improved insulin sensitivity and glucose tolerance. Mechanistically, GULP1 counteracted ACAP1’s inhibition of ARF6 activity, thereby facilitating insulin-stimulated GLUT4 trafficking.ConclusionGULP1 can bind to ACAP1 and ARF6, and alleviate the inhibitory effect of ACAP1 on ARF6 activity, thereby enhancing insulin-mediated glucose uptake and improving insulin sensitivity in obese mice. This study reveals a novel mechanism of GULP1 in glucose metabolism and provides a new therapeutic target for treating obesity-related metabolic diseases. Subject terms: Monosaccharides, Type 2 diabetes

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a metabolic disorder characterized by insulin resistance (IR), manifested as impaired insulin-dependent glucose uptake in adipose and skeletal muscle tissues [1–6]. The global prevalence of T2DM is projected to rise substantially [7]. Adipose dysfunction can lead to abnormal secretion of adipokines, including exosomal miRNAs, inducing insulin resistance in adipose tissue, muscle, and liver, thereby driving obesity-related T2DM [8–15]. In obese states, the expression profile of specific miRNAs is significantly altered, influencing various signaling pathways involved in T2DM pathogenesis [16–21].

Our previous study demonstrated that miR-548ab downregulates GULP PTB domain-containing engulfment adaptor 1 (GULP1), inhibits glucose uptake in adipocytes, and impairs glucose homeostasis in HFD-induced obese mice and db/db mice [22]. GULP1 is linked to hemoglobin A1c levels [23], and GULP1 knockdown in 3T3-L1 cells delays adipogenesis and reduces insulin-stimulated glucose uptake [24]. These findings suggest that GULP1 plays a significant role in the regulation of glucose metabolism, but its exact mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated.

ADP ribosylation factor 6 (ARF6), a small GTPase, regulates multiple cellular processes, including endocytosis, secretion, phagocytosis, cell adhesion, and migration. Its activity depends on GTP binding (active state) and GDP binding (inactive state) [25–30]. ARF6 plays a key role in cortical actin reorganization and GLUT4 translocation to the plasma membrane [30, 31]. In 3T3-L1 adipocytes, a dominant-negative ARF6 (T27N) selectively inhibits F-actin formation and GLUT4 translocation to the plasma membrane. Additionally, ARF6 activity is positively regulated by GRP1, as a guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF), while ArfGAP with Coiled-coil, ankyrin repeats Protein 1 (ACAP1), acting as an ARF6 GTPase-activating protein (GAP), negatively regulates ARF6-mediated trafficking pathway [31–39]. ACAP1 interacts with GLUT4 storage vehicles (GSVs) and recruits clathrin, modulating clathrin-dependent endocytosis via its interaction with ARF6, but its role in GLUT4 translocation to the plasma membrane remains unclear. Upon insulin stimulation, ARF6 interacts with GSVs, promoting translocation of GSVs to the plasma membrane [30, 40–44]. GULP1, an adaptor protein with multiple protein interaction domains, has been shown to interact with ACAP1 and ARF6 [45]. GULP1 binds to ARF6-GTP via its PTB domain, reversing the ACAP1-induced decrease in ARF6-GTP levels in mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs), suggesting that GULP1, ACAP1, and ARF6-GTP may form a trimeric complex [45]. However, the role of GULP1 in GLUT4 membrane translocation through its interaction with ACAP1 and ARF6 remains to be explored.

A total of 20 individuals were enrolled in this study, comprising 10 with normal weight (NW, 18.5 ≤ BMI < 24 kg/m²) and 10 with obesity (OB, BMI ≥ 28 kg/m²). The baseline characteristics of the individuals are shown in Table 1. Omental adipose and skeletal muscle tissues from individuals with normal weight and with obesity were collected aseptically during surgery (≈3 × 3 cm adipose, 1 × 1 cm muscle), frozen in liquid nitrogen. Sample size was based on sample availability.

Determination of GULP1 protein content in adipose tissues

After homogenizing the adipose tissue, the GULP1 protein content was measured using a human GULP1 ELISA kit (COIBO BIO, CB18987-Hu, Shanghai, China).

Cell lines and transfection

Mouse preadipocyte 3T3-L1 and myoblast C2C12 were from the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China), and the human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293 T cells were purchased from Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). All cell lines were authenticated by the suppliers using short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and tested negative for mycoplasma contamination upon receipt. 3T3-L1 cells were differentiated over 8 days with sequential DMEM: 10% FBS + 1 μM Dexamethasone (Sigma, D4902, USA) + 0.5 mM IBMX (Sigma, I5789, USA) + 10 μg/mL Insulin (2 days) → 10% FBS + 10 μg/mL Insulin (2 days) → 10% FBS (4 days), with medium changes every 2 days. C2C12 myoblasts were differentiated in 2% horse serum-DMEM for 4 days. HEK-293T cells were cultured in 4.5 g/L D-glucose-DMEM (10% FBS + 1% penicillin streptomycin) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Transfection used Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Scientific, 2276051, USA).

Isolation of Primary White Adipocytes

Inguinal white adipose from 2- to 4-week-old mice was minced and digested with collagenase I (2 mg/mL) at 37 °C (40–60 min). The digestion was stopped by adding 10% FBS. The cell suspension was filtered (100 μm), centrifuged (200 × g, 5 min), resuspended in DMEM/F12 + 10% FBS + 1% penicillin streptomycin, and cultured (37 °C, 5% CO₂, medium changed after 12 h).

Plasmid and siRNA fragments

GULP1/ACAP1 plasmids and GULP1/ARF6 siRNAs were constructed at Beijing Hesheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Antibodies

Antibodies used for western blot (all 1:1000 dilution): GULP1 (Abcam, ab71309, UK), ACAP1 (Santa Cruz, sc-376574, USA), ARF6 (Santa Cruz, sc-7971, USA), Arf6-GTP (NEWLF, NL23008, China), GLUT4 (Proteintech, 66846-1-Ig, Wuhan, China), mCherry (Proteintech, 26765-1-AP, Wuhan, China), Na+/K+ ATPase (Proteintech, 14418-1-AP, Wuhan, China), GEP100 (TargetMol, TMAB-06437, USA), ACAP2 (Proteintech, 14029-1-AP, Wuhan, China), CYTH2 (Proteintech, 10405-1-AP, Wuhan, China), ARAP2 (Proteintech, 25143-1-AP, Wuhan, China), GAPDH (Zhongshan Jinqiao, TA-08, Beijing, China), and β-Tubulin (Zhongshan Jinqiao, TA-10, Beijing, China).

Glucose uptake in cells

Cells seeded in plates were cultured 24 h post-transfection, then starved in serum-free low-glucose DMEM for 6 h, incubated with 100 nM 2-NBDG (MCE, 186689-07-6, USA) for 15 min, washed with PBS, and detected via fluorescence microplate reader (excitation wavelength: 467 nm, emission wavelength: 542 nm).

Membrane/intracellular protein separation

The experiment was performed according to the instructions of the membrane protein extraction kit from Proteintech. Cells or minced fresh tissue (100 mg/mL) were lysed in Extraction Buffer A supplemented with protease inhibitors (1:100 v/v) using a vortex-lysis protocol (10 s on/10 s off, 5 cycles, ice bath). Tissue homogenates were mechanically disrupted with a glass homogenizer (50 strokes, ice bath). Following centrifugation at 700 × g (10 min, 4 °C), supernatants containing intracellular proteins were collected. Membrane proteins were isolated by sequential high-speed centrifugation (16,000 × g, 30 min → 10 min, 4 °C) with Extraction Buffer B resuspension (100 μL, 3 vortex cycles). Protein samples were denatured in SDS-PAGE loading buffer (3:1 v/v) at 100 °C (10 min) or 37 °C (45 min) for intracellular and membrane fractions, respectively. Trypan blue exclusion confirmed >80% lysis efficiency.

Co-immunoprecipitation

GULP1’s IP-grade antibody (Santa Cruz, sc-374591, USA) was diluted in PBST, and 20 μL of magnetic beads were added. The mixture was incubated on a rotating mixer at 4 °C for 2 h. After magnetic separation, the beads were collected and washed three times with PBST. The beads were then mixed with whole-protein lysate and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing, immunoprecipitated complexes were eluted in loading buffer, boiled, and analyzed by Western blot.

Western blot

Equal protein amounts were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked with 5% non-fat milk-TBST, and incubated with primary antibodies (5% BSA, 4 °C overnight). After TBST washes, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (room temperature, 2 h), washed, and proteins visualized. Band intensities were quantified using Image J.

ARF6 activity assay

Cells were lysed (RIPA + PMSF). Active ARF6 monoclonal antibody (1 μL) plus protein A/G beads (20 μL) were incubated (4 °C, 1 h, shaking). The mixture was centrifuged at 5000 × g for 1 min, the supernatant was discarded, and the beads were washed three times with 500 μL lysis buffer through sequential centrifugation and supernatant removal. The samples were resuspended in 20 μL 2× SDS-PAGE sample buffer and subjected to western blot analysis.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were fixed (4% PFA, 20 min, 4 °C), washed three times with PBS, blocked (5% BSA, 30 min, RT), incubated with primary antibody (overnight, 4 °C), rewarmed (37 °C, 30 min), washed three times with PBST, incubated with fluorescent secondary antibody (1:1000, 37 °C, 1 h, dark), nuclei were stained (DAPI, 1 μg/mL, 5 min), washed, mounted, imaged (Nikon confocal laser scanning microscope).

Oil Red O staining

Cells were washed with PBS, fixed in 10% formaldehyde (15 min, RT), then stained with filtered Oil Red O working solution (freshly prepared by mixing 0.5% Oil Red O in isopropanol with distilled water at 3:2 v/v, filtered through 0.22 μm membrane) for 1 h at room temperature. After staining, cells were washed three times with PBS and examined microscopically.

H&E staining

Paraffin sections were deparaffinized (3× xylene, 5 min each), rehydrated in descending ethanol (100%-70%, 5 min each), stained with Mayer’s hematoxylin (10 min), differentiated in acid alcohol (1% HCl in 70% ethanol, 5 s), counterstained with 0.5% eosin Y (10 min), dehydrated in ascending ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam for microscopy.

Insulin treatment

Cells were treated with insulin at a concentration of 100 nM for 30 min, mice were i.p. injected with insulin (0.5 U/kg) for 30 min.

Animal experiment

Four-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Slake Jingda Experimental Animal Company. All mice were maintained under a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle (temperature 22 ± 2 °C, humidity 50 ± 5%). Mice were randomly assigned to receive a high-fat diet (Medicience, MD12033, 60% fat kcal%, China) or a normal diet (Medicience, MD12062, 10% fat kcal%, China). To generate mouse models with GULP1 overexpression or knockdown specifically in inguinal white adipose tissue and skeletal muscle, adeno-associated virus 9 (AAV9, Genepharma, Shanghai, China) carrying mCherry-OE-GULP1 or mCherry-shGULP1 was injected into the inguinal adipose tissue and skeletal muscle of C57BL/6 mice. Group allocation for AAV9 injection was randomized, and investigators were blinded throughout the experiment (n = 6 per group based on previous studies).

Glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity test

Glucose-tolerance tests were performed in mice after 12 h fasting. After determination of FBG levels, each mouse was intraperitoneally injected with 2 g/kg of glucose. Blood glucose levels were measured at 30, 45, 60, and 120 min. After fasting for 6 h, mice underwent insulin sensitivity testing and were injected intraperitoneally with 0.5 IU/kg of insulin. Blood glucose levels were detected after 30, 45, 60, and 120 min.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0. The sample size (n) represents the number of biologically independent experiments or individual participants. Data distribution was assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous data are presented as mean ± SEM (for experimental bar graphs) or mean ± SD / median (IQR) (for baseline characteristics of individuals). For comparisons between two groups of normally distributed data, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test was used. For comparisons involving multiple experimental groups against a single control group, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test. Non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test (for two groups) or the Kruskal-Wallis test (for multiple groups). Correlation analysis was performed using Pearson’s (for normally distributed data) or Spearman’s (for non-normally distributed data) correlation coefficient. A two-tailed P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

GULP1 expression is down-regulated in adipose and skeletal muscle of people with obesity and HFD-fed mice

C57BL/6 mice were fed a normal diet for 4 weeks, and after sacrifice, liver, skeletal muscle, and adipose tissues were collected to assess GULP1 expression profile in vivo. As shown in Fig. 1, GULP1 was expressed in adipose and muscle (Fig. 1A). In people with obesity, GULP1 mRNA and protein levels were significantly reduced in both adipose (Fig. 1B, C) and skeletal muscle (Fig. 1F, G) tissues, while ACAP1 and ARF6 expression remained unchanged. The GULP1 protein content in adipose tissue homogenates of individuals with obesity was significantly lower than that of individuals with normal-weight, and GULP1 content showed a significant negative correlation with homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) (Fig. 1D, E). Consistent results were obtained in diet-induced obese mice (Fig. 1H–K).

GULP1 increases glucose uptake in adipocytes by promoting GLUT4 translocation to plasma membrane

To further investigate the role of GULP1 in insulin-regulated GLUT4 trafficking and glucose metabolism, we performed gain-of-function studies in both 3T3-L1 and primary white adipocytes. 3T3-L1 preadipocytes differentiated into lipid-laden adipocytes, as confirmed by Oil Red O staining (Fig. 2A). Transfection of GULP1 plasmid into mature adipocytes for 48 h significantly elevated GULP1 mRNA levels (Fig. 2B). Under insulin stimulation, the abundance of GLUT4 protein in the plasma membrane was significantly increased (Figs. 2C, S6A). Immunofluorescence further confirmed that GLUT4 was markedly translocated to the plasma membrane in insulin-treated cells (Figs. 2D, S6B). Insulin-stimulated glucose uptake measured by 2-NBDG assay was significantly elevated in GULP1-overexpressing adipocytes (Fig. 2E).

Parallel experiments in primary white adipocytes corroborated these findings. Successful differentiation was confirmed by lipid droplet accumulation via Oil Red O staining (Fig. 2F). GULP1 plasmid transfection similarly increased GULP1 mRNA expression in mature adipocytes (Fig. 2G). Following insulin treatment, the GLUT4 levels in plasma membrane fractions accompanied by decreased intracellular GLUT4, while total cellular GLUT4 protein remained unchanged (Figs. 2H, S8A). Immunofluorescence imaging further illustrated insulin-dependent GLUT4 redistribution to the plasma membrane (Figs. 2I, S8B), and 2-NBDG assays confirmed augmented insulin-stimulated glucose uptake (Fig. 2J).

Next, we assessed the consequences of GULP1 depletion on insulin-mediated GLUT4 trafficking and glucose uptake. In 3T3-L1 adipocytes, transfection with four independent siRNA constructs targeting GULP1 for 48 h reduced GULP1 mRNA levels, with siGULP1-2 demonstrating the most robust and consistent knockdown efficiency (Fig. 3A, B). Under insulin stimulation, GULP1 knockdown markedly attenuated GLUT4 translocation to the plasma membrane, as evidenced by reduced GLUT4 protein in plasma membrane (Figs. 3C, S6C) and diminished plasma membrane fluorescence signal (Figs. 3D, S6D). Consequently, insulin-stimulated glucose uptake quantified by 2-NBDG assay was significantly impaired in siGULP1-2-transfected adipocytes (Fig. 3E).

Parallel experiments in primary white adipocytes recapitulated these findings. We identified siGULP1-1 as the most effective construct for GULP1 mRNA suppression (Fig. 3F, G). In insulin-treated cells, GULP1 depletion substantially reduced GLUT4 in plasma membrane (Figs. 3H, I, S8C, D). This impaired translocation correlated with a significant decrease in insulin-dependent glucose uptake measured via 2-NBDG assay (Fig. 3J).

GULP1 increases glucose uptake in skeletal muscle cells by promoting GLUT4 translocation to the plasma membrane

Before differentiation induction, C2C12 myoblasts exhibited characteristic spindle-shaped morphology with robust adhesion to the culture surface. Following 8 days of differentiation, qRT-PCR analysis revealed significant upregulation of myogenic markers (Fig. S1A). Transfection of GULP1 plasmid into myotubes for 48 h significantly elevated GULP1 mRNA levels (Fig. S1B).

Furthermore, overexpression of GULP1 enhanced insulin-induced GLUT4 redistribution, with GLUT4 in plasma membrane increasing (Figs. S1C, S7A). GLUT4 was markedly translocated to the plasma membrane in insulin-treated cells (Figs. S1D, S7B), and 2-NBDG assays confirmed augmented insulin-stimulated glucose uptake (Fig. S1E).

In C2C12 myotubes, transfection with four independent siRNA constructs targeting GULP1 for 48 h reduced GULP1 mRNA levels, with siGULP1-1 demonstrating the most robust and consistent knockdown efficiency (Fig. S1F, G). Under insulin stimulation, GULP1 knockdown markedly decreased GLUT4 translocation to the plasma membrane, as evidenced by reduced GLUT4 protein in plasma membrane (Figs. S1H, S7C) and diminished fluorescence signal (Figs. S1J, S7D). Meanwhile, insulin-stimulated glucose uptake was significantly impaired in siGULP1-1-transfected myotubes (Fig. S1I).

GULP1 promotes GLUT4 translocation to plasma membrane in inguinal white adipose tissue and improves insulin sensitivity in mice

To investigate the metabolic effect of GULP1 in adipose tissue, we generated an in vivo mouse model of GULP1 overexpression by injecting AAV9-GULP1-mCherry into the inguinal white adipose tissue of C57BL/6 mice (Fig. S2A). GULP1 overexpression exhibited no significant effect on mouse weights, liver weights, fat weights, or lipid droplet morphology (Fig. S3A–D). mCherry detection and small animal imaging showed mCherry fluorescence enrichment in inguinal white adipose tissue (Fig. S3E, F), and GULP1 mRNA levels were elevated (Fig. S3G, H), confirming successful model generation.

The translocation of GLUT4 to the plasma membrane is a critical step in insulin-stimulated glucose uptake [46, 47]. Assuming that GULP1 can bind to and activate ARF6, thereby facilitating GLUT4 translocation to the plasma membrane through enhanced vesicular trafficking, we therefore examined ARF6 activity. GULP1 overexpression led to a significant increase of activated ARF6 and cell surface GLUT4 compared to the control group (Figs. S2B, C, S3I). Immunofluorescence results confirmed GULP1-mediated promotion of GLUT4 translocation to the plasma membrane in inguinal white adipose tissue (Figs. S2D, S8E). Further glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity tests showed that GULP1 overexpression in inguinal white adipose tissue resulted in significant improvement of glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity in diet-induced obese mice (Fig. S2E, F).

Then, a GULP1-knockdown mouse model was constructed by injecting an AAV9 expressing GULP1 shRNA with an mCherry tag into the inguinal white adipose tissue of C57BL/6 mice (Fig. S2G). As shown in Fig. S3, GULP1 knockdown in inguinal white adipose tissue showed no alterations in mouse weights, liver weights, fat weights, or lipid droplet morphology (Fig. S3J–M). Small animal imaging revealed that mCherry fluorescence enrichment was detected in inguinal white adipose tissue (Fig. S3N), and GULP1 mRNA levels were reduced (Fig. S3O, P), confirming successful model generation.

GULP1 knockdown significantly reduced ARF6 activity and inhibited GLUT4 translocation to the plasma membrane in inguinal white adipose tissue compared to the control group (Figs. S2H–J, S3Q, S8F). Further glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity tests showed that GULP1 knockdown in inguinal white adipose tissue markedly impaired glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity in mice (Fig. S2K, L).

GULP1 promotes GLUT4 translocation to plasma membrane in skeletal muscle and improve insulin sensitivity in mice

Next, we generated a mouse model by injecting an AAV9 overexpressing GULP1 with an mCherry tag into the skeletal muscle of C57BL/6 mice (Fig. S4A). Compared to the control group, overexpression of GULP1 in skeletal muscle tissue had no significant effects on body weights, liver weights, or the weights of adipose tissue in various depots (Fig. S5A–C). mCherry fluorescence was enriched in skeletal muscle (Fig. S5D, E), and the mRNA expression level of GULP1 in skeletal muscle was significantly increased (Fig. S5F, G). These results confirm successful model generation.

Successful overexpression of GULP1 activated ARF6 and promoted GLUT4 translocation to the plasma membrane in skeletal muscle tissue. (Figs. S4B–D, S5H, S8G). Meanwhile, overexpression of GULP1 significantly improved glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity in diet-induced obese mice (Fig. S4E, F).

Then, a mouse model was established by injecting skeletal muscle tissue with AAV9 vectors expressing shRNA against GULP1 (Fig. S4G). Compared to the control group, GULP1 knockdown in skeletal muscle had no significant effects on body weights, liver weights, or the weights of adipose tissue in various depots (Fig. S5I–K). mCherry fluorescence was enriched in skeletal muscle (Fig. S5L), and the mRNA expression level of GULP1 in skeletal muscle was significantly reduced (Fig. S5M, N). These results confirm successful model generation.

After successful GULP1 knockdown, the activity of ARF6 was significantly reduced, and GLUT4 translocation to the plasma membrane in skeletal muscle tissue was inhibited (Figs. S4H-J, S5O, S8H). Meanwhile, skeletal muscle-specific GULP1 knockdown significantly impaired glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity in mice (Fig. S4K, L).

GULP1 binds to ACAP1 and ARF6

To further elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying GULP1-mediated GLUT4 membrane translocation, we determined potential interactions between GULP1 and the GSVs trafficking regulators ACAP1 and ARF6. Multicolor immunofluorescence of mouse skeletal muscle sections revealed colocalization of GLUT4, ACAP1, ARF6, and GULP1 (Fig. 4A). Additionally, immunofluorescence analysis in 3T3-L1 adipocytes and HEK-293T cells showed colocalization of GULP1 and ACAP1, with Pearson’s correlation coefficients ranging from 0.5 to 1, indicating significant colocalization (Fig. 4B, C). Moreover, co-immunoprecipitation assay was used to verify the interaction of GULP1 with ACAP1 and ARF6 in 3T3-L1 adipocytes and C2C12 cells (Fig. 4D, E). We observed strong detection of ACAP1 and ARF6 after precipitation with GULP1’s IP-grade antibody, suggesting the direct interactions of GULP1 with ACAP1 and ARF6. To further assess whether GULP1, ACAP1, and ARF6 can be present within a common complex, sequential co-immunoprecipitation experiments were performed. In 3T3-L1 cells and C2C12 cells, cell lysates were first immunoprecipitated with a GULP1 antibody to capture the endogenous protein complex. Subsequently, the resulting immunocomplex was gently eluted and subjected to a second round of immunoprecipitation using an ACAP1 antibody. Finally, specific enrichment of ARF6 was successfully detected in the product of the second precipitation (Fig. 4F, G). These results indicate that GULP1, ACAP1, and ARF6 associate within a common protein complex.

To explore the specificity of GULP1 function, we assessed its interactions with other representative ARF6 GEFs (GEP100, CYTH2) and GAPs (ACAP2, ARAP2). In 3T3-L1 and C2C12 cells, endogenous GULP1 did not bind to these proteins, while, as anticipated, GULP1 interacted with ACAP1 and ARF6 (Figs. S6G, S7G).

GULP1 promotes GLUT4 translocation and glucose uptake by reversing the inhibitory effect of ACAP1 on ARF6 activity

To investigate the molecular mechanism by which GULP1 promotes insulin-mediated GLUT4 translocation and glucose uptake, GULP1 overexpression plasmids were transfected into 3T3-L1 and C2C12 cell lines. Compared with the control group, GULP1 overexpression led to a significant increase in activated ARF6 and cell surface GLUT4, and enhanced glucose uptake. Notably, co-transfecting with ARF6 interference reversed these promoting effects (Figs. 5A–F, S6E, S7E). Furthermore, ACAP1 overexpression plasmid was transfected into 3T3-L1 and C2C12 cell lines. Compared with the control group, ACAP1 overexpression led to a significant decrease in activated ARF6 and cell surface GLUT4, and inhibited glucose uptake. As shown in Fig. 5, co-overexpression of GULP1 alongside ACAP1 reversed the aforementioned inhibitory effects (Figs. 5G–J, S6F, S7F). Collectively, GULP1 can alleviate the inhibitory action of ACAP1 on ARF6 activity, thereby facilitating GLUT4 translocation to the membrane and enhancing glucose uptake.

Discussion

T2DM is characterized by insulin resistance in adipose tissue and skeletal muscle, with impaired insulin-stimulated GLUT4 translocation to the cell surface a central defect that disrupts systemic glucose homeostasis [46, 47]. This study identifies GULP1 as a novel key regulator of this pathway.

GULP1, initially characterized in phagocytosis and apoptosis, has been implicated in diverse pathologies [48–50]. Emerging evidence points to a potential metabolic role [23, 51]. This study finds that GULP1 expression is significantly downregulated in the adipose and muscle tissues of individuals with obesity and high-fat diet-fed mice. Tissue-specific overexpression of GULP1 mediated by AAV9 effectively improves glucose homeostasis in obese mice without altering body weight or fat mass. This aligns with studies showing that targeted enhancement of metabolic tissue function can improve systemic glucose metabolism independently of reversing obesity [52–54].

The core discovery of this study is the elucidation of the underlying mechanism. This study defines the GULP1-ACAP1-ARF6 axis as a previously unrecognized regulatory hub in insulin-stimulated glucose uptake. While the interaction between GULP1, ACAP1, and ARF6 was known in the context of cell migration [45], and GULP1 knockout was shown to reduce glucose uptake [24], its precise role in insulin signaling was unknown. ARF6 plays a critical role in endocytic recycling, integrin recycling in a stimulation-dependent manner, and insulin-stimulated GLUT4 recycling [32, 45]. In this study, GULP1 sustains the insulin-stimulated activated state of ARF6 by antagonizing the inhibitory effect of ACAP1 on ARF6 activity, thereby specifically driving GLUT4 membrane translocation. These findings demonstrate that GULP1 regulates the terminal execution step of the insulin signaling pathway. This study innovatively extends the role of this GULP1-ACAP1-ARF6 axis from regulating cell migration [45] to insulin-stimulated systemic glucose metabolism, and for the first time provides a direct clinical correlation between GULP1 levels and human HOMA-IR.

From a translational perspective, targeting the GULP1-ACAP1-ARF6 axis presents a novel therapeutic strategy for T2DM. Compared to current insulin sensitizers like thiazolidinediones (TZDs), which act via PPARγ activation and are often limited by side effects such as weight gain and edema [55–57], enhancing GULP1 function improved glucose metabolism in our model without increasing adiposity, suggesting a potentially more favorable profile. Given that this pathway acts directly on the distal step of GLUT4 exocytosis in insulin signaling, developing small-molecule modulators targeting it represents a mechanistically novel and more tissue-specific strategy to overcome insulin resistance.

Nevertheless, our study has limitations that outline important future directions. First, while AAV-mediated gain- and loss-of-function studies are compelling, the physiological necessity of GULP1 requires validation in adipose- and muscle-specific knockout mouse models. Second, although metabolic improvements were observable within 7 weeks, the long-term effects on body weight dynamics, lipid profiles, and the generalizability to different sexes or more severe diabetic models require further investigation. Finally, at the cellular level, future work employing techniques like fluorescence resonance energy transfer or real-time imaging of fluorescently tagged GLUT4 vesicles could provide dynamic, high spatiotemporal resolution insights into the assembly of this complex and the kinetics of GLUT4 trafficking.

In summary, this study demonstrates that GULP1 can bind to ACAP1 and ARF6, and alleviate the inhibitory effect of ACAP1 on ARF6 activity, thereby enhancing insulin-mediated glucose uptake and improving insulin sensitivity in obese mice (Fig. S9), providing a new therapeutic target for the treatment of obesity-related metabolic diseases.
