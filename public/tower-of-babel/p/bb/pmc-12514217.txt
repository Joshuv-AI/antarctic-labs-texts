Place attachment and walkability and their associations with walking in urban neighbourhoods
Authors: Justin Eng, Gavin R. McCormack
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Place attachment has yet to be explored in relation to objectively measured neighbourhood walkability and walking. We aimed to estimate the associations between place identity (PI) and place dependence (PD) and walking for recreation (WR) and transportation (WT) among urban adults, and to assess the modifying role of neighbourhood walkability. We analyzed cross-sectional data from 2006 Calgary adults (2007–2008), including PI, PD, and walking. Neighbourhood walkability was determined via street pattern. Generalized linear models assessed associations (odds ratios [OR] and incidence rate ratios [IRR]) and interactions between place attachment, walkability, and walking. PI was lower in curvilinear versus warped grid neighbourhoods. PD was associated (p < 0.05) with sufficient WT (≥ 150 min/week) in curvilinear (OR 1.92) and grid (OR 1.62) neighbourhoods. PI was associated with sufficient WR (ORs 1.58–1.82) and more frequent WT and WR (IRRs 1.17–1.41). Findings highlight nuanced relationships between the built environment, place attachment, and walking.

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that adults accumulate at least 150 min of moderate physical activity (or equivalent) each week for leisure, transportation, work, play, sports, or exercise to achieve optimum health benefits and protect against chronic diseases1. Despite its benefits, the global prevalence of sufficient physical activity is declining2. While some high-income western countries, such as Canada, have experienced slight improvements, they remain off-track to meet the WHO global target of a 15% relative reduction in insufficient physical activity by 20301,2. Walking is a popular physical activity among North American adults3,4 and commonly undertaken within residential neighbourhoods5,6. Walking can be incorporated into daily routines for transportation (e.g., travel to work, school, and other destinations) and recreation (e.g., exercise, enjoyment, leisure)6,7. While walking behaviour is shaped by numerous personal, social, and environmental factors8,9, enhancing the built environment as a strategy for promoting physical activity has great potential due to its population-level impact and long-term influence10.

The built environment encompasses the totality of human-designed places and spaces such as buildings, transportation and recreational infrastructure, public open areas, and neighbourhoods11. Systematic reviews on the associations between the neighbourhood built environment and walking have found land use mix, proximity and availability of destinations, street connectivity, sidewalk availability, green space, density positively associated with WT, though less consistently with WR12–14. The combination of built attributes contributes to a neighbourhood’s walkability, which reflects the overall design and layout of urban spaces that prioritize pedestrian transversability, safety, convenience, and comfort and that are compact, destination dense, and enticing15,16. Enhancing neighbourhood walkability holds promise for promoting physical activity, yet limited research has explored how the combined influence of the built environment and residents’ emotional or functional connection to their neighbourhood shape walking behaviour.

Brunelli et al.’s17 Salutogenic Urban Design Framework demonstrates how people ascribe meaning and sustain health and well-being through interactions with their everyday environments, utilizing available social and material resources to foster good health. Their study of older adults found that local high streets support well-being not only through accessibility and vibrancy, but also by fostering emotional bonds and a sense of place through regular use and social interaction, highlighting the subjective nature of built environment experiences17. Place attachment refers to the social interaction between people and environments that reflects the affective, emotional, and functional connection individuals form with a specific place18,19. Over time, people assign meaning and value to particular environments, transforming them into places with personal and intrinsic value18,19. These bonds are inherently personal and context-dependent, shaped by repeat exposure and interaction, and often strengthen over time20–22.

Definitions of place attachment vary, often emphasizing different aspects of the person–environment relationship including the functional or process dimension (i.e., practical reliance on a place to carry out preferred activities) or the affective or outcome dimension (i.e., the sense of belonging toward a place)23,24. Research supports place attachment as a multidimensional construct with two important sub-dimensions: place identity and place dependence24. Place identity, an emotional attachment, refers to the symbolic importance or centrality of a place in a person’s life24 and contributes to self-identity as places become imbued with personal experiences and relationships25. Place dependence, a functional attachment, reflects a person’s reliance on a particular place to undertake desired activities24,25. It is often driven by the unique socio-physical attributes of a place that enable behaviours not easily or conveniently supported in other environments.

Place attachment has practical implications for supporting behaviour, particularly in the context of walking. For instance, a person may have a strong attachment to a particular trail due to its attributes (e.g., functionality, aesthetics, or safety) that supports their ability to walk. The removal or modification of this trail could negatively impact the person’s ability to walk as usual, especially if the person is dependent on this setting for walking. Koohsari et al.26 found that place attachment was positively linked to walking via perceived neighbourhood walkability. Specifically, they found full and partial mediation effects of perceived neighbourhood walkability between place identity and place dependence with WT, respectively. Similarly, Van Riper et al.27 found that motivation to engage in outdoor recreation stems from the intensity and type of attachment that individuals form with settings. While not focussed specifically on walking, Budrik and Stanis28 found that place attachment can motivate continued visits to a setting. Given the everyday interactions and exposure to neighbourhoods and their importance for walking12–14, individuals who form stronger affective, emotional, and functional connection with their neighbourhood, may engage in more walking compared to individuals with weaker connections to their neighbourhood.

Previous research has examined the relationship between perceived and objectively measured neighbourhood built environment, finding that they are related but capture distinct concepts29,30, and that their interaction is associated with physical activity31,32. For instance, perceived mixed utilitarian destinations31 and perceptions of neighbourhood aesthetics, pedestrian infrastructure, and safety32 can moderate the associations between objectively measured walkability and physical activity. The interactions between neighbourhood perceptions and neighbourhood urban form are therefore relevant when exploring their relationships with physical activity. Associations between place attachment and perceived and objective measures of built attributes supportive of physical activity have also been reported26,33,34. Perceived walkability may strengthen place attachment and indirectly promote walking through social interaction and neighbourhood satisfaction34. Findings from a recent study indicated that perceived walkability may mediate associations between place attachment and walking26. However, because both perceived walkability and place attachment are self-reported perceptions of the same environment, their association may partly reflect common-method (same source) bias and a general positive (or negative) sentiment toward the setting (e.g., neighbourhood)35,36. Moreover, positing place attachment as a mediator between the built environment and walking, while plausible, also assumes a unidimensional and temporal causal ordering exists, yet place attachment is shaped by lived experiences over time, personal, social, and cultural factors that may pre-exist and or co-evolve with neighbourhood exposure23,37. Therefore, we hypothesize that the effects of place attachment on walking may be amplified or attenuated by neighbourhood walkability. To our knowledge, no studies have examined the interaction between objectively measured walkability and place attachment (i.e., identity or dependence) and their effect on neighbourhood walking.

The aim of our study was to estimate the associations between place identity and place dependence and neighbourhood walking for transportation and recreation among urban adults, and to assess the extent to which walkability (based on neighbourhood typology) modified these associations (Fig. 1).

Methods

Study design and data source

This study included an analysis of data from a survey conducted in Calgary as part of the Economic Evaluation of using Urban Form to Increase Activity (EcoEUFORIA) project. The city of Calgary is a large urban municipality located in southern Alberta, Canada east of the Rockey Mountains. In 2007, Calgary had a geographical area of approximately 5100 square kilometers and a population of 1.05 million38. Calgary also experiences a continental climate with hot, dry summers and cold, dry winters39. The primary aim of the EcoEUFORIA project was to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of using urban form to promote physical activity and health40,41. The project surveyed two random cross-sectional samples of Calgary adults (≥ 18 years of age), who completed structured telephone-interviews between August and October 2007 (n = 2199; response rate = 33.6%) and January and April 2008 (n = 2223; response rate = 36.7%)40,41. The survey included recruitment at different times of the year to account for seasonal variation in physical activity. Household telephone numbers were sampled from an existing list of Calgary and area landline numbers using random digit dialing, where the final digit of each listed number was replaced with a random digit to ensure both unlisted and listed telephone numbers were included. A pseudo-random approach (i.e., last birthday selection method) was used to recruit one adult per household for the telephone interview.

Of the 4422 participants who completed the telephone interviews, 2006 (45.4%) also completed a follow-up postal survey. The telephone and postal surveys captured participants’ sociodemographic characteristics, neighbourhood perceptions, physical activity, and several other health outcomes. Compared to the telephone survey sample, the postal survey subsample included a slightly higher proportion of women, university-educated individuals, households without dependents, and homeowners, as well as participants who were older and had lived in their neighbourhoods longer42,43. Participants’ residential 6-digit postal codes were also collected. Geolocated postal codes were matched to Community Administrative Boundaries44; a distinct geographical area that defines where a neighbourhood starts and ends in Calgary. These administrative boundaries were categorized into three previously classified neighbourhood types according to their street pattern design (i.e., curvilinear, warped-grid, and grid)45,46. The University of Calgary Conjoint Research Ethics Board approved this study (REB24-1339; October 13, 2024). All study participants provided informed verbal (telephone survey) and written (postal survey) consent prior to data collection.

Variables

Neighbourhood typology (walkability)

Using both spatial data and historical periodization, Sandalack and Nicolai46 and Sandalack et al.45 previously described and implemented a procedure for categorizing Calgary neighbourhoods based on their street patterns. Street patterns provide an urban structure or framework upon which other built characteristics are designed and built. Street patterns in and of themselves represent networks that facilitate movement for passive (i.e., motor vehicles) and active (i.e., walking and cycling) modes of transportation. Calgary consists of three general neighbourhood types, distinguished by their predominate street pattern: grid, warped grid, and curvilinear neighbourhoods45,46. These three neighbourhood types correspond with those identified based on similar classification schemes applied by others to the Calgary47,48 and other contexts49. Previous studies also report associations between these neighbourhood types and health outcomes50,51, including physical activity41,52,53.

Calgary’s grid, warped grid, and curvilinear neighbourhoods are considered to have high, medium, and low levels of walkability, respectively, based on their objectively measured built attributes41. Grid neighbourhoods marked the first phase of Calgary’s urban development, emerging before the Second World War. These communities feature high pedestrian and street connectivity, permeability, route choice, population density, public transit access, available destinations, and mixed land use characteristics46. Following the Second World War and continuing through to the 1960s, warped-grid neighbourhoods formed Calgary’s middle ring of development. These neighbourhoods introduced new urban planning elements, such as land use zoning and varied street designs including crescents and curved roads. Compared to traditional grid neighbourhoods, warped grid neighbourhoods tend to be less walkable due to having fewer available destinations, lower population density, and reduced street connectivity46. Curvilinear neighbourhoods represent the most recent phase of Calgary’s urban development, beginning in the 1970s as private automobiles became widespread across North America. Curvilinear neighbourhoods are distinguished by their “loops and lollipops” street pattern with high-volume collector roads and absence of sidewalks on one side of each street. These neighbourhoods are considered low walkable due to limited pedestrian and street connectivity, sidewalk availability, available destinations, and public transit. However, curvilinear neighbourhoods contain a higher proportion of green space and path/cycling infrastructure compared to the other neighbourhood types46.

Neighbourhood-based walking

During the telephone-interview, physical activity was collected using the Neighbourhood Physical Activity Questionnaire (NPAQ)54. The NPAQ captured frequency and minutes of WT and WR undertaken inside the neighbourhood during a usual week (within a 15-min walk from home). Minutes of neighbourhood WT, WR, and total walking (WT + WR) were each dichotomized to correspond with the threshold of moderate-intensity physical activity considered sufficient to accrue optimal health benefits (≥ 150 min per week)1. In addition to WT and WR, we estimated frequency of total walking (WT + WR) per week inside the neighbourhood. NPAQ measured walking has acceptable reliability54,55, and associated with objective measures of the neighbourhood built environment41,56.

Place attachment

Using an adapted version of an existing place attachment tool24, the postal survey captured two dimensions of place attachment specific to the neighbourhood, including place identity and dependence. The six place identity and six place dependence items were modified to reflect a focus on identity or dependence on the neighbourhood, the later of which were specific to physical activity26. All items were rated on a 4-point Likert scale from strongly disagree (1) to stronger agree (4). Place identity and dependence scores were estimated by summing individual item scores and dividing by the total number of completed items. Most participants responded to all 12-items (> 98%) and both place identity and dependence scales had acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α > 0.79) and test-retest reliability (intraclass correlations > 0.74)26.

Sociodemographic variables (covariates)

Several sociodemographic variables were collected from the telephone and postal surveys, including age, gender, highest education completed, annual gross household income, marital status, home ownership, and number of children under 18 years at home. Length of residence in the neighbourhood was also collected and included as a covariate in our analysis as time residing in a neighbourhood is associated with the development of place attachment22,57.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations [SD], and frequencies) were computed for all variables. One-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA; Welch’s) with post hoc Games-Howell tests was used to assess differences in mean weekly frequency of WT, WR, total walking, place identity, and place dependence across grid, warped-grid, and curvilinear neighbourhoods. Pearson’s Chi-square tests with post hoc z-tests estimated differences in the proportion (%) achieving sufficient WT, WR, and total walking by neighbourhood type. Covariate-adjusted generalized linear models (GZLMs) estimated main effects (neighbourhood type and place attachment variables) and interaction effects (neighbourhood type-by-place attachment) on walking outcomes. For walking frequency, GZLMs used a negative binomial distribution with a log link to estimate Incident Rate Ratios (IRR), whereas for sufficient walking, GZLMs used a binomial distribution with a logit link to estimate Odds Ratios (OR). Models with statistically significant interaction effects were re-run stratified by neighbourhood type. Given the exploratory nature of the study, a less conservative p-value < 0.10 was used for interaction effects, while a p-value < 0.05 determined statistical significance for all other estimates. In total, our analysis set out to test 24 GZLMs, of which 12 included only main effects (i.e., 2 place attachment variables × 6 walking outcomes), and 12 included both main effects and interaction terms. This count excludes post hoc stratified models conducted in response to significant interactions. For ANOVAs and GZLMs, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed. Analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA; https://www.ibm.com/products/spss-statistics).

Results

Sample characteristics

Complete data from 1877 participants were analyzed (Table 1). The mean age of the sample was 50.9 years, 62.6% were female, 44.1% had completed a university degree or higher, 32.8% had an annual gross household income of $60,000 to $119,999, 86.0% reported owning a home, 66.4% were married or living together, and 33.4% had at least one child living at home. The mean time residing in the current neighbourhood was 13.2 years. Average scores for place identity and place dependence were 2.9 (SD 0.7) and 2.2 (SD 0.6), respectively. Participants reported a mean weekly frequency of 2.8 (SD 3.8) for WT, 3.5 (SD 3.5) for WR, and 6.3 (SD 5.6) for total walking in the neighbourhood. The proportions achieving ≥ 150 min per week of WT, WR, and total walking in the neighbourhood were 14.5%, 37.0%, and 51.8%, respectively (Table 1).

Differences in neighbourhood walking by neighbourhood type

The Chi-square results showed significant (p < 0.05) differences in the proportions of participants accumulating ≥ 150 min per week of WT between curvilinear (9.3%), warped grid (14.9%), and grid (25.5%) neighbourhoods (Table 2). Compared to curvilinear neighbourhoods (49.4%), a higher proportion (p < 0.05) of those residing in grid neighbourhoods (59.6%) accumulated ≥ 150 min per week of total walking. The ANOVA results showed that the mean frequency of WT (4.46 times/week) and total walking (8.10 times/week) was higher (p < 0.05) among those from grid neighbourhoods, compared to those residing in curvilinear (2.18 and 5.71 times/week, respectively) and warped grid (2.50 and 5.99 times/week, respectively) neighbourhoods. No other significant differences in neighbourhood walking were observed.

Differences in place attachment by neighbourhood type

The ANOVA results showed that on average, participants residing in curvilinear neighbourhoods reported significantly (p < 0.05) lower place identity (2.80; 95% CI 2.75, 2.84), compared to those residing in warped grid neighbourhoods (2.91; 95% CI 2.85, 2.96) (Table 2). We observed no significant neighbourhood differences in place dependence.

Associations between neighbourhood type, place attachment, and sufficient neighbourhood walking (main effects)

Adjusting for covariates and place identity, compared to participants in curvilinear neighbourhoods, those in grid (OR 2.80, 95% CI 1.97, 3.98) and warped grid (OR 1.97, 95% CI 1.40, 2.77) neighbourhoods were more likely (p < 0.05) to accumulate sufficient WT in their neighbourhood (Table 3). Compared to curvilinear neighbourhoods, those in warped grid neighbourhoods were less likely to accumulate sufficient WR (OR 0.74, 95% CI 0.58, 0.93), and those in grid neighbourhoods were more likely to accumulate sufficient total walking (OR 1.37, 95% CI 1.05, 1.78). We observed the same pattern of associations between neighbourhood type and WT, WR, and total walking in models that adjusted for covariates and place dependence (Table 3). Stronger place identity and place dependence were associated (p < 0.05) with increased odds of accumulating sufficient WT (OR 1.33, 95% CI 1.08, 1.63 and OR 1.49, 95% CI 1.20, 1.85), WR (OR 1.47, 95% CI 1.27, 1.71 and OR 1.54, 95% CI 1.32, 1.80), and total walking (OR 1.53, 95% CI 1.32, 1.76 and OR 1.68, 95% CI 1.44, 1.96), respectively, independent of covariates and neighbourhood type.

Associations between neighbourhood type, place attachment, and frequency of neighbourhood walking (main effects)

Adjusting for covariates and place identity, participants in grid (IRR 1.81, 95% CI 1.56, 2.10) and warped grid (IRR 1.32, 05% CI 1.15, 1.50) neighbourhoods were more likely (p < 0.05) to achieve a higher frequency of walking (times/week) in their neighbourhood compared to curvilinear neighbourhoods (Table 4). Additionally, participants in grid neighbourhoods were more likely than those in curvilinear neighbourhoods to achieve a higher frequency of total walking (IRR 1.33, 95% CI 1.11, 1.29). Similar patterns were observed between neighbourhood type and WT, WR, and total walking in models that adjusted for covariates and place dependence (Table 4). We found no associations between neighbourhood type and frequency of WR. Stronger place identity and place dependence were positively associated (p < 0.05) with frequency of WT (IRR 1.14, 95% CI 1.05, 1.25 and IRR 1.16, 95% 1.06, 1.27), WR (IRR 1.24, 95% CI 1.15, 1.34 and IRR 1.30, 95% CI 1.20, 1.41), and total walking (OR 1.19, 95% CI 1.11, 1.29 and OR 1.25, 95% CI 1.15, 1.35), respectively, independent of covariates and neighbourhood type.

Discussion

Our study estimated the associations between neighbourhood types that differed in their walkability, place attachment, and neighbourhood WT and WR among urban adults. The strengths of our study included a random sample recruited across all seasons, neighbourhood-match built environment exposure and walking outcomes, a physical activity-specific measure of place dependence, reliable and valid self-report measures, and the use of validated framework for classifying neighbourhoods according to their predominant street pattern. Expectedly and congruent with evidence elsewhere12–14,41, we found that compared to curvilinear neighbourhoods, residing in grid neighbourhoods was associated with higher frequency of WT and total walking and increased likelihood of achieving sufficient WT and total walking in the neighbourhood during a usual week. However, our study also contributes novel findings that advances current evidence on the interrelationships between the neighbourhood walkability, place attachment, and physical activity26,33,34,58. Specifically, we observed positive associations between place identity and place dependence with the frequency and achievement of sufficient neighbourhood WT, WR, and total walking, independent of neighbourhood type (i.e., grid, warped grid, curvilinear). Moreover, in support of our hypothesis, we found evidence of neighbourhood type modifying the relationship between place attachment (identity and dependence) and frequency of and achieving sufficient walking inside the neighbourhood. Though sometimes only marginally, all but one positive association between the place attachment variables and neighbourhood walking was higher in magnitude for grid versus curvilinear and warped grid neighbourhoods.

Studies have found links between neighbourhood walkability and place attachment33,58. Li et al.58 estimated associations between walkable built environment metrics and place attachment among adults in Japan and found several objectively measured (e.g., availability of destinations, population density, street integration) and perceived (e.g., public green spaces, aesthetics, crime safety, public transport) attributes positively associated with place attachment. Similarly, Van den Berg et al.33 found both objective and perceived neighbourhood walkability had a positive effect on place attachment. Notably, a recent systematic review on links between engagement in natural and built pedestrian environments to place attachment and sense of community reported that the limited number of studies comparing pedestrian-friendly and conventional neighbourhood layouts found only weak or non-significant differences in people’s place attachment59. We observed a significant, albeit small magnitude, difference in place identity between curvilinear and warped grid neighbourhoods only. On average, participants residing in warped grid neighbourhoods had stronger place identity compared to those residing in curvilinear neighbourhoods. Despite this statistically significant result, this difference is unlikely meaningful as reflected in the point estimates. We also found no significant neighbourhood differences for place dependence. It is possible that defining neighbourhoods primarily based on street pattern (curvilinear, warped, and grid) masked the variability in built attributes among neighbourhoods of the same type that might be relevant for enhancing place identity and dependence33,34,58. Our null findings could also suggest that place identity and dependence, as it relates to physical activity, might be determined by more than a neighbourhood’s overall urban design and instead influenced by other intrapersonal and interpersonal (e.g., social interactions, social connectedness) factors60,61.

To date, studies exploring interrelationships between the neighbourhood built environment, place attachment, and physical activity have done so assuming mediation exists26,34, thus there a little direct evidence with which to compare our findings. We found five significant interactions that included place identity. Stratified analysis revealed that associations between place identity and frequency of walking and achieving sufficient walking, while different between curvilinear, warped grid, and grid neighbourhoods were all either positive or null. Place identity associations with sufficient WR and sufficient total walking and frequency of WT, WR, and total walking in the neighbourhood, were strongest in magnitude for grid neighbourhoods. Place identity conveys an emotional bond with a place24. Grid neighbourhoods featured high pedestrian and street connectivity, permeability, route choice, population density, public transit access, available destinations, and mixed land use characteristics46. These built attributes can contribute to a neighbourhood’s character, enhance opportunities for social interactions, and promote vibrancy62–64 and may encourage local walking. Place identity was also positively associated sufficient WR and sufficient total walking, and frequency of WT, WR, and total walking in curvilinear neighbourhoods, and with sufficient total walking, and frequency of WR in warped grid neighbourhoods. The magnitude of these associations was, however, weaker for curvilinear relative to grid neighbourhoods. Undertaking walking may be more difficult in these less walkable neighbourhoods, even among those with a strong sense of place identity. Regardless of neighbourhood type, place-based interventions aimed at enhancing place attachment, and in particular place identity, may promote local walking. Community gardens59,60; social events and festivals65; resident-led place-making and beautification initiatives66,67; installing cultural and heritage relevant local art68,69; improving recreational infrastructure such as parks and trails59; and implementing programs that encourage social engagement, increase social ties, and build collective efficacy70, may enhance neighbourhood place attachment.

Only one significant interaction between neighbourhood type and place attachment included place dependence (for sufficient WT). The lack of findings was somewhat surprising given that place dependence reflects a person’s reliance on a place or location to perform preferred tasks and activities24,25 and that we anticipated the differences in walking opportunities afforded by curvilinear, warped grid, and grid neighbourhoods to be reflected as differences in the associations between place identity and walking. Also surprising was that the association between place dependence and sufficient WT was strongest in magnitude for curvilinear relative to grid and warped grid neighbourhoods. This may suggest, that to overcome built environment barriers to walking for transportation (e.g., limited connectivity, sidewalks, destinations, and public transit) in curvilinear neighbourhoods, a stronger sense of place is required. Notably, our measure of place dependence was specific to an individual’s dependence on the neighbourhood for physical activity generally and lacked specificity regarding physical activity type or purpose. We might anticipate finding a greater number of neighbourhood differences in associations between place dependence and walking had our measure of place dependence been specific to walking.

While not the main focus of this study, our finding that participants living in grid and warped grid neighbourhoods were more likely to achieve a higher frequency of WT and accrue sufficient levels of WT in the neighbourhood align with previous evidence12–14. Grid neighbourhoods appear to be the most supportive for WT and total walking in the neighbourhood, but not for WR. We found that while there were no neighbourhood differences in the frequency of WR during a usual week, the odds of achieving sufficient physical activity through WR in more walkable neighbourhoods (i.e., grid and warped grid) was lower relative to curvilinear neighbourhoods. Though less supportive of WT, curvilinear neighbourhoods generally offer more green space and path/cycling infrastructure compared to the other neighbourhood types in Calgary46 and potentially more opportunities for WR.

Despite its strengths, our study also had several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design prevents causal inferences about the associations between neighbourhood type, place attachment, and walking. We cannot rule out residential self-selection bias, whereby walking preferences influence neighbourhood choice71,72 and potential inform neighbourhood perceptions. Second, the data were collected in 2007–2008. While we expect relationships between variables to remain stable, changes in walking patterns and urban design of newer neighbourhoods over time may affect the generalizability of our findings. Third, despite the sampling design enhancing representativeness, it resulted in disproportionate representation across neighbourhood types. The smaller sample size for grid neighbourhoods may have reduced estimate precision and statistical power to detect differences. Future studies could use stratified sampling with oversampling of grid neighbourhood participants for better group balance. Fourth, our study relied on self-reported walking measures, which are prone to reporting bias and recall errors73. Moreover, the definition of neighbourhood as a 10 to 15-min walking distance from home may not match participants’ conceptualization of neighbourhood when responding to place attachment measures, potentially attenuating estimated associations74. Finally, our exploratory analysis did not adjust for multiple significance testing and is therefore vulnerable to Type Ⅰ error75. As such, our novel findings should be considered preliminary until corroborated by future studies.

Results from our study have important implications for research and practice related to neighbourhoods and physical activity. Although some effect sizes were modest, even small increases in walking can have meaningful public health implications when applied across large populations. Increasing moderate-to-vigorous physical activity by as little as 10–15 min daily can reduce the risk chronic disease76 and lower mortality77,78, suggesting that even modest improvements in neighbourhood walkability or place attachment could still enhance population health. Identifying strategies that nudge physical activity levels upwards remains vital given that almost one-third of adults globally do not achieve sufficient levels2. Our findings emphasize the need to create or redesign neighbourhood environments to better support walking. Notably, our finding that neighbourhood type modified the relationship between place attachment and walking suggests that improving walkability may also help cultivate place attachment, contributing to more frequent and sustained walking. The association between place attachment and walking, regardless of neighbourhood type, suggests that strategies for enhancing place identity and place dependence should be incorporated into policies and interventions aimed at encouraging walking. These built environment improvements should be complemented by neighbourhood and community programs and events that foster social connection and increase awareness of local opportunities for physical activity. Integrating these approaches into public health strategies may enhance the effect of built environment interventions on physical activity.
