Radiation therapy in breast cancer patients in south west Nigeria: an evaluation of protocols in a specialist cancer centre
Authors: Adewumi Alabi, Francis Boateng, William Swanson, Anthonia Sowunmi, Bolanle Adegboyega, Victoria Ainsworth, Abdulrazzaq Lawal, Bukola Oshikanlu, Ademola Oyekan, Inioluwa Ariyo, Onyinye Balogun, Emadelden Fouad, Fallon Chipidza, Wilfred Ngwa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBreast cancer is the most common malignancy among women worldwide and carries disproportionate mortality in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where late presentation and constrained radiotherapy capacity are common. We report the initial institutional dosimetric experience of postoperative breast irradiation delivered with intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) at a new comprehensive cancer centre in South-West Nigeria.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 67 consecutive patients (age 29–72 years; median 50.4 years) treated following definitive surgery with adjuvant locoregional irradiation (whole breast or chest wall ± regional nodes) using multi-field intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) on VitalBeam and Halcyon linear accelerators. Clinicopathologic, surgical, systemic therapy and treatment planning data were collected. Organs-at-risk metrics included ipsilateral lung V20Gy and V25Gy and mean dose; heart V5Gy and V25Gy and mean dose; mean thyroid dose; and contralateral breast mean dose. Biologically effective dose (BED) and EQD2 (α/β = 3.0) were used to harmonise heterogeneous prescriptions. Planning objectives were derived from QUANTEC/RTOG-based institutional protocols, and all plans underwent peer review prior to delivery.ResultsThe cohort was predominantly composed of patients with locally advanced disease at presentation, with T2 tumours accounting for 23.9% and T3–T4 disease collectively accounting for 23.8%, while nodal involvement was frequent, with N1 and N2–N3 disease observed in 23.9% and 16.4% of patients, respectively. Metastatic disease (M1) was present in 19.4% of cases. Surgery was predominantly modified radical mastectomy (55/67, 82.1%), and axillary lymph node dissection was performed in 54/67 (80.6%). Regional nodal irradiation was delivered in 63/67 patients (94%). Systemic therapy exposure was common, with most patients receiving anthracycline- and/or taxane-based chemotherapy, and a minority receiving anti-HER2 therapy (7.5%). Prescriptions clustered around hypofractionated regimens (42.0 Gy/15 fractions, n = 28; 45.0 Gy/18 fractions, n = 28), with a median prescribed dose of 44.0 ± 2.0 Gy; hypofractionation was used in the majority (≈ 60/67). Cohort dosimetry demonstrated a mean heart dose of 9.2 Gy, with heart V5Gy 61.2 ± 30.1% and V25Gy 4.9 ± 6.0%; ipsilateral lung V20Gy 19.3 ± 10.8% and V25Gy 12.8 ± 7.5%; mean thyroid dose 19.9 ± 10.1 Gy; and contralateral breast mean dose approximately 2.4 ± 1.3 Gy.ConclusionThese findings provide an institutional baseline of postoperative breast radiotherapy dosimetry in a resource-constrained setting and identify practical targets for ongoing quality improvement. The observed dosimetric profile demonstrates acceptable target coverage alongside measurable cardiac, pulmonary and thyroid exposure, reflecting the extent of nodal irradiation in this population. This work provides a foundation for prospective evaluation of treatment-related toxicity and long-term oncologic outcomes, and supports continued refinement of radiotherapy planning and delivery practices within the region.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16483-4.Keywords: Breast cancer, Radiotherapy, Organs at risk, Dosimetry, Surgery

Introduction

Globally, breast cancer is the most prevalent cancer among women [1–3]. In Africa, by incidence and mortality, breast cancer is a severe burden on the continent. The cancer burden in Africa is expected to continue to grow significantly from 186,000 new breast cancer patients (and 86,000 mortalities) in 2020 to an estimated 337,000 (and 163,000 mortalities) in 2040 [4].

Radiation therapy (RT) plays a vital role in the adjuvant management of breast cancer following surgical procedures such as breast-conserving surgery (BCS) or mastectomy [2]. The goal of post-operative RT is to eradicate microscopic cancer cells left behind on the chest wall or lymph nodes to reduce the risk of recurrence [2].

In high-resource countries such as the United States and Europe, where early cancer screening and diagnosis are common, breast-conserving treatment consisting of breast conserving surgery and postoperative whole breast RT are more frequent [1, 5, 6]. However, in low - middle income countries such as Nigeria, where advanced stage disease is more common, the most common treatment is mastectomy followed by adjuvant radiotherapy to the chest wall [1, 7]. To address the growing burden of cancer in Nigeria, new cancer centers, including the NSIA-LUTH Cancer Centre (NLCC), continue to be established. This NLCC began operations in 2019 through a public - private partnership between the Nigerian Sovereign Investment Authority (NSIA) and the Lagos University Teaching Hospital (LUTH). The NLCC is equipped with 3 megavoltage teletherapy units, one of which is the first halcyon machine installed in Africa. While significant progress is still being made to reach the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) - recommended minimum coverage of 1.5 units per million population [8], NSIA-LUTH Cancer Centre significantly increases access to both local and continental cancer care.

This retrospective review documents the initial experience of radiotherapy delivery at this center. Patients in this review underwent oncologic surgery as appropriate for the stage, followed by intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT). Although postoperative RT can be delivered with conventional wedged tangential fields for early-stage breast cancers, IMRT is the preferred treatment at our center. This is because IMRT allows for more precise targeting of tumors and minimizes radiation exposure to healthy tissues, enhancing treatment effectiveness and reducing potential side effects [9, 10]. This reasoning is further justified by previous studies conducted at our center, showing that IMRT had better planning target volume (PTV) coverage and lowered ipsilateral lung and heart doses when compared with VMAT and superior homogeneity and reduced high dose volumes to organs at risk (OAR) when compared with 3D Conformal Radiation Therapy (3DCRT). Pre-RT surgery included modified radical mastectomy (MRM), MRM with axillary lymph node dissection (ALND), simple total mastectomy, and breast-conserving surgeries such as lumpectomy and wide local excision (WLE). Radiation treatment plans’ quality was assessed using internationally established metrics [11], stated in more detail below. We assessed the results relative to published work to determine necessary interventions [10, 12]. Ultimately, we hope that by reporting the center’s experience, we can provide a framework that can be adopted at similar centers to help improve treatment methodology.

Materials and methods

This study was conducted at the NSIA-LUTH Cancer Centre (NLCC) in Nigeria, West Africa, between May 2019 and April 2021 and approved by Health Research Ethics Committee (HREC), Lagos University Teaching Hospital (LUTH), Lagos, Nigeria (Approval No. ADM/DCST/HREC/APP/3783). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and all applicable institutional guidelines and regulations governing research involving human participants. Selection was based on histopathological confirmation of breast cancer and planned surgical intervention. Inclusion criteria comprised adult patients with histologically confirmed breast cancer who underwent definitive surgery and subsequently received adjuvant locoregional radiotherapy at NLCC during the study period, and for whom complete planning, delivery and dosimetric records were available. Patients underwent definitive surgery followed by adjuvant locoregional irradiation (whole-breast or chest-wall ± regional nodes) guided by surgical findings and disease factors. The prespecified primary endpoint is locoregional recurrence at 2 and 5 years after surgery and adjuvant radiotherapy. While these timepoints define our intent-to-follow outcomes, the present manuscript focuses on the cohort’s dosimetric performance and plan-quality metrics; formal time-to-event analyses will be reported once follow-up matures. Secondary objectives reported in this study included evaluating dose distribution, target coverage, dose homogeneity, and dose conformity to the target volume, and comparing these values with published reports from other low and middle-income countries. Data collected included medical, treatment planning and radiotherapy technique data. Data were also extracted from hospital medical records, Varian Eclipse planning systems, and ARIA. Data analysis was done using SPSS vs. 24 software. Regarding Fractionation, choices reflected contemporaneous clinical practice at NLCC during 2019–2021; multiple consultant teams were using established, patient-tailored schedules (40.5 Gy/15 fx, 42.56 Gy/16 fx, 45 Gy/18 fx and 50 Gy/25 fx). This retrospective analysis documents real-world institutional patterns with the explicit aim of informing a unified, evidence-based institutional protocol. Where necessary, case selection for a given fractionation followed the treating consultant’s clinical judgment considering tumor stage, extent of nodal irradiation and patient comorbidities.

Patient data

The 67 breast cancer patients involved in this study underwent either Mastectomy or breast-conserving surgery, with Axillary Lymph Node Dissection as deemed necessary. Patient clinical data retrieved included the laterality such as the left, right, or bilateral breast involvement. Relevant clinicopathologic information including histological type, disease stage, tumour grade, size, surgery type and radiation treatment was retrieved. Resection Margin status, lymph node surgery, estrogen/progesterone receptor status were also analyzed.

Treatment planning

Target volume and Organ at Risk (OAR) Delineation (Contouring): Delineated (contoured) OARs included the lungs, the heart, contralateral breast, and the thyroid gland. The RTOG Breast Atlas Contours were followed [10, 11]. The lungs were auto-contoured using Eclipse wizard software and smart segmentation and were manually corrected by the radiation oncologist using lung window levels. The heart was contoured superiorly from below the arch of the aorta down to include all visible pericardium inferiorly.

Treatment Beam Placement / Planning: Treatment plans were created by the center’s medical physics team using Varian Eclipse treatment planning system (TPS) v15.6. This study also used an Intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) planning technique with the photon optimizer (PO) v15. The plans utilized an average of nine photon beams of 6-MV energy with one isocenter. Dose calculations were performed with the analytical anisotropic algorithm (AAA) v15.6. The beam arrangements used by the medical physicists were based on a critical evaluation of patient anatomy and target geometry.

The planning objective was to give at least 95% of the prescribed dose to at least 95% of the PTV while minimizing the dose to the organs at risk. Plan evaluation was done by the treating radiation oncologist and peer reviewed by biomedical physics and radiation oncology team members. Nodal irradiation planning conformed to the same objectives as stated for the PTV. While the majority of patients underwent axillary node irradiation, a select few were treated with internal mammary nodal (IMN) radiation. The protocol for IMN radiation is based on the guidelines established by the Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) for patients presenting with inner quadrant tumors or larger tumors.

Once the plans satisfied the desired coverage and constraints, no more adjustments were made. It was then peer-reviewed by the medical physics team and approved by a radiation oncologist before treatment. Institutional OAR guidelines were adopted from objectives presented by QUANTEC analysis (Quantitative Analyses of Normal Tissue Effects in the Clinic) and RTOG report 62 guidelines of normal tissue effects [12, 13]: that no more than 10% volume of the heart and ipsilateral lung should receive 25 Gy (V25 Gy ≤ 10%) and a mean dose of not more than 3 Gy to the contralateral breast. These dosimetric parameters were recorded for all patients. Additional parameters such as V20Gy for the lung, V5Gy for the heart, and mean dose to the thyroid gland were assessed after the treatment planning to further compare to similar planning modalities from the published data [14]. Regional nodal irradiation (RNI) was delivered according to institutional practice and RTOG-based selection criteria. IMN coverage was selective and applied for primary tumours located in the inner quadrant or where there was high nodal burden, following RTOG guidance; when included, IMN targets were planned to the same PTV acceptance objective used for the primary target (PTV V95% ≥ 95%).

Data analysis

Dose‑volume histograms (DVHs) were analyzed for the created plans and compared with the published treatment plans with similar treatment protocols from other developing countries [15]. Our results were compared with literature from other LMICs. Valuable dosimetric parameters such as V20 Gy and V25 Gy for lung, and V5 Gy and V25 Gy for heart dose for the radiation plan were compared. The mean values, standard deviation, and median were calculated.

Results

A total of 67 patients were included. Most patients were aged between 41 and 60 years, with the highest proportion in the 41–50-year group (35.8%). Left-sided breast cancers were slightly more frequent than right-sided disease (56.7% vs. 43.3%). Based on standardized staging extracted from available records, the cohort was predominantly composed of locally advanced cases, with T2 disease (23.9%) and T3–T4 disease (23.8%) forming a substantial proportion. Nodal involvement was common, with N1 and N2 disease observed in 23.9% and 13.4% of patients, respectively, while metastatic disease (M1) was present in 19.4%. A palpable breast lump was documented in 73.1% of patients, and axillary lymph node involvement was recorded in 61.2%. Invasive ductal carcinoma was the predominant histological subtype (92.5%). Most tumours were low to intermediate grade, with SBR grade I and II accounting for 52.2% and 32.8% of cases, respectively. Regarding molecular subtype, triple-negative breast cancer was the most common (35.8%), followed by luminal A (17.9%) and luminal B (10.4%); HER2-enriched disease accounted for 7.5%, while receptor status was unknown in 28.4%. Surgical management was predominantly mastectomy-based (95.5%), with breast-conserving surgery performed in 4.5% of patients. Axillary lymph node dissection was the principal nodal procedure (80.6%). Chemotherapy exposure was common, with most patients receiving anthracycline-based and/or taxane-containing regimens, while platinum- and capecitabine-containing therapies were less frequent. Anti-HER2 therapy was documented in a small subset of patients (7.5%) corresponding to the number patients which had HER 2-enriched tumours (Table 1).

Conventional treatment plan OAR doses by surgical procedure

In the conventional fractionation cohort (n = 7), mean ipsilateral lung dose was 14.8 ± 1.0 Gy overall and was similar between mastectomy (14.6 ± 0.9 Gy) and breast-conserving surgery (BCS) patients (14.9 ± 1.4 Gy). Low-dose lung exposure showed marked variability, with higher ipsilateral lung V5Gy in the BCS group (95.2 ± 3.0%) compared with mastectomy (47.2 ± 34.0%), while intermediate-dose volumes were comparable (V20Gy: 18.2 ± 3.9% vs. 23.6 ± 9.2%). Mean heart dose across the cohort was 7.7 ± 3.6 Gy, with higher cardiac exposure observed in BCS patients (9.8 ± 1.5 Gy) than in those undergoing mastectomy (6.0 ± 4.2 Gy). A similar pattern was seen for heart V5Gy (83.8 ± 11.9% vs. 44.9 ± 33.0%). Thyroid exposure remained substantial, with a mean dose of 23.2 ± 13.7 Gy overall and higher values in the BCS subgroup (28.3 ± 8.7 Gy) compared with mastectomy (19.4 ± 16.2 Gy). Overall plan metrics demonstrated a mean delivered dose of 54.3 ± 7.3 Gy for the cohort, with higher mean dose in mastectomy patients (56.7 ± 4.7 Gy) than in BCS patients (50.0 ± 0.0 Gy) (Table 3).

In the hypofractionated cohort (Table 4; n = 60), mean ipsilateral lung dose was similar across disease stages, ranging from 14.0 ± 1.8 Gy in stage II to 15.0 ± 1.1 Gy in stage IV, with consistently high low-dose lung volumes (V4Gy 93.1–98.8% and V5Gy 90.6–96.7%). Intermediate lung dose exposure remained comparable across stages, with ipsilateral lung V16Gy between 27.9 ± 8.3% and 30.5 ± 7.8%. Mean heart dose increased with advancing stage, from 8.1 ± 1.9 Gy in stage I and 8.7 ± 3.8 Gy in stage II to 9.9 ± 3.1 Gy in stage III and 11.0 ± 1.7 Gy in stage IV. Correspondingly, heart V16Gy rose from 9.1 ± 6.6% in stage I to 19.7 ± 6.4% in stage IV, and heart V20Gy increased from 2.7 ± 2.7% to 9.9 ± 4.8% across the same stages. Contralateral organ doses were low across all stages, with contralateral lung mean dose ranging from 4.1 ± 0.7 Gy to 5.5 ± 2.1 Gy and contralateral breast mean dose between 2.6 ± 0.9 Gy and 2.9 ± 1.4 Gy. Thyroid exposure was substantial in all stage groups, with mean thyroid dose between 19.9 ± 8.6 Gy and 22.8 ± 9.4 Gy and V25Gy ranging from 38.2 ± 22.6% to 47.2 ± 28.0%.

Overall plan metrics demonstrated mean delivered doses between 52.5 ± 8.3 Gy and 60.0 ± 0.0 Gy across stages, with maximum plan dose ranging from 104.6 ± 0.0 Gy to 108.6 ± 4.1 Gy and median dose values of 55–60 Gy.

Discussion

This study reports the institutional dosimetric experience of modern IMRT-based postoperative breast radiotherapy for 67 consecutive patients treated at a new tertiary radiotherapy centre in South-West Nigeria. The cohort was comparatively young (median age 50.4 years) and predominantly presented with locally advanced disease (stage III 44.8%; stage II 28.4%) (Table 1). Younger age distribution and advanced stage at presentation are consistent with epidemiologic patterns reported across sub-Saharan Africa and other LMIC settings, where delayed diagnosis remains common [1–4]. The implications are clinically significant: younger patients face a longer lifetime risk window for late radiation-related cardiac, pulmonary and endocrine effects [1–4].

The predominance of advanced disease explains the high rate of regional nodal irradiation (63/67 patients) and the predominance of modified radical mastectomy over breast-conserving surgery (Table 1). Similar treatment patterns have been reported in African surgical and oncology series, where late presentation and infrastructural limitations shape operative and adjuvant decisions [5–8]. Comprehensive nodal irradiation inevitably increases dose to adjacent organs at risk, as demonstrated in IMRT versus 3DCRT comparisons and contouring guideline analyses [9–12]. PTV coverage in the hypofractionated cohort was acceptable and aligned with ICRU reporting standards [13]. Mean PTV was approximately 44 Gy with D95 ≈ 42.6 Gy and D98 ≈ 41.9 Gy (Table 6). These metrics are consistent with modern IMRT chest wall planning benchmarks and comparable to reported hypofractionated IMRT experiences [14–17]. The 45 Gy subgroup predictably demonstrated higher central tendency values, while smaller prescription cohorts generated statistical outliers, warranting cautious interpretation. The dominant IMRT dosimetric signature was a pronounced ipsilateral lung low-dose bath. In the hypofractionated cohort, V4Gy and V5Gy were large, whereas higher-dose lung volumes such as V16Gy were substantially lower (Table 2).

Mean ipsilateral lung dose clustered around 14–15 Gy across stages (Tables 2, 4). This pattern reflects the expected beam-angle distribution characteristics of multi-field IMRT, which increases low-dose spread while limiting high-dose hotspots [18–20]. Comparative dosimetric analyses have consistently demonstrated this trade-off between conformity and low-dose lung exposure [21–23]. From a normal-tissue perspective, lung dose constraints should be interpreted in light of established dose-response analyses [12]. Contralateral organ sparing was effective. Contralateral breast and lung doses remained modest (Tables 2, 4). Compared with published TomoTherapy and VMAT experiences, our cohort demonstrated lower contralateral breast and mean heart doses while maintaining comparable ipsilateral lung means (Supplementary Table 5a), consistent with modality-dependent geometric effects [24–26].

Cardiac exposure was measurable and stage-dependent. Mean heart dose averaged approximately 9.4 Gy in the hypofractionated cohort and increased progressively with stage (Table 4). Expanded nodal volumes in advanced disease explain this gradient. Epidemiologic data demonstrate a linear increase in major coronary events with rising mean heart dose, without a clear safe threshold [12]. Consequently, heart doses approaching 10 Gy in a relatively young population carry meaningful long-term implications.

To refine cardiac risk assessment, the left anterior descending artery (LAD) was retrospectively contoured. Mean LAD dose was 12.49 ± 8.23 Gy, with 28.3% of patients receiving > 20 Gy (Supplementary Table 7A; Supplementary Table 7B).

Coronary substructure dose has been increasingly recognized as clinically relevant, particularly for left-sided and nodal irradiation [15, 16]. The observed LAD distribution reflects left-sided predominance and routine supraclavicular coverage. These findings support prospective cardiac substructure contouring and LAD-guided optimization in future planning.

Deep inspiration breath-hold (DIBH) techniques have demonstrated consistent heart and LAD dose reduction in breast radiotherapy [27]. Implementation of DIBH represents an immediate evidence-based quality improvement strategy. Furthermore, broader improvements in radiotherapy planning and delivery standards in LMIC contexts remain essential to optimize cardiac sparing [28–31].

Thyroid exposure was notable. Mean thyroid dose exceeded 20 Gy with substantial V25Gy values (Tables 2, 4, 6). Supraclavicular irradiation is a well-established risk factor for radiation-induced hypothyroidism, with multiple longitudinal series documenting clinically significant dysfunction at comparable dose ranges [32–35]. Structured thyroid surveillance should therefore be integrated into survivorship protocols for this population. The small conventional-fractionation subgroup (n = 7) demonstrated similar overall patterns but substantial heterogeneity driven by single-patient values and boost inclusion (Tables 3, 4, 5, 6, 7). Given limited numbers, these findings are descriptive rather than inferential.

Importantly, the dosimetric characteristics observed here must be contextualized within structural realities of cancer care delivery in Nigeria and sub-Saharan Africa. Radiotherapy access remains limited, with workforce shortages, equipment constraints, and late-stage presentation shaping clinical practice [36–43]. Addressing these systemic barriers is critical to improving both technical quality and population outcomes.

Limitations of the present work warrant careful consideration. The retrospective, single-centre design inherently limits generalisability and introduces the potential for selection and documentation bias. Given the role of our institution as a major regional referral centre for radiotherapy, a substantial proportion of patients were referred after initial diagnostic evaluation and treatment elsewhere. Consequently, staging and systemic therapy data were derived from available clinical records and were not uniformly generated within the study centre, resulting in incomplete TNM documentation in a subset of patients.

In addition, systemic therapy details, including the timing of chemotherapy relative to radiotherapy, could not be consistently reconstructed as neoadjuvant or adjuvant due to the retrospective nature of the dataset. Toxicity outcomes were not available for detailed analysis in this report and will require prospective longitudinal follow-up, particularly in light of the observed cardiac and thyroid dose exposure. Measurement of thyroid volumes was also limited in a subset of cases, reflecting both contouring variability and data availability constraints. Finally, the relatively small conventional-fractionation subgroup limits the strength of comparative subgroup analyses and precludes inferential interpretation.

Notwithstanding these limitations, the present study provides a well-characterised institutional dosimetric baseline for postoperative breast radiotherapy in a resource-constrained setting. Multi-field IMRT at our centre achieved acceptable target coverage while demonstrating the expected dosimetric profile of increased low-dose lung exposure alongside measurable cardiac and thyroid doses, largely attributable to comprehensive nodal irradiation (Tables 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and Supplementary Table 7A–7B). The incorporation of left anterior descending artery (LAD) dosimetry adds clinically relevant substructure-level insight into cardiac exposure.

From a quality improvement perspective, these findings highlight clear priorities, including the routine implementation of deep inspiration breath-hold (DIBH), prospective incorporation of cardiac substructure-guided planning, and structured thyroid surveillance within survivorship care pathways. Furthermore, refinement of institutional planning protocols to align with contemporary breast radiotherapy dose constraint frameworks represents an important next step.

Overall, this analysis defines a realistic and context-specific benchmark for radiotherapy planning in our setting and provides a foundation for prospective evaluation of treatment-related toxicity and long-term oncologic outcomes.
