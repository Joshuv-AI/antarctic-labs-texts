Level of physical activity during pregnancy among Sri Lankan primigravida mothers: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Sepalage Nilanthi Chathurika, Rathnayake Mudiyanselage Chathura Rathnayake, Deepika Indumathie Nanayakkara, Sampath Udaya Bandara Thennakoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough physical activity during pregnancy is recommended, many women reduce their activity levels due to misconceptions, cultural beliefs, and inadequate guidance. In Sri Lanka, limited evidence exists on physical activity patterns among pregnant women, particularly primigravida mothers. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the level of physical activity during pregnancy among primigravida mothers attending an antenatal clinic at a tertiary care hospital in Sri Lanka.MethodsA descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted in the ante-natal clinic from September’2019 to March’2020. Primigravida mothers who were in first (n1=181), second (n2=225) and third (n3=178) trimesters were recruited (N = 584) using systematic random sampling. Data collection was done through a self-administered pregnancy physical activity Questionnaire (PPAQ) and analyzed using SPSS version 25.0. Pearson chi-square tests were adopted in data analysis to find associations between socio-demographic data and PA level. Outcome variables compared among trimesters by Kruskal Wallis H test.ResultsMean age of participants was 25.88 ± 3.18 years and their response rate were 96.4%. Mothers who did not follow international recommendation of PA were 19.3%, 29.3% and 32.0% for first, second and third trimesters, respectively. Statistically significant association was found in between PA level and the trimester (p = 0.015). Also, significant associations were observed in between PA level and the family type (p = 0.001), and the living area (p = 0.031) and the pre-pregnancy BMI (p = 0.016) in first trimester. Further, no significant association was present in between level of education and level of PA within any trimester (p > 0.05). Mean duration for total activity and energy expenditure of pregnant mothers were not significantly differ among the trimesters (p > 0.05). Most favored modes of PA were household/care giving and light intensity activity in each trimester.ConclusionsNearly one-quarter of pregnant mothers did not meet the recommended level of physical activity during pregnancy. The proportion of inactive mothers increased progressively with advancing trimester. Therefore, structured education programmes demonstrating safe and appropriate physical activities according to gestational age are recommended. Particular attention should be given to inactive mothers to enhance knowledge and promote motivation to achieve recommended physical activity levels.

Background

Physical activity (PA) is defined as any bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles that requires energy expenditure [1]. Pregnancy is associated with considerable physiological and psychological changes, which may influence women’s daily activity patterns. Many pregnant women tend to reduce their level of physical activity as pregnancy progresses [2].

Insufficient physical activity during pregnancy is associated with several adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes, including excessive gestational weight gain, gestational diabetes mellitus, hypertensive disorders, and poor psychological well being [3, 4]. Although the benefits of physical activity during pregnancy are well recognized and international guidelines recommend regular moderate-intensity activity, a considerable proportion of pregnant women do not achieve the recommended levels [5].

In Sri Lanka, antenatal care services are well established and widely accessible. However, routine assessment and monitoring of physical activity during pregnancy are not consistently emphasized. Cultural beliefs, fear of pregnancy-related complications, lack of awareness, and inadequate counselling may contribute to reduced physical activity among pregnant women in the Sri Lankan context [6]. Furthermore, most local maternal health studies have focused on nutritional status and pregnancy outcomes, while limited research has directly assessed physical activity patterns among pregnant women, especially primigravida mothers [7].

Physical activity levels may vary across trimesters due to early pregnancy symptoms such as nausea and fatigue, and later physical discomfort and reduced mobility. Therefore, it is important to assess physical activity throughout all three trimesters to identify possible changes during pregnancy.

However, evidence on trimester-specific physical activity levels among Sri Lankan pregnant women remains limited. Hence, This study aimed to assess physical activity levels among primigravida mothers attending an antenatal clinic in a Sri Lankan tertiary care hospital.

Materials and methods

Study design & participants

A descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted in the ante-natal clinic, Teaching Hospital, Peradeniya, Sri Lanka from September’2019 to February ‘2020. The sample size was calculated using a standard formula [8] for estimating a population proportion based on a previously reported prevalence of adequate physical activity during pregnancy [9]. The minimum required sample was adjusted for a non-response rate of 10%.

Primigravida mothers who were in first (n1=181), second (n2=225) and third (n3=178) trimesters attending the antenatal clinic during the study period were recruited (N = 584) using systematic random sampling. Based on the average daily clinic attendance register, every kth eligible mother was invited to participate after determining the sampling interval (k = total number of eligible mothers attending the clinic during the data collection period / required sample size). Recruitment was continued until the required number of participants from each trimester was achieved.

Mothers were considered eligible, if they had following characteristics; Sinhala speaking, pre-pregnancy BMI ≤ 30 kg, 20 to 30 years of age, and having singleton pregnancy. Mothers with pre-existing chronic medical conditions (e.g., diabetes mellitus, chronic hypertension, cardiac disease), pregnancy-related complications (e.g., pre-eclampsia, gestational diabetes mellitus, placenta previa, threatened miscarriage, or preterm labor), or those advised medical activity restriction were excluded. Restriction to primigravida mothers and BMI ≤ 30 kg/m² was applied to minimize confounding effects of parity and obesity on physical activity patterns and pregnancy outcomes.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics and Research Committee, Faculty of Medicine, University of Peradeniya (Protocol No.2019/EC/16). Permissions were received from the Director and Consultant obstetrician, ante-natal clinic, teaching Hospital, Peradeniya (THP/Planning/Approval research/02/2019) prior to active phase of the research.

Data collection tools & procedure

Data collection was conducted by the principal investigator (PI). No additional data collectors were employed. Socio-demographic information was obtained using a pre-tested interviewer-administered questionnaire. Anthropometric data (height and pre-pregnancy weight) were extracted from antenatal clinic records.

Physical activity during pregnancy was assessed using the Pregnancy Physical Activity Questionnaire (PPAQ), a semi-quantitative instrument consisting of 32 items covering household/caregiving, occupational, sports/exercise, transportation, and inactivity domains during the current trimester [10].

As no officially published Sinhala version was available, the PPAQ was translated into Sinhala following standard forward–backward translation procedures. The translated version was reviewed by bilingual experts in maternal health and pre-tested among 20 pregnant mothers attending a different antenatal clinic to assess clarity and cultural appropriateness. Necessary modifications were made prior to data collection. However, full psychometric validation has not yet been formally published.

After giving detailed explanation about the study, written informed consent was obtained from eligible participants prior to data collection. Consented participant mothers were interviewed first for gathering socio-demographic data. Then, the participants were given PPAQ, and adequate time was provided to complete.

Analysis of socio-demographic data

Participant mothers’ age, income, family type, living area, level of education, employment, type of employment, working hours of employed mothers and sleeping hours per day were obtained as socio-demographic data. Height and pre-pregnancy weight were taken as anthropometric measurements and used to calculate pre-pregnancy BMI. Pre-pregnancy BMI is presented as categories according to the WHO cut-off values [11] (underweight < 18.5 kg/m2, normal-18.6–24.9 kg/m2, overweight-25–29.9 kg/m2).

Analysis of PA data

Physical activity was quantified using the PPAQ scoring protocol [10]. Time spent in each activity (hours per week) was multiplied by the corresponding metabolic equivalent (MET) value to estimate weekly energy expenditure (MET-hours/week).

According to the PPAQ, activities were classifieds into four groups by intensity (sedentary, light intensity, moderate intensity and vigorous activity) and five groups by type (household, occupational, sports/exercises, transportation and inactivity). Total activity was calculated by summing up average weekly energy expenditure of all categories of intensity levels. Although vigorous activity is not routinely recommended for previously inactive pregnant women, it was included in analysis as reported in the PPAQ to capture the full range of activities performed.

The primary outcome variable was physical activity level (active/inactive), defined according to international recommendations (≥ 150 min of moderate-intensity activity per week) [12]. Participants meeting this threshold were categorized as active; others were classified as inactive.Secondary outcomes included total duration of activity (hours/week) and total energy expenditure (MET-hours/week).

Statistical analysis

Collected data were entered to the statistical software after cross checking. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0. First, all variables were tested to determine whether they followed a normal distribution by using statistical tests (Kolmogorov–Smirnov test) and graphs (histogram with normal distribution curve). Continuous data were presented as means and standard deviations. Categorical data were presented as numbers and percentages.

Chi-square test was performed to find association between socio-demographic characteristics and the PA level separately for each trimester. Duration and energy expenditure of all modes of activities in first, second and third trimester were tested to determine presence of significant difference among pregnant mothers in relation to trimester by non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis H test. Then, pairwise comparisons were performed by same test followed by Bonferroni test to detect any significant difference between each pairs of trimesters. P value < 0.05 is considered as statistically significant for all tests.

Results

The response rate was 96.4%. From the total sample, 21 questionnaires were excluded due to inadequacy of physical activity (PA) data.

Characteristics of the participants

Table 1 summarizes the categorical and continuous socio-demographic data of the participants (N = 584) according to trimester. The majority of mothers had normal pre-pregnancy BMI (61.8%), while 24.3% were overweight and 13.9% were underweight. Most participants belonged to nuclear families (68.5%) and were from semi-urban (44.0%) or rural areas (44.3%). Nearly half had completed Advanced level education (47.4%), and the majority were unemployed (72.8%), with similar distributions across trimesters. The overall mean age was 25.88 ± 3.18 years and mean height was 153.96 ± 5.66 cm. Working hours were highest in the second trimester, while sleeping hours showed minimal variation across trimesters.

Physical activity level based on the trimester

The proportion of mothers who were not following at least 150 min of moderate intensity PA per week (inactive group) was 19.3% in the first trimester, 29.3% in the second trimester and 32.0% in the third trimester. Chi-square test revealed a statistically significant association between PA level and trimester (χ²=8.28, p = 0.015, df = 2). A gradual decrease in the proportion of active pregnant mothers was observed with advancing trimester (Fig. 1).

Among activity types, transportation and inactivity showed significant differences across trimesters for both duration and energy expenditure, while household activity demonstrated significant variation only in energy expenditure. No significant differences were observed for vigorous, sports/exercise, or occupational activities. These findings indicate a shift from moderate intensity activities toward increased light intensity and sedentary behaviors with advancing gestation.

Discussion

This study assessed trimester-specific physical activity (PA) patterns among primigravida mothers attending a tertiary care antenatal clinic in Sri Lanka. A relatively high proportion of participants met the recommended ≥ 150 min of moderate-intensity physical activity per week, although adherence gradually declined from the first to the third trimester. International guidelines recommend at least 150 min of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week during pregnancy for women without contraindications [3, 5, 12]. While the prevalence observed in the present study appears higher than reported in several international studies [13–17], direct comparisons should be interpreted cautiously due to methodological differences, variations in assessment tools, and population characteristics.

The observed reduction in moderate-intensity activity and increase in inactivity during the third trimester are consistent with physiological adaptations to advancing pregnancy, including weight gain, fatigue, musculoskeletal discomfort, and altered balance [2, 17]. Similar trimester-related declines have been reported in other cohort and cross-sectional studies [17, 19–23]. These findings suggest that late pregnancy represents a critical period for targeted intervention to prevent unnecessary reductions in activity.

Household and caregiving activities contributed substantially to total physical activity across all trimesters. This pattern reflects the socio-demographic profile of the study population, where the majority of mothers were unemployed and primarily engaged in domestic responsibilities. Although such activities contribute to overall energy expenditure, they may not consistently achieve moderate-intensity thresholds required to meet international recommendations [3, 5]. Therefore, antenatal counselling should clearly differentiate between routine household activity and structured moderate-intensity exercise.

Energy expenditure patterns demonstrated a shift from moderate- to light-intensity activity with advancing gestation, despite no significant difference in total energy expenditure across trimesters. This qualitative redistribution of activity intensity has also been reported in previous studies using the Pregnancy Physical Activity Questionnaire [10, 15]. Maintenance of adequate activity intensity may therefore be as important as total duration when evaluating adherence to guidelines.

The proportion of mothers who did not meet recommended PA levels increased in the third trimester. Cultural beliefs and family influences in Sri Lanka may encourage rest and restriction of movement as pregnancy advances [6]. While intended to safeguard maternal and fetal wellbeing, such practices may inadvertently contribute to physical inactivity. Future qualitative research is needed to explore sociocultural perceptions of physical activity during pregnancy in the Sri Lankan context.

Employment status was significantly associated with PA level in the second and third trimesters. In this study, “active” was defined as achieving ≥ 150 min of moderate-intensity activity per week [3]. Employed mothers were more likely to meet this recommendation, likely due to occupational activity contributing to total weekly activity. Similar associations between occupational engagement and overall activity levels have been observed in other populations [20, 21].

This study has several limitations. PA was assessed using the self-reported PPAQ [10], which is subject to recall bias. The cross-sectional design included independent trimester groups rather than longitudinal follow-up, limiting causal inference. Furthermore, recruitment from a single urban tertiary hospital restricts generalizability to the broader Sri Lankan population. Inclusion was limited to primigravida mothers with BMI ≤ 30 kg/m², which further narrows applicability. Although a Sinhala translation of the PPAQ was used, formal published validation in Sri Lanka remains limited.

Despite these limitations, this study provides important trimester-specific data addressing a gap in local maternal health research [7], offering preliminary evidence to inform antenatal counselling practices. Routine, trimester-specific physical activity counselling should be incorporated into antenatal care in accordance with international guidelines [3, 5]. Healthcare providers should address cultural misconceptions that encourage unnecessary activity restriction during late pregnancy [6] and promote safe moderate-intensity activity. Further longitudinal research and formal validation of the Sinhala PPAQ are also recommended to strengthen local evidence.

Conclusion

More than two-thirds of primigravida mothers in this urban tertiary setting met recommended physical activity levels; however, adherence declined with advancing gestation, with the highest inactivity observed in the third trimester. Although total energy expenditure remained similar across trimesters, a shift from moderate- to light-intensity activity was evident.

These findings highlight the need for structured, culturally appropriate physical activity guidance within Sri Lanka’s antenatal care system to prevent avoidable reductions in safe activity during late pregnancy.
