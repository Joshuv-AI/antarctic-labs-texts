A deep learning model for anti-inflammatory peptides identification based on deep variational autoencoder and contrastive learning
Authors: Yujie Xu, Shengli Zhang, Feng Zhu, Yunyun Liang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
As a class of biologically active molecules with significant immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory effects, anti-inflammatory peptides have important application value in the medical and biotechnology fields due to their unique biological functions. Research on the identification of anti-inflammatory peptides provides important theoretical foundations and practical value for a deeper understanding of the biological mechanisms of inflammation and immune regulation, as well as for the development of new drugs and biotechnological applications. Therefore, it is necessary to develop more advanced computational models for identifying anti-inflammatory peptides. In this study, we propose a deep learning model named DAC-AIPs based on variational autoencoder and contrastive learning for accurate identification of anti-inflammatory peptides. In the sequence encoding part, the incorporation of multi-hot encoding helps capture richer sequence information. The autoencoder, composed of convolutional layers and linear layers, can learn latent features and reconstruct features, with variational inference enhancing the representation capability of latent features. Additionally, the introduction of contrastive learning aims to improve the model's classification ability. Through cross-validation and independent dataset testing experiments, DAC-AIPs achieves superior performance compared to existing state-of-the-art models. In cross-validation, the classification accuracy of DAC-AIPs reached around 88%, which is 7% higher than previous models. Furthermore, various ablation experiments and interpretability experiments validate the effectiveness of DAC-AIPs. Finally, a user-friendly online predictor is designed to enhance the practicality of the model, and the server is freely accessible at http://dac-aips.online.

Introduction

Motivation

Anti-inflammatory peptides (AIPs) are peptides that possess anti-inflammatory properties and are typically composed of short amino acid chains1,2. These peptides can regulate and inhibit inflammatory responses, aiding in alleviating symptoms and progression of inflammation-related diseases3–5. AIPs can act through various pathways, such as inhibiting the release of inflammatory mediators, regulating immune cell activity, and promoting tissue repair6–8. Due to their anti-inflammatory effects, AIPs are widely researched in the medical and biotechnology fields and are potential drug candidates for treating inflammatory diseases9–11. Research on AIPs identification not only contributes to finding new treatment methods but also explores the potential of the natural immune system to combat multidrug-resistant microorganisms12–15. It also provides new ideas and possibilities for biotechnological applications. Studies in these areas will provide important theoretical foundations and practical guidance for advances and innovations in the biomedical field16. However, traditional experimental methods are expensive, time-consuming, and labor-intensive, making it necessary to develop computational models for identifying AIPs to achieve more efficient research.

Related work

There exist numerous computational models for identifying AIPs, and some experimental achievements have been made. In 2017, Gupta et al. extracted sequence-based features and utilized Support Vector Machine (SVM) and Random Forest (RF) as classifiers to construct the first computational model for identifying AIPs, named AntiInflam17. Subsequently, Manavalan et al., Khatun et al., and Zhang et al. utilized RF as the base classifier and employed different ensemble methods in 2018, 2019, and 2021, respectively, to construct AIPpred18, PreAIP19, and AIEpred20. Besides, Wei et al.21 and Zhang et al.22. respectively constructed prediction tools for therapeutic peptides including AIPs using random forest in 2019 and 2020, and introduced adaptive feature representation strategies and features based on physicochemical properties respectively to better represent therapeutic peptides. Guo et al.23 and Yan et al.24 respectively constructed therapeutic peptide predictors through ensemble learning in 2021 and 2022, both of which were integrated by multiple feature extractors and classifiers. Similarly, in 2021, Zhao et al. proposed iAIPs25, which integrated five machine learning algorithms including RF and Gradient Boost (GB) as classifiers, achieving superior classification performance compared to previous models. In recent years, with the rise of deep learning26–29, its applications in the biomedical field have become increasingly widespread. For instance, Mui-zzud-din et al.30 proposed using a stacked convolutional neural network model to predict melanoma of skin cancer. Guo et al.31 proposed a context-aware poly (A) signal prediction model based on deep spatial–temporal neural networks. Li et al.32 proposed a spatio-temporal embedding learning based on gated convolutional recurrent networks for translation initiation site prediction. Yang et al.33 proposed an ensemble learning method for low-light image enhancement. These computational models have made significant contributions to related biomedical research. Conforming to the development of computational models, research on AIPs prediction has also been inspired. In 2023, Raza et al. and Guan et al. used deep learning algorithms to identify AIPs, where Raza et al. proposed AIPs-SnTCN34, employing an ensemble algorithm of Deep Neural Networks (DNN), Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN), and others, while Guan et al.’s work35 fused traditional machine learning algorithms with deep learning algorithms to create a composite model. As the state-of-the-art method, IF-AIP36 was proposed by Gaffar et al. in 2024, still utilizing an ensemble model of five machine learning algorithms including RF, achieving the most advanced performance. Overall, it can be observed that the current application of deep learning algorithms in the field of AIPs identification is relatively limited, and there is still room for improvement in the performance of AIPs identification models.

In this study, deep learning algorithms continue to be used to construct AIPs identification model. We encode sequences using an improved version of one-hot encoding and employ deep variational autoencoders along with contrastive learning to build a model named DAC-AIPs, which exhibits superior classification performance compared to previous studies. Materials and methods

Data preparation

Data collection

A good dataset can provide rich and diverse samples for the deep learning model, so that the model can learn more generalized features and thereby improve its performance. In this study, we use the same dataset as IF-AIP36, which collect the datasets from iAIPs25 and AntiInflam17, resulting in two benchmark datasets. We merge the two benchmark datasets into one dataset comprising 1948 positive samples and 2896 negative samples. Subsequently, the CD-HIT37 tool is applied to remove redundancy (threshold set to 0.9), resulting in a benchmark dataset with 1450 positive samples and 2339 negative samples. Furthermore, independent datasets from AntiInflam17 and iAIPs25 are collected, consisting of 173 positive samples and 252 negative samples, and 420 positive samples and 629 negative samples respectively. The final model is tested using the two independent datasets. Data augmentation

In deep learning, having a sufficient amount of training data is often crucial for achieving better performance, and insufficient training data is a common issue. One effective approach to address the problem of insufficient data is data augmentation, which increases the amount of data available for training. In this study, we apply three different data augmentation methods to the training data, namely random interception, sequence reversing, and noise addition to peptide sequences. These methods aim to increase the volume of training data while reducing overfitting and enhancing the robustness of the model. Specifically, random interception involves randomly selecting a starting position and length within the peptide sequence, then extracting this subsequence from the original sequence as a new sample. This helps increase data diversity, allowing the model to learn features from different lengths and positions during training. Sequence reversing simply reverses the order of the peptide sequence. By reversing the sequence, new sequence features are introduced, helping the model better understand specific sequence patterns and structures within the peptide chain. The noise addition operation randomly replaces some amino acids within the sequence. This method aids in training the model's robustness to noise and variations, reducing the risk of overfitting while increasing data diversity and coverage. It is important to note that data augmentation does not change the sample's class label. Positive samples, after undergoing data augmentation, are still considered positive samples. The combination of these three data augmentation methods can significantly improve the model's generalization ability and performance in peptide sequence analysis tasks, enabling it to better handle peptide data of different lengths, structures, and noise levels.

Model overview

DAC-AIPs is mainly constructed based on deep variational autoencoder (VAE) and contrastive learning. In the sequence encoding part, we improve the traditional one-hot encoding by incorporating multi-hot features to enhance the ability of capturing sequential information. In this study, the deep VAE consists of two parts: an encoder and a decoder. The encoder comprises convolutional layers, max-pooling layers, and linear layers, while the decoder consists of linear layers, upsampling layers, and transposed convolutional layers. The VAE maps input data to a latent space, samples data points from the latent space, and then reconstructs them through the decoder. The latent features output by the encoder are fed into the output layer for classification. On the other hand, we extract latent features from different category data and compute contrastive loss. In the process of contrastive learning, the goal is to reduce the distance between samples of the same label and increase the distance between samples of different labels by minimizing the loss, thereby improving classification performance. Sequence encoding

One-hot encoding is widely used in machine learning for feature representation38–40. It converts peptide sequences into numerical vectors acceptable by models through binary encoding. For a peptide sequence of length L, each amino acid in the sequence is represented by a 20-dimensional binary vector. These 20 dimensions represent the sequential arrangement of the 20 types of amino acids, with only the element corresponding to the amino acid being 1, and all other elements being 0. However, one-hot encoding suffers from sparsity. More importantly, each amino acid vector in the encoded sequence is independent of each other, meaning it cannot capture the sequence's order and positional information. Therefore, we simultaneously employ the multi-hot method for encoding. Multi-hot first divides the sequence into continuous “words” using a sliding window of size k, and then encodes each word. In this encoding vector, all positions corresponding to amino acids in the word are set to 1. Deep variational autoencoder

Encoder-decoder

Autoencoder41,42 is a type of neural network based on unsupervised learning, aiming to reconstruct input samples that have been compressed through parameter adjustments. The transformation from the input layer to the intermediary layer is denoted as encoding, whereas the transformation from the intermediary layer to the output layer is labeled as decoding. Typically, an autoencoder first obtains a compressed vector through encoding and then reconstructs it through decoding. In this study, the encoder part consists of sequential layers including two convolutional layers, two max-pooling layers, and two linear layers. The last linear layer outputs the distribution of the latent space (mean μ and variance σ), from which sampling is performed, and the sampled vector is input into the decoder. The decoder's layers are ordered as two linear layers, two upsampling layers, and two transposed convolutional layers. Finally, the decoder outputs the reconstruction vector, and the reconstruction loss is calculated together with the input vector. An intuitive model structure is illustrated in Fig. 1C.

Autoencoders can learn compact representations of data, which can serve as the basis for feature learning and assist in subsequent supervised learning tasks. Through self-supervised training, autoencoders can obtain a latent feature encoding from the original features, achieving automated feature engineering and the goals of dimensionality reduction and generalization.

Variation

Autoencoders typically do not constrain the structure of the latent space, thus learned representations may be discontinuous, which can lead to generated data being incoherent or not conforming to the data distribution. Variational Autoencoder (VAE)43 utilize variational inference techniques to constrain the distribution of the latent space, making the learned latent representations more continuous and aiding in generating coherent data.

In VAE, the hidden layer obtained through neural network encoding is assumed to follow a standard Gaussian distribution, outputting the mean (μ) and variance (σ) of a multi-dimensional Gaussian distribution. Subsequently, a feature z is sampled from this distribution, which is then used for decoding, aiming to reconstruct results similar to the original input. The structure of the latent space is normalized by constraining the distribution returned by the encoder to approximate a standard Gaussian.

Contrastive learning

Contrastive learning allows models to extract meaningful representations from unlabeled data. By leveraging similarities and dissimilarities, contrastive learning enables models to map similar instances closely together in the latent space while separating different instances45. This approach has been proven effective in various domains such as computer vision, natural language processing (NLP), and reinforcement learning.

The fundamental idea of contrastive learning is to learn compact representations of data by maximizing the similarity between similar samples and minimizing the similarity between dissimilar samples. In this study, anchor samples (samples under test), positive samples (samples of the same class as anchor samples), and negative samples (samples of different classes from anchor samples) are simultaneously input into the deep VAE for training. Contrastive loss is computed using the latent features extracted by the encoder corresponding to the three samples. In this study, triplet loss is utilized as the contrastive loss. By calculating the distance between features, the triplet loss function aims to reduce the feature distance between samples of the same class, while simultaneously enlarging the feature distance between samples of different classes. In addition, set a marginal distance, which is the minimum distance between samples of different classes, to prevent features from being compressed into a small space. Performance evaluation metrics

To evaluate the performance of the model, we use five commonly used evaluation metrics: Accuracy (ACC), Sensitivity (Sn), Specificity (Sp), Area Under Curve (AUC), and Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC)46–50. They respectively measure the proportion of correctly classified samples, the ability to correctly identify positives, the ability to correctly identify negatives, the performance of the model at different classification thresholds, and the overall performance of the model. With the exception of MCC, their values fall within the 0 to 1 range, with higher values indicating superior model performance. MCC, on the other hand, spans from -1 to + 1, where a score of + 1 signifies flawless prediction, 0 denotes random prediction, and − 1 signifies entirely contradictory prediction. Hyperparameter learning

Hyperparameter optimization is a challenging task. Optuna51 is a Python tool for hyperparameter optimization that leverages the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE)52 technique. Its aim is to determine the most promising hyperparameter configurations through Bayesian probability inference and iteratively optimize the search process. Optuna offers various optimization algorithms and parallelization strategies, along with an intuitive API and visualization tools, making hyperparameter optimization more straightforward and intuitive. In this study, we use the Optuna tool to optimize several key hyperparameters, including learning rate (lr), batch size, hidden size in the encoder, and three weights in the loss function (α, β, γ). After 30 searches, we find an optimal hyperparameter combination, providing accurate guidance for the final hyperparameter determination. In this study, data augmentation techniques play a crucial role in the data processing phase, and to validate their effectiveness, we conduct an ablation experiment. Initially, we compute the Jaccard similarity between the training and test sets before and after data augmentation to ensure minimal overlap between them post-augmentation. Subsequently, we train the model on both unaugmented and augmented training sets separately and evaluate its performance. The experimental results are depicted in Fig. 4A. The results demonstrate that both before and after data augmentation, the similarity between the training and test sets remains low. Importantly, after data augmentation, the model's performance notably improves, with an increase in accuracy of approximately 7%. This indicates that data augmentation effectively provides additional usable data for the training set, facilitating improved learning capabilities due to the larger dataset size. Sequence encoding ablation

The traditional one-hot encoding exhibits sparsity and lacks the ability to capture sequential information. Improved multi-hot encoding, which simultaneously encodes the neighboring amino acids of the amino acid, helps alleviate sparsity and captures positional information of amino acids. To validate the impact of multi-hot encoding on model performance, we conduct ablation experiments. We train and valid our model using only one-hot encoding, multi-hot encoding with k = 2, multi-hot encoding with k = 3, and a fusion of all three encoding methods. The experimental results, as depicted in Fig. 4, show varying performance across different encoding methods. Considering the ROC and PRC curves presented in Fig. 4C and D, multi-hot encoding outperforms one-hot encoding, validating our hypothesis and demonstrating the advantages of multi-hot encoding. Importantly, the fused encoding method performs better than any single encoding method, indicating that information extracted solely from a single scale may be insufficient. Consequently, integrating encoding methods of different scales proves to be a more effective choice.

Contrastive learning ablation

In DAC-AIPs, contrastive learning plays an indispensable role. To validate its importance and effectiveness, we conduct ablation experiments specifically targeting contrastive learning. Figure 4B illustrates the comparison of model classification performance with and without contrastive learning. It's evident from the experimental results that models lacking contrastive learning experience a significant performance decline, highlighting the crucial role of contrastive learning. Contrastive learning achieves this by reducing the distance between similar samples and increasing the distance between dissimilar ones. This enables the model to better capture the features of similar samples, thereby facilitating more accurate classification. In essence, contrastive learning enhances the model's understanding of data features and improves classification accuracy, providing an effective approach for enhancing model performance.

Discussion

We propose a deep model based on variational autoencoders and contrastive learning for the identification of AIPs. During the training process, we employ data augmentation and hyperparameter optimization operations to enhance the quality of the trained model. According to experimental results, our model DAC-AIPs indeed achieves good classification performance, at least compared to the currently most advanced models. However, it still has some limitations, such as low sensitivity, indicating a weak ability of the model to identify positive samples. This may be attributed to the imbalance in the dataset, where the scarcity of positive samples results in a weaker recognition capability compared to the trained negative samples. Further research is necessary to mitigate this deficiency. Moreover, it is hoped that more accessible anti-inflammatory peptide sequence data will be available for research on AIPs identification, which will be crucial for advancing such studies.

The application prospects of AIPs identification models are undeniable. Investigating the mechanisms of AIPs recognition aids in understanding the natural defense mechanisms of organisms against inflammation and infection, thereby inspiring the development of novel immunotherapies and preventive strategies. Therefore, the application prospects of AIPs identification models are extensive, spanning multiple fields such as medicine, biomedical research, and biomaterials, and they hold significant scientific research and practical value.

Conclusions

Research on the recognition mechanisms of AIPs contributes to a deeper understanding of immunoregulation processes, which is crucial for treating autoimmune diseases and preventing excessive immune activation. Developing computational models to identify AIPs is a challenging yet highly promising and valuable task. In this study, we propose a deep learning model named DAC-AIPs based on variational autoencoder and contrastive learning for the accurate identification of AIPs. For sequence encoding, we integrate multi-hot encoding with traditional one-hot encoding to help capture richer sequence information. In constructing the model, we utilize convolutional layers and linear layers to form an autoencoder, which learns latent features and reconstructs features. Additionally, variational inference is employed to enhance the representational power of latent features. Furthermore, contrastive learning methods collaborate with the autoencoder to improve the model's classification ability. In the experimental section, we first optimize several important hyperparameters using Bayesian probability-based optimization tool to improve training efficiency. Through cross-validation and independent set testing experiments, DAC-AIPs achieves superior performance compared to existing state-of-the-art models. Specifically, the classification accuracy reaches around 88% in cross-validation, which is 7% higher than previous models. Moreover, we conduct ablation experiments to separately validate the importance of multi-hot encoding and contrastive learning. Finally, some feature visualization and sequence analysis experiments enhance the interpretability of the model and validate the effectiveness of DAC-AIPs. In summary, we believe that DAC-AIPs promotes the development of AIPs recognition research and look forward to making further contributions to the biomedical field.
