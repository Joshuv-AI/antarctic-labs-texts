Comparative effectiveness of take-home dosing schedules for opioid agonist treatment in British Columbia, Canada: a target trial emulation protocol using a population-based observational study
Authors: Megan Kurz, B Carolina Guerra-Alejos, Md Belal Hossain, Jeong Eun Min, Ruyu Yan, Julie Bruneau, Nicole L A Catherine, Sander Greenland, Paul Gustafson, Lindsay Hedden, Ehsan Karim, Lawrence McCandless, Seonaid Nolan, Robert W Platt, Paxton Bach, Shaun Seaman, U Siebert, M Eugenia Socías, Bohdan Nosyk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionThe introduction of fentanyl and its analogues in the illicit drug supply has prompted greater emphasis on refining clinical treatment protocols to ensure sustained retention in opioid agonist treatment (OAT). Take-home dosing may lessen the treatment burden on clients and thus reduce the risk of treatment discontinuation. The evidence base supporting the use of take-home dosing, including the optimal duration of dispensations, is, however, limited. The objective of this study is to determine the comparative effectiveness of alternative take-home dosing schedules, as observed in clinical practice in British Columbia, Canada from 2010 to 2022.Methods and analysisWe propose to emulate a target trial with a population-level retrospective study of individuals initiating methadone or buprenorphine/naloxone between 1 January 2010 and 31 December 2022 who are 18 years of age or older and not currently incarcerated or pregnant with no history of cancer or palliative care. Our study will draw on nine linked health administrative databases from British Columbia and will evaluate take-home doses of 2–5 days, 6 days or >6 days compared with continuous daily dosing. The primary outcomes include OAT discontinuation and all-cause mortality on treatment. A causal per-protocol analysis is proposed with longitudinal matching and inverse probability of censoring weighting approaches to adjust for time-fixed and time-varying confounding. A range of sensitivity analyses will be executed to determine the robustness of results.Ethics and disseminationThe protocol, cohort creation and analysis plan have been classified and approved as a quality improvement initiative by Providence Health Care Research Ethics Board and the Simon Fraser University Office of Research Ethics. Results will be disseminated and shared with local advocacy groups and decision-makers, developers of national and international clinical guidelines, presented at national and international conferences and published in peer-reviewed journals electronically and in print.Keywords: EPIDEMIOLOGY, Substance misuse, STATISTICS & RESEARCH METHODS

Introduction

Opioid agonist treatment (OAT), such as methadone or buprenorphine/naloxone, for people with opioid use disorder (OUD) is classified as a WHO Essential Medicine1 and reduces harm across multiple outcomes, while also increasing engagement in care for other comorbidities2,4; However, long-term treatment engagement remains difficult,5,7 and a potential hindrance to retention is the requirement of daily supervised ingestion for OAT medication options, such as methadone.8,10 While recently updated guidelines in British Columbia (BC), Canada recommend take-home doses can be initiated immediately for buprenorphine/naloxone,11 daily supervised ingestion of methadone has historically been recommended to prevent drug diversion and the subsequent risk of drug poisoning for clients and the general population.12,14 In prior guidelines, take-home dosing for buprenorphine/naloxone was always recommended to be granted earlier in the treatment course compared with methadone,12 15 16 partly due to the inclusion of the naloxone component to dissuade non-prescribed use, the faster stabilisation period of buprenorphine/naloxone and the ceiling effect of a partial opioid-agonist on respiratory depression. In contrast, take-home dosing guidelines in the UK, New Zealand and Australia are not differentiated by medication type.17,19 In the USA, methadone has a time-in-treatment requirement that does not apply to buprenorphine/naloxone due to federal regulations for treatment centres.20 Nonetheless, the guidelines note that eligibility for take-home dosing still depends on client characteristics and indications of clinical stability.17,21 Despite BC guidelines reinforcing patient-centred care and non-punitive approaches, take-home doses may confer increased independence by reducing the burden of attending a clinic or pharmacy daily which, in turn, may act as a form of contingency management that provides incentive for treatment adherence.22 23

Current clinical guidelines for OUD in BC recommend that individuals demonstrating clinical and psychosocial stability are eligible to initiate take-home dosing, which is assessed by the ability to attend appointments and the absence of unstable psychiatric comorbidities, severe behavioural issues, severe sedation and high-risk substance use patterns.11 Additionally, to receive methadone take-home doses, urine drug test results to provide evidence of medication adherence for a minimum of 4 weeks are required. BC OUD guidelines otherwise recommend starting with a single take-home dose of methadone per week, with gradual increases possible thereafter.11 In contrast, individuals receiving buprenorphine/naloxone can initiate take-home dosing immediately, with a 1–2 week supply.11 Reassessment of clinical stability requires consideration of individual circumstances before increasing or curtailing take-home doses,11 which can be influenced by restricted pharmacy availability (eg, closed on Sundays).21

International guidelines are largely concordant with BC’s approach (table 1) and suggest buprenorphine/naloxone take-home doses can be provided during induction, apart from Australia and New Zealand where take-home dosing for buprenorphine/naloxone is allowed only after completing induction.18 19 24 25 Methadone take-home dosing had more stringent criteria; 7 out of the 13 provincial and national guidelines we surveyed recommended only 1 day of take-home dosing per week allowed after completing induction and gradual increases thereafter.1117 20 21 26,28 Prior to COVID-19, clinical guidelines from the USA recommended a maximum of one take-home dose per week for methadone, provided only after the first 90 days in treatment, with gradual increases every 90 days thereafter.20 27 29

The COVID-19 pandemic prompted decision-makers to move away from supervised ingestion (given the need to limit human contact), thus providing new evidence on the outcomes of clients receiving OAT take-home dosing. In the USA, federal opioid treatment programmes allowed for the provision of methadone take-home doses lasting up to 28 days.30 After the change in take-home dosing policy, methadone-involved overdose deaths remained stable.31 Three additional studies showed an increase in methadone-involved overdose32 33 and methadone-related exposures reported to poison control centres34; however, two of these studies did not address the overall increase of overdoses that occurred during the same time period.32 34 A study from Oregon reported that take-home OAT dosing was associated with improved retention among clients engaged in treatment for at least 180 days.35 Furthermore, a population-based retrospective cohort study from Ontario concluded that transitioning from daily dispensed methadone to 5 or 6-day take-home doses was associated with lower risks of overdose, OAT discontinuation and treatment interruption.36 Take-home doses that extended to at least 13 days were associated with lower risks of OAT discontinuation and treatment interruption compared with individuals remaining on 5–6-day take-home doses per week.36 Following this updated evidence, a recent synthesis on Canadian guidelines found that increased take-home dose allowances prompted by the COVID-19 pandemic were included in updated guidelines.37 Similarly, although introduced as a temporary measure, the federal policy in the USA that extended the length of take-home doses was established permanently.38

Client perspectives on pre-COVID take-home dosing policies have highlighted the limited access to extended take-home doses and how the requirement of daily attendance at a clinic or pharmacy creates challenges in treatment engagement, interferes with regular employment and impacts the stigma associated with supervised ingestion.8,10 These requirements have also been linked to higher provincial spending as BC provides coverage for OAT and associated expenses, such as daily witnessed ingestion and pharmacy dispensation fees with costs estimated to be over $30 million CAD per year between 2014 and 2020.39 With COVID policies, take-home medications and deliveries allowed for a better life balance, improved treatment goals, reduced travel times35 40 and the ability to self-manage treatment, whereas the inability to access take-home dose flexibility was seen as unjust, burdensome, discriminatory and inequitable.41 Overall, conflicting perspectives on daily supervised ingestion and concerns regarding diversion indicate additional evidence is required to support decision making.

Clinical guidelines for OUD acknowledge that the provision of OAT take-home dosing needs to balance the treatment benefits for clients with the risk of harm to the general population through diverted medication.1517,21 24 A 1995 US committee on methadone diversion concluded that, while the regulatory policies introduced in the 1970s prevented diversion, the benefits of treatment outweighed the risks to public health and public safety.13 Although diversion of OAT medications has been reported as common,42,44 two systematic reviews suggested that diverted medications were most often used to self-manage and self-treat opioid withdrawal.43 44 A Cochrane review published in 2017 suggested it was unknown whether take-home dosing is associated with increased risk of diversion and medication misuse45; this conclusion was based on a single trial classified as very low quality with moderate risk of bias as the study was not clear if the data referred to diversion.42,44 Notably, Lofwall et al highlighted that US rates of diverted buprenorphine were similar to rates for other diverted medications (such as allergy medications and antibiotics), and suggested supervised dosing does not eliminate diversion.42 In contrast, a study from England and Scotland found that annual trends in methadone-related deaths per million daily methadone doses prescribed declined between 1993 and 2008, with the decreases linked to the implementation of supervised dosing policies.46 In BC, 7.4% of illicit drug deaths between 2015 and 2017 had OAT involved, of which 38.5% did not have an active methadone prescription in the 60 days prior to death.47 As such, diverted methadone was implicated in less than 3% of all illicit drug deaths in this timeframe. Further, the BC Coroners Service reported that between July 2020 and December 2022, methadone was detected in only 7% of suspected drug toxicity deaths.48 However, it remains unknown whether this is due to the lack of a direct relationship between methadone and drug overdoses or if it is attributable to restrictive prescribing practices.

We aim to determine the comparative effectiveness of alternative take-home dosing schedules on OAT retention and mortality, as observed in clinical practice in BC, Canada: 2010–2022. This study will generate robust evidence on how lengths of take-home doses affect OAT retention in real-world practice, before and after the introduction of fentanyl into the illicit drug supply, in the interest of improving retention for these life-saving medications.49

Methods

Study setting and data sources

The study will emulate a target trial using a linkage of nine provincial health administrative databases from BC, Canada where residents are required to enrol in the provincial single-payer health insurance plan. We propose a population-level retrospective study among adults initiating OAT. The BC PharmaNet database50 (capturing medication dispensations) will be used to identify OAT dispensations for directly observed doses and OAT take-home doses during the follow-up period. The other databases we will include are the Discharge Abstract Database51 (DAD; records of hospitalisations), Medical Services Plan52 (MSP; physician billing records), client roster (demographic and geographic information for the Ministry’s clients),53 BC Vital Statistics54 (capturing deaths and their underlying cause), BC Provincial Corrections55 (records of entry into incarcerations and releases to community), Perinatal Care Database56 (capturing maternal/infant care and outcomes), National Ambulatory Care Reporting System Database57 (NACRS; capturing emergency department visits) and BC Social Development and Poverty Reduction database58 (capturing social assistance receipt). The databases are deterministically linked based on exact matches of a unique, de-identified individual-level personal health number.

Study population

Individuals 18 years of age or older with no history of cancer or palliative care (online supplemental table A1) who initiated an OAT regimen from 01/01/2010 to 12/31/2022 (or latest available date at the time of analysis) will be included, providing a comprehensive follow-up history from the linked datasets. Those in palliative care or a history of cancer will be excluded due to clinical considerations that may impact take-home dosing, particularly for methadone as it can be used as pain management.59 60 Women currently pregnant at OAT initiation will be excluded from the analysis, as care for pregnant women may differ from the general population with OUD. Individuals who initiate OAT in provincial correctional facilities will also be excluded as they are not eligible for take-home doses during the incarceration period. We will execute both incident user and prevalent new-user study designs to ensure the clinical experience of those accessing OAT in successive attempts is captured in our analyses. The incident user study design requires no prior OAT dispensations (to be confirmed with historical prescription records dating back to 1 January 1996). For prevalent new-user design, we will include subjects who were previously OAT, but we will impose a 30-day washout period (ie, no OAT dispensations received in the 30 days prior to the episode initiation) to ensure take-home dose lengths are not influenced by an automatic resumption of prior take-home doses. OAT dispensations will be identified with PharmaNet by unique drug identification numbers used specifically for OAT and are attached to each specific drug dispensation (online supplemental table A2). Take-home dose length will be identified through the days supplied field in PharmaNet.

Study design

Our aim is to emulate a target trial to determine effectiveness of alternative take-home dosing schedules on OAT discontinuation and all-cause mortality. Our study design and analytical approach will feature a causal per-protocol analysis with longitudinal matching of sequential cohorts to estimate the average treatment effect on the treated (table 2). While our previous protocols for similar analyses implemented a clone-censor-weight approach61,63 (creating one copy of each individual to assign to each strategy and censoring each clone if and when it deviates from its assigned strategy), we have not used this approach here, because our treatment strategies are known at time zero.

Time zero will be defined as the date of first ≥2 day take-home dose, among episodes meeting inclusion criteria. Each individual receiving their first take-home dose will be matched to an individual on daily ingestion based on the equivalent time since OAT initiation, who will have the same time zero. This time-based matching aims to ensure that those who receive take-homes much earlier in treatment are matched with a control that is similarly stable early in treatment and will create separate time-based trials (figure 1).

A minimum of a 2-day take-home dose requirement is specified as lengths of 1 day may be required based on pharmacy availability in rural areas with closures on Sundays, and not due to clients meeting eligibility criteria for take-home dosing.64 We will require take-home dose initiation to be within the first 26 or 52 weeks of OAT engagement, for buprenorphine/naloxone or methadone respectively, to ensure there are available individuals for matching (described in the Longitudinal matching section) to a daily dosing control. Though there are no guidelines that outline the maximum time prior to take-home dose initiation, the decision is aligned with a similar cut-off imposed in a paired analysis that aims to estimate the effect of timing for initiating take-home doses.63 Additionally, the cut-off will be altered in sensitivity analyses (detailed in the Sensitivity analyses section).

The study will be executed using a weekly counting process, following individuals from time zero until the primary or secondary outcome or otherwise meeting exclusion criteria, medication switch (as analyses are stratified based on medication because treatment strategies and covariates such as dosage depend on medication type), taper initiation (defined as two consecutive daily dose decreases without an intervening increase) or end of follow-up (31 December 2022). All analyses will be stratified according to the type of medication received at OAT initiation. OAT episodes initiated on slow-release oral morphine, first introduced in BC as a medication option in 2017,15 will be included if sufficient sample sizes are available. Individuals initiated on injectable forms of OAT, introduced in 2016, will be excluded as the intensity of the medication does not allow the flexibility of take-home doses.

Primary treatment strategies

Our proposed primary treatment strategies are the duration of OAT take-home doses assigned at time zero (take-home dose initiation). We aim to compare strategies which differ with respect to the length of take-home doses provided. We will consider strategies with take-home dosing of 2–5 days, 6 days or >6 days, with a control strategy defined as daily dosing. We will include these strategies given that the length of OAT take-home dosing is not provincially or federally regulated in BC and treatment schedules could extend beyond guideline recommendations. Sensitivity analyses will include different strategy definitions and lengths of take-home doses, as well as estimation strategies which allow for increased take-home dose durations over time that may depend on individual characteristics.

Primary outcomes

Our primary outcomes are time to OAT discontinuation and all-cause mortality. Discontinuation will be defined as a break in prescribed doses (ie, the last date for which medications were prescribed, rather than the last dispensation date) lasting at least 5 days for methadone and at least 6 days for buprenorphine/naloxone, consistent with clinical guidelines recommending reversion to starting doses after these durations.15 These gap times will be included in the outcome measurement, and as analyses are stratified based on medication, these gap times will be equivalent for all outcomes. Individuals hospitalised while on OAT will be assumed to be given their medications while in hospital as inpatient dispensations are unavailable. As such, OAT dosage and take-home durations will be treated with a last observation carried forward approach to account for unavailable dispensations during hospitals. All-cause and overdose-related acute care use will be considered as secondary outcomes, observed through hospitalisations or emergency department visits (online supplemental table A3).

Statistical analysis

The analytical approach will include longitudinal matching to obtain a matched control group of individuals receiving daily dosing that is similar temporally and in terms of their clinical and sociodemographic characteristics. Both time-dependent propensity score and high-dimensional propensity score (hdPS) matching will be used to control for measured factors that may influence the selection into take-home dosing schedules. Proxy dimensions in the hdPS analysis will be selected from hospital procedure codes, diagnosis codes from acute care or physician billing records and medication dispensations within the twelve months prior to time zero.

A per-protocol analysis entails strict adherence to the assigned treatment strategy, with censorship after 14 consecutive days with no take-home dosing (ie, reversion to daily dosing), at initiation of take-home dosing for those assigned to daily dosing or a change in take-home dosing length outside of the assigned treatment strategy. Requiring less than 14 consecutive days between take-home dosing will capture those who receive intermittent take-home doses, while still censoring those who have a reversion to daily dosing.

Longitudinal matching

Longitudinal matching will be implemented with sequential cohort propensity score matching using time-varying week-specific propensities65,68 to identify the control group to ensure that time zero for the control group is matched to the treatment strategy based on the time since OAT initiation and calendar year. This will ensure time on OAT, a major factor in take-home dosing eligibility, does not create immortal time bias among those who receive take-home dosing. Both those who are receiving take-home dosing and the matched controls will require no prior take-home dosing during the current OAT episode. Those who have not yet initiated a take-home dose will be eligible to be selected as a daily dosing control (figure 1). We will match with covariates (selection process described below) to ensure those who received their first take-home doses are otherwise comparable to individuals who may be eligible but have not yet initiated take-home dosing. The time scale will be measured on a weekly basis and use the entire covariate history up to the current week to estimate weekly propensities. For each week interval from OAT initiation, we will use both propensity scores and hdPS to estimate the weekly probability of receiving a take-home dose of any length and one-to-one optimal matching without replacement for each week, within a maximum calliper width equal to 0.2 of the SD of the logit of the estimated propensity.69 If sample sizes are sufficient, we will consider two-to-one matching. Overall, this estimation approach will create pseudo-trials for each week based on individuals’ time since OAT initiation (figure 1). These pseudo-trials will be combined to estimate a naïve pooled treatment effect, controlling for the time since OAT initiation for each pseudo-trial. To assess covariate balance, standardised differences will be reported between those receiving take-home doses and those selected as the daily dosing controls across all pseudo-trials.

Per-protocol analysis

For the per-protocol analysis, individuals will be censored if they deviate from the assigned strategy, allowing for a 14-day grace period. This will induce informative censoring (ie, post baseline selection bias), and inverse-probability of censoring weights will be implemented to adjust for this potential bias. These censoring weights will be estimated as the product of the probabilities of remaining adherent to one’s assigned treatment strategy at each week through a pooled logistic regression model70:

Here, Nk is an indicator of censoring due to non-adherence at week k, A0 is the assigned treatment at time zero, Ck is an indicator of administrative censoring or deviation from eligibility criteria at week k, Sk and Pk are different groups of time varying factors at week k and V is the vector of baseline covariates. Similarly, an additional pooled logistic regression model will estimate the probability at each week k of censoring due to deviation from eligibility criteria, including: medication switching, incarceration, pregnancy, cancer diagnosis and palliative care initiation. The two sets of inverse-probability of censorship weights will be multiplied together for a final weight.

The assumptions for inverse-probability of censorship weighting to correct for informative censoring introduced by non-adherence include: (1) no uncontrolled confounding (exchangeability); (2) correct model specification within the model to derive the weights; (3) positivity (the probability of adhering to the assigned treatment strategy is non-zero at all follow-up times and for each client) and (4) consistency (the observed outcome under a treatment strategy is identical to what would have been observed had we assigned patients to the treatment strategy).70 71 We will investigate the stability of the weights by plotting the distribution. If there are extreme values after truncation at the 99th percentile, we will report on the weights but will not present the final analysis results for these models.

For the primary and secondary outcomes, we will present hazard ratios that will be approximated by ORs obtained from the naïve pooled sequential cohorts with linear and quadratic terms for the time since OAT initiation, estimated by a weighted pooled logistic regression model with inverse probability of censoring weights and sandwich estimator for robust variance72 to control for weighted observations and clustering of observations within individuals.

Covariate selection

While the assumption of no uncontrolled confounding cannot be verified in observational settings, we aim to adjust for all potential confounders available within our linked database,73 after carefully identifying potential colliders (a covariate that is influenced by both treatment strategy and outcome) and mediators (a covariate lying on the causal pathway between treatment strategy and outcome) via construction of a directed acyclic graph (figure 2). We will otherwise survey the clinical advisory committee for this project on prognostic factors that influence their treatment decision to provide take-home doses. We previously identified covariates by conducting a systematic literature review to identify factors associated with OAT retention.74 We propose to augment this listing with additional variables available in our linked administrative data which were hypothesised to influence both take-home dosing schedules and OAT retention, while also controlling for changes in policy and practice following the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. The additional measures proposed will include variables to capture the prescriber-client relationship such as percentage of other non-OAT billing records within the past 12 months attributed to same prescriber (categorical), and the size of the clinic the prescriber practices within (based on number of other prescribers at the clinic). Additionally, to adjust for confounding due to client instability, we will include measurements on OAT interruptions and housing stability defined through presence of International Classification of Diseases-9/10 codes for unstable housing or no fixed address indicated in social assistance receipt. All time-varying covariates will be updated weekly. Further, as the follow-up period extends past 17 March 2020, we will adjust for COVID-19 restrictions using the COVID-19 stringency index developed by the Bank of Canada. We will otherwise include three time-varying covariates capturing healthcare interventions introduced during COVID-19: receipt of OAT by delivery; concurrent receipt of prescribed safer supply (indication of both available within the PharmaNet database); and use of virtual care (available within the MSP database). Depending on the duration of the interruption, an individual may have their take-home dosing privileges revoked or delay eligibility. As such, we included the duration of total interruptions within a given week of follow-up as a time-varying covariate.

Sensitivity analyses

We propose to execute sensitivity analyses by restricting the study population, study timeline, altering treatment strategies and outcome definitions as well as applying an alternative estimation strategy to account for time-dependent treatment strategies (ie, take-home dose durations increasing over time) as a robustness check against the primary model (table 3). As disruptions in care and policy changes prompted by the COVID-19 pandemic prompted increased take-home dosing flexibility, we will include a sensitivity analysis that will restrict follow up to 17 March 2020 and will further restrict the study timeline to between 17 March 2020 and 31 December 2022 to confirm how impacts of take-home dosing schedules may have changed due to more flexible policies. Since methadone guidelines suggest individuals need to be stabilised (ie, having completed induction) before receiving take-home doses, we will include a sensitivity analysis for methadone initiation that will consider the first take-home dose after completed induction (defined as the end of 2 weeks with no increase in daily dosage) as time zero. For individuals to meet eligibility criteria in this sensitivity analysis, we will require no take-home doses prior to completing induction. Finally, as guidelines typically recommend starting with one take-home dose and slowly increasing the number of take-home doses over time, we will include an additional methodological strategy using a marginal structural model that will be able to handle the dynamic nature of take-home dose increases and can provide estimates for several specific dynamic strategies. One dynamic strategy that will be estimated follows the timing and length recommendations in the BC clinical guidelines.64 That is, a treatment strategy where a single take-home dose is provided after 4 weeks on OAT with increasing additional non-consecutive take-home doses during weeks 6 and 8. Consecutive take-home doses will be allowed starting week 13, starting with 4 increased to 5 and 6 on week 15 and 17, respectively. Any post hoc additions to this protocol will be identified as such in final reports.

Ethics and dissemination

As part of the response to the provincial opioid overdose public health emergency, databases were made available by the BC Ministries of Health and Mental Health and Addiction to the research team. The study is classified as a quality improvement initiative. Simon Fraser University Office of Research Ethics and the Providence Health Care Research Institute determined the analysis meets criteria for exemption per Article 2.5 of the 2018 Tri-Council Policy Statement: Ethical Conduct for Research Involving Humans.75 The study will follow the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for study reporting.76 Additionally, we will administer the ‘Risk of Bias in Non-Randomised Studies of Interventions’ tool to a multidisciplinary scientific advisory committee for ex post evaluation. Results will be disseminated to decision-makers and local advocacy groups alongside national and international clinical guideline developers, presented at international conferences, and published in peer-reviewed journals electronically and in print.

Patient and public involvement

The conception of this study was influenced by prior engagement with local advocacy organisations of people who have accessed OAT and people who use drugs.77 No patients were explicitly involved in the design of this study. Qualitative feedback on this and other related objectives outlined in the parent grant R01DA050629 was used to prioritise this analysis given the potential impact on client engagement. Findings will be shared with local advocacy groups following completion of the analysis.
