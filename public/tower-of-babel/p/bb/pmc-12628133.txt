Autograft-matching wireless bioelectronic conduits: Magneto-electric coupling enabled taurine metabolism activation for peripheral nerve repair
Authors: Anmin Wang, Wenjing Song, Wentai Guo, Baolei Huang, Xingqiang Zhu, Hailin Zhang, Wei Zhai, Ye Tian, Li Ren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Peripheral nerve injuries remain a clinical challenge due to the limitations of autografts and unstable electrical signals in existing bioelectronic therapies. It is necessary to develop innovative strategies to achieve wireless, controllable peripheral neural regeneration (PNR). In this study, we developed a magneto-electric coupling-driven electroactive nerve guidance conduits (PCLG/AgNF NGCs) with a moving magnetic field (MMF) for PNR with wireless magneto-electric coupling electrical stimulation (MECES). PCLG/AgNF displayed high conductivity (25.48 ± 3.77 S/cm) and wireless controllability of generating electrical pulses (16.67 ± 0.47 μA to 475.7 ± 9.71 μA) with an MMF. The MECES produced by PCLG/AgNF with the MMF significantly promoted cell proliferation, cell migration, and upregulated the expression of β3-tubulin, neurofilament heavy chain and growth-associated protein 43, compared to PCLG/AgNF and MMF used individually. Mechanistically, we identified that PCLG/AgNF with the MMF activated the metabolism of taurine and hypotaurine corroborated by elevated intracellular taurine, which is crucial for MECES mediated repair processes. In a rat peripheral nerve defect model, the PCLG/AgNF NGCs with the MMF showed promising results in nerve regrowth, myelination, and functional recovery, performing comparably to autografts. This strategy offers PCLG/AgNF NGCs as a wireless, controllable, precision-enabled approach and provides novel insights for the effective PNR.

Introduction

Peripheral nerve injuries (PNIs) typically arise from mechanical trauma, compression, or ischemic damage, resulting in neurological deficits ranging from sensory impairment to motor paralysis. Current clinical strategies predominantly involve microsurgical interventions and autologous nerve grafting, with the latter remaining the gold standard for repairing extended nerve defects due to its established efficacy; however, challenges persist, including donor site morbidity, procedural risks, and incomplete functional restoration [1].

Recent advances in therapeutic strategies for PNIs focus on applying nerve guidance conduits (NGCs) or combining them with physical cues [2]. However, some repair strategies have overlooked the intrinsic electrical activity of neural tissues. Conductive NGCs have garnered significant attention for their potential to enhance neurogenesis and functional recovery. Such NGCs depend on a passive electrical stimulation mechanism, which suffers from inherent limitations including weak stimulation intensity and insufficient controllability [3].

Beyond NGCs, electrical stimulation has emerged as both a promising adjunct therapy for PNIs rehabilitation and a driving force in tissue engineering advancements, significantly propelling research progress in nerve regeneration [4]. Conventional electrical stimulation approaches require the surgical implantation of devices, which may lead to complications or necessitate secondary surgical interventions [5]. Recently, wireless technologies has emerged as an innovative approach with potential for promoting neural repair. The piezoelectric effect enables the conversion of kinetic energy from voluntary and autonomic muscle movements into electrical energy and is commonly combined with ultrasound devices for active treatment [6]. Compared to traditional devices that rely on external power sources, piezoelectric technology combined with NGCs operates independently, thereby reducing infection risks and simplifying surgical procedures, while continuously generating microcurrents to stimulate nerve regeneration and functional recovery [7]. Additionally, wireless neural stimulation technology based on ultrasonic-electric conversion can further implement active and deep pulsed electrical stimulation as required by treatment [8]. However, piezoelectric generators have limitations that the generated electrical signals without ultrasound devices are often random and unstable, with limited output power, which fails to meet precise control requirements [9].

In contrast, the magneto-electric coupling electrical stimulation (MECES) technology presents significant advantages. Its magnetic fields can penetrate human tissues more deeply, enabling precise stimulation of deep tissue; additionally, it offers stable signals and strong controllability, allowing for precise adjustment of stimulation parameters based on therapeutic needs [10]. Interestingly, multiple studies have indicated that magnetic fields play a positive role in nerve repair as well [11]. Ping Wu et al. developed a biodegradable hyaluronic acid conductive hydrogel based on germanium phosphide nanosheets. This system generates wireless in situ electrical stimulation driven by an alternating magnetic field, successfully promoting motor function recovery and nerve regeneration in rats with spinal cord injury [12]. Yu-Jen Lu et al. developed an injectable conductive microporous hydrogel decorated with gold nanoyarn balls. This system generates wireless in situ electrical stimulation driven by a high-frequency alternating magnetic field, significantly promoting angiogenesis and neurological functional recovery in mice with traumatic brain injury [10b]. In summary, the development of a NGCs with good biocompatibility that can wirelessly provide regular, sustained, and controllable electrical signals through MECES holds significant importance for promoting peripheral nerve regeneration. This innovative technology will not only address existing methodological limitations but also offers new solutions to complex neural repair demands.

Based on MECES strategy, we developed a wireless stimulation system including 3D-printable PCLG/AgNF with enhanced electrical conductivity and a magnetic generation device. To achieve this, highly conductive silver nanoflakes (AgNFs) were incorporated into biodegradable polycaprolactone (PCL) through a 3D printing process, resulting in a PCLG/AgNF with significantly improved electrical conductivity. The designed PCLG/AgNF exhibited excellent biocompatibility and mechanical properties while maintaining high conductivity. Furthermore, the PCLG/AgNF demonstrated superior magneto-electric coupling capabilities, enabling it to generate regular and sustained electric pulse signals with a moving magnetic field (MMF). Importantly, these electric pulses could be precisely regulated via the external magnetic generation device. The effect of the PCLG/AgNF with an MMF was demonstrated through in vitro PC12 cell proliferation, migration, and differentiation experiments, as well as in vivo rat sciatic nerve defect repair model (Fig. 1). Furthermore, the promoting effects of MECES, MMF, and PCLG/AgNF were evaluated separately in this system. Materials and methods

Preparation of PCLG/AgNF electroactive nerve conduit

The PCLG/AgNF electroactive composite scaffolds were prepared by a pneumatic 3D printer with the method of solvent evaporation. Briefly, 2 g commercial AgNFs (DK101-3, Beijing Deke Daojin Science and Technology Co. Ltd.) were dispersed into ethanol (50 mL), and KI aqueous solution (0.01 M, 4 mL) was added to the Suspension to vortex 20 min. After the vortex, the AgNFs were cleaned with deionized water and ethanol before dried in vacuum to obtain AgNFs without oleic acid. Then, AgNFs without oleic acid (0.48 g) and commercial silver nanoplates (0.32 g) were added into dichloromethane solution (5 mL). After 30 min sonication, 1 g PCL pellets (Mn = 80000–100000, Aladdin, Shanghai) were added to the suspension. The suspension was vortexed for 15 min at least to disperse it evenly to get a 3D printing ink. A pneumatic, dual extruder 3D printer was used to manufacture electroactive composite scaffolds. The 3D printing ink was loaded into a polypropylene syringe with a 32 G stainless steel needle to generate PCL/AgNF electroactive composite scaffolds. The PCL/AgNF electroactive composite scaffolds were successively soaked in NaOH solution (2 M) for 6 h, followed by treatment with 10 % (w/v) gelatin solution for 24 h. After vacuum freeze drying treatment, the PCLG/AgNF electroactive composite scaffolds were rolled up. More detailed printing parameters are listed in Table S2.

Preparation of PCLG nerve conduit

Briefly, 1 g PCL pellets (Aladdin Mn = 80000–100000, Shanghai) were added to dichloromethane solution (5 mL). After 15 min vortex, PCL solution was loaded into a polypropylene syringe with a 32 G stainless steel needle to generate PCL scaffolds. The PCL scaffolds were successively soaked in NaOH solution (2 M) for 6 h, followed by treatment with 10 % (w/v) gelatin solution for 24 h. After vacuum freeze drying treatment, the PCLG scaffolds were rolled up. More detailed printing parameters are listed in Table S2.

Morphology and element characterization of the composite scaffolds

The morphology (surface and cross-section) of the composite scaffolds was observed by SEM (Zeiss Merlin, German). The elemental distribution on the surface of the PCLG/AgNF scaffolds were determined by Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy. The samples were sputter-coated with gold for 60 s and imaged at 5 kV.

Morphology characterization and thermal analysis of the commercial AgNFs

The morphology of commercial AgNFs was observed by SEM and TEM (Thermo Fisher Talos F200x, America). Thermal analysis of commercial AgNFs and commercial AgNFs treated with potassium iodide solution was performed using a TG-DSC synchronous thermal analyzer (NETZSCH STA 449 F5, German).

Mechanical testing

The mechanical properties of the composite materials were examined by cyclic compression tests and tension tests at room temperature using a universal testing machine (Instron 5967, America) and a 10 g weight was suspended through a 4-0 surgical suture on the PCLG/AgNF composite scaffold to verify its suture resistance. The experiments were repeated three times.

Electrical conductivity measurement

The conductivity of the composite materials was determined using a four-point probe conductivity conductivity meter (NUOLEI XINDA RTS-9, China) in form of composite films. The experiments were repeated three times.

Magnetic flux distribution measurement

The magnetic flux density vertical distribution of the periphery of a long bar NdFeB magnet (75 mm ∗ 15 mm ∗ 10 mm) was determined using a gaussmeter (TUNKIA TD8650, China). The experiments were repeated five times.

Response system for magneto-electric signals

Magnetic generation device: After fixing the NdFeB magnet to the end of a PTFE stirring bar, an MMF was generated by the operating numerical control stirrer (MIULAB SP-18, China) fixing the stirring bar. Magnetoelectric signal collection platform: The magnetoelectric signal collection platform was fabricated from a polyethylene (PE) sheet, upon which symmetrically distributed conductive copper tape was applied along the sides in the height direction. The top end of the conductive copper tape on the PE sheet was connected to a PCLG/AgNF composite scaffold, while the bottom end was connected to the electrochemical workstation (Ingsens IGS6030, China). Magnetoelectric signal collection method: the scaffold fixed on the magnetoelectric signal collection platform was placed vertically under the periphery of the long bar magnet before the operation of magnetic generation device. Finally, the magnetoelectric signal was collected by the chronoamperometry method of the electrochemical workstation after starting the numerical control stirrer. The experiments were repeated three times.

Cell culture

L929 cell culture: L929 were cultured in complete culture media (DMEM containing 10 % fetal Bovine Serum and 1 % Pen-Strep) and media was refreshed every 2–3 days. Upon 80 % confluence, they were either further passaged or used for experiments. PC12 cell culture: Rat pheochromocytoma cell line (PC-12) was cultured in complete medium supplemented with horse serum (DMEM containing 10 % horse serum, 5 % fetal Bovine Serum and 1 % Pen-Strep) and media was refreshed every 2–3 days. Upon 80 % confluence, they were either further passaged or used for experiments.

PC12 cell proliferation and Migration Assays

Based on whether PC12 cells are co-cultured with PCLG/AgNF scaffolds under conditions with or without an MMF, four experimental groups were established as follows: 1) Blank group: PC12 cells are not co-cultured with PCLG/AgNF scaffolds and no MMF is present. 2) Blank + MMF group: PC12 cells are not co-cultured with PCLG/AgNF scaffolds but an MMF is present. 3) PCLG/AgNF group: PC12 cells are co-cultured with PCLG/AgNF scaffolds and no MMF is present. 4) PCLG/AgNF + MMF group: PC12 cells are co-cultured with PCLG/AgNF scaffolds and an MMF is present. Proliferation Assay: The prepared PCLG/AgNF scaffolds were placed in the upper chamber of a 24-well transwell system (PET membrane, pore size 8.0 μm), with the scaffolds closely attached to the PET membrane. PC12 cells were seeded in the well plate at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well, and each well was filled with 600 μL of complete medium supplemented with horse serum. The cells were then incubated overnight at 37 °C for 12 h to allow cell adhesion. Subsequently, an MMF was applied for 1 h per day for 3 consecutive days, and the fresh medium was replaced every other day. CCK-8 assay was carried out on day 1 and day 3. Migration Assay: PC12 cells were seeded in the upper chamber of a 12-well transwell system (PET membrane, pore size 8.0 μm) at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. After 2 h of cell adhesion, the PCLG/AgNF scaffolds were placed in the lower chamber, followed by a 1-h MMF. The cell migration was observed by crystal violet staining 24 h later. The experiments were repeated three times.

PC12 cell differentiation experiment and gene expression analysis

PC12 cells were seeded into low-adhesion plates and co-cultured them with PCLG scaffolds or PCLG/AgNF scaffolds with/without MMF conditions. The scaffolds were placed in a 12-well low-adhesion plate. Then, 2 mL of complete medium supplemented with horse serum was added to each well, and 2 × 105 cells were seeded per well. An MMF was applied for 1 h per day or not. The culture medium was replaced every 2 days. The cells in the wells were collected on days 1, 3, and 5 for qPCR detection. After inoculation for 1,3,5 days, PC12 were collected and lysed using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) to extract RNA using a HiPure Total RNA Micro Kit (Magen). The RNA quality and concentration were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). A PrimeScript RT reagent kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa Biotechnology, Japan) was used to reverse transcribe the same amount of RNA into cDNA according to the manufacturer's protocol. The primers for the target gene are listed in Table S3. Real-time PCR detection was performed using SYBR Green reagents (GeneCopoeia) on an RT-PCR instrument (QuantStudio 6 Flex, Life Technologies) with 40 amplification cycles. Cells cultured on PCLG scaffolds without an MMF were used as a reference for comparison. GAPDH was used as an endogenous control for normalization of the expression levels of genes using the ΔΔCt method. The experiments were repeated three times.

RNA-seq analysis

The experiment was divided into four groups: PC12 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well (represented as Plate), PC12 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well with an MMF stimulation 1 h per day (represented as Plate + MMF), PC12 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well and the PCLG/AgNF scaffolds were placed in the plates (represented as PCLG/AgNF) and PC12 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well with an MMF stimulation 1 h per day and the PCLG/AgNF scaffolds were placed in the plates (represented as PCLG/AgNF + MMF).

RNA extraction from PC12 ells was used TRIzol™ (Invitrogen™ 15596026) and Direct-zol™ RNA Miniprep (zymo R2050) at the 3rd day. The samples were sent to SequMed Biotech Inc. for RNA-seq analysis. Briefly, RNA purity and concentration were checked using the NanoDrop One (Thermo, USA) and Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer (Thermo, USA). RNA integrity was measured using the Qsep100 (Bioptic, China). Sequencing libraries were generated using the Hieff NGS®Ultima Dual mode mRNA Library Prep Kit (Cat#12309, China). A total amount of 0.2 μg RNA per sample was used as input material for the RNA sample preparations. The first step is to enrich mRNA by Poly(A) method with mRNA Capture Beads (BOX-I 12603-A), after mRNA Purification and Fragmentation, the RNA fragments are concentrated and the homogeneity. Followed by Synthesis of the 1st strand cDNA and the 2nd strand cDNA, 3′ ends adenylated and 5′ ends repaired, dA-tailing, Adapter Ligation, Library Amplication, at last, PCR products were purified (Hieff NGS® DNA Selection Beads (Yeasen Cat#12601) and library quality was assessed on Qubit dsDNA HS Fluorometer (Thermo, USA) and the Bioptic Qsep100 system. The library preparations were sequenced on MGI SEQ-T7 and 150bp paired-end reads were generated. Differential expression analysis of two conditions/groups was performed using the DESeq2 v1.34.0 R package. DESeq2 provide statistical routines for determining differential expression in digital gene expression data using a model based on the negative binomial distribution. The resulting P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini and Hochberg's approach for controlling the false discovery rate. Genes with an adjusted P-value <0.05 found by DESeq2 were assigned as differentially expressed. Enrichment analysis of DEGs and GSEA analysis was implemented by the clusterProfiler v4.2.2 R package. The Gene Ontology (GO) annotation was from the Gene Ontology database and the GO.db v3.14 R package. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway annotation was from KEGG database. GO terms or KEGG pathway with corrected P-value less than 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Taurine content determination in PC12 cells

Taurine concentration in PC12 cells was determined by a rat taurine ELISA kit (Guangzhou Huabin Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). The experiments were repeated three times.

Western blots assay

GAPDH (1:10000, 60004-1-Ig), ADO (1:300, 16479-1-AP) and FMO1 (1:10000, 83156-3-RR) were purchased from Proteintech Group, Inc. Additionally, BAAT (1:500, YN0506) was purchased from ImmunoWay. The extraction of PC12 cells protein was achieved through the lysis utilizing 1 mM PMSF (meilunbio, Dalian, China) and RIPA Lysis Buffer (meilunbio, Dalian, China) in a frigid environment, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 5 min, and subsequent collection of the supernatant. The concentration of the total protein was ascertained using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (KeyGEN Bio TECH, Jiangsu, China). The 10x Sample Buffer in the kit was diluted to 0.1x Sample Buffer. The total volume of each well was 4.5 μL of samples. The 5x Master Mix was fixed to 0.9 μL, and the 0.1x Sample Buffer and protein sample were 3.6 μL in total. The final protein loading concentration was 2 μg/μL. Quantitative protein analysis was conducted employing the Simple Westerns™ System alongside Compass software for the visualization and evaluation of outcomes. The experiments were repeated three times.

In vitro cytocompatibility

The PCLG/AgNF (14 mm ∗ 14 mm) were soaked in ethanol for 24 h and then soaked in sterile PBS for 6 h. After twice washing with sterile PBS, 653 μL of complete culture medium was added to each scaffold, and the scaffolds were soaked at 37 °C for 72 h. The extract was obtained through a 0.22 μm filtration membrane.

L929 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2000 cells per well. After cell adhesion, the cells were cultured with 100 μL of the extract per well for 5 days, and the medium was changed every 2 days. The cell viability was evaluated by a Calcein-AM/PI Double Staining Kit (Dojindo Molecular Technologies) and CCK-8 (Dojindo CK04, Japan) on days 1, 3, and 5. The experiments were repeated three times.

Sciatic nerve repair

Adult male SD rats (7–8 weeks old, weight: 200–250 g) were used as a sciatic nerve defect model. Animal study procedures were approved by the Laboratory Animal Center of South China University of Technology. Animals provided free access to food and water were randomly divided into six experimental groups: the Autograft group (represented as Autograft) (n = 6), the PCLG/AgNF scaffold graft group with an MMF stimulation 1 h per day (defined as PCLG/AgNF + MMF) (n = 6), the PCLG/AgNF scaffold graft group (described as PCLG/AgNF) (n = 6), the PCLG scaffold graft group (described as PCLG) (n = 6), the defect group with an MMF stimulation 1 h per day (described as Defect + MMF) (n = 6) and the defect group (described as Defect) (n = 6). Surgical procedures were performed under anesthesia using 2 % (w/v) pentobarbital sodium solution (40 mg/kg). Under aseptic conditions, the skin and subcutaneous muscle layers in the right hip joint were incised to expose the right sciatic nerve. Nerve fibers were transected at 5 mm proximal to the nerve bifurcation and removed, leaving a 10 mm gap. Next, each end of the transected sciatic nerve was inserted into a 14 mm long individual NGC (n = 6 per group) and then tied to the epineuria sheath using 10-0 nylon. The muscle, subcutaneous layer, and overlying skin were sutured using 4-0 silk. Following resuscitation from anesthesia, rats were housed in their cages as usual. At 4 and 8 weeks, rats were sacrificed using carbon dioxide.

Walking track analysis

To evaluate the recovery of sciatic nerve function in rats of each group, footprint analysis was performed weekly after the operation. Briefly, the hind paws of the rats were painted with black ink. Then, the rats were led to the entrance of a narrow passage (10 cm × 10 cm × 60 cm), and the bottom of the passage was covered with yellow cardboard. The rats were guided to walk towards the exit, and the walking tracks were recorded. The footprints were measured using several parameters according to previous literature. SFI was calculated according to the formula:(1)SFI=‐38.3×(EPL‐NPL)NPL+109.5×(ETS‐NTS)NTS+13.3×(EIT‐NIT)NIT‐8.8where, EPL and NPL are the distances from the heel to the tip of the third toe. ETS and NTS are distances from the first toe to the fifth toe. EIT and NIT represent the distance from the second toe to the fourth toe. Where E represents the operative side and N represents the nonoperative side. The experiments were repeated three times.

Electrophysiological assessment

Electrophysiological assessments were performed to assess functional recovery at week 4 and week 8 after surgery. The RM6240 E/EC multi-channel physiological signal acquisition and processing system (Chengdu Yichuangsi Technology Co., Ltd.) was used to record CMAPs. Specifically, the rat was anesthetized, and the postoperative sciatic nerve was re-exposed. A bipolar electrode connected proximally to the regenerating nerve was stimulated to generate electrical signals. Of note, stimuli were delivered in pulse form (stimulus intensity = 1.0 V, duration = 0.1 ms). The grounding electrode is connected through the rat's skin. Receiving electrodes were inserted into the ipsilateral gastrocnemius muscle to record the CMAPs. The experiments were repeated three times.

Morphological analysis of the regenerated nerves

The regenerated nerve was dissected immediately after the electrophysiological assessment at weeks 4 and 8 post-surgery. H&E, TEM and immunohistochemical staining were used to observe the cross-sectional morphology of regenerated nerves. For H&E staining, all nerve samples were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and cut into sections of 5 μm with a microtome (Leica). Paraffin sections were dewaxed in a dewaxing agent for 10 min, dehydrated in gradient ethanol (absolute → 95 % → 85 % → 75 % ethanol) for 5 min each, then rinsed with running water for 1 min. Stain with Harris hematoxylin for 4 min and rinse thoroughly. Differentiate with 0.8 % hydrochloric acid - alcohol for 2 s, rinse, then blue with lithium carbonate aqueous solution and wash for 2 min. Stain with alcohol-soluble eosin for 20 s, adjust with 95 % ethanol for 5 s, dehydrate with absolute ethanol for 2 min. Clear with a clearing agent, mount with neutral gum, and observe under a microscope. For immunofluorescence staining, sections were incubated with S100 rabbit-derived antibody (1:1000 dilution, Abcam) and β3-tubulin rabbit-derived antibody (1:2000 dilution, Abcam) at 4 °C overnight. The next day, sections were washed with PBS and then incubated with Alexa Fluor 647-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:4000 dilution, Abcam) and Alexa Fluor 488-labeled donkey anti-rabbit IgG (1:400 dilution, ThermoFisher) as secondary antibodies for 45 min. Cell nuclei were counter-stained with DAPI for 10 min. The sections were observed under a fluorescence microscope for fluorescence imaging. For TEM, all nerve samples were fixed with 2.5 % glutaraldehyde, after fixation, tissues were rinsed thrice with 0.1 M PB (pH 7.4) for 15 min each. Subsequently, post-fixation with 1 % OsO4 in 0.1 M PB (pH 7.4) was conducted for 2 h at room temperature, followed by thorough rinsing. Dehydration ensued through a graded ethanol series (30 %–100 %) and acetone, with specific time intervals. Resin infiltration and embedding were executed with precision, involving sequential incubations at 37 °C and overnight polymerization. Ultrathin sectioning (60–70 nm) was achieved using an ultramicrotome, and sections were mounted on formvar-coated grids. Staining with 2 % uranium acetate and lead citrate was performed, adhering to strict protocols to avoid light and CO2 interference. Finally, specimens were observed and imaged under TEM. The diameter of axon and thickness of the myelin sheath were quantitatively analyzed using Image J software.

Statistics of regenerated vessels

On the obtained H&E-stained sections of regenerated sciatic nerves, a region with an area of 0.2 mm2 was randomly selected, and the regenerated vessels with a diameter of no less than 10 μm in the selected region were counted. The experiment was repeated 3 times in total.

Gastrocnemius muscle wet weight and histological evaluation

After sacrificing the rats, the gastrocnemius muscles on the surgical side of the rats were harvested at 4 and 8 weeks after implantation. The harvested muscles (n = 3) were washed in PBS and weighed using an electronic balance. Then, the harvested muscles were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 5 μm thick sections. Representative sections were stained with Masson's trichrome. The images of muscle tissue were captured under a microscope, and three random fields of view were selected for each group. The Image J software was used to analyze the degree of muscle fibrosis to calculate the average percentage of muscle fibers in the gastrocnemius muscle. The experiments were repeated three times.

The samples were removed at a predetermined point in time, washed with water to remove excess salt, and weighed after drying. Serum biochemical analysis

After sacrifice, 3 mL of blood was collected from SD rats via cardiac puncture. The collected blood samples were rested in a 4 °C refrigerator for 2 h. Finally, the blood was centrifuged at 2000g for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected and analyzed using a fully automatic biochemical analyzer (HITACHI 3500, Japan). Liver and kidney observation

At week 20, H&E staining was used to observe the cross - sectional morphology of the livers and kidneys of rats implanted with PCLG/AgNF nerve conduits. For H&E staining, the samples were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and cut into sections of 5 μm with a microtome.

Statistical analysis

The data presented herein are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless stated otherwise. Each experiment was repeated at least three times. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA with multiple comparison analysis using Tukey's post hoc test, and p-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant (∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001). All statistical tests were generated using GraphPad Prism 9.0.0 software.

Results and discussion

Fabrication and characterization of highly conductive PCLG/AgNF

In this study, a highly conductive PCLG/AgNF capable of responding to magnetoelectric coupling signals was engineered. Unlike conventional nerve conduits designed with drug-loading capabilities or other structural modifications, the present work introduces silver nanomaterials into PCL to endow the construct with electroactivity [13]. Silver nanomaterials exhibit superior electrical conductivity, biocompatibility, and antimicrobial characteristics, with silver nanofibers (AgNFs) demonstrating geometrically advantageous high aspect ratios. PCL exhibits excellent biocompatibility, printability, and controlled degradability, aligning with the long-term repair demands of peripheral nerve regeneration processes [14]. 3D printing technology enables precise regulation of the internal porosity, pore size, and connectivity of scaffolds to mimic the microenvironment of natural tissues, thus being widely applied in the field of tissue engineering [15]. Consequentially, through 3D bioprinting technology, we strategically incorporated these commercial AgNFs (Figs. S1–S2) as conductive fillers into PCL matrices, thereby engineering functional PCL/AgNF composite materials. In addition, gelatin typically has the characteristics of a natural source and good cell compatibility [16]. To achieve high conductivity NGCs, the formation of a continuous conductive network is crucial. The evolution of physical properties with filler volume fractions in composites, as governed by percolation theory, exhibits distinct patterns [17]. Below the percolation threshold, fillers are dispersed without forming a connected network, leading to gradual changes in properties such as electrical and thermal conductivity. As the volume fraction approaches the threshold, fillers begin to form a conductive network, resulting in abrupt performance changes—for example, a sharp increase in electrical conductivity. Beyond the threshold, further increases in filler content enhance material performance. However, the rate of improvement slows due to the network's near-complete formation. In our system (PCL/AgNF composite materials), the theory could be directly described as the “sudden transition of the conductive network from an isolated to a connected state” and the non-linear growth of conductivity with silver content (Fig. 2c). By incorporating mathematical models of percolation theory, the behavior of high-aspect-ratio AgNFs (with diameter-to-thickness ratios ranging from 10 to 30) were analyzed. These flakes preferentially form “bonds” through edge or face interactions rather than isolated point contacts, leading to a “bond percolation” regime. Compared to point percolation involving spherical particles, the percolation threshold for such high-aspect-ratio AgNFs is generally lower and exhibits significant transition characteristics [18]. Near the percolation critical region, the variation in conductivity can be described by a power-law relation:(4)σ(ϕ)=A(ϕ‐ϕc)ˆτwhere σ represents the conductivity of composite material, ϕ is the volume fraction of AgNFs, ϕc is the percolation threshold, A is a material-dependent constant, and τ is the critical exponent [19]. Based on this principle, we firstly prepared the 3D printing inks with mass ratios of AgNFs to PCL at 40 %, 60 %, 80 %, 100 %, and 120 %. Using these inks, we fabricated corresponding composite materials: PCL/AgNF0.4, PCL/AgNF0.6, PCL/AgNF0.8, PCL/AgNF1.0, and PCL/AgNF1.2. Subsequently, the electrical properties of these composites were systematically characterized using a four-point probe conductivity tester, yielding conductivities of 0.32 ± 0.02 S/cm, 0.96 ± 0.17 S/cm, 1.26 ± 0.16 S/cm, 6.40 ± 1.22 S/cm, and 19.45 ± 4.81 S/cm, respectively (Fig. 2c). The results revealed that conductivity remained relatively constant as the mass ratio increased from 40 % to 80 %, whereas it exhibited a sharp increase when the mass ratio was raised from 80 % to 120 %. These observations suggested that the percolation threshold lies between 80 % and 120 %.

Typically, the surface of commercial AgNFs is coated with a layer of long-chain fatty acid salts (confirmed to be oleate salts through verification with the supplier), which serves to prevent cold welding during processing and hinders electrical conductivity between adjacent silver particles in composite materials. To enhance the conductivity of the AgNFs, we treated the commercial AgNFs with 0.01 M potassium iodide to eliminate the oleic acid coating. The complete removal of the oleic acid was confirmed through thermal analysis, as demonstrated in Fig. S3 [13b]. Subsequently, using these uncoated AgNFs, we formulated five additional inks with weight ratios of coated vs. uncoated AgNFs at 10:0, 8:2, 6:4, 4:6, and 2:8 while maintaining a constant conductive filler to PCL ratio of 80 %. These formulations were subjected to printing via a 3D printer, with the resulting groups named as PCL/AgNF0.8–10:0, PCL/AgNF0.8–8:2, PCL/AgNF0.8–6:4, PCL/AgNF0.8–4:6, and PCL/AgNF0.8–2:8. However, during the printing process, nozzle clogging issues were encountered with the PCL/AgNF0.8–4:6 and PCL/AgNF0.8–2:8 formulations. This was attributed to the reduced oleic acid content leading to rapid AgNFs aggregation and sedimentation (Fig. S4). Consequently, further investigation into these two groups was discontinued. To evaluate the performance of the remaining three formulations, conductivity tests (n = 3) were conducted. The measured conductivities were 1.26 ± 0.16 S/cm, 11.01 ± 4.22 S/cm, and 26.99 ± 2.27 S/cm respectively (Fig. 2d). These results indicate that the electrical conductivity of the composite material increases progressively with the augmented proportion of uncoated AgNFs. Notably, the PCL/AgNF0.8–6:4 formulation exhibited the highest conductivity. This enhancement was attributed to the increased electrical performance of AgNFs resulting from removal of the lubrication layer, despite a possible slight reduction in filler dispersity.

To achieve notable MECES signals in specific devices, it is imperative for the composite materials to possess high electrical conductivity. Given this requirement, PCL/AgNF0.8–6:4 was selected for further experimentation and abbreviated its name to PCL/AgNF for simplicity. Finally, we processed the PCL/AgNF by subjecting it to alkaline treatment, followed by gelatin soaking and freeze-drying, thereby fabricating a PCLG/AgNF. It is noteworthy that although the gelatin coating might potentially influence electrical conductivity, experimental data revealed that the conductivity remained at a level of 25.48 ± 3.77 S/cm (Fig. 2e).

To systematically investigate the structural and electrical properties of PCLG/AgNF, their surface and cross-sectional morphologies were characterized by using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The results revealed that AgNFs were uniformly embedded in the polymer matrix both on the surface and within the cross-section. Energy dispersive spectroscopy mapping further demonstrated a uniform distribution of silver elements throughout the scaffold surface, which is critical for achieving stable and excellent electrical properties (Fig. 2f–h). As shown in Fig. 2i, the PCLG/AgNF exhibited consistent electrical conductivity under various deformation conditions. To evaluate the mechanical performance, we fabricated dumbbell-shaped specimens from the materials and tested their stress-strain curves using a universal mechanical tester. Compared to PCL surface-treated with gelatin (PCLG) strips, the incorporation of AgNFs into PCLG significantly enhanced Young's modulus by 59.93 %–113.81 ± 6.26 MPa (Fig. S5). This improvement in mechanical strength is primarily attributed to the role of AgNFs as reinforcing agents, which not only enhances material integrity but also impart excellent suture resistance (Fig. 2j) [20]. Such enhanced mechanical properties are particularly important for neural conduit applications, where maintaining a stable hollow tubular structure is critical for guiding regenerative nerve growth. The compressive behavior of PCLG/AgNF conduits were further characterized by performing 100 compression cycles perpendicular to the lumen direction. Despite undergoing 100 compression cycles, the PCLG/AgNF conduits retained approximately 91 % of its initial compressive stress without experiencing significant structural damage (Fig. S6). This excellent shape retention capability is crucial for long-term in vivo applications, where maintaining a stable tubular structure is essential for guiding nerve regeneration.

Electrical signal acquisition via magneto-electric coupling in PCLG/AgNF

From a microscopic perspective, when a conductor is placed in an MMF, the free charges within the conductor are subjected to Lorentz forces. Under the influence of these forces, free charges experience directed movement within the conductor. Typically, free electrons are driven by the Lorentz force toward one end of the conductor, leading to an accumulation of negative charges at that end and a corresponding positive charge deficit at the opposite end. This creates an electric potential difference across the two ends of the conductor, known as dynamic electromotive force. If the circuit is closed, an induced current flows due to this electromotive force (Fig. 3a) [21]. The magnitude of dynamic electromotive force is determined by:(5)E=BLνsinθwhere E represents the dynamic electromotive force, B is the magnetic field strength, L is the effective length of the conductor, v is the velocity of the conductor relative to the magnetic field and θ is the angle between the conductor's motion direction and the magnetic field [21b]. From Equation (5), it is evident that the dynamic electromotive force depends solely on the parameters (B, L, v and θ) and is independent of the intrinsic properties of the conductor, except for its effective cutting length L. By applying Ohm's Law, the expression for the induced current was derived:(6)I=σSBLνlsinθwhere I represents the induced current, σ is the electrical conductivity, S is the cross-sectional area and l is the length of the conductor. From Equation (6), it can be observed that under identical conditions, materials with higher electrical conductivity exhibit greater current responses. When comparing the electrical conductivities of other six different electroactive materials, PCLG/AgNF was found to possess the highest conductivity (Fig. S7) b, d), [22]. To investigate the electrical response of PCLG/AgNF with an MMF, we employed a DIY-assembled magneto-electric signal collection system (Fig. 3b). The magnetic generator was operated at a constant speed of 500 rpm. By adjusting the vertical distance between the PCLG/AgNF and the outer edge of the cylindrical neodymium-iron-boron magnet, the amplitude of the electrical signal generated by the PCLG/AgNF decreased progressively from 475.7 ± 9.71 μA to 16.67 ± 0.58 μA as the vertical distance increased from 0.5 cm to 2.5 cm (Fig. 3c and Fig. S8). Notably, the rate of change in signal amplitude was more pronounced at closer distances. This behavior can be attributed to Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction, which states that, under stable conductor properties, the induced current is proportional to the external magnetic flux density. As shown in Fig. S9, the spatial distribution of magnetic flux density from our magnetic generator is non-linear, which directly influences the magnitude and variation rate of the electrical response. Additionally, we investigated the effects of horizontal displacement. Under the conditions of a controlled vertical height of 1 cm and a fixed rotation speed of 500 rpm, the electrical output showed a continuous decay with the increase of horizontal displacement; when the horizontal offset reached 4 cm, the electrical output was only 19.14 % of that under the non-offset condition (Fig. S10).

The effects of rotational speed on the electrical response of PCLG/AgNF with an MMF were further investigated. With the vertical distance between the scaffold and the outer edge of the cylindrical neodymium-iron-boron magnet fixed at 5 mm, the magneto-electric coupling signal amplitudes recorded under rotational speeds of 300 rpm, 400 rpm, and 500 rpm were 351.67 ± 7.10 μA, 408.33 ± 5.86 μA, and 475.67 ± 9.71 μA, respectively (Fig. 3d and Fig. S11). These results demonstrate a consistent increase in the magneto-electric coupling signal amplitude with increasing rotational speed. As shown in Fig. 3e and Table S1, this result demonstrated that PCLG/AgNF exhibited excellent electrical response capability compared to other wireless electrical devices used in tissue engineering and regeneration applications, demonstrating its potential as a physiologically compatible stimulation source [6], a, b, [12], [23]. Both magnetoelectric conversion technology and ultrasound-electric conversion-based wireless neural stimulation techniques demonstrate substantial application potential. These two modalities circumvent limitations inherent to traditional tethered systems (e.g., electrical wires, optical-fibre cables, or fluidic tubing) while enabling focused power delivery to implanted medical devices with high spatial resolution [5,24]. However, ultrasound-based approaches require direct transducer-skin contact to mitigate significant impedance mismatch at the tissue-air interface. Furthermore, the bulky dimensions of commercially available ultrasound transducers impede their practical deployment in awake, freely behaving small-animal models [5]. In contrast, magnetoelectric conversion technology exhibits relative insensitivity to dielectric environmental variations and physical obstructions owing to its non-radiative nature [25]. Therefore, in the presence of open skin wounds (e.g., lacerations, ulcers, burns), ultrasound-electric conversion-based wireless neural stimulation techniques may cause secondary damage at the wound site, limiting their application. In contrast, magnetoelectric conversion technology does not require direct contact with the skin, thus its application remains unrestricted in the presence of open skin wounds.

These findings indicated that the system's electrical response is closely related to both magnetic field properties and operational conditions, providing a foundation for precise wireless signal control. However, due to the simplicity of the magnetic generator, more complex magnetic signals could not be generated, limiting the acquisition of intricate wireless electrical signals responses. Nonetheless, these results offer practical groundwork for future design and fine-tuning of magneto-electric coupling signal systems.

This indicated a high sensitivity of neural and related pathways to MECES.

Currently, taurine has been applied in the treatment of diabetic peripheral neuropathy and has shown significant therapeutic effects in promoting peripheral nerve regeneration [33]. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed notable upregulation of the taurine and hypotaurine metabolism pathways with MECES conditions, suggesting their potential involvement in peripheral nerve regeneration. To further elucidate the regulatory effects of MECES on taurine metabolism, key genes within this pathway were analyzed based on sequencing data. Heatmap visualization of the key pathway genes demonstrated enhanced expression of aldehyde dehydrogenase (ADO) and flavin-containing monooxygenase 1 (FMO1) and weakened expression of bile acid-CoA: amino acid N-acyltransferase (BAAT) in the endogenous taurine biosynthesis cascade (Fig. 5d). Therefore, it was hypothesized that MECES had modulated the activity of enzymes involved in taurine and hypotaurine metabolism, thereby altering taurine levels and subsequently influencing peripheral nerve regeneration. As a result, ELISA quantification confirmed a significant increase in cellular taurine concentration following 3-day MECES treatment. Specifically, taurine levels rose from 134.45 ± 2.08 ng/mL in the Plate + MMF group to 162.22 ± 2.08 ng/mL in the PCLG/AgNF + MMF group (Fig. 5e). Furthermore, the expression levels of key proteins involved in taurine and hypotaurine metabolism were determined by Western blots assay (Fig. 5f). The results demonstrated that the protein expression level of ADO in the PCLG/AgNF + MMF group was remarkably increased compared to the other three groups, while the protein expression level of FMO1 in the Plate + MMF group was remarkably elevated relative to the other three groups. Furthermore, the protein expression level of BAAT in the PCLG/AgNF + MMF group exhibited a slight reduction compared to both the Plate group and the Plate + MMF group. These results were consistent with the taurine levels detected by ELISA. Under MECES, the increased ADO content promoted the initial pathway of endogenous taurine synthesis. The FMO1 content showed no significant difference compared to the Plate group, indicating that the intermediate pathway remained unaffected, while the slight decrease in BAAT content ultimately led to a significant increase in intracellular taurine levels. Under MMF, only the FMO1 protein content was increased, which enhanced the intermediate pathway of endogenous taurine synthesis, ultimately resulting in an elevation of intracellular taurine levels. However, since the initial pathway of endogenous taurine synthesis was not promoted, the synthesis rate of hypotaurine (an intermediate product) remained unchanged. Consequently, the promotional effect of FMO1 on the further synthesis of taurine from hypotaurine was limited by the rate of hypotaurine synthesis. Consequently, the PCLG/AgNF + MMF group exhibited the highest taurine content, followed by the Plate + MMF group. Based on the results presented in Fig. 5c-f, it can be confirmed that MECES indeed activates the taurine and hypotaurine metabolic pathways. Specifically, it promotes an increase in taurine content by regulating the expression levels of relevant proteases involved in the endogenous taurine synthesis pathway (Fig. 5g). Taurine has antioxidative, osmoregulatory, and anti-inflammatory functions, among other cytoprotective properties and the multiple regulatory functions of taurine in neurons are mainly exerted through its own chemical structure [34]. Therefore, it can be anticipated that the taurine and hypotaurine metabolism pathways are actively involved in the process of peripheral nerve regeneration and play an important role.

In comparison, the Defect group showed an SFI similar to and without significant difference from the Defect + MMF group (Fig. 6e). By week 8, the PCLG/AgNF + MMF group achieved an improved SFI of −56.54 ± 2.12, approaching the Autograft group's SFI of −48.51 ± 5.05 and significantly surpassing the Defect + MMF group's SFI of −83.40 ± 2.71 and the Defect group's SFI of −83.62 ± 1.73. The PCLG/AgNF group maintained a better SFI of −63.58 ± 1.15 compared to the PCLG group's SFI of −76.43 ± 1.70. Meanwhile, the Defect group exhibited an SFI comparable and without significant difference from the Defect + MMF group (Fig. 6f). These results imply that the combined effects of MECES and PCLG/AgNF significantly promote sciatic nerve repair in rats. Notably, the absence of a significant difference in SFI between the Defect + MMF group and the Defect group suggests that, under this magnetic generator, an external MMF stimulation alone may not produce a substantial impact on sciatic nerve repair in vivo.

At weeks 4 and 8, the regenerated sciatic nerves were exposed, and compound muscle action potentials (CAMP) were recorded using the RM6240XC multi-channel physiological signal acquisition and processing system (Fig. 7a, Fig. S17a). The results demonstrated that in both week 4 and week 8, the amplitudes of CAMP signals were significantly higher in the PCLG/AgNF + MMF group and PCLG/AgNF group compared to the PCLG group, Defect + MMF groups and Defect groups. Specifically, at week 4, the amplitude of CAMP of the PCLG/AgNF + MMF group (12.75 ± 1.10 mV) was closest to that of the Autograft group (14.58 ± 1.18 mV). Comparisons between groups revealed distinct effects on nerve function recovery. When comparing the PCLG/AgNF + MMF group with the Defect + MMF group, it was evident that MECES significantly improved neural function. Similarly, comparisons between the PCLG/AgNF group and PCLG group highlighted the beneficial role of PCLG/AgNF nerve conduits in enhancing neural recovery. This functional recovery effect may arise from the excellent conductivity of PCLG/AgNF, enabling electrophysiological signals to be more efficiently transmitted. In contrast, no significant improvement in neural function was observed when comparing the Defect + MMF group with the Defect group, indicating that an MMF alone had a minimal impact on nerve repair. The functional recovery achieved by the PCLG/AgNF + MMF group, as evidenced by its CAMP signals being closest to those of the Autograft group, likely arises from the synergistic effects of MECES and PCLG/AgNF nerve conduits.

After completing the electrophysiological testing, the regenerated sciatic nerves and the gastrocnemius muscles were harvested from both sides of the rats' hind limbs for further histological analysis (Fig. 7c(i), Fig. S17b). Following sciatic nerve injury, the loss of innervation leads to disuse-related molecular changes in the gastrocnemius muscle, shifting protein metabolism from synthesis to degradation and ultimately reducing muscle fiber size. Severe muscle mass reduction and connective tissue proliferation are characteristic features of muscle atrophy [35]. At week 4, the wet weight ratio of the gastrocnemius muscle in the Defect + MMF group was significantly lower compared to both the PCLG/AgNF + MMF group and the Autograft group. Similarly, the PCLG group showed a significant reduction in muscle mass compared to the Autograft group. However, no significant differences were observed among the other groups regarding wet weight ratios. By week 8, differences in gastrocnemius muscle recovery became more evident across groups. The wet weight ratios increased progressively from the PCLG group to the PCLG/AgNF group and then to the PCLG/AgNF + MMF group, although these changes did not reach statistical significance. Interestingly, the Defect + MMF group showed a significant increase in muscle mass compared to the Defect group (Fig. 7d). Notably, more pronounced differences were evident in the gastrocnemius muscles fiber diameter data. The PCLG/AgNF + MMF group exhibited significantly larger muscle fiber diameters than the Defect + MMF group, while the PCLG/AgNF group also showed a significant increase compared to the PCLG group. Although the Defect + MMF group had a higher fiber diameter than the Defect group, this difference remained non-significant. In terms of gastrocnemius muscle fiber diameter at week 8, the PCLG/AgNF + MMF group continued to exhibit significantly larger values than the Defect + MMF group. However, the difference between the PCLG/AgNF group and the PCLG group was no longer significant, and similarly, no significant difference was observed between the Defect + MMF group and the Defect group (Fig. 7e). The histological data from the gastrocnemius muscle regeneration experiments demonstrate that MECES consistently and significantly promotes the overall regenerative tissue process. In contrast, the effects of an MMF alone and the PCLG/AgNF itself appear complex and less pronounced potential for repair.

Histological assessment via hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and immunohistochemical staining systematically characterized neural regeneration patterns across experimental groups (Fig. 8a and Fig. S18). At 4 weeks post-operation, H&E results revealed extensive distribution of regenerative axon bundles in the Defect, Defect + MMF, and PCLG groups. This morphological feature, typically associated with early-stage neural regeneration, suggested delayed repair processes in these groups. In contrast, the Autograft, PCLG/AgNF + MMF, and PCLG/AgNF groups demonstrated uniformly organized regenerative neural tissues [36]. Notably, conspicuous vascular networks were observed in all groups except the Defect group, indicating enhanced nutritional support for regenerating nerves (Fig. S19). By week 8, axon bundles persisted predominantly in the Defect and Defect + MMF groups, with minimal presence in the PCLG group. All groups exhibited extensive vascularization at this stage; however, the PCLG/AgNF + MMF group and Autograft group still showed the highest number of blood vessels (Fig. S19). Furthermore, PCLG/AgNF conduits maintained superior hollow structural integrity in vivo, providing essential spatial and mechanical support for neural regeneration. In the context of nerve regeneration, remyelination is critical for achieving functional recovery. Myelin, which serves as a specialized insulating sheath around axons, not only provides physical protection to nerve fibers but also enables efficient saltatory conduction of electrical impulses, thereby optimizing signal transmission along the axonal membrane [37]. The PCLG/AgNF + MMF group demonstrated myelin sheath thickness statistically equivalent to the Autograft group, while significantly exceeding other experimental groups (Fig. 8b). Although axonal diameters in the PCLG/AgNF + MMF group showed non-significant increases compared to Autograft at both timepoints, they exhibited statistically superior dimensions relative to all other intervention groups (Fig. 8c). The PCLG/AgNF group displayed marginally greater myelin thickness and axonal diameter compared to the PCLG group at week 4 and 8, suggesting potential benefits of PCLG/AgNF conduits. Additionally, the Defect + MMF group showed significantly larger axonal diameters than the Defect group only at week 4, with a non-significant trend toward improved myelination at week 4 and 8. Immunofluorescence analysis further evaluated neural regeneration. In addition to β3-tubulin, a structural protein present in myelin and axons, S100 served as a cytoplasmic protein marker for Schwann cells, indicative of neuronal proliferation and functional protein expression [38]. At week 4, the PCLG/AgNF + MMF group exhibited the largest fluorescence area percentage for S100 and the second largest for β3-tubulin, demonstrating results closest to the Autograft group compared to all other groups (Fig. S20). However, neither MMF alone nor PCLG/AgNF alone promoted neural regeneration. By week 8, the PCLG/AgNF + MMF group displayed the largest fluorescence area percentages overall, and this difference was not statistically significant only when compared to the Autograft group (Fig. 8d,e). While the effects of MMF alone and PCLG/AgNF alone showed improvement compared to week 4, only the β3-tubulin fluorescence area percentage in the PCLG/AgNF group was statistically significantly greater than that in the PCLG group. During sciatic nerve defect repair, the PCLG/AgNF + MMF group demonstrated stable, highly effective regeneration outcomes comparable to the Autograft group, robustly validating its efficacy in promoting sciatic nerve repair. The PCLG/AgNF NGCs alone exhibited the third-highest repair efficacy among all groups, surpassed only by the PCLG/AgNF + MMF and Autograft groups. This performance may be attributed to their exceptional mechanical properties (maintaining structural integrity) and superior electrical conductivity (facilitating cellular electrophysiological communication). In contrast, the therapeutic contribution of MMF alone remained insignificant. While in vitro experiments revealed a mild yet statistically significant enhancement in PC12 cell proliferation and migration under MMF exposure, in vivo assessments provided limited evidence of regenerative benefits. Significant improvements were only observed in muscle wet weight ratio (Week 8) and diameter of axon (Week 4). This observation appears to be at odds with some previous studies. This may be attributed to two aspects. On one hand, the magnetic devices in previous studies were usually composed of coils and generated high-frequency magnetic signals for stimulation, which are significantly different from the magnetic signal generation mode and related parameters in this work. On the other hand, tissue repair usually involves multiple aspects (immune microenvironment, vascularization), while the magnetic stimulation in this work may not have significant effects on these aspects. Therefore, the MMF itself in this work did not show a significant effect in the repair of sciatic nerve defects.

The potential toxicity of silver as a material has always raised concerns. The degradation capability of PCLG/AgNF NGCs and the cumulative release concentration of silver were evaluated in both PBS and lipase solution. PCLG/AgNF NGCs exhibited excellent mass stability in PBS, demonstrating minimal degradation over the 16-day degradation period (Fig. S21a). By day 16, PCLG/AgNF NGCs showed a mass loss of approximately 5 % in 5 U/mL lipase solution, indicating retained favorable mass stability. Furthermore, PCLG/AgNF NGCs released silver in both PBS and lipase solution (Fig. S21b). By day 16, the cumulative release concentrations of PCLG/AgNF NGCs in PBS and lipase solution were approximately 1 μg/mL and 1.11 μg/mL, respectively, corresponding to release rates of approximately 0.06 μg/mL/day and 0.07 μg/mL/day. The toxicity of silver nanoparticles typically exhibits dose dependency. Sapkota K. et al. observed no effect on keratinocyte viability after 48 h exposure at 10 μg/mL (viability: 98.76 %). However, viability decreased to 74.5 % when silver nanoparticles concentration increased to 100 μg/mL. The silver release rate observed in this study was demonstrated to be safe. Additionally, the biocompatibility of PCLG/AgNF NGCs were evaluated by comparing the biochemical indices in serum samples obtained from rats repaired with and without the PCLG/AgNF at week 8 post-operation. No abnormal changes were observed in the serum sample between the two groups (Fig. S22). Furthermore, H&E staining was used to examine the liver and kidney tissues of rats repaired with PCLG/AgNF NGCs at week 20 post-operation; these organs appeared healthy and structurally intact with no obvious pathological alterations (Fig. S23). These findings demonstrate that PCLG/AgNF NGCs exhibit good biocompatibility in the medium-to-short term. However, considering that neural repair is a long-term and complex biological process, further studies are required to investigate the long-term implantation effects and systemic toxicity of the material and its degradation products.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the integration of conductive biomaterials with wireless electromagnetic fields establishes a synergistic platform capable of effectively promoting neural regeneration and functional recovery. The composite system exhibited stable electroactive properties, enabling precise modulation of electrical stimulation signal and tissue remodeling processes. Compared to isolated electromagnetic or conductive material interventions, the combined strategy significantly enhanced axonal regrowth, myelination, and structural integrity of regenerated nerves through coordinated bioelectrical and biochemical mechanisms. Molecular characterization revealed activation of critical neurogenic pathways associated with cellular differentiation and metabolic regulation. In the rat model of sciatic nerve injury models, the therapeutic outcomes achieved functional restoration approaching gold-standard clinical benchmarks. This work not only validates the therapeutic superiority of MECES technology but also provides a novel wireless platform for precise neural stimulation. Future studies will focus on long-term biocompatibility assessments, optimization of stimulation parameters for clinical translation, and exploration of broader applications in neuroengineering and regenerative medicine.
