Whole genome sequencing and metabonomics analysis reveal the biodegradation process of deoxynivalenol in a safety Bacillus velezensis T-3 isolated from the gut of sea cucumber
Authors: Xinghua Cai, Shasha Luo, Xuewen Chen, Ping Yi, Fengdie Huang, Jinrong Huang, Chenghuan Qin, Caixia Zou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeoxynivalenol (DON) is a typical mycotoxin mainly produced by Fusarium, which seriously endangers the safety of global agriculture, feed and food. To further explore the biodegradation strategy of DON, a safety Bacillus velezensis T-3 was identified. The biodegradration process of DON by B. velezensis T-3 was further revealed via target fermentation and metabonomics analysis. Results showed that B. velezensis T-3 could effectively antagonize the growth of mycotoxin-producing Fusarium graminearum, Penicillium chrysogenum and Aspergillus flavus. Moreover, metabonomics analysis indicated that DON could be completely removed by B. velezensis T-3 within 72 h. Further analysis found that DON could be converted into low-toxic or non-toxic compounds such as retinoic acid, glycine, etc. In application stage, the highest degradation rate of DON in corn by-products was 49.59%, B. velezensis T-3 could also effectively inhibit the growth of molds to prolong the mildew cycle of corn by-products, thus remarkably reducing the harmfulness of DON.Keywords: Deoxynivalenol, Mycotoxin, B. velezensis, Biodegradation

Introduction

Deoxynivalenol (DON), commonly known as vomitoxin, is a secondary metabolite mainly produced by mycotoxin-producing fungi such as Fusarium graminearum, Penicillium chrysogenum, Penicillium viridum, etc.) (Thapa, Horgan, White, & Walls, 2021). In 1973, DON was defined as one of the most dangerous natural food pollutants by the World Health Organization (WHO) (Yao & Long, 2020). The pollution rate of DON in cereals can be as high as 90%, causing huge economic losses worldwide every year (Marin, Ramos, Cano-Sancho, & Sanchis, 2013; Sumarah, 2022). Moreover, DON not only mainly pollutes a variety of cereal crops, including wheat, barley and corn but deposits in meat, eggs, and milk via the food chain. Even at low dose levels (500 μg/kg), DON can further harm human health, causing vomiting, diarrhea, shock, and even death (Ganesan et al., 2022; Park, Chang, Kim, Chung, & Lee, 2018). Therefore, it is necessary to develop new strategies to effectively control the pollution and degradation of DON.

The inhibition of mycotoxin-producing molds growth or elimination of the mycotoxins that have been produced are critical to deal with the DON pollution. Therefore, a number of chemical, physical, and biological methods for mycotoxin control have been reported. However, there are still some shortcomings of these methods (Feizollahi & Roopesh, 2022). For example, chemical detoxification usually requires strong acid or alkali treatment or redox reaction, which is extremely expensive and easy to causes secondary environmental pollution (Mir et al., 2021). Therefore, chemical methods to control mycotoxins have been almost eliminated at present. Physical detoxification of DON often requires high-temperature treatment (more than 110 °C) or the use of physical adsorbents (e.g., montmorillonite, mannan). However, high-temperature treatment is difficult to be applied in industrial production due to the extreme energy consumption (Crudo et al., 2021; Pronyk, Cenkowski, & Abramson, 2006). Physical adsorbents will cause the loss of nutrients (Avantaggiato, Havenaar, & Visconti, 2004) due to the non-specific which can also adsorbs abundant nutrients (e.g., vitamins, amino acids, etc.). More importantly, it is still unclear whether the mycotoxins will be released and shown to be toxic in vivo, thereby bringing more uncertain risks. Unlike chemical and physical detoxification, bio-detoxification of DON via bio-enzymes or microorganisms seems to be more targeted, efficient, safe and eco-friendly, so that it becomes the most widely used detoxification method at present (Jason, Hassan, Dion, & Ting, 2018). However, the bio-enzymes are sensitive to temperature and pH, which makes them easy to lose activity during the processing of feed or food. Furthermore, currently used bio-enzymes or microorganisms are trend to degrade the DON already produced. Few reports focus on inhibiting the growth of mycotoxin-producing molds and simultaneously degrading the already produced DON. Therefore, it is necessary to find strains that can inhibit the growth of DON-producing fungi and degrade DON efficiently.

Previous studies found that epoxy structures at the C12-C13 and C3-OH position of DON are the main toxic groups (Eriksen, Pettersson, Lundh, & Toxicology, 2004). DON could be converted into deepoxy-deoxynivalenol (DOM-1), 3-keto-deoxynivalenol (3-keto-DON), 3-epi-deoxynivalenol (3-epi-DON), 3-acetyldeoxynivalenol (3-AcDON) or the other compounds with lower toxicity via ring-opening oxidation pathway in the C12-C13 position or destroying the structure C3-OH group, so as to realize effective biological detoxification (Freire, Sant’Ana, & Toxicology, 2018). For example, He et al. (He, Shi, Yang, Huang, & Liao, 2020b) characterized a quinone-dependent dehydrogenase gene QDDH via comparative genomics and transcriptomics, and found that the enzyme could convert DON into 3-keto-DON. Moreover, they also presented the aldo/keto reductase genes AKR13B2, AKR6D1 from a strain Devosia sp. D6–9, and found that these enzymes could further convert 3-keto-DON into 3-epi-DON (He, Shi, Yang, Huang, & Liao, 2020a), thus significantly reducing the hazard of DON. In addition, it was also reported that many microorganisms can effectively degrade DON, such as Clostridium sp. WJ06 (Fuchang, Jinquan, Libo, Hongju, & Chunyang, 2017) Eggerthella sp. DII-9 (Gao et al., 2017), Bacillus sp. LS100 (Li et al., 2011). These microorganisms mainly produce the functional enzymes to catalyze the biotransformation or catabolism of DON to significantly reduce or eliminate its toxicity. However, most of them focus on removing DON by simple group modification or identifying the proximal degradation metabolites. Few studies have revealed the metabolic pathway and remote metabolites of DON, so it is still unknown whether DON can be biodegraded into safer, non-toxic or low-toxic metabolites (Zhang et al., 2023). Therefore, it is necessary to reveal the potential metabolic pathway of DON, and further identify its bio-detoxification compounds.

In our previous experiments, a safety strain named Bacillus velezensis T-3 was isolated from the gut of sea cucumber. The strain could effectively antagonize the growth of typical toxin-producing fungus, including Fusarium graminearum and Penicillium chrysogenum, and could grow in a single carbon medium containing DON, the removal rate of DON could reach 100%. Based on these important findings, we hypothesized that Bacillus velezensis T-3 is able to biodegrade DON or convert it into various metabolites with low or no toxicity. To verify this hypothesis, the metabolites of DON degradation by Bacillus velezensis T-3 was systematically investigated. This study will provide a new technical scheme for the biological control of DON.

Materials and methods

Strains and sea cucumber

Fusarium graminearum (GDMCC 3.507), Penicillium chrysogenum (CGMCC 3.4517), and Aspergillus flavus (CGMCC 3.493) were obtained from Guangdong Microbial Strain Conservation Center (Guangzhou, China). The sea cucumber was obtained from Bohai bay in China (39°54′4″N, 119°36′26″E).

Reagents

Glucose, sodium chloride, ammonium sulfate, dipotassium hydrogen phosphate, potassium dihydrogen phosphate, magnesium sulfate, peptone, tryptone, yeast extract, beef paste, and agar were purchased from Beijing Soleberg Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China). Deoxynivalenol standard was purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The AgraQuant® DON Detection Kit was purchased from ROMER International Trade Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China). The corn by-products was provided by Guangxi UBIT Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Nanning, China).

Target screening of the deoxynivalenol-degrading strain

The sea cucumber was immersed in 0.9% saline for 4 h to make it swell and soft, which was then immersed in 75% ethanol solution for surface disinfection. Subsequently, the sea cucumber was cut open, its intestinal tissues were taken out and placed in 100 mL sterile saline. The samples were shaken for 60 s to suspend the strains. Further, 1 mL of the bacterial suspension was diluted to103–105 folds, and then coated on LB medium (1% tryptone, 0.5% yeast extract, 1% NaCl, pH 7.0 ± 0.2) to isolate and purify the single strain. Cell morphological characteristics of the strains were observed under an optical microscope after gram staining. Various strains were further targeted incubated in the selective medium (0.05% NaCl, 0.2% (NH4)2SO4, 0.05% K2HPO4, 0.05% KH2PO4, 0.02% MgSO4•7H2O, H2O) contained 10 mg/L DON as the single carbon source. Subsequently, various strains were cultured at 35 °C and 150 rpm for 0–72 h. The viable counts of each strain were detected by plate counting method, thus screening a strain T-3 which can effectively metabolize DON to grow.

The selected strain T-3 was cultured and enriched using LB medium. Its genomic DNA was then extracted using genomic DNA extraction kit and purified by DNA purification kit (Beijing Kengke Biotechnology Co. Ltd., Beijing, China). The strain were preliminary identified by 16S rDNA sequencing (primers: F:5′-AGTTTGATCMTGTGCTCAG-3′, R:5′-GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′). Sequencing results were compared with the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database via Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST). The phylogenetic tree for preliminary identification of strain T-3 was constructed via Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis version 7.0 (MEGA v7.0) (Huang et al., 2022). Moreover, the genomic DNA of strain T-3 was sent to Shanghai Luming Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) to complete the whole genome sequencing. Whole genome raw data of strain T-3 was uploaded to NCBI database with accession number of SAMN47421939 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra/PRJNA1237420).

Heat tolerance of the strain

To evaluate the heat tolerance of strain T-3, 2% of the bacterial suspension was inoculated in the Luria-Bertani (LB) medium (v/v), and then incubated at 30 °C, 150 rpm for 24 h. Subsequently, the strain was treated for 1 h in a water bath at 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80, and 90 °C, respectively. The viable counts were respectively tested by plate counting method to assess the heat-resistance of strain T-3.

pH tolerance of the strain

2% of strain T-3 suspension (v/v) was respectively inoculated in the LB medium with pH of 3.0, 4.0, 5.0, 6.0, 7.0, 8.0, 9.0, 10.0 and 11.0, all the samples were then incubated at 30 °C, 150 rpm for 24 h. The viable counts of different samples were detected by plate counting method to evaluate the pH tolerance of the strain.

Salt tolerance of the strain

In the LB medium containing 0%, 2%, 4%, 6%, 8% and 10% NaCl, respectively, 2% of strain T-3 bacterial suspension was inoculated, followed by they were incubated at 30 °C, 150 rpm for 24 h. The viable counts of various samples were detected to assess the salt tolerance of the strain.

Drug sensitive test of the strain

Taken out 200 μL of the strain T-3 suspension, and then coated it on the LB plate medium. Subsequently, four different antibiotics (including erythromycin, chloramphenicol, ampicillin and cephatriaxone) were placed on each plate. The culture plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to observe whether there will be a bacteriostatic circle. Drug sensitive of strain T-3 was determined via measuring the diameter of bacteriostatic circle with a vernier caliper (Pal, 2000).

Hemolytic test of the strain

Take out 200 μL of the strain T-3 suspension, and then inoculate it into the prepared blood plates. Subsequently, the strain T-3 was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to observe whether the hemolytic loops generated. If there is a grass green ring around the colony, the strain would be defined as α-hemolysis. When there is a transparent hemolytic ring around the colony, it would be defined as β-hemolysis. Both of α-hemolysis and β-hemolysis suggested that strain T-3 is a potentially harmful strain. However, while there is no change or no hemolytic ring ocurred around the colony, the strain T-3 would be defined as γ-hemolysis, which suggested that it is a potential safe strain (Garbowska et al., 2023).

Bacteriostasis effect of the strain on mycotoxin-producing fungi

On the solid Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) medium plate (20% potato, 2% glucose, 2% agar), 100 μL suspension of the typical toxicity-producing fungi Fusarium graminearum suspension, Penicillium chrysogenum suspension, and Aspergillus flavus suspension were respectively coated on the medium. Subsequently, 10 μL bacterial suspension of strain T-3 was spotted on the culture medium plate. Then, the samples were cultured at 30 °C for 5–7 days to observe whether the bacteriostatic circle would appear around the strain T-3. The bacteriostasis effect of strain T-3 on mycotoxin-producing fungus was evaluated by measuring the diameter ratio of the bacteriostatic circle (bacteriostatic circle diameter/colony diameter, D/d).

Identification of the degradation compounds of DON

In this work, the DON-degrading compounds in strain T-3 were further identified by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) (Li, Duan, Ren, Francis, & Li, 2022). Firstly, 400 μL of the fermentation broth of strain T-3 (inubated with DON as the single carbon source) was taken at 0 h and 72 h, respectively, which were then added 0.4 mL of 70% methanol solution (pre-cooled to 4 °C), fully mixed and ultrasonic ally treated for 10 min. Subsequently, the supernatant was collected by centrifugation for 20 min at 14000 ×g, 4 °C. The supernatant was further concentrated for 4 h in a vacuum, and then re-dissolved in 0.1% aqueous formic acid. Finally, the samples were filtered by a 0.22 μm filter membrane, filtrate was then transferred into the LC injection vial to complete the sample preparation.

Parameters of chromatography-mass spectrometry: the samples were separated on an ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) system Acclaim PepMap® RSLC C18 column. The column temperature was set at 25 °C, flow rate was 0.4 μL/min, and the injection volume was 2 μL. Mobile phase was composed of A: 0.1% formic acid solution, B: acetonitrile. The gradient elution procedure is as follows: 0–15 min: 5% B-32% B; 15–20 min: 32% B-60% B; 20–28 min: 60% B-90% B. Separation of the fractions was carried out on an Acclaim PepMap® RSLC C18 column at a flow rate of 0.4 μL/min (Zelena et al., 2009). Mass spectrometry conditions: FTMS Analyzer; Full scan mode; Resolution of 15,000–60,000; Polarity: “+” or “-”; Scan Range from 100 to 1000 m/z; Activation type: HCD; Normalized collision energy: 10. (Want et al., 2013).

Data processing: The LC-MS/MS data were converted to .abf format using ABF converter, peak alignment, retention time correction and peak area extraction were carried out using data-independent MS/MS deconvolution for comprehensive metabolome analysis (MS-DIAL) programme, and the compounds were defined as significant difference when the abundance change meets the requirements of fold change ≥2.0 and p < 0.05 (Li et al., 2022). The degradation compounds and metabolic pathway of DON were further analyzed using NCBI, KEGG and other databases to reveal the potential degradation mechanism of DON.

Biodegradation of DON in corn by-products

In a 500 g fermentation system, 400 g of sterilized corn by-products infected with Fusarium graminearum (viable counts of 105 CFU/g), 25 g of molasses, and 75 mL of strain T-3 suspension were mixed. While the control was added 75 mL sterile water instead of adding strian T-3 suspension. The fermentation process lasted for 0–30 days at 30 °C. Subsequently, sampling on the 0, 5, 10, 20, and 30th day to detect the content of DON in the corn by-products. In addition, the viable molds under each condition were detected by plate counting method according to the Chinese National Standard method (GB 13078) (Guan et al., 2023).

Moreover, this study investigated the effects of temperature (20–60 °C) and pH (5.0–9.0) on the degradation of DON by strain T-3. The fermentation was conducted in a 500 g system containing 400 g of sterilized corn by-products, 25 g of sterilized molasses, and 75 mL of T-3 strain suspension. To evaluate the influence of temperature, samples were incubated at 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 °C. For assessing pH effects, samples were respectively adjusted to 5.0, 6.0, 7.0, 8.0, and 9.0 using 6 mol/L NaOH or HCl. The fermentation lasted for 10 days, with samples collected on 0 d and 10 d for determination the content of DON in the fermented corn by-products.

Results

The strain is identified as Bacillus velezensis T-3

As shown in Fig. 1, the strain were isolated from the intestinal tissue of sea cucumber (Fig. 1a). Its colony is white, opaque, rough in surface and irregular in edges (Fig. 1b). The gram staining results showed that strain T-3 was gram-positive, its cell morphology is rod-shaped (Fig. 1c). The growth trend of strain T-3 using DON as the single carbon source was shown in Table 1. When the culture medium is without any carbon source, the viable counts of strain T-3 gradually decreases with prolonging the culture time. However, when DON was added, the viable counts of strain T-3 increased at first and then decreased gradually. Compared with the initial state (8.3 × 104 cfu/mL), the maximum viable counts of strain T-3 (9.2 × 105 cfu/mL) increased by 10.08-folds, which indicated that strain T-3 could grow via metabolizing DON, suggesting that there is a metabolic regulatory network in the genome of strain T-3 responsible for utilization of DON.

Biochemical characterization of strain T-3

The heat resistance of strain T-3 showed that it could grow well at 20–60 °C, and the number of viable bacteria could reach more than 107 CFU/mL (Fig. 3a). Moreover, when strain T-3 was treated at 80 °C for 1 h, it could still maintain the viable counts of 2 × 106 CFU/mL. Even if the strain was treated at 90 °C for 1 h, it could not be completely killed, and its viable counts still reached 5 × 105 CFU/mL). These results indicated that strain T-3 has a heat tolerance as high as 90 °C, suggesting that it has important application potential in the agriculture, feed and food industry.

Biochemical characterization of the strain T-3; (a) Temperature tolerance of the strain; (b) pH tolerance of the strain; (c) Salt tolerance of the strain; (d) Drug sensitive of strain T-3 to chloramphenicol and erythromycin; (e) Drug sensitive of strain T-3 to ampicillin; (f) Drug sensitive of strain T-3 to ceftriaxone; (g-i) The positive control of microbial hemolytic characteristics; (j) Hemolysis test of strain T-3.

pH tolerance of strain T-3 showed that it could grow well under pH 4.0–8.0, its viable counts could always reach more than 107 CFU/mL (Fig. 3b). For example, when the pH was set at 6.0, the highest viable counts of strain T-3 could reach up to 3.1 × 108 CFU/mL. When the pH condition is lower than 4.0 or higher than 9.0, growth of strain T-3 was significantly inhibited. However, when pH is lower than 3.0 or higher than 10.0, strain T-3 could hardly grow. Therefore, the suitable pH condition for strain T-3 should be controlled during 4.0–8.0.

Salt tolerance of strain T-3 indicated that it could grow well under conditions of containing 0% ∼ 4% NaCl and keep the number of viable counts higher than 106 CFU/mL (Fig. 3c). However, salt-free condition is the most suitable growth condition for strain T-3, and its highest viable counts could reach 8.4 × 107 CFU/mL. When NaCl stress exceeded 6%, the growth of strain T-3 was obviously inhibited, its viable counts were quickly decreased to 104 CFU/mL. When the concentration of NaCl increased to 10%, the viable counts of strain T-3 further decreased to 103 CFU/mL. These results showed that strain T-3 has good salt-tolerant viability.

Safety assessment of strain T-3

Safety assessment of strain T-3 were performed via drug sensitivity test and hemolysis test. The drug sensitivity results of strain T-3 indicated that it is all sensitive to chloramphenicol, erythromycin, ampicillin, and ceftriaxone (Fig. 3d, e, f; Table 2), the diameter of bacteriostatic circle could reach 22.0 mm, 15.7 mm, 26.4 mm and 36.7 mm, respectively. Suggesting that strain T-3 could be effectively killed by these antibiotics and without widespread risk. In addition, antibiotic sensitivity test of strain T-3 contribute to avoiding using non-sensitive antibiotics and causing abuse of antibiotics.

Anti-mildew and mycotoxin degradation ability of strain T-3

As shown in Fig. 4, strain T-3 was able to effectively antagonize the growth of Fusarium graminearum, Aspergillus flavus, and Penicillium viridicatumi (Fig. 4a, b, c), and the diameters of bacteriostatic circle are 1.71 cm, 2.23 cm and 1.06 cm respectively. The diameter ratio of inhibition zone (inhibition zone diameter: D/diameter of colony: d) was 1.55, 1.96 and 1.71, respectively. These results suggest that strain T-3 could obviously inhibit the growth of toxin-producing fungus and contribute to reducing the risk of mildew.

The potential metabolites of DON

As shown in Fig. 5, when DON is the single carbon source for strain T-3 incubated for 72 h, there are 36 significantly up-regulated differential metabolites (DMs, Supplementary material S1) were identified, suggesting that these compounds could be biosynthesized by strain T-3. These compounds mainly belong to organic acids (e.g., lichesterinic acid, 16-Hydroxyhexadecanoic acid), alkaloids (e.g., guan-fu base Y), alcohols(e.g., vestitol, zedoarondiol), amino acids (e.g., glycine), and functional products (e.g., 7-hydroxyflavone, indole, adenosine monophosphate). Furthermore, the metabolic profile indicates that DON is likely catabolized by strain T-3 into low-toxicity or non-toxic derivatives through a cascade of enzymatic modifications. This observation results support the hypothesis that strain T-3 possesses a specialized metabolic pathway for DON detoxification.

The potential metabolic pathway of DON in strain T-3

According to the whole genome analysis of strain T-3, there are 108 kinds of annotated carbohydrate active enzymes (Fig. 6a) in the genome of stain T-3, among which the proportion of GHs, GTs, PLs, CEs, AAs and CBMs is 39.81%, 31.48%, 2.78%, 15.74%, 5.56% and 4.63% (Fig. 6b). These carbohydrate-active enzymes play important role in catalyzing the acetylation, hydroxylation, carbonylation, oxidation, and isomerization of DON, thus contributing to promoting the degradation of DON into low-toxic or non-toxic products.

The carbohydrate active enzymes and potential metabolic process of DON in strain T-3. (a) Carbohydrate-active enzymes analysis of strain T-3 according to its whole genome data; (b) Proportion analysis of various carbohydrate-active enzymes; (c) The potential metabolic pathway of DON in strain T-3.

Furthermore, combined with the whole genome data of strain T-3, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis, and chemical structure of the DMs containing carbon element (Table 3), the potential metabolic process of DON in strain T-3 was deduced (Fig. 6c). Firstly, the C3 and C5 sites of DON could be acetylated to generate 3,15-Acetyldeoxynivalenol by acetylase, which could be further poduced 15-Deacetylcalonectrin by hydroxylation, dehydroxylation, and deacetylation at C7, C8, and C15 sites, respectively. Subsequently, C3 and C15 sites of 15-Deacetylcalonecrin could be dehydroxylated and deacetylated to generate Trichodiene, which could be transformed into Farnesyl pyrophosphate (FPP) by decyclization. As an important intermediate metabolite, FPP could be transformed into Geranylgeranyl diphosphate under the action of isopenyldiphosphate farnesyltransferase (R02061). Subsequently, Geranylgeranyl diphosphate could enter the carotenoid biosynthetic route and finally generate Beta-carotene. Beta-carotene could be further transformed into retinoic acid under the action of oxidoreductase enzymes (R00032, R02123). In addition, FPP could generate pyruvic acid catalyzed by oxidoreductases (R05884, R08689, R10859, etc.) or produce Acetyl-CoA catalyzed by oxidoreductases (R02003, R01121, R03245, etc.), this is the key process for strain T-3 to capture energy to grow. And further, pyruvic acid could be entered the pathway of phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis (map00400) to form indole. Acetyl-CoA could be entered fatty acid metabolism to form palmitoyl-CoA, which will be finally transformed into 16-Hydroxypalmitic acid under the action of hydroxylase (R01274) and hydrolase (R09451). Moreover, Acetyl-CoA could also involve in the pathway of purine metabolism and β-alanine metabolism, and finally be transformed into glycine and malonic acid. Therefore, strain T-3 could degrade DON through a complex biochemical metabolic network, and is able to effectively transform DON into a variety of safety metabolites, and gain energy for growth and reproduction, thus achieving effective bio-detoxification. This work provided new insights for the current research related to the metabolic pathway of DON in Bacillus velezensis.

Efficient degradation of DON in corn by-products

As shown in Fig. 7a, when strain T-3 was added into the corn by-products containing DON and fermented for 0–30 days, the content of DON was increased first and then decreased gradually. Compared with the initial state (0 d), the content of DON reached the highest yield of 3720 μg/kg on the 5th day. However, when the fermentation time was extended to 30 days, the content of DON dropped to 1802 μg/kg, the degradation rate of DON reached 49.59% compared with the initial state (3575 μg/kg). Therefore, adding strain T-3 is contrbute to reducing the content of DON in corn by-products.

Biodegradation of DON and anti-mildew effect of strain T-3 on the corn by-products; (a) Efficient degradation of DON by strain T-3 incubated for 0–30 days; (b) The anti-mildew effect of strain T-3 on the corn by-products within 30 days; (c) Efects of different temperatures on the degradation of DON; (d) Efects of different pH levels on the degradation of DON.

Anti-mold effect of strain T-3 on corn by-products is shown in Fig. 7b, results indicated that the viable molds in corn by-products were 35 × 102 CFU/g in the initial state (0 d) of the control, and the viable molds continued to rise when the fermentation time were gradually extended to 30 days, which reached the highest value of 165 × 102 CFU/g on 30th day. However, regarding the treatment group, the viable molds showed a decreasing trend within 30 days, and were lowest to 0 × 102 CFU/g on 10th day, which is decreased by 100% compared to the initial state (0 d). These results indicated that strain T-3 could significantly inhibit the growth of molds and prolong the mildew cycle of corn by-products, thus contributing to reducing the risk of mycotoxins-producing fungus.

The effect of different temperatures (20–60 °C) on the degradation of DON in corn by-products by strain T-3 is shown in Fig. 7c. The results indicated that degradation rates exceeded 30% across the temperature range of 20–50 °C, with the highest degradation rate of 34.55% observed at 30 °C, where the DON concentration decreased from 5207 μg/kg to 3408 μg/kg. At 60 °C, the degradation rate was 29.36%. These data demonstrate that temperature variation within the 20–60 °C range had a limited influence on DON degradation by strain T-3; however, maintaining temperatures between 30 and 40 °C resulted in relatively higher degradation efficiency.

As depicted in Fig. 7d, the influence of different pH levels on DON degradation in corn by-products by strain T-3 was evaluated. After 10 days of fermentation at pH values ranging from 5.0 to 9.0, the DON content in corn by-products decreased from an initial range of 4906–5102 μg/kg to 3385–3472 μg/kg. The highest degradation rate of 30.85% was achieved at pH 9.0. Overall, degradation rates under all tested pH conditions fell within a narrow range of 28.31%–30.85%, indicating that pH variation between 5.0 and 9.0 had no obviously effect on the degradation of DON strain T-3.

Discussions

Obtaining safety and efficient strains is the key prerequisite for exploring biological detoxification of feed. Accordingly, microorganisms with bio-detoxification functions from various natural environments or animals have been continuously discovered. However, there is little research on the gut microorganisms of sea cucumbe. With the development of aquaculture and the more and more common application of feed in sea cucumber, intestinal microorganisms of sea cucumber have attracted widespread attention in recent years (Gao, Li, Tan, Yan, & Sun, 2014). Reports indicated that the dominant gut microorganisms of sea cucumbers are mainly Bacillus thickeniensis, Aspergillus, Actinobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Vibrio spp., Bacillus sp. etc. (Feng et al., 2021; Sha et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2013). These microorganisms play an important role in improving the efficiency of organic matter decomposition and nutrient digestion and absorption in the sea cucumber gut. However, few reports have investigated the Bio-detoxification functions of gut microorganisms in sea cucumber, thus limiting their further development and utilization in feed and aquaculture. In this work, we presented a safety strain Bacillus velezensis T-3 isolated from the gut of sea cucumber, and systematacially characterized its biological detoxification function. Results showed that Bacillus velezensis T-3 could survive in extreme acid or alkaline environment (pH 4.0–10), high-temperature treatment (lower than 80 °C), and high-salt conditions (within 10% NaCl). More importantly, this presented strain has a good effect in antagonizing the growth of mycotoxin-producing mold and degrading mycotoxins. This study further enriched the source of mycotoxin-degrading strains, an is contributed to developing the novel microbial detoxification technology.

Regarding the degradation of DON by microorganisms, it has been reported that many strains from different environments could effectively degrade DON. Such as bacteria, fungi, and actinomycetes isolated from soil, digestive tract, and marine environments, etc. (Gao, Zhu, & Toxins, 2020; Islam et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2020). For example, Wang et al. Reported a Devosia insluae A16 which could degrade 88% of DON under aerobic conditions at 35 °C (Gang, Yanxia, Fang, Limei, & Chemistry, 2019). Repedkiene et al. screened four fungus for the degradation of DON in feed, results showed that 67.50% ∼ 92.31% of DON could be effectively removed (Repečkienė, Levinskaitė, Paškevičius, & Raudonienė, 2013). He et al. presented a strain Devosia sp. D6–9 that could completely degrade the DON within 2 h (He et al., 2020a). Yu et al. (Yu et al., 2021) found a lipopeptide mycosubtilin purified from Bacillus subtilis ATCC6633 significantly suppressed the growth of F. graminearum PH-1 and F. verticillioides 7600 in vitro. Additionally, mycosubtilin significantly decreased the production of deoxynivalenol (DON) and B-series fumonisins (FB1, FB2 and FB3) in infected grains, with inhibition rates of 48.92%, 48.48%, 52.42%, and 59.44%, respectively. Wang et al. (Wang et al., 2020) presented a Bacillus licheniformis strain named YB9 with a strong ability to detoxify DON was isolated and characterized from a moldy soil sample. YB9 could degrade more than 82.67% of 1 mg/L DON within 48 h at 37 °C. However, current studies paid few attention to the degradation of DON by Bacillus velezensis, and the safety analysis of the potential degradation products of DON are less involved. For example, Palazzini et al. (Palazzini, Dunlap, Bowman, & Chulze, 2016) reported a Bacillus velezensis RC218 which could reduce disease severity and the associated mycotoxin (deoxynivalenol) accumulation, sugested that it could be used as a biocontrol agent to reduce Fusarium head blight. But the mechanism still unknown and needs further study. In this work, we provided a Bacillus velezensis T-3 which could not only antagonize Fusarium graminearum, Fungi Imperficti, and Aspergillus flavus, but also degrade DON, ZEA, and AFB1 effectively, and could metabolize DON into a variety of low-toxic or non-toxic products (e.g., retinoic acid, glycine, malonic acid), these results provided a rich reference for further revealing the degradation mechanism of DON.

At present, few reports have clearly revealed the complete metabolic pathway of DON in Bacillus. However, many studies proved that the biosynthesis pathway of DON may consists of cyclisation, hydroxylation, epoxidation, acetylation and other reactions catalyzed by a variety of enzymes(Yao & Long, 2020). Subsequently, DON could be further degraded via de-epoxygenation, hydrolysis, glycosylation, and acetylation, etc. (Shanakhat, McCormick, Busman, Rich, & Bakker, 2022). Therefore, destroying the toxic groups of DON and transform it into various compounds with lower toxic or non-toxic is the key to realize effective detoxification. According to the chemical structure of DON, reports showed that the C12, C13 positions and C3-OH group of DON are the main toxic groups (Hou et al., 2024). In view of these toxic groups, Ito et al. found that the P450 cytochrome system in Sphingomonas sp. KSM1 is able to oxidize DON into 16-hydroxydeoxy Fusarium oxysporum allyl alcohol (16-HDNO), such significantly reduce the toxicity of DON (Ito et al., 2013). Poppenberger et al. presented a UDP-glycosyltransferase in Arabidopsis thaliana, which could catalyze DON into 3-O-glucopyranosyl-4-DON at the C3 position of DON (Poppenberger et al., 2003). Accordingly, Schweiger et al. expressed the gene encoding UDP-glycosyltransferase in Arabidopsis thaliana, thus increased its DON-resistant ability (Schweiger et al., 2010). However, although these studies have made gratifying achievements in DON degradation, most of them still focus on changing or destroying a certain group of DON to achieve detoxification, but lack of in-depth disclosure of the downstream metabolites of DON. In contrast, DON was used as a single fermentation carbon source for Bacillus velezensis T-3 in this work, and then the potential degradation products of DON systematically identified by metabonomics technology. Additionally, the potential degradation pathway of DON was deduced according to the chemical structure of some identified compounds and their known metabolic pathway, thus provided new insights for the biodegradation proccess of DON.

Corn by-products is a kind of important agricultural product widely used in feed and food production, which is easily contaminated by DON, suggesting that it is urgent to control the pollution of DON (Selvaraj et al., 2015). However, most of current studies used probiotics such as Bacillus subtilis, Bacillus licheniformis and Bacillus amyloliquefaciens or combine them with some enzymes (e.g., Laccase, oxidase, lactone hydrolase) to degrade the already produced DON (Li, Duan, et al., 2022), but few reports provided the strain that could effectively inhibit the growth of mycotoxins-producing molds and simultaneously degrade the already produced DON. To solve this problem, we presented a safety Bacillus velezensis T-3, which could obviously inhibit the growth of typical mycotoxins-producing fungi such as Fusarium graminearum, Penicillium chrysogenum and Aspergillus flavus, and effectively degrade the produced DON in corn by-products, thus showing important application value in the anti-mildew and fresh-keeping of corn by-products.

In summary, this work presented a safety B. velezensis T-3 which has good inhibitory activity against Fusarium graminearum, Penicillium chrysogenum, and Aspergillus flavus, and is able to degrade DON into a variety of low-toxicity or non-toxic products, thus contributing to reducing the hazard of DON. This study provides new technical method for mycotoxins prevention in the fields of agriculture, feed and food.
