Companion animals as potentially important reservoir of methicillin-resistant and virulent strains of coagulase-positive Staphylococcus spp
Authors: Agata Hahaj-Siembida, Aneta Nowakiewicz, Monika Greguła-Kania, Aleksandra Trościańczyk, Marcelina Osińska, Anna Tracz, Agnieszka Korzeniowska-Kowal, Ryszard Iwanicki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMicroorganisms such as Staphylococcus spp., in particular coagulase-positive strains, continue to pose a serious challenge to human and veterinary medicine. These bacteria can be a component of the natural biota of the body but can also cause infections in companion animals, including dogs and cats. Therefore, the aim of this study was to analyze the occurrence of Staphylococcus bacteria in companion animals, with particular emphasis on predilection sites, and to characterize these strains in the context of their epidemiological significance and threat to human health.ResultsWe demonstrated that the dominant species were S. aureus and S. pseudintermedius, isolated primarily from the skin and oral mucosa of dogs. The most frequently tested strains were phenotypically resistant to penicillin, tetracycline, clindamycin, and erythromycin (80.2%, 64.4%; 62.4% and 59.4% respectively), while phenotypic resistance to methicillin was showed in 15 S. aureus and 42 S. pseudintermedius strains (56.4%). However, ultimately, 39 strains originating solely from dogs (22,2%) were classified as methicillin-resistant based on presence of mecA gene. Multidrug resistance was confirmed for 69.3% of the strains. Detailed characterization of the MRSA and MRSP strains revealed resistance or intermediate susceptibility to vancomycin for six and 12 strains, respectively. Since, more than one strain was present in the same dog, VIS and VRS strains were isolated from 11 of the 90 dogs tested (12%).The dominant sequence types in MRSP were the expansive, multidrug-resistant clone ST551, while ST398 was the dominant one in S. aureus, which colonizes livestock and occasionally causes infections in these animals as well as companion animals.ConclusionOur studies have confirmed that clinically healthy dogs are a significant reservoir of multidrug-resistant and potentially virulent strains of S. aureus and S. pseudintermedius. Our research also confirmed that the same animal can harbor several strains belonging to the same species, differing in resistance and virulence. We also confirmed the increasing prevalence of methicillin- and vancomycin-resistant strains in the microbiota of the skin and mucous membranes of healthy dogs. In summary, our results confirm that companion animals are a relevant reservoir of Staphylococcus spp., including coagulase-positive Staphylococcus strains, which are resistant to multiple antimicrobials. Therefore, protocols for the use of antimicrobials in companion animals should be very critically reviewed, as they can be a crucial link in the chain of spread of MRSA and MRSP strains.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05593-7.Keywords: Antimicrobial resistance; Dogs; Cats; MRSA, MRSP; MLST; Staphylococcus; Virulence genes

Background

Companion animals such as dogs (Canis familiaris) and cats (Felis catus) have long been part of human life. The domestic dog (Canis familiaris) is a species descended from the gray wolf (Canis lupus), which inhabited Western Europe and Siberia. The dog was the first animal domesticated by humans, but the exact time of dog domestication is still a controversial topic among researchers. However, archaeological and genetic evidence indicates that domestication was a multi-phase process that began at least 15,000 years ago or more than 30,000 years ago [1, 2]. Another species that has acquired a global range of occurrence, adapted to the human environment, and, like the domestic dog, has undergone thousands of years of genetic change, is the domestic cat (Felis catus). The domestic cat probably descended directly from the African wild cat (Felis silvestris lybica). While some researchers often attribute the first domestication of cats to Egyptians around 4,000 years ago, numerous discoveries suggest that the species was actually domesticated approximately 9,500 years ago in Cyprus or other areas of the Middle East [3–5]. This centuries-old tradition suggests a long-standing relationship and close contact between these animals and humans, which is why they are called companion animals. Sharing a living space also means sharing the natural microbiota found in humans and animals, carrying the same microorganisms, and sharing the consequences of exposure to the same pathogens. One such shared microorganism is the genus Staphylococcus.

In a constantly changing society, increasingly stronger relationships are being observed between owners and their animals. Companion animals, especially dogs and cats live in close contact with their owners in their homes, but also share many public spaces where they come into contact with other animals or other people who are not their owners. In this context, a further problem is growing, as many pathogenic and commensal bacteria, especially those originating from animals, can spread and further acquire many new types of antimicrobial resistance. This consequently leads to the emergence of multidrug resistance (MDR), which is one of the most important public health problems [6–9]. Moreover, according to the latest list of drug-resistant bacteria posing the greatest threat to human health, methicillin-resistant strains of S. aureus are still classified as a group of high-priority pathogens [10].

Companion animals and their owners are frequent carriers of Staphylococcus spp [11]. As is known, both coagulase-positive Staphylococcus (CoPS) and coagulase-negative Staphylococcus (CoNS) can be a component of the body natural microbiota. Both transient and constant presence of Staphylococcus have been described in many different species, including humans and pets [12]. In healthy individuals, prevalence of Staphylococcus spp. is common in many areas of the body, especially on the skin and the mucus membranes of the oral cavity [12]. In favorable conditions, they can also be responsible for various types of infections in both humans and animals, such as wounds or surgical injuries, upper respiratory tract infections, urinary tract infections, and many others [9, 13, 14].

Many studies also indicate that CoPS species, including S. aureus and S. pseudintermedius, are a natural component of the skin and mucous membranes microbiota of pets, especially dogs [14–16]. The high frequency of CoPS isolation from this animal species indicates that these microorganisms are well adapted to the conditions prevailing on the skin and mucous membranes of dogs. Therefore, the detection of CoPS in dogs, especially S. pseudintermedius, is associated with long-term presence, rather than periodic colonization [16]. Therefore, companion animals, especially dogs, can constitute a significant reservoir of these microorganisms for both, other sensitive animal species and humans [14, 16, 17].

Due to their widespread occurrence, ease of spread, and potential public health threat, coagulase-positive Staphylococcus strains still require intensive monitoring and analysis, especially in term of diverse interactions with the host (as a commensal or pathogen) [18]. High adaptability to various conditions and hosts of these bacteria and remarkable variability resulting from their genomic plasticity, certainly determine the dual nature of Staphylococcus [19]. Hence, animal species that are particularly closely associated with humans should continue to be monitored for the occurrence and characteristics of this group of bacteria. Therefore, the aim of this study was to analyze the occurrence of Staphylococcus bacteria in companion animals, with particular emphasis on predilection sites, and to characterize the strains in the context of their epidemiological significance and threat to human health.

Results

Isolation and identification of Staphylococcus spp

Strains assigned to the Staphylococcus spp. were isolated from a total of 79 animals out of 105 tested (52.3%), with the dogs being the main carriers, as only 32 of them (35.5%) did not demonstrate the presence of these bacteria in any of the examined body regions. On the contrary, only 34.4% (n = 21) of the cats were carriers, and most of isolated strains belonged to CoNS, which were isolated from 18 cats. From six cats, seven strains CoPS were isolated. CoNS strains were also found in 18 dogs, but only in one case were they present in combination with CoPS. In the case of 39 dogs, the presence of one strain or strains belonging to CoPS was demonstrated. However, taking into account four different isolation sites, of which more than one strain was isolated from the individual, a total of 145 strains were obtained (117 from the dogs and 28 from the cats). Among all Staphylococcus isolates, 101 (69.9%) strains were identified as coagulase-positive Staphylococcus (CoPS) and 44 (30.4%) were coagulase-negative Staphylococcus (CoNS) (Table 1). This number included strains belonging to the same Staphylococcus species and isolated from the same individual, but they were analyzed as distinct due to differences in the phenotypic resistance profile and/or differences in the profiles of resistance and/or virulence genes and/or different profiles obtained in molecular typing ( see additional files Table S1, Figs S1 and S2). In the CoPS group, only two species were identified: most were S. pseudintermedius (n = 79; 78.2%), and only 22 strains (21.8%) were identified as S. aureus, including two and five strains isolated from cats respectively (Table 1).

The species diversity of the CoNS strains was much greater, as our study showed the presence of as many as 10 different species, including two belonging to the genus Mammaliicoccus (M. sciuri and M. lentus constituted 25% of all isolated CoNS); the remaining species were classified as Staphylococcus, among which the following were dominant: S. xylosus, S. warneri, S. haemolyticus, and S. simulans (Table 1).

Considering the site of isolation, the highest percentage of Staphylococcus strains originated from the skin (n = 53; 36.5%) and a comparable percentage from the oral mucosa (n = 47; 32.4%). The lowest percentage was found in the rectal swabs (n = 17; 11.7%). However, no statistically significant differences in occurrence were found between the CoPS and CoNS groups or between S. aureus and S. pseudintermedius at a significance level of p < 0.05 (Table 2).

Phenotypic drug resistance of coagulase-positive Staphylococcus (CoPS)

The resistance to all antimicrobial agents tested was observed, however in the case of linezolid, nitrofurantoin, rifampicin, and quinupristin/dalfopristin only single CoPS strains demonstrated phenotypic resistance (Table 3). The majority of strains in the CoPS group were resistant to penicillin (n = 81; 80.2%). A similarly high resistance rate of 56.4%-64.4% was observed for tetracycline, clindamycin, erythromycin, and cefoxitin/oxacillin (Table 3). In the case of strains isolated from cats, only resistance to penicillin and cefoxitin or oxacillin was observed (in five and three strains, respectively), and only one strain of S. aureus was resistant to erythromycin (Table 3). For the remaining antibiotics, such as sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim, ciprofloxacin, and gentamicin, the resistance rate was lower by at least half (31.7%-44.6%), and only single strains demonstrated phenotypic resistance in the case of linezolid, nitrofurantoin, rifampicin, and quinupristin/dalfopristin (Table 3). Statistically significant differences between S. aureus and S. pseudintermedius (p < 0.05) were observed only for sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim (Table 3).

Occurrence of resistance and virulence genes of CoPS

A relatively broad panel of resistance genes was analyzed, but none of the tested strains demonstrated mupirocin resistance or biocide resistance genes. For the remaining genes, a representative presence of resistance genes was demonstrated in each antibiotic group tested (Table 5).Statistically significantly higher levels of occurrence of four resistance genes, were observed in S. pseudintermedius (Table 5): the blaZ gene was associated with β-lactam resistance, the msrA gene was primarily responsible for macrolide resistance, the tetM gene determined tetracycline resistance, and aac(6′)-Ie-aph(2″)) gene reflected resistance to a broad spectrum of aminoglycosides (Table 5). A comparable relatively high percentage of the mecA gene was observed in both CoPS species (31.8 and 40.5% in S. aureus and S. pseudintermedius, respectively), however, the presence of the mecC gene was not confirmed in any of the tested strains. In general, in the case of S. pseudintermedius, a richer panel of resistance genes was observed, as only in this species was the presence of genes ermB, ermC, msrA, tetM, aac(6′)-Ie-aph(2″)), aph(3)-IIIa, and fusB detected (Table 5). Only one strain of S. aureus isolated from a cat was characterized by the presence of the blaZ gene (Table 5).

Despite the species specificity of S. aureus and S. pseudintermedius, a similar phenomenon was also observed for virulence genes. While in the case of S. aureus only a high percentage of the seB gene encoding enterotoxin (45.5%), LukED leukotoxin (36.4%), and Panton Valentine leukocidin (13.6%) were demonstrated, the S. pseudintermedius strains presented a much wider range of genes encoding virulence factors at a substantially higher level of occurrence (Table 6). In the case of toxins, the highest percentage was observed for two-component pore-forming leukocidin genes (lukF and lukS, 77.2% and 86.1%, respectively). Among the genes encoding enterotoxins, seL and seCcanine were dominant (87.3% and 86.1%, respectively). The gene encoding exfoliative toxins was mainly represented by the siet gene, occurring in 86.1% of strains. Genes encoding hemolysins hlgA and hlgB were also found at high levels (75.9% and 77.2%, respectively), as were genes responsible for biofilm formation, icaA and icaD (86.1%) (Table 6; Fig. S1).

Both strains of S. pseudintermedius isolated from cats were also characterized by a very broad panel of virulence genes, whereas among five S. aureus isolates from the same host species, only two were characterized by the presence of genes encoding leukotoxin, and one was found to contain the seB gene (Table 6).

Characteristics of methicillin-resistant strains (MRSA and MRSP)

MIC analysis

Seven S. aureus strains and 32 S. pseudintermedius strains were classified as methicillin-resistant based on presence of mec gene. All confirmed strains originated from dogs. MRSA strains were isolated from four animals, and MRSP strains were obtained from 17 animals, whereas both MRSA and MRSP were isolated from dog ID 10, methicillin resistant strains were detected in a total of 20 of the 90 dogs tested (22.2%). Strains belonging to the same Staphylococcus species isolated from the same animal differed in both their phenotypic resistance profiles and the panel of genes encoding resistance and virulence (Table 7). Minimum inhibitory concentrations were determined for these strains against 11 antimicrobials, including vancomycin.

In the vast majority of cases, the results of the classification of strains as resistant, intermediate, and susceptible with the microdilution method matched the results of the disk diffusion method for the tested strains. The MIC values for oxacillin obtained for the tested strains in all cases were at the level classifying the strains as resistant (MIC = 8–128 µg/ml). However, in the case of susceptibility to vancomycin, MIC results indicated intermediate susceptibility (VIS) to this antibiotic (MIC = 8 µg/ml) for two S. aureus strains and 10 S. pseudintermedius strains as well as four vancomycin-resistant (VRS) S. aureus strains (MIC = 64–128 µg/ml) and two S. pseudintermedius strains (Table 7). VIS and VRS strains were isolated from 11 of the 90 dogs tested (12%) and, similarly to MRSA and MRSP, dog no. 10 showed both vancomycin-insensitive CoPS species (two and one VRS strain of S. aureus and S. pseudintermedius, respectively) (Table 7).

MLST analysis

Based on the MLST analysis, the MRSA and MRSP isolates were classified into 11 different STs (sequence types), including 6 new ones that had not been reported before (Table 7). New STs were identified in two isolates of S. aureus (ST9311, ST9312) and four isolates of S. pseudintermedius (ST 2739, ST 2740, ST 2741, ST 2742). Other known sequence types were also detected in S. aureus, such as ST 398 (n = 4) and ST 7649 (n = 1). It should be emphasized, however, that different sequence types were also demonstrated in the S. aureus isolated from the same animals, i.e., S. aureus strains ST 9311, 7649, and 398 were isolated from dog no. 10, and ST398 and 9312 were detected in strains from dog no. 136.

In the case of S. pseudintermedius strains, apart from the new types, the dominant ST type was 551 (n = 23), and three and two strains, respectively, were assigned to ST 45 and ST 71. Sequence types ST 45 and 71 were isolated from different animals, while sequence type ST 551 in few cases occurred in the same animals, but the S. pseudintermedius strains in such animals differed significantly from each other in terms of both their resistance profile and virulence (Table 7). The nucleotide sequences of the housekeeping genes were deposited in GenBank under the following accession numbers for S. pseudintermedius: ack: PQ394212.1–PQ394181.1, cpn60: PQ381165.1–PQ381134.1, fdh: PQ394276.1–PQ394245.1, pta: PQ136690.1 –PQ136660.1, purA: PQ136723.1–PQ136691.1, sar: PQ394244.1–PQ394213, and tuf: PQ394180.1–PQ394149.1 and for S. aureus: tpi: PQ130075.1–PQ130069.1, aroE: PQ119405.1–PQ119399.1, arcC: PQ119393.1–PQ119387.1, glpF: PQ119417.1–PQ119411.1, gmk: PQ130051.1–PQ130045.1, pta: PQ130063.1–PQ130057.1, and ygiL: PQ130087.1–PQ130081.1.

ADSRRS-fingerprinting analysis

The dendrograms of similarity were based on MRSA and MRSP isolates originating exclusively from the dogs, as methicillin-resistant strains were only detected from this animal species. Due to the small number of S. aureus strains compared, a very low degree of differentiation was demonstrated. Based on a similarity coefficient of 90%, genomic profiles would not have been identified (Fig. S1), which is further confirmed by the images of very similar electrophoretic patterns of the tested strains (Fig. S2), regardless of the source of the strain and their ST types. In the case of S. pseudintermedius strains, the differentiation was slightly greater also due to the larger number of strains tested. However, based on a similarity coefficient of 90%, nine genotypic profiles were identified, which were similar to each other at varying levels, ranging from as little as 12% to as much as 82%. Individual profiles clustered from one to even 15 strains (Fig. S3), which were different in their source of origin, ST sequence type, and resistance and virulence profiles, and despite the high degree of similarity of the electrophoretic profiles, no correlation was found between them.

Discussion

Companion animals such as dogs and cats have been accompanying humans for hundreds of years, maintaining close contact with them, sometimes even being treated as family members. Therefore, it is important to remember that such close and constant contact carries a high risk of transferring microorganisms with varying levels of virulence, including multidrug-resistant microorganisms. Bacteria of the genus Staphylococcus occur in both humans and companion animals, so bidirectional transfer of this group of bacteria is possible and frequent, particularly coagulase-positive species [16]. Our study demonstrated a relatively high prevalence of Staphylococcus spp. in the animals studied, particularly in the dogs, in which these microorganisms were detected in 52.3% of the dogs tested. Among the cats, we demonstrated an only 34.4% occurrence rate, with coagulase-negative strains predominating among the species identified, as only 25% of all strains isolated from the cats were assigned to two coagulase-positive species: S. aureus and S. pseudintermedius. Interestingly, S. felis, a species closely associated with the cat as a host, was recovered from only one cat.

Our results are quite variable compared to studies conducted by other authors in Poland, but it should be noted that the isolation rate and species composition of Staphylococcus spp. depend largely on the clinical status of the host, the isolation site, and the level of contact with humans. Screening studies conducted by Miszczak et al. [20] on clinically healthy dogs and cats and with symptoms of infection showed a higher prevalence of Staphylococcus spp. in the cats compared to the dogs and a predominance of S. felis and S. epidermidis. A study carried out by Bierowiec et al. [21], in turn, demonstrated that keeping cats together with dogs determines the variability of the Staphylococcus species profile and promotes a higher prevalence of S. pseudintermedius, a species associated with the dog as a host. Moreover, increased contact with humans may lead to a higher prevalence of S. aureus [22]. The study region is also a factor modulating the Staphylococcus species profile in companion animals and even their owners. The species of companion animals and the level of contact between humans and these animals depends largely on cultural differences and traditions prevailing in a given region or country. For example, in a study conducted by Abdullahi et al. [23] on healthy dogs and their owners in Spain, S. epidermidis and S. hominis predominated in both species, which may indicate close contact and mutual exchange of microorganisms. Furthermore, a study by Cuny et al. [11] demonstrated that S. aureus is transmitted more frequently between humans and animals than S. pseudintermedius between dogs and their owners.

However, similar to studies reported by other authors [24, 25], the highest percentage of Staphylococcus isolates was obtained from the skin and oral mucosa of the studied animals, confirming that these areas are the most important predilection sites for collecting material in screening tests. Collecting only feces or rectal swabs for Staphylococcus screening tests may result in a significantly lower percentage of positive isolations than in the case of skin, oral mucosa or nostrils, which are the dominant sites of these microorganisms not only in animals [18, 26] but also in humans [27]. The low proportion of Staphylococcus species in the gut microbiota in dogs is also confirmed by studies based on whole mitochondrial genome sequencing [23].

Because the coagulase-positive species isolated in the present study, S. aureus and S. pseudintermedius, are of greatest epidemiological importance, further analysis was limited to these species and included only 101 isolated strains. In general, a very high percentage of phenotypic resistance to commonly used antibiotics, mainly β-lactams, tetracycline, clindamycin, and erythromycin, was observed, ranging from 80 to 60% in the tested strains, which is related to the widespread use of these antibiotic groups in dogs, especially β -lactams [28]. No statistically significant differences in the level of resistance were found between S. aureus and S. pseudintermedius; resistance in S. pseudintermedius was significantly higher than in S. aureus only in the case of sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim.

The high level of resistance to individual antibiotics also translated into the percentage of multidrug resistance, which in both species was 60–70%, although, again, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two species. This result is not surprising, as the strains of both species originated from the same animals and were subjected to the same selective pressure. It should also be noted that the phenotypic resistance often coincided with the presence of the most prevalent resistance genes. In this case, among the S. pseudintermedius strains, we detected the presence of resistance genes at higher, statistically significant levels, including blaZ, msrA, ermB, tetM, and aac(6′)-Ie-aph(2″)), which, with the exception of blaZ, were present only in S. pseudintermedius. The presence of ermC, aph(3)-IIIa, and fusB was also observed only in this species. Therefore, it should be emphasized that the panel of resistance genes among the studied S. pseudintermedius strains was significantly richer, compared to the S. aureus isolates. Similar results were obtained by Miszczak et al. [15], although the statistical significance of the differences between S. aureus and S. pseudintermedius resistance was not determined in this study. It is worth emphasizing that the plasmid-mediated fusB gene for fusidate resistance was demonstrated among three S. pseudintermedius strains. Due to their very narrow spectrum of activity, fusidates are mainly used in humans to treat infections with methicillin-resistant S. aureus strains. Generally, even among MRSA strains isolated from humans, resistance to fusidates is quite low and does not exceed a few percent [29], while in our study as many as two fusB-positive strains belonged to MRSP, which seems to be quite an alarming phenomenon.

Another noteworthy fact is the presence of the aac(6′)-Ie-aph(2″)) gene, encoding an aminoglycoside-modifying enzyme, at a level of as much as 24% among the S. pseudintermedius strains. The gene is responsible for the inactivation of a very wide range of aminoglycosides, including gentamicin, kanamycin, tobramycin, netilmicin, and amikacin. They are widely used in both human and veterinary medicine, and resistance to these antibiotics significantly limits therapeutic options, including the abolition of the synergistic effect when used in combination with beta-lactams and many other drugs [30]. Among the tested isolates, 39 isolates (mainly belonging to S. pseudintermedius) were finally classified as methicillin-resistant strains based on the presence of the mecA gene. This indicates that, in the studied pool of 145 isolates, they constituted as much as 27%, although it should be noted that these isolates originated from 20 dogs, which constituted 25% of the studied animal population. The presence of this gene was not confirmed in the other 18 strains phenotypically resistant to cefoxitin or oxacillin. However, there are many other mechanisms of reduced susceptibility to methicillin independent of the presence of the mecA gene, particularly among borderline oxacillin-resistant S. aureus, modified S. aureus, or with overproduction or overexpression of genes encoding β-lactamase [31]. Although the mecC gene is increasingly becoming a factor determining phenotypic resistance to beta-lactams, especially among S. aureus strains, it is characterized by a much lower percentage of occurrence [32], which is also indicated by the results of our studies. Initially, this gene was associated primarily with livestock-associated strains (LA-MRSA), but its presence has also been demonstrated in strains from both humans and other animal species. It has also been confirmed that infections in humans and animals with mecC-positive strains were identified decades ag

o [33], although its spread appears to be much slower [32]. Moreover, the mecC gene is mainly associated with the S. aureus species, which was not the dominant species in the present study, whereas among MRSP strains the dominant variant is mecA [34].

The colonization of at least one-quarter of the animal population by MRS strains (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus) is currently not surprising in light of the results reported by other authors, who showed a similar (24%) [35] or even 48% percentage of MRS strain carriers among dogs and cats [36]. This percentage appears to have increased significantly in recent years compared to data from 2019 to 2021 in Germany [37] and 2019–2023 in Poland [15], which is also clearly indicated in the study conducted by Sobkowich et al. [38] in 2021–2022, illustrating a clearly increasing trend in the occurrence of MRSA and MRSP among companion animals.

The virulence gene profile among the tested strains was also particularly rich among the S. pseudintermedius strains. It should be noted that the tested strains exhibited a high percentage of genes encoding toxins (TSST-1), enterotoxins (SEs), and exfoliative toxins, which may have superantigen activity and, in the case of systemic infection, may cause a cytokine storm, which can lead to toxic shock symptoms, including host death [39]. Equally noteworthy is the presence of genes encoding the Panton Valentin toxin in three S. aureus strains. This leukocidin has the strongest cytotoxic effect on human leukocytes, causing cell lethality [40]. Therefore, PVL-positive strains are frequently associated with cases of necrotizing pneumonia and chronic or recurrent soft tissue and skin infections (SSTIs) in humans [41]. The equivalent of leukocidin in S. pseudintermedius is the two-component toxin Luk I-S and Luk I-F, which has the ability to kill canine polymorphonuclear cells by pore formation and lysis [42] and is quite common among S. pseudintermedius strains, especially those isolated from cases of infection [43, 44]. Similarly to studies reported by other authors, the S. pseudintermedius strains isolated in this study contained genes encoding the SEL and SEQ enterotoxins [45] and a variant of the SEC enterotoxin (seCcanine), which, together with the exfoliative toxin encoded by the siet gene, may exacerbate cases of atopic dermatitis in dogs [46]. A large percentage of dogs also demonstrated the presence of the expB gene (also called exiB), which encodes a toxin responsible for the degradation of canine desmoglein [39], thus causing splitting of the subcorneal layer of the epidermis, actively participating in the pathogenesis of impetigo in dogs [44]. We also demonstrated that most of the tested S. pseudintermedius strains have the potential to produce biofilm [47], as we detected the ica operon genes in over 80% of the isolates.

Similarly, the high percentage of epb and spsE genes, encoding proteins involved in adhesion, i.e., elastin-binding protein EbpS, fibrinogen/fibronectin-binding protein SpsE, and components A and B of γ-hemolysin [48], means that the S. pseudintermedius strains isolated in the current study as commensals have a very high invasive potential, and these factors may significantly facilitate adhesion and invasion in the development of potential infection [49].

MRSA and MRSP strains currently pose a significant therapeutic challenge, as infections caused by these bacteria are very difficult to treat and usually require the use of so-called last-resort drugs [50]. Such drugs include glycopeptides; therefore, in the case of methicillin-resistant strains, we expanded the analysis panel to include vancomycin MIC assessment, sequence typing, and genotyping using the ADSRRS-fingerprinting method. Our study revealed that 11 dogs carried vancomycin-resistant (VRS) (MIC > 8 µg/mL) or vancomycin-intermediate (VIS) (MIC = 4–8 µg/mL) strains. Some animals carried more than one strain, for example, dog no. 10 was a carrier of as many as four strains belonging to both species, which may indicate the presence of genes that spread easily through horizontal exchange. The most common genes responsible for generating resistance to glycopeptides, as in Enterococcus spp., include the vanA and vanB genes. In a study conducted by Miszczak et al. [15] on dogs and cats from Poland, the authors also demonstrated a relatively high prevalence of these genes, with a significant predominance of the vanA gene among S. aureus and S. pseudintermedius strains. In turn, a study by Carvalho dos Santos [51] showed a carriage rate of 29% for vancomycin-resistant strains isolated from dogs, although molecular mechanisms of resistance were not investigated in this study either. Vancomycin resistance among strains isolated from animals varies considerably over the years and also depends on the animal species. The ban on the use of growth promoters, including avoparcin, which cross-generated vancomycin resistance, resulted in a gradual decrease in the percentage of strains resistant to this antibiotic, primarily among farm animals (pigs and cattle) [52, 53]. However, in some countries, the level of vancomycin-resistant Staphylococcus strains remains quite high, even exceeding 70% [54], while in others these values are lower, at a few percent, but still significant for public health safety [50, 51].This indicates that the occurrence of VRS and VIS strains in companion and farm animals still poses a real threat to public health, particularly in the context of methicillin resistance in these strains [55, 56]. However, while the mechanism of vancomycin resistance among strains isolated from farm animals results from the unauthorized use of glycopeptides in the past, it is not obvious in companion animals, as glycopeptides were used very rarely and in very limited cases [57]. Due to the fact that the most common genetic mechanism of resistance to glycopeptides is located on elements easily transferred through horizontal gene transfer, their exchange between different species and genera of bacteria is also possible. Studies by Vo et al. [58] have shown that coinfection of patients with vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus and S. aureus strains also generates vancomycin insensitivity in the latter species, associated with the acquisition of a van-A containing plasmid and its integration into the chromosome, followed by further vertical dissemination. It should also be noted that plasmids containing vancomycin resistance genes often also carry resistance genes to other groups of antimicrobials, therefore the spread of vancomycin resistance is not always associated with selection pressure [59]. Unfortunately, our studies did not include confirmation with the presence of the van operon genes. This is certainly a limitation of this study, but it should be noted that in the case of resistance, especially in the case of VIS, there are at least several other mechanisms associated with changes in regulation or the occurrence of mutations in specific genes, which allow Staphylococcus strains to adapt to vancomycin and result in a change in the MIC value [60]. Therefore routine diagnostic testing drug susceptibility for Staphylococcus spp. strains isolated from companion and farm animals, should include this antimicrobial and other last-resort antibiotics in the tested profile. Such strategy would allow for tracking potential developing resistance trends in bacteria originated from animals which have a significant impact on the level and scope of drug resistance in microorganisms isolated from clinical cases of human infections in the context of one health. The only problem with assessing vancomycin resistance is the necessity to use the microdilution method (which we also used in this study); however the disk diffusion assay is the most common method in veterinary diagnostic laboratories.

For MRSA and MRSP strains, we also determined sequence types for epidemiological purposes. It should be noted that the newly identified STs among both S. aureus and S. pseudintermedius demonstrate the ongoing evolution of high genomic plasticity in these species, and the obtained data are even more valuable because they include methicillin-resistant strains.

ST398 was the dominant type in the case of the remaining five MRSA strains; it was noted among MRSA strains rather as colonizing livestock and occasionally causing infections in these animals and companion animals [61]. However, its zoonotic potential as a causative agent of systemic infections in humans has also been demonstrated [62]. It should also be emphasized that our two ST398 strains are PVL positive, which significantly increases their infectious potential [63]. ST551 clearly dominated among the S. pseudintermedius strains. An extremely multidrug-resistant MRSP strain with the same sequence type was found in a dog with otitis externa [64]. However, it appears that MRSP strains with this sequence type are widespread in Poland, and as predicted by Kizerwetter Świda et al. [65], this sequence type displaced ST71, a finding also confirmed by our present research. It should be noted that ST551 strains possess a very rich panel of virulence factors, which may favor their spread and colonization of new hosts. The high adaptive potential of this type is also evidenced by its widespread occurrence not only in Europe but also in South Africa and the USA [66]. It should be emphasized that the other two sequence types identified in this study, ST 71 and ST45, are also alarming and widespread pathogens characterized by high drug resistance and zoonotic potential [12, 67].

Despite demonstrating slight differentiation and identifying nine genotypic profiles involving ADSRRS-fingerprinting, we did not observe any correlation between the profile affiliation, sequence type, or resistance or virulence gene profile. Our previous studies conducted using this method for S. aureus and S. pseudintermedius strains [68] demonstrated differentiated electrophoretic profiles; however, due to the highly diverse sources of strain isolation (free-living animals) and the small number of detected resistance genes, we did not analyze such a correlation in our previous study. In this case, selecting only MRSA and MRSP strains for the study may have resulted in the method failing to demonstrate the expected discriminatory potential. Another reason could be the use of a different, rarely cutting restriction enzyme (BglI) than in the studies of the method’s precursor, Krawczyk et al. [69], who used BamHI, which may also have reduced the ability to differentiate strains. This result constitutes a limitation of the study and indicates the need to modify the method when analyzing MRSA or MRSP strains, especially those isolated from the same animals.

However, the diverse resistance and virulence gene panels, phenotypic resistance results, and different sequence types isolated from the same animals clearly indicate that a single host can be a source of strains differing in terms of resistance and virulence, not only belonging to the same species but also all characterized by methicillin resistance. Our study also has some limitations related to the relatively narrow panel of analyzed resistance mechanisms, because we only took into account the most common resistance mechanisms transmitted via horizontal gene transfer.

Conclusion

It is important to note that our studies have confirmed that clinically healthy dogs can be a significant reservoir of multidrug-resistant and potentially virulent strains of S. aureus and S. pseudintermedius. This reservoir should not be underestimated, as despite their typical animal host, the increasing adaptation of S. pseudintermedius to humans and their ability to cause have been repeatedly confirmed. On the other hand, it is also necessary to point out a certain limitation of this study on dogs, which, in order to fully meet the criteria for the definition of carrier, and thus reservoir, should be repeated after a certain period of time in order to confirm the permanent presence in dogs of strains of significant importance for public health. However our studies also confirmed a disturbingly high percentage of methicillin-, vancomycin-, and multidrug-resistant strains, which were reported as isolated cases in animals only a few years ago. This growing tendency may already constitute confirmation of the phenomenon of carriage of these strains in dogs rather than just transient colonization. The high potential for variability, ease of spread, and plasticity of the Staphylococcus genome, as also indicated by the changing profile of dominant sequence types (ST71 dominated before 2015, and now ST551), mean that companion animals may now constitute a reservoir of strains with epidemiological significance comparable to humans and may constitute a crucial link in the chain of their spread. Therefore, there is a justified need for full monitoring and the development of highly restrictive protocols regarding the use of specific groups of antimicrobials in veterinary medicine, not only for animals that are a source of food but also for companion animals, which, as the name suggests, very often accompany people in everyday life and remain in close contact, thereby facilitating the transfer of alarming pathogens.

Materials and methods

Collection of samples

The research material was collected as swabs from 151 companion animals (90 dogs and 61 cats) from four different body areas (groin area skin, external ear canal, anus, oral mucosa). The material was collected with a sterile swab moistened with physiological fluid during a routine clinical examination by veterinarians. Finally, a total of 604 swabs were collected from animals in veterinary clinics located in Lublin Voivodeship, eastern Poland (51°15′N, 22°34′E). The age, sex, and breed of the animals were not considered as criteria, whereas the exclusion criterion was any infection and/or antibiotic therapy within the last three months of sampling.

Isolation and identification of Staphylococcus spp

The material was transported on AMIES medium (Medlab-Products, Raszyn, Poland) to the Department of Veterinary Microbiology, University of Life Sciences in Lublin immediately after collection. Then, the samples were cultured in nutrient broth supplemented with 6.5% NaCl (Biomaxima, Lublin Poland) and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. The next steps involved using standard media for selection and differentiation of Staphylococcus spp. (Mannitol Salt LAB Agar and Baird Parker LAB Agar, Biomaxima, Lublin, Poland), and the samples were incubated again for 24 h at 37 °C. Additionally, the samples were simultaneously cultured on Chromagar MRSA medium (Graso Biotech, Poland) and incubated under the same conditions. One typical colony was selected from each positive sample, for further analysis. Then, for preliminary identification, Gram staining (Gram Staining Kit), catalase (3% hydrogen peroxide solution), and coagulase tests (BBL™ Coagulase Plasma) were performed according to the manufacturers’ instructions (BD Polska sp. z o.o., Warsaw, Poland, Merck, sp. z o.o., Warsaw, Poland). The test tubes used rabbit plasma (0.5 ml) and overnight broth cultures of the tested strains. Clot formation (positive reaction) was checked periodically for up to 24 h. Analysis of all bacterial isolates was performed using the Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption Ionization Time-of-Flight (MALDI TOF) method according to a procedure described previously [70].

Isolation of bacterial DNA was performed using a commercial Gram Plus and Yeast Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Eurx, Gdańsk, Poland) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Coagulase-positive strains with an identification result ranging from 3,000 to 1,700 log scores (2.300–3.000: Highly probable species identification, 2.000–2.299: Secure genus identification, probable species identification, 1.700–1.999: Probable genus identification, according to protocol Bruker Daltonics GmbH & Co. KG) were additionally confirmed by a molecular method based on the analysis of nuc gene polymorphism [70–72]. S. pseudintermedius ATCC 49,444 and S. aureus ATCC 25,923 were used as a quality control.

Disk diffusion test

Drug susceptibility of isolated strains was assessed using the disk diffusion method (Kirby–Bauer test) on Mueller Hilton medium (Biomaxima, Lublin, Poland). Fourteen antimicrobial substances were selected: erythromycin 15 µg, oxacillin 1 µg (S. pseudintermedius only), cefoxitin 30 µg (S. aureus only), gentamicin 10 µg, rifampicin 5 µg, quinupristin/dalfopristin 15 µg, clindamycin 2 µg (alone and in tests detecting induced resistance to clindamycin in Staphylococcus spp.), penicillin G 10 U, sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim 25 µg, tetracycline 30 µg, nitrofurantoin 300 µg, linezolid 30 µg, ciprofloxacin 5 µg, and chloramphenicol 30 µg (Thermo Scientific™ Oxoid™). The procedure and interpretation of the results were in accordance with the recommendations of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute [73, 74]. S. aureus ATCC 25,923 was used as a quality control. Multidrug resistance of strains was classified according to the criteria and antimicrobial agents defined as resistance to three or more antimicrobial classes [75].

Detection of resistance genes

All coagulase-positive Staphylococcus strains were tested for the presence of genes encoding phenotypic resistance. The genes encoding resistance to β-lactams, macrolides, phenicols, tetracyclines, aminoglycosides, fusidates, mupirocin and biocides were selected (Table S2) [76–81].

DNA amplification was performed under the conditions described previously [82], using Silver Taq Mix (Syngen Biotech, Wrocław, Poland) and appropriately selected primers (Genomed, Warsaw, Poland).

Detection of virulence genes

For S. aureus isolates, the following virulence genes were selected: seA, seB, seC, seD, seE (encoding enterotoxins SEA-E), tsst (encoding toxic shock syndrome toxin-1 (TSST-1), etA, etB (encoding exfoliative toxins ETA and ETB), PVL (encoding leukocidin components S and F), and LukE-LukD (encoding leukotoxin LUKE/LUKD) [70, 81]. S. pseudintermedius was assessed for the presence of other virulence genes: ebpS (encoding elastin binding proteins), spsE (encoding fibronectin binding proteins), lip (encoding lipase), hlgA, hlgB (encoding hemolysins), lukF, lukS (encoding components of leukocidin), icaA, icaD (encoding components of biofilm formation), siet (encoding exfoliative toxin), tsst (encoding shock toxin), seL, seM, seQ, seCcanine(encoding enterotoxins),expA,expB(encoding exfoliative toxins),andbap(encoding biofilm)(Table S2)[44, 49,82,83].

Water was used as a negative control instead of the matrix, while validated DNA templates containing the target resistance and/or virulence genes obtained from strains from the Department’s own collection and the ATTC collection were used as positive controls (S. aureus ATCC 43300, ATCC 33591, ATCC 49775, ATCC 13565).

Analysis of methicillin-resistant coagulase-positive Staphylococcus strains

Minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC)

The susceptibility of isolated methicillin-resistant coagulase-positive Staphylococcus was evaluated with the microdilution method described by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute [73, 74]. The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC) of the following antimicrobials were tested: oxacillin, penicillin, erythromycin, clindamycin, chloramphenicol, tetracycline, gentamicin, rifampicin, nitrofurantoin, ciprofloxacin, and vancomycin (0.25–128 µg/mL) (Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany). S. aureus ATCC 29,213 was used as a quality control.

Multilocus sequence typing (MLST)

MLST analysis of S. aureus and S. pseudintermedius strains was performed according to the protocols described by Enright et al. [84] and Solyman [85] respectively. Specific multiplex-PCR reactions for S. aureus were performed based on seven housekeeping genes: arcC, aroE, glp, gmk, pta, tpi, and yqiL, and for S. pseudintermedius: ack, cpn60, fdh, pta, purA, sar, and tuf. The reactions were performed in a volume of 25 µl using Silver Taq MIX (Syngen Biotech, Wrocław, Poland) and specific primers (Genomed S.A., Warsaw, Poland). Then, the Clean-up kit (A&A Biotechnology, Gdańsk, Poland) was used to purify PCR products. DNA sequencing was performed using the Sanger method by Genomed S.A. (Warsaw, Poland). The obtained nucleotide sequences were compared with alleles in the PubMLST (https://pubmlst.org/) database. Then, based on allele combinations, STs and clonal complexes were assigned. Nucleotide sequences of genes were deposited in GenBank (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genbank/).

Amplification of DNA fragments surrounding rare restriction sites (ADSRRS-fingerprinting)

ADSRRS-fingerprinting was performed according to the protocol described by Krawczyk et al. [86] with additional modifications [68, 87]. First, genomic DNA was digested with two restriction enzymes: XbaI (10U) and BglII (5U) (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, USA) at 37 °C for 1 h. The next step involved a ligation reaction (25 °C for 1 h) using T4 DNA ligase (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, USA) and appropriate adapters (Genomed S.A., Warsaw, Poland). Then, after heat inactivation at 70 °C for 5 min, a PCR reaction was performed in a volume of 25 µl containing 1 µl of ligation mix, Silver Taq MIX (Syngen Biotech, Wroclaw, Poland), and 50 pmol of each primer (Genomed S.A., Warsaw, Poland). PCR was performed using the following temperature-time profile: 94 °C–5 min, 72 °C–5 min, 94 °C–5 min, during 22 cycles (94 °C–30 s, 60 °C–30 s and 72–90 s), and 72 °C–7 min. Electrophoresis of PCR products was performed in an 8% polyacrylamide gel, which were then stained with ethidium bromide. The gel was visualized and documented in the GelDoc Go System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, USA). Subsequently, the analysis of similarity of electrophoretic profiles was performed with the UPGMA method using the BIO-1D + + 11.9 gel analysis software (Vilber, Lourmat, France) based on the Jaccard correlation coefficient (level of optimization 1%) [63, 82].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Statistica 13.1 software (StatSoft, Cracow, Poland). The Mann-Whitney U test (p < 0.05) was used to analyze statistically significant differences between S. aureus and S. pseudintermedius in terms of the isolation site, drug resistance, and occurrence of antibacterial resistance genes as well as MDR.
