Immense potential for self-healing with conservative management of a large radicular cyst associated with pulpectomized primary tooth: A 40-month follow-up
Authors: Morankar Rahul, Riya M. Johnson, Sruthila Raghuthaman, Nitesh Tewari
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Radicular cysts are one of the most common cysts of inflammatory origin involving the maxillofacial region that arise from the remnants of Herwig’s epithelial root sheath. These are often diagnosed as incidental findings on radiographs, which are seldom symptomatic and are slowly progressive. Decompression could be used as a conservative treatment approach for the management of large radicular cysts in children to have satisfactory healing and minimum damage to adjacent vital structures, simultaneously allowing the eruption of succedaneous tooth. This paper discusses the management of a large radicular cyst in a 12 year old, causing displacement of the mandibular canal and developing tooth germ using decompression that resulted in bony healing and spontaneous eruption of displaced succedaneous premolar, with a periodic follow-up for 40 months. Although decompression is a well-known approach, the present case with long-term follow-up emphasizes the potential of spontaneous healing in pediatric radicular cyst cases restoring the anatomy to normal without any functional impairment. This paper also highlights the importance of periodic clinical and radiographic follow-up in pulpally treated nonvital primary teeth.

INTRODUCTION

Periapical cysts, also known as radicular cysts, are the most commonly occurring maxillofacial odontogenic cysts of inflammatory origin. These cysts are thought to arise from the epithelial rests of Malassez, which are the remnants of Hertwig’s epithelial root sheath and are seen associated with a nonvital tooth either due to caries, trauma, or an endodontically treated tooth.[1] Radicular cyst associated with the primary tooth is a rare occurrence, and their reported incidence ranges from 0.5% to 3.3%.[23] In the initial stages, they remain asymptomatic and are diagnosed on routine radiographic investigations. However, large cystic lesions can cause pain, soft tissue swelling, facial disfigurement due to cortical bone expansion, tooth mobility, and displacement of permanent tooth buds and nerve canals.[4]

Conservative management strategies for periapical cysts include nonsurgical endodontic therapy, extraction of an offending tooth, decompression, marsupialization, or a combination of the above techniques depending on the clinical scenario.[4] This paper discusses the management of a large periapical cyst associated with a previously endodontically treated primary tooth #75 [FDI Notation] by decompression method using a modified acrylic splint with suction catheter tubing intra-alveolar extension resulted in the spontaneous eruption of a displaced permanent successor tooth. This novel appliance design used for decompression can 1) maintain the patency of the cystic cavity, 2) serve as a nonfunctional space maintainer to prevent space loss, 3) have the simplicity of fabrication and retention with the help of adjacent teeth, 4) have the ease of maintenance of oral hygiene as being removable, and 5) allow the sequential trimming of the acrylic plug to allow the tooth eruption.

CASE REPORT

A 12-year-old male child reported a complaint of intermittent pain and recurrent swelling concerning the upper right back tooth region for the last five months. The patient gave a history of sharp, continuous, non-radiating pain concerning carious tooth # 65 [FDI notation] with pain and tenderness on percussion, which aggravated during meals and relieved only on medication [Figure 1]. There was a history of pulpectomy treatment with tooth # 75 [FDI notation] three years back, and the patient has been asymptomatic since then. The patient was in the late mixed dentition stage, and, thus, an orthopantomogram (OPG) was done as a part of a routine investigation. The OPG revealed an incidental finding of a large, unilocular, well-defined radiolucency around the apical area of the primary mandibular left-second molar extending in mesiodistal direction, mesially to the distal root of tooth # 33 [FDI notation] and distally up to the mesial root of the tooth # 36 [FDI notation], enclosing the tooth bud of permanent second premolar [Figure 1]. There were no significant clinical findings about this region on extraoral as well as intraoral examination. A cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) revealed a large radiolucent lesion with the expansion of the buccal cortex and thinning of the buccal cortical plate with the displacement of the inferior alveolar canal [Figure 1]. The lesion appears to have originated from the previously endodontically treated tooth 75 [FDI notation], also encircling the developing tooth bud of tooth # 35.

Preoperative radiographic findings depicting the extent of the cystic cavity

Blood investigations revealed that the parameters were within normal limits. After the patient's consent, a diagnostic aspiration was performed demonstrating a pale, straw-colored fluid with a shimmering effect. Considering the extent of the lesion, proximity to the vital structures, and thinning of cortical plates, cyst decompression was planned. An alginate impression was made followed by mock grooving of the cast and the appliance fabrication with an acrylic plug component that extends intra-alveolar to maintain the patency of the cystic cavity and helps to drain the cystic fluid [Figure 2]. The intra-alveolar extension was later modified and fitted with a suction catheter tube [Size 10] for flexibility and ease of placement and removal owing to the configuration of the cystic cavity. Extraction of tooth # 75 was performed under local anesthesia (lignocaine 2% with 1:80000 adrenaline). The mucoperiosteal flap was elevated buccal to the teeth 34, 75, and 36, and access was gained to the cystic lumen [Figure 2]. The part of cystic lining was removed and sent for histopathological examination that revealed the arcading pattern of stratified squamous epithelium with dense inflammatory cell infiltration under 4× magnification and cystic lumen with 1–3 layers of cuboidal, nonkeratinized stratified squamous epithelium and connective tissue interspersed with dense inflammatory cells, fibroblasts, and blood vessels under 10× magnification, suggestive of the radicular cyst [Figure 3]. The patient was further scheduled for a regular follow-up to assess the bony healing, spontaneous eruption, and alignment of the succedaneous premolar. Simultaneously, the other carious teeth were also treated as per the need.

The patient was asymptomatic on follow-up evaluations, and a decompression appliance was in place for the initial three months with a gradual reduction in height of the intra-alveolar suction catheter tube extension. The instructions were given to the patient during this period regarding the maintenance of oral hygiene with periodic recalls at every three-month interval. The appliance was further replaced with a lingual holding arch to prevent space loss. At the 40-month follow-up, the patient continued to be asymptomatic with bony healing and continuous root development and eruption of tooth #35 [FDI notation] [Figures 4 and 5]. A significant change can be noticed in the position of the displaced inferior alveolar canal after the healing [Figure 6].

DISCUSSION

Pulp inflammation and necrosis following dental caries are the most common etiological factors for the development of a radicular cyst.[56] Nonvital pulp therapy is the recommended treatment option for primary teeth with irreversible pulpal damage or apical periodontitis.[7] There seems to be a connection between the characteristic intraepithelial inclusions in the cyst walls and the intracanal medications, and this could serve as a site for ongoing antigenic stimulation.[8] The majority of cysts associated with primary molar are developed in the inter-radicular area, where they can damage the developing permanent successors or cause bony expansion and resorption.[9] Most of the radicular cysts are slowly progressive, asymptomatic, and incidentally discovered when radiographs are taken like the incidental discovery of the lesion in the present case.[10]

The various treatment options, including enucleation with/without peripheral ostectomy, marsupialization, and decompression, have been reported in the literature with variable success. This depends on the age and cooperation of the child, history of any prior treatment, location of the cyst, size of the lesion, vital structures involved, and the anticipated recurrence rate of the lesion.[11] The objective is to select a therapeutic approach that will completely eradicate the lesion with the least amount of morbidity and recurrence risk. Although the enucleation aids in the removal of the complete cystic lining, decompression is a conservative approach in the child patient as it is less invasive and preserves the permanent tooth bud and blood vessels and nerves in its proximity.[5] Russel[12] (1936) suggested relieving intraluminal cystic pressure to facilitate the complete resolution of the lesion and subsequent regeneration of bone with less chance of recurrence. Moreover, the time required for decompression is less in children due to their higher osteogenic activity, thus reducing the appliance wear period.[13] The present case report describes the conservative management of a radicular cyst associated with a previously endodontically treated primary molar tooth with a 40-month follow-up. The size of the cystic lesion was unusually large in the present case with the displacement of a premolar tooth germ and inferior alveolar canal; therefore, decompression was planned to have satisfactory healing and minimum damage to adjacent vital structures. Pei et al.[5] (2022) demonstrated that decompression was an effective option for the management of radicular cysts associated with primary teeth with an average healing time of 4.96 ± 2.07 months with a greater reduction rate among larger cysts when compared to smaller cysts. Similarly, Gao et al.[14] (2014) in their study concluded that the change in bone density was affected by the pathologic type of the lesions with the fastest healing in radicular cysts compared to the keratocystic odontogenic tumors and ameloblastomas.[14] This emphasizes the importance of the choice of a treatment plan according to the patient’s age, size, and location of the cyst and its pathologic variant.

The pulpectomy treatment in a primary tooth is intended to remove an irreversibly inflamed or necrotic dental pulp. The necrosis of the dental pulp usually causes a continuous source of inflammation, which may give rise to radicular cysts from epithelial residues of the periodontal ligament.[15] The presence of cysts can be either from foci of infection from a carious tooth or because of obturating material causing continuous irritation. Thus, along with dental caries, a pulpectomized tooth shall also be considered a potential risk factor for the development of radicular cyst necessitating a need for periodic follow-up, which was not performed in the present case due to the nonreporting of the patient during follow-up. The present case report thus explains the healing potential of pediatric radicular cysts; in addition, it also advocates the importance of routine follow-up of pulpectomized primary teeth for early intervention in clinically asymptomatic cases.[16]

CONCLUSION

The radicular cysts are often asymptomatic and slowly progressive; diagnosed as an incidental finding on radiograph, it emphasizes the importance of periodic radiographic examination, especially the primary teeth with dental caries and those that have undergone endodontic treatment. Decompression can be used as a conservative treatment approach for the management of large radicular cysts in children to have satisfactory healing with the minimum damage to adjacent vital structures simultaneously allowing the development and eruption of succedaneous teeth.
