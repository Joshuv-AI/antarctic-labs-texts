L3 paravertebral block combined with retro-psoas compartment block versus femoral nerve block for postoperative analgesia in total knee arthroplasty: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Huili Li, Yue Ma, Songchao Xu, Ruijuan Guo, Yun Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:We proposed that the L3 paravertebral block (PVB) combined with the retro-psoas compartment block (RPCB) would provide a better postoperative analgesia for total knee arthroplasty (TKA) than the femoral nerve block (FNB) alone.Materials and Methods:A total of 66 patients scheduled for TKA were randomly allocated to receive either FNB or L3 PVB-RPCB. Postoperative patient-controlled analgesia with intravenous sufentanil was administered. The primary endpoint was the total sufentanil consumption within the first 24 hour postoperative. Secondary outcomes assessed included pain intensity, sensory dermatomal coverage 20 min after administering the blocks, rescue analgesia requests, satisfaction scores, and the incidence of nausea, vomiting, itching, and posterior knee pain.Results:Patients in the L3 PVB-RPCB group consumed significantly less sufentanil in the first 24-hour post-surgery compared to those who received an FNB, with intake measuring 30 [28 to 33] ug versus 43 [37 to 46] ug, respectively, (P < 0.01). Furthermore, the pain scores were significantly lower in patients with L3 PVB-RPCB at 6 hour and 12 hours at rest (P < 0.01), and at 12 hours on movement (P < 0.01). This group also showed a reduced need for rescue analgesia and experienced less posterior knee pain (P < 0.01). There were no significant differences in satisfaction scores or in the occurrence of opioid-related side effects.Conclusion:The reduction of sufentanil consumption within the initial 24 hour after TKA demonstrates a beneficial effect of L3 PVB-RPCB over the FNB in providing the postoperative analgesia.

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is frequently conducted to alleviate pain from knee osteoarthritis.[1] Key objectives post-TKA involve effective pain management to facilitate early physical therapy and enable patients to quickly regain mobility and expedite hospital discharge. Inadequately managed pain can lead to ineffective physical therapy, extended hospital stays, and diminished patient satisfaction.[2] Consequently, ensuring the adequate pain relief following TKA continues to be a critical area of emphasis.

Postoperative analgesia following TKA typically requires targeting the L2–S3 nerve distribution to adequately manage the sensory innervation of the knee joint.[34] Current pain management strategies after total knee arthroplasty (TKA), include patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) with opioids and femoral nerve block (FNB). PCA opioids can lead to side effects like nausea, vomiting, pruritus, sedation, and drowsiness.[5] In contrast, FNB typically offers superior post-operative pain relief with fewer opioid-related adverse effects.[6] Nonetheless, FNB alone may not fully alleviate postoperative pain as the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve, obturator nerve (ON), and sciatic nerve also contribute to knee innervation. In recent years, many ultrasound-guided nerve block techniques were developed to improve the postoperative analgesia after TKA, such as fascia iliac compartment (FIC) block.[7] However, the FIC block does not typically affect the ON.[8] The sciatic nerve block combined with FNB could worsen weakness in the calf and foot, impairing weight-bearing capacity and increasing the risk of falls, thus, delaying functional recovery and hindering rehabilitation.[910] The limitation in covering all pain pathways relevant to TKA highlights the need for either supplementary blocks or alternative strategies to achieve comprehensive analgesia.

The retro-psoas compartment block (RPCB), which targets the compartment posterior to the psoas major muscle with the needle tip position anterior to lumbosacral ligament has shown the block action on the ON and lumbosacral trunk.[11] This method, offering complete sensory anesthesia across dermatomes L3–S3. However, the efficacy of blocking the anterior ramus of the L2 spinal nerve was notably suboptimal, with a success rate of approximately 47%.[12] Recognizing that the sensory innervation of the knee primarily originates from the L2 to S2 spinal segments, we hypothesized that combining the L3 PVB with RPCB could enhance the quality of analgesia for knee procedures beyond what is achieved with the traditional FNB alone.

To evaluate this hypothesis, we conducted a prospective, randomized controlled trial comparing these two analgesic techniques following TKA. The primary outcome was the cumulative dose of sufentanil administered during the first 24-hour post-surgery.

Material and Methods

Study design

This study was planned as a single center, randomized, clinical trial. Ethical approval for this study was provided by the ethics committee of Beijing Chaoyang Hospital, Capital Medical University, under reference number 2019-ke-326. The study was registered at www.chictr.org.cn (registration identifier ChiCTR1900021349) on February 16, 2019 and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. Following written informed consent, patients slated for elective TKA under general anesthesia were enrolled from March 5, 2019 to November 26, 2021 at Beijing Chaoyang Hospital, Capital Medical University, following the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines [Figure 1].

Study participants

Patients eligible for this study were determined based on the following criteria: American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–II, aged 50–75 years, with a body mass index (BMI) of 20–30 kg.m-2, and undergoing their initial unilateral TKA without any prior knee surgeries. Exclusion criteria for patients included pregnancy, with chronic pain or long-term opioid use, allergies to medications in the standardized anesthesia protocol, any local or systemic infection, psychiatric disorders or other impairments affecting their ability to comprehend pain assessments, and inability to use the PCA system.

Demographic information for the patients, including age, sex, height, and weight, was collected. A random number table, created by computer software, was sealed in sequential envelopes. Patients eligible for the study were assigned randomly to receive either FNB or L3 PVB-RPCB on the affected side before surgery with a 1:1 ratio between groups. Prior to surgery, all patients were trained on how to operate the PCA pump and assess their pain using the NRS. Trained regional anesthesia specialists with prior experience in administering these blocks performed both procedures. Before induction of general anesthesia, a FNB or L3 PVB-RPCB was performed under ultrasound guidance (Huasheng, Inc., Shenzhen, Guangdong, China) with adherence to strict aseptic precautions.

Interventions

To administer the FNB, the patients were subject to the supine position. After sterilizing the skin, the transducer was set transversely just inferior to the inguinal ligament. Then, the needle was introduced in-plane from the lateral edge of the transducer towards the femoral nerve, advancing medially until it penetrated the iliac fascia [Figure 2]. The needle’s position was verified by injecting 2 ml to 5 ml aliquots of 0.9% saline. Once correctly placed, 20 ml of 0.3% ropivacaine (Aspen Pharma, Australia) was administered slowly, following multiple negative blood aspirations to ensure safety.

To perform the L3 PVB-RPCB, the patients were subject to the lateral decubitus position with the affected side upward. The transducer was first positioned at the L3/4 level for a paramedian transverse oblique scan. Then 2 ml–3 ml 1% lidocaine (Qilu Pharma, Shandong, China) was administered to the skin and subcutaneous tissue by the operator using the dominant hand, 1 cm–2 cm lateral to the spinal midline at the US beam plane. The 26G needle (Tuoren, Henan, China) was aligned with the ultrasound beam and inserted at the site of skin infiltration, aiming its tip toward the paravertebral space [Figure 3]. Following confirmation of the needle’s proper placement through the injection of 2 ml to 5 ml aliquots of 0.9% saline, 15 ml of 0.3% ropivacaine was administered gradually. This careful approach included periodic aspiration to ensure that the local anesthetic did not enter any blood vessels.

The transducer was then positioned at the L5/S1 level for a paramedian transverse scan, revealing the hyperechoic intertransverse and lumbosacral ligaments [Figure 4]. Following local anesthetic infiltration of the skin, the needle was introduced in an out-of-plane approach, directed towards the retro-psoas compartment. Subsequently, once the accurate placement of the needle was verified via small saline injections ranging from 2 ml to 5 ml, 15 ml of 0.3% ropivacaine was administered cautiously, with repeated aspirations to prevent the accidental entry of the local anesthetic into any blood vessels.

Intraoperative management

In this study, a uniform anesthetic protocol was applied to all participating patients. Upon entry into the operating room, standard ASA monitors were set up for electrocardiography, noninvasive blood pressure, pulse oximetry, and end-tidal CO2 measurements. Next, peripheral venous access was secured, followed by the commencement of an intravenous infusion of Ringer’s lactate. An invasive radial arterial line was then inserted.

Induction of general anesthesia was achieved using intravenous propofol (2–2.5 mg.kg-1, Fresenius Kabi AB, Uppsala, Sweden), sufentanil (0.2 μg.kg-1, Yichang Renfu Pharma, Yichang, China), and rocuronium (0.8 mg.kg-1, Xianju Pharma, Xianju, China). This was followed by routine tracheal intubation and the initiation of mechanical ventilation. Anesthesia was maintained through a continuous drip of propofol (0.1–0.15 mg.kg-1.min-1) and remifentanil (0.1–0.3 μg.kg-1.min-1, Yichang Renfu Pharma). Rocuronium boluses were administered as necessary throughout the surgery. To manage postoperative pain, sufentanil (0.1 μg.kg-1) was given intravenously 15 min prior to concluding the procedure. At the completion of the surgery, neostigmine (40 μg.kg-1, Shanghai Xinyijinzhu Pharma, Shanghai, China) was administered intravenously to reverse neuromuscular blockade.

Prior to suturing the incision, a cocktail mixture was infiltrated periarticularly into the patients. This solution consisted of 10 ml of 1% ropivacaine, 0.5 mg morphine (Northeast Pharma, Shenyang, China), 5 mg diprospan (Zhucheng Pharma, Tianjin, China), and 90 ml normal saline. It was administered into various tissue layers including the posterior capsule, periosteum, fascia, anterior capsule, and subcutaneous tissues.

Postoperative pain management and assessment of outcomes

Following surgery, all patients were transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) where they received standard monitoring and began using an intravenous PCA system, which included sufentanil with a background infusion of 1 μg.h-1, a demand dose of 2 μg, and a 15-min lockout interval. A blinded staff assessed patients’ pain levels at rest and on movement at 0.5-, 6-, 12-, and 24-hour post-surgery using the NRS, which ranges from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst imaginable pain). A single cough was used to assess dynamic pain scores and an initial rescue dose of 50 mg of flurbiprofen (Beijing Taide Pharma, Beijing, China) was administered intravenously if a patient’s resting pain score exceeded 4 within 24-hour post-surgery.

The sensory block dermatomes were measured by ice block method at 20 min after blocks.[13] For postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), intravenous 0.1 mg.kg-1 of ondansetron (Lanling Pharma, Changzhou, China) was administered as needed to manage symptoms. At 24 hours after surgery, patients were asked to quantify their satisfaction with the administered analgesic approach using a 4-point scale (1= “very satisfied,” 2= “satisfied,” 3= “dissatisfied,” 4= “very dissatisfied”). Posterior knee pain refers to discomfort or soreness arising from the posterior aspect of the knee joint, typically occurring within 24 hours following TKA.[14]

The primary outcome assessed was the total sufentanil usage within the first 24-hour post-surgery. Second outcomes included the following: NRS scores at rest and coughing, the numbers of patients requiring rescue analgesia, the distribution of sensory block dermatomes, the satisfaction scores, and the incidence of the posterior knee pain, PONV, and pruritus.

Statistical analysis

The sample size for this study was selected based on prior observations at our institution. From preliminary data and a power analysis, we estimated the average sufentanil requirement for patients after TKA surgery receiving a FNB at 40 ug ± 14 ug within the first 24 hour. We hypothesized that a L3 PVB-RPCB would reduce sufentanil use by 25% compared to FNB within the first 24 hour following TKA. To identify this difference with 80% power and a 5% two-tailed α error, a sample size of 30 patients per group was deemed necessary. To account for possible dropouts, 66 participants (33 per group) were included in the study.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS for Windows (Version 20.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA). The Shapiro–Wilk test determined data normality. Qualitative results are shown as percentages and analyzed using Fisher’s exact test, while quantitative results are presented as mean ± SD or median [IQR]. The Mann–Whitney U-test evaluated differences between groups with non-normal distributions.

Results

Initially, 82 patients were evaluated for eligibility; of these, 10 declined participations, three had coagulation disorders, two suffered from chronic pain syndrome, and one was unable to use the PCA pump. Consequently, 66 patients were randomly assigned to one of two groups, with each group receiving an equal number of participants. During the study, two patients were lost to follow-up. Ultimately, 64 patients were included in the final analysis. The analysis focused on demographic variables and operation times, which were statistically similar across both groups [Table 1].

The NRS scores were significantly lower in patients who received the L3 PVB-RPCB at both 6 hour and 12 hours at rest (P < 0.01), as well as at 12 hours on movement (P < 0.01), when compared with patients in the FNB group. However, no significant differences in NRS scores were observed between the two groups at other time points (P > 0.05). Patients who received the L3 PVB-RPCB reported significantly less posterior knee pain compared to those in the (FNB group, with only 9.4% of patients in the L3 PVB-RPCB group experiencing posterior knee pain, compared to 53.1% in the FNB group (P < 0.01).

There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of satisfaction with the analgesic treatment (P > 0.05). Additionally, no significant differences were observed in the incidence of opioid-associated adverse effects, such as nausea, vomiting, or pruritus (P > 0.05).

In all patients, the sensory block ranges of L3–S1 were obtained 20 min after injection in the L3 PVB-RPCB group, while L3–L4 were acquired in the FNB group within the same time frame.

Discussion

This prospective, randomized controlled trial demonstrates that the L3 PVB-RPCB technique reduces sufentanil consumption by 30.2% within the first 24-hour post-surgery compared to the FNB method. As such, L3 PVB-RPCB proves to be a practical and efficient strategy for improving postoperative analgesia in TKA patients.

Our study demonstrated that, compared with FNB, the L3 PVB-RPCB significantly reduced postoperative sufentanil consumption within the first 24 hours following TKA. This enhanced analgesic efficacy is likely attributable to the more comprehensive sensory blockade achieved, encompassing dermatomes L2–S2. Specifically, in the L3 PVB-RPCB group, the sensory block extended from L2 to S2 within 20 min of injection, aligning with findings from previous studies.[12] Conversely, in the FNB group, sensory blockade was confined to the L2–L4 dermatomes within the same time frame. Notably, all patients in the L3 PVB-RPCB group achieved consistent sensory blockade from L3 to S1 at 20 min post-injection, whereas the FNB group demonstrated a more restricted range, limited to L3–L4. This disparity underscores the superior efficacy of L3 PVB-RPCB in achieving extensive sensory anesthesia, thereby contributing to a greater reduction in postoperative opioid requirements.

Patients who received L3 PVB-RPCB exhibited significantly lower NRS scores at 6 hour and 12 hours postoperatively at rest, as well as at 12 hours during movement, compared to those in the FNB group. These results suggest that L3 PVB-RPCB provides superior pain relief not only at rest but also during mobilization, which is essential for enhancing patient comfort and promoting early postoperative rehabilitation. Effective pain management during the early postoperative period plays a crucial role in improving patient outcomes, as it facilitates early mobilization, reduces the risk of complications such as deep vein thrombosis, and accelerates the recovery process.[151617] In addition, the requirement for rescue analgesia was markedly reduced in the L3 PVB-RPCB group. Only 15.6% of patients in this group required rescue analgesia, despite undergoing multiple analgesic strategy, in stark contrast to 65.6% in the FNB group, underscoring the enhanced analgesic efficacy of the L3 PVB-RPCB in achieving more sustained and effective pain control.

This disparity in analgesic management can be attributed to the inherent limitations of FNB in addressing the posterior aspect of the knee, a critical area for postoperative analgesia following TKA.[34] Specifically, 53.1% of patients in the FNB group reported experiencing posterior knee pain, a complication that is well-established as a limitation of FNB. In contrast, only 9.4% of patients in the L3 PVB-RPCB group experienced similar posterior knee pain, a difference likely attributable to the more comprehensive blockade of the sacral nerves achieved by the PVB-RPCB technique. The ability of L3 PVB-RPCB to address this issue more effectively highlights its potential as a superior alternative to FNB for postoperative pain management in TKA patients.

Opioid-related adverse effects, such as nausea, vomiting and pruritus, were evaluated as secondary outcomes in this study to assess the broader implications of pain management strategies on patients. The analysis revealed no significant differences in the incidence of opioid-related adverse effects between the L3 PVB-RPCB group and the FNB group within the first 24 hour following surgery, potentially because of the limited sample size. Furthermore, all participants reported either good or very good levels of satisfaction, with no discernible differences between the two groups. This high level of satisfaction, regardless of the analgesic technique employed, may be attributed to the perioperative multimodal analgesia tailored to TKA.

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, while the L3 PVB-RPCB effectively provides analgesia for TKA, we did not assess indicators of functional recovery such as quadriceps strength recovery. Research by Michèle Angers et al.[18] has shown that FNB adversely affects quadriceps strength recovery both in the short and mid-term and is therefore not recommended for post-TKA analgesia. Secondly, while FNB is generally recognized as safe, our study did not address complications associated with L3 PVB-RPCB, such as infections, postoperative paresthesia, bleeding, and neuropathic pain.[192021] Including a larger patient cohort might have enabled us to analyze these potential complications as a secondary outcome between the two treatment modalities. Thirdly, the absence of detailed information on preoperative opioid use among patients may represent a subtle limitation of our study, with potential implications for the broader applicability of our findings, particularly regarding postoperative analgesic consumption.

In summary, the ultrasound-guided L3 PVB-RPCB provides superior analgesic effects relative to the FNB in individuals undergoing TKA. This technique not only reduces the need for postoperative analgesic drugs but also effectively alleviates postoperative pain, indicating the modified SQLB is a compelling and optimistic block technique for these patients.
