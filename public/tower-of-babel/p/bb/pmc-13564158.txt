Advances in Light-Driven Nanocrystal Assembly: From Two-Dimensional Patterning to Three-Dimensional Manufacturing
Authors: Jie Guan, Xinyan Zhang, Yuanyuan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Colloidal nanocrystals serve as a versatile platform for solution-processed electronics, optoelectronics, and photonics. While early efforts centered on two-dimensional (2D) patterned films for planar devices, increasing demands for integration density and multifunctionality are driving a transition toward three-dimensional (3D) nanocrystal architectures. Light-driven assembly has emerged as a powerful, noninvasive approach, enabling remote activation, high spatial selectivity, and broad compatibility across nanocrystal compositions and surface chemistries. Recent advances in photolithography and laser-based fabrication have bridged planar patterning and volumetric construction. Here, we present a unified framework for light-driven nanocrystal assembly based on fundamental energy-transduction pathways, including photopolymerization, photoreduction, and photoinduced interparticle coupling. We discuss how these mechanisms govern high-fidelity 2D patterning and enable emerging strategies for 3D structuring and manufacturing. Finally, we highlight key challenges and opportunities in achieving structural precision, functional integrity, multimaterial integration, and device-level implementation, and outline future directions toward programmable nanocrystal-based manufacturing.

Introduction

Colloidal nanocrystals (NCs) have emerged as a foundational materials platform in nanoscience, enabled by continued progress in synthetic control and assembly methodologies. Their utility stems from solution-phase processability, compositional tunability, and precisely adjustable physicochemical properties dictated by size and morphology. − These features make NCs attractive for a wide range of applications, including transistors, light emitting devices, photodetectors, lasers, sensors, and energy related technologies. − Realizing these functions, however, it is essential to organize NCs into solid architectures with controlled geometry, structural uniformity, and spatially defined functionality.

Early efforts in NC integration predominantly focused on two-dimensional (2D) patterning strategies, which align naturally with established planar device architectures and microfabrication workflows. Various methods have been developed to define nanocrystal films on substrates, such as inkjet deposition, soft lithographic transfer, and lift off processing. − Established high-resolution micro- and nanofabrication techniques, including electron beam lithography, X-ray lithography, and molecular beam epitaxy, provide powerful tools for nanoscale pattern definition or epitaxial film growth. However, these methods often involve vacuum processing, high-energy irradiation, resist-assisted pattern transfer, or stringent growth conditions, which may be less compatible with ligand-stabilized colloidal NCs and solution-derived NC films. Among different NC patterning techniques, light-directed patterning has attracted particular attention because it is noncontact, spatially selective, and compatible with many nanocrystal compositions and ligand environments. Light-induced processes can regulate ligand transformation, interparticle coupling, and solubility contrast, enabling the direct formation of stable nanocrystal patterns under relatively mild conditions. − These advantages have supported the development of highly resolved two-dimensional nanocrystal structures with preserved optical and electronic characteristics. −

Despite this progress, planar assembly alone is no longer sufficient for many emerging device concepts. Advanced photonic and optoelectronic systems increasingly require precise material placement along all three spatial axes. Representative examples include three-dimensional photonic architectures, vertically integrated functional components, and multifunctional sensing platforms. These demands have shifted attention from surface confined patterning to three-dimensional (3D) structuring of nanocrystal solids. Such a transition is essential for expanding the design freedom and functional complexity of NCs based materials. Recent studies have shown that the principles governing light controlled 2D assembly can also support volumetric fabrication. Photochemical reactions triggered by localized irradiation can induce cross-linking, bonding, or solidification within nanocrystal containing media. As a result, light can be used not only to define lateral features on a substrate, but also to construct free-standing architectures with programmed 3D geometry. This development marks an important shift from planar pattern generation toward additive manufacturing of functional nanomaterials.

In this Review, we present a unified framework of light-driven NC assembly (Figure ), tracing its evolution from 2D patterning methodologies to emerging strategies for true 3D manufacturing. Although several recent reviews have summarized quantum dot photolithography or 3D printing strategies, most of them focus on individual fabrication technologies. The present Review is distinct in that it organizes light-driven nanocrystal assembly within a cross-dimensional mechanistic framework. Rather than treating 2D photopatterning and 3D manufacturing as separate topics, we emphasize that both can be understood through common energy-transduction processes, including light-induced polymerization, photoreduction, and photoinduced interparticle bonding. This perspective allows us to trace how surface-confined planar processing evolves into voxel-resolved volumetric fabrication and to identify general design principles for programmable NC manufacturing. In addition, among various NC systems, semiconductor and perovskite quantum dots (QDs) currently represent the most extensively investigated platforms for light-driven photopatterning and optoelectronic integration. Therefore, this Review places particular emphasis on QD-based systems, while metallic, oxide, magnetic, catalytic, and hybrid NCs are discussed where they illustrate generalizable mechanisms or emerging opportunities.

Light-Driven Two-Dimensional Patterning of NCs

The controlled assembly of nanocrystals into 2D patterned architectures represents a critical foundation for the integration of nanocrystal-based materials into functional devices. Among the various patterning approaches developed to date, light-driven strategies have emerged as particularly attractive due to their intrinsic advantages in spatial selectivity, resolution, and process compatibility. By using light as a remote and precisely addressable stimulus, these methods enable localized modulation of nanocrystal solubility, surface chemistry, or interparticle interactions, thereby allowing direct writing of well-defined 2D patterns without physical contact or aggressive processing conditions.

Photoresist- and Polymer-Assisted Photolithography

The development of light-driven two-dimensional patterning of NCs parallels the transformation of colloidal NCs from chemically tunable nanostructures into device-compatible functional materials. Early strategies (Figure a) were adapted from conventional microfabrication, in which NC films were integrated with photoresists or sacrificial layers and structured by optical exposure. − Although these strategies established the compatibility of nanocrystals with lithographic processing, they generally required several sequential steps, such as photoresist deposition, development, and etching or lift off. Exposure to these relatively harsh fabrication conditions can disrupt surface ligands and lead to deterioration of optoelectronic properties.

To simplify processing and improve materials compatibility, subsequent efforts focused on embedding NCs directly within photoactive polymer matrices, enabling pattern formation through localized photochemical cross-linking. In a representative early study, Ingrosso et al. incorporated CdSe@ZnS nanocrystals into a negative-tone epoxy resist, achieving micrometer-scale, high-aspect-ratio structures while preserving quantum dot emission and ultraviolet (UV) patternability. This work established NC-polymer hybrids as viable photostructurable systems. Thereafter, physical blending of NCs into into photoresist systems became widespread, − including epoxy-based resists , and commercial resists such as SU-8. − These systems enabled high-resolution patterning with largely retained optical properties, supporting their application in photonic and optoelectronic devices.

However, direct blending exposed intrinsic limitations, including phase separation, limited loading, and colloidal instability arising from the incompatibility between alkyl-capped QDs and polar photoresists. Surface engineering therefore became essential. As shown in Figure b, silica encapsulation followed by functionalization improved miscibility with polar solvents and SU-8, enabling stable patterned arrays and enhanced device performance. − Introduction of epoxy groups on silica-coated NCs further increased loading (up to ∼25%) and photoluminescence efficiency, while dispersant- or binder-assisted systems achieved sub-20 μm features. Nevertheless, increasing formulation complexity often led to higher viscosity and aggregation, compromising film quality. Ligand-exchange strategies subsequently provided a more direct route to homogeneous composites (Figure c). Replacement of native ligands with polymerizable functionalities rendered NCs intrinsically compatible with photocurable matrices. Mercapto-silane ligands enabled siloxane-based NCs inks resistant to aggregation and amenable to UV curing, − while multifunctional ligands bearing carboxyl and acrylate groups facilitated covalent integration into cross-linked networks, improving lithographic fidelity. ,−

In addition to improving compatibility through ligand and matrix engineering, an alternative strategy involves incorporating NCs precursors into reactive monomer systems, such that photopolymerization and nanocrystal formation are integrated within a single platform. In these formulations, optical exposure establishes the polymer network, while NCs are generated in situ either before or after pattern definition. A representative example is the direct in situ photolithography approach reported by Zhang et al., in which perovskite precursor patterns are first defined and then converted into NCs, eliminating lift-off steps and reducing UV- or solvent-induced degradation (Figure a). In this system, lead bromide complexes catalyze thiol–ene polymerization without external photoinitiators, enabling uniform emission and resolutions up to 2450 ppi. Related strategies likewise pattern precursor-loaded resists before inducing NCs crystallization, preserving optical properties under stringent processing conditions. − To address line-edge roughness associated with radical diffusion, Wu et al. introduced a photoinitiator-grafted resist that spatially confines polymerization and improves pattern fidelity (Figure b). More recently, simplified UV-curable monomer platforms, including isobornyl acrylate (IBOA)-based formulations, have enabled high quantum yield, full-color patterning with minimal photoluminescence degradation throughout lithographic processing (Figure c).

Resist-Free Photolithography (DOLFIN)

Actually, most as-synthesized NCs are capped with long-chain alkyl ligands to ensure colloidal stability and effective surface passivation, so these ligands can become the primary reactive sites under high-energy irradiation, such as X-rays. − Exposure induces dehydrogenation and rehybridization of the alkyl chains, leading to the formation of CC or C–C bonds between neighboring ligands. − The resulting covalently cross-linked NCs network becomes insoluble in typical casting solvents, thereby enabling direct patterning without the need for an additional photoresist layer.

In 2017, Talapin and co-workers reported direct optical lithography of functional inorganic nanomaterials (DOLFIN), establishing a new paradigm for NCs patterning. The strategy underlies the development of photochemically active ion-pair ligands (CatX–), in which either the cation or anion contains a photosensitive moiety (Figure a). These systems were initially responsive to deep UV wavelengths but have since been engineered to operate across near-UV and visible regions, expanding their practical utility (Figure b). , Direct optical lithography of functional inorganic nanomaterials (DOLFIN) avoids resist-induced contamination and interfacial incompatibility, allowing precise pattern transfer while preserving the intrinsic properties of NCs. The approach relies on photosensitive inks composed of NCs and photochemically active molecules. These molecules are required to preserve colloidal stability prior to irradiation, undergo well-defined photochemical transformations that modulate interparticle interactions, and generate products that do not compromise the intrinsic optoelectronic properties of the NCs (Figure c).

Photosensitive Ligands

Following this principle, considerable effort has been devoted to engineering photosensitive ligands that enable direct photopatterning under moderate irradiation conditions. Kim and co-workers reported azide-functionalized ligands, including 4-(2-azidoethyl)­benzoic acid (AzL1) and 4-(4-azidobutoxy)­benzoic acid (AzL2), which were introduced onto NCs during synthesis. Upon 254 nm exposure, azide groups generate nitrene intermediates capable of C–H insertion, resulting in covalent interparticle cross-linking. This strategy simplifies processing and maintains photoluminescence comparable to that of conventionally capped QDs, enabling micrometer-scale pattern formation without substantial optical loss. Polymeric photo-cross-linkable ligands have further broadened this strategy. Bang and co-workers functionalized QD surfaces with a random copolymer, poly­(vinyltriphenylamine-random-azidostyrene) (PTPA-N3–SH), combining azide moieties for UV-induced cross-linking with triphenylamine units for hole transport (Figure a). Thiol anchoring groups reduced luminescence degradation during ligand exchange, and limiting the azide fraction preserved photophysical performance after cross-linking (254 nm, 21 mJ cm–2). The resulting patterned films supported QD-light emitting diodes (LEDs) with external quantum efficiencies up to 6.25%. This approach was extended to CsPbX3 (X = Cl, Br, I) QDs using cinnamoyl-containing polymer ligands activated at 365 nm, yielding full-color RGB microarrays with subpixel dimensions of 10 μm × 40 μm (≈465 ppi) (Figure b).

Subsequently, Bae and colleagues introduced benzophenone-derived photo-cross-linkable ligands operable under i-line irradiation (365 nm) (Figure c). Incorporation of less than 10 mol % cross-linkable ligands, alongside conventional dispersing ligands, afforded mechanically robust films while largely retaining photoluminescent and electroluminescent characteristics. This dual-ligand design enabled ultrahigh-resolution RGB patterning (>15,000 ppi) and passive-matrix-driven QD-LED arrays. Taken together, these advances demonstrate that the DOLFIN concept has catalyzed a range of ligand-engineering strategies, wherein judiciously designed photosensitive ligands enable direct, low-dose photopatterning while preserving the optical and electronic performance required for device applications.

Photosensitive Additives

Additive-assisted DOLFIN represent a streamlined formulation strategy in which photopatternability is introduced by incorporating a small amount of functional additive directly into a colloidal QD ink. This minimalist design reduces compositional complexity during film formation and exposure, thereby improving pattern fidelity and preserving the native ligand environment. Photo-cross-linkers are among the most widely adopted additives in this context. Under UV exposure, these compounds generate reactive species that are typically radicals or radical derived intermediates. Such species couple with the surface ligands of quantum dots and induce network formation among adjacent particles, thereby decreasing their solubility in nonpolar developing solvents. Azide-based cross-linkers remain particularly prevalent due to their broad compatibility with diverse QD compositions. Under UV exposure (254 or 365 nm), diazides release nitrogen to generate nitrene intermediates that undergo C–H insertion into alkyl ligands such as oleates or oleylamines, forming covalent bridges between adjacent nanocrystals (Figure a). Molecular design strategies, including perfluorophenyl-substituted bis-azides, have been employed to suppress side reactions and minimize perturbation of the QD electronic structure, thereby enabling direct photolithography with limited optical penalty. − Advanced multiazide architectures further increase cross-link density and patterning efficiency while reducing additive loading, although excessive steric bulk may compromise compatibility with the QD surface (Figure b). Alternative cross-linking additives have also been explored. Diazo-derived carbenes can promote C–H insertion with potentially improved selectivity and reduced excitonic quenching. − In some cases, carbene species act as Lewis bases that coordinate surface metal sites, offering simultaneous cross-linking and defect passivation. ,

Hydrogen-abstraction cross-linking is typically mediated by Type II photoinitiators, including aromatic ketone derivatives such as benzophenone and acetophenone, which generate radicals under UV irradiation. Gu et al. developed an acetophenone-based system (PC-H and PC-Me) for nondestructive photolithography (Figure c). By tailoring initiators with strong absorption in the deep-UV region (254 nm), they promoted selective hydrogen abstraction and covalent bonding with alkyl ligands while minimizing DUV-induced photodamagean issue that has long constrained short-wavelength patterning. In the same year, Zhai and co-workers demonstrated a direct photolithographic strategy based on a bis-benzophenone cross-linker (biBPS) (Figure d), achieving efficient covalent coupling between QD surface ligands while maintaining a photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) as high as 95% after patterning. Thiol-mediated coupling reactions, particularly thiol–ene chemistry, provide another mild and efficient pathway (Figure e). Multifunctional thiols not only enhance interparticle connectivity and solvent resistance but, under appropriate conditions, can even increase relative PLQY by passivating surface defects. − These results established practical design principles for thiol-based cross-linkers in direct optical lithography processes. Furthermore, Guan et al. presented a novel patterning strategy based on triphenylphosphine (TPP), which photoinitiated oxygen-mediated radical cross-linking while preserving high photoluminescence and device efficiency (External Quantum Efficiency (EQE) > 20% for RGB QLEDs). Collectively, these cross-linking approaches demonstrate that additive-assisted systems can produce high-quality QD pixel arrays with minimal polymer dilution and without extensive ligand exchange, although long-term film stability and thickness control remain relevant considerations for device integration.

Beyond cross-linking, certain additives operate through light-induced ligand exchange or stripping mechanisms that alter QD solubility in situ. Photoacid generator (PAG)-based lithography exemplifies this approach, eliminating the need for prior ligand modification. Talapin and co-workers formulated photosensitive inks by dissolving core/shell QDs and small amounts (1–3 wt %) of PAGs such as 2-(4-methoxystyryl)-4,6-bis­(trichloromethyl)-1,3,5-triazine (MBT) or 2-diazo-1-naphthol-4-sulfonic acid (DNS) in nonpolar solvents. Upon irradiation, MBT generates chlorine radicals that ultimately produce HCl, protonating surface-bound oleate ligands and enabling in situ ligand exchange (Figure a). The resulting solubility contrast afforded patterns with resolutions down to 1.5 μm while retaining approximately 75% of the initial PLQY. Photoamine generators (PAmGs) represent another additive class. Upon irradiation, these molecules release amines that coordinate to exposed surface metal sites (Figure b), decreasing QD solubility in polar solvents while simultaneously passivating defects. Such systems have demonstrated pattern formation with retention of over 90% of the original PLQY. A related carbocation-enabled ligand stripping (CELS) strategy employs low concentrations (∼2 wt %) of photosensitive triphenylmethyl chloride (TPCl) (Figure c). Photodissociation produces strongly Lewis acidic carbocations that remove native ligands and reduce QD solubility, while chloride anions coordinate to surface metal sites and provide defect passivation. This approach yields high-resolution patterns (∼4 μm) with minimal optical loss and improved photostability. For more air-sensitive systems such as InP QDs, however, excessive acid generation or residual additives can introduce deep traps and reduce PLQY, necessitating post-treatment strategies such as ligand pair treatment (LPT) to restore surface passivation (Figure d).

Mask-Free Laser Writing

The direct laser writing (DLW) originates from early laser-based microfabrication strategies developed for high-vacuum thin-film processing, including laser ablation for integrated circuit patterning, , photoresist exposure, thin-film deposition, , and semiconductor annealing. , Concurrent advances in laser-induced transfer, lift-off processes, nanomaterial sintering, and laser-assisted chemical reduction established a broader conceptual framework: lasers are not limited to material removal or crystallization, but can also induce spatially confined chemical transformations and structural reconfiguration. Against this backdrop, DLW evolved into a mask-free patterning strategy that defines functional architectures through localized material transformation. Rather than relying on conventional lithographic masks, DLW exploits controlled photon-matter interactions to selectively modify composition, phase, and microstructure, thereby laying the groundwork for NC patterning via targeted structural and chemical modulation.

The exceptionally high peak power of laser enables nonlinear absorption processes, including two-photon and multiphoton excitation within a tightly confined focal volume. This characteristic allows laser to function as an optical pen, writing NCs architectures with nanoscale precision inside transparent dielectrics − or polymer matrices. − A representative example was reported by Qiu’s group, who utilized femtosecond laser processing in glass to fabricate tunable perovskite NCs microstructures with emission spanning 480–700 nm, highlighting the feasibility of multicolor patterning for micro-LED technologies (Figure a). These pioneering studies stimulated considerable interest in glass-based DLW of highly emissive NCs patterns. Compared with glass, polymer substrates offer greater processability and compatibility with device integration. Nie and co-workers introduced a continuous-wave laser writing strategy to directly pattern CsPbX3 (X = Br, I) perovskite NCs within a polyvinylidene difluoride matrix (Figure b). Multicolor patterning is achieved through halide composition modulation, producing vivid green, yellow, and red images with high spatial precision. The patterned films exhibit excellent optical transparency under visible light and strong emission under UV excitation, while maintaining remarkable mechanical robustness, that they can be bent, rolled, or even immersed in water or alcohol without degradation.

Early demonstrations established the feasibility of in situ NC generation under focused femtosecond irradiation. Semiconductor precursors embedded in transparent hosts could be converted into luminescent nanocrystals through nonlinear absorption at the focal voxel, producing arbitrary microscale patterns without global heating. − The development of precursor-based DLW further refined this concept: instead of patterning preformed NCs, laser exposure triggered sequential photopolymerization and nanocrystal formation, yielding improved structural definition and uniformity. In 2016, Chou et al. first demonstrated laser-induced growth and patterning of MAPbBr3 NCs from precursor solutions using a tunable continuous-wave laser (λ = 750 nm). Exploiting the inverse temperature–solubility relationship of perovskites, localized photothermal heating triggered in situ crystallization, allowing programmable pattern extension without predefined templates. Subsequent work by Chen et al. achieved direct writing of CsPbBr3 NCs using a 405 nm laser, where line width and morphology were systematically tuned by adjusting laser fluence, spot size, and scanning speed, and large-area (100 × 100 mm2) patterning was realized.

Apart from relying on matrix, Kim and co-workers directly demonstrated submicrometer line-width patterning of Ag nanoparticles using near-infrared pulsed laser irradiation as early as 2011. In their approach, densely packed Ag NCs films absorbed incident photons, generating localized heating that thermally cleaved the labile moieties of surface ligands. The unexposed regions retained their dispersibility and could be removed by simple rinsing, whereas the irradiated areas remained anchored to the substrate. This strategy established a direct route toward mask-free nanoparticle patterning without the need for external additives or photoresists, highlighting the feasibility of exploiting intrinsic light-matter interactions within nanocrystal assemblies. Building on this concept, subsequent studies increasingly employed tightly focused femtosecond laser beams to drive nanocrystal assembly and microstructure formation with enhanced spatial resolution. −

Mechanisms of Light-Driven Nanocrystal Assembly across Dimensions

Light-driven nanocrystal assembly can be understood as a set of fundamental energy-transduction mechanisms that govern interparticle interactions and structural evolution across dimensions. As this field has progressed from predominantly two-dimensional (2D) patterning toward increasingly complex three-dimensional (3D) manufacturing, these mechanisms have been exploited across different length scales and dimensional regimes. In 2D systems, light typically induces localized changes in surface chemistry, ligand binding, or interparticle forces, leading to controlled lateral organization. Key mechanisms include light-induced polymerization, photoreduction and interparticle bonding (Figure ), all of which can direct nanocrystal interactions and assembly pathways. By integrating these mechanisms with controlled irradiation conditions and suitable material systems, it becomes possible to bridge planar patterning and volumetric fabrication, establishing a unified basis for hierarchical nanocrystal manufacturing.

Light-Induced Polymerization

Light-induced polymerization represents a matrix-mediated energy-transduction pathway in which optical excitation is converted into localized cross-linking reactions that immobilize nanocrystals (Figure a). , In a typical system, preformed NCs or soluble molecular precursors are dispersed in a photocurable formulation containing monomers or oligomers together with a photoinitiator. The system is subsequently subjected to light irradiation, where photon absorption Upon light exposure, the photoinitiator absorbs photons and produces reactive radical or cationic species, which trigger polymerization within the illuminated regions. The resulting chain growth or step growth processes lead to the formation of a cross-linked polymer network that immobilizes NCs in place. ,,

Two situations should be distinguished in this category. In the first and more common case, NCs act mainly as functional fillers embedded in a light-cross-linked polymer matrix. − Here, the primary photophysical and photochemical events occur in the photoinitiator, monomer, oligomer, or polymer network, rather than in the NCs themselves. The NCs are spatially fixed by the matrix after exposure and development, but they do not necessarily undergo autonomous NC–NC assembly. Therefore, this pathway is more accurately described as matrix-mediated immobilization or light-directed organization of NC-containing composites. Its importance lies in the fact that optical exposure defines where the NC-containing material is solidified, patterned, or printed, even though the light-driven event resides primarily in the polymer-forming reaction.

Because the reaction is confined to exposed areas, the lateral distribution of NCs can be defined with high spatial resolution. After exposure, unreacted components are removed by solvent development, leaving well-defined 2D patterns. When molecular precursors are used instead of preformed NCs, the polymer network can also confine the precursors and provide a localized environment for nucleation and growth, enabling in situ formation of NCs within patterned regions. , This approach improves both pattern fidelity and compositional uniformity.

In the second case, NCs participate more actively in the photopolymerization process. Certain NCs or NC-related precursors can serve as local photoactive centers, photosensitizers, or nanoscale photoinitiators. Upon irradiation, photoexcited NCs generate charge carriers or interfacial reactive species that can initiate monomer polymerization, activate cross-linking reactions, or promote ligand-bond transformation near the NC surface. For example, semiconductor NCs with suitable band structures may mediate interfacial electron- or hole-transfer reactions, while strongly absorbing NCs can localize photochemical activation within NC-rich regions. In such NC-mediated photopolymerization systems, the NCs are not merely passive fillers; instead, they contribute directly to the initiation or acceleration of the polymerization/cross-linking reaction.

This distinction is important for defining the role of light-driven in polymerization-based NC fabrication. In conventional matrix-mediated systems, light primarily drives polymer formation and NCs are immobilized as functional components. In NC-mediated systems, light excitation of the NCs themselves can participate in initiating polymerization or interfacial chemical transformation. Nevertheless, in both cases, the final pattern formation generally relies on localized polymerization, cross-linking, or solubility contrast. Thus, light-induced polymerization provides a versatile and lithographically compatible route for 2D patterning and 3D additive manufacturing of NC-based materials, while the degree of NC participation should be clearly specified for each system.

Light-Induced Photoreduction

Light-induced photoreduction constitutes a redox-driven energy-transduction pathway in which optical excitation generates reactive charge carriers that directly modify the composition and electronic structure of NCs (Figure b). In a typical system, metal precursors, redox-active species, or ligand-stabilized NC dispersions are incorporated into a photoresponsive medium containing either intrinsically photoactive NCs or external photosensitizers. − Upon light irradiation, light absorption generates excited electrons or reactive intermediates that selectively reduce metal ions, surface species, or interparticle interfaces within the exposed regions.

This localized reduction can initiate the nucleation and growth of metallic or semiconducting domains, induce partial ligand removal, and promote interparticle coupling through reduction-assisted bonding. As a result, NC formation and electronic connectivity are confined to irradiated areas, producing well-defined planar architectures after removal of unreacted species. Compared to polymerization-based approaches, photoreduction directly modifies the composition and electronic structure of NCs, providing tighter control over functional properties.

The spatial resolution is governed by the diffusion of reactive species and the kinetics of charge transfer, both of which must be managed to suppress undesired lateral spreading. These same factors become even more critical when extending photoreduction-based processes from 2D patterning to 3D manufacturing, where reactions occur in a volumetric environment and require precise control over both spatial confinement and particle transport. With appropriate tuning of irradiation conditions and precursor chemistry, light-induced photoreduction establishes a mechanistic basis for translating planar patterning strategies into 3D fabrication of conductive, catalytic, and plasmonic NC architectures.

Light-Induced Interparticle Bonding

Photoinduced interparticle bonding defines an interfacial energy-transduction pathway in which photoexcitation activates surface reactions that promote direct coupling between nanocrystals (Figure c). In 2D patterns, light can modulate surface charges by generating photoexcited carriers that alter the balance between surface-bound ligands and charged species. This process reduces electrostatic repulsion between NCs and promotes their local aggregation or attachment within irradiated regions.

At the same time, photoinduced ligand transformationssuch as ligand desorption, radical formation, or chemical conversioncan expose undercoordinated surface atoms that serve as reactive sites for interparticle bonding. − In some systems, additional cross-linking reactions between ligands or linkers further strengthen interparticle connections, forming continuous networks that are insoluble in development solvents. As a result, NC assemblies are selectively formed and stabilized in patterns defined by the light field, while unexposed regions remain dispersed and can be removed. In addition to surface charge modulation, photoactivated cross-linking reactions can directly connect neighboring NCs through their native ligands or added linkers, forming a continuous network within a confined volume. ,,,

Because bonding occurs directly at the nanocrystal interface, this pathway provides fine control over interparticle spacing, orientation, and connectivityparameters that are critical for tuning optical coupling, charge transport, and mechanical stability. Importantly, this mechanism can be extended from 2D patterning to 3D fabrication by confining photoinduced interfacial reactions within a defined volume, enabling the formation of robust, freestanding NC networks without the need for a bulk polymer matrix. Consequently, photoinduced interparticle bonding represents a powerful route for direct, ligand-mediated assembly of nanocrystals into structurally integrated 3D architectures.

Unified Perspective across Dimensions

Taken together, matrix-mediated polymerization, redox-driven photoreduction, and interfacial photoinduced bonding provide a mechanistic framework for understanding light-driven NC assembly across dimensions. These pathways differ in where optical energy is absorbed and how it is converted into structural evolution. In matrix-mediated polymerization, the primary light-driven event occurs in the monomer, photoinitiator, or polymer matrix, leading to localized immobilization of NC-containing materials. In photoreduction and photoinduced bonding, optical excitation more directly modifies the NC surface, ligand shell, or interparticle interface, thereby promoting local chemical transformation, nucleation, or NC-NC coupling.

It should also be emphasized that laser-based fabrication does not always proceed through direct photochemical reactions. A substantial number of DLW examples, especially those involving precursor-containing, glass-confined, or polymer-confined systems, rely primarily on photothermal energy conversion. , In such cases, optical absorption by the matrix, precursor, or embedded species is converted into localized heat, which subsequently drives nucleation, crystallization, sintering, or phase transformation within the irradiated region. This pathway is mechanistically distinct from photochemical DLW, where absorbed photons directly induce ligand cleavage, photoreduction, surface transformation, or interparticle bonding through excited-state or charge-transfer processes.

Therefore, photochemical and photothermal should be distinguished according to the primary energy-conversion process. Photochemical pathways are mainly governed by photon absorption, excited-state lifetime, charge transfer, ligand reactivity, and diffusion of reactive intermediates. By contrast, photothermal pathways are controlled by light absorption efficiency, heat generation, thermal diffusion, precursor solubility, nucleation barriers, and crystallization kinetics. Although both pathways use spatially confined optical excitation to define the position and geometry of NC-based structures, they differ in their reaction origin, resolution limits, material compatibility, and potential influence on NC surface chemistry and optical properties.

Crucially, the transition from 2D patterning to 3D manufacturing does not necessarily require fundamentally new chemical mechanisms, but it does require more precise control over where optical energy is deposited and how the resulting chemical or thermal processes evolve in space. In photochemical systems, the structural evolution is usually linked to local changes in surface chemistry, solubility contrast, or interparticle bonding. In photothermal systems, the final NC architecture is instead produced through localized thermal transformation, such as in situ crystallization or laser-induced phase conversion.

Light-Driven Three-Dimensional Manufacturing of Nanocrystals

The evolution from light-driven two-dimensional photopatterning to 3D manufacturing of NCs marks a fundamental transition from laterally confined surface structuring to volumetric, voxel-resolved architectural fabrication. Rooted in the mechanistic frameworks of light-induced polymerization, photoreduction, photoexcitation-driven interparticle bonding, and photothermal modulation, light-directed 3D manufacturing integrates precisely controlled optical fields with spatiotemporal regulation of interparticle potentials and matrix dynamics. In this paradigm, light operates not merely as a lithographic patterning stimulus but as a volumetrically addressable energy input that governs localized cross-linking, redox transformations, and particle coalescence in a layer-by-layer or voxel-by-voxel fashion, thereby enabling the programmable construction of hierarchically organized NC-based architectures.

Photopolymerization-Based 3D Manufacturing

Mechanistically, light-induced polymerization represents one of the most widely adopted strategies in additive manufacturing. Within the broader photopolymerization framework, two-photon polymerization (TPP) provides a particularly powerful route for nanoscale fabrication. By selecting appropriate photoresists, TPP can be readily adapted to a wide range of organic material systems. Recent efforts have sought to extend this approach to inorganic and metallic architectures.

One widely explored route involves the fabrication of polymer scaffolds, followed by infiltration with metal salt precursors and subsequent thermal removal of the template to yield a metallic framework (Figure a). However, this templating strategy often suffers from nonuniform metal deposition, residual carbonaceous contaminants, and structural porosity generated during template removal, which collectively obscure the intrinsic mechanical and electrical properties of the resulting metals. An alternative strategy introduces NCs or metal precursors directly into photocurable resins, enabling 3D fabrication of metal-containing nanocomposites. − For example, Desponds et al. introduced a composite resin containing 45 wt % ultrasmall (∼5 nm) zirconia-stabilized nanoparticles within a zirconium acrylate precursor. In this formulation, the NCs act both as crystallization seeds that regulate the phase evolution during postmanufacturing calcination and as structural stabilizers that mitigate shrinkage in the resulting ceramic architectures. Owing to the relatively high refractive index of the nanocomposite (≈2.1), patterned surfaces fabricated from this system exhibit diffraction efficiencies sufficient to achieve microfocusing, enabling structures with line widths around 0.1 μm and microlens arrays with periodicities as small as 0.8 μm.

Beyond photocurable resins, hydrogel-based platforms offer a distinct paradigm in which the polymer matrix not only provides mechanical support but also governs nanomaterial localization through multivalent intermolecular interactions. − Han et al. have demonstrated that femtosecond (fs) laser irradiation can induce localized restructuring of hydrogel networks, producing regions with reduced cross-link density and increased binding accessibility that preferentially recruit suspended nanoparticles (Figure b). This interaction-mediated, kinetically controlled assembly enables the fabrication of complex three-dimensional architectures with nanoscale feature definition and high particle packing density, without relying on specific chemical functionalization or linker molecules. Such observations point to a complementary design principle for NC-loaded photocurable systems, where spatially programmable modulation of polymer networks and interfacial interactions may be exploited to enhance dispersion stability, positional selectivity, and functional integration in next-generation NC-based 3D manufacturing systems.

While these photopolymerization-based approaches enable high-resolution 3D manufacturing, their reliance on polymeric matrices imposes inherent constraints. Even when the polymer component is removed during postprocessing, the accompanying thermal treatment often leads to structural contraction, residual impurities, or deterioration of the intrinsic material properties. These limitations motivate the development of polymer-free strategies for direct inorganic nanomanufacturing.

Photoreduction-Based 3D Manufacturing

Light induced photoreduction of metal precursors offers a direct route to fabricate metallic micro- and nanostructures without relying on sacrificial polymer matrices. In this method, irradiation of a photosensitive metal salt solution triggers the reduction of metal ions into neutral atoms, which subsequently nucleate and grow within the highly localized laser focal volume. − By translating the focal point in three dimensions, metallic architectures can be written directly from solution in a template-free manner.

An illustrative example is a polymer-free ultrafast two-photon decomposition laser writing strategy reported by Wang et. To further improve reduction kinetics and structural quality, precursor solutions are often supplemented with photosensitizing dyes or photoreducing surfactants that facilitate electron transfer during irradiation. , For instance, Cao et al. introduced n-decanoylsarcosine sodium (NDSS) as a surfactant capable of suppressing particle coalescence. Increasing the NDSS concentration effectively limited nanoparticle growth, producing particles whose diameter decreased with surfactant concentration and reached a stable size of approximately 20 nm when the NDSS level exceeded 0.033 M. The surfactant-mediated growth control also improved the structural uniformity of the printed features, enabling the fabrication of nanopillars with characteristic dimensions down to ∼180 nm and highly reproducible free-standing silver pyramid arrays (Figure b).

Photoinduced Interfacial Bonding for 3D Assembly

In contrast to in situ photoreduction approaches, presynthesized NCs can be directly employed as functional building blocks through light-induced interfacial reactions. In these systems, two-photon excitation is confined to the ligand shells of colloidal nanocrystals, triggering localized chemical transformations that enable interparticle bonding.

Liu et al. introduced a photoexcitation-induced chemical bonding (PEB) strategy that enables femtosecond laser direct writing of semiconductor quantum dots into densely packed 3D architectures (Figure c). In this process, optical excitation generates holes that migrate to the nanocrystal surface and trigger the detachment of surface ligands, thereby activating chemically reactive sites that promote interparticle bonding and structural consolidation during laser scanning. This mechanism allows dispersed quantum dots to be assembled into complex microstructures with sub-100 nm resolution while preserving their intrinsic optical characteristics, including size-dependent emission properties.

Subsequent work further generalized this concept by introducing photochemical cross-linking strategies capable of bonding colloidal nanocrystals through their native surface ligands, enabling direct 3D manufacturing of inorganic nanomaterials without polymer resins. The resulting structures exhibit a high inorganic mass fraction exceeding ∼90% and retain the intrinsic physical properties of the constituent nanocrystals, including their optical and electronic characteristics. Importantly, this approach has demonstrated broad material compatibility, enabling the fabrication of three-dimensional architectures composed of semiconductors, metal oxides, metals, and hybrid compositions using the same photochemical bonding mechanism (Figure d).

Perspectives and Outlook

Light-driven NC assembly has progressed from planar photopatterning to three-dimensional manufacturing, providing an effective route to spatially defined NC-based structures. As discussed in this Review, light can regulate NC-containing systems through photopolymerization, photoreduction and interparticle bonding. Despite these advances, further progress requires improved generality, controllability, and reliability in light-driven NC fabrication. In particular, the field should move beyond method-centered demonstrations toward application-guided process design, in which the choice of material system, assembly mechanism, and manufacturing route is determined by the required function and operating environment.

Application requirements should be considered together with manufacturing routes and long-term stability. Displays and color-conversion devices require high-resolution patterning, high photoluminescence retention, low optical crosstalk, and uniform film thickness. Photonic structures require precise control over three-dimensional geometry, refractive-index contrast, and optical loss. Photodetectors demand continuous charge-transport pathways, whereas sensing and plasmonic systems require stable nanoscale gaps and reproducible local-field enhancement. Catalytic architectures need exposed active surfaces, chemical stability, and efficient mass transport, while quantum devices require low-defect interfaces and precise emitter positioning. These diverse requirements indicate that polymer-assisted and polymer-free routes should be selected according to the target function. Polymer-assisted methods offer good film formation, mechanical stability, and compatibility with established photolithography or photopolymerization-based printing, but may dilute the active NC phase and restrict surface accessibility or charge transport. Polymer-free methods based on photoreduction, photocatalytic patterning, ligand transformation, or direct interparticle bonding can generate NC-rich or fully inorganic structures, although challenges remain in ink stability, shrinkage, cracking, porosity, and mechanical reliability. In parallel, environmentally benign NCs, including InP-based QDs, lead-free perovskites, carbon-based nanomaterials, oxide NCs, and other low-toxicity systems, should be developed together with mild photochemistry, stable surface passivation, effective encapsulation, and robust interparticle bonding to improve operational stability under light, heat, electrical bias, oxygen, moisture, or chemical exposure.

A second priority is to improve the universality of light-driven assembly methods across different NC materials and processing conditions. Many reported strategies have been developed using semiconductor and perovskite QDs as model systems because of their strong optical responses and mature ligand chemistry. However, direct transfer of these methods to metallic NCs, oxide NCs, magnetic NCs, catalytic NCs, or hybrid NCs remains nontrivial. These materials differ in optical absorption, surface binding, redox activity, thermal stability, and interparticle interactions. Therefore, future work should move from material-specific demonstrations toward adaptable design rules. Photochemistry, ligand structure, solvent environment, development conditions, and irradiation mode should be adjusted according to the properties of each NC system and the desired structure. Such adaptability will be essential for extending light-driven NC assembly from QD-based patterning to a broader range of functional nanomaterials.

Another important direction is to make better use of the intrinsic properties of NCs during fabrication. In many polymer-assisted systems, NCs mainly act as functional fillers immobilized by a light-cross-linked matrix. Although these approaches are useful for forming uniform films and mechanically stable structures, they do not fully exploit the active role of NCs. Future strategies should use NCs more deliberately as photoactive or chemically active components. NCs with strong light absorption can serve as local photoactive centers; semiconductor NCs can participate in interfacial charge-transfer reactions; plasmonic NCs can provide local-field enhancement or photothermal effects; and redox-active NCs can promote photoreduction or surface transformation. Direct photocatalytic patterning is a representative example, where photoexcited NCs act as nanoscale photoinitiators or photosensitizers to initiate monomer polymerization, ligand activation, or ligand-bond transformation near the NC surface. This NC-mediated process can reduce the required UV dose and suppress photoinduced damage, highlighting the value of treating NCs not only as final functional materials but also as active participants in light-driven assembly.

Finally, the optical-processing strategy itself should be further diversified. Most current studies rely on mask photolithography, direct optical lithography, or serial laser writing. These approaches have established clear links between 2D patterning and 3D manufacturing, but scalability and throughput remain important limitations. Parallel optical approaches, including digital micromirror device (DMD)-based projection, spatial light modulation, and multibeam exposure, may improve processing efficiency while retaining spatial programmability. , Optical trapping and plasmon-driven assembly provide complementary routes in which light directly regulates particle position, local electromagnetic fields, photothermal response, or interparticle interactions. Photoresponsive DNA-functionalized NC assembly may also offer reversible and programmable organization through light-regulated molecular recognition. Although these approaches are not the main focus of this Review, they point to useful directions for expanding the optical toolbox. Overall, future progress will depend on combining adaptable materials chemistry, active use of NC properties, diversified optical control, and application-oriented process design to develop more reliable and scalable light-driven NC fabrication strategies.
