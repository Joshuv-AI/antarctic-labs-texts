Charting a path through high-risk medical device regulation: recommendations from the CORE-MD consortium
Authors: Jessica N Holtzman, Rita F Redberg
Published: 2025

Novel medications and medical devices offer the opportunity to help reduce the burden of chronic disease worldwide. Accordingly, millions of adults have implantable medical devices, with a recent assessment suggesting that more than 30% of adults over the age of 40 in the USA have an implantable metal device.1 Given the expanding use of medical devices, the generation of meaningful clinical data is key to understanding their long-term safety and effectiveness. As most devices—even high-risk ones—enter the market based upon small, nonrandomised studies at best,2 it is important that there be ongoing collection and availability of high-quality data in the postmarket setting. However, to date, the rigour of pre-market approval data has been highly variable internationally, resulting in an exaggerated reliance upon ongoing evidence generation following preliminary device approval.3

The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), as well as the Australian Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA), provide guidance on the recommended hierarchy of clinical evidence for the approval of high-risk medical devices.4,5 In the USA, such evidence is meant to provide a reasonable assurance of safety and effectiveness; in Australia, approval is contingent upon evidence that a medical device adheres to essential principles that include not compromising health and safety and being designed for long-term safety. Although randomised controlled trials (RCTs) are considered to be the gold-standard of evidence generation, there is increasing use of “real-world” data from registries and post-market surveillance.6 Only about 30% of trials used to support the FDA premarket approval of high-risk cardiovascular medical devices are randomised, and even fewer are blinded.2 Likewise, in an analysis of the approval data for 71 high-risk cardiovascular devices from 2000 to 2021 in Europe, no RCTs were identified as published at the time when the European Union mark of approval was obtained.7

In order to address the gap between the current state of evidence generation and optimal standards, the European Commission convened the Coordinating Research and Evidence for Medical Devices (CORE-MD) consortium with the charge of “translating expert knowledge into advice for [European Union] regulators”.8 The consortium of regulators, public health representatives, academics, patients, and medical associations sought to generate expert regulatory guidance, establishing a hierarchy of study designs and characteristics to support high-quality evidence generation for high-risk medical devices. In the summary Health Policy paper published in The Lancet Regional Health - Europe, Alan Fraser and colleagues8 present clear and nuanced regulatory guidance. The authors should be commended for their clearly defined recommendations for study designs and methodologies from initial to long-term clinical studies.

Compared with the FDA, which defines three key study stages of evidence generation (first-in-human and feasibility studies, pivotal studies, and post-market studies),4 the CORE-MD consortium defined four key stages (initial clinical studies, early clinical studies, confirmatory clinical studies, and long-term clinical studies).8 They suggest that the strength of evidence determined by the study design should be appropriate for the risk class and life-cycle stage of the medical device, with modified criteria for breakthrough or orphan medical devices. The authors emphasise the importance of implementing comparative study designs where feasible. They make the specific recommendation that for RCTs, an active comparator (the current best proven treatment) should be used, with a sham control if there is no approved existing intervention and after careful consideration of the ethical implications.9

Beyond the choice of study design, Fraser and colleagues present strong, well-defined statistical recommendations.8 They clearly delineate expectations around designing sufficiently powered trials, ensuring realistic follow-up and trial duration, avoiding surrogate endpoints in favour of validated clinical endpoints where feasible, providing a clear definition of non-inferiority, and encouraging adaptive trial design.

As noted by Fraser and colleagues, current European Union regulation prohibits notified bodies (independent, contracted certification organisations) from advising device manufacturers on study design ahead of evidence evaluation.8 Considering ways to enhance bidirectional communication between manufacturers and notified bodies during the trial design phase might promote parsimonious data generation in a manner that is least burdensome to manufacturers. In Table 3, Table 4, and Figure 2 of their Health Policy paper, the CORE-MD consortium defines a framework for the recommended level of clinical evidence that should optimally be generated for medical device approval. The authors acknowledge that the unique needs of distinct medical devices with varying levels of pre-existing data will require flexibility, such that a “one-size fits all” approach to data generation is not appropriate. As Fraser and colleagues note, the European Union Medical Device Regulations (MDR) Article 61 refers to “clinical data providing sufficient clinical evidence”, but it is unclear how “sufficient” is determined. In this statement, the CORE-MD consortium makes important progress towards defining what was previously unclear. Developing a mechanism for accountability and being certain that patient safety and benefits are the primary goal for evidentiary requirements will pose a crucial challenge to the implementation of the proposed standards. Different stakeholders bring different perspectives to the table, and CORE MD has done the difficult and important work of bringing them all together in this document.

The authors' carefully arrived at and reasonable recommendations seek to prioritise long-term patient safety without making data requirements overly onerous. We look forward to seeing these thoughtful recommendations implemented by the European Union task force of medical device regulators, which we hope will eventually inform global standards for regulatory guidance.
