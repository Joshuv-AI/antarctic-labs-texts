Thinking Ideologically: The Limited Role of Left and Right Labels as Policy Shortcuts
Authors: Sarah Lachance, Clareta Treger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHow do voters use left-right ideological labels as shortcuts for policy positions in evaluating electoral candidates? We offer a distinction between maximal and minimal forms of ideological thinking. While maximal thinking implies that voters rely on ideological proximity as a proxy for policy congruence with candidates, minimal thinking requires only that voters use ideological labels to infer candidates’ positions—even if their own ideological identification is inconsistent with policy preferences. Drawing on original experimental data from Canada (N = 1,087)—a multiparty system with a fluid ideological landscape—we find that voters’ ideological self-placement is often misaligned with their policy positions, especially among right-leaning individuals. However, voters still use ideological proximity to infer candidates’ policy stances in the absence of policy information, supporting the Minimal Theory. These findings contribute to theories of political decision-making beyond the United States and have implications for substantive representation in systems with centrist or ideologically flexible parties.Keywords: Ideology, Policy positions, Heuristics, Voting behavior, Conjoint

Introduction

How do voters use left and right labels as information shortcuts for policy positions when evaluating electoral candidates? While this question has been extensively studied by political scientists, it remains relevant and unsettled as the political landscape within countries changes, as does the meaning of left-right ideology over time. These shifts may alter what it means to “think ideologically,” namely to use ideological labels in political decision-making.

Traditionally, left-right ideology was understood as a system of beliefs about the role of the government in economic and social matters, with the left demanding more state intervention, and the right opposing it (Ellis and Stimson 2012). Thus, left-right ideology had clear policy implications. From this perspective, as ideological polarization among elites in many democracies is on the rise, there should be a clear mapping between the politicians’ ideological labels and their policy positions. In this case, using ideological labels as a shortcut for policy positions would be an efficient strategy for voters.

However, more recently left-right ideology has come to encompass a much broader set of connected ideas that goes beyond policy positions related to government intervention in socioeconomic issues, including moral values, attitudes toward social groups, and attitudes toward governance, suggesting that ideological identification is more than the sum of one’s policy positions (Lambert et al. 1986; Knutsen 1995; Malka and Lelkes 2010; Rovny and Edwards 2012; Bauer et al. 2017; Goren, Motta, and Smith 2020; Merkley 2022).

If left-right ideology has no fixed meaning, but is rather defined by the associations between different social groups and their ideas, who tend to amalgamate in left and right clusters (Cochrane 2015), then voters may be basing their self-placement on the left-right spectrum on factors other than policy positions. This would result in the two being correlated yet not perfectly aligned, and even possibly inconsistent. In political systems in which parties’ ideological labels map onto distinct policy platforms, but voters’ ideological identities do not, voting based on ideology could result in choices that conflict with one’s policy preferences, undermining substantive representation.

Thus, we need to understand how and to what degree voters use ideological labels as referring to policy positions. We argue that voters could use ideological labels as shortcuts for policy positions in a maximal or a minimal manner. The first requires that voters think of their own ideological placement as a summary of their policy preferences and use proximity between this self-placement and candidates’ ideological labels as a heuristic for assessing policy congruence. A less demanding option—what we call a “Minimal Theory”—is that voters use ideological labels to infer candidates’ policy positions even if they do not hold ideologically consistent policy preferences themselves, or if their ideological self-placement also relates to elements beyond policies.

To test whether voters “think ideologically” in one of these two ways, we study Canada, a case where the role of left-right ideology in voter decision-making is not settled. While elite ideological polarization in Canada has increased and the party system has reorganized itself along a left-right ideological dimension in past decades (Carty, Young, and Cross 2000; Cross and Young 2002; Cochrane 2015; Merkley 2022; Lachance and Beauvais 2024), the usefulness of ideological labels as heuristics is not as clear as in the United States.

We begin by examining the relationship between Canadians’ self-placement on the standard left-right ideological scale and their policy preferences on salient issues, setting the stage for testing the Maximal and Minimal theories. Then, using an original conjoint experiment, we investigate whether voters use ideological proximity with electoral candidates to infer policy congruence (Maximal Theory) through a mediation analysis. Focusing on the centrist Liberal Party, we can credibly manipulate the candidates’ ideological labels (Barber and Pope 2019). We also test the Minimal Theory by examining how the ideological labels of candidates impact the relationship between the policy positions of voters and vote choice in the absence of policy information.

The findings show that voters “think ideologically” according to the Minimal Theory. First, for a large share of voters, especially on the right, ideological self-placement is not aligned with policy positions. Second, while proximity between the voters’ self-placement and the candidates’ labels has a positive effect on vote choice, voters do not use it to infer policy congruence.

However, voters use the candidates’ ideological labels as a shortcut for the candidates’ policy positions, in support of the Minimal Theory. In sum, while voters’ ideological self-placement is not a perfect summary of their policy preferences, voters can still use candidates’ left-right ideology to infer how likely they are to represent their policy interests to some extent.

This study makes several contributions. Theoretically, it introduces a distinction between minimal and maximal uses of ideological labels as heuristics for policies—an approach that extends beyond the well-studied two-party US context. Methodologically, it applies an underutilized mediation analysis within an experimental design to make causal claims about how voters use ideological proximity as a shortcut for policy congruence. Empirically, the study finds that Canadians use ideological labels in a limited manner, despite the realignment of the Canadian party system along the left-right ideological dimension. These findings may be generalizable to multiparty systems featuring meaningful centrist parties or parties with flexible platforms.

Finally, our findings have important implications for democratic representation: if voters’ ideological identification is not consistent with their policy positions, yet they use proximity between their ideological self-placement and the candidates’ ideological labels in their vote choice, this may lead to legislatures that poorly represent citizens’ policy preferences. Yet this may be less concerning for representation if voters use ideology in ways that extend beyond policy positions, such as signaling values, or identity (Huddy, Mason, and Aarøe 2015), as our findings suggest, by elimination.

Different Ways to Think Ideologically

Left-right ideology is a fluid concept that is made of a web of associated ideas that vary across places, time, and individuals (Laponce 1975; Lambert et al. 1986; Huber and Inglehart 1995; Zschirnt 2011; Bauer et al. 2017; Ostrowski 2023). While the meaning of left and right is not fixed, it is not indeterminate either. In Cochrane’s words, the connections between the concepts that define left and right are more like family resemblance; we know they are linked without there being a perfect correspondence (Cochrane 2015). Most importantly, left-right ideology can shape politics—rather than just be a handy concept to simplify a complex reality—because the media, politicians, and ordinary citizens use these labels. According to Ellis and Stimson (2012, 2): “There is, however, a reality defined by usage. When political actors are publicly labeled—by themselves or by others—with these terms, then their particular constellations of views on the issues of the day become the reality.” In sum, it is the actors and the social groups that claim or are assigned the labels of left and right that impart meaning to ideological labels.

Traditionally, left-right ideology has been used to summarize the debate about the role of the state in the economy, with the left demanding more intervention, and the right promoting the free market (Ellis and Stimson 2012). Later, noneconomic issues have also been added to this dimension (De Vries, Hakhverdian, and Lancee 2013), with people on the left taking “progressive” positions advocating for the intervention of the state to protect minorities, the environment, public health, and personal freedoms, and people on the right taking “conservative” positions that aim at limiting the interference of the state in what they consider private matters, while perceiving it the state’s role to uphold traditional values.1 Considering the multidimensionality of left and right, a recent study of Western European countries found an asymmetry in left-right identification, such that support for the left is common among voters with progressive attitudes on both economic and cultural issues, while it is enough to be conservative on just one issue to support the right (Gidron 2022).

Hence, left-right ideology has been used primarily by the media and political actors to discuss policy matters. It is less clear, however, how citizens use these concepts to make sense of the political world. Specifically, do they use left and right labels to make inferences about the policy positions of parties and electoral candidates?

Parties must organize their policy positions in an ideologically consistent manner, for ideology to serve as an effective shortcut for parties’ policy positions. There is mounting evidence of this type of ideological polarization in the United States (Fiorina and Abrams 2009), Europe (van der Eijk, Schmitt, and Binder 2005; Lachat 2008; Schmitt and Freire 2012; Dahlberg and Harteveld 2016), and Canada (Cochrane 2015; Johnston 2023; Merkley 2022). In these countries, the labels left and right more clearly link to different sets of policy orientations. The ideological differentiation of party platforms should make it easier for voters to vote, as they can use ideological labels to infer where the parties stand on most issues (Levendusky 2010; Dahlberg and Harteveld 2016). In Canada, there is some indirect evidence that cues from parties with a clearer ideological stance have stronger effects on policy preferences, thereby suggesting that Canadian voters associate ideological labels with policies (Merolla, Stephenson, and Zechmeister 2008, 2016; Merkley 2021).

Maximal Theory: Using Ideological Labels to Infer Congruence on Policy Positions

To use ideological labels as shortcuts for candidates’ policy positions, voters need to link these labels to particular policies. One way to do it, which we call the Maximal Theory, is when voters’ ideological self-placement is aligned with their policy positions, and then they use the labels to infer policy congruence between themselves and the candidates.

Studies of voter behavior in the United States and Europe show that ideological polarization at the party level has made left-right ideological self-placement a stronger predictor of vote choice (Levitin and Miller 1979; van der Eijk, Schmitt, and Binder 2005; Jost 2006; Lachat 2008; Kinder and Kalmoe 2017). In their extensive cross-national study, Malka, Lelkes, and Soto (2019) find that citizens in developed countries are more likely to utilize the left-right dimension, exhibiting a positive correlation between their right-wing cultural and economic attitudes.

Moreover, there is evidence that the policy positions of Canadian voters can be summarized into multiple dimensions, including left-right ideology, that influence vote choice (Nevitte 2000; Blais et al. 2002; Scotto, Stephenson, and Kornberg 2004; Gidengil et al. 2006; Gidengil 2013; Malka, Lelkes, and Soto 2019; Lucas and Armstrong 2021), and that in Canada there is a correlation, although not perfect, between ideological self-placement and policy positions (Lucas and Armstrong 2021; Merkley 2022). Taken together, this evidence suggests there exists some level of policy-based “ideological thinking” among Canadian citizens.

Under the Maximal Theory, voters compare candidates’ ideological labels to their own ideological self-placement—thereby assessing ideological proximity—to infer policy congruence. If ideological proximity is used as a shortcut for policy congruence, its effect on vote choice should decrease when voters have information about the candidates’ policy stances, compared to when they have no policy information at all. This is because having explicit and readily available information about the policy positions of the candidate would eliminate the need for a shortcut to guess this information. If voters think ideologically in such a way, we expect that:

H1: The positive effect of ideological proximity with a candidate on the probability of voting for the candidate is weaker when information about their policy positions is available than in the absence of policy information.

Minimal Theory: Using Ideological Labels to Infer Candidates’ Policy Positions

The theory above may be overly demanding of voters. Indeed, a long tradition of scholarship on American voter behavior stemming from Converse’s landmark study (1964) has demonstrated that voters do not exhibit the same level of ideological consistency across their policy positions as elites do, and that their policy positions are not in alignment with their ideological self-placement (Levitin and Miller 1979; Baldassarri and Gelman 2008; Treier and Hillygus 2009; Ellis and Stimson 2012; Feldman and Johnston 2014; Ahler and Broockman 2015; Claassen, Tucker, and Smith 2015; Lupton, Myers, and Thornton 2015; Kinder and Kalmoe 2017; Lesschaeve 2017; Ahler and Broockman 2018; Kalmoe 2020). As Converse (1964) puts it, the state that best represents the ordinary voter is “ideological innocence.” More recently, Ahler and Broockman (2015, 2018) argued that voters do not think about politics in ideological terms, as most hold a mix of leftist and rightist policy positions, and seek candidates who match their particular mix. Similarly, Klar (2014) shows that even among Americans, there are those whose attitudes are not captured well by a single left-right ideological dimension.

In line with these findings, the comparative analyses in Cochrane (2015) and Rodon (2015) show that in multiple countries, citizens’ policy positions across issues are not organized ideologically, nor are they consistent with their self-placement on a left-right scale. Moreover, in many nondeveloped countries, the correlation between right cultural and economic attitudes is shown to be negative (Malka, Lelkes, and Soto 2019).

Under the Minimal Theory of thinking ideologically, even if voters have loosely structured policy positions that are not correlated with their ideological self-placement, they could use the candidates’ ideological labels as a shortcut for missing information about their policy positions. In other words, voters could expect ideological labels to be associated with certain policy sets even if they haven’t brought their own policy positions in line with their ideological identity.

If voters use the candidates’ left and right labels to infer alignment between their own policy-based ideological position—the overall ideological direction of a voter’s policy positions across issues—and the politicians’ policy positions, then we expect that:

H2: In the absence of policy information, as congruence between the candidate’s ideological label and the policy-based ideological position of the voter increases, the likelihood of voting for the candidate increases.

Identity-Based Ideological Thinking

Finally, ideological thinking could be unrelated to policies. The evidence of a lack of a perfect mapping between voters’ policy positions and their ideological self-placement suggests that left-right ideology is not a mere summary of one’s policy positions. As left-right self-placement is often correlated with vote choice, this non-policy-based interpretation of the left-right dimension is relevant for voter behavior.

From a social identity perspective, ideological labels can act as group markers that guide behavior, often overriding self-interest (Tajfel and Turner 2000; Achen and Bartels 2016). According to Ellis and Stimson (2012), voters’ ideological self-placement reflects identity rather than coherent policy views. In this respect, left-right ideology is symbolic, with symbols and values—religious, patriotic, moral—playing a central role (Conover and Feldman 1981; Swedlow and Wyckoff 2009; Feldman and Johnston 2014; Goren, Motta, and Smith 2020). This option is supported by evidence of strong correlations between social group membership and ideological self-placement (Ellis and Stimson 2012; Claassen, Tucker, and Smith 2015).

Thus, left and right can signify social group belonging, influencing affect and assumptions about others’ beliefs (Conover and Feldman 1981; Malka and Lelkes 2010; Huddy, Mason, and Aarøe 2015). As elites polarize, ideological labels become stronger cues for group alignment and affective reactions (Mason and Wronski 2018; Oshri, Yair, and Huddy 2022). In this context, political actors use left-right labels strategically to signal group loyalties and mobilize voters.

While our primary focus is on testing the evidence for the policy-based ideological thinking, a lack of supporting evidence for the Maximal Theory (H1) would point—by elimination—toward the plausibility of identity-based ideological thinking. The reason is that if voters use left-right labels to reflect identity, then supplying policy information should not affect the role of ideological proximity with candidates in their decision, since they do not use ideological labels as shortcuts for policy.

The Canadian Case Study

We test our hypotheses in the Canadian multiparty system. It consists of left-leaning (NDP, Green), center-left (Liberal), right-leaning (Conservative, The People’s Party2), and regional (Bloc Québécois) parties.3 Figure 1 presents the distribution of ideological self-placement by party identification in our sample. It reflects that the above ideological mapping of parties by experts is generally shared by party supporters, with voters identifying with the Liberal Party and the Green Party being slightly more centrist.

Distribution of ideological self-placement by party identification. N = 983.

Several reasons make the Canadian context a suitable setting to test the degree to which voters think ideologically in terms of policies. The Canadian political system has long been characterized as devoid of ideology, due to the brokerage politics required to maintain national unity (Carty, Young, and Cross 2000; Henderson 2004; Cochrane and Perrella 2012; LeDuc and Pammet 2016; Johnston 2017; Clarke et al. 2019). In such a system, parties seek to resolve internal policy disagreements by brokering between different factions (Carty 2015), and interparty disagreements by compromise to form governing coalitions. This produces parties with more flexible policy platforms that were historically only loosely tied to ideology.

However, since the 1980s, the Canadian party system has reorganized along a left-right ideological dimension (Carty, Young, and Cross 2000; Cross and Young 2002; Cochrane 2010; Johnston 2023; Lachance and Beauvais 2024), with parties becoming increasingly associated with specific ideological families (Cochrane 2015). Moreover, increasing ideological polarization among elites (Johnston 2023; Merkley 2022) should make their ideological labels more informative heuristics for inferring their policy positions.

Yet, evidence suggests that voters have not followed suit and remain concentrated around the ideological center (Johnston 2023; Merkley 2022), which aligns with the narrative of the “ideologically innocent” public (Converse 1964; Kinder and Kalmoe 2017). Also, voters’ party identification in Canada is considered flexible, resulting in an electorate described as volatile (Johnston 2017; Clarke et al. 2019), unlike in the US two-party and highly polarized political system. The ideological centrism of Canadians and the previously documented lack of consistency between their ideological self-identification and their policy positions, combined with their flexible party allegiances, make it unclear what role “ideological thinking” should play in their decision-making, even in a context of elite ideological polarization.

Moreover, parties’ ideological labels and policies are still not perfectly sorted. The Liberal Party, one of the two main Canadian parties, which has held power since the 2015 election,4 has been historically considered a center party, adopting both left- and right-leaning policies, depending on the electoral context. However, in recent decades, it has been moving leftward and is now considered a center-left party (Cochrane 2015; Merkley 2022; Dassonneville and Koedam 2024). The Liberal Party’s center position, fluid ideological identity, and flexible platforms provide us with an opportunity to test the role of ideological labels by credibly manipulating the ideological label of this party in our experiment.5

We take the Canadian case to be representative of multiparty systems, particularly those with viable center parties that can shift coalitions and adapt their policy platforms depending on the electoral context. In such contexts, we expect left-right labels to serve as weaker heuristics than in two-party systems (holding ideological polarization constant). Since there are multiple parties on the same ideological side (left or right)—and parties with the same ideological labels could have slightly different policies—the use of ideological heuristics will result in more error and make the mapping between ideological labels and policies more ambiguous. In addition, voters are likely to have ideologically inconsistent policy positions. Therefore, it is more likely that voters will engage in minimal rather than maximal ideological thinking.

Methods

Data

To test the hypotheses, we fielded a survey experiment from June 19 through July 1, 2023, with a sample of 1,087 Canadian voters recruited by Abacus (see Appendix A for more details). Respondents with missing values were excluded from the relevant analysis, resulting in a final sample of 983. See Supplementary Material section A for demographic information of the sample and a comparison to population benchmarks. The pre-experimental module was used to measure respondents’ ideological self-placement and policy positions. In the experimental module, the respondents completed three paired conjoint tasks, as we elaborate below.6

Measures

The pre-experimental module included a standard 0–10 ideological left-right self-placement scale. A respondent’s ideological orientation was coded as left when the score on ideological self-placement is below 5, centrist when it equals 5, and right when above 5. Figure 2 presents the ideological distribution of the sample. It shows that the center is the modal category, with more than 25 percent of the respondents identifying as centrists, and that the sample is slightly skewed to the right.

Left-right ideological self-placement in the sample. Red and orange represent left-leaning ideology; white represents the ideological midpoint; and dark blue and light blue represent right-leaning policies. N = 983.

The pre-experimental module also included items measuring policy positions on four issues: deficit, climate action, immigration, and COVID-19 vaccine mandates. We chose issues on which we could list policies that clearly map onto the traditional understanding of left-right as government intervention. There were two reasons for this. First, this enables us to measure the ideological consistency of our respondents across issues. Second, only ideologically divisive issues would render ideological labels relevant for inferring the positions of candidates and parties on these issues (as opposed to valence issues like creating jobs). Three of the issues were volunteered by Canadians among the top ten most important issues during the period of designing and administering the survey.7 While COVID-19 was not one of the most important issues at that time, it was previously a highly divisive issue along the left-right dimension in Canada, and it taps directly into the desired degree of government intervention in socioeconomic affairs. We acknowledge that the effect of ideological proximity as a shortcut for policy congruence may vary with the set of policy issues included, yet focusing on issues salient at the time of the survey or in the recent past should capture the use of such a heuristic well, if it is indeed applied by voters. The policy positions for each issue in the pre-experimental module match the levels of the policy attributes in the conjoint (see table 1 below).8

The experimental module consisted of a conjoint in which respondents were asked to evaluate the profiles of two electoral candidates with the following attributes in random order: ethnicity, gender, party affiliation, ideological position, and policy positions on the four issues. Table 1 lists the attributes and levels.9 We hold the candidates’ party affiliation constant at “Liberal Party” to avoid confounding due to respondents inferring the party affiliation of the candidates based on the policy and ideology attributes. The Liberal Party, one of the two main Canadian parties, is a center party that has historically shifted between left- and right-leaning positions depending on the political context, and was considered more left-leaning at the time of the study. As such, it is credible for a Liberal candidate to be identified with either a left- or right-wing ideological label. Moreover, Treger et al. (2026) find that voters do not consistently associate the Liberal Party with clearly left- or right-wing policy positions.

Following the conjoint task, the respondents were asked to choose between two electoral candidates, and rate their support for each candidate in an election on a scale of 1–7. We report here the results for the choice outcome.

We coded ideological proximity as the negative absolute distance between a respondent’s self-placement on the ideological scale and each candidate’s ideological label. This transformation ensures that greater proximity is associated with higher support for the candidates, facilitating interpretation of its expected positive effect on candidate ratings and vote choice. For example, if a respondent places herself at 5 on the scale and a candidate is positioned at 7.5, the ideological proximity score would be –2.5.

We coded the policy-based ideology of the respondent by taking the proportion of policy positions in the pre-experimental module that could be labeled as right across issues according to the traditional understanding of left and right. Specifically, policies that involve government intervention (cutting emissions, mandatory COVID-19 vaccination, government spending, and increased immigration) were coded as left, and those that aim at decreasing government intervention as right. Higher values on this scale indicate that the respondent leans more to the right. For example, if a respondent chose a right-leaning policy position on all four issues, the policy-based ideology variable would take the value of 1.

Identification Strategy

We begin by examining the extent to which Canadians’ ideological self-placement aligns with their policy preferences. Next, to test the Maximal Theory, we conducted a mediation analysis where the effect of the candidates’ ideological labels on vote choice is mediated by inferences about their policy positions based on these labels. In this, we follow the strategy employed by Sen (2017) in her study of race as a shortcut for the party affiliation of judicial nominees.10

To assess whether ideological labels are used as shortcuts for policy by voters, we randomly assigned half of the sample to the full conjoint with all the attributes, and the other half to a reduced conjoint without the policy attributes. In the analysis, we take the difference between the effect of ideological proximity on candidate preferences in the full conjoint and the reduced conjoint to recover the part of the effect of ideological proximity on vote choice that is mediated by inferences about the policy positions of the candidate. We identify this difference by including an interaction between ideological proximity and the experimental condition in a linear regression model. To support the Maximal Theory, the positive effect of ideological proximity on the probability of voting for the candidate needs to be weaker when information about their policy positions is available (H1).

If we fail to support H1, we will turn to testing the Minimal Theory, namely whether the interaction between the voter’s policy-based ideology and the candidate’s ideological position has an effect on vote choice, in the subset of the sample that was assigned to the reduced conjoint (with no information about the candidates’ policy positions). To support the theory, the results have to show that as the voter’s policy positions move in the direction of the candidate’s ideological label, the likelihood of voting for the candidate increases (H2). This would translate into a positive effect of voter policy-based ideology on the likelihood of voting for right candidates, and a negative effect of voter policy-based ideology on the likelihood of voting for left candidates (as respondents with higher values on policy-based ideology are more right-leaning).

Results

Congruence Between Ideological Self-Placement and Policy Positions

Does ideological self-placement correlate in meaningful ways with the positions voters take on issues? Are voters who place themselves on the left (right) of the ideological scale more likely to take policy positions that are associated with the left (right)? To answer these questions, we looked at the distribution of policy-based ideology within ideological groups: left, center, and right (based on the classification described in the methods section). Figure 3 shows that self-identified leftists have relatively consistent issue positions, but self-identified rightists lean more to the left on policy than what we would expect. While only 11 percent of leftists have inconsistent (right) policy positions on a majority of issues, 43 percent of rightists take inconsistent (left) policy positions on issues. For these voters, using ideological proximity as a shortcut for policy congruence may lead to “incorrect” voting.11 This finding suggests that ideological proximity with candidates is a less useful shortcut for policy congruence among voters who identify as right than it is among voters who identify as left-leaning. Centrists are normally distributed across the policy-based ideology scale, as we would expect, with 25 percent holding an even split between left and right positions, and 42 percent leaning more to the left.

While voters who place themselves on the left of the ideological scale tend to take issue positions that are more to the left (around 70 percent across issues), there is no meaningful pattern for voters who place themselves on the right. On all issues, they tend to split equally between left and right positions. For instance, even on the classic left-right issue of the deficit, more than 50 percent of voters who identify as right take a leftist position that involves increasing the deficit. For voters who identify at the center of the ideological scale, we observe (as would be expected) a roughly even split between support for left and right policies across issues.

Overall, these analyses corroborate previous findings regarding the weaker association between ideology and policy preferences among the Canadian electorate. The results suggest that ideological proximity may not be a particularly helpful shortcut for policy congruence, as many (especially right-leaning) voters do not hold ideologically consistent policy preferences. We now turn to examine this, by testing our hypotheses.

Testing the Maximal Theory

If voters use ideological proximity between their ideological self-placement and the candidates’ ideological labels to infer policy congruence, its effect would weaken when information about candidates’ actual policy positions—its intended substitute—is available (H1). In other words, the presence of ready-to-use information about the policy positions of candidates would eliminate the need for a shortcut to guess missing policy information.

To test the Maximal Theory, we check whether there is a difference between the effect of ideological proximity on vote choice in the full conjoint and its effect in the reduced conjoint. Figure 5 shows that ideological proximity has a substantial positive effect on vote choice, but this effect is similar in the absence and presence of policy information. More formally, the effect of the interaction between ideological proximity and the experimental condition on vote choice is consistent with H1 (positive for the reduced conjoint), yet is very small and not statistically significant at conventional levels (b = 0.01, p = 0.20).13

To assess whether our null finding could be attributed to insufficient power, we conducted a simulation-based post-hoc power analysis (see Supplementary Material section D). The minimal detectable interaction effect was 0.075. Thus, while we cannot exclude the possibility of a subtle moderation effect by the presence (absence) of policy information, the results suggest that any such effect is negligible relative to the main effect of ideological proximity. Supplementary Material figure C1 further shows that in the full conjoint, both ideological proximity and policy congruence affect support, suggesting that these policies matter for vote choice.

We examined whether these results are conditional on voters’ ideological extremity, comparing centrists (self-identifying at 5 on the ideological scale) to noncentrists.14 The results remain unchanged for leftists and rightists. For centrists, the effect of the ideological label is much smaller in the presence of policy information than in its absence, as would be expected. Yet, the difference between the experimental conditions is also not statistically significant (b = 0.02, p = 0.13) (see Supplementary Material section C.3 for the regression results and AMEs).

As our main effects do not support the Maximal Theory, it could be that ideological labels stand for more than policy preferences. We return to this possibility in the Discussion.

Testing the Minimal Theory

While the findings show that most voters do not use ideological proximity as a shortcut for policy congruence, they may instead adopt a more frugal strategy and use the candidates’ ideological labels, independent of their own ideological self-placement. To provide evidence for this Minimal Theory, the results must show that voters who hold policy positions that are more to the right have a higher likelihood of voting for a candidate with a right label than voters who take policy positions that are more to the left, and vice versa (H2).

Overall, we find support for the Minimal Theory. Discussion

We set out to examine whether and how voters in the Canadian multiparty system—one that has been polarizing, but has lower levels of ideological sorting and polarization as compared to the United States—“think ideologically.” We offer a distinction between two forms of policy-based ideological thinking: Maximal thinking requires that voters rely on ideological proximity as a proxy for policy congruence with candidates and assumes voters’ ideological identification is consistent with their policy preferences. Minimal thinking requires only that voters use ideological labels to infer candidates’ positions—even if their own ideological identification is inconsistent with policy preferences.

Using an original conjoint experiment, we find support for minimal, but not maximal, ideological thinking. Voters use candidates’ left and right ideological labels to infer the policy positions of candidates in voting decisions, irrespective of their own ideological identification. This is in line with our descriptive analysis that shows that among a large share of voters, especially on the right, policy positions are not consistent with ideological self-placement. Notably, this is consistent with Gidron’s (2022) findings from Western Europe that it is enough to hold some (but not only) right policy positions to identify as right (although Malka, Lelkes, and Soto [2019] find a positive correlation between the economic and cultural policy dimensions in Canada). In sum, left and right are useful as policy shortcuts, to a limited extent.

Our finding that ideological proximity has a substantive positive effect on vote choice even in the presence of policy information on salient issues suggests—by elimination—that for voters, these labels go beyond policies. One option is identity-based ideological thinking—ideological labels may represent similar moral values, personality traits, and shared affect toward social groups. Hence, ideological proximity may be used by voters to infer whether the candidate belongs to their in-group.

An alternative explanation of this finding is that respondents were using ideological labels to infer policy positions on issues that were not included in the experiment, such as cultural issues. This is a limitation of our design, as we focused on the conventional understanding of left and right as government intervention, and even within this dimension had to decide which policies to exclude. We made an effort to include issues on which policies clearly map to left and right and that were salient at the time of the survey. Yet, further experimental research that includes more issues is needed to test our conclusion using additional left-right policy dimensions.

Another limitation relates to our choice to fix the party in our experiment. While we leveraged the Canadian Liberal Party to credibly manipulate its ideological label (similar to Barber and Pope 2019), we acknowledge that this limits the generalizability of our findings. Our results are most relevant for parties with a flexible ideological identity or those that adopt a mix of left- and right-leaning policies—most commonly, though not exclusively, centrist parties. In multiparty systems, such parties compete against other parties with clearer and more consistent ideological platforms (see Gidron 2022), requiring voters to choose between them for various reasons. Our design does not capture such interparty choices directly, nor does it apply to candidate choices within parties with clear left- or right-leaning ideologies. Yet, by holding the party constant, we are able to isolate the role of ideology and provide evidence of how voters think ideologically when a party’s positioning is ambiguous, independent of partisan attachments. This matters not only for intraparty competition but also for understanding how centrist parties compete with parties across the ideological spectrum. We therefore view this as a contribution to the literature on the role of left-right ideology in voter decision-making, extending beyond the extensively studied two-party US political system.

We make several contributions. Theoretically, we offer a distinction between minimal and maximal uses of ideological labels as heuristics for policies. This distinction is theoretically meaningful, as each form of ideological thinking may influence vote choice through different pathways. Methodologically, we employ an underutilized mediation analysis within an experimental design to make causal claims about how voters use ideological proximity as a shortcut for policy congruence. Thereby, we contribute to the literature by empirically disentangling the two ways of ideological thinking.

Empirically, we explore what it means to be on the left or the right in multiparty systems featuring electorally viable center parties or parties with flexible platforms (e.g., France, The Netherlands, and Israel). We find that Canadians use ideological labels in a limited manner, despite the realignment of the Canadian party system along the left-right ideological dimension. Even when elites and parties sort along this dimension, having clear policy implications from their point of view, it may not apply similarly to voters. For voters, who often lack political sophistication, assuming a preexisting set of coherent beliefs about how society should be organized and how power should be distributed—a political ideology—that can be translated into policy positions is a much stronger assumption. Moreover, the fact that partisans sort themselves on the left-right axis in expected ways does not mean they are strictly policy-driven. In fact, parties—and their ideological labels—may be associated with other things than policy in voters’ minds, like social groups, moral values, and even personality traits (Huddy, Mason, and Aarøe 2015).

Finally, our findings have implications for democratic representation. If voters’ ideological self-identification does not align with their policy preferences, but they nonetheless base their vote choices on proximity to candidates’ ideological labels, this may result in legislatures that inadequately reflect the public’s policy preferences. Yet this may be less concerning if voters care about other dimensions of representation (Pitkin 1967), and use ideology in ways that extend beyond policy positions, such as signaling values, or identity.
