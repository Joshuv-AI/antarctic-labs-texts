Transcaval SESAME, LAMPOON, and Valve-in-MAC
Authors: Frederik Uttenthal, Phillip Freeman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe SESAME (SEptal Scoring Along Midline Endocardium) procedure is early in its evolution but seems to be an effective adjunct to facilitate transcatheter mitral valve replacement (TMVR) in patients with complex anatomy.Case SummaryAn 80-year-old woman was admitted with pulmonary edema and diagnosed with severe mitral annular calcification (MAC) and significant mixed mitral disease prohibitive for surgery. She had a history of hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy previously treated with alcohol septal ablation. We performed a SESAME followed by LAMPOON (Laceration of the Anterior Mitral leaflet to Prevent Outflow ObstructioN) to enable a successful transcatheter valve-in-MAC.DiscussionValve-in-MAC constitutes a high risk for major complications. This case demonstrates an approach with different minimally invasive electrosurgical procedures to enable successful TMVR.Take-Home MessageThe case highlights the importance of collaboration, pre- and intraprocedural imaging, and preparation in order to predict and prevent the risk of iatrogenic left ventricular outflow tract obstruction when performing TMVR in complex patients.Key words: imaging, mitral valve, valve replacement

History of Presentation

An 80-year-old woman was admitted with acute hypertensive pulmonary edema and severe combined hypoxic and hypercapnic respiratory failure, eventually leading to loss of consciousness. Arterial carbon dioxide tension was 10 kPa with a pH of 7.08. She was quickly alleviated with intravenous furosemide, nitroglycerin, and noninvasive ventilation in the intensive care unit. Physical examination displayed a systolic and diastolic murmur. She had minor pleural effusion. Her body mass index was 22 kg/m2, and she had a blood pressure of 172/55 mm Hg. The electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm with left bundle branch block, unchanged from previous assessment. Blood tests demonstrated normal hemoglobin and a C-reactive protein level of 23 mg/L (normal range: <8 mg/L). High-sensitivity cardiac troponins were not requested.

Past Medical History

Five years prior to the current hospitalization, the patient had been treated with alcohol septal ablation (ASA) for a clinical and echocardiographic diagnosis of hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy with severe symptoms including dyspnea, pulmonary edema, and exertional syncope. She had transient complete heart block during the procedure and permanent left bundle branch block. The septal reduction therapy was, however, sufficient with complete relief of left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction. She was effectively treated with amiodarone and apixaban for paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Differential Diagnoses

Considerations included myocardial infarction with potential mechanical complications (ventricular septal defect, papillary muscle rupture) and severe valve disease (aortic or mitral stenosis/insufficiency).

Investigations

Transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography displayed severe mitral annular calcification (MAC), severe mitral regurgitation, and stenosis (Figure 1, Video 1), as well as a high estimated pulmonary arterial systolic pressure (tricuspid regurgitation contour: 55 mm Hg), left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy with a sigmoid septum, and no sign of LVOT obstruction (Video 2). The LV ejection fraction was 45% to 50%. Three-dimensional reconstruction analyses (3-Mensio software) of a full-phase cardiac computed tomography (CT) scan confirmed massive MAC (Figure 2), a large mitral annulus of 770 mm2, and—with a 33-mm virtual valve implant—a predicted life-threatening narrow neo-LVOT of 29.9 mm2 (Figure 3). Neoskirt LVOT after leaflet modification with the LAMPOON (Laceration of the Anterior Mitral leaflet to Prevent Outflow ObstructioN) technique was predicted to be 246 mm2. Considering the patient's history of ASA, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging with late gadolinium enhancement showed no significant fibrosis in the basal septum (Figure 4). Upon evaluation with the multidisciplinary heart team, the patient was turned down for open heart surgery, as severe MAC was considered a poor surgical target for successful valve replacement.

Management

The percutaneous options were evaluated at a multidisciplinary team conference. The anatomy was difficult and did imply a high risk of dangerous outflow obstruction even with mitral leaflet modification (LAMPOON)1 preceding transcatheter mitral valve replacement (TMVR). The significant septal hypertrophy also increased the risk of valve delivery interaction with the septum and complication during treatment. Additionally, CT had revealed severe bilateral iliofemoral calcification (Figure 5) too small for a safe arterial transfemoral access with larger sheaths. Consensus was reached to attempt an enlargement of the LVOT and thereby enable TMVR by performing a transcaval2 SESAME (SEptal Scoring Along Midline Endocardium) procedure.3,4 The target laceration depth was approximately 50% of the septal myocardium thickness based on experiences from the Emory Structural Heart and Valve Center (Atlanta, Georgia) and the University of Washington Medical Center (Seattle, Washington),3,4 and the intramyocardial traversal was accomplished after uncomplicated transcaval access and cautious CT planning with biplane fluoroscopy and transesophageal echocardiography guidance (Figure 6, Video 3, Video 4, Video 5, Video 6, Video 7, Video 8, Video 9).

Four weeks after SESAME, a full-phase cardiac CT confirmed maturation of the myotomy and enlargement of the neo-LVOT up to 144 mm2 (Figure 7). There was a clear “LVOT gutter” from the LV to the aortic valve, with significant reduction in the barrier of the septum allowing valve implantation. The patient was once again admitted with hypertensive pulmonary edema 1 month after SESAME and treated lege artis in the intensive care unit. An even larger skirt neo-LVOT was clearly obtainable by performing the LAMPOON before valve implantation. Standard base-to-tip LAMPOON was then executed5 (Video 10, Video 11, Video 12, Video 13, Video 14). Just prior to the LAMPOON, percutaneous LV-apical access was gained, guided by ultrasound and confirmation of coronary anatomy (left anterior descending artery) position. Access site position aimed at being coaxial to the mitral annulus and allowing precise “canting” and depth implantation of the TAVI valve during delivery. To allow this, a 6-F bright-tip femoral sheath was inserted into the LV, and a snare was used to capture the valve delivery wire delivered transeptally. Valve positioning was attained by 2 operators, one at the apex with the externalized delivery wire and a sheath abutting the valve delivery nose cone, and one on the femoral side using a series of opposing forces to allow precise placement and delivery of a 32-mm balloon-expandable valve (Myval Octapro, Meril Lifesciences) (Video 15) with an additional 4 mL contrast.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Confirmation of valve function, assessing for paravalvular leak (PVL) and outflow tract obstruction, was performed with transesophageal echocardiography. This confirmed optimal position of the valve and no PVL or LVOT obstruction (Videos 16 and 17). At the end of the procedure, full-dose protamine was administered, followed by percutaneous closure of the LV apex (Amplatz ADOII 6/4), and hemostasis was confirmed with left ventriculogram (Video 18). The patient was discharged 2 days later. Her condition has gradually improved. Transthoracic echocardiography 2 months after TMVR revealed a well-functioning valve with a trivial valvular insufficiency, a mean gradient of 4 mm Hg, no PVL, and laminar blood flow through LVOT via space gained in the septum after SESAME and through the struts of the TAVI valve allowed by the LAMPOON (Video 19, Video 20, Video 21). LV ejection fraction has slightly dropped to 40% postprocedure, probably reflecting the impact of the former mitral disease on LV function. The patient had an admission secondary to atrial fibrillation with a fast ventricular rate causing decompensation, stabilized with amiodarone in preparation for biventricular pacemaker followed by atrioventricular node ablation. Her NYHA functional class has improved from III/IV to IIa.

Discussion

Valve-in-MAC is a challenging procedure that has a high-risk profile in terms of major complication, including death at 30 days, of up to 34.5%.6 Recognizing and predicting how anatomical features of calcium pattern may complicate valve function remains difficult.

Minimally invasive electrosurgical intracardiac procedures characterizes an evolving field where modification of common interventional guidewires allows the operator to traverse blood vessels and lacerate myocardium or leaflets with high accuracy. Transcaval aortic access is an established percutaneous maneuver that has proven feasible and safe,7 mostly to enable transcatheter aortic valve replacement when iliofemoral arteries are too calcified or somewhat small to accommodate the sheath. The SESAME procedure is technically challenging and is still early in its evolution, however pre-TMVR septal modification seems to be an effective adjunct to mitral valve therapies. We were careful to undertake a strong collaboration and training with colleagues at the Emory Structural Heart and Valve Center and the University of Washington Medical Center before the first SESAME procedure outside the United States (to our knowledge) prior to TMVR was completed at Aalborg University Hospital in October 2024.

Upon discussion at our multidisciplinary team conference, we decided to continue with SESAME before LAMPOON and valve-in-MAC. The previous ASA in our patient posed a risk for endocardial fibrosis, making it difficult to enter and navigate through the myocardium even though magnetic resonance imaging did not display any significant fibrosis. The goal was to generate a longitudinal laceration in the midline basal septal myocardium to see the myocardium splay typically within weeks, thereby enlarging the neoskirt LVOT and reducing mechanical interaction during TMVR. Current data indicate a substantially reduced risk for complete heart block and pacemaker with SESAME compared with ASA prior to TMVR (5%-6%2,3 vs 35%8). The laceration appears stable during follow-up, and the number of patients with a neoskirt LVOT after SESAME inadequate to enable TMVR tends to be lower (14%2) compared with ASA (27%8). In the current case, the calculated gain in neo-LVOT was around 110 mm2, and importantly, the interaction between the septum and the implanted valve was negated.

We performed a base-to-tip antegrade LAMPOON to allow blood flow through exposed stent struts in the LVOT and further reduce the risk of LVOT obstruction. The procedure has previously been described in detail5,9 and typically precedes TMVR with ballon-expandable valves. Valve-in-MAC constitutes a higher risk for significant PVL, valve migration, and embolization than valve-in-ring or valve-in-valve transcatheter mitral procedures (6.9% vs 1.4% vs 0.9%, respectively6,10), in particular because of the challenges concerning valve sizing, the irregular dispersal of calcium in mitral annulus, and the unpredictable nature of the calcium.

Conclusions

In the current case, we demonstrate 3 novel electrosurgical procedures to accomplish a technically successful TMVR in a patient with hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy and severe MAC with severe mixed mitral disease. The only option to improve this patient's symptoms and quality of life was to perform this series of percutaneous procedures.

LVOT on biplane TEE

Via transcaval access, an Agilis steerable catheter (Abbott Cardiovascular) was inserted up to the level of the ascending thoracic aorta, allowing precise positioning of a 6-F Champ 1.5 guiding catheter (Launcher, Medtronic). A standard J-tip 0.035-inch wire was used to cross over the aortic valve and into the left ventricle. Positioning of the Champ 1.5 catheter was performed with biplane TEE imaging to a preplanned position 7 mm under the aortic valve between the left and right coronary cusp. Transverse outflow tract imaging was used to ensure a position around 5 to 6 o'clock, safely distant from the membranous ventricular septum. All TEE positioning was confirmed with biplane fluoroscopy. Initial entry into the ventricular septum was achieved with a combination of a caravel microcatheter and a straight-tipped Astato 40 wire (Asahi) together with 30 W pure cut from diathermy machine. Entry into the septum was difficult, probably secondary to previous ASA, despite no severe fibrosis being seen on cardiac MRI. Following this, a 1.5-mm PCI balloon was used to gently dilate the tract.

Video 10

Step 1: Base-to-Tip LAMPOON

Initial steps. Femoral vein access, 24-F DrySeal sheath (Gore Medical) inserted and left atrial access via transseptal crossing inferior and posterior, aiming for as much height over the mitral annulus as possible. Height is important to allow a precise LAMPOON via pulling back on guiding catheters during LAMPOON rather than having to pull Agilis catheters causing a lateral to medial oblique cut in the anterior mitral leaflet. After securing an Amplatz super stiff wire (Boston Scientific) through the septum and into the left upper pulmonary vein, an atrial septostomy was performed with a 12-mm Armada balloon (Abbott Cardiovascular), wire exchange allowing positioning of 2 Agilis small-curve steerable catheters (Abbott Cardiovascular) inserted into the left atrium. Through one Agilis, a balloon-tipped catheter was floated through the mitral valve and deflected up toward and through the aortic valve with confirmation that no mitral valve chordae were entangled. An 0.015-inch wire was passed through the balloon-tipped catheter and was snared with an En Snare (Merit Medical) in the ascending thoracic aorta. The balloon-tipped catheter then exchanged for an EBU 3.5 6-F guiding catheter, and this V-to-A rail was used to position an En Snare in the LVOT just behind the base of the anterior mitral leaflet.

Video 11

Step 2: TEE: surgeon's view

The second Agilis was then used in combination with a JR4 6-F guiding catheter (Runway, Boston Scientific). With a combination of TEE and biplane fluoroscopy, the tip of the catheter was positioned at the base of the anterior mitral leaflet, mid leaflet.

Video 12

Step 3: LAMPOON procedure

With a combination of a Caravel 135-cm microcatheter (Asahi) and an Astato 20 wire (Asahi) used together with 30 W pure-cut diathermy, a transition burn was made in the anterior mitral leaflet (basal and mid), then snared in the LVOT. Correct positioning was confirmed on TEE and fluoroscopy. A flying V was formed on the Astato 20 wire outside the body, then positioned at the base of the anterior mitral leaflet, and a base-to-tip LAMPOON was performed with 70 W of pure-cut diathermy energy. Confirmation of correct position of the cut was made on TEE (see Video 14).

Video 13

TEE Biplane Color Doppler Confirming a Torrential Regurgitation After LAMPOON

To allow hemodynamic support between LAMPOON and valve delivery, an intra-aortic balloon pump was used. However, because of the severe iliac disease, an alternative access was gained via the left brachial artery, implanting the intra-aortic balloon pump “upside down” in the descending thoracic aorta, maintaining good hemodynamic support.
