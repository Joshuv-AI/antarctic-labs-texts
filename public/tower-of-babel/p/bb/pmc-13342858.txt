Urodynamic Study Revisited: An Evolving Tool in Functional Urology
Authors: Jae Heon Kim
Published: 2026

Urodynamic study remains the gold standard for evaluating lower urinary tract (LUT) dysfunction. However, it is invasive, often uncomfortable for patients, dependent on interpretation, and sometimes limited in its ability to predict clinical outcomes. The current issue of the International Neurourology Journal presents 9 articles that collectively reexamine the role of urodynamics. These studies explore ways to improve patient comfort, enhance diagnostic value, better understand the biological basis of urodynamic findings, and increase the reproducibility and accessibility of both urodynamic interpretation and technology.

The issue begins with 2 randomized controlled trials focusing on a challenge often considered unavoidable: patient discomfort and anxiety during urodynamic testing. In the first study, 84 patients were randomized to receive either immersive virtual-reality glasses or standard care during the procedure. Although pain scores were similar between groups, patients in the virtual-reality group reported significantly lower anxiety after the test and showed modest improvements in physiological stress markers [1]. In the second trial, slow-tempo instrumental music reduced pain during the most invasive parts of the examination, including uroflowmetry and catheter insertion, without affecting embarrassment levels or urodynamic results [2]. Together, these studies demonstrate that simple, low-cost, nonpharmacological interventions can improve the patient experience without compromising data quality.

The focus then shifts to the diagnostic strengths and limitations of urodynamics. In a large retrospective study of 1,452 men with benign prostatic hyperplasia, urodynamic bladder outlet obstruction (BOO) was associated with the severity of bladder trabeculation observed during cystoscopy. Higher trabeculation grades were also related to older age, larger prostate volume, detrusor overactivity, stronger contractility, and a higher BOO index [3]. However, urodynamics cannot explain every symptom. A study investigating post-micturition dribbling— a common symptom not included in the International Prostate Symptom Score—found that, despite strong associations with storage symptoms and reduced quality of life, poor bladder compliance was the only urodynamic or ultrasonographic parameter that predicted its presence [4]. These findings highlight that while urodynamics provides valuable information, comprehensive symptom assessment remains essential.

Understanding urodynamic findings also requires an understanding of the underlying biology. The first review article in this issue addresses this need by integrating human transcriptomic data with short- and long-term rat models of BOO. The review describes how BOO leads over time to detrusor underactivity and increased postvoid residual urine. Key mechanisms include interactions among fibroblasts, macrophages, and epithelial cells, as well as conserved signaling pathways such as transforming growth factor beta, nuclear factor-kappa B, and phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B. Potential regulatory molecules, including Cthrc1, are also discussed [5]. By presenting residual urine as the result of a dynamic and progressive process rather than a single measurement, the review suggests that future molecular biomarkers may help identify bladder decompensation before irreversible damage occurs.

Another article examines wearable ultrasound devices for continuous bladder monitoring in patients with neurogenic LUT dysfunction, where accurate timing of clean intermittent catheterization is critical [6]. The authors carefully distinguish between technological promise and current evidence. While devices such as SENS-U have shown good performance in pediatric patients with functional voiding disorders, their accuracy remains limited in neurogenic bladder populations, and validation studies in adults are largely lacking. Regulatory approval also varies widely, ranging from certified medical devices to consumer products. The authors conclude that these technologies are not yet ready for routine use in neurogenic bladder management, although newer B-mode and deep-learning– based systems may overcome many current limitations.

Two additional studies focus on improving the precision and reproducibility of urodynamic measurements. One study applied time-series analysis to detrusor pressure curves during voiding and identified mean detrusor pressure during voiding as a simple but highly informative parameter. This metric showed a strong correlation with the BOO index (r=0.89) and accurately identified obstruction (area under the curve=0.95) [7]. These findings suggest that additional clinically useful information can be extracted from data already collected during routine testing. However, reproducibility remains a challenge. In children with spina bifida, interobserver agreement was only moderate for detrusor overactivity and weak for bladder compliance, even among experienced clinicians [8]. This study emphasizes the need for standardized interpretation criteria to ensure consistent clinical decision-making across institutions.

The final article demonstrates how urodynamic findings can directly support clinical decision-making. Among women undergoing cystocele repair, preoperative urgency urinary incontinence, higher detrusor pressure at maximum flow, and lower vaginal parity were independently associated with improvement in urgency symptoms after surgery [9]. This study illustrates how combining clinical and urodynamic information can help identify patients most likely to benefit from surgical treatment.

Taken together, these 9 studies highlight the current direction of urodynamic research. They improve patient comfort [1, 2], critically evaluate diagnostic utility [3, 4], connect urodynamic findings with molecular mechanisms [5], assess emerging technologies that may complement traditional testing [6], enhance data interpretation [7], address concerns about reproducibility [8], and support personalized surgical decision-making [9]. A common theme across these contributions is cautious optimism. Urodynamics is neither a perfect tool nor an outdated one. Rather, it continues to evolve through improvements in patient-centered care, quantitative analysis, standardization, and biological understanding.

Importantly, the future success of urodynamics will depend not only on improving diagnostic accuracy but also on making the test easier to perform and more accessible in everyday practice. As demonstrated by the randomized trials in this issue, reducing patient discomfort and anxiety is an important step toward improving the overall testing experience. However, simplification of the procedure itself may be equally important. A less invasive, less time-consuming, and more user-friendly urodynamic assessment would likely increase acceptance among both patients and healthcare providers, facilitating broader clinical adoption. Ultimately, the greatest value of future innovations may lie not only in generating better data, but also in transforming urodynamics into a more practical and accessible tool for routine clinical care. We hope this issue provides both practical insights for clinicians and inspiration for the future validation studies needed to advance the field.
