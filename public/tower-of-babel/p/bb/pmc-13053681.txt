Defining “Dominance” in Atypical Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome: Distinguishing Amplification From Initiation
Authors: Michael A Cole, Robert A Brodsky
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

We read the article by Santarsiero et al.,1 where the authors concluded that the alternative pathway (AP) is “dominant” in atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome based on endothelial ex-vivo assays. Although the data confirm that AP blockade dampens complement deposition in remission samples, the conclusion of “dominance” is methodologically undercontrolled and obscures the distinction between in vitro biological suppression and clinical efficacy.

The pathophysiology of atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome and complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy is multifactorial, and no single ex vivo system, whether human microvascular endothelial cells-1, glomerular endothelial cells, or HEK293, can fully recapitulate its biology. Cell-based assays share a limitation—they cannot model the dynamic in vivo microenvironment, including the interplay of shear stress, glycocalyx denudation, inflammatory microparticles, and coagulation activation. These systems are tools to interrogate intrinsic serum stimuli and model disease biology.

To claim “dominance” requires a comparative analysis; however, the study design lacks necessary controls to evaluate the classical pathway. For the assays, the authors used buffers permissive of all pathways rather than AP-specific conditions (e.g., Magnesium- ethyleneglycol-bis-(β-aminoethylether)-N,N,N′,N′-tetraacetic acid) and failed to employ C4d staining or specific classical pathway inhibition (e.g., sutimlimab). Without these controls, the authors cannot distinguish between an isolated AP dysregulation and a strong classical pathway stimulus that merely uses the AP for amplification. Our previous work demonstrated increased C4d deposition on human microvascular endothelial cells-1 that was blocked by C1s inhibition.2

Although the authors show AP inhibition is effective in remission samples, they do not demonstrate that AP inhibitors block C5b-9 deposition in acute phase samples. To support dominance, efficacy must be demonstrated when complement activation is most pronounced.

Furthermore, the authors’ critique of HEK293 models is experimentally unsupported. They argue HEK293 cells lack endothelial antigen repertoires but do not perform head-to-head comparison with glomerular endothelial cells under identical conditions. Paradoxically, their focus on differing “antigen repertoires” presupposes a role for pattern recognition inherent to the classical or lectin pathways, as the surface agnostic AP requires only good nucleophiles for covalent attachment and amplification.

Finally, interpretation of the inhibition data merits closer scrutiny. AP inhibition reduced serum activity only to the level of healthy controls (“normalization”), rather than the complete abrogation. This residual activity, potentially driven by the classical pathway and/or bypass mechanisms, is likely clinically relevant.3 “Normalization” in a static assay may not equate to “safety” in a patient experiencing dynamic thrombotic microangiopathy. Although AP inhibitors are a welcome addition to the armamentarium, it is unclear whether they will be as clinically effective as C5 inhibitors.
