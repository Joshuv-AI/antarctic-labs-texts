High-capacity reversible hydrogen storage in Na-decorated Al–N–O nanocluster: a DFT-D3 study
Authors: S. Almenia, Kamal A. Soliman, S. Abdel Aal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Efficient and reversible hydrogen storage remains a major challenge for clean energy technologies. Here, DFT-D3 calculations combined with PDOS, TD-DFT, QTAIM, and RDG analyses were used to evaluate a Na-decorated Al4N4O2 nanocluster as a solid-state hydrogen storage material. The optimized Al–N–O framework is structurally stable, with a formation energy of − 4.645 eV, and shows strong electronic polarization due to charge redistribution between Na and O/N sites. Ab initio molecular dynamics simulations at 600 K predict high thermal stability of the Al4N4O2Na4 nanocluster during the simulated hydrogen adsorption–desorption process. The resulting electropositive Na and electron-rich O/N regions create dual adsorption sites that promote polarization-induced physisorption of H2. Upon successive hydrogenation, adsorption energies range from − 0.470 to − 0.309 eV per H2, with desorption enthalpies of − 28.56 to − 21.45 kJ mol-1 per H2, suggesting reversible hydrogen release at 244–347 K. The predicted gravimetric capacity reaches 20.13 wt%, exceeding the DOE 2025 target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-61747-5.

Introduction

The accelerating global demand for sustainable, carbon-neutral energy technologies has intensified research into alternative energy carriers beyond fossil fuels. In the field of sustainable energy carriers, hydrogen is regarded as a highly promising clean fuel attributed to its exceptional gravimetric energy density, abundance, and zero-emission combustion products1–4. However, efficient hydrogen storage remains a critical challenge in establishing a viable hydrogen-based energy system. The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) has outlined rigorous targets for onboard hydrogen storage, requiring gravimetric capacities of 4.5 wt% (2020) and 5.5 wt% (2025) under 35–100 bar and 40–60 °C5. To achieve reversible adsorption–desorption close to ambient conditions, adsorption energies should range between 0.15 and 0.60 eV per H2 molecule6,7, emphasizing the demand for designing materials that simultaneously fulfill thermodynamic and kinetic conditions. Various hydrogen storage materials, such as metal hydrides, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), and covalent organic frameworks (COFs)8, MXenes9, carbon-based nanostructures10,11. Nanoclusters and two-dimensional (2D) systems have been explored extensively12. Their large surface-to-volume ratio, tunable electronic structure, and multiple adsorption sites enable enhanced interaction with hydrogen molecules. However, pristine nanostructures generally exhibit weak van der Waals interactions with H2, resulting in limited adsorption capacity and limited reversibility13.

To address these limitations, surface modification through metal decoration and non-metal doping has emerged as an effective approach to tune binding strength, improve charge transfer, and increase adsorption sites14–16. Decoration with alkali metals (Li, Na, K)17,18, alkaline-earth metals (Mg, Ca)19,20, and transition metals (Ti, Sc, or V) promotes hydrogen uptake through polarization of H2 molecules and formation of electrostatic and orbital interactions21,22. Similarly, nonmetal doping (such as B, Al, or N) can alter the local electronic environment and stabilize dispersed metal atoms, suppressing aggregation and improving material stability23. Such synergistic modification strategies have been shown to enhance hydrogen adsorption performance significantly. Within this context, Group III nitrides, particularly aluminum nitride (AlN), have recently attracted considerable attention owing to their wide band gap, excellent thermal conductivity, mechanical strength, and chemical durability, which support their application in high-power electronics and UV photonics24. Quantum-chemical analyses reveal that Al4N4 adopts a planar D4h24 configuration composed of an alternating eight-membered Al–N ring with an average bond length of ~ 1.75 Å and a HOMO–LUMO separation of ≈ 2.07 eV25. The pronounced stability of this configuration arises from efficient p-orbital overlap and minimized ring strain, which favor covalent Al–N interactions over N–N associations. This distinctive bonding pattern not only stabilizes the cluster but also controls its frontier-orbital distribution, resulting in semiconducting behavior and high charge-transfer capability.

Surface termination with –O, –OH, and –F groups enables charge redistribution that facilitates H2 binding26. Recently, aluminum–nitride–oxide (Al–N–O) frameworks have garnered attention for their high structural stability, strong polar bonds, and ability to effectively anchor metal atoms27. Such hybrid systems combine ionic and covalent characteristics, providing abundant active sites for molecular hydrogen adsorption. In particular, the Na–decorated Al4N4O2 nanocluster exhibits distinctive electronic polarization due to strong charge transfer between Na atoms and the Al–N–O matrix, which enhances hydrogen–substrate interactions.

Na was selected as the decorating species owing to its low atomic mass, efficient charge-donation capability, and hydrogen-binding energies associated with reversible hydrogen adsorption. Li frequently produces stronger H2 binding as a consequence of its higher charge density, whereas the larger atomic mass of K increases the gravimetric penalty and is often accompanied by weaker polarization of hydrogen molecules. Na provides efficient electron transfer to the Al–N–O framework while maintaining adsorption strengths within the range commonly associated with reversible hydrogen storage. The resulting electropositive Na centers effectively polarize H2 molecules and promote stable molecular adsorption without excessive binding. Previous studies on Na-decorated hydrogen-storage materials have reported similar behavior, highlighting the ability of Na to combine high storage capacity with favorable adsorption thermodynamics28.

In this study, we theoretically investigate the hydrogen-storage potential of the Al4N4O2Na4 nanocluster. The structural stability, charge distribution, adsorption energy, and electronic properties are systematically analyzed to reveal the adsorption mechanism of hydrogen molecules on the noble-metal-free Na-decorated Al–N–O framework. The objective is to identify an efficient, thermodynamically stable nanocluster capable of achieving a reversible hydrogen storage capacity exceeding the DOE target (5.5 wt%). This combined metal-decorated and electronically modulated strategy provides theoretical design guidelines for the development of next-generation hydrogen-storage materials and motivates future experimental investigations.

Results and discussion

Structural and electronic characteristics of the Al4N4O2Na4 nanocluster

To clarify the intrinsic stability and bonding features of the system, the geometry of the Na–decorated Al4N4O2 nanocluster was fully optimized Fig. 1 and The Cartesian coordinates of optimized Al4N4O2 nanoclusters were seen in Table S1. The calculation produced a compact, symmetric Al–N–O framework with a formation energy of − 4.645 eV, reflecting a strongly stabilized atomic arrangement. The optimized structure exhibits average Al–N and Al–O bond lengths of 1.76 Å and 1.72 Å, respectively, while the longer Na–O bonds (≈ approximately 2.21 Å) reflect their ionic nature. The shorter Al–N and Al–O bonds provide rigidity and strength, whereas the longer Na–O bonds contribute to charge balance and structural flexibility. The calculated Al–N and Al–O bond lengths are consistent with those reported for Na-decorated AlN-based clusters (1.70–1.78 Å), supporting the accuracy of the present structural optimization29. The coexistence of covalent and ionic bonding gives the cluster both mechanical stability and intrinsic polarity, properties that are favorable for hydrogen adsorption.

Ab initio molecular-dynamics simulations at 600 K for 10 ps using a 1 fs time step, verify the thermal stability of the optimized Na–decorated Al4N4O2 configuration. The potential energy is stabilized around − 1987.597 Hartree with minimal fluctuations, confirming that the system retained structural stability without bond dissociation or atomic rearrangement throughout the simulation (Fig. S1). This stability highlights the ability of the cluster to maintain its structural framework during hydrogen adsorption and desorption.

Natural-bond-orbital (NBO) analysis reveals distinct charge polarization, with Al + 1.81 e, N − 1.91 e, O − 1.40 e, and Na + 0.71 e (Table 1). Consequently, Na transfers electron density from its 3s and 3p orbitals to the surrounding Al–O and Al–N framework, forming polarized regions that serve as preferred sites for adsorption. Analysis of the frontier orbitals reveals an energy gap of 3.287 eV, characteristic of a semi-insulating material with notable electronic stability and moderate polarizability. The molecular-electrostatic-potential (MESP) map displays electron-rich areas around oxygen and electron-deficient regions adjacent to sodium atoms. The HOMO is mainly localized on the Al–N bonds, while the LUMO is concentrated on Na and O atoms, confirming clear donor and acceptor regions that promote charge transfer during molecular adsorption (Fig. S2). The projected density-of-states (PDOS) analysis highlights the electronic distribution within the Al4N4O2Na4 cluster. Valence states (− 8 to − 4 eV) derive predominantly from Al-3s/3p and O-2p orbitals with minor N-2p contributions. Conduction states are dominated by Na-3p orbitals slightly overlapping with Al-3p close to the Fermi level (Fig. 2a). This partial overlap indicates charge transfer from Na to the Al–O and Al–N framework, producing n-type semiconducting behavior and enhancing surface polarization. The reduced-density-gradient (RDG) analysis of the Na-decorated Al4N4O2 nanocluster provides insight into the noncovalent interactions that stabilize the cluster. The RDG plots show blue, green, and red regions corresponding to attractive, weak, and repulsive forces. Deep blue lobes along Al–N bonds (sign(λ₂)ρ < −0.03 a.u.) reflect strong electrostatic attraction and partial covalency; green regions around Na and O (sign(λ2)ρ ≈ 0) indicate van der Waals stabilization; and small red patches between Al atoms (sign(λ2)ρ > 0.03 a.u.) represent slight steric repulsion (Fig. 1b, c). The charge density is concentrated on nitrogen and progressively delocalized toward aluminum, forming polar Al–N dipoles that generate local electric fields. This behavior is consistent with previously reported semi-insulating Al–N–O nanoclusters, confirming that Na inclusion enhances n-type characteristics compared to undoped Al4N4.

The electron-localization function (ELF) map of the Na-decorated Al4N4O2 cluster reveals a highly polarized electronic framework characterized by mixed ionic–covalent bonding, Fig. 1d. Intense red–yellow regions concentrated around nitrogen and oxygen atoms indicate strong electron localization due to lone-pair density, whereas the continuous green belts between Al and N atoms (ELF ≈ 0.55–0.70) denote shared-pair σ interactions with partial delocalization, confirming polar covalent Al–N bonding. The sodium atoms occupy electron-deficient blue regions (ELF < 0.25), reflecting charge depletion resulting from electron donation from Na 3s orbitals toward electronegative O/N sites. This charge transfer generates pronounced polarization across the cluster, stabilizing the Al–N–O framework while creating electron-rich zones favorable for H2 adsorption. The coexistence of localized and semi-delocalized ELF domains suggests dual-site behavior: O/N centers act as Lewis-basic regions for H2 polarization, while Al and Na sites provide electropositive fields enhancing weak chemisorption. Such spatial charge distribution ensures moderate adsorption energies within the ideal 0.15–0.60 eV per H2 range, highlighting the Na-decorated Al4N4O2 cluster as a promising candidate for reversible hydrogen storage.

Hydrogen adsorption and desorption on double-sided Na-decorated Al4N4O2 nanocluster

The interaction between molecular hydrogen and the Na-decorated Al4N4O2 nanocluster was explored to clarify the adsorption mechanism and evaluate its potential for reversible hydrogen storage. Firstly, the optimized configurations of H2 molecules adsorbed on the Na–decorated Al4N4O2 nanocluster were examined (Fig. 3). A single H2 molecule was initially attached to each Na site, followed by sequential addition of molecules in successive increments until the system carried 36 H2 molecules. The variation in adsorption energy (Eads) for successively hydrogenated systems, from (H2)4/Al4N4O2Na4 nanocluster up to (H2)36/Al4N4O2Na4 nanocluster, was analyzed and is presented in Fig. 4a. The adsorption energies for successive hydrogenation range from − 0.470 eV for (H2)₄ to − 0.309 eV for (H2)36. All adsorption energies are negative, confirming spontaneous and thermodynamically favorable interactions for all configurations. At low coverage, the (H2)4 complex exhibits the strongest interaction (Eads = − 0.470 eV), reflecting efficient polarization of the H2 σ orbitals induced by the surface Na+ and Al–O/N dipoles. With additional adsorption up to (H2)8, (H2)12, and (H2)16, the Eads values slightly reduce to − 0.463, − 0.443, and − 0.416 eV, respectively, signifying modest intermolecular repulsion among neighboring H2 molecules as surface sites become progressively occupied. At higher coverage levels, from (H2)20 to (H2)36, the adsorption energy steadily decreases from − 0.416 eV to − 0.309 eV. The consistent decrease in adsorption strength demonstrates that hydrogen attachment is dominated by electrostatic physisorption rather than covalent bonding, ensuring efficient and fully reversible hydrogen adsorption–desorption behavior near ambient-temperature conditions.

All adsorption energies are consistent with the thermodynamically ideal range of 0.20–0.60 eV per H2, as recommended by the DOE criteria commonly used to assess hydrogen-storage materials. Consequently, the Na–decorated Al4N4O2 nanocluster can accommodate up to thirty-six hydrogen molecules while maintaining structural and thermal stability during adsorption, highlighting its potential as a hydrogen-storage material. The gravimetric hydrogen storage capacity of the Na-decorated Al4N4O2 nanocluster increases from 2.724 wt% for (H2)4 to 20.131 wt% for (H2)36, demonstrating continuous and efficient hydrogen uptake Fig. 4b. The Cartesian coordinates of optimized (H2)36/Al4N4O2Na4 complex was seen in Table S1.

The maximum hydrogen-storage capacity was achieved for the (H2)36/Al4N4O2Na4 configuration, corresponding to a gravimetric density of 20.13 wt%. This elevated value originates from the low mass of the Al4N4O2Na4 framework together with the high density of Na-induced adsorption sites that stabilize molecular hydrogen through polarization-driven interactions. The presence of 36 adsorbed H2 molecules at the highest stable loading level highlights the exceptional hydrogen-loading capability of the nanocluster. Comparison with previously reported Na-decorated hydrogen-storage materials (Table 2) demonstrates that the calculated gravimetric density exceeds that reported for many related systems, despite maintaining adsorption energies within the range commonly associated with reversible hydrogen adsorption and desorption.

The hydrogen-uptake behavior of the Na-decorated Al4N4O2 nanocluster is characterized by a gradual decrease in the average adsorption energy from − 0.470 eV at low coverage to − 0.309 eV at saturation, indicating that hydrogen adsorption remains energetically favorable throughout the entire loading sequence. A comparison with previously reported Na-decorated hydrogen-storage materials demonstrates that the average adsorption energy obtained for the present system (0.31 eV per H2) exceeds those reported for Na-functionalized Si2BN (0.13 eV, 8.70 wt%)30, Na-decorated silicon-doped boron phosphide (0.27 eV, 7.45 wt%)21, Na-decorated B-doped tin carbide (0.19 eV, 6.66 wt%)32, Na-decorated B7N5 monolayer (0.20 eV, 7.70 wt%)33, Na-decorated s-C3N6 monolayer (0.15 eV, 11.27 wt%)34, and Na-decorated SiB monolayer (0.18 eV, 9.09 wt%)35. Despite the stronger interaction, the calculated adsorption energy remains within the 0.20–0.60 eV interval 33generally considered favorable for reversible hydrogen adsorption and release. Furthermore, the maximum gravimetric capacity reaches 20.13 wt%, substantially exceeding the values reported for the Na-based systems summarized in Table 2. The enhanced adsorption performance can be attributed to the polarized Al–N–O framework, which facilitates efficient charge redistribution from the Na centers and strengthens the electrostatic interaction with molecular hydrogen. For completeness, Li-decorated B4N4 (0.16 eV)36, Li-functionalized boron monoxide (0.21 eV)37, and K-functionalized siligene (0.183 eV)38 are also included in Table 2, further demonstrating that the adsorption energies obtained for Al4N4O2Na4 remain fully competitive with those reported for other alkali-metal-decorated hydrogen-storage materials. These results suggest that the Na-decorated Al4N4O2 nanocluster combines strong yet reversible H2 binding with exceptionally high hydrogen-storage capacity relative to previously reported alkali-metal-decorated nanostructures.

As shown in Fig. 4a, the average desorption energy (Edes) becomes progressively less negative as the number of adsorbed hydrogen molecules increases from 4 to 36. At low coverage, (H2)4/ Al4N4O2Na4 exhibits the most stable hydrogen binding with Edes −0.470 eV, indicating a relatively strong interaction between the first few H2 molecules and the Na-decorated Al–N–O framework. With additional hydrogen uptake up to (H2)24/ Al4N4O2Na4, Edes evolves from − 0.362 to − 0.293 eV, reflecting a systematic weakening of the interaction as adsorption sites become progressively occupied and H2–H2 repulsion increases. Edes decreases sharply from − 0.293 eV at (H2)₂₄ to − 0.149 eV at (H2)28 and then approaches − 0.137 and − 0.132 eV for (H2)32 and (H2)36, respectively. At higher coverages (28–36 H2), the magnitude of Edes falls below 0.15 eV, showing that the outer hydrogen layer interacts only weakly with the surface.

Hydrogen desorption enthalpy and temperature

The desorption temperature (TD) describes the thermal stability and energetics of hydrogen release from the Na-decorated Al4N4O2 framework. A consistent decrease in TD from 347.19 K for (H2)4/ Al4N4O2Na4 to 243.99 K for (H2)32/Al4N4O2Na4 reflects the reduction in H2–surface interaction strength with successive hydrogen adsorption (Fig. 5a). At the initial adsorption stage, the relatively high TD values (≈ 347 K) correspond to stronger polarization of H2 molecules bound to Na sites. As hydrogen coverage increases, charge redistribution within the Na–O–Al region reduces the electrostatic potential, thereby weakening the interaction energy and lowering TD values. At higher coverage, physisorption becomes dominant, characterized by dispersion-type H2···H2 and H2···surface interactions39,40. The predicted desorption temperature below 300 K (≈ 244 K at 36 H2 coverage) suggests weak, potentially reversible hydrogen binding near ambient-temperature conditions.

The enthalpy (ΔH) values (Fig. 5b) provide a comprehensive thermodynamic perspective on the strength and reversibility of hydrogen binding within the temperature interval 100–700 K. ΔH decreases from − 23.84 kJ mol− 1 at 100 K to − 21.45 kJ mol− 1 at 700 K, confirming that all desorption processes are exothermic and that the interaction weakens as temperature increases. The most exothermic value (− 28.561 kJ mol− 1) observed at 300 K indicates the equilibrium state in which adsorption and desorption occur in comparable proportions, reflecting the optimal temperature for reversible hydrogen exchange41. At lower temperatures (100–200 K), the pronounced exothermicity (− 23.84 to − 27.68 kJ mol− 1) arises from strong polarization of H2 molecules by the Na–O–N active domains. Beyond 300 K, ΔH becomes progressively less negative (− 27.78 to − 21.45 kJ mol− 1), indicating relaxation of the surface–adsorbate interaction as thermal energy facilitates molecular desorption. Thermodynamic analyses identify the 20–30 kJ·mol− 1 H2 enthalpy interval as the domain supporting reversible H2 adsorption and release within the temperature range considered favorable for hydrogen-storage applications42,43. This interval is consistent with thermodynamically favorable reversible adsorption–desorption behavior under moderate temperatures. These ΔH values correspond to desorption pressures of approximately 1–2 bar at 298 K, as estimated from the Van’t Hoff equation, which falls within a range considered favorable for hydrogen-storage systems from a theoretical perspective. This thermodynamic behavior is consistent with that reported for Na-decorated B6 clusters44, indicating that the Al4N4O2Na4 cluster combines favorable exothermicity with structural stability. The evolution of ΔH shows a strong correlation with variations in desorption temperatures (TD) derived from kinetic analysis, indicating the internal consistency between thermodynamic parameters and the system’s kinetic description. This correlation suggests favorable kinetic characteristics for reversible hydrogen exchange within the theoretical model. The recovery time data (Fig. 6a) further suggest the reversible nature of hydrogen desorption. The recovery time decreases from 8.91 × 10− 5 s at (H2)4/ Al4N4O2Na4 to 1.695 × 10− 7 s at (H2)36/ Al4N4O2Na4, indicating faster desorption with higher hydrogen concentration. This clear correlation between ΔH, TD, and recovery time indicates favorable theoretical kinetics and a calculated gravimetric capacity exceeding the DOE benchmark.

Projected density of states (PDOS) analysis

To understand the hydrogen adsorption mechanism in more detail, the projected density of states (PDOS) of the hydrogen-adsorbed systems was analyzed. Upon hydrogen adsorption, new H-1s features emerge in the low-energy region between − 13 and − 11 eV, corresponding to the σ-bonding orbitals of molecular H2. In contrast, the σ* antibonding orbitals appear in the unoccupied region between 4 and 6 eV. The large energy separation between these σ and σ* states confirms the molecular integrity of hydrogen and indicates that adsorption proceeds through weak physisorption rather than chemisorption45. In the (H2)4/ Al4N4O2Na4 complex (Fig. 2b), the H-1s orbitals remain narrow and well-localized, showing minimal overlap with the conduction band and suggesting that adsorption is primarily governed by van der Waals and polarization interactions. As hydrogen coverage increases to (H2)8 - (H2)16 (Fig. S3), the σ* orbitals gradually shift toward 3–4 eV, indicating partial polarization of H–H bonds under the electrostatic influence of Na⁺ sites, whereas the Al–O/N valence band and Na-3p conduction edge remain almost unchanged.

For (H2)20–(H2)32 (Fig. S4), the PDOS exhibits higher H-1s intensity and slight splitting of peaks, revealing multiple adsorption environments with different local electrostatic fields. The σ* bands broaden and shift downward by approximately 1 eV, reflecting stabilization caused by dipole–dipole coupling between neighboring hydrogen molecules. The Na-3p orbitals stay close to the Fermi level, preserving the system’s n-type electronic behavior. At higher hydrogen concentrations, such as (H2)32/ Al4N4O2Na4 (Fig. S4), the PDOS remains stable, emphasizing the electronic stability of the Na-decorated Al4N4O2 surface. The deep H-1s states close to − 12 eV display slight splitting due to weak interactions among closely packed hydrogen molecules, whereas the σ* orbitals appear at 2–3 eV and remain unoccupied, confirming the absence of electron population in the antibonding levels. Electronic analysis indicates that adsorption preserves the structural and electronic stability of the Al–O/N framework. As visualized in Fig. 2c, the progressive broadening and splitting of H-1s orbitals with increasing hydrogen concentration illustrate weak intermolecular coupling and polarization effects induced by Na⁺ centers. The s orbital of hydrogen broadens and splits into multiple peaks as the number of adsorbed hydrogen molecules increases, showing the interaction between the H2 molecules. The H-1s states of the fully hydrogenated (H2)36/Al4N4O2Na4 complex are distributed between approximately − 13 and − 11 eV and split into several distinct peaks. The splitting of the H-derived states reflects enhanced interactions among the adsorbed H2 molecules at high coverage. In addition, the H-1s band becomes noticeably broader compared with the low-loading configuration ((H2)4/Al4N4O2Na4). The broadening of the H-1s band arises from H2···H2 interactions as additional hydrogen molecules are adsorbed, resulting in a wider distribution of hydrogen-derived electronic states. The simultaneous increase in the intensity of the H-1s contribution confirms the substantial accumulation of hydrogen-derived states in the maximum-capacity configuration.

The variation in the electronic energy gap (Egap) across the hydrogenation sequence of the Na–decorated Al4N4O2 nanocluster provides a distinct indication of the tunable electronic properties of the cluster upon successive H2 adsorption, Fig. 6a. The pristine Al4N4 cluster exhibits a 2.478 eV energy gap, consistent with earlier theoretical reports25. Decoration with Na and O atoms substantially widens the gap to 3.287 eV, demonstrating that Na–O incorporation stabilizes the electronic framework and enhances structural rigidity. Upon hydrogen adsorption, the energy gap increases from 3.542 eV for (H2)4/ Al4N4O2Na4 to 4.046 eV for (H2)36/ Al4N4O2Na4, indicating progressive electronic saturation and reduced frontier-orbital overlap between the adsorbate and substrate (Fig. 6a). This consistent widening reflects weak physisorption interactions that preserve the host’s semiconducting nature, ensuring thermodynamic reversibility of H2 adsorption–desorption cycles.

Infrared analysis of H2 adsorption

The infrared spectra of the Na–decorated Al4N4O2 nanocluster before and after hydrogenation demonstrate the structural stability of the Na-decorated Al–N–O cluster (Fig. 7). In the pristine form, the cluster exhibits prominent Al–N stretching vibrations within 820–1150 cm− 146, along with Na–O and Al–O modes appearing between 400 and 650 cm⁻¹, consistent with terminal Na–O linkages stabilizing the Al–N–Al backbone. After adsorption of four hydrogen molecules, two sharp high-frequency peaks appear close to 4160 cm⁻¹ and 4280 cm⁻¹, which correspond to the ν(H–H) stretching of physisorbed H2. These peaks show slight red shifts (Δν ≈ 10–20 cm⁻¹) relative to free H2, confirming molecular adsorption dominated by polarization rather than charge transfer. At maximum hydrogen coverage, (H2)36/ Al4N4O2Na4 exhibits intensified and broadened ν(H–H) bands spanning 4100–4400 cm− 1, arising from dipole–dipole coupling among adjacent adsorbed molecules. This vibrational pattern is in full agreement with the IR features previously documented in47. The persistence of all Al–N and Al–O vibrational features with only minimal displacement verifies that hydrogen adsorption occurs without structural distortion, confirming that the Na–decorated Al4N4O2 nanocluster maintains both mechanical stability and reversible adsorption capacity suitable for reversible hydrogen storage.

The TD-DFT results elucidate the effect of hydrogen adsorption on the electronic excitation profile of the Na-decorated Al4N4O2 nanocluster. Noncovalent interaction (NCI) analysis

Noncovalent interaction (NCI) and reduced density gradient (RDG) analyses were employed to clarify the spatial nature of the weak forces responsible for successive hydrogen adsorption49 on the Na–decorated Al4N4O2 nanocluster. Figures 8 and 9 display the RDG isosurfaces, RDG–sign(λ2)ρ scatter plots, and electron density contour maps, providing a clear visualization of how electron density reorganizes as molecular hydrogen interacts with Na centers on the cluster surface. Upon adsorption of four hydrogen molecules (Fig. 9), green and light-blue RDG regions emerge around Na atoms and extend toward the H2 molecules, indicating weak van der Waals and dipole–induced dipole interactions. These low-gradient zones (s < 1.0) and sign(λ2)ρ values close to zero confirm noncovalent attachment with minimal orbital overlap between the H2 molecules and the Na–decorated Al4N4O2 nanocluster. Electron density shows slight polarization around Na sites, with the electron-rich ends of H2 directed toward electropositive Na atoms. The progressive change in density from blue (electron-depleted) close to Na to yellow (electron-enriched) around H2 reflects an electrostatic induction mechanism rather than extensive charge transfer. The disappearance of red regions near H2 molecules indicates that adsorption proceeds via noncovalent interactions. For full hydrogen coverage [(H2)36/ Al4N4O2Na4; Fig. 10], the RDG topology evolves into an extensive network of green and light-blue isosurfaces enveloping nearly the entire nanocluster. The persistence of green regions and the disappearance of red isosurfaces confirm that adsorption is fully noncovalent, governed by van der Waals forces and polarization effects throughout the hydrogenation sequence.

To further elucidate the nature of the hydrogen–surface interaction, the adsorption mechanism was analyzed in the context of the classical Kubas model and polarization-induced electrostatic adsorption reported for alkali-metal-decorated hydrogen-storage materials. The adsorption characteristics obtained for Al4N4O2Na4 indicate that hydrogen uptake proceeds through polarization-induced electrostatic physisorption rather than a Kubas-type interaction. This conclusion is supported by several independent observations. The H–H bond length remains nearly unchanged throughout the adsorption sequence (0.747–0.751 Å), demonstrating preservation of the molecular integrity of hydrogen. The PDOS spectra show no evidence of significant H2–Na orbital hybridization, and the H2 σ* antibonding states remain essentially unoccupied. Consistent with these observations, the QTAIM analysis reveals closed-shell interaction characteristics, while the RDG isosurfaces are dominated by green and light-blue regions associated with weak attractive interactions.

These features differ substantially from the classical Kubas mechanism, in which simultaneous σ donation from H2 to the metal center and π back-donation from metal d orbitals into the H2 σ* antibonding orbital produce partial activation of the H–H bond and measurable bond elongation50. Such electronic signatures are absent in the present system. Instead, electron transfer from Na atoms to the Al–N–O framework generates electropositive adsorption centers that polarize approaching H2 molecules and stabilize adsorption through electrostatic interactions. Similar adsorption mechanisms have been reported for alkali-metal-decorated hydrogen-storage materials, where charge-transfer-induced polarization constitutes the dominant driving force for hydrogen uptake and reversible storage. Collectively, the structural, electronic, and topological analyses consistently identify polarization-induced electrostatic physisorption as the governing adsorption mechanism in Al4N4O2Na4.

Interaction of O2, H2O, CO, and NH3 with the Na-decorated Al4N4O2 nanocluster

For convenient operating conditions, the effect of oxygen, water vapor, carbon monoxide, and ammonia contamination from the surrounding atmosphere or residual impurities on the Na-decorated Al4N4O2 nanocluster was investigated. It is well established that O2 and H2O are ubiquitous species in air, whereas CO and NH3 may be encountered during hydrogen production, storage, and transportation. Therefore, the interaction of O2, H2O, CO, and NH3 with the Na-decorated Al4N4O2 nanocluster should be analyzed. To examine the competition between H₂ and impurity molecules at the Na-decorated Al4N4O2 nanocluster, the adsorption energies of O₂, H2O, CO, and NH3 were evaluated and compared with those of hydrogen. The calculated adsorption energies follow the order O₂ (− 4.769 eV) > H₂O (− 3.785 eV) > CO (− 2.583 eV) > NH₃ (− 1.435 eV) > H₂ (− 0.470 to − 0.309 eV per H₂). The calculated binding energies suggest that there is competition between hydrogen and impurity molecules for the available adsorption sites. Obviously, O2 exhibits the strongest interaction with the Na-decorated Al4N4O2 nanocluster, followed by H2O and CO, whereas NH3 displays comparatively weaker adsorption. Consequently, the presence of atmospheric or residual impurities may reduce the hydrogen-storage capacity of the proposed nanocluster. Although oxygen, water vapor, carbon monoxide, and ammonia contamination can interfere with hydrogen adsorption by blocking adsorption sites, the present results provide useful insight into the design and improvement of alkali-metal-decorated hydrogen-storage materials.

Conclusion

Comprehensive DFT-D3 calculations reveal that the Na-decorated Al4N4O2 nanocluster forms a stable, polarized Al–N–O framework, characterized by covalent–ionic coupling, charge separation, and n-type semiconducting behavior. The electropositive Na centers and electron-rich O/N sites generate dual adsorption domains that facilitate molecular hydrogen binding through polarization-induced physisorption without H–H cleavage. The average adsorption energy per H2 molecule (− 0.470 to − 0.309 eV H2⁻¹) and desorption enthalpies (− 28.56 to − 21.45 kJ mol− 1 H2) correspond to reversible release within 244–347 K, suggesting hydrogen release near ambient temperatures. A gravimetric capacity of 20.13 wt% exceeds the DOE 2025 benchmark. The increase in the electronic energy gap from 2.478 eV to 4.046 eV upon hydrogenation reflects enhanced electronic stability. The PDOS, RDG, and QTAIM analyses consistently verify a purely electrostatic, non-dissociative mechanism. The IR spectra confirm molecular adsorption through red-shifted ν(H–H) vibrations, and the UV–Vis results reveal blue-shifted absorptions associated with widened band gaps, both supporting the physisorption nature of the interaction. The correlation among ΔH, TD, and Eads, together with the ultrafast recovery time (10− 7 s range), indicates favorable theoretical kinetics for cyclic adsorption–desorption processes.

Overall, the calculations identify Al4N4O2Na4 as a promising theoretical candidate exhibiting high predicted hydrogen-storage capacity, favorable adsorption–desorption characteristics, and electronic stability. The present results provide theoretical insight into the hydrogen-storage potential of the Al4N4O2Na4 nanocluster. Experimental synthesis and characterization will be required to validate the predicted adsorption, thermodynamic, and storage properties.

Computational framework

A comprehensive investigation of the geometric, electronic, and thermochemical properties of hydrogen interaction with the Al4N4O2Na4 nanocluster was performed using density functional theory (DFT-D3). The DFT-D3 method includes Grimme’s dispersion correction to account for long-range van der Waals interactions, which are essential for accurately describing weak adsorption phenomena51. All calculations were carried out with the B3LYP hybrid functional, which effectively accounts for exchange–correlation effects in light-element and alkali-metal systems52. The B3LYP method combines Becke’s three-parameter exchange functional with the Lee–Yang–Parr (LYP) correlation functional53. The unrestricted hybrid (U)-B3LYP formalism was applied to properly describe spin polarization induced by Na atoms, a method that has been proven reliable for alkali metal-decorated nanomaterials28. The 6-311G(d, p) basis set was used for Al, N, O, Na, and H atoms to achieve a balance between accuracy and computational efficiency54. Geometry optimization and frequency calculations were performed with the Gaussian 09 software package55. Harmonic vibrational-frequency calculations were carried out for all optimized structures at the same level of theory. No imaginary frequencies were detected for the pristine Al4N4O2Na4 nanocluster or any hydrogenated configuration, confirming that all optimized geometries correspond to true minima on the potential-energy surface.

The convergence thresholds were set to 1 × 10− 5 Hartree for SCF energy, 0.009 Å for displacement, and 0.0001 Hartree Å−1 for the maximum force, with no symmetry constraints imposed.

Spin polarization was included for all open-shell configurations, and multiplicities were assigned based on electronic structures. Visualization of molecular geometries and electronic densities was carried out using GaussView 6.0. Projected density of states (PDOS) analysis was performed using GaussSum 3.056. The quantum theory of atoms in molecules (QTAIM) and noncovalent interaction (NCI) analyses were investigated with Multiwfn 3.857 and Visual Molecular Dynamics (VMD)58.

Ground state properties, including optimized geometries, electronic structures, adsorption energies, and vibrational frequencies, were evaluated using the B3LYP functional, which has been extensively employed in structural and energetic investigations of nanoclusters and hydrogen-storage materials. Moreover, to examine the optical response, time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) calculations were performed using the CAM-B3LYP functional with the 6-311G(d, p) basis set59 to simulate the UV–Vis absorption spectra of the pristine and Na-decorated Al4N4O2 nanoclusters. The long-range corrected formulation of CAM-B3LYP alleviates the underestimation of excitation energies commonly associated with conventional hybrid functionals and provides an improved description of charge-transfer excitations and electronic absorption spectra.
