Surface materials and biofilm formation affect tropodithietic acid biosynthesis gene expression in Phaeobacter piscinae S26 and its interaction with Vibrio anguillarum
Authors: Xavier Ferrer-Florensa, Yuyan Liu, Mikael L Strube, Paul J Kempen, Claus Sternberg, Rafael Taboryski, Lone Gram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTropodithietic acid (TDA) is the antimicrobial compound mediating the probiotic activity of Phaeobacter spp. against pathogenic vibrios in marine larviculture. In the related genera Tritonibacter, TDA is mainly produced during biofilm growth. The purpose of the study was to determine if biofilm formation increases expression of TDA biosynthesis genes in Phaeobacter piscinae and whether surface properties can promote this expression for aquaculture. Using a GFP reporter under the tdaCDE promoter and flow cytometry, TDA biosynthesis gene expression was two-fold higher in biofilms than in planktonic cells. Biofilm formation on four surfaces showed that the most hydrophobic material, PDMS, supported ∼30-fold higher early attachment than PMMA, COC and PP, while mature biovolume was similar across materials. The TDA gene expression to biovolume ratio increased as biofilms developed. P. piscinae biofilms inhibited Vibrio anguillarum, reducing counts to 105 CFU/ml compared to the control that was 108 CFU/ml. In the presence of V. anguillarum, PDMS biofilms showed 1.5-fold higher biovolume and 2.5-fold higher TDA biosynthesis gene expression, suggesting an antagonistic response. Thus, Phaeobacter is a more effective Vibrio antagonist in the biofilm state, supporting its potential as a probiotic for future aquaculture applications.

Sustainability Statement

This study supports Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 14 (Life Below Water) by investigating a biofilm-based application of a marine probiotic bacterium to inhibit fish-pathogenic Vibrio and thereby contribute to more sustainable disease management in aquaculture. By providing an alternative or complement to antibiotics for controlling bacterial outbreaks, this approach may also contribute to SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-Being) and, in the longer term, support SDG 2 (Zero Hunger) through improved stability and sustainability of aquatic food production.

Introduction

Secondary metabolites are produced by many bacteria, fungi and plants and are believed to be non-essential for normal growth and development, as opposed to the molecules involved in primary metabolism (Macheleidt et al. 2016, Olaf Tyc et al. 2017, Pant et al. 2021). In microorganisms, secondary metabolites play critical roles in signaling, pigmentation, antimicrobial activity, biofilm formation, nutrient scavenging, and UV protection (Vaz Jauri et al. 2013, Chevrette et al. 2019, Essarioui et al. 2020); furthermore, many of these compounds are of significant medicinal and pharmaceutical value (Vaz Jauri et al. 2013, Chevrette et al. 2019). Most studies of bacterial secondary metabolite production have been done using planktonic cultures, as this ensures homogenous cultures that can be manipulated and are easily extracted for further chemical analysis. However, the high densities and close interactions in bacteria living in biofilms create an environment where cells interact, partly via the secondary metabolites they produce. Thus, the production of secondary metabolites is likely different, both in terms of quantities and structures produced, in biofilms as compared to planktonically growing cells.

Several studies have pointed to a relationship between bacterial biofilm formation and bacterial production of secondary metabolites. In some species of Pseudomonas, Bacillus, and Aspergillus fumigatus, higher production of secondary metabolites has been observed in the biofilm state compared to planktonic growth (Bruns et al. 2010, Rieusset et al. 2020, Tang Z et al. 2023). Conversely, some secondary metabolites can also influence biofilm formation of the producing organisms (Beyersmann et al. 2017, Borlee et al. 2022). Pyocyanin production in Pseudomonas aeruginosa enhances biofilm formation and antibiotic resistance (Das and Manefield 2012, Al-Qaysi et al. 2020), while phenazines increase biovolume and motility (Ramos et al. 2010). Similarly, surfactin from Bacillus spp. promotes biofilm formation and root colonization (Zhang Y et al. 2022) and deleting holomycin production in Photobacterium galatheae reduces its biofilm formation (Zhang SD et al. 2021). The exogenous addition of antibiotics at sub-inhibitory concentrations can also enhance biofilm formation. Tetracycline and ciprofloxacin can stimulate biofilm development in Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Linares et al. 2006), while ciprofloxacin enhances biofilm growth in Escherichia coli (Rafaque et al. 2020) and vancomycin enhances the biofilm formation of Staphylococcus aureus (Hsu et al. 2011). Thus, secondary metabolites both added exogenously and produced by the bacteria can influence and, typically, enhance biofilm formation.

Most studies on bacterial biofilm formation have dealt with their detrimental effects, with innumerable studies focused on how to develop surfaces that prevent or minimize biofilm formation and fouling. However, bacterial biofilms are desired in wastewater treatment (Abeysinghe et al. 1996, Prol-García et al. 2014, Ruiz et al. 2020) and in plant biocontrol bacteria where the colonization of the roots is advantageous (Bais et al. 2004, Seneviratne et al. 2011, Sun et al. 2022). The finding that secondary metabolite production may be enhanced in bacterial biofilms, as compared to planktonic bacteria, could be useful for both production purposes and in biocontrol. Surface characteristics such as surface relief patterns, roughness, and wettability significantly influence bacterial biofilm formation (Donlan 2002, Fruleux et al. 2024), and determining how such surface characteristics affect biofilm formation and biofilm-mediated secondary metabolite production is relevant for wastewater biofilters, sustainable aquaculture, bioreactors, agricultural biocontrol and more (Gutierrez-Wing and Malone 2006, Ercan and Demirci 2015, Hettiarachchi et al. 2017, Yousra Turki et al. 2017, Ruiz et al. 2020). Many studies suggest that bacterial attachment often increases on hydrophobic surfaces due to reduced interactions with surrounding water molecules (Hadjiev et al. 2007, Verran et al. 2010, Al-Amshawee et al. 2021, Jiang et al. 2021, Yang et al. 2022, Yan et al. 2023). In contrast, other studies suggest that hydrophilic surfaces may enhance biofilm formation under specific conditions (Vermeltfoort et al. 2004, Tang H et al. 2009, Abban et al. 2012, De Giorgi et al. 2017). Thus, the impact of surface wettability on biofilm formation remains complex and appears highly dependent on the bacterial strain studied.

The purpose of the present study was to determine if—and how—surface characteristics affected the biofilm formation and the production of anti-pathogen molecules of a bacterium from the marine Roseobacter group. Several species are both potent biofilm formers (Dang and Lovell 2000, Rao et al. 2005) and can produce tropodithietic acid (TDA), a secondary metabolite that can have antibiotic effects (Brinkhoff et al. 2004, Bruhn et al. 2005, Geng et al. 2008). In some species, such as Tritonibacter mobilis, TDA production occurs only in the biofilm state, e.g. at the air-surface interface (Berger et al. 2011). T. mobilis does not express the TDA biosynthesis genes when grown as single swimming cells but the genes are expressed immediately upon attachment (D’Alvise et al. 2014) and TDA production has been correlated to c-di-GMP signalling, a central biofilm-associated secondary messenger (D’Alvise et al. 2014). Species of the Phaeobacter genera typically produce more TDA than Tritonibacter (Porsby et al. 2008) and have been widely researched as potential probiotics in marine larviculture with TDA being the main effector molecule of the probiotic effect against the fish pathogen Vibrio anguillarum (D’Alvise et al. 2012a, Rasmussen et al. 2018, Sonnenschein et al. 2021). In aquaculture, probiotics are commonly administered through dry feed, live-feed organisms, or by direct addition to the rearing water (Villamil et al. 2010, Skjermo et al. 2015). However, commercial probiotic use in aquaculture remains limited and using the approach suggested in our study, i.e. introducing beneficial bacteria via biofilms has not yet been developed (BACTOCELL | Lallemand Animal Nutrition 2017, Prol-García et al. 2014).

TDA effectively inhibits Vibrio species without harming fish larvae (D’Alvise et al. 2012a) and D’Alvise et al. demonstrated the effectiveness of different Phaeobacter biofilms in inhibiting V. anguillarum (D’Alvise et al. 2010). However, their study did not explore the structural and metabolic interactions between Phaeobacter biofilms and pathogens at high concentrations, leaving key aspects of their dynamic relationship unexplored. Given the potential application of the TDA-producing Phaeobacter in aquaculture, we set out to determine if biofilm formation affects the expression of TDA biosynthesis genes in Phaeobacter piscinae, how surface characteristics affect both biofilm formation and expression of TDA biosynthesis genes, and how P. piscinae biofilm is affected while interacting with high-concentrated V. anguillarum.

Materials and methods

Design, construction and characterization of surfaces

4 polymer surfaces were utilized in this study. 500 µm thick polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) foil (Crylon clear, Linatex, Herlev, Denmark) and 250 µm thick cyclic olefin copolymer (COC) foil (grade 5013, TOPAS Advanced Polymers Inc., Raunheim, Germany) were acquired commercially. 50 µm thick polypropylene (PP) foil was fabricated by the roll-to-roll extrusion method (Inmold, Nivå, Denmark). 500 µm thick polydimethyl-siloxane (PDMS) was fabricated with a SYLGARD 184 Silicone elastomer kit (Dow, Michigan, United States). The mixture was prepared with PDMS base and curing agent with a ratio of 10:1, after stirring for 5 minutes, the mixture was poured to a baker and degassed for 15 minutes to remove the air bubbles. Then the mixture was cured in the oven at 80 ⁰C for 2 hours.

The contact angle was measured by goniometer (Attension Theta Flow Auto 4, Biolin Scientific, Gothenburg, Sweden). Deionized water (DI) and Diiodomethane (818 153, Merck Millipore, Massachusetts, United States) were employed for contact angle measurement at room temperature. The drop size was 4 µL, and the drop rate was 2 µL/s.

Surface energy was calculated with the Owens-Wendt-Rabel & Kaelble model (OWRK) (Owens and Wendt 1969, Kaelble 1970, Waldner and Hirn 2023). The roughness of polymer foils was measured with the Icon AFM atomic force microscope (Bruker, Massachusetts, United States). Suitable scan areas and scan rates were chosen (Feidenhans’l et al. 2015). The scan size was 8 µm x 8 µm, and the scan rate was 1 Hz.

All the polymer foil was cut into 5 × 5 mm squares by Guillotine (Dahle, New Hampshire, United States). The cut square foils were attached to 5 mm thick PMMA carriers. As described below, the surface squares were placed in microtiter plates (see below) with bacteriological growth media, inoculated with bacteria and sampled at regular intervals for quantification of bacterial biofilm and expression of a specific gene-reporter fusion.

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

A list of bacterial strains used in this study can be found in Supplementary Table 1. The wild type (WT) Phaeobacter piscinae S26, isolated from Tetraselmis algae (Grotkjær et al. 2016), and the mutant S26 PtdaCGFP (Alfastsen et al. 2026 in prep) were used alongside the bacterial pathogen Vibrio anguillarum 90–11-286 isolated from Danish waters from a rainbow trout (Skov et al. 1995). Bacteria were cultured on Marine Agar (MA) (Difco 2216; 55.1 g/L), in Marine Broth (MB) (Difco 2216 Marine Broth; 37.4 g/L) or in 3% (w/v) Instant Ocean (IO: Instant Ocean Sea Salt, No. SS15-10) supplemented with 0.3% (w/v) Bacto Casamino Acids, 0.3% (w/v) HEPES and 0.2% (w/v) glucose, with pH adjusted to 6.8–7. Cultures were incubated at 25 ⁰C with shaking (200 rpm) unless otherwise mentioned. Escherichia coli strains were cultured in Luer-Hinton Broth (LB) or Luer-Hinton Agar (LA), and the media for E. coli WM3064 was supplemented with 0.3 mM of diaminopimelic acid (DAP) at 37⁰C with shaking (200 rpm). Stock cultures were stored in 18% glycerol at -80 ⁰C. Antibiotic concentrations used for selection were as follows: chloramphenicol (Cm) at 15 µg/mL for E. coli and 1 µg/mL for V. anguillarum mutant strains in LB or MB, respectively; kanamycin (Kan) at 50 µg/mL for E. coli and 200 µg/mL for V. anguillarum in LB or MB. Antibiotics were only used during the construction of the red fluorescence-tagged V. anguillarum. No antibiotics were used in subsequent experiments.

Construction of red fluorescence-tagged Vibrio anguillarum

A list of primers and plasmids used in this paper can be found in Supplementary Table 1. The suicide plasmid pDM4-PA1/04/03-mLychee-FlaC was designed using the AoE -A plasmid editor software and assembled by the Gibson Assembly method (Gibson et al. 2009). The plasmid was conjugated into V. anguillarum using E. coli WM3064 and a one-step homologous recombination, where the flaC sequence facilitated the integration of the plasmid into the chromosome. The flaC locus was chosen as previous studies have tagged V. anguillarum at this site without detectable effects on growth or motility, and the constitutive PA1/04/03 promoter has previously been used successfully in V. anguillarum (Almeida et al. 2026). Single-cell microscopy and colony PCR were used to confirm both the integration and the fluorescence signal. Methods for plasmid construction, conjugation and recombination can be found in the Supplementary Methods.

Comparison of tdaCDE gene expression in P. piscinae planktonic or biofilm cells

Biofilms were cultivated on a Calgary Biofilm Device (Ceri et al. 1999). Overnight cultures of P. piscinae S26 PtdaCGFP and WT were diluted 100-fold in IOCG and transferred to an MBEC Assay Biofilm Inoculator (Calgary Device) (Innovatech) to grow and form biofilms. Plates were incubated for 24 hours at 25⁰C with shaking (140 rpm). After incubation, 150 µL of the cell suspension from each well was transferred to a Nunclon 96-well flat bottom microtiter plate; this fraction would correlate to planktonic cells. Biofilm-covered lid pegs were washed with sterile 3% IO and placed in a Nunclon 96-flat bottom microtiter plate (ThermoFisher Scientific) with 200 µL of 3% IO. Both plates were sonicated for 5 minutes in a water bath sonicator at 50/60 Hz (Aerosec Industries® Delta 220T) to standardise handling and detach the biofilm cells. Planktonic cells were collected from the wells where biofilm cells were collected from the pegs. Four biological replicates were used for the analysis. Bacterial counts were determined by dilution and plating before and after sonication of the planktonic fraction to determine if sonication affected viability of the bacteria.

Flow cytometry analysis

Flow cytometry was used to measure the GFP intensity per cell in biofilm or planktonic cells of P. piscinae S26 PtdaCGFP. Cell suspensions were gently vortexed and diluted in sterile 3% IO to obtain a suitable event rate (<200 events/s) and to minimise cell aggregates. Measurements were performed on a MACSQuantVYB flow cytometer (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) using SSC and FSC as triggers; a total of 10 000 events were analyzed per sample. GFP signals were recorded in the B1 channel with a 488 nm laser and 525/50 nm filter. FlowJo10.10.0 software was used for data analysis, where a buffer sample was used to remove salt background noise, followed by gating cells expressing GFP based on a sample of the P. piscinae S26 PtdaCGFP. Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) and counts were analyzed and plotted.

Biofilm formation on polymer surfaces

Overnight cultures of P. piscinae S26 PtdaCGFP were diluted 100-fold in IOCG and transferred to a Nunclon 96-round bottom microtiter plate (ThermoFisher Scientific) containing the different 5 × 5 mm square surfaces mentioned above. Milli-Q water was added to the border wells to prevent desiccation during incubation with shaking (140 rpm). Every 24 hours, surfaces were washed with 3%IO to remove planktonic cells and transferred to a new microtiter plate with fresh IOCG medium. Biofilm volume and expression of the PtdaCGFP reporter in the biofilm were determined using a Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope and later the BiofilmQ software. At each sampling, four surfaces per treatment were analysed and not returned to the wells. All experiments were performed in four biological replicates.

Interaction between the fish pathogen Vibrio anguillarum and biofilms of P. piscinae S26

Biofilms of P. piscinae PtdaCGFP were grown on PP and PDMS as described above. After 16 hours, the surfaces were washed in sterile IOCGH and transferred to wells containing 200 μL of a 1 000-fold dilution (in IOCGH) of an overnight culture of V. anguillarum_mLychee giving an estimated cell concentration of 106 CFU/ml. Controls included surfaces without P. piscinae biofilms as well as surfaces with P. piscinae biofilms being transferred to sterile IOCGH. The surfaces were incubated for 6 hours before being analysed in the Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope and all treatments were analysed using four biological replicates. The number of V. anguillarum before and after exposure to P. piscinae biofilms was determined by plating 10-fold serial dilutions on TSA plates, as P. pisicinae cannot grow on TSA that does not contain marine salts (Rasmussen et al. 2018). Surfaces were analysed for biofilm volume and expression of the PtdaCGFP reporter using a CLSM and analysed by BiofilmQ software. All experiments were performed in four biological replicates.

Staining and confocal laser scanning microscope and image analysis

Surfaces with P. piscinae biofilms were rinsed with 3%IO and stained for 10 min with Syto62 (Invitrogen) at a concentration of 2 µM to analyse biovolume. Surfaces with or without P. piscinae biofilm and exposed to mLychee-labelled V. anguillarum were stained with Syto41 (Invitrogen) using the same concentration and time to quantify biovolume. The surfaces were incubated for 10 minutes in a humidity chamber consisting of a Petri dish with a wet paper towel to prevent evaporation and allow GFP protein maturation, which is dependent on oxygen. Surfaces were mounted on glass microscope slides, which kept the coupons in place during imaging. Images were obtained using an inverted Leica TCS SP8 confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica Microsystems, Mannheim, Germany). GFP was detected with a laser excitation of 488 nm and an emission range of 493–577 nm, while Syto 62 was detected using a laser excitation of 650 nm and an emission range of 670–700 nm. Syto 41 was detected with a laser excitation of 405 nm and an emission range of 410–488 nm, while mLychee was detected with a laser excitation of 552 nm and an emission range of 591–766 nm. Images were captured with a HC PL Fluotar 20x/0.55 dry objective at a resolution of 1024 × 1024 px to assess bacterial coverage, taking 10 pictures per replicate. Z-stacks were taken with an automatically calculated interval to acquire the slices, with a resolution of 512 × 512 px, to measure biovolume, TDA gene expression, and presence of V. anguillarum based on Syto62/Syto41, GFP intensity and mLychee intensity, respectively. Four stacks were taken per replicate. Data was processed and quantified using the software BiofilmQ with the RobustBackground method as a threshold, with a sensitivity of 0.7 for segmenting (Hartmann et al. 2021).

Statistical analysis

To determine whether the expression from the PtdaCGFP differed between bacteria grown in biofilms or planktonically, a two-sample t-test was applied to the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) values of the FlowJo data from the flow cytometric measurements. Differences between biovolume, coverage and GFP intensity and GFP/biovolume ratios of P. piscinae S26 PtdaCGFP on different surfaces and time points were analyzed by a one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by post-hoc Tukey’s test for pairwise group comparisons. Data was log10-transformed to control variance, and data-points having standardized residuals exceeding ± 2.5 were identified as outliers and removed from analysis.

TDA biosynthesis gene expression in planktonic and biofilm P. piscinae

Flow cytometry was used to determine GFP intensity from expression of P. piscinae PtdaCGFP. After exclusion of background events using control samples, approximately 9 000 biofilm and 8 000 planktonic events remained from the initial 10 000 events (Supplementary Fig. 1). Gating based on the wild-type P. piscinae S26 control yielded approximately 7 000–9 000 events per biofilm sample and 6 000–8 000 per planktonic sample (Supplementary Fig. 1). The per cell fluorescence intensity was significantly higher in biofilm cells than in planktonic cells (Fig. 2A) as the mean fluorescence intensity of biofilm cells was twice that of the planktonic counterpart (p-value = 0.0017), suggesting an elevated TDA biosynthesis gene expression in the biofilm cells (Fig. 2B).

Biovolume and TDA biosynthesis gene expression of P. piscinae S26 PtdaCGFP on different surfaces

Since PDMS promoted higher initial attachment of P. piscinae, the four polymers were analyzed after 24 and 96 hours to determine if this also affected to more mature biofilm (Fig. 4). Both log10-transformed biovolume and total GFP intensity (Supplementary Fig. 2A, B), reflecting TDA biosynthesis gene expression, generally increased over time on all surfaces. A two-way ANOVA with time and surface revealed a significant effect of time on both biovolume and GFP intensity (p-value = 0.00167, 0.00002 respectively), whereas the effect of surface was modest (p-value = 0.05, 0.93 respectively). Thus, the overall trend was an increase in biovolume and total GFP intensity over time (Supplementary Fig. 2C, D; Fig. 4C, D), but not all pairwise time-point comparisons for individual surfaces were significant in Tukey’s post hoc test. The GFP-to-biovolume ratio, reported as log10 (GFP/Biovolume) was similar for all surfaces (Fig. 4A, B), although there was a significant increase over time (p-value = 0.0002). When averaged across surfaces, log10 (GFP/biovolume) increased from -1.67 ± 0.19 to -1.45 ± 0.12 at 24 and 96 hours, respectively.

Biovolume and TDA biosynthesis gene expression of P. piscinae S26 PtdaCGFP on PP overtime

Given the consistency across surfaces, PP surfaces were selected for studying biovolume and TDA biosynthesis gene expression dynamics in P. piscinae S26 PtdaCGFP biofilm over time. Log10 transformed biovolume increased (Supplementary Fig. 3A, C) significantly between 3 to 4 days (p-value = 0.01021), showing an exponential growth phase before stabilizing after 4 days. Log10 total GFP intensity followed a similar trend (Supplementary Fig. 3B, D) with a significant difference between 3 and 4 days (p-value = 0.03054), indicating a proportional increase in TDA biosynthesis gene expression with biofilm maturation. The relation between TDA biosynthesis gene expression and biovolume was positively correlated (Fig. 5A), and ANOVA analysis identified time and biovolume as highly significant factors for TDA biosynthesis gene expression (p-value < 0.00002, 0.00002). The interaction between time and biovolume shows a marginal effect (p-value = 0.056), indicating that the relation between TDA gene expression and biovolume stabilize over time. The ratio of GFP intensity to biovolume, reported as log10(GFP/biovolume) (Fig. 5B), was significantly affected by time (p-value = 0.027), with the largest changes occurring at the earlier timepoints while stabilizing in the later stages.

Interaction between the fish pathogen Vibrio anguillarum and biofilms of Phaeobacter piscinae S26

Based on the difference in bacterial coverage observed on PDMS during early-stage attachment, PDMS and PP were selected to assess antagonism of 16-hour P. piscinae biofilms against a mLychee-tagged V. anguillarum (Supplementary Fig. 4). As expected, log10-transformed biovolume and total TDA gene expression (total GFP intensity) were significantly higher on PDMS compared to PP, with a 2.5- and 2.8-fold difference (Fig. 6, Supplementary Fig. 5; p-value < 0.0005, 0.0247, respectively). However, GFP-to-biovolume ratio, log10(GFP/biovolume), did not differ between surfaces (Supplementary Fig. 5; p-value = 0.581). V. anguillarum_mLychee_FlaC grew from 106 CFU/ml to 108 CFU/ml when exposed to a surface with no P. piscinae biofilm. V. anguillarum_mLychee_FlaC remained planktonic, with no detectable surface attachment on PDMS or PP. In contrast, numbers of V. anguillarum_mLychee_FlaC were reduced when exposed to P. piscinae biofilms on PDMS and PP to 2.0 × 105 CFU/ml and 3.2 × 105 CFU/ml, respectively (Fig. 6; p-value < 0.00001). The decrease caused by PDMS biofilm was significantly larger, with a 1.5-fold decrease compared to PP (p-value = 0.043347). Interestingly, the presence of V. anguillarum_mLychee_FlaC led to a 2-fold increase in P. piscinae biofilm log10-transformed biovolume on PDMS (Fig. 6; p-value = 0.03123). In the presence of V. anguillarum_mLychee_FlaC, total TDA biosynthesis gene expression (log10 total GFP intensity) also increased 2.5-fold and 1.1-fold in biofilms on PDMS and PP, respectively, although these differences were not statistically significant (p-value = 0.3036). The ratio of GFP intensity to biovolume, reported as log10(GFP/biovolume), remained unaffected under V. anguillarum_mLychee_FlaC (p-value > 0.5).

Discussion

The ability to produce secondary metabolites with antibiotic activity can influence the ability of a bacterium to form biofilm (Zhang SD et al. 2021 2022) and, conversely, biofilm formation may in some bacteria enhance secondary metabolite production (Liang 2015, Chen et al. 2017, Nuzzo et al. 2021, Latta and Bechthold 2022, Wang et al. 2024). We previously found (Lindqvist et al. 2023) that motility of Phaeobacter piscinae increased when the ability to produce the antimicrobial molecule, tropodithietic acid (TDA) was deleted, and in this study, we observed that P. piscinae cells growing in biofilms showed higher expression of genes involved in TDA biosynthesis than planktonic cells. Taken together, the biofilm-associated cells are therefore accompanied by increased TDA biosynthesis gene expression, in agreement with similar observations for another TDA-producer, Tritonibacter mobilis (D’Alvise et al. 2014).

Determining concentrations of TDA in biofilms and planktonic cells in a liquid system is difficult as the molecule diffuses, and chemical detection requires cell densities at 107 CFU/ml (or above) (Svendsen et al. 2024) and we therefore determined TDA biosynthesis gene expression at the single cell level as a proxy for TDA production. The results align with findings in other bacteria where secondary metabolite production increases in the biofilm states, as found by both chemical analyses and by biosynthesis gene expression (Malone et al. 2010, Tran et al. 2011, Liang 2015, Chen et al. 2017, Xu et al. 2019, Makitrynskyy et al. 2020, Nuzzo et al. 2021, Latta and Bechthold 2022, Wang et al. 2024). Biofilm growth is accompanied by many regulatory and physiological changes. Although the mechanism underlying the increased TDA biosynthesis gene expression in P. piscinae biofilms remains to be determined, it could potentially involve biofilm-associated signalling pathways, including QS and c-di-GMP. Since we observed that biofilm cells of P. piscinae were associated with higher expression of TDA biosynthesis genes, we explored if different solid surfaces could affect biofilm formation and TDA biosynthesis gene expression. We chose four surfaces of different hydrophobicity as several studies have suggested that hydrophobic surfaces promote bacterial attachment (Almaguer-Flores et al. 2015, Chan et al. 2017, Wassmann et al. 2017, De-la-Pinta et al. 2019, Lubov Donaghy et al. 2020). Hydrophobic surfaces often support the formation of more irreversible biofilms, as they repel water molecules, leading to stronger adhesion forces between bacteria and the surface (Pan et al. 2021). In contrast, superhydrophobic and super hydrophilic surfaces have the potential to effectively inhibit bacterial attachment (Yoon et al. 2014), and were, therefore not included in the study.

The most hydrophobic surface, PDMS, promoted early attachment of P. piscinae S26, with 40% coverage compared to only 5% on PP, despite an only modest difference in water contact angle (112° vs. 104°). Coverage on PMMA and COC was as low as on PP, despite PMMA (86°) having a lower WCA compared to PP (104°) and COC (97°). This indicates that wettability alone does not explain differences in bacterial attachment, and other properties should be considered. One such factor is surface energy; however, only PP with a surface energy of ∼30 mJ/m² fell within the range proposed to retain less biomass (20–30 mJ/m²) (Baier 2006), thus surface energy does not explain the low adhesion on PMMA and COC. Material stiffness also influences adhesion and PDMS was by far the least stiff surface of the ones tested (Sugimoto et al. 1995, O’Neil et al. 2016). Recent studies have linked higher stiffness of hydrophilic surfaces to increased bacterial attachment (Saha et al. 2013, Kolewe et al. 2015, Kolewe et al. 2018, Vigué et al. 2022), whereas on hydrophobic surfaces, lower stiffness tends to enhance attachment (Fernández et al. 2010, Song and Ren 2014, Song et al. 2017, Cai et al. 2019, Vigué et al. 2022). Although the precise mechanisms remain unclear, it has been hypothesized that softer surfaces provide more favorable interaction dynamics for bacteria (Song and Ren 2014). Surface chemistry, which was not systematically measured in our study, may also affect protein adsorption and bacterial interactions (Oh et al. 2018). Over time, we observed no difference in biovolume or TDA biosynthetic gene expression between surfaces, suggesting that once an initial surface layer is established, the growth of the biofilm depends on the bacteria and the growth substrate surrounding the biofilm rather than the initial inert surface.

The ratio of TDA biosynthesis gene expression to biovolume, log10 (GFP/biovolume), increased over time suggesting that gene expression is enhanced during biofilm development rather than being solely a consequence of biomass accumulation. As biofilms mature, structural and metabolic heterogeneity emerges, leading to the formation of specialized subpopulations (Pisithkul et al. 2019). In Pseudomonas aeruginosa, persister cells cannot produce pyocyanin due to their reduced metabolic activity (Žiemytė et al. 2021), whereas other subpopulations remain metabolically active and can display distinct signalling and gene-expression programs within the biofilm (Nair et al. 2017, Klauck et al. 2018, Kindler et al. 2019). Similar observations have been reported in biofilms of the marine Tritonibacter mobilis, where TDA gene expression is promoted by elevated levels of c-di-GMP (D’Alvise et al. 2014), and in Pseudoalteromonas spp., where pyomelanin production increase as biofilms mature (Zeng et al. 2017). To fully understand the dynamics of TDA production in biofilms, further experiments are required to elucidate the interplay of secondary messengers, biofilm maturation, and secondary metabolite expression over time.

V. anguillarum is inhibited by P. piscinae, due to its TDA production (D’Alvise et al. 2010, 2012b, 2014, Lindqvist et al. 2023). Most studies have demonstrated inhibition during planktonic competition (Porsby et al. 2008, Rasmussen et al. 2018), however, a few have demonstrated inhibition of V. anguillarum in the presence of Phaeobacter biofilms, though these used mature biofilms and long exposure time (D’Alvise et al. 2010, 2012a). In our case, the inhibitory effect was stronger on PDMS than on PP, likely because PDMS supported greater biofilm biovolume and therefore higher TDA production. V. anguillarum cells were rarely detected on the surfaces, consistent with their reported poor biofilm-forming ability (Tan et al. 2020, Kalatzis et al. 2023). We also unexpectedly observed that the presence of V. anguillarum coincided with an increased biovolume of P. piscinae and TDA biosynthesis gene expression on the PDMS surfaces, whereas this correlation was not evident in PP. Although the increase was modest and not statistically significant, these observations suggest that competition interactions can modulate biofilm growth and secondary metabolite production (Zhao et al. 2016). Such effects may be mediated through quorum sensing, as AHLs are known to promote both biofilm formation and TDA production in P. gallaeciensis (Berger et al. 2011) and V. anguillarum is a potent AHL producer (Buch et al. 2003). More broadly, many bacterial secondary metabolites are influenced by QS signals, and competitors can trigger responses: for example, Bacillus subtilis can mobilise and increase the production of secondary metabolites in the presence of the competitor Pseudomonas spp. releasing pyochelin (Andrić et al. 2023). However, little is known about the interaction between V. anguillarum and P. piscinae, thus, it highlights an important and underexplored aspect of TDA regulation.

From an applied perspective, P. piscinae could potentially be introduced into aquaculture systems as a pre-established biofilm rather than as planktonic cells, either on submerged carrier materials or as part of biofilter systems as used in recirculating aquaculture systems (Martins et al. 2010, Li et al. 2017). Biofilms are already present in aquaculture systems, particularly in biofilters, where they contribute to nitrogen and phosphorus removal and the degradation of organic matter (Perales-Pérez et al. 2023, Yi et al. 2023, Su et al. 2025). Thus, P. piscinae biofilms could potentially be grown on biofilter materials as a strategy to support pathogen control, although this approach would require validation under relevant aquaculture conditions.

In conclusion, we show that biofilm cells of Phaeobacter piscinae exhibited higher TDA biosynthesis gene expression compared to their planktonic counterparts, suggesting that introducing the probiotic bacteria to aquaculture units as a biofilm could facilitate the probiotic effect. While PDMS promoted early attachment, differences in long-term biofilm development and TDA gene expression were minimal across surfaces, highlighting that time, rather than material, is the main driver of TDA expression per biovolume. On PDMS, the presence of Vibrio anguillarum was associated with enhanced biofilm growth and TDA gene expression of the P. piscinae biofilm, suggesting that interspecies interactions may enhance TDA production.
