Sociodemographic Inequities in Telemedicine Use Among US Patients Initiating Treatment in Community Cancer Centers During the Ongoing COVID-19 Pandemic, 2020-2022
Authors: Jenny S Guadamuz, Xiaoliang Wang, Trevor J Royce, Gregory S Calip
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractPURPOSEAlthough telemedicine was seen as a way to improve cancer care during the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic, there is limited information regarding inequities in its uptake. This study assessed sociodemographic factors associated with telemedicine use among patients initiating treatment for 20 common cancers.METHODSThis retrospective cohort study used deidentified electronic health record–derived patient data from a nationwide network of community cancer practices, linked to area-level Census information. We included adults (age 18 years and older) who initiated first-line systemic cancer treatment between March 2020 and December 2022 (follow-up through March 2023). Exposures include race/ethnicity, insurance status, and area-level social determinants of health (eg, block group socioeconomic status [SES]). The outcome was telemedicine use within 90 days after treatment initiation. Associations were examined using logistic regression models adjusted for age, sex, performance status, stage, and cancer type.RESULTSThis study included 36,993 patients (48.6% women; median age, 69 years), of whom 15.1% used telemedicine services. Black (12.2%; odds ratio [OR], 0.78 [95% CI, 0.70 to 0.88]) and uninsured (9.2%; OR, 0.59 [95% CI, 0.48 to 0.73]) patients were less likely to use telemedicine services than their White and well-insured counterparts (14.5% and 15.0%, respectively). Patients in rural (9.7%; OR, 0.54 [95% CI, 0.46 to 0.57]), suburban (11.8%; OR, 0.67 [95% CI, 0.61 to 0.74]), and low SES areas (9.9%; OR, 0.39 [95% CI, 0.35 to 0.43]) were less also likely to use telemedicine than their counterparts in urban (16.6%) or high SES (21.6%) areas.CONCLUSIONNearly one sixth of patients initiating cancer treatment during the pandemic used telemedicine, but there were substantial inequities. The proliferation of telemedicine may perpetuate cancer care inequities if marginalized populations do not have equitable access.

INTRODUCTION

In March 2020, regulators in the United States announced expanded coverage for health care services delivered via telemedicine (also known as telehealth) for Medicare beneficiaries in response to the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic.1 Most public and private insurers followed suit.2 These changes allowed patients to receive cancer care via telemedicine.

Telemedicine was not widely used for cancer care before the pandemic,3,4 but its use increased to 15%-25% of patients at community-based cancer centers early in the pandemic,5 as in-person clinical visits declined.6 However, preliminary national surveys indicate that uptake has varied by race/ethnicity and area-level social determinants of health (SDoH) such as socioeconomic status (SES).7 Although the COVID-19 pandemic has exacerbated existing inequities in cancer care delivery, inequities in telemedicine use among patients initiating cancer treatment are largely undescribed.

This study examines sociodemographic inequities in telemedicine use among US patients initiating treatment for 20 common cancers at community-based centers during the COVID-19 pandemic. We focused on telemedicine use during the first 90 days after treatment initiation because telemedicine was recognized early in the COVID-19 pandemic as a way to mitigate infection risk among patients with cancer, especially those on active immunosuppressive treatment.8

METHODS

Design and Participants

This retrospective cohort study used the nationwide Flatiron Health Research Database, a longitudinal database of deidentified patient-level electronic health record (EHR)–derived data from approximately 280 US cancer centers (approximately 800 sites of care), curated via technology-enabled abstraction.9,10 Using patients' latest residential addresses, we linked patient data to information from the American Community Survey (2015-2019)11 and the US Department of Agriculture (USDA, 2010).12

This study included patients age 18 years and older who initiated first-line systemic cancer treatment between March 2020 and December 2022 (minimum 90-day follow-up with information through March 2023) in a community-based cancer center. Treatment initiation was defined as the first documented receipt of systemic antineoplastic therapy (or stem-cell transplantation for blood cancers) for an initial, advanced, or metastatic cancer diagnosis (depending on specific cancer; Data Supplement, Table S1, online only). Patient Characteristics

The primary exposures were race/ethnicity, insurance status and area-level region (state), rurality (Census tract), and SES (Census block group). Insurance status was based on the latest payer before or within 1 year of treatment initiation and categorized as well-insured (commercial or Medicare), underinsured (Medicaid or other insurance), and uninsured (no documented insurance).

Area-level SES was measured using the Yost Index methodology, which incorporates income, home values, rental costs, poverty, blue-collar employment, unemployment, and education information.13 Quintiles were determined from the US population and applied to patients. The Yost Index is more effective at stratifying areas and detecting cancer inequities than other SES indices.14 Additional information regarding the application of this SES measure in this EHR-derived data set is available elsewhere.15 Rurality was defined using USDA rural-urban community (RUCA) codes: urban (RUCA 1.0 and 1.1), suburban (RUCA 2.0, 2.1, and 3.0), and rural (all other codes).12

Clinical characteristics included age (at treatment initiation), sex, stage (at initial diagnosis), and Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG-PS, maximum value within 30 days of treatment initiation). The system(s) to categorize stage at initial diagnosis varied by cancer type (Data Supplement, Table S1).

Outcome

Our primary outcome was having at least one telemedicine visit during a follow-up period beginning at treatment initiation and ending 90 days afterward. Other studies examining telemedicine inequities have focused on this period.16 Telemedicine visits were defined as billable evaluation and management visits conducted by a physician or advanced practitioner, either via video call or telephone (in this evaluation, we cannot determine the mode or purpose of any given telemedicine visit).16 Specifically, telemedicine visits were identified using a combination of visit information, billing information, and patient documents on the basis of the following criteria: (1) any billing code specific to a remote evaluation and management visit; or (2) any billing code specific to an in-person evaluation and management visit in combination with a visit name or a document title that has a keyword match for telemedicine. Our definition is holistic (ie, any remote audio, remote audio/video billed visit, or visit coded as in-person but documented in the EHR to be a telemedicine visit) and incorporates all telemedicine visits regardless of clinic billing practices.

Statistical Analysis

We used Χ2 tests and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests to evaluate differences. To examine associations, we used logistic regressions for binary outcomes and linear regressions for continuous outcomes.

Informed by the Institute of Medicine's definition of health care disparities—or differences not justified by clinical characteristics or patient preferences17—clinical factors were considered potential confounders.18,19 Therefore, to examine telemedicine inequities across race/ethnicity and SDoH factors, we used models adjusted for clinical factors (age, sex, ECOG-PS, stage, and cancer type) but did not present fully adjusted models in the primary results. For example, to assess the association between race/ethnicity and telemedicine, we do not adjust for insurance (which is likely on the causal pathway).

As a sensitivity analysis, we examined these inequities in a fully adjusted model. However, these estimates should be interpreted with caution because this approach (residual direct effect) underestimates inequities by ignoring distinctions between variables that may be legitimate sources of differences in utilization and those that may represent disadvantages.18 More explicitly, fully adjusted models reporting on controlled direct effects almost always underestimate inequities because mediators representing structural disadvantages are included in the model.18

As additional sensitivity analyses, we evaluated whether the magnitude of inequities remained similar when telemedicine use was measured using longer follow-up periods (180 and 365 days). We also examined the number of total and in-person visits within 90 days of treatment initiation to determine whether the inequities observed in telemedicine use were similar to those in other types of care.

Statistical analyses were conducted using R, version 4.2.2(The R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). P values of < .01 and < .05 were considered statistically significant.

Institutional review board approval of the study protocol was obtained before study conduct, and included a waiver of informed consent.

RESULTS

This study included 36,993 patients (48.6% female; median age, 69 years; 42.2% had a de novo metastatic diagnosis; 12.7% had an ECOG-PS ≥2; Table 2). Most patients were non-Latinx-(NL)-White (59.0%), well-insured (85.0%), and lived in the southern (51.5%) and urban counties (73.5%). One in six patients initiating cancer treatment used telemedicine services (15.1%). Telemedicine users were more likely to be well-insured and live in the Northeast and West regions of the United States and in urban and high SES neighborhoods (all P < .01).

Clinical factors are associated with telemedicine use (Data Supplement, Table S2). For example, older age and being male were associated with lower telemedicine use. Other clinical factors associated with telemedicine use included ECOG-PS and specific cancer types.

In models adjusted for clinical factors, we observed substantial inequities in telemedicine use (Table 3). Fewer NL-Black patients (12.2%; adjusted odds ratios [ORs], 0.78 [95% CI, 0.70 to 0.88]) used telemedicine services than Latinx (15.7%; OR, 1.06 [95% CI, 0.93 to 1.21]) and NL-White patients (14.5%; reference). Uninsured patients (9.9%; OR, 0.59 [95% CI, 0.48 to 0.73]) were less likely to use telemedicine than well-insured patients (15.0%; reference). Area-level SDoHs were also associated with telemedicine use. For example, patients in the South (7.6%; OR, 0.26 [95% CI, 0.24 to 0.28]) and Midwest (14.9%; OR, 0.54 [95% CI, 0.49 to 0.60]) were less likely to use telemedicine than those in the West (28.4%; OR, 1.27 [95% CI, 1.17 to 1.37]) and Northeast (24.2%; reference). Patients living in rural (9.7%; OR, 0.54 [95% CI, 0.49 to 0.60]) and suburban areas (11.8%; OR, 0.67 [95% CI, 0.61 to 0.74]) were less likely to use telemedicine services than patients in urban areas (16.6%; reference). Telemedicine use was lower among patients living in the lowest (9.9%; OR, 0.39 [95% CI, 0.35 to 0.43]) versus highest SES neighborhoods (21.6%; reference).

In our sensitivity analysis, observed associations between individual- and area-level SDoH and telemedicine use remained similar in a fully adjusted model (Data Supplement, Table S3). The magnitude of sociodemographic inequities also remained similar when telemedicine use was measured with longer follow-up periods of 180 and 365 days (Data Supplement, Table S4). We also observed that the total number of visits within 90 days of treatment initiation was higher among telemedicine users, suggesting that this population may have greater access to care than patients who do not use telemedicine (Data Supplement, Table S5). Moreso, there is no evidence that patients who are less likely to use telemedicine have a preference for in-person visits. For example, we observed that Black patients, as well as patients in marginalized neighborhoods, have fewer in-person visits than their counterparts.

The proportion of patients who used telemedicine within 90 days of their treatment initiation decreased from 27.6% of those initiating treatment in March 2020 to 13.2% of patients initiating treatment in December 2022 (Fig 1). The observed inequities in telemedicine use persisted across early and later periods of the COVID-19 pandemic (Data Supplement, Table S6).

Telemedicine use also varied by cancer type, ranging from approximately 20% among patients with metastatic breast cancer to approximately 10% among patients with acute myeloid leukemia, follicular lymphoma, and mantle cell lymphoma (Data Supplement, Fig S1). Across the 20 cancer types evaluated, inequities by insurance status, region, rurality, and area-level SES (Data Supplement, Figs S2-S5) were generally consistent, although not always significant because of lower statistical power in subcohorts.

DISCUSSION

One in six patients initiating treatment for an initial, advanced, or metastatic cancer diagnosis in community cancer centers from March 2020 to December 2022 used telemedicine services within 3 months of starting their first-line therapy. However, Black and uninsured patients were less likely to use these services, as were patients in nonurban and lower SES neighborhoods. Although the expansion of telemedicine services was seen as an opportunity to improve access to oncology care (through potentially more convenient, high-quality care) and subsequently improve cancer care equity,20 this study demonstrates that people of color and those living in marginalized areas did not have equitable access throughout the COVID-19 pandemic. This is especially alarming, given that telemedicine use during cancer treatment is thought to reduce the risk of COVID-19 infection,21 yet the same patients of color who experienced greater rates of COVID-19 mortality had worse telemedicine access. These findings are also timely, considering efforts to make coverage of telemedicine services permanent after the end of the federal COVID-19 emergency declaration.22-24 It is essential to assess the uneven adoption of technology-driven advances during this period to understand unintended consequences, including the potential to exacerbate health inequities.24 It is increasingly recognized that clinical practices required because of the pandemic-related circumstances should not be misconstrued as best practices solely because they have become commonplace.24

Expanding on earlier findings,3,16 this study demonstrates that the adoption of telemedicine for cancer care—at least early in patients' treatment journey—has not been uniform across cancer types, regions, and other SDoH strata. However, these findings warrant further research. For example, we need to determine why telemedicine use was more common among patients with solid cancers than those with blood cancers. Although our findings are consistent with previous work demonstrating regional variation in the adoption of telemedicine for cancer care during the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic,16 it remains to be determined whether state differences in COVID-19 response policies explain these differences. Patients in the South, a region with less generous public health insurance coverage (including telemedicine reimbursement) and more lax COVID-19 restrictions early in the pandemic,24-26 were less likely to use telemedicine services than those elsewhere.

Although efforts to increase access to telemedicine have primarily focused on insurer coverage and reimbursement parity between in-person and telemedicine visits,23 our work also demonstrated the importance of considering and mitigating barriers stemming from a patient's SDoH. For example, limited access to broadband internet, more common in lower SES and nonurban areas, is associated with reduced telemedicine use.25 Without addressing the SDoHs that adversely influence traditional health care delivery,26 telemedicine will not improve entrenched cancer care inequities. For instance, although telemedicine use is associated with patient savings in time and travel costs,27 patients without insurance—who are more likely to experience financial toxicity28—are less likely to use telemedicine.

Although telemedicine use declined from approximately 25% in the first 6 months of the pandemic to approximately 10% in 2022, the use of these services is much higher than prepandemic levels.4,28,29 This greater acceptance of telemedicine may improve cancer care for all patients, as recent studies have demonstrated that telemedicine can yield high diagnostic concordance compared with in-person care for new oncology concerns, reduce patient costs, and improve quality of life during survivorship.27,29,30 However, additional research is needed to determine whether the quality of telemedicine-provided care (eg, guideline concordance) varies across sociodemographic groups. Future work should also evaluate methods to implement telemedicine equitably, as marginalized patients continued to have worse access throughout the ongoing pandemic. This is important because telemedicine is increasingly seen as a way to equitably deliver cancer care, including clinical trials. The pandemic has prompted the shift to decentralized clinical trials, which use telemedicine and other digital tools to conduct these studies.24 However, our study suggests that if telemedicine inequities are not addressed, decentralized clinical trials may not produce more inclusive studies. As with in-person care and other health care advances,31 concentrated efforts are needed to ensure that the proliferation of telemedicine does not perpetuate inequities in cancer care and outcomes.

This study has several limitations. First, our findings may not be generalizable to all patients with cancer in the United States. Although the real-world database used for this study has distributions of sex and geographic regions similar to other large cancer databases (eg, SEER and National Program of Cancer Registries),10 there may be differences across other factors of interest including race/ethnicity and area-level SDoH. This study may not be generalizable to patients who received care at academic centers nor patients with other cancer types or early-stage diagnosis (12 cancer types in this cohort were captured at advanced/metastatic diagnosis). However, our population of interest was community patients with cancer treated outside of large academic medical centers, which may be more representative of the underlying population.32 Second, missingness and unmeasured confounders may affect observed inequities. For example, approximately 15% of the cohort has missing race/ethnicity data, and this will likely attenuate observed inequities, as patients of color are more likely to have missing data.33 We also do not capture nonsystemic treatment (eg, surgery or radiotherapy) and cannot determine whether types of initial treatment modalities affect the use of telemedicine. Third, we could not examine telemedicine use in the pre–COVID-19 era nor determine whether inequities changed during the pre- versus post–COVID-19 eras. Fourth, we measured telemedicine use within 3 months after first-line treatment initiation, and use may differ later in the treatment course. Finally, we could not determine patients' preference for telemedicine, the reason for telemedicine visits, or whether visits were audio-only.

In conclusion, Black and uninsured patients were less likely to use telemedicine services within 3 months of starting their first line of cancer therapy, as were patients living in nonurban areas and neighborhoods with low SES. Despite the promise of telemedicine to expand access to cancer care, the proliferation of these services may fail to reduce inequities if marginalized sociodemographic groups continue to lack equitable access.
