Lower limb amputation in incident dialysis patients in England: incidence, outcomes and inter-centre variation from a national registry study
Authors: Nithin Bodapati, Sherry Masoud, Javeria Peracha, David Pitcher, Anna Casula, Retha Steenkamp, James Medcalf, Dorothea Nitsch, Gerry Rayman, William McKane, Graham Lipkin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundLower limb amputation (LLA) is a devastating complication in dialysis patients, often related to diabetes and peripheral vascular disease. National data on LLA incidence, variation and outcomes in England are limited.MethodsA retrospective cohort study conducted using linked data from the UK Renal Registry (UKRR) and Hospital Episode Statistics for incident dialysis patients in England (2003–17). Cumulative incidence plots assessed LLA rates across three patient subgroups: primary renal disease (PRD) diabetes, diabetes as comorbidity and no diabetes. Fine–Gray competing risk models identified independent risk factors for LLA. One-year mortality following LLA was presented as Kaplan–Meier curves and analysed using age- and sex-adjusted standardized mortality rates (SMRs).ResultsAmong 69 701 incident dialysis patients, 9.1% with PRD diabetes underwent LLA within 3 years, versus 2.6% with diabetes as comorbidity and 0.6% without diabetes. Risk factors for LLA included diabetes [subdistribution hazard ratio (sHR) 9.3 for PRD diabetes, sHR 3.6 for diabetes as comorbidity], peripheral vascular disease (sHR 2.11), prior amputation (sHR 2.19), ischaemic heart disease and White ethnicity. Major amputation rates declined over time, while minor amputation rates remained stable. Significant inter-centre variation was observed after adjustment. One-year mortality after LLA exceeded 40% in all subgroups. SMRs were highest in the PRD diabetes group compared with the general population (34.7), but highest in the diabetes as comorbidity group compared with the dialysis population (5.31).ConclusionIncident dialysis patients, particularly those with PRD diabetes, face high LLA risk and very poor survival after amputation. Persistent inter-centre variation suggests scope for national audit, standardized foot care and preventive strategies.Keywords: diabetes, dialysis, inter-centre variation, lower limb amputation

INTRODUCTION

Lower limb amputation (LLA) is a severe, life-altering complication in people receiving dialysis, driven predominantly by peripheral vascular disease (PVD) and diabetic foot disease [1]. This is associated with significant mortality, major disability, psychological distress and high healthcare use [2–4]. Although the burden is well recognized, national data from England on LLA incidence, variation and outcomes in dialysis patients are limited.

People receiving dialysis have a markedly higher risk of LLA than those with advanced chronic kidney disease not yet on dialysis [5]. This risk is compounded by a high prevalence of PVD, cardiovascular disease, poor glycaemic control and diabetes-related complications. Kidney failure–specific factors, including chronic inflammation, uraemia and frequent vascular access procedures, further predispose to limb threatening complications [6–8]. Moreover, self-management challenges like visual impairment, mobility limitations and the burden of frequent dialysis alongside other appointments can delay recognition and treatment of early foot problems. Among risk factors, diabetes is consistently the strongest predictor of LLA in the dialysis population [6, 9, 10].

Globally, LLA prevalence in dialysis populations range from 6% to 18% [6, 9–11]. The US renal registry data have reported 1-year mortality following LLA at 43.6% and a median survival of only 6 months for patients undergoing above knee amputations [3, 11]. Similarly, European registry studies from the Netherlands and Denmark have provided important insights but have generally not disaggregated major and minor amputations, examined centre-level variation or incorporated socioeconomic analyses [12, 13], and are not directly comparable to the UK population given the differences in patient demographics, comorbidity profiles, healthcare systems and access to them [6, 9–11].

In the UK, most evidence comes from single-centre or regional studies, and no prior national study has comprehensively assessed LLA incidence, type-specific trends, centre-level variation and socioeconomic disparities in the dialysis population [5, 9, 10]. This gap limits the ability to monitor trends, identify high-risk groups or address potential inequities in care provision.

High quality Multi-disciplinary Diabetes Foot Team (MDFT) care has been shown to reduce the incidence of LLAs and developing such services has been a key feature of the ‘Diabetes Transformation Fund 2017–2019’ and the ‘2019 NHS Long Term Plan’ in England [13, 14]. However, provision of MDFT care for dialysis patients with diabetes varies considerably across the country and the impact of this on LLA rates is not clear.

We hypothesized that LLA rates in incident dialysis patients and mortality following LLA are high, particularly among those with primary renal disease (PRD) diabetes. Using linked UK Renal Registry and Hospital Episode Statistics data of incident dialysis patients, we aimed to provide national estimates of LLA incidence, survival and socioeconomic disparities, and to investigate inter-centre variation—offering a comprehensive assessment of LLA burden. Accurately quantifying this burden and describing key risk factors are essential first steps towards effective prevention strategies and service development in this area.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We undertook a retrospective analysis of linked UK Renal Registry (UKRR) and Hospital Episode Statistics (HES) data for incident dialysis (haemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis) patients between 2003 and 2017 in England. The UK Renal Registry receives data for all kidney replacement therapy patients in the UK and from the 49 renal centres in England, including quarterly biochemistry results.

Demographic data at dialysis start included age, gender, ethnicity and Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD). Comorbidities included: ischaemic heart disease (IHD), PVD, hypertension, diabetic retinopathy and prior amputation were extracted from the UKRR and HES databases. C-reactive protein and albumin levels were excluded from analyses as the missing data percentage was very high. Data are presented as percentages for categorical variables and median (interquartile range) for continuous variables. The date of the first non-traumatic, non-malignant LLA within 3 years of dialysis initiation was identified for each patient. In our study, minor amputations were defined as those occurring at the level of the toe, foot or ankle, while major amputations were defined as those occurring below or above the knee (refer to the Supplementary data for the OPCS-4 codes used). Three years was chosen because previous work has shown there is an accelerated risk of LLA in first 3 years after dialysis initiation [15]. HES codes were used to identify major and minor LLA amputations and augment comorbidities from UKRR dataset.

Cumulative incidence plots were used to illustrate incidence of amputations in three groups of interest: PRD diabetes, diabetes as a comorbidity (other primary renal disease) and no diabetes. Patients were censored at renal recovery or death. For all three groups, time trends in incidence of any major or minor amputations, and not limited to first amputation, within 3 years of dialysis start were assessed using Poisson regression for the cohort’s incident to dialysis between 2004 and 2016.

To examine risk factors for first LLA, subdistribution hazard ratios (sHRs) were calculated using Fine–Gray competing risk to account for the competing risk of death; explanatory variables included diabetes status, sex, age, ethnicity, IMD, diabetic retinopathy, prior amputation, PVD, IHD, hypertension and dialysis modality from within 90 days of kidney replacement therapy initiation.

Centre-level variation in the amputation rate among people with diabetes within 3 years of dialysis initiation (not limited to first amputation) were compared using funnel plots adjusting for age, sex and diabetes as PRD.

To quantify the impact of LLA on overall mortality in the first year following the initial amputation within each of the three groups, we calculated age- and sex-adjusted standardized mortality rates (SMRs) to account for differences in age and sex distribution between groups. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were also generated to visualize differences in mortality over time across the groups. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4.

Ethics approval

This study was conducted using anonymized data from the UKRR linked to HES. Approval of the use of UKRR data was granted by the UK Renal Registry Data Release Group and complies with the UK Data Protection Act and NHS Information Governance requirements. Ethical approval was not required as this was a secondary analysis of routinely collected data.

RESULTS

Between 2003 and 2017, there were 69 701 incident dialysis patients in England. Of these, 21 061 (30%) had diabetes as PRD, 7973 (11%) had comorbid diabetes but not listed as cause of kidney failure, and 40 667 (58%) did not have a diagnosis of diabetes.

The median ages at dialysis initiation for patients with PRD diabetes, diabetes as a comorbidity and no diabetes cohorts, were 64, 71 and 65 years, respectively (Table 1). Patients were predominantly White (67%–84%), with 22%–29% in the most deprived quintile. The proportion of patients receiving haemodialysis at 90 days varied between the three groups from 76% to 85%, with the diabetes comorbidity cohort having the highest proportion on haemodialysis. There was a high prevalence of hypertension (74%–92%) and IHD (20%–41%). Fifty-three percent of patients with PRD diabetes had diabetic retinopathy compared with 7.6% in the diabetes comorbidity group, possibly reflecting an earlier or milder stage of disease in the latter. Some 15.8% patients with PRD diabetes had a history of PVD and 6.2% had had a prior amputation. For those without diabetes, 5.3% had a history of PVD and 0.3% had had a prior amputation.

A multivariable Fine–Gray competing risks model (Table 3) was used to identify several factors independently associated with an increased risk of LLA, accounting for the competing risk of death. Diabetes as PRD was associated with a 9.3-fold increased risk of amputation, while diabetes as a comorbidity conferred a 3.6-fold increased risk. Among all covariates, PVD and a history of amputation had the highest HRs of 2.11 (95% CI 1.89–2.35) and 2.19 (95% CI 1.91–2.51), respectively. Dialysis modality was not independently associated with LLA risk; however, interpretation is limited by high attrition rates and frequent transfers to haemodialysis during the follow-up period.

The SMR, adjusted for age and sex (Table 4) , within 1 year of a first minor or major LLA was markedly elevated compared with both the general population and the dialysis population. Among patients undergoing either a minor or major amputation, those with diabetes as the PRD had an SMR of 34.7 (95% CI 32.2–37.2) compared with the general population and 4.87 (95% CI 4.52–5.22) versus the dialysis population. Patients with diabetes as a comorbidity also had high mortality, with SMRs of 26.5 (95% CI 21.5–31.5) versus the general population and 5.31 (95% CI 4.30–6.31) versus the dialysis population. In contrast, the ‘no diabetes’ group had comparatively lower, though still elevated, SMRs of 21.7 (95% CI 17.8–25.5) and 4.66 (95% CI 3.84–5.48) versus the general population and the dialysis population, respectively.

When analysis was restricted to major amputations, mortality was substantially higher across all groups. In the PRD diabetes group, the SMR rose to 45.8 (95% CI 41.6–49.9) compared with the general population and 6.36 (95% CI 5.79–6.94) compared with the dialysis population. Patients with diabetes as a comorbidity had similarly elevated SMRs: 40.2 (95% CI 31.2–49.2) versus the general population and 7.46 (95% CI 5.79–9.13) versus the dialysis population. Although the no diabetes cohort had comparatively lower SMRs, rates remained high at 28.8 (95% CI 22.5–35.1) and 5.67 (95% CI 4.43–6.91) compared with the general and dialysis population, respectively.

Kaplan–Meier curves in Fig. 2A show that mortality within 1 year of first minor or major amputation was lowest in PRD diabetes group at 38%, and higher in the no diabetes and diabetes as comorbidity cohorts at 48% and 52%, respectively, with no statistically significant difference between the latter two (Fig. 3). Sensitivity analysis showed that although survival rates declined with increasing age, age-stratified analysis of major amputations showed no statistically significant differences between diabetes groups in the 18–59 and 60–74 years age groups, as shown in Supplementary data, Fig. S2A and B. However, in patients aged ≥75 years (Fig. 2B), survival differed significantly between groups (log-rank P < .001), with the PRD diabetes group showing markedly better survival than the no diabetes and diabetes as comorbidity.

Poisson regression demonstrated no change in incidence of minor amputation rates across calendar years. However, as shown in Fig. 3, there was a yearly reduction in the major amputation rates of 2.5% (95% CI 1.2–3.9) in PRD diabetes group, 5.6% (95% CI 2.1–9.0) in the non-diabetes group and overall, 2.7% (95% CI 1.4–3.9%) in all people with diabetes. DISCUSSION

In this large study of over 67 000 incident dialysis patients in England (2003–17), we show a high burden of LLA in patients who started dialysis due to PRD diabetes, with 9.1% developing a first LLA within 3 years of dialysis initiation. Although minor amputation rates remained unchanged over time, major amputation rates reduced by 5.6% in people without diabetes and 2.7% in people with diabetes since 2003. While factors such as history of PVD (sHR 2.11, 95% CI 1.89–2.35) and previous LLA (sHR 2.19, 95% CI 1.91–2.51) had higher risk of LLA, female sex and non-White ethnicity were associated with a lower risk of LLA. Mortality was high in all groups.

Our findings extend prior evidence. A meta-analysis of 30 non-randomized kidney failure studies by Kaminski et al. found that diabetes was associated with a 7-fold increased risk of amputation, with longer diabetes duration and diabetic retinopathy as key risk factors [8]. Compared with UK general population data reported by Holman et al. (2.51 per 1000 person-years for major amputations in people with diabetes) [16], our study found approximately a 10-fold higher rate of minor amputations and a 26-fold higher rate of major amputations in incident dialysis patients with diabetes. Although these comparisons involve different populations and methodologies, they underscore the elevated risk of LLA in patients with diabetes and kidney failure.

Similar to Franz et al., our study showed that 1-year mortality after first LLA on dialysis was high in all cohorts [11, 12]. Unadjusted mortality shown in Kaplan–Meier curves, was highest in the diabetes as comorbidity group for both minor/major and major amputations, likely reflecting older age and a higher comorbidity burden. In contrast, the PRD diabetes group had better survival than those without diabetes for both minor/major and major amputations, consistent with findings by Tamaki et al. [17]. This is possibly a result of intensive and frequent follow-up including MDFT, which is easily accessible to diabetic patients. When compared with the general population, the PRD diabetes group had the highest age- and sex-adjusted SMR post-LLA, reflecting the combined burden of early-onset diabetes, severe vascular disease and kidney failure. However, when compared with the dialysis population, the diabetes as comorbidity group had a higher SMR post-LLA, possibly due to heavier comorbidity burden which led to LLA (as not explained by the diabetes only) and reference group effects, whereby a greater representation of PRD diabetes group in the comparator population (21061 vs 7973 in diabetes as comorbidity) lowers expected mortality rates [18]. Survivor bias may also contribute, where individuals with diabetes surviving long enough to reach dialysis and subsequently undergo amputation may represent a relatively healthier set of patients with good healthcare engagement compared with non-diabetics on dialysis who require LLA. Additionally, potential misclassification of PRD diabetes and diabetes as comorbidity could have influenced the observed patterns.

Despite the slight differences, both diabetes subgroups showed consistently elevated mortality. This aligns with previous studies showing poor survival among dialysis patients with diabetes, likely due to high prevalence of comorbidities, particularly cardiovascular disease [19, 20]. These subgroup distinctions in incidence and mortality are clinically relevant as they may guide risk stratification and tailored management approaches.

The LLA rate was lower in non-White ethnicities in this study. This is consistent with previous UK studies which demonstrated that people with diabetes of South Asian and Afro-Caribbean ethnicity had lower rates of foot ulceration, even when controlling for relevant risk factors such as diabetes status and peripheral neuropathy [21, 22]. This may indicate an alternative aetiopathogenesis of diabetic foot disease, and understanding these protective factors could inform preventive strategies for all ethnic groups.

Franz et al. reported a decline in overall amputation rates in US dialysis patients, whereas our study observed reductions only in major amputation rates [11]. In England, Maheswaran et al. found falling rates in peripheral arterial disease between 2006 and 2018, with only minimal change in those without diabetes [23]. The improved major LLA rates over time reported in our study also align with the decreasing national rates as shown by Meffen et al. [24], possibly reflecting earlier recognition, routine foot checks during dialysis, aggressive risk factor management and possibly timelier revascularization/amputation pathways [13, 24, 25]. Collectively, this suggests that national strategies are translating into improved outcomes—particularly for major amputations.

We observed marked centre-level variation with adjusted rates ranging from 2.01 to 11.71 per 100 patient-years, and more than a quarter of centres in the study above the 95% confidence limit. This variation persists even when adjusted for ethnicity (please refer to Supplementary data, Fig. S1), highlighting the importance of region-specific evidence to inform preventive strategies and service improvement in the UK. These variations may also partly explain the unchanged minor amputation rates.

A key strength of this study is the use of linked UKRR and HES data, enabling analysis of a nationally representative cohort over 14 years with precise identification of LLAs, diabetes status and comorbidities, and robust follow-up for mortality. Importantly, we separated diabetes as PRD from diabetes as a comorbidity—groups often combined in prior studies—revealing marked differences in LLA incidence and post-amputation survival. These findings support more accurate risk stratification and tailored prevention. Unlike analyses from the Netherlands and Denmark, we disaggregated major and minor amputations, quantified temporal trends, and examined inter-centre variation using age-, sex- and diabetes-adjusted funnel plots—revealing persistent differences in amputation rates that are not explained by case mix.

By incorporating socioeconomic deprivation and ethnicity, we provided a granular analysis to help target specific populations. Furthermore, use of Fine–Gray competing risk models, Kaplan–Meier curves and SMRs enabled comparisons both within the dialysis population and against the general population, making this the most comprehensive national assessment of amputation burden, variation and prognosis in dialysis patients, generating insights directly applicable to clinical care and quality improvement initiatives.

Our study lacked data on glycaemic control, smoking and unit-level foot care practices, which may have influenced observed patterns. Coding variation and completeness, particularly for minor amputations, may vary across centres. Important patient-centred outcomes like quality of life and functional independence were not captured. The studies that have explored this have shown longer length of stay, lower quality of life scores and reduced functional independence when comparing those with and without kidney failure [2, 4, 26, 27]. Finally, our study includes data up to 2016; recent reports indicate that amputation rates have increased in the years following the COVID-19 pandemic, after an initial decline [28, 29]. Ongoing surveillance is therefore essential to understand emerging trends and their drivers.

In conclusion, we show a high cumulative incidence of LLA within 3 years of dialysis initiation, particularly those with PRD diabetes, and a very high 1-year mortality across all groups with unchanged minor amputation rates and substantial inter-centre variation. There is potential to reduce the amputation rates further by standardizing practice across centres in the UK through national quality improvement initiatives, developing multidisciplinary foot care services and implementing proven preventive strategies like routine foot screening in dialysis units. Further research is needed to evaluate the impact of glycaemic control, pre-dialysis foot care and post-LLA quality of life on patient outcomes. These targeted efforts could improve the overall care of dialysis patients at high risk for LLA (Fig. 5, Table 4).
