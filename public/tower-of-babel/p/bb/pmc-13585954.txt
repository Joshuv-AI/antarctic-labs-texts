Tandem column coupling in supercritical fluid chromatography-mass spectrometry for the multiresidue analysis of pesticides in complex Allium vegetables
Authors: Víctor Cutillas, Alba Cano, María Jesús Martínez-Bueno, Amadeo R. Fernández-Alba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Supercritical fluid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (SFC-MS/MS) has established itself as a powerful, environmentally sustainable alternative to conventional liquid chromatography for the multiresidue analysis of pesticides in food matrices. While single-column SFC-MS/MS configurations based on C18 stationary phases have demonstrated clear analytical advantages, their performance can be severely compromised in difficult matrices such as Allium vegetables, where dense co-extractive backgrounds cause significant ion suppression and limit method sensitivity. The serial coupling of columns with complementary selectivities is an inherently SFC-compatible strategy to improve chromatographic resolution without the pressure constraints that restrict analogous approaches in liquid chromatography. In this study, two tandem column configurations were evaluated for the SFC-MS/MS determination of 216 pesticides in onion, leek, and garlic: a 15 cm Silica column coupled upstream to a 25 cm C18 column, and a 15 cm Fluorophenyl column coupled to the same C18 column. Method performance was assessed by comparing limits of quantification (LOQ), matrix effects (ME), and reproducibility (RSD) against a reference single-C18 configuration across the three matrices. The assessment of method performance revealed that both tandem configurations effectively mitigated matrix effects relative to the single C18 setup, though with distinct trade-offs. The Fluorophenyl + C18 coupling provided a meaningful reduction in signal suppression across all matrices but this came at the cost of deteriorated reproducibility and a compound-dependent effect on sensitivity (in garlic it improved the LOQ of 27 compounds while worsening 23). Conversely, the Silica + C18 configuration proved to be the most balanced and robust strategy. It dramatically improved matrix effect mitigation (up to 78.7% of compounds showing negligible effects in onion), lowered LOQs for 38 compounds in garlic, and successfully preserved method precision by reducing the fraction of compounds with highly variable RSDs (> 20%) from 7.4% to just 1.4% in garlic. Introduction

Supercritical fluid chromatography (SFC) coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) has emerged as a highly efficient and environmentally friendly alternative to conventional liquid chromatography (LC-MS/MS) for the rapid analysis of small molecules [1]. By utilizing supercritical carbon dioxide as the primary mobile phase, SFC significantly reduces organic solvent consumption and analysis time while providing superior chromatographic resolving power and sharper peak shapes. In routine high-throughput bioanalysis, SFC-MS/MS has demonstrated exceptional sensitivity and success rates comparable to gold-standard reversed-phase LC-MS/MS methods [2]. The economic and environmental sustainability of SFC also makes it highly attractive for agricultural and food sciences [3, 4].

When dealing with complex samples, SFC combined with advanced mass spectrometry provides notable advantages regarding sensitivity and the mitigation of matrix effects. In comprehensive comparative studies of multiresidue pesticide analysis in food matrices, SFC-MS/MS consistently outperformed reverse-phase LC by providing enhanced ionization efficiency in the electrospray source and reducing the signal suppression caused by natural co-extracted compounds [5]. Furthermore, novel hyphenations such as SFC coupled with ion mobility quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (IM-Q-TOF/MS) have pushed the boundaries of analytical limits even further, allowing for the green and precise detection of multiple pesticide species in yam and potato at extremely low detection limits [6].

The critical role of the stationary phase in SFC cannot be overstated, as retention mechanisms are highly sensitive to both the chemistry of the column and the mobile phase modifiers. Recent comprehensive evaluations using machine learning models across 15 different stationary phases have demonstrated that analyte retention behavior is governed by complex, multifaceted interactions [7]. Understanding and optimizing these interactions is essential for achieving the desired selectivity in complex food and biological samples [8]. When single-column separations reach their peak capacity limits in extraordinarily complex matrices, multidimensional chromatography offers a powerful solution to further increase resolution [9, 10].

The rational selection of stationary phases in SFC has been placed on a quantitative footing through the linear solvation energy relationship (LSER), or solvation-parameter model, which decomposes retention into defined molecular interactions and allows phases to be classified and compared on a common basis, most usefully through the so-called spider-diagram representation [11, 12]. Complementary studies of the retention mechanism on packed columns and of solute interaction descriptors have clarified how polar, apolar, and aromatic surfaces contribute to selectivity [13, 14]. These frameworks underpin the present choice of a polar silica phase and an aromatic fluorophenyl phase as orthogonal complements to a C18 column.

However, two-dimensional configurations can be instrumentally complex to optimize, and similar advantages in separation efficiency and peak capacity can often be achieved much more simply by coupling columns in series. Unlike conventional liquid chromatography, which is severely restricted by the high pressure drops generated across extended column lengths, SFC is ideally suited for tandem configurations. The carbon dioxide-based mobile phase in SFC exhibits low viscosity and high diffusivity, allowing multiple columns to be serially connected while maintaining high flow rates [15]. For instance, analysts can connect two, three, or even four columns in series to dramatically increase theoretical plate numbers without exceeding the pressure limits of standard instruments [16]. By strategically combining stationary phases with orthogonal or complementary selectivities, this approach creates unique separation environments capable of resolving highly complex mixtures [17, 18]. These predictive strategies have been successfully applied to optimize the separation of challenging stereoisomers, proving that the precise tuning of column combination, order, and back-pressure can achieve complete baseline resolution where single columns inevitably fail [19].

Serial coupling of complementary stationary phases is a well-established route to tune selectivity in SFC, where the low viscosity of CO2-based mobile phases makes extended column beds practical. The selectivity of coupled phases combines as a sequential add-up rather than a simple average, and this has been exploited for systematic method development and non-target screening [20, 21], while automated method-scouting platforms for selecting columns and conditions have been available for several years [22, 23]. Building on this established methodology, the novelty of the present work lies not in the coupling concept itself but in its targeted application to the mitigation of severe matrix effects in complex Allium vegetables.

Building upon our previous foundational work that established the advantages of SFC-MS/MS over LC-MS/MS in terms of sensitivity and reduced matrix interference, this study explores the untapped potential of tandem column coupling to further push the boundaries of analytical performance. While the serial coupling of columns has proven highly effective for resolving complex chiral mixtures and expanding peak capacity, its specific impact on mitigating severe matrix effects in challenging food analysis remains largely unexplored. To address this, the present work evaluates the analytical improvements, specifically regarding sensitivity and the reduction of matrix effects, achieved by strategically coupling two distinctly different stationary phases in SFC-MS/MS. Rather than relying solely on a conventional single C18 setup, we investigate the novel orthogonal combinations of a 15 cm Silica column (which offers strong polar/hydrogen-bonding interactions) coupled upstream to the usual 25 cm C18 column, as well as a 15 cm Fluorophenyl column (which offers π-π and strong dipole interactions) coupled to the same C18 column. By applying these targeted column configurations to difficult fruit and vegetables matrices (onion, leek, and garlic) this research aims to demonstrate how tandem column coupling in SFC-MS/MS can effectively overcome residual matrix interferences and enhance overall sensitivity.

Material and methods

Reagents and materials

The standards of pesticides included in the multiresidue method of study were provided by LGC (Teddington, United Kingdom) and Dr. Ehrenstorfer (Augsburg, Germany). The analytical standards were stored at −30°C. The standard-mix solution was prepared using individual stock solutions. Individual stock solutions (800–1000 mg/L) were prepared from each standard and stored in the dark at −40°C in amber glass vials.

Acetone and ethyl acetate were obtained from Fluka Analytical (Steinheim, Germany). The salts employed in the QuEChERS extraction (anhydrous magnesium sulfate, sodium chloride, sodium hydrogenocitrate sesquihydrate, and sodium citrate tribasic dihydrate) were supplied by Sigma-Aldrich (Steinheim, Germany) except for PSA that was obtained from Supelco (Bellefonte, PA, USA). Ultra-gradient HPLC-grade acetonitrile was obtained from Honeywell (Charlotte, NC, USA).

Sample preparation

The three matrices (onion, leek, and garlic) used in this study were sourced from a local market in Almería, Spain. These samples were analyzed to ensure that these matrices were free from detectable pesticide residues that could interfere with the study, a Citrate-buffered QuEChERS extraction method with dispersive solid-phase extraction (dSPE) clean-up was employed [24]. The resulting extracts, containing 1 g of matrix per mL, were analyzed. Matrix-matched vials were prepared by evaporating 100 µL of each blank extract under a gentle stream of nitrogen and reconstituted with the same volume of acetonitrile containing the mixture of the analyzed pesticides at the desired concentration.

Supercritical fluid chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry

The SFC analysis was performed using a Nexera UC (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). In addition to the standard devices of liquid chromatography (binary pumps, column oven, and autosampler), this system was equipped with a CO2 pump and a back-pressure regulator (BPR) splitless device just before the MS source.

Methanol with 1 mM Ammonium formate was used as a co-solvent (modifier) and mixed with the carbon dioxide during the gradient. The make-up solvent used after the column was methanol with no additives. The make-up solvent was introduced in the system isocratically at 0.080 mL/min. The analytes separation was carried out on three different columns: Shimadzu C18 stationary phase column Shimpack UC-X RP (3 μm, 250 × 2.1 mm), Restek Fluorophenyl Raptor column (2.7 μm, 150 × 2.1 mm) and Restek Allure Silica column (5 μm, 150 × 2.1 mm). The oven temperature was set at 40°C. The BPR pressure and temperature were established at 150 bar and 50 °C, respectively. The total flow used was kept constant at 1.3 mL/min (1 column experiment) and 1.5 mL/min (2 column experiments) and 2μL was the injection volume. The gradient used during the analysis was as follows: an isocratic flow of 2% of modifier was kept for 2 min; the modifier percentage increased linearly to 4% at min 4 and was increased to 8% at minute 8. After 1 min, the modifier percentage increased to 20%, and this condition was kept for 2 min. The modifier percentage was then reduced from 20 to 2% to recover initial conditions and maintained over 3 min.

The SFC chromatograph was coupled to a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer 8060 (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). The study was carried out employing an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. The interface temperature was set at 300 °C, the desolvation line (DL) was set at 200 °C and the heat block was set at 400 °C. The interface voltage used was 4 kV. Regarding nebulizer, heating, and drying gas flows: 3 L/min, 10 L/min and 10 L/min were used, respectively.

Optimization of the MS/MS parameters

The Shimadzu Extended multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) Library was used for the creation of the multiresidue method. Only the top three transitions per pesticide were chosen from the numerous transitions available based on their sensitivity ranking. To confirm the selected transitions, individual standard solutions of the pesticides were injected. The transition with the highest signal (quantifier) and the second most sensitive transition (qualifier) were selected for each pesticide.

For accurate identification, the guidelines established by the SANTE Document No. 11312/2021 were employed: two transitions must be detected with an ion ratio difference less than 30% and a retention time drift below ± 0.1 min from the average of the calibration standards [25]. Acquisition windows of ± 0.4 min were established for each pesticide. The 216 pesticides of the multiresidue method and their MS parameters appear in the supplementary table. The retention times are also described in the table.

Results and discussion

Optimization of tandem column configuration and flow dynamics

To enhance the separation of target analytes from the complex co-extractives of onion, leek, and garlic, we evaluated the serial coupling of two distinct stationary phases: a 15 cm Silica column coupled to a 25 cm C18 column, and a 15 cm Fluorophenyl column coupled to the same C18 column. Method development in tandem SFC requires careful consideration of fluid dynamics, as supercritical fluids are compressible; extending the total stationary phase to 40 cm increases the internal pressure and mobile phase density, which in turn alters the eluotropic strength and analyte retention [15].

The overall selectivity of a tandem system is a retention time weighted average of the combined columns, but the specific order of the columns (upstream versus downstream) heavily dictates retention times and resolution due to varying pressure drops across the coupled segments [18]. Specifically, the shorter, highly selective columns (Silica and Fluorophenyl) were intentionally positioned upstream of the C18 column. In this configuration, the upstream column experiences a significantly higher internal pressure because it bears the additive pressure drop of the downstream column. This higher pressure compresses the supercritical solvent, enhancing its solvation power and proportionally reducing solute retention on that specific upstream segment. This phenomenon is highly advantageous: it allows us to exploit the strong orthogonal interactions of the Silica or Fluorophenyl phases (such as normal-phase polarity or π–π interactions) to selectively delay the polar or aromatic co-extractives, while the denser mobile phase ensures these matrix components do not become irreversibly trapped or excessively broadened. By acting as a chemical pre-filter, the upstream column effectively fractionates the complex Allium matrix, preventing the downstream C18 column from becoming overloaded and drastically reducing ion suppression in the mass spectrometer. Concurrently, the downstream 25 cm C18 column is freed up to dictate the primary reversed-phase separation and provide the bulk of the method’s peak capacity.

The practical benefits of this tandem configuration were clearly evident in the chromatographic performance of several target analytes. Compounds such as acetamiprid, butoxycarboxim, carbendazim, ethirimol, imazalil, nitenpyram, and tricyclazole clearly benefited from the use of two columns, exhibiting significantly improved peak shapes compared to a single-column setup. While many other analytes experienced slight enhancements, the most striking improvements were observed for these specific compounds. As an illustrative example, Fig. 1 displays the chromatograms of acetamiprid across the different tested column configurations. Beyond stationary phase selection, the flow dynamics were further optimized to maintain high analytical throughput. Initially, an attempt was made to adapt the gradient specifically for the new 40 cm combined column configuration while maintaining the original flow rate of 1.3 mL/min; however, this extended the method run time to an undesirable 20 min. Rather than making major modifications to the gradient profile to compensate, the gradient was kept largely the same as the original method, and the flow rate was instead increased to 1.5 mL/min. In traditional liquid chromatography, such a flow increase on an extended column bed would induce severe back-pressure constraints and a detrimental loss of theoretical plates due to mass transfer limitations. However, the remarkably low viscosity and high diffusivity of the supercritical carbon dioxide mobile phase allow for elevated linear velocities with minimal impact on column efficiency or system pressure limits [16]. This was confirmed by the system inlet pressures recorded from the instrument logs at the start of the gradient (2% modifier, isocratic conditions). With the BPR set-point fixed at 150 bar for all configurations, the column pressure drop (inlet pressure minus BPR set-point) was 128 bar for the single C18 column at 1.3 mL/min. At the higher flow rate of 1.5 mL/min, the coupled configurations produced column pressure drops of 190 bar (Silica + C18) and 265 bar (FP + C18); accounting for the flow-rate difference, the 15 cm upstream column contributed approximately 42 bar (Silica) and 117 bar (FP) of additional resistance. All values remained well within the instrument’s operational limits, confirming that serial column coupling in SFC does not impose the back-pressure penalty that an equivalent column extension would in HPLC. Notably, the substantially higher resistance of the FP column compared to Silica is consistent with differences in surface chemistry between the two stationary phases. By leveraging these unique mobile phase properties, this simple flow rate adjustment allowed us to utilize the familiar gradient profile while bringing the total analysis time to a highly efficient 15 min, demonstrating that tandem SFC can dramatically enhance resolving power and minimize matrix effects without sacrificing rapid analysis times.

Retention behavior and orthogonality of the coupled phases

To characterize the retention mechanism introduced by each coupling, the retention times of all 216 pesticides (Supplementary Table S1) were compared with those obtained on the single C18 column (Fig. 2). Regression of tandem against single-column retention shows that the silica pre-column behaves predominantly as an additive, orthogonal polar-retention layer: retention on Silica + C18 scaled with single-C18 retention with a slope of 1.03 and an offset of + 0.97 min, that is a near-uniform delay (mean ΔRT = + 1.06 min) on which large, selective increments are superimposed for polar and hydrogen-bond-donor analytes (for example fenpropidin + 4.7 min, butoxycarboxim + 3.5 min and oxadixyl + 2.6 min). In contrast, retention on FP + C18 lay close to the single-column line (slope 0.98, offset + 0.20 min; mean ΔRT = + 0.15 min) but with a genuinely reordered subset: 67 of the 216 compounds, predominantly π-rich species, benzoylureas (diflubenzuron, flufenoxuron and chlorfluazuron) and fungicides such as famoxadone and fenamidone, eluted earlier than on the single C18 column, reflecting altered π-π and dipole interactions. The two couplings can be ranked quantitatively using orthogonality indices computed from the retention data: relative to the single C18 column, the Pearson R2 was 0.917 (Silica + C18) and 0.939 (FP + C18), and the Kendall rank-correlation coefficient τ was 0.816 and 0.920, respectively, so the silica coupling is the more orthogonal of the two phases (orthogonality 1-τ = 0.18 versus 0.08). Consistently, the silica coupling reordered the elution sequence of 9.2% of all compound pairs against 4.0% for the fluorophenyl coupling. The pronounced and systematic delay produced by the silica phase shifts the target analytes away from the dense, early-eluting polar co-extractives of the Allium matrices, which is consistent with the superior matrix effect mitigation and sensitivity reported in the following sections.

Sensitivity

The instrumental limits of quantification (LOQs) distribution across the 216 evaluated pesticides, as compiled in Table 1, shows that onion consistently gave the best (lowest) LOQs of the three matrices, irrespective of the column configuration employed. Instrumental LOQs were defined as the lowest calibration level meeting the SANTE identification criteria for ion ratio and retention time drift. In onion, approximately 97% of compounds achieved LOQ = 2 µg/kg with all three configurations (96.8% for C18 and Silica + C18, rising marginally to 97.7% for FP + C18), reflecting the relatively simpler chemical background of this matrix. In contrast, leek and garlic imposed substantially greater analytical challenges, with 85.6% and 77.4% of compounds reaching the lowest LOQ when using the single C18 column, respectively. This gradient in matrix complexity is consistent with the known richness in organosulfur compounds and other co-extractable matrix constituents present in Allium species with stronger flavor profiles, which can suppress ionization and increase chromatographic interferences at trace analyte concentrations. The addition of a secondary stationary phase prior to the C18 column produced markedly different outcomes depending on the chemistry of the coupling. The Silica + C18 configuration delivered the most consistent sensitivity improvements across the two more complex matrices, reducing the number of compounds with LOQ = 5 µg/kg from 28 to 12 in leek and from 38 to 10 in garlic, while simultaneously cutting the count of compounds with LOQ ≥ 10 µg/kg from 11 to 6 in garlic. Globally, the Silica + C18 coupling improved LOQ for 38 compounds in garlic and 27 in leek relative to the single C18 column, whereas worsening was observed in only 8 and 11 compounds, respectively. As evidenced by the retention behaviors detailed in the section, the Silica coupling induced a substantial and consistent overall delay in analyte elution times. This systematic shift likely facilitated a more effective spatial separation of the target pesticides from the dense, early-eluting polar co-extractives characteristic of Allium vegetables, thereby minimizing competitive ionization and maximizing method sensitivity. The FP + C18 coupling, while also providing benefits in leek (24 compounds improved), showed a more ambivalent behavior in garlic, where it improved 27 compounds but simultaneously worsened 23 others and resulted in the highest count of LOQ ≥ 10 µg/kg entries (17 compounds), suggesting that the fluorophenyl phase can introduce additional matrix-related interactions for certain compound classes when the matrix is particularly complex.

Notably, five compounds, pendimethalin, malathion, cyhalofop-butyl, kresoxim-methyl, and dichlorvos, exhibited elevated LOQs (≥ 5 µg/kg) across all three matrices when using the single C18 column, identifying them as intrinsically challenging analytes regardless of the sample type. For this group, the Silica + C18 coupling proved most effective: dichlorvos, kresoxim-methyl, and cyhalofop-butyl all dropped to LOQ = 2 µg/kg in onion, leek, and garlic with this configuration. Malathion and pendimethalin, however, remained problematic even after coupling. Additional compounds such as prothiophos, tau-fluvalinate, pyridalyl, and propiconazole were consistently among the worst performers specifically in garlic, with LOQs equal or above 10 µg/kg across configurations, probably their physicochemical properties (high lipophilicity, structural rigidity) favour co-elution with garlic-specific matrix components.

Evaluation of matrix effects

The evaluation of matrix effects (ME) highlighted notable signal alterations across the analyzed food samples. Matrix effects were evaluated by comparing the slopes of matrix-matched calibration curves against those prepared in solvent, as detailed elsewhere [26]. While these effects can include both signal enhancement and suppression, they were predominantly characterized by signal suppression, with complexity varying considerably depending on the vegetable extract. Garlic proved to be the most challenging matrix, where 53.2% of the evaluated compounds suffered from significant signal suppression (ME values < −50%) when analyzed using a single C18 column. This severe suppression is directly attributable to the complex biochemical profile of crushed garlic, which rapidly generates a dense background of polar sulfinic acids and thiosulfinates [27]. Leek extract was comparatively less complex, though still problematic, resulting in strong signal suppression for 30% of the analytes on the standalone C18 column. However, in the onion matrix, only three compounds exhibited severe overall matrix effects. Consequently, the percentage of compounds exhibiting negligible or acceptable matrix effects (−20% to 20%) was limited to only 16.2% in garlic, 25.0% in leek, and 52.7% in onion when relying solely on the single C18 stationary phase.

The implementation of tandem column configurations successfully mitigated these pronounced matrix effects, as illustrated by the distribution of ME percentages per matrix and experiment (Fig. 3). Coupling a Fluorophenyl (FP) column to the C18 stationary phase provided moderate improvements, increasing the proportion of compounds with acceptable ME to 22.7% in garlic, 34.3% in leek, and 58.8% in onion. However, the 15 cm Silica + 25 cm C18 combination demonstrated the most superior performance across all matrices. This orthogonal coupling significantly minimized co-elution with matrix interferences, boosting the share of compounds with negligible ME to 29.6% in garlic, 40.7% in leek, and an impressive 78.7% in onion extracts. While serially coupled columns successfully mitigated matrix effects for the majority of analytes, a small subset of compounds remained highly susceptible to signal suppression regardless of the column configuration employed. Compounds such as chlorfluazuron, boscalid, epoxiconazole, and penthiopyrad suffered from persistent suppression averaging greater than −40%. Furthermore, in the highly complex garlic extract, specific analytes like tau-fluvalinate, fenpropathrin, and etofenprox presented extreme signal suppression greater than −90%, even when analyzed with the optimal Silica + C18 tandem system. It is worth noting that many of the analytes exhibiting this extreme suppression are highly lipophilic and classically considered more amenable to gas chromatography (GC) analysis due to their inherently poor ionization efficiency in electrospray sources. For this group of compounds, chromatographic coupling alone is insufficient to overcome the suppression; the use of compound-specific isotopically labelled internal standards or more selective clean-up strategies would be advisable. Routine quantification in this study relied on matrix-matched calibration throughout, in line with SANTE 11312/2021 [25].

Reproducibility

Table 2 shows that reproducibility, measured as the relative standard deviation (RSD), remained generally strong for the single C18 and especially for the Silica + C18 configuration, whereas the FP + C18 coupling consistently produced a broader dispersion of RSD values across the three matrices. For onion, the percentage of compounds with RSDs in the 0–10% range was almost identical for C18 and Silica + C18 (88.0% and 87.0%), but the fraction with RSDs above 20% dropped from 2.8% to 0.5% when silica was coupled upstream, indicating a small gain in robustness. A similar pattern was observed in leek and garlic, where Silica-C18 reduced the proportion of compounds with RSD > 20% from 6.0% to 1.4% and from 7.4% to 1.4%, respectively, despite only a modest decrease in the 0–10% class relative to C18. This suggests that the main benefit of the silica coupling was not necessarily to increase the number of very low-RSD measurements, but rather to suppress the tail of poorly reproducible analytes. In contrast, the FP + C18 arrangement showed a clear deterioration in reproducibility, most notably in garlic, where only 47.7% of compounds remained within the 0–10% RSD interval and 47.2% fell in the 10–20% range. The same trend was already visible in onion and leek, with FP + C18 lowering the proportion of compounds in the best reproducibility category to 69.0% and 65.7%, respectively, compared with 88.0% and 79.2% for the single C18 method. Overall, the data indicate that column coupling does not automatically improve method precision; rather, its effect depends strongly on the selectivity introduced by the first stationary phase. From a discussion standpoint, these results support Silica + C18 as the most balanced strategy for complex matrices such as onion, leek, and garlic, because it preserves the good reproducibility of the original C18 method while markedly reducing the proportion of compounds with poor repeatability.

Real samples

To evaluate the applicability of the optimized method in routine monitoring, 20 commercial samples including 15 onions, 4 garlics, and 1 leek were analyzed. These analyses were performed exclusively with the Silica-C18 configuration, as it demonstrated the best balance of sensitivity, reproducibility, and matrix effect mitigation during method development. As can be observed in Table 3, the quantitative results for the positive detections reveal the presence of 13 different pesticide residues across the tested samples. The concentrations of all detected pesticides were remarkably low, ranging from 2.0 to 7.3 µg/kg, demonstrating the excellent trace-level detection capability of the tandem column setup in highly complex food matrices. Conclusion

This work demonstrates that the serial coupling of orthogonally selective stationary phases in SFC-MS/MS offers a practical route to overcoming severe matrix interferences in complex Allium extracts. By strategically positioning the Silica or Fluorophenyl columns upstream of the primary C18 column, the tandem system successfully fractionates matrix components. Because the low viscosity of the supercritical mobile phase prevents the mass transfer limitations typical of liquid chromatography, the flow rate could be adjusted to 1.5 mL/min, ensuring the enhanced resolving power of the tandem system was achieved without sacrificing the rapid 15-min analysis time.

While both evaluated tandem configurations successfully reduced average matrix effects compared to a standalone C18 column, they differed substantially in their overall analytical performance. The Fluorophenyl + C18 arrangement successfully enhanced ionization quality and reduced overall signal suppression, but ultimately resulted in poorer reproducibility, particularly in the highly complex garlic matrix. The Silica + C18 coupling, however, delivered simultaneous improvements in sensitivity, matrix effect mitigation, and reproducibility, establishing it as the most reliable strategy for routine multi-residue pesticide analysis in challenging foods. Nonetheless, a subset of highly lipophilic analytes continued to exhibit extreme signal suppression (> 90%) across all configurations, indicating that while column coupling is a powerful tool for chromatographic optimization, it must remain complementary to rigorous sample clean-up or procedural matrix calibration for the most severely affected compounds.
