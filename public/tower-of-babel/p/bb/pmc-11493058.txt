Photocatalysis of Adsorbed Catechol on Degussa P25 TiO2 at the Air–Solid Interface
Authors: Md Ariful Hoque, Josiel Barrios Cossio, Marcelo I. Guzman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Semiconductor photocatalysis with commercial TiO2 (Degussa P25) has shown significant potential in water treatment of organic pollutants. However, the photoinduced reactions of adsorbed catechol, a phenolic air pollutant from biomass burning and combustion emissions, at the air–solid interface of TiO2 remain unexplored. Herein we examine the photocatalytic decay of catechol in the presence of water vapor, which acts as an electron acceptor. Experiments under variable cut-off wavelengths of irradiation (λcut-off ≥ 320, 400, and 515 nm) distinguish the mechanistic contribution of a ligand-to-metal charge-transfer (LMCT) complex of surface chemisorbed catechol on TiO2. The LMCT complex injects electrons into the conduction band of TiO2 from the highest occupied molecular orbital of catechol by visible light (≥2.11 eV) excitation. The deconvolution of diffuse reflectance UV–visible spectral bands from LMCT complexes of TiO2 with catechol, o-semiquinone radical, and quinone and the quantification of the evolving gaseous products follow a consecutive kinetic model. CO2(g) and CO(g) final oxidation products are monitored by gas chromatography and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy. The apparent quantum efficiency at variable λcut-off are determined for reactant loss (Φ−TiO2/catechol = 0.79 ± 0.19) and product growth ΦCO2 = 0.76 ± 0.08). Spectroscopic and electrochemical measurements reveal the energy band diagram for the LMCT of TiO2/catechol. Two photocatalytic mechanisms are analyzed based on chemical transformations and environmental relevance.

Introduction

Natural and anthropogenic processes such as wildfires, agro-industrial burns, and fossil fuel combustion emit large quantities of pollutants, among which are abundant phenolic compounds, with detrimental effects on air quality, visibility, public health, and climate.1−7 Increasing levels of phenolic compounds in air are a potential environmental concern due to their participation in tropospheric ozone production and secondary organic aerosol (SOA) formation.8,9 Recently, the atmospheric fate of phenolic compounds has attracted significant attention from a chemical perspective. Phenolic compounds present on the surface of SOA can undergo direct photolysis, and oxidation reactions with hydroxyl radicals (HO•), nitrate radicals (NO3•), and ozone (O3), which lead to their transformation during atmospheric transport and ulterior removal.10−14 Furthermore, an average of 19 (±1) Tg of mineral dust (made of metal oxides, clays, and carbonates) is suspended in the atmosphere at any given time.15,16 The adsorption and processing of other atmospheric pollutants (e.g., NO) on the surface of widespread semiconductor oxides (e.g., TiO2) present in mineral dust has been demonstrated.17 Therefore, phenolic compounds adsorbed on the surface of such semiconductor oxides18,19 should also be chemically altered and possibly efficiently removed under sunlight irradiation in the atmosphere.

Among all metal oxide semiconductors, titanium dioxide (TiO2) is the most widely studied photocatalyst due to its large photostability, low price, and widespread abundance.9,20−22 As a consequence of the action of winds over desert regions and open-pit mining operations (for industrial production of nanomaterials and goods), there is a continuous supply of minerals containing TiO2 to the atmosphere.9,23,24 Other less significant environmental sources of TiO2 include wood combustion, vegetative detritus, food, and personal care products.24,25 Given its structure and electronic properties, TiO2 can easily adsorb pollutants from air such as phenolic compounds, and facilitate their interfacial photoprocessing.26,27 Thus, it is important to first understand the surface interaction of phenolic compounds with TiO2 to shed light on a variety of physicochemical processes experienced by particulate matter during atmospheric transport. Among these processes, the surface of minerals containing TiO2 can be altered, affecting the optical properties of particles absorbing in the visible range of the solar spectrum.28−31

Catechol (1,2-dihydroxybenzene), one of the most abundant phenolic compounds found in the atmosphere, behaves as a binding ligand to TiO2.32−34 The chelation of surface hydroxyl groups (HO−) of TiO2 by the two vicinal HO– in C1 and C2 of catechol with the loss of water forms a TiO2/catechol charge-transfer complex. The electronic behavior of the TiO2/catechol (TiO2/QH2) charge transfer complex is depicted by a ligand-to-metal charge-transfer transition (LMCT) in Scheme 1.35 Catechol’s optimum geometry to chelate a surface Ti atom involves the formation of a five-membered-ring coordination complex, which retains the octahedral geometry of hexa-coordinated surface Ti atoms in TiO2.32,36 This binding favors the charge-transfer process across the TiO2/catechol interface,36 which creates a visible absorbing LMCT band.37 The LMCT absorbing in the visible can find applications in the fields of optical sensing devices, optoelectronics, solar cells, environmental photocatalysis, and biotechnology.38

Previous photocatalytic work for the reaction of catechol adsorbed on TiO2 was performed in aqueous media,39−41 except a study that probed the degradation based on constrained observations made exclusively by solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy.32 Indeed, many questions remain unanswered to fully understand this system at the air–solid interface and under atmospheric relevant conditions. Herein, we tackle this challenge by preparing a solid film of the TiO2/catechol complex, which was designed to contain a monolayer coverage of the ligand. The photocatalysis experiments presented below expose the film to UV–visible light under 1 atm air saturated with water vapor. Different cut-off wavelengths of irradiation at λcut-off ≥ 320, 400, and 515 nm are used to distinguish the direct contribution of UV and visible light to the mechanism of excitation of TiO2 and the LMCT band, respectively. The binding of TiO2/catechol is spectroscopically monitored during irradiation experiments as well as the volatile products evolved. The new kinetic information for reactant decay and product formation enabled the first determination of the apparent quantum efficiency of this reaction at the air–solid interface.

Experimental Section

Preparation of TiO2 with a Monolayer Coverage of Catechol

Ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ cm, ELGA PURELAB flex, Veolia) was used in all experiments. Commercial grade titanium(IV) oxide, TiO2 (Degussa Aeroxide P25, Across Organics, 99.0%), and catechol (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.9%), were used as received. The selected Degussa P25 TiO2 is made of 75% anatase and 25% rutile,42 has a point of zero charge 6.5,43 and is widely recognized as a reference material in environmental atmospheric chemistry laboratory studies.9 A stock suspension of 60.0 mg TiO2 in 500.0 μL water was freshly prepared in a 1.7 mL polypropylene microcentrifuge tube (PGC Scientifics) and sonicated (Branson Ultrasonic 3510) for 2 min. Five hundred microliters of freshly prepared 10.0 mM catechol solution was added to the stock suspension to ensure a monolayer coverage in the working suspension of TiO2.44 The working suspension was allowed to equilibrate for 1 h to reach adsorption–desorption equilibrium. The stoichiometric chemisorption for this monolayer coverage has been conclusively explained in terms of the multisite surface complexation model.44

Photocatalytic Experiments

A micropipette (Eppendorf, Research Plus) was used to deposit 150.0 of the prepared suspension with 30.0 mg TiO2 and 5.0 mM catechol onto the center of a UV–visible transparent quartz microscope slide (Technical Glass Products). The material was dried (Fisher Scientific, Isotemp Oven, 6917) for 30 min at 50 °C to create a film. The films in the quartz slide were placed in homemade borosilicate glass reactors with an optical path of 5.00 cm. The atmosphere of the reactor was saturated with a certified air mixture (American Welding Gas, Purity Plus, 400 ppmv CO2, and 20.9% O2 balance in N2) saturated in water vapor every time the film was placed inside. For the previous purpose, 150.0 cm3 min–1 balanced air was passed through a wash bottle for gases filled with water and circulated through the reactor for 2 min. The reactor was kept sealed and in the dark for 30 min before starting irradiation with a 1.0 kW high-pressure Hg(Xe) arc lamp (Newport, 66,921) using cut-off filters at λcut-off ≥ 320, 400, and 515 nm (Newport). The 5.0 cm in diameter light beam was filtered with water to remove infrared radiation prior to the cut-off filter as described previously for this setup.45−50

Diffuse Reflectance UV–Visible Spectroscopy Analyses

The prepared film was analyzed by diffuse reflectance UV–visible spectroscopy (DRUVS) during fixed irradiation intervals using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Evolution 220, ISA-220 accessory). An empty quartz slide was used for the background subtraction. A built-in 10 mm silicon photodiode with a 60 mm Spectralon sphere was used together with a certified Spectralon standard as a reference to register the Kubelka–Munk function.

Analysis of Gas Phase Products by Gas Chromatography and Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy

The photocatalytic reaction was performed in a previously described 135.0 cm3 customized quartz photoreactor with flat windows (5.08 cm diameter)51 to monitor the gas phase products. One milliliter of the TiO2/catechol working suspension was deposited on one of the internal windows of the photoreactor and dried for 3 h at 50 °C to create a film. The reactor atmosphere was then flushed for 30 min with a flow of 400.0 cm3 min–1 of saturated humid balanced air, sealed with septa, and allowed to equilibrate for 1 h in the dark before irradiation at the selected λcut-off. A GC system (SRI 8610C, Multiple Gas #3 GC) was used to analyze 1.00 mL gas samples from the headspace of the reactor using the same optimized conditions as our previous work.51 Briefly, the GC was equipped with two columns (a Molecular Sieve 13X and a HayeSep-D), a thermal conductivity detector, and a flame ionization detector interfaced with a methanizer.

Additional experiments were used to complete the analysis of 50.0 mL samples from the headspace of the reactor with the film by Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy before and after 2 h irradiation of films. For this purpose and to avoid the disturbance of the headspace due to the large volume sampled, films were prepared in two identical reactors as described for the GC analysis. Each sample was withdrawn through the septum with a syringe provided with a stainless-steel needle and transferred into a 2.4 m path-length IR gas cell with ZnSe windows (PIKE) for analysis in the same FTIR instrument as previously described.48,49,51,52 Briefly, the FTIR spectra before and after irradiation was registered using an iZ10 FTIR module connected to the infrared microscope (Thermo Scientific, Nicolet iN10). The 50.0 mL of the gas sample was injected into the gas cell, where it was kept for 30 min to allow internal mixing before recording the infrared spectrum (64 scans at 0.5 cm–1 resolution). The background interferogram was registered under a nitrogen atmosphere, and the products’ spectra at each time (t) reported were subtracted from the sample before irradiation (t = 0 h).

Determination of Photocatalytic Activity

The apparent quantum efficiency (Φ) for reactant loss and product formation were determined from the ratio of their initial reaction rates (R0) based on DRUVS and GC analyses; and the effective absorbed photon flux (Ieff): Φi (%) = 100 × R0/Ieff. This Φi value represents a lower limit for the actual quantum efficiency because light extinction by absorption and scattering of the film is challenging to determine. For this purpose, the effective absorbed light intensity was assessed by convoluting the DRUVS spectrum of the film and the emission spectrum of the lamp recorded at each λcut-off with a calibrated solar radiometer (Ocean Optics). The determination of Ieff involved subtracting from the incident photon flux (I0) on the film, the amounts transmitted through the film as well as those determined scattered photon fluxes measured with the detector at angles of ±0° (reflected), ±30°, ±45°, ±60°, and ±90°.

Determination of the Flat Band Potential

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was employed to determine both the UFB and the donor density (ND) of bare TiO2 and TiO2/catechol using the Mott–Schottky technique. For this purpose, a potentiostat/galvanostat (CHI660A) was used with a three-electrodes array configuration. Further experimental details of EIS measurements are provided in the Supporting Information.

Results and Discussion

DRUVS of Catechol, TiO2, and TiO2/Catechol

The Kubelka–Munk theory53 enables the determination of the band gap (Eg) of a semiconductor material for the transition of an electron from its VB to the CB.31,38 Experimentally, Eg is determined from the UV–visible spectrum registered by DRUVS. Briefly, the behavior of light pathing through a solid medium is described by a function of the scattering (S) and absorption (K) coefficients in eq 11where F(R∞) is the Kubelka–Munk function (KMF), R∞ is the absolute diffuse reflectance at each wavelength of an infinitely thick sample referenced to a standard, K is the absorption coefficient, S is the scattering coefficient, and α is defined as the absorption coefficient of the semiconductor material in this work. Because F(R∞) is proportional to α, the semiconductor Eg can be evaluated by DRUVS using the Tauc expression54 in eq 22where h is the Planck constant, υ is the frequency of light, n = 2 is the exponent for the allowed indirect transition of TiO2, and A is a constant.45,51,54,55 Thus, the Tauc plot of (αhυ)1/n vs hυ is used to reveal the value of Eg, as recently recommended, from the intersection point between the best fitting of the linear region of the curve for the band gap transition (fundamental band) and the baseline near zero absorbance.56

The DRUVS spectrum of a catechol film displayed as the black trace in Figure 1 exhibits a band centered at 274 nm. This band corresponds to the π–π* transition of catechol between the HOMO to the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital.26,44 The blue trace spectrum in Figure 1 shows the intense fundamental band of TiO2 with an absorption edge near 400 nm. For the analysis of TiO2, only the KMF < 1 is significant to determine Eg.31

Neither catechol nor TiO2 individual spectra show any intense band in the visible region of Figure 1, where sunlight promotes photocatalysis efficiently.9,21,38 For the TiO2/catechol case (Figure 1c, red), in addition to observing the band gap transition of TiO2, the binding of catechol on the surface of TiO2 creates a strong and broad absorption in the visible range with maximum wavelength λmax = 439.0 nm.38 This absorption band by TiO2/catechol displays a tail that extends up to λtail = 587.6 nm and corresponds to a ligand (catechol) to metal [Ti(IV) site] LMCT band.32,34,36 The LMCT transition results from the direct injection of an electron from the HOMO of catechol to the CB of TiO2.38 Thus, the observed LMCT transition between the Ti(IV) surface site and catechol at λmax = 439.0 nm in Figure 1 confirms the five-membered ring charge-transfer complex depicted in Scheme 1 is formed.27,37,39

The determination of Eg = 3.11 eV for bare TiO2 (blue trace) is demonstrated in Figure 1 by the intercept of the two green lines in the inset, one for the local baseline and one from the linear region of the KMF, which represents (αhυ)1/2 versus hυ (eq 2).9,54,56−59 For TiO2/catechol (Figure 1c, red trace in the inset), Eg = 3.30 eV is within the experimental error of the value for bare TiO2. The careful assessment of the band gap value of TiO2/catechol is resolved from the intercept of the linear fitting (green lines in Figure 1) of the local regions of the fundamental and LMCT bands.56 Overall, these Eg values for bare TiO2 and the adsorbed complex TiO2/catechol are in good agreement with previous reports.56,60,61 Finally, the absorption edge of the LMCT band for TiO2/catechol is revealed to end at 2.11 eV by the intercept of two additional green linear fittings to the left-hand side in the Tauc plot (inset of Figure 1).

Upon illumination with visible light, an excited state is created through the LMCT transition of TiO2/QH2 in which an electron from the HOMO of catechol is transferred to the CB of the semiconductor (Scheme 1). The fate of catechol in this excited state should result in an adsorbed semiquinone radical, and to a lesser extent (based on known surface reactions of unbound catechol)63 in the generation of oligomerization products from coupling reactions. However, the formation of oligomers is discarded as no oligomeric features are observed in the fingerprint region of the infrared spectrum. Thus, the formation of a stable adsorbed semiquinone radical intermediate on the surface of TiO2 (peaks 3 and 4 in Figure 2) dominated the loss of TiO2/QH2 upon illumination with visible light. The formation of such a stable TiO2/QH• intermediate is in agreement with reports from electrochemical studies using a radical scavenger.64 Therefore, catechol is oxidized on the surface of TiO2 to semiquinone radical and ortho-quinone, all bound species, as depicted in Scheme 2. While the initial oxidation state of +4 for the Ti atom in TiO2/QH2 (Scheme 2) drops to +3 in TiO2/QH• and +2 in TiO2/Q, the species are represented to remain electroneutral due to the loss of electrons. In addition, it must be noted that the reactions in Scheme 2 are written as reversible equilibrium, but the generation of carbon oxides discussed later in this work directs the process in the forward direction only.

Photocatalytic Activity Determination

The values of Φ falling in the range from 0 to 1 are determined here as a valid parameter to describe the photocatalytic activity under variable λcut-off. Analysis of the Photocatalytic Reaction Mechanism

The two characteristic transitions for TiO2/catechol in the DRUVS spectrum should be recalled first for this analysis. These are the fundamental band for the optical band gap of TiO2 at 398.7 nm and the LMCT band centered at 439.0 nm with an extended tail up to λtail = 587.6 nm. Herein, the transformation of surface bound catechol to TiO2 during irradiation is interpreted to proceed via two pathways: (1) semiconductor charge separation by photons energetic enough to excite the optical band gap of TiO2, and/or (2) the ligand-to-metal charge-transfer process.

From the reactions above it arises that several reactive oxygen species (ROS) are generated (O2–•, 1O2, HO•, and H2O2) and can contribute to the oxidation of the catechol moiety. In addition, catechol can be oxidized by the hVB+ through a semiquinone radical in path to produce the quinone. Further oxidative processing ultimately results in the formation of CO2(g), CO(g), and H2O(g) as final products.

In the second pathway considered, the oxidation of the pollutant proceeds exclusively by excitation of the LMCT transition of TiO2/catechol depicted in Scheme 1. There are seven processes in this mechanism: (1) the formation of a chelate between catechol and TiO2. (2) The photoexcitation of the surface-complexed TiO2/catechol species with λmax = 439.0 nm, with electron transfer from the HOMO level of catechol in the complex to the CB of TiO2. (3) Formation of a bound semiquinone radical intermediate. (4) Recombination of the previous electron with the formed bound semiquinone radical that returns to a catechol donor moiety in the complex. (5) Alternative reaction of the electron in the CB with an adsorbed species such as an oxygen molecule that produces superoxide radical anion and other ROS exemplified above. (6) A second electron transfer from the bound semiquinone radical formed that results in a chemisorbed complex of TiO2 with o-quinone. (7) Further quinone oxidation to CO2(g), CO(g), and H2O(g) final products by reactions with hVB+ and ROS.

Under irradiation with UV–visible light (λcut-off ≥ 320 nm), both the band gap of TiO2 and the LMCT transition of TiO2/catechol are excited. The loss of catechol proceeds at λcut-off ≥ 320 nm by both mechanistic pathways. Instead, under visible light irradiation at λcut-off ≥ 400 nm and λcut-off ≥ 515 nm catechol is exclusively loss through the LMCT mechanism, resulting in marginally larger Φ-TiO2/catechol, ΦCO2, and ΦCO than for λcut-off ≥ 320 nm. Remarkably, the mechanism proceeds through a primary intermediate resulting from the one electron oxidation of adsorbed catechol, the stable bound semiquinone radical monitored as peak 4 by DRUVS, which is later converted to an orthoquinone moiety on the surface of TiO2. The short presence of this TiO2/QH• intermediate in the kinetics at λcut-off ≥ 320 nm (Figure 3) is due to its simultaneous transformation to orthoquinone that is rapidly loss to other gaseous products by the ROS generated by both band gap and LMCT excitation mechanisms. Therefore, future studies should explore the role of ROS in these mechanisms.

The largest difference among both mechanisms is that the oxidation of water proceeds or not due to the excitation of TiO2 or the LMCT for the complex, respectively. Environmental Implications of the Photocatalytic Mechanism

Addressing the significance of the mechanism of catechol loss on the surface of atmospheric particles with TiO2 by excitation of the LMCT transition is an important issue. This work provides the first report, based on the Φ−TiO2/QH2 = 0.76 (±0.08) determined at λcut-off ≥ 320 nm in Figure 7, of the photocatalytic reaction of catechol adsorbed on the surface of TiO2 under sunlight irradiation in humid air. This ΦTiO2/QH2 value serves to calculate the photolysis rate (jcatechol) from the integral of the variable actinic flux FA and the cross-section of the TO2/QH2 complex is αλ: jaq = ∫FA(λ)Φ(λ)α(λ) dλ. Available data for a sunny and cloudless day (19 August 2018) in Lexington (38° latitude), KY (USA) at 230 m above ground level,72 is chosen to represent FA.73 The value of αλ ≅ F(R∞) is estimated from the Kubelka–Munk reading, based on eq 1, which should be representative for the partitioning of 50 ppbv catechol in a biomass burning plume onto the surface. Thus, when the biomass burning and combustion pollutant encounters TiO2 on the surface of atmospheric particles, a value of jTiO2/catechol = 3.33 × 10–3 s–1) is obtained. The lifetime for the photocatalytic loss of catechol on the illuminated surface is calculated from τTiO2/catechol) = (jTiO2/catechol)−1 = 5.01 min.

For comparison, the lifetime for catechol loss against major oxidizers has been recently reported under relevant tropospheric conditions.74 The loss of catechol in the gas phase during daytime under dry conditions was estimated to proceed with a τcatechol+O3 = 22.6 h and τcatechol+HO• = 1.7 h for an average 40 ppbv O3 and [HO•] = 1.6 × 106 radicals cm–3, respectively.75 Because catechol does not absorb actinic radiation, its direct photolytic loss in the atmosphere is unimportant. During nighttime, catechol undergoes heterogeneous (nonphotocatalytic) oxidation reactions on the surface of aqueous particles following a Langmuir–Hinshelwood type of dependence6,63 For example, catechol on an aqueous particle surface exposed to 40 ppbv O3 at 70% relative humidity shows a lifetime τcatechol+O3 = 6.5 d, and for an exposure to 15 pptv NO3 oxidizer at the same relative humidity τcatechol+NO3 = 2.6 min63,74 While the reactivity with NO3 dominates the nighttime loss of catechol, during daytime due to the short τTiO2/catechol, the LMCT pathway appears key in the processing of phenolic pollutants. Moreover, τTiO2/catechol is considerably shorter than the residence time for tropospheric aerosols against loss by deposition or for a pollution plume (∼1 week).76 Therefore, the LMCT mechanism presented here has direct global impacts by quickly altering the structure and absorption properties of atmospheric particles during transport.

Conclusions

The transformation of catechol on the surface of TiO2 can proceed in the presence of visible light photons with a 79% apparent quantum efficiency through a ligand-to-metal charge-transfer transition. DRUVS and FTIR analyses at the air/solid interface show the conversion of catechol to ortho-quinone via a semiquinone radical intermediate. FTIR and GC analyses of evolved gases during irradiation show the completion of a consecutive kinetic mechanism as o-quinone is ultimately oxidized into CO2(g) and CO(g). The sum of apparent quantum efficiencies for CO2(g) and CO(g) production practically matches that for the reactant loss, supporting the kinetic model. While sunlight photons in the visible region can only promote the transformation of catechol bound to TiO2 by the LMCT mechanism, UV photons can additionally facilitate a direct band gap excitation mechanism. The two mechanisms discussed above involve the participation of generated ROS from water oxidation and oxygen reduction photocatalytic reactions that should be the focus of future studies on the TiO2/catechol surface.
