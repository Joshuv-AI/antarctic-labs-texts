Clinical features and sequelae of pediatric osteomyelitis following surgical treatment: a 10-year retrospective study
Authors: Hongri Wu, Shulin Wang, Jie Shen, Dong Sun, Xiaohua Wang, Tianming Xu, Shengpeng Yu, Zhao Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimOsteomyelitis (OM) in children is a severe infectious disease, and its sequelae are common. The purpose of this study was to analyze the clinical features and sequelae of pediatric patients following surgical treatment.Patients and methodsWe reviewed all pediatric patients with OM at our clinical center who underwent surgical debridement and antimicrobial therapy from January 2013 to December 2023. Patients were followed for at least 1 year to evaluate clinical outcomes and the occurrence of sequelae. Risk factors associated with sequelae were analyzed via Logistic regression.ResultsA total of 220 pediatric patients with a mean age of 11.69 ± 3.79 years were included. Of these, 151 (68.6%) were male. The majority were due to hematogenous infection (56.8%), while a significant proportion of patients had chronic infections with 149 (67.7%) had more than three month infection duration. The most frequently involved bones were the tibia (44.5%) and femur (29.5%). In addition, 103 patients (46.8%) had a detected pathogen, with Staphylococcus aureus (34.1%) being the most common, followed by Pseudomonas aeruginosa (4.1%) and Staphylococcus epidermidis (3.2%). After a median follow-up of 2 years (range: 1–6 years), 8 patients (3.6%) had infection recurrence; However, they were completely cured after revision surgery. A total of 37 patients (16.8%) reported sequelae, including growth arrest (n = 21), joint injury (n = 16), limb deformities (n = 7), chronic pain (n = 3), and pathologic fractures (n = 2). Regression analysis showed that the use of an external fixator (OR = 8.85, p = 0.001), traumatic infection (OR = 9.88, p = 0.002), prolonged infection duration (≥ 3 months) (OR = 8.00, p = 0.007), and multiple debridements (n ≥ 2) (OR = 5.49, p = 0.021) were risk factors for sequelae, after adjusting for the age and infection site of patients.ConclusionThe data indicated that a significant proportion of children with traumatic and chronic OM suffered surgical intervention, although hematogenous infections remain the predominant type. Meanwhile, the infection cure was often achievable. However, clinicians should still be aware of the potential sequelae and related risk factors following surgical treatment.

Introduction

Pediatric osteomyelitis (OM) is a relatively common and severe infectious disease. Global health data has revealed that the annual incidence rate is about 43 to 83 cases per 100,000 children [1–4]. The limbs are most commonly affected, and patients usually had pain, swelling, sinus tract formation, drainage, and limb dysfunction. It not only causes devastating effects on children’s physical and mental health but also leads to a heavy medical and economic burden. OM in children is often divided into acute and chronic clinical types [1, 2]. Acute or sub-acute OM is defined as a bone infection occurring within several weeks, while chronic OM is a more persistent or complex process, characterized by progressive osseous destruction, sequestrum formation, or recurrent episodes. With advances in imaging techniques (such as high-resolution magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]) [2] and various detection methods [5, 6], the diagnosis of pediatric OM has significantly improved.

In terms of treatment, early use of antibiotics is the first-line option [6–8]. However, a subset of patients requires surgical management [1, 8–10] due to factors such as severe bone destruction, abscess or sequestrum formation, or failure of antibiotics in the initial treatment. Surgical intervention in these cases offers the advantage of radical debridement of necrotic tissue, removal of infected bone, and promotion of healing by creating an environment conducive to tissue regeneration. Previous literature has reported a cure rate of 80%−100% for surgical treatment of pediatric OM [1–4], although the treatment methods are different (such as percutaneous bone puncture or open corticotomy). Due to the high regenerative potential in children, the OM in children usually has a relatively satisfactory outcome (both in terms of infection cure and final bone union) following surgical treatment. Despite advances in treatments, the OM in children is likely to develop some sequelae [11–15], including growth arrest, limb deformities, joint destruction, deep vein thrombosis, pathological fractures, or even death. Therefore, the incidence of sequelae in these patients has become a significant concern for researchers.

Currently, only a few large-scale studies on the outcomes of pediatric OM have been conducted, and the reported sequelae and risk factor analyses are also different among them. Given the vulnerable nature of children and the fact that treatment still largely relies on the physician’s experience [16–18], more studies are needed to investigate the clinical outcomes and sequelae in these patients. Therefore, this study reported the clinical features and sequelae of OM in children following surgical treatment. Given the large sample size, the risk factors associated with sequelae were further analyzed. These findings provide new evidence to the existing literature and establish a basis for clinical optimization.

Patients and methods

Study design and population

This was a retrospective cohort study focusing on pediatric OM. Data were obtained from the local database of the bone infection treatment center at Southwest Hospital in China. All cases of musculoskeletal infections are included in this database. Two dedicated researcher are responsible for the collection of patient information and clinical follow-up in this database. Each patient’s outcome was followed up for at least 12 months, with routine outpatient visits conducted at 2, 6, 9, and 12 months post-treatment to assess therapeutic efficacy. This study was approved by the local medical ethics committee, and the investigation was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the informed consent was waived as this is an observational cohort study.

We reviewed all patients with musculoskeletal infection at our institution between January 2013 and December 2023 (Fig. 1). Inclusion criteria for this study were pediatric patients (< 18 years old) with extremity OM who undergone surgical treatment and had at least 1 year of follow-up at clinical center. The clinical characteristics and outcomes of the included patients were systematically analyzed. The diagnosis and treatment of OM were primarily determined collaboratively by surgeons and a multidisciplinary team (MDT). Those often includes orthopedic surgeons, plastic surgeons, radiologists, infectious disease specialists, and clinical pharmacists. After receiving approval from the institutional review board, patients were identified through medical records and matched to our follow-up database. To ensure accuracy and consistency, two independent researchers thoroughly reviewed and verified all of these medical records.

Diagnostic criteria and definition of OM and sequelae

The initial diagnosis of OM was confirmed through a comprehensive evaluation of symptoms, radiological imaging, and laboratory findings. OM was defined as an infection involving the bone and its marrow space, most commonly caused by bacteria [1, 5, 6]. Among which, acute or sub-acute osteomyelitis evolves over several days or weeks as opposed to chronic osteomyelitis [1], and chronic infections are defined as symptoms for 3 months to years. In specific, hematogenous osteomyelitis results from bacterial seeding via the bloodstream from a distant focus, while post-traumatic osteomyelitis [5, 6] is secondary to open fractures, surgical procedures, or contaminated wounds, allowing direct bacterial invasion into bone. The distinction between hematogenous and traumatic osteomyelitis is made based on the patient’s history, clinical presentation, and imaging findings. Diagnostic criteria for infection recurrence, relative to infection cure, adhered to one or more established consensus standards for fracture-related infection (FRI) [19–21]. These included the presence of a sinus tract or fistula, wound drainage with purulent exudate or intraoperative pus, identification of a pathogenic organism through at least two separate deep tissue cultures, or confirmation by histopathological examination.

Sequelae was defined as clinical conditions following infection control, such as reduced range of motion in the adjacent joints, limb growth disturbances, permanent deformities, limb length discrepancies, chronic pain, or severe outcomes like amputation or death, resulting from OM-related complications that was not present before the infection episode [14–16]. Among which, chronic pain refers to persistent pain that lasts for more than three months after infection control and affects the patient’s daily activities. Joint stiffness indicates a restricted range of motion in joints, resulting in functional impairment. These definitions are based on clinical assessments and patient subjective reports, with reference to existing medical literature [5, 15, 16]. A clinically trained researcher conducted the follow-up for patients via regular telephone calls and outpatient visits.

Data collection and analytical parameters

Additionally, patient information were collected, including age at onset, gender, presenting signs and symptoms, comorbidities, skin lesions, primary bone injuries, blood parameters at presentation (white blood cell count, erythrocyte sedimentation rate [ESR], and C-reactive protein [CRP]), and radiological imaging (ultrasonography, computed tomography, MRI, and bone scintigraphy). Parameters related to antibiotic use, surgical procedures, and follow-up duration were also investigated. These clinical and demographic characteristics laid the foundation for subsequent comparisons between patients with and without sequelae, thereby facilitating the identification of significant predictors for the development of sequelae in pediatric osteomyelitis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 22.0 statistical software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, LLC. Version 9.5.1). Continuous variables were expressed as means and standard deviations, whereas those with non-normal distributions were presented as medians and ranges. Categorical variables were described using frequencies and percentages, and were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Logistic regression analysis was used to identify significant predictors for the development of sequelae. Forest plots of the significant variables from the multivariate analysis were used to visually show the contributing factors to sequelae. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The most common infection sites were the tibia (44.5%), femur (29.5%), and humerus (6.4%). The majority of patients (56.8%) had a hematogenous origin, while the remaining 43.2% of patients had traumatic infections. Upon admission, most patients presented with the symptoms of swelling and tenderness (58.6%) at the infection site, or a sinus tract (40.9%), with fever (25.5%) observed in a subset of patients. Besides, the majority of patients displayed chronic infection due to delayed source control, with 149 (67.7%) had more than three month infection duration at the time of admission.

All patients included in this study met the surgical indications, which were characterized by the formation of localized abscesses (Fig. 2), failure of initial antibiotic therapy, and the presence of dead bone or implants (Fig. 3). Consequently, all patients underwent surgical debridement, with a subset of patients requiring additional reconstructive procedures due to bone defects (61.4%) and soft tissue defects (10.4%). Upon admission, 64% of the patients had received antibiotic treatment before surgery. However, culture results from surgical site samples or blood cultures (mainly from acutely infected patients) identified pathogens in 46.8% of patients, with 11.4% involving polymicrobial infections. Among these, Staphylococcus aureus (34.1%) remained the most prevalent pathogen, including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) in 6.4% of cases, followed by Pseudomonas aeruginosa (4.1%), Staphylococcus epidermidis (3.2%), and Acinetobacter baumannii (2.7%). Empirical broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotic therapy was initiated upon admission for patients with acute onset, while others began antibiotic treatment after intraoperative sampling. Antibiotic regimens were subsequently adjusted based on bacterial culture and drug susceptibility results. For culture-negative OM, antibiotic therapy was guided by local epidemiological data and under the recommendations of pharmacists. The most commonly used intravenous antibiotics included cephalosporins and vancomycin (for MRSA infection). Upon discharge, patients transitioned to oral antibiotics, with quinolones and linezolid being the most frequently prescribed. The median duration of intravenous and oral antibiotic therapy was 21 days (range: 14–28 days) and 14 days (range: 7–42 days), respectively (Table 1). Additionally, a proportion of patients (52.3%) with large bone defects or dead spaces received adjunctive local antibiotic therapy (Figs. 2 and 3), with polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) combined with vancomycin and gentamicin being administered. Moreover, 23 (10.5%) patients required additional flap surgery after debridement.

All 220 pediatric patients with OM completed at least one year of follow-up, with a median follow-up duration of 2 years (range: 1–6 years). Eight patients experienced recurrent infections, all of which were successfully treated with revision surgery. No amputations or deaths occurred during the final follow-up. After infection control, a total of 37 (16.8%) patients developed sequelae, including limb growth arrest (n = 21), joint stiffness (n = 16), limb deformities (n = 7), chronic pain (n = 3), and pathological fractures (n = 2) (Fig. 4). In study period, five patients in older populations (13–17 years) with severe growth deformities were surgically corrected, while the remaining treated conservatively. The total number of surgeries was a median of 2 (range:1–5) times in all patients.

Univariate logistic regression analysis found that several factors were associated with the increased risk of sequelae, as showed in Table 2. These include age < 5 years old, infection in the lower extremities, prolonged infection duration, repeated debridements, traumatic infection, positive CRP at admission, polymicrobial infection, Gram-negative bacterial infection, use of external fixation, as well as the need for additional bone and soft tissue reconstruction. In addition, multivariate analysis further confirmed that the use of an external fixator (OR = 8.85, 95%CI = 2.37–33.07, p = 0.001), traumatic infection (OR = 9.88, 95%CI = 2.38–41.11, p = 0.002), prolonged infection duration (≥3 months) (OR = 8.00, 95%CI = 1.79–35.82, p = 0.007), and multiple debridements (n≥2) (OR = 5.49, 95%CI = 1.29–23.36, p = 0.021) had significant adverse effects on the occurrence of sequelae, after adjusting for the age and infection site of patients (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Pediatric OM is a severe infectious disease in orthopedics, often leading to poor outcomes and significant sequelae in children, with a devastating impact on quality of life [1, 12–16]. Enhancing our understanding of the clinical characteristics and associated sequelae of pediatric OM can facilitate more accurate prognostic counseling and guide the implementation of effective preventive interventions, such as early diagnostic imaging, prompt antibiotic therapy, timely surgical source control, and appropriate rehabilitation measures to preserve limb function and growth. However, clinical reports on pediatric OM remain limited due to its sporadic occurrence. This study offers novel insights to the existing literature and identifies key areas for further research.

Similar to adult OM, pediatric cases can generally be classified into two main infection types [1–3, 6]; hematogenous and traumatic/fracture-related infections. Previous literature has shown that hematogenous infection is the most common origin of infection in pediatric OM, particularly in acute cases. However, societal changes, including increased participation in high-impact sports, rising rates of traffic accidents, and more frequent use of internal fixation in fractures, have led to a increase in traumatic infections [2–4, 21]. Our traumatic osteomyelitis group encompassed open fractures, closed fractures and postoperative infections following fixation. The study supports the fact that while hematogenous infections remain the majority, a significant proportion of patients had post-traumatic infections, with some requiring bone reconstruction and skin flap coverage surgeries. Furthermore, our multivariate analysis found that traumatic infections to be an independent risk factor for the occurrence of sequelae, including limb deformities, limb length discrepancies, and decreased joint mobility. These findings suggest that FRIs should be a focus of future pediatric research, encompassing diagnosis, treatment, and prevention. However, due to data collection constraints, this study did not delve into detailed comparisons between these traumatic subgroups (open fractures, closed fractures, postoperative infections). Future research should further subdivide traumatic osteomyelitis types to explore their distinct impacts on outcomes.

Due to the vulnerable nature of children, the literature consistently highlights that sequelae after treatment for pediatric OM are both frequent and severe [13–16]. Our cohort demonstrated similar outcomes, with some children developing limb growth arrest, joint stiffness, limb deformities, chronic pain, and pathological fractures, even after successful infection control. Among these, limb growth arrest and joint stiffness were most prevalent sequelae, with incidence rates of 9.5% and 7.3%, respectively. These results were consistent with previous studies [12, 16], which has drawn significant attention and investigation from scholars. Notably, no cases of deep venous thrombosis (DVT) were observed, despite its recognition as a severe potential complication in OM patients, particularly those with prolonged immobilization [22]. Nevertheless, vigilance for DVT remains important in managing pediatric OM. Furthermore, our analysis identified several risk factors for sequelae, some of which were modifiable and others unmodifiable. Our data showed that children younger than five years were more susceptible to related sequelae, although the sample size for this subgroup was limited. Importantly, our study identified a prolonged duration from symptom onset to definitive surgery as a key finding and a significant risk factor for sequelae. This delay is multifactorial, primarily stemming from initial misdiagnosis in primary care settings, unsuccessful trials of conservative antibiotic therapy at local hospitals, and subsequent delays in referral to a specialized tertiary center. As a referral center for complex cases, we often receive patients after these initial management steps have failed. This highlights a critical area for improvement in the overall healthcare system.

Early diagnosis and treatment are crucial for infection cure and reducing the risk of sequelae [8, 18]. For bacteriological diagnosis, this study displayed a relatively higher negative result. It may lie to many reasons [6, 18, 21], including prior-antibiotic exposure and sub-optimal sampling practices, the delays in laboratory processing. As the hematogenous infection is more common in children and is usually treated with antibiotics before surgery, the former cause is more common in practice. In this study, 64% of the patients had received antibiotic treatment before surgery. On the other hands, a significant proportion of patients had chronic infections, predominantly arising from acute OM due to delayed or inadequate treatment. Most importantly, our results indicated that multiple debridement procedures and prolonged infection duration were associated with a higher incidence of severe sequelae and were identified as independent risk factors for sequelae in the multivariate analysis. These findings emphasized the importance of prompt and effective source control in pediatric patients to achieve satisfactory clinical outcomes.

Antibiotics remain the first-line treatment for acute and subacute OM. However, no consensus exists regarding the standard antibiotic therapy for OM in either adult or pediatric patients [7, 23]. Most literature [1, 6–8] recommends long-term intravenous antibiotic therapy, typically more than three weeks, to mitigate complications and prevent recurrent infections in pediatric OM, with durations extending up to six weeks for chronic cases. Increasing evidence also supports transitioning from short-term intravenous therapy to oral antibiotics after discharge [8]. However, this transition is primarily based on clinicians’ experience, partly guided by indicators such as normalized body temperature and CRP levels, without established threshold for clinical management. Furthermore, local antibiotic therapy, primarily using antibiotic-loaded PMMA, was specifically employed as an adjunctive treatment for patients with substantial bone defects or dead space that required stabilization and local high-dose antibiotic delivery. The 52.3% usage rate reflects the proportion of patients in this cohort who had these specific indications for local therapy. Overall, the antibiotic regimens adhered to a general institutional protocol which planned for a defined period of intravenous therapy followed by oral suppression, with the exact duration and transition timing guided by clinical and serological response. Further research is needed to determine the optimal strategies of antibiotic treatment in pediatric patients with OM, including investigations into the potential toxicities and long-term side effects.

Another major concern is how to identify suitable pediatric OM patients for early surgical intervention. The rationale for surgery includes excising necrotic bone and soft tissue, promoting healing, obtaining tissue samples to guide antibiotic therapy, and minimizing the pathogen burden to prevent additional complications from persistent infection. Though there is no absolute consensus, surgical intervention [1, 8, 9] is often indicated when initial antibiotic therapy fails, or when there are abscesses, septic arthritis, bone necrosis, sinus tracts, or prosthesis involvement. Our cohort adhered to these principles and observed favorable infection control outcomes, which were comparable to the latest literature reports. Although multiple debridements were identified as a risk factor for sequelae, the surgical strategy was tailored to individual. In this study, many patients required staged surgeries due to extensive bone involvement, soft tissue defects, or persistent infection. The two-stage surgical strategy for bone defects also leverage the advantages of a PMMA spacer [24] for dead space management and local antibiotic delivery, achieving rapid infection control and temporary restoration of limb function. Other surgical techniques, we preferred low-burden methods for patients requiring fixation, such as antibiotic cement-coated locking plates. Though a direct comparative analysis of external versus internal fixation methods was not conducted specifically within this pediatric cohort. We previously reported that these methods [25–28] significantly increased comfort and facilitated functional recovery in adult patients. We speculated that these approaches might be especially suitable for pediatric patients, as long-term external fixation and prolonged infection duration were identified as significant risk factors for sequelae.

Our study had several limitations. First, as a retrospective analysis, there may exist potential omission or incomplete clinical data. Second, the study was conducted at a single tertiary referral center, which may limit the generalizability of findings despite the large sample size. Third, in the future, more detailed clinical parameters including sub-group of traumatic osteomyelitis and fixation methods (external vs. internal) requires further investigation in dedicated pediatric comparative studies. Nevertheless, our data provide valuable insights into the clinical characteristics and prognosis of pediatric OM after surgical treatment.

Conclusion

Pediatric OM is a complex disease with significant implications for children’s quality of life. Our study showed that a substantial proportion of children with traumatic and chronic OM required surgical intervention. This highlights a new clinical challenge in its management, despite hematogenous infections remaining the predominant type. Although satisfactory infection control can be achieved through surgical treatment, clinicians must remain vigilant regarding potential sequelae and associated risk factors. Future research should prioritize the continuous optimization of antibiotic regimens and surgical techniques to improve treatment outcomes.
