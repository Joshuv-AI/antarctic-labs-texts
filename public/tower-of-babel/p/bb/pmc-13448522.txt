A framework for the development and validation of an aptamer-based assay for pathogen detection using SARS-CoV-2 as a model
Authors: Steev Loyola, Leonardo J. Monroy-Cruz, Miguel Quiliano, Mirko Zimic, Gerónimo Fernandez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The COVID-19 pandemic exposed major disparities in global diagnostic capacity, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where reliance on conventional assays and external technologies hindered rapid response. Aptamers offer a promising alternative; however, practical and experimentally validated frameworks that connect aptamer discovery to assay development and evaluation in LMIC settings remain limited. Here, we present an integrated and stepwise framework and its feasibility for developing and validating aptamer-based testing tools using SARS-CoV-2 as a model. Native viral particles served as targets in a nine-round systematic evolution of ligands by exponential enrichment (SELEX) protocol optimized for low- to medium-complexity laboratories. High-throughput sequencing and bioinformatic analyses identified candidate sequences, which were then experimentally assessed under defined experimental conditions. The top-performing aptamer, APT-35b, was incorporated into a DNA aptamer-based qPCR assay (Apta-qPCR). When evaluated using 475 clinical samples, the Apta-qPCR achieved 80.8% sensitivity and 95.3% specificity for Omicron BA.1/BA.1.1, with no cross-reactivity to other human coronaviruses. However, performance decreased for more evolved Omicron subvariants (n = 68). This framework offers a practical and adaptable roadmap that links aptamer discovery with functional assay development and can be applied to diverse pathogens, ultimately supporting enhanced diagnostic preparedness and response capacity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55568-9.

Introduction

Emerging infectious diseases continue to challenge global health systems, highlighting the need for testing tools that are both highly accurate and adaptable to novel pathogens or variants. Real-time PCR (qPCR), which targets nucleic acids, remains the gold standard for pathogen detection due to its sensitivity and specificity; however, its reliance on prior genomic knowledge and its vulnerability to mutations in primer or probe binding regions can limit performance. Rather than replacing conventional or established methods, these constraints underscore the need to expand the toolbox with complementary and scalable assays based on alternative modes of molecular recognition. In this context, there is a need for frameworks that support the development and validation of testing solutions. Such frameworks should build on existing infrastructure, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), while enabling the adoption of non-conventional assays to strengthen response capacity against emerging infectious threats.

The coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic exposed profound disparities in global diagnostic capacity and underscored the dependence of LMICs on assays developed externally, largely in Asia and Europe1–3. During the early stages of the pandemic, qPCR was rapidly adopted as the method for identifying cases through the molecular detection of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2). Although the rapid commercialization of numerous molecular assays enabled early case detection and informed public health interventions, many LMICs encountered significant barriers to accessing, implementing, and scaling qPCR-based diagnostics1,2. These challenges emerged not only from technological dependence but also from various policy issues while awaiting advancements from regions with greater capacity and faster response. Later, the introduction of antigen-based lateral flow assays enhanced and decentralized diagnostic capacity4,5. These tests offered clear advantages over qPCR; however, they suffered from limited sensitivity, often requiring serial testing and creating new diagnostic and logistical challenges4,6,7. Collectively, these obstacles have constrained the diagnostic response in LMICs, underscoring the need for locally driven initiatives that support both the development and implementation of alternative methods. Although such challenges may appear region-specific, the resulting gaps in preparedness and response to health crises represent a global concern1,2,5.

Recognizing the persistent risk posed by emerging pathogens, the World Health Organization has prioritized the development of innovative tools capable of enabling timely and effective responses8. In this regard, aptamer-based assays represent a promising approach for enhancing preparedness through non-conventional means9. Aptamers are synthetic oligonucleotides of variable length and low molecular weight that fold into defined structures, enabling high-affinity and selective binding to diverse targets10,11. Their stability across a wide range of chemical and physical conditions, ease of synthesis, and low production costs make them attractive candidates for viral detection11–13. Early aptamer-based assays developed for SARS-CoV-2 demonstrated comparable performance to qPCR and lateral flow assays14–17. However, despite their advantages and potential, they were not scaled for widespread use nor translated into clinically relevant platforms, likely due to the complexity of aptamer selection through the systematic evolution of ligands by exponential enrichment (SELEX) method and the absence of flexible and adaptable frameworks that effectively integrate molecular discovery with downstream detection platforms.

In this study, we present the feasibility of an integrated and stepwise framework that encompasses aptamer discovery against native viral particles, assay development, and validation of a DNA aptamer-based assay coupled with qPCR (Apta-qPCR) for pathogen detection. This framework is intended to serve as a flexible and transferable roadmap that can be adapted and refined to diverse pathogens and settings, enabling the systematic development and evaluation of aptamer-based assays. By articulating this initial effort, we aim to address translational gaps in diagnostics, support the diversification of approaches and testing tools, and contribute to ongoing efforts to strengthen global preparedness for emerging infectious diseases.

Results

Framework overview

Figure 1 illustrates the framework that guided the study. The process begins with the target preparation, which includes the generation of viral seed stocks and the subsequent immobilization of viral particles. Then, aptamers are selected through SELEX, followed by high-throughput sequencing and bioinformatic analysis to identify and characterize sequences. Subsequently, the performance of selected synthesized aptamers is evaluated, and the top-performing candidate is integrated into a detection platform, thereby enabling assay development. Next, the aptamer’s capacity to recognize defined viral targets is characterized, and finally, the performance of the resulting aptamer-based assay is evaluated. Although this framework offers a reference pathway from discovery to validation, it is not meant to be a rigid pipeline. Instead, we present it as a baseline that can be refined, optimized, and adapted based on available resources and pathogen priorities. Results are presented below following each phase of the framework.

Target preparation

Viral seed stock

Cell lysis was observed as the predominant cytopathic effect in more than 75% of Vero cells at eight days’ post-infection. The cycle threshold (Ct) value of the inoculum used for infection was 18.3 ± 0.3. At harvest, Ct values were 20.2 ± 0.9 for Vero-76 and 12.1 ± 0.4 for Vero-81. Plaque assay of the Vero-76 harvest did not yield quantifiable plaques, whereas the Vero-81 harvest produced a viral titer of 6.0 × 10^5 plaque-forming units per milliliter (PFU/mL). This Vero-81 harvest was used as the viral seed for subsequent experiments.

Viral particle coating

Figure 2A shows Ct values from serial dilutions of the viral seed used for coating in polystyrene strips, along with values obtained from the recovered material post coating. Although complete coating was not achieved, coating with 50 µL of 6.0 × 10^0 PFU/mL (Ct = 32.7 ± 0.3) resulted in detectable virus recovery by qPCR (Ct = 36.9 ± 0.4).

The detailed protocol is provided in Supplementary Data (Supplementary Table 1), and the characteristics of each round are summarized in Table 1. Briefly, binding assays indicated that sequences isolated in round 6 exhibited the higher ∆Ct, followed by rounds 3 and 4. Based on these results and considering modified stringency conditions, rounds 4, 5, 6, and 9 were selected for high-throughput sequencing.

Sequencing and analysis

High-throughput sequencing generated 1,004,361 reads (Q > 7; median length: 76 bp), of which 28.2% corresponded to round 4, 19.4% to round 5, 32.6% to round 6, and 19.8% to round 9. After normalization and analysis using global, round-specific, and enrichment-based approaches, top sequence clusters per approach were consolidated into 51 unique sequences (Supplementary Table 2). Of these, 14 sequences were prioritized for folding prediction and synthesis based on abundance, detection by at least two approaches, and/or evidence of enrichment across rounds. Sequence information and predicted secondary structures are provided in Supplementary Data (Supplementary Tables 3 and Supplementary Fig. 1).

Relative binding evaluation

Among the 14 synthesized sequences, all except APT-6 produced amplification signals (Table 2; Figs. 3A–B), while APT-8a displayed no detectable signal in non-target assays (Table 2). Differential performance across conditions was observed, with distinct subsets of candidates exhibiting positive ΔCt values at 50 and 100 ng/well (Fig. 3A). Aptamer selection was guided by a predefined criterion that prioritized, for each condition, the three sequences with the highest positive ΔCt values, as these indicate greater relative binding discrimination between target and non-target assays. Applying this criterion, a subset of six sequences was selected and advanced for further evaluation: APT-1, APT-7, APT-8a, APT-13, APT-35a, and APT-35b. To note, APT-35b is a variant of APT-35a, which differs by one nucleotide (Supplementary Table 3).

Assay development

The development of the Apta-qPCR assay was structured as a sequential and results-driven process, progressing from controlled experimental conditions to clinical sample evaluation. The first three stages were conducted with viral seed stocks, while the final stage incorporated SARS-CoV-2 Delta-positive clinical specimens.

Stage 1: Viral particle coating on qPCR plates. For assay development, 20 µL of viral particles were coated in triplicate onto 0.2 mL transparent qPCR plates, and each technical replicate was subsequently evaluated following the procedures described above. Although not all input particles were retained during coating, the overall performance of this stage was considered satisfactory (Supplementary Table 4).

Stage 2: Determination of the optimal aptamer concentration. To establish assay conditions, APT-1 was selected as a representative candidate for optimization, as it was the most abundant sequence identified and exhibited the highest relative binding across evaluated conditions (Fig. 3A). Binding assays with and without target were performed using APT-1 at 5.0 ng, 0.5 ng, and 0.05 ng/well. An additional Tween 20 double-wash step was included to reduce unbound sequences, and the amplification mix was added directly to the wells. Technical details and results are provided in Supplementary Fig. 2. Briefly, 0.05 ng/well yielded amplification signals proportional to the concentration of coated viral particles.

Stage 3: Evaluation in virus-spiked transport medium. To mimic clinical samples and conditions, two commercial transport media were spiked with viral seeds at two concentrations (6 × 10^4 and 6 × 10^3). Non-spiked media served as negative controls. To streamline the workflow, coating time was reduced to 2 h. Full details and results are presented in Supplementary Fig. 3. Briefly, among the six sequences tested, APT-35b consistently distinguished negative controls from spiked samples and better discriminated between the two viral concentrations. Consequently, APT-35b was selected for all subsequent experiments.

Stage 4: Optimization with clinical samples. To address potential sensitivity loss caused by dilution during coating, direct coating and alternative conditions using KPL Coating Solution and viral transport medium were evaluated (Supplementary Fig. 4). Three SARS-CoV-2 Delta-positive clinical samples with varying viral loads and one negative sample were assessed under these conditions. Briefly, direct coating of undiluted samples produced quantitative results most consistent with routine diagnostic assays (Supplementary Fig. 4). A graphical summary of the final Apta-qPCR protocol is provided in Supplementary Fig. 5.

Target recognition

Regardless of the concentrations tested (100 or 200 ng/well) or the incubation time (15–30 min) with recombinant proteins (spike S1 or envelope), results were comparable to those of the negative controls (Supplementary Table 5). Thus, under evaluated in vitro conditions, it was not possible to identify the target recognized by APT-35b.

Our molecular docking methodology for studying the binding mode of an aptamer to a protein was validated by redocking with the DNA aptamer-human alpha-thrombin complex (PDB ID: 1HAO), yielding low root mean square deviation (RMSD) values (mean: 1.366 Å) and confirming the protocol’s ability to reproduce the conformation observed in the X-ray structure (Fig. 4A). The modeled structure of APT-35b is presented in Fig. 4B. Using our validated protocol, APT-35b was docked onto spike protein structures from the Delta (PDB ID: 7VHH), Omicron B.1.1.529 (PDB ID: 7QO7), and Omicron XBB (PDB ID: 8XUT) variants following a blind docking protocol to estimate binding energies. In all three variants, the highest-ranked docking poses suggested potential localization of APT-35b near regions adjacent to the receptor-binding domain (Fig. 4C).

Assay validation

In the Apta-qPCR, 15.4% (8/52) of clinical positive samples showed no amplification signals (Fig. 5A, blue arrow), while 84.6% (44/52) yielded Ct values ranging from 21.5 to 29.3. Among clinical negative samples, 12.5% (53/423) produced Ct values between 28.1 and 29.9 (Fig. 5A, red arrow). Based on the established cutoff (Fig. 5B), the Apta-qPCR exhibited 80.8% sensitivity and 95.3% specificity for detecting Omicron BA.1/BA.1.1, achieving acceptable discrimination and agreement levels (Table 3).

In an exploratory analysis, 68 additional clinical samples positive for non-BA.1/BA.1.1 Omicron subvariants were included (Supplementary Fig. 6A). Since various positive samples (n = 35) displayed no amplification in the Apta-qPCR (Supplementary Fig. 6A, arrow), analyses were stratified by subvariants (Supplementary Fig. 6B–F). When stratified for BA.4 and BA.5 detections, the assay yielded acceptable to high performance and discrimination (Table 3), whereas performance for BQ.1 and XBB.1 was suboptimal.

Assay reproducibility was confirmed across seven independent experiments, where the positive control consistently amplified with Ct values between 25.4 and 26.7. Intra-assay triplicates yielded coefficients of variation ranging from 0.8% to 2.3%, and the overall inter-assay variation was 1.4%. Analytical specificity was assessed using ten FilmArray-confirmed clinical samples positive for non–SARS-CoV-2 coronaviruses (five HCoV-OC43, four HCoV-NL63, and one HCoV-229E), all of which were also negative by routine diagnostic qPCR. None of these samples produced amplification signals in the Apta-qPCR.

Discussion

Aptamers are ligands with high affinity and specificity that can be rapidly selected and integrated into diverse non-conventional testing platforms, offering an attractive alternative to conventional diagnostic development pipelines9–11. Herein, we demonstrate the feasibility of an end-to-end framework for aptamer-based pathogen detection, encompassing aptamer discovery and selection, and assay development and performance assessment. Specifically, by integrating whole-virus SELEX with high-throughput sequencing, bioinformatics, structural modeling, and performance evaluation in over 470 clinical samples, we discovered a best-performing aptamer under assay-development conditions and successfully translated it into a functional Apta-qPCR assay. Although SARS-CoV-2 served as the model system, the broader significance of this work lies in providing a detailed framework that can be readily adapted for emerging or rapidly evolving pathogens. This framework also addresses a critical challenge revealed during the pandemic: the common reliance on conventional workflows that move sequentially from pathogen isolation to genome sequencing, primer design, testing and validation. Such linear pipelines can slow and centralize innovation, thereby constraining responsiveness. Conversely, aptamer-based approaches potentially create alternative and local pathways that drive diversification and accelerate innovation, particularly in settings where access to novel technologies tends to be slow and limited.

The framework presented here could serve as a practical foundation for assay development. By outlining a stepwise process, it provides a basis that can be modified or optimized in laboratories with varying levels of complexity. Specifically, the described SELEX protocol was intentionally adapted from existing methodologies18–25 to rely on “conventional” molecular biology consumables and standard laboratory equipment, thereby ensuring that it functions as an adaptable working protocol. In this study, aptamers were selected against native viral particles to preserve natural conformational epitopes and minimize reliance on recombinant antigen production or engineering. However, as described elsewhere21–27, recombinant proteins or other non-native viral structures could serve as alternative targets, avoiding the need for viral isolation. In parallel, aptamer diversity and enrichment across SELEX rounds were systematically assessed by integrating ONT high-throughput sequencing with bioinformatics pipelines under a defined candidate selection strategy, enabling data-driven prioritization. Although a formal evaluation of cost-effectiveness against other frameworks, procedures or strategies15,23,28–30 was beyond the scope of this study, the approach described here contributes a reference point for future comparative and implementation studies. Notably, by avoiding reliance on proprietary reagents and region-specific supply chains, this framework could leverage procedures and resources, particularly in settings where molecular biology infrastructure and instrumentation, sequencing and bioinformatics support have been established. Accordingly, the framework can be tailored to address local pathogen priorities, facilitating the development of context-specific testing tools that strengthen biosurveillance.

Aptamer discovery via SELEX does not follow a single standardized approach; therefore, different strategies and selection round numbers have been reported to yield from single to multiple candidates15,22,23,26–30. Herein, incremental stringency applied across nine selection rounds resulted in the enrichment of sequences with differential assay performance, leading to the identification of APT-35b as the best-performing candidate. To note, APT-35b was identified through the enrichment-based bioinformatics approach and was one of the most abundant sequences in round 9. Because APT-35b was selected through whole-virus SELEX, its molecular target could not be experimentally established and may involve spike protein, conformational epitopes, glycan-associated features, or other virion-associated structures. Therefore, docking analyses using SARS-CoV-2 spike proteins from Delta and selected Omicron subvariants were conducted solely as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating approach, based on the biological plausibility and structural accessibility of spike as a candidate target and its precedent in previous aptamer studies15,22–27. These analyses identified hypothetical interaction poses that may warrant future experimental investigation but should not be interpreted as evidence of target identity or binding mechanism. Although reduced sensitivity was observed against more evolved variants during the Apta-qPCR validation, our data does not establish whether such differences are attributable to spike-associated changes or other virion-associated features. Beyond the use of SARS-CoV-2 as model, our methods and preliminary observations may inform future aptamer discovery efforts targeting critical sites by prioritizing enriched sequences to accelerate the identification of best-performing candidates, while preserving efficacy despite viral evolution.

Apta-qPCR can be positioned as a complementary testing tool that expands, rather than replaces, the capabilities of conventional methods. By integrating aptamer-based target recognition with the analytical robustness of qPCR, our approach introduces an additional molecular recognition layer that enables detection based on structural features, thereby reducing reliance on prior genomic characterization and mitigating the impact of genetic variability within primer-binding regions. In this context, Apta-qPCR may be particularly valuable in cases where direct detection of viral particles provides relevant information, when genomic data is limited for primer design, or in circumstances in which existing assays remain suboptimal or are not sufficiently optimized. In addition, its potential to simplify workflows, such as by bypassing nucleic acid extraction or reverse transcription, and to detect particles further supports its applicability, while leveraging existing molecular infrastructure. Within this framework, Apta-qPCR, as presented here or with modifications to the aptamer or alternative formats28,31–37 not explored in this study, may serve as a temporary solution while more optimized or fully validated tools are developed, or as a complement to current diagnostic strategies where gaps persist.

Aptamers against SARS-CoV-2 were rapidly isolated in 202014–17, but only a few progressed into assays tested on small sample sets17. Later studies expanded their use across serological, molecular, and biosensor platforms, reporting sensitivities of 61–100% and specificities of 98–100% in evaluations involving up to 232 samples28,31–38. Notably, some demonstrated strong specificity against SARS-CoV-2 when challenged with other respiratory viruses32,33,35,36,38. Extending these advances, the Apta-qPCR was evaluated here with one of the largest clinical datasets yet reported for an aptamer-based assay. Developed with APT-35b, originally selected against the Delta variant, it retained functionality in detecting Omicron BA.1/BA.1.1, though performance varied across other more evolved subvariants. To note, in the primary validation, performance was similar to other aptamer-based assays21,28,31,38, and antigen- and molecular-based diagnostics39; however, the performance of the Apta-qPCR described here reflects a proof-of-concept evaluation and should be interpreted accordingly. Overall, these results highlight both the potential and the limitations of aptamer-based assays: while they can remain “valuable” against certain variants, their performance is vulnerable to viral evolution – a challenge also faced by other testing tools. Although the Apta-qPCR does not yet surpass established diagnostics platforms39, its development and validation mark a critical step toward innovation for future health crises25.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted using SARS-CoV-2 as the model pathogen, and the scalability of the proposed framework to other priority pathogens remains to be demonstrated. Second, binding performance was assessed using ΔCt values derived from assays conducted in the presence and absence of the viral target, rather than an orthogonal quantitative binding measurement such as a dissociation constant (Kd). Hence, the reported metrics should be interpreted as relative and context-dependent measures and not as direct measures of binding affinity or specificity. Although these composite readout ΔCt values may incorporate biases derived from previous steps (e.g., coating efficiency, wash variability, recovery, and amplification bias), their potential use as a surrogate metric should be further evaluated against established methods described elsewhere40,41. Third, although the framework and the Apta-qPCR were developed under controlled conditions in an equipped laboratory, their operational feasibility, reproducibility, implementation and applicability in decentralized or resource-limited settings, including those leveraging existing capabilities, remain to be formally evaluated. Fourth, validation relied on a limited number of samples for certain SARS-CoV-2 variants and coronaviruses, and testing was restricted to a single laboratory and one routine qPCR platform. Broader systematic evaluations incorporating geographically diverse sample sets, including specimens negative and positive for other respiratory viruses or structurally similar particles, as well as inter-laboratory benchmarking, are beyond the scope of the present study but will be essential to confirm robustness. Fifth, although the Apta-qPCR showed acceptable performance, subsets of false positives and negatives were observed, underscoring the need to refine procedures (e.g., coating, washing, amplification), and/or aptamer re-selection or engineering. Nonetheless, it is important to note that the aim of this work was not to deliver a fully validated testing tool, but rather to propose a framework for pathogen detection. Adapting and refining this framework may help sustain testing reliability in the context of pathogen evolution. Finally, while docking analyses provided preliminary insight into APT-35b–spike interactions, experimental validation of the binding site remains incomplete, and, given the current epidemiological context, further pursuit may not be appropriate for advancing efforts.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the feasibility of implementing the proposed framework while developing an aptamer-based assay. Here, by integrating various wet and dry lab approaches, we established a streamlined workflow for aptamer discovery, enabling systematic identification, screening, and validation of candidates against viral particles. The resulting Apta-qPCR assay exemplifies how this framework can be translated into a non-conventional functional testing tool, with the potential to be rapidly reconfigured for emerging pathogens or variants, while strengthening resilience. However, beyond its application, this work illustrates how aptamer technologies can be harnessed to expand the global testing toolbox, particularly in settings where flexible and accessible solutions are most needed. By bridging innovation with translational utility, the described framework provides a blueprint for pandemic-ready frameworks that align with the demands of a rapidly evolving global health landscape and operational realities.

Methods

Viral culture and quantification

Vero-76 (American Type Culture Collection [ATCC]; CRL-1587) and Vero-81 (ATCC; CCL-81) cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) (Gibco, 11885076) supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Sigma-Aldrich, F4135) and 2% penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, P4333). For culture, DMEM was supplemented with 10% FBS. A total of 1.0 × 10^6 cells in 5 mL were seeded into T25 flasks and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 to form confluent monolayers within 3 days. Confluent monolayers were infected with a SARS-CoV-2 Delta variant (GISAID accession number: EPI_ISL_8035465). Briefly, culture medium was discarded, and 500 µL of a 1:4 dilution of a Delta-positive clinical specimen prepared in maintenance medium was used for the infection. Flasks were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2, and homogenized gently every 15 min. Then, 5 mL of maintenance medium was added. Cultures were inspected daily for cytopathic effect (CPE), and supernatants were harvested once ≥ 75% of cells showed CPE to generate viral seed stocks. For quantification, viral RNA was extracted using the QIAamp Viral RNA kit (Qiagen, 52904) and a qPCR was performed with the Bio-Speedy Direct RT-qPCR SARS-CoV-2 kit (https://www.fda.gov/media/141823/download), including controls and following manufacturer’s instructions. Molecular assays were run in triplicate, and Ct values were recorded as relative viral loads. Plaque assays were performed as described elsewhere42, and titers expressed as PFU/mL.

Coating of viral particles

The viral seed stock was serially diluted tenfold in maintenance medium to obtain dilutions ranging from 6.0 × 10^4 to 6.0 × 10^-1. Fifty microliters of each dilution were added in triplicate to 8-well polystyrene strips (Costar, 2580) using KPL Coating Solution (Seracare, 5150-0015)43. Strips were incubated for 4 h at 4 °C and washed three times with 100 µL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Residual PBS was removed by inversion, strips were gently tapped on absorbent paper, and 50 µL of nuclease-free water heated at 95 °C was added to the wells for recovering viral particles attached to polystyrene. Viral RNA extraction and qPCR were performed as described above, and coating efficiency was determined by comparing Ct values between the input and recovered material.

Systematic evolution of ligands by exponential enrichment (SELEX) implementation

SELEX was implemented by using the RND40 single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) library, consisting of 76 unmodified bases with a 40-nucleotide randomized central region flanked by primer binding sites F3 (5’-GCGGATGAAGACTGGTGT-3’) and R3 (5’-GTTGCTCGTATTTAGGGC-3’)19. The viral seed stock was used to select aptamers against its native forms, adapting described protocols and recommendations18–21. The implementation was adjusted to establish a workflow suitable for execution in low- to medium-complexity laboratories.

Each SELEX round began with a positive selection (50 µL of viral seed stock for coating), or a negative selection (50 µL of culture medium for coating) followed by a positive selection. The workflow comprised: (i) folding, (ii) selection, (iii) recovery and pre-amplification, (iv) amplification evaluation, (v) library amplification, and (vi) concentration (Fig. 2B). At the end of each round, a backup of 20 µL was stored at -80 °C for later use. Reactions included the RND40 library (100 µM), PBS, 50 mM MgCl₂ (Invitrogen, Y02016), GoTaq DNA Polymerase (Promega, M300F), nuclease-free water, 10 mM dNTPs (Invitrogen, 18427089), and primers F3 and R3 (2.5 µM each). Amplification products from step (iii) were resolved by agarose (Invitrogen, 16500500) gel electrophoresis (120 V, 45 min), stained with SYBR Safe (Invitrogen, S33102), and results were used to define the optimal PCR cycle number for step (iv).

After completion of SELEX, frozen backups from each round were tested for relative binding performance. Binding assays were performed in wells coated with either culture medium (non-target control) or viral seed stock (3.0 × 10^4 PFU/well). For each assay, 5 µL backup was mixed with 95 µL PBS containing 1 mM MgCl₂ and folded. Fifty microliters of folded sequences were added to non-target control and virus-coated wells. Bound ssDNA sequences were recovered with nuclease-free water heated at 95 °C and then amplified by qPCR using PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher, A25742). Each reaction contained 10 µL Master Mix, 7.5 µL nuclease-free water, 0.25 µL F3 (2.5 µM), 0.25 µL R3 (2.5 µM), and 2 µL recovered ssDNA. Amplification was performed on a 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher, 4351106) under the following program: 50 °C for 2 min, 95 °C for 2 min, and 30 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 57 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 1 min. Relative binding performance was assessed by calculating ΔCt between non-target control and virus-coated wells, where higher ΔCt indicated greater binding discrimination under the assay conditions.

Aptamer identification and characterization

Best-performing SELEX rounds were subjected to high-throughput sequencing for aptamer identification and characterization as described elsewhere18,19,44,45. Selected rounds were sequenced in triplicate within a single run.

SELEX rounds were quantified using the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Invitrogen; Q32851) and normalized to 100 ng in 7.5 µL. End repair was performed with the NEBNext Ultra II End Repair/dA-Tailing Module (New England Biolabs, E7546S) in a reaction containing 1.2 µL End Prep Reaction Buffer, 0.5 µL End Prep Enzyme Mix, and 8.3 µL normalized library. Incubation was performed at 21 °C for 15 min, 65 °C for 15 min, and 4 °C for 1 min. Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) barcoding was performed with 5 µL Ultra II Ligation Master Mix (New England Biolabs, E7595S), 0.15 µL Ligation Enhancer, 2.85 µL nuclease-free water, and 1.25 µL barcodes. Reactions were incubated at 21 °C for 20 min, 65 °C for 10 min, and 4 °C for 1 min, then pooled to generate the sequencing library. The library was purified with AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, A63880) at a 1:1 ratio, washed with 250 µL ONT short fragment buffer (SFB), and eluted in 30 µL of 10 mM Tris (pH 8.0). A total of 28 µL was ligated to 3 µL ONT adapters (ONT, EXP-AMII001) in a reaction containing 8 µL NEBNext Quick Ligation Reaction Buffer and 4 µL Quick T4 DNA Ligase (New England Biolabs, B6058S). The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 20 min, followed by a second AMPure XP bead purification, yielding 15 µL of eluate in ONT elution buffer (ONT, EXP-AMII001). The library was quantified with the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit and sequenced on an R9.4.1 flow cell using the MinION Mk1C for 48 h44,45.

Basecalling with a high-accuracy model and demultiplexing were performed with Guppy v6.1, quality control with FastQC v0.12.1, adapter trimming with Porechop v0.2.4, and length trimming with Seqtk v1.4.r122. Reads with Q < 7 were excluded as previously reported45 using Chopper v0.7.0. All bioinformatics analyses were conducted in Ubuntu v18.04.5. Aptamers were identified and characterized with Aptasuite v0.9.8 by detecting conserved 5′ and 3′ regions and extracting the 40-nucleotide variable region, as described elsewhere46,47. Briefly, clustering and motif analyses were performed using three approaches: (i) global (for identifying frequent sequences across the dataset), (ii) round-specific (frequent sequences within each round), and (iii) enrichment-based (sequences consistently identified in ≥ 2 rounds). Candidate aptamers were evaluated for folding and secondary structure using the DNA Folding Form tool (www.unafold.org/mfold/applications/dna-folding-form.php) with the following parameters: linear DNA, 37 °C melting temperature, 150 mM Na⁺, and 1 mM Mg²⁺. Based on these results, candidate aptamers were synthesized as unmodified 76-nucleotide ssDNA.

Relative binding evaluation

Binding was evaluated using virus-coated wells (3.0 × 10^4 PFU/well) and non-target controls (culture medium). Aptamers were tested at 50 ng and 100 ng per well. Folded aptamers (50 µL) were added, incubated at 37 °C for 15 min, and recovered with 50 µL nuclease-free water heated at 95 °C. Aptamers were amplified by qPCR in reactions containing 10 µL PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix, 7.5 µL nuclease-free water, 0.25 µL primer F3 (2.5 µM), 0.25 µL primer R3 (2.5 µM), and 2 µL recovered aptamers. Amplification was performed on a 7500 Real-Time PCR System with the following cycling: 50 °C for 2 min, 95 °C for 2 min, 30 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 57 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 1 min. A melting curve was generated at a 1 °C/s ramp from 60 °C to 90 °C. Experiments were performed in triplicate and high positive ∆Ct values were interpreted as the detection of target-specific aptamers.

Development of Apta-qPCR

The assay was adapted from previously described methods48,49, and iteratively refined based on experimental results. To minimize time and cost, all reactions were performed directly in transparent 0.2 mL qPCR plates (Brand; 781373). Each amplification reaction contained 10 µL PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix, 9.5 µL nuclease-free water, 0.25 µL primer F3 (2.5 µM), and 0.25 µL primer R3 (2.5 µM). Amplification was performed on a 7500 Real-Time PCR System under the following cycling conditions: 50 °C for 2 min, 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 57 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 1 min. For assay development, KPL Coating Solution, and two transport media; “UTM ad-bio Viral Transport Medium” (ADBIO; ADMP126-36) and “Universal Transport Medium RT” (COPAN; 3C047N), were evaluated. A wash buffer was prepared in PBS and supplemented with 0.05 M Tris, 0.138 M NaCl, 0.0027 M KCl, and 0.05% Tween-20. The Bio-Speedy RT-qPCR was used as the reference method. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Identification of viral targets

Wet lab:

Recombinant spike subunit 1 (Invitrogen, RP-87679) and envelope protein (Invitrogen, RP-87682) were coated onto polystyrene wells at 100 ng/well and 200 ng/well. Folded aptamers were added to the wells, incubated at 37 °C for 15 min or 30 min, recovered, and amplified by qPCR with PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix. Experiments were performed in triplicate, and Ct values were recorded.

Dry lab:

Aptamer modeling followed a published protocol50. Briefly, secondary structures were predicted using RNAfold WebServer (http://rna.tbi.univie.ac.at/cgi-bin/RNAWebSuite/RNAfold.cgi), substituting thymine residues with uracil in the original sequence. The resulting predicted structure was subsequently submitted to RNAcomposer (https://rnacomposer.cs.put.poznan.pl) to generate a three-dimensional model. The folded RNA structure was then modified by replacing uracil bases with thymine and ribose sugars with deoxyribose. Finally, the structure underwent an energy minimization using the Molecular Operating Environment (MOE) software v.2024.060150,51. The molecular docking protocol was first validated by a redocking experiment utilizing a DNA aptamer in complex with human alpha-thrombin (Protein Data Bank id: 1HAO). Spike is the most structurally accessible viral protein for ligand interaction, exhibits the highest mutational variability across variants, and has also been consistently identified as an aptamer-recognized target in studies employing whole-virus and alternative SELEX approaches15,22–27,52. To note, its interaction with aptamers was previously resolved by cryogenic electron microscopy52. On this basis, spike was selected for docking analysis due to its demonstrated feasibility as a target. Blind docking was executed over the whole structure of spike protein using the MOE software with default parameters, including a protein-protein placement algorithm and rigid body refinement, comprising 10,000 steps in the pre-placement phase, 1,000 steps in the placement phase, and producing 100 refined poses. The RMSD was employed as the validation metric, and the analysis was performed in triplicate. All protein structures were prepared using the QuickPrep module of MOE with default settings. For each aptamer-protein complex, one hundred docking poses were generated and ranked according to the GBVI/WSA ΔG score, a forcefield–based function that approximates the binding free energy of each ligand pose.

Validation using clinical samples

Sample size was calculated based on previously described criteria53,54, with 90% statistical power, a 5% type I error, and reference test sensitivity and specificity of 95% and 100%39, respectively. Apta-qPCR sensitivity and specificity were hypothesized at 80% and 99%, assuming performance comparable to lateral flow assays39. Based on SARS-CoV-2 positivity of 10.9% (from local data at a reference laboratory in Cartagena, Colombia) and applying Yates’ continuity correction, 475 samples were required to assess sensitivity and 373 to assess specificity. To evaluate both parameters, the larger sample size was selected: 52 qPCR- and sequencing-confirmed44,55 SARS-CoV-2-positive samples and 423 qPCR-negative samples. At the time of validation, Omicron BA.1/BA.1.1 was the predominant variant and was therefore selected for testing. Local health authorities in Cartagena, Colombia, collected clinical samples through passive surveillance from individuals with suspected symptomatic COVID-19 during the acute phase of illness. These samples were then transported to the Unidad de Investigación Molecular (UNIMOL), where they were stored at − 80 °C. As a reference laboratory, UNIMOL routinely supported the national SARS-CoV-2 surveillance program led by the Colombian National Institute of Health. For the present study, only deidentified samples were used, following an Institutional Review Board–approved protocol (No. 16330052367) from the Universidad de Cartagena. Exploratory analyses included evaluation of Apta-qPCR performance with other less prevalent Omicron subvariants, analytical specificity against non-SARS-CoV-2 coronaviruses, and intra- and inter-assay variability. Apta-qPCR Ct values were recorded and analyzed.

Statistical analysis

Technical replicates were summarized using means and standard deviations. The optimal cutoff for classifying Apta-qPCR results was determined using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis to maximize sensitivity-specificity balance and correct classification rate. Using routine diagnostic qPCR as the reference, concordance was assessed with the Kappa statistic, and performance was evaluated by calculating sensitivity and specificity. Exact binomial 95% confidence intervals were computed, and all analyses were performed in Stata v18 (StataCorp. 2023. Stata 18 Statistical Software. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC).
