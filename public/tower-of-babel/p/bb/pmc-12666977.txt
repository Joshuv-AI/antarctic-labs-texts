Noninvasive risk stratification falsely reassuring? A catecholamine-sensitive accessory pathway

Introduction

Noninvasive risk stratification for Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) atrioventricular (AV) accessory pathways is recommended in the current supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) guidelines to help clinicians and patients estimate the risk of a fatal arrhythmia related to an accessory pathway without an invasive electrophysiology (EP) study. We present a case of a healthy 42-year-old woman, which highlights the potential pitfalls of relying on noninvasive risk stratification alone.

Case report

A 42-year-old woman presented with recurrent tachypalpitations and unexplained syncope. Her medical history is notable for 4 previous EP studies and ablations. During those procedures, WPW AV accessory pathways were targeted at right posteroseptal and posterolateral locations, as well as both typical and atypical AV nodal reentrant tachycardia, with ablation of the right inferior and left inferior slow pathways. Her previous accessory pathway ablations were limited by mechanical trauma to the pathway, leading to empiric ablation at presumed early sites on the tricuspid annulus. Despite previous ablation attempts, she was noted to have intermittent pre-excitation on resting electrocardiogram (ECG).

Preablation testing included a resting ECG (Figure 1), which demonstrated sinus rhythm at 85 beats per minute with 3 beats of intermittent pre-excitation. During exercise, she was able to increase her heart rate to 170 beats per minute without manifest pre-excitation on ECG. Transthoracic echocardiogram demonstrated a structurally normal heart with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 58% and no significant valvular heart disease.

Resting electrocardiogram demonstrating sinus rhythm with intermittent pre-excitation. Delta wave is + in lead I, isoelectric in lead II, iso in V1, + in V2, and − in aVF and lead III.

Given her recurrent symptoms and a new episode of unexplained syncope, she was brought to the EP laboratory for a fifth procedure. Standard diagnostic catheters were placed. At baseline, she had manifest pre-excitation with a similar delta wave to what was seen on her resting ECG. The delta wave polarity suggested a right posterior accessory pathway.1 Baseline PR was 67 ms, QRS 110 ms, QT 453 ms, and RR 836 ms. HV was measured at −11 ms. As expected, ventricular pacing showed that the earliest atrial signal was along the lateral right atrial catheter. After withdrawal of sedation, the retrograde pathway effective refractory period (ERP) was 560 ms. With atrial pacing close to the pathway, the antegrade pathway ERP was 460 ms. These measurements were consistent with a lower risk pathway.

We then continued the EP study with isoproterenol testing (0.04 μG/kg/min). Atrial pacing showed consistent pathway conduction down to 210 ms (Figure 2). Atrial fibrillation was induced, and the shortest pre-excited R to R interval was 243 ms. Given the robust antegrade conduction properties of the pathway while on isoproterenol, this was now deemed a high-risk pathway and mapping and ablation were pursued.

We performed delta wave mapping while pacing close to the pathway on the lateral right atrium. The earliest ventricular signal was recorded 42 ms before the onset of the QRS and was located off the septum at the 7:30–8:00-o’clock position along the tricuspid annulus as seen in the left anterior oblique view. At this site, a complex electrogram was observed, consistent with a potential accessory pathway signal. Irrigated radiofrequency ablation was delivered at 30 W (30 mL/min irrigation flow) at this location resulting in the elimination of pathway conduction within 1.6 seconds. Postablation monitoring and a repeat EP study were performed without return of pathway conduction in either direction, and no additional SVT was inducible.

Discussion

Manifest pre-excitation on an ECG suggestive of an accessory pathway is found in between 1 and 3 per 1000 individuals in the general population.2 Accessory pathways can bypass normal AV conduction through the AV node and His-Purkinje system, potentially allowing for rapid conduction to the ventricle, which may precipitate a fatal arrhythmia. Accordingly, when manifest pre-excitation is noted, risk stratification is indicated, even in asymptomatic individuals.3

The 2015 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association/Heart Rhythm Society guidelines for the management of adult patients with SVT recommend that intermittent loss of pre-excitation on ECG is a class I indication for identifying low-risk accessory pathways.3 EP study for invasive risk stratification in the setting of manifest pre-excitation is a class IIa indication, with catheter ablation indicated if a high-risk pathway (accessory pathway ERP of ≤240 ms or the “Shortest Pre-Excited R to R Interval” of ≤250 ms) is found even in the absence of symptoms or documented SVT.3

Our patient exhibited intermittent manifest pre-excitation on a resting ECG and during an exercise stress test. Based on the guideline criteria, this pattern might suggest a relatively low-risk accessory pathway. However, during EP study, the pathway exhibited significant catecholamine sensitivity with robust antegrade conduction down to 210 ms, clearly meeting criteria for a high-risk pathway, which could lead to a fatal arrhythmia in the future.

One important clinical feature of our patient was the incidence of a syncopal episode. It is impossible to definitively know the precipitating reason for her syncope, but the occurrence of syncope in a patient with a WPW pattern should give the clinician a very high degree of suspicion for a high-risk accessory pathway. In our view, suspicious syncope in the setting of WPW should prompt an invasive risk stratification strategy. Furthermore, isoproterenol or an adjunctive agent should strongly be considered during any invasive risk stratification procedure to ensure that the full properties of an accessory pathway are assessed.

Understanding the physiology of an accessory pathway is crucial. The amount of pre-excitation at any given time is dependent on the relative conduction properties between the accessory pathway and the AV node. Pre-excitation is manifest when conduction occurs relatively faster over the pathway than the AV node; that is, the pathway “wins the race.” When using an exercise ECG for risk stratification, one must distinguish “abrupt loss of pre-excitation” from a gradual transition. Physiologically, conduction through the AV node increases during exercise owing to fluctuations in adrenergic tone. Conduction may then preferentially travel through the normal conduction system, and the ECG may exhibit slow or gradual loss of pre-excitation. The idea behind the abrupt loss of pre-excitation is that when the sinus rate reaches and exceeds the pathway ERP, QRS fusion between the AV node and the accessory pathway abruptly switches to only AV node conduction within 1 beat. We did not see this clear abrupt loss of pre-excitation on our patient’s exercise stress test, and therefore, this would not be deemed a useful prognosticator for future risk. Intermittent loss of pre-excitation on a resting ECG or ambulatory monitoring may suggest weak conduction over the pathway, especially with lower heart rates. However, this too may be falsely reassuring, owing to the same physiological phenomenon.

This phenomenon has been well described in the pediatric EP literature, where loss of pre-excitation during noninvasive testing is a less sensitive method to exclude high-risk pathways.4 In the current case report, we illustrate these findings in an adult patient. Although the pediatric EP community has appreciated the nuances surrounding noninvasive risk assessment, a similar understanding may be lacking in the adult EP community.

Conclusion

We describe this case to outline (1) the importance of careful interpretation of intermittent pre-excitation and (2) the pitfalls of relying on noninvasive testing alone for ruling out high-risk accessory pathways. Symptomatic patients with a previous syncopal episode and WPW pattern should strongly be considered for invasive testing with adrenergic provocation, even when noninvasive testing is reassuring. This will potentially mitigate high-risk pathway-related adverse outcomes in the future.
