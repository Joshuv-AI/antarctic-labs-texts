Role and importance of quality control in improving compliance of enhanced recovery after surgery to overcome postoperative complications
Authors: In Kyeong Kim, Jung Hoon Bae, Soo Ji Park, Yoon Suk Lee, In Kyu Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEnhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) has been proven to reduce postoperative complication and length of hospital stay. However, implementation varies among centers, and not all protocols can be applied uniformly in clinical practice. We aimed to evaluate the role and importance of overall ERAS compliance through quality control rather than assessing the appropriateness of individual components.MethodsPatients who underwent colon cancer surgery were stratified into two phases based on quality control implementation: Phase I (January 2017 to November 2019) and Phase II (December 2019 to December 2022). More stringent compliance criteria were implemented in Phase II. We performed propensity score matching by age, ASA score, and tumor location to reduce the selection bias.ResultsMean ERAS compliance showed no significant difference between phases (phase I vs. II, 77.28 ± 11.66 vs. 77.79 ± 11.99, P = 0.498). Total complications significantly decreased (phase I vs. II, 78 (16.1%) vs. 48 (9.9%), p = 0.005), with non-surgical complications such as postoperative ileus, respiratory complications significantly lower in Phase II. However, major complication showed no significant difference between phases. Multivariate analysis revealed that high compliance (≥ 70%) and Phase II were associated with lower complications and significantly reduced lengths of stay. Several surgical stress-related inflammatory markers showed significantly reduced changes from Phase I to Phase II, suggesting that the postoperative stress response can be attenuated through ERAS implementation and reinforcement.ConclusionsAlthough ERAS protocol compliance cannot prevent major surgery-related complications, high compliance through overall application enables earlier recovery and shorter hospital stays for patients with major complications by reducing surgical stress-related inflammatory responses.Keywords: Enhanced recovery after surgery, Perioperative care, Postoperative complications, Colon cancer

Introduction

Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) are widely applied after colorectal cancer surgery to optimize physiological recovery from surgical stress [1–3]. Good adherence to ERAS, as outlined in the ERAS Society guidelines for perioperative care in elective colorectal surgery [4], could reduce the incidence of postoperative complications and the length of hospital stays (LOS), facilitating reincorporation into daily life [5–8]. These benefits have been demonstrated in colorectal, gastric, lung, and other cancers [9, 10]. Moreover, high adherence may be associated with improved long-term survival in patients with colorectal cancer [11–13].

Some surgeons have questioned the causal relationship between ERAS compliance and postoperative complications. First, compliance may be considered a consequence rather than a cause of postoperative outcomes. It remains questionable whether high ERAS compliance prevents complications, or whether patients without complications are simply more capable of adhering to ERAS protocols. Second, whether compliance can affect surgery-related complications, such as anastomotic leakage, bleeding, or deep organ-space infections remain unclear [13–16].

Our institution is a well-established and active center that has implemented 25 items of ERAS protocols since 2017. Continuous audit and data collection has been conducted since the protocol’s implementation. By analyzing the effect of auditing, our study sought to analyze the relationship between ERAS compliance and postoperative complications [17].

We hypothesized a mechanism by which enhanced quality control and ERAS implementation reduce postoperative complications through three integrated pathways. Systematic optimization of perioperative care (pain control, early mobilization, nutrition) that reduces surgical stress response, attenuated postoperative inflammatory response, and accelerated recovery even in presence of major complications. We predicted that improved compliance quality control between Phase I and Phase II would demonstrate this mechanism through significantly reduced inflammatory biomarkers and faster recovery, despite similar overall compliance percentages. Furthermore, rather than focusing on individual protocol items or simple compliance scores, we aimed to determine the effect of enhanced ERAS implementation on patient outcomes by implementing more stringent monitoring and quality control criteria.

Materials and methods

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Catholic University of Korea, Seoul St. Mary’s Hospital, Seoul, Republic of Korea (No. KC23RISI0717). All individual information was anonymized before data processing, and the study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to the retrospective study, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Implementation and improvement of ERAS

In 2016, our colorectal team designed and developed a multidisciplinary ERAS program including surgeons, anesthesiologists, ward nurses, ERAS-managing nurses, nutritionists, and project coordinators. From January 2017, the ERAS protocol, which comprises 22 items, was applied to all patients who underwent colon cancer surgery. In November 2017, these items were modified and administered to patients with rectal cancer. Annual audits checking for compliance and quality control were conducted with continuous feedback.

Through continuous monitoring and feedback, ERAS items such as perioperative pain control, timing of urinary catheter and drainage tube removal [18], and perioperative fluid restriction [19] were modified and improved with enhanced quality control measures emphasizing earlier removal protocols. Significant modifications and strengthened criteria of compliance were implemented in November 2019, and establishing 25 items.

Patients treated after November 2019 were classified as ‘phase II’ with reinforced quality control criteria. The most significant change was the addition of multimodal pain control systems pre-, intra-, and post-operatively [20, 21]. Patients and data collection

We prospectively collected data on all consecutive patients undergoing elective colon cancer surgery at Seoul St. Mary’s Hospital between January 2017 and September 2022. These prospectively recorded clinical data were then analyzed retrospectively to compare two distinct phases of ERAS implementation.

Based on the November 2019 protocol marking a significant change in criteria, patients were divided into Phase I (treated between January 2017 and November 2019, N = 488) and Phase II groups (treated between December 2019 and December 2022, N = 521).

Patients who did not consent to the ERAS protocol, had incomplete records, underwent a diverting enterostomy, had rectal cancer, or underwent emergency or palliative surgery were excluded. A total of 1,009 patients were included in this study.

The ERAS compliance rate (%) was defined as the ratio of satisfied items to the total number of implemented items. Compliance ≥ 70% was considered high compliance (HC), and < 70% was considered low compliance (LC).

Postoperative complications within 30 days of surgery were evaluated. Each complication was classified according to the Clavien-Dindo Classification (CDC), and all complications per patients were weighted using the Comprehensive Complication Index (CCI), with scores ranging from 0 to 100. Patients were divided into major (CDC ≥ 3) and minor (CDC < 3) complication groups. Major complications (CDC ≥ 3) require surgical, endoscopic, or radiological intervention, while minor complications (CDC < 3) can be managed with conservative treatment [22, 23].

Routine blood tests were performed before surgery, immediately after surgery, and on postoperative days 1 and 3 or 4. The neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was calculated as the simple ratio between the neutrophil and lymphocyte counts in peripheral blood and serves as a marker of physiological stress and systemic inflammation. NLR has been shown to be independently correlated with mortality. Normal NLR are approximately 1–3, values of 6–9 suggest mild stress, and values ≥ 9 occurs in critically ill patients [24, 25].

The time to normalize the white blood cell (WBC) count was calculated as the time required to reach a normal value of 4,000–10,000/mm3 in a postoperative blood test.

The modified Glasgow prognostic score (mGPS), based on serum concentrations of C-reactive protein (CRP) and serum albumin, reflects systemic inflammation and nutritional status [26 ,27]. The mGPS was calculated based on hypoalbuminemia (< 3.5 g/L) and/or elevated CRP (> 10 mg/L), with scores ranging from 0 to 2 (both normal, 0; one abnormal, 1; both abnormal, 2).

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcome was the effect of strengthened quality control measures on postoperative outcomes between Phases I and Phase II. Secondary outcomes included LOS, readmission rates, mortality, and postoperative complications categorized according to severity. To determine how HC affected patients with major complications, LOS was analyzed according to patients with major and minor complications. The relationship between postoperative inflammation-related markers and compliance was also analyzed.

Statistical analysis

Demographic, surgical, and postoperative data were retrospectively collected from electronic records. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software, version 24.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics®, Armonk, NY, USA). Between-group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test, Pearson’s chi-square test, and Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate for the data type and distribution. Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical data and the Mann–Whitney U-test was used for continuous data that did not meet normality assumptions.

To reduce selection bias in this retrospective comparative analysis between the two sequential treatment phases, 1:1 propensity score matching analysis (PSM) was conducted. Propensity scores were calculated using a non-parsimonious logistic regression model based on pre-defined covariates including Age, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) scores, and tumor location.

Univariate and multivariate statistical analyses were performed. For univariate analysis, Pearson’s chi-squared and Fisher’s exact tests were applied for discrete variables, and Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test for continuous variables, depending on normality. For multivariate analysis, logistic regression was performed using the least-squares method.

The analyzed independent variables were included due to a P value of 0.05 or lesser on simple regression analysis with 1 independent variable. Subsequently, we performed stepwise backwards variable elimination with threshold P value > 0.05. P values of < 0.05 were considered as statistically significant.

Results

Participants and clinical characteristics

Patients in Phase II were significantly older (≥ 65 years) and had more comorbidities (ASA score ≥ 3) than patients in Phase I (Phase I vs. II: 258 (52.9%) vs. 308 (59.1%), P = 0.046, and 19 (3.9%) vs. 59 (11.3%), P < 0.001, respectively). The majority of surgical approaches involved minimally invasive surgery (MIS), and no significant differences were observed in other organ resections or intraoperative transfusions. After PSM, 483 patients from each phase were selected for analysis. Age, ASA score, and tumor location showed no significant difference between Phase I and Phase II (Table 1).

Compliance with ERAS protocols

The overall rate of ERAS compliance was 77.50%. However, there was no significant difference between the Phase I and Phase II groups (Phase I vs. Phase II: 77.28% vs. 77.79%, P = 0.498) (Table 2). The compliance rate for each item, and how it changed between Phases I and Phase II were analyzed. There was increased compliance with the use of prophylactic antibiotics, intraoperative and postoperative nausea and vomiting prophylaxis, intraoperative pain control, laxative use, postoperative pain control, early mobilization, and early diet resumption on the day of surgery. However, there was decreased compliance with perioperative counseling, intraoperative fluid restriction, and items with strengthened criteria, such as chewing gum, early intravenous line removal, and surgical drain removal (Table 2; Fig. 1).

Clinical postoperative outcomes

The mean operative time was slightly shorter in Phase II (Phase I vs. Phase II: 179.31 ± 58.67 min vs. 171.63 ± 52.13 min, P = 0.032); however, the estimated blood loss was higher in the Phase II group (64.42 ± 65.82 ml vs. 99.67 ± 160.78 ml, P < 0.001).

The LOS was not significantly different between the two groups (5.03 ± 3.62 vs. 5.12 ± 3.97, P = 0.722) (Table 3). Quarterly LOS trends throughout the study period were analyzed. Since ERAS implementation in January 2017, LOS has gradually decreased from an initial average of approximately 7–8 days to 5 days in Phase II (Fig. 2), demonstrating continuous improvement through enhanced ERAS implementation and quality control measures.

Total complications were significantly lower in Phase II compared with Phase I (78 (16.1%) vs. 48(9.9%), P = 0.005). According to the CCI score-based severity, the mean CCI was lower in Phase II within 30 days after surgery. Patients with CCI < 26 were significantly reduced in Phase II, but there was no difference in severe complications (CCI ≥ 26). Readmission rates (12 (2.5%) vs. 8 (1.7%), P = 0.499) and overall mortality (Phase I vs. Phase II: 2 (0.4%) vs. 0 (0.0%), P = 0.499) were not significantly different.

When classified by type of postoperative complication, minor complications (CDC < 3) including wound complication, postoperative ileus, and respiratory complications, decreased significantly in Phase II compared with Phase I. However, there was no significant difference in major complications related to surgical problem (CDC ≥ 3) (Table 3).

Univariate and multivariate analysis of risk factors about postoperative LOS and complication

Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed to identify the risk factors for total complications and LOS. In the univariate analysis for factors related to LOS, gender, tumor stage ≥ 3, surgical approach, operative time, HC (≥ 70%), and Phase II were associated. In the multivariate analysis, MIS (odds ratio [OR] 0.428; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.198–0.924, P = 0.031) and HC (≥ 70%) (OR 0.213; CI, 0.153–0.297, P < 0.001) were factors associated with decreased LOS. Mean operative time (≥ 176 min) (OR 2.167; 95% CI, 1.637–2.869, P < 0.001) and tumor stage (OR 1.442; 95% CI, 1.087–1.911, p = 0.011) were factors associated with increased LOS (Supplement Table 2).

For total complications, age ≥ 65, female gender, surgical approach, compliance, and Phase II were significantly associated with complications in both univariate and multivariate analyses (Supplement Table 3). These results show that HC (≥ 70%) was a factor that reduced both postoperative complications and LOS.

HC was consistently associated with significantly shorter LOS across all complication groups. In the overall analysis, HC reduced LOS in CCI ≥ 26 (8.95 ± 8.35 vs. 16.68 ± 11.48 days, P = 0.017), CCI < 26 (6.17 ± 3.79 vs. 10.18 ± 5.14 days, p < 0.001), and all complications (7.03 ± 5.69 vs. 12.68 ± 8.70 days, P < 0.001).

However, in severe complications, the benefits did not reach statistical significance within individual phases. These findings indicate that high ERAS compliance reduces hospital stay duration in patients with complications, with the most consistent benefits observed in patients with minor complications.

In all patients, HC was associated with significantly lower NL ratio (3.80 ± 2.65 vs. 5.23 ± 3.64, p < 0.001), shorter WBC normalization period (1.71 ± 1.39 vs. 2.16 ± 1.56 days, p < 0.001) and higher proportion of patients with favorable mGPS scores (46.8% vs. 21.1%, p < 0.001) compared to LC. In patients with CCI ≥ 26, no significant differences were observed in any inflammatory markers between HC and LC groups (NL ratio p = 0.388, WBC normalization p = 0.659, mGPS p = 0.732. In patients with CCI < 26, HC showed a trend toward lower NL ratio (P = 0.057) and significantly higher proportion of favorable mGPS scores (42.4% vs. 13.3%, p = 0.002) compared to LC.

Phase-specific analysis revealed that HC consistently improved inflammatory markers in both Phase I and Phase II, with significant reductions in NL ratio (P < 0.001 for both phases) and WBC normalization period (Phase I: P = 0.003, Phase II: P = 0.006). The benefit on mGPS scores was significant only in Phase II (P < 0.001). These findings suggest that high ERAS compliance reduces postoperative inflammatory responses, particularly in patients with minor complication.

Discussion

The importance of ERAS compliance has been well established and is known to reduce postoperative complications and LOS [3, 28, 29]. However, the relationship between ERAS and postoperative outcomes has been subject to ongoing debate regarding causality. Our study addresses this fundamental question by examining the impact of enhanced quality control measures on postoperative outcomes. Our findings shed light on the crucial role of ERAS protocol implementation on postoperative complications through inflammatory responses.

A critical finding of our study was that despite similar overall compliance rates between Phase I and Phase II (77.28% vs. 77.79%, P = 0.498), significant improvements in postoperative outcomes were observed in Phase II. This paradox provides compelling evidence that systematic quality improvement, rather than numerical compliance scores alone, drives better outcomes. Phase II included higher-risk patients with more comorbidities and older age. After propensity score matching, patient characteristics were balanced, yet the superior outcomes in Phase II persisted, eliminating patient selection as a confounding factor and supporting a causal relationship between enhanced quality control and improved outcomes. The maintenance of high compliance despite implementing more stringent monitoring criteria and enhanced quality control measures underscores the importance of systematic implementation over simple metric achievement.

Our analysis revealed that ERAS primarily affect non-surgical complications while having limited impact on major surgical complications. Total complications significantly decreased, with notable reductions in minor complications including postoperative ileus and respiratory complications. However, major complications (CDC ≥ 3) showed no significant difference between phases. This differential effect supports the mechanistic understanding that ERAS target perioperative physiological optimization—pain management, early mobilization, optimized nutrition—rather than technical surgical factors. While ERAS cannot prevent anastomotic leakage or bleeding, it effectively reduces complications related to postoperative care and recovery processes.

The relationship between ERAS compliance and outcomes among patients with complications provides critical evidence for causality. High compliance consistently reduced LOS across all complication groups. Crucially, Phase II patients with high compliance demonstrated significantly shorter hospital stays across all complication groups (P = 0.003), while Phase I showed limited benefits. This phase-specific difference confirms that enhanced quality control amplifies the benefits of compliance, providing strong evidence that systematic quality improvement, not just individual patient adherence, drives superior outcomes.

Among specific ERAS components, multimodal pain control emerged as a key contributor to improved outcomes in phase II. The introduction of comprehensive pain management protocols—including preoperative oral NSAIDs, intrathecal morphine injection [20, 21], and transverse abdominis plane blocks [30]—reduced pain scores and facilitated earlier mobilization and faster recovery, demonstrating how targeted quality improvements within individual ERAS components contribute to overall success.

Finally, ERAS compliance was associated with postoperative stress-related inflammatory responses. Our institution had previously conducted a study comparing ERAS with conventional care. In the ERAS group, the postoperative WBC count and time to normalization were significantly reduced, along with lower NLR and CRP levels [31]. The inflammatory marker analysis provides objective biological evidence supporting the causal relationship between ERAS compliance and improved outcomes. HC was consistently associated with reduced inflammatory responses: lower NL ratio, shorter WBC normalization period, and improved mGPS scores. These benefits were maintained across both phases, with particularly pronounced effects in Phase II where quality control was enhanced. Importantly, these objective biomarkers eliminate the possibility of subjective bias and provide mechanistic insight into how ERAS reduces postoperative stress responses.

Our findings have important implications for healthcare institutions implementing ERAS protocols. The emphasis should shift from achieving arbitrary compliance percentages to establishing robust quality control systems with enhanced monitoring, continuous auditing, and systematic feedback mechanisms. The gradual reduction in LOS from 8 days in 2017 to stable 4–5 days demonstrates the importance of institutional learning curves and continuous quality improvement. Even temporary increases in LOS during safety protocol implementation and system adaptations reflect appropriate prioritization of quality over metrics.

The differential effectiveness based on complication severity suggests that ERAS protocols should be viewed as perioperative optimization tools rather than surgical complication prevention strategies. This understanding helps set appropriate expectations and focuses implementation efforts on areas where ERAS provides maximum benefit.

This study’s primary strength lies in addressing the fundamental causality question regarding ERAS compliance and outcomes through a controlled comparison of quality control approaches while maintaining similar compliance rates. The unique methodology of analyzing enhanced quality control measures rather than simple compliance metrics provides valuable insights into what truly drives improved postoperative outcomes. The use of objective inflammatory markers, propensity score matching to control for patient characteristics, and analysis of higher-risk patients in Phase II provides robust evidence supporting causal relationships rather than mere correlations.

Our findings clarify the scope and limitations of ERAS effectiveness. While ERAS cannot prevent major surgical complications such as anastomotic leakage, bleeding, or deep organ-space infections, they consistently improve recovery processes through reduced inflammatory responses and optimized perioperative care. Even when major complications occur, high compliance facilitates faster recovery and shorter hospital stays, demonstrating that ERAS benefits extend beyond complication prevention to recovery optimization.

This study has several limitations. This study was conducted at a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other settings. Although the data was collected prospectively, the analysis was performed retrospectively, which may introduce potential selection and information biases. Future multi-center prospective studies examining different quality control approaches and long-term outcomes would strengthen the evidence base and establish broader applicability of these findings.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that enhanced compliance of ERAS through quality control are the key drivers of improved clinical postoperative outcomes. While ERAS cannot prevent major surgical complications, it consistently improves perioperative care and accelerates recovery when implemented within robust monitoring frameworks. These findings emphasize that systematic quality improvement, not simple metric achievement, optimizes patient outcome.
