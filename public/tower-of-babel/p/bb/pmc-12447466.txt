Intensive reassortment and frequent intercontinental transmission revealed by long-term genetic analysis of H10 avian influenza viruses in Taiwan
Authors: Wan-Chen Li, Chiu-Yen Chang, Yu-Pin Liu, Li-Hsuan Chen, Yu-Nong Gong, Yu-Ju Lin, Guang-Wu Chen, Shin-Ru Shih
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTH10 subtype avian influenza viruses primarily circulate among wild waterfowl but can occasionally infect mammals, including humans, and recent sporadic human cases have raised significant public health concerns. In this study, we sequenced and analysed 59 H10 subtype viruses isolated from wild birds in Taiwan. Results showed that all isolates were genetically distinct from human and other mammalian H10 subtype isolates. Taiwanese isolates exhibited high genetic diversity and could be categorized into 34 distinct genotypes, with each genotype circulating only in a single migratory season and not recurring during subsequent seasons. Additional analyses revealed that certain gene pools frequently circulate in the Pacific Rim, with evidence of North American lineage genes establishing long-term populations in Eurasia and vice versa. Although no characteristics indicative of mammalian adaptation was found in the Taiwanese isolates, temporal changes in the haemagglutinin cleavage site sequences were observed. This study provides a comprehensive overview of the evolutionary dynamics of H10 avian influenza viruses isolated from wild birds in Taiwan, emphasizing the complexity of intercontinental gene flow and viral reassortment. Currently, no consistent molecular markers indicating the impact of H10 avian influenza viruses on mammals or poultry have been identified. Notably, the observed changes in the haemagglutinin cleavage site sequences among Eurasian viruses suggest potential antigenic variations, indicating a need for further investigation into these changes. This potential highlights the necessity of ongoing surveillance to track the evolution and transmission dynamics of these viruses.KEYWORDS: Avian influenza, H10 subtype, intercontinental transmission, reassortment, wild bird

Introduction

Avian influenza viruses (AIVs), members of the Orthomyxoviridae family, have been identified in a diverse range of wild and domestic avian species. Their natural reservoirs are primarily wild waterfowl, with 16 haemagglutinin (HA) and 9 neuraminidase (NA) subtypes detected in avian species. The H10 subtype of AIV is widely present in wild waterfowl and generally does not pose a threat to poultry. However, the H10 AIVs can occasionally infect mammals, leading to increased mortality rates. For instance, an outbreak of H10N4 AIV in Swedish mink farms in 1984 and another of H10N7 AIV in seals in northern Europe in 2014 led to high mortality rates [1–5]. Interspecies transmission among mammals may drive viral adaptation, raising public concern [4].

H10 AIVs can enter the poultry industry, and the circulation of AIVs within poultry populations may lead to zoonotic transmission. The first documented human infection with an H10 AIV occurred in Egypt in 2004, followed by cases of H10N7 infection among abattoir workers in Australia in 2010, where only mild clinical symptoms were observed [6,7]. However, more severe and even fatal cases have since been reported in China, including H10N8 in 2013–2014, H10N3 in 2021–2022 and H10N5 in 2023, raising increasing public health concerns [8–10]. Prior contact with domestic poultry in these human cases suggested an avian-to-human transmission route. While H10 AIVs have shown intraspecies transmission within mammals such as minks and seals, no human-to-human transmission has been documented.

Taiwan is situated at the midpoint in the East Asian–Australasian Flyway. This flyway is a major migratory route for waterfowl and encompasses the regions where the human H10N8 and H10N3 cases in China and H10N7 cases in Australia were documented. This geographical relationship increases the potential risks these viruses pose in Taiwan. Additionally, the East Asian–Australasian Flyway intersects with the Pacific Flyway, located on the west coast of the Americas, which may enhance the genetic diversity of AIVs between these flyways. However, the genetic diversity of H10 AIVs and Taiwan’s role in their evolution have not been studied.

Given the concerns about high-risk H10 AIVs, especially the lethal isolates, this study aimed to investigate the potential for their impacts in Taiwan via migratory wild birds. We examined the phylogenetic relationships, diversity, and dynamics of H10 AIVs collected from wild bird surveillance in Taiwan between 1998 and 2017. Additionally, the genetic characteristics of these H10 AIVs and their implications for the risks posed to both the poultry industry and public health were investigated.

Materials and methods

Sample collection, virus isolation and virus identification

Avian influenza surveillance in wild birds in Taiwan began in 1998 and has since been conducted annually, from October to April of the following year. Sampling sites include various wetlands across Taiwan and the islets of Penghu and Kinmen. Samples were collected from wild waterbirds and included fresh faecal droppings and carcasses retrieved during field surveillance. All H10 viruses isolated in this study originated from faecal samples. The samples were submitted to the Veterinary Research Institute, Ministry of Agriculture, with a cold chain.

For virus isolation, the samples were inoculated into the allantoic cavities of 10-day-old embryonated chicken eggs. Virus activity in allantoic fluid collected from the inoculated eggs was screened by the haemagglutination test. Positive samples were subjected to nucleic acid extraction using the MagNA Pure Compact Nucleic Acid Isolation Kit I (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) and were subsequently tested for the matrix protein (M) gene of influenza A virus using real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT–PCR) [11]. The HA and NA subtypes were identified by RT-PCRs with specific primers [12–14].

Genome sequencing and dataset generation

The complete viral genome sequences of isolated H10 subtype AIVs were amplified by RT-PCRs with primer sets specifically designed for this study. The primer sequences are available upon request. The RT-PCRs were performed using the SuperScript III One-Step RT–PCR System with Platinum Taq Polymerase High Fidelity (Invitrogen, Life Technologies, Carlsbad, California, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Amplicons of expected length were sequenced by a commercial sequencing service (Mission Biotech, Taipei City, Taiwan) using a 3700xl DNA Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, California, USA). The sequences of H10 AIVs in this study were deposited in the database of the Global Initiative on Sharing All Influenza Data (GISAID; https://gisaid.org) with accession numbers EPI_ISL_19052930–EPI_ISL_19052988 (Supplementary S1).

Phylogenetic analyses, genotype categorization, and visualization of geographic distribution

For phylogenetic analyses, datasets were constructed for each gene segment of the viruses, comprising sequences from the Taiwanese H10 isolates and those retrieved from the GenBank (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genbank) and GISAID databases. The datasets from the public databases included HA gene sequences of H10 AIVs from 1998 to 2018, the top 100 closely related sequences for the six internal and NA gene, and sequences from mammalian-associated isolates, including human H10N8 and seal H10N7 viruses. Due to the sequences of the Australian human H10N7 isolates being incomplete, analogous sequences from local poultry isolates were employed based on their close relationships to the HA gene of the human isolates.

Maximum-likelihood phylogenetic trees for each gene segment were constructed in RAxML v8.2.12 with a general time-reversible nucleotide substitution model and 1000 bootstrap iterations to assess reliability. When multiple nucleotide sequences from the same year and country/state/province clustered together, only one or two sequences were selected as representatives. Phylogenetic trees were annotated using MEGA 7 (https://www.megasoftware.net), and monophyletic groups were named according to the number of Taiwanese isolates within each group. The genotypes of H10 AIVs from Taiwan were classified according to their phylogenetic clustering across eight gene segments.

Following the phylogenetic analyses, viruses within the datasets containing gene segments identified as having the potential for intercontinental transmission (Supplementary S1 and S3) were mapped to their geographic locations. The distribution of these viruses was visualized using the Microreact online service (https://microreact.org/) to highlight potential intercontinental transmission events and their predominant distribution areas.

Molecular analysis

To examine the viruses’ molecular traits, sequence alignment was performed using the ClustalW method available in MEGA 7. Nucleotide sequences were converted to deduced amino acid sequences. The deduced amino acid sequences of HA were then converted to the H3 numbering system using the HA subtype conversion tool available in the Bacterial and Viral Bioinformatics Resource Center (https://www.bv-brc.org).

Results

H10 avian influenza viruses from wild birds

Between 1998 and 2017, 79,504 samples were collected from wild birds and tested, and 66 H10 AIV isolates were thus obtained. Seven of these isolates were excluded due to the detection of more than one HA or NA subtype within a sample (Supplementary S1). A peak in H10 AIV isolations in 2006 was revealed during the surveillance period, with 12 isolates detected (Figure 1). With regard to viral subtypes, H10N3 (21 isolates; 31.8%) and H10N7 (24 isolates; 36.4%) were the most frequently identified. Subtype H10N3 was commonly found in 2003 and 2006, whereas H10N7 became the predominant subtype after 2007. The subtypes H10N1, H10N4, H10N5, H10N6, H10N8, and H10N9 were isolated at lower frequencies, with each yielding between two and three isolates. No H10N2 viruses were detected.

Phylogenetic analyses of Taiwanese H10 avian influenza viruses

Phylogenetic analysis of the HA gene indicated that the Taiwanese isolates clustered into two groups: the Eurasian lineage (EAL), comprising 55 isolates, and a smaller North American lineage (NAL), designated “WB2961,” which included 4 isolates with high sequence homology across all genes (Figure 2(A)). Within the EAL, isolates displayed a temporal distribution and formed three distinct subgroups. The human H10N8 and poultry H10 viruses identified in eastern China in 2013–2014 were clustered into a monophyletic group that was distinct from contemporary wild bird viruses (Figure 2(B) and Supplementary S2).

NA gene analysis revealed all N1, N3, N4, N5, N6, and N9 genes of Taiwanese isolates belonged to the EAL. Most N7 genes also fell within the EAL, except for those of WB2961, which grouped under the NAL. Moreover, the N3 phylogenetic tree further revealed a close NA gene relationship between Taiwanese wild bird H10N3 AIVs (late 2015) and the H5N3 high pathogenicity avian influenza (HPAI) viruses that devastated Taiwanese poultry in early 2015 (Supplementary S2).

Our analyses indicated significant genetic diversity among the internal genes (Figure 3 and Supplementary S2). The internal genes, PB2 (polymerase basic protein 2), PB1 (polymerase basic protein 1), PA (polymerase acidic protein), NP (nucleoprotein), M (matrix protein), and NS (non-structural protein) of the Taiwanese isolates, were classified into four, five, nine, seven, two, and two groups, respectively, in the corresponding phylogenetic trees (Supplementary S3). The two NS groups corresponded to NS allele A and B lineages, with 96.6% of Taiwanese H10 isolates belonging to allele A and 3.4% to allele B. Within NS allele A, the Taiwanese H10 viruses appeared to exhibit relatively limited diversification in the phylogenetic analysis compared to other gene segments. This pattern may be attributed to the inclusion of Australian H10N7 viruses and Chinese human H10N8 viruses, which may have led to a visually tighter clustering of the Taiwanese isolates in the phylogenetic tree. Furthermore, the NS gene sequences did not show well-supported subclade structures and were therefore not further subdivided in our subgroup classification. Additionally, the internal genes, excluding the M gene, of the clade 2.3.4.4 H5 subtype viruses were closely related to wild bird viruses in Eurasia.

These analyses (Figure 3, Supplementary S2 and S3) also revealed bidirectional intercontinental gene flow of the AIVs. Most groups of the internal genes were part of the EAL, with one group each for the PB1 and PA genes belonging to the NAL. Certain genes belonging to the EAL occasionally emerged in North America, such as PB2 group 1, PB1 group 3, NP group 3, and M group 2. Furthermore, PA group 7 indicated the presence of a long-term NAL population in Asia, while, in contrast, PA group 6 suggested the establishment of a long-term EAL population in North America.

The representative isolate WB2961 (A/Duck/Taiwan/WB2961/2013) shared high nucleotide identity (98.7% – 99.5%) with the viruses from North America and East Asia (Supplementary S4). Its HA (NAL), NA (NAL), and PA (EAL) genes were closely related to the viruses from the west coast of North America. Its remaining gene segments exhibited close relationships with viruses from East Asia. In addition, except for the NA gene, nearly all genes of WB2961 and the Australian H10N7 viruses belonged to the same monophyletic group in their corresponding phylogenetic trees, although there were long branches between the Australian isolates and the viruses from wild birds (Figures 2 and 3 and Supplementary S2).

The phylogenetic analyses revealed that most genomes of Taiwanese H10 isolates belonged to the EAL, exhibiting close genetic relationships with viruses from East Asia, particularly those from Japan and Korea. Some Taiwanese isolates contained partial NAL genes that were genetically close to those from North American west coast viruses. Notably, none of the Taiwanese isolates showed close phylogenetic relationships with mammalian-related viruses, such as human H10N8, Australian H10N7, or seal H10N7. The internal genes of human H10N8 viruses and all genes of Australian H10N7 viruses exhibited long evolutionary branches relative to their wild bird counterparts. Within the scope of this study, seal-associated H10N7 viruses – phylogenetically linked to European wild bird lineages – showed no detectable evidence of reintroduction into avian reservoirs or geographical spread to other regions (Supplementary S2).

Genotypic analysis of Taiwanese H10 AIVs and their intercontinental transmission

A genotype chart (Figure 4) of the studied Taiwanese H10 AIVs was constructed based on the phylogenetic relationships of the eight gene segments, and 34 distinct genotypes were discovered. No genotypes were observed to recur in subsequent bird wintering seasons. The number of identified genotypes varied from one to eight during each wintering season. The period from November 2015 to March 2016 showed the greatest diversity, as it included eight genotypes among the 11 isolates collected during that migratory season. Since 2013, the genomic composition of Taiwanese H10 viruses has diverged significantly from previous patterns, particularly in the PB2, PA, NP, and M genes, which shifted to previously unassociated phylogenetic groups.

Twelve (20%) of the Taiwanese H10 isolates were found to carry NAL genes. Among these isolates, four H10N7 isolates, known as WB2961, harboured two NAL genes (HA and NA), while the remaining isolates carried single NAL genes.

The phylogenetic analyses identified specific groups of gene segments with the potential for intercontinental transmission (Supplementary S2 and S3). The locations of viruses possessing these genes segments were mapped, as illustrated in Figure 5, and the map demonstrated their predominant distribution in the Pacific Rim region. The mapping revealed notable instances of intercontinental transmission events in northeastern Asia and North America, particularly in Alaska (Figure 5).

Molecular characterization

Amino acid sequence comparison of the receptor-binding sites of HA protein in Taiwanese H10 AIVs demonstrated that these AIVs retained characteristic avian properties, particularly residues 226 and 228 (H3 numbering). The internal genes of these AIVs exhibited a lack of mammalian adaptation mutations, such as PB2 E627 K and D701N (Supplementary S5).

Taiwanese H10 AIVs lack the multibasic amino acid sequences at the haemagglutinin cleavage site (HACS) typically associated with high pathogenicity (Supplementary S5). A notable shift in the sequences at the HACS of Taiwanese H10 isolates occurred around 2010. Before this year, the predominant sequence was PEIMQGR, found in 30 isolates, which then shifted to PELMQGR in 18 isolates from 2011 to 2016 (Figure 6(A)). This shift was also observed in H10 AIVs across Eurasia. Furthermore, a notable surge of the distinct PELIQGR variant at the HACS was observed amongst the isolates from China between 2013 and 2015, setting them apart from the other Eurasian H10 AIVs (Figure 6(B)).

Discussions

This study provides the first comprehensive overview of the evolutionary dynamics of H10 AIVs isolated from wild birds in Taiwan. The majority of Taiwanese H10 AIVs exhibited a close genetic relationship with wild bird AIVs from Eurasia, while 20% of Taiwanese H10 isolates possessed partial genes phylogenetically related to viruses originating in North America (Figures 2 and 3 and Supplementary S2). These intercontinental reassortant isolates generally displayed a low proportion of NAL genes (Figure 4), which is likely attributable to the NAL viruses undergoing continuous reassortment with EAL viruses during their southward migration with wild birds following their arrival on the Eurasian continent. We have documented the establishment of long-term NAL-derived gene pools in Asia, comprising the N8 and PA genes, alongside the establishment of long-term EAL-derived gene pools in North America, such as the PA gene (Supplementary S3). The Pacific Rim (Figure 5) is the primary distribution area for intercontinental gene pools connected to Taiwanese H10 AIVs. Our findings suggest that these gene pools circulate within specific species or regions and that the viruses, carried by migratory birds, occasionally reach lower-latitude areas such as Taiwan.

Furthermore, it is evident that specific gene pools frequently participate in intercontinental transmission (Supplementary S3). The WB2961 isolates examined in this research and the mammalian-related Australian H10N7 viruses are excellent illustrations of AIVs utilizing these gene pools. Despite the significant genetic distances between WB2961 and Australian H10N7 viruses, multiple gene segments were observed to cluster within the same groups in the phylogenetic trees (Figures 2 and 3 and Supplementary S2). These viruses exhibited similar reassortment patterns, likely as a result of utilizing comparable gene pools within the Pacific Rim.

The multiple genotypes of Taiwanese H10 viruses reflect frequent reassortment among wild bird viruses. As illustrated in Figure 4, 34 genotypes of H10 AIVs were identified during the two-decade study period, revealing the high genetic diversity of their internal genes. Unlike some HPAI viruses [15], no fixed association between specific gene segments was noted among the Taiwanese AIVs we examined. The absence of recurring genotypes in subsequent wintering seasons suggested that these viruses were transiently carried by migratory birds and were rarely established sustained circulation in Taiwan's poultry. Consistently, poultry surveillance during the study period did not detect any H10 viruses in domestic flocks. Most of the poultry isolates were low pathogenic H5N2 and H6N1 [16,17], with sporadic detections of clade 2.3.4.4 HPAI H5Nx viruses after the 2015 outbreak [15,18]. Occasionally, other subtypes such as H3, H4, low pathogenic H5 and H7 were found in domestic waterfowl (data not shown). The absence of H10 circulation in poultry is important for public health, particularly regarding subtypes like H10, since H10 AIVs have caused human infections and could pose greater risks to mammals if they circulate in poultry.

Taiwanese H10 viruses did not have a direct genetic link with mammalian-related H10 viruses across all genes (Figure 2 and Supplementary S2). Human H10N8 viruses have internal genes with high homology to H9N2 AIVs prevalent in Chinese poultry [9], and the HA/NA genes of H10N8 show the closest identity to local poultry viruses while also being phylogenetically close to East Asian wild bird viruses (Supplementary S2). This relation suggests that novel reassortant viruses carrying HA/NA from wild bird AIV and poultry H9N2 internal genes could infect humans without requiring prolonged adaptation. However, it is also possible that the phylogenetic proximity of the HA/NA segments to wild bird strains reflects ongoing circulation of such viruses in wild bird populations in mainland China. The recent instances of human infections have highlighted a significant public health threat, as those infections are associated with multiple novel reassortant viruses that contain internal genes derived from poultry H9N2 viruses [9,19–22]. If such internal genes were to become established in virus pools of wild birds, the viruses could evolve such that they have global implications. Although this H10-focused study found no direct evidence of such gene flow (Supplementary S2), it cannot be completely excluded due to sampling limitations.

The discontinuous phylogenetic topology observed in the H10 gene (Figure 2) also existed in other AIV subtypes. This topology bore resemblance to the patterns seen in the H4 subtype in Europe [23]. Moreover, a significant shift in the HACS sequences of Eurasian H10 AIVs around 2010 and a markedly distinct HACS pattern in Chinese poultry H10 AIVs compared to wild birds were observed (Figure 6). These observations raise questions about potential antigenic shifts in H10 of Eurasia and changes in virus–host interactions, highlighting the need for antigenic characterization and cross-species transmission risk assessment in the future.

Taiwanese wild bird H10 AIVs lack significant molecular markers for mammalian adaptation (Supplementary S5). However, consistent molecular markers indicative of mammalian adaptation remain elusive [4,24–29], meaning that current molecular assessments cannot reliably exclude the potential public health risks posed by H10 AIVs. Furthermore, reports indicate that, between 2019 and 2021, the H10N3 AIVs circulating in poultry in eastern China, which were implicated in human infections, carried G228S substitutions in the HA, a change that may enhance viral affinity for mammalian cells [27,30]. This raises concerns regarding the potential acquisition of further adaptive features by such viruses and their possible spillover into wild bird populations, where broader dissemination may occur. Given these uncertainties, it is essential to continue identifying key molecular markers, monitor viral circulation, and track the emergence of phenotypic changes for early risk detection and preparedness.

In summary, the study highlights frequent reassortment among wild bird AIVs, including HPAI viruses, and intercontinental gene flow around the Pacific region. As H10 continues to invade poultry industries in some regions, further characterization of its molecular markers, antigenic properties, and reassortment patterns upon entering poultry is needed. Given the ongoing threat of avian influenza to humans, poultry, and wildlife, these findings reinforce the necessity of continuous monitoring and a deeper understanding of the evolutionary dynamics of wild bird AIVs.

The limitation of the current study is that it only includes H10 virus isolates collected up to 2017. While this 20-year dataset enabled robust evaluation of historical genetic diversity and intercontinental reassortment, it does not account for potential changes in recent years. Future surveillance should aim to include more recent H10 strains to capture ongoing viral evolution and emerging public health risks.
