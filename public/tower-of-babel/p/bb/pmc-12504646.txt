Fracture behavior of sustainable wood and PLA composites fabricated by fused deposition modeling using essential work of fracture
Authors: Ehsan Kargar, Seyed-Amir Hosseini-Taklimi, Ahmad Ghasemi-Ghalebahman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The integration of additive manufacturing (AM) and green composites has wide potential for designing sustainable and eco-friendly structures. Green composites, made of natural fibers and bio-matrix matrices, balance mechanical performance against environmental responsibility. However, their structural reliability is based on fracture toughness, a field poorly developed for AM-manufactured materials. Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM), an extrusion-based AM process, enables the manufacture of low-cost fiber-reinforced thermoplastics with intricate geometries. This study investigates the essential work of fracture in a green wood-reinforced PLA composite fabricated by FDM. In this study, double-edge notch tension (DENT) specimens were evaluated under tension, and in addition to determining the specific work of fracture, the unnecessary work and the material ductility level were also evaluated. The DENTs were printed with three build orientations, 100% infill density, and a steel nozzle to evaluate process-induced anisotropy and inherent fracture resistance. Specific fracture work ranged from 11.62 to 76.06 kJ/m², with a maximum value in on-edge orientations. Ductility level ranged from 0.11 to 0.32, indicating medium deformability before failure. Morphology of the fracture surface was examined by Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FE-SEM) to understand failure mechanisms, where microstructural features controlling crack growth were seen. This integrated experimental research, using commercial filaments, highlights the importance of build direction to anisotropic fracture behavior and warrants the use of sustainable composites in engineering systems demanding mechanical performance and environmental sustainability.

Introduction

Additive manufacturing technology has been a topic of interest in the last few years as a new and cost-effective process for manufacturing complex structures with special mechanical properties at a low production cost1–4. Low-cost manufacturing is the process by which rapid processing is possible, along with high-dimensional accuracy, without taking the conventional machining route. The application of additive manufacturing technology, in addition to speeding up productivity, introduced more integration into the process of manufacturing, which is among the most technologically advanced and efficient methods of producing parts with intricate geometries5,6. The additive manufacturing technology has been well accepted as a revolutionary breakthrough, in which three-dimensional products are manufactured layer by layer. Among all the additive manufacturing processes, fused deposition modeling (FDM) is a cost-effective option and is possessed of an incredibly broad range of applications, with each of them possessing its strengths7. In FDM, parts are deposited one layer after another in molten polymer form. Key tunable parameters in FDM, such as fill density, layer thickness, infill pattern, layer height, print speed, nozzle diameter, and temperature, are dominant factors on the mechanical properties of printed parts. Of special note is the orientation of test samples concerning the print bed as a resounding influence on compressive strength, tensile strength, and fracture toughness8–11.

An innovative approach to improving material properties and achieving environmental objectives has attracted significant attention by incorporating polylactic acid (PLA) with natural fibers known as bio-composites. The development of various PLA-based composites has been extensively explored and evaluated across multiple biomedical applications, including drug delivery, implants, sutures, and tissue engineering, demonstrating their potential to advance these fields12–14. Due to their exceptional biocompatibility and superior mechanical properties, PLA and its reinforcing natural fibers represent a critical advancement in tissue engineering, serving as key agents for damaged tissues and functional enhancement15. Conventional technologies for manufacturing green composites include Vacuum Bagging, Injection Molding, and Extrusion. However, in recent years, additive manufacturing, particularly 3D printing, has gained considerable attention as an innovative method for the production of these materials16–18.

Due to their extensive range of applications, the extensive use of polymer composite filaments based on wood fibers has led to higher consumer penetration and higher manufacturing efficiency. The trend not only brings excellent opportunities for the development of related industries but also attracts more attention from manufacturers in the manufacturing of consumer goods and from researchers exploring the usage of natural fibers. Since there is a high volume of wood waste generated by the wood processing industry, recycling such waste as raw material in producing goods that effectively satisfy consumer demands holds high potential19,20.

The employment of natural fibers, instead of synthetic glass and carbon fibers, is meritorious for various reasons, such as being cheaper, light density, comparable tensile properties, zero wear on processing equipment harmless to the skin, reducing energy consumption, reducing health risks, being biodegradable, and being recyclable21. Green composites, due to their environmental advantages, offer superior performance over synthetic fiber composites in most applications, most importantly in environmentally sensitive industries, e.g., the automobile and electric cars sector, where the utilization of natural fibers is more commonly placed at the top of the agenda. The advantages are most pronounced in the current scenario. The diversified collection of materials, including natural fibers, utilized in the production of green composites provides a cost-effective solution to synthetic materials due to their enhanced environmental properties and optimum efficiency. The transition assists in reducing the adverse environmental impacts associated with synthetic polymers while sustaining the performance and functionality of the materials at an ideal pace. Moreover, these innovations are complementary to the attainment of sustainability and environmental objectives and therefore put the use of natural fibers not only as a means to produce products of high value and high performance but also as a key driver of environmental and sustainability agendas in various industries22–25.

Mechanical behavior of 3D-printed composites is significantly controlled by the adhesion strength and bonding characteristics between deposited filaments, which are controlled by process parameters. In the literature to date in this area, extensive efforts have been devoted to mitigating the inferior mechanical performance of FDM-manufactured components, with a major agenda of improving their load-bearing potential for structural applications. However, the majority of the attempts have been directed at the optimization of printing parameters to improve the elastic behavior, strength, and water resistance of the composites. Dong et al.26 compared pure PLA and wood/PLA composites in terms of the impact of the number of shells. The findings indicated that an increase in the number of shells reduced porosity and consequently proportionally enhanced the mechanical properties of the material. Ayrilmis et al.27 pointed to a key observation of the impact that an increase in layer height results in an increase in porosity and a corresponding decrease in the mechanical properties. However, these pores contribute to enhanced water absorption. Essassi et al.28 investigated the bending fatigue behavior of re-entrant honeycomb cores. They examined four different core densities for comparison, and the results indicated that sandwiches with lower core densities exhibited superior fatigue life. Kain et al.29 conducted a study on the effect of layer orientation and fiber volume fractions of 15% and 25% wood fibers in a PLA matrix. The study focused on evaluating compressive strength, tensile strength, and Charpy impact resistance, both notched and unnotched. The findings indicated that a 25% fiber content demonstrated more favorable mechanical properties compared to a 15% fiber content. Furthermore, the research highlighted a direct relationship between the layer deposition angle and the mechanical properties of the composite. Travieso-Rodriguez et al.30 examined the effects of nozzle diameter, infill density, and printing speed under cyclic bending fatigue loading. The research, conducted with both pure PLA and wood/PLA composites, revealed that poor adhesion led to a reduction in the fatigue life of the wood/PLA samples. Another key finding of this study was the negligible effect of printing speed on fatigue life. Furthermore, it was reported that an increase in nozzle diameter contributed to an improvement in fatigue life. Estakhrianhaghighi et al.31 investigated the fabrication of wood/PLA composite filaments with varying wood content (2.5%, 5%, 10%, and 15% by weight) using both commercially available and laboratory-extruded pure PLA. The study focused on evaluating the bending strength of these composites. The findings demonstrated that the wood/PLA composite with a 5% wood content exhibited superior performance compared to both commercial and laboratory-extruded pure PLA, which showed relatively weaker performance. Building on these promising results for green composites, the study further explored the bending behavior of cellular beams designed for lightweight applications. Additionally, one notable application of the green composites produced through additive manufacturing (AM) is their potential use as sound insulation materials. Sailesh et al.32 evaluated the performance of this bio-based material. The findings demonstrated that incorporating wood fibers as a reinforcement in PLA resulted in significant benefits, particularly in terms of sound energy absorption and reflection. The presence of wood fibers effectively obstructed and reflected acoustic energy. Lares Carrillo et al.33 investigated the impact of adding wood to PLA, with the most significant observation being the relatively ductile-like behavior of the wood/PLA composite, in contrast to the brittleness inherent in pure PLA. Salamat-Talab et al.34 investigated the effect of a 3D-printed wood-PLA interlayer on the fracture toughness of glass/epoxy composites. The interlayer significantly improved load-bearing capacity and displacement, while enhancing resin-rich areas and improving fiber-resin adhesion. This resulted in improved fracture behavior, including crack pinning and deflection. Kianifar et al.35 investigated the fatigue behavior of PLA and PLA-wood composites under cyclic loading, as well as the effects of immersion in simulated body fluid (SBF). They found that PLA-wood composites exhibited superior fatigue resistance compared to neat PLA; however, prolonged exposure to SBF significantly reduced the fatigue lifespan of both materials, highlighting the detrimental impact of fluid absorption on their mechanical durability.

One of the significant properties of polymers employed in engineering is their fracture toughness, which is often examined to establish performance. The fracture mechanics that assesses the behavior of cracked material under loads applied and provides methods to measure toughness is normally used to characterize polymer toughness. In the case of ductile materials, the post-yielding fracture mechanics (PYFM) model is often used to calculate their fracture toughness. Of the established methods, the J-integral test and the essential work of fracture (EWF) test stand out to assess the toughness of ductile polymers. The EWF test offers notable advantages over the J-integral in that it applies more straightforward testing procedures, less complicated sample preparation, and the capability for measuring fracture behavior during crack initiation and crack extension. The concept of Essential Work of Fracture (EWF) has been applied to thermoplastics and 3D-printed composite thermoplastics. Cuesta et al.36 investigated the mechanical response of 3D-printed polymers using Deeply Double-Edge Notched Tensile (DDENT) specimens and compared their performance to Deeply Double-Edge Notched-Small Punch (DDEN-SP) specimens. He et al.37 utilized the EWF approach to analyze the fusion bonding of carbon fiber-reinforced polyamide. In a subsequent study, He et al.38 independently examined the fracture properties of pure polyamide, emphasizing the effects of temperature on fusion bonding. This research included an in-depth analysis of internal structures and fracture surfaces of thin polymeric layers, while the thermal history of deposited filaments was monitored using thermal imaging. Among the parameters studied using the EWF method, the raster angle in 3D-printed polymers was identified as the most influential39–43. Altuntas et al.44 employed the EWF method to assess the interfacial fracture toughness of bioinspired layers formed between soft TPU and rigid PLA polymer phases. By optimizing the 3D printing process, the study controlled the interpenetration lengths of interfacial protrusions and established a correlation between plastic energy dissipation and interfacial morphology. The concept of Essential Work of Fracture (EWF) has been applied to thermoplastics and, to a lesser extent, to 3D-printed composite thermoplastics. While its application in composites remains relatively unexplored, conducting extensive research in this area is of significant importance.

While the application of green composites continues to expand in engineering, their fracture behavior, especially when manufactured with Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM) technology, is seriously under-documented. Wood/PLA green composites, as currently available off-the-shelf, commercially supplied materials, thus represent a fascinating compromise between biodegradability and mechanical properties. However, their load-carrying capacity as cracked members has not been experimentally validated in a form of use value for predictive design or comparative benchmarking.

The current literature states that despite the increasing progress of research in the field of green composites produced by additive manufacturing, a comprehensive study has not been conducted on the failure behavior of these sustainable green materials. This study addresses that very need with a systematic experimental investigation of the fracture properties of FDM-printed wood/PLA composites under the Essential Work of Fracture (EWF) protocol. By controlled fracture testing in three build directions, we developed a wide-ranging, orientation-dependent data set that captures the anisotropic energy absorption during the crack progression. This comprehensive experimental data speaks directly to the issue of how process parameters influence resistance to fracture, information unavailable in the current literature. and unnecessary for performance-critical usage. Apart from this, the integration of EWF with high-resolution fracture surface FE-SEM imaging provides a clear view of failure mechanisms at the microstructure level. These results provide insight into how interfacial bonding, fiber pull-out, and matrix-fiber interactions evolve under different printing conditions and lead to brittle or ductile-like behavior. While bio-based composites and additive manufacturing technologies are rapidly gaining interest, published literature lacks in-depth studies integrating fabrication parameters and build orientation into fracture and ductility research. By filling this knowledge gap, the present work presents new information regarding the crack growth behavior and mechanical properties of particle-reinforced composites produced using FDM.

This research is the first systematic investigation to apply EWF to 3D-printed wood/PLA green composites and relate it to microstructural evidence, and present quantitative as well as mechanistic insight. The findings fill a clear research gap and provide grounds for safe utilization of FDM-fabricated wood/PLA composites in real structures in which structural integrity as well as environmental concerns are necessary.

Essential work of fracture theory

The concept of dividing the non-elastic zone near a crack tip into discrete regions based on energy dissipation mode was initially introduced by Broberg45,46. Then, in 1977, Cotterell and Reddel47 formulated the Essential Work of Fracture (EWF) theory for metals. Later, Mai and Cotterell48 extended this theory and used it to describe ductile polymer fracture behavior. Since then, the EWF approach has also been applied in numerous ductile composite materials49,50 and their fracture mechanics were better represented by these theories. Materials and methods

Sample preparation

The wood/PLA filament, with a density of 1.05 g/cm³ and a diameter of 1.75 mm, was procured from FILATECH® (UAE). The material exhibits a tensile strength of 35 MPa, an elongation of 6%, and a melt flow index (at 190 °C, 2.16 kg). The samples were printed using a 3D FDM printer in double-edge tension mode. Dents were printed using a steel nozzle of diameter 0.5 mm, and all other printing parameters except build angle remained the same, as shown in Table 1. The steel nozzle was selected due to its wear resistance, and the nozzle diameter was selected to prevent clogging and ensure continuous extrusion31,52,53. The nozzle temperature and print speed were selected based on the material datasheet recommendation. DENT dimensions were 120 × 45 × 2 mm³. DENT wood/PLA samples were manufactured as per ISO 23,524, as shown in Fig. 2. Notches were incorporated during the design phase, resulting in notched samples. A sharp crack was introduced parallel to the width of the specimens using a razor blade. Ligament lengths of 7, 9, 11, and 13 mm were considered. Each ligament was fabricated in three different build orientations upright, on-edge, and flat, as shown in Fig. 3.

Experimental methodology

The fracture properties were evaluated using the STM-400 SANTAM universal testing machine. Figure 4 STM-400 SANTAM setup and the wood/PLA DENT Specimen. Three replicates were considered for the experiment. The machine’s tensile load cell can measure forces up to 400 kN. An extensometer was used to accurately measure elongation during the tensile testing of the DENT samples. All tests were performed at room temperature (25 °C) with a constant crosshead speed of 2 mm/min.

Fig. 4STM-400 SANTAM tensile testing machine setup for wood/PLA DENT specimens.

STM-400 SANTAM tensile testing machine setup for wood/PLA DENT specimens.

Fracture surface characterization

In order to carry out an extensive investigation on the fracture mechanisms, the DENT specimens’ surfaces were examined using a Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (FE-SEM, Zeiss Sigma 300 HV). The samples were sputter-coated with a thin gold coating before imaging in a bid to enhance conductivity and quality of the images. Micrographs were acquired at various magnifications ranging from 85X to 1000X so that features of the fracture were seen in high detail.

Results and discussions

Load-displacement behavior

The wood/PLA DENT specimens were subjected to tensile loading at a rate of 2 mm/min in mode I, as results shown in Fig. 5a–c. The force-displacement curves for the DENT specimens in three build orientations flat, on-edge, and upright are presented for four different ligament lengths. The self-similar behavior observed in each specimen with increasing ligament length, as noted by Karger-Kocsis54, indicates proper loading and crack propagation conditions, independent of ligament length. This observation aligns with the crack propagation patterns in the IFPZ region of the ligament. As shown in Fig. 5, both the peak force and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}_{f}$$\end{document} increase with ligament length. The load-displacement curves for the three build orientations exhibit an initial linear elastic response, followed by a distinct plastic deformation behavior after the elastic region.

To further investigate the conditions under Mode I loading, ligament 11 from each build orientation was examined. Figure 6 presents all three build orientations with a ligament length of 11 mm. The use of different printing orientations resulted in distinct crack patterns. Furthermore, the upright samples demonstrated a lower elastic-to-plastic ratio, with a significant decrease in the slope of the force-displacement curve after complete ligament yielding. This behavior indicates that the upright samples are less ductile than those in the other orientations. While all three orientations exhibited quasi-brittle failure, crack growth in the upright samples was markedly more sudden and brittle55. This behavior in the upright orientation can be attributed to the increased distance of the IFPZ from the print bed and the excessive separation from the bed’s heat source. Printing in the upright orientation reduces the print path compared to the other two orientations, leading to an increased number of deposition cycles. This, in turn, results in weaker adhesion and improper layer bonding41. The image of the loaded area in the upright sample, shown in Fig. 6, reveals a relatively uniform region with low ductility, which is further confirmed by filament debonding observed at the fracture surface.

Fig. 6Load-displacement curves for three orientations with a ligament length of 11 mm.

Load-displacement curves for three orientations with a ligament length of 11 mm.

As shown in Fig. 6, the on-edge specimen exhibited a gradual slope to failure, with a slow force drop occurring after the ligament had fully yielded. Additionally, the plastic displacement following crack propagation exceeded that of the elastic region. The alignment of the print layers and the construction direction relative to the loading direction resulted in the maximum tensile force being observed in the on-edge orientation. Notably, this alignment did not induce any deviation in crack growth, which is a crucial factor in validating the failure method. Furthermore, the highest displacement was associated with this fabrication angle. As seen in Fig. 6, crack propagation leading to failure in the on-edge specimen was irregular, and the force drop with increasing displacement rate remained minimal. This jagged surface in the force-displacement curve is attributed to the non-uniform crack growth at this orientation, as evidenced by the fracture image in Fig. 6. as thoroughly discussed in the fracture surface analysis, this behavior indicates significant plastic deformation. Unlike the upright orientation, the on-edge construction exhibited acceptable adhesion, as demonstrated by the post-crack growth process56. The behavior observed in the flat orientation differed significantly from the other fabrication angles. This orientation led to regular crack growth in the wood/PLA composite sample. The maximum yielding of the ligament was also found to occur under reduced tensile stress than the two other orientations, and the slope of the elastic region was greater than in the on-edge and upright samples. Even though there were two layers of shells, characteristic of this fabrication angle, the crack initiation occurred from the sharp notch, and the shell layers did not influence the direction of crack propagation. The IPFZ also exhibited quasi-brittle behavior characteristic of the material. The on-edge sample displayed the highest average peak load, as shown in Fig. 7a, while the upright sample had the lowest. The difference in average peak load between the upright and flat orientations for ligament lengths of 11 mm and 13 mm was negligible, suggesting similar tensile strengths for these two fabrication directions. Validation of the plane stress criterion revealed that the flat angle exhibited the lowest stress values for ligament lengths of 11 mm and 13 mm, while the upright angle showed the highest stress values for the same ligament lengths. According to the Sai-Hill criterion64, the maximum net cross-sectional stress should fall between 1.1 and 0.9 of the maximum stress. As shown in Fig. 7b-d, the entire test adhered to this criterion.

The wood/PLA composites are more porous due to the non-uniform distribution of sawdust and the absence of perfect bonding61,62. The localized wood filler accumulation has a significant effect and plays an important role in the initiation and propagation of cracks and the overall crack growth mechanism. Additionally, the proportion of wood filler in molten PLA directly impacts the mechanical performance of the composite63,64. During the layer-by-layer extrusion process, closely spaced rasters may result in the formation of air gaps41. The presence of porosity and closely spaced air gaps is indicative of failure in wood/PLA composites, typically arising from poor matrix-fiber bonding and, on a larger scale, insufficient raster adhesion. These contribute to crack initiation and the acceleration of crack growth. In addition, in the same materials under tensile loading, poor bonding between the wood fibers and matrix has been shown to occur, indicating that changes in the percentage of wood filler have a great influence on adhesion and ultimately on the strength of the overall composite31.

Microporosity is the predominant failure mechanism across all three studied angles65. Porosity observed on fracture surfaces, stemming from the incomplete overlap of weld lines during printing, is a frequent issue in polymers fabricated through the additive manufacturing method66. These intralayer and interlayer porosities exert significant influences on the fracture mechanism and alter the micro-crack propagation behavior67,68. In the FDM-produced specimens, these inherent porosities may significantly influence the fracture mechanism, particularly for ductile failure. Furthermore, another inherent defect in the FDM-made parts is air gaps. This is process-parameter-dependent upon nozzle geometry and layer thickness, and may be controlled with process modification69. As demonstrated from load-displacement analysis, DENT specimens fabricated in the upright orientation exhibited severe brittle-like behavior. Examination of FE-SEM images, Fig. 10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{a}_{1-3}$$\end{document} reveals that the stress concentration region in these specimens is characterized by two distinct zones: a smooth surface zone and a cavitation zone. The smooth surface zone is indicative of brittle fracture, while the cavitation zone represents ductile fracture. The smooth surface region is identified as the primary yield zone, which accelerates fracture propagation. This phenomenon occurs independently of the raster angle in polymers and polymer matrix composites, as noted in previous studies70.

The cavitation zone, frequently in the stress concentration areas of the fracture surface, is associated with gross deformation and nonlinear behavior71. This weak fiber-matrix adhesion significantly reduces the effectiveness of the filler in mitigating applied stresses. Additionally, while a smooth surface region does exist, its size is significantly smaller in the flat orientation. Instead, a very rough, ductile fracture surface dominates, showing a trend towards ductile compared to brittle failure modes74. Extensive filaments delamination between rasters on the fracture surface of the flat orientation, as evidenced in Fig. 10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{c}_{1-3}$$\end{document}, suggests poor inter-filament adhesion75. Bridging is also apparent, due to the interaction between fibers and the PLA matrix in the crazing region following loading. The fibers debond as additional loading separates the matrix from the fibers until the pull-out of the fibers occurs76,77. This process is a sign of activation of a toughening mechanism78. Also visible is fiber failure, which is clearly seen through a combination of fiber breakage and matrix fracture on the fracture surface. Filament debonding is also visible, further showing the poor adhesive strength of the matrix to the surrounding layers. These observations show the dominant role of the fabrication angle in controlling the fracture response of the composite. The fabrication angle significantly influenced stress distribution, interlayer adhesion, and directions of crack propagation. Plane surface zones were accountable for initiating brittle failure and facilitating sudden rupture following ligament yielding. Incorporation of wood fibers also improved adhesion reduction between filaments and the matrix, accelerating processes such as filament debonding, fiber pull-out, and filament rupture. In the on-edge orientation, extensive plastic deformations along with cavitation led to an increase in ductile mechanical properties and improved energy absorption. The upright orientation, nevertheless, was more brittle as a result of a lack of interlayer adhesion and stress concentration. Importantly, wood fiber incorporation allowed for greater ductility of mechanical responses in comparison to unmodified PLA. Process-induced defects, i.e., air gaps intrinsic to additive manufacturing, detrimentally affect mechanical properties by reducing material toughness. In summary, the presence of micro-porosity in green composite printed samples was identified as a major contributor to fracture behavior, with the potential to connect multiple failure initiation sites and influence the deflection of the crack path. More significantly, they indicate that micro-porosity control is a viable avenue for further study, particularly in green composites, where mechanical performance enhancement without environmental compromise is an increasingly urgent priority. As the materials assume increasing significance in applications ranging from biomedical to consumer products to eco-friendly transportation, a deeper understanding of their microstructural reliability is not merely beneficial but essential. Considering the behavior of fracture and the mode of fracture, wood/PLA composites seem worthy to be applied to non-critical applications in tension, like finger splints or auxiliary physiotherapy supports.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the analysis of the essential work of fracture and the fracture morphology of the wood/PLA green composite fabricated by FDM, significant insights were obtained into the quasi-brittle behavior of the green composite present at three different fabrication angles:

Among them, on-edge orientation exhibited the highest fracture toughness and upright orientation the lowest. More specifically, a 57.36% decrease in fracture energy was observed from on-edge to flat samples, and a further 37.94% decline from flat to upright specimens. Fracture behaviors were strongly dependent on print orientation and affected ductility, stress distribution, and crack propagation paths. On-edge samples exhibited large-scale plastic deformation and cavitation, whereas upright samples underwent brittle fracture, resulting in smooth fracture surfaces. Load–displacement curves bore witness to self-similarity, and the applicability of the EWF method was validated by extending the Sai–Hill criterion to plane stress conditions. These results demonstrate the structural role of fabrication angle not as a novelty per se, but as a tool to access and govern the microstructural behavior and toughening mechanisms of wood fiber-filled biopolymers. Incorporation of wood fibers in the PLA matrix introduced essential fracture mechanisms such as filament debonding, fiber pull-out, and interfacial failure. These processes converted the failure mode of the composite from brittle to ductile-like, showing an enormous deviation from that of pure PLA. FE-SEM images confirmed poor interfacial bonding along filament edges, further revealing the pressing need for microstructural optimization of FDM-based natural composites. This study, for the first time, applies the “basic work of failure” approach to wood/PLA composites produced via FDM, filling the gap in the fracture mechanics of additively fabricated green composites.

Future research should explore methods to enhance the mechanical performance of green composites against damage, including natural fibers (e.g., hemp, cotton, bamboo), interface enhancement operations, and advanced characterization techniques to better understand the damage evolution. Future research could focus on optimizing the fiber content and treatment in the fiber-matrix adhesion performance and reducing micropores, and focusing on the reduction of micro-voids. This finding makes wood/PLA composites suitable for application on small loads on medical or assistive devices, especially customizable and renewable applications.
