Challenging the Role of Routine Thromboprophylaxis in Foot and Ankle Surgery: A Comparative Study of Aspirin, Enoxaparin, and No Prophylaxis
Authors: Young Uk Park, Jinyoung Jun, Seugyeob Sakong, Myungsub Lee, Young Wook Seo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDeep vein thrombosis (DVT) is a common postoperative complication in orthopedic surgery and may result in life-threatening events such as pulmonary thromboembolism (PTE). However, evidence on the optimal prophylactic strategy for DVT following foot and ankle surgery remains limited. This study aimed to assess the effect of pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis in patients undergoing foot and ankle surgery with postoperative immobilization.MethodsA retrospective review was conducted of 451 patients who underwent foot and ankle surgery followed by short-leg splint immobilization with restricted ankle motion for more than 2 weeks between April 2022 and February 2024. All patients underwent routine screening duplex ultrasonography at 2 weeks postoperatively, regardless of symptom status. Patients were categorized into 3 groups based on postoperative pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis: no anticoagulation, aspirin (Aspirin Protect, Bayer AG) 100 mg once daily for 14 days, and enoxaparin (a low–molecular-weight heparin; Clexane, Sanofi) 40 mg once daily during hospitalization followed by aspirin after discharge to complete a total of 14 days. The incidence of DVT was compared using the chi-square test, and multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to adjust for potential confounders.ResultsOf the 451 patients, 73 patients (16.2%) developed DVT. The incidence of DVT was comparable among the 3 groups, with no statistically significant differences observed (no-anticoagulation group: 24 of 147 [16.3%]; aspirin group: 25 of 145 [17.2%]; enoxaparin group: 24 of 159 [15.1%]; p = 0.34). Most DVTs were asymptomatic (66 / 73, 90.4%) and confined to the distal calf veins (70 / 73, 95.9%), while proximal DVT was rare (3 / 73, 4.1%), occurring in 1 patient in each group. Multivariate logistic regression analysis demonstrated no significant association between pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis and postoperative DVT after adjustment for confounding variables.ConclusionsIn this retrospective cohort, pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis with enoxaparin or aspirin was not associated with a reduced incidence of postoperative DVT following foot and ankle surgery with immobilization. These findings should be interpreted cautiously given the observational design and short duration of prophylaxis. A uniform anticoagulation strategy may not be appropriate for all patients.

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) is a frequent and potentially life-threatening complication in patients undergoing orthopedic surgery.1) The reported incidence of DVT in orthopedic surgical populations ranges from 0.8% to 23.5%, reflecting substantial variability depending on procedure type, patient characteristics, and prophylactic strategies employed. Pulmonary thromboembolism (PTE), a major consequence of DVT, may result in significant morbidity and mortality, highlighting the importance of effective pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis.23)

Advanced age, postoperative immobilization, and surgical intervention are established risk factors for DVT.124) Our previous study involving 278 patients undergoing foot and ankle surgery with immobilization for more than 2 weeks demonstrated a DVT incidence of 14.7%. Significant risk factors included recent trauma (within 2 weeks), prior DVT, and age ≥ 60 years, with odds ratios of 2.44, 6.40, and 1.16, respectively.5)

Foot and ankle surgery often requires postoperative immobilization and prolonged non-weight-bearing to protect bone union, tendon healing, or joint fusion. This extended restriction of mobility may increase the risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE), underscoring the clinical importance of defining appropriate thromboprophylaxis strategies in this patient population. Despite these risks, evidence-based guidelines specifically addressing thromboprophylaxis in foot and ankle surgery remain limited and inconsistent. Current strategies include mechanical methods, such as graduated compression stockings and intermittent pneumatic compression devices (IPCDs), and pharmacologic agents, including aspirin (Aspirin Protect, Bayer AG) and enoxaparin (a low–molecular-weight heparin [LMWH]; Clexane, Sanofi) used for pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis. However, the effectiveness of these agents in reducing postoperative DVT in patients undergoing foot and ankle surgery with immobilization has not been clearly established.678)

Accordingly, this study aimed to evaluate the association between pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis and postoperative DVT in patients undergoing foot and ankle surgery with postoperative immobilization by comparing outcomes among patients managed with no anticoagulation, aspirin, or enoxaparin. The findings are intended to inform risk-based clinical decision-making rather than to support routine anticoagulation for all patients.

METHODS

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Ajou University School of Medicine (IRB No. AJOUIRB-DB-2025-187), and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design of the study. All procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients

This retrospective study reviewed patients treated between April 2022 and February 2024. A total of 451 patients who underwent foot and ankle surgery followed by short-leg splint immobilization with restricted ankle motion for more than 2 weeks were enrolled in the study. Patients were excluded if they had a history of coagulopathy or other bleeding disorders; were receiving anticoagulant therapy for unrelated medical conditions; had known contraindications to anticoagulant agents (such as allergies or adverse reactions); had severe hepatic or renal impairment; or were pregnant. All patients who met the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria were included, and no additional exclusions were made after eligibility assessment. Demographic data, including diagnosis, surgical procedure, duration of immobilization, and potential risk factors for DVT such as age, body mass index (BMI), and other medical histories, were collected. Surgery-related variables, including tourniquet time, were also recorded for analysis.

Prophylaxis Protocol

Because of the retrospective design, thromboprophylaxis strategies were not assigned according to a predefined protocol or randomization but were determined by surgeon preference and prevailing institutional practice during the study period, based on perioperative clinical judgment rather than formal risk stratification. Importantly, the thromboprophylaxis strategy evolved over time in response to accumulating institutional experience and observed postoperative outcomes. During the early study period, no routine pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis was administered. Following the occurrence of postoperative DVT and related complications, aspirin prophylaxis was subsequently adopted. Later, as routine screening ultrasonography revealed a relatively high incidence of postoperative DVT, including asymptomatic cases, enoxaparin was introduced for inpatient use, followed by aspirin after discharge. Accordingly, the 3 study groups represent sequential phases of real-world institutional practice rather than prospectively assigned treatment arms.

Patients were classified into 3 groups based on postoperative pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis: (1) the no-anticoagulation group, in which patients received no anticoagulant therapy after surgery; (2) the aspirin group, in which patients received aspirin (Aspirin Protect, Bayer AG) 100 mg once daily starting on postoperative day 1 and continuing through day 14; and (3) the enoxaparin group, in which patients received enoxaparin, an LMWH (Clexane, Sanofi), 40 mg once daily during hospitalization, beginning on postoperative day 1. Upon discharge, patients in the enoxaparin group were transitioned to aspirin (Aspirin Protect) 100 mg once daily to complete a 14-day course. All patients also received mechanical prophylaxis, including graduated compression stockings and IPCDs. Postoperatively, patients were not prescribed absolute bed rest. Early mobilization was encouraged as tolerated, depending on the type of surgery and patient condition, using crutches or a walker with non- or limited weight-bearing on the operated extremity during the immobilization period.

DVT Diagnosis

All patients underwent routine screening duplex ultrasonography at 2 weeks postoperatively, regardless of symptom status. Examinations were performed in a vascular surgery laboratory by a single experienced examiner with more than 10 years of expertise in vascular ultrasonography, who was blinded to the thromboprophylaxis regimen. The examination protocol systematically evaluated both proximal and distal venous segments, including the common femoral vein, femoral vein, popliteal vein, and calf veins (anterior tibial, posterior tibial, and peroneal veins).

Statistics

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS for Windows version 22.0 (IBM Corp.). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient demographics and baseline clinical characteristics. The incidence of DVT among the 3 thromboprophylaxis groups was initially compared using the chi-square test. In addition, multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate the association between thromboprophylaxis strategy and postoperative DVT after adjustment for potential confounding variables, including advanced age, diabetes mellitus, smoking status, BMI, sex, and trauma status. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were calculated. A 2-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The sample size was calculated as described by Müller and Büttner9) and Walter et al.10) The G*Power version 3.1.9.7 software (Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf) was used for the calculation. The sample size was calculated based on our previous studies, which reported a DVT incidence rate of 14.7% in similar populations.5) To detect a statistically significant difference in DVT incidence between the groups with 80% power and a significance level of 0.05, a minimum of 265 patients was deemed necessary. This sample size ensured adequate power to identify clinically meaningful differences in DVT rates between the study groups.

RESULTS

Demographics

A total of 451 patients who underwent foot and ankle surgery with postoperative immobilization were included in the final analysis, comprising 147 patients in the no-anticoagulation group, 145 in the aspirin group, and 159 in the enoxaparin group. The mean age was 54.9 ± 15.8 years in the no-anticoagulation group, 56.1 ± 14.3 years in the aspirin group, and 56.7 ± 13.1 years in the enoxaparin group, with no significant differences among groups (p > 0.05). The male-to-female ratios were comparable across the 3 groups (p > 0.05). Baseline comorbidities—including hypertension (35.7%), diabetes mellitus (22.3%), and cardiovascular disease (12.0%)—did not differ significantly among the groups (p > 0.05). The mean BMI was 27.9 ± 3.5 kg/m2 in the no-anticoagulation group, 27.5 ± 3.6 kg/m2 in the aspirin group, and 26.8 ± 3.7 kg/m2 in the enoxaparin group (range, 18.5–34.7 kg/m2), with no statistically significant differences among the groups. Regarding smoking status, current smokers accounted for 27.9% of patients in the no-anticoagulation group, 22.8% in the aspirin group, and 27.7% in the enoxaparin group, with no statistically significant differences among the 3 groups (p > 0.05) (Table 1). Among patients in the enoxaparin group, the mean duration of enoxaparin administration during hospitalization was 4.2 days.

DVT Incidence

Of the 451 patients, 73 (16.2%) developed postoperative DVT. The incidence of DVT was 16.3% (24 / 147) in the no-anticoagulation group, 17.2% (25 / 145) in the aspirin group, and 15.1% (24 / 159) in the enoxaparin group. There were no statistically significant differences in DVT incidence among the 3 groups (p = 0.34) (Table 2). Multivariate logistic regression analysis demonstrated no significant association between thromboprophylaxis strategy and postoperative DVT after adjustment for potential confounding variables (p > 0.05).

Among the 73 patients diagnosed with DVT, 70 cases (95.9%) were confined to the calf veins. The remaining 3 cases (4.1%) involved proximal veins, as follows: 1 case in the distal popliteal vein, 1 in the distal femoral vein, and 1 involving the common femoral, superficial femoral, and popliteal veins. Proximal DVT occurred in 1 patient in each thromboprophylaxis group (0.7% in the no-anticoagulation group, 0.7% in the aspirin group, and 0.6% in the enoxaparin group), and all proximal DVTs occurred in patients with ankle fractures.

Of the 73 DVT cases, 66 patients (90.4%) were asymptomatic. The remaining 7 patients (9.6%) reported mild symptoms, including lower leg pain and swelling. Symptomatic DVT occurred in a small number of patients, with no statistically significant difference among the 3 thromboprophylaxis groups (2.0% [3 / 147] in the no-anticoagulation group, 1.4% [2 / 145] in the aspirin group, and 1.3% [2 / 159] in the enoxaparin group). One patient presented with chest discomfort, which led to a diagnosis of PTE following cardiology consultation. This patient was managed conservatively without surgical intervention and was treated with oral rivaroxaban (Xarelto, Bayer AG) at a dose of 20 mg once daily for 3 months, resulting in complete clinical recovery.

None of the patients diagnosed with DVT required surgical intervention. All patients were managed conservatively with oral rivaroxaban (Xarelto, Bayer AG) 20 mg once daily for 3 months, in addition to compression stocking use and early ambulation as tolerated. All symptomatic patients experienced complete resolution of symptoms within 6 weeks postoperatively.

Complications Related to Antithrombotic Therapy

No complications related to antithrombotic therapy were observed in any of the 3 groups. Specifically, there were no cases of major bleeding, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, or other clinically significant adverse events, and no patients required discontinuation of thromboprophylaxis due to adverse effects.

DISCUSSION

The most important finding of this study is that the incidence of DVT following foot and ankle surgery with postoperative immobilization was 16.2%, and that prophylactic anticoagulation with either aspirin (Aspirin Protect, Bayer AG) or enoxaparin (an LMWH; Clexane, Sanofi) did not significantly reduce this risk. These findings indicate that routine pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis was not associated with a reduction in DVT incidence in this cohort, rather than implying that prophylaxis is unnecessary in all patients. Instead, prophylactic strategies should be individually tailored, and further prospective studies are warranted to identify subgroups who may benefit from targeted anticoagulant therapy.

The relatively high incidence of DVT observed in this study should be interpreted in the context of routine postoperative duplex ultrasonography performed in all patients, which likely increased the detection of asymptomatic distal thrombi. As most detected DVTs were asymptomatic and confined to distal veins, the reported incidence should not be directly equated with clinically meaningful or symptomatic VTE. Nevertheless, asymptomatic distal DVTs were intentionally included in the primary analysis to assess the biological and pharmacologic effectiveness of thromboprophylaxis on venous thrombus formation. Although their immediate clinical impact may be limited, distal DVT represents an early manifestation of thrombogenesis and provides a sensitive outcome measure for evaluating preventive strategies in a surveillance-based cohort.

Despite the existence of standardized guidelines for major orthopedic procedures, such as total joint arthroplasty and hip fracture surgery, thromboprophylaxis protocols for foot and ankle surgery are inconsistent. The 2012 American College of Chest Physicians (ACCP) guidelines for other orthopedic procedures, such as arthroplasty and hip fracture surgeries, recommend the use of LMWH for at least 10 days postoperatively.8) For major surgeries, use of dual prophylaxis with an antithrombotic agent and IPCD is advised during hospital stay, with extended thromboprophylaxis for up to 35 days postoperatively.8) Additionally, DVT screening using ultrasonography is recommended for asymptomatic patients.8) However, there are no established guidelines specifically for foot and ankle surgery, leading to variability in clinical practice.

A recent 2024 global survey involving 773 orthopedic foot and ankle surgeons from 49 countries highlighted this variability.11) Although 97% of surgeons acknowledged the importance of thromboprophylaxis, 59% reported an absence of institutional or national protocols, and 84% expressed willingness to adopt standardized guidelines if available.11) This underscores the need for standardized evidence-based guidelines to ensure the consistency and effectiveness of VTE prophylaxis for foot and ankle surgeries. Mechanical methods, such as graduated compression stockings and IPCD, are commonly used alongside pharmacologic agents, including aspirin and enoxaparin.11) Regional differences were observed in the choice of pharmacologic agents: aspirin was more commonly used in North America, enoxaparin in Europe, and direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) in South America and Africa.11) These findings highlight the urgent need for globally standardized, evidence-based guidelines for thromboprophylaxis in foot and ankle surgery.

Previous studies have presented mixed results regarding the VTE risk following lower limb immobilization. Some reports, including those by Ettema et al.12) and Zee et al.13) found no significant increase in DVT risk compared to the general population. Nilsson-Helander et al.14) reported a 36% incidence of DVT in patients with Achilles tendon rupture screened via ultrasonography, highlighting a notably elevated thromboembolic risk. Persson et al.15) further emphasized the need for specialized prophylactic measures in this high-risk group. A 2016 meta-analysis concluded that routine pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis is not universally necessary for all foot and ankle surgeries, but targeted strategies, such as IPCD use, may benefit high-risk patients like those with Achilles tendon ruptures.6) These findings emphasize the need for individualized prophylaxis based on risk assessment.6) Robinson et al.7) reported that routine LMWH use in such surgeries provides minimal benefit at high cost, questioning its cost-effectiveness in low- to moderate-risk cases. These findings underscore the importance of a tailored prophylactic approach, particularly in high-risk patients. Therefore, understanding the risk factors for DVT is crucial for its prevention.

Several well-established clinical factors—such as restricted weight-bearing, obesity, smoking, prior VTE, and thrombophilia—are known to increase the risk of DVT.241617) The Padua Prediction Score identifies key contributors to elevated thromboembolic risk, including previous VTE, immobility exceeding 3 days, recent surgery, advanced age, and elevated BMI.18) A cumulative score of ≥ 4 on this scale is considered high risk. These findings reinforce the importance of individualized risk stratification in determining the need for prophylaxis.1819) Our previous study further supports this approach, identifying acute trauma, a history of DVT, and advanced age as significant risk factors, with ORs of 2.44, 6.40, and 1.16, respectively.5) However, the present study did not perform formal subgroup analyses based on these risk categories, primarily due to its retrospective design and the limited number of clinically significant events, particularly proximal and symptomatic DVTs, which restricted the statistical power for meaningful stratified comparisons. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as cohort-level associations rather than risk-specific effects of pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis. Nonetheless, the absence of a significant reduction in DVT incidence across the overall cohort suggests that universal pharmacologic prophylaxis may not be justified for all patients and highlights the potential value of targeted, risk-based prophylactic strategies. Future prospective studies incorporating predefined risk stratification—such as age, trauma status, fracture patterns, procedure type, and prior thromboembolic history—are warranted to identify high-risk subpopulations that may derive the greatest benefit from tailored thromboprophylaxis regimens.

Although various pharmacologic agents are available for VTE prophylaxis, none provide complete protection. The role of chemoprophylaxis in foot and ankle surgery remains controversial, as high-level evidence supporting its efficacy is limited in this population. Agents such as warfarin and DOACs (e.g., dabigatran, rivaroxaban) have not been extensively evaluated outside of major joint arthroplasty. Aspirin has gained attention due to findings from the Pulmonary Embolism Prevention (PEP) trial, which reported a 33% relative risk reduction in DVT and pulmonary embolism during high-risk periods.20) However, Griffiths et al.21) found no significant difference in symptomatic thromboembolism rates between aspirin users and non-users. LMWH is currently the standard comparator in prophylactic studies and has demonstrated efficacy in reducing VTE incidence.6) Despite its advantages—such as fixed dosing and no need for monitoring—it carries potential risks, including bleeding, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, and, rarely, osteoporosis with prolonged use.13)

The optimal duration of pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis remains undetermined. Some protocols recommend administration only during hospitalization, while others suggest continuation throughout the immobilization period, ranging from 2 to 35 days.71213) The 2012 ACCP guidelines recommend a minimum of 10 days of LMWH administration following arthroplasty or hip fracture surgery.17) In our study, enoxaparin (Clexane) was administered for a relatively short duration during hospitalization (mean, 4.2 days), followed by aspirin after discharge. This regimen reflects real-world institutional practice within the Korean healthcare system, where early postoperative discharge is common and extended inpatient administration of LMWH is frequently limited by national insurance coverage and reimbursement policies, rather than a guideline-driven prophylaxis protocol. This discrepancy between guideline-recommended and actual prophylaxis duration may have attenuated the potential protective effect of LMWH and contributed to the absence of significant differences in DVT incidence among the study groups. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted in the context of short-term inpatient LMWH use followed by outpatient antiplatelet therapy, and should not be extrapolated to longer or guideline-based prophylaxis regimens. At the same time, this pragmatic approach enhances the external validity of our results by reflecting contemporary clinical practice, rather than idealized treatment protocols. Further prospective studies comparing different durations and strategies of thromboprophylaxis—particularly within varying healthcare systems—are warranted to define optimal prophylaxis regimens for patients undergoing foot and ankle surgery with postoperative immobilization.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective, single-center design and absence of randomization limit causal inference, and the findings should therefore be interpreted as associations within a retrospective cohort rather than definitive evidence regarding the superiority or necessity of any specific thromboprophylaxis strategy. Second, the allocation of pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis was not based on a standardized risk-stratification protocol but was primarily influenced by surgeon preference and perioperative clinical judgment, which may have introduced selection bias and unmeasured confounding. Third, the retrospective design precluded standardized control of other potential confounders, including surgical technique, patient comorbidities, and postoperative mobilization or mechanical prophylaxis. In addition, because this was a single-center study, institutional practice patterns may differ from those of other centers or healthcare systems, which could limit the external validity of the findings. Finally, although routine duplex ultrasonography was performed at 2 weeks postoperatively in all patients regardless of symptoms, follow-up imaging to assess thrombus progression or resolution was not systematically performed, and the clinical relevance of asymptomatic distal DVT remains uncertain. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted as reflecting the effect of pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis on thrombus detection rather than on clinically symptomatic VTE.

In this retrospective cohort study, the incidence of DVT following foot and ankle surgery with postoperative immobilization was 16.2%. Pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis with enoxaparin or aspirin was not associated with a statistically significant reduction in postoperative DVT incidence in this cohort. These findings should be interpreted cautiously given the observational study design, the relatively short duration of pharmacologic prophylaxis, and institution-specific perioperative protocols. Our results do not preclude the potential benefit of anticoagulant prophylaxis in carefully selected high-risk patients but suggest that a routine, uniform prophylactic strategy may not be appropriate for all individuals. Future well-designed prospective, multicenter, risk-stratified studies are required to define optimal thromboprophylaxis strategies for patients undergoing foot and ankle surgery.
