An Inverted U-shaped Relationship between Glycemic Variability and Mortality in Patients with Severe Pulmonary Embolism: A Retrospective Study
Authors: Huihui Tan, Zihao Tang, Renyi Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aims: Hyperglycemia and glycemic variability (GV) are predictors of adverse outcomes in critically ill patients; however, their roles in pulmonary embolism (PE) remain unclear. This study investigated the association between the mean blood glucose (MBG), GV, and mortality in patients with PE. Methods: A retrospective cohort study of patients with PE was conducted using the MIMIC-IV 3.0. GV was quantified using the coefficient of variation (CV). Patients were stratified according to tertiles of MBG and CV. Multivariable logistic regression, restricted cubic splines (RCS), and ROC curves were used to evaluate the associations with ICU and in-hospital mortality, supplemented by the threshold effect, sensitivity, and subgroup analyses. Results: Among the 1,493 PE patients, the ICU and in-hospital mortality rates were 12.99% and 20.90%, respectively. A higher MBG or CV was significantly associated with an increased risk of ICU mortality and in-hospital mortality. RCS revealed a linear association between MBG and mortality, whereas CV exhibited an inverted U-shaped relationship with inflection points at 38.523% (ICU) and 37.038% (in-hospital). For every 1% increase in CV to the left of the inflection point, the relative risks of ICU and in-hospital mortality increased by 0.035. The combined model (MBG + CV + ERS (European Respiratory Society)) achieved AUCs of 0.735 (ICU) and 0.693 (in-hospital) for mortality prediction. Conclusions: MBG and CV are independent predictors of mortality in critically ill patients with PE, suggesting that optimal glycemic control may benefit this population.

1.Introduction

Acute pulmonary embolism (PE), the most severe manifestation of venous thromboembolism, has an annual incidence of approximately 39-115 cases per 100,000 individuals 1 , 2) . In high-risk cases, the 30-day mortality rate can be as high as 22% 3) . These patients often present with hemodynamic instability or circulatory shock, necessitating admission to the intensive care unit (ICU) for stabilization, thereby highlighting the critical importance of prognostic evaluation and management in this population.

Clinically, most PE cases originate from lower-extremity deep vein thrombosis (DVT). The triad of venous stasis, hypercoagulability, and vascular endothelial injury constitutes the fundamental pathogenesis of DVT 4) . Hyperglycemia potentiates thrombotic risk through platelet activation and accelerated coagulation factor synthesis, thereby increasing susceptibility to embolic events 5) . Emerging evidence indicates the widespread dysregulation of glucose metabolism in patients with PE. Severe hyperglycemia independently correlates with elevated in-hospital mortality 6 , 7) , and higher blood glucose levels at admission are strongly associated with disease severity 8) , a phenomenon consistently observed even in non-diabetic populations 9) . Notably, intensive insulin therapy has not demonstrated any mortality reduction in critically ill patients and it may paradoxically increase hypoglycemia-related risks 10 , 11) .

Recent studies have highlighted the prognostic significance of metabolic disturbances in critical illnesses. Glycemic variability (GV), a comprehensive metric reflecting both glucose fluctuations and overall exposure, is closely linked to macrovascular and microvascular outcomes 12 - 14) . Robust evidence confirms GV’s independent predictive value of GV for mortality in sepsis, ischemic stroke, coronary artery disease, and heart failure 15 - 19) . However, few studies have so far explored the role of glucose-related parameters in PE outcomes. Although limited studies suggest that elevated admission blood glucose levels increase the mortality risk in PE patients, isolated assessment of admission glucose inadequately captures the overall glycemic control status or fluctuations during hospitalization. Therefore, we conducted a retrospective study to examine the associations of the mean blood glucose (MBG) and GV with ICU and in-hospital mortality in patients with PE.

2.Methods

2.1 Data Sources

Original data were sourced from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC-IV 3.0). The MIMIC database is a large, publicly accessible database developed and maintained by the Laboratory for Computational Physiology at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT). Version 3.0, released on July 19, 2024, includes hospitalization data for 94, 458 ICU patients from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center’s intensive care unit between 2008 and 2022 20) . One of the authors (Zihao Tang) completed the National Institutes of Health (NIH) training course on the protection of human research participants, passed the Cooperative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI) examination, and obtained authorization for data access (Record ID: 13448082). This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. As the database did not contain protected information and the patients were anonymous, we were granted an exemption from informed consent.

2.2 Subjects

Patients who were diagnosed with PE were included in the present study according to the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revision (ICD-9 and ICD-10) codes. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ＜18 years, (2) admitted to the ICU for ＜24 h or ＞30 days, and (3) fewer than three glucose measurements. For patients with multiple admissions, only data from the first hospitalization were included, and 1,493 patients were ultimately included in the study. A screening flowchart is shown in Fig.1 .

Patient Screening Flowchart

2.3 Data Extraction

PostgreSQL (version 16) and Navicate Premium (version 16.211) software programs were used to extract data. We defined the baseline as the first 24 h after ICU admission. For patients with multiple ICU admissions, we only extracted data from the first ICU stay. The extracted data included:(1) demographic information: gender, age; (2) vital signs: heart rate, respiratory rate (Resp Rate), blood pressure (BP), and oxygen saturation (SpO2); (3) comorbidities were determined based on the simplified international coding systems ICD-9 or ICD-10: hypertension, deep vein thrombosis (DVT), venous thromboembolism (VTE), chronic heart failure, acute kidney injury (AKI), diabetes mellitus, chronic pulmonary disease, malignancy, acute myocardial infarction, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, chronic kidney disease (CKD), atrial fibrillation (AF), sepsis, and the Charlson comorbidity index; (4) laboratory indicators: platelet count (PLT), creatinine (Cr),blood urea nitrogen (BUN), activated partial thromboplastin time (PTT), international normalized ratio (INR); (5)disease severity scores: Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), and simplified Pulmonary Embolism Severity Index (sPESI), Pulmonary Embolism Severity Index (PESI) risk strata and the 2019 European Respiratory Society (ERS) risk assessment for pulmonary embolism prognosis; (6) treatment: mechanical ventilation, glucose lowering therapy, thrombolytic therapy, anticoagulant therapy. The average value was used for all laboratory indicators with multiple results. Hemodynamic decompensation in the ERS risk model includes one of the following clinical presentations: cardiac arrest, obstructive shock (systolic BP ＜90 mmHg or vasopressors required to achieve a BP ＞90 mmHg despite an adequate filling status in combination with end-organ hypoperfusion), or persistent hypotension (systolic BP ＜90 mmHg or a systolic BP drop ≥ 40 mmHg for ＞15 min, not caused by new-onset arrhythmia, hypovolemia, or sepsis) 21) . Variables with missing values exceeding 10% were excluded to reduce potential bias ( Supplementary Table 1 ) . For variables with missing values less than 10%, multiple imputation was performed using the random forest method from the mice package in the “R” language software program 22) .

2.4 Exposure

The mean blood glucose level of the enrolled patients was calculated using the average of all venous blood glucose measurements taken during their ICU stay. GV represents temporal fluctuations in blood glucose levels and can be assessed using different metrics from the literature 12) . In the present study, GV was quantified using the coefficient of variation (CV), defined as the ratio of the standard deviation (SD) of all repeated blood glucose measurements to the MBG, expressed as a percentage 23 , 24) .

2.5 Outcomes

The primary outcome of the study was ICU mortality, which was assessed based on the vital status of the patients at the time of ICU discharge. Similarly, the secondary outcome was in-hospital mortality, determined by the vital status of the patients at the time of hospital discharge, distinguishing between survivors and non-survivors.

2.6 Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were presented as the mean±SD or median (interquartile range) according to data distribution, whereas categorical variables were expressed as numbers or percentages (%), and differences were compared among groups using the Pearson chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to evaluate the normality of the continuous parameters. The analysis of continuous variables was performed using t-test or ANOVA if they presented a normal distribution and using Mann–Whitney U-test or Kruskal–Wallis test if they were non-normal distribution. We classified the MBG and CV into three categories according to the tertiles levels (MBG＜119.33 mg/dL, 119.44 mg/dL＜MBG＜143.55 mg/dL, MBG＞143.61 mg/dL; CV＜15.02%; 15.03%＜CV＜24.81%; CV＞24.88%). A univariate analysis was used to explore the preliminary relationship of all variables with ICU and in-hospital mortality. Clinically relevant and prognosis-associated variables (p＜0.05) were included in the multivariate logistic model as confounding variables. Logistic regression models were applied to evaluate the odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). MBG and CV were analyzed as both continuous and categorical variables, and potential confounders were gradually adjusted in the four models. We progressively adjusted for the following confounders: sex, age, myocardial infarction, chronic kidney disease, malignant cancer, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, Charlson comorbidity index, diabetes, AF, sepsis, DVT, AKI, SBP, DBP, respiration rate, SpO2, sPESI, GCS, SOFA, SAPSII, mechanical ventilation, vasopressin, warfarin sodium, insulin, PLT, PTT, INR, Cr, and the BUN level.

Additionally, a four-knot restricted cubic spline (RCS) was used to present a potential non-linear relationship between glycemic variability and MBG and the outcomes. RCS was adjusted for the same variables as in multivariate logistic regression. For the observed nonlinear relationship, a two-piecewise logistic regression model was used to define the interval and determine the threshold effect, which was conducted according to the pre-specified subgroups, including age (＞65 or ≤ 65 years), comorbidities (sepsis and diabetes), interventions (mechanical ventilation), and sPESI score. Exploratory sensitivity analyses illustrated the distribution of blood glucose measurements during ICU stay and hypoglycemic events during ICU stay across tertiles of MBG and CV. All statistical analyses were performed using the R statistical package ( version 4.4), and P＜0.05.

3.2 GV and MBG were Associated with ICU and in-hospital Mortality

The patients were divided into three groups based on their MBG level and CV during ICU hospitalization: MBG tertiles (＜119.33 mg/dL, 119.44 mg/dL–143.55 mg/dL, ＞143.61 mg/dL) and CV tertiles (＜15.02%, 15.03%–24.81%, ＞24.88%). As the MBG levels increased, the risk of ICU and in-hospital mortality also increased ( Fig.2 ) : low group, 6.4% (ICU), 13.2% (in-hospital); middle group, 10.7% (ICU), 18.2% (in-hospital); high group, 21.9% (ICU), 31.3% (in-hospital). The mortality trend was similar between the CV groups ( Fig.3 ) .

A restricted cubic spline analysis ( Fig.4 ) revealed a linear relationship with ICU (p for nonlinear = 0.295) and in-hospital mortality (p for nonlinear = 0.062). In contrast, CV levels demonstrated a significant inverted U-shaped relationship with ICU mortality (p for nonlinear = 0.013) and in-hospital mortality (p for nonlinear = 0.009), with inflection points of 38.523% and 37.038%, respectively. The threshold effect analysis indicated that on the left side of the inflection points, the risk of mortality gradually increased with increasing CV levels. For every 1% increase in CV, the odds ratios for ICU mortality and in-hospital mortality increased by 0.035 ( Table 5 ) .

3.4 Combined Prediction of Mortality Risk in Pulmonary Embolism Patients Using MBG, CV, and ERS

As shown in Fig.5 , based on a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, the combined model of MBG, CV, and ERS risk demonstrated significant advantages in predicting both ICU mortality and in-hospital mortality. For ICU mortality prediction, the combined model achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.735 (95% CI: 0.712–0.758), which was significantly higher than the AUCs of MBG alone (AUC = 0.681, p＜0.001) and CV alone (AUC = 0.660, p＜0.001). This advantage was also observed in in-hospital mortality prediction, where the combined model had an AUC of 0.693 (95% CI: 0.670–0.716), showing statistically significant improvements compared with MBG alone (AUC = 0.641) and CV alone (AUC = 0.627) (p＜0.001). These results indicate that a multi-parameter combined strategy can effectively enhance the predictive performance of mortality risk in critically ill patients.

4.Discussion

This study systematically revealed the complex prognostic value of MBG and GV in critically ill PE patients with pulmonary embolism. The main findings include the following: (1) MBG exhibits a linear dose-effect relationship with mortality risk, while CV demonstrates an inverted U-shaped nonlinear association. (2) The prognostic value of glycemic parameters showed significant population heterogeneity; MBG maintained a linear association in the high-risk ERS subgroup, whereas the inverted U-shaped characteristic of CV was more pronounced in the intermediate-risk subgroup. (3) The combined assessment of MBG, CV, and ERS significantly enhanced the predictive performance of mortality risk. These findings provide important evidence for blood glucose management in PE patients with pulmonary embolism.

A substantial body of evidence-based medical research indicates that hyperglycemia and increased GV during hospitalization are independent predictors of adverse outcomes in critically ill ICU patients 25 - 27) . A complex bidirectional relationship exists between critical illnesses and glucose metabolism disorders. On one hand, trauma-induced stress, neuroendocrine activation, and inflammatory storms lead to insulin resistance, resulting in characteristic hyperglycemia and exacerbated glucose fluctuations 28 - 30) . However, this metabolic imbalance accelerates multi-organ dysfunction through mechanisms such as oxidative stress 31) , pro-inflammatory cytokine release 32) , and immune suppression 33) . Compared with sustained hyperglycemia, high GV is more strongly associated with endothelial dysfunction, microvascular complications, and elevated inflammatory markers 34) . It poses particularly significant risks of damage to cardiovascular/neuronal cells, kidneys, and pancreatic β-cells 35 , 36) , thereby increasing the mortality risk in critically ill patients. Dramatic glucose fluctuations may also worsen clinical outcomes by exacerbating the incidence of cardiovascular events and the overall metabolic homeostasis imbalance 13 , 37) . Notably, in non-diabetic patients with pulmonary embolism, acute thrombotic stress responses can trigger a sharp rise in blood glucose levels, further aggravating hypercoagulability through the activation of oxidative stress-inflammatory cascades 38) . Diabetic populations, owing to long-term metabolic adaptation, exhibit greater tolerance to glucose fluctuations 39) , which explains why the association between MBG and mortality risk is weaker in this group.

Studies have shown that diabetes is independently associated with in-hospital mortality in patients with pulmonary embolism 40) . Scherz et al. 9) demonstrated that elevated blood glucose levels on admission in patients with acute pulmonary embolism were significantly associated with 30-day mortality and readmission rates. This suggests that pulmonary embolism patients may experience glucose metabolism disorders, and that blood glucose levels could be an important factor influencing their prognosis. However, in the ICU setting, single-time-point blood glucose measurements are insufficient to comprehensively reflect the metabolic status, necessitating simultaneous attention to both MBG and GV. Our study utilized all venous biochemical glucose measurements during ICU stay to assess the average glucose levels and variability during ICU stay. Previous studies have used glucose measurements within the first 72 h of ICU admission to calculate GV and evaluate the correlation between short-term glucose fluctuations and outcomes in critically ill patients 41) . Based on this, our analysis of glucose measurement distribution in pulmonary embolism patients during their ICU stay revealed that the median duration of ICU stay duration was 3.81 days, with 50% of glucose measurements concentrated within the first 3.35 days after admission. Further analysis showed that MBG and CV calculated using glucose values within the first 72 h of ICU admission were significantly correlated with the results from the entire dataset (Pearson test), confirming that short-term monitoring (≤ 3 days) can effectively assess glucose control throughout the ICU stay. This phenomenon may be attributed to the relatively short ICU stay for most patients or the stabilization of parameter calculations due to the increased glucose monitoring frequency in long-term hospitalized patients.

This study found that the MBG levels were independently associated with ICU and in-hospital mortality risks, and a linear dose-effect relationship persisted after adjusting for confounding factors. This result is consistent with the findings of Jovanovic et al. 6) who observed that in pulmonary embolism patients, higher admission glucose (AG) levels (＞7.9 mmol/L) were associated with significantly higher 30-day all-cause mortality than lower AG levels (＜5.5 mmol/L). However, a subgroup analysis revealed significant population heterogeneity: elevated MBG levels significantly increased the mortality risk in non-diabetic patients, but no such association was observed in diabetic patients. Akirov et al. 42) , also found that non-diabetic patients with significantly elevated AG levels had a 2.4-fold increase in all-cause mortality, whereas no significant correlation was observed between AG levels and mortality in diabetic patients. This difference may be attributed to metabolic adaptive mechanisms in patients with diabetes under chronic hyperglycemic conditions.

Studies have shown that GV is closely associated with adverse outcomes in vascular diseases, such as cerebral infarction 43) , myocardial infarction 44) , and coronary heart disease 45) . This study found that increased CV significantly increased the risk of ICU and in-hospital mortality in patients with pulmonary embolism, further confirming the independent impact of glucose fluctuations on the prognosis of critically ill patients. The underlying mechanisms may involve multiple pathways through which glucose metabolism disorders promote thrombosis, including oxidative stress, endothelial damage, impaired fibrinolytic system function, platelet activation, and inflammatory response activation 46) However, a large Dutch case-control study did not find a direct relationship between diabetes and venous thromboembolism 47) . This suggests that blood glucose levels and their variability may play a complex role in the pathophysiological mechanisms of pulmonary embolism, but the specific pathological mechanisms still require in-depth exploration.

An RCS analysis demonstrated an inverted U-shaped association between glycemic variability and mortality in patients with pulmonary embolism. Mortality risk increased significantly with increasing glycemic variability to the left of the inflection point, whereas no statistically significant association was observed beyond this threshold. Importantly, this pattern is unlikely to indicate a protective effect of extremely high glycemic variability but rather reflects a combination of statistical and clinical factors. First, the asymmetric effect of hypo- and hyperglycemia may have contributed to this phenomenon. Sensitivity analyses showed a higher incidence of hypoglycemic events among patients categorized into the high CV group. In critically ill settings, hypoglycemia, particularly severe episodes, has a strong and independent association with mortality 48) , potentially dominating risk attribution and attenuating the apparent contribution of variability at higher CV levels. Consequently, the risk curve may plateau or change the slope beyond the inflection point. Second, treatment-related confounding factors cannot be excluded. In ICU practice, marked glucose fluctuations often prompt intensified monitoring and timely therapeutic adjustments such as modifications to insulin regimens or nutritional support. These interventions may partially mitigate the adverse effects of extreme variability, leading to apparent flattening of the risk curve in observational analyses. Finally, data sparsity at extreme CV values may increase the uncertainty at the tails of the restricted cubic spline curve, which is an inherent methodological limitation. Nevertheless, the identified inflection point appears to represent a clinically meaningful risk threshold: when glycemic variability approaches this level, mortality risk increases substantially, even if mean glucose remains within acceptable ranges, warranting closer clinical monitoring. Overall, these findings highlight the prognostic importance of glycemic variability in critically ill patients with PE, suggesting that excessive glucose fluctuations within a specific range may confer increased mortality risk. The inverted U-shaped associations observed in certain subgroups may reflect different pathophysiological contexts, including metabolic adaptation to chronic hyperglycemia in diabetes 49) , inflammation-driven dysregulation in sepsis 50) , heterogeneity of disease severity in patients with sPESI ＞1 or intermediate- to high-risk PE, and sustained stress responses related to the ongoing thrombotic burden in non-thrombolysis patients 51) .

A major strength of this study is the development of a predictive framework that jointly incorporates MBG and CV, which was established for the first time in a large, critically ill ICU population. Through a comprehensive adjustment for potential confounders, we demonstrated that glycemic parameters retain independent prognostic significance, thereby highlighting their potential value as an integrated biomarker system for more precise risk assessment in patients with pulmonary embolism. Importantly, the identification of a threshold effect for CV provides novel mechanistic insights into the relationship between glycemic variability and clinical outcomes. This finding helps clarify why treating CV as a linear continuous variable failed to reveal a significant association at the population level, likely because of the nonlinear and highly dynamic nature of glucose regulation in critically ill conditions 52) . Notably, when CV values were below the inflection point, incremental increases in CV were associated with a substantially higher risk of adverse outcomes, suggesting that maintaining tight glycemic stability may be particularly relevant for ICU patients with severe PE. Rather than supporting a uniform linear association, the observed inflection point indicates a change in the risk pattern of glycemic variability, allowing for a more refined stratification of prognosis. In this context, the identified threshold should be regarded as a reference marker for risk stratification, rather than a definitive clinical target. To our knowledge, this study is among the first to propose such a quantitative benchmark for GV in critically ill patients with PE. This reference may facilitate comparisons across different cohorts and clinical settings, and contribute to more individualized glucose management strategies, although validation in external populations remains necessary.

Distinct prognostic patterns across ERS risk subgroups likely reflect the different underlying pathophysiological stages. In the high-risk ERS subgroup, characterized by shock or severe hemodynamic instability, sustained hyperglycemia primarily reflects profound metabolic decompensation and severe organ dysfunction 53) , resulting in a strong and linear association between mean blood glucose and mortality, while the prognostic contribution of glycemic variability appears to be attenuated. In contrast, patients in the intermediate-risk ERS subgroup seem to occupy a transitional and partially compensatory state. In this setting, increasing glycemic variability may indicate worsening disease activity, whereas hypoglycemic events, acting as independent adverse prognostic factors 48) ，can amplify overall variability, contributing to the observed inverted U-shaped association between glycemic variability and mortality.

However, this study is associated with several limitations. First, despite controlling for confounders, such as differences in pulmonary embolism subtypes through multivariate adjustments, its retrospective design limits the ability to infer causality due to unmeasured clinical variables. Second, the inclusion of only patients with at least three blood glucose measurements during hospitalization may introduce a selection bias, restricting the generalizability of the results. Third, inconsistencies in the timing and frequency of measurements inherent in the database may affect data accuracy. Fourth, because the data were sourced from a single-center database (MIMIC), the geographical limitations of the findings may reduce their applicability to other regions, ethnicities, and cultural groups. Finally, future studies should employ prospective cohort designs and incorporate continuous glucose monitoring technology to explore the universality of MBG and CV thresholds and their underlying pathological mechanisms in diverse populations.

5.Conclusions

MBG and CV were independently associated with the mortality risk in patients with pulmonary embolism. The combined assessment of MBG, CV, and ERS significantly enhanced the predictive performance of mortality risk. Implementing blood glucose management in patients with pulmonary embolism may help to reduce the risk of mortality.
