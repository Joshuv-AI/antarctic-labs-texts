A randomized controlled superiority trial of 1 L (Plenvu) versus 2 L (Moviprep) polyethelene glycol plus ascorbate in a FIT-based colorectal cancer screening setting (PLEMO)
Authors: Søren Roepstorff, Thomas Bryrup, Morten Rasmussen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSufficient cleansing of the colonic mucosa is of great importance during colonoscopy emphasizing the need for effective preparation agents. Studies evaluating the efficacy of a novel 1 L polyethylene glycol with ascorbate (1 L-PEG-Asc), Plenvu®, versus standard 2 L polyethylene glycol with ascorbate (2 L-PEG-Asc), Moviprep®, have shown promising results regarding bowel cleansing. Nonetheless superiority of 1 L-PEG-Asc has not been shown.MethodsIn this single-center randomized controlled superiority trial, participants of the Danish national colorectal cancer screening program scheduled for colonoscopy were randomized to either 1 L-PEG-Asc or 2 L-PEG-Asc administered as evening/morning split-dose. Primary endpoint was efficacy of the bowel preparation assessed as bowel cleansing success according to Boston Bowel Preparation Scale (BBPS), adequate defined as BBPS ≥ 2 in each segment. Secondary outcomes included excellent bowel preparation defined as BBPS = 3 in each segment, nausea, vomiting, willingness to repeat the cleansing, and lesion detection defined as polyp, adenoma, sessile serrated lesion, and cancer detection.Results1275 participants were randomized to 1 L-PEG-Asc (n = 629) or 2 L-PEG-Asc (n = 646). 1 L-PEG-Asc achieved superior frequencies for adequate (92.2% vs. 86.1%, p < 0.001) and excellent preparation (59.2% vs. 43.8%, p < 0.001). Nausea (57.4% vs. 46.6%, p < 0.001) and vomiting (13.9% vs. 8.7%, p = 0.006) were more frequent in the 1 L-PEG-Asc group. Despite this, more participants were willing to repeat cleansing with this agent again (82% vs. 76%, p = 0.009). No difference was seen among lesion detection between the groups.Conclusions1 L-PEG-Asc showed superior bowel cleansing efficacy, but increased rates of vomit and nausea compared to 2 L-PEG-Asc. Despite this, willingness to repeat the cleansing with the same agent was more frequent in the 1 L-PEG-Asc group.Trial registrationEudraCT number: 2018-003304-39. Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains the third most common cancer worldwide and is the second leading cause of cancer mortality in developed countries [1]. Screening for CRC with use of faecal occult blood test and subsequent colonoscopy has proven to reduce mortality of CRC [2–4]. Polyp detection rate (PDR) and caecal intubation rate have been shown to correlate inversely with cleansing quality [5, 6]. Furthermore, inadequate bowel cleansing increases the risk of procedural difficulties and has shown association with increased costs, higher adenoma miss rate, lower caecal intubation rate and increased risk of post-colonoscopy colorectal cancer deaths [7–10]. Multiple studies have shown the efficacy of split bowel preparation consisting of polyethylene glycol (PEG) with or without ascorbate [11, 12]. However, the 4 L volume PEG bowel preparation can result in nausea, vomiting, bloating and abdominal distention [13, 14]. These conditions could, individually and in conjunction, lead to reduced adherence and subsequent inferior cleansing.

A few studies have evaluated efficacy of 1 L polyethylene glycol with ascorbate (1 L-PEG-Asc), Plenvu® (Norgine Danmark A/S, Valby, Denmark) compared to standard 2 L polyethylene glycol with ascorbate (2 L-PEG-Asc), Moviprep® (Norgine Danmark A/S, Valby, Denmark) for colon cleansing for colonoscopy (ingredients of the products are summarized in Table 1). In a randomized multicenter phase 3 trial Bisschops et al. reported an adequate bowel cleansing rate of 90.5% vs. 85.3% (p = 0.039) in the 1 L-PEG-Asc and 2 L-PEG-Asc groups [15], and in an Asian randomized multicenter trial by Hong et al. the rates of 1 L-PEG-Asc and 2 L-PEG-Asc were 93.1% vs. 91.9 (pnoninferiority < 0.001) [16]. A prospective multicenter observational study Maida et al. showed an overall cleansing success for participants using split dosing of 91.9% vs. 88.3% (p = 0.884) in the 1 L-PEG-Asc and 2 L-PEG-Asc groups [17]. These previous studies were conducted as non-inferiority trials limiting the possibility to show superiority and thereby to assess whether 1 L-PEG-Asc is superior to 2 L-PEG-Asc.

In this randomized, prospective superiority trial, we compare 1 L-PEG-Asc to 2 L-PEG-Asc for bowel cleansing in an faecal immunochemical test (FIT)-based colorectal cancer screening population.

Methods

Study/trial design

This study was a randomized, single centre, investigator blinded, superiority trial conducted at the Digestive Disease Center, Bispebjerg Hospital, Denmark. The study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration, the CONSORT guidelines and met the standard for Good Clinical Practice. The study was approved by the Danish Medicines Agency and the Ethic Committee of Capital Region of Denmark (EudraCT number: 2018-003304-39, trial registration date 23/8 2018).

Participants

Eligible participants were patients in the Danish national bowel cancer screening scheduled for colonoscopy at Bispebjerg Hospital. Men and women aged 50–74 with an FIT > 100 µg per ml faeces (≥ 20 µg haemoglobin/g faeces) were scheduled for screening colonoscopy. Participants were contacted by a study nurse for the purpose of study recruitment. Study information and consent was sent and signed electronically. Exclusion criteria were: inability to understand, read or speak Danish; inability to receive and sign documents sent via e-Boks (digital mailbox); anticipated compliance problems; hypersensitivity to the active substances in 1 L-PEG-Asc or 2 L-PEG-Asc; gastrointestinal obstruction or perforation; disorders of gastric emptying; ileus; phenylketonuria; glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency; toxic megacolon; severe chronic inflammatory bowel disease; pregnancy; breastfeeding; severe renal impairment (creatinine clearance less than 30 ml/minute/1.73 m2); cardiac failure (grade III or IV of NYHA); those at risk of arrhythmias; dehydration.

Interventions

Bowel cleansing was administered as split dosage for both groups. First dose was taken the evening before colonoscopy (approximately 18.00) and second dose on the day of examination 4 h prior to colonoscopy. In the 1 L-PEG-Asc group each dose was dissolved in 500 ml of water and taken over a 30-minute period, followed by at least 500 ml of clear fluid over the next 30 min. In the 2 L-PEG-Asc group each dose was dissolved in 1 L of water and taken over a period of one to two hours, followed by at least 500 ml of clear fluid. Instructions were based on the applicable Summary of Product Characteristics. Participants followed a low-fiber, seedless diet for five days leading up to the procedure. On the day before the colonoscopy, they were permitted breakfast, lunch, and a light dinner. However, once the first dose of the preparation began, only clear liquids were allowed. Both groups were encouraged to drink as much additional clear fluid as possible and received the same instructions with regards to cessation of medicines, such as iron supplements. Table 1 summarizes the ingredients of the two bowel cleansing products.

Outcomes

Primary endpoint was the efficacy of the bowel preparation assessed by the colonoscopist using the validated Boston Bowel Preparation Scale (BBPS) [18, 19]. Adequate bowel preparation was defined as a score of ≥2 according to the BBPS for each colonic segment. All endoscopists were educated in scoring according to the BBPS prior to enrolment of study participants. Assessment of the bowel preparation was made after all cleansing maneuvers were completed. In case of inadequate bowel preparation, the procedure would be aborted and categorized as incomplete.

Colonoscopies were performed by experienced endoscopists, all of whom had performed more than 500 colonoscopies overall. This included both doctors and nurse practitioners. Nurse practitioners were specially trained endoscopic nurses educated in performing colonoscopies working under supervision of a very experienced doctor who could be summoned at all times. This practice was in accordance with Danish national bowel screening program.

Secondary outcomes were excellent bowel preparation defined as BBPS = 3 in each segment, colonoscopy completion rate, polyp detection rate (PDR), overall polyp detection, adenoma detection rate (ADR), overall/mean adenoma detection, detection of sessile serrated lesions (SSL) and cancer detection rate. Incomplete colonoscopies were stratified by following parameters: inadequate cleansing, pain, malignant obstruction, or technical obstruction.

A questionnaire evaluating tolerability and adverse events was answered prior to colonoscopy. Following tolerability parameters was addressed: Vomiting, nausea, bloating, headache, quality of sleep (none, mild, moderate, severe, very severe); willingness to repeat preparation (yes/no); and amount bowel cleansing consumed/supplementary fluids consumed (0–24%, 25–49%, 50–74%, 75–99%). Consumption of at least 75% of bowel preparation was defined as sufficient. A 30-days follow up was assessed by information from patient medical file.

Sample size

A power calculation based on an expected cleansing success of 90% in the control group (2 L-PEG-Asc) and 95% in the intervention group (1 L-PEG-Asc), a significance level of 5%, power of 90% resulted in a sample size of 579 participants in each group [20]. A dropout rate of 10% was expected resulting in a sample size of 637 participants in each group and allows for showing superiority of 1 L-PEG-Asc. We expected a cleansing success of 95% based on standard by the European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE) [21].

Randomization and blinding

Participants were randomized (1:1) to receive 1 L-PEG-Asc or 2 L-PEG-Asc according to computer generated simple randomization sequence in REDcap. The study nurse conducted the randomization electronically after written consent was obtained. Randomization was blinded to the investigators. Participants were encouraged not to unveil their randomization to the endoscopist, yet strict endoscopist blinding was not possible.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.2 (R. R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). P values of less than 0.05 were considered significant. Continuous variables were summarized using mean ± SD. Normally distributed data were compared using unpaired Student t-test. Wilcoxon rank sum test or Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare non-normally distributed data in two or multiple groups respectively. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentage and compared using Fishers Exact test and Chi-squared test with Yates correction. A logistic regression model was used to analyse the factors associated with adequate and excellent bowel preparation scores. All randomized participants were included in analysis.

Results

From September 2019 until April 2022 a total of 1275 participants were recruited and randomized. 32 patients were excluded, and the remaining 1243 participants were included in the per protocol analysis with 615 and 628 in the 1 L-PEG-Asc and 2 L-PEG-Asc groups respectively (Fig. 1).

Efficacy of the bowel cleansing

BBPS-scores were missing in two (0.33%) and one (0.16%) participant in the 1 L-PEG-Asc and 2 L-PEG-Asc group respectively. 1 L-PEG-Asc achieved statistically significant higher bowel efficacy scores compared to 2 L-PEG-Asc for each colonic segment and for the total score for the primary endpoints of overall and adequate bowel cleansing. Mean BBPS overall scores were significantly higher in the 1 L-PEG-Asc group compared to the 2 L-PEG-Asc group (right 2.54 vs. 2.30, p < 0.001; transverse 2.60 vs. 2.36, p < 0.001; left 2.63 vs. 2.39, p < 0.001). In terms of adequate bowel cleansing defined as a BBPS score ≥ 2 in each segment, 1 L-PEG-Asc rates were statistically significantly higher compared to 2 L-PEG-Asc (right 93.2% vs. 88.1%, p = 0.001; transverse 95.0 vs. 89.2, p < 0.001, left 95.6 vs. 91.1, p < 0.001) (Table 3). Furthermore, excellent bowel cleansing defined as a BBPS score of 3 in each segment was significantly higher in the 1 L-PEG-Asc vs. 2 L-PEG-Asc group (right 65.9 vs. 50.8, p < 0.001; transverse 68.3 vs. 54.9, p < 0.001; left 68.5 vs. 53.5, p < 0.001) (Table 3).

Tolerability and fluid consumption

Questionnaire data used to assess tolerability was present in 599/615 (97.4%) in the 1 L-PEG-Asc group and 618/628 (98.4%) in the 2 L-PEG-Asc group. Answers for vomiting, nausea, bloating and headache were pooled to present (mild, moderate, severe and very severe) or none. Table 5 summarizes tolerability data. In the 1 L-PEG-Asc group nausea (57.4% vs. 46.6%, p < 0.001) and vomiting were significantly more frequent (13.9% vs. 8.7%, p = 0.006). Despite this, participants in the 1 L-PEG-Asc group were significantly more willing to repeat the given cleansing regime (82.1% vs. 75.9%, p = 0.009).

Subgroup analysis on participants aged ≥ 70 years showed no difference in frequency of nausea, vomiting, bloating and headache when compared to participants younger than 70 years.

Treatment related adverse events were recorded in 60 cases (9.8%) in the 1 L-PEG-Asc group and 43 cases (6.8%) cases in the 2 L-PEG-Asc group (p = 0.062). All adverse events can be seen in Table 6. Hospitalization was needed in additionally 9 cases which are summarized in Appendix 1. All severe events, as judged by the investigators, were found not likely to be due to the bowel cleansing. Discussion

In this single-center randomized superiority trial 1 L-PEG-Asc demonstrated statistically significant higher bowel cleansing efficacy across all colonic segments compared to 2 L-PEG-Asc, with a particularly pronounced effect in the right colon. Overall adequate bowel cleansing was achieved in 92.2% vs. 86.1% (p < 0.001), a margin that aligns with the 5% efficacy difference hypothesized in our power calculation. Furthermore, the 92.5% completion rate for the 1 L-PEG-Asc group meets the ESGE minimum standard of 90% although the target standard of 95% is not met [21]. Our efficacy results are consistent with published data, including two randomized controlled trials and a multicenter observational study comparing 1 L-PEG-Asc to 2 L-PEG-Asc [15–17].

Because excellent cleansing of the right colon correlates with higher detection rate of sessile serrated lesions, it remains a critical parameter in high-quality colonoscopy [22]. We found a statistically significant increase in excellent right colon cleansing with 1 L-PEG-Asc compared to 2 L-PEG-Asc (65.9% vs. 50.8%, p < 0.001). These findings mirror those reported by Hong et al. (60.9% vs. 48.8%, p = 0.024), Bednarska et al. (77% vs. 51%, p < 0.001), Lopez-Jamar et al. (1 L-PEG-Asc 65.4%), and Serradesanferm et al. (1 L-PEG-Asc 57.3%) [16, 23–25]. While Bisschops et al. observed generally lower rates of excellent cleansing in the right colon, a statistically significant higher rate was still seen for 1 L-PEG-Asc compared to 2 L-PEG-Asc (24.0% vs. 13.2%, p < 0.001) [15]. The discrepancy in percentages may be explained by their use of blinded central readers, whereas our study and others relied on on-site endoscopists.

Despite superior bowel cleansing in the 1 L-PEG-Asc group, lesion detection rates were comparable to the 2 L-PEG-Asc group for both PDR (59% vs. 58%, p = 0.791) and ADR (50% vs. 48%, p = 0.447). Existing literature remains divided. While Bisschops et al. observed no difference in ADR (26.5% vs. 26.8%, p = 0.569), they did report higher PDR in the right colon (23.3% vs. 16.2%, p = 0.024) [15]. Similarly, Hong et al. demonstrated an increased PDR in the 1 L-PEG-Asc group (48.9% vs. 37.8%, p = 0.038) [16], while Ahmad et al. found no significant differences in an observation study comparing 1 L-PEG-Asc and 2 L-PEG-Asc in patients undergoing screening colonoscopy (ADR 74% vs. 73%, p = 0.26; PDR 84% vs. 83%, p = 0.81) [26]. Consistent with the study of Ahmad et al. our detection rates were derived from a CRC screening program. This context likely explains the higher detection rates in observed in both studies.

Abdominal discomfort during the preparation was very common in both groups with nausea and vomiting being significantly more frequent in the 1 L-PEG-Asc group (Table 5). While these findings align with trials by Bednarska et al., Hong et al. and Serradesanferm et al. [16, 24, 25], other studies have reported markedly lower incidences [15, 17, 23]. It should be noted that the questionnaire in our study was not validated. Nonetheless the overall picture shows that tolerability is statistically significant lower with 1 L-PEG-Asc. This increased incidence of vomiting may be attributed to the higher ascorbate concentration in the second dose, which elevates the level of poorly absorbed osmotic agents [17]. Despite higher rates of nausea and vomiting a significantly greater proportion of participants were willing to repeat the 1 L-PEG-Asc preparation over the 2 L-PEG-Asc. This paradox, although difficult to understand, may be explained by the reduced volume of the 1 L-PEG-Asc allowing participant to consume larger quantities of other liquids (Table 5).

This study has several strengths. Most notably, the randomized controlled superiority design allowed for a direct assessment of whether the intervention drug showed superior efficacy, rather than the standard non-inferiority trial setup. Both endoscopists and investigators were blinded to the randomization, and bowel preparation was evaluated using the validate BBPS. Given the BBPS’s established inter-rater reliability [18], inter-individual variation among endoscopists was expected to be negligible. Furthermore, this trial was conducted within a real-world CRC screening population, enhancing its practical relevance.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The single-center design may limit external validity. Additionally, the recruitment process required telephone interviews and a digital mailbox, which may have introduced selection bias by excluding certain demographics. While endoscopists were blinded, it is possible that participants inadvertently revealed their preparation group, potentially introducing performance bias. Although central readers could further mitigate this risk, this trail was deliberately designed to reflect the performance of the two bowel cleansing agents in everyday endoscopy unit.

While three recently published randomized controlled trials have shown non-inferiority and safety of 1 L-PEG-Asc compared to known standard bowel preparation [15, 27, 28], they were not designed to show superiority of 1 L-PEG-Asc, and to our knowledge the current study is the first trial designed to do so. This study demonstrates a 6-percent-point increase in the adequate bowel preparation rate for the 1 L-PEG-Asc group compared to 2 L-PEG-Asc within an FIT-based colorectal cancer screening population. This margin of superiority is clinically relevant and could be pivotal in establishing the optimal foundation for high-quality colonoscopy.

Conclusions

In conclusion this single-center randomized controlled superiority trail showed superior bowel cleansing efficacy of 1 L-PEG-Asc compared to 2 L-PEG-Asc in all colonic segments. Furthermore, 2 L-PEG-Asc showed significantly reduced rates of nausea and vomiting while a significantly higher proportion in the 1 L-PEG-Asc group showed willingness to repeat the bowel preparation.
