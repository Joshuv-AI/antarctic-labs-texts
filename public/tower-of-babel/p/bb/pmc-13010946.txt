Sweetness enhancement and mechanism by sweet aroma compounds in the sucrose solution using sensory, electronic tongue, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulation
Authors: Jiao Liu, Yunwei Niu, Jiancai Zhu, Rujun Zhou, Zhimin Guo, Zhaohong Miao, Qi Lu, Yamin Yu, Jing Zhang, Zuobing Xiao, Qing Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the sweetness-enhancing effects of nine sweet aroma compounds from sweet orange on 5% sucrose solution. Through static sensory evaluations and electronic tongue analysis, carveol was found to enhance sweetness most significantly (28.8%), followed by γ-decalactone (23.2%), γ-nonalactone (23.0%), and δ-decalactone (20.0%). By using the time-intensity method for dynamic sensory evaluation, it was shown that the perception of sweetness increased rapidly and the duration was slightly prolonged after the addition of sweet aroma compounds. Molecular docking showed a binding free energy of −5.9 kcal/mol for the T1R2/T1R3-sucrose complex. Ternary complexes with added aromatic compounds had substantially lower energies (−8.62 to −10.5 kcal/mol), indicating enhanced receptor-ligand affinity. Molecular dynamics results showed that the addition of aromatic compounds led to the decrease in the binding energy of the T1R2/T1R3-sucrose system, the enhancement of hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, and the stabilization of dynamic changes in protein conformation.Keywords: Sweetness enhancement, Electronic tongue, Molecular docking, Sweet orange, Sweet aroma compounds, Molecular dynamics

Introduction

Citrus fruits, due to their rich variety and enormous yield, occupy an important position in the global agriculture and food industry. Their flavors, especially the unique flavor of sweet orange (Citrus sinensis), are highly favored in the food industry due to their rich nutritional value and broad application potential. Urgent innovative breakthroughs need to be achieved in the extraction of flavor compounds (Ferreira et al., 2023), stability maintenance, and new scenario development.

Faced with consumer demands for healthy diets (Chen et al., 2022) and the Outline of the Healthy China 2030 Initiative (W. Wang, 2020), health issues including obesity caused by high-sugar diets have highlighted the challenge of sugar reduction (Stanner & Spiro, 2020). Meanwhile, traditional non-nutritive sweeteners suffer from taste defects (Silva et al., 2023), and simple sugar reduction leads to the disrupts of flavor balance. In this context, the “aroma-based sugar reduction” strategy emerges (Reis et al., 2017), in which natural aroma components from citrus are utilized to activate olfactory-gustatory synergism. This can not only reduce the sugar content and maintain sweetness but also avoid the risks associated with artificial additives. This strategy provides a natural, healthy solution for reducing sugar in food, and is key to meeting consumer demands and promoting innovation in the food industry.

Cross-modal interactions, particularly enhancing the perception of sweetness (Q. J. Wang et al., 2024) through the addition of specific aromas, have emerged as an effective sugar-reduction strategy (Oliveira et al., 2021). Cross-modal synergy involves enhancing sensory experience through the integration of multiple sensory (Bertelsen et al., 2021) inputs (including visual, auditory, olfactory, and gustatory modalities). This synergy extends beyond the interaction between sweetness and aroma, encompassing synergistic effects between basic tastes (such as salty, umami, and spicy) and complex odor components. Initial research has evinced that introducing specific flavor compounds including vanilla (Alcaire et al., 2017) and strawberry aromas, can not only amplify the perception of sweetness (Oliveira et al., 2021) but also improve the overall flavor experience through olfactory-gustatory integration, a phenomenon widely used in the food industry (Bertelsen et al., 2020). Building on these findings, our research team conducted a series of systematic studies to explore how specific aroma compounds influence the perception of sweetness. Contrasting with the mixed aroma profiles in vanilla or strawberry flavors, the research focuses on the use of pure aroma compounds for enhancing the perception of sweetness. Through sensory evaluations and mechanistic investigations, several key aroma compounds have been identified that, when combined with sucrose, the perception of sweetness was amplified. These compounds generate a harmonious and appealing sweet-aromatic profile, thereby enhancing the overall sweet taste experience.

Limonene, one of the major aroma compounds under investigation, is identified as one of the primary volatile substances in sweet orange peel. It is not only the characteristic sweet-fruity aroma of orange formed by its contribution, but it is also speculated to trigger positive emotional responses such as pleasure and relaxation. The palatability of foods is not only enhanced by it, but sugar consumption is significantly reduced through cross-modal synergistic effects. Additionally, molecular dynamics (MD) simulation technology was employed to unveil the dynamic binding process between aroma compounds and sweet taste receptors. The limitations of traditional static sensory evaluation have been addressed by this dynamic analysis, which lays a theoretical foundation for the development of novel hypoglycemic strategies.

A multi-dimensional research system was established by integrating sensory evaluation, electronic tongue technology, and molecular simulation. Additionally, the mechanism through which sweet orange aroma compounds enhance sweetness via allosteric regulation of the sweet taste receptor T1R2/T1R3 was revealed. Traditional sensory evaluation lacks dynamic analysis and objective verification, as static assessments often fail to replicate real-world application scenarios. By contrast, dynamic sensory evaluation better reproduces human sensory experiences in natural states. Electronic tongue technology (Lu et al., 2022) achieves real-time electrical signal conversion of sweetness and acidity through a bionic sensor array. For instance, it has been applied in wine quality control (Rodríguez-Méndez et al., 2016), and was further employed to analyze umami differences in apples (Qiu & Wang, 2015), providing quantitative data for cross-modal research. Mechanistic studies of flavor perception rely heavily on molecular simulation techniques.

The sweet taste receptor T1R2/T1R3 is categorized as a class C G protein-coupled receptor (Sanematsu et al., 2014), formed by the non-covalent association of T1R2 and T1R3 subunits into a heterodimer. Its extracellular amino-terminal domain contains a Venus flytrap domain (VFD), which is regarded as the primary binding site for sweet substances. Within this dimer, the recognition of natural sugars (e.g., sucrose, fructose) is mediated by the VFD of the T1R2 subunit, while the T1R3 subunit is involved in conformational changes and signal transmission as the common partner. The transmembrane domain is primarily activated by artificial sweeteners (e.g., aspartame), indirectly improving the binding efficiency of natural sweeteners through allosteric modulation. Dynamic processes are further captured by MD simulations. Following ligand-receptor binding, the root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) of the VFD is found to be decreased, indicating stabilization of the dimer conformation. This stability is attributed to the strengthening of non-covalent interactions (such as hydrogen bond networks and hydrophobic effects) at the T1R2/T1R3 interface, facilitating sustained sucrose binding. The mechanism underlying the activation of the dimer involves closure of the T1R2-VFD, triggering downstream coupling to the Gα-gustducin protein. Consequently, calcium release is triggered and cell membrane depolarization is achieved via the PLCβ2-IP3 pathway, ultimately leading to the transmission of the sweet signal to the central nervous system (Evangelista-Falcón et al., 2023).

This study examined the effects of nine aroma compounds from sweet orange on taste perception. Firstly, sweetness enhancement and palatability modulation in a 5% sucrose solution were assessed by combined human sensory analysis and electronic tongue measurements. Secondly, binding affinities and interactive forces were determined using molecular docking. Thirdly, MD simulations of the T1R2/T1R3-sweet aroma-sucrose ternary system were performed to characterize dynamic binding processes. This study revealed the mechanism by which sweet aroma compounds enhance the perception of sweetness, providing foundational insights for developing healthier yet palatable reduced-sugar foods.

Materials and methods

Experimental materials

To investigate the sweetness enhancement effect of sweet orange aroma, nine representative sweet aroma compounds were selected in this study. The selection criteria included their identification as sweet orange aroma-active compounds(Rouseff et al., 2009; Shui et al., 2019), structural diversity (alcohols, ketones, esters, lactones), and potential for sweetness enhancement(Zhou et al., 2024). These nine sweet aroma compounds were: carvone, carveol, citronellol, benzyl alcohol, δ-decalactone, ethyl phenylacetate, furanone, γ-decalactone, and γ-nonalactone. All compounds were purchased from Shanghai Meixin Chemical Technology Co., Ltd. Sucrose was obtained from Henan Wanbang Co., Ltd., and distilled water (Watsons brand) was bought from Shanghai Titan Technology Co., Ltd. All materials were food-grade and stored under appropriate conditions.

Experimental methods

Threshold determination of sweet aroma compounds

Since the maximum recommended doses of aroma compounds vary, exceeding these limits may pose health risks. Previous studies focused on single-environment measurements, whereas the current research explored compound characteristics in a 5% sucrose solution. According to GB/T 22366–2008 (Standard for threshold determination of aroma compounds in water), concentrations were set to suprathreshold, threshold, and subthreshold levels (Kim et al., 2021). Compounds were dissolved in a 5% sucrose solution, with poorly water-soluble compounds dissolved using food-grade propylene glycol. The final concentration of propylene glycol (PG) used in this study was very low (0.1–0.2%). Solutions were serially diluted, and the threshold determination followed the three-alternative forced choice method (3-AFC). Sensory panelists identified the spiked sample from three options, starting at the highest concentration. Tests proceeded to lower concentrations until correct identification failed. Three replicates were performed at each concentration, with validity requiring at least two correct identifications.

The probability of detection (correct responses/total responses) was calculated and corrected using (P* = (3P-1)/2), where P* is the corrected value and P denotes the measured probability. Thresholds were defined as the concentration at P = 0.5 via S-curve fitting (Origin 2018).

Determination of the concentration of aroma compounds

Concentrations were determined utilizing S-curve-derived thresholds and recommended doses from flavor preparation guidelines. High concentrations did not exceed safety limits, while low concentrations remained above threshold levels to ensure detectability.

Sensory evaluation

Sensory evaluation was conducted with human volunteers after obtaining ethical approval. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Human Ethics Committee of the Shanghai Institute of Technology (Approval No. SIT-2025-LL43, Date: June 11, 2025). All sensory members were fully informed about the research purpose and potential risks, and provided their consent to participate in the research. They retained the right to withdraw at any time. Throughout the study, the rights and privacy of sensory members were strictly protected, with no disclosure of personally identifiable information.

The sensory evaluations were undertaken by 10 evaluators (five males and five females with an average age of 24 y), all of whom were employed by the School of Perfume and Aroma Technology, Shanghai Institute of Technology, and had previously received professional training.

All sensory evaluation experiments were performed in a temperature-controlled sensory laboratory, with the room temperature maintained at 20–22 °C. Before testing, samples were taken out of refrigeration at 4 °C and allowed to stand in the laboratory for at least 30 min to ensure temperature equilibration with the test environment.

Before the sensory tests, all panelists were required to complete three training sessions to become familiar with the relevant rating scales. On the test day, they were asked to avoid spicy foods or strongly scented personal care products, and to abstain from smoking or eating for at least one hour before testing. In this study, participants were tasked with evaluating two attributes: sweetness and comfort. The sensory tests adopted a 10-point scale (1−10) to assess the sweetness of solutions with a series of concentration gradients. Specifically, 1 indicates nearly no sweetness, while 10 represents very high sweetness, with a gradual increase from 1 to 10. In the control group, the fixed values for the sweetness of a 5% sucrose solution were set to 5. The comfort level indicates the perceived acceptability of the test solution to team members. Evaluation results were recorded to one decimal place. Each sample set was tested under ventilated conditions, then allocated to sensory panelists for the evaluation of sweetness and hedonic preference, and was finally expectorated. Between samples, palate cleansing was undertaken by drinking water. Each sample was tested with at least a 2-min interval between events. To ensure anonymity, samples were presented to participants in varying orders during the experiment. This sensory panel participated in all the experiments involving sensory evaluation in this study.

Dynamic sensory evaluation (time-intensity method)

The time-intensity (TI) method (He et al., 2021) is a dynamic and quantitative sensory evaluation approach, by which evaluators continuously record the processes of enhancement and diminishment in the intensity of perceived sensory attributes within a specific time frame. Ten trained panelists assessed temporal changes in the sweetness intensity using SensoMaker software. After placing samples in the mouth for 15 s, sweetness was scored (0−10) at defined intervals, and each group of samples was repeated three times. After 15 s, the sample was spat out, with the tongue kept stationary in the mouth to avoid interference from uncontrollable variables as far as possible. The 5% sucrose solution was tested first, followed by samples with optimal concentrations of aroma compounds. Rinsing and environmental controls matched static evaluation protocols (Lorido et al., 2015). The method enables a more comprehensive description of the dynamic changes in the sensory characteristics of food, particularly the temporal evolution of perceived sweetness.

Electronic tongue

The electronic tongue is an intelligent analytical instrument (Lu et al., 2022) for simulating the human taste system (Qiu & Wang, 2015), achieving objective quantitative detection of taste characteristics through bionic sensor arrays and pattern recognition technologies. Its core principle involves using artificial lipid membrane sensors to mimic the response mechanism of taste cells, converting basic tastes such as sour, sweet, bitter, salty, and umami into electrical signals, which are then analyzed through algorithms such as principal component analysis (PCA) and artificial neural networks (ANN). The Insent TS-5000Z electronic tongue (Insent Inc., Atsugi, Japan) measured the intensity of sweet taste via its GL1 sweet sensor. Sensor responses to electrode potential differences were recorded for the 5% sucrose solution containing aroma compounds. Pre-balanced sensors were immersed in 40 mL samples, with six 30-s measurement cycles per sample. Data were exported for analysis.

Construction of sweet taste receptors

The amino acid sequences of TAS1R2 (T1R2, ENTRY: Q8TE23) and TAS1R3 (T1R3, ENTRY: Q7RTX0) were all derived from UniProtKB (https://www.uniprot.org/). Alphafold 2.0 was used to predict the structure of the T1R2/T1R3 heterodimer and evaluate the quality of the results.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking analyses were performed to investigate the interactions of nine sweet aroma compounds (Carvone, carveol, citronellol, benzyl alcohol, δ-decalactone, ethyl phenylacetate, furanone, γ-decalactone, and γ-nonalactone) with the sweet taste receptor T1R2/T1R3.

Ligand SDF files (PubChem) were converted to .pdb format (Openbabel). Aroma compounds and sucrose were docked to T1R2/T1R3 using AutoDockTools V1.5.6 and AutoDock Vina V1.2.0. Active sites were predicted via DeepSite, with T1R2's VFD cavity coordinates set to (17.9, −20.0, −16.6). Semi-flexible docking allowed the ligand flexibility. Preprocessing included receptor dehydration, hydrogenation, and charge addition. Ligands were energy-minimized before docking. PyMol and LigPlot+ visualized binding interactions.

MD simulations

In this study, molecular docking was performed using Schrodinger software. After the .pdb files of taste receptor-aroma substance combinations were imported into the software, preprocessing of proteins was conducted, including correcting bonding information, adding hydrogen atoms, capping with NME and ACE, and removal of water molecules (Xiao et al., 2024). Subsequently, an aqueous environment system was established. The SPCE model was taken as the water model. A boundary with a buffer distance of 10 Å was set, and a salt concentration of 0.15 mol/L was set to simulate the in-vivo environment. Compared with other water models, the most accurate internal energy and enthalpy of vaporization were obtained by the SPCE model. Moreover, it can reflect the short-range ordered and long-range disordered microstructures of water molecules, and the relative error of the self-diffusion coefficient is far better than that of other models. A 100-ns MD simulation was conducted. A total of 1000 frames were recorded at 100-ps intervals. The MD simulation was performed using an isothermal, isobaric ensemble (NPT) simulation system at a temperature of 300.0 K and a pressure of 1.01325 bar. The post-simulation results were analyzed in the “Simulation Interactions Diagram” module of the software.

Data and statistical analysis

Sensory and electronic tongue data were compiled in MS-Excel®. SPSS performed one-way ANOVA and Duncan's multiple range test (p < 0.05) for the significance of sweetness. Origin software was used to plot subsequent graphs.

The results indicate that the thresholds of carvone, δ-decalactone, ethyl phenylacetate, and γ-nonalactone are decreased in a 5% sucrose solution compared to those in water. This reduction suggests a synergistic effect between these compounds and sucrose, enhancing the perception of their aromas. Water molecules that are colorless, odorless, and small, exhibit weak molecular interactions with sweet aroma compounds, causing relatively low thresholds for these compounds. In contrast, interactions between sweet aroma compounds and sucrose molecules restrict their diffusion, resulting in an increased threshold in the sucrose solution.

Effect of sweet aroma on sweetness of a 5% sucrose solution

Sensory analysis of nine sweet aroma compounds on sucrose

In these experiments, the cross-modal interaction between sweet aroma compounds and perception of sweetness varied depending on the compound concentration. Adding appropriate (Bagnulo et al., 2024) concentrations of sweet aroma compounds to a 5% sucrose solution enhanced the perception of sweetness; however, exceeding compound-specific thresholds reduced sensory comfort, causing adverse effects. Results indicated that within certain concentration ranges, sweetness scores rose with an increasing concentration for compounds including carveol, benzyl alcohol, δ-decalactone, ethyl phenylacetate, furanone, and γ-decalactone. As displayed in Fig. 1, carveol achieved the peak sweetness and comfort scores at 2.5 mg/kg, above which both declined.

Due to their inherently pleasant aromas, moderate concentrations of these compounds enhanced the solution comfort. For example, furanone with its distinct caramel aroma and subtle pineapple notes, evokes associations with caramelized sugar or roasted flavors, enhancing the perception of warmth and sweetness. Its fruity undertones also add freshness and appeal. Carvone, characterized by mild mint and dill notes, synergizes with sweeteners to amplify the perception of sweetness indirectly through its refreshing aroma.

Sweetness and comfort were not, however, always positively correlated. For carvone, citronellol, and γ-nonalactone, sweetness scores initially increased but later declined with rising concentrations. Their optimal sweetness scores occurred at 6 mg/kg, 24 mg/kg, and 10 mg/kg, respectively, while peak comfort scores were observed at much lower concentrations (0.006 mg/kg, 0.01875 mg/kg, and 0.05 mg/kg). In particular, citronellol exhibited a steady decline in comfort scores as its concentration increased, likely due to its strong lemongrass aroma, which can lead to irritation or discomfort at higher concentrations.

Fig. 1(k) illustrates the percentage increase in sweetness for each compound relative to a 5% sucrose solution (blank control). These data reveal the relative sweetening effects at various concentrations. Among them, carvone, γ-decalactone, and γ-nonalactone show the most significant sweetening enhancement, with noticeable sensory effects. Statistical analysis using one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan's multiple range test (p < 0.05) confirmed that the sweetness scores at the optimal concentration of each compound were significantly increased compared to the 5% sucrose control. Furthermore, carveol showed a significantly greater enhancement than all other compounds (p < 0.05), whereas no significant difference was observed between γ-decalactone and γ-nonalactone (p > 0.05).

In conclusion, the sensory evaluation results of each compound were compared with their corresponding electronic tongue measurements. The results indicate that certain compounds, including ethyl phenylacetate and furanone, possess higher levels of sweetness and good sensory acceptability. Other compounds may have unique advantages or limitations in specific applications.

Dynamic sensory analysis of nine sweet aroma compounds on sucrose

Dynamic sensory analysis (Ruangjiranan, 2016) revealed distinct temporal profiles in the perception of sweetness (de Medeiros et al., 2021) between sucrose and aroma compounds, with shifts in dominance occurring within 0–15 s post-ingestion. Temporal data showed that all samples exhibited a rapid increment of perceived sweetness intensity upon initial contact, indicating a strong immediate sensory response. The control (a 5% sucrose solution) displayed a relatively gentle profile, with sweetness peaking at 4–5 s and declining slowly to a stable state, consistent with the characteristic slow onset and sustained sweetness of sucrose.

In contrast, aroma compounds induced more drastic changes in sensory sweetness. Based on their intensity profiles (Fig. 2), the nine compounds were classified into four groups: γ-nonalactone and γ-decalactone, rapid peak intensity (c. 4 s), followed by a gradual decline while maintaining high sweetness; furanone and ethyl phenylacetate, rapid peak intensity (c. 6 s), followed by a slow decrease, maintaining moderate-high sweetness; δ-decalactone and benzyl alcohol, slower peak intensity (c. 8 s), followed by a gradual decline to a plateau; citronellol, carveol, and carvone, slowest peak intensity (c. 10 s), followed by a gradual decrease until stabilization.

All aroma compounds significantly enhanced the maximum perceived sweetness intensity (Imax) compared to the sucrose control. This reflects a potent sweetness-enhancing effect, particularly for carvone, citronellol, γ-decalactone, and γ-nonalactone. However, peak timing and sweetness persistence varied considerably. Sweet aroma compounds such as furanone and benzyl alcohol produced a strong initial impact (early, high peak) but declined rapidly. Carvone and γ-nonalactone maintained high levels of sweetness throughout the test period, implying superior duration and stability. Conversely, carveol and δ-decalactone exhibited consistently low level of sensory sweetness. The transient peak observed in δ-decalactone (c. 4.5 s) is likely attributed to its rapid dissolution and release of aroma molecules, which trigger an initial perception of sweetness, thereby enhancing the overall sweet sensation.

Characterized by a creamy aroma and commonly utilized in food flavoring, δ-decalactone exhibited a peak in perceived sweetness at approximately 4.5 s. This peak may be attributed to several factors: upon entering the oral cavity, the substance containing δ-decalactone is rapidly dissolved and mixed with saliva, releasing its characteristic aroma molecules. These molecules were detected by olfactory receptors in the nasal cavity, which sent signals to the brain, triggering the perception of sweetness. Within this short timeframe, the concentration of δ-decalactone reaches its peak, corresponding to the maximum intensity in sensory evaluation. When combined with a 5% sucrose solution, δ-decalactone enhances the overall sweet experience.

In summary, this experiment revealed the sensory characteristics and temporal evolution of various aroma substances. Analysis of these data provides a better understanding of how various ingredients interact, enabling us to optimize food formulations to meet consumer need.

Sweetening effect of nine sweet aroma compounds determined by electronic tongue

The electronic tongue is considered as a promising taste analysis tool, which simulates the mammalian taste system by detecting taste compounds in liquid samples (Lu et al., 2022). After six electronic tongue tests, the average response value of the 5% sucrose solution was measured as 19.29. The value of 19.29 served as the baseline response of the 5% sucrose solution. Responses exceeding this baseline indicate a relative enhancement in sweetness intensity detected by the sensor. Electronic tongue results showed increased sweet taste response values for all nine sweet aroma compounds after addition. Among these, γ-decalactone (0.025 mg/kg) yielded the highest response (23.94), while ethyl phenylacetate (0.0625 mg/kg) produced the lowest (18.87, no sweetening effect). The agreement was found between the electronic tongue and sensory results for δ-decalactone, carveol, furaneol, ethyl phenylacetate, and carvone. However, the electronic tongue response for citronellol contradicted the sensory data, where an increment of sweetness was shown with rising concentrations.

A significant mechanism of aromatic sweetness involves the stimulation of oral and nasal receptors through the retronasal route. Sweet aromatic compounds strengthen the perception and recognition of sweetness by activating both the olfactory and gustatory systems. An electronic tongue produces (Vanaraj et al., 2025) electrical signals through interactions between its sensors and flavorful compounds. While it can simulate certain facets of taste perception, it cannot entirely reproduce the intricate mechanisms of the human gustatory system. Particularly, stimulation of nasal receptors by aromatic compounds is a critical factor that the electronic tongue fails to emulate. Hence, discrepancies may exist between the sensory outcomes of aromatic sweetness and the assessments made by the electronic tongue. Consequently, discrepancies may arise between sensory results of aromatic sweetness and evaluations made using the electronic tongue, underscoring the importance of sensory analysis in conjunction with instrumental methods. In contrast, human sensory perception involves a complex integration of olfactory and gustatory signals within the central nervous system.

To quantify the relationship between the two methods, Pearson correlation analysis was performed. The results showed that a weak to moderate positive correlation trend was observed between the response values of the electronic tongue GL1 sensor and sensory sweetness intensity, but the correlation did not reach statistical significance (r = 0.374, p = 0.32). The coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.140) indicated that electronic tongue data could only explain approximately 14% of the variation in sensory sweetness. This low consistency was not considered a methodological failure. Instead, it profoundly reflected the “cognitive gap” between instrumental detection and human perception. The electronic tongue detects physicochemical interactions between compounds and an artificial lipid membrane. In contrast, human perception represents the final output following the integration of olfactory, gustatory, and trigeminal sensations, as well as prior experience and expectations, within the central nervous system. It has also been suggested that the sweetness enhancement effect largely originates from this higher-level cross-modal integration (Q. J. Wang et al., 2024). Therefore, the results of this study precisely emphasize that in the investigation of “ aroma-based sugar reduction” strategies, instruments such as the electronic tongue are more suitable as rapid screening tools for the preliminary assessment of whether a compound interacts with the receptor. The final flavor effect and consumer acceptance, however, must be verified and confirmed through human sensory evaluation. This multidimensional research system, which combines instrumental analysis with sensory science, was regarded as one of the core methodologies of this study.

Effects of aroma compounds on the interaction between sucrose and T1R2/T1R3 using molecular docking

Molecular docking results indicated that all nine sweet aroma compounds-docked with sucrose. Leveraging the LigPlot software, the interactions between T1R2 and each of the nine sweet aroma compounds in the sucrose complex were analyzed. The VFD of the T1R2 subunit is an active cavity, also known as the orthosteric site, and is considered the primary structure for taste regulation. Molecular docking results suggest that the interaction between sucrose and T1R2/T1R3 is located in the VFD region of T1R2, a finding consistent with previous studies. The VFD of the T1R2 monomer is the preferred binding site for certain sweeteners, but not for T1R3. Molecular docking results (Fig. 5A) reveal that the binding energy for the T1R2/T1R3-sweet aromatic compound-sucrose complex ranges between −10.5 kcal/mol and − 8.62 kcal/mol, which is lower than that of the T1R2/T1R3-sucrose system (−5.9 kcal/mol). Currently, there is no definitive evidence to prove whether the observed sweetening effect after the addition of aromatic compounds can be explained by the higher binding affinity between the interceding ligand and receptor in this system. The analysis unveiled that their interactions were primarily mediated by hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions (Bulusu & Desiraju, 2020). Specifically, the amino acid residues TYR103, GLU178, and ASN107 were frequently involved in hydrophobic interactions with the sucrose molecule.

As the number of amino acid connections increases, this increase may enhance the binding stability between sucrose and the taste protein. This enhanced binding may render the taste protein more sensitive to sucrose, thereby enhancing the sweetening effect to a certain extent. Among them, the aroma molecules of γ-nonalactone and furanone are connected to a larger number of amino acids, while the aroma molecules of carveol and carvone are connected to a smaller number of amino acids. More amino acid connections may provide more binding sites for the sucrose molecule, allowing it to bind more firmly to the taste protein, inducing a stronger taste signal transduction and thereby exhibiting a more pronounced sweetening sensation. Alternatively, the presence of aroma compounds may affect the sweetening effect by influencing the connections of amino acids. The binding of aroma molecules to the taste protein may alter the protein conformation, thereby influencing the number of amino acid connections involved in the binding of sucrose to the taste protein (Miao et al., 2022).

The molecular docking diagram of T1R2/T1R3-ethyl phenylacetate-sucrose is used as an example, both with 3D and 2D images. The hydroxyl groups of sucrose form hydrogen bonds with specific amino acid residues in the T1R2/T1R3 sweet taste receptor, primarily including: GLU178, VAL108, HIS50, and PHE159. The amino groups on these amino acid residues form hydrogen bonds with two hydroxyl groups on sucrose, with bond lengths of 2.7 Å, 2.4 Å, 3.2 Å, and 2.7 Å, respectively, and also exhibit hydrophobic interactions with residues such as ALA176, THR280, THR242, and ILE104 (Fig. 3). Therefore, it can be inferred that adding sweet aromatic compounds stabilizes the conformation of VFD, thus enhancing the affinity for sucrose and improving the perceived intensity of sweetness, resulting in a sweetening effect.

MD simulation

Dynamic stability analysis of T1R2/T1R3-γ-nonalactone-sucrose complex by MD simulations

In this experiment, the kinetic simulation results of the T1R2/T1R3-γ-nonalactone-sucrose complex were analyzed, and the mechanism of the interaction between aroma substances and sucrose was revealed. The sweetening effect on the sucrose solution is demonstrated by γ-nonalactone in artificial sensory evaluation, electronic tongue analysis, and molecular docking. Therefore, to investigate the binding interaction of T1R2/T1R3-sucrose after aroma addition, γ-nonalactone was selected for MD simulations in the ternary copolymer system.

The stability of all dynamic simulation models was evaluated based on the root mean square deviation (RMSD), where this parameter can characterize the structural changes of the receptor protein before and after ligand binding and is adopted to analyze the structural stability, flexibility, and possible conformational changes of the protein. When the ligand binds to the protein, local or global structural changes in the protein may be induced. The results are shown in Fig. 4.

The RMSD and RMSF in the ternary systems of T1R2/T1R3 with sucrose after the addition of γ-nonalactone, carveol, and δ-decalactone were respectively demonstrated.

A smaller amplitude of RMSD values changing over time is considered to indicate better stability during the simulation process, and the stable binding between the ligand and the receptor is also indicated by this result. As illustrated in Fig. 4, the RMSD of the T1R2/T1R3-sucrose complex fluctuated significantly during the 0–20 ns and 45–50 ns intervals. Particularly around 50 ns, pronounced fluctuations in T1R2/T1R3 were observed, suggesting that significant conformational changes in the protein structure occurred during this simulation phase. As the simulation time extended to 60 ns, the protein conformation reached equilibrium, with its RMSD stabilizing at approximately 7.5 Å. This means that the protein maintained good structural stability when bound to the ligand during this phase. Fig. 4 shows that the RMSD of the T1R2/T1R3-γ-nonalactone-sucrose complex also fluctuated significantly during first 15 ns, and the protein conformation changed substantially during this simulation phase, particularly around 13 ns. As the simulation time prolonged to 20 ns, the protein conformation reached equilibrium, with its RMSD stabilizing at approximately 8 Å. This indicates that the protein maintained good stability when bound to the ligands during this phase. Therefore, when γ-nonalactone was added to the T1R2/T1R3-sucrose complex, the fluctuation time of the system decreases significantly, indicative of good stability of the ligand within the protein-binding pocket. The complex thereby acquires a stable conformation, which in turn promotes the overall stability of the system. It is suggested that γ-nonalactone may enhance the affinity between T1R2/T1R3 and sucrose.

RMSF is calculated to measure the movement of individual amino acids during simulations, and it is used to reflect the local changes in specific amino acid residues within the entire protein chain. Fig. 4 shows the RMSF values of residues during the simulation of the binding of sucrose to the T1R2/T1R3 and T1R2/T1R3-γ-nonalactone complex. It can be found that the RMSF values of most residues fluctuate around 5.0 Å. However, there are also regions with relatively large RMSF values, including PRO3 (11.78), ALA5 (9.14), THR7 (9.4) in the T1R2 chain, and LYS850 (11.65), ASP842 (9.51), GLY3 (8.89), GLU852 (12.89), ASN844 (10.65), MET1 (8.72), and GLN840 (8.90) in the T1R3 chain. This indicates that the amino acids at these positions fluctuate significantly during the simulation. The peaks represent the protein regions with the greatest fluctuations during the simulations. The degree of atomic position fluctuations reflects the dynamic behavior of each atom in the molecule. After the sucrose ligand is bound to the T1R2/T1R3 protein, a relatively high structural flexibility is observed in the region spanning residues 800–870 AA. This region is exactly the junction between the C-terminus of T1R2 and the N-terminus of T1R3. The fluctuations of amino acids at both ends (N-terminus and C-terminus) of the receptor proteins in the two systems are much greater than those in other parts of the protein structure. Hence, in the regions with smaller RMSF values, the conformational changes of the complex are maintained in a relatively stable state. Similarly, the RMSF trends of the T1R2/T1R3-γ-nonalactone-sucrose and T1R2/T1R3-sucrose complex are similar. This finding indicates that the stable state of the original system is not altered by γ-nonalactone, and the system still maintains an equilibrium conformation. Furthermore, the lower maximum peak value shows that under the influence of the aroma compound, more amino acids can be attached to the sweet taste receptor proteins T1R2/T1R3. As a result, the ligand-receptor binding is promoted, and the stability of the ligand-receptor protein structure is enhanced.

Analysis of interactions between receptor and ligands in the T1R2/T1R3-γ-nonalactone-sucrose complex

The interactions between the protein and the ligand can be monitored by the interaction scores throughout the simulation (Evangelista-Falcón et al., 2023). The interactions can be divided into four types: hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, ionic bridges, and water bridges. As illustrated in the diagram of simulated interactions (Fig. 5), in the T1R2/T1R3-γ-nonalactone-sucrose complex system, the scores of the amino acid residues SER105, ASN107, GLU178, and THR179 over the entire simulation time are all higher than 1, indicating that there are multiple different or multiple identical interaction forces between sucrose and the amino acid residues. In the binary system, the simulation time fractions of residues LYS65, GLU302, ASP278, and SER144 all exceed 1, suggesting that multiple or several identical interactions occur between sucrose and these residues. The T1R2/T1R3-γ-nonalactone-sucrose ternary complex system, the simulation time fractions of residues SER105, ASN107, GLU178, and THR179 also surpass 1, suggesting that there are similar interactions between sucrose and these residues. In addition, the 2D diagram in Fig. 5(B) shows that for the binding between sucrose and the amino acid residues of the A-chain of the T1R2/T1R3 protein, direct hydrogen bonds are formed with LYS65, ASP278, GLU302, SER165, and SER303, while indirect hydrogen bonds are generated by water bridges with residues ASP142, GLU302, VAL384, SER303, ASP278, and SER144.

These results clearly demonstrate that hydrogen bonds constitute the primary force driving the formation of stable conformations within the T1R2/T1R3- sucrose complex. As hydrogen bonds are regarded the primary force inducing the formation of ligand-protein complexes, and the hot spot residues involved in the binding of sucrose ligand to the sweet taste receptor are polar amino acids, structural insights into the formation of intermolecular hydrogen bonds were further explored. As displayed in Fig. 5(B), in the T1R2/T1R3-γ-nonalactone-sucrose complex system, 15 residues: GLU178, SER158, SER105, ILE104, VAL108, ASN107, and THR179 directly form hydrogen bonds; GLU178, GLN55, PHE159, HIS50, SER105, ASN107, ARG177, and THR149 indirectly form hydrogen bonds via water bridges. This indicates that the addition of γ-nonalactone enhances the interactions between T1R2/T1R3 and sucrose, thus demonstrating a promoting effect, which is consistent with the results of artificial sensory and bionic electronic tongue analyses. It is noteworthy that when the binding energies of sucrose and the T1R2/T1R3-γ-nonalactone-sucrose complex are compared with the relative sweetness measured by artificial sensory experiments, the binding energy trends of the complex are found to be consistent with those trends in sweetness observed experimentally.

Comparison of MD binding energies between the T1R2/T1R3-sucrose complex and its complexes with three sweet aroma compounds

The introduction of sweet aroma compounds has been influenced on the complex intermolecular interactions in the solution, particularly for the hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions between amino acid residues and sucrose. As a class C G protein coupled receptor with multiple flexible regions, T1R2/T1R3 undergoes conformational adjustments upon interaction with sweet aroma compounds. These adjustments disrupt originally stable hydrogen bond networks while facilitating the formation of new hydrogen bonds at specific sites, concurrently enhancing hydrophobic interactions that contribute to the stabilization of the receptor-ligand complex. MMGBSA binding energy calculations confirm the enhanced stability of the ternary system compared to the binary system. The total binding energy of the binary system (−32.08 kcal/mol) is significantly reduced upon the addition of different aroma compounds, γ-nonalactone lowers it to −47.58 kcal/mol, carvone to −44.92 kcal/mol, and δ-decalactone to −75.6 kcal/mol. As shown in Fig. 4, this energy reduction directly reflects the strengthened affinity between T1R2/T1R3 and sucrose, which is further supported by the conformational stability observed in RMSD analysis.

Dynamic tracking of binding energy reveals that the binary system reaches equilibrium after 60 ns, while the ternary systems achieve equilibrium as early as 20 ns (e.g., γ-nonalactone) with a smaller fluctuation amplitude during the equilibrium phase. This accelerated equilibrium and reduced energy fluctuation validate the role of aroma compounds in stabilizing the dynamic behavior of the receptor-ligand complex. Decomposition of energy components (Table 2) uncovers compound specific enhancement mechanisms, δ-decalactone exhibits the most pronounced reduction in hydrophobic energy (Lipo), from −9.75 kcal/mol to −14.42 kcal/mol, which aligns with its aroma characteristics and 20.0% sweetness enhancement rate in sensory experiments. Carvone primarily strengthens electrostatic interactions, as evidenced by the decrease in Coulomb energy from −35.12 kcal/mol to −39.61 kcal/mol. γ-nonalactone shows a slight change in hydrogen bond energy, but achieves synergistic stabilization through the combined enhancement of hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions.

Furthermore, the magnitude of the reduction in binding energy exhibits a positive correlation with the rate of sensory sweetness enhancement (r2 = 0.61, Fig. 5C). This relationship defines a closed loop in which energy fluctuations modulate molecular stability, thereby shaping sweetness perception. By bridging molecular-level interactions with sensory outcomes, this correlation provides a mechanistic foundation for the “aroma-based sugar reduction” strategy.

With the addition of aroma compounds, the binding regions, sites, configurations, and conformations of the original T1R2/T1R3-sucrose complex system may be altered, thus affecting the recognition and affinity between T1R2/T1R3 and sucrose. When the ternary complex of T1R2/T1R3-sweet aroma compounds-sucrose is formed, hydrophobic interactions can be significantly enhanced. This enhancement is primarily attributed to the presence of hydrophobic amino acid residues in the system. A hydrophobic microenvironment for sucrose molecules is created by these amino acid residues, which not only has binding space provided for them but also significantly enhances the stability of the binding between sucrose and the receptor (Evangelista-Falcón et al., 2023). Consequently, this stabilized binding prolongs the activation of the T1R2/T1R3 receptor, facilitating sustained signal transduction via the PLCβ2-IP3 pathway and culminating in enhanced sweetness perception. Collectively, these findings underscore the indispensable role of hydrophobic interactions in the formation and stabilization of the ternary complex, identifying them as a key driver of the sweetness enhancement elicited by sweet aroma compounds.

Furthermore, conformational alterations in T1R2 and T1R3 induced by aroma compounds optimize the receptor's recognition and affinity for sucrose. These modifications refine the binding regions, sites, and configurations of the original binary complex, yielding a more favorable interaction interface. The synergistic effects of hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and electrostatic forces collectively enhance the stability of the receptor-ligand complex, a finding corroborated by molecular docking results indicating reduced binding free energy within the ternary system. The convergence of evidence from multiple analytical techniques (sensory evaluation, electronic tongue, molecular docking, and MD simulation) confirms the reliability of the proposed mechanism.

Correlation and differences between molecular docking and MD simulation energy results

A significant discrepancy is observed in the absolute values between the predicted binding free energies of the T1R2/T1R3-aroma compound-sucrose ternary complex from molecular docking (−8.62 to −10.5 kcal/mol) and the total binding free energies calculated by MM/GBSA from MD simulations (−32.08 kcal/mol to −47.58/−44.92/−75.6 kcal/mol). This phenomenon originates from the fundamental differences in the model rigor and physical completeness of the two computational methods.

Molecular docking essentially represents a “static snapshot” calculation, where semi-flexible models and simplified scoring functions are employed for rapid conformational search and ranking, its energy values are primarily used for comparing relative affinities. In contrast, MD simulation is intrinsically a “dynamic process-based” calculation conducted in an explicit aqueous environment. After extensive equilibrium sampling, a more comprehensive consideration of solvation effects, receptor flexibility, and detailed non-bonded interaction energies (Coulombic, van der Waals, hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic effects, etc.) is achieved by the MM/GBSA method. Consequently, its calculated results are considered closer to the actual binding free energy under physiological conditions.

Although the absolute values are not directly comparable, a consistent trend is revealed by both approaches. The incorporation of aroma compounds is found to significantly lower the system energy and enhance complex stability in all cases. This agreement in trends corroborates the sweet taste enhancement effect observed in sensory experiments, jointly pointing to a potential mechanism whereby aroma molecules function by stabilizing the receptor-ligand complex.

Conclusion

Nine sweet compounds were screened for sweetness-enhancing effects when added to the 5% sucrose solution. Through combined sensory evaluation and electronic tongue analysis, all compounds were found to enhance the sucrose sweetness to varying degrees while maintaining palatability. Among them, carveol exhibited the most significant sweetness enhancement (28.8%, p < 0.05 compared with all other compounds), followed by γ-decalactone (23.2%) and γ-nonalactone (23.0%), which showed no significant difference from each other (p > 0.05), and δ-decalactone (20.0%). Molecular docking was performed with the sweet taste receptor T1R2/T1R3 against nine sweet compounds. The docking results revealed that binding energies with sucrose were reduced (from −10.5 kcal/mol to −8.62 kcal/mol) in the presence of sweet compounds (Fig. 5A), suggesting enhanced complex stability and potential enhancement of sweetness.

Finally, MD was employed to assess effects of γ-nonalactone, carvone, and δ-decalactone on the T1R2/T1R3-sucrose system. As demonstrated in Table 2, hydrophobic interactions were significantly enhanced. Total system energy reduced (−32.08 kcal/mol to −47.58/−44.92/−75.6 kcal/mol). MD simulations (0–100 ns) revealed stable sucrose binding and reduced early-stage van der Waals energy versus the binary system. Coulomb, van der Waals, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic energies were identified as primary drivers of sweetness enhancement.

It is important to note that the current study primarily focused on a limited set of compounds from a single source, and the correlation between instrumental readouts and human perception showed variability due to the complexity of cross-modal mechanisms. The robustness of statistical reporting and the rationale for compound selection are crucial to the credibility of the mechanism. While our current findings offer a feasible pathway for “aroma-based sugar reduction,” there are inherent limitations in fully elucidating the perception mechanism solely based on the present methodologies.

Future research should aim to expand the compound library to validate the generalizability of these findings across different chemical structures. Furthermore, to bridge the gap between molecular interactions and sensory perception, integrating advanced neurophysiological techniques (such as fMRI or EEG) may be necessary to directly monitor the central nervous system's response to cross-modal stimuli. Such multi-dimensional approaches will be essential for building a more comprehensive and robust theoretical framework for flavor modulation.

The mechanism underpinning the interactions was elucidated, revealing synergistic sweetening effects between compounds and sucrose. The perception of sweetness is enhanced through aroma modulation, enabling food and beverage products to be developed with significantly reduced sugar and artificial sweeteners (up to 30%) while maintaining sweetness. Market demands for healthier, yet palatable, options can thus be met, delivering substantial health benefits and commercial value.
