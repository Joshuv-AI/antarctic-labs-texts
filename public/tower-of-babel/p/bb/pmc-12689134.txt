Introduction

Obturator hernia (OH) is considered a rare type of abdominal hernia, most frequently occurring in elderly, emaciated women, which has led to its colloquial designation as the “little old lady’s hernia”[1]. Clinically, it often manifests as small bowel obstruction accompanied by pain in the medial thigh or knee on the affected side. The condition was first identified by Arnaud de Ronsil in 1724, and Obre performed the first successful operation in 1851[2]. Its reported incidence has risen in recent years, likely due to global population aging[3]. The hernia develops through the obturator canal, which contains the obturator nerve and vessels. Diagnosis is frequently delayed due to the condition’s nonspecific and subtle clinical presentation, contributing to a mortality rate as high as 70% in cases complicated by strangulation or bowel ischemia[5]. Unlike other abdominal wall hernias, OHs usually do not produce a palpable mass, and imaging studies may fail to provide definitive findings. Compression of the obturator nerve and vessels can lead to poorly localized symptoms such as referred leg pain. The Howship–Romberg sign, characterized by medial thigh pain exacerbated by hip movement, is a notable clinical indicator but is present in only about half of cases[5].

Because of the diagnostic difficulties and high risk of complications, OHs demand urgent surgical management, as most present with bowel incarceration or obstruction[5]. Increasing clinical vigilance may facilitate earlier diagnosis and subsequently improve patient outcomes by reducing both morbidity and mortality[6].

In this context, we present a case involving an elderly female with bowel obstruction caused by a strangulated OH. This case report adheres to the SCARE Guideline Checklist 2025[7].

Case presentation

We report the case of a 76-year-old female patient who was treated for Tako-Tsubo syndrome in 2023 and is being followed for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease under treatment, portal vein thrombosis under anticoagulation, and an ascending aortic aneurysm under surveillance. She was admitted to the emergency department due to the onset of an obstructive syndrome, presenting with acute abdominal pain associated with vomiting, which had started 2 days before her admission, with no other associated signs. On general examination, the patient was conscious with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) of 15 out of 15, hemodynamically and respiratory stable, and afebrile. Upon examination of the hernia orifices, a painful and irreducible right-sided swelling was observed. The abdomen was tender, bloated, and tympanic overall. Biologically, there were no disturbances in the electrolyte balance, and all tests were normal. A chest and abdomen X-ray revealed small bowel air-fluid levels (Fig. 1). A nasogastric tube and urinary catheter were placed. The patient was then taken to the operating room for surgical exploration and underwent a median laparotomy. Exploration of the peritoneal cavity confirmed the radiological diagnosis of bowel obstruction due to a strangulated loop of small intestine entering the right obturator foramen. The hernia sac was very edematous and adhered within the obturator canal. Upon releasing the loop, signs of non-advanced ischemic damage were noted, and it fully recovered. A cloudy serous effusion was present in the pelvis and was collected for bacteriological analysis. The rest of the bowel was examined and showed no abnormalities. The peritoneum was sutured with Vicryl 4.0 to close the wound, and the round ligament was also sutured to the OH. No ileal resection was necessary due to the vital condition of the intestine. Aponurotic closure of the median laparotomy incision was then performed with PDS 2/0. The patient began oral feeding and passed stool on the 3rd postoperative day (POD); she was discharged on the 4th POD after a complete restoration of bowel function. At a follow-up appointment 6 weeks later, the patient showed no pain, no masses, and the surgical scars were well healed with no signs of infection. She was discharged in good condition.

Discussion

OH is an uncommon but clinically significant condition, with only 541 cases documented in the literature by 1980[8]. This type of hernia occurs more frequently in elderly, multiparous women due to a wider pelvis, a larger obturator canal, and greater laxity of pelvic tissues[9]. Anatomically, OHs may be bilateral but are more commonly right-sided, occurring three times as often as left-sided cases, likely because the sigmoid colon provides partial protection to the left obturator foramen[10].

The hernia forms through the obturator foramen, an opening inferior to the acetabulum bordered by the ischial and pubic rami (Fig. 3A). This foramen is covered by the obturator membrane, formed by contributions from the obturator internus and externus muscles. The membrane opens anterosuperiorly to form the obturator canal, a narrow passage transmitting the obturator nerve, artery, and vein. Within the canal, the obturator nerve divides into anterior and posterior branches. The anterior branch travels superficial to the obturator externus, supplying motor innervation to the pectineus, gracilis, and adductor longus and brevis, as well as sensory innervation to the medial thigh. The posterior division passes through the obturator externus and courses between the adductor brevis and magnus muscles, providing motor input to these muscles. Weakening of the obturator membrane can cause enlargement of the canal, permitting herniation. Due to anatomical differences, females are six times more likely to be affected than males. Three types (or stages) of OH are described based on the underlying anatomic defect. Hernias containing only preperitoneal connective tissue and fat (pilot tag) are classified as type I (Fig. 3B). Once the peritoneum indents upon the internal opening of the obturator canal, the hernia progresses to an invaginate peritoneal sac and is classified as type II. At cross-sectional imaging, this usually presents with a fluid-filled sac. Further herniation of pelvic or peritoneal viscera such as bowel, bladder, or ovary classifies the hernia as type III.

Clinically, patients often present with features of small bowel obstruction, such as abdominal pain, vomiting, and constipation, and a palpable mass is rare. Neurological signs such as the Howship–Romberg sign, pain radiating along the medial thigh exacerbated by hip movements, are present in 15–50% of cases[12]. This results from compression of the anterior division of the obturator nerve. Another sign, Hannington–Kiff, involves the loss of the adductor reflex while retaining a normal patellar reflex, due to posterior obturator nerve involvement[13]. It is important to note that type I hernias, which contain only preperitoneal fat, are often asymptomatic and found incidentally in up to 64% of women during cadaveric dissection. Symptomatic hernias (types II and III) involve the peritoneum and potentially viscera such as bowel, bladder, or ovary. Approximately 80% of patients develop bowel obstruction, often partial, due to Richter-type herniation (type I) of bowel into the canal. Pain is typically localized between the inguinal crease and medial thigh, mimicking musculoskeletal or neurologic disorders, thus delaying diagnosis. Early diagnosis is essential but often difficult due to the nonspecific nature of the symptoms. CT of the abdomen and pelvis remains the most sensitive and specific imaging modality[14,15]. The most characteristic CT finding is bowel herniation between the pectineus and obturator muscles. However, reliance solely on imaging can delay surgical intervention, particularly in frail patients[16].

Surgical repair is the definitive treatment for OH. Various approaches exist, including abdominal, inguinal, retropubic, and laparoscopic methods. In emergency settings, especially with complications like strangulation or ischemia, a midline laparotomy is typically preferred. This approach offers wide exposure of the obturator ring and pelvic floor and facilitates bowel resection if necessary[17]. Although primary suture repair can close the hernia defect, this does not address the weakened obturator canal and may lead to recurrence. Thus, filling the defect with adjacent structures such as the greater omentum or bladder is often recommended for a more durable repair.

Although relatively underreported due to the rarity of the condition and technical challenges, laparoscopic repair is gaining traction. It provides several advantages, such as reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stays, and quicker recovery[18]. It also allows for better visualization of both obturator canals and enables bilateral exploration. The transabdominal preperitoneal and total extraperitoneal techniques have both been described successfully[19,20]. A transperitoneal approach may be the most favorable because it can establish the diagnosis, provide adequate exposure, protect the obturator vessels, and allow the compromised bowel to be identified and resected when necessary[21]. In elective or stable cases without signs of peritonitis or ischemia, laparoscopy can be both diagnostic and therapeutic, helping avoid unnecessary laparotomy in frail patients.

Delayed diagnosis contributes significantly to the high morbidity and mortality rates associated with OHs, up to 70% in cases with strangulation or bowel ischemia[22]. This underscores the importance of clinical awareness, especially in high-risk populations. Conclusion

Strangulated OH is an uncommon yet serious condition, often identified at advanced stages due to the nonspecific nature of its symptoms. This diagnosis should be particularly considered in older women, who may experience this condition more frequently due to the anatomical characteristics of the pelvis and the increased tissue laxity that occurs with age. Clinical diagnosis remains rare, with the Howship–Romberg sign observed in approximately 50% of cases[18]. While ultrasound can be helpful when performed by an experienced clinician, contrast-enhanced CT scanning is generally considered the most reliable method for confirming the diagnosis[28].

Timely surgical intervention is crucial to minimize the high morbidity and mortality associated with this condition. Surgical management typically involves reducing the hernia contents, evaluating the viability of any incarcerated tissue, and closing the obturator foramen, often with the use of adjacent structures such as the bladder or omentum.
