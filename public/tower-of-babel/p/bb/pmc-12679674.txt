Digital hybrid technique for immediate complete denture fabrication: A clinical report
Authors: Amr Farrag, Amira Fouda, Sadaf Kashani, Anthony McCullagh, Chris Wyatt, Caroline Nguyen, Mohamed Gebril
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article presents three cases outlining the benefits of utilizing a combination of digital and conventional workflows for the fabrication of immediate complete dentures. This combined digital and conventional approach addresses the recording of denture border extensions, digital articulation, and fabrication of complete dentures.

Immediate dentures are fabricated prior to extraction of compromised teeth and are delivered at the surgical appointment. 1 Immediate dentures help reduce the psychological and social impact of tooth extraction; 2 maintain patients' esthetics and facial appearance, vertical dimension of occlusion (VDO), musculature and muscle tone, and residual ridge anatomy; and promote healing after tooth extraction. 3 , 4

The introduction of computer‐aided design and computer‐aided manufacturing (CAD‐CAM) technology was a breakthrough in the field of removable prosthodontics. However, different digital fabrication methodologies have been utilized to fabricate conventional and immediate complete dentures. 5 Subtractive manufacturing technology (milling) and additive manufacturing technology (3D printing) have been explored, but the subtractive approach is now considered to be the technology of choice because of the documented superior mechanical properties, less bacterial adhesion, color stability, and, more importantly, esthetics. 5 , 6 This article presents three cases outlining the benefits of utilizing a combination of digital and conventional workflows for the fabrication of immediate complete dentures.

CLINICAL REPORTS

Patient #1

A 71‐year‐old male patient presented complaining that he was uncomfortable wearing and masticating using his maxillary partial removable dental prosthesis. Medical history was reviewed, indicating that the patient had type II diabetes, gastric reflux, and hypertension. It was confirmed that both the hypertension and the type II diabetes were well controlled with medication (metformin and atenolol) under the care of the medical physician.

Upon detailed clinical and radiographic examination, the patient was diagnosed with maxillary and mandibular partial edentulism. The remaining maxillary right second premolar, right canine, and left second premolar were periodontally compromised with Grade I to Grade II mobility. The mandibular arch consisted of a six‐unit metal ceramic fixed dental prosthesis with adequate margins restoring all anterior teeth, as well as an adequate removable partial dental prosthesis.

Different treatment options were discussed with the patient, including conventional complete dentures as well as implant rehabilitation options (fixed and removable). The patient consented to proceed with an immediate maxillary complete denture.

The maxillary partial denture was virtually aligned using 3Shape software (3Shape A/S). The generated standard tessellation language (STL) file format was saved and sent to the dental laboratory for the design and fabrication of a modified 3D‐printed prototype. After alignment of the scans, the new virtual teeth setup was designed and digitally articulated based on the existing occlusal vertical dimensions. The modification included a window opening within the maxillary denture prototype to accommodate the remaining dentition. The modified maxillary denture was used to verify the VDO, teeth mold, size, and color. Phonetic and esthetic outcomes were verified and approved by the patient. The extension of the borders and the denture fit, as captured on the digital scan, was adequately extended (Figure 2). Using the modified maxillary prototype denture, a vinyl polysiloxane (VPS, Aquasil Ultra+, Dentsply Sirona) light body wash impression was recorded to properly capture the intaglio of the complete denture (Figure 3).

The maxillary prototype with the wash impression was sent back to the dental laboratory and scanned. The dental technician surgically removed the remaining maxillary dentition virtually and subtractively manufactured a polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) (Ivotion, Ivoclar Vivadent) immediate complete denture utilizing the recorded information from the physical prototype. After the fabrication of the immediate complete denture, the patient's remaining maxillary teeth were extracted under local anesthesia, and the extraction sockets were curetted to remove any granulation tissues, irrigated with saline, and sutured. The immediate maxillary complete denture was inserted. At insertion, the prosthesis had acceptable retention, support, and stability. Considering the nature of immediate dentures, soft relining (Coe‐Soft resilient denture liner; GC America, Inc.) was performed initially to accommodate tissue changes and ensure proper retention. After 6 months, hard relining was performed to achieve the ultimate fit of the maxillary immediate denture and full tissue healing (Figure 4). For the hard relining, a wash impression was taken using the complete denture, and then it was processed with laboratory heat‐cured acrylic.

The maxillary arch after full healing of the soft and hard tissue, and the maxillary complete denture.

Patient #2

A 63‐year‐old male patient presented to the clinic to replace his missing teeth. The patient's past medical history was reviewed and found to be non‐contributory to the current presentation. The patient was diagnosed with generalized stage IV grade C periodontitis following radiographic and clinical examination, and all remaining teeth were deemed hopeless. This included maxillary right first molar, second premolar and maxillary left central incisor, lateral incisor, canine, first premolar, second premolar, and first molar. The treatment plan for this patient was to start with the maxillary arch clearance and replacement with a complete denture, and then proceed with the treatment of the mandibular arch and fabricate an immediate denture. An implant locator overdenture would only be offered to the patient if he improved his oral hygiene. Preliminary alginate impressions (Kromopan, LASCOD S.p.A.) and IOS (Trios 3, 3Shape) were performed as part of the initial records. Thereafter, a face‐bow transfer was performed, and the 3D‐printed models were mounted on a semi‐adjustable articulator (Denar Mark 330, Whip Mix). A custom tray was fabricated for the patient conventionally on the stone model.

At the second appointment, border molding was performed using green stick compound (Figure 5c) to allow for accurate border recording for the prospective denture, and an alginate impression was taken after blocking out the interdental undercuts using utility wax, as the teeth were severely mobile (Figure 5e). The impressions were poured using Microstone (ISO type 3 Model stone, Whip Mix) (Figure 5b).

Border molding and impression.

The stone master casts were modified using the panoramic radiograph as a guide to approximate the bone level to be reflected on the model and the guide (Figure 5a,b,d), and sockets were beveled and finished, removing 2 mm from the buccal surface and 1 mm from the lingual surface (Figure 5b,d). The master casts were sent to the laboratory to be scanned using the laboratory scanner (3Shape E4 lab scanner, 3Shape). The generated STL file was imported into 3Shape software using the complete denture module, and the immediate complete denture was designed using both the digitized model and the extraoral photos, which were superimposed onto the model using the palate before removing the teeth. No facial scan was performed for this patient. The models were related to the patient's face to predict the approximate incisal edge position and smile. The denture was designed accordingly (Figure 6) using both the digitized model and the patient's extraoral photos. This design was then sent to the lab along with the mounted diagnostic casts to print the immediate denture prototype. The same casts were used to fabricate a surgical guide using an Essix retainer sheet and a thermoforming machine (Essix Ace Plastic, Dentsply Sirona).

3Shape software using the complete denture module to design the maxillary immediate denture.

On the day of the surgery, the extractions were performed using an atraumatic extraction technique, and the planned alveoloplasty was verified using the surgical guide. The surgical template was tissue‐supported, and was made on the modified model; minimal alveoloplasty was needed, and the guide was used to detect the blanching in the mucosa and remove the bone accordingly.

The denture stability and support were acceptable, but a Coe‐Soft liner (Coe‐Soft resilient denture liner; GC America, Inc.) was added for more comfort and to help with retention. The patient was instructed that this material is easy to peel and to ensure careful brushing while cleaning the denture. The immediate denture was relined and inserted for the patient, and the patient was informed that the denture should not be removed for 24 h. The immediate maxillary denture showed acceptable retention, support, and stability when clinically assessed. Occlusion (bilateral contact was obtained), phonetic, and esthetic outcomes were verified and approved. Healing and follow‐up showed a favorable outcome (Figure 7).

Follow‐up photo of the patient after the insertion of the immediate denture.

Patient #3

A 61‐year‐old female patient presented with chief complaints of bad breath, gum bleeding, complications associated with previous dental restorations, and an inability to reposition a dislodged fixed dental prosthesis. The history of her chief complaint included a full mouth rehabilitation that was performed over 13 years ago. The medical history was significant for hyperthyroidism controlled by medication, alongside a smoking habit of five to six cigarettes per day. Methimazole 10 mg/day was taken for hyperthyroidism.

The clinical examination revealed a spectrum of problems, including missing teeth, ineffective oral hygiene, multiple splinted restorations with recurrent caries, missing prosthesis, and gingival inflammation. Periodontal examination disclosed deep pockets for 75% of the dentition and bleeding upon probing in all periodontal pockets. Radiographic examination confirmed severe generalized bone loss and inadequate restorations (Figure 8). The patient was diagnosed with stage IV grade C periodontitis, partial edentulism, failing dentition, compromised restorations, and inadequate oral hygiene. Each tooth was assigned either a poor or hopeless diagnosis. Multiple treatment options were discussed, ranging from tooth extraction and replacement with conventional or immediate complete dentures to rehabilitation via implant‐supported prosthesis. Despite the patient's interest in implant‐based restoration for the failing dentition, immediate implant placement was deemed unsuitable due to active periodontal disease. Consequently, the recommended course of action involved extracting all teeth except mandibular canine teeth, which exhibited the least amount of bone loss. These two teeth were to be retained and used in future implant planning, while the missing teeth would be replaced with a maxillary complete immediate denture and mandibular immediate acrylic partial dental prosthesis with clasps. The treatment plan for this patient was to start with the maxillary arch clearance and replacement with a complete denture, then proceed with the treatment of the mandibular arch and fabricate an immediate denture. An implant locator overdenture would only be offered to the patient if she improves her oral hygiene. This treatment included the extraction of maxillary right first molar, maxillary right second premolar, maxillary left central incisor, maxillary left lateral incisor, maxillary left canine, maxillary left first premolar, maxillary left second premolar, and maxillary left first molar.

Radiographic examination confirmed severe generalized bone loss and inadequate restorations.

Considering the mobility of the patient's teeth, the decision was taken to proceed with digital scans and the creation of digital prostheses. Notably, the patient expressed satisfaction with the esthetics and length of her maxillary anterior dentition. Therefore, it was determined that her current maxillary dentition should be utilized as a reference point for setting the anterior teeth in the new denture. However, the patient's occlusion exhibited overclosure and an inadequate mandibular occlusal plane. Consequently, it was decided to adjust the VDO during the denture design process.

The treatment commenced with the acquisition of a digital IOS capturing the maxillary and mandibular arches, remaining teeth, denture‐bearing areas, and centric relation occlusal record at the desired occlusal level, utilizing the Trios 3 scanner (3Shape) (Figure 9). Subsequently, the obtained data were digitally mounted in a virtual articulator by incorporating the use of a 3D facial scanner (RAYFace Scanner; Ray America Inc.), resulting in the creation of a virtual patient. The generated STL files were then saved and forwarded to the dental laboratory for denture design and the fabrication of a 3D‐printed prototype for the mandibular arch (Figure 10). Following the creation of the 3D‐printed prototype, it was utilized to verify the VDO and the mandibular denture border. The prototype prosthesis was utilized to verify the centric relation records at the new proposed VDO, which was increased by 3 mm. Minimal adjustment was needed before capturing a new occlusal record using the IOS, which was then sent to the dental laboratory. Next, the borders of the prototype mandibular denture were molded with heavy‐body VPS (Aquasil Ultra+, Dentsply Sirona) to accurately capture the denture border. After the prototype was returned to the dental laboratory, instructions were provided to finalize the denture design (Figure 11). Once the design was approved, it was transferred to the CAM software (MILLBOX, CIMsystem). Then, subsequently, the data were exported to a 5‐axis milling machine (DWX‐52DCi, Roland DG) for the denture fabrication process. The pink PMMA disk (IvoVase CAD Pink‐V, Ivoclar Vivadent) and white PMMA disk (SR Vivodent CAD Multi BL3, Ivoclar Vivadent) were used to mill the denture base and tooth arch, respectively. The milled teeth arch was then bonded to the denture base using PMMA base bonding material (Ivotion Bond Kit 10, Ivoclar Vivadent). For the mandibular partial denture, after milling the denture base and before bonding the teeth, minor grooves were created on the denture base for the wrought wire clasps. The wrought wire clasps were added to the milled denture base using PMMA‐based acrylic material (ProBase, Ivoclar Vivadent).

Intraoral scan (IOS) captured at the desired vertical dimension using Trios 3 scanner (3Shape).

Prototype design for the mandibular arch.

Final denture design created with 3Shape software (Dental System; 3Shape).

Following the teeth extractions, the immediate maxillary and mandibular dentures were inserted with minimal need for adjustments (Figure 12). The patient underwent a follow‐up visit 24 h post‐extraction, during which the dentures were removed, and the extraction sockets were rinsed with saline. Additionally, the dentures were cleaned and assessed. Adequate retention, support, and stability of the dentures were noted, and the patient expressed satisfaction with the fit, esthetics, and phonetics despite this being her first experience with removable prostheses. However, during the subsequent 3‐week follow‐up, a decrease in retention, stability, and support was observed. This issue was addressed through a chair‐side reline (Coe‐Soft resilient denture liner; GC America, Inc.), with plans for a hard reline once full healing of the sockets is confirmed.

DISCUSSION

Multiple case reports 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 have focused on presenting a combination of digital and conventional workflows to take advantage of the techniques of digital impression of mucostatic tissues and also the benefits of physical conventional capture of the border molding areas. The use of intraoral scanners for the capture of the nonmobile tissue in the edentulous arch is predictable; however, the capture of the border extensions is still a limitation of intraoral scanners. 11 , 12 This is because of the limited ability of an intraoral scanner. The capture of the functional movements is crucial to capture muscular movements that replicate the functional movements of patients wearing complete removable dentures and for the retention and fit of removable complete dentures. For immediate dentures, the use of soft relining material can improve the border extension initially, and finally, converting it into hard relining after complete tissue healing would address any border extension deficiency. The scanning pattern has a direct impact on the accuracy of the edentulous arch scan. 7 , 9 , 12 Furthermore, the capture of a properly extended final impression for an immediate denture can be challenging due to tooth mobility and bony extensions.

In this case series, multiple clinical scenarios were described with different combinations of the digital conventional workflows for the fabrication of an immediate complete removable denture. In the first case, the patient's existing partial dental prosthesis was used for the fabrication of a 3D‐printed prototype that was used to verify the occlusal vertical dimension, phonetics, and esthetics, as well as occlusion. The prototype was also a support for the wash impression that was then used for the fabrication of the milled immediate complete maxillary denture. In the second case, another hybrid technique was presented where the initial impression was done conventionally, but then the mounted diagnostic casts were digitized, and the fabrication of the maxillary immediate denture was finalized digitally. In the third case, a novel fully digital approach for the fabrication of a maxillary immediate denture was utilized. This included the merging of clinical photos, IOSs, and facial scans for the fabrication of immediate maxillary and mandibular removable dentures. In the three cases reported, all the parameters of assessment of complete dentures were verified, including speech, phonetics, esthetics, OVD, function, and comfort. The fully digital technique is especially beneficial for cases involving patients with severely periodontally involved teeth, prostheses with significant undercuts that complicate impression‐taking, and patients who are unwilling to go without teeth, even briefly. This approach is ideal for immediate dentures, which are intended for use until a permanent replacement can be fabricated in the foreseeable future.

CONCLUSION

This case series outlines multiple techniques that can be utilized for the fabrication of an immediate removable dental prosthesis based on the clinical presentation of the patient. The hybrid digital conventional workflows combine the advantages of conventional workflows digital workflows.
