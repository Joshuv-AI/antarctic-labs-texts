Comparison of triglyceride-glucose index and atherogenic index of plasma in euthyroid Hashimoto’s thyroiditis patients and healthy controls
Authors: Semra Özkan, Mustafa Harı
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimThe relationship between insulin resistance and atherosclerosis in euthyroid Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (HT) remains unclear. This study aimed to compare the atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) and triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index between patients with euthyroid HT and healthy controls.Materials and methodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study examined 1,000 patients aged 18–50 who visited the internal medicine outpatient clinic at Mersin City Training and Research Hospital between January 1, 2020, and June 1, 2022. The study population was separated into two groups: 495 adult patients diagnosed with eutyroid HT and a control group of 505 individuals who visited the internal medicine outpatient clinic of our hospital. The two groups were compared with respect to the following parameters: T3, T4, and TSH levels; thyroglobulin antibody (TgAb) and thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPOAb) levels; high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, fasting blood glucose, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and triglyceride levels; TyG index; AIP; and body mass index.ResultsPatients with euthyroid HT had higher blood triglyceride, low-density lipoprotein, TgAb, TPOAb levels, AIP, and TyG index than those in the control group (p < 0.001). Correlation analysis showed a moderate significant correlation between AIP and TyG index, TPOAb, and TgAb levels (p < 0.05).ConclusionThe euthyroid HT group exhibited higher AIP and TyG index values than the control group. These findings suggest the presence of insulin resistance and atherosclerosis in patients with HT, independent of thyroid function, and suggest that euthyroid patients with HT should be closely monitored for the development of these conditions.

Introduction

Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (HT) is an autoimmune thyroid disease that results in hypothyroidism and is common in women [1, 2]. Thyroid hormones affect the basal metabolic rate, protein, carbohydrate, and fat metabolism, as well as thermogenesis, thereby negatively impacting the quality of life of patients with HT [2]. HT affects many systems, including the cardiovascular, hematopoietic, reproductive, pulmonary, gastrointestinal, neuropsychiatric, and skin systems [3]. The cardiovascular system is one of the systems affected by HT. Higher or lower than normal TSH levels can negatively affect the cardiovascular system [4, 5]. Subclinical hypothyroidism increases cardiovascular risk factors, such as insulin resistance (IR), impaired lipid profiles, increased oxidative stress, increased vascular stiffness, and endothelial dysfunction [6–8]. In contrast, subclinical hyperthyroidism may cause changes in cardiovascular function, including increased heart rate, predisposition to endothelial dysfunction, increased left ventricular mass, and left ventricular diastolic dysfunction [9–11]. High thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPOAb) levels in patients with HT have been associated with metabolic disorders such as diabetes and dyslipidemia [12]. The inflammatory nature of dyslipidemia and HT significantly increases the risk of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [13, 14]. Autoimmune-mediated cytokine release, oxidative stress, and changes in adipokine profiles have also been proposed as mechanisms linking HT to cardiometabolic disturbances [15, 16].

The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index is a new, simple, reliable, and easily calculable indicator of IR [17]. The atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) is based on the ratio of plasma triglyceride (TG) molar concentrations to logarithmically transformed high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and is known to be important in terms of atherogenic risk [18]. IR and atherosclerosis are critical factors in cardiovascular disease, and their relationship with autoimmune thyroid disorders, such as HT, has not been sufficiently explained in euthyroid conditions.

Despite extensive research, the relationship between HT and cardiovascular risk factors during the euthyroid phase remains unclear. Considering these systemic effects, new markers such as the TyG index and AIP provide promising information regarding the metabolic effects of HT, particularly in relation to IR and atherosclerotic risk factors. Given the broad systemic effects of HT, particularly on cardiovascular health, this study focused on new markers (TyG index and AIP) to better understand the relationship between HT, IR, and atherosclerosis, even in the absence of thyroid dysfunction. The purpose of this study was to investigate the relationship between TyG index (associated with IR) and AIP (associated with atherosclerosis) in patients with euthyroid HT.

Material and methods

Patient selection

This retrospective study analyzed 495 patients aged 18–50 diagnosed with HT and admitted to the Internal Medicine outpatient clinic of the Mersin City Training and Research Hospital between January 1, 2020, and June 1, 2022. The study included patients diagnosed with HT based on positive TPOAb levels (70-1500 U/mL), positive thyroglobulin antibody (TgAb) levels (> 1,3 IU/mL), and those with previous ultrasonography findings compatible with chronic autoimmune thyroiditis. Euthyroid HT patients were selected based on their FT3 ((normal range, 2.3–4.2 pg/ml)) and FT4 (normal range, 0.89–1.76 ng/dl)levels, as well as TSH (normal range, 0.55–4.78 pg/ml), which were within the normal range.

During the same period, 505 healthy individuals were examined and selected for the control group of the study. All individuals in the healthy control group were selected from among individuals with negative TPOAb and TgAb levels within the normal range of the laboratory kit and normal thyroid ultrasonography. In the control group, patients with additional chronic diseases such as cancer, rheumatological or hematological diseases, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, chronic kidney and liver disease, and neurological diseases, as well as those with a history of using medication or herbal supplements for any disease were excluded from the study.

The exclusion criteria for the euthyroid HT group were as follows: 1. Those with additional chronic diseases, such as cancer, rheumatological or hematological diseases, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, chronic kidney and liver diseases, and neurological diseases. 2. Patients taking supplements for hyperlipidemia or those using antidiabetic or antihypertensive medications. 3. Patients who had previously received or were currently receiving levothyroxine replacement therapy, had undergone thyroid surgery, or had received radioactive iodine therapy were excluded. 4. Individuals with a history of subacute thyroiditis, Graves’ disease, thyroid cancer, acute suppurative thyroiditis, levothyroxine or antithyroid medication use, or abnormal thyroid function test results were excluded. 5. Patients diagnosed with subclinical or overt hypothyroidism or subclinical or overt hyperthyroidism.

Individuals in the euthyroid HT group with fasting blood glucose (FBS) levels between 100 and 125 mg/dL were included in the study after confirming the absence of diabetes mellitus via a 75-gram oral glucose tolerance test.

Patients’ laboratory and demographic data were obtained from an electronic information system.The study compared the two groups in terms of LDL-C, FBS, HDL-C, TG, fT3, fT4, TSH, TgAb, and TPOAb levels. Blood tests were performed using an Abbott Architect 16,200 autoanalyzer (Abbott, Princeton, NJ, USA). The TyG index was found by using the formula Ln [fasting TG (mg/dl) × FBS (mg/dl)/2] [17], and AIP was calculated using the formula log(TG/HDL-C) [18].

Statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated with α = 0.05, power (1-β) = 0.95, effect size (d) = 0.2, and allocation ratio N1/N2 = 1, resulting in 1000 [19].

A special statistical program (SPSS for Windows version 22, IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) was used to analyze the data. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to determine whether the variables were normally distributed. Frequencies and percentages were used to present categorical variables. A comparison of these variables was made using the chi-square test. As all variables were not normally distributed, demographic data and outcome variables are expressed as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). The two groups were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test to evaluate the parameters. The relationships between thyroid hormone levels, TPOAb, TgAb, TyG index, and AIP were examined using Spearman’s test. The P value was established at p < 0.05.

We evaluated the relationship between AIP and the TyG index, as well as fT3, fT4, TSH, TPOAb, and TgAb levels using correlation analysis. Spearman correlation analysis results indicated that the TyG index showed weak to moderate positive correlations with TgAb (r = 0.269, p < 0.001) and TPOAb (r = 0.196, p < 0.001), whereas weak negative correlations were observed with fT3 (r = − 0.089, p = 0.003) and fT4 (r = − 0.054, p = 0.045). Furthermore, AIP was moderate positive correlated with TgAb (r = 0.222, p < 0.001) and TPOAb (r = 0.160, p < 0.001), whereas its correlations with fT3 (r = − 0.046, p = 0.074) and fT4 (r = − 0.028, p = 0.186) were not statistically significant. Additionally, TSH levels were weak but significantly positively correlated with the TyG index (r = 0.137, p < 0.001) and AIP (r = 0.081, p = 0.005). Discussion

Emerging evidence suggests that low-grade systemic inflammation observed in HT may play a pivotal role in disrupting insulin signaling pathways, thereby promoting metabolic dysregulation. Moreover, alterations in thyroid hormone levels, even within the subclinical range, can adversely affect lipid metabolism, endothelial function, and arterial stiffness, all of which contribute to the pathogenesis of cardiovascular diseases [20, 21]. The relationship between HT and cardiovascular disease risk and IR in the hypothyroid phase is well known, but there is a gap in the literature regarding studies investigating the relationship between HT, cardiovascular disease, and IR in the euthyroid phase independent of thyroid function. This study is the first to compare euthyroid HT patients with healthy controls in terms of the TyG index and AIP, which are associated with IR and cardiovascular disease. Our study results showed that the AIP and TyG index levels, which are associated with IR and cardiovascular disease risk, were significantly higher in the euthyroid HT group than in the control group. In addition, AIP and TyG indices showed a moderate positive correlation with TPOAb and TgAb levels.

Studies have shown that patients with HT may experience alterations in lipid-glucose metabolism, which may potentially result in the development of atherosclerosis due to hypercholesterolemia and impaired free fatty acid metabolism [22]. Previous studies have shown positive correlations between TSH levels and total cholesterol and TG levels [14, 23, 24]. Similarly, in our study, LDL-C and TG levels were higher in the euthyroid HT group than those in the control group.

There is limited literature on the relationship between thyroid hormones and the TyG index [25, 26]. A study revealed a negative relationship between the TyG index and fT4 in males, as well as a positive association between the TyG index and TSH in euthyroid females [25]. However, the TPOAb positivity rate in the euthyroid group included in that study was only 4%, and no information was provided about the HT patient population. In another recent study, the TyG index was found to correlate negatively with fT4 and positively with TSH and thyroid autoantibodies. In this study, the TyG index was higher in patients with subclinical hypothyroidism than in euthyroid individuals [26]. This study was not specific to HT, and only 12% of the study population had autoimmune thyroid disease. We observed a significantly higher TyG index in the euthyroid HT group than in the control group. Our study is the first in the literature to compare euthyroid HT patients and controls in terms of the TyG index. In our study, the TyG index showed weak to moderate positive correlations with TgAb and TPOAb, whereas weak inverse correlations were observed with fT3 and fT4.

Few studies have examined the relationship between AIP and thyroid function, which indicates the risk of atherosclerosis. Two new studies have shown that AIP levels are higher in individuals with subclinical hypothyroidism than in those with euthyroidism. However, HT was not present in the individuals included in these two studies [27, 28]. However, HT was not present in the individuals included in these studies. In another study, AIP was found to be increased in hypothyroid patients along with age and TSH, and a strong relationship was found between AIP and TSH levels [29]. However, this study was not specific to patients with HT. In our study, AIP was higher in patients with euthyroid HT than in healthy controls. Additionally, while showing moderate correlation with AIP, TgAb, and TPOAb, it did not correlate with fT3 and fT4. Our study is the first study in the literature that examined the relationship between AIP and euthyroid HT and compared it with controls.

Although most confounding factors were excluded, our study had some limitations. First, because of its retrospective design and single-center setting, the study reflects the characteristics of a specific geographical area’s patient population. These results may be affected by regional and ethnic differences, which could limit how widely the findings can be applied. Second, the study’s robustness was limited by the lack of data on patients’ dietary habits and physical activity levels. Third, the lack of validation using invasive imaging methods alongside AIP to assess atherogenicity is another limitation of our study. Fourth, since HT is much more common in women than in men, it is unclear whether the fact that the study population consisted mainly of women affected the study. Finally, the lack of comprehensive data on HT duration, severity, and lifestyle factors was a significant limitation of our study. These findings require confirmation and strengthening through multicenter prospective studies that incorporate invasive diagnostic techniques and more diverse population samples. These studies should also consider lifestyle factors, physical activity levels, HT duration and HT severity.

Conclusion

In our study, the AIP and TyG indices were higher in euthyroid patients with HT than those in the control group. These findings suggest the presence of atherosclerosis and IR in HT independent of thyroid function and indicate that the AIP and TyG indices are high even in the euthyroid phase of HT. Patients with HT should be closely monitored for the development of cardiovascular disease and IR due to high AIP and TyG indices, even if they are in the euthyroid phase after diagnosis. The use of AIP and TyG indices in patients with HT may be useful for the early detection and monitoring of cardiometabolic risk. Early detection of IR and atherosclerosis can improve the health and quality of life of patients with HT and reduce healthcare costs. Prospective long-term studies are needed to collect more detailed clinical and lifestyle data, including genetic and inflammatory markers.
