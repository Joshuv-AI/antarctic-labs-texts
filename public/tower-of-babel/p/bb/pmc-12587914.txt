Characterisation of patients with antifibrotic-treated non-idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis progressive pulmonary fibrosis: a retrospective real-life study
Authors: Andreas Renner, Elisa Vertanen, Eva Sutinen, Mari Ainola, Marjukka Myllärniemi, Maria Hollmén
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveNintedanib is approved for the treatment of interstitial lung disease (ILD) with progressive pulmonary fibrosis (PPF). Real-life data on nintedanib treatment for this indication and on the combined use of antifibrotic and immunomodulatory drugs are limited. This study was aimed to collect detailed real-life data from all patients with non-idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) PPF at the Hospital District of Helsinki and Uusimaa to identify the gap between patients included in randomised controlled trials and real-life patients.Design and settingThis retrospective, real-life study characterised all patients with non-IPF PPF diagnosed and treated at the Hospital District of Helsinki and Uusimaa.ParticipantsThirty-one patients were identified as having non-IPF fibrotic ILD and meeting the PPF criteria in 2022 and 2023.ResultsThe most common diagnosis was connective tissue disease–ILDs, with 13 patients (42%), followed by fibrotic idiopathic non-specific interstitial pneumonia (iNSIP), with 10 (32%) patients. The most common radiological pattern was NSIP in 18 (58%) patients.Among the 30 patients treated with nintedanib, six (20%) had to permanently discontinue treatment owing to side effects. Of the 19 patients who continued to receive nintedanib at the end of the follow-up period and for whom the dose was known, 9 (47%) continued to receive nintedanib at the full dose (150 mg two times per day) and 10 (53%) at the reduced dose (100 mg two times per day). Twenty-seven (87%) patients received immunomodulatory treatment at the time of PPF.ConclusionsIn our cohort, the underlying diagnoses and immunomodulatory treatments received significantly differed from those in the phase III licensing trial. More patients received nintedanib at a reduced dose than at a full dose, despite limited evidence of its efficacy and effectiveness. This highlights the large gap in evidence and the need for additional real-world data.

Introduction

Interstitial lung disease (ILD) is a group of respiratory diseases that lead to the thickening of lung tissue and eventually fibrosis. Fibrotic changes caused by ILD are irreversible.1 In the most common form of ILD, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), two antifibrotic medications, pirfenidone2 and nintedanib,3 have been approved for almost a decade to slow down fibrotic lung changes.

Other forms of ILD, such as idiopathic nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (iNSIP), hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) and the group of ILDs associated with connective tissue diseases (CTD–ILDs), have for a long time been solely treated with immunomodulatory medications. Although disease progression is a hallmark criterion for IPF, it is frequently observed in other ILDs. Up to 40% of patients with fibrotic ILD appear to develop progressive pulmonary fibrosis (PPF).4 5 Progressive phenotypes are common in rheumatoid disease-associated ILDs, such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA)-ILD and systemic sclerosis (SSc)-ILD, and in the fibrotic subtypes of HP and iNSIP. PPF in fibrotic ILDs other than IPF (non-IPF PPF) is defined by radiological evidence of pulmonary fibrosis on high-resolution CT (HRCT) and two of the following three criteria within 1 year: (1) worsening respiratory symptoms; (2) absolute forced vital capacity (FVC) or haemoglobin-corrected diffusion capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCOc) decline of 5% and 10%, respectively; and (3) progression on HRCT.4

Nintedanib is commonly used for IPF and slows disease progression.3 It also decreases the progression of ILD in patients with PPF compared with placebo.6 Nintedanib is currently the only antifibrotic drug approved for the treatment of non-IPF PPF. The Social Insurance Institution of Finland (Kela) has approved the indication for the use of nintedanib for PPF since February 2022. As treatment is expensive and can cause significant side effects, it is important to collect real-life data on patients receiving antifibrotic therapy, drug doses, treatment complications and drug efficacy in all subgroups of patients with ILD. To our knowledge, there is no real-life data on the use of nintedanib in non-IPF PPF. Additionally, there is a lack of information regarding the combined use of antifibrotic and immunomodulatory drugs, particularly in patients with PPF, in CTD–ILD. In particular, the combined use of mycophenolic acid, rituximab and nintedanib has not been extensively studied in a real-life cohort.

This study aimed to collect detailed real-life data from all patients with non-IPF PPF at the Hospital District of Helsinki and Uusimaa (HUS) to identify the gap between patients included in randomised controlled trials (RCTs)6 and real-life patients. This study aimed to lay the foundation for future prospective, real-life studies investigating the effectiveness of antifibrotic treatments in these patients.

Methods

This retrospective, real-life study characterised all patients with non-IPF PPF diagnosed and treated at the Hospital District of HUS. The HUS area is the largest hospital district in Finland, with a background population of 2.2 million, that is, 40% of the Finnish population. All patients who met the American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society (ATS/ERS) PPF criteria were screened from the HUS data, and the Epic-based digital patient record system was screened for patients who met the ATS/ERS PPF criteria. In 2022, the Finnish national ILD board decided that all PPF patients for whom antifibrotic treatment was considered should be evaluated for antifibrotic therapy in multidisciplinary (MDT) meetings at university hospitals. All MDT meetings in the Hospital District of HUS between 1 January 2022 and 6 May 2024 were systematically reviewed. Since no patients in the Hospital District of HUS were able to receive nintedanib for PPF without MDT approval, we are confident that the patients of this cohort are representative of PPF patients in the region. All patients who met the consensus diagnosis of non-IPF PPF criteria in accordance with the ATS/ERS/Latin American Thoracic Association guidelines4 were included in this cohort. Follow-up data of the identified patients, particularly antifibrotic medication and dosage, were reviewed up to 6 May 2024 (end of follow-up).

Permission to screen and study patient electronic records was provided by the Institutional Review Board of the Hospital District of HUS (HUS/8/2022, THL/568/5.05.00/2020, HUS/144/2020).

Finally, Finland-wide reimbursement data for nintedanib as an indication for non-IPF PPF were obtained from the Social Insurance Institution of Finland (Kela) to assess the representativeness of this cohort of Finnish patients with non-IPF PPF. All data in this study, with the exception of nintedanib reimbursement data, were sourced from HUS digital patient records.

Rheumatologists are participating in every MDT ILD meeting. Additionally, our centre is one of the few centres, which offers joint pneumological–rheumatological appointments for patients with CTD-ILD, and the close spatial proximity of pneumological and rheumatological outpatient clinics facilitates interdisciplinary discussions on a day-to-day basis. Immunomodulatory treatments were decided in the MDT meetings of the Helsinki University hospital, based on guideline recommendations and RCT data when available, if indicated for the underlying condition.

Clinical parameters

Results from spirometry (forced expiratory volume in the first second (FEV1) and FVC, both in per cent predicted and litres) and lung diffusion testing (DLCOc and Krogh factor (DLCOc/alveolar volume) in per cent predicted) were obtained from the time of initial ILD diagnosis and time of PPF. As significant differences between absolute and relative FVC decline have been found in patients with IPF,7 both absolute and relative FVC and DLCOc declines were calculated. The overall decline from the initial ILD diagnosis to the time of PPF and the annualised decline were calculated.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) reports within a year of PPF were reviewed by a physician experienced in the diagnosis and treatment of pulmonary hypertension and a decision was made as to whether there were direct, indirect or no signs suggestive of pulmonary hypertension.

Additionally, data on smoking history, medication and comorbidities were collected. As this is a retrospective analysis of routine clinical data standardised dyspnoea or cough scores were not analysed as they were not available in the majority of patients.

Statistical analyses

Data are reported as either means±SD or medians with IQRs, depending on normality. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess data normality. Categorical data are presented as numbers and percentages. The subgroups were compared using two-tailed t-tests. P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS V.28 (IBM, Armonk, New York).

Due to the small sample size, and the study design as a retrospective single-centre study, the decision was made to include all available patients and forgo an a priori sample size calculation.

Patient and public involvement

Patients were not directly involved in the design of this study.

Results

At the Hospital District of HUS, 31 patients had non-IPF fibrotic ILD who met the PPF criteria in 2022 and 2023. The median time from the initial ILD diagnosis to PPF was 2.5 years. The date of initial ILD diagnosis widely varied, ranging from 2000 to 2023. Two patients died during the follow-up time. One patient underwent double-lung transplantation. The decline in pulmonary function test parameters from the time of initial ILD diagnosis to the time of PPF is shown in online supplemental e-Table 2. Almost 20% of the patients (n=6) had an obstructive ventilatory pattern, defined as an FEV1/FVC ratio of <0.70. As mentioned above, due to the nature of the retrospective data collection, standardised patient-centred outcome parameters such as dyspnoea or cough scores were not available in the majority of patients and thus not analysed.

Twenty-four (77%) patients had undergone diagnostic bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL), of which BAL was usable for flow cytometric analysis in 22 patients. Of these, eight (36%) patients had a normal BAL cell pattern, five (23%) had neutrophilic alveolitis, five (23%) had combined lymphocytic–neutrophilic alveolitis, three (14%) had lymphocytic alveolitis and one (5%) had eosinophilic alveolitis. Nine (41%) patients had an elevated total cell count in BAL. Among the 21 patients whose CD4+/CD8+ ratios could be determined, 15 (71%) had a normal ratio, 4 (19%) an elevated ratio, and 2 (10%) a low ratio.

In 11 (35%) patients, TTE within a year of PPF diagnosis was available. In these patients, the mean tricuspid regurgitation gradient was 34±17 mm Hg. Based on the TTE findings, one patient had indirect signs and four patients showed direct signs suggestive of pulmonary hypertension. The comorbidities are listed in online supplemental e-Table 3 in online supplemental material. Only the comorbidities that occurred more than two times were reported. The most common comorbidity was metabolic syndrome, followed by atrial hypertension, asthma, coronary heart disease and reflux disease. Nine (29%) patients received long-term oxygen treatment during PPF.

Nintedanib therapy was initiated after MDT in 30 patients. In one patient with fibrotic HP, pirfenidone was initially prescribed because of human error. However, pirfenidone was discontinued owing to side effects (not specified). Of the 30 patients treated with nintedanib, six (20%) permanently discontinued treatment owing to side effects (one patient experienced diverticulosis two times, another patient alveolar haemorrhage attributed to nintedanib and apixaban drug interaction, a third patient with nausea and elevated liver enzyme levels >3× upper limit of normal (ULN) and three other patients with diarrhoea despite concomitant loperamide treatment). Two patients died during follow-up, and nintedanib was discontinued in one patient after double-lung transplantation. Altogether, 21 patients were still on nintedanib. In nine (47%) patients, nintedanib was administered at the full dose (150 mg two times per day) and 10 (53%) at the reduced dose (100 mg two times per day). In two patients, the current dose was unknown because they resided outside the Helsinki area. The reasons for reducing the nintedanib dose were diarrhoea despite concomitant loperamide treatment (in eight patients) and elevated liver enzyme levels >3× ULN (in two patients). None of the three patients in whom nintedanib was either reduced (two patients) or discontinued (one patient) due to elevated liver enzyme levels >3× ULN had concomitant immunomodulatory treatment of a dose or substance, which were exclusion criteria in the INBUILD trial.6 Neither the underlying diagnosis nor the concomitant immunomodulatory treatments showed significant differences between patients receiving nintedanib at the full dose and those receiving the reduced dose. Patients receiving nintedanib at the full dose were younger (53.6±16.2 years) compared with patients with a reduced dose (66.0±9.3 years). However, the difference was not statistically significant (p=0.088).

The number of patients receiving reimbursement from the Social Insurance Institution of Finland (Kela) for non-IPF PPF in Finland increased from 11 patients at the beginning of 2022 to 69 patients by mid-2023.

Twenty-seven (87%) patients received immunomodulatory treatment at the time of PPF, and in only 2 (6%) patients, no history of immunomodulatory treatment was found in the medical records. Twenty-three (74%) patients were on long-term systemic corticosteroids at the time of PPF, with a mean dose of 8.2±4.5 mg prednisolone daily or equivalent, whereas 27 (87%) patients had a history of long-term systemic corticosteroid treatment. At the time of PPF, five (16%) patients were on mycophenolic acid, with a mean daily dose of 2200±447 mg; four (13%) patients on hydroxychloroquine, all receiving 300 mg daily; three (10%) patients on methotrexate, with a mean weekly dose of 18.3±2.9 mg; and two (7%) patients on azathioprine, both receiving 150 mg/day. Nine (29%), eight (26%) and five (16%) patients had a history of hydroxychloroquine, methotrexate and azathioprine treatments, respectively. One (3%) patient with fibrotic iNSIP received rituximab during the PPF, whereas two (7%) patients had a history of rituximab treatment. One patient with RA-ILD (3%) received golimumab during PPF.

Discussion

This was a real-life study of patients with PPF on antifibrotic therapy at the Hospital District of HUS, with a background population of 2.2 million (40% of the Finnish population). We found that age, FVC and DLCOc were similar to the baseline data from the INBUILD trial.6 However, in our study, the predominant radiological pattern was fibrotic NSIP in more than half (58%) of the patients, and only 19% had a usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) pattern. This differs markedly from the INBUILD trial in which most patients (62.1%) had a UIP pattern.6 Moreover, in our cohort, the proportion of patients with a diagnosis of CTD–ILD (42% vs 24.7%) and fibrotic iNSIP (32% vs 18.3%) was higher than in the INBUILD trial, whereas fibrotic HP was less prevalent in our cohort (19% vs 25.3) compared with the INBUILD trial.6 There is a possibility for selection bias contributing to the high number of CTD-ILD patients, as in the advent of antifibrotic treatment for non-IPF PPF patients, rapid initiation of treatment was probably prioritised by clinicians for SSc-ILD patients due to multiple factors (younger age, worse prognosis, higher likelihood of contraindications for lung transplant). Also, we do not have any patients with smoking-related interstitial fibrosis in our cohort, probably due to the low smoking rate in Finland, and the rate of HP is lower in Finland compared with other countries.8 Nonetheless, the biggest difference between patients in our cohort and those in the INBUILD trial population lies in the immunomodulatory treatments. In the INBUILD trial, the exclusion criteria were treatment with azathioprine, cyclosporine, mycophenolate mofetil, tacrolimus, rituximab, cyclophosphamide or oral glucocorticoids (at a dose of >20 mg/day for glucocorticoids),6 whereas in our cohort, 87% had immunomodulatory treatments. Applying these exclusion criteria to our cohort, 13 (42%) patients would not have been eligible for INBUILD inclusion based solely on immunomodulatory treatments for ILD.6 This difference might explain the higher proportion of CTD–ILD and fibrotic iNSIP in our cohort and the extension of the differences in HRCT patterns. In the clinical trials, the patient population was selected, highlighting the need for more real-life data to assess the effectiveness and tolerability of nintedanib in patients with PPF.

The rate of patients discontinuing nintedanib owing to side effects (7/30, 23%) is slightly higher, but considering the small sample size comparable, than in the INBUILD trial (19.6%).6 The side effects cited here as the reasons for treatment discontinuation are generally well described in phase III approval studies for nintedanib in IPF3 and PPF6 and in real-life IPF studies.9 10 Discontinuation for these reasons was also recommended by a position paper that used the Delphi process.11 The same position paper also advised against starting nintedanib in the patient in which off-label pirfenidone was started.11 This highlights the need for more treatment options for PPF, as almost a quarter of the patients had to discontinue antifibrotic treatment owing to side effects.

In our cohort, more than half (53%) of the patients who were still receiving nintedanib at the end of the follow-up period were receiving it at a reduced dose of 100 mg two times per day. Although there is some real-life evidence of the effectiveness of nintedanib at a reduced dose in patients with IPF,12 13 direct evidence from RCTs and evidence in patients with non-IPF PPF are lacking. This means that, in our cohort, most patients receiving antifibrotic treatment were doing so at a dose with an implied, as well as indirect, but no direct evidence for effectiveness or efficacy. Prospective and long-term retrospective real-life studies on reduced nintedanib doses in patients with non-IPF PPF should be conducted.

Our data, particularly the high rate of treatment discontinuation, highlight the need for more treatment options for non-IPF PPF. In IPF, the availability of a second licensed antifibrotic medication, pirfenidone, with a slightly different mechanism and side effect profile, allows for a switch of treatment in case of side effects or marked progression under treatment.

Although this real-life analysis was limited by its small sample size, the cohort comprised almost half of all the patients who received nintedanib for non-IPF PPF in Finland. Thus, the cohort was representative of Finnish patients with PPFs. A large portion of the patients patients treated in Finland are located in the Helsinki metropolitan area. Due to a well-established single-payer healthcare system funded by the national government, access to medical treatment and medications, including anti-fibrotic treatment, does not vary among hospitals or regions in Finland. The geographical distribution of the patients does therefore not create a bias in the representation of Finnish PPF patients. The time from initial ILD diagnosis to PPF diagnosis is likely to be biased, as many patients might have met the criteria previously; however, PPF was only confirmed in the literature in 2022.4 This limitation is inherent to the new definition of PPF, and selecting only patients with a more recent initial ILD diagnosis is unfeasible. To gain a better understanding of the progression of patients to PPF from the initial ILD diagnosis and to better stratify which patients are at higher risk, further studies applying the PPF criteria to historical lung function testing are required. This study under-represents patients with SSc-ILD receiving antifibrotic treatment, as nintedanib is approved for SSc-ILD irrespective of the PPF criteria14 and our cohort only included patients with PPF.

This study has some major limitations. This was a retrospective, single-centre study, and the patient population was small. In addition to the small sample size, no a priori sample size was calculated (see also above in the Methods section), which limits the generalisability of the findings. Also, while data on tolerability are already available and presented here, there is not yet any data on effectiveness or other outcome parameters. Applicability of data derived from a specific healthcare system is always somewhat limited to regions with very differing healthcare systems. Our data do not address problems related to access to healthcare as well as cost of medication, as this is not a very relevant issue in this population. Our study has several strengths. The background population of the HUS area was 40% of the Finnish population. Data were collected meticulously and were readily available.

Conclusion

This study provides a valuable characterisation of real-life patients with non-IPF PPF. There are two main findings from this study. Both the underlying diagnoses and concomitant treatments received vastly differed from those in the phase III licensing trial.6 In our cohort, most patients receiving antifibrotic treatment were administered a dose with no direct evidence of effectiveness or efficacy. Future studies should evaluate the effectiveness of antifibrotic treatment with nintedanib in real-life patients with non-IPF PPF.
