Risk factors for refractory malignant pleural effusion and the impact on prognosis in non-small cell lung cancer patients with advanced EGFR mutations: a retrospective cohort study using propensity score matching
Authors: Heng-Ning Lian, Guang-Qing Shi, Huan Wang, Zhen-Yan Wu, Li-Jie Ma, Zhen-Liang Xiao, Jing Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalignant pleural effusion (MPE), which arises from pleural metastasis, frequently occurs in advanced lung adenocarcinoma with epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations (EGFR+) and is often associated with a worse prognosis. Observations in clinical settings have indicated that a subset of patients undergoing first-line therapy with EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKIs) may continue to experience persistent pleural effusion. This occurs even when other systemic lesions are controlled, leading to respiratory distress due to pleural effusion compression, a condition known as refractory MPE. The factors contributing to the emergence of refractory MPE and their effect on patient prognosis are not yet fully understood. This clinical study aimed to evaluate the risk factors for refractory MPE in individuals diagnosed with EGFR+ non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and their impact on prognosis.MethodsWe retrospectively screened 157 patients with advanced EGFR-positive NSCLC and MPE who were initially diagnosed at the General Hospital of Western Theater Command between January 2019 and June 2022. According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 98 cases were included. Among them, 18 had refractory MPE. Using a 1:2 propensity score-matching method, we matched these patients with 32 patients with non-refractory MPE who also had EGFR+ NSCLC. We monitored clinical characteristics, progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), and incidence of adverse events between the two groups. Univariate and multivariate analyses were conducted to identify the factors associated with prognosis.ResultsCompared to patients with non-refractory MPE, those with refractory MPE exhibited a higher proportion of EGFR 21 L858R mutations (P=0.03) and co-occurring TP53 mutations (P=0.03). Additionally, a greater number of patients with refractory MPE had larger volumes of pleural effusion at first drainage (P<0.001), higher levels of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) in the pleural fluid at initial diagnosis (P=0.01), and elevated serum carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) levels (P=0.002). These patients were also less likely to receive intrapleural treatment (P=0.001) and had shorter PFS (P=0.04) and OS (P=0.01). Multivariate analysis revealed that the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) score, first-line treatment options, and liver metastases were independent risk factors for PFS. The independent risk factors for OS included ECOG PS, histology, EGFR mutation type, combination systemic therapy, liver metastases, and pre-treatment serum CEA levels.ConclusionsPatients with advanced NSCLC and refractory MPE have a poorer prognosis after first-line targeted therapy than those with non-refractory MPE. More aggressive systemic and local treatment approaches may offer better survival benefits in these patients.Keywords: Refractory pleural effusion, malignant pleural effusion (MPE), EGFR mutation, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)

Highlight box

Key findings

• Refractory malignant pleural effusion (MPE) is significantly linked to epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) L858R and concurrent TP53 mutations, liver metastasis, large initial effusion volume, and high lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) levels. It leads to poorer progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS). Independent PFS predictors are Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) status, first-line therapy, and liver metastasis. For OS, independent predictors include ECOG status, histology, EGFR mutation type, systemic combination therapy, liver metastasis, and pretreatment CEA.

What is known and what is new?

• Refractory MPE is common in advanced EGFR-mutant non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and worsens survival and quality of life. Elevated pleural lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), large effusion volumes, co-existing liver metastases and high serum CEA have been individually linked to poorer outcomes.

• First propensity-matched analysis to identify and quantify independent risk factors for refractory MPE in advanced EGFR-positive NSCLC. Refractory MPE was tied to EGFR L858R mutation, TP53 co-mutation, larger effusion, higher pleural LDH, elevated CEA and less intrapleural therap.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Screening for the identified risk factors at baseline can help clinicians stratify patients at higher risk for refractory MPE and consider early, proactive local interventions alongside systemic treatment to mitigate adverse outcomes.

• Future trials should evaluate combined systemic-local treatment strategies in high-risk EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients to determine whether aggressive management of MPE can improve survival. Additionally, prospective multicenter studies are warranted to validate the predictive utility of the identified risk factors and assess their integration into individualized treatment algorithms.

Introduction

Lung cancer is a highly malignant neoplasm characterized by an alarmingly high incidence and mortality rate. Previous data show that approximately 2.48 million new cases of lung cancer occurred in 2020, leading to an estimated 1.8 million fatalities (1). Among patients diagnosed with lung cancer, the prevalence of malignant pleural effusion (MPE)-characterized by positive cytology from metastatic non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and associated with a significantly poorer prognosis—ranges from 7% to 23% (2,3). Numerous studies have established a correlation between EGFR-mutated NSCLC and MPE development, indicating that the latter is particularly prevalent in patients harboring targetable actionable mutations (4,5). Notably, >50% of patients with MPE experience recurrence within 90 days (6). The emergence of targeted therapies, specifically epidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKIs), has revolutionized the treatment of advanced NSCLC with EGFR mutations, significantly extending progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) in these patients (7). However, EGFR-TKIs exhibit heterogeneity in treating EGFR+ NSCLC patients with MPE. For instance, a study by Yang et al. (8) indicated that in EGFR+ patients, TKI treatment showed better efficacy in solid tumor components than in pleural effusion (61.5% vs. 88.5%, P=0.03). Nokihara et al. (9) also noted that third-generation osimertinib treatment in patients with MPE was unsatisfactory. Clinically, we observed that a small subset of EGFR+ patients with MPE exhibited a suboptimal local response to EGFR-TKI therapy. As assessed by imaging, this was characterized by a reduction in the primary and metastatic lung tumor lesions. Simultaneously, pleural effusion continued to recur, leading to symptoms such as dyspnea requiring repeated thoracentesis (10). Although changes in pleural effusion are defined as non-target lesions according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) guidelines (version 1.1), a recurrent increase in pleural fluid volume often necessitates additional local treatment strategies beyond targeted therapy, such as repeated thoracentesis, antiangiogenic drugs, and intrapleural administration of chemotherapy drugs. These treatments have varied efficacies and numerous side effects, significantly reducing patients’ quality of life.

Currently, the risk factors for the development of refractory MPE in patients with advanced lung cancer receiving EGFR-TKI treatment, and whether it is an adverse prognostic factor, remain unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the risk factors associated with MPE that are unresponsive to EGFR-TKI therapy and their influence on patient prognosis, ultimately contributing to developing more effective treatment strategies for this population. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-1223/rc).

Methods

Study population

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of General Hospital of Western Theater Command (approval No. 2024-K007-01). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Patients with advanced EGFR-positive NSCLC and MPE treated at the General Hospital of Western Theater Command between January 2019 and June 2022 were included to ensure adequate follow-up. All patients underwent thoracentesis to drain the pleural effusion and were excluded if they had other diseases causing pleural effusion. Refractory pleural effusion is defined as occurring 2 months after EGFR-TKI with reduced or stable primary and metastatic lung lesions on imaging follow-up, but no significant reduction in pleural effusion volume compared to the initial diagnosis or dyspnea due to pleural effusion compression requiring repeated thoracentesis. All patients with a first-time diagnosis underwent complete drainage via thoracentesis. If residual fluid persisted, the need for pleurodesis or indwelling pleural catheter (IPC) placement was assessed using ultrasound and computed tomography (CT) imaging. In this cohort, these interventions were not routinely performed unless indicated by recurrent symptomatic effusion, and none were conducted before the 2-month evaluation. Trapped lung, defined as failure of lung re-expansion post-drainage and confirmed by chest CT, was evaluated in all cases but did not occur among the included patients. At the 2-month follow-up, repeat chest CT or ultrasound was performed; if the pleural effusion volume exceeded 50% of the baseline level and symptoms recurred necessitating intervention, the effusion was qualitatively assessed as showing no significant reduction. AND the definition of initial pleural volume as the total volume drained during the first thoracentesis, quantified via ultrasound-guided measurement. The last follow-up date was December 1, 2024.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) histologically confirmed advanced or metastatic NSCLC with stage IV disease and presence of pleural effusion at the time of initial imaging diagnosis; (II) EGFR mutations verified using Amplification Refractory Mutation System PCR or next-generation sequencing (NGS) by analyzing histological and cytological specimens obtained from primary and metastatic lesions; (III) positive for malignant cells in pleural effusion, as confirmed by cytopathology or patients with confirmed pleural metastasis by positron emission tomography (PET)/CT; (IV) patients who received first-line EGFR-TKIs for at least two months and achieved a disease control rate (DCR), defined as the sum of complete response (CR), partial response (PR), and stable disease (SD), as confirmed by imaging of both primary and metastatic lung lesions (according to RECIST 1.1. as assessed by the investigators); and (V) availability of patient clinical and pathological data, laboratory tests, and follow-up data; (VI) patients alive without progression at last follow-up (Dec. 1, 2024) were censored.

The exclusion criteria were the following: (I) presence of other malignant tumors; (II) pleural effusion caused by non-neoplastic reasons; and (III) incomplete clinical or imaging data.

Follow-up and data collection

We collected comprehensive patient information on the initial diagnoses including basic demographics (sex, age, and smoking history), physical status [Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS)], tumor-related details (histological type, metastatic sites, and genetic testing reports), treatment information, CT or ultrasound imaging data, routine pleural fluid and biochemical test results, pathological reports, and other blood test data. TP53 data were obtained via NGS from primary tumor tissue (80%) or effusion cytology (20%). In addition, we collected follow-up CT or ultrasound imaging data, blood test results, and adverse reaction records after the first month of EGFR-TKI therapy.

Primary endpoints were PFS and OS. PFS is the time from initiating EGFR-TKI therapy to disease progression or death. OS was defined as the time from the start of EGFR-TKI treatment to the date of death. Secondary endpoints included treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 25.0; SPSS Inc., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality was tested using continuous variables. Continuous variables that followed a normal distribution were analyzed using t-tests. In contrast, those with skewed distributions were analyzed using Mann-Whitney U tests, with results presented as medians and ranges. The Shapiro-Wilk test or the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to judge the normality of two data groups. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages and analyzed using the Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test. Given the differences in clinical baseline characteristics between the two groups in this retrospective study, we performed propensity score matching (PSM) using a 1:2 nearest-neighbor matching scheme with a caliper value of 0.2 to reduce bias between subgroups. The variables used for PSM in this study are derived from the baseline characteristics presented in Table 1, which were likely included to balance the refractory and non-refractory groups. These variables are as follows: age, gender, body mass index, smoking history, histology (adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma), ECOG PS, EGFR mutation type (deletion in exon 19, 21 L858R, others), combined with TP53 mutations. Serum albumin, programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) tumor proportion score (TPS), radiographic pleural septum, total pleural effusion at first drainage, pleural effusion tests [monocyte ratio, Rivalta test positive, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), adenosine deaminase (ADA), cytopathology positive], first-line treatment options (first/second/third generation of TKI), combination therapy (systemic use of bevacizumab, systemic use of chemotherapy drugs), intrathoracic injection of drugs. Kaplan-Meier survival curves and log-rank tests were used to evaluate differences in PFS and OS between the two groups. The effects of patient characteristics on survival were assessed using univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models, with reported hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

After rigorous screening, this study ultimately included 98 patients with advanced EGFR-positive NSCLC who also had MPE. Among these patients, 18 met the diagnostic criteria for refractory MPE. In contrast, the remaining 80 were classified as having non-refractory MPE (Figure 1). Before matching, significant differences (P<0.05) were observed between the two groups in ECOG PS, EGFR mutation types, local intrapleural injection treatment, total volume of initial pleural fluid drainage, and pleural fluid LDH levels. Using PSM with a caliper value of 0.2, we matched 16 patients with refractory MPE to 32 patients with non-refractory MPE in a 1:2 nearest-neighbor scheme. Survival analysis before and after PSM

The median follow-up period was 29 months. Before PSM, the median PFS and OS in the refractory MPE group were 12.05 and 20.9 months, respectively, which were significantly shorter than those in the non-refractory MPE group, where the median PFS and OS were 17.45 and 29.70 months, respectively (Figure 2A, P<0.001; Figure 2B, P<0.001). After PSM, the median PFS and OS in the refractory MPE group were 13.35 and 23.20 months, respectively, while those in the non-refractory MPE group were 16.30 and 31.70 months, respectively. Significant differences were observed between the two groups (Figure 3A, P=0.04; Figure 3B, P=0.01).

Discussion

Approximately 150,000 new MPE cases are diagnosed annually in the United States. Lung cancer is the primary cause of MPE, with approximately one-third of MPE cases attributable to NSCLC (11). NSCLC is a sign of lung cancer invading the pleura, often causing symptoms such as dyspnea, coughing, and persistent chest pain due to compression by pleural effusion. This also indicates that the patient is in an advanced stage of tumor progression and has lost the opportunity for curative surgery. Previous studies have shown that the median survival for patients with MPE is typically 3 to 12 months (12,13). Minimal pleural effusion (<10 mm in thickness) indicated poor prognosis (14). And the malignant pleural fluid exhibits pro-growth, pro-migratory, and immunosuppressive properties (15-17). Therefore, the poorer survival observed in the refractory cohort may be attributed to increased exposure to malignant pleural fluid, which could induce a more aggressive and invasive tumor phenotype at the pleural lining. EGFR-TKIs significantly extended the survival of patients with EGFR-positive MPE. However, some patients develop refractory pleural effusion despite the absence of primary resistance. These patients are more likely to experience dyspnea, pain, and cachexia. Due to the relatively low proportion of these patients in clinical settings, few reports or studies on the clinical characteristics of this patient population are available. For patients with MPE caused by NSCLC, our institution has not yet routinely performed talc pleurodesis, primarily due to the lack of legally compliant talc-based agents required for this procedure. Based on clinical practice, we would like to supplement the following in our discussion: The definitive thoracic drainage management for these patients involves central catheterization, with implementation timing determined by a dual criterion of “symptom-driven approach + effusion volume assessment”. For patients diagnosed with MPE via chest ultrasound/CT showing fluid depth >3 cm, regardless of whether systemic anti-tumor therapy (e.g., TKIs, chemotherapy) has been initiated, if significant dyspnea [e.g., resting oxygen saturation <94%, New York Heart Association (NYHA) class II or higher] or activity limitation occurs, a 16–18 F silicone pleural tube is routinely placed within 48 hours of diagnosis. Continuous low-flow drainage (total daily volume ≤1,500 mL) rapidly alleviates symptoms, providing sample support for subsequent effusion cytology and tumor marker analysis. Suppose a patient experiences recurrent pleural effusion after chest tube placement (defined as: recurrence of dyspnea within 72 hours after tube occlusion closure, or sustained daily drainage exceeding 200 mL for three consecutive days with open drainage). In that case, local intrathecal drug administration should be administered after excluding complications such as chest infection or catheter blockage. The medication selection is based on the patient’s prior treatment history and physical condition. Cisplatin (40–60 mg/m2 dissolved in 50 mL saline solution) is typically used for patients without previous chemotherapy. For patients with prior chemotherapy failure or poor physical tolerance (ECOG PS score ≥2), bevacizumab (10 mg/kg dissolved in 50 mL saline solution) is prioritized. After injection, the catheter should remain occluded for 24 hours before resuming drainage to enhance local drug concentration and improve effusion control efficacy.

In this study, after PSM to exclude confounding factors, we found that patients with refractory MPE predominantly had the EGFR L858R mutation (exon 21) and co-occurring TP53 mutations. These results are consistent with those of previous studies. Previous studies indicate that the expression of EGFR-L858R in lung cancer cells leads to CXCR4 upregulation, which is associated with increased cancer cell invasiveness and MPE formation (18). Additionally, in NSCLC patients with MPE, the EGFR genotype is an independent predictor of PFS and OS. Specifically, patients with exon 19 deletions have significantly longer median PFS and OS than those with L858R mutations (19). Approximately 54.6–68.8% of EGFR-mutated NSCLC cases harbor TP53 mutations, making TP53 the most common co-mutation with EGFR (20). Patients with TP53 mutations have a lower response rate to EGFR-TKI treatment and a poorer prognosis (21). Previous studies have shown that TP53 mutations are independent risk factors for MPE (22). This is consistent with our finding that refractory MPE is more likely to occur in patients with L858R and co-occurring TP53 mutations.

Patients with refractory MPE exhibited larger pleural effusion volumes, LDH levels in pleural fluid, and elevated serum CEA levels. This finding is consistent with a report by Schwalk et al. (23) who noted that a larger volume of pleural effusion on chest radiography, higher LDH levels in the pleural fluid, and positive cytology results were associated with an increased risk of pleural effusion recurrence. Similarly, the study by Xu et al. (24) proposed that high levels of serum CEA are a risk factor for MPE recurrence. Additionally, patients with lung adenocarcinoma with pleural LDH levels exceeding 1,500 IU/L at initial diagnosis tended to have shorter survival periods, typically less than 1 year (25). This is consistent with the results of our study, confirming that the volume of pleural effusion, LDH levels in the pleural fluid, and serum CEA levels are independent prognostic factors for MPE. Higher expression of these indicators is more likely to result in refractory MPE.

We found that patients with an EGFR+ status and concurrent liver metastasis had a higher probability of developing refractory MPE. Patients with liver metastases often have impaired liver function and reduced protein synthesis, leading to hypoalbuminemia, which may further promote pleural effusion. In addition, liver lesions may directly stimulate the diaphragmatic pleura and increase pleural fluid accumulation. Furthermore, this could be related to poorer responses to TKI in patients with liver metastasis. Previous studies have shown that compared to monotherapy with EGFR-TKIs, combining EGFR-TKIs and chemotherapy resulted in longer first-line PFS. The median PFS was 12.7 months for combination therapy versus 7.4 months for monotherapy (26). However, no definitive studies have explained the contribution of liver damage to refractory pleural effusion (27,28). Further research is necessary to clarify the mechanisms through which liver metastasis leads to refractory MPE.

We analyzed factors influencing the prognosis of patients with EGFR+ advanced lung cancer who also had MPE. The results showed that the ECOG PS, histology, EGFR mutation type, combination systemic therapy, liver metastases, and pretreatment serum CEA were independent OS risk factors. Systemic combination therapy primarily includes the combination of TKIs, chemotherapy, and bevacizumab. Regarding the combination of TKIs and chemotherapy, the FLAURA2 study demonstrated significant PFS and potential OS benefits when chemotherapy was added to the first-line osimertinib treatment of EGFR-mutated advanced NSCLC (29). A systematic review of combining TKIs with bevacizumab indicated that EGFR-TKI treatment combined with bevacizumab resulted in significantly better PFS and ORR in patients with EGFR-mutated advanced NSCLC. However, it also significantly increased the risk of grade ≥3 TRAEs (30). Intrapleural injection of bevacizumab combined with cisplatin showed substantially better efficacy than cisplatin alone (31). Combining bevacizumab and EGFR-TKIs may be a valuable treatment option for patients with NSCLC and MPE who have developed resistance to EGFR-TKI therapy, especially those with acquired T790M mutations (32). Additionally, early control measures for MPE in lung cancer provide extra benefits in reducing the need for reintervention and extending the time to reintervention for MPE, independent of systemic anticancer therapy (33). Other studies have drawn contradictory conclusions, suggesting that intrapleural chemotherapy with TKIs does not improve the efficacy of controlling MPEs in patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC, but may increase adverse events, which are typical side effects of chemotherapy (34). Consequently, there is a need for larger systematic studies to confirm the prognostic factors in patients with advanced lung cancer and MPE who are EGFR positive.

Recent studies investigating the mechanisms underlying MPE revealed significant genomic associations. Notably, mutations and activations in genes such as EGFR, KRAS, PIK3CA, BRAF, MET, EML4/ALK, and RET strongly correlate with an increased incidence of MPE. KRAS mutations are predominantly observed at distant metastatic sites, whereas EGFR mutations are more prevalent in regional and invasive tumors (35). Research indicated that EGFR-positive lung cancer is associated with a more aggressive clinical phenotype and an elevated risk of pleural metastasis (5). A retrospective study by Zou et al. (36) assessed the clinical characteristics of patients with EGFR-mutated and wild-type lung adenocarcinomas in a single-center context. The findings of this study revealed that among patients diagnosed with stage IV lung adenocarcinoma, the incidence of EGFR mutations was significantly higher in those presenting with MPE than in those without this condition. Furthermore, EGFR mutations were found to be an independent predictor of MPE. Previous studies have also confirmed a positive correlation between EGFR mutations and T790M and MPE, suggesting that EGFR mutations may play an essential role in the formation of MPE (37,38).

There are several limitations in this study. First, this was a retrospective study, and it was difficult to avoid biases that may have affected the results. The single-center design, regional clinical practices, baseline patient characteristics (e.g., physical status scores, comorbidities), and institutional treatment preferences, the sample may not fully represent similar patient populations across different regions or medical institutions, resulting in limited representativeness. As a key prognostic predictor of TKI treatment in EGFR+ NSCLC patients, PD-L1 TPS still showed intergroup imbalance and missing about 33% after matching, which limited the evaluation of the effectiveness of PSM balanced baseline characteristics. Second, the study was limited by a small sample size, as it involved a limited population of patients with advanced EGFR-positive lung cancer and refractory pleural effusion, all from a single center. Although we included all patients who still had pleural effusion after 2 months of TKI treatment, some patients may have exhibited delayed responses to TKI, with gradual control of pleural effusion after 2 months. Therefore, they were not strictly defined as “uncontrolled”. Additionally, owing to the limited number of patients, although there was a trend toward better OS for patients who received TKI combined with systemic chemotherapy as the initial treatment, this did not reach statistical significance in the subgroup analysis. Finally, current treatments for MPE include not only drug therapy but also local interventions such as thoracoscopy with drug instillation, photodynamic therapy, and hyperthermia, which may positively impact patient outcomes. However, this study did not include these treatments because of the limited number of patients. Future research is required to broaden the scope and gather data from a larger cohort of patients.

Conclusions

Patients with advanced NSCLC with lower ECOG PS, squamous cell carcinoma, EGFR exon 21 L858R mutation, high serum CEA levels, and liver or pleural metastases are more likely to develop refractory MPE, which can reduce PFS and OS. For these patients, a more aggressive initial treatment approach, including combined local and systemic therapies such as TKI plus chemotherapy, appears associated with improved prognosis and warrants further investigation.
