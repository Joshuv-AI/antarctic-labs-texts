Neuroguided Surgery: A Safe Technique to Prevent Nerve Injuries in Facelift Surgery
Authors: Marcelo de Oliveira e Silva, Paulo Liborio, Rosa Maria Marquezini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Summary:Facelift surgery (rhytidectomy) is one of the most performed aesthetic procedures. However, with the growing number of procedures, the incidence of complications, particularly nerve injuries, has also increased. Facial nerve injury is a very feared complication, as it can result in facial paralysis, leading to aesthetic and functional impairments. To address this, neuronavigation has emerged as an effective and viable solution. By precisely locating the facial nerve branches, neuronavigation ensures the safe execution of facial surgery with millimetric accuracy, even in altered anatomical conditions. This article introduced the concept of neuroguided facial surgery—a technique that can be integrated with facelift procedures to prevent iatrogenic injuries to the facial nerve branches. This study aimed to describe the neuroguided approach, highlighting its effectiveness in reducing nerve injury risk compared with traditional techniques.

Takeaways

Question: This work proposes neuroguided facial surgery as a viable solution, aimed at integrating with established facelift techniques to ensure precise and objective safety, thus avoiding iatrogenic injuries to the facial nerve and its branches.

Findings: This article presents a surgical approach that uses neuronavigation during rhytidectomy, serving as an efficient safety measure regardless of the specific technique used, inspired by the principle of preventing nerve injuries through accurate localization, similar to practices in neurological surgery.

Meaning: By using neuronavigation, surgeons can conduct dissections with heightened precision near facial ligaments, facilitating more effective releases and optimizing outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Facelift surgery or rhytidectomy is one of the most popular aesthetic procedures worldwide with numerous techniques developed for facial rejuvenation. As the field has evolved, so too have the complications associated with it. Among these, facial nerve injuries are of significant concern. Damage to a facial nerve branch can result in paralysis of facial muscles, leading to facial asymmetry, functional impairments, and psychosocial consequences.1

Nerve injuries occur in up to 4% of cases,2 with permanent damage leading to facial muscle atrophy, oral asymmetry, nasal collapse, lower lip deformities, ectropion, lagophthalmos, and keratitis. These complications vary depending on the etiology, level of injury, duration of paralysis, and individual biological characteristics.3,4 They are often irreversible and can have profound aesthetic and functional repercussions. The treatment of the paralyzed face remains a challenge for plastic surgeons and has limited results. Restoring the fullness of symmetrical facial expression is almost impossible.5–7

As surgical techniques become more complex, the risk of nerve damage increases. For example, the deep plane facelift technique uses deeper dissection planes and releases the facial ligaments close to the facial nerves. Furthermore, the increasing use of collagen biostimulators, traction threads, and fillers in preoperative facial treatments may alter normal anatomical structures, presenting an additional challenge for surgeons. In this context, the need for more accurate and reliable methods to prevent nerve injury is evident.

In the fields of neurological and craniofacial surgery, where nerve damage is similarly a concern, the use of neuronavigation has proven invaluable. This technology allows the precise identification of key anatomical structures, including cranial nerves, enhancing the surgeon’s ability to perform procedures with millimetric accuracy and minimizing the risk of injury.2,8

Inspired by this approach, this article presented the Neuroguided Face Surgery technique, an innovative technique that can be widely associated with facelift techniques, where real-time intraoperative navigation and monitoring are used to protect the facial nerve and its branches, avoiding iatrogenic injuries during rhytidectomy.

OBJECTIVE

This article described the Neuroguided Facial Surgery technique, focusing on the safe identification of the facial nerve branches and the reduction of iatrogenic nerve injuries during facelift surgery, emphasizing its benefits in preventing nerve damage.

METHODS

This study involved 10 patients (7 women and 3 men) aged 45–67 years, who were sequentially selected for deep plane facelift surgery. None of them had undergone previous facial surgery. Two patients had no previous aesthetic procedures, whereas the others had undergone treatments such as injectable collagen biostimulators (polylactic acid and calcium hydroxyapatite) and fillers.

Preoperatively, all patients underwent bilateral facial electroneuromyography (ENMG) within 30 days to rule out preexisting nerve injury. No abnormal findings were noted.

Surgical Procedure

Patients were positioned in the dorsal decubitus under general anesthesia without muscle relaxants to not interfere with nerve stimuli. A sterile preparation using 2% dermal chlorhexidine and 0.2% aqueous chlorhexidine was applied to the face, followed by the placement of sterile drapes. Neuronavigation was then installed bilaterally on the face to monitor the susceptible areas to nerve damage during dissection (ie, facial danger zones).9

Before beginning the surgery, a 0.9% saline-adrenaline solution (1:200,000) without any local anesthetic was infiltrated subdermally (25 mL per side), in a semicircle pattern of 4 cm in radius, centered on the anterior edge of the tragus, to minimize bleeding during the subcutaneous dissection, before changing to the sub-submuscular aponeurotic system layer.

The electrodes were inserted obliquely (30–45 degrees) into the subdermal plane and protected with sterile transparent dressings (Fig. 1). Intraoperative Neuromonitoring

During surgery, the Medtronic NIM Response 3.0 system was used to monitor facial nerve activity. The surgeon used an electrostimulator probe connected to the system. The distal portions of the electrodes and the electrostimulator probe were connected to the same interface. The parameters included an intensity that varied from 0.5 to 1.0 mA, which guaranteed a depth margin and approximation of 0.3–0.5 mm between the stimulus point and the nerve.

If any facial nerve branch was at risk of injury, either through direct contact or excessive traction, an audible alarm was emitted, and a change in the ENMG wave was recorded, according to the intensity of the stimulus. The dissection was performed using a high-frequency electrosurgical scalpel, set to the 10 MHz coagulation mode, which provided precise tissue separation while preserving nerve integrity. The surgeon monitored the neuronavigation screen and electromyography feedback to ensure safe dissection, making real-time adjustments as needed. At the end of the procedure, the integrity of each facial nerve branch was reassessed using the probe by passing it through the skin, the electrodes were removed, and the surgery was completed with sutures and bandages. Postoperative ENMG was conducted 30 days after the surgery to confirm nerve function and ensure no complications.

RESULTS

The comparative analysis of preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative electromyography showed that no patients experienced facial nerve injury. All facial nerve branches remained intact, and no damage was recorded during the surgery or in the 30-day follow-up.

DISCUSSION

The increasing complexity of facial rejuvenation procedures, particularly those involving deeper anatomical planes, has raised concerns about nerve injury. The proximity of facial nerve branches to key surgical sites combined with facial anatomy changes due to previous aesthetic treatments highlights the need for more precise surgical techniques to minimize the risk of injuries.

The neuroguided facial surgery technique, using neuronavigation and electromyographic monitoring, offers a highly effective solution. The real-time identification of nerve branches and the ability to monitor proximity during surgery significantly reduce the risk of injury. Furthermore, this approach allows surgeons to perform precise dissection with greater confidence, even in altered anatomical conditions.

This study confirmed that the neuroguided technique is a 100% effective method for preventing nerve injuries, providing valuable surgical safety and reassurance. The integration of intraoperative monitoring has the potential to become a standard practice in aesthetic surgery, minimizing risks. It is strongly supported that the surgeon should carry out intraoperative monitoring of the facial nerve branches whenever possible. Furthermore, the neuroguided facial surgery technique provides key reassurance that can open the door to the development of new surgical techniques for facial rejuvenation.

CONCLUSIONS

In neuroguided facial surgery, the use of neuronavigation and electromyography offers a safe, effective, and precise technique for protecting facial nerve branches during facelift surgery. This method significantly reduces the risk of iatrogenic injuries, ensures safer dissection near the facial ligaments, and allows for more effective release of the submuscular aponeurotic system layer.

This article underscores the need for continuous innovation in aesthetic surgery, particularly in the integration of technology to enhance safety. The use of neuroguided surgery in rhytidectomy paves the way for safer and more predictable outcomes in the field of facial rejuvenation.
