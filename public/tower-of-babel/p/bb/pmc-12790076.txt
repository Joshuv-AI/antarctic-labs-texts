Seeding the Soil for HFpEF
Authors: Lei Xu, Wenzhe Zhao
Published: 2025

We commend Berlin et al1 for their meticulously conducted study, which provides a critical long-term view of cardiac function following modern breast radiotherapy (RT) in a real-world cohort exposed to systemic therapies. Although the lack of a clinically significant decline in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) over 5 years is reassuring, there are important subtle but significant changes—worsening diastolic function (E/e′ ratio), circumferential strain, and ventricular-arterial coupling—that point to the emergence of an early phenotype of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF).

This observation is of clinical importance. HFpEF is a growing cause of cardiovascular morbidity, and its diagnosis in cancer survivors is often challenging, with symptoms such as dyspnea frequently attributed to deconditioning or pulmonary issues. The work by Berlin et al1 moves beyond suggestion to provide robust, quantitative evidence that modern RT, even with its relatively low mean heart dose, acts as a “second hit” that seeds the soil for this specific clinical syndrome. This aligns with prior retrospective evidence showing that HFpEF is the predominant form of heart failure diagnosed after contemporary breast RT.2

Furthermore, Berlin et al1 link these functional changes to the maximum dose to the left anterior descending coronary artery, proposing coronary microvascular impairment as a key underlying mechanism. This pathophysiological link is supported by studies in noncancer populations demonstrating a strong association between coronary microvascular dysfunction and HFpEF.3 For clinicians, this has important implications: a breast cancer survivor, particularly after left-sided RT, who presents with exertional intolerance despite a normal LVEF warrants a high index of suspicion for HFpEF. This study provides an evidence-based rationale for pursuing comprehensive diastolic function assessment and potentially even functional testing in a symptomatic population.

In conclusion, the study by Berlin et al1 shifts the clinical narrative from a general concern about LVEF decline to a specific focus on radiation-induced HFpEF. It provides a clear mandate for the cardio-oncology community to recognize this diastolic dysfunction–predominant pattern. The next steps in research should not only focus on advanced left anterior descending coronary artery–sparing RT techniques but also explore targeted pharmacologic strategies aimed at preventing or treating the coronary microvascular dysfunction that may be central to this challenging clinical entity.
