Patient Subgroups and Predictors of Improvement in Chronic Neuropathic Pain: A Trajectory‐Based Analysis
Authors: Xavier Moisset, M. Gabrielle Pagé, Bruno Pereira, Manon Choinière
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThere is limited real‐world evidence on predictive factors for good outcomes in patients with chronic neuropathic pain (NP) treated in multidisciplinary tertiary care centres. This study aimed to identify subgroups of NP patients with similar pain trajectories and evaluate associated factors.MethodsWe analysed data from 912 patients with chronic NP (age: 53.6 ± 13.3 years; 51.5% female) enrolled in the Quebec Pain Registry, all of whom reported a baseline pain intensity of ≥ 4/10. Patients completed standardised questionnaires prior to treatment initiation, as well as at 6 and 12 months. Pain trajectories were identified using group‐based trajectory modelling (GBTM), with multiple imputation performed to address missing data. The results were confirmed using group‐mixture modelling on the non‐imputed dataset and using GBTM in the subgroup of patients with complete data.ResultsA three‐class trajectory model best fitted the data for both pain intensity and interference. Only 23.1% of patients showed a clear improvement in pain intensity, while 23.5% showed improvement in pain interference. Key predictors of pain intensity improvement included lower baseline pain intensity and interference. Improvement in pain interference was associated with lower baseline interference and depression scores, as well as shorter pain duration. Notably, receiving a strong opioid significantly increased the risk (RR = 1.45 [1.17; 1.78]) of belonging to the persisting severe pain trajectory.ConclusionsA minority of chronic NP patients demonstrated significant improvement with multidisciplinary treatment. These findings highlight the limitations of current management strategies and emphasise the need for novel therapeutic approaches to address the burden of chronic NP effectively.SignificanceThis study highlights the value of trajectory analysis in identifying subgroups of patients with chronic neuropathic pain (NP) who exhibit different patterns of treatment response. Only a minority of patients—approximately 23% for both pain intensity and pain interference—demonstrated meaningful improvement. Lower baseline pain intensity and interference emerged as key predictors of better outcomes, while strong opioid use was associated with persistent severe pain. These findings underscore the importance of trajectory‐based approaches for understanding the course of NP and for guiding more personalised and effective management in multidisciplinary care settings.

Introduction

Neuropathic pain (NP) is present in 7%–10% of the general population (van Hecke et al. 2014). Such pain type is difficult to treat, and a limited number of patients obtain significant pain relief, even in tertiary care centres offering multidisciplinary treatment (Moulin et al. 2015; Torrance et al. 2013). Up to now, there are few real‐life studies evaluating predictive factors of good outcome in patients with chronic NP managed in such pain facilities.

The Quebec Pain Registry (QPR) has been implemented in 2008. Close to 9000 patients have been included and have provided consent for their data to be used for research purposes. Patients with chronic NP followed for at least 6 months in the participating pain clinics have been described in a previous article (Moisset et al. 2022). It was shown that the Cohen's D effect sizes at 6 months were 0.37 for pain and 0.42 for interference, corresponding to small effect sizes (Cohen 1988). More specifically, 23% of patients showed a 30% improvement in pain intensity scores from baseline to M6, and 31% showed a similar improvement in pain interference.

Beyond overall treatment outcomes, clinicians need tools to predict which patients are most likely to benefit from specific therapeutic approaches. Access to dedicated multidisciplinary pain management centres is often limited and associated with long waiting times (Choinière et al. 2020), and such care—typically combining pharmacotherapy and psychotherapy within a biopsychosocial framework—is resource‐intensive. While this ‘classical’ management may lead to meaningful improvement in a subset of patients, it is important to identify those most likely to benefit from it and ensure they receive timely access. For others—particularly the most complex or treatment‐resistant cases—standard approaches may prove insufficient. These patients should not be excluded from tertiary care; rather, they may require additional or alternative interventions, such as repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), as recently recommended in international guidelines, or spinal cord stimulation in selected cases, even if the current evidence base remains limited. Tailoring therapeutic strategies in this way allows for a more efficient and equitable use of specialised resources, while improving care for all patients.

The objectives of this study were to identify subgroups of patients with chronic NP who share similar pain trajectories and to assess changes in pain intensity and interference, as well as the factors associated with these trajectories in patients treated in multidisciplinary pain clinics.

Materials and Methods

Participants

Study participants were selected from patients enrolled in the Quebec Pain Registry (QPR) (Choinière et al. 2017; Moisset et al. 2022) (http://www.quebecpainregistry.com). In short, the QPR was developed and implemented to monitor the condition of patients suffering from chronic pain who were referred to large university‐affiliated multidisciplinary pain treatment clinics in the province of Quebec (Canada) using uniform outcome measures (Choinière et al. 2017). Treatment was multidisciplinary and individualised to patient needs. In the present study, patients living with chronic (> 3 months) NP (Moisset et al. 2022; Treede et al. 2019), with a baseline pain intensity level ≥ 4/10, were selected, as recommended by IMMPACT guidelines (Dworkin et al. 2012). Patients with NP corresponded to patients with both a diagnosis of NP made by the pain physician at the multidisciplinary pain treatment clinic and presenting a score on the DN‐4 screening questionnaire of at least 3 out of 7 (Bouhassira et al. 2008). We excluded patients with complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS) as CRPS type was not specified. Moreover, we excluded patients with a trigeminal neuralgia diagnosis as this neuropathic type of pain responds to specific treatments that are different from other NP conditions (Bendtsen et al. 2019).

Procedure

The QPR project was approved by the institutional research ethics boards (REBs) of the participating pain clinics (Moisset et al. 2022). Successive patients who came for a first appointment at one of the five participating pain clinics were enrolled in the QPR. Information collected in the QPR was used both for clinical and administrative purposes. In addition, patients were invited to sign the REB‐approved consent form if they agreed to the use of their QPR data for research purposes.

Biopsychosocial data including pain intensity (Numerical Pain Intensity Scale; Dworkin et al. 2005), pain interference (Interference items of the Brief Pain Inventory; Cleeland and Ryan 1994; Tyler et al. 2002), sleep quality (Sleep Problem Index; Kosinski et al. 2007), tendency to catastrophize in the face of pain (Pain Catastrophizing Scale; Sullivan et al. 1995), depressive symptoms (Beck Depression Inventory; Beck et al. 1996), and physical and mental health‐related quality of life (SF‐12: 12‐item Short Form Survey; Ware et al. 1996) were collected with a self‐report questionnaire (patient self‐administered questionnaire) while medical/clinical data (e.g., pain duration, pain diagnosis, NP questionnaire [DN4]; Bouhassira et al. 2005, and so forth) were gathered by the QPR nurses using a structured interview protocol (nurse‐administered questionnaire) prior to the patient's first appointment at the pain clinic (baseline). The same questionnaires were administered 6 and 12 months later (M6 and M12). Past and current pharmacological treatments were collected. We focused on four drug regimens: anti‐neuropathic antidepressants (i.e., tricyclic antidepressants and serotonin‐norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors), anti‐neuropathic antiepileptics (i.e., gabapentin and pregabalin), weak opioids (i.e., tramadol, codeine, dextropropoxyphene) and strong opioids (e.g., morphine, fentanyl, oxycodone, hydromorphone, tapentadol, buprenorphine, methadone). All participants with NP were required to complete the patient and nurse questionnaires at baseline and at least once during follow‐up (M6 and/or M12). A pain reduction at follow‐up times compared to baseline was noted with a negative score.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous data are presented as the means ± standard deviation (SD) or medians [first quartile–third quartile], depending on their distribution. The assumption of normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Categorical data are presented as numbers and associated percentages.

Participants with missing questionnaires at one of the time points (M6 or M12) (n = 711) were compared with those who completed all the questionnaires (n = 201) using independent Student 𝑡‐tests for continuous variables and chi‐squared tests for categorical variables. Multiple imputation was performed to address missing data. For patients with missing values at M6, pain intensity and pain interference scores were imputed as the mean of the baseline and M12 values. For patients with missing data at M12, imputation was based on a multivariate model incorporating age, sex, baseline pain intensity, change in pain intensity between baseline and M6, pharmacological treatments at baseline and pain aetiology. To identify distinctive pain intensity (average pain in the last 7 days) and pain interference (interference items of the Brief Pain Inventory) trajectories, semi‐parametric mixture models (group‐based trajectory model, noted GBTM) were carried out to model the relationship between pain and time for each trajectory, the shape of the trajectory, and the estimated proportion of the population belonging to each trajectory, that is, the posterior probability of group membership. To create the profiles, individuals were assigned to the trajectory group they were most likely to belong to based on their pain severity. This type of analysis provides a formal way to determine the best‐fit number of trajectories and a precise estimate of group membership allocation, which can be expressed using observed probabilities and posterior probabilities. Nagin lays out five statistically oriented criteria for assessing model adequacy (Nagin 2005). These include: (a) obtaining for each trajectory group a close correspondence between the estimated probability of group membership and the proportion assigned to that group based on the posterior probability of group membership, (b) ensuring that the average of the posterior probabilities of group membership for individuals assigned to each group exceeds a minimum threshold of 0.7, (c) establishing that the odds of correct classification based on the posterior probabilities of group membership exceed a minimum threshold of 5, (d) observing reasonably tight confidence intervals around estimated group membership probabilities and (e) not presenting trajectories with < 5% of the whole group. GBTM was tested with 2, 3, 4 or 5 trajectories using linear, quadratic and cubic polynomial type. The best‐fitting model is selected according to the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Model selection was also based on interpretability of trajectories (Lord et al. 1968) and favoured a parsimonious model. GBTM results indicated that a three‐group model with three quadratic trajectories was the most appropriate. Group mixture modelling (GMM) was also conducted and confirmed these findings. All the data presented in this paper are based on the GBTM results.

Mixed models were used to confirm the quality of obtained trajectories. To analyse the trajectory of pain intensity and pain interference, random‐effects models for repeated data were performed, with time as a fixed effect and patient as a random effect to take into account between and within patient variability. To compare changes between trajectory groups, group × time‐point evaluation interaction was studied. A Sidak's type I error correction for multiple comparisons was applied to protect against Type I error. The results were expressed as effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Effect sizes were interpreted according to Cohen (1988).

Sensitivity analyses were conducted using group‐mixture model (GMM) on the full sample of 912 patients without imputed data. In addition, GBTM was applied to the subset of 201 patients with complete follow‐up data. These analyses confirmed that the identified pain trajectories were consistent across models.

Factors associated with the pain trajectories were explored with univariate analyses using Chi‐squared or Fisher exact tests for categorical variables. When appropriate (omnibus p‐value < 0.05), a Marascuilo post hoc procedure was performed. The comparisons for continuous variables between pain trajectories were carried out using ANOVA or the Kruskal‐Wallis test when the assumptions of ANOVA were not met. The homoscedasticity was evaluated with the Bartlett test. When appropriate (omnibus p‐value < 0.05), post hoc tests were performed (Tukey–Kramer test after ANOVA and Dunn's test after Kruskal–Wallis).

To analyse the relationships between drugs and pain trajectory, we considered all the drug regimens taken at the 6‐month evaluation. The objective being to know at the first visit the probability of a patient to belong to one of the trajectories, only the treatments received at M0 and prescribed to be used between M0 and M6 were taken into account. Given the nature of the data collected, we were able to categorise treatments taken at baseline only, at 6 months only, or at both time points.

To determine the parameters associated with pain trajectories, multivariable analyses (i.e., mixed multinomial polytomous logistic regression) were carried out. No specific statistical strategy approach, such as stepwise, was used to select predictor variables. They were chosen with caution based on their clinical relevance and the univariate results (Mickey and Greenland 1989). It is crucial for confounding adjustment that known clinically significant variables are included in the regression model. A clinically significant variable may well be an important confounder while it is not statistically significant in the univariate analysis (Sun et al. 1996). Two multivariable regression analyses were performed. The first model included all variables selected based on their clinical relevance and the results of univariate analyses, excluding baseline pain intensity for the pain intensity trajectories and baseline pain interference for the pain interference trajectories. These exclusions were made to avoid circular reasoning, as the trajectories were derived from these respective variables. A second analysis was then conducted, this time adjusting for baseline pain intensity and pain interference, to confirm that these factors were indeed key determinants of the observed trajectory patterns. Particular attention was paid to multicollinearity and interactions between covariates by studying the relationships between the covariates and evaluating the impact of adding or deleting variables in the multivariable model. Particularly, drug regimens taken at the 6‐month evaluation were correlated to other covariates related to pain intensity and pain interference trajectories. So, sensitivity analyses were conducted for the multivariable analyses with and without treatments as covariates. Results are expressed as relative risks (RR) and 95% CI.

All analyses were performed using Stata software (version 15; StataCorp, College Station, USA). A two‐sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. There was no missing data for pain intensity and pain interference.

Pain Intensity Trajectories

As shown in Figure 2, the group‐based trajectory model identified three pain intensity trajectories: 21.0% (192/912) of the patients were in the persisting severe pain intensity trajectory (initial pain intensity above 8/10, very small improvement during follow‐up), 56.7% (517/912) were in the persisting moderate pain intensity trajectory remaining moderate (initial pain intensity around 7/10, very small improvement during follow‐up); and 23.1% (211/912) were in the moderate pain intensity trajectory gradually decreasing (initial pain intensity around 6/10, large improvement during follow‐up).

Mixed models were used to confirm the quality of obtained trajectories. Effect sizes for all the variations (baseline to 6‐month follow‐up; 6‐ to 12‐month follow‐up; baseline to 12‐month follow‐up) are presented in Table 2. There was a significant interaction between pain intensity trajectories and time (p < 0.001). The whole cohort showed a significant decrease in pain intensity during the 12‐month follow‐up with a moderate effect size of −0.59. The main improvement was seen during the first 6 months with an effect size of −0.44. Of note, there was no significant variation during the period between M6 and M12, for the whole cohort or for any of the three trajectories (Table 2).

Multivariable analysis showed that initial pain interference, sleep quality and catastrophising scores were associated with the risk of belonging to the two trajectories with limited improvement (Table S2). Lumboradicular pain was linked to a reduced likelihood of being in the worst trajectory. A sensitivity analysis including baseline pain intensity was conducted and showed that it was the strongest predictor of 12‐month pain trajectory: the higher the initial pain intensity, the greater the likelihood of belonging to one of the two trajectories with limited improvement (Table S3). However, since baseline pain intensity is a key variable used in defining the trajectories themselves, its inclusion in the multivariable model raises concerns about circular reasoning and potential overfitting.

The results for the sensitivity analysis taking into account the treatments received up to M6 were similar. Moreover, by adding the treatments received continuously between M0 and M6, it appears that receiving a strong opioid resulted in an RR = 1.45 [1.17; 1.78] (p = 0.001) and RR = 1.40 [1.16; 1.69] (p < 0.001), respectively, for the persisting severe pain intensity trajectory, taking into account baseline pain intensity or not.

Pain Interference Trajectories

As for pain intensity, GBTM results indicated that a cubic three‐group solution had the smallest BIC. Group mixture modelling (GMM) was also conducted as a secondary analysis and confirmed that the three‐group model was the most appropriate.

The progression of the pain for the whole cohort and for the 3 trajectories is presented in Figure 3. Concerning the 3 trajectories, 21.0% (192/912) of the patients were in the persisting severe pain interference trajectory (initial pain interference around 8/10, very small improvement during follow‐up); 55.5% (506/912) were in the persisting moderate pain interference trajectory (initial pain interference around 6/10, very small improvement during follow‐up); and 23.5% (214/912) were in the moderate pain interference trajectory gradually decreasing (initial pain interference around 4/10, clearly significant improvement during follow‐up).

Predictive factors of belonging to the moderate pain interference trajectory gradually decreasing (most favourable evolution) were analysed, and results for univariate analysis are presented in Table 5. Overall, 110 patients (i.e., 51% of the patients in the most favourable trajectory for pain intensity) belonged to the most favourable trajectory for both pain intensity and pain interference, whereas only 12/912 patients belonged to the most favourable trajectory in one case and to the severe pain trajectory remaining severe in the other. Impact of Pharmacological Treatments

Overall, 59.3% (N = 541) of the patients were receiving pharmacological treatment for NP at baseline, some of them (N = 258; 28.3%) receiving 2 or more treatment classes at the same time. Close to one third of the patients (N = 296; 32.5%) were receiving strong opioids, 10.4% (N = 95) weak opioids, 15.6% (N = 142) antidepressants and 36.4% (N = 332) gabapentinoids and antiepileptics (Table 1). All treatments used initially or at M6 are shown in Figure 4. There were significant differences in the use of strong opioids between the 3 trajectories. Indeed, patients in the most favourable trajectory for pain intensity used fewer strong opioids (p < 0.001). The same was true for the most favourable trajectory for pain interference (p < 0.001).

Patients With Complete Follow‐Up

Among the 201 patients with complete follow‐up, the same analyses were run again. As with the full cohort, a model with 3 trajectories was best suited to describe the progression of pain intensity (Figure S1) and pain‐related interference (Figure S2). Effect sizes for all the variations (baseline to 6‐month follow‐up; 6‐ to 12‐month follow‐up; baseline to 12‐month follow‐up) are presented in Table S5 for pain intensity and Table S6 for pain interference. Univariate comparisons of baseline characteristics among the 3 pain intensity trajectories are presented in Table S7. Accordingly, univariate comparisons of baseline characteristics among the 3 pain interference trajectories are presented in Table S8. Multivariable analyses were then conducted for pain intensity evolution, excluding initial pain intensity (Table S9) and taking initial pain intensity into account (Table S10). The same was done for pain interference (Tables S11 and S12).

All these data confirm what was seen on the whole cohort. Patients with the highest probability to belong to the most favourable trajectory had a lower initial pain intensity and initial pain interference. These two factors were correlated with catastrophising and depression. The most favourable trajectory was also associated with a shorter pain duration at baseline and a pain aetiology consistent with lumboradiculalgia. Age, sex and presence of allodynia or hypoesthesia were not predictive of the patient evolution.

Discussion

This study assessed, in a real‐life setting, the evolution of pain intensity and pain interference over a 12‐month period among patients with chronic NP treated in multidisciplinary pain clinics. The analytic models allowed us to define 3 clearly different trajectories. Nearly a quarter of the patients clearly improved over time in terms of pain intensity and pain interference. However, this also means that around three quarters of the patients had no or very limited improvement in pain severity after 12 months of multidisciplinary treatment. The presence of allodynia or hypoesthesia, age and sex of the patients were not associated with trajectory membership. Patients with lower pain intensity and lower pain interference at baseline were the most prone to have the best outcomes.

The observation that only a minority of patients strongly improved at follow‐up probably reflects what is going on with patients who are referred to multidisciplinary pain treatment clinics. They had a median pain duration of 3 years, had already failed multiple treatments, and were still presenting with severe pain. It can be noted that only 60% of the patients were taking a pharmacological treatment, which clearly shows that many patients who fail these treatments no longer use them and turn to other therapies. At baseline, 45% of patients in this cohort had already tried or were currently receiving physical therapies, and 35% had already tried or were currently receiving psychological interventions. However, it is important to note that the collection of data on non‐pharmacological treatments was less systematic and less reliable than that for pharmacological treatments. This information was likely more complete for patients seen at all three time points than for those with only two visits, which may partly explain the differences observed between patients with and without complete follow‐up. In this diverse population of NP patients, strong opioids were more often prescribed at baseline and/or 6‐month follow‐up to the patients with the worst pain intensity and pain interference trajectories. Of note, only a small proportion of chronic pain patients have access to such multidisciplinary pain clinics. This result is not very surprising, as a lower proportion of improved patients among those receiving strong opioids has already been described in patients with NP followed for 6 months in Canadian pain clinics (Moisset et al. 2022).

As mentioned before, the presence of allodynia or hypoesthesia was not associated with a specific trajectory. However, several examples exist of treatments that are more efficient depending on the phenotype. That is the case for lacosamide, which seems to be more efficient in patients with Nav1.7‐related small‐fibre neuropathy (de Greef et al. 2019). Similarly, oxcarbazepine has been reported to be effective in patients with an ‘irritable nociceptor’ sensory phenotype (Demant et al. 2014). More recently, a post hoc analysis of the results obtained in NP patients treated with botulinum toxin showed that those with evoked pain or deep pain had a higher probability of benefit than those with pinpointed pain (Bouhassira et al. 2021), highlighting that pain stratification is relevant. Thus, such simple phenotyping is relevant in certain contexts focusing on a single treatment, although probably not adapted to help define trajectories when combining various treatments in a heterogeneous population followed in a real‐life study. Based on the encouraging results in single‐treatment studies (Demant et al. 2014; de Greef et al. 2019), we propose to use trajectory‐based modelling in all future clinical trials to allow the finding of predictive factors. In particular, we suggest using pain descriptors or quantitative testing to determine the likelihood of a better response among specific subtypes of patients (irritable nociceptor, hyposensitive…).

Pagé et al. (2017) analysed pain severity trajectories in the whole cohort of the Quebec Pain Registry regardless of pain type. Their results showed that 75.5% (1430/1894) had no significant improvement after 24 months of follow‐up (Pagé et al. 2017). Results of a logistic regression analysis performed using baseline characteristics revealed that the presence of NP characteristics, either alone or combined with other pain types, was associated with a better outcome. However, the present study showed that positive evolution was limited to a small subgroup of patients with NP.

In the present cohort, the main risk factor for poor evolution was baseline pain intensity. Such factor has already been described in other cohorts (Chen et al. 2018; Danielsen et al. 2024). Other predictive factors were higher pain interference scores, poor sleep quality, and a greater tendency to catastrophise in the face of pain. This highlights the importance of assessing and taking care of the patients' function and thoughts to improve the patient's quality of life.

Given the limited efficacy of treatments available in multidisciplinary pain clinics for most patients with chronic NP, alternative approaches must be considered. Non‐invasive neuromodulation is one such option, with promising data (Duarte‐Moreira et al. 2025). In France, repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) has been recommended for several years (Moisset et al. 2020; Thomas et al. 2025) and was recently recognised as a third‐line treatment in international guidelines (Soliman et al. 2025). Invasive neuromodulation, including spinal cord and motor cortex stimulation, may also be an option for select patients (Seznec et al. 2025). However, double‐blind randomised controlled trials are still needed to support earlier implementation of these techniques. Although psychotherapy is already offered to most patients in this cohort, it should be more widely accessible—particularly cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)—and introduced earlier in the course of chronic pain (Bäckryd et al. 2024).

Study Strengths and Limitations

This study uses a real‐world data set of 912 clearly defined patients with chronic NP attending a multidisciplinary centre for chronic pain treatment. A 12‐month follow‐up of such a large number of patients is unusual and is therefore of major interest. Nonetheless, there are some limitations to this study. First, drop‐out rates at 12 months were high, as discussed in a previous pain trajectories study using the Quebec Pain Registry data (Pagé et al. 2017). Although no major differences were found between patients who completed and did not complete all the questionnaires at each time point, it is possible that this factor might have influenced the results. Second, pain scores were obtained at baseline as well as 6 and 12 months after initiating multidisciplinary treatment. Most patients had data available for only two of the three time points, requiring imputation for 26% of the data used in the analysis. Even with three time points, the approach may not have captured short‐term fluctuations in pain severity occurring between assessments. Nevertheless, the consistency between the main analysis and the sensitivity analyses supports the robustness of the findings. Third, the data were collected between 2009 and 2014, more than 10 years ago. Although oral pharmacological treatments have evolved little in the last 10 years, topical treatments using capsaicin patches and botulinum toxin type A are now available but were not evaluated in this study. Neuromodulation techniques using rTMS or tDCS were also not used. More recent pain registry initiatives are ongoing, collecting real‐time data at closer time points during treatment (Delage et al. 2024; Kerckhove et al. 2022).

In conclusion, the results of this study showed three different evolution trajectories in terms of pain intensity and interference among patients with chronic NP followed at multidisciplinary pain clinics. The most important predictive factor for good evolution was initial pain intensity (the lower, the better). Other influential factors were initial pain interference and catastrophising. Receiving strong opioids was associated with a slightly higher risk of not improving in terms of pain intensity and interference, highlighting that this class of medication may not be the best treatment for patients with chronic NP.
