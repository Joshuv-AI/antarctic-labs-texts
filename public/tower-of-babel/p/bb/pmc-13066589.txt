A hybrid SMOTE and Gaussian mixture model based optimized XGBoost framework for bipolar disorder detection
Authors: Santosh Kumar, Deeksha Kumari, Arvind Panwar, Shrddha Sagar, Lukas Herout, Hamidreza Namazi, Nitesh Singh Bhati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The identification of bipolar disorder (BD), a severe psychiatric condition characterized by recurrent mood fluctuations, remains challenging due to substantial inter-individual variability, symptom overlap with other mental disorders, and imbalanced clinical data. Delayed or inaccurate diagnosis often leads to inappropriate treatment strategies and adverse clinical outcomes, highlighting the need for reliable, data-driven decision-support tools. In this study, we propose a robust hybrid machine learning framework that integrates class balancing, latent subgroup discovery, and ensemble learning to improve the accuracy and consistency of BD identification from tabular clinical data. The framework applies the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) exclusively to the training data to address class imbalance, followed by Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) based clustering to uncover latent patient subgroups and generate informative probabilistic features. These enriched features are subsequently used to train an optimized Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) classifier. Experimental evaluation on an independent test set demonstrates that the proposed model achieves 93% accuracy, 97% sensitivity (recall), 93% precision, 95% F1-score, and 79% specificity. When evaluated under identical experimental conditions, the proposed framework consistently outperforms baseline classifiers, including Support Vector Machine, Decision Tree, Logistic Regression, and Random Forest, with performance improvements ranging from 6 to 12%, depending on the comparator. The results indicate that combining SMOTE-based data balancing, GMM-driven latent feature enrichment, and gradient-boosted decision trees yields a scalable, interpretable, and clinically relevant decision-support system. This study supports the adoption of hybrid, data-driven approaches for early BD screening and personalized treatment planning in psychiatric healthcare settings.

Introduction

Recurring episodes of mania, hypomania, depression, or mixed states are among the hallmarks of bipolar disorder (BD), a serious and prolonged psychological condition that frequently presents alongside accompanying symptoms that make reliable assessment and treatment increasingly challenging1,2. Long-term general wellness, effectiveness, and standard of life are all significantly impacted by the illness; delaying or incorrectly diagnosing it often leads to prolonged suffering, hospitalization, and less-than-ideal treatments3. substantial behavioral variations or the causes of episodes of depression may be missed by conventional approaches of identification, which primarily rely on subjective clinical assessments, retrospective patient self-reporting, and periodic examinations4,5. This early detection examination approach emphasizes the pressing requirement for anticipatory and knowledge-based diagnostic approaches that can detect a wide range of BD manifestations as well as complex developmental differences6–8. By offering chances for varied gathering of information and reliable pattern identification, recent developments in artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) have shown a great deal of promise for enhancing psychological assessments9,10. In comparison to traditional healthcare methods, investigation employing wearables, neuroimaging, smartphone-based behavioral tracking, and electronic health records has demonstrated prospective in identifying BD behaviors more rapidly and accurately11–14. The diversity of BD subtypes, the class gap in psychological documentation, and the underuse of independent learning to uncover concealed particular classifications, however, still pose challenges15–17. Although exaggerated approaches like SMOTE and associated approaches have been proposed to address class inequities, several approaches still fail to achieve greater assessment sensitivity, particularly for minority or unusual appearances of BD18,19. The current study addresses these gaps by introducing a hybrid machine learning approach (GMM-SMOTE-XGBoost) that utilizes the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) for classification equilibrium to increase evaluation accuracy, GMM-based grouping for category recognition, and a customized XGBoost algorithm for dependable characteristic-based prediction20–22. Because it uses both clinical and cluster-derived variables to improve technique applicability while maintaining affordability, the method is a beneficial guidance strategy in medical environments23–25. The method expands upon recent work in multimodal training, feature development, and collaborative methods26 and surpasses common classifications such as Logistic Regression, Decision Trees, and Support Vector Machines. This research fixes major shortcomings of existing BD identification methods and adds a flexible, effective diagnostic tool for behavioral healthcare applications by combining gradient-boosted decision trees, dynamic replication, and clustering-based findings.

Motivation

Because BD has highly individualized progression patterns, prevalent complications, and indicators that correlate with various psychological and psychotic disorders, it is still one of the most difficult conditions in mental health to diagnose accurately and promptly1,3. Initial indications or subtle variations in feelings are frequently missed through conventional diagnostic techniques, which mainly rely on assessments that are subjective, sporadic clinical observations, and retrospective patient reporting4,5. Innovative, data-driven approaches are necessary because this diagnosis ambiguity leads to longer delays in therapy, higher admission rates, and a lower quality of life for patients6,7. In mental health, assessments based on machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) have shown great promise by revealing unnoticed trends in extensive behavioral, neuroimaging, and clinical datasets8–11. Initial manifestations of depression can currently be found using methods like wearable detection, smartphone-based digital phenotyping, and multifaceted sensory incorporation12–14. Nonetheless, the practicality for numerous present approaches is restricted by enduring problems like class imbalance, data sparsity, and model generalizability15–17. Despite being important strategies for tackling such problems, statistical methodologies such as the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) and its adaptive variants are frequently neglected in psychological evaluations18,19. The absence of autonomous simulation methods that can uncover hidden BD subgroups, providing enhanced healthcare comprehension and more individualized strategy development, is a further significant hurdle20,21. In various medical instances, Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) have demonstrated efficacy in revealing concealed groupings, indicating their potential for enhancing the identification of BD subtypes22–24. These methods can greatly improve the accuracy and specificity of BD evaluation when paired with combination classifiers such as XGBoost, which are excellent at processing extremely inconsistent medical data25,26. While several prior studies(2,9,13,24) have employed SMOTE or GMM within hybrid learning frameworks, these approaches generally use each component in isolation or in loosely coupled stages, without a unified optimization pipeline. The present research distinguishes itself through its integrated, sequentially optimized architecture that combines unsupervised clustering (GMM), synthetic minority oversampling (SMOTE), and regularized ensemble learning (XGBoost) within a single data flow. Specifically, the GMM component in our model is not merely used for data partitioning but for probabilistic feature augmentation embedding posterior cluster responsibilities as new latent variables that directly inform the subsequent boosting classifier. This enriches the feature space with hidden behavioral and psychometric structures not captured by conventional statistical features. Moreover, SMOTE is strategically applied before GMM clustering to ensure balanced subgroup discovery, mitigating the bias toward majority classes typically present in earlier methods. The XGBoost module, further optimized through grid-based hyperparameter tuning, then leverages both original and GMM-derived attributes to produce a stable, interpretable classifier with superior sensitivity (97%) and recall gains (≈15%) over baseline models. Hence, unlike previous GMM–SMOTE or ensemble-based models, the proposed system contributes a cluster-informed, balance-aware, and interpretable boosting pipeline specifically designed for psychological diagnostic data where sample imbalance, hidden heterogeneity, and explainability are critical.

Problem formulation

Considering its similar appearance, different patient profiles, and class imbalance in diagnostic records, BD is a persistent mental illness that often leads to an enhanced frequency of incorrect assessments and therapeutic delays. It is characterized by repeated manic, hypomanic, and depressed phases. Standard evaluation techniques may overlook significant mood swings or indications of repetition since they heavily rely on ratings by prior participants and physicians, which may have a direct effect on medical results. Among the computational challenges related to BD detection include considerable disparities in class, concealed subdivision variability, and the need for clear yet flexible techniques suitable for medical deployment. Methodology

The selection of GMM, SMOTE, and XGBoost within the proposed hybrid framework was guided by their complementary strengths, computational efficiency, and interpretability key priorities for clinical decision-support systems. Although recent deep learning architectures (e.g., CNNs, LSTMs, and transformers) have shown superior representation learning in image or temporal data, their performance advantage diminishes on structured, tabular clinical datasets such as those used in this study. Deep networks typically require extensive data and prolonged training cycles, and their “black-box” nature limits interpretability, which is critical in psychiatric diagnostics. In contrast, the Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) provides a mathematically grounded and transparent mechanism for discovering latent subgroups within heterogeneous patient data, offering probabilistic cluster memberships that can be clinically meaningful. SMOTE was selected for its proven robustness in addressing class imbalance, a pervasive issue in psychological health datasets where minority cases are underrepresented. Finally, XGBoost offers a highly optimized and regularized ensemble learning algorithm capable of capturing complex non-linear relationships with controllable overfitting and feature-level interpretability. Collectively, this combination balances accuracy, transparency, and data efficiency, making it more suitable for real-world diagnostic contexts than purely deep learning-based alternatives. The proposed hybrid system is mathematically formulated to model the complete flow of data transformation from raw input features to the optimized decision boundary. The model integrates three core components: data pre-processing and balancing, latent structure extraction via Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM), and gradient boosting based classification (XGBoost). Latent subgroup discovery using Gaussian mixture model

Following class balancing, a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) is trained on the SMOTE-balanced training data. This ordering is critical, as applying clustering on imbalanced data would bias the Gaussian components toward the majority class, thereby masking meaningful latent structures within minority bipolar disorder cases. The GMM estimates posterior cluster membership probabilities for each training sample. These probabilistic assignments capture hidden subgroup characteristics and are appended as additional latent features to the original feature space. For test samples, cluster membership probabilities are inferred using the pre-trained GMM model only, ensuring that no information from the test data influences the clustering process.

Feature augmentation and classification

The enriched feature vectors, consisting of original clinical attributes and GMM-derived latent features, are used to train an optimized XGBoost classifier. Hyperparameter tuning is performed using five-fold cross-validation on the training set. Model evaluation is conducted on the independent test set using Accuracy, Precision, Recall, Specificity, F1-score, and ROC–AUC metrics.

Missing value handling and normalization

Following the standard evaluation protocol proposed by Chawla et al.29 and Chen and Guestrin26, the dataset was first partitioned into training and testing subsets using an 80:20 stratified split. Class balancing using SMOTE was applied exclusively to the training data. Missing values were addressed using mean imputation, ensuring that no samples were lost during the modeling process. Latent feature extraction using Gaussian mixture model (GMM)

Clinical manifestations of bipolar disorder (BD) are highly heterogeneous, with patients exhibiting diverse symptom patterns, severity levels, and behavioral profiles. Traditional supervised classifiers trained solely on observed clinical variables may fail to capture such latent heterogeneity, which can limit predictive performance and interpretability. To address this challenge, the proposed framework employs a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) for latent feature extraction and subgroup discovery. A GMM models the underlying data distribution as a weighted combination of multiple Gaussian components, allowing each data point to belong to multiple clusters with different probabilities. By incorporating GMM-based latent features, the framework captures underlying patient heterogeneity and complex inter-feature relationships, enhancing the discriminative capability of the downstream XGBoost classifier. This integration contributes to improved sensitivity and robustness while maintaining interpretability, making the approach particularly suitable for mental health screening applications where latent symptom patterns play a critical role.

Validation strategy

To ensure reliable performance estimation, prevent data leakage, and maintain full reproducibility, a rigorous validation strategy was adopted throughout this study. The dataset was first partitioned into training and testing subsets using an 80:20 stratified split, preserving the original class distribution of bipolar disorder (BD) and non-BD samples in both sets. This separation was performed prior to any data-driven transformation to ensure that the test set remained completely independent. All model training and optimization procedures were confined strictly to the training data. Class balancing using SMOTE was applied only to the training set after stratified splitting, and the test set was neither oversampled nor altered in any form. Similarly, Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) clustering was learned exclusively from the balanced training data. For test samples, latent cluster membership probabilities were inferred using the pre-trained GMM model without re-estimating cluster parameters, thereby preventing information leakage. Hyperparameter tuning of the XGBoost classifier was conducted using five-fold cross-validation on the training data, with stratification applied within each fold. During cross-validation, SMOTE and GMM were applied independently within each training fold to preserve fold-level independence. This nested design ensured that validation folds did not influence the training process, yielding an unbiased estimation of model performance. The final optimized model was evaluated on the untouched test set using multiple performance metrics, including Accuracy, Precision, Recall (Sensitivity), Specificity, F1-score, and ROC–AUC. This multi-metric evaluation provides a comprehensive assessment of both overall predictive accuracy and minority-class detection capability, which is particularly important for psychiatric screening tasks where false negatives carry significant clinical risk. To enhance reproducibility, all experiments were conducted with fixed random seeds, and identical preprocessing, training, and evaluation protocols were applied across baseline and proposed models. This validation strategy ensures that the reported results accurately reflect the generalization ability of the proposed framework and supports fair, transparent comparison with existing methods.

Discussion

Clinical relevance and validation

Clinical grounding in DSM-5 and ICD-11 criteria

Bipolar disorder (BD) is clinically characterized by recurrent episodes of mania, hypomania, and depression, as defined by established diagnostic frameworks such as the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) and the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11). These frameworks emphasize symptom domains including mood instability, changes in energy and activity levels, sleep disturbances, impaired functioning, and behavioral dysregulation. The proposed model is not intended to replace clinical diagnosis based on DSM-5 or ICD-11 criteria. Instead, it is designed as a screening and decision-support system that assists clinicians by identifying individuals who may warrant further structured psychiatric evaluation. This positioning aligns the model with real-world clinical workflows, where automated tools are used to support but not substitute expert judgment.

Conclusion

This study presents a robust and interpretable hybrid machine learning framework for the detection of bipolar disorder (BD) from tabular clinical and psychometric data by integrating SMOTE-based class balancing, Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) driven latent feature enrichment, and an optimized XGBoost classifier. The proposed approach systematically addresses key challenges in psychiatric data analysis, including class imbalance, hidden patient heterogeneity, and diagnostic variability, while maintaining suitability for structured clinical datasets. Experimental evaluation on an independent test set demonstrates that the proposed framework achieves 93% accuracy and 97% recall, along with 93% precision, 95% F1-score, and 79% specificity, indicating a strong balance between sensitivity and overall predictive reliability. When assessed under identical experimental conditions, the proposed model consistently outperforms conventional machine learning classifiers, including Support Vector Machine, Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, and Random Forest, with performance improvements ranging from 6 to 12%, depending on the baseline comparator. The integration of GMM-based latent subgroup discovery enhances feature representation by capturing underlying patient heterogeneity, while SMOTE improves minority class sensitivity without introducing data leakage. Combined with the interpretability and robustness of XGBoost, the framework offers a scalable and clinically relevant decision-support tool rather than a black-box diagnostic system. Overall, this work demonstrates that hybrid, data-driven learning strategies can significantly improve the accuracy and consistency of BD screening and supports their potential role in early detection and personalized treatment planning within psychiatric healthcare settings.

Limitations

Despite the promising outcomes of the proposed hybrid model, certain limitations must be acknowledged. The study relies on a single publicly available dataset, which may not comprehensively represent the diversity of bipolar disorder (BD) cases across different populations, age groups, or clinical settings. Consequently, the model’s generalizability to broader psychiatric cohorts requires further validation using multicenter and longitudinal datasets. Additionally, the Gaussian Mixture Model assumes that the latent subgroups follow Gaussian distributions, which may not always hold true for highly non-linear or multimodal clinical data. Although SMOTE effectively addresses class imbalance, it can sometimes introduce synthetic samples near class boundaries, potentially affecting decision precision. Furthermore, this work focuses exclusively on structured clinical and psychometric attributes, excluding multimodal inputs such as EEG, speech, or neuroimaging signals that could enrich diagnostic context. Finally, while XGBoost offers partial feature interpretability, the overall framework would benefit from integrating explainable AI (XAI) mechanisms to improve transparency and clinical acceptance.

Future work and case-based discussion

Building on the present findings, future research will aim to expand the proposedhybrid model into more comprehensive and clinically integrated diagnostic systems. A key future direction is the inclusion of multimodal data sources such as EEG signals, neuroimaging biomarkers, and speech or behavioral tracking data to capture the full spectrum of bipolar disorder (BD) manifestations. Integrating these heterogeneous modalities within the same learning pipeline would allow the model to detect subtle temporal or neurophysiological cues that purely tabular clinical data may overlook. Furthermore, the incorporation of Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) mechanisms such as SHAP or LIME interpretability layers can provide clinicians with transparent reasoning for each prediction, thereby improving trust and clinical usability. From a case-based perspective, the current study underscores the model’s clinical relevance in real-world diagnostic settings. For example, when applied to cases exhibiting overlapping symptoms between BD and unipolar depression, the GMM-driven latent subgrouping effectively differentiated patient clusters by probabilistic membership, reducing misclassification rates. Similarly, the SMOTE-based augmentation proved valuable for underrepresented cases, such as early-onset BD or subthreshold manic episodes, where limited samples typically hinder classifier sensitivity. In practice, this suggests that the hybrid pipeline could serve as an early screening or decision-support tool, assisting psychiatrists in recognizing atypical or borderline BD cases more accurately. Future clinical validation should involve prospective case studies across diverse hospitals or mental health centers to test model robustness, scalability, and patient-specific adaptability. Additionally, integrating longitudinal monitoring and federated learning architectures could further enhance privacy-preserving data sharing while improving generalization across different demographic and regional datasets. By combining probabilistic modeling, adaptive data augmentation, and interpretable ensemble learning, the proposed framework holds potential to evolve into a reliable, case-sensitive diagnostic aid in precision psychiatry.
