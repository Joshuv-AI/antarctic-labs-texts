Effects of floating sleeve geometry on thermal selectivity in dual-slot microwave coaxial antennas for microwave ablation
Authors: Donghyuk Kim, Hyunjung Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microwave ablation (MWA) is widely used as a local tumor treatment, and its therapeutic outcome is strongly affected by antenna configuration and operating power. In the present work, a dual-slot microwave coaxial antenna (MCA) equipped with a floating sleeve was examined to clarify how sleeve-related design parameters influence ablation performance. Numerical simulations were carried out by varying the floating sleeve length, the separation distance between the slot and the sleeve, and the applied input power. The electromagnetic response inside tissue and the subsequent heat transfer behavior were predicted using coupled electromagnetic and bioheat analyses, and tissue damage was assessed quantitatively with the Arrhenius damage model. The results confirmed that both the extent and spatial pattern of tumor damage changed noticeably according to floating sleeve geometry, even at identical power levels. In addition, the antenna design altered the power required to induce sufficient tumor necrosis, which in turn affected the degree of unwanted thermal damage in adjacent normal tissue. A floating sleeve length of 15 mm combined with a slot-to-sleeve distance of 6 mm achieved complete tumor damage at 13 W while suppressing collateral damage to surrounding tissue. These results indicate that careful adjustment of floating sleeve geometry can improve ablation efficiency and thermal selectivity, offering a practical basis for antenna optimization in MWA applications.

1. Introduction

With recent advances in science and technology, along with the extension of average life expectancy and the deterioration of environmental conditions, the global incidence of cancer has continued to rise.1–3 Currently, cancer treatment is performed using various approaches, including surgical resection, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy, and each modality is selectively applied depending on the patient’s condition and the characteristics of the tumor.4–7 However, surgical resection, which is the most commonly used treatment, has limitations in treating tumors located deep within the body, and the bleeding that accompanies the procedure poses a risk of complications such as secondary infection. 8 In addition, nonsurgical treatment modalities such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy have also been reported to cause various side effects, including drug toxicity and damage to normal tissues due to radiation exposure.9,10 Owing to these limitations, extensive research has been conducted in the medical and engineering fields on minimally invasive treatment techniques that can complement or replace conventional therapeutic methods.

Microwave thermal ablation (MWA) is one of these alternative treatment modalities and is a thermal therapy that induces tumor cell death through temperature elevation.11–13 MWA is a minimally invasive treatment technique in which microwave energy is delivered to biological tissue, generating localized heating through dipole rotation and ionic conduction, and the procedure is performed by inserting a microwave coaxial antenna (MCA) into the tumor site.14,15 As a result, the treatment can be performed with relatively small incisions and can be effectively applied even to tumors located deep within the body.

MWA is a treatment modality that integrates thermal, electromagnetic, and biological phenomena. In the field of heat transfer, therapeutic performance has been evaluated through numerical analysis based on the temperature rise and distribution induced by microwaves emitted from the MCA. Preechaphonkul et al. 16 presented a coupled numerical model incorporating electromagnetic wave propagation, heat transfer, and tissue deformation in order to compare the Pennes bioheat model and the porous media model in liver tumor MWA. The results showed that the porous media model, by accounting for convective effects, predicted temperature distribution, thermal damage progression, and tissue deformation more realistically under long-duration heating conditions, whereas the Pennes model tended to predict faster thermal damage in both the tumor and adjacent normal tissues. These findings suggest that the choice of heat transfer model can influence treatment accuracy in MWA treatment planning. Radosevic et al. 17 comparatively analyzed microwave emission in MWA by classifying it into continuous mode and pulse mode. Based on a numerical electromagnetic–thermal coupled analysis, the coagulation zone formed under each mode was evaluated. The results showed that, in general, the short pulse mode produced a larger coagulation zone than the continuous mode.

In addition, MWA has the limitation of producing a droplet-shaped ablation zone. To overcome this drawback, studies have been conducted to generate a more spherical ablation zone by incorporating a floating sleeve inside the MCA. Zhang et al. 18 prospectively evaluated the therapeutic performance of a dipole antenna-based MWA system incorporating floating-sleeve and anti-phase technologies. Treatment of 77 liver tumors in 50 patients achieved a primary technique efficacy of 97.4%, with a major complication rate of 4%. The system also produced reproducible and relatively spherical ablation zones, demonstrating the clinical efficacy and safety of the antenna design. Du et al. 19 proposed an enhanced cooling system with an extended cooling length in a microwave antenna incorporating a floating sleeve structure and validated its performance through numerical analysis as well as ex vivo and in vivo experiments. By designing the system such that cooling reached both the sleeve and the antenna tip, backward heating and tissue carbonization were effectively suppressed, and a more spherical ablation zone was achieved compared with the conventional cooling structure. This study quantitatively demonstrated that the cooling configuration and sleeve design have decisive effects on ablation shape and treatment safety. Ibitoye et al. 20 investigated a dual-slot microwave antenna equipped with a floating metallic sleeve by varying the sleeve position and length and evaluating the reflection coefficient, specific absorption rate (SAR) distribution, backward heating, and ablation shape. Their results demonstrated that an appropriate sleeve design could reduce backward heating and produce a more spherical ablation zone. However, their study primarily focused on the electromagnetic performance of the antenna and the geometric characteristics of the ablation zone. In contrast, the present study varies not only the floating-sleeve length and position but also the input power and uses the Arrhenius damage integral to simultaneously evaluate complete thermal damage in the tumor tissue and the minimization of thermal damage to the surrounding normal tissue.

Taken together, previous studies have mainly focused on predicting temperature distribution and thermal damage behavior induced by microwave irradiation through numerical analysis, and on evaluating therapeutic performance based on these results. Although the accuracy of temperature and thermal damage prediction has been investigated under various heating conditions, most studies have been limited in that the MCA structure was kept fixed while treatment performance was assessed. Meanwhile, in order to address the problem of the droplet-shaped, nonspherical ablation zone generated during MWA, efforts have been made to control energy distribution by introducing a floating sleeve inside the MCA. However, these studies have largely been confined to verifying the effectiveness of specific sleeve configurations, and systematic optimization analyses that simultaneously consider sleeve length, sleeve position, and input power conditions remain insufficient. In addition, most previous studies have focused primarily on the tumor itself, while quantitative evaluation of thermal damage to the surrounding normal tissue has been relatively limited.

Accordingly, in this study, a floating sleeve was integrated into a dual-slot MCA, and the distance from the upper slot to floating sleeve, the floating sleeve length, and the input power were selected as the main design variables to systematically analyze the therapeutic performance of MWA. Through coupled electromagnetic–thermal numerical analysis, the temperature distribution and thermal damage behavior under each design condition were evaluated, and, based on the Arrhenius damage integral, the optimal treatment conditions were identified to achieve complete ablation of the tumor while simultaneously minimizing thermal damage to the surrounding normal tissue. By presenting an optimization approach that integrally considers antenna geometric design and input power conditions, this study is expected to contribute to improving the precision and safety of MWA treatment.

2.3 Thermal damage calculation

Excessive absorption of microwave energy within the tissue induces localized temperature elevation, which in turn leads to thermal damage. In this study, A and E a were set to 7.39×1039 s-1 and 2.58×105 J/mol, respectively. 30 Ω represents the degree of irreversible thermal damage in tissue. An Ω value of 4.63 is generally regarded as corresponding to a 99% probability of thermal damage, whereas an Ω value of 1 corresponds to a 63% probability. 31 Based on this criterion, the Arrhenius thermal damage ratio (ϕ Arrh ) was used to quantitatively evaluate thermal damage in the tumor and surrounding normal tissue. 32 As shown in Eq. 2.4 Numerical analysis conditions

In this study, MWA of a tumor located at the center of liver tissue was simulated through three-dimensional numerical analysis. Accordingly, the numerical model is presented in Figure 1.Figure 1.Numerical modeling and antenna configuration.

Numerical modeling and antenna configuration.

A tumor with a radius of 10 mm was assumed to be located at the center of liver tissue with a radius of 40 mm and a height of 80 mm, and the MCA was assumed to be inserted through the center of the tumor, with its tip positioned 1 mm above the lower boundary of the tumor. The MCA consisted of a catheter with a thickness of 0.3 mm, an outer conductor with a thickness of 0.125 mm, a dielectric with a thickness of 0.335 mm, and an inner conductor with a thickness of 0.135 mm. A dual-slot design was adopted, in which two slots, each 1 mm in length, were positioned with a spacing of 1 mm, starting at a location 2 mm from the MCA tip. The floating sleeve had a thickness of 0.15 mm, and both the distance from the upper slot to the floating sleeve (d fs ) and the length of the floating sleeve (l fs ) were selected as numerical analysis parameters.

Finally, the numerical analysis parameters considered in this study were d fs , l fs , and P in . The microwave frequency was set to 2.45 GHz, and the exposure time was fixed at 300 s. The value of d fs was varied from 0 to 10 mm at intervals of 2 mm, while l fs was varied from 5 to 20 mm at intervals of 5 mm. P in was varied from 0 to 25 W at intervals of 0.5 W. Numerical analysis was performed for all cases, resulting in a total of 1,224 cases, and the thermal damage to both tumor and normal tissues was evaluated for each case. Based on these results, the conditions that achieved thermal damage throughout the entire tumor region while minimizing thermal damage to the surrounding normal tissue were identified. No separate mathematical optimization algorithm was employed in this study. Instead, an exhaustive parametric analysis was conducted for all combinations of l fs , d fs , and P in . For each floating-sleeve geometry, the minimum P in at which complete tumor damage was first achieved, defined as ϕ Arrh = 1, was identified. Among the conditions satisfying this criterion, the condition with the lowest thermal damage ratio in the surrounding normal tissue, ϕ N , was selected. The overall optimal condition was subsequently determined as the combination that achieved complete tumor damage while minimizing thermal damage to the surrounding normal tissue.

3. Results and discussion

3.1 Validation of numerical modeling

A mesh independence test was conducted to verify the reliability of the numerical model developed in this study. The test was performed under the conditions of l fs = 5 mm, d fs = 4 mm, and P in = 10 W. Radial temperature distributions and temporal temperature variations were compared for different mesh counts.

Figure 2 presents the results of the mesh independence test, in which the total number of mesh elements was varied from approximately 20,000 to 420,000. The results showed that the calculated temperature converged, with differences of less than 0.001 °C, when the number of mesh elements exceeded approximately 230,000. Therefore, considering both computational accuracy and efficiency, approximately 234,000 mesh elements were employed in the subsequent numerical analyses.Figure 2.Mesh independence test results.

Mesh independence test results.

Also, to validate the numerical model employed in this study, a comparative verification was conducted against the results reported by Yang et al. 33 In that model, as shown in Figure 3(a), a plastic template with a thickness of 10 mm was placed on top of bovine liver tissue with a radius of 40 mm and a height of 50 mm. For temperature measurement, the temporal temperature variation was evaluated at points located 5 mm above the center of the slot, with radial distances of 2 mm, 4 mm, and 8 mm, respectively. For the MCA design, the slot and floating sleeve configuration reported by Yang et al. 34 was adopted. Figure 5 shows SAR distribution in tissue for various l fs under the conditions of P in = 15 W, d fs = 0 mm, and t = 300 s. As shown in the figure, the spatial distribution of energy absorption within the tissue varied noticeably with the floating sleeve length. As l fs increased, the energy absorption region extended farther upward along the antenna axis, indicating that these differences would subsequently affect the temperature distribution within the tissue. In particular, for l fs = 10 and 15 mm, most of the high SAR region was concentrated within the tumor tissue, while unnecessary energy absorption in the surrounding normal tissue was relatively suppressed. These results demonstrate that appropriate adjustment of the floating sleeve length can concentrate microwave energy deposition within the tumor tissue and reduce energy absorption in the surrounding normal tissue.

Figure 6 shows SAR distribution in tissue for various d fs under the conditions of P in = 15 W, l fs = 10 mm, and t = 300 s. As shown in the figure, the spatial distribution of energy absorption within the tissue was also strongly affected by the position of the floating sleeve. As the sleeve was positioned farther from the antenna tip, the energy absorption region shifted toward the upper portion of the tumor tissue, while its radial extent decreased. These results indicate that the floating sleeve length and position can jointly control the axial and radial distributions of microwave energy deposition within the tissue. Therefore, appropriate adjustment of these geometric parameters may enable microwave energy to be concentrated within the tumor tissue, thereby maximizing tumor damage while minimizing thermal injury to the surrounding normal tissue.

Figure 7 shows the temperature distribution in tissue for various l fs under the conditions of P in = 15 W, d fs = 0 mm, and t = 300 s. In the figure, the black line indicates the tumor boundary, the red line represents the boundary at which Ω = 1, and the blue line within the tumor denotes the boundary at which Ω = 4.63. As shown in the figure, even under the same heating conditions, the patterns of thermal damage in both the tumor and the surrounding normal tissue varied depending on l fs . In addition, the shape of the region in which thermal damage occurred in the surrounding normal tissue was also found to differ according to l fs . Quantitatively, the regions within the tumor where thermal damage occurred with a 99% probability (Ω ≥ 4.63) were calculated to be 86.0%, 92.9%, 86.8%, and 88.1%, respectively, depending on l fs . These results suggest that, under the same applied P in , there exists an optimal floating sleeve length.

However, when the region of thermal damage in the surrounding normal tissue was considered, the optimal l fs was found to be different. Quantitative analysis showed that the regions of thermal damage in the surrounding normal tissue (Ω ≥ 1) were 46.3%, 49.2%, 42.4%, and 48.4%, respectively, depending on l fs . In summary, although the greatest thermal damage within the tumor was observed when l fs = 10 mm, the least thermal damage in the normal tissue occurred when l fs = 15 mm. In addition, the shape of the thermally damaged region in the surrounding normal tissue was found to vary with l fs . Furthermore, the aspect ratios of the ablation area with increasing l fs were 1.79, 1.48, 1.37, and 1.58, respectively, indicating that the case with l fs = 15 mm produced an ablation area closer to a spherical shape.

Figure 8 shows the temperature distribution in tissue for various d fs under the conditions of P in = 15 W, l fs = 10 mm, and t = 300 s. The line definitions are the same as those in Figure 7. Even under the same l fs , the patterns of thermal damage in the tumor and surrounding normal tissue were found to vary depending on d fs . Quantitatively, the regions within the tumor where thermal damage occurred with a 99% probability were calculated to be 92.9%, 99.6%, and 98.5%, respectively, depending on d fs . Similar to the results shown in Figure 7, these findings suggest that, under the same applied P in , there exists an optimal position of the floating sleeve. Likewise, when the region of thermal damage in the surrounding normal tissue was considered, the optimal d fs was found to be different. The regions of thermal damage in the surrounding normal tissue were calculated to be 49.2%, 64.4%, and 62.9%, respectively, depending on d fs . Among the three cases, when only thermal damage within the tumor was considered, the case with d fs = 4 mm was identified as the optimal condition. However, the minimum thermal damage in the normal tissue occurred in the case with d fs = 0 mm. As d fs increased, the aspect ratios of the ablation area were 1.48, 1.47, and 1.51, respectively, indicating that the case with d fs = 4 mm produced an ablation area closer to a spherical shape. Since the objective of treatment is to maximize thermal damage to the tumor while minimizing thermal damage to the surrounding normal tissue, both factors should be considered simultaneously when determining the antenna design conditions.

3.3 Therapeutic effect analysis

In the previous section, it was confirmed that the extent and pattern of thermal damage in both the tumor and the surrounding normal tissue varied depending on the internal antenna design conditions. As mentioned earlier, the goal of treatment is to maximize thermal damage to the tumor while minimizing thermal injury to the surrounding tissue. Accordingly, these two effects were quantitatively analyzed for all cases using ϕ Arrh and ϕ N , respectively, and the conditions yielding the optimal therapeutic performance were identified.

Figure 9 presents the values of ϕ N at the points where ϕ Arrh first reached 1, after analyzing ϕ Arrh for all P in with respect to l fs and d fs . Here, ϕ Arrh = 1 indicates that the entire tumor region was fully ablated with a 99% probability. In the figure, the cases marked with green boxes represent the conditions yielding the minimum ϕ N for each l fs . To compare the effect of the floating sleeve, the case without a floating sleeve was also included in the results. First, it was confirmed that, for all l fs , there existed an optimal d fs at which thermal damage to the surrounding normal tissue was minimized. When d fs was too small, the floating sleeve excessively restricted the electromagnetic field near the slot, thereby reducing the radiation efficiency of the antenna and requiring a higher P in . As a result, heat transfer to the surrounding normal tissue increased, leading to greater thermal damage. In contrast, when d fs was excessively large, the corrective effect of floating sleeve became weaker, resulting in an increase in the required P in to induce thermal damage throughout the entire tumor region. In addition, no distinct trend was observed with respect to l fs . This is because the shape of the heated region varied depending on l fs . Ultimately, the condition that achieved thermal damage at all locations within the tumor while minimizing thermal damage to the surrounding normal tissue was obtained when l fs = 15 mm and d fs = 6 mm, with an applied P in of 13 W, for which ϕ N = 65.5%. Although ϕ N = 65.5% may appear high, it should be noted that, as defined in Eq. (20), the criterion for normal tissue was set to Ω ≥ 1, which corresponds to a 63% probability of thermal damage. Therefore, the actual volume of normal tissue undergoing substantial thermal damage is expected to be much smaller than 65.5%. 4. Conclusions

In this study, the effects of sleeve length, the distance between the upper slot and floating sleeve, and input power on the therapeutic performance of MWA using a dual-slot MCA with a floating sleeve were systematically analyzed through three-dimensional numerical simulations. Electromagnetic wave behavior was calculated based on the Helmholtz equation, while temperature distribution was determined using the Pennes bioheat equation. In addition, thermal damage to both tumor tissue and the surrounding normal tissue was quantitatively evaluated using the Arrhenius thermal damage integral.

The simulation results showed that, even under the same input power condition, the distribution of thermal damage within the tumor and the pattern of thermal damage in the normal tissue varied significantly depending on the length and position of the floating sleeve. In particular, when the floating sleeve was placed too close to the slot, a higher input power was required due to reduced radiation efficiency. In contrast, when the floating sleeve was positioned excessively far from the slot, its corrective effect weakened, resulting in a decline in therapeutic performance. In addition, no monotonic trend was observed with respect to floating sleeve length, which is considered to be due to differences in the power concentration region and heating pattern associated with each sleeve length.

Comprehensive analysis of all design conditions showed that the optimal treatment condition was achieved when l fs = 15 mm and d fs = 6 mm with an applied P in of 13 W, under which thermal damage was induced throughout the entire tumor region while thermal damage to the surrounding normal tissue was minimized. Compared with the case without a floating sleeve, this condition reduced thermal damage to the normal tissue by approximately 5.3%. By presenting an optimization approach that simultaneously considers the geometric design parameters of the floating sleeve and the input power, this study is expected to contribute to improving the precision and safety of MWA treatment. Nevertheless, this computational study was based on idealized tissue geometry and constant physiological properties, including blood perfusion and metabolic heat generation, while the antenna insertion depth was fixed. Future studies should incorporate patient-specific anatomical structures, temperature- or damage-dependent tissue properties, and various antenna insertion depths, together with further experimental and clinical validation.
