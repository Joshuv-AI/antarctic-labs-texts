Interleukin-38 promotes alveolar bone repair in periodontitis by suppressing the nuclear factor kappa B pathway
Authors: Yalin Han, Xi Xu, Shan Zhao, Hui Wang, Shengmei Wei, Jia Xu, Yina Wang, Yuan Hu, Xinyue Li, Jiamin Ai, Guangping Lang, Yingying Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeriodontitis, characterized by chronic inflammation and alveolar bone loss, poses significant challenges to oral health. Interleukin-38 (IL-38) has emerged as a potential therapeutic agent due to its anti-inflammatory properties. This study aimed to investigate whether and how IL-38 protects against periodontitis in a ligation-induced periodontitis (LIP) mouse model and an inflammatory osteogenic model using human periodontal ligament stem cells (hPDLSCs).Methods40 mice were divided into 4 groups (n = 10/group): Control, LIP, LIP + phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), LIP + IL-38. The animals were euthanized and tissue samples (jaws and gingivae) were collected on day 5. Recombinant mouse IL-38 (rmIL-38) lentivirus was used for IL-38 overexpression or knockdown in hPDLSCs in vitro, and shIL-38 hPDLSCs were ultimately treated with the NF-κB inhibitor Bay11-7082. The above tissue and cellular samples were collected for micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) imaging, histological, polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and Western blot (WB) to analyze bone microstructure, inflammatory markers, osteogenic markers, and the relevant protein level of the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) pathway. Introduction

Periodontitis is a common, chronic infectious disease triggered by biofilm-producing microorganisms, which causes periodontal attachment loss and alveolar bone destruction [1]. Current standard therapies, including supragingival scaling, subgingival scaling, root planing, and adjunctive antibiotics or surgery, target microbial control and halt tissue destruction but fail to reliably regenerate periodontal tissues, yielding unpredictable clinical outcomes [2]. Numerous studies indicate that the pathogenesis of periodontitis is intricately linked to a complex network of cytokines. The excessive production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., Il1b, Il6 and Il17a) not only triggers persistent tissue inflammation but also accelerates pathological destruction of the gingival and periodontal tissues. In contrast, anti-inflammatory or regulatory cytokines exert a pivotal regulatory role in maintaining the inflammatory balance and promoting tissue repair processes [3, 4]. Therefore, identifying immunologically driven strategies to promote alveolar bone regeneration represents a critical frontier in regenerative dentistry. In this context, we focused on interleukin-38 (IL-38)—an emerging immunoregulatory cytokine—as a promising therapeutic target.

IL-38, an IL36 subfamily cytokine identified in 2001 [5], binds IL36 receptor (IL36R) and related receptors to suppress inflammatory signaling [6, 7]. Wang et al. demonstrated significantly reduced IL-38 mRNA levels in the palatal mucosa of individuals with suboptimal periodontal health status compared to periodontally healthy controls, implying a link between IL-38 and periodontal disease [8]. Similarly, IL-38 expression in gingival tissues is significantly reduced in a mouse model of periodontitis induced by ligature [9]. These findings indicate that IL-38 may participate in the pathogenesis of periodontitis, but there is currently a lack of experimental studies that clarify the specific role of IL-38 in periodontitis. In addition, in an experimental rheumatoid arthritis model, IL-38 promoted Osteoprotegerin (OPG) expression while reducing Receptor Activator of Nuclear Factor Kappa-B Ligand (RANKL) and Receptor Activator of Nuclear Factor Kappa-B (RANK) expression, thereby alleviating stress in a rat model of joint injury [10]. Alveolar bone destruction in periodontitis is also critically mediated by the RANKL/OPG axis [11–13]. However, it remains unclear whether IL-38 can regulate the bone homeostasis in periodontitis. Notably, another study on osteoarthritis (OA) demonstrated that IL-38 overexpression significantly decreased the phosphorylation level of p65, a key Nuclear Factor Kappa B (NF-κB) signaling protein, thereby inhibiting apoptosis, degradation, and inflammatory responses in chondrocytes [14]. As a classic inflammatory pathway, NF-κB plays a regulatory role in periodontitis [15, 16], and its critical function in alveolar bone destruction has been demonstrated [17, 18]. Additionally, recent studies have found that inhibiting the NF-κB pathway promotes the regeneration of alveolar bone in periodontitis [19, 20]. Taken together, previous studies have evidenced that IL-38 mitigates inflammation-driven bone loss and suppresses NF-κB activation [10, 14]. This leads us to hypothesize that IL-38 may attenuate bone resorption in periodontitis, in which NF-κB signaling likely plays a central role.

This study utilized a ligature-induced periodontitis mouse model and a human periodontal ligament stem cells (hPDLSCs) culture system to clarify the protective mechanism of IL-38. We aimed to investigate whether IL-38 inhibits alveolar bone loss in vivo and enhances osteogenic differentiation of hPDLSCs by suppressing NF-κB signaling. The null hypothesis was that IL-38 exerts no significant effect on alveolar bone loss or osteogenic differentiation of hPDLSCs through the NF-κB pathway. These results may establish IL-38 as a promising therapeutic candidate for periodontal regeneration via cytokine-mediated bone preservation.

Materials and methods

All experiments in this study were performed between March 2022 and December 2025.

Animal model establishment and treatment

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Zunyi Medical University (China). Forty male C57BL/6J mice (8 weeks old) were procured from the Animal Experiment Center of Zunyi Medical University. They were fed a standard diet and provided with water and light according to their natural circadian rhythms. Forty mice were randomly allocated into 4 groups (Control, LIP, LIP + phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), LIP + IL-38, n = 10 per group). Mice for the ligature-induced periodontitis (LIP) model were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane in pure oxygen, and a 5-0 silk ligature was placed around the cervix of the maxillary second molar to induce periodontitis. The ligature was maintained for 5 days to establish a reproducible model of acute periodontal inflammation and early alveolar bone loss, as previously described [21]. This time point allows for effective evaluation of the acute protective effects of IL-38 without advanced irreversible tissue destruction. In addition, based on previous research [9], 200 ng recombinant mouse IL-38 (rmIL-38; Adipogen, USA) dissolved in 10 µL PBS was injected into the gingival tissue (buccal, palatal, mesial, and distal of the second molar, 2.5 µL/site) using a microsyringe (25-µL syringe) in the LIP + IL-38 group for 5 consecutive days. An equal amount of PBS was administered to the LIP + PBS group. All injections were administered daily starting on the day of ligature placement. All animals were euthanized via cervical dislocation in compliance with the institutional animal welfare guidelines. Animals with obvious health abnormalities, severe weight loss, or tissue damage during sampling were excluded from final analysis, to guarantee the overall reliability of all in vivo experimental data. This study adheres to the ARRIVE Guidelines 2.0, and all experimental procedures are reported accordingly.

Cell isolation and cultivation

hPDLSCs were isolated from healthy, noninflammatory teeth of donors (14–25 years) at the Affiliated Stomatological Hospital of Zunyi Medical University with ethical approval. Cells were maintained in high-glucose DMEM (Gibco, USA) with 10% FBS (BI, Israel) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Solarbio, China) at 37℃ under 5% CO2. Medium was replaced every 72 h. Upon reaching 80% confluence, they were subcultured using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA solution (Solarbio, China), and cells from passages 3 to 5 were used for subsequent experiments. For osteogenic induction, cells were cultured in an osteogenic medium consisting of high-glucose DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (BI, Israel), 1% pen-strep (Solarbio, China), 10 mM β-glycerophosphate (Sigma - Aldrich, Germany), 50 mg/L ascorbic acid (Solarbio, China) and 10 nM dexamethasone (Solarbio, China); meanwhile, the inflammatory microenvironment was established by adding 10 ng/mL tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α; Peprotech, USA) and 5 ng/mL interleukin-1β (IL-1β; Peprotech, USA).

Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT)

For the measurement of osseous parameters, maxillae from mice were harvested and subjected to evaluation via micro-computed tomography (micro-CT). Following fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), the specimens were scanned using a Hiscan XM micro-CT platform (Hiscan Information Technology Co., China). Scanning was performed at a 25 μm voxel resolution utilizing a 0.5° rotational increment over a full 360° with an exposure of 50 ms per increment, and the X-ray source was run at 80 kV and 100 µA. The acquired datasets were reconstructed using Hiscan Reconstruct software, followed by quantitative analysis with Hiscan Analyzer software (Hiscan Information Technology Co., China). Quantitative microarchitectural parameters including the cementoenamel junction–alveolar bone crest (CEJ-ABC) distance, bone volume/tissue volume (BV/TV), bone surface area/tissue volume (BS/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular spacing (Tb.Sp), and bone mineral density (BMD) were assessed.

Tissue preparation and sectioning

The maxillae were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, China) at 4 °C for 24 h, followed by decalcification in 10% EDTA (pH 7.6–8.0; Servicebio, China) at 4 °C for 14 days. The EDTA solution was refreshed every 3 days, and the decalcification endpoint was confirmed by needle penetration. Subsequently, tissues were processed for routine paraffin embedding and sectioned into 9 µm-thick slices.

Immunofluorescence (IF) analysis

Sections were first subjected to antigen retrieval, followed by blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at room temperature for 60 min. Next, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies against tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP; Servicebio, GB11416) or alkaline phosphatase (ALP; Servicebio, GB113915). After thorough rinsing with PBST, the sections were incubated with goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Servicebio, G1231) for 60 min, and then stained with DAPI solution for 10 min at room temperature under dark conditions. After air-drying, the sections were mounted using an anti-fluorescence quenching medium (Servicebio, China). Fluorescence images of the mounted specimens were acquired, and the signal intensities were further quantified using ImageJ software (Version 1.54, USA).

Masson’s trichrome staining

Sections were first subjected to deparaffinization and gradient rehydration with sequential treatments of xylene and ethanol. They were then stained with Weigert’s iron hematoxylin (prepared by mixing equal volumes of components A1 and A2). Sections were then differentiated in Acid Differentiation Solution, followed by bluing in Bluing Solution. For trichrome staining, sections were initially stained with Ponceau Acid Fuchsin Solution. After removing excess liquid, they were incubated in Phosphomolybdic Acid Solution and further stained with Aniline Blue Solution. Finally, the sections were dehydrated in a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and permanently mounted in a resin-based mounting medium. Unless otherwise specified, all the primary reagents used in this staining protocol were purchased from Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Solarbio, China).

Cell proliferation assay

A Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; MCE, USA) was used to analyze cell proliferation according to the manufacturer’s instructions. First, hPDLSCs were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10⁴ cells per well. On the following day, the original culture medium was replaced with the respective medium tailored for different experimental groups (Nor and Infla). After 1, 3, 5 or 7 days, the cells were given fresh medium supplemented with 10% CCK-8 solution and then incubated at 37 ℃ for 2 h. Finally, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

Cell migration assay

Cells were cultured in complete medium until they reached approximately 90% confluence in 12-well plates, after which a wound healing assay was performed by scratching the cell monolayer with sterile pipette tips and washing away detached cells with PBS. The monolayers were then incubated in DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 0.5% fetal bovine serum (FBS; BI, Israel), and the progression of wound closure was monitored and documented via microscopy at 0, 24, and 72 h.

ALP staining and quantitative analysis of ALP activity

To determine alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity, both ALP staining and quantitative activity analysis were performed. For the staining assay, hPDLSCs were stained with an NBT/BCIP staining kit (Beyotime, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions, and the stained cells were subsequently observed under a microscope. For the quantitative analysis, hPDLSCs were lysed and assayed via an ALP assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China).

Alizarin Red staining (ARS)

Mineral formation was quantified using Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining followed by quantitative calcification analysis. hPDLSCs were seeded at a density of 2.5 × 10⁴ cells per well in 12-well plates. Upon reaching 80% confluence, the cells were maintained in osteogenic differentiation medium, which was replaced every 72 h. After 21 days of induction, the cells were rinsed with PBS (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany), fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, and stained with Alizarin Red S solution (Solarbio, China) for 10 min. Mineralized nodules were subsequently visualized under a light microscope.

Lentivirus transfection

For the induction of IL-38 overexpression or targeted knockdown in hPDLSCs, lentiviral vectors encoding the IL-38 overexpression cassette (catalog number: H21904), IL-38 shRNA knockdown cassette (catalog number: Y32424), as well as the matched negative control cassettes (catalog numbers: GL107 and GL427) were custom-synthesized by OBiO Technology Co., Ltd. (China). Subsequently, hPDLSCs were subjected to lentiviral transfection by incubating the cells in culture medium containing viral particles and polybrene (OBiO Technology Co., Ltd., China) for a duration of 24 h. Transfected hPDLSCs were subsequently selected with puromycin (Solarbio, China) for 48 h to establish stably transduced cell lines.

Quantitative real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from hPDLSCs or mouse gingival tissues using TRIzol Reagent (Vazyme, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions, and the purity and concentration of the isolated RNA were assessed via spectrophotometry. 500 ng of total RNA was subjected to reverse transcription in a 10 µl final reaction volume using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Japan). The resulting cDNA was then subjected to quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China), and the relative expression levels of target genes were quantified via the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Western blot (WB) analysis

Total protein was extracted from hPDLSCs or mouse gingival tissues using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Solarbio, China). Protein concentrations were quantified via a BCA assay kit (Solarbio, China), and 20–50 µg lysates were resolved by 8–15% SDS-PAGE before transfer onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 (TBST; Servicebio, China) for 2 h at room temperature, then probed with primary antibodies against alkaline phosphatase (ALP; Abcam, USA), runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2; Abcam, USA), and collagen type I alpha 1 chain (COL1A1; Proteintech, China), toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4; Abcam, USA), myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (MYD88; Abcam, USA), phosphorylated inhibitory kappaB kinase α/inhibitory kappaB kinase α (p-IKKα/IKKα; CST, USA), phosphorylated inhibitory kappaB kinase β/inhibitory kappaB kinase β (p-IKKβ/IKKβ; CST, USA), and phosphorylated p65/p65 (p-p65/p65; Abcam, USA) at 4 °C overnight. After TBST washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Beyotime, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Chemiluminescent signals were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescent (ECL) system (Life-iLab, China) and quantified by ImageJ software (Version 1.54, USA).

Sample size determination

Sample sizes for all cellular and animal experiments were determined based on experimental robustness, data stability, and practical feasibility. For in vitro assays, independent biological replicates ranged from n = 3 to 11. Assays with low intrinsic variability were performed using a minimum of n = 3, while assays with higher biological variability or labor-intensive procedures were replicated up to n = 11 to ensure statistical reliability and reproducibility. For in vivo studies, the sample size range was standardized to n = 5 to 10 per group in the revised manuscript. This sample size selection follows conventional cohort sizes commonly adopted in preclinical rodent studies of similar research contexts. In histological staining and micro-CT analyses, animals with technical failure or poor specimen quality were excluded, and only valid analyzable samples were included in the final statistical evaluation.

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcomes of this study were to evaluate the osteogenic and bone-regenerative effects of IL-38. In vivo, new bone formation and bone mass parameters were assessed using micro-CT and histological staining. In vitro, the osteogenic differentiation capacity of hPDLSCs was determined by detecting ALP activity and mineralization nodule formation.

The secondary outcomes focused on the regulatory effect of IL-38 on the NF-κB signaling pathway. Pharmacological inhibition of NF-κB was further applied to confirm the essential role of this pathway in IL-38-mediated osteogenic differentiation.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated at least 3 times, and the results are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Student’s t - test was used for intergroup comparisons, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for multigroup comparisons, with differences considered statistically significant at P < 0.05 (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001). The statistical analyses and generation of images were performed with GraphPad Prism 10.0.

Results

All animal data were pooled from two independent replicate experiments. Animals were randomly divided into four groups: Control, LIP, LIP + PBS, and LIP + IL-38, with an initial allocation of 10 mice per group. Three mice died during the experimental period: two in the LIP + PBS group and one in the LIP + IL-38 group. No obvious complications were observed in the surviving animals. All deceased mice were excluded from subsequent statistical analyses. The final valid sample sizes were n = 10 (Control), n = 10 (LIP), n = 8 (LIP + PBS), and n = 9 (LIP+IL-38).

Effect of RmIL-38 on periodontal inflammation and NF-κB signaling in LIP mice

To investigate the functional role of IL-38 in the murine periodontitis experiment, rmIL-38 was administered into the gingival tissue of mice. The grouping and injection procedure for C57BL/6J mice is schematically represented (Fig. 1A). We observed significant downregulation of IL-38 mRNA expression in ligature-induced periodontitis (LIP) mouse gingival tissue when compared with the control group (Fig. 1B), indicating the potential involvement of IL-38 in periodontitis pathogenesis. As expected, rmIL-38 treatment markedly suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokines Il17a and Il23a mRNA expression levels while enhancing Il10 expression (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, we examined the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway, and found that the phosphorylation levels of p65 and IκBα showed a downward trend in the LIP + IL-38 group (Fig. 1D). However this decreasing trend did not reach statistical significance. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that IL-38 mitigate inflammatory responses, while its specific molecular mechanism remains to be further elucidated in future investigations.

Role of rmIL-38 in alveolar bone repair in LIP mice

To assess the therapeutic potential of rmIL-38 against alveolar bone resorption in a LIP murine model, we conducted comprehensive molecular and microarchitectural analyses. qRT-PCR revealed that rmIL-38 significantly downregulated osteoclast-related marker (Ctsk) while upregulating the osteoblast-related marker (Opg), resulting in an increased Opg/Rankl ratio (Fig. 2A). Micro-computed tomography analysis demonstrates that rmIL-38 administration reversed ligature-induced increases in CEJ–ABC distance at distal, buccal and palatal sites (Fig. 2B–C). Quantitative microarchitectural analysis showed that rmIL-38 enhanced BV/TV, BS/TV, Tb.N, Tb.Th and BMD, while reducing trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) (Fig. 2D). Masson trichrome staining further indicated significantly increased collagen deposition around the second molar at mesial furcation and distal sites in rmIL-38-treated mice (Fig. 2E–F). Further immunofluorescence assays validated the above-described structural ameliorations; specifically, ligature-induced periodontitis led to increased TRAP-mediated osteoclast activity and decreased ALP-related osteoblast signaling, whereas IL-38 treatment exerted a pronounced corrective effect on these dysregulated cellular processes (Fig. 2G, H). Collectively, these data demonstrate that IL-38 attenuates alveolar bone resorption and promotes osteogenic regeneration in a murine model of experimental periodontitis.

Impact of inflammatory microenvironment on hPDLSCs proliferation, migration, and osteogenic differentiation

To determine whether inflammatory stimulation impairs the proliferation, migration, and osteogenic differentiation of hPDLSCs, cells were cultured in osteogenic medium and exposed to TNF-α (10 ng/mL) and IL-1β (5 ng/mL). CCK-8 assays and wound-healing (scratch) assays revealed that cytokine treatment significantly suppressed both cell viability and migratory capacity at 24 h post-stimulation (Fig. 3A, B). Moreover, qRT–PCR and Western blot analyses performed after 7, 14, and 21 days of culture showed that mRNA and protein levels of key osteogenic markers—ALP, RUNX2 and COL1A1—were markedly downregulated in the inflammation-plus-osteogenic (Infla-Osteogenic) group compared with the osteogenic control group (Fig. 3C–H). Notably, however, the protein expression of ALP and RUNX2 exhibited no substantial change following 21 days of cytokine exposure, suggesting a potential plateau or compensatory adaptation in late-stage osteogenic signaling under chronic inflammatory conditions. Both ALP staining intensity and the extent of mineralized nodule formation were significantly diminished in the Infla-Osteogenic group relative to the Osteogenic control group (Fig. 3I, J). These data confirmed that the proliferation, migration and osteogenic differentiation of hPDLSCs were significantly inhibited by the inflammatory microenvironment.

Effect of IL-38 overexpression on osteogenic differentiation and NF-κB signaling in hPDLSCs

To confirm the osteogenic function of IL-38 in in vivo experiments, hPDLSCs were transduced with IL-38-encoding lentiviral vectors (oeIL-38) or control vectors (oeCTRL), achieving more than 90% transduction efficiency confirmed by fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 4A). qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses verified significant IL-38 upregulation at both mRNA and protein levels in oeIL-38 compared with oeCTRL cells (Fig. 4B), indicating that IL-38 was successfully overexpressed in hPDLSCs. Given that proliferative capacity critically determines the efficacy of hPDLSC-mediated osseous regeneration, a CCK-8 assay was performed first to analyze the effects of IL-38 overexpression on hPDLSC proliferation. CCK-8 assays demonstrated that the proliferative capacity was enhanced in IL-38-overexpressed hPDLSCs (Fig. 4C). After 7, 14 and 21 days of osteogenic induction, IL-38 overexpression significantly upregulated both mRNA and protein levels of key osteogenic markers ALP, RUNX2, and COL1A1 in hPDLSCs (Fig. 4D, E). Concurrently, ALP staining revealed markedly enhanced signal intensity in hPDLSCs transduced with IL-38-overexpressing lentivirus (oeIL-38) compared to those transduced with control vector (oeCTRL) at both day 7 and day 14 of osteogenic induction (Fig. 4F). Consistent with the enhanced ALP staining, quantitative enzymatic assays confirmed significantly elevated ALP activity in the oeIL-38 group (Fig. 4G). ARS analysis further revealed significantly greater mineralized deposition in oeIL-38 cultures following 21-day osteogenic induction compared to oeCTRL hPDLSCs (Fig. 4H). Together, these data suggest that IL-38 overexpression substantially promoted the osteogenic differentiation of hPDLSCs.

To explore the potential mechanism by which IL-38 promotes the osteogenic differentiation of hPDLSCs, we detected several key signaling molecules of the NF-κB pathway. Compared with those in oeCTRL hPDLSCs, WB quantification showed that the protein expression of TLR4, MYD88, phosphorylated IKKα, phosphorylated IKKβ and phosphorylated p65 were significantly lower in oeIL-38 hPDLSCs (Fig. 4I). These findings suggest that IL-38 may promote hPDLSC osteogenic differentiation by inhibiting the activation of NF-κB signaling pathway.

Effect of IL-38 knockdown on osteogenic differentiation and NF-κB signaling in hPDLSCs

To further validate the pro-osteogenic role of IL-38, we stably knocked down IL-38 in human hPDLSCs using lentiviral vectors expressing IL-38-targeting short hairpin RNA (shIL-38) or a non-targeting control (shCTRL). qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses confirmed that both mRNA and protein levels of IL-38 were significantly reduced in shIL-38-transduced hPDLSCs compared with shCTRL group (Fig. 5A), verifying efficient knockdown. We next assessed the impact of IL-38 deficiency on osteogenic differentiation by performing qRT–PCR, Western blot, ALP staining and ARS assays. qRT–PCR revealed that knockdown of IL-38 markedly suppressed the expression of early osteogenic marker ALP, while RUNX2 and COL1A1 expression levels were not obviously altered in shIL-38 group (Fig. 5B). At the protein level, IL-38 depletion exerted a broader inhibitory effect on osteogenic commitment, significantly reducing the expression of ALP, RUNX2, COL1A1 and osteoprotegerin (OPG) at day 7 of osteogenic induction (Fig. 5C). By days 14 and 21, qRT-PCR revealed variable but consistent downregulation of osteogenic-related transcripts, while Western blotanalysis showed a modest reduction in corresponding protein levels (Fig. S1A–D). Furthermore, ALP staining exhibited less intensity in shIL-38 hPDLSCs compared to shCTRL cells at both day 7 and day 14 (Fig. 5D, E). In addition, alizarin red staining and quantitative analysis revealed that shIL-38 hPDLSCs formed a reduced number of mineralized nodules in comparison to shCTRL cells following 21 days of osteogenic induction (Fig. 5F). These data demonstrate that knockdown of IL-38 impairs the osteogenic differentiation capacity of hPDLSCs. To further interrogate the molecular mechanism underlying impaired osteogenesis, we assessed key components of the NF-κB signaling pathway. At day 7 of osteogenic induction, shIL-38 hPDLSCs exhibited significantly elevated protein levels of TLR4, MYD88 and phosphorylated IKKα compared to shCTRL controls; phosphorylation of IKKβ, IκBα and p65 also increased, though not to statistical significance (Fig. 5G). By days 14 and 21, multiple NF-κB pathway components remained upregulated, albeit to varying degrees in IL-38-deficient cells (Fig. S1E-F). Collectively, these results demonstrate that IL-38 knockdown suppresses osteogenic differentiation while concomitantly enhancing NF-κB pathway activation in hPDLSCs, a finding consistent with the reciprocal phenotype observed in IL-38-overexpressing (oeIL-38) cells.

Role of NF-κB signaling in IL-38-mediated osteogenic differentiation of hPDLSCs

ShIL-38 and shCTRL hPDLSCs were cultured under inflammatory osteogenic conditions in the presence or absence of the NF-κB inhibitor Bay11-7082 and harvested on days 7 and 21. qRT-PCR analysis revealed that IL-38 knockdown suppressed mRNA expression of the osteogenic markers ALP, RUNX2, and COL1A1, compared to control cells,an effect largely reversed by Bay11-7082 treatment (Fig. 6A). At the protein level, Western blot analysisconfirmed marked reductions in ALP and RUNX2 in shIL-38 hPDLSCs, while COL1A1 showed no significant change (Fig. 6B). Notably, NF-κB inhibition further enhanced RUNX2 and COL1A1 protein expression in IL-38-deficient cells relative to the shIL-38 group, although ALP levels were unaffected. Functionally, ALP staining on day 7 and ARS staining at day 21 demonstrated that Bay11-7082 restored osteogenic differentiation in IL-38-deficient hPDLSCs (Fig. 6C, D). Together, these findings establish that IL-38 preserves hPDLSC osteogenic potential under inflammatory stress through suppression of the NF-κB signaling pathway.

Discussion

This study provides comprehensive evidence that Interleukin-38 (IL-38) serves as a pivotal regulator in the pathogenesis of periodontitis, effectively bridging the gap between inflammation resolution and tissue regeneration. Our core null hypothesis—that IL-38 exerts no significant effects on periodontal inflammation, alveolar bone loss, or osteogenic differentiation of hPDLSCs—was clearly rejected.

We demonstrated that local administration of recombinant murine IL-38 (rmIL-38) not only suppressed periodontal inflammation by downregulating key pro-inflammatory cytokines (Il17a, and Il23) but also significantly inhibited alveolar bone resorption and promoted collagen integrity in a ligature-induced periodontitis model. Notably, the observed downregulation of Il38 mRNA in our experimental model corroborates clinical findings [9], underscoring the pathophysiological relevance of IL-38 deficiency in periodontal tissue destruction. The current study addresses a critical therapeutic gap in periodontal medicine: the lack of agents capable of simultaneously controlling inflammation and promoting regeneration. While conventional therapies focus on mechanical debridement and microbial control, they often fall short in resolving the host-mediated inflammatory cascade that drives tissue destruction [22, 23]. Our findings position IL-38 as a promising biological agent that fulfills the dual criteria of immunomodulation and osteoprotection.

The observed anti-inflammatory effects of rmIL-38 in vivo are consistent with its established role as an IL-36 receptor antagonist [24]. By binding to the IL-36R, IL-38 effectively inhibits the Th1/Th17 inflammatory axis, which is a hallmark of autoimmune pathologies such as psoriasis and rheumatoid arthritis [25]. The suppression of Il17a and Il23 in the gingival tissues of rmIL-38-treated mice observed in our study suggests a similar mechanism operates in periodontitis. This is particularly significant given the established role of IL-17 in promoting osteoclastogenesis via RANKL upregulation. Our data further extends this understanding by demonstrating that IL-38 not only dampens inflammation but also directly tips the balance away from bone resorption, as indicated by the reduced expression of the osteoclastogenic marker TRAP.

A novel and compelling aspect of this research is the elucidation of IL-38’s role in stem cell biology, specifically within hPDLSCs. While previous studies have implicated IL-38 in murine intestinal stem cells and bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells [26, 27], its function in periodontal regeneration has been unexplored. Our in vitro data provide the first evidence that IL-38 acts as a positive regulator of osteogenic differentiation. This discovery is clinically relevant because the regenerative potential of endogenous hPDLSCs is often compromised in the inflammatory periodontal microenvironment. By enhancing the osteogenic capacity of these cells, IL-38 therapy could potentially restore the functional tissue architecture lost to disease.

The mechanistic exploration of the NF-κB pathway provides a solid molecular foundation for the observed phenotypes. The NF-κB pathway is a master regulator of inflammation and a known suppressor of osteoblast function [28, 29]. Our results demonstrate that IL-38 acts as an endogenous brake on this pathway by inhibiting the phosphorylation of IκBα and the subsequent nuclear translocation of p65. This inhibition appears to occur via the TLR4/MYD88 signaling axis, suggesting that IL-38 may interfere with the downstream signaling cascades triggered by pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) or damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). The rescue experiment using Bay11-7082 is particularly convincing; by showing that pharmacological inhibition of NF-κB mimics the pro-osteogenic effects of IL-38, we confirm that the osteogenic impairment associated with IL-38 deficiency is directly attributable to unchecked NF-κB activity. This establishes a clear causal chain: IL-38 - NF-κB inhibition - Enhanced Osteogenesis. These findings align with and extend the existing literature on IL-38’s osteoprotective roles, such as its ability to reduce Rankl expression in arthritis models [10, 30], by detailing the intracellular signaling mechanisms specific to periodontal ligament stem cells.

Limitations and future directions

Despite these insights, several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, we acknowledge that the ligature-induced periodontitis (LIP) model represents an acute, localized inflammatory reaction that does not fully recapitulate the chronic, polymicrobial, and host-modulated characteristics of human periodontitis. This discrepancy may limit the direct translational relevance of our in vivo findings. Notably, although ligature placement introduces a minor mechanical component, the ensuing inflammation and alveolar bone loss are primarily driven by microbial dysbiosis and subsequent host immune responses. The silk ligature serves as a retentive nidus that facilitates plaque accumulation and microbial overgrowth, as previously reported [21]. Thus, while this model is suitable for dissecting key inflammatory signaling pathways, its inherent limitations should be considered when extrapolating current findings to clinical settings. Consequently, future studies employing more clinically relevant polymicrobial or spontaneous periodontitis models are warranted to further validate our observations.

Second, regarding the experimental duration, the 5-day time frame primarily captures the acute inflammatory phase and early osteoclastic bone resorption. We acknowledge that this short duration is insufficient to reflect the full spectrum of alveolar bone remodeling, including the late reparative and anabolic stages of bone turnover. Nevertheless, this acute experimental setting remains well-suited for evaluating the early protective effects of IL-38, as well as its regulatory function in sustaining bone homeostasis during the initial stage of inflammatory tissue injury.

Third, regarding the mechanistic exploration, our study has limitations concerning both cellular complexity and molecular specificity. In vitro, the monoculture system, while allowing us to precisely dissect the cell-autonomous effects of IL-38 on hPDLSCs, does not fully replicate the native pathophysiological complexity involving sophisticated crosstalk among immune cells, osteoclasts, fibroblasts, and microbial communities. Furthermore, from a molecular perspective, although pharmacological inhibition with Bay11-7082 supports the involvement of NF-κB signaling, the potential for off-target effects precludes definitive causal confirmation. Hence, future studies employing genetic strategies (e.g., siRNA-mediated silencing or CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout) and multicellular co-culture systems will be necessary to establish more conclusive mechanistic evidence. Additionally, our focus on the NF-κB pathway does not exclude the involvement of other signaling cascades. Beyond NF-κB, IL-38 may also modulate key pathways governing bone metabolism, including MAPK and Wnt/β-catenin. Given that IL-38 acts within a complex cytokine network, comprehensive profiling of these parallel signaling pathways will further clarify its multifaceted regulatory functions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identifies IL-38 as a critical regulator of periodontal homeostasis by exerting dual protective effects: attenuating inflammatory damage and enhancing stem cell-mediated osteogenic regeneration, at least partially through the inhibition of NF-κB signaling. These findings establish a theoretical foundation for the development of novel biological therapies. Targeting the IL-38 pathway shows considerable promise not only for periodontitis but also for other inflammatory bone disorders characterized by a dysregulated balance between bone destruction and repair.
