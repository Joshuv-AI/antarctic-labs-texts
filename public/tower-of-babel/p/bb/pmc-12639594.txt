Ultrasmall Pt nanoparticles modified bioenergetic-active implant for osteoporotic bone treatment via MSCs reprogramming and immunomodulation
Authors: Shi Cheng, Chongquan Huang, Hongqin Zhu, Ren Yang, Yu Zhang, Lidan Liu, Jiongliang Li, Haifeng Zhang, Xuanyong Liu, Feng Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdenosine triphosphate (ATP) generation of the cells in osteoporosis microenvironment is inhibited by the high level of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and then results in the dysfunctions of stem cells and immune cells. Herein, we present a bioenergetic-active bone implant (TNT@Pt), which features TiO2 nanotubes decorated with ultrasmall Pt nanoparticles on Ti surface. Thanks to the strong SOD-like and CAT-like enzymatic activities of ultrasmall Pt nanoparticles (3–4 nm), the TNT@Pt implant can scavenge ROS effectively, and then restore the mitochondrial membrane potential and recover ATP synthesis. The ATP produced by OVX BMSCs cultured on the surface of TNT@Pt samples increased by 108 %. With an enhanced ATP generation ability, the TNT@Pt implant induce a better bone differentiation of MSCs via activating PI3K-Akt, MAPK, and Calcium signaling pathways. Furthermore, the TNT@Pt implant can also enhance the ATP production of macrophage, and thus shifting its polarization to an anti-inflammatory phenotype, and finally mediating an improved osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. Mouse air-pouch model and rat femur implantation model verify the superior immunomodulation and bone regeneration capabilities of the TNT@Pt implant in vivo. Collectively, the novel bioenergetic-active bone implant shows promising potential for the clinic treatment of osteoporotic bone defect.Keywords: Bone implant, Bioenergetic-active material, Osteoporosis, Immunomodulation, Bone regeneration

Introduction

Osteoporotic fractures are among the most common bone-related issues, affecting over 200 million people globally. It is estimated that approximately one-third of women and one-fifth of men over the age of 50 will experience an osteoporotic fracture in their lifetime [1]. Surgical intervention is the primary approach for treating osteoporotic fractures; however, existing bone implant materials often yield unsatisfactory outcomes, highlighting the need for advanced bone repair materials specifically designed for osteoporosis patients [2].

Osteoporosis and associated fractures often involve mitochondrial dysfunction (including oxidative stress) and inflammatory responses within various cellular systems, such as basic multicellular units (BMUs), bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), and macrophages [3,4]. Researches demonstrated that elevated oxidative stress and mitochondrial impairment in BMSCs, particularly those derived from osteoporotic models, significantly reduce their capacity for osteogenic differentiation [5,6]. Mitochondria in these cells fail to adequately regulate reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. As the primary target of ROS, mitochondrial function, especially ATP synthesis, becomes compromised, thereby impairing critical cellular pathways essential for BMSCs differentiation into bone-forming cells [7,8].

Beyond the mechanisms mentioned, osteoporosis and the resulting fractures are often accompanied by inflammatory responses, where the polarization state of macrophages also influences BMSCs differentiation trends and the progression of osteoporosis [9]. Mitochondrial dysfunction in macrophages can lead to elevated ROS levels, promoting M1 polarization [10]. M1 macrophages release inflammatory factors such as TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1α, and IL-1β, hindering the recovery from osteoporosis [11,12]. ATP deficiency inhibits M2 polarization of macrophages, which are primarily responsible for anti-inflammatory responses and tissue repair [13]. When mitochondrial function is impaired, the expression of M2-associated genes declines [14]. Thus, prolonged stagnation in the transition from M1 to M2 is detrimental to bone healing [15]. Reducing ROS levels and restoring mitochondrial function may decrease the M1/M2 ratio of macrophages, shortening the transition time from M1 to M2, promoting bone regeneration process.

Bioenergetic-active materials (BAM) were proposed by Liu et al. in 2020, referring to a novel class of bioactive materials that significantly promote tissue reconstruction and regeneration by regulating cellular energy metabolism states, including mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP levels, during tissue engineering and reconstruction processes [16]. For example, Liu et al. constructed a hydrogel material loaded with bioenergetic-active exosomes (Suc-EXO). The ATP content of Suc-EXO is 5.43 times that of ordinary exosomes, providing continuous fuel for the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA cycle). Under sustained energy supply, Suc-EXO promotes BMSC chondrogenic differentiation through the P2X7-mediated PI3K-AKT pathway and improves chondrocyte anabolic metabolism and mitochondrial homeostasis via the P2X7-mediated SIRT3 pathway, ultimately facilitating high-quality regeneration of articular cartilage and long-term homeostasis maintenance [17]. Therefore, developing bone implant materials that simultaneously possess ROS-scavenging activity and mitochondrial function repair (restoring mitochondrial membrane potential and increasing ATP levels) to promote osteoblast differentiation and regulate inflammatory responses is a promising strategy for achieving effective and long-lasting therapeutic effects for osteoporosis bone defect.

Herein, we designed a bioenergetic-active implant to treat osteoporosis bone defect (Fig. 1). Ultrasmall Pt nanoparticles were deposited within the TiO2 nanotube modified Ti (TNT@Pt). The TNT@Pt showed excellent catalase- (CAT) and superoxide dismutase- (SOD) like abilities to scavenge ROS. With the antioxidant capacity, the TNT@Pt could restore the mitochondrial membrane potential of the cells, and increase the ATP production. With sufficient energy supply, rBMSCs exhibited improved osteogenic differentiation through activating calcium, PI3K-Akt, and MAPK signaling pathways. Furthermore, it supports the M2 polarization of macrophages after mitochondrial function recovery. This study elucidates the basis for bone regeneration of ROS-scavenging bone repair materials from the perspective of bioenergetic-active materials, providing a new perspective for the design of novel osteoporotic bone repair materials.

Results and discussion

Sample characterization

To fabricate a high content of nano-Pt particle loaded Ti implant, TiO2 nanotubes were first constructed on Ti surface. As shown in Fig. 2a, the inner diameter and thickness of the nanotubes on the TNT sample were approximately 50–60 and 9 nm, respectively. After H2PtCl4 was reduced by NaBH4, obviously nanoparticles were deposited on TNT@Pt surface. To evaluate the Pt nanopaticles grown on the inner nanotubes, the coating was ion thinned, and then observed by TEM (Fig. 2b). A large amount of ultrasmall nanoparticles (3–4 nm) are uniformly distributed within the TiO2 nanotubes. This may be due to the nanotubes restricting the free movement of ions, resulting in insufficient reactive solution for the growth of Pt particles to increase in size. HR-TEM revealed a lattice spacing of 0.244 nm for the nanoparticle, which is corresponding to (111) of Pt (Fig. 2b and Fig. S1). Furthermore, the mapping result, XPS full spectrum, and EDS spectrum further confirmed the existence and even distribution of Pt element (Fig. 2b, Fig. S2 and S3). Meanwhile, the EDS result suggested a 2.32 at.% of Pt content on the TNT@Pt surface. Fig. 2c displays the XRD patterns of various samples. The diffraction peaks of Ti phase were detected on all the samples. The characteristic peaks of titanium dioxide, including (101) lattice planes of anatase and (110) lattice planes of rutile, were observed on the TNT and TNT@Pt groups, revealing that nanotube on the samples’ surface were mainly titanium dioxide. No characteristic peaks of Pt were detected, which may be due to the low amount of Pt and its presence in the form of uniformly dispersed ultrasmall nanoparticles.

(a) SEM images of Ti, TNT, and TNT@Pt; (b) TEM images and HR-TEM images of TNT@Pt and corresponding element mapping; (c) full XPS spectra of Ti, TNT, and TNT@Pt; (d) Pt 4f high-resolution spectrum of TNT@Pt; (e) zeta potential and (h) water contact angle of Ti, TNT, and TNT@Pt; (i) SOD-like and (j) CAT-like activities of Ti, TNT, and TNT@Pt; (k) total ROS scavenging ability of Ti, TNT, and TNT@Pt.

The corrosion resistance of various samples were investigated via electrochemical test. As shown in Fig. 2f, the corrosion currents of the Ti and TNT samples were similar in magnitude. However, after deposition of Pt nanoparticle, TNT@Pt sample displayed a significantly lower corrosion current than the other two samples, suggesting its superior corrosion resistance. This may be because Pt has relatively inert chemical properties, and its presence reduces the current corrosion area of the TNT@Pt surface, thereby decreasing the corrosion current. A desirable corrosion resistance of bone implant can reduce the generation of corrosion particle and product, and thus reducing the risk of inflammatory reaction [18]. The results of zeta potential are shown in Fig. 2g, which demonstrate that higher potential values of the TNT and TNT@Pt samples than that of the untreated Ti. Due to the presence of components such as phospholipids, carbohydrates, and proteins on the cell membrane, cells generally carry a negative charge [19]. Therefore, a higher potential of TNT@Pt means it has better affinity to cells. Furthermore, the TNT@Pt showed a lower water contact angle than untreated Ti (Fig. 2h), demonstrating that TNT@Pt surface is more favorable for cell adhesion [20].

The bone microenvironment of osteoporosis patients often characterized by high ROS level, and thus inhibiting ATP generation in surrounding cells, finally leading to cell dysfunction [21,22]. Therefore, bone implants with sufficiently ROS scavenging capability are more suitable for osteoporosis-related bone defect therapy. ·O2− and H2O2 scavenging behavior of the TNT@Pt sample under in vitro conditions were detected using SOD assay kit and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) assay kit, respectively. As shown in Fig. 2I, 2J and S4, TNT@Pt shows effectively ·O2− (∼80 %) and H2O2 (∼75 %) scavenging ability, whereas barely scavenging effect was detected in the Ti and TNT groups. Furthermore, directly quantification of O2 formation during H2O2 decomposition by TNT@Pt showed that approximately 25 mg/L O2 concentration was detected in the microenvironment (Fig. S3). Furthermore, 2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH•) was chosen as the oxidative probe to investigate the total anti-oxidation ability of the TNT@Pt sample. A total of 85 % free radical can be reduced by the TNT@Pt sample (Fig. 2Kand S4). Furthermore, the TNT@Pt samples retained its ROS-scavenging activity even after two weeks of immersion in PBS, suggesting its long-term stability (Fig. S5).

These results confirmed that TNT@Pt can effectively consume ROS in the surrounding environment. The ROS scavenging ability of TNT@Pt sample originates from the Pt nanoparticles on the surface and within the TiO2 nanotubes. Pt has high surface activity and good electron transfer capability, which can facilitate redox reactions and effectively decompose ROS [23]. Specially, in this work, we prepared ultrasmall Pt nanoparticle whitin the TiO2 nanotubes. Smaller Pt nanoparticles have a larger specific surface area, which can provide more active sites for reactions, thereby enhancing catalytic activity [24].

Intracellular redox status and energy production

Mitochondria are a major site for production of ROS, while the excess ROS could damage the function of mitochondria thus may give rise to many diseases. Osteoporosis have been demonstrated as closely associated with unbalanced ROS generation, which results in mitochondrial dysfunction and decrease of ATP generation [21,22]. The fate of MSCs and immune cells can be disturbed under oxidative stress environment. Therefore, restore the mitochondrial function and ATP generation ability of bone-related cells by scavenging excessive ROS is crucially for the treatment of osteoporosis.

In this study, rat bone MSCs (rBMSCs) were obtained from ovariectomy (OVX) in female Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats to mimic the osteoporotic bone microenvironment. For the in vitro cell experiments, each condition was independently repeated n = 3 times (biological replicates). To detect the ROS levels in rBMSCs mitochondrial, DCFH-DA was used as ROS indicator. As shown in Fig. 3a, strong and large scale of green fluorescence were observed on the Ti and TNT groups, whereas the green fluorescence strikingly diminished on the TNT@Pt group. The quantantive analysis with flow cytometry also demonstrated consistent results with staining, which suggested that the positive rate of TNT@Pt group was less than 10 % (Fig. 3b, Fig. S6). The intracellular antioxidant property of rBMSCs were also confirmed using CAT Assay Kit and Total SOD Assay Kit. As expected, the results in Fig. 3c–d demonstrated that the Ti and TNT groups showed few CAT- and SOD-like activities, while the TNT@Pt group showed an obvious CAT- and SOD-like activities. Intracellular ROS acts on unsaturated fatty acids in lipids, generating lipid peroxides, which gradually decompose into a series of complex compounds, including malondialdehyde (MDA) [25]. Therefore, by detecting the level of MDA, we can assess the level of lipid oxidation, reflecting the degree of oxidative damage within the cells. The MDA formation was detected using MDA Assay Kit, among which thiobarbituric acid can react with MDA to produce a red product with an absorption peak at 532 nm. The results of MDA detection are shown in Fig. S7. The cells in TNT@Pt group exhibited lowest MDA content among the three groups, reflecting its the best protective ability against oxidative stress damage.

Intracellular ROS level and mitochondrial function investigation of rBMSCs obtained from OVX rats cultured on Ti, TNT, and TNT@Pt. (a) Intracellular ROS staining images; (b) corresponding quantitative analysis; (c) intracellular SOD-like activity and (d) intracellular CAT-like activity; (e) MMP of rBMSCs determined by the green/red percentage in JC-1 staining; (f) flow cytometry and (g) corresponding quantitative analysis of JC-1 monomer staining; (h) ATP production level.

To further investigate the mitochondrial function, mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) detection was performed via JC-1 staining. When the MMP is normal, JC-1 enters the mitochondria through the mitochondrial membrane in a J-type manner and forms aggregates that emit red fluorescence due to increased concentration. When the mitochondrial transmembrane potential becomes depolarized, JC-1 is released from the mitochondria, leading to a decreased concentration and a reversal to monomeric forms that emit green fluorescence. As shown in Fig. 3e, obvious and large area of red fluorescence was observed on the TNT@Pt group, revealing a significant enhancement of MMP. In addition, quantative analysis of JC-1 monomers via flow cytometry also proved this trend, which showed only 26.5 % of rBMSCs cultured on TNT@Pt group with negative MMP, less than 45 % of the Ti group and 38.5 % of the TNT group (Fig. 3f and g). Furthermore, we also evaluate the MMP of BMDMs cultured in various samples. Both the fluorescence staining (Fig. S8) and flow cytometry analysis (Fig. S9) results showed the similar trend that the cells cultured in TNT@Pt exhibited best MMP among the three groups. The integrity of the MMP is a key factor in maintaining cellular energy supply. The generation of ATP in the cells cultured on various groups were then detected. The results in Fig. 3h suggested that TNT@Pt group can produce a significantly higher ATP amount than the other two groups. These results illustrated that TNT@Pt sample can consume the excessive ROS within the cells, and then restore MMP, increasing the ATP generation. Hence, our constructed ultrasmall Pt nanoparticles-loaded TiO2 nanotube modified Ti implant is a bioenergetic-active implant.

In vitro biocompatibility of TNT@Pt

Good biocompatibility is a prerequisite for biomaterials to be implanted in the body. To evaluate the cytocompatibility of TNT@Pt implant, rBSMCs obtained from OVX rats were cultured on various samples, and then conducted with live/dead staining, CCK-8 test, and cytoskeleton staining. According to the live/dead fluorescent imaging, living cells were covered all the sample’ surfaces after 3 days of culture, and almost no dead cells were observed (Fig. 4a). Furthermore, the same live/dead results were also observed for BMDMs (Fig. S10), suggesting that TNT@Pt had no side effect on cell viability. The CCK-8 results are displayed in Fig. 4b. The cells cultured on various samples showed no significant difference in proliferation at day 1. However, after 5 days of culture, the cells cultured on the TNT@Pt sample exhibited a higher proliferation than the on the other two samples. The cell cytoskeleton was stained after cultured on various samples for 12 h. Compared with the Ti and TNT groups, the TNT@Pt group was more suitable for the spreading of cell filopodia (Fig. 4c), and the calculated results suggested a highest spreading area of TNT@Pt group (Fig. 4d). For TNT@Pt sample, both TiO2 nanotube and Pt nanoparticles biologically inert materials, so they will not affect cell viability and proliferation. However, as proved above, TNT@Pt a bioenergetic-active implant. Therefore, the cells cultured on TNT@Pt can produce more ATP for cell adhesion and proliferation. Notably, better cell adhesion always associates with greater and faster osteogenic ability [26,27]. Hence, an enhanced bone regeneration of TNT@Pt implant is promising.

(a) Live/dead staining after cultured for 3 days; (b) cell proliferation; (c) cytoskeleton staining after cultured for 24 h and (d) corresponding spreading area; (e) quantitative analysis of ALP analysis; (f) relative mRNA expression of bone-related genes.

Implant materials will inevitably come into contact with blood; therefore, the hemolysis rate of biomaterials is also very important. As shown in Fig. S11, all the samples showed a hemolysis rate lower than 1 %, which meets requirement of clinic application (below 5 %). This can also be ascribed to the bioinert property of the coating compounds, which do no damage to red blood cells.

TNT@Pt reprograms rBMSCs behavior in vitro and the behind mechanism study

The activity of alkaline phosphatase (ALP) is closely related to the proliferation and differentiation of osteoblasts [28]. During the process of bone formation, osteoblasts secrete ALP to promote the mineralization of the bone matrix. Additionally, ALP hydrolyzes phosphates, releasing inorganic phosphate and increasing the local concentration of phosphates, which in turn facilitates the formation of hydroxyapatite. Therefore, we first evaluate ALP activity in rBMSCs cultured on various samples. As shown in Fig. 4e, the ALP activity of TNT@Pt group was approximately 35.7 % higher than that of Ti and TNT groups at day 3. When the culture time is extended to 7 days, the difference in the TNT@Pt group compared to the other two groups increased to 2.3 times. To further evaluate the osteogenesis induction of TNT@Pt in molecular level, RT-PCR analysis was applied and the results are described in Fig. 4f. After 14 days of culture, all the detected genes, including COL-I, RUNX2, OCN, OPN, and ALP, showed higher expression in the TNT@Pt group than the other two groups. Notably, slightly increased expression of some genes (COL-I, OPN, RUNX2, and ALP) was shown in TNT group at day 14, no significantly difference was observed. Both ALP activity and gene expression results verified the strongest osteogenesis capability in vitro of the TNT@Pt implant.

The excellent intracellular ROS eliminate ability and recovery of mitochondrial function results in superior osteogenesis performance in vitro. This superior function drew our interests to deeply investigate its underlying mechanism. RNA sequencing was used to evaluate the whole gene expression profiles of rBMSCs cultured on the Ti and TNT@Pt samples. As shown in Fig. 5a, the Pearson's correlation values demonstrated satisfied clustering between the two analyzed groups. After treated with TNT@Pt, totally 1286 genes were differently expressed compared with Ti group, among them 616 were upregulated while 670 were downregulated (Fig. 5b and c). GO enrichment analysis was performed to deeply understand the functions of involved different expressed genes (DEGs). In terms of cellular components (Fig. 5d), most of DEGs were associated with membrane and cytoplasm, both of which were key elements in cellular metabolism. As to molecular function, protein binding, metal ion binding and ATP binding were significantly highlighted. In terms of biological process, signal transduction, cell differentiation and cell adhesion were found activated during treatment. Based on GO analysis data, it can be speculated that the TNT@Pt film could influence cellular metabolism and energy supply, activating signal transduction via metal ion releasing and finally promote cellular adhesion and differentiation. KEGG enrichment analysis was further used to identify the essential signaling pathways participated in treatment response. The top 10 remarkable differentially expressed signaling pathways were exhibited in Fig. 5e. Focal adhesion and ECM-receptor interaction were the most influenced signaling pathways, both which are closely related to the cell adhesion, migration, and differentiation. This is consistent with the results of GO analysis. On a deeper level, we noted that Calcium, PI3K-Akt, and MAPK signaling pathways were enriched. Calcium ions, as secondary messengers, can regulate various signaling pathways through changes in their intracellular concentrations, including the activation of transcription factors (such as NFAT and CREB), thereby promoting the expression of osteogenic genes [29]. The PI3K-Akt signaling pathway has a direct impact on the mineralization process of the bone matrix, promoting the synthesis of mineralization components [30], while the MAPK signaling pathway enhances the activity and matrix synthesis of osteoblasts by activating transcription factors such as AP-1 [31]. KEGG results were also studied using the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). As shown in Fig. 5f, the normalized enrichment score (NES) of Calcium, PI3K-Akt, and MAPK signaling pathways were all positive regulated. These pathways interact extensively: for instance, Ca2+ influx directly regulates PI3K-AKT activation in osteoblasts under mechanical stretch, where extracellular calcium entry through stretch-activated channels phosphorylates AKT, enhancing cell survival and proliferation—processes critical for regeneration [32]. Calcium signaling also converges with MAPK, as Ca2+-dependent mechanisms activate MAPK to modulate inflammatory responses during bone repair [33]. Furthermore, PI3K-AKT and MAPK facilitate crosstalk in endothelial cells during osteogenesis-angiogenesis coupling, supporting bone formation [34]. In the context of bone regeneration, this interconnected network promotes fracture healing and defect repair by driving osteogenic commitment and maintaining balanced resorption-formation dynamics.

It is important to note that this study observed the inhibitory effect of TNT@Pt on senescence-related signaling pathways (Fig. 5e). Previous studies have demonstrated that the scavenging of ROS and the improvement of mitochondrial function indirectly regulate the process of cellular senescence [35,36]. Therefore, we suggest that this phenomenon is more likely a secondary effect of ATP restoration and mitochondrial function improvement (Fig. 3). However, three unbeneficial signaling pathways including cellular senescence, inflammation related NF-κB signaling pathway and TNF signaling pathway were demonstrated downregulated in TNT@Pt gourp.

Heat maps of typical DEGs involved in Calcium, PI3K-AKT, and MAPK signaling pathways during treatment were demonstrated in Fig. 6a. Among the listed DEGs, some are closely related with osteogenesis such as COL-1, PDGF, and VEGF, which are upregulated in TNT@Pt group, indicating an activated osteogenesis and vascularization procession. The upregulation of Cacna1 and downregulation of pln confirmed the activation of Calcium signaling pathway, because Cacna1 mediates the entry of calcium ions into the cell membrane, whereas pln pumps calcium ions from the cytoplasm into the endoplasmic reticulum, reducing the intracellular calcium concentration. To confirm the results of RNA sequencing, RT-PCR of typical genes were analyzed. In the previous section, we have already proved the enhanced expression of bone-related genes in rBMSCs cultured on TNT@Pt sample (Fig. 4f). Therefore, here we focused on the genes related cell adhesion, migration, angiogenesis, and Calcium pathway. As shown in Fig. 6b, cell adhesion-related gene (Itga ll), cell migration-related gene (TGF), angiogenesis-related gene (VEGF and PDGF) were all upregulated in the TNT@Pt group. Among the three groups, TNT@Pt showed highest expression of Cacnalg gene while lowest pln gene, which are in agreement with heatmap results. In addition, the activation of calcium signaling pathway was confirmed by immunofluorescence staining of intracellular calcium. As exhibited in Fig. 6c and d, the intracellular calcium ion which staining in green was significantly higher than the other groups, and the quantitative analysis of intracellular calcium concentration also confirmed this trend (Fig. 6e).

The synthesis of ATP is usually accompanied by the production of ROS. Conversely, the excessive production of ROS inhibits ATP synthesis. Excessive ROS can lead to oxidative damage of the mitochondrial membrane and increase the permeability of the mitochondrial inner membrane, thereby affecting the function of the electron transport chain (ETC), and finally resulting in reduction of ATP synthesis. In contrast, the clearance of ROS promotes ATP generation. Ohta el al. reported that a high K+ concentration (∼100 mM) would result in the increase of H+ concentration with the matrix, and thus suppressing ROS production, and finally boosting ATP synthesis [37]. ATP, as the energy source for cells, is closely related to various cellular activities. Therefore, by mediating ATP generation in cells through biomaterials, it is possible to regulate cell fate. In a study by Zhang et al., they incorporated succinate into the 3D polymer scaffold. The succinate released from the scaffold can take part in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and subsequently accelerating bone regeneration [38]. In another study, they constructed a succinate loaded exosome to fuel TCA cycle, and thus strengthening energy supply to promote BMSC chondrogenic differentiation via the P2X7-mediated PI3K-AKT pathway [39]. Taking all the above cues into consideration, we can conclude that the TNT@Pt modified Ti as a bioenergetic-active implant can consume the excessive ROS surrounding/within the cells, and thus strengthening energy supply. With sufficient energy supply, the cells show improved adhesion and migration, and meanwhile activating Calcium, PI3K-AKT, and MAPK signaling pathways to enhance bone differentiation.

TNT@Pt shows immunomodulation ability and then mediates osteogenesis

The downregulation of inflammation related pathways such as NF-κB and TNF signaling pathways indicated the TNT@Pt film may have better performance in reducing implant-related inflammation (Fig. 5f), which is highly related macrophage polarization. Therefore, we next focus on the immune response of macrophages in different groups. It is known that inflammatory stimuli, including ROS generated from damaged or necrotic tissue or osteoporosis environment, can induce a shift in macrophage polarization from the M2 to the M1 phenotype [40]. The M1 phenotype is associated with elevated inflammatory cytokine levels (e.g., TNF-α, iNOS, and CCL-3), which promote further recruitment of immune cells [41]. Additionally, M1 macrophages produce high levels of ROS, which reinforce osteoclastogenesis and bone resorption. In contrast, the M2 phenotype of macrophage could secrete cytokine, including IL-10, CD206, Arg-1, to promote tissue regeneration thus accelerate osteogenesis process.

In order to mimic the pathological microenvironment of osteoporosis, bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were cultured on various surfaces and supplied with 100 nM H2O2. Then, we evaluated the inflammation-related protein and gene expression in BMDMs. As shown in Fig. 7a, the specific protein related to M1 phenotype (CD86) is highest expressed in the TNT@Pt group, whereas protein related to M2 phenotype (CD206) is lowest expressed in the TNT@Pt group. Furthermore, the expression of M1-related genes including TNF-α, CCL-3, and iNOS were all significantly downregulated in TNT@Pt group, while the expression of M2-related genes including IL-10, CD206, and Arg-1 were upregulated (Fig. 7b). In addition, immunofluorescence staining of iNOS and Arg-1 also supported this trend (Fig. S12). These results suggested that TNT@Pt could promote the M2 polarization of macrophage, while inhibit its M1 polarization. To further investigate the immune regulation ability of TNT@Pt in vivo, classic mouse air-pouch model and OVX rat femur implantation model were conducted. After 5 days of implantation in the constructed air pouch model, the staining image results illustrated that the thickness of infiltrating immune cells followed the trend: Ti > TNT > TNT@Pt (Fig. 7b). Furthermore, obviously fibrous layer can be seen in the Ti and TNT groups from Masson's trichrome staining results, whereas almost no fibrous layer was found surrounding TNT@Pt implant (Fig. 7c). In the femur implantation model, large and strong positive staining of CD86 were observed on the Ti and TNT groups, especially for the Ti group. In contrast, the tissue surrounding TNT@Pt implant was mainly stained with CD206 protein antibody (Fig. 7d–e). Both the air-pouch and femur implantation models revealed that, compared with the other two groups, the tissue surrounding the TNT@Pt implant can transition from an acute inflammatory state to a pro-repair immune state at the fastest rate, suggesting its best potential in tissue regeneration.

Our previous study have reported that larger TiO2 nanotube (inner diameter: 70–80 nm vs 30–40 nm) on Ti implants showed better immune response for bone regeneration [42]. Specially, TiO2 nanotubes with 30–40 nm inner diameter had few influence on the polarization of macrophages. In this work, we prepared TiO2 nanotube with 50–60 nm inner diameter on Ti surface. On one hand, the design of smaller diameter (<70–80 nm) of TiO2 nanotube is to reflect the immune regulatory effect of Pt nanoparticles after clearing ROS; on the other hand, a slightly larger pore size (>30–40 nm) is more favorable for the reactive solution to enter the pores of TiO2 nanotube and then deposit Pt nanoparticles. At the same time, relatively larger TiO2 nanotube also facilitates the entry of body fluids (containing excessive ROS) into the pores, thereby catalyzing the clearance of ROS. Actually, the present study also demonstrated that TiO2 nanotubes with an inner diameter of 50–60 nm positively influenced the behavior of BMDMs under a rich ROS environment. This effect was evidenced by a reduction in CCL-3 gene expression, along with an increased expression of the CD206 and Arg-1 genes in BMDMs (Fig. 7b). Additionally, fewer infiltrating immune cells were observed in the air-pouch model (Fig. 7c, and reduced CD86 protein expression was detected in the femur implantation model (Fig. 7d–e), when compared to the Ti group. Nevertheless, after deposition of ultrasmall Pt nanoparticle, the TNT@Pt implant showed the best capability to shift polarization of macrophages from M1 to M2 phenotype. This can be ascribed to the ROS scavenging of Pt nanoparticle, and then restore MMP (Fig. S6), enhancing energy supply, and finally reprogramming BMDMs polarization.

To further investigate the impact of macrophage polarization on the osteogenic and vasculogenic behavior. Macrophage-conditioned medium (MCM) was collected and used for incubation of rBMSCs and HUVECs. The corresponding procedure is illustrated in Fig. 8a. As early mark of bone differentiation, ALP activity was analyzed both quantitatively and qualitatively (Fig. 8b and c), and the results showed an osteogenic vitality trend of TNT@Pt > TNT ≈ Ti. In the later stages of osteogenic differentiation of stem cells, the extracellular matrix (ECM) secreted by the cells gradually mineralizes, forming bone-like tissue [43]. Therefore, ECM is commonly used as a marker for the later stages of stem cell osteogenic differentiation. As shown in Fig. 8d and e, the TNT@Pt group exhibited a largest amount of stained calcium nodules, which is consistent with the result of ALP activity test. In addition, tube formation assay suggested that TNT@Pt group is most favorable for HUVECs to form tube structures (Fig. 8f and g), indicating its best angiogenesis. The above results confirm that the TNT@Pt implant can promote a mild microimmune environment conducive to osteogenesis and angiogenesis.

Bone regeneration ability of TNT@Pt implant in OVX model

To investigate the bone regeneration ability of TNT@Pt in osteoporotic microenvironment, an OVX model was constructed. OVX in rats leads to a dramatic decrease in estrogen levels, which in turn affects bone metabolism and bone density, thereby establishing a pathological condition that simulates postmenopausal osteoporosis in humans [44]. The procedure is displayed in Fig. 9a. After implantation for 8 and 12 weeks, the femur were obtained and subjected to micro-CT analysis. As shown in Fig. 9b, more new bone tissue was observed surrounding the TNT@Pt implant. The calculated values of bone mineral density (BMD), trabecular number (Tb. N), bone volume/total volume (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th) were all highest for TNT@Pt group than the other two groups, while exhibited lowest trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) values (Fig. 9c and Fig. S13-14). The BV/TV metric, which represents the proportion of bone tissue within the total volume of the analyzed region, serves as a direct indicator of bone mass or density, with higher values reflecting greater structural integrity. Similarly, Tb.Th measures the average thickness of trabeculae, indicating the robustness of the trabecular bone, while Tb.N quantifies the number of trabeculae per unit length, providing insight into trabecular density and network integrity. Conversely, Tb.Sp, which quantifies the average distance between trabeculae, offers a measure of trabecular connectivity or porosity, with lower values suggesting a denser and more interconnected network. Together, these findings demonstrate the superior bone microarchitecture and density in the TNT@Pt group, highlighting its potential advantages in bone-related applications.

Both the staining results suggested that around the TNT@Pt implant, not only is there a greater amount of newly formed bone, but the newly formed bone also integrates more closely with the implant (Fig. 9d–e and Fig. S15-16). The bone femur with the implants were also subjected for Histological and immune-histological analysis. Furthermore, the results of osteoprotegerin-related immunohistochemical staining (Fig. 10e–h and Fig. S17) revealed the largest positive expression area of RUNX-2, OPN, and OCN in the TNT@Pt group. As expected, all the above results confirmed that the TNT@Pt implant possessed superior osteogenesis and osteointegration ability than the Ti and TNT implants.

Conclusions

Ultrasmall Pt nanoparticles were successfully deposited on the TiO2 nanotube-coated Ti implant. The nano-Pt coated Ti could scavenge excessive ROS content, and then restore the MMP in the cells, increase ATP production. With sufficient energy supply, rBSMCs obtained from OVX model showed enhanced bone differentiation through activating Calcium, PI3K-Akt, MAPK signaling pathways. Furthermore, BMDMs tended to polarize to M2 phenotype, and then the secreted cytokines support the bone differentiation of rBMSCs and angiogenesis of HUVECs. Finally, the femur implantation model in OVX rats demonstrated the boosted osteogenesis and osteointegration of nano-Pt coated Ti implant in vivo. In summary, these results suggest that ROS scavenging bioenergetic-active implants is a promising strategy to reprogram MSCs differentiation and regulate immune microenvironment for osetoporotic bone defect therapy.

Experiment section

Materials

Ti sheets (purity ∼99.285 %, grade TA2) were sourced from SaiTeSiMai Titanium Co., Ltd (Xi'an, China). Hydrofluoric acid (HF, 40 %) was purchased from Lingfeng Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Nitric acid (HNO3, AR), Sodium borohydride (NaBH4, 98 %), Hexachloroplatinic acid (H2PtCl6, 99.995 %) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). RNA extraction and PrimeScript RT reagent kits were obtained from Takara Bio Inc. (Dalian, China). CCK-8 and Alizarin Red (ARS), the hydrogen peroxide detection kit, total SOD assay kit with WST-8, catalase activity assay kit, lipid peroxidation MDA assay kit, and MMP assay kit were bought from Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology (Haimen, China). ATP assay kits were obtained from Solarbio (Beijing, China). The target and reference genes of mRNA were provided by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China).

Sample preparation

The Ti sheets were ultrasonically cleaned in a mixed acid solution (HF: HNO3: H2O = 1: 5: 34) for 5 min, rinsed with ultrapure water, and dried at 60 °C. The prepared Ti samples were experienced a anodic oxidation process by a programmable DC power supply (62024P-600-8, Chroma, Taiwan) in a electrolyte of 1 wt% HF solution at 20 V for 30 min. Thereafter, the samples were heat-treated in a muffle furnace at 450 °C for 2 h with a heating rate of 5 °C/min. The samples were then cooled to room temperature and labeled as TNT. The TNT samples were immersed in 1 % H2PtCl6 solution for 18 h, followed by the addition of NaBH4 solution (0.1 M). The mixture was shaken for 30 min, after which the samples were rinsed with ultrapure water and dried at 60 °C, and the obtained samples were denoted as TNT@Pt.

Characterization of the samples

The surface morphology of the Ti, TNT, and TNT@Pt samples was examined using a field emission scanning electron microscope (SEM, SU8220, Hitachi, Japan). The TNT@Pt sample, after focused ion beam (FIB) thinning, was further characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-F200, JEOL, Japan) for morphology and elemental composition analysis through EDS. The crystal structure and phase composition of the nanocomposite films were analyzed by X-ray diffractometry (XRD, D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany). The surface composition and elemental valence states were analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, PHI5300, Perkin-Elmer, USA). The surface wettability of the samples was assessed using a contact angle instrument (SL200B, Solon, China). Surface zeta potential of samples was determined using a Surpass electrokinetic analyzer (Anton Parr, Austria). The corrosion resistance of the samples was evaluated by potentiodynamic polarization tests and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) via a CHI760C electrochemical analyzer (Shanghai, China) in phosphate buffer saline (PBS) at 37 °C.

ROS scavenging detection

H2O2 detection kit (Beyotime) was used to assess the H2O2 scavenging effect of the samples. In this method, H2O2 reacts with Fe2+, forming Fe3+, which then combines with xylenol orange to produce a compound with a characteristic absorption peak at 560 nm. All samples were incubated with H2O2 at a concentration of 300 μM at 37 °C. The concentration of the remaining H2O2 was measured according to the protocol provided with the detection kit.

The SOD-like catalytic activity of the samples was evaluated using a SOD assay kit (Beyotime) following the provided protocols. Water-soluble tetrazolium salt (WST) working solution and enzyme working solution were added to the samples at a final concentration of 300 μg/mL. The samples were then thoroughly mixed and incubated at 25 °C for 30 min. Following incubation, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. The SOD-like activity of the samples was determined by comparing the slope of the inhibition rate against that of the blank control.

The total antioxidant activity of the coating was assessed using a DPPH assay. Briefly, all substrates were soaked in a 0.5 mM DPPH solution in ethanol for 30 min at 37 °C. After incubation, the absorption of the reaction solutions was measured at 517 nm for colorimetric monitoring.

Preparation of osteoporotic model and culture of OVX BMSCs

All the animal experiment in this study was approved by the Animal Care and Experiment Committee of Guangdong Provincial People's Hospital (KY2023-821-01). An osteoporotic model was induced by OVX in female Sprague-Dawley rats. Briefly, a minimal bilateral lumbar incision was made to expose the enterocoel, and the ovaries were found out and removed after vessel ligation. Postoperative antibiotic treatment was administered. Three months after the ovariectomy, the animals were scan with micro-CT to confirm successful established of osteoporotic model. Then they were sacrificed, and their femora were harvested. Both ends of the femora were cut off, and the bone marrow was flushed out and centrifuged to obtain a pellet of primary OVX rBMSCs. The pellets were suspended, placed in a dish, and incubated. The culture medium was replaced every 2–3 days to remove nonadherent cells, and the cultures were maintained under conditions of 5 % CO2 and 37 °C. Cells from passages 2 to 4 were used for the subsequent experiments.

Evaluation of intracellular oxidative level and MMP

rBMSCs were seeded onto different samples at a density of 2 × 105 cells and cultured for 2 days. Then the cells were stained with 10 μM DCFH-DA for 30 min at 37 °C to evaluate intracellular ROS levels. The stained cells were then analyzed using flow cytometry and observed under a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM).

To measure intracellular ROS scavenging activity, 2 × 105 cells were seeded on various samples for 3 days. The total SOD activity was assessed using a Total SOD Assay Kit, and the CAT activity was determined using a Catalase Activity Assay Kit according to the standardized protocol. In brief, the cells were lysed, and the supernatant was collected after centrifugation. The supernatant (50 μL) was mixed with the test solution (100 μL) for 20 min. The absorbance was then measured with a spectrophotometer at 450 nm (SOD activity test) or 560 nm (CAT activity test).

In addition, the cultured rBMSCs were stained with JC-1 fluorochrome to detect the MMP, which serves as an indicator of cellular oxidative stress levels. The stained cells were then analyzed using flow cytometry and observed under CLSM.

Intracellular ATP production

The intracellular ATP level was measured using an ATP Assay Kit (Beyotime) following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, 3 × 105 cells were seeded onto different substrates and cultured for 3 days. Afterward, 0.1 mL of RIPA lysis buffer was added to each well. The supernatant was collected after centrifugation of the lysate and analyzed using the ATP Assay Kit. The ATP concentration in each sample was calculated based on a standard curve and normalized to the total protein content, measured with a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Biocompatibility investigation

rBMSCs were seeded onto different samples at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well. Live/dead staining were conducted to assess cell viability after 3 days of culture. Following the manufacturer's instructions, the working solution was prepared and added to each well (200 μL/well). After a 30-min incubation, living cells were stained with Calcein AM, which fluoresces green, while dead cells were labeled with propidium iodide (PI), which fluoresces red. Cell proliferation was evaluated using the CCK-8. On certain time, 200 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well containing DMEM and incubated for 1 h. The absorbance was then measured at 480 nm using an ELX Ultra microplate reader (BioTek, USA).

rBMSCs were seeded onto different samples (2 × 104 cells/well) for 1 day. rBMSCs were then fixed with 4 % PFA and stained with rhodamine phalloidin (TRITC Phalloidin). The stained rBMSCs were observed under a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM). The spreading area was figured using Image J software.

In vitro bone differentiation of rBMSCs cultured on samples directly

rBMSCs (3 × 104) were cultured on various samples for 3 and 7 days. Intracellular ALP activity was quantitatively evaluated using the ALP Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) and normalized to the total intracellular protein content measured by the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

rBMSCs were seeded onto different samples at a density of 2 × 104 cells and cultured for 3, 7, and 14 days. RNA was extracted from the cells using the E.Z.N.A.® Total RNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, USA). The extracted RNA was then reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the TransScript® First-Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix Kit (TransGen Biotech, China). The cDNA was analyzed using the Bio-Rad CFX 96 Real-Time PCR System with SYBR Green Real-Time PCR Master Mix (TransGen Biotech, China). The expression of osteogenesis-related genes, including COL-1, OPN, RUNX2, OCN, and ALP, was evaluated. The used sequences are listed in Table S1.

RNA sequence analysis

rBMSCs were seeded onto the Ti and TNT@Pt surfaces for 3 days. Then, the total RNA was isolated by TRIzol (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The qualities of the RNA samples were assessed, and those with a 28S/18S RNA ratio ≥0.7 were acquired. For sequence and primary analysis, Cutadapt software was employed to remove the reads that contained adapter contamination, and then HISAT2 software was used to map the reads to the genome. Then, all transcriptomes from each sample were merged to reconstruct a comprehensive transcriptome. The differentially expressed mRNAs were selected with a fold change >2 or fold change <0.5 and P < 0.05. GO enrichment and KEGG enrichment analyses of the differentially expressed mRNAs were carried out.

Pathway validation

rBMSCs were seeded onto different samples (1 × 105) for 3 days. RNA was extracted from the cells using the E.Z.N.A.® Total RNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, USA). The expression of target genes, including VEGF, ITGA, PDGF, TGF, CANCA1, and PLN, was evaluated using RT-PCR. The sequences for these genes are listed in Table S1.

rBMSCs (3 × 104 cells/well) were on various samples for 3 day. After that, the cells were stained with the Fluo-4 calcium ion detection kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) to assess intracellular calcium ions, and observed using an optical microscope (Olympus, Japan) and the relative fluorescence intensity was evaluated using Image J software. The intracellular calcium ion concentration was quantitatively measured using the calcium content colorimetric detection kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China).

BMDMs isolation and MCM preparation

After anesthetizing adult SD rats, the femur and tibia are isolated, muscle tissue is carefully removed, and the bones are rinsed with sterile PBS. Under aseptic conditions, PBS is used to flush the bone marrow cavity, collecting the bone marrow cell suspension. Red blood cells are lysed using a red blood cell lysis buffer, followed by centrifugation to collect the bone marrow mononuclear cells. The cells are then seeded in complete medium containing 10 % FBS and supplemented with M-CSF (20 ng/ml) to promote macrophage differentiation. Cells are incubated at 37 °C in a 5 % CO2 atmosphere for 5–7 days to allow mononuclear cells to differentiate into mature BMDMs, which are then suitable for further experimentation.

BMDMs were seeded onto different samples at a density of 5 × 105 cells and cultured for 3 days in RPMI 1640 medium with 10 % FBS and 20 ng/ml M-CSF. Afterward, the cells were cultured for 48 h in DMEM with 1 % FBS to collect the conditioned medium, which was then mixed 1:1 with osteogenic induction medium to obtain MCM.

BMDMs polarization in vitro

BMDMs (5 × 104 cells/well) were seeded onto various samples, and the cultured was supplied with 100 nM H2O2. After 3 days, the RNA was extracted from the cells. The expression of M1-related genes (iNOS, TNF-α, and CCL3) and M2-related genes (IL-10, Arg-1, and CD206) were evaluated. The used sequences are listed in Table S2.

Mouse air-pouch model

Nine male Balb/c mice (6 weeks old) were anesthetized, and the hair on the backs was shaved. Subsequently, 3 mL of sterilized air was injected subcutaneously into the back of each mouse, followed by an additional 2 mL of sterilized air 4 days later to form stable air pouches. Three days after the second injection, the samples were implanted into the air pouches. Four days post-implantation, the tissues covering the implants were collected, fixed, embedded, sliced, and stained with H&E and Masson's trichrome.

MCM mediated osteogenesis and angiogenesis behaviors

rBMSCs (3 × 104) were cultured in the MCM for 3 and 7 days. After the culture period, the BCIP/NBT ALP Color Development Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) was used to stain ALP in the cells, which were then observed under an optical microscope (Olympus, Japan). Intracellular ALP activity was quantitatively evaluated using the ALP Assay Kit as mentioned before.

rBMSCs (3 × 104) were cultured in the MCM for 14 and 21 days. Afterward, the cells were stained with alizarin red (AR; 40 mM, Leagene Biotechnology) and observed using an optical microscope. The bound dye was quantitatively analyzed by dissolving it in 10 % cetylpyridinium chloride (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and the absorbance of the solution was measured at 620 nm.

Culture plates and tips were precooled at −20 °C, and then the plates were coated with Matrigel (150 μL/well). After incubated for 30 min at 37 °C, HUVECs were suspended and seeded onto the preplated Matrigel (2 × 105 cells/well). After the cells were attached, the MCM were added, and a microscopy was used to observe tube formation after 4 h. The length of tube was analyzed with Image J software.

OVX bone implantation model

An osteoporotic model was first induced in female Sprague-Dawley rats through ovariectomy for 6 weeks. To detect early in vivo immune response, materials were implanted into the femoral condyles for 5 days. Afterward, the femoral condyles were decalcified, and the implants were removed. The femoral condyles were then fixed, embedded, sliced, and stained with antibodies for CD86 and CD206. Implants were inserted into the femoral condyles of the subjects (n = 6 per group) and the rats were fed for 8 and 12 weeks. The collected femoral condyles were scanned using a Micro-CT analyzer (Inveon™ Multi-Modality; Siemens Medical Solutions, Germany). The BV/TV, Tb.N, BMD, Tb.Sp were calculated using VG Studio MAX software, while three-dimensional (3D) images were reconstructed using Multimodal 3D Visualization (Siemens Medical Solutions, Germany) software. Furthermore, the femoral condyles were decalcification and stained with H&E and Masson's trichrome. In addition, the femoral condyles were decalcification and stained with OCN, OPN, RUNX2, CD31, and VEGF. For biosafety examination, organs including heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney were harvested and then stained with H&E.

Statistical analysis

The data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Student's t-test and one-way ANOVA were used to test the statistical significance using the software package Origin Pro (version 9.0). Significant differences: p < 0.05 (∗), p < 0.01 (∗∗), and p < 0.001 (∗∗∗).
