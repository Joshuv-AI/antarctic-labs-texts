Comparative benefits of Ki and SUV images in lesion detection during PET/CT imaging
Authors: Danjie Cai, Yibo He, Haojun Yu, Yiqiu Zhang, Hongcheng Shi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinical application of the tracer net influx rate (Ki) imaging in PET/CT remains limited, due to a lack of evidence demonstrating the superiority of Ki images in lesion detection, and guidelines on when to utilize Ki images. This study aims to compare the benefits of Ki and standardized uptake value (SUV) images in lesion detection during PET/CT imaging. By analyzing the performance of both techniques in identifying tumor lesions, the study seeks to provide guidance for the clinical application of Ki images.ResultsThis retrospective study included 134 patients with 244 pathologically confirmed lesions (200 malignant and 44 benign). Patients with a histopathological diagnosis received a weight-based 18F-FDG injection and underwent 60-min total-body PET/CT dynamic imaging. SUV images were reconstructed using data collected from the last 10 min of the scans. Ki images were generated using the Patlak methods with data from minutes 12–60. The background SUVmax, SUVmean, SUVSD, Kimax, Kimean, and KiSD values were recorded. The signal-to-noise ratios of the SUV (SUVSNR) and Ki (KiSNR) images were calculated. The lesion detection rate and sensitivity of the SUV and Ki images were evaluated. The lesion-detection rates were 97.7% (214/219) and 99.5% (218/219) for the SUV and Ki images, respectively (p = .22). Five false-negative lesions on the SUV images were true-positive on the Ki images (3 hepatic malignancies and 2 metastatic lymph nodes). The sensitivity (94.0% vs. 96.0%, p = .22), specificity (41.9% vs. 41.9%, p > .99), accuracy (84.4% vs. 86.1%, p = .61), positive predictive value (87.9% vs. 88.1%, p = .94), negative predictive value (60.0% vs. 69.2%, p = .47), and the area under the curve [0.68 (95% confidence interval, 0.61–0.73) vs. 0.69 (95% confidence interval, 0.62–0.74)] were similar in the SUV and Ki images (all p ≥ .10).ConclusionKi images exhibit benefits in lesion detection compared to SUV images, particularly in organs with high background such as liver. The enhanced contrast provided by Ki imaging is recommended to clinically improve detection rates in such cases.Keywords: PET/CT, Dynamic imaging, Lesion detection rate, Ki image

Introduction

PET imaging, using 2-deoxy-2-[18F] fluoro-D-glucose (18F-FDG), is widely used in clinical oncology for diagnosis, staging, restaging, and assessment of treatment efficacy [1–3]. Standardized uptake value (SUV) images rely on semi-quantitative analysis. Nuclear medicine physicians scrutinize these static PET images, discerning normal and abnormal patterns to diagnose tumors, inflammation, and various diseases [4, 5].

SUV images could not deliver optimal diagnostic performance in every case. This limitation of SUV images is evident in visual evaluation method. Distinguishing lesions from the surrounding normal tissue based on visual SUV differences is essential. However, in specific scenarios, the difference in SUV values can be minimal, leading to false-negative results. For example, the liver which normally has high background, only approximately 50-70% of hepatocellular carcinoma show higher glucose metabolism, hence the PET/CT SUV images play a subordinate role in primary diagnosis [6, 7]. Furthermore, in organs with low background, such as lung cancer, SUV image staging also presents approximately 20% false negative results [8, 9].

Static uptake metrics, such as SUV, can serve as surrogates for kinetic parametric but do not directly estimate specific biological processes. This is because these SUV values represent an aggregate of many kinetic processes. Notably, SUV cannot account for nonspecific radiotracer uptake, directly impacting their evaluation of tumors with low baseline uptake. The advantage of dynamic imaging lies in its ability to estimation of biologic processes relevant to disease. Through mathematic analysis of the interactions of a radiotracer with tissue, information can be gleaned from PET imaging beyond static uptake measures [10]. The Patlak graphical analysis is derived from the irreversible two-compartment model, which is widely used in PET kinetic models [11]. This analysis provides two parameters: an estimate of tracer net influx rate (Ki, ml/min/g), which provides a measure of 18F-FDG metabolism, and the intercept, which approximates the distribution volume of all compartments in the reversible compartment model. The significance of Ki images lies in their ability to visualize cellular metabolism and 18F-FDG uptake, overcoming the limitations of SUV images in accounting for nonspecific radiotracer uptake. This aims to improve lesion detection rates.

So far, the comparison of lesion detection rates between dynamic parameter imaging and SUV images has been the subject of only a few studies [12–14]. Fahrni et al. [12] analyzed 19 tumor types (40 proven malignant and 78 unproven malignant) and concluded that Ki images could achieve equivalent and superior (one additional malignant lesions were identified) lesion detection ability to that of SUV images. Dias et al. [13] analyzed 109 patients and 310 lesions and found that no additional malignant lesions were identified in MRFDG (metabolic rate of FDG, MRFDG = Ki × blood glucose) images. These studies were based on conventional PET/CT. Total-body PET scanner increases the axial coverage, allowing for whole-body imaging while simultaneously imaging aorta. This enables the measurement of image-derived input functions without significant partial volume effects. The increased sensitivity also allows for relatively noise-free time-activity curves using short time bins in the early phases of imaging. In a recently published case report using total-body PET/CT, Fu et al. [14] observed that the Ki images exhibited higher contrast in one distant lymph node metastasis of a lung cancer patient compared to the SUV image, thereby enhancing diagnostic confidence. In short, whether the introduction of Ki imaging adds clinical information beyond standard static FDG PET/CT remains controversial. This is especially crucial with the introduction of total-body PET/CT into clinical practice, as replacing simple clinical methods with more laborious procedures should benefit patients.

Thus, this retrospective study aims to investigate the potential benefit of Ki images over SUV images in lesion detection and provide guidance for the clinical when to utilize Ki images.

Materials and methods

Patients

This study retrospectively analyzed the dynamic imaging data and clinical information of 134 patients with a heterogenous group of tumor types from December 2019 to February 2022 (Fig. 1). The Ethics Review Committee of (Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University) approved this study [approval number: B2021-329(3)]. Informed consent was waived by the committee due to the retrospective nature of the study. All patients were instructed to refrain from strenuous exercise for at least 24 h and fast for at least 6 h before a weight-based (ultra-low dose: 0.37 MBq/kg; half dose: 1.85 MBq/kg; full dose: 3.7 MBq/kg) injection of 18F-FDG. Blood glucose level before FDG administration was measured to guarantee lower than 11 mmol/L.

Patient selection flowchart. 18 F-FDG, 2-[18 F]fluoro-2-deoxy-D-glucose

Inclusion criteria: (1) tumors confirmed by biopsy or surgical pathology; (2) image quality deemed acceptable for analysis. Exclusion criteria: (1) incomplete data or poor image quality; (2) undefined pathology results; (3) inability to obtain background values due to diffuse liver lesions.

PET/CT examination

The procedure followed the European Association of Nuclear Medicine 18F-FDG tumor imaging guidelines [15]. The patients received a weight-based 18F-FDG injection (full-dose, 3.7 MBq/kg, half-dose 1.85 MBq/kg, and one-tenth-dose 0.37 MBq/kg) [16, 17]. The patients were supine on the table with arms at the body’s sides. A low-dose CT scan (120 kV, 9 mAs; pitch, 1.0125; 3-mm slice thickness, and 3-mm slice increment) was performed before injection for attenuation correction to reduce the patient’s radiation dose. A 60-minute total-body dynamic PET scan (uEXPLORER, United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China) was initiated immediately after injection, and raw PET data in 3D list-mode were acquired. Diagnostic CT imaging was performed after PET scanning (Fig. 2).

Image collection and reconstruction flow chart. ACCT, Attenuation correction CT

The acquisition parameters of diagnostic CT: tube current modulation; voltage, 120 kV; pitch, 0.9625; 1-mm slice thickness; and 1-mm slice increment. No contrast agent was applied. All subsequent image analysis was performed on a commercial medical image-processing workstation (uWS-MI, United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China).

Image reconstruction

SUV images were reconstructed using data collected from the last 10 min (i.e., minutes 50–60) [16–18]. The Ordered Subsets Expectation Maximization algorithm reconstructed the PET images with the following specific parameters: time of flight and point spread function, three iterations, and 20 subsets; matrix, 192 × 192; slice thickness, 1.443 mm; field of view, 600 mm (voxel, 3.125 × 3.125 × 1.443 mm3); 3-mm Gaussian filtering. All necessary corrections, such as those for attenuation and scattering, were enacted.

The 60-minute dynamic data were reconstructed into 55 frame static images (36 × 5s, 19 × 180s). A 3.125 × 3.125 × 2.886 mm3 voxel was used in static image reconstruction. Other reconstruction parameters were the same as in the SUV image reconstruction method. The Ki images were generated using the Patlak linear graphical analysis based on irreversible two-compartment kinetic model on a workstation with specialized software (UWS-MI R001, United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China). The image-derived input function was automatically generated by delineating a cylindrical volume of interest with a diameter of 4 mm in the descending aorta, with manual adjustments made as necessary. Ki-parameter maps were derived from the Patlak graphical analysis of the dynamic PET frames acquired 12–60 min (frames 39–55) after 18F-FDG injection as in previous studies [19, 20]. This was done to increase the number of data points in the Patlak plot to obtain more reproducible Ki values.

Quantitative analysis of PET image

Two 20-mm diameter VOIs, one at the portal vein level of the right hepatic lobe and the other on the right gluteus maximus, were drawn to record the background signal of the liver and muscle, respectively. Another 20-mm diameter VOI was placed on normal tissue near the lesion (liver background was used for hepatic malignancies, and abdomen adipose tissue was used for lymph node metastasis). The VOI outlining for each image group was based on the image alignment linkage tool that comes with the workstation software and was then visually evaluated and manually adjusted as needed to ensure the accuracy and consistency of the outlining. The background SUVmax, SUVmean, SUVSD, Kimax, Kimean, and KiSD values were recorded. The liver signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) in the SUV images (SUVSNR) was the SUVmean to SUVSD ratio, and that in the Ki images (KiSNR) was the Kimean to KiSD ratio.

Lesion analysis

The SUV and Ki images of all suspicious malignant lesions were visually inspected by two nuclear medicine physicians with over three and 13 years of experience in nuclear oncology (Y.B.H. and Y.Q.Z.), respectively. Pathological results were the gold standard for diagnosing benign and malignant lesions. Image-positive lesions were defined as any 18F-FDG uptake incompatible with a physiological normal uptake. Lesions that did not show abnormal 18F-FDG uptake or uptake levels close to the background tissue were considered image-negative lesions. Detectable lesions were defined as image-positive lesions on either Ki or SUV images. All detectable lesions were compared to the patient’s pathology results, and the numbers of true-positive, false-positive, true-negative, and false-negative lesions were determined. The Ki or SUV lesion-detection rate was the ratio of image-positive lesions in the Ki or SUV images to the number of detectable lesions.

For SUV images, the ratio of tumor to background (liver/muscle/normal tissue) is calculated by dividing the lesion SUVmax by the background SUVmax, and is abbreviated as TLR, TMR, and TNR respectively. The TLR, TMR, and TNR of Ki images were calculated using the same formula.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp.), and MedCalc Statistical Software, Version 20 (MedCalc Software). The Shapiro-Wilk test assessed the normality of data distribution. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies (percentages), and continuous variables are expressed as means ± SDs or medians (IQRs). The SNR and TLR between SUV and Ki images were compared using the paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Correlation analysis based on lesions’ SUVmax and Kimax was performed using Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation test. For lesion-detection performance, the sensitivity and specificity of the two modalities were compared using McNemar’s test, and their accuracy, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and lesion-detection rate were assessed using the Chi-squared test. The areas under the receiver-operating characteristic curves (AUCs) were compared using the Delong test. All p values were two-tailed and considered statistically significant when < 0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics

Of the 161 patients who underwent 18F-FDG PET/CT dynamic imaging, 134 (78 males and 56 females, age 58 ± 10 years) with definitive histopathological findings were included in this study. Among the 134 patients, 129 were presenting for initial staging, 2 for restaging, and 3 for therapy response. Three patients had two different malignancies: one with lung adenocarcinoma accompanied by gastric neuroendocrine carcinoma, one with ascending colon adenocarcinoma accompanied by hepatocellular carcinoma, and one with intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma accompanied by chronic lymphocytic leukemia. There were two cases of double primary lesions, one with colorectal adenocarcinoma and the other with lung adenocarcinoma. Pathology samples were collected by surgical resection from 94 patients (201 lesions) and by biopsy from 40 patients (43 lesions). The primary tumor locations included the liver (n = 83), colorectum (n = 21), lung (n = 18), stomach (n = 4), breast (n = 4), esophagus (n = 2), uterus (n = 1), and small intestine (n = 1). A total of 244 lesions were included (200 malignant and 44 benign), with lymph node involvement observed in 21 cases (37 malignant lymph nodes), and distant metastasis present in 9 cases (16 liver metastases). Comparison of background and lesion uptake between the SUV and Ki images

The liver SNRs in the SUV images were higher than in the Ki images [14.59 (12.01–18.61) vs. 2.74 (1.95–3.78); p < .001]) and so did in muscle SNR [8.69 (7.50–11.00) vs. 3.13 (2.22–4.36); p < .001]. The normal tissue SNR in the SUV images was also significantly higher than that in the Ki images [9.85 (6.11–13.99) vs. 2.09 (1.11–3.63); p < .001]. In contrast, compared to the Ki images’ uptake of malignant lesions (n = 200), the SUV images had considerably lower TLR [2.96 (IQR, 1.81–4.34) vs. 5.93 (IQR, 2.69–9.65)], TMR [11.03 (IQR, 7.24–17.35) vs. 14.45 (IQR, 7.83–23.63)], and TNR [3.80 (IQR, 2.26–7.46) vs. 6.81 (IQR, 3.82–13.47); all p < .001] (Fig. 3).

Correlation between the SUV and Ki images

The results revealed a substantial positive correlation between SUVmax and Kimax for all lesions (n = 244; r = .96, p < .001) and a strong correlation in all malignant lesion subgroups (all r ≥ .88, p < .001; Table 2; Fig. 4). The strongest correlation among malignant tumors was in pulmonary malignant lesions (r = .99, p < .001). Furthermore, a significant correlation (r = .79; 95% confidence interval, 0.65–0.88; p < .001) was found between the SUVmax and Kimax of benign lesions (n = 44; Table 2).

Lesion-detection performance of the SUV and Ki images

This analysis included all 244 pathologically confirmed lesions (134 patients), 200 malignant and 44 benign. Among them, 219 were detectable lesions. Of the 244 lesions, 214 image-positive and 30 image-negative lesions were found in the SUV images, 188 true-positive lesions (sensitivity, 94.0%; in the Ki images: 187 true-positive and one false-negative lesion), 12 false-negative lesions (seven false-negative and five true-positive lesions in the Ki images), 18 true-negative lesions (same for the Ki images), and 26 false-positive lesions (same for the Ki images; mainly from inflamed lymph nodes; Table 3). We detected 218 image-positive lesions in the Ki images, 192 of which were true-positive (sensitivity, 96.0%; Fig. 5). The number of false-negative findings in the Ki images (n = 8) was smaller than in the SUV images (n = 12). Notably, one liver metastatic lesion of colorectal cancer was detected on SUV images but missed on Ki images, while five lesions (two cholangiocarcinoma, one hepatocellular carcinoma, and two metastatic lymph nodes) were detected on Ki images but missed on SUV images (Figs. 6 and 7). The SUV and Ki images showed similar sensitivity (94.0% vs. 96.0%, p = .22), specificity (40.9% vs. 40.9%, p > .99), accuracy (84.4% vs. 86.1%, p = .61), positive predictive value (87.9% vs. 88.1%, p = .94), negative predictive value (60.0% vs. 69.2%, p = .47), and AUC [0.68 (95% confidence interval, 0.61–0.73) vs. 0.69 (95% confidence interval, 0.62–0.74); all p ≥ .10; Fig. 8). The SUV and Ki images also showed similar lesion-detection rates [97.7% (214/219) vs. 99.5% (218/219), p = .22].

However, significant differences were found between TMRSUV and TMRKi (AUC, 0.90 vs. 0.87, p = .03), and between TNRSUV and TNRKi (AUC, 0.61 vs. 0.70, p = .002). For both image types, TNR showed significantly lower diagnostic performance than the other three indicators (all p < .001).

Discussion

This study found that Ki images significantly improved the TBR of lesions than SUV images. Ki images have some unique advantages in certain situations, such as lesions in tissues with high background signal like liver, where they can supplement SUV images.

This study analyzed 244 lesions (200 malignant and 44 benign), finding five false-negative lesions on the SUV images that were true-positive on the Ki images. The Ki images showed superior lesion-detection performance in these five cases; however, Ki and SUV images showed similar detection performance when assessing the entire study population. Of these five lesions, three were diagnosed as hepatic malignancies and two as metastatic lymph nodes. The 18F-FDG uptake of these lesions in the SUV images was similar to the liver background; due to the strong liver background signal, the lesions could not be clearly displayed. The Ki value represents the net FDG influx rate of the liver, which was lower than the liver background SUV value. Therefore, the Ki images had a higher TLR than the SUV ones. This finding has certain implications for clinical practice. For example, in patients with high suspicion of hepatocellular carcinoma, when performing 18F-FDG PET imaging, consideration can be given to incorporating Ki imaging analysis to potentially achieve improved diagnostic efficacy. One patient was diagnosed with invasive lung adenocarcinoma. The lesion was not clearly identified on SUV images (but was not image-negative) due to its low glucose metabolism but was clearly displayed on Ki images.

Fahrni et al. [12] analyzed 118 lesions and concluded that Ki images could achieve equivalent and superior (one additional malignant lesions were identified) lesion detection ability to that of SUV images, which consistent with our results (5 additional malignant lesions were identified). Dias et al. [13] analyzed 109 patients and 310 lesions and found that no additional malignant lesions were identified. However, both studies, despite their differences, arrived at the same conclusion as our research: Ki images show better TBR values in lesion visualization than SUV images. The improved lesion contrast does not directly lead to the detection of additional true-positive lesions in Ki image, which can explain why the two previous studies, despite having similar experimental results, made different recommendations on the clinical use of Ki. Compared to the previous two studies, this study additionally calculated the SNR of the normal tissue background in Ki images, which represented image quality. This study found that the SNR of Ki images were lower than those of SUV images. This may be due to the use of total-body PET/CT in this study, which significantly improved the quality of SUV images.

The patients included in this study were primarily diagnosed with liver malignancies. Applying dynamic analysis to the liver necessitates a careful selection of blood supply and kinetic models [21]. Choosing the appropriate input function is vital for dynamic parameter imaging [9]. Previous studies used a single input function — the hepatic artery for liver dynamic modeling [22, 23]. Given the physiological characteristics of the liver, integrating a dual input function of both the hepatic artery and portal vein is thought to yield more accurate results [24, 25]. Notably, studies comparing various blood supply and compartment models found no difference in the calculated Ki values of hepatocellular carcinoma lesions between single and dual input function models [26], suggesting that using a single input function in clinical practice could streamline the complex calculation process and circumvent potential deviations in the portal vein input function due to partial volume effects and respiratory movements.

We found a strong correlation between tumor SUVmax and Kimax values. Further investigation revealed that utilizing TNR values to differentiate between benign and malignant tumors in Ki images yielded significantly higher diagnostic efficiency than in SUV images. This could be attributed to the outstanding contrast in the Ki images. Nevertheless, it is essential to acknowledge that due to insufficiently large SNR values stemming from inferior image quality, Ki images alone cannot meet clinical diagnostic requirements. As demonstrated in the patient shown in Fig. 7, high noise in the Ki image might mask the lesion, leading to false-negative results in certain situations. Consequently, SUV images maintain their indispensable attributes.

Dynamic acquisition and parameter imaging have expanded the clinical application of PET/CT [27]. The utilization of total-body PET/CT system provides a wide field-of-view, enabling the inclusion of patients with distant metastases without the need for multi-bed acquisitions. This standardized acquisition protocol ensures consistency across all patients, facilitating image analysis and enhancing the reliability of the study results.

This study had some limitations. This study is based on a single-center retrospective analysis, which may introduce some bias in the selection of patient types. Considering the convenience of clinical use, this study did not use dual blood supply and compartment models to reconstruct the Ki images. However, as discussed above, this has a limited impact on their clinical use. The slice thickness of the reconstructed Ki images was twice that of the original SUV images (2.886 vs. 1.443 mm), which theoretically could lead to a decreased detection rate and poor image quality for the Ki images. More than half of the patients had liver lesions, potentially biasing the results. This study demonstrated a potential advantage of Ki images over SUV images in lesion detectability. Future research could explore the impact of different reconstruction parameters on Ki image quality, aiming to enhance their diagnostic value. Additionally, incorporating machine learning algorithms for multi-level analysis of Ki images could further optimize their application in clinical practice.

Conclusion

Ki images could supplement the SUV images, particularly for lesions in organs with a strong background signal. We recommend Ki images as a supplement in routine PET imaging of liver cancer patients to improve their lesions detection rate and staging value. Future research directions include improving the image quality of Ki image and incorporating machine learning analysis.
