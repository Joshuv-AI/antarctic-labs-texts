Differential cognitive enhancer effects of acetylcholinesterase inhibitors and memantine on age-related deficits in vigilance and sustained attention: a preclinical validation study
Authors: Zsolt Kristóf Bali, Nóra Bruszt, Kitti Göntér, István Hernádi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe psychomotor vigilance task (PVT) is a cognitive test commonly used to measure sustained attention and vigilance in humans in healthy and diseased states. Here, we aimed to utilize a recently designed rat version of the PVT to assess potential cognitive enhancer effects of various pharmacological compounds in a natural model of age-related cognitive decline. Therefore, we treated aged rats (> 28 months old) with different doses of three approved Alzheimer’s disease drugs: donepezil, galantamine, and memantine.ResultsAged rats made significantly slower responses to the cue stimuli compared to young animals and fewer correct responses, mainly because of an increased number of missed trials (i.e., when the trial was not initiated by the rat). Donepezil improved the performance of aged rats by accelerating their responses at a dose of 0.03 mg/kg. However, galantamine treatment showed no beneficial effects on either reaction time or the number of correct trials. Furthermore, both donepezil (0.3 and 1.0 mg/kg) and galantamine (3.0 mg/kg) increased the reaction time and number of missed trials at high doses. Memantine did not affect the reaction time of aged rats, but it increased the number of correct responses at 0.1 and 0.3 mg/kg doses.ConclusionsHere, we showed that PVT is suitable for addressing pharmacological effects on various cognitive domains in a single behavioral paradigm. Our findings also indicate that different cognitive enhancer compounds (even when their targets are thought to be the same) may differentially influence distinct cognitive domains and modulate task performance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12993-025-00302-0.Keywords: Psychomotor vigilance task, Donepezil, Galantamine, Memantine, Rat, Behaviour, Aging, Reaction time, Cognitive enhancer, Translation

Background

The development of novel medications for neurocognitive disorders, such as Alzheimer's disease (AD), is a central aim of current drug discovery research. However, in human clinical trials, the vast majority of compounds did not provide the expected effectiveness based on preclinical studies in in vivo animal models. One possible explanation for this discrepancy may arise from the fact that the currently used animal models and behavioral tasks are not directly translatable to human cognition, especially in neuropathological states. Therefore, there is a growing need for better translatable behavioral tasks that can be similarly used and interpreted in rodents, non-human primates, and humans, and may allow the assessment of the same (or very similar) cognitive domains and the underlying brain functions in different species. Furthermore, the most frequently applied rodent behavioral tasks (e.g., water maze, novel object recognition, passive avoidance) mainly focus on the measurement of different kinds of explicit memory, while other cognitive domains, such as sustained attention and vigilance, which are of similar importance in the cognitive and behavioral deficits of patients with neurocognitive disorders, are much less addressed in preclinical animal studies [1] (to review different preclinical paradigms and the challenges of their translation, refer to [2–4]). Here, we assessed the cognitive deficits in aged rats and the effects of several approved AD medications in a rat version of the psychomotor vigilance task (PVT).

The PVT is a classical tool for examining sustained attention, alertness, and vigilance in humans [5, 6]. The PVT is a very simple task that is based on measuring the speed of responses to randomly appearing stimuli. The PVT is mostly used for the diagnosis of sleep disorders and in the research of pharmacological compounds that treat sleep disorders [7–9]. As attention and vigilance are basic cognitive functions that are affected in neurocognitive disorders already in the early phases of cognitive decline [10], it is reasonable to suggest that the PVT may serve as a simple and useful tool for investigating age-related neurodegenerative diseases not only in humans but also in preclinical animal models of aging. Furthermore, we hypothesize that the PVT may also be used to test the procognitive effects of novel drug candidates that are under development for treating neurocognitive disorders. Because of its simplicity, PVT can be conducted in virtually the same manner in humans, non-human primates, and rats; therefore, it may provide a high translational potential between different species. Recently, we established a rat version of the PVT in our laboratory [11, 12]. In the present study, our main aim was to validate the general suitability of the PVT for testing novel drug candidates by investigating the effects of the following approved AD medications on various task parameters measured in the PVT.

Donepezil and galantamine are potent acetylcholinesterase inhibitor (AChEI) compounds commonly prescribed for Alzheimer's disease [13]. They mainly act by increasing the amount of available ACh in neuronal synapses by blocking the degradation of ACh by AChE. In addition, galantamine acts as a positive allosteric modulator (PAM) of the alpha7 nicotinic ACh receptor (nAChR), which also plays an important role in cognitive processes [14]. Third, memantine, a medication prescribed to treat neurocognitive disorders, targets substantially different neurotransmitter systems than the cholinergic system. According to the dominant view of the mechanism of action of memantine, it is an antagonist of NMDA-type glutamate receptors and improves normal neuronal processing by decreasing the activity of extrasynaptic NMDA receptors [15]. However, memantine has also been shown to exert affinity for several other receptors, including the alpha7 nAChR, which may also be involved in the beneficial effects of memantine on cognitive performance [16]. In the present study, we aimed to investigate and compare the procognitive effects of donepezil, galantamine, and memantine on the performance of aged rats in the PVT.

Materials and methods

Animals

Altogether 22 male Long Evans rats (Janvier Labs, Le Genest-Saint-Isle, France) were used in this study out of which 12 were 28 months old (aged rats) and 10 were 6 months old (young adult rats) at the beginning of the experiments. During the study, animals were pair-housed under a 12/12 h daily light/dark cycle with controlled temperature and humidity in the animal house of the Szentágothai Research Centre, University of Pécs, Hungary. In the animal house, the lights were ON from 7 a.m. to 7 p.m., and the animals were tested during the light period. Rats were fed daily with a controlled amount of food (17 g/animal laboratory chow) to prevent the development of obesity and other related health problems. Water was provided ad libitum. The experiments were approved by the Animal Welfare Committee of the University of Pécs and the National Scientific Ethical Committee on Animal Experimentation (ÁTET) at the Ministry of Agriculture (license no. BA02/2000-30/2021). All procedures fully complied with the Decree No. 40/2013. (II. 14.) of the Hungarian Government and EU Directive 2010/63/EU on the Protection of Animals Used for Scientific Purposes.

Psychomotor vigilance task

Our in-house developed rat psychomotor vigilance task (PVT) was conducted in the same manner as in our recent study, where the training procedure was also described in detail [12]. As a basis for the rat PVT, we used a standardized operant conditioning apparatus for rats (Habitest System, Coulbourn Instruments, Holliston, MA, USA) equipped with stimulus generators (feeder light, color LED lights), response sensors (levers, photocell), and reward pellet delivery modules (feeder with delivery trough). The rats were gradually trained in a 10-step procedure to perform the final version of the PVT with the longest possible fixation time (5000 ms) in the task (see later). Aged animals needed a maximum of 67 sessions (range: 50–67), while young animals needed a maximum of 63 sessions (range: 48–63) to master the PVT.

Every trial of a PVT session was run as follows: rats had to respond to the onset of the light in the feeder module by nose-poking into the pellet delivery trough of the feeder module. They were then required to hold their nose in the delivery trough until the feeder light was turned off (fixation time). The fixation time (foreperiod) varied randomly between 0 and 5000 ms. At the end of the fixation time, the feeder light was turned off, and the color LED lights were illuminated above the two levers located to the left and right of the central feeder module. These stimuli served as cues for the rats to pull their nose out of the pellet delivery trough and press one of the levers within 10 s. After the lever press response, the rat received a single reward pellet (45 mg dustless precision pellet, Bio-Serv, Flemington, NJ), the color LED lights were turned off, and a 15-s intertrial interval (ITI) began. If the animal accomplished the actual trial, the total reaction time (RT) was measured as the time from the offset of the feeder light to the lever press response. In the analysis, the total RT was split into two phases: 1) the time from the cue (feeder light off, color LEDs on) until the nose was pulled out from the pellet delivery trough was considered the decision phase of the response and was referred to as “noseout” time; 2) the time from pulling the nose out until pressing the lever was considered as the motor part of the response and was referred to as “lever press” time. If the trial was not performed correctly, the following errors were defined and registered. If the animal did not put its nose in the feeder trough within 10 s after the onset of the feeder light, the trial was not initiated, and it was considered a missed trial. If the animal removed its head from the feeder trough before the offset of the feeder light during the nosepoke period, a premature response was recorded. If the animal succeeded in the foreperiod but did not press the lever after the onset of the cue lights, it was considered an omission error (lapse). All types of errors resulted in the offset of all light stimuli, and a 5-s punishment period (no food reward) followed by an ITI of 15 s. In each experimental session, the rats performed the task for 60 min or until 90 trials were initiated.

Experimental design

Altogether five separate experiments were run in this study, where an experiment refers to a set of four consecutive sessions in which four treatments (vehicle and three different pharmacological treatments) were distributed between sessions and subjects according to a counterbalanced within-subject design. The four sessions of a given experiment were run over a 2-week timeframe, and the five experiments were conducted over 2 months. In experiments that investigated the effects of donepezil and galantamine, altogether five different doses of the compounds were tested in two separate experiments that were analyzed together. In one experiment with donepezil, the following treatments were applied in random order: vehicle (saline), 0.1 mg/kg, 0.3 mg/kg, and 1.0 mg/kg donepezil (aVEH, DON0.1, DON0.3, and DON1.0, respectively). In an additional experiment with donepezil, the following treatments were applied: vehicle, 0.01 mg/kg, 0.03 mg/kg, and 0.1 mg/kg donepezil (aVEH, DON0.01, DON0.03, and DON0.1, respectively). In one experiment with galantamine, the following treatments were tested: vehicle, 0.03 mg/kg, 0.1 mg/kg, and 0.3 mg/kg galantamine (aVEH, GAL0.03, GAL0.1, GAL0.3, respectively). In an additional experiment, the following treatments were applied: vehicle, 0.3 mg/kg, 1.0 mg/kg, and 3.0 mg/kg galantamine (aVEH, GAL0.3, GAL1.0, and GAL3.0, respectively). The initial doses of AChEIs were selected according to our earlier (pilot) experiments, and the initial doses were later adjusted for the additional experiments according to the findings. In the experiment that investigated the effects of memantine, the following treatments were applied: vehicle, 0.1 mg/kg, 0.3 mg/kg, and 1.0 mg/kg memantine (aVEH, Mem0.1, Mem0.3, and Mem1.0, respectively). The doses of memantine were selected according to our earlier studies [17–19]. Young rats (yVEH) were treated with saline prior to each session. All treatments were administered subcutaneously (s.c.) in a 1 ml/kg injection volume 45 min prior to testing. A washout period of at least 72 h was applied between two experimental sessions. Donepezil hydrochloride, galantamine hydrobromide and memantine hydrochloride were purchased from Tocris (Bristol, UK).

Data analysis and statistics

The total reaction time and its components were averaged over all trials. Data are presented as mean ± SEM and were analyzed using Microsoft Excel (Albuquerque, NM) and IBM SPSS Statistics v28 (Armonk, NY). All statistical comparisons (including between-subject comparisons of young and aged rats and within-subject comparisons of aged rats after different treatments) were made in a single linear mixed-effects model with a random intercept which took into account the correlation between repeated measurements on the same animals (i.e., mostly equivalent with a repeated measures ANOVA). Pairwise comparisons were made using the LSD method. Uncorrected p-values of pairwise comparisons were reported throughout the study. Main effects and pairwise differences were considered significant if p < 0.05, while a marginal significance was considered in the case of 0.05 < p < 0.1.

Results

Low dose of donepezil improved reaction time of aged rats

Treatments exerted a significant main effect on the total reaction time of rats (F(6, 58.7) = 10.064, p < 0.001) (Fig. 1). The reaction time of aged rats was significantly longer than that of young rats. Donepezil in a low dose (0.03 mg/kg) marginally significantly improved the reaction time of aged rats. However, high doses of donepezil significantly increased the reaction time to the stimuli. The effects on the total reaction time also manifested in the first component of the reaction time that was measured from the cue until pulling out the head from the feeder trough (“noseout” time) (F(6, 58) = 8.949, p < 0.001). In addition, the noseout time of aged rats was significantly longer than that of young rats. Treatment with 0.03 mg/kg donepezil significantly improved the performance of aged rats in the noseout time, whereas 0.3 and 1.0 mg/kg donepezil worsened it resulting in a U-shaped dose–response curve. The lever-press time (the second component of the total reaction time) was not significantly affected by either the age of the animals or the applied donepezil treatments (F(6, 68.2) = 1.551, p = 0.175).

Two doses of donepezil significantly decreased the number of correct trials performed by aged rats (F(6, 69.5) = 4.839, p < 0.001). The treatments showed a marginally significant main effect on the number of premature responses (F(6, 72.7) = 2.043, p = 0.071). Only treatment with 0.03 mg/kg donepezil increased the number of premature responses in aged rats. Omission errors (when rats failed to press the lever after the cue) were significantly more frequent in aged rats than in young rats (F(6, 60.5) = 7.116, p < 0.001). Higher doses of donepezil further increased the number of omission errors of aged rats. The number of missed trials (when rats failed to initiate a trial by nose-poking in the feeder trough) was also different between groups and treatments (F(6, 69.2) = 10.677, p < 0.001). Aged rats missed more trials than young rats. Donepezil at 0.01 and 1.0 mg/kg doses further increased the number of missed trials in aged rats. No beneficial effects of galantamine on PVT performance of aged rats

In the experiments that investigated the effects of galantamine, the reaction time of aged rats was again slower than that of young rats (F(6, 63) = 6.494, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). Galantamine treatment did not improve the reaction time of aged rats, moreover, its high dose (3.0 mg/kg) further increased the reaction time of aged rats. Similarly, the first component (noseout) of the reaction time was significantly slower in aged rats than in young rats, and the performance of aged rats was further worsened by the 3.0 mg/kg dose of galantamine (F(6, 60.8) = 4.551, p < 0.001). Aged rats were also slower than young rats in the lever-press component of the reaction time (F(6, 71) = 4.116, p < 0.001). Galantamine treatment at 3.0 mg/kg dose in aged rats further increased lever-press time.

Similarly to donepezil treatment, 0.1 and 3.0 mg/kg doses of galantamine also decreased the number of correct trials performed by aged rats (F(6, 74.1) = 7.601, p < 0.001). Premature responses were not different between young and aged rats and were not affected by galantamine treatment (F(6, 71.3) = 1.017, p = 0.421). On the other hand, aged rats made significantly more omission errors and missed trials than young rats (Omission: F(6, 73.4) = 4.514, p < 0.001; Missed trials: F(6, 71.5) = 12.498, p < 0.001). Galantamine did not affect the number of omission errors in any doses. However, the number of missed trials was significantly increased by the 0.1 mg/kg and 3.0 mg/kg doses of galantamine. For detailed statistical data, please refer to Supplementary Table 1.

Memantine increased the number of correct trials performed by aged rats

In the experiments investigating the effects of memantine, the reaction time of aged rats was significantly slower than that of young rats (F(4, 37) = 4.074, p = 0.008) (Fig. 3). Memantine did not exert any effect on the reaction time of aged rats at any dose. Similar results were found in the analysis of the noseout and lever-press components of reaction time. Both the first and second components of the responses were significantly slower in aged rats than in young rats (noseout: F(4, 36.6) = 3.451, p = 0.017; lever-press: F(4, 36.4) = 2.781, p = 0.041), and memantine did not change the speed of aged rats in any doses.

Panels D-F show different types of errors. yVEH: young adult rats treated with vehicle (saline); aVEH: aged rats treated with vehicle. The numbers after Mem show the dose of memantine in mg/kg (Mem0.1–1.0). Asterisks indicate significant differences compared to vehicle-treated aged rats: * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001

Unlike donepezil and galantamine, memantine increased the number of correct trials performed by aged rats (F(4, 36) = 5.759, p = 0.001). Without treatment, aged rats performed fewer correct trials than young rats; however, memantine at doses of 0.1 mg/kg and 0.3 mg/kg increased the number of correct trials, thus improving the task performance of aged rats. The number of premature responses was not affected by age or memantine treatment (F(4, 38.4) = 0.404, p = 0.804). However, memantine significantly decreased the number of omission errors that were more frequently made by aged than young rats (F(4, 35.8) = 3.009, p = 0.031). Furthermore, memantine also decreased the number of missed trials in aged rats (F(4, 37.1) = 6.922, p < 0.001). For detailed statistical data, please refer to Supplementary Table 1.

Discussion

In the present study, we assessed the effects of approved AD medications, donepezil, galantamine, and memantine, on various cognitive behavioral domains in aged rats measured using the PVT. In line with our previous findings, where the PVT performance of aged and young rats was compared, the performance of aged rats was similarly impaired in the PVT, which was manifested in longer reaction times and more errors compared to young rats [12]. An age-related decrease in vigilance and sustained attention was also reported earlier in the 5-choice serial reaction time task (5-CSRTT) [20]. Importantly, in the present study only male rats were used, therefore, further studies are needed to assess whether gender-specific differences influence the performance of aged rats in the PVT or the effects of AD medications.

We also assessed the effects of general AD medications in the PVT: of the two tested AChEIs, only donepezil improved the speed of responding in the PVT, while galantamine failed to improve the performance of aged rats in the applied dose range. Furthermore, the results showed that there are differences between the cognitive enhancer effects of donepezil and memantine. While donepezil improved the reaction time of aged rats (mainly in the initial or decision phase of the responses), memantine increased the number of correct responses (i.e., decreased the number of errors) made by aged rats, leaving the reaction time unaffected. A large body of evidence shows that donepezil effectively improves hippocampus-dependent memory in a wide variety of rodent models of cognitive impairment (for a review, see [21]). Beyond memory, donepezil also influences other cognitive domains. In an earlier study, donepezil decreased impulsivity in a touchscreen-based continuous performance task but had negligible effects on attentional performance [22]. In another study, low doses of donepezil effectively reversed dizocilpine-induced impairment in a visual signal detection task in rats [23]. However, in the 5-CSRTT, a high dose of donepezil (2 mg/kg) impaired the attention of rats [24]. These findings are in line with our present study as we also report here that high doses of donepezil increase reaction time and the number of omission errors in the PVT. As low doses of donepezil improved the performance of rats in the PVT, our findings also emphasize the significance of the applied doses when their beneficial effects on attention and vigilance are considered. Based on the present results, we conclude that donepezil improves attentional performance at low doses, while high doses may exert adverse effects on attention and vigilance, resulting in a typical U-shaped dose–response curve. Similarly, donepezil also showed dose-dependency in its effects on different cognitive domains in nonhuman primates, where lower doses improved cognitive flexibility and higher doses improved attention [25]. Furthermore, in another PVT study from our laboratory conducted in nonhuman primates, we found that donepezil improved alertness and task performance in a pharmacological model of cognitive decline without enhancing temporal expectation (i.e., the gradual decrease in reaction time with foreperiod time) [26]. Donepezil also improved PVT performance in healthy young adults in a human study [27].

As an AChEI, donepezil acts through the increase of ACh levels in the brain [28]. Surprisingly, another AChEI, galantamine, failed to improve the performance of aged rats. There is a body of evidence in the literature that suggests that galantamine is not only an AChEI but also acts on nAChRs as a PAM [14]; however, this PAM activity of galantamine is disputed based on in vitro experiments [29]. We recently showed that the experimental activation of alpha7 nAChRs improves the reaction time of aged rats in the PVT, especially increasing the speed of the decision phase of responding [12]. Studies in other tasks of attention and vigilance also suggested that the stimulation of nicotinic receptors improves the performance of rodents [20, 30, 31]. Nevertheless, galantamine in the present study did not show a cognitive enhancer effect that would be expected either because of an AChEI or nAChR PAM activity. Similarly, in a recent study, galantamine alone was not effective in the 5-CSRTT, which is another paradigm for measuring attentional performance [32].

The present study also showed that the PVT sensitively detects the side effects of pharmacological treatments on attention and vigilance. High doses of donepezil and galantamine markedly increased the reaction time of rats and the number of missed trials, which is considered a measure of the loss of motivation. These findings are in line with earlier reports on the specific side effects of AChEIs, which decrease the locomotor activity and exploration behavior of rodents [33]. We recently used the rat PVT in another study to detect putative sedating side effects of anxiolytic compounds: while diazepam significantly increased the lever press time, the number of omission errors, and missed trials, songorine, a natural compound with anxiolytic properties, did not affect any measures of the PVT [11]. These present and earlier results show that the PVT is also suitable for assessing the side effects of various drugs on vigilance and for differentiating between the therapeutic effects and side effects of these compounds.

Similar to donepezil, memantine also improved the PVT performance of aged rats, but in a different manner. Memantine did not affect the response speed of animals, but it increased the number of correctly performed trials by decreasing the number of errors, such as omissions of lever-press and missed trials. Thus, memantine treatment resulted in a more precise performance of rats in the PVT. Memantine is an approved drug for the treatment of Alzheimer’s disease and is effective against experimentally induced cognitive impairment in different rodent models [21]. Memantine also influences measures of attention and vigilance in both rodents and humans. Memantine reduced measures of impulsivity in a touchscreen-based continuous performance task but had worsening effects on attentional performance at doses substantially higher than those applied in the present study [22]. In an earlier study, memantine increased the wakefulness of rats, as measured using electroencephalography, and ameliorated the effects of sleep deprivation [34]. Memantine also counteracts vigilance deficits in elderly human subjects [35]. Furthermore, memantine improved the performance of patients with dementia in a choice reaction time task [36]. Electrophysiological measures associated with attention and working memory were also improved by memantine in patients with fragile-X-associated tremor/ataxia syndrome [37]. Preliminary clinical data also suggest that the co-administration of memantine with psychostimulant medications may be beneficial in patients with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder [38]. These previous data, together with the present results, emphasize that the beneficial effects of memantine are not limited to the improvement of memory but memantine also positively affects other cognitive domains, such as attention and vigilance. Memantine is primarily considered an NMDA receptor antagonist. However, some studies suggest that memantine binds to alpha7 nAChRs with higher affinity than to NMDA receptors, and this cholinergic effect may critically contribute to the observed procognitive effects [16, 39]. Indeed, in our recent electrophysiological study, we demonstrated that memantine blocks NMDA-evoked firing activity of hippocampal neurons only at high doses (3.0 mg/kg), while the ACh-evoked firing responses were facilitated by much lower doses of memantine (0.1 mg/kg). Furthermore, the combined effects of low doses of memantine and alpha7 nAChR ligands (PHA-543613 and an experimental PAM compound) on hippocampal neurons also suggested interactions between memantine and the cholinergic system [40].

The combination of memantine with other cognitive enhancer compounds has been frequently proposed in recent years [41, 42]. Memantine is most frequently combined with AChEI compounds, and the combination of memantine and donepezil has already been approved by the FDA. However, this combination has not been approved by the European Medicines Agency because of the lack of evidence for the benefits of combination therapy over corresponding monotherapies [43]. Some papers suggest that the combination of memantine with galantamine may provide better outcomes partly because of the putative alpha7 nAChR PAM activity of galantamine [44]. Indeed, our previous studies on various models and cognitive tasks demonstrated the facilitation of the procognitive effects of memantine by activating alpha7 nAChRs [17–19]. The findings of the present study that memantine and donepezil improved different cognitive and behavioral aspects of the performance of rats in the PVT also support the relevance of the combination strategy to enhance the efficacy of therapies for neurocognitive disorders. However, further studies are needed to clarify whether the differential effects of donepezil and memantine add up and increase efficacy during combination treatment. Regarding the interpretation of the pharmacological findings of our present study, it must be noted that we assessed acute effects of parenterally administered AD medications on the cognitive performance of rats while human patients receive the medications daily in escalating oral doses. Therefore, the findings of the present study are not directly translatable to typical therapeutic processes in human patients; rather, it serves as a proof-of-concept study for the use of the PVT in preclinical drug development.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we first demonstrated the differential effects of donepezil, galantamine, and memantine in the rat PVT, also validating that the PVT is a sensitive task for the assessment of the effectiveness of pharmacological agents in models of neurocognitive disorders such as aged animals. The present results indicate that donepezil and memantine have beneficial effects on distinct cognitive and behavioral domains, while also emphasizing the importance of careful dosing by showing possible side effects of high doses of these compounds on attention and vigilance.
