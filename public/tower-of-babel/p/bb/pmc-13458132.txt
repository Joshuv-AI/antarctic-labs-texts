Temporoinsular Extraventricular Neurocytoma Initially Diagnosed as Glioblastoma in a 57-Year-Old Woman: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Valeria J Martínez Evangelista, Monica S Garcia Bravo, Astrid I Machuca Ruiz, Virginia Aguila Dueñas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Extraventricular neurocytoma is a rare neuronal neoplasm that may radiologically mimic high-grade glial tumors. We present the case of a 57-year-old woman with focal seizures, right-sided hemiparesis, and language impairment, whose computed tomography scan revealed a left temporoinsular mass with a cystic-necrotic component, extensive vasogenic edema, and significant mass effect, initially interpreted as glioblastoma. Subtotal resection was performed through a left frontotemporal craniotomy because of tumor adherence to the corticospinal tract. Histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation demonstrated synaptophysin positivity, focal glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) expression, a Ki-67 proliferation index of approximately 2%, and negativity for isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 (IDH1), chromogranin, and AE1/AE3, establishing the diagnosis of extraventricular neurocytoma, central nervous system (CNS) WHO grade 2. Because of residual disease, adjuvant radiotherapy followed by temozolomide was administered. At 14 months of follow-up, the patient remained free of radiological progression and demonstrated significant functional recovery. This case highlights the importance of considering extraventricular neurocytoma in the differential diagnosis of supratentorial intra-axial lesions with radiological features suggestive of high-grade glioma and underscores the value of clinicopathological and radiological correlation in establishing an accurate diagnosis and guiding treatment.

Introduction

Extraventricular neurocytoma (EVN) is an uncommon neuronal neoplasm classified by the World Health Organization (WHO) within the group of neuronal and glioneuronal tumors and is currently recognized as a central nervous system (CNS) WHO grade 2 neuronal neoplasm according to the 2021 WHO Classification of Central Nervous System Tumors [1]. Unlike central neurocytoma, which typically arises within the lateral ventricles near the foramen of Monro, EVN develops within the brain parenchyma without connection to the ventricular system and may occur in various supratentorial locations, particularly within the frontal and temporal lobes [1,2]. EVN is an exceptionally rare tumor, accounting for less than 0.5% of all primary central nervous system neoplasms, and predominantly affects young adults, although sporadic cases have been reported in older patients [2,3]. Clinical presentation depends largely on tumor location and commonly includes seizures, headache, and focal neurological deficits secondary to mass effect [4].

Preoperative diagnosis remains challenging because radiological findings are often nonspecific and may mimic high-grade intra-axial neoplasms. Features such as solid-cystic components, vasogenic edema, heterogeneous contrast enhancement, and midline shift may lead to an erroneous diagnosis of glioblastoma or other glial tumors [5]. Accurate distinction between EVN and high-grade gliomas is clinically important because these entities differ substantially in biological behavior, prognosis, and therapeutic management. Therefore, definitive diagnosis requires careful clinicoradiopathological correlation supported by immunohistochemical studies.

Histologically, extraventricular neurocytomas are characterized by a proliferation of relatively uniform cells with round nuclei and finely granular chromatin embedded within a delicate vascular network. Synaptophysin positivity represents the most characteristic immunohistochemical finding, whereas focal GFAP expression may occasionally be observed in tumors exhibiting limited glial differentiation [6,7]. Maximal safe surgical resection remains the cornerstone of treatment, whereas adjuvant radiotherapy and, in selected cases, chemotherapy may be considered for patients with residual disease or other high-risk features [2,3].

Given the rarity of extraventricular neurocytoma and its ability to closely mimic high-grade gliomas on neuroimaging, additional well-characterized cases are essential to improve diagnostic recognition and optimize therapeutic decision-making. We report a temporoinsular extraventricular neurocytoma initially suspected to represent glioblastoma, illustrating the diagnostic challenges posed by this rare entity and emphasizing the value of multidisciplinary clinicoradiopathological evaluation for establishing an accurate diagnosis and guiding individualized management.

Case presentation

A 57-year-old woman with a medical history significant for type 2 diabetes mellitus and systemic arterial hypertension presented to our institution. Two months before admission, she experienced two self-limited focal seizures that were neither investigated nor treated. Seven days prior to presentation, her husband noticed a progressive gait disturbance characterized by dragging of the right lower extremity. The patient was admitted to the emergency department because of acute neurological deterioration manifested by inability to move the right side of her body and language impairment characterized by incoherent speech, verbal perseveration, and impaired comprehension. Neurological examination revealed a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 13, right-sided hemiparesis with muscle strength graded as 3/5, mixed sensorimotor aphasia with an anomic component, dysdiadochokinesia, and bilateral flexor plantar responses.

Neuroimaging studies demonstrated a heterogeneous intra-axial lesion measuring approximately 6 × 4 cm in the left temporoinsular region, associated with significant mass effect, rightward midline shift, and compression of the ipsilateral ventricular system without evidence of obstructive hydrocephalus. Based on its clinical and radiological characteristics, an initial diagnosis of left frontotemporal glioblastoma associated with dorsolateral prefrontal syndrome was considered (Figure 1). Magnetic resonance imaging was not available during the initial diagnostic evaluation because of resource limitations at our institution, and treatment decisions were based on contrast-enhanced computed tomography findings.

Contrast-enhanced brain computed tomography (axial view)

Contrast-enhanced brain computed tomography demonstrating a heterogeneous intra-axial lesion located in the left temporoinsular region (blue arrow), characterized by irregular peripheral ring enhancement, a central cystic-necrotic component, extensive surrounding vasogenic edema (red arrow), and associated mass effect resulting in compression of the ipsilateral lateral ventricle and mild rightward midline shift (yellow arrow).

The patient was evaluated by the Neurosurgery Department and subsequently underwent a left frontotemporal craniotomy measuring approximately 10 × 15 cm. Neuronavigation and intraoperative ultrasound confirmed the presence of a predominantly cystic lesion. Drainage of the cystic component and subtotal tumor resection were performed. During the procedure, residual tumor tissue was found to be firmly adherent to the corticospinal tract, a finding confirmed by intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring. Because of the risk of permanent neurological deficits, a subtotal resection (>90%) was intentionally performed. No ventricular origin or ventricular extension was identified on either radiological or intraoperative evaluation, confirming a completely intraparenchymal location.

Histopathological examination of the resected specimen revealed findings consistent with neurocytoma (Figure 2). Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated cytoplasmic positivity for synaptophysin, confirming neuronal differentiation, one of the most characteristic immunohistochemical features of neurocytomas. Focal GFAP expression was also observed, a finding occasionally reported in tumors with limited glial differentiation. Chromogranin staining was negative, supporting the diagnosis and helping exclude other neuroendocrine tumors with more mature differentiation. The Ki-67 proliferation index was approximately 2%, indicating low proliferative activity. In addition, tumor cells were negative for IDH1 and cytokeratins AE1/AE3 (Figure 3), excluding epithelial differentiation and helping rule out metastatic carcinoma or primary epithelial neoplasms. Taken together, the morphological, immunophenotypic, and radiological findings were consistent with extraventricular neurocytoma, CNS WHO grade 2.

Because of subtotal resection and persistent residual disease in an eloquent brain region, the case was discussed in a multidisciplinary setting involving the Neurosurgery and Medical Oncology teams. Adjuvant radiotherapy consisting of 60 Gy in 34 fractions was administered, followed by temozolomide at a dose of 150 mg/m²/day for five consecutive days every 28 days for six cycles. This treatment strategy was adopted because of the presence of residual disease and the limited evidence available for this rare entity, with the aim of treating residual tumor and reducing the risk of recurrence.

A follow-up computed tomography (CT) scan obtained 14 months after surgery and completion of adjuvant therapy demonstrated no radiological evidence of residual or recurrent tumor, mass effect, or midline shift (Figure 4). To further characterize the postoperative findings, brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), including T2-weighted Fluid-Attenuated Inversion Recovery (FLAIR), diffusion-weighted, and non-contrast T1-weighted sequences, was subsequently performed and demonstrated stable postoperative changes without imaging findings suggestive of tumor recurrence (Figure 5). As our institution is a public tertiary referral hospital, postoperative imaging surveillance is performed according to the diagnostic resources routinely available, and contrast-enhanced MRI could not be obtained during follow-up. Although gadolinium-enhanced MRI remains the preferred imaging modality for detecting residual or recurrent disease, the combined CT and MRI findings, together with the patient's favorable clinical course, supported a sustained therapeutic response without radiological progression. Longer follow-up is warranted because late recurrences have been reported.

During follow-up, the patient continued rehabilitation therapy. At 14 months, she remained free of radiological progression, with significant improvement in motor and language deficits, while maintaining independence in activities of daily living. Her functional status was ECOG Performance Status 1 with a Karnofsky Performance Status score of 80%. She currently remains under close clinical and radiological surveillance.

Discussion

Extraventricular neurocytoma is an uncommon entity whose diagnosis remains challenging because of its low incidence and the heterogeneity of its clinical and radiological manifestations. The 2021 WHO Classification recognizes this neoplasm as a distinct entity within the group of neuronal and glioneuronal tumors, differentiating it from central neurocytoma by its entirely intraparenchymal origin and lack of anatomical association with the ventricular system [1].

Our case is consistent with the currently accepted diagnostic criteria for extraventricular neurocytoma, as the lesion was located within the left temporoinsular region without radiological or intraoperative evidence of ventricular origin. Although compression of the ipsilateral lateral ventricle was present, it resulted from the significant mass effect exerted by the lesion rather than intraventricular growth. Byun et al. reported that extraventricular neurocytomas may occur in multiple supratentorial locations, with the frontal and temporal lobes representing some of the most frequently involved sites [2].

The age at presentation in our patient represents another noteworthy aspect. Although most reported cases occur during the second to fourth decades of life, exceptional presentations in older adults have been described. Tish et al. reported an extraventricular neurocytoma with ganglioid differentiation in a 70-year-old patient, while contemporary case series and systematic reviews have confirmed that this entity may occur outside the classic age range [3,8]. The occurrence of this tumor in a 57-year-old woman further expands the recognized clinical spectrum of this neoplasm. From a clinical perspective, the manifestations observed in our patient were consistent with the anatomical location of the tumor. Focal seizures, right-sided hemiparesis, mixed aphasia, and cognitive impairment can be explained by involvement of the left temporal and insular regions as well as the significant associated mass effect. Messina et al. reported that seizures and focal neurological deficits represent some of the most common clinical presentations of extraventricular neurocytoma [4].

The radiopathological correlation observed in our case is particularly illustrative. Radiologically, the lesion exhibited features highly suggestive of glioblastoma, including a cystic-necrotic component, irregular peripheral enhancement, extensive vasogenic edema, and midline shift. Jiang et al. described these findings as relatively common in extraventricular neurocytoma and correlated them with the histological heterogeneity, cystic degeneration, and vascular proliferation observed microscopically [5]. This radiological resemblance to high-grade glial tumors explains the difficulty of establishing a preoperative diagnosis and underscores the importance of definitive histopathological evaluation.

Histologically, the proliferation of monomorphic cells with round nuclei and finely granular chromatin arranged within a delicate vascular network observed in our patient is consistent with the classic features described for neurocytomas [6,7]. Immunohistochemistry was pivotal in confirming the diagnosis, with synaptophysin positivity supporting neuronal differentiation and focal GFAP expression representing a finding previously reported in neurocytomas with limited glial differentiation [6-8]. The absence of chromogranin, IDH1, and cytokeratin AE1/AE3 expression helped exclude relevant differential diagnoses, while the low Ki-67 proliferative index (approximately 2%) was consistent with favorable biological behavior. Byun et al. associated low proliferative indices with improved clinical outcomes, whereas Valerio et al. emphasized the importance of integrating histopathological and immunohistochemical findings to establish a definitive diagnosis of extraventricular neurocytoma [8,9].

Molecular profiling was not performed because these studies were not routinely available at our institution during the patient's diagnostic workup. Therefore, the diagnosis was established on the basis of the characteristic histopathological, immunohistochemical, radiological, and intraoperative findings, which were considered sufficient to support the diagnosis of extraventricular neurocytoma according to the available WHO diagnostic criteria [9]. Maximal safe surgical resection remains the treatment of choice. In a systematic review and individual patient data meta-analysis, Kweh et al. demonstrated that gross total resection is associated with improved progression-free survival and local tumor control [3]. However, subtotal resection may represent the safest option when lesions involve eloquent brain regions. In our patient, residual tumor adherent to the corticospinal tract necessitated preservation of a small tumor remnant to minimize the risk of permanent neurological deficits.

Adjuvant radiotherapy following subtotal resection has shown benefits in terms of local disease control. Samhouri et al. reported improved outcomes in patients with residual disease who received postoperative radiotherapy, particularly when complete resection was not feasible [10]. Given the persistence of residual tumor within a functionally critical location, adjuvant radiotherapy was considered a reasonable therapeutic strategy in our patient.

The role of chemotherapy in extraventricular neurocytoma remains controversial. In the largest review currently available addressing chemotherapy in neurocytoma, Johnson et al. documented the use of temozolomide primarily in patients with residual, recurrent, or progressive disease, emphasizing the lack of an established treatment standard [11]. Similarly, Han et al. suggested that systemic therapy should be individualized according to each patient's clinical presentation, tumor location, extent of resection, and anticipated surgical morbidity [12]. In our patient, subtotal resection was intentionally performed because the residual tumor was firmly adherent to the corticospinal tract, making further resection associated with an unacceptable risk of permanent neurological deficits. Consequently, adjuvant temozolomide was selected as an individualized therapeutic strategy following radiotherapy to treat residual disease while avoiding the morbidity associated with reoperation. Although robust evidence supporting this approach remains limited and no consensus guidelines currently recommend routine chemotherapy for EVN, the favorable radiological and functional outcomes observed during follow-up suggest that this strategy may be considered in carefully selected patients after multidisciplinary discussion.

Regarding radiological follow-up, post-treatment surveillance was performed with computed tomography because our institution is a public referral hospital with routine access to CT imaging but limited availability of magnetic resonance imaging for serial oncologic surveillance studies. Despite this limitation, CT imaging adequately documented the absence of tumor recurrence, mass effect, or radiological progression throughout follow-up. The patient's clinical course was favorable. At the 14-month follow-up, the patient remained free of radiological progression, with significant improvement in motor and language deficits while remaining independent in activities of daily living, an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) Performance Status of 1 [13], and a Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) score of 80% [14].

This case highlights the ability of extraventricular neurocytoma to radiologically mimic glioblastoma, particularly when presenting as a temporoinsular lesion with a cystic-necrotic component and extensive vasogenic edema. Furthermore, it underscores the importance of clinicoradiopathological correlation in establishing the correct diagnosis and supports the potential benefit of a multidisciplinary treatment strategy based on maximal safe resection, adjuvant radiotherapy, and individualized systemic therapy.

Conclusions

Extraventricular neurocytoma is a rare neoplasm that may clinically and radiologically mimic glioblastoma, making preoperative diagnosis particularly challenging. In the present case, integration of the clinical, radiological, histopathological, and immunohistochemical findings enabled the definitive diagnosis of extraventricular neurocytoma, CNS WHO grade 2. The combination of maximal safe resection, adjuvant radiotherapy, and individualized systemic therapy was associated with effective disease control and favorable functional recovery at 14 months of follow-up. Although this favorable outcome is limited to a single patient with relatively short-term follow-up, it suggests that this multidisciplinary approach may represent a reasonable therapeutic option in carefully selected cases. Extraventricular neurocytoma should be considered in the differential diagnosis of supratentorial intra-axial masses with imaging features suggestive of glioblastoma, particularly when histopathological and immunohistochemical studies demonstrate neuronal differentiation. Longer-term follow-up and additional clinical studies are needed to better define recurrence patterns and long-term outcomes in this rare entity.
