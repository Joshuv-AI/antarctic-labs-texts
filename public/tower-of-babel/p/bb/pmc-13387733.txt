Comparative Profiles of Pediatric Mendeliome: A Single-Center 572-Whole-Exome Sequencing Study in Xinjiang
Authors: Yan Li, Chen Cao, Yanfei Luo, Guanghui Sun, Reyilanmu Baoerhan, Dilihuma Julaiti, Junkang Li, Yiping Shen, Miriguli Maimaiti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe aim of the study was to compare the diagnostic yields and utility of whole-exome sequencing (WES) for Mendelian disorders in pediatric patients of different ethnic backgrounds in Xinjiang.MethodsA retrospective analysis of 572 pediatric patients suspected of Mendelian disorders, admitted to the Pediatric Center of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University from January 2016 to June 2020. WES was performed in accredited laboratories, and reports were verified by supervising physicians.ResultsThe overall diagnostic yield was 42.3%, with a higher yield in Uyghur than Han patients (46.5% vs. 36.2%). Inheritance pattern distributions differed by ethnicity: autosomal recessive findings were more frequent in Uyghur (60.8%) than Han (29.8%), whereas autosomal dominant (46.4%) and de novo variants (38.1%) comprised larger proportions in Han than in Uyghur (31.6% and 22.8%); X-linked findings were less common in both groups. Diagnostic yield varied by phenotype category (33.3%–73.1%), was generally higher in consanguineous than non-consanguineous cases, and increased with phenotypic complexity (16.7%, 54.1%, and 85.7% across increasing phenotype burden). Among patients with follow-up, WES results influenced medication management (61.0%), clinical/dietary management (89.2%), and subspecialist referral (36.9%).ConclusionExome testing shows higher diagnostic yield and clinical utility, especially for patients from consanguineous families in Xinjiang. Carrier screening could effectively prevent severe genetic disorders in the region, expanding the genotype and phenotype in the Chinese pediatric population.

Introduction

Genetic diseases are a major cause of childhood mortality. About 70% of rare diseases have pediatric onset, and approximately 30% of affected children die before the age of five; moreover, genetic disorders account for around 80% of rare diseases worldwide [1–4]. Consequently, infants with genetic disorders often present with multiple comorbidities and make up more than 25% of pediatric inpatients [5]. In Europe, more than 6,000 rare diseases affect over 30 million people [6]. Mendelian disorders (MDs) are typically caused by single-gene mutations and are often described in the context of disrupted enzyme function and abnormal biochemical reactions in intermediary metabolic pathways [7, 8].

A timely molecular diagnosis is essential for MD because it can directly inform disease-specific management and has become increasingly integrated into clinical practice [9]. With genome-wide sequencing approaches such as whole-exome sequencing (WES), it is now feasible to evaluate patients with complex phenotypes or atypical presentations that are difficult to resolve by conventional testing alone [10, 11].

In China, WES has been implemented rapidly and is increasingly used in the diagnostic workup of genetic diseases. However, evidence supporting its use as a first-line test remains limited, which has restricted wider adoption beyond a small number of leading hospitals. In addition, WES studies in ethnic populations are still scarce, particularly in pediatric cohorts from Western China. Xinjiang is characterized by a unique ethnic composition and a high rate of consanguinity, features that are uncommon in other parts of the country. To address the lack of data on WES diagnostic yield and the genetic spectrum of the pediatric Mendeliome in Xinjiang, we analyzed a cohort of 572 children from the largest pediatric metabolic center in Northwest China. We aimed to assess the clinical utility of WES and to compare genetic profiles across different ethnic groups. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic WES study in pediatric patients from this ethnic region. By describing the Mendeliome in ethnic minorities, we hope to broaden the genotype-phenotype resources for Chinese pediatric patients. Our variant-level and case-level analyses also suggest that carrier exome screening may be a practical strategy to reduce the burden of severe genetic disorders in Xinjiang, and they further contribute to expanding genotype-phenotype resources in Chinese pediatric patients.

Methods

Clinical Samples and Exome Reporting

In this study, we reviewed 572 children with findings relevant to MD at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University (FAH-XMU) who were referred for WES gene panel sequencing between January 2016 and June 2020. With informed consent, genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples obtained from the probands (and parents, when available) using a DNA Extraction Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

WES was performed by Sierra Vast Bio-Medical (Shanghai, China) and GiantMed Diagnostics (Beijing, China) using the HiSeq X Ten platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The average sequencing depth across the target regions was approximately 150×, with 98% of target bases covered at >20×.

Bioinformatics secondary analysis was conducted using the Sentieon Genomics pipeline. Reads were aligned with Sentieon BWA to the UCSC hg19 reference genome. Sequencing depth and per-base variant calling metrics were derived from the genome sequencing data, and variants were annotated using variant effect predictor. The Human Feb. 2009 (GRCh37/hg19) genome reference, the Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD, 2017 Professional Edition), and the ClinVar database (www.clinicalgenome.org) were used to identify and interpret relevant genomic variants. Each variant was evaluated according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines and reported using Human Genome Variation Society (HGVS) nomenclature. Collectively, trio exome, proband-and-one-parent exome, and proband-only exome were conducted on 530, 40, and 2 cases, respectively (Fig. 1). We recorded the general information and evaluated the clinical and genetic traits from the WES testing.

WES workflow and patient management outcomes.

Assessment of Consanguinity and Criteria

Consanguinity was assessed at enrollment by a structured family history interview and construction of a pedigree (at least three generations). Parental relatedness was recorded based on self-report and classified by the degree of relationship. Consistent with standard usage in medical genetics, a union between individuals related as second cousins or closer was considered consanguineous.

Molecular Diagnoses and Human Phenotype Ontology Analysis

WES was performed for probands and their parents (when available). For a proband to be considered positive, one or more relevant findings had to be identified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic (LP) variants in a gene with a good phenotypic match. Each variant was comprehensively reevaluated by clinicians and geneticists and systematically reviewed with reference to the published literature and the OMIM database [12] to support an accurate diagnosis of known or novel variants and to better understand their molecular mechanisms. After reevaluation, three reports were downgraded from positive to negative, 17 reports initially classified as having uncertain pathogenicity were downgraded to negative, and one case previously classified as LP was reassigned to the benign group (Fig. 1).

Clinical data were manually reviewed and annotated into human phenotype ontology (HPO) categories according to the database [13]. Analyses were performed within each subgroup to determine diagnostic yield and to summarize sequencing results across HPO terms.

Results

Patient Demographics

Of the 572 pediatric metabolic probands who underwent WES at our center, 42.3% (242 patients) were diagnosed as positive with at least one conclusive variant, at least one variant that was either pathogenic (P) or LP in a gene with remarkable clinical association with an identified Mendelian condition (Fig. 2a). According to the ACMG guidelines [12], we identified 158 characterized pathogenic variants and 54 uncertain variants in 173 patients who received a definitive molecular diagnosis; 73 LP mutations and 54 uncertain variants were characterized in 69 LP patients; seven benign variants were found in 7 probands (Fig. 2a). There were 259 girls (45.3%) and 313 boys (54.7%) with diagnostic rates of 41.2% and 43.6%, respectively (Fig. 2b). Among them, 232 Han Chinese children had an overall diagnostic rate of 36.2%, while a higher rate of 46.5% was reached on Uyghur patients (Fig. 2c). When grouped by age, 233 probands were 2–5 years old and had a relatively high positive rate of 48.1% (Fig. 2d). Consanguinity was observed in 30 cases where the positive rate was higher than in the non-consanguineous group (70.0% vs. 40.8%, Fig. 2e). Furthermore, 24 cases that reported positive family history showed a diagnostic rate of 58.3%, while 57.7% was observed in the remaining (Fig. 2f).

Collectively, the overall diagnostic yield of Uyghur children was higher than Han (46.5% vs. 36.2%, Table 2).

Out of the 84 Han and 158 Uyghur positive cases, the distribution of inheritance patterns differed between ethnicities. In Han patients, autosomal dominant was the most frequent pattern (39/84, 46.4%), followed by de novo variants (32/84, 38.1%) and autosomal recessive (AR) inheritance (25/84, 29.8%). In contrast, Uyghur patients showed a predominance of AR inheritance (89/158, 60.8%), whereas autosomal dominant (50/158, 31.6%) and de novo variants (36/158, 22.8%) accounted for smaller proportions. X-linked inheritance was less common in both groups, including X-linked recessive (11/84, 13.1% in Han vs. 10/158, 6.3% in Uyghur) and X-linked dominant (3/84, 3.6% vs. 6/158, 3.8%). We also identified 33 and 57 novel variants in Han and Uyghur patients, respectively. The overall diagnostic yield was 36.2% (84/232) in Han patients and 46.5% (158/340) in Uyghur patients (Table 2).

Classification of HPO Clinical Phenotype Abnormalities

We used the HPO term to analyze the positive rates of cases with different phenotypes (Fig. 3) and listed the HPO-encoded clinical phenotypes and molecular test results of all 572 probands in detail (online suppl. Table 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000552488). For FAH-XMU is one of the largest pediatric endocrine disease centers in Western China, endocrine system disorders constituted the largest subgroup referred for WES; 92 of 270 cases were positive (diagnostic yield, 34.1%; Fig. 3a). We next compared diagnostic yield across phenotype categories between consanguineous and non-consanguineous cases. In the subgroups of blood and blood-forming tissues, ear, eye, head or neck, integument, musculature, and respiratory system, consanguineous cases showed a diagnostic yield of 100%. Across most phenotype categories, diagnostic yield was higher in consanguineous than in non-consanguineous cases (Fig. 3b).

We further explored the relationship between the amount of involved phenotype and the diagnostic yield and consanguinity. We found the most cases had 2 to 5 phenotypes involved, and their overall diagnostic yield was 54.1%. Twenty-one consanguineous and 332 non-consanguineous patients with 2 to 5 phenotypes showed diagnostic yields of 71.4% and 53.0%, respectively (Table 4). Similar results were also seen in other subgroups, indicating a higher diagnostic yield in more involved phenotype (Table 4).

Clinical Management and Novel Phenotype

Positive diagnostic findings from WES can inform treatment strategies and clinical management, including genetic counseling and referral for subspecialist evaluation. In this cohort, treatment follow-up was available for 241 patients with positive findings; 1 patient was excluded because of death from respiratory and heart failure associated with a persistent increase in lactic acid 48 h after hernia repair surgery. Medication management was affected in 61.0% of patients (147/241), and clinical or dietary management was changed in 89.2% (215/241). In addition, 36.9% of patients (98/241) were referred to a subspecialist for further evaluation and care (Table 5).

For example, 1 patient presented with repeated falls for 1 year and had distinctive facial features, abdominal bulging, inguinal hernia, and multiple skeletal deformities on physical examination. WES identified mucopolysaccharidosis type II, attributed to a pathogenic homozygous missense variant in IDS (c.1562A>T). The patient underwent allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation from his father, and bone marrow reconstitution was observed 24 days after treatment. At 1-year follow-up, the patient showed complete chimerism, normal enzyme activity, and improved walking ability; corneal opacity and respiratory symptoms were absent. Facial features, mental development, and the condition of the heart, brain, and skeletal system remained stable.

For a better interpretation of known variants, we recorded novel phenotypes in 18 patients from our cohort. These phenotypes are summarized using HPO terms in Table 5. For example, an AR variant in CEP57 was associated with cryptorchidism, which, to our knowledge, has not been reported previously. Other novel phenotypes observed for known variants were not reported or were rarely described in prior studies, and all were confirmed through an extensive review of the published literature (Table 6).

Molecular Findings

Of 242 patients with positive findings, we observed a total of 231 variant genes, which consisted of 153 point mutations with missense, nonsense, or termination codon mutations (66.2%, 153/231), 52 frameshift mutations with deletion or duplication mutations (22.5%, 52/231), and 26 chromosome copy number mutations (11.3%, 26/231), all of which were DNA missing fragments. Besides, we determined overall 140 sorts of inheritance phenotypes from the 242 positive cases and found congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) was the most commonly diagnosed disorder with a rate of 16.1% (39 patients); among them, the single variant was detected in 22 patients, and the remaining patients showed two or more variants. Of the 21 CAH-related variants, we found in our cohort CyP21A2 gene c.293-13C/A>G (I2G) was the most common (56.4%, 22/39), followed by CyP21A2 gene c.518T>A (p.I173N) in 11 cases (28.2%, 11 /39), which are both hot spot mutations in CAH. Also, we detected three novel variants that have not been reported, namely, CyP21A2 gene c.1267_1350dup84, c.149T>C (p.Leu50Pro), CYP11B1 gene c.715-731delTTCATGCC CAGGAGCCT deletion mutation.

For the interpretation of the 961 variants, we examined two or more variants on nine probands, and four of them eventually received dual molecular diagnoses (Table 7). Case 1 had FGFR3-related achondroplasia and epiphyseal dysplasia caused by variants on the COMP gene. Case 2 was diagnosed with PNKP-related microcephaly, seizures, developmental delay, and ADNP-related Helsmoortel-Van der Aa syndrome. Discussion

Despite substantial advances in genomic diagnostics, routine clinical workflows still face challenges in integrating phenotypic information with genomic data to support accurate variant interpretation and gene-level disease attribution. Current guidelines recommend chromosomal microarray as a first-line genomic test for adolescents [14, 15]; however, since 2011, WES and WGS have increasingly been adopted for etiologic diagnosis of inherited disorders, particularly in patients with heterogeneous or atypical presentations. In this context, we analyzed WES results from 572 probands evaluated at a comprehensive pediatric center in Xinjiang. This region has a distinctive ethnic composition, and the local population shows genetic features related to both Eastern and Western Asia, together with a higher prevalence of consanguineous marriage than other parts of China, providing an informative setting to examine diagnostic yield and the spectrum of MDs.

Large-scale studies have reported that WES yields a diagnosis in approximately 25%–30% of children with broad clinical presentations [16]. Hu et al. [17] reported a diagnostic yield of 28.8%, which is substantial but lower than that observed in our cohort. In our study, the overall diagnostic yield of WES was 42.3%. Nevertheless, a proportion of probands remained undiagnosed despite the identification of numerous variants in genes associated with MDs, suggesting that additional MD-related genes remain to be discovered. In principle, nondiagnostic WES results may reflect two main scenarios: (i) the causative variant lies outside the captured exome sequence, and (ii) the phenotype is incompletely defined, or current technical and knowledge limitations prevent correct interpretation [18]. These limitations may be mitigated through two complementary advances: continued improvements in genetic testing technologies (for example, approaches that increase sequencing depth to better detect copy number variation) and ongoing expansion of the medical literature and curated databases describing gene-phenotype relationships.

Trio exome sequencing is particularly informative because it facilitates the identification of de novo variants and clarifies variant configurations in recessive genes [17]. In our cohort, 92.7% of probands underwent trio exome sequencing, and de novo variants were identified in 66 children. Trio-based data can also improve interpretation of high-impact variants (including nonsense, frameshift, and canonical splice-site variants), even when de novo status cannot be definitively established. Accordingly, after reevaluation in our cohort, 17 variants that had initially been classified as VUS were reclassified as negative.

Consanguinity increases the likelihood of homozygous variants underlying AR disorders. This may account for the lower proportion of de novo findings observed in Uyghur patients, given the higher incidence of consanguinity in this population. In parallel, higher consanguinity has been associated with increased diagnostic yield [19], which was also supported in our cohort when comparing groups with different consanguinity incidence rates. Together, these observations indicate that genomic testing can be a valuable diagnostic strategy in developing countries, particularly in populations with high consanguinity, where consanguineous marriages contribute to a higher burden of AR disorders. In our study, the diagnostic yield was 42.3% overall and 46.5% in Uyghur patients, which is lower than the 48.3% reported by Nader Al-Dewik in 509 patients [20] and the 49% reported by Alfares et al. [21] in 459 patients. This difference may reflect the higher proportions of parental consanguinity and the higher rate of positive family history among the Middle Eastern families included in those studies.

Across phenotype categories, we observed the highest diagnostic yield in patients with skeletal system disorders, which differs from several MD-focused studies in which the nervous system category showed the highest diagnostic rate [20, 22]. We also found that diagnostic yield increased with phenotypic complexity: among 33 probands annotated with a single HPO phenotype, only 16.7% received a molecular diagnosis, whereas the yield was 54.1% in probands with 2–5 phenotypes and 85.7% in more complex cases. This pattern is consistent with the findings reported by Daniel Trujillano et al. [19] and further supports the clinical utility of WES in diagnostically challenging presentations. As additional cases with specific genotypes are recognized, the phenotypic spectrum of individual conditions may continue to expand. Furthermore, we demonstrated novel mutations in both Han and Uyghur probands. Certainly, our methods could not discover new disease-causing variants or confirm novel phenotypes in known variants. The new mutations and clinical phenotypes we reported remain meaningful for giving an insight into the discovery of new diseases and improving the diagnostic yield of WES.

Finally, we did not identify any variants that could be confidently classified as founder variants specific to the Xinjiang region. Although our cohort is relatively large for a single-center pediatric study, the sample size may still be insufficient to robustly detect regional founder effects, particularly for rare MDs. Second, many of the pathogenic and LP variants identified in our cohort have been previously reported in public databases, suggesting shared genetic architectures with other populations rather than region-specific enrichment. Population-based studies may be required to systematically assess the presence of potential founder effects in Xinjiang. Participants in our study were treated at one comprehensive pediatric center in Northwest China. Adding cases treated in other regions with different ethnic backgrounds might expand the disease reference databases and improve the chief strengths of exome sequencing.

Conclusions

Our results indicated that exome genetic testing is of higher diagnostic yield and clinical utility for pediatric patients in Xinjiang, particular for those from consanguineous families. The findings also implicated that carrier screening would be most effective for preventing severe genetic disorders in Xinjiang. Revealing the Mendeliomes of the minority groups helps us expand the genotype and phenotype in the Chinese pediatric patient population.
