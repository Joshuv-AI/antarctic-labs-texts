Characteristics of Patients With Severe Hypochloremia in the Emergency Department: A Single Center Retrospective Study
Authors: Teppei Koyama, Naohiko Imai, Yohei Kita, Ryuichiro Makinouchi, Shinji Machida, Sayuri Shirai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsHypochloremia is an electrolyte disturbance in which the serum chloride concentration is abnormally low. Hypochloremia is associated with poor outcomes in critically ill patients in the intensive care unit (ICU) and coronary care unit (CCU), but little is known about its prevalence and prognosis in the emergency department (ED). We investigated the real‐world prevalence and prognosis of severe hypochloremia in the ED.MethodsAll adult patients (18 years old or older) with serum chloride levels measured at our ED between 2018 and 2023 were included. Hypochloremia and severe hypochloremia were defined as a serum chloride level of ≦97 and ≦80 mEq/L, respectively. Information collected included age, sex, serum chloride, and serum creatinine.Results11,065 adult patients had their serum chloride measured at the ED. The mean age of patients was 67 ± 20 years, and 53% were male. The mean serum creatinine and eGFR were 1.4 ± 1.8 mg/dL and 66 ± 48 mL/min/1.73 m2. The prevalence of hypochloremia and severe hypochloremia was 9.2% and 0.3%, respectively. A total of 8,135 adult patients were admitted, and the 30‐day mortality of patients with normochloremia and hypochloremia was 13% and 23%, respectively (p < 0.001). A total of 30 patients with severe hypochloremia were admitted, and 10 patients (33%) died within 30 days.ConclusionThe 30‐day mortality of patients presenting to the ED with severe hypochloremia was high. Hypochloremia may be associated with poor short‐term outcomes in patients presenting to the ED.

Introduction

Chloride is the second most abundant electrolyte in serum after sodium and plays an important role in the regulation of body fluids, electrolyte balance, and acid‐base balance [1]. Until now, clinical interest has focused on abnormalities in sodium and potassium rather than chloride, but recent studies have highlighted the prognostic significance of chloride in critically ill patients. Indeed, hypochloremia, where serum chloride levels fall below the normal range, has been associated with increased mortality in a variety of clinical settings, including intensive care units (ICUs) and coronary care units (CCUs) [2, 3, 4]. Depending on the definition of hypochloremia, the prevalence of hypochloremia in those settings has been reported to range from 6.7% to 14% [2, 3, 4]. In acute decompensated heart failure, hypochloremia was observed in about 10%–13% of patients [5, 6]. Lower admission chloride levels were independently and inversely associated with long‐term mortality, even after adjusting for sodium and renal function [6]. Chloride provided stronger prognostic information than sodium, highlighting its clinical relevance in risk stratification [6]. Despite this growing recognition of the prognostic value of chloride, hypochloremia is an electrolyte abnormality that is often overlooked in the Emergency Department (ED), and the prevalence and prognosis of hypochloremia in patients presenting to the ED have not been fully investigated. Therefore, this retrospective study investigated the prevalence and clinical outcomes of emergency department patients presenting with severe hypochloremia.

Material and Methods

We analyzed all adult patients (aged 18 years or older) who had their serum chloride levels measured at the emergency departments of our hospital between 2018 and 2023. Based on previous studies, normochloremia was defined as serum chloride levels of 98–106, hypochloremia as ≤ 97 mEq/L, and severe hypochloremia as ≤ 80 mEq/L [3, 4]. Patient data, including age, sex, serum chloride, and serum creatinine levels, were obtained from electronic medical records. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease (MDRD) study equation standardized to isotope dilution mass spectrometry, modified with coefficients for Japanese individuals as officially adopted by the Japanese Society of Nephrology, according to the following formula: eGFR (mL/min/1.73 m²) = 194 × (serum creatinine) −1.094 × (age) −0.287 × 0.739 (for women) [7]. As this was a retrospective study utilizing anonymized clinical data, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Statistical Analyses

Data analysis was performed using SPSS, Version 21.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY). Continuous variables were expressed as mean and standard deviation, and categorical variables as percentages. Comparisons of continuous variables were performed using Student's t‐test. Comparisons of proportions between groups were performed using the χ 2 test. A p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient Characteristics and Prevalence of Hypochloremia

A total of 11,065 adult patients had their serum chloride levels measured in the ED (Figure 1). The mean patient age was 67 ± 20 years, with 53% being male (Table 1). The mean serum creatinine was 1.4 ± 1.8 mg/dL, and the mean eGFR was 66 ± 48 mL/min/1.73 m². Among them, 6873 patients (62%) had normochloremia, with a mean age of 66 ± 20 years and 53% male. Their mean serum creatinine and eGFR were 1.3 ± 1.8 mg/dL and 67 ± 39 mL/min/1.73 m², respectively. Hypochloremia was observed in 1,014 patients (9.2%), who had a higher mean age (72 ± 16 years) and a higher proportion of males (60%). Their mean serum creatinine was 1.7 ± 2.4 mg/dL, and their mean eGFR was 64 ± 62 mL/min/1.73 m².

30‐day mortality of patients with normochloremia and hypochloremia

A total of 8125 patients were hospitalized following their ED visit. Among them, 4862 (60%) had normochloremia, while 837 (10%) had hypochloremia. The 30‐day mortality rate was significantly higher in the hypochloremia group (22%) than in the normochloremia group (13%) (p < 0.001).

Characteristics of Patients With Severe Hypochloremia

Of those who had their serum chloride measured in the ED, 31 (0.3%) patients had severe hypochloremia, and 30 patients were admitted (Table 2). The mean age of patients with severe hypochloremia was 67 ± 16 years, and 58% of patients were male. The 30‐day mortality of patients admitted with severe hypochloremia was 33%. The major symptoms of the patients were weakness (23%), cardiopulmonary arrest (19%), altered mental status (16%), dyspnea (16%), and loss of appetite (13%). Six patients (19%) were taking diuretics. 22 patients (71%) had severe hyponatremia (≦119 mEq/L), and six patients (19%) had moderate hyponatremia (120–129 mEq/L).

Discussion

In this large retrospective study of over 11,000 emergency department patients, we found that hypochloremia was present in 9.2% of cases, consistent with previous ICU studies reporting a prevalence of 8.8% [4]. Severe hypochloremia was detected in 0.3% of our cohort, and to our knowledge, this is the first study to describe its prevalence in the ED setting. The prognostic implications of these findings are noteworthy. A 30‐day mortality of 16% and 24% has been reported in patients with hypochloremia in the ICU and coronary care unit (CCU), respectively [2, 3]. In our study, the 30‐day mortality of patients with hypochloremia was 22%, which is comparable to these earlier observations, suggesting that the adverse prognostic impact of hypochloremia extends beyond critically ill populations to the broader ED setting. More strikingly, the 30‐day mortality in patients with severe hypochloremia reached 33%, a level substantially higher than that observed in patients with less marked chloride reduction. Although only a few studies have specifically reported short‐term mortality in patients with severe hypochloremia, the markedly elevated risk observed in our cohort appears reasonable given the central role of chloride in regulating body fluids, electrolyte balance, and acid–base homeostasis. Severe hypochloremia may therefore reflect profound systemic derangements, including impaired renal handling of electrolytes, diuretic‐associated chloride loss, and concurrent hyponatremia, all of which could contribute to its particularly adverse prognosis. Taken together, these results highlight the clinical relevance of chloride abnormalities in the ED and emphasize the need to recognize even relatively rare cases of severe hypochloremia. Severe hypochloremia may reflect profound systemic illness and may be associated with poor short‐term outcomes in patients presenting to emergency care.

Hypochloremia does not present with specific signs and symptoms. Hypochloremia may result from extrarenal or renal abnormalities in chloride intake or losses, or from changes in the total body water volume [8]. Severe hypochloremia of 48 mEq/L has been reported from vomiting [9]. In our study, four patients (13%) had a loss of appetite, which could cause severe hypochloremia due to decreased sodium chloride intake. Five patients (16%) had altered mental status, which could also be the cause of severe hypochloremia due to decreased sodium chloride intake. Six patients (19%) were taking diuretics. Loop and thiazide diuretics can potentially cause hypochloremia [10]. Hyponatremia often coexists with hypochloremia [11, 12]. Of those admitted with severe hypochloremia, in our study, 22 patients (73%) had severe hyponatremia (≦119 mEq/L), and six patients (20%) had moderate hyponatremia (120–129 mEq/L). The higher prevalence of hyponatremia in patients with severe hypochloremia was likely related to the severity of hypochloremia.

There are several limitations to this study. First, this was a single‐center study, and there is a possibility of selection bias in the patients enrolled. Second, as with all cross‐sectional studies, no definitive conclusions can be drawn regarding causal relationships between the parameters studied. Third, we extracted chloride data only at admission to the ED. Fourth, we did not perform multivariable regression analysis to adjust for potential confounders; therefore, the association between hypochloremia and 30‐day mortality should be interpreted with caution. Fifth, in our cohort, six patients with severe hypochloremia presented with cardiopulmonary arrest (CPA). When these cases were excluded, the 30‐day mortality was 17%. However, CPA cases were also present among patients with normochloremia and less severe hypochloremia, and we were unable to identify all CPA cases across these groups in the available dataset. Therefore, a direct comparison of mortality after excluding CPA patients between groups could not be performed.

In conclusion, severe hypochloremia in the ED was associated with higher 30‐day all‐cause mortality after admission. Our findings need to be confirmed in larger multicenter studies.
