A multi-layered burden or a right fit? Physicians’ perspectives on multidose drug dispensing in Denmark
Authors: Stine Søndersted Clausen, Cille Bülow, Tina Drud Due, Kirsten Høj, Anette Vik Jøsendal, Mette Bech Risør, Frans Boch Waldorff, Sofie Rosenlund Lau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMultidose drug dispensing (MDD) provides machine-packed, patient-specific dose pouches to improve medication safety and adherence, streamline medication management, and reduce pressure on healthcare systems. Despite the benefits, uptake in Denmark remains lower than in neighbouring countries, even after MDD prescriptions were integrated into the national electronic Shared Medication Record (SMR). This study explores general practitioners’ (GPs) and hospital physicians’ experiences with MDD following SMR integration.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted through semi-structured interviews with 15 physicians (8 GPs, 7 hospital physicians) from the Capital Region and the North Region in Denmark between November 2022 and May 2023. Data was analysed using systematic text condensation.ResultsTwo main code groups with six subgroups were constructed. “A Multi-layered Burden for Physicians”, reflects an experience of an increased workload and added complexity resulting from IT challenges, dosing period calculations, and inconsistent communication with pharmacies. “Finding the Right Fit”, highlights uncertainty about which patients and medications are suitable for MDD, together with the differentiated roles of healthcare professionals in managing the MDD system. While some physicians considered MDD beneficial for patients with complex needs, others found it unsuitable for those with fluctuating conditions, especially during hospitalisations.ConclusionAlthough MDD offers potential benefits, its practical implementation is constrained by digital, organisational, and interprofessional challenges. Assessing patient suitability is essential, but rather than excluding patients, the findings suggest reconceptualising MDD as a flexible, adaptable system, capable of meeting individual care needs and responding to changing clinical situations.Keywords: Multidose drug dispensing (MDD), Shared Medication Record (SMR), care transitions, primary care, interprofessional collaboration, medication management

Introduction

Multidose drug dispensing (MDD) is a healthcare service where patients’ medications are machine-dispensed into individual pouches (dose unit bags), called a dose roll, typically over a 14-day period [1–3]. Each dose unit bag contains the medications intended for a single intake time (e.g. morning, midday, evening) and is labelled with patient data, medication contents, and date and time of intake. MDD is implemented in both primary and secondary care settings in Denmark as well as in other countries such as Sweden, Norway, Finland, and the Netherlands [4–7]. Studies suggest that MDD enhances medication adherence, reduces medication administration errors, and simplify the medication administration process, particularly for home-dwelling patients with stable medication regimens [1,7–11]. Its adoption has also been seen as a potential solution to ease the growing strain on healthcare systems, especially given the shortages in healthcare staff [1,6,7]. By automating the packing and dispensing procedures, MDD may reduce the time healthcare workers spend on manual medication dispensing, primarily in the home care service, resulting in better resource utilisation [1,7].

The use of MDD was introduced in Denmark in 2002 and increased steadily until 2012, where over 60,000 citizens were using the solution [1,7,12,13]. Between 2011 and 2013, Denmark introduced an electronic Shared Medication Record (SMR), a national mandatory prescribing system, providing all citizens and healthcare professionals across settings with access to current medication data. However, MDD was initially not an integrated part of the SMR, resulting in different prescribing processes for MDD and ordinary prescriptions. It led to significant challenges in handling and, not least, communicating about MDD medication for physicians, municipalities, and pharmacies [7]. In this period, MDD usage declined to approximately 40,000 citizens per year in 2020 [1,7,13]. In June 2020, the MDD module was fully integrated into the SMR. This meant that citizens and all healthcare professionals such as general practitioners (GPs), pharmacists, hospital physicians and homecare staff got access to the same electronic MDD record, called ‘dosing card’, within their local electronic health record (EHR) [1,7,14].

Despite the improvements in shared information brought by the integration of MDD in SMR, only a slight increase in the use of MDD is observed in the recent years, and the adoption of MDD in Denmark still remains significantly lower than in the neighboring countries [7,13]. This suggests that the barriers to broader use extend beyond technical or IT-related issues [15,16]. Resistance from healthcare professionals, particularly physicians, has been suggested as a potential barrier to the implementation of MDD [1,17]. Reported challenges include an increased administrative load for the physicians and complexities associated with managing medication adjustments during care transitions, especially when patients are hospitalised [6,9,16,18]. Studies have shown that patients using MDD are at higher risk of medication errors during transitions [6,18,19], e.g. medication changes made during hospital stays are not always communicated to GPs or pharmacies, leading to discrepancies between a patient’s actual medication needs and the doses provided in the MDD system [6,18,19]. However, there is limited research on the experiences of both GPs and hospital physicians regarding challenges with MDD during care transitions. This study aims to explore physicians’ perspectives on MDD in Denmark, following its integration into the SMR, with particular focus on managing medication adjustments during care transitions. Gaining insight into physicians’ experiences with MDD is essential for identifying and addressing barriers to its adoption and for assessing MDD’s effectiveness within the healthcare system.

Materials and methods

Research design

We employed a qualitative research design, conducting semi-structured individual interviews with GPs and hospital physicians as part of a major ongoing qualitative study. The overarching rationale of the major study is to generate an understanding of how information about medication changes is shared between general practices and hospital settings in Denmark. The study explores the challenges and opportunities physicians encounter in this process by mapping how medication information circulates within and across healthcare sectors, and how these flows are shaped by both humans and technologies. It also examines physicians’ experiences of managing medication changes via the Shared Medication Record (SMR) including how coordination around the SMR is enacted in everyday practice.

The study focuses on the perspectives of GPs and hospital physicians, who are responsible for both prescribing and adjusting medications, including those dispensed through MDD. The interview guide (see appendix) was based on prior participant observations and designed to gain insights into the physicians’ experiences with cross-sectoral sharing of medication information, particularly regarding changes in patients’ medication. The guide was initially tested and refined through pilot interviews with two GPs and one hospital physician. Although MDD was not included in the original interview guide, the participants spontaneously identified it as a key factor influencing medication changes and the exchange of information during transitions between primary and secondary care. In response, the interview guide was revised during data collection to include the question: ‘What are your experiences with medication changes and MDD in transitions between hospitals and general practice?’ The participants’ strong interest in the topic led to MDD becoming a recurring theme, discussed in depth across the interviews, ultimately positioning it as the central focus of this study.

The major study is informed by theories of science and technology studies (STS) and information infrastructures [15,20,21], with a general focus on human-technology interactions. In the broader project, these theoretical perspectives provide a sensitising lens for understanding the complexities and dynamics of information exchange and for highlighting the socio-technical conditions shaping cross-sectoral medication information sharing. In the present article, however, STS functions primarily as a conceptual backdrop that informed the analysis and interpretation, rather than as an explicitly applied analytical framework.

MDD settings and procedures

To outline the context of the physicians’ perspectives, we provide an overview of the setting and procedures around MDD in Denmark. The target group for MDD consists of citizens on stable medication regimens without expected changes, primarily those receiving municipal home care or living in care homes or supported accommodation [22]. MDD is usually initiated by GP’s, with the citizen’s consent, often based on inquiries from municipal home care. MDD medication is dispensed by specialised pharmacies using automated packing systems, which prepare and deliver dose bags directly to citizens [1,14,23]. Regular pharmacies forward MDD prescriptions to these dispensing units. In secondary care, MDD is most often suspended during hospital admission, thereby halting the delivery of dose unit bags, and resumed upon discharge [1,24].

All MDD prescriptions (the dosing card) are managed electronically via the SMR, where physicians can renew, pause, or adjust prescriptions. The system provides information on the 14-day dose period, deadlines for prescription changes, and when changes take effect. Most medications in solid forms such as tablets and capsules can be included in MDD pouches, but some medications, such as antibiotics or medications sensitive to environmental conditions, are excluded due to contamination risks or stability issues [1,23]. Medications that cannot be dispensed in pouches must be managed separately in dose boxes and in the SMR (side dosing). Consequently, physicians must consider medication eligibility when making medication changes for MDD in the SMR. Medication changes are automatically updated and incorporated into the next scheduled dose roll. Urgent medication changes can be managed throughan ‘emergency order’, with replacement rolls effectuated within 24–72 h [1,14]. The SMR automatically sends a prescription renewal request approximately 40 days before an MDD prescription expires. Regarding costs, citizens pay for their medications plus a mandatory packaging fee per 14-day dose roll, amounting to approximately 390 DKK annually, subject to reimbursement schemes [2,7]. GPs receive a one-off fee of 450 DKK for initiating MDD, covering future adjustments. If MDD is discontinued by someone other than the patient’s own GP, and the municipal staff later request a re-initiation, the fee may be charged again [25].

Recruitment, setting, and data collection

Participants were selected from The Capital Region and The North Region of Denmark. Selecting these regions enabled us to include physicians operating under distinct organisational, geographical and demographic conditions, while keeping the study’s scope manageable. These regions represent, respectively, a geographically dispersed healthcare system, where GPs assume a broader range of responsibilities, and a dense, urban and highly specialised healthcare system, where physicians typically collaborate with a larger number of actors. This distinction led us to consider participants from these two regions sufficient for the purpose of the study.

A combination of purposive and snowball sampling was used to recruit an adequate number of participants with the specific knowledge and experience needed to address the overarching research question concerning the challenges and opportunities of sharing medication information between general practices and hospitals [26]. The purposive sampling deliberately sought diversity in speciality, seniority, organisational context and professional role to capture a broad spectrum of experiences. This variation facilitated the identification of both shared patterns and contrasting perspectives, thereby strengthening the analytical depth of the findings.

General practices and hospital departments were identified through public registers [27], local websites, phone calls, and emails. A total of 22 physicians were contacted via email or phone, resulting in the recruitment of seven hospital physicians and eight GPs, aged between 31 and 70 years, with clinical experience ranging from 3 to 39 years. Three GPs and four hospital departments (two surgical and one medical) declined participation due to COVID-19 constraints, work overload, or involvement in other studies. Among the participants, four held political roles, and three were leading senior physicians in major medical departments. The political roles encompass their involvement with medical professional associations and interest organisations that advocate for collaborative initiatives across local, regional, and national levels, including advisory roles to the government.

The interviews were conducted between November 2022 and May 2023. Notably, the interviewer (SC) had previously observed ten of the participants in their clinical settings, as part of the major study. The interviewer had no prior personal or professional relationship with the participants before conducting the observations.

Eleven interviews took place in the participants’ own clinics, while four preferred online interviews via Teams. The interviews lasted between 45 and 80 min and were recorded and transcribed verbatim by the interviewer.

Data analysis

Three of the authors (SC, MBR and SRL) read and discussed the transcribed material and participated in the data analysis. The transcripts were coded using NVivo 14 software. Data was analysed using systematic text condensation (STC), a thematic, cross-case method described by Malterud [26,28], which is suitable for maintaining an open inductive approach. The procedure, consisting of four steps, is exemplified in Figure 1. First, an overview of the transcriptions was created, and preliminary themes were identified concerning the physicians’ experiences with MDD. The data were reviewed multiple times to identify and categorise meaning units related to the preliminary themes. This process involved cross-case coding, and organising meaning units into code groups and subgroups, which was continuously discussed within the research group. Within the code groups, text was compiled to a collective condensate, written in the first person, reflecting the thematic content of the data. In the final step, condensates and quotations were transformed into analytical texts to describe the code groups. The analysis was conducted in Danish, and the results were later translated to English. All authors discussed the interpretations of the findings.

The first author’s background as a physician spans general practice and hospital care, and although not directly involved MDD after its integration into the SMR, a particular interest in rational pharmacotherapy provided insights into clinical routines and intersectoral coordination before initiating the study. This background shaped the study’s focus and interpretation, though the analysis was conducted collaboratively with an anthropologist and a pharmacist, broadening the lens toward social interaction, professional cultures, and medication practices. This composite positioning enabled attention to how prescribing practices, technological infrastructures, and professional roles are configured in clinical work.

Results

The analysis resulted in two code groups and six subgroups. The first code group, “A Multi-layered burden for Physicians,” encapsulates the physicians’ perceptions on how MDD increases workload and adds complexity to their daily routines. The second code group, “Finding the right fit,” reflects physicians’ perspectives on the eligibility of patients, types of medications, and professional roles in the context of MDD.

A Multi-layered burden for physicians

Overall, most physicians reported that managing patients on MDD created additional work for them, particularly in terms of administrative tasks. The term “multi-layered burden” reflects their perception that this increased workload arose from a combination of factors, including issues with technical solutions, manual calculations, and inconsistent communication. It was particularly complex around the time of a patient’s hospitalisation, even with minor medication adjustments. Most of the physicians found that the SMR in general worked well, but the ‘dosing card’ posed a significant challenge in the system. The technical aspects of prescribing, such as setting start and end dates, calculating weeks, and dealing with backorders (drug shortage) posed significant challenges. As a result, making dose prescriptions often required a lot of back-and-forth communication with pharmacies.

Technical solutions fail to fully support the dose dispensing

Most physicians reported that the IT systems, including the SMR and local solutions, did not sufficiently support the processes involved in managing MDD, particularly regarding medication changes. As one GP remarked,

The biggest challenge I’ve encountered with medication changes is undoubtedly MDD. It’s awful. Every time I deal with it, things go completely wrong in the SMR. Generally, I think the SMR works very well, but when it comes to MDD, it’s just a mess. The module is difficult to work with, and all sources of error multiply when MDD is involved. I think everyone who works with it gets a bit frustrated.

– GP (no. 4).

This statement was echoed by other GPs as well as hospital physicians, who found that the complexities of MDD amplified the potential for errors in the SMR. Hospital physicians highlighted numerous instances where, upon a patient’s hospitalisation, the technical aspects of the dosing card and SMR became problematic. They had to suspend the dosing card at admission and remember to reactivate it at discharge, which was complicated when medication changes were made. Some said that a single dose adjustment, such as adding or removing a medication, necessitated discarding all existing medication because the system seemed unable to modify one medication without resetting the entire dosing card.

Many GPs also noted that even minor medication adjustments could be extremely time-consuming. Some believed that the core issue lay in the technical structure of the dosing card itself, although most found it difficult to pinpoint exactly what made the process so challenging. It was widely reported that updating a dosing prescription often took considerably longer than issuing a standard prescription and was frequently accompanied by uncertainty, with comments such as: ‘I hope I’ve done it correctly.’ Some GPs pointed out that the dates recorded in the system did not always align with the actual start dates of the next dosing period. This made the process of managing medication changes particularly cumbersome, often necessitating multiple prescriptions for the same medication.

However, some GPs also reported a shift in their understanding and use of MDD in the SMR over time. Initially, they found it nearly impossible to grasp the technical workings of the system. Yet, with more frequent use, they developed greater confidence and a sense of satisfaction when their input appeared correctly in the SMR and matched the pharmacies’ dose periods. They explained that the more they used it, the more familiar they became with the technical possibilities and limitations, and the overall processes. This improvement was not consistent with the hospital physicians, who engaged less frequently with MDD and expressed that they never fully understood the technical processes.

The struggle with dose period calculations

One of the key issues physicians faced in managing medication changes in the dosing card was obtaining an overview of the dose periods. Both hospital physicians and GPs found it difficult to time and calculate when adjustments should take effect, especially when medications were prescribed in 14-day cycles, with variations in timelines across different pharmacies. Technically, managing the start and end dates when adding or modifying medications was considered challenging, particularly in cases involving multiple prescriptions that needed to commence at different times, including complex dosing regimens. This complexity is evident in one GP’s account:

The difficulty I face with time calculations is determining when the next dosing period starts. I can try to plan 14 days ahead, but this timing also shifts due to pharmacy opening hours, holidays, delays or backorders. For patients with frequent medication changes, waiting for the next dispensing isn’t practical. I may ask the patient when the next dose roll is due and decide if changes can wait. However, it becomes complicated when some adjustments are urgent, while others can be postponed. I always hope that the changes align with the end of a dosing period, making the transition smoother.

- GP (no.1).

This reflects the broader experience of most physicians, who found that while some medication changes could wait until the next dose roll (dosing period), this approach was often unsuitable for patients requiring frequent or multiple adjustments. Physicians could prescribe ‘emergency’ replacement rolls to implement changes immediately, but this process often caused uncertainty in managing the SMR and concerns about medication waste when patients still had remaining dose rolls at home. Hospital physicians also noted that patients often brought their dose rolls upon admission, which were frequently discarded directly into the bin. If MDD was not properly paused in the IT system, deliveries continued to the patient’s home, leading to further waste after discharge and raising questions about who should be held responsible for the cost of unused medication. Moreover, arranging ‘emergency’ replacement rolls required contacting the pharmacy, a process often regarded as complex and time-consuming. In addition, replacement rolls often required the involvement of home care staff, who either manually removed pills from existing dose rolls or dispensed new medications separately to accommodate changes until the replacement roll could be delivered.

Inconsistent communication routes to pharmacies

The physicians frequently encountered challenges with MDD and medication changes in the collaboration processes with pharmacies. Identifying and contacting the responsible pharmacy concerning medication changes often proved difficult, as many of the physicians could not find the information directly in their IT system. This became particularly challenging in hospitals, where patients often came from a variety of regions, turning the process into detective work. A hospital physician mentioned that, despite ongoing training on MDD procedures and the use of written correspondence instead of phone calls to contact pharmacies, they still struggled to identify the correct contact persons. Often only a specific individual at the pharmacy could provide assistance, making the process very time-consuming, as explained:

Collaborating with the pharmacies is extremely frustrating. I wish there were a more direct line of communication between me and the person responsible for MDD, so I wouldn’t have to spend time finding the right contact every time I need to make a change – Because I don’t do it – I skip contacting them or skip the MDD. We constantly try to follow the rules, but when it comes to MDD, we deviate from them, because we know that every time we encounter it, we sigh heavily.

– Hospital physician (no. 12).

This was also highlighted by many GPs, emphasising the importance of a more effective collaboration with pharmacies to implement medication changes. However, they noted that it often took months to determine a consistent approach, especially in the Capital Region, as the different pharmacies lacked a unified method for handling dose prescriptions. Additionally, some physicians reported inadequate communication with pharmacies regarding backorder issues and MDD, with delays in being informed about unavailable medications leading to unnecessary back-and-forth correspondence. Conversely, other GPs had established more effective collaboration with pharmacies that had implemented dedicated ‘dose-phonelines’ and structured communication. These GPs also reported improvements in the management of backorders, as pharmacies now contacted them directly with substitution proposals. Both hospital physicians and GPs proposed that pharmacies be authorised to independently substitute backordered medications without requiring prior approval from physicians, to further streamline the process.

Finding the right fit

The physicians identified several challenges related to MDD, arising from the varying characteristics of medications, patients, and healthcare professionals. These challenges were framed as a matter of ‘finding the right fit’, referring to difficulties in assessing which medications are appropriate for MDD and in identifying suitable patient candidates. This also extended to considerations of professional roles and responsibilities, particularly regarding which healthcare professionals are suited to manage MDD.

Medication eligible for plastic pouches

Physicians expressed frustration with not knowing which medications could be dose-packaged and which required separate dosing, often spending considerable time with prescriptions in the SMR on this issue. They requested a consolidated list to easily identify medication eligible for dose packaging. Although such a national list already existed [29], it was either poorly known or infrequently used due to many ‘clicks’ to access it and the lack of clarity in a long, unwieldy Excel spreadsheet. As a result, several physicians admitted to overlooking whether the medications they prescribed were suitable for dose packaging. The SMR did not always provide accurate warnings regarding medication eligibility, which subsequently led to pharmacies contacting the physicians for corrections. One GP explained:

The problems with medication changes multiply when it comes to MDD because I simply don’t know which medications can be placed in pouches and which cannot. Some medications, particularly sustained-release ones, are not suitable for MDD, but this isn’t something I can remember each time, I must look it up. So that’s what I spend my time on…

– GP (no. 7).

The situation became more complex when new medications were added. Physicians faced dilemmas of whether to include these in the dose pouches or to provide them separately as ‘side doses’ until it was clear if the patient tolerated the medications. Problems also arose when medications were discontinued, requiring physicians to notify homecare nurses to remove some of the pills from the existing pouches. They said that this task was particularly challenging when nurses were unfamiliar with the physical appearance of individual pills, complicating accurate identification and removal.

Additionally, several physicians noted an increasing demand from home nurses for dose packaging of redundant medications and over-the-counter products, such as various vitamins. While they acknowledged that including these items in the dose pouches might simplify administration, the physicians did not consider it their responsibility to manage them. They emphasised that the inclusion of such products made the medication list in the SMR longer and more confusing, necessitating further evaluation to determine the suitability of these products for dose dispensing.

Patients eligible for MDD

The physicians held strong, albeit differing, opinions on which patients were suitable for MDD. Hospital physicians, in particular, expressed that most of their patients were poor candidates for MDD due to unstable clinical trajectories requiring frequent medication adjustments. By contrast, one GP argued that all patients taking more than four medications could benefit from MDD.

Several physicians noted that MDD worked well for patients who were ‘stable’, such as home care patients who had been on unchanged medication regimens for years and were closely monitored by their GPs. However, they emphasised that hospital admission and frequent outpatient follow-ups typically signalled a loss of stability, as medication changes were almost always required. This pertained particularly to chronically ill patients with shifting needs. One hospital physician highlighted this perspective, reflecting an attitude shared by most hospital physicians:

Mentioning MDD to a hospital physician is like immediately raising the temperature into the red zone. For my patients, it does not work at all. All my patients are chronically ill, they have numerous conditions, and their status is unstable. We are constantly having to adjust their medication up and down, so MDD really feels like something created by the devil. When a patient is admitted to hospital, it is because they are ill, and this is precisely when the medication needs to be changed. That is why MDD is not suitable for these patients.

– Hospital physician (no. 10).

In such cases, hospital physicians often had to suspend or discontinue MDD and were uncertain about when, or whether, to resume it following discharge or during outpatient visits, as patient stability frequently remained unclear. This caused frustration among both hospital physicians and GPs, who perceived MDD as inflexible for the patients across care settings.

Conversely, some physicians argued that those who could benefit most from MDD were precisely the patients with the most complex medication regimens—those who were seriously ill, had multiple diagnoses, were frequently admitted to hospital, and might never achieve full stability. One GP stressed that it was a misconception to postpone or avoid initiating MDD while waiting for a patient’s condition to stabilize, highlighting the additional safety that MDD provides:

In my opinion, all patients taking four or more medications should have all their regular prescriptions dose dispensed, and this should be a clear criterion. This is particularly important for the elderly, who often struggle to manage their medications correctly, leading to serious errors and uncertainties. The added safety that MDD offers is substantial. There should be a strong and active justification for not using MDD, rather than clinging to outdated debates about its effectiveness and who should receive it.

– GP (no. 6).

This GP further argued that MDD should be continued during hospital stays, with necessary medication adjustments implemented in-hospital or communicated directly to the patient’s GP. The GP regarded MDD as the safest way to manage medications, even for patients with unstable conditions and frequent medication changes. However, this presupposed that physicians were familiar with MDD and that it had been effectively adapted to the clinical context. Although this view was not universally shared, most GPs agreed that a greater number of patients could benefit from MDD, raising ongoing questions as to whether it was the patients themselves or the clinical contexts that were not well suited to its use.

Healthcare professionals eligible for MDD

The debates associated with MDD extended beyond specific patient groups and contexts, touching on broader professional concerns that elicited strong emotions among the physicians. Both hospital physicians and GPs expressed frustration that managing MDD fell outside their core clinical responsibilities, pointing to tasks such as deciding on the packaging of medications, understanding pharmaceutical physical properties and assortments, and handling logistical considerations. This tension was evident in hospital settings, where physicians often suspended or stopped MDD to accommodate medication changes, without reactivating it upon discharge. This left GPs with the responsibility of reconciling discrepancies and re-establishing the dosing card, deepening their sense of frustration with MDD. A GP highlighted these frustrations:

We have a ‘heart patient’ who uses MDD. It’s been a complete mess because the hospital can’t manage to get the MDD to work properly. They just make regular prescriptions without including them in the dosing card, and I end up having to get involved. I’ve made it clear that I don’t want to deal with MDD for this patient, as his heart medication is constantly being changed. The patient insists on having it, so I’ve told him that the hospital should handle it.

– GP (no. 4).

Both GPs and hospital physicians expressed dissatisfaction with the inefficiencies and unclear responsibilities in the MDD process, particularly during transitions of care and when multiple health care professionals were involved. They reported limited insight into each other’s workflows, often lacking awareness of how the MDD system functioned for others. Hospital physicians and current hospital guidelines [24] suggested that GPs, as the primary care providers, should assume responsibility for managing MDD and reactivate the dosing card, believing it would streamline the process after discharge. Conversely, most GPs argued that hospitals should ensure accurate MDD medication management during hospital stays and outpatient visits. They expressed that all physicians should be responsible and eligible to manage MDD and have oversight of the entire MDD process, thereby ensuring more accurate collaboration and smoother transition for patients between sectors.

Physicians across both sectors also advocated for pharmacies to assume greater responsibility, particularly in maintaining accurate MDD regimens. They argued that pharmacists, with their expertise, were better equipped to manage the practical aspects of MDD, including determining which medications are eligible for MDD, adjusting dispensing periods, managing backorders, and facilitating communication and support between sectors. This reallocation of responsibilities would allow physicians to devote greater attention to direct patient care. Collectively, both GPs and hospital physicians emphasised the need for clearer role definitions and enhanced mutual understanding among healthcare professionals to improve coordination of MDD across sectors (Figure 2).

Discussion

The findings of this study can be viewed in the context of existing national and international literature, which broadly acknowledges both the potential and the complexity of implementing multidose drug dispensing (MDD) within modern healthcare systems [3,5,16,18,30–33]. There is broad consensus that MDD might enhance medication safety, adherence, and resource efficiency [1,5–11,31]. Nonetheless, several studies have highlighted substantial intersectoral challenges, including inadequate communication, insufficient follow-up, heightened risks of polypharmacy and inappropriate prescribing, and medication discrepancies during transitions of care [6,18,19,33]. In addition to these findings, this study highlights that the practical implementation of MDD remains challenging, particularly due to the increased administrative burden on physicians, technical challenges, and difficulties in intersectoral coordination, including agreeing on suitable patient populations.

Increased workload and technical challenges

Although policy narratives frequently depict MDD as a strategy to enhance efficiency and alleviate resource constraints, especially for nurses in home care settings [1,7], such representations may neglect the considerable preparatory and follow-up tasks imposed on physicians and other healthcare professionals. Rather than reducing the overall workload, MDD tends to redistribute tasks and responsibilities across sectors. Our findings support existing concerns that MDD increases both workload and complexity, especially where digital systems are poorly understood or inadequately integrated [16,30,33–35]. Physicians described a “multi-layered burden” associated with managing MDD, which stemmed from administrative demands, technical constraints, and fragmented communication with limited understanding of MDD workflows across sectors. They emphasised that the additional tasks in managing MDD, especially around hospitalisation and dosage adjustments, were insufficiently supported by current digital systems.

These challenges are closely linked to the technical infrastructure underlying MDD. Since MDD is built on an older SMR platform that must interface with general practice systems, municipal electronic care records, hospital electronic patient records, and pharmacy IT systems, technical difficulties are likely to occur at the transition points between these infrastructures [14,15]. Consistent with studies on information infrastructures, the effectiveness of MDD is thus shaped by existing standards, system architecture, and organisational setups [15,20,21]. These aspects are typically beyond physicians’ awareness or control, yet they can conflict with clinical workflows, increasing the risk of errors and contributing to clinician dissatisfaction, as also observed in prior research on the implementation and utilisation of healthcare IT systems [15]. However, cross-country comparisons should be approached with caution, as national IT systems and digital infrastructures vary significantly.

Professional roles and collaboration

Historically, MDD transitioned from a pharmacist-led, safety-oriented initiative to a more logistics-focused service [7,9]. This reflects broader trends in healthcare rationalisation and has significantly reshaped professional roles and organisational cultures surrounding medication management. Early evaluations of MDD implementation in Denmark, dating back over two decades, already highlighted the need for stronger collaboration with pharmacies and clearer delineation of health care professionals’ roles and responsibilities [9]. Physician resistance was notable even then, suggesting a persistent scepticism rooted in the perception that MDD imposes time consuming tasks beyond the traditional scope of medical practice. Despite the subsequent integration of MDD into the SMR, our study shows that many of the same challenges remain. Difficulties in coordination, such as the synchronisation of dispensing cycles with pharmacy workflows, illuminate structural shortcomings that become particularly pronounced when multiple healthcare professionals are involved. These findings reinforce previous calls for streamlined communication infrastructures and more clearly defined roles in MDD management [3,32,36,37]. Across both primary and secondary care, physicians continue to advocate for a clearer division of labor, with many supporting a more prominent role for pharmacists in managing the technical and logistical components of MDD, thereby enabling physicians to focus more fully on clinical decision-making [6,37].

Patient suitability and adaptation to a clinical context

In this study there is a divergence in how MDD is perceived by the physicians. Some regard MDD as a valuable tool for enhancing medication safety, even for patients with complex needs. In contrast, others - particularly hospital physicians - often consider it too rigid for acute and outpatient settings where frequent medication changes are necessary. A recurring issue is the difficulty physicians experience in agreeing on suitable candidates for MDD. MDD is primarily designed for older adults with stable conditions and consistent medication regimens [22]. In practice, however, many patients enrolled in MDD are multimorbid and take multiple medications [32], and its effectiveness declines during periods of clinical instability, such as hospitalisations [6]. In these situations, MDD may need to be paused or significantly modified, resulting in care discontinuities and, as this study shows, also increased physician workload. This creates a paradox: although these patients are the intended beneficiaries of MDD, they often receive suboptimal support under current models. Rather than excluding them, the findings underscore the need to reconceptualise MDD as a dynamic and adaptable system, capable of responding to changing and individualised care needs and diverse clinical contexts. A possible solution may lie in the dissemination of unit-dose dispensing systems in hospital settings, where daily medications are prepared and dispensed in-house based on up-to-date prescriptions [38]. Ultimately, it is worth remembering that if healthcare professionals themselves find MDD complex to manage, it raises important concerns about how patients are expected to maintain oversight of their own medication regimens.

Implications for practice

The integration of MDD into the SMR has made underlying challenges in medication management more apparent. The findings demonstrate that effective medication coordination depends not only on technical solutions, but also on how clinical work is organised, how responsibilities are allocated across care settings, and on established professional expectations. Attending to these interdependencies is essential for strengthening coordination and developing a more robust, patient-centred medication infrastructure.

Safe and effective use of MDD requires enhancing healthcare professionals’ understanding of MDD processes, alongside clearer definition of roles across the entire MDD trajectory. In this context, pharmacies may take a more active role in maintaining accurate medication lists and managing technical and logistical aspects of MDD. Greater visibility of the MDD infrastructure also raises fundamental questions about the purpose of MDD and about who ultimately benefits from its implementation, underscoring the need for greater transparency regarding its practical consequences. Many patients receiving MDD have complicated and fluctuating medication needs that current systems struggle to accommodate. Rather than treating MDD as a static solution for a narrowly defined patient group, it should be understood as a flexible, interprofessionally coordinated component of a broader medication safety strategy.

These insights provide a foundation for identifying concrete areas where practice can be strengthened. Based on the findings and the preceding discussion, the following action points outline key directions for improving the implementation and use of MDD in clinical practice.

Strengths and limitations

The study provides valuable insights into how general practitioners and hospital physicians experience and work with MDD, related technologies, and the associated communication processes. By combining semi-structured interviews with prior observations in both general practice and hospitals, the study captures practical and organisational challenges, as well as the interactions between professional roles and digital infrastructures.

The diverse sample in terms of age, gender, professional experience, and geographical location, together with the inclusion of both GPs and hospital physicians, allowed for a comprehensive understanding of perspectives on MDD use across general practice and hospitals. The first author’s medical background facilitated participant access and contextual understanding. While this familiarity enhanced interpretation of the material, it also carried a risk of unexamined assumptions. This was mitigated through close collaboration and critical discussion within the research team to ensure reflexivity. The use of systematic text condensation further supported a rigorous, reflexive, and transparent analytical process, making it well-suited for researchers with a medical background.

Several limitations should be noted. Informants’ perspectives were shaped by their personal experiences, local healthcare structures, and varying familiarity with digital systems such as the SMR. The possibility of selection bias cannot be excluded, as physicians with particularly strong opinions may have been more inclined to participate. Additionally, participants in managerial or political roles may have expressed views influenced by organisational or strategic considerations. The study’s primary focus on hospitals and general practices, along with the absence of perspectives from other healthcare professionals and patients, may further limit the scope of the findings. Nevertheless, the data provides valuable insights into similar healthcare contexts and highlight areas for potential improvements.

Conclusion

This study illustrates the multifaceted challenges that hospital physicians and GPs encounter with MDD in Denmark. Despite the intended benefits, real-world use is hampered by complex digital and communicative infrastructures, and a misalignment with the needs of patients with multiple conditions. Physicians often experience MDD as challenging and at times burdensome, particularly during hospitalisations and care transitions, and feel unsupported by current organisational and technological systems, including the SMR. Importantly, the prevailing notion that MDD is only suitable for stable patients is challenged by our findings. Some physicians advocated for broader inclusion of patients, suggesting that even patients with fluctuating medication needs could benefit from a more adaptable and supportive MDD model across sectors.
