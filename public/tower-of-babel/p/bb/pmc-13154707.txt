The effect of traumatic birth perception and fear of childbirth on cesarean section intention
Authors: Betul Uncu, Emine Serap Cagan, Rukiye Duman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe aim of this study is to examine the effect of traumatic birth perception and fear of childbirth on the intention to undergo cesarean delivery in primiparous women.MethodsThe study is a descriptive cross-sectional. The study was conducted between April and July 2025 with 311 primiparous pregnant women. Data were collected using the “Information Form,” “Traumatic Birth Perception Scale,” “Wijma Birth Expectation/Experience Scale,” and “Cesarean Intention Scale.”ResultsThe average score for pregnant women on the Traumatic Birth Perception Scale is 126.70 ± 5.80, indicating that their perception of traumatic birth is very high. The average score for pregnant women on the Wijma Childbirth Expectancy/Experience Scale is 77.20 ± 11.98. Pregnant women experience moderate levels of fear of childbirth. The average score for the “Cesarean Intention Scale” among pregnant women is 38.76 ± 6.13. When examining the correlation analysis between scale scores, a positive and significant relationship was found between the Wijma Childbirth Expectancy/Experience Scale and the Cesarean Intention Scale (rs = 0.533; p < 0.001). As pregnant women’s fear of childbirth increased, their positive attitude toward cesarean delivery also increased.ConclusionThis study found that pregnant women have high levels of fear of childbirth and that fear of childbirth has a significant and positive effect on the intention to undergo a cesarean section. The findings suggest that interventions aimed at reducing fear of childbirth during pregnancy may be important in reducing cesarean section rates.Keywords: Traumatic birth, Perception of traumatic birth, Wijma childbirth expectation, Wijma childbirth experience, Fear of childbirth, Cesarean intention

Introduction

Pregnancy and childbirth are not only a physiological experience for women, but also an important turning point that has profound psychological effects. While some women experience this process with happiness and excitement, others may experience negative emotional side effects such as fear of childbirth and traumatic birth perceptions [1, 2]. Women’s traumatic birth experiences are often shaped by intense fear, pain, violation of privacy, and inadequate support during childbirth. These experiences can lead to psychological problems, fear of childbirth, and even a tendency to delay or completely reject pregnancy in some women [3]. Indeed, it has been found that anxiety and depression symptoms are more intense in pregnant women with a high level of traumatic birth perception, whereas pregnant women with a low level of traumatic birth perception have higher birth satisfaction [4].

When women perceive childbirth as traumatic, they often associate it with factors such as loss of control, fear for the baby’s health, and intense pain [5]. In addition, there are other individual and environmental factors that influence the perception of childbirth as traumatic. Factors such as an individual’s personality traits, previous birth experiences, birth stories heard from others, the meaning society attaches to childbirth, cultural norms, and exposure to negative birth narratives in the media play an important role in the traumatic perception of childbirth [6].

Fear of childbirth has been associated with factors such as previous negative birth experiences, poor mental health, and inadequate social support. This fear significantly influences the demand for cesarean sections. This situation demonstrates that the psychosocial risks causing fear of childbirth are identifiable and that this fear can be an influential factor in elective cesarean section decisions [7]. Research shows that fear of childbirth and the perception of traumatic birth are decisive factors in pregnant women’s decisions regarding the mode of delivery. In particular, cesarean delivery may be preferred as an escape or a control mechanism by many women who are trying to cope with childbirth fear [8, 9].

Cesarean section rates have risen rapidly worldwide since 1990, with 21.1% of births occurring by cesarean section as of 2018. Regional differences are striking, with rates ranging from 5% in sub-Saharan Africa to 42.8% in Latin America. It is projected that this rate will rise to 28.5% by 2030 [10]. In line with this global upward trend, cesarean section rates are also quite high in Turkey. Between 2018 and 2023, 57.55% of the 6,161,976 births in Turkey were cesarean sections, and the primary cesarean section rate was reported to be 28.83% [11].

A large-scale study conducted in Turkey found that 42.4% of pregnant women experience fear of childbirth. Among the situations that women fear most are the development of unwanted complications during childbirth, uncertainty, indecision about the method of delivery, negative childbirth experiences, and not feeling prepared for childbirth [12]. Women who perceive childbirth as traumatic may view cesarean delivery as a solution to cope with these feelings and regain a sense of control. In this context, it is important to examine the effect of traumatic birth perception and fear of childbirth on women’s intentions to undergo cesarean delivery. This study aims to examine the effects of traumatic birth perception and fear of childbirth on pregnant women’s intentions to undergo cesarean delivery. Understanding the effects of these emotional processes on birth preferences can contribute to the development of healthier, more informed, and supportive approaches to childbirth.

Design

The study employed a descriptive cross-sectional design.

Sample

The study population consists of pregnant women who visited the obstetrics clinics of Agri Education and Research Hospital for follow-up and underwent NST (Non-Stress Test); the sample consists of primiparous pregnant women. The average number of primiparous pregnant women receiving services from the monthly NST unit at the hospital is 900. According to the sample size calculation based on a known population, a minimum of 252 primiparous pregnant women was required to achieve a statistical power of 90%. The study was completed with 311 primiparous pregnant women who met the inclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria for the study: being primiparous, being 18 years of age or older, and being in the second or third trimester of pregnancy. Exclusion criteria for the study were a history of uterine surgery.

Data collection tools

The “Information Form,” “Traumatic Birth Perception Scale,” “Wijma Birth Expectation/Experience Scale,” and “Cesarean Intention Scale” were used as data collection tools in the study.

Information form

This form, prepared by researchers in line with the literature, includes descriptive questions such as the age of pregnant women and their partners, educational status, gestational age, and income level [6, 13].

Traumatic birth perception scale

The scale developed by Yalnız et al. [14] to measure women’s general perceptions of childbirth consists of 13 items. Scores on the scale range from 0 to 130. As scores on the scale decrease, traumatic perceptions of childbirth decrease. The scores obtained from the scale are evaluated as follows: 0–26 is very low, 27–52 is low, 53–78 is moderate, 79–104 is high, and 105–130 is very high traumatic birth perception. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale is 0.895. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was found to be 0.845.

Wijma birth expectation/experience scale

It was developed by Wijma et al. [15] to measure women’s fear of childbirth. It was adapted into Turkish by Körükçü and colleagues [16]. The scale consists of 33 items. The minimum score on the scale is 0, while the maximum score is 165. As the score increases, so does the fear of childbirth experienced by women. Cesarean intention scale

It was developed by Naghibi et al. [18] to measure pregnant women’s intention to choose cesarean section. Taşkın et al. [19] conducted a Turkish validity and reliability study. A high score on the scale indicates that women have a more positive attitude toward cesarean section. The Cronbach’s alpha value was found to be 0.821 in the adaptation of the scale into Turkish. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was calculated as 0.966.

Statistical analysis

SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) Version 25.0 software was used for statistical analysis of the data. Continuous variables were presented as arithmetic mean (x̄) and standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were presented as number (n) and percentage (%). Skewness and kurtosis tests were performed to assess the normality of continuous variables. The analysis revealed that the data did not follow a normal distribution. Therefore, nonparametric tests were used. Spearman’s correlation analysis was used for correlation analysis in the study. Since the data did not meet the normality assumption, the robust regression analysis method was used for multiple regression analysis. Robust regression analysis is used to obtain more reliable results in situations where the assumptions of classical regression methods (especially the Ordinary Least Squares - OLS method) are violated. In this study, the MM-estimator was used as the robust regression technique. The MM-estimator is an advanced method used in robust regression analysis and was developed specifically to produce results that are both robust to the presence of high outliers and statistically efficient [20, 21].

Results

When examining the demographic characteristics of the participants, the mean age of pregnant women was 22.81 ± 1.82 years, the mean age of their partners was 24.96 ± 1.67 years, and the mean gestational week was 34.90 ± 3.13. More than half of the pregnant women (59.2%) and the majority of their partners (92%) were employed. Most of the participants (40%) had a high school education, 70.7% lived in nuclear families, and 91.6% had planned their pregnancies. Nearly all women (89.7%) received prenatal care, and 65.6% attended antenatal classes. Overall, 98.4% of the participants reported fear of childbirth, and 13.2% were considering a cesarean delivery (Table 1).

When the relationships between demographic variables and scale scores were examined, significant associations were identified between age, education, employment status, and childbirth-related attitudes. Older pregnant women and their partners showed higher levels of fear of childbirth (rs = 0.151, p < 0.001; rs = 0.238, p < 0.001), and higher maternal age was also associated with a more positive attitude toward cesarean section (rs = 0.167, p < 0.001). Working women had lower levels of fear of childbirth (Z=-2.109, p < 0.001) and more positive attitudes toward cesarean section (Z=-4.210, p < 0.001) than non-working women. As the educational level of pregnant women and their partners increased, fear of childbirth decreased (p < 0.001), whereas women with lower education levels showed a more positive attitude toward cesarean birth. These and other demographic comparisons are presented in Table 1.

The mean scores of the scales were as follows: Traumatic Birth Perception Scale: 126.70 ± 5.80 (indicating a very high level of traumatic birth perception), Wijma Birth Expectation/Experience Scale: 77.20 ± 11.98 (moderate level of fear of childbirth), Cesarean Intention Scale: 38.76 ± 6.13 (Table 2). Correlation analysis revealed a moderate positive relationship between fear of childbirth and cesarean intention (rs = 0.533, p < 0.001), whereas no significant correlation was found between traumatic birth perception and cesarean intention (rs = 0.034, p = 0.551) (Table 3). As pregnant women’s fear of childbirth increased, their positive attitude toward cesarean section also increased.

Discussion

The increasing rate of cesarean sections worldwide is noteworthy [22]. This increase is not limited to medical outcomes; the intensive use of medical interventions and hospital resources also creates an indirect environmental burden in terms of global climate change [23]. Among the factors affecting cesarean section rates, traumatic birth perceptions and fear of childbirth among pregnant women stand out. While pregnancy and childbirth are met with happiness and excitement for some women, for others, they can be shaped by negative emotional experiences such as fear of childbirthand traumatic birth perception [1, 2]. Fear of childbirth and traumatic birth perception play a decisive role in pregnant women’s preferences for the mode of delivery [7].

However, as the current study reveals, this relationship may not always be straightforward and may vary across sociocultural contexts. Perceptions of birth and the emotional responses surrounding it are shaped by a complex interplay of psychological, cultural, and contextual factors. Studies show that low levels of comfort during birth increase the perception of birth as traumatic, leading to postpartum trauma symptoms [24]. Similarly, health anxiety experienced during pregnancy increases the perception of birth as traumatic and increases levels of postpartum depression, anxiety, and stress [25]. Furthermore, prospective healthcare professionals who witness birth may also be affected by these experiences, and their perceptions of birth may be shaped during this process [26]. Furthermore, culturally sensitive, non-clinical interventions, such as birth dance, can increase comfort and reduce the perception of birth as traumatic [27]. These findings reveal that women’s birth experiences and perceptions are context-dependent and shaped by their psychological states and sociocultural environment.

Many women who have had a negative birth experience develop a traumatic birth perception. However, traumatic birth perception is not limited to women who have experienced childbirth; it can also be observed in individuals during childhood and adolescence, or in nulliparous women who have not yet given birth [28, 29]. In the current study, it was determined that primiparous pregnant women have a very high level of traumatic birth perception. Bay and Sayıner [1] found that 26.9% of women experienced a high level of traumatic birth perception. Another study found that women postponed their pregnancy plans or did not want to become pregnant due to fear of childbirth associated with traumatic birth [3]. Ünal et al. [30] reported that as traumatic birth perceptions increased, belief in and preference for vaginal birth decreased; conversely, the likelihood of choosing cesarean birth increased. However, this study found no relationship between traumatic birth perceptions and the intention to have a cesarean section.

Another important finding of our study was that pregnant women had moderate levels of fear of childbirth and intention to undergo cesarean delivery, and that as fear of childbirth increased, their positive attitude toward cesarean delivery also strengthened. Our study determined that a significant proportion of primiparous pregnant women experience fear of childbirth. Similarly, Gün and Ege [13] also found that primiparous pregnant women experience moderate levels of fear of childbirth. When the fear of childbirth remains at a certain level, it can help the woman prepare for birth and adapt to the process, while an increase in the fear of childbirth can lead women to choose a cesarean section. Weeks et al. [31] found a significant association between childbirth anxiety and the preference for cesarean delivery, with individuals experiencing childbirth anxiety being four times more likely to prefer cesarean delivery. Our study shows similar results to other studies and, as noted in the literature, fear of childbirth is one of the major reasons for choosing cesarean delivery.

It is important to determine women’s cesarean section intentions and to identify the factors that influence their choice of cesarean section to reduce the increasing cesarean section rates [32] Research has determined that many factors, including sociodemographic characteristics, culture, beliefs, women’s cesarean section preference, and social media, play a role in women’s cesarean section intentions. For example, age, education level, employment, and participation in prenatal education programs have been associated with preferences for cesarean Sects. [32, 33]. Our findings also highlight that women who are unemployed, less educated, or who have not attended prenatal education courses are more likely to have a cesarean section. These findings suggest that educational and social interventions for expectant mothers may be effective in reducing the demand for unnecessary cesarean sections.

A significant relationship has been observed between women’s and their partners’ fear of childbirth, and fear has been found to increase the tendency for both partners to choose a cesarean Sect. [34]. Therefore, treating the couple as a unit in prenatal counseling may be an effective strategy to reduce fear of childbirth and promote informed decision-making. Factors that increase fear of childbirth include the age of the pregnant woman and her partner, the pregnant woman’s employment status, the partner’s employment status, whether the pregnancy is spontaneous, and whether the pregnancy is high-risk [12, 35, 36]. Inconsistencies between studies may be due to differences in sample characteristics and the inclusion or exclusion of psychosocial factors such as anxiety, self-efficacy, and perceived social support.

The study found that women who obtained information about childbirth from social media had higher levels of fear surrounding childbirth. Social media can create additional pressures on women by shaping the perception of normal, physiological childbirth within an ideological framework, and the medicalized narrative of childbirth has become dominant on these platforms. At the same time, social media can both support and limit women’s access to information and their autonomy in making decisions related to childbirth [37]. Among Norwegian first-time pregnant women who use social media, it has been found that anxiety during pregnancy and postpartum is common and particularly pronounced among women without economic security [38].

Overall, our findings highlight that the perception of traumatic birth and fear of childbirth are multidimensional constructs influenced not only by personal experiences but also by sociocultural, educational, and media-related factors. In the Turkish context, where medical birth practices are common, addressing these perceptions through midwife-led prenatal education, respectful birth care, and public health messaging may help normalize vaginal birth and reduce unnecessary cesarean sections. Future studies should explore culturally tailored interventions that strengthen women’s self-confidence and autonomy during childbirth.

Conclusion

Reducing anxiety in subsequent pregnancies for women with traumatic birth perception is extremely important for both the mother and the baby. The study found that pregnant women had very high levels of traumatic birth experiences, moderate levels of fear of childbirth, moderate levels of intention to have a cesarean section, and that as their fear of childbirth increased, they showed a more positive attitude toward cesarean sections. For women who have experienced traumatic childbirth, continuous and holistic care provided by experienced midwives with counseling skills can help women strengthen their sense of control, make decisions about their birth plans, and have a positive experience by bridging the gap between past trauma and subsequent childbirth. Ensuring access to an evidence-based, multidisciplinary education program that includes the voices of women with birth trauma experiences should be a priority for professional development for all birth care providers.

Limitation

The fact that the study was conducted at a single center and with a specific sample group constitutes its limitations.
