Effects and mechanism of carrier-free self-assembled nanoparticles of natural products in attenuating pulmonary edema
Authors: Jin Yan, Kaixin Liu, Yuqi Yan, Zhenzhen Wang, Junfang Wang, Yingbo Wang, Yuanyuan Hou, Erwei Hao, Xiaotao Hou, Jie Gao, Gang Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary edema, a life-threatening condition in acute lung injury/acute respiratory distress syndrome (ALI/ARDS), is driven by dysregulated inflammation, barrier disruption, and alveolar fluid accumulation. Effective therapies that simultaneously target these multiple pathological processes with high lung specificity are urgently needed.ResultsWe engineered carrier-free, self-assembled nanoparticles from the natural products magnolol (Mag) and atractylenolide I (ATI) at an optimal 4:1 molar ratio. The Mag-ATI nanoparticles (MA NPs) exhibited an average hydrodynamic diameter of approximately 220.2 nm, excellent colloidal stability, and a pH-responsive release profile favorable for the acidic pulmonary microenvironment. The drug loading was 81.7 ± 0.356% for Mag and 18.2 ± 0.129% for ATI, encapsulation efficiency exceeded 97% for both. In a murine lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced ALI model, MA NPs potently ameliorated pulmonary edema, outperforming individual drugs or their physical mixture. Biodistribution studies confirmed the MA NPs’ efficient lung accumulation, with a fluorescence intensity 3.6–5.9 times higher than in other organs, and specific targeting to alveolar epithelial cells. Mechanistically, Mag inhibited TRPV4-mediated Ca2+ influx, thereby suppressing the downstream cAMP/AQP5 axis to limit water permeability and restore alveolar fluid balance. Mag and ATI attenuated inflammatory signaling via the protein kinase B (AKT)/nuclear factor kappa B (NF‑κB) pathway. Furthermore, Mag and ATI preserved epithelial barrier integrity by stabilizing tight junction proteins. These multi-modal mechanisms were systematically validated both in vitro and in vivo. Importantly, MA NPs demonstrated a favorable safety profile with no significant systemic toxicity.ConclusionThis study presents a novel, carrier-free nanotherapeutic platform that integrates the efficacy-enhancing rationale of traditional herbal medicine with advanced nanoassembly. By enabling coordinated modulation of inflammation, barrier function, and fluid homeostasis, MA NPs offer a potent, targeted, and translational strategy for the treatment of ALI/ARDS.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04777-5.Keywords: Pulmonary edema, Carrier-free nanoparticles, Natural product, Self-assembly, TRPV4, AQP5

Background

Pulmonary edema, a life‑threatening clinical hallmark of acute lung injury/acute respiratory distress syndrome (ALI/ARDS), remains a critical therapeutic challenge with high mortality worldwide [1]. The COVID‑19 pandemic has further underscored the urgent need for effective interventions against ALI/ARDS‑related pulmonary edema [2]. Pathologically, dysregulated inflammation triggered by direct or indirect insults disrupts the alveolar‑capillary barrier, increases vascular permeability, and leads to alveolar fluid accumulation, a process governed by epithelial sodium channels (ENaC), Na⁺/K⁺‑ATPase, and aquaporin‑5 (AQP5) [3, 4]. Central to this cascade is the overactivation of the transient receptor potential vanilloid 4 (TRPV4) channels, which drives pathological Ca²⁺ influx, disrupts tight junctions, promotes inflammatory cascades, and dysregulates AQP5‑mediated water transport, collectively exacerbating edema and impairing gas exchange [5, 6].

Current management relies mainly on supportive care, including lung‑protective ventilation, fluid restriction, and pharmacotherapy such as glucocorticoids, β₂‑agonists, and diuretics [7]. While these approaches provide symptomatic relief, they do not adequately address core pathological drivers such as TRPV4 overactivation or AQP5 mislocalization. Emerging targeted agents (e.g., TRPV4 antagonists) and biological therapies (e.g., stem cells, JAK inhibitors) remain under investigation [8, 9], yet a clinically viable strategy that simultaneously restores barrier integrity, resolves inflammation, and normalizes fluid homeostasis is still lacking.

Natural compounds with multi‑target potential offer a promising therapeutic avenue. Magnolol (Mag), derived from Magnolia Officinalis Cortex (Magnolia officinalis), acts as a TRPV4 inhibitor and exerts anti‑inflammatory effects [10]. Atractylenolide I (ATI), derived from Rhizoma Atractylodis Macrocephalae (Atractylodes macrocephala), displays anti‑inflammatory, antioxidant, and mild aldosterone‑modulating diuretic properties [11, 12]. Crucially, the concomitant use of their parent herbs constitutes a classic TCM compatibility for regulating respiratory fluid, a practice famously exemplified in the Sanhan Huashi Formula for treating lung exudation [13]. This clinical evidence provides a robust theoretical rationale for combining Mag and ATI, suggesting that their joint application could exert improved efficacy against pulmonary edema. However, both compounds suffer from poor solubility and low bioavailability, limiting their translational potential.

Nanotechnology provides a powerful means to overcome these limitations. In particular, carrier-free self-assembled nanodrugs, composed solely of active pharmaceutical ingredients, offer distinct advantages, high drug loading capacity [14], straightforward fabrication, and avoidance of carrier-related toxicity [15]. Such systems not only enhance bioavailability but also enable targeted pulmonary accumulation, deep alveolar penetration, and pH‑responsive drug release [16]. To date, however, few studies have integrated the efficacy-enhancing rationale of traditional herbal pairing with modern carrier‑free nanoassembly to precisely target the multi‑faceted pathology of pulmonary edema.

Here, we design and evaluate carrier‑free self‑assembled nanoparticles (MA NPs) based on the coordinated combination of Mag and ATI at an optimized molar ratio. We hypothesize that MA NPs will effectively attenuate pulmonary edema by concurrently targeting TRPV4‑mediated barrier disruption, inflammatory signaling, and fluid transport dysregulation. Using a combination of computational simulation, in vivo imaging, transcriptomics, and molecular assays, we elucidate the assembly mechanism, pulmonary targeting behavior, and multi‑modal therapeutic actions of MA NPs in LPS‑induced ALI models. This work not only presents a novel nanotherapeutic strategy but also bridges traditional compatibility theory with contemporary nanobiotechnology for the treatment of ALI/ARDS.

Animals

Six-week-old KM male mice of specific pathogen-free (SPF) grade weighing 25 ± 2 g were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. Mice were housed in a controlled environment meeting SPF condition, maintained at 23 °C with a 12 h light/dark cycle. All animal procedures complied with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care Committee of Nankai University (Approval No. 2024-SYDWLL-000776).

Cell culture

Murine Lung Epithelial-12 (MLE-12) cells were purchased from Fuheng Biology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 medium (DMEM/F12, Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Wuhan, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, TransGenBiotech CO., LTD, Beijing, China) and penicillin-streptomycin solution (P/S, a mix of 10,000 IU/mL penicillin and 10,000 µg/mL streptomycin, TransGenBiotech CO., LTD, Beijing, China). Wuhan, China) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. All cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified environment with 5% CO₂.

Preparation of MA NPs and p-MA NPs

To prepare MA NPs, Mag and ATI were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to obtain a 10 mg/mL stock solution. Subsequently, 7.7 µL of Mag, 2.2 µL of ATI, and 10 µL of DMSO were thoroughly mixed. The drug mixture was then slowly added to 2 mL of water at a uniform rate under ultrasonic conditions to form MA NPs. After dialysis to remove the extra organic solvents, the MA NPs were freeze-dried with a freeze dryer (Thermo Scientific, USA) and stored at -20 °C for later use.

The alkyne-modified Mag (Al-Mag) probe which can click with azide fluorescent substrate BDP [10], and vinyl ether-modified ATI (Ve-ATI) probe which can photo-crosslink with AMPD molecule [17], were respectively synthesized as detailed in Additional file 1 (Fig. S1, S2). To indicate the distribution of MA NPs, MA probe nanoparticles (p-MA NPs) were prepared. The preparation of p-MA NPs was in accordance with that of MA NPs.

Characterization of MA NPs

The MA NPs were systematically characterized to assess their physicochemical properties and performance. Hydrodynamic diameter, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential were measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS; Zetasizer Nano ZS, Malvern Panalytical, UK) using a suspension of 10 mM MA NPs. The optimal formulation was selected based on the criteria of smallest size, lowest PDI, and highest absolute zeta potential. Storage stability of MA NPs was evaluated over 7 days at 25 °C by monitoring daily changes in size and PDI.

The morphology of the selected formulation was examined using cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (Cryo-TEM; Talos L120C G2, FEI, Czech Republic). Samples were prepared by applying 10 µL of MA NPs suspension onto a glow-discharged grid, blotting, and plunge-freezing in liquid ethane. MA NPs stability was confirmed by a monomodal DLS size distribution and a distinct Tyndall effect when illuminated with a 650 nm laser.

The chemical stability of MA NPs in different solvents (DMSO, NaCl, SDS) was evaluated by comparing UV-Vis spectra before and after addition. To verify nanoparticle formation and chemical integrity, the optimized MA NPs, Mag and ATI were compared using Fourier Transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) and proton nuclear magnetic resonance (¹H NMR). For FT-IR, freeze-dried NPs were mixed with KBr (1:100, w/w), pressed into pellets, and analyzed (Bruker TENSOR 37). For ¹H NMR analysis, 3 mg of freeze-dried MA NPs were dissolved in DMSO‑d6 and analyzed by an AVANCE NEO 400 MHz spectrometer (Bruker, Switzerland).

Molecular dynamics simulation of MA NPs

Molecular dynamics simulations were performed in GROMACS 2023.02 using the OPLS-AA force field with 1.2 CM5 charges. Initial systems were prepared by homogeneous mixing in Packmol. Energy minimization (3,000 steepest descent steps) was followed by 100 ps NVT/NPT equilibration at 298.15 K (1.0 fs timestep). Production runs (50 ns NPT, 2.0 fs timestep) employed periodic boundaries, with temperature maintained at 298.15 K (V-rescale thermostat, 0.5 ps coupling) and pressure at 1 bar (Parrinello-Rahman barostat, 2.0 ps coupling). Trajectories were analyzed using GROMACS and Visual Molecular Dynamics (VMD) 1.9.3.

Drug release evaluation of MA NPs

The drug release profile was evaluated using dialysis under different pH conditions. Briefly, the pH of distilled water was adjusted to 5.4, 6.5, and 7.4 using hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide. Three milliliters of MA NPs suspension (100 mM) were placed in a dialysis bag (molecular weight cutoff: 1000 Da). The dialysis bag was immersed in 30 mL of the corresponding release buffer and stirred in a water bath at 37 °C. At predetermined time intervals (0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, and 24 h), 1 mL of the external medium was sampled and replaced with fresh buffer. The cumulative release was determined by measuring the absorbance at 290 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Evolution 201, Thermo Fisher Scientific). All release experiments were performed in triplicate.

Nanoparticle biodistribution

To assess the biodistribution of nanoparticles in vivo, mice were divided into probe tracing and background control groups. The probe tracing groups received intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of p-MA NPs (10 mg/kg), Al-Mag (8 mg/kg), or Ve-ATI (2 mg/kg), respectively. Correspondingly, the background control groups received i.p. injections of MA NPs (10 mg/kg), Mag (8 mg/kg), or ATI (2 mg/kg), respectively. All injections were administered three times at 8-hour intervals. Two hours after the final dose, the main organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, were collected and processed for detecting MA-probe, Al-Mag, or Ve-ATI via click chemistry. For the precise cellular localization of MA NPs in lungs, the tissues sections were co-stained with antibodies against E-cadherin (epithelial cell marker) or α-SMA (smooth muscle cell marker) before the click reaction.

LPS-induced pulmonary edema and treatment evaluation in mice

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of MA NPs on pulmonary edema, mice were randomly assigned to nine groups: Con, Mod, Spi (20 mg/kg), Dobu (5 mg/kg), MA NPs (10 mg/kg), MA NPs (3.3 mg/kg), Mag (8 mg/kg), ATI (2 mg/kg), and Mag (8 mg/kg) + ATI (2 mg/kg). Spi, an aldosterone receptor antagonist representing diuretic therapy [18] and Dobu, a β₁ receptor agonist representing inotropic therapy [19], which cover two distinct clinical strategies for pulmonary edema, were used as positive controls. Doses were selected based on the results of preliminary experiments and the previously reported effective ranges [20–22]. Acute lung injury was induced by intratracheal instillation of LPS (10 mg/kg) [23], while the Con group received saline as a sham treatment. Immediately after modeling and again 8 h later, different drugs were administered via i.p. injections.

After 24 h in metabolic cages, urine was collected for volume measurement. Mice were then euthanized, and blood and lung tissues were harvested. The lung weight/body weight (LW/BW) ratio and lung wet/dry weight (W/D) ratio were determined to assess edema. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was analyzed for total protein and inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) by ELISA. Lung lobes were either fixed for H&E staining and histopathological scoring (Szapiel and Ashcroft scores, Additional file 1) or snap-frozen for further analysis [24, 25]. Serum aldosterone (ALD) levels were measured using corresponding assay kit.

Barrier integrity was assessed via immunofluorescence (IF) detecting ZO-1, and AQP5 expression was examined by IHC on adjacent sections. AKT and NF-κB signalings were evaluated using IF. Standard protocols included deparaffinization, antigen retrieval, blocking with 5% goat serum, and overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by appropriate secondary reagents [10]. Images were captured using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope and analyzed with ImageJ.

Transcriptomic analysis of lung tissue

Lung samples from the Con, Mod, and MA NPs-10 mg/kg treatment group were collected for transcriptomic analysis following the previously described procedures [26]. Fresh tissues were processed by Majorbio (Shanghai, China) for RNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis. Differentially expressed genes between groups (Mod vs. Con, Mod vs. MA NPs) were screened out using p < 0.05 and |log2(fold change)| > 1.2, and visualized as volcano plots. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed and visualized via the Majorbio Cloud Platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com/page/tools/).

Evaluation of anti-inflammatory effects in cells

To examine the anti-inflammatory effect of MA NPs at the cellular level, MLE-12 cells were evenly seeded in a 96-well plate and divided into 8 groups: Con, Mod (LPS 10 µg/mL), TAK242 (10 µM), Mag (8 µM), ATI (2 µM), and MA NPs (10, 1, 0.1 µM). Preliminary CCK‑8 assays indicated that Mag and ATI did not significantly affect cell viability at concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 100 µM (Additional file 1: Fig. S3 E, F). After 12 h treatment, cell culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged. The levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β were quantified using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Trans-epithelial electrical resistance (TEER) measurement

To assess cell barrier integrity, the resistance of the monolayer of cell culture was measured using a Millicell ERS-2 V-Ohm meter (Millipore, Massachusetts, USA). MLE-12 cells were seeded on collagen-coated 0.4 μm pore filters in 24-well plates and cultured for 5–7 days to form a confluent monolayer. TEER stabilized over 2 days, confirming barrier formation. During treatment, 1.4 mL of drug-containing medium was added to the basolateral chamber and 600 µL to the luminal chamber. Then the TEER was measured at various time points.

Dual-luciferase assays of TRPV4-mediated calcium influx and cAMP signaling

To improve transfection efficiency, HEK‑293T cells were used in place of MLE‑12 cells. HEK-293T cells were seeded evenly into 96-well plates and divided into nine groups, including one non-transfected control group (Blank) and eight groups transfected with the TRPV4 plasmid. The eight transfected groups consisted of Con and Mod groups, groups treated with the TRPV4 inhibitor GSK2193874 (GSK219), Mag, or ATI, and MA NPs groups (10, 1, 0.1 µM).

To monitor intracellular calcium and cAMP levels, the reporter plasmids pGL4.30[luc2P/NFAT-RE/Hygro] (pGL4.30, E8481) and pGL4.29[luc2P/CRE/Hygro] (pGL4.29, E8471) were purchased from Promega (Madison, WI, USA). Lipofectamine™ 3000 Transfection Reagent (L3000015) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). For calcium measurement, the above cells were co-transfected with the pGL4.30 firefly luciferase reporter plasmid, and a Renilla luciferase plasmid (as an internal control). After being transfected for 12 h, cells (except for the Blank and Con groups) received a 6 h drug pre-treatment and were then stimulated with LPS (10 µg/mL, 2 h). Subsequently, a dual-luciferase assay was performed, and firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity to correct transfection efficiency. cAMP signaling was determined in the same way, except that the pGL4.29 reporter plasmid, instead of pGL4.30, was co-transfected together with TRPV4 and the Renilla luciferase plasmid.

Real-time calcium flux assay, IF staining and Western blotting

For calcium imaging, MLE-12 cells were seeded in 48-well plates. Cells were pretreated with the indicated compounds for 6 h, followed by co-incubation with LPS and the respective compounds for another 12 h. Subsequently, cells were loaded with Fluo-4/AM containing Pluronic F-127, and real-time intracellular Ca²⁺ fluctuations were recorded upon stimulation with the TRPV4 agonist GSK1016790A (GSK101).

For immunocytochemistry, MLE-12 cells cultured in confocal dishes were pretreated for 6 h with TAK242 (10 µM), Mag (8 µM), ATI (2 µM), and MA NPs (10, 1, 0.1 µM). This was followed by co-stimulation with LPS (10 µg/mL) and the corresponding compounds for 12 h. Cells were then fixed, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100, and blocked. Samples were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C and secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. To assess AQP5 membrane localization, non-permeabilized cells were stained with an anti-AQP5 antibody and DiI for membrane labeling, utilizing GSK219-10 µM as a positive control. Images were acquired using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope and analyzed via ImageJ.

For protein analysis, membrane proteins were extracted via freeze-thaw cycles followed by gradient centrifugation for AQP5 detection, whereas total cell lysates were prepared for ZO-1, AKT signaling, and NF-κB signaling detection. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, and probed with specific primary antibodies. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) and quantified with ImageJ. TAK242 (10 µM) was served as a positive control for p-AKT and p-NF-κB assays, while GSK219 (10 µM) for AQP5 and ZO-1 analysis.

Hydrochloric acid aspiration-induced pulmonary edema and treatment evaluation in mice

To further evaluate therapeutic efficacy, a hydrochloric acid (HCl) aspiration-induced lung injury model was established. Mice were randomly assigned to six groups: Con, Mod, Dex (30 mg/kg), MA NPs (40 mg/kg, 20 mg/kg, and 10 mg/kg). Acute lung injury was induced through a single intratracheal instillation of HCl (0.1 M, 1.5 mL/kg), while the Con group received saline as a sham treatment. Drug administration of MA NPs was performed in the same way as described for the LPS model. For short‑term evaluation, mice were sacrificed at 24 h post‑injury. For subacute evaluation, a separate cohort was monitored for 1 week with once‑daily i.p. administration of MA NPs. At each endpoint, lung edema (LW/BW and W/D ratios), BALF total protein and cytokines (TNF‑α, IL‑1β, IL‑6), and pulmonary function parameters [forced vital capacity (FVC), dynamic compliance (Cdyn), airway resistance (Re) and lung resistance (RL)] were assessed. Histopathological changes and collagen deposition were also evaluated by H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining.

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). A Student’s t-test was utilized to compare two datasets, whereas one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied for the comparison of multiple datasets. All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.5 (La Jolla, CA, USA). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Optimization and characterization of MA NPs

MA NPs were prepared by self-assembly of Mag and ATI at various molar ratios (Mag: ATI, 8:1 to 1:8) in aqueous solution (Fig. 1A). Among the formulations tested, the 4:1 ratio yielded the smallest particle size and the highest zeta potential (Fig. 1B, C), and was therefore selected as the optimal formulation. This ratio also demonstrated the strongest efficacy in reducing LPS-induced pulmonary edema in mice, as evidenced by significant decreases in both LW/BW ratio (Fig. 1D) and W/D ratio (Fig. 1E). The synergistic effects on TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6 release, as well as on TEER, were also verified in LPS-treated MLE-12 cells (Fig. S3). Furthermore, the drug loading of 81.7 ± 0.356% for Mag and 18.2 ± 0.129% for ATI, and encapsulation efficiency of 97.6 ± 0.658% and 97.9 ± 1.43%, respectively (Additional file Table. S1). The optimized MA NPs exhibited a spherical morphology under TEM (Fig. 1F), with an average hydrodynamic diameter of 220.2 ± 7.07 nm, a PDI of 0.226 ± 0.061, and a zeta potential of -20.37 ± 0.042 mV (Fig. 1F, G). A clear Tyndall effect was observed, indicating good colloidal stability (Fig. 1G), which was further supported by consistent particle size over 7 days in aqueous buffer (pH 7.4, Fig. 1H). The stability of MA NPs in PBS, plasma and BALF was also confirmed (Fig. S4).

To evaluate the potential for microenvironment-responsive drug release, in vitro release profiles were examined under different pH conditions. As shown in Fig. 1I, MA NPs displayed a pH-dependent release pattern, with markedly accelerated release at pH 5.4, particularly within the first 0.5–2 h, compared to release at physiological pH 7.4. Given that pulmonary infection decreases the pH of the alveolar microenvironment to approximately 6.0 [27], these results suggest that MA NPs are capable of pH-responsive drug release at the site of infection injury.

Self-assembly mechanisms of MA NPs

The self‑assembly mechanism of MA NPs was investigated using a combination of spectroscopic techniques and computational simulation. UV-vis spectroscopy under various conditions revealed distinct environmental responses. In DMSO, peak broadening and a blue shift indicated partial disassembly, likely due to disrupted hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions. In NaCl solutions, reduced peak intensity reflected salt-dependent weakening of hydrophobic and hydrogen-bond networks from charge shielding. With SDS, a pronounced red shift suggested surfactant-driven molecular rearrangement, highlighting the role of hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 2A–C). FTIR analysis further revealed significant spectral shifts (Fig. 2D). The -OH stretching vibration of Mag shifted from 3152.60 to 3154.40 cm⁻¹, which implies an altered hydrogen-bonding environment. The C-H stretching peak of ATI shifted from 2937.43 to 2933.84 cm⁻¹, possibly indicating changes in hydrophobic domain packing or van der Waals interactions. A slight shift of the ester C=O stretch from 1771.90 to 1772.10 cm⁻¹ was also observed, potentially mirroring weak dipole interactions or subtle changes in hydrogen bonding.

Self-assembly mechanisms of MA NPs. (A-C) UV-vis absorption spectra of MA NPs or free Mag in different dispersion media. (D) FTIR spectra of Mag, MA NPs, and ATI. (E) ¹H NMR spectra of Mag, MA NPs, and ATI. (F) Proposed self-assembly process of MA NPs. (G) Computed 3D structures of MA NPs

Further insight was provided by ¹H NMR spectroscopy, showing clear changes in proton signals associated with the phenolic hydroxyl of Mag and carbonyl groups of ATI. The ¹H NMR and ¹³C NMR spectra of Mag, ATI, and MA NPs are provided in Additional file 1 Fig. S5 and Fig. S6. The loss or decrease of characteristic NMR signals indicates intermolecular hydrogen bonding between Mag (δ 9.00) and ATI (δ 5.76, 4.90, 4.66). A new averaged signal at δ 3.28 (d, 8 H) reflects reorganization of the hydrogen-bonding network. These spectral changes provide direct evidence for intermolecular hydrogen bonding during self-assembly (Fig. 2E). Molecular dynamics simulations over 50 ns further confirmed the role of specific non‑covalent interactions. Stable hydrogen bonds and π–π stacking between the aromatic rings of Mag and ATI were observed throughout the simulation, supporting a cooperative assembly process (Fig. 2F, G). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the self‑assembly of MA NPs is driven by a combination of directional hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and π–π stacking, which together confer structural stability and microenvironment responsiveness to the nanoparticles.

MA NPs ameliorate LPS-induced pulmonary edema in mice

The therapeutic efficacy of MA NPs was assessed in a murine model of LPS-induced pulmonary edema (Fig. 3A), using Spi and Dobu as positive controls. Histopathological analysis via H&E staining revealed that LPS challenge induced pronounced inflammatory cell infiltration and alveolar wall thickening in the Mod group (Fig. 3B). Histopathological evaluation was demonstrated in Fig. 3C, based on a semi-quantitative scoring system (Additional file 1). Consistent with these structural alterations, the LW/BW ratio and W/D ratio were significantly elevated in Mod mice compared to Con group, confirming successful establishment of pulmonary edema (Fig. 3D, E). Treatment with MA NPs substantially alleviated these pathological features, demonstrating stronger therapeutic effects than either Mag (8 mg/kg) or ATI (2 mg/kg) alone, or their physical mixture group (Mag 8 mg/kg + ATI 2 mg/kg) (Fig. 3B–E), indicating that the nanoformulation enhances efficacy beyond simple drug combination.

The levels of the pro‑inflammatory cytokines TNF‑α, IL‑1β, and IL‑6 in BALF were significantly elevated in the Mod group but were suppressed by MA NPs in a dose‑dependent manner (Fig. 3F–H). MA NPs also reduced the total protein concentration in BALF, indicating reduced pulmonary leakage (Fig. 3I). Additionally, MA NPs at the higher dose exhibited a mild diuretic effect (Fig. 3J). Although serum ALD was not significantly elevated in LPS-challenged mice, a slight decrease was still observed after treatment with MA NPs (Fig. 3K), consistent with the previously reported ALD-suppressing activity of ATI [12]. Given that ALD was not pathologically upregulated in this model, this modest reduction might be an auxiliary fluid- and electrolyte-regulating effect of ATI. In addition, compared with the Mod group, MA NPs decreased serum sodium and increased serum potassium (Fig. 3L, M), while decreasing urinary potassium and increasing urinary sodium (Additional file 1: Fig. S7A, B). These findings collectively suggest that MA NPs promote natriuresis and potassium retention, thereby helping to restore fluid and electrolyte balance.

MA NPs target alveolar epithelial cells in ALI mice

To track the in vivo distribution of MA nanoparticles, we prepared p-MA NPs by simultaneously coupling with two probes (Al-Mag and Ve-ATI), respectively. Click reaction and phot-crosslinking enabled p-MA NPs to be efficiently coupled with BDP (Ex: 546 nm, Em: 572 nm) and AMPD (Ex: 351 nm, Em: 385 nm) (Fig. 4A, B). This allows simultaneous detection of Al-Mag and Ve-ATI at different fluorescence wavelengths. The particle size and potential of p-MA NPs were similar to those of MA NPs (Fig. 4C). The biological equivalence of p-MA NPs and MA NPs was confirmed by the comparable effects of anti-inflammation and cellular barrier integrity between them in LPS-treated MLE-12 cells (Additional file 1: Fig. S8A–E).

Subsequently, paraffin sections of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) from mice treated with p-MA NPs or MA NPs were used to evaluate the in vivo distribution of MA NPs. Subtract the basal fluorescence values of the corresponding MA NPs group from the fluorescence values of the p-MA NPs group organs to obtain the normalized fluorescence intensity. The results indicated that both Al-Mag and Ve-ATI in p-MA NPs accumulated more in the lungs than in other organs (Fig. 4D, E, Additional file 1: Fig. S9A, B). Moreover, formulating Mag and ATI into nanoparticles enhanced lung accumulation more effectively than using either agent alone (Additional file 1: Fig. S9D, E).

Nanoparticle accumulation in the lungs is significantly influenced by pulmonary physiology and particle characteristics. The preferential accumulation of MA NPs in inflamed lungs is likely attributable to passive extravasation driven by inflammation‑enhanced permeability [28]. The lack of mucociliary clearance in the alveoli leads to a prolonged retention of nanoparticles [29]. Moreover, appropriately sized and surface-modified nanoparticles are capable of evading alveolar macrophage uptake [30]. To further explore which cells can take up the MA NPs in the lungs, immunofluorescence staining was carried out on mouse lung paraffin sections to detect the colocalization of p-MA NPs with various cell markers. In comparison with the smooth muscle cell marker α-SMA, p-MA NPs (both Al-Mag and Ve-ATI) exhibited a higher level of colocalization with the alveolar epithelial cell marker E-Cadherin (Fig. 4F, G, statistical analysis in Additional file 1 Fig. S9C). Moreover, the uptake of MA NPs was also observed in MLE-12 cells. The disassembly of the two probes increased over time, demonstrated by a decreasing Pearson Correlation Coefficient (PCC) value (Fig. 4H). These results demonstrated that MA NPs specifically target alveolar epithelial cells, internalized in the form of nanoparticles and subsequently disassembled, to exert their effects separately.

Transcriptomic profiling reveals multimodal mechanisms in combating pulmonary edema

To systematically elucidate the therapeutic mechanisms of MA NPs, we performed transcriptomic analysis of lung tissues from LPS‑challenged mice. Principal component analysis (PCA) showed clear separation among the Con, Mod, and MA NPs groups (Fig. 5A). Differential expression analysis identified 3,260 up‑ and 3,474 down‑regulated genes in Mod vs. Con mice. MA NPs treatment counteracted this dysregulation, with 225 genes up‑regulated and 674 down‑regulated in the Mod vs. MA NPs comparison (Fig. 5B, C). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis highlighted that LPS challenge activated key pathological pathways involved in inflammation (NF‑κB signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway) and barrier disruption (Focal adhesion, ECM‑receptor interaction, Calcium signaling pathway, PI3K‑AKT signaling pathway). MA NPs treatment effectively normalized these pathways while simultaneously influencing calcium and cAMP signaling cascades (Fig. 5D, E).

(B) Volcano plot of differentially expressed genes between the Mod and Con groups. (C) Volcano plot of differentially expressed genes between the Mod and MA NPs treated groups. (D) KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes between Mod and Con groups. (E) KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes between Mod and MA NPs treated groups. The pathways in red are inflammation-related, whereas the ones in blue are barrier integrity-related

Specifically, suppression of Focal adhesion and ECM‑receptor interaction indicates improved cell‑matrix adhesion and epithelial anchoring [31, 32]. Down‑regulation of Calcium signaling reduces a critical trigger of vascular permeability [33], while modulation of PI3K‑AKT signaling attenuates inflammatory alveolar leakage [34]. Furthermore, enrichment of aldosterone‑regulated sodium reabsorption pathways aligns with the known role of ATI in regulating aldosterone synthesis, supporting local fluid homeostasis [12]. Importantly, MA NPs also suppressed the calcium and cAMP signaling cascades, which contributes to mitigating the excessive accumulation of pulmonary edema [35]. Collectively, transcriptomic data demonstrate that MA NPs ameliorate pulmonary edema through coordinated regulation of three core pathological processes: suppression of inflammatory signaling, restoration of alveolar barrier integrity, and reduction of alveolar fluid accumulation. The multi‑modal mechanism underlines the comprehensive therapeutic potential of MA NPs in treating pulmonary edema.

MA NPs preserve alveolar epithelial barrier integrity via inflammation suppression and Mag-mediated calcium regulation

Guided by the anti‑inflammatory signature identified in transcriptomic analysis, we evaluated the effect of MA NPs on cytokine production in LPS‑stimulated MLE‑12 alveolar epithelial cells. ELISA of cell supernatants confirmed that MA NPs (10 µM) significantly attenuated the release of pro‑inflammatory cytokines (TNF‑α, IL‑1β, and IL‑6), outperforming equivalent doses of Mag (8 µM) or ATI (2 µM) alone (Fig. 6A–C). These in vitro results align with the reduced cytokine levels observed in BALF from MA NPs‑treated mice. Western blot analysis revealed that MA NPs effectively suppressed LPS‑induced phosphorylation of AKT and NF‑κB p65 (Fig. 6D, E). Consistent with the transcriptomic pathway enrichment, MA NPs also inhibited NF‑κB nuclear translocation (Fig. 6F, G), confirming the downregulation of inflammatory signaling observed in vivo. Although both Mag and ATI possessed certain anti-inflammatory effects, MA NPs exerted more potent anti-inflammatory activity. These data confirm that the co-delivery of Mag and ATI within a single nanoplatform generates a synergistic effect greater than that of either compound alone.

Disruption of the alveolar epithelial barrier is a central event in pulmonary edema formation. We evaluated the barrier‑protective effect of MA NPs by measuring TEER in LPS‑stimulated MLE‑12 monolayers. LPS challenge markedly reduced TEER over 24 h, whereas both Mag and ATI, particularly Mag, partially restored resistance. MA NPs (10 µM) exhibited a time‑dependent and superior barrier‑restoring effect, recovering TEER to 76.03% of baseline at 24 h, significantly outperforming Mag (63.82%) or ATI (46.33%) alone (Fig. 7A, B).

Immunofluorescence and Western blot analyses of the tight junction protein ZO‑1 also corroborated these findings. LPS severely disrupted ZO‑1 localization at cell borders, while MA NPs treatment effectively preserved its distribution and expression, again showing greater efficacy than individual compounds (Fig. 7C, D).

Previously, we have reported that Mag functions as a TRPV4 antagonist to inhibit the pathological influx of Ca²⁺ to alleviate pulmonary fibrosis in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [10]. Notably, the pathological Ca²⁺ influx is a crucial factor driving junctional disassembly and increased permeability in pulmonary injuries [36]. Therefore, the barrier-protective action of MA NPs could be partially attributed to the calcium regulation by Mag. Simultaneously, anti-inflammatory molecules impede signaling pathways such as NF-κB, reducing the release of pro-inflammatory factors and alleviating tissue inflammation, thus promoting lung barrier repair [37]. This mechanism was supplemented by the anti-inflammatory activity of ATI, which alleviated cytokine-induced barrier damage [38]. Collectively, the two components of MA NPs offer coordinated protection for alveolar epithelial integrity via inflammation suppression and calcium regulation.

MA NPs rectify alveolar fluid accumulation by modulating the calcium-cAMP-AQP5 axis

Building on MA NPs’ role in calcium regulation indicated by transcriptomics, we first assessed their effect on TRPV4‑mediated Ca²⁺ influx. Live‑cell calcium imaging in LPS‑treated MLE‑12 cells showed that MA NPs dose‑dependently suppressed the Ca²⁺ influx induced by the TRPV4 agonist GSK101, an effect primarily attributable to the Mag component (Fig. 8A, B). Given the known interplay between Ca²⁺ and cAMP in alveolar fluid transport [39], we next examined calcium fluxes and cAMP dynamics using TRPV4‑overexpressing HEK-293 cells transfected with calcium or cAMP‑sensitive reporters. During the treatment process, LPS stimulation elevated intracellular cAMP levels, which changed synchronously with calcium influx. This increase was concentration‑dependently reversed by MA NPs, and the effect was comparable to that of Mag or the TRPV4 inhibitor GSK219 (Fig. 8C, D). The lack of additional modulation by ATI confirms that the regulatory effect is mainly driven by Mag through TRPV4/Ca²⁺ inhibition.

We then evaluated the functional consequence of this signaling modulation on AQP5, which is the main water channel on alveolar epithelial cells. AQP5 translocate to the plasma membrane under the regulation of cAMP [40]. Notably, a 2 h LPS challenge was found to exacerbate this membrane translocation of AQP5, in accordance with previous report [41], which can lead to alveolar fluid accumulation [42]. Western blot analysis of membrane fractions revealed that LPS increased AQP5 membrane localization, while normalized by MA NPs treatment (Fig. 8E). Additionally, immunofluorescence further demonstrated that LPS induced translocation of AQP5 to the plasma membrane, quantified by increased PCC with the membrane marker DiI. MA NPs significantly attenuated this aberrant localization, primarily driven by Mag (Fig. 8F, G). These data demonstrate that MA NPs, chiefly through Mag, inhibit TRPV4‑mediated Ca²⁺ influx, downregulate consequent cAMP elevation, and thereby prevent membrane accumulation of AQP5. This coordinated regulation of the calcium-cAMP-AQP5 axis helps rectify alveolar fluid homeostasis and contributes to the anti-edema action of MA NPs. Given that alveolar fluid clearance (AFC) was not directly measured, future studies employing ex vivo lung perfusion to quantify AFC will be essential to provide stronger evidence for validation.

MA NPs suppress inflammation, repair the alveolar barrier, and down-regulate AQP5 with high biosafety

To confirm the key regulatory mechanisms in vitro, we performed parallel analyses in lung tissues from LPS-challenged pulmonary edema mice. Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that MA NPs treatment significantly reduced the levels of p-NF-κB p65 (Fig. 9A) and p-AKT (Fig. 9B), while effectively restoring the continuous alveolar expression of the tight junction protein ZO-1 (Fig. 9C). Immunohistochemistry revealed that LPS-induced upregulation of AQP5 was markedly attenuated by 10 mg/kg MA NPs administration (Fig. 9D). Additionally, the effect of AQP5 regulation by MA NPs was also verified by the preserved regulatory volume decrease (RVD) function in LPS‑treated MLE‑12 cells under hypotonic challenge (Additional file 1: Fig. S10). These results collectively verify that MA NPs mitigate pulmonary edema through coordinated suppression of inflammatory signaling, preservation of epithelial barrier integrity, and normalization of AQP5 expression.

Additionally, safety evaluation indicated no significant pathological alterations in the heart, liver, spleen, lung, or kidney of MA NPs-treated mice, as assessed by H&E staining (Fig. S11A in Additional file 1). Serum biochemical analysis further showed that levels of creatinine (CRE), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) remained within normal ranges, confirming the absence of overt hepatic or renal toxicity under the experimental conditions (Fig. S11B in Additional file 1).

MA NPs alleviate hydrochloric acid aspiration-induced pulmonary inflammation and fibrosis in mice

We further evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of MA NPs in a murine model of hydrochloric acid (HCl) aspiration-induced lung injury, utilizing acute (24 h) and subacute (1 week) observation time points to assess both the acute and recovery phases. In the acute phase, MA NPs effectively alleviated HCl-induced acute pulmonary inflammation (Fig. S12). For the subacute phase (experimental timeline in Fig. 10A), the Mod group exhibited a partial spontaneous resolution of acute inflammation, showing moderate elevations in W/D and LW/BW ratios (Fig. 10B, C). Furthermore, H&E staining and Masson’s trichrome staining revealed inflammatory cell infiltration and prominent collagen deposition, which are indicative of early-stage pulmonary fibrosis in the Mod group (Fig. 10D, E). In contrast, MA NP treatment significantly accelerated subacute recovery. Lung function parameters (FVC, Cdyn, Re, RL) were restored (Fig. 10F–I), and BALF levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and total protein were also markedly improved (Fig. 10J–M). Additionally, the progression of HCl aspiration-induced early pulmonary fibrosis was also alleviated (Fig. 10D, E).

Furthermore, one-week administration of MA NPs (20 and 40 mg/kg) demonstrated excellent in vivo biosafety, inducing no detectable toxicity in major organs as assessed by H&E staining (Additional file 1: Fig. S12N). Collectively, data from both the acute and subacute phases confirm that MA NPs possess potent anti-inflammatory, anti-edematous, lung-protective, and anti-fibrotic effects, strongly supporting their potential for ALI/ARDS therapy.

Conclusions

In summary, we developed a carrier‑free, self‑assembled nanoplatform (MA NPs) for co‑delivery of Mag and ATI, enabling targeted treatment of LPS‑induced pulmonary edema. The nanoparticles demonstrate pH-responsive release and ultrahigh drug loading, promoting efficient lung accumulation and microenvironment‑triggered drug release at alveolar epithelia. Mechanistically, Mag inhibits TRPV4‑mediated Ca²⁺ influx, suppressing the cAMP/AQP5 axis to rectify alveolar fluid accumulation, while both drugs synergistically attenuate inflammation via AKT and NF‑κB pathways. Mag and ATI further stabilize tight junctions to preserve epithelial barrier integrity. This strategy concurrently addresses inflammation, barrier dysfunction, and fluid imbalance (Fig. 11). By integrating synergistic natural products combination rationale with carrier‑free nanotechnology, MA NPs exhibit potent efficacy and favorable safety, offering a promising translatable candidate for ALI/ARDS induced pulmonary edema therapy.
