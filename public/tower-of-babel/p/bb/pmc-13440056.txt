Optimized functional zoning based on land use carbon emissions and carbon balance: a case study in Shaanxi Province, China
Authors: Qi Mu, Xia Jia, Yonghua Zhao, Lei Zhang, Kun Ren, Wenhong Kang, Yueying Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLand use carbon emissions (LUCE) is crucial for achieving carbon neutrality and sustainable development goals. How to coordinate ecological and economic benefits and formulate targeted regional carbon balance strategies based on local conditions remains to be explored. Therefore, Shaanxi Province in China was selected as the case for this study due to its rich ecological and energy resources and its significant differences. LUCE was calculated for each county in the region from 2000 to 2020. The contribution of carbon ecological capacity was quantified by calculating the carbon ecological support coefficient (ESC). The economic output per unit of carbon emission was measured using the carbon economic contribution coefficient (ECC). Based on the above, an optimized functional zoning for LUCE and carbon balance was constructed. Results showed that LUCE in Shaanxi Province increased from 2,088.18 × 104 tonnes in 2000 to 19,330.99 × 104 tonnes in 2020, with central region accounting for 64.81% of the total, northern region 22.92%, and southern region 12.27%. About 53.27% of counties maintained ecological deficits (ESC < 1) in the Northern Shaanxi Energy Zone and the Guanzhong Belt, while 56.07% exhibited an imbalance between economic output and carbon emissions (ECC < 1) in the Northern periphery and the Southern Ecological Zone. Economic growth was positively correlated with LUCE and negatively with ESC, but had a relatively small relationship with ECC and carbon absorption. Subsequently, all counties in Shaanxi Province were classified into five functional types: Carbon Sink Zones, Low-Carbon Economic Zones, Economic Development Zones, Carbon Intensity Control Zones and High-Carbon Optimization Zones, and a targeted strategy was proposed for each functional type. These results could provide a practical and transferable framework for supporting carbon neutrality planning and sustainable land use management in ecologically sensitive regions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13021-026-00471-z.Keywords: Land use carbon emissions, Land-use and land-cover change, Carbon ecological support, Carbon economic contribution, Carbon balance, Functional carbon zoning

Introduction

Global warming driven by continuous greenhouse gas emissions causes ecological and environmental risks and threatens sustainable development [1–3]. Currently, many countries have put forward carbon neutrality goals, and achieving carbon neutrality has become a global consensus [4–6]. Within the global carbon cycle, land serves as both a carbon source and a carbon sink [7, 8]. Land-use and land-cover changes (LULCC), along with the pattern and intensity of land use, directly or indirectly shape the carbon sequestration capacity of terrestrial ecosystems [9–11]. Meanwhile, as the space for human activities, land is also the basis for many socioeconomic processes, and its use produces significant carbon emissions [12]. Historical research has shown that land use carbon emissions (LUCE) have long been a major part of global carbon emissions and have played a key role in regional carbon balances [13–15]. From the early stages of the Second Industrial Revolution to the end of the twentieth century, LUCE accounted for over 30% of global anthropogenic carbon emissions [16]. Even from 2000 to 2009, this proportion remained as high as 12.5% [17]. Therefore, studying LUCE can help us understand regional carbon emission dynamics, assess regional carbon balance, and provide a scientific basis for low-carbon land use planning.

However, LUCE is more difficult to quantify than fossil fuel emissions because it is spatially diffuse and temporally variable [18]. Current methods for estimating LUCE include the bookkeeping model [19–21], the inventory method [22, 23], and the IPCC recommended technical approach [24]. The bookkeeping model often overlooks indirect LUCE, and the inventory method is limited by high costs and poor historical data availability. In contrast, the IPCC approach accounts for both the direct LUCE resulting from LULCC and the indirect LUCE driven by land-use energy consumption. This approach involves a standardized calculation process and modest data requirements, making it widely applicable.

Previous studies have primarily focused on the spatiotemporal change in LUCE [25–27], the driving factors [18, 28, 29], and future multi-scenario simulations [30–32], but carbon balance analysis has received little attention. Beyond quantifying carbon sources and sinks, carbon balance assessments must also consider the tension between economic carbon emissions and ecological carbon sequestration. Economic growth is closely linked to energy consumption, the use of energy is often accompanied by substantial carbon emissions [33]. In developing countries such as China, the processes of industrialization and urbanization, employment rates, and the accumulation of social wealth exhibit a significant positive correlation with carbon emissions [34]. Such strong correlations indicate that any single emissions reduction measure could impose significant pressure on regional economic activities. To analyze this contradiction more scientifically and provide quantitative assessment tools, the Carbon Ecological Support Coefficient (ESC) and the Carbon Economic Contribution Coefficient (ECC) have been introduced into carbon balance research. The ESC evaluates equitable contributions in terms of carbon ecological capacity among different regions, thereby reflecting each region’s carbon sequestration ability [35]. Conversely, the ECC quantifies both the economic output efficiency from regional LUCE and the equity in associated economic value allocation across specified regions [36]. Compared to traditional single indicators, the combination of these two coefficients can identify the trade-off between ecological carbon sequestration capacity and economic emission pressure in different regions, providing theoretical support for formulating spatially differentiated emission reduction policies for regions with different resource endowments. Based on the above, this study adopted the county level as the analytical scale. Although counties follow unified national policies, they differ substantially in land-use patterns, ecological resources, population density, and industrial structure [37–39]. Working at the county scale allows for a finer representation of both carbon emissions from economic activities and variations in ecosystem carbon sinks, making it possible to more clearly capture the interactions between economic development and ecological carbon cycles and to provide a sound basis for spatially differentiated governance strategies.

The selection of Shaanxi Province as the case study area was motivated by several considerations. First, Shaanxi is located in western China and serves as both a national ecological security barrier and a major energy base, which gives it important strategic value [40]. Second, the province extends from the ecologically fragile Loess Plateau to the ecologically rich Qinling Mountains, and this range presents clear ecological diversity and spatial heterogeneity [41]. However, rapid urbanization driven land use transitions and increasing energy consumption have intensified carbon emission pressures [29]. These dynamics further highlight regional development conflicts, including high carbon industrial agglomerations in Northern Shaanxi, pollution driven by urban cluster expansion in Central Shaanxi, and ecological degradation triggered by resource exploitation in Southern Shaanxi. This situation highlights the regional pressure to balance ecological protection and economic development under the carbon neutrality target. Consequently, the combination of strategic importance, environmental complexity, and transition pressure makes Shaanxi Province an appropriate region for county-level carbon balance research, with clear relevance for policy implementation and practical application. Unlike previous studies in Shaanxi Province that primarily focused on spatiotemporal carbon accounting, this study integrated the ESC and ECC into a unified assessment framework. This framework quantified the key trade-off between ecological equity and economic efficiency, thus providing a foundation for developing spatially differentiated carbon neutrality governance strategies. Using this innovative assessment framework, this study aims to answer the following three key questions: (1) What are the spatiotemporal patterns of LUCE in Shaanxi Province? (2) How can the contributions of ecological capacity and carbon emission economic efficiency to carbon balance be evaluated? (3) Based on the carbon balance assessment, how should carbon balance zoning be planned, and what spatially differentiated governance strategies can support the achievement of carbon neutrality?

Materials and methods

Study area

Shaanxi Province (105°29’–111°15’E, 31°42’–39°35’N), situated in the inland region of China within the middle reaches of the Yellow River basin, is one of China’s five northwestern provinces and serves as the geographical center of the country (Fig. 1). With a total area of approximately 206,000 km2, the province is divided into 10 prefecture level cities and comprises 107 counties (Fig. S1 and Table S1). Geographically, it can be classified into three main regions: Northern Shaanxi (Shaanbei), Central Shaanxi (Guanzhong), and Southern Shaanxi (Shaannan). Northern Shaanxi, located within the Loess Plateau region, is rich in mineral resources, and its energy and chemical sectors are the primary drivers of its industrial economy. Central Shaanxi, characterized by flat terrain and a dense population, serves as the economic core of the province and the northwest region. Southern Shaanxi, primarily hilly and mountainous, is vital for ecological conservation, notably the Qinba Mountainous region, renowned as the Biological Gene Bank. Despite rapid socioeconomic development, urbanization, and industrial expansion in recent years, Shaanxi faces challenges concerning its local ecosystem and carbon emissions.

Data sources and processing

Land use data were derived from the GlobeLand30 dataset, released by the National Geomatics Center of China (http://www.globallandcover.com). Vector data of Shaanxi Province were employed to mask and extract information from GlobeLand30, resulting in land cover data for Shaanxi Province from 2000 to 2020, all with a spatial resolution of 30 m. These data were subsequently reclassified into six primary land use types for Shaanxi Province, including cultivated land, forest, grassland, water, built-up land, and unused land. The vector data were sourced from the 1:1,000,000 National Basic Geographic Database available through the National Catalog Service for Geographic Information (https://www.webmap.cn). Moreover, fossil energy and socioeconomic data for Shaanxi Province were obtained from the China Energy Statistical Yearbook and the Shaanxi Provincial Statistical Yearbook.

Methods

The overall research methodology framework was illustrated in Fig. 2. This study was divided into the following key stages: (1) data preparation for LUCE and carbon balance assessments; (2) quantification of LUCE using IPCC methods, along with the analysis of spatiotemporal characteristics and identification of linkages with LULCC drivers; (3) characterization of the ESC and ECC, followed by spatiotemporal evolution analysis and validation of impact mechanisms; and (4) delineation of carbon balance zones, diagnosis of spatiotemporal dynamics, and formulation of spatially targeted policy recommendations.

Results

Characteristics of LUCE

LUCE in Shaanxi exhibited an upward trend, increasing from 2,088.18 × 104 tonnes in 2000 to 9,625.69 × 104 tonnes in 2010, and further rising to 19,330.99 × 104 tonnes in 2020. From a county-level perspective, there were significant disparities in LUCE and net carbon emissions (NCE) across Shaanxi Province, with these differences expanding between 2000 and 2020 (Fig. 3). In 2000, the highest LUCE was observed in Xi’an’s main urban districts (Yanta, Xincheng, Lianhu, Beilin, and Weiyang). By 2010, Shenmu County ranked first, followed by Xi’an’s urban core. In 2020, Yanta District reclaimed the top position, followed by Shenmu District. The top ten regions were predominantly Xi’an’s urban districts and Yulin’s Shenmu, Yuyang, and Fugu. Ningshan County consistently had the highest CA.

Carbon dynamics by administrative unit in Shaanxi Province from 2000 to 2020: (a-c) LUCE, NCE and CA values per county; (d) LUCE and CA proportions per city

Regarding spatial distribution, LUCE in Shaanxi Province showed distinct patterns, with higher emissions in Northern Shaanxi, elevated emissions in Central Shaanxi, and lower emissions in Southern Shaanxi (Fig. 4a–c). CA showed an inverse pattern, peaking in the south, declining in the north, and reaching minimal levels in the central region (Fig. 4d–f). In Northern Shaanxi, LUCE accounted for 14.74% of the provincial total (307.88 × 104 tonnes) in 2000. By 2010, emissions surged to 2,631.03 × 104 tonnes (27.33%), and by 2020, they further increased to 4,429.86 × 104 tonnes (22.92%), primarily driven by Shenmu, Yuyang, and Fugu. In the Guanzhong region, emissions dominated throughout the period, contributing 80.55% (1,681.96 × 104 tonnes) of the provincial total in 2000. By 2020, emissions surged to 12,527.52 × 104 tonnes (64.81%), with Yanta exhibiting the most pronounced growth. Southern Shaanxi had relatively low LUCE but maintained the highest CA. Spatial autocorrelation analysis was conducted on LUCE in the study area, revealing a clear positive spatial autocorrelation (Table 4). Notably, the global Moran’s I index decreased from 0.6779 in 2000 to 0.5193 in 2020, indicating a weakening of spatial clustering. This decline was mainly attributed to the dispersion of emission sources due to urban expansion and the disruption of the original carbon sink pattern by ecological projects. Nevertheless, a local spatial autocorrelation analysis showed stable spatial agglomeration patterns across the study area (Fig. 4g–i). Areas with high-high values represented emission hotspots driven by concentrated human activities and intensive energy consumption. These hotspots were predominantly situated in the central Guanzhong area and the northern part of Northern Shaanxi, notably expanding in Xi’an City by 2020. This expansion highlighted the need for coordinated regional emission reduction. Conversely, areas with low-low values signify ecological cold spots with strong carbon sequestration capacities. These ecological zones were concentrated in specific regions of Southern Shaanxi, with a decrease observed over time. This trend indicated increasing environmental pressure on regional carbon sinks.

Built-up land became the core contributor to LUCE, with its proportion rising from 87.97% in 2000 to 98.44% in 2020, reflecting the dominant trend of cropland reduction and urban expansion in land use. This spatial evolution exhibited significant regional differentiation. The Guanzhong urban agglomeration showed continuous built-up area sprawl, while the energy base in Northern Shaanxi formed a unique expansion pattern (Fig. 5a–j). Although cropland continued to generate carbon emissions, its contribution rate sharply decreased from 12.03% to 1.56%. This reflected the impact of ecological policies such as the Grain for Green Program. While the reduction in farmland area lowers the absolute amount of direct carbon sources and sinks in agricultural ecosystems, the expansion of forest land significantly increased the region’s overall carbon sink capacity. However, against the backdrop of overall ecological carbon sequestration, land conversion analysis also revealed key shifts leading to increased carbon emissions. From 2000 to 2010, 1.70 × 105 hm2 of cropland was converted to built-up land, accounting for 38.42% of total cropland conversion during this period. From 2010 to 2020, 3.76 × 105 hm2 of grassland was reclaimed as cropland, representing 48.51% of total grassland conversion (Fig. 5h–m). This chain reaction of built-up land encroaching on cropland and grassland reclamation compensating for cropland triggered dual carbon losses. It weakened grassland carbon sink functions and accelerated soil carbon pool depletion through cultivation. The CA pattern demonstrated stronger spatiotemporal stability. In 2020, forests contributed 550.22 × 104 tonnes of CA, accounting for 97.91% of carbon sink capacity, but their spatial distribution remained highly concentrated in the Qinling-Bashan mountainous area. Although grasslands, water bodies, and unused land collectively contributed 2.09% of CA, their weak carbon sequestration capacity failed to offset LUCE. Notably, the rapid increase of LUCE from built-up land led to its overwhelming dominance. Emissions far exceeded the carbon sink capacity of forests, pushing the regional carbon balance into a state of severe imbalance.

Characteristics of ESC and ECC

Between 2000 and 2020, the ESC of Shaanxi Province’s counties remained generally stable, though significant internal disparities were evident (Fig. 6a–c). Across the province, the proportion of counties with an ESC above 1 fluctuated narrowly between 42% and 46% (44.86% in 2000, 42.99% in 2010, and 45.79% in 2020), with annual variations of less than 4 percentage points. However, in terms of spatial distribution, 53.27% of the counties consistently experienced an ecological deficit (ESC < 1). This indicated that in some counties, the ecosystems could not compensate for the LUCE generated by energy consumption. Moreover, the “lower in the north, higher in the south” spatial pattern revealed that counties with intensive energy activities and economically active regions generally faced insufficient carbon sequestration capacity. For instance, major coal production regions such as Yulin and Shenmu, due to intensive resource development, increased LUCE. This resulted in their ecosystems being unable to counterbalance energy consumption emissions. In contrast, urban agglomerations such as Xi’an and Xianyang, with continuously expanding built-up land, were under dual pressure from compressed ecological space and declining carbon sequestration functions. In stark contrast, counties in the Qinba mountainous area of Southern Shaanxi had built highly efficient carbon sequestration systems by leveraging their abundant forest resources, demonstrating significant environmental benefits.

The spatial distribution of ESC (a-c) and ECC (d-f) in Shaanxi Province from 2000 to 2020

From 2000 to 2020, the ECC of counties in Shaanxi Province showed a pattern of overall balance with local polarization (Fig. 6d–f). Over time, the province’s average ECC rose from 0.90 in 2000 to 0.98 in 2020, indicating a gradual improvement in the resilience of the overall relationship between economic performance and LUCE. The study showed that ECC values in 81.3% of counties fell within the equilibrium range of 0.8 to 1.2, suggesting that economic development and LUCE growth were largely synchronized in most regions. During 2000 to 2020, 56.07% of the counties consistently showed an imbalance between economic development and carbon emissions (ECC < 1), revealing a structural problem of high emissions combined with low efficiency. In terms of spatial patterns, ECC showed a gradient of three levels. The coordinated core zone of Guanzhong, centered on the contiguous cluster of high values around Yanta and Beilin districts, consistently maintained ECC values above 1. This strong coupling with zones dense in tertiary industries demonstrated how industrial agglomeration improved economic efficiency in technology intensive sectors. In the transitional improvement zone of Northern Shaanxi, energy bases such as Shenmu and Yuyang exhibited punctuated breakthroughs with high values, although peripheral counties like Jia remained dependent on resource intensive, low efficiency growth models. In the equilibrium constrained zone of Southern Shaanxi such as Zhashui and Ningshan, the ECC growth rate was the slowest, constrained by the dynamic balance between ecological protection and economic development. This core periphery gradient pattern essentially represents a spatial mapping of regional development models. In the central Guanzhong Plain, a contiguous cluster of high values has formed. This cluster strongly overlaps with the economic radiation belts of Xi’an and Xianyang. These belts are areas influenced by the economic spillover effects of the core cities. In contrast, the energy zone in Northern Shaanxi and the ecological zone in southern Shaanxi were scattered with secondary high value units, reflecting the differentiated support that unique resource endowments provide for regional low-carbon transitions. The differentiated evolution of carbon emissions economic efficiency was shaped by three pathways. Technology intensification in Guanzhong, energy upgrading in Northern Shaanxi, and ecological constraints in Southern Shaanxi all contributed to this pattern.

To provide a clearer representation of the correlation between economic, environmental, and land use performance in Shaanxi Province, their relationship was depicted (Fig. 7). The results indicated that GDP growth correlated positively with LUCE and negatively with ESC. This pattern likely reflected the tendency for economic expansion to coincide with increased construction land and higher energy consumption. Such spatial shifts frequently overlapped with the reduction of ecological lands. These ecological lands previously supported carbon sequestration and ecosystem services. However, GDP growth exhibited no significant correlation with ECC or CA. This observation might be related to mutual offsetting trends between LUCE and economic metrics. Additionally, cropland and forestland comprised the majority of land use, and variations in their proportions corresponded with changes in CA.

Characteristics of carbon balance zoning

From the perspective of spatiotemporal distribution characteristics, the carbon balance zoning in this region underwent significant restructuring between 2000 and 2020 (Fig. 8). Carbon Sink Zone dramatically contracted from widespread coverage (27.37% area proportion in 2000) to sporadic distribution (1.78% in 2020). Core ecological areas such as Taibai County and Foping County were converted into the Economic Development Zone, and the number of regional units plummeted from 21 to just 1. Economic Development Zone aggressively expanded, with its area proportion surging from 23.96% to 48.21%. It absorbed 28 counties, including Linyou County and Ganquan County, forming a contiguous economic base coverage. Low-Carbon Economic Zone exhibited weak growth momentum, with its area proportion increasing by merely 1.45%. Most regions such as Chencang District and Nanzheng District transitioned to the Economic Development Zone, while only a few areas like Chenggu County and Baota District experienced low-carbon attribute reversals. Carbon Intensity Control Zone and High-Carbon Optimization Zone demonstrated bidirectional transitions: Areas including Lintong District and Fengxiang County shifted from the Carbon Intensity Control Zone to the High-Carbon Optimization Zone, whereas traditional High-Carbon Optimization Zones like Yuyang District and Shenmu City were reversed into the Carbon Intensity Control Zone through policy interventions. This resulted in the former’s area proportion first increasing to a peak of 17.07% and then declining to 14.28%, while the latter’s area proportion decreased despite its unit count rising from 30 to 32, intensifying spatial fragmentation. Some regions such as Mei County and Huayin City underwent repeated functional adjustments, reflecting the dynamic strategic interplay between policy interventions and economic forces. The overall spatial pattern initially featured an interleaving of the Carbon Sink Zone and High-Carbon Optimization Zone. Over time, the Economic Development Zone became dominant, accounting for nearly half of the area. This shift highlighted the increasing spatial competition between ecological conservation and economic growth.

Discussion

Research significance

The green development and low-carbon transition in Shaanxi Province provides an important example for ecological protection and high quality development in the Yellow River Basin. It also contributes to achieving carbon peak and carbon neutrality goals. In recent years, while accelerating industrialization and urbanization by leveraging its abundant energy resources and the China Western Development strategy, Shaanxi has concurrently faced challenges, including a singular energy structure, weakened soil and water conservation and carbon sequestration capacities, and pronounced regional disparities in carbon emission intensity [48–50]. These circumstances urgently necessitate research on carbon balance zoning oriented towards ecological preservation and economic development.

Traditional carbon accounting often overlooks local ecological and economic feedbacks. The county-level carbon balance zoning framework developed in this study goes beyond merely assessing regional carbon emissions and sinks. It evaluates the ecological carbon capacity and economic carbon contribution of each region using the ESC and ECC indicators. By considering regional economic output and green development potential, the framework ultimately provides tailored policy recommendations for each zone. These findings provide scientific support for optimizing spatial development and coordinating ecological protection with high quality development. The green development and low carbon transition observed in Shaanxi Province offer important insights for implementing similar strategies in other ecologically sensitive regions. Moreover, the county-level carbon balance zoning framework and its associated evaluation indicators provide a transferable methodological basis for guiding carbon neutrality planning and sustainable land-use management in regions facing similar ecological and socioeconomic challenges.

Comparison and innovation with other studies

The study revealed that from 2000 to 2020, LULCC in Shaanxi Province exhibited a significant characteristic of continuous encroachment of built-up land on natural land, which aligned with previous research on LULCC in Shaanxi [51]. This phenomenon was particularly pronounced in rapidly urbanizing areas, such as the Yangtze River Delta [52] and the Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei [53]. LULCC directly influenced the dynamics of carbon sources and sinks, thereby affecting LUCE and CA. Specifically, the expansion of built-up land elevated aggregate LUCE and triggered high-carbon spatial sprawl. Particularly during rapid urbanization, LUCE significantly outpaced CA. This phenomenon has been validated in developing countries including Pakistan, India, and China, revealing a positive correlation between accelerated urbanization and expanding net carbon emissions [54–56]. The reduction of forests and grasslands further weakened CA capacity, creating an imbalance between accelerated carbon emission growth and stagnant carbon sink maintenance.

In addition to the carbon imbalance caused by excessive disparities between carbon emission and absorption capacities, there was also an imbalance between the carbon economy and carbon ecology. The introduced ESC and ECC indicators can better reflect this phenomenon. The results indicated that the coexistence of a high economic carbon contribution and a high ecological deficit was mainly driven by two distinct spatial evolution pathways. In urban agglomerations featuring intensive technology and capital, industries with high technology intensity significantly improve the economic output efficiency per unit of carbon emission. However, they also exacerbate the ecological deficit. These regions can reference Tokyo’s green building initiatives and circular economy policies to reduce ecological risks while maintaining economic density [57]. In heavy energy and chemical regions reliant on resources, industries with high energy consumption directly cause severe ecological deficits. This aligns with the dilemmas faced by traditional industrial bases such as Shanxi in China and the Ruhr region in Germany [58, 59]. The key solution is establishing ecological reclamation and industrial transformation models for mining areas. Conversely, regions with rich ecological endowments maintain carbon sink resilience, yet their ecological carbon sinks grow slowly. This reveals the green poverty paradox commonly observed in ecologically rich areas. Strategic frameworks from Yunnan Province could be referenced, utilizing carbon trading systems to make carbon sequestration an economically viable undertaking and promote the conversion of ecological assets into economic capital [60]. Achieving sustainable development requires resolving conflicts between socioeconomic growth and safeguarding ecological lands.​

Overall, the findings verified the universal pattern of carbon budget imbalances driven by rapid urbanization [53, 61, 62]. They also analyzed the evolution of ecological and economic contributions of LUCE in Shaanxi Province from a county perspective. The county scale carbon balance analysis effectively revealed the feedback mechanisms between heterogeneous spatial development patterns and carbon budget dynamics, addressing the gap in existing research that prioritizes urban agglomeration level mitigation while neglecting grassroots governance units. By establishing county-level carbon balance zoning, this approach not only identified spatial differentiation between counties with high economic contribution and with high ecological sequestration but also deciphered the evolutionary pathways of carbon metabolism characteristics across functional zones, providing a scientific basis for formulating differentiated spatial governance strategies. This refined assessment method linked local land use planning with national carbon neutrality goals and helped shape a coordinated low-carbon territorial layout at multiple scales through a mechanism of carbon deficit and carbon surplus spatial compensation, providing practical support for regional coordinated development.

Policy implications

Each functional zone was assigned corresponding carbon reduction targets and strategies. This zoning scheme, oriented toward low-carbon goals, helps control total carbon emissions in Shaanxi Province, achieves coordinated emissions reduction, and serves as a reference for future development. It’s worth noting that significant spatial differences exist within each county, and regional functional positioning should be determined by considering other influencing factors comprehensively.

Carbon Sink Zone: This region possesses a favorable ecological environment and strong carbon sink capacity, although its economic development lags relatively behind. Considering the declining area proportion of this region and the transition of core ecological counties like Taibai and Foping into economic development zones, there is an urgent need to implement protection and establish horizontal ecological compensation mechanisms across regions.This region is recommended as the recipient of carbon compensation. Areas with high carbon emissions such as Guanzhong and Northern Shaanxi should quantitatively compensate for the carbon sequestration services provided by these ecosystems through financial transfer payments or carbon quota purchases. Fully utilizing their carbon sequestration capacity will alleviate provincial carbon emission pressure, promote green industries, and achieve coordinated development between the economy and ecology.

Low-Carbon Economic Zone: These regions exhibit a high economic contribution rate and strong ecological carbon carrying capacity. Considering the weak growth momentum in recent years and the degradation of some parts into economic development zones, it is imperative to strictly control ecological degradation in this region. Regions that have successfully reversed their attributes towards low carbon emissions, such as Chenggu County and Baota District, should serve as benchmarks. These areas should utilize their abundant carbon sequestration resources and regional ecological advantages to actively explore new paths for economic development. This will promote industrial transition towards green development and low carbon emissions while protecting the environment.

Economic Development Zone: During the study period, this zone expanded significantly. It absorbed 28 counties, including Lintong County and Ganquan County, forming a continuous economic coverage base. Faced with spatial expansion on this scale, these areas must curb extensive land expansion. These regions typically have low levels of energy utilization and a small economic contribution from carbon emissions. These regions often possess vast agricultural land or abundant forest resources, providing them with a high capacity for carbon sinks. Therefore, they can focus on strategies for coordinated ecological and industrial development. Developing green industries and improving overall carbon efficiency will enhance ecological security and promote sustainable development.

Carbon Intensity Control Zone: In these regions, a lower ecological carbon carrying capacity indicates highly active economic activities. This results in a higher overall LUCE. However, traditional zones for optimizing high carbon emissions, such as Yuyang District and Shenmu City, successfully transitioned into this zone through policy interventions. This proved the effectiveness of the carbon reduction paths in this region. Relying on a stable economic foundation, counties in this area should consolidate existing intervention results. They should accelerate the industrial transition towards low carbon emissions. Furthermore, they should promote the deep decoupling of economic growth from carbon emissions through technological upgrades to achieve carbon reduction goals.

High-Carbon Optimization Zone: Compared to other regions, these areas exhibit a significant gap in both ecological carbon environment quality and economic development. It is necessary to implement enduring policies to narrow this gap. Their primary task is to eliminate fluctuations in local policies. They must curb the disorderly expansion of projects with high energy consumption and high emissions. Efforts should focus on regulating fossil energy consumption and developing clean and renewable energy. These regions should achieve emission reductions through technological innovation. They should also implement measures like afforestation and land reclamation to enhance carbon sink capacity. Meanwhile, they should accelerate the development of an economy with low carbon emissions by participating in carbon market trading.

Limitations and future research

There are clear differences in LUCE and carbon balance research across spatial scales. Smaller scales allow more precise accounting and can better inform policy decisions. This study focused on the county level, while future research could extend to the township level, forming a nested framework from urban clusters and cities down to counties and townships. Although the IPCC coefficient method improves accounting efficiency, carbon emission and carbon absorption coefficients were treated as fixed values in this study. This simplification is a limitation of this study because it ignores dynamic changes over long periods. Furthermore, the emission and absorption coefficients for key land types such as grasslands and forests still rely largely on literature values and lack calibration from local ecological observations. Incorporating remote sensing data together with field monitoring could further enhance the accuracy and spatial resolution of carbon accounting. Furthermore, inter-regional carbon flows and compensation mechanisms remain insufficiently studied. Carbon compensation based on spatial patterns can support carbon balance and provide a basis for allocating responsibilities and benefits according to regional differences in carbon and ecosystem conditions. In county to county compensation, regional function and resource endowments should be taken into account, and standardized quantitative rules should be established.

Conclusion

In this study, county-level LULCC and LUCE in Shaanxi Province from 2000 to 2020 were analyzed. Regional carbon ecological capacity and economic contribution were assessed using the ESC and ECC indicators. Based on these results, a county-level carbon balance zoning framework was developed. The results indicated that LUCE escalated 9.3-fold from 2,088.18 × 104 tonnes in 2000 to 19,330.99 × 104 tonnes in 2020. This sharp increase led to an extreme imbalance in the spatial distribution of regional carbon emissions and ecological capacity. ESC remained generally stable but showed marked spatial disparities, with 53.27% of counties, including Yuyang and Shenmu in the Northern Shaanxi Energy Zone and Yanta and Beilin in the Guanzhong Economic Belt, exhibiting persistent ecological deficits. ECC increased from 0.90 to 0.98, yet 56.07% of counties still showed economic imbalances in carbon emission activities. This indicated that regional economic growth remained heavily dependent on increasing carbon emissions and sacrificing ecological space. Based on LUCE, CA, ESC, and ECC indicators, Shaanxi counties were categorized into five functional zones: Carbon Sink Zone, Low-Carbon Economic Zone, Economic Development Zone, Carbon Intensity Control Zone, and High-Carbon Optimization Zone. Overall, the county-level carbon balance zoning framework and the associated evaluation indicators offer a scientific basis for harmonizing ecological protection with high quality development and guiding low-carbon transition strategies in regions with complex ecological and socioeconomic conditions.
