metaRLK 2.0: An updated database of plant receptor-like kinases developed with structure- and deep learning-based functional annotation and classification
Authors: Zheng Zhang, Xingzhe Li, Jinger Li, Qian Liu, Weida Li, Jiani Liu, Dandan Liu, Long Wang, Zhi Yan, Ping Fu, Feng Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReceptor-like kinases (RLKs) are the largest class of signal transduction proteins in plants and play crucial roles in adaptation to diverse ecological environments. The previously published metaRLK database contains 311 581 RLKs from 508 plant species; however, 13% lack clear domain or family assignments, and structural features are not systematically integrated. Here, we present metaRLK 2.0, a structure-enhanced RLK resource that integrates large-scale structure-based annotation, fold classification, network analysis, and semantic functional inference. Structural reannotation identified 677 distinct domain types, increasing the number of recognized types by 62.7%. CATH-based classification revealed that RLK domains are enriched in rigid β-fold architectures. A structural similarity network identified 70 newly defined RLK families, 50 of which are predicted to be associated with plant cell wall–related processes. Overall, 8% of previously unclassified RLKs were assigned to known or newly defined families. MetaRLK 2.0 provides an expanded, structure-informed platform for the exploration of RLK diversity, evolution, and structure–function relationships and serves as a comprehensive resource for future research in plant receptor biology.Key words: RLK, domain, bioinformatic

Introduction

Organisms typically rely on surface receptors to perceive and respond to a wide range of environmental signals. Although animals primarily use G-protein-coupled receptors for signal transduction, plants predominantly use receptor-like kinases (RLKs) (Hanlon and Andrew, 2015). RLKs are ubiquitous in plants and regulate essential processes, including cell division, plant development, flowering, and responses to biotic and abiotic stresses (Li and Tax, 2013). The extensive expansion and diversification of RLKs among plants likely reflect evolutionary adaptation to a sessile lifestyle, in which direct and continuous monitoring of the extracellular environment is essential (Tang et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2024a). Understanding RLK diversity, function, and evolution is therefore critical to understanding plant environmental adaptation.

RLKs were initially identified and classified in Arabidopsis thaliana as a plant-specific clade related to animal receptor kinases (Shiu and Bleecker, 2001). The substantially larger number of RLKs in plants compared with receptor kinases in animals suggests either plant-specific expansion following the divergence of the two kingdoms or extensive gene loss in animals (Shiu and Bleecker, 2001; Lehti-Shiu and Shiu, 2012). The widely used RLK classification system proposed by Shiu has served as a framework for nearly two decades, grouping RLKs into distinct families according to their domain composition, largely based on analyses of model plant species (Shiu and Bleecker, 2001). Building on this framework, recent large-scale analyses have incorporated domain information to refine RLK classification. For example, Yin et al. classified 220 038 RLKs from 300 plant species into 18 families using sequence-based approaches (Yin et al., 2024). Similarly, we analyzed 311 581 RLKs from more than 500 plant genomes, assigning them to 21 known families and annotating 416 domains, resulting in the metaRLK database (Liu et al., 2024b). Despite these advances, 40 357 RLKs (13.0%) remain unclassified and may represent highly divergent members of known families or entirely novel lineages.

The three-dimensional (3D) structure of a protein is intrinsically linked to its biological function. Structure-based methods enable precise domain-level analyses of protein structure and function (Holm, 2022; Zhu et al., 2023). However, traditional methods are computationally intensive and therefore unsuitable for large-scale analyses (Shindyalov and Bourne, 1998; Zhang and Skolnick, 2005; Holm, 2020). Recent breakthroughs in deep learning have revolutionized structure-based domain parsing and comparison, as exemplified by tools such as UniDoc and Foldseek (Zhu et al., 2023; Heinzinger et al., 2024; Van Kempen et al., 2024). In parallel, deep-learning-based models provide new opportunities for functional inference of previously uncharacterized proteins (Gane et al., 2022).

In the present study, we developed a structure-based strategy encompassing structure-based alignment, structural similarity network analysis, and deep-learning-based functional prediction tools to annotate and classify RLKs in the metaRLK database. This approach expanded the known diversity of RLK domains and revealed their underlying fold architectures. We constructed an interactive structural similarity network comprising more than 300 000 RLKs and identified 70 putative new RLK families, 50 of which are associated with plant-cell-wall-related processes. Our data mining and analysis results are publicly accessible through the metaRLK 2.0 web portal (http://www.metarlk.com).

Results

Structure-based alignment for domain annotation in RLKs

In metaRLK 2.0, we compiled a comprehensive dataset of 311 581 RLKs from 508 plant species (Supplemental Table 1). Although traditional sequence-based methods classify these RLKs into 21 known families (Liu et al., 2024b), family assignments for 40 357 RLKs (13.0%) could not be clearly determined (Supplemental Table 2). Given the close relationship between 3D structure and protein function, we implemented a structure-based strategy to enhance domain detection and refine family classification. To achieve this, we developed a structure-guided annotation pipeline. The extracellular regions of RLKs with available 3D structures (n = 136 766) were segmented into domains using UniDoc and subsequently annotated using Foldseek, whereas RLKs lacking available 3D structures (n = 174 815) were annotated directly with Foldseek without prior domain parsing (Figure 1A). This pipeline identified 677 distinct RLK domains, of which 467 were uniquely detected by the structure-based alignment, representing a 62.7% increase in domain types compared with the sequence-based alignment (Liu et al., 2024b) (Figure 1B and Supplemental Table 3). Notably, structural domain annotation enabled the reclassification of 8998 (2.9%) previously unassigned RLKs into existing families by identifying structurally conserved domains that were undetectable or ambiguous at the sequence level (Supplemental Table 4).

Domains annotated by both sequence- and structure-based methods were primarily functionally essential and evolutionarily conserved, including leucine-rich repeat (LRR; PF13855, PF14580, PF08263, PF13306, PF20141, and PF12799), malectin (PF11721), legume lectin (L-LEC; PF00139), and cysteine-rich repeat (CRR; PF01657) domains (Figure 1C and Supplemental Table 5). These domains are widely distributed across plant lineages and play central roles in immune signaling, cell-surface recognition, and developmental regulation (Shiu and Bleecker, 2001; Zhang et al., 2016).

The structure-based method uniquely identified 467 novel domains, revealing a substantial and underappreciated layer of RLK diversity. Eight of these structurally defined domain types were highly abundant, each comprising more than 1000 members despite being entirely undetected by traditional sequence-based alignment (Figure 1D). Interestingly, four of these eight domain types were β-propeller folds (Pfam clan: CL0186), which have highly symmetrical architectures (Figure 1D). β-propeller domains are structurally stable but display substantial sequence variability (Bonnardel et al., 2019). As a result, they can be reliably detected by structure-based alignment but often evade sequence-based identification (average sequence identity = 0.11). This property likely explains why RLKs annotated with the representative β-propeller domain regulator of chromosome condensation 1 (RCC1; PF00415 and PF13540) by sequence-based annotation were resolved into more than 10 distinct β-propeller domain types by structure-based annotation.

In contrast, the sequence-based alignment also uniquely identified the Xiao and Xiang domains (Figure 1C), which are recently characterized plant-specific domains annotated in metaRLK (Liu et al., 2024b); however, they currently lack reliable 3D templates in the Protein Data Bank (PDB), which limits their detection by structure-based alignment. Notably, sequence-based alignment identified 32 282 RLKs as containing wall-associated kinase (WAK) domains (PF07645, PF00008, PF12662, PF12661, PF13947, PF12947, PF14380, and PF08488), far exceeding the number identified by structure-based alignment, which highlights an inherent limitation of relying solely on PDB-dependent structural alignment. Together, these results indicate that comprehensive and robust RLK annotation requires the integration of both sequence- and structure-based approaches.

Composition of multi-domain RLKs

To adapt to the diverse and complex extracellular environment, plant RLKs often incorporate multiple domains (≥2; e.g., S-domains), which enhance or diversify their functional capabilities (Dievart et al., 2020). Before domain annotation, we used a structure-based domain segmentation approach, guided by structural biology principles, to precisely divide multi-domain RLKs into their individual domains (Figure 1A). This approach facilitates accurate domain-based structural and functional analyses.

Using 3D structural information, we annotated a total of 66 632 multi-domain RLKs across 136 766 RLKs. Among these RLKs, proteins with two domains were the most common (Supplemental Figure 1). To evaluate how structure-based domain segmentation improves structural resolution and functional interpretability, we compared these annotations with those derived from the sequence-based method (Liu et al., 2024b). Overall, 54 200 RLKs (81.3%) showed consistent family assignments between the two approaches, including 39 583 classified as LRR-RLKs. Although these LRR-RLKs were annotated as single-domain proteins by sequence-based analysis, structure-based segmentation revealed that they contain multiple tandem independently folded LRR units (Supplemental Figures 1C and 1I). Importantly, structure-based segmentation identified additional domains in 1897 RLKs (2.9%) that were not detected by sequence-based alignment. For example, 63 RLKs previously classified as LRR-RLKs were found to harbor an additional protein kinase domain (Supplemental Figures 1C, II). Moreover, 528 RLKs that lacked any sequence-based domain annotation were identified as LRR-RLKs through structure-based analysis (Supplemental Figures 1C, IV). Therefore, the refined domain segmentation does not alter RLK family classification but substantially improves domain resolution and functional interpretability.

Interestingly, the structure-based analysis also revealed rare domain configurations containing both glycoside hydrolase family 16 (PF00722) and WAK domains (Supplemental Table 6). The former adopts a repetitive α/β-fold architecture, whereas the latter forms a rigid β-fold. This contrasting fold architecture suggests potential functional interplay between flexible enzymatic modules and rigid recognition scaffolds.

Enrichment of rigid β-fold architectures in RLK domains

RLK domains act as modular building blocks that mediate extracellular signal perception. The internal fold architecture of a domain defines its structural configuration, which in turn determines its stability, flexibility, and potential binding specificity. To investigate how folds are distributed across RLK domains, we performed domain-level fold classification using the protein structure classification (CATH) hierarchy (Sillitoe et al., 2021; Lau et al., 2024) (Supplemental Table 7). In total, we identified 31 folds, with β-folds being the most common, comprising 16 types, including multiple β-hairpins, β-propellers, and the more rigid β-barrels (Figures 2A and 2B). In addition, eight α/β folds (e.g., α/β horseshoe and α/β barrel) and four α folds were detected, the latter largely occurring in extracellular protein kinases (PF00069 and PF07714) (Supplemental Table 8).

Among the most prevalent domains, LRRs typically adopt an α/β fold that forms a horseshoe-like structure involved in ligand recognition (Kobe and Deisenhofer, 1996; Liu et al., 2008) (Figure 2A). In LRR-RLKs, such as BRI1 (locus: At4g39400), parallel β-sheets form a stable scaffold and α-helices confer flexibility, together creating a concave binding surface optimized for ligand interaction (Hothorn et al., 2011; She et al., 2011). Another representative α/β fold is the α/β barrel, such as the glycoside hydrolase family 18 (PF00704) domain found in glycoside hydrolases. These enzymes regulate cell wall remodeling by catalyzing polysaccharide metabolism (Hurtado-Guerrero et al., 2009; Rai et al., 2016). The α/β barrel fold consists of an enclosed central β-barrel surrounded by eight α-helices. This highly symmetrical architecture provides both structural rigidity and enzymatic precision, with a central catalytic pocket that remains stable even in complex extracellular environments (Figure 2C).

Notably, our structure-based analysis uncovered a much broader diversity of β-folds than previously recognized. Previously, β-folds in RLKs were predominantly represented by the β-hairpin arrangements found in WAK domains (Zhang et al., 2005). We found that some pore-forming domains adopt β-barrel structures, a feature more commonly associated with proteins in animals and microorganisms (Zhang and Han, 2016). These include domains such as porin_3 (PF01459), OmpA_membrane (PF01389), and Omp85 (PF01103) (Figure 2D). The porin_3 domain is typically found in the mitochondrial outer membrane of eukaryotes and facilitates the passive diffusion of small hydrophilic molecules such as ATP, ADP, and phosphate (Shoshan-Barmatz and Ben-Hail, 2012). The OmpA_membrane and Omp85 domains are best known from bacterial outer membranes; OmpA interacts with the peptidoglycan layer to provide mechanical strength (Reusch, 2012), whereas Omp85 facilitates β-barrel insertion and protein translocation (Doyle and Bernstein, 2022). In plant RLKs, these β-barrel domains may contribute to extracellular structural support or facilitate protein localization at the cell surface.

Many β-fold domains, such as L-LEC and Peptidase_S9_N, are composed of multiple β-hairpins or β-propeller architectures. These domains are known to mediate interactions with complex extracellular macromolecules: L-LEC domains act as carbohydrate-binding modules that recognize glycoproteins or polysaccharides, such as pectin or xyloglucans (Damme et al., 1998), whereas peptidase_S9_N domains, as seen in animal dipeptidyl peptidase 4 (DPPIV) enzymes, engage in protein–protein or protein–peptide interactions through their β-propeller substructures (Aertgeerts et al., 2005). Notably, the number of β-propeller domain types was substantially expanded by the structure-based method. These domains exhibited diverse symmetry patterns—including 3-, 5-, and 7-bladed configurations—rather than strictly canonical forms (Figure 2E and Supplemental Figure 2), suggesting functional specialization or modular diversification among RLK subtypes. Strikingly, we identified a previously unreported architecture: a mirrored dual 3-fold β-propeller composed of two tandem symmetric three-bladed units (Figure 2E). This fold likely arose through duplication and fusion events that conferred enhanced structural stability and an enlarged interface for multivalent ligand recognition. Collectively, these observations support the idea that β-fold-rich domains are particularly well suited for stable, multivalent recognition of large and heterogeneous ligands in extracellular environments. Their rigid architectures reduce conformational entropy loss upon binding, potentially enhancing both affinity and specificity.

Overall, these results reveal a surprising predominance of structurally rigid β-fold architectures in RLKs, challenging traditional assumptions of α/β-fold prevalence and underscoring the structural and functional diversity encoded within plant receptor systems (Lehti-Shiu et al., 2009).

Structural similarity network analysis

The uncharacterized RLKs, hereafter referred to as “dark RLKs,” are resistant to classification because of a lack of corresponding 3D structures. To address this limitation, we constructed the first large-scale structural similarity network of RLKs, allowing us to explore potential family relationships and functional clustering beyond conventional annotation pipelines.

We first performed structure-based clustering of all RLKs and retained 6753 clusters (encompassing 98.8% of all RLKs) that contained at least two RLKs. Based on structural similarity among cluster representatives (Durairaj et al., 2023), we then constructed a structural similarity network comprising 6179 connected nodes and approximately 1.05 million edges (Figure 3A), successfully incorporating 34 227 dark RLKs (94.5%).

The network contained 124 components with at least two nodes, with the largest (component 1) encompassing approximately 92.2% of all nodes (Figure 3A and Supplemental Table 9). Further modular analysis of component 1 revealed 12 distinct communities, in which 19 known RLK families were tightly clustered. Many communities were highly enriched for a single family: community 6 was dominated by the LRR family (98.8%), community 7 by the L-LEC family (97.8%), and community 8 by the Xiao family (93.0%) (Supplemental Table 9). In particular, component 2 formed a well-separated subnetwork composed predominantly of the Xiang family (93.3%) (Figure 3A). These results corroborate the previously reported sequence-based classification by Liu et al. and provide independent structural evidence that Xiao and Xiang are evolutionarily distinct and well-defined RLK families (Liu et al., 2024b).

Although not all subnetworks were dominated by a single family (Supplemental Table 9), family members generally formed compact, minimally overlapping clusters, reflecting strong intra-family structural conservation and the robustness of the network (Figure 3A). These characteristics provide a solid framework for the discovery of novel RLK families and for reclassifying previously unannotated dark RLKs within an evolutionary context.

Reassignment of dark RLKs through network-based prediction

Within the structural similarity network, 25 933 dark RLKs clustered in components 1 and 2 but could not be reliably annotated using structure-based alignment alone (Supplemental Figure 3). To overcome this, we used ProtNLM-based functional prediction and compared the inferred functional profiles of dark RLKs with those of known families within the same network communities. When the predicted functions were consistent, dark RLKs were reassigned accordingly. Using this integrative strategy, 18 571 dark RLKs in community 3 were reassigned to the WAK family, 1306 in community 10 to the lysin-motif (LysM) family, and 3225 in communities 5, 6, and 11 to the LRR family (Figure 3B and Supplemental Table 10). Using the same method for component 2 reassigned an additional 301 dark RLKs to the Xiang family. In total, 23 403 dark RLKs (90.2%) were successfully reclassified.

Overall, this network-guided integration of structural clustering and functional inference provides a scalable framework for classifying dark RLKs beyond the limits of sequence- or structure-only methods.

Unclassifiable RLKs: The remaining dark space

Despite integrating structural annotation, clustering-based network analysis, and large-language-model-based functional prediction, 6792 dark RLKs remained unclassified, lacking confident family assignments or functional predictions (Supplemental Table 11). These dark RLKs represent a substantial unresolved region of the RLK landscape.

Among them, 1963 dark RLKs formed singleton clusters and were therefore excluded from the structural similarity network. The remaining 4829 dark RLKs were grouped into three categories: first, 2031 RLKs appeared as isolated nodes in the network; second, 248 RLKs from different components lacked links to known RLK families and showed inconsistent functional predictions (Supplemental Table 11); and third, 2550 RLKs were connected to multiple known RLK families but lacked consistent functional signatures. Among the 752 RLKs with available structures, UniDoc segmentation revealed that 578 could be partitioned into multiple domains, which may explain their broad structural similarity across different families. To enhance classification accuracy, we integrated findings from Liu et al., 2024b and reclassified 1885 dark RLKs (Supplemental Table 11).

Definition of 70 novel RLK families

Based on the structural similarity network, we observed that many RLK families formed distinct and well-separated subnetworks (Figure 3A). We defined a novel RLK family when more than 80% of a subnetwork (each containing ≥2 nodes and ≥10 RLKs) shared the same domain type or were classified as dark RLKs. For example, community 9, comprising 83.2% β-propeller domains (Pfam clan: CL0186), was designated as a new β-propeller RLK family containing 34 structurally distinct β-propeller domains (Supplemental Table 2). Using the same criteria, 2565 dark RLKs were grouped into 68 small, disconnected components that showed no detectable similarity to known RLK families (Figure 3C and Supplemental Table 9).

Unlike these independently clustered families, protein kinase domains (highlighted in red in Figure 3A) are widely distributed across multiple subnetworks but predominantly converge near the center of the network. These “pkinase” nodes exhibit the highest average betweenness centrality among all RLK families, which suggests that they serve as key structural bridges connecting distinct RLK families (Figure 3A and Supplemental Table 12). Although they do not form an isolated subnetwork or represent a dominant family, their cohesive distribution pattern and central location indicate shared structural features and potential functional significance. Based on these network topological characteristics, we therefore classify them as a novel RLK family—the Pkinase family (Supplemental Table 2).

Overall, the 68 isolated subnetworks, together with the β-propeller and Pkinase families, resulted in the definition of 70 novel RLK families. This represents a 333.3% increase in RLK family diversity relative to previous classifications (Figure 3D), substantially expanding our understanding of RLK structural and evolutionary complexity.

Distribution and evolutionary patterns of RLK families

Among the 91 identified RLK families, comprising 21 known and 70 newly defined families, we found that 15 of them were broadly distributed across more than 400 plant species. These included well-established families such as LRR, WAK, malectin-like, L-LEC, Xiao, and Xiang (Figure 4A and Supplemental Figure 4).

The LRR family was the most abundant and widespread, present in 507 species. Interestingly, the only exception was Caulerpa lentillifera, which contained only a single RLK with a domain annotated as IL-6Ra-bound (PF09240). Other widely distributed families included PAN (371 species), malectin (287 species), thaumatin (163 species), component 3 (160 species), and component 4 (134 species), underscoring their broad evolutionary presence across the plant kingdom (Supplemental Figure 4 and Supplemental Table 2).

In contrast, most of the 70 newly identified RLK families exhibited clear lineage specificity; except for Pkinase and β-propeller, which were broadly distributed, these families were enriched in either eudicots or monocots (Supplemental Figure 4 and Supplemental Table 2). Notably, the component 31 and von Willebrand factor type A (VWA) families were exclusively found in bryophytes, primarily in Physcomitrella patens. Similarly, the component 72 family, identified in five species, consisted exclusively of plants from the Fabaceae family, four of which belong to the Arachis genus (Figure 4B; Supplemental Table 2).

These findings suggest that although a core set of RLK families is conserved across land plants, many of the newly identified families exhibit clade-specific expansions, likely reflecting lineage-dependent functional diversification.

Functional annotation of newly defined RLK families

To infer the potential functions of newly identified RLK families, including 68 novel families composed of 2565 dark RLKs, as well as the previously defined Xiao and Xiang families (Liu et al., 2024b), we used ProtNLM, which predicts and classifies the functions of dark RLKs with high confidence using a structural similarity network (Figure 3B).

Remarkably, ProtNLM predicted that 49 of the 68 novel families exhibited functions associated with vegetative cell wall protein GP1-like (Supplemental Table 13), which binds oligogalacturonides (degradation products of pectin), thereby participating in cell wall integrity sensing and potentially contributing to salt–alkali stress adaptation in Oryza sativa (locus: Os03g0852400) (Zhu et al., 2024). In addition, component 38 was predicted to correspond to proline-rich protein HaeIII subfamily 1-like (Boron et al., 2014), further supporting a role in plant cell wall biology. For four families (components 10, 11, 34, and 119), ProtNLM returned low-confidence outputs, preventing functional assignment (Figure 5A).

We also annotated the plant-specific Xiao and Xiang families originally defined by Liu et al. ProtNLM predicted that Xiao is homologous to ALE2, a protein kinase in Arabidopsis that contains the ALE2-N domain, which has a structure similar to Xiao despite limited sequence conservation (Figures 5B and 5C and Supplemental Table 13). ALE2 is involved in epidermal differentiation and cuticle formation (Tanaka et al., 2007). Similarly, Xiang was predicted to resemble the SPARK domain (PF19160), which is involved in plant–fungus symbiosis (Montero et al., 2021) (Figures 5B and 5D and Supplemental Table 13).

Overview of metaRLK 2.0

Overall, to achieve a more accurate and comprehensive understanding of RLKs, we substantially upgraded the metaRLK database using a structure-based strategy. The updated version, metaRLK 2.0, integrates domain-level structural comparison, fold classification, structural clustering, family classification, and functional prediction. Using a structural similarity network and large language models, we conducted a systematic analysis of RLK families. The database also provides two search tools—Diamond (based on sequence similarity) and Foldseek (based on structural similarity)—to enable flexible and efficient exploration of RLKs. Currently, metaRLK 2.0 includes 883 domains (the combined set derived from both sequence- and structure-based annotation strategies) from 508 plant species, classified into 31 distinct folds and 91 RLK families, including 70 newly identified families (Figure 6). The database is freely accessible at www.metaRLK.com. In future updates, we plan to incorporate RLK variant sites, predict potential ligands, and further investigate their biological functions in plants.

Discussion

In this study, we comprehensively re-annotated plant RLKs through structure-based analysis and uncovered previously unrecognized layers of domain and family diversity that extend beyond the limits of traditional sequence-based classification (Liu et al., 2024b; Yin et al., 2024). Because protein structures are generally more conserved than sequences, structure-based methods enable more sensitive detection of domains, particularly for those with divergent sequences or repetitive structural features (Lau et al., 2023). Using this approach, we identified 677 distinct RLK domains, representing a 62.7% increase over the sequence-based approach (Liu et al., 2024b). Many of these newly identified domains display repetitive structural motifs, such as β-propeller domains. Furthermore, we identified numerous extracellular protein kinase domains; through motif detection (based on the GxGxxG, HRD, and DFG motifs), 38.1% of these protein kinase domains were classified as pseudokinases.

Interestingly, our findings suggest that some RLK families, particularly those newly identified in this study, may represent lineage-specific or even species-specific innovations. These families often display unique structural characteristics (e.g., as indicated by isolated subnetworks) and are restricted to specific plant lineages. For example, the VWA family was found to occur in eight bryophyte species across a dataset of 508 plant species (Figure 4B and Supplemental Table 2), reinforcing its bryophyte-specific distribution (Lehti-Shiu and Shiu, 2012). Notably, the VWA domain has also been detected in higher plants (e.g., Oryza sativa), where its expression is associated with responses to various biotic and abiotic stresses (including pathogens, drought, and salinity) (Karkute et al., 2022). In addition, we classified component 31 as another bryophyte-specific RLK family and identified functional predictions associated with AAI domain-containing proteins, which were detected exclusively in six bryophyte species (Figure 4B and Supplemental Table 2). Strikingly, ProtNLM predicted that 49 of the newly identified RLK families were GP1-like, with 41 of these showing strong lineage specificity (more than 90%)—especially those in Brassicaceae (9 families), Poaceae (8 families), Rosaceae (5 families), Fabaceae (5 families), and Malvaceae (5 families) (Supplemental Table 2). These taxonomically narrow distributions underscore the lineage-specific evolution and possible adaptive specialization of the newly defined RLK families. However, because of limited sampling and current computational constraints, their fold types and 3D structures remain largely uncharacterized.

A major unexpected finding was the enrichment of rigid β-fold architectures, such as β-hairpin, β-propeller, and β-barrel structures, in RLKs. These architectures may be involved in interactions with complex extracellular polysaccharides or structural components of the cell wall (Kohorn and Kohorn, 2012; Solan et al., 2021). This is consistent with the unique constraints of plant cell biology, in which a rigid extracellular matrix imposes functional demands not present in animal systems. Unlike animals, which predominantly use G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) embedded in flexible plasma membranes, plants have evolved RLKs with rigid, repetitive extracellular structures to mediate signal transduction through the cell wall. The functional implications of this adaptation are substantial and suggest that RLKs are structurally optimized for robust ligand recognition and mechanical stability in a cell-wall-constrained environment.

Although recent advances in large language models offer promising tools for protein function prediction, these predictions remain preliminary. To improve their reliability, we integrated them with structural similarity networks and used shared functional patterns within subnetworks to reclassify dark RLKs and infer potential functions for novel families. As research progresses, the functions of these novel families will become clearer, allowing them to be renamed. In future work, we plan to validate these functional predictions experimentally, such as through ligand-binding assays and substrate identification, to clarify the upstream and downstream roles of RLKs.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the effectiveness of integrating structure-based alignment, structure similarity networks, and large-language-model-based functional inference to identify novel RLK families and domain architectures. Using this expanded framework, we successfully reclassified 25 044 of the 40 357 RLKs that were previously unannotated by sequence-based methods and defined 70 novel RLK families. These findings not only improve RLK classification but also reveal deep, previously unrecognized evolutionary innovations, particularly among lineage-specific and structurally atypical receptors. Continued integration of computational predictions with experimental validation will be essential to fully elucidate the biology and evolution of this highly diverse RLK superfamily.

Methods

RLK data from metaRLK

We obtained RLK data for 508 plant species from metaRLK in June 2024 (Liu et al., 2024b). This study focused only on canonical RLKs; therefore, we only selected members from metaRLK if they had an extracellular domain, one transmembrane domain, and one intracellular kinase domain. To ensure data accuracy, we re-ran TMbed (v1.0.0) (python -m tmbed predict -f ./input -p ./result.pred --format 4) to predict the transmembrane topology of RLK members from the metaRLK database, ensuring all members met the requirements (Bernhofer and Rost, 2022). Ultimately, 311 581 RLKs from 508 plant species were retained.

Annotation of RLK domains using Foldseek

We used a structure-guided annotation pipeline. The extracellular regions of RLKs with available 3D structures (n = 136 766) were segmented into domains using UniDoc (v.20 251 022) (Zhu et al., 2023) and subsequently annotated using Foldseek, whereas RLKs lacking 3D structures (n = 174 815) were annotated directly using Foldseek without prior domain parsing. The command used for domain parsing was python Run_UniDoc_from_scratch_structure.py -i ./input_pdb -c A -o ./result.

Foldseek (v.9.427df8a) was used to align the extracellular region of each RLK against a 3D domain dataset (Heinzinger et al., 2024; Van Kempen et al., 2024). We created this dataset of 889 291 3D domain structures obtained from the PDB database as PDB or mmCIF files. The mapping between domains and PDB entries was derived from the SIFTS database in September 2024. We performed a high-coverage, high-sensitivity 3Di/AA-based Foldseek search for all 311 581 RLKs. The domain annotation procedure was as follows:

Step 1 (data preparation):

For 136 766 RLKs with 3D structures: foldseek createdb ./input_stru ./stru_db.

For 174 815 RLK sequences: foldseek createdb ./input_seq ./seq_db --prostt5-model weights.

For the domain database: foldseek createdb ./input_domain ./domain_db.

Step 2 (alignment): foldseek search ./stru_db (or ./seq_db) ./domain_db ./result_stru (or ./result_seq) tmp -s 9.5 -e 0.1.

Step 3 (output): foldseek convertalis ./stru_db (or ./seq_db) ./domain_db ./result_stru ./result_stru.m8 (or ./result_seq.m8).

Among the annotated results, domains labeled as putative, hypothetical, uncharacterized, of unknown function, or containing domain of unknown function (DUF) annotations were considered uninformative. After excluding these entries, 275 372 RLKs were successfully annotated, whereas 36 209 RLKs remained effectively unannotated. To evaluate the catalytic potential of protein kinase domains, we assessed the presence of the three canonical catalytic motifs—GxGxxG, HRD, and DFG. Domains lacking all three motifs were classified as pseudokinases in Supplemental Table S3 and the metaRLK 2.0 web portal (Taylor and Kornev, 2011). To assess potential false positives among detected single-occurrence domains, singleton domains were classified as low-confidence and labeled accordingly in Supplemental Table 3.

Fold distribution analysis of domains

To characterize the structural folds associated with each domain, we systematically mapped domain entries to the CATH database (v4.3) using the commonly applied threshold of 60% query domain coverage (Lau et al., 2024). This approach enabled the precise annotation of fold types and their corresponding spatial intervals (lengths) for each domain. Three dominant fold architectures were identified: α, β, and α/β folds. Large-scale structural similarity network

To facilitate the classification of unannotated RLKs, we constructed a large-scale structural similarity network based on clusters of extracellular regions. We used Foldseek to cluster the extracellular regions of all RLKs, resulting in 10 398 clusters. From these, we selected 6753 clusters that each contained at least two RLKs for subsequent network construction (Durairaj et al., 2023). The RLK clustering command steps were as follows:

Step 1: combine the RLK structure database (stru_db) and sequence database (seq_db) into a combined database (RLK_db).

Step 2 (alignment): foldseek search ./RLK_db ./RLK_db aln tmpFolder -s 9.5 -c 0.8 -e 0.001.

Step 3 (clustering): foldseek clust ./RLK_db aln clu.

Step 4 (output): foldseek createtsv ./RLK_db ./RLK_db clu clu.tsv.

To establish the structural similarity network, we used Foldseek to align the representative structure of each cluster. This procedure generated a structural similarity network comprising 6179 connected nodes and approximately 1.05 million edges (Durairaj et al., 2023). The RLK network construction steps were as follows:

Step 1 (data preparation): foldseek createdb ./clusters ./clusters_db

Step 2 (alignment): foldseek search ./clusters_db ./domain_db ./result tmp -s 9.5 -c 0.5 -e 0.0001.

Step 3 (output): foldseek convertalis ./clusters_db ./domain_db ./result result.m8.

We focused on the largest connected component, component 1, which encompassed the majority of RLK structural diversity. To investigate community structure within this component, we performed a modularity-based analysis using the louvain package (v1.4.2) in R. To evaluate the importance of each RLK family within component 1, we calculated the betweenness centrality of each node using the igraph package (v2.1.4) in R and calculated the average betweenness centrality for each RLK family based on its members.

Functional prediction using ProtNLM based on the structural similarity network

For dark RLKs located within subnetworks (components or communities) of the structural similarity network that contain known RLK families, we used the ProtNLM web server (https://neurosnap.ai/service/ProtNLM) to predict their functions (Gane et al., 2022). If any of the top five functional predictions matched those of a known RLK family, the corresponding dark RLK was reassigned to that family.

For the 68 novel RLK families, we excluded uninformative predictions such as “hypothetical protein” or “secreted protein,” and selected the top-ranked functional prediction—based on both frequency and confidence score—as the primary functional annotation. The same approach was used for the previously uncharacterized LRR, WAK, LysM, Xiao, and Xiang families.

Data analysis and plotting

All analyses and statistical tests were performed using Python (version 3.9). Statistical plots were generated using R (version 4.3.1). Network plotting was performed using Cytoscape (version 3.8.2). Protein structures were obtained using AlphaFold3. Similarity scores between protein sequences and structures were calculated using TM-align (https://zhanggroup.org/TM-align/).
