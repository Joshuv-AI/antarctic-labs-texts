Short‐Term Effects of Personal‐Level Environmental Temperature on Ambulatory Blood Pressure in Patients With Hypertension: A Multicity Panel Study
Authors: Hanrui Liu, Zhennan Lin, Xueli Yang, Jie Cao, Jianxin Li, Keyong Huang, Shufeng Chen, Xiangfeng Lu, Dongfeng Gu, Fangchao Liu, Jianfeng Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe inverse association between blood pressure (BP) and ambient temperature is known, but evidence on personal‐level environmental temperature (PET) and ambulatory BP among patients with hypertension remains limited.MethodsThis 3‐phase panel study recruited 277 patients with hypertension with intermediate‐to‐high cardiovascular disease risk in 4 cities in China from November 2017 to August 2019. Continuous PET and ambulatory BP were recorded by personal portable monitors. We used generalized linear mixed‐effect models to explore the associations of 24‐hour daytime and nighttime ambulatory BP, BP variability, and circadian rhythm with PET. Generalized additive mixed models were used to fit the exposure–response curves. Subgroup analyses were stratified by BP control status, body mass index, age, and sex.ResultsAn acute, inverse, and near‐linear association of PET with ambulatory BP, especially daytime systolic BP, were observed, with a 0.61 (95% CI, 0.48–0.75) mm Hg increase per 1 °C decrease of PET on the concurrent day. BP variability was linearly correlated with PET decline across all exposure windows. Circadian indicators, including morning BP, morning BP surge, and nocturnal BP decline, were also significantly associated with PET. Stronger associations were found among women and those with uncontrolled BP. Particularly, per 1 °C decline in PET on the concurrent day resulted in elevated daytime systolic BP of 0.98 (95% CI, 0.67–1.29) mm Hg, 0.43 (95% CI, 0.30–0.56) mm Hg among those with uncontrolled and controlled BP, respectively (P for interaction=0.001).ConclusionsA decrease in short‐term PET exposure could lead to adverse impacts on ambulatory BP indicators. This study highlights the beneficial role of BP control in response to temperature decline in hypertension management.

High blood pressure (BP) is the leading risk factor for global attributable deaths, which accounted for 10.9 million deaths in 2021 worldwide. 1 Elevated BP is also known to be responsible for the majority disease burden of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), 2 emphasizing the importance of controlling BP. Apart from traditional lifestyle risk factors (such as dietary sodium intake, alcohol intake, cigarette smoking, obesity) for hypertension, 3 environmental stressors can also contribute to increased BP, 4 , 5 , 6 such as fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and nonoptimal temperature. Particularly, with the increasing frequency and intensity of extreme temperature events, such as heat waves and severe cold spells in some regions, driven by climate change, exposure to nonoptimal temperature has imposed a growing disease burden on cardiovascular‐related conditions. 7 Therefore, more attention should be given to its impact on BP.

Numerous studies have consistently shown inverse associations between ambient temperature and different BP indices 5 ; however, several research gaps need to be highlighted. First, most available evidence was based on outdoor or indoor temperature measurements from monitoring stations or room thermometers, separately. Because of individual differences in activity patterns, relying solely on either outdoor or indoor temperature can lead to exposure misclassification, especially in winter or during the nighttime, when the temperature difference between indoor and outdoor environments can be substantial. Moreover, fixed‐station data fail to capture the heterogeneity of exposure at the individual level. This underscores the importance of using personal‐level environmental temperature (PET) for more accurate assessment by integrating both temperature scenarios. Second, clinic BP was commonly used. Due to the fluctuation of BP with circadian rhythms or other factors, ambulatory BP monitoring (ABPM), rather than clinic BP measurements at certain time points and typically during daytime hours, can capture dynamic 24‐hour BP changes, 8 including 24‐hour daytime and nighttime BP, BP variability, and several circadian rhythm indicators (ie, morning BP surge, nocturnal BP decline). The ABPM measurements not only reduce the potential bias of single‐time‐point readings but also provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the acute impact of temperature on BP across different periods of the day, especially during the nighttime. Third, single‐center design of previous small‐sample studies also limits the generalizability of results given the geographic disparity in temperature and BP associations. 9 , 10 Moreover, considering that short‐term changes in BP can lead to CVD in patients with hypertension or preexisting CVD, 11 , 12 who are also more susceptible to temperature changes, 13 it is essential to explore the patterns of PET’s impact on BP among populations with hypertension with CVD risk. Meanwhile, identifying susceptible subgroups of BP in response to temperature among patients with hypertension enables more targeted interventions during temperature fluctuations and provides valuable insights for routine hypertension management.

Collectively, by conducting a 3‐phase multicenter panel study including participants with hypertension with intermediate‐to‐high cardiovascular risk in China, we aimed to explore the short‐term effect of PET on ambulatory BP indicators, including the exposure–response relationship. Furthermore, we aimed to identify the potential modification effects of BP control status, body mass index (BMI), and demographic factors.

METHODS

Data are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Population and Design

In our panel study from November 2017 to August 2019, participants at intermediate to high risk for CVD were recruited from communities in 4 cities of China, namely, Beijing, Shanghai, Xi’an, and Wuhan. The intermediate to high risk for CVD was defined as systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≤130 to ≤159 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≤80 to ≤99 mm Hg, or taking antihypertensive medications and with at least 1 factor of central obesity, dyslipidemia, or diabetes diagnosis (or being treated for hypoglycemia). Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) subjects aged 35 to 74 years, (2) nonsmokers or former smokers who had not smoked for ≥1 year, and (3) subjects who resided in the study site ≥6 months. Moreover, subjects with a history of CVDs, chronic respiratory diseases, and other severe diseases; with occupational dust exposure; or who were pregnant were excluded. Participants who had acute infection within 1 week before each visit were rescheduled when fully recovered.

Participants experienced 3 repeated visits in summer, winter, and transition seasons (spring or autumn), respectively, and each visit lasted for 4 consecutive days. On the first day of each visit, participants completed questionnaires including information on sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle risk factors, and history of diseases and medications. All participants underwent physical examination including height, weight, waist circumference, and clinic BP. Additionally, a 72‐hour monitoring of PET, relative humidity, and individual PM2.5 were also performed. On the morning of the third day, subjects wore ambulatory BP monitors for the following entire 24 hours. Phone calls were required to ensure all devices functioned well during measurement.

A total of 320 subjects were enrolled at baseline, of which 23 were lost to follow‐up. We then excluded participants with prehypertension (n=14), with missing ABPM data (n=1) or not fulfilling the valid criteria of ABPM data (n=5), and finally, 277 subjects were eligible for the current analyses (Figure S1).

All staff underwent standardized training and were qualified at each phase to ensure the quality of research. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Fuwai Hospital in Beijing, and written informed consents were obtained from all participants.

PET and Other Meteorology Data

During the first 3 days of each study phase, continuous PET and relative humidity were automatically monitored by a personal portable recorder (HOBO MX1100 Data Logger; Onset Computer Corporation, Bourne, MA) every minute, which was fitted to the backpack. Participants also carried a portable monitor (MicroPEM, RTI Iternational, Durham, NC; or SidePak AM520, TSI Inc., Shoreview, MN) to obtain information on individual PM2.5 exposure. Except for sleeping and bathing, subjects were encouraged to wear the monitors at all times, and to put the backpack by the bed when sleeping.

Ambulatory BP Monitoring

A noninvasive and portable device (KC‐2300A; Beijing Kangkang Shengshi Information Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) were used to record ABPM data. The ABPM device met the Association for the Advancement of Medical Instrumentation/European Society of Hypertension/International Organization for Standardization validation standard. 14 The cuff was worn on the left arm, and its lower edge was 1 to 2 cm away from the elbow fossa; the tightness could be adjusted by participants, ensuring 2 fingers could fit between cuff and arm. The monitor was programmed to record BP at 20‐minute intervals from (6:00 am to 10:00 pm) and at 30‐minute intervals from 10:00 pm to 6:00 am. SBP and DBP were averaged hourly, and SBP variability and DBP variability were calculated as the SD of BP. Circadian rhythm indicators were also calculated. Morning BP referred to the mean BP during the first 2 hours after waking. Prewaking surge was defined as the difference between mean SBP 2 hours before and after waking, while sleep‐trough surge was calculated as morning SBP minus the mean SBP of the lowest nighttime SBP and the SBP before and after the lowest one. Furthermore, nocturnal BP decline=(daytime mean BP−nighttime mean BP)/daytime mean BP.

Covariates

Current regular drinking was defined as drinking alcohol ≥12 times in the past year. 15 Ideal physical activity level was defined as ≥150 min/wk of moderate physical activity or ≥75 min/wk of vigorous physical activity or an equivalent combination of both. BMI was calculated as weight divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2). Taking antihypertensive drugs within the past 2 weeks was considered as antihypertensive medication use. Diabetes was defined as fasting blood glucose ≥126 mg/dL, self‐reported physician diagnosis, or use of antidiabetic medication. Dyslipidemia was defined as any of the following: total cholesterol ≥200 mg/dL, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol ≥130 mg/dL, triglycerides ≥150 mg/dL, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol <40 mg/dL, 16 physician diagnosis, or lipid‐lowering therapy.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics of included subjects, PET, PM2.5 concentration, and relative humidity were expressed as mean±SD for continuous variables, and frequency with percentage for categorical variables. Differences of BP indices in different seasons were performed by 1‐way repeated‐measures ANOVA.

PET was averaged into multiple exposure time windows, including the concurrent ABPM day, lag 1 day, lag 2 days, and 2‐day moving average as well as daytime, nighttime, and preceding night of the ABPM measurement day (Figure S2). Relative humidity and the concentrations of PM2.5 were also set to the same exposure windows. To assess the association between PET and ambulatory BP, we used generalized linear mixed‐effect models to estimate changes and 95% CIs of ABPM with per 1 °C decline in PET exposure. Different ABPM indicators were considered as response variables, PET in different time windows was fitted as a fixed term, and random intercepts composed of subject level nested within city level to account for within‐subject correlations of repeated measurements and site‐related confounders. The models were also adjusted for age, sex, current regular drinking, ideal physical activity level, BMI, antihypertensive medication, diabetes, dyslipidemia, PM2.5, and relative humidity (degrees of freedom=1) concurrent with PET. Models were further adjusted for mean BP when BP variability was treated as the outcome. A generalized additive mixed model was used to fit the exposure–response curve in the association of ambulatory BP with PET exposure on lag 0 day. PET was processed by cubic spline functions, with degrees of freedom selected according to the Akaike information criterion. We also explored the impact of PET on ambulatory BP and daytime and nighttime BP variability, respectively. Furthermore, subgroup analyses were conducted on the basis of fitted models, stratified by BP control status (controlled [BP<140/90 mm Hg] versus uncontrolled), BMI (≥24 kg/m2 versus <24 kg/m2), age (≥60 years versus <60 years), and sex (male versus female), separately, so as to identify potential modification effects. We conducted 2 sensitivity analyses: (1) additionally adjusting for occupation and education level; and (2) representing BP variability using the coefficient of variation, calculated as mean±SD×100%.

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). All tests were 2‐sided, with statistical significance of P<0.05.

RESULTS

Overall, among 277 participants with average age of 59.1±8.5 years, 113 (40.8%) were men. People with central obesity accounted for 66.4% of the total population, and only 36.5% participants had physical activity at an ideal level. The average SBP and DBP was 134.5±13.6 mm Hg and 80.4±10.8 mm Hg, respectively. A total of 254 subjects (91.7%) took antihypertensive medications, of which calcium channel blockers (58.1%) were mostly used. Also, the mean PET was 21.4±3.6 °C (Table 1).

As shown in Figure 1, PET was inversely associated with 24‐hour and daytime ambulatory BP, especially daytime ambulatory SBP, with the estimated changes of 0.61 (95% CI, 0.48–0.75) mm Hg, 0.61 (95% CI, 0.49–0.73) mm Hg, 0.59 (95% CI, 0.46–0.71) mm Hg, and 0.60 (95% CI, 0.48–0.73) mm Hg per 1°C decrease of PET during lag 0 day, lag 1 day, lag 2 days, and 2‐day moving average, respectively. Specifically, almost linear negative correlations were found between PET of lag 0 day and ambulatory BP (Figure 2). As for BP variability, higher 24‐hour, daytime, and nighttime BP variability levels were correlated with PET decline across all exposure windows. The linear curves were also observed throughout lag 0 day (Figure S3). Moreover, morning BP surge and morning BP were inversely associated with PET exposure (Figure S4), while the increase in nocturnal SBP and DBP decline was observed as the temperature declined per 1 °C in all exposure windows (Figure S5). Nonlinear negative relationships were found between PET and morning BP surge during the same time exposure window, as there was a sharp decline in BP surge at about 25 °C (Figure S6). According to Figure 3, ambulatory BP in the daytime, but not nighttime, was significantly associated with average PET exposure on concurrent daytime and preceding nighttime. The significant association between BP variability and concurrent PET was also observed in both the daytime and nighttime among patients with hypertension. In sensitivity analyses, our findings remained robust after further adjustment for occupation and education level (Figure S7). Moreover, the results remained consistent using the coefficient of variation to assess BP variability (Figure S8).

In subgroup analyses, we found that the inverse association was amplified in the uncontrolled BP group. For instance, daytime ambulatory SBP was more susceptible to PET changes among those with uncontrolled BP, with estimated changes of 0.98 (95% CI, 0.67–1.29) mm Hg on lag 0 day, compared with 0.43 (95% CI, 0.30–0.56) mm Hg (P for interaction=0.001) in the controlled BP group per 1 °C decrease of PET. Similar patterns were observed during lag 1 day (uncontrolled: 0.92 [95% CI, 0.64–1.19] mm Hg versus controlled: 0.44 [95% CI, 0.32–0.56] mm Hg; P for interaction=0.002), and lag 2 days (uncontrolled: 0.91 [95% CI, 0.62–1.21] mm Hg versus controlled: 0.43 [95% CI, 0.31–0.56] mm Hg; P for interaction=0.003). The significant difference was also observed in 24‐hour and daytime ambulatory DBP between the 2 groups (Figure 4). Of note, the response of ambulatory BP to temperature was more pronounced on lag 0 day among participants with uncontrolled BP. Additionally, both SBP and DBP of those with uncontrolled BP were more likely to be influenced by concurrent PET than those with controlled BP during the daytime (Figures S9 through S12). When stratified by sex, we observed that 24‐hour DBP, daytime DBP, and 24‐hour DBP variability of women were more sensitive to temperature changes (Figure S13). For circadian rhythms of BP, women were also more prone to have higher morning SBP and morning DBP in response to decline of PET (Figure S9 and Figure S10). However, there was no modification effect of age on the associations of PET exposure with ABPM indicators (Figures S9 through S12 and Figure S14). Furthermore, the results generally showed no evidence that BMI modified the PET–ABPM associations, except for morning DBP (Figures S9 through S12 and Figure S15).

DISCUSSION

In this 3‐phase, multicenter panel study, we observed an inverse association of PET not only with BP levels, but with other ABPM indices including BP variability, morning BP, prewaking surge, sleep‐trough surge, and nocturnal BP decline. Notably, the increase in ambulatory BP in relation to PET changes was more pronounced among those with uncontrolled BP and among women, especially on the concurrent day, providing supporting evidence for the importance of BP control and more timely protective measures in these susceptible subgroups.

The inverse relationship between ambient temperature and BP among population with CVD‐related conditions was observed in previous studies, 13 , 17 , 18 which is in line with our findings. A global meta‐analysis showed that a 1 °C decline in daily temperature led to an increase in SBP of 0.34 (95% CI, 0.13–0.55) mm Hg and DBP of 0.14 (95% CI, 0.139–0.15) mm Hg among patients with hypertension and diabetes, whose effect size was larger than those in the general population. 5 However, given the differences in temperature‐related BP changes between indoor and outdoor environments, using personal‐level data can precisely assess the meteorological levels to which populations are truly exposed. 19 To date, evidence from PET is still lacking. Among individuals with hypertension, Modesti et al reported the changes in daytime SBP in relation to PET. 20 However, due to the seasonal variation in BP, the results of this cross‐sectional study may not be representative. By using assessment of PET and repeated measurements of ABPM throughout the year, we found the lag effect of temperature on 24‐hour and daytime BP within 72 hours among participants at intermediate to high CVD risk. Also, the instantaneous changes in daytime BP after exposure were observed on a within‐day time scale. The above results suggest that daytime BP is more prone to being influenced by short‐term temperature fluctuations, possibly due to higher metabolic processes during the day compared with the night. Additionally, the limited nighttime BP response may be partially attributed to the thermal effect of bedding on skin temperature, which likely attenuated the impact of ambient temperature on BP at nighttime. 21 With respect to the exposure–response relationship between temperature and BP, several studies demonstrated a clear inverse linear association of temperature and BP, including in individuals with hypertension, 8 , 17 , 22 which is generally consistent with our findings. On the other hand, V‐shaped, 23 U‐shaped, 24 and parabolic‐shaped 10 exposure–response curves were also observed. The heterogeneity of these studies may be explained by variations in the magnitude of the exposure window, population characteristics, and, of note, the temperature range of individual exposure.

ABPM could offer additional clinical insights into BP profiles, such as BP variability. Taking advantage of this, our study also provided novel evidence beyond the simultaneous effect of temperature on BP variability in participants with hypertension, 25 suggesting that temperature reduction could linearly increase BP variability with a daily lagged effect. Interestingly, our study found that low PET was associated with increased nighttime BP variability but not mean nighttime BP. This pattern may be attributed to the predominance of parasympathetic activity during sleep, however, intermittent activation of the sympathetic nervous system triggered by cold exposure may cause short‐term BP fluctuations and greater BP variability without altering the mean BP. Future studies are warranted to further explore the underlying mechanisms. Besides, it has been acknowledged that BP follows a circadian oscillation, with morning surge and nocturnal dipping. 26 Apart from existing evidence that there was an inverse association of temperature with morning BP in the exposure windows of both the concurrent and preceding day among patients with hypertension, 27 our study first clarified the adverse impacts of low temperature on morning BP surge and nocturnal BP dipping status. Since accumulating evidence has pointed out that increased CVD risk attributed to BP variability and circadian rhythm is independent of mean BP, 28 , 29 , 30 our findings may shed light on the potential link between nonoptimal temperature and CVD risks and have significant implications in the management of hypertension and prevention of further cardiovascular outcomes.

In our subgroup analysis, we found the modification effect of sex on the association between temperature and BP profiles among patients with hypertension. Similar to our results, several studies showed that BP levels in women are vulnerable to temperature changes, which could be attributed to differences in physiological sensitivity across sexes. 8 , 24 , 31 , 32 , 33 Nevertheless, some evidence indicated that men were more vulnerable. 34 The inconsistent findings may be explained by differences in study design, including variations in temperature exposure assessment, BP measurement, and subject characteristics. The modifying role of BMI in the temperature–BP associations remained controversial. Some studies suggested that individuals with obesity may be more susceptible 17 ; others found stronger effects in those with normal weight 34 or reported no modifying effect of BMI. 10 , 33 These issues warrant further investigation in future studies. Notably, the present study first reported that poorly controlled BP status could intensify the adverse effect of drop in temperature, especially on the concurrent day, highlighting the importance of reinforcing BP control among patients with hypertension in routine hypertension management. Furthermore, the identification of specific time windows emphasizes the necessity of prompt actions to mitigate PET‐induced BP fluctuations, particularly among patients with hypertension with inadequate BP control. The underlying mechanisms are scarcely discussed; thus, further studies are warranted. One possible explanation for this might be that patients with hypertension with uncontrolled BP have worse performance of sympathetic tone withdraw. 35

At present, the mechanism of the acute impact of temperature on BP profiles remains to be elucidated. The sympathetic nervous system might play a role in temperature‐related variations in BP. Exposure to low temperature resulted in increased activity of muscle nerve sympathetic activity, and peripheral vasoconstriction contributed to BP elevation. 36 , 37 This mechanism was further supported by findings from the Hypertension and Ambulatory Recording Venetia Study (HARVEST), which reported significantly higher 24‐hour norepinephrine in colder seasons. 25 On the contrary, reduced peripheral resistance to heat dissipation through sweating resulting from higher temperature could lead to decreased BP. 8 Additionally, current evidence suggested that the rise in morning BP surge was mediated by the reactivity of the sympathetic nervous system in response to the cold pressor test, which might explain the exaggerated surge in morning BP associated with low temperature (Figure S16). 38

This study has several strengths. First, we used portable devices to assess PET, which could reduce exposure misclassification commonly present in most of the previous studies that relied on outdoor temperatures from fixed‐site monitoring stations. Also, this approach takes into consideration of BP changes attributed to both outdoor and indoor temperature, providing a more accurate estimation of individual‐level exposure. Second, compared with the single‐point office BP measurements, the high temporal resolution of ABPM data enables the capture of circadian rhythms of BP and minimizes the bias caused by white‐coat hypertension and masked hypertension. Third, this study adopted a multiphase and multicenter design, with full consideration of the seasonal and regional differences in the temperature–BP associations, thus strengthening the reliability and generalizability of our results. Some limitations should also be noted. First, despite the accuracy of measurements, the application of PET assessment and ABPM limited the sample size of this panel study. However, this is the largest multicity panel study combining repeated PET with ABPM measurements to date. Second, due to missing information on waking time, we used BP measurements from 6:00 to 8:00 am as a substitute to calculate the morning surge. 39

CONCLUSIONS

Based on ABPM, this panel study demonstrated an acute, inverse, and almost linear association between PET and BP among patients with hypertension with intermediate to high CVD risk, especially in the daytime. We also indicated the adverse effects of decline in temperature on BP variability, morning BP surge, and nocturnal BP dipping. Women and individuals with uncontrolled BP were more susceptible to PET‐associated elevation in ambulatory BP. Our study underscores the importance of addressing temperature changes in BP management and the crucial role of controlling BP in patients with hypertension.
