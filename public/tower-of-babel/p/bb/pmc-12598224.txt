A Modified Double-Row Suture Repair Technique for Anterior Rotator Cable Preservation in Isolated Subscapularis Tears
Authors: Chang Hee Baek, Chaemoon Lim, Jung Gon Kim, Bo Taek Kim, Seung Jin Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
After identification of rotator cuff pathology, the treatment of subscapularis tear has increased to restore the anterior rotator cable and increase the internal rotation. Initially, open repair was performed to treat subscapularis tears. However, arthroscopic repair is now mainly performed following the development of arthroscopy, and results show that it is effective. There are various arthroscopic repair techniques, such as single-row, double-row, and transosseous suture bridge. The biceps pulley is a structure directly involved in the stability of the long head of the biceps and is located within the rotator interval between the anterior edge of the supraspinatus tendon and the superior edge of the subscapularis tendon. The biceps pulley consists of the superior glenohumeral ligament and coracohumeral ligament, which is anatomically connected to the anterior rotator cable. Therefore, it is thought that damage to the rotator cable may also affect the bicep pulley, affecting its stability. In this Technical Note, we described a modified double-row suture repair technique for isolated subscapularis tears. Using this technique, we could keep the benefits of the double-row suture repair technique, including better biomechanical effect and footprint coverage; but also preserving the anterior rotator cable and biceps pulley using a modified suture vector from the existing double-row technique.

The subscapularis tendon plays an important role as the only anterior rotator cuff tendon in glenohumeral kinematics and stabilization of the humeral head.1 The subscapularis tendon provides an anteroposterior force couple against the infraspinatus in the axial plane during external rotation.2 It provides a superoinferior force couple by resisting the shear force of the deltoid in the coronal plane during abduction.3 If the subscapularis tendon is torn, shoulder instability may occur due to loss of the force couple. A repaired subscapularis tendon restores the function of the posterior rotator cuff and reduces the tension of the supraspinatus tendon.4

The reported prevalence of subscapularis tears varies widely. The prevalence of subscapularis tears detected by arthroscopy is reported to be around 10% to 30%.5 Most cases of subscapularis tears involve the superior third of the tendon, and subscapularis partial tears associated with an anterosuperior rotator cuff tear lesion are common.6 Isolated subscapularis complete tears are less common than superior cuff or combined tears but are reported to cause pain and functional impairment in patients.7 Repair of isolated subscapularis tears accounts for about 4% of all cases of arthroscopic cuff repairs.8

The biceps pulley is a structure directly involved in stability of the long head of the biceps and is located within the rotator interval between the anterior edge of the supraspinatus tendon and the superior edge of the subscapularis tendon.9 The biceps pulley consists of the superior glenohumeral ligament and coracohumeral ligament (CHL). The CHL is divided into the medial CHL and lateral CHL, and the lateral CHL is anatomically connected to the anterior rotator cable.10 Therefore, it is thought that damage to the rotator cable may also affect the bicep pulley, affecting its stability.11

In previous studies on subscapularis repairs, the double-row suture repair technique was performed by exposing the biceps groove for the cable-involved anterosuperior rotator cuff tear.12,13 While the double-row suture repair technique has superior biomechanical properties, using it on isolated subscapularis tears could cause injuries to the anterior rotator cable and biceps pulley described earlier.

Therefore, we describe a modified double-row suture repair technique for patients with isolated subscapularis tears whose anterior rotator cables and bicep pulleys are preserved. Using this technique, we can keep the benefits of the double-row suture repair technique, including a better biomechanical effect and footprint coverage. Thus, we describe a modified double-row suture repair technique for isolated subscapularis tears using a 30° arthroscope.

Patient Position and Diagnostic Arthroscopy

The patient is placed in a lateral decubitus position with 30° of abduction and 20° of flexion of the shoulder under an interscalene nerve block. The ipsilateral arm is prepared and draped sterilely. For portal placement, 4 portals are used: the posterior viewing portal and 3 different anterior portals (anterosuperior, anteromedial, and anteroinferior). The anterosuperior portal is made roughly 2 finger-breaths off the anterolateral edge of the acromion for approximation of the torn subscapularis tendon. The anteroinferior portal is established lateral to the coracoid for the suture pathway. An anteromedial portal is made beneath the acromioclavicular joint for anchor placement (Fig 1).Fig 1Portal placement of this technique. The diagnostic arthroscopy examination is carefully performed to examine the glenohumeral joint, the condition of the subscapularis, and the long head of the biceps tendon (LHBT) through the standard posterior portal. The lever push technique is used to simultaneously push the proximal humerus posteriorly and pull the distal humerus anteriorly to increase the visualization. With the lever push, the diagnostic arthroscopy examination can be performed by using a 30° arthroscope in the posterior portal. After confirmation of the subscapularis tear, evaluation of the LHBT is important. If there is no LHBT pathology, including instability or subluxation, the modified double-row suture repair technique is performed (Fig 2).Fig 2Diagnostic arthroscopy examination using a 30° arthroscope in the posterior viewing portal of a right shoulder in the lateral decubitus position. Subscapularis Release

An anterosuperior portal should be placed parallel to the subscapularis tendon (Fig 3). For a clear visual field and an easy pathway of equipment and threads, the rotator interval window in the triangular space between the biceps, the subscapularis, and the coracoacromial ligament is released. The anteroinferior portal is made lateral to the coracoid for a suture pathway. A traction suture is made to the subscapularis through the anteroinferior portal for better exposure and preparation of the footprint (Fig 4). While pulling the traction suture, the subscapularis is released extensively to obtain full excursion to the footprint of the lesser tuberosity (of the humerus). Three suture anchors are used for a secure modified double-row suture repair, as well as 1 inferior lateral row suture anchor and 2 superior medial row anchors (Fig 6). The inferior lateral row suture anchor (double-loaded anchor; Arthrex) is inserted through the anteromedial portal while controlling the traction stitch for an easy pathway (Fig 8, Table 2). One suture of the inferior lateral row suture anchor is used as a mattress suture for the superior border of the subscapularis. The 2 limbs of the suture pass through the superior border of the subscapularis via the anteroinferior portal with a lasso suture passer. The other suture of the inferior lateral row suture anchor is used as a mattress suture for the inferior border of the subscapularis. The 2 limbs of the suture pass through the inferior border of the subscapularis via the anteroinferior portal with a lasso suture passer (Fig 9). The subscapularis is reduced on the footprint by pulling the subscapularis with a retriever, which comes in through the anterosuperior portal (Fig 10, Table 2). After reduction of the subscapularis, the inferior lateral row sutures are tied through the anteromedial portal (Fig 11). Then, 2 superior medial row anchors (PushLock Knotless suture anchor; Arthrex) are inserted by a modified double-row suture repair technique through the anteromedial portal while reducing the subscapularis through the anterosuperior portal (Fig 12, Table 2). The modified double-row suture repair threads are confirmed with a posterior viewing portal and an anterosuperior viewing portal. Postoperative Rehabilitation

After the operation, the operated shoulder is immobilized with an abduction brace for 6 weeks. Finger, wrist, and elbow exercises are allowed for this period. After removal of the brace, the patient starts a gentle passive range-of-motion exercise for 4 weeks and progresses to active range-of-motion exercise. Three months after the operation, the patient can return to daily activities and sports without any restriction.

Discussion

After identification of rotator cuff pathology, the treatment of subscapularis tears has increased to restore the anterior rotator cable and improve internal rotation.14 Initially, open repair was performed to treat subscapularis tears.15,16 However, arthroscopic repair is now mainly performed following the development of arthroscopy, and results show that it is effective.2,6 There are various arthroscopic repair techniques, such as the single-row, double-row, and pseudo-double row techniques and transosseous suture bridge. Techniques for knot configuration include the mattress, Mason-Allen, modified Revo, U-stitches, and lasso-loop methods.17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23

Regarding suture techniques, studies reported the double-row suture bridge technique to result in a biomechanically higher ultimate load, higher stiffness, and smaller elongation, as well as to maximize contact area for tendon-to-bone healing in the subscapularis tendon compared to the single-row technique, as had also been reported for the superior cuff repair.6,12,24 The double-row technique has been clinically reported to have a lower retear rate and improve tendon healing.12,13 Burkart et al.25 proposed an anatomic structure named the rotator cable and explained that it plays a biomechanical role of acting as a stress shielder for the rotator crescent, which is the avascular insertion site of the supraspinatus and infraspinatus. They compared the rotator cable to a suspension bridge, explaining that it can protect the rotator crescent, which is susceptible to tears, by keeping load stress out of the rotator crescent. In a study using a cadaveric shoulder model, Mesiha et al.26 observed that anterior cable-involved tears have a bigger tear size and are accompanied by reduced tendon stiffness and increased tendon stress. Cho et al.27 found larger and more complex tear patterns, more fatty degeneration, and higher retear rates in anterior rotator cable–involved cuff tears. Denard et al.28 mentioned the relation between cable integrity and pseudoparalysis.

In this Technical Note, we described a modified double-row suture repair technique for isolated subscapularis tears. Using this technique, we can keep the benefits of the double-row suture repair technique, including a better biomechanical effect and footprint coverage, but also preserve the anterior rotator cable and biceps pulley using a modified suture vector from the existing double-row technique.
