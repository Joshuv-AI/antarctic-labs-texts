Robot-assisted versus conventional posterior lumbar interbody fusion for lumbar spinal stenosis: a propensity score-matched cohort study on total and hidden blood loss
Authors: Junjie Qiao, Xinyao Lv, Yuyu Fan, Ruizhao Zhao, Xiutong Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHidden blood loss (HBL) represents an important component of perioperative bleeding after posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF), especially in multilevel procedures. Whether robot-assisted PLIF (R-PLIF) is associated with lower total blood loss (TBL) and HBL than conventional PLIF remains uncertain.MethodsThis was a single-center retrospective cohort study of consecutive patients who underwent robot-assisted PLIF (R-PLIF) or conventional PLIF between January 2019 and December 2023. After common-support restriction and 1:1 propensity score matching with an exact-match constraint on fused levels, 102 R-PLIF cases were matched to 102 controls; 58 patients fell outside the region of overlap and 138 additional eligible patients were not retained because no acceptable match was available under the prespecified caliper. TBL was calculated using Nadler’s and Gross’s formulae; visible blood loss (VBL) was defined as intraoperative loss plus postoperative drainage; HBL was derived rather than directly measured, using HBL = TBL − VBL + transfusion volume; the postoperative hematocrit used for the Gross calculation was obtained on postoperative day 1 (within 24 h). A linear model with robust standard errors clustered by matched pair was used to assess the interaction between surgical approach and number of levels.ResultsTBL was significantly lower in the R-PLIF group (500.5 ± 276.6 mL) than in the PLIF group (629.0 ± 293.0 mL) (MD − 128.4 mL, 95% CI − 148.7 to − 108.1; P < 0.001). Compared with conventional PLIF, R-PLIF was associated with lower VBL (308.1 ± 146.6 vs. 401.4 ± 173.8 mL; P < 0.001) and lower HBL (224.8 ± 175.0 vs. 282.5 ± 194.9 mL; MD − 57.8 mL; P = 0.001). An exploratory approach × level interaction (P < 0.001) suggested that the between-group difference in TBL was larger among patients undergoing ≥ 2-level fusion.ConclusionsIn this propensity score–matched retrospective cohort, R-PLIF was associated with lower estimated TBL, VBL, and formula-derived HBL than conventional PLIF. Background

Lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) is a common degenerative condition that often requires posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF) when conservative treatment fails [1]. PLIF can effectively decompress neural elements and restore segmental stability, but it is frequently accompanied by considerable perioperative blood loss, especially in multilevel procedures [2, 3]. In addition to the visible blood loss recorded intraoperatively and via drainage, a substantial proportion of perioperative loss occurs as hidden blood loss (HBL), which may lead to postoperative anemia, delayed mobilization, and even transfusion [4]. Therefore, strategies that can reduce not only total blood loss (TBL) but also HBL are of particular clinical relevance in lumbar interbody fusion surgery.

Recent perioperative blood-management pathways for lumbar fusion emphasize preoperative anemia optimization, restrictive transfusion triggers, judicious drainage strategies, and antifibrinolytic use (e.g., tranexamic acid), which collectively aim to reduce both visible blood loss (VBL) and HBL [5]. In this context, approaches that reduce iatrogenic tissue disruption and standardize instrumentation may complement modern blood-management bundles and potentially mitigate perioperative anemia.

Robot- or navigation-assisted spinal surgery has been introduced to improve the accuracy of pedicle screw placement, to standardize screw trajectories, and to minimize repeated soft-tissue manipulation [6, 7]. Several clinical studies and recent summaries of robotic spine surgery have confirmed its technical advantages, including reduced fluoroscopy exposure and more precise instrumentation, particularly in complex or multilevel constructs [8, 9]. Recent robotic lumbar fusion studies have provided important evidence regarding robot- or navigation-assisted pedicle screw instrumentation. In a large propensity score–matched cohort, Asada et al. reported lower intraoperative blood loss and shorter length of stay after robot-navigated pedicle screw insertion in primary lumbar fusion surgery [9]. Hwang et al. further compared freehand, fluoroscopy-guided, and robot-assisted techniques in a propensity score–matched cohort and demonstrated superior screw accuracy with robotic assistance, with attention to clinical and radiological outcomes [10]. However, these studies primarily addressed intraoperative estimated blood loss, surgical efficiency, screw accuracy, or general clinical outcomes; they did not specifically evaluate formula-derived total blood loss (TBL) and hidden blood loss (HBL) in a homogeneous PLIF-only cohort. Accordingly, the incremental novelty of this retrospective, propensity score–matched study was to evaluate whether robot-assisted PLIF is associated with lower estimated total blood loss and formula-derived hidden blood loss than conventional PLIF in a homogeneous lumbar spinal stenosis cohort, and whether this association varies by fused-level category.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a retrospective, single-center, propensity score–matched cohort study conducted at the Department of Spine Surgery, Beijing Shijitan Hospital, Capital Medical University. Consecutive patients who underwent posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF) for degenerative lumbar disease between January 2019 and December 2023 were screened. The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Shijitan Hospital (approval No. sjtky11-1x-2023(044)). Written informed consent was waived because the study used de-identified data only.

Participants and eligibility criteria

Patients were eligible if they met all of the following criteria: (1) definite diagnosis of lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) with symptoms refractory to ≥ 6 months of conservative treatment; LSS was defined based on the correlation of clinical symptoms with MRI findings, specifically excluding cases with concomitant foraminal stenosis, facet cysts, or dynamic instability without listhesis. Operationally, central canal stenosis was identified on axial T2-weighted MRI by the presence of dural sac narrowing, along with crowding of the cauda equina or obliteration of the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) signal. Lumbar central stenosis severity was graded on axial T2-weighted MRI using the qualitative Schizas classification based on dural sac morphology [11]. Variables included Schizas grade, lateral recess stenosis severity, number of decompressed levels, laminectomy extent, medial facetectomy grade, cage count, posterior release/osteotomy are summarized in Additional file 1: Table S1. (2) first-time lumbar fusion surgery with complete perioperative records; and (3) surgery performed by the same senior spine surgeon to minimize technical heterogeneity. Before the study period, the surgeon had completed approximately 100 robot-assisted thoracolumbar/lumbar screw-placement cases using the same platform. To mitigate proficiency-related confounding, we included index year in the propensity score model and examined the chronological distribution of R-PLIF and conventional PLIF cases before and after matching. While restricting operations to a single senior surgeon improves internal consistency and minimizes learning-curve effects, this design may limit generalizability to other surgeons, centers, or robotic platforms. Exclusion criteria were: (1) pathology such as spinal tuberculosis, degenerative spondylolisthesis, spinal deformity (standing coronal Cobb angle > 10°), tumor, or active infection; (2) pre-existing coagulopathy or use of anticoagulant/antiplatelet agents that could not be safely discontinued; (3) revision or staged lumbar procedures, because these cases have inherently higher and less predictable blood loss; and (4) intraoperative cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage, because CSF-related management, including dural repair, altered drainage, bed rest, fluid adjustment, and transfusion timing, may distort hematocrit-derived TBL/HBL estimates and reduce between-group comparability. Accordingly, the primary estimand was perioperative blood-loss estimates in first-time PLIF patients with lumbar spinal stenosis without intraoperative CSF leakage, all of whom met the same clinical/MRI-based diagnostic criteria and underwent standardized drainage and hematocrit assessment.

Among eligible patients, 132 underwent R-PLIF and 268 underwent conventional PLIF. After propensity score matching (see below), 102 R-PLIF patients were successfully matched 1:1 with 102 PLIF patients and entered the final analysis set (Fig. 1). This reporting follows the STROBE recommendations for observational studies.

Surgical procedures

All operations were performed under general anesthesia by the same senior surgeon using a standard posterior midline approach. In both groups, the incision was made in the midline, followed by bilateral subperiosteal exposure of the posterior elements and facet joints at the target level(s). A standard posterior self-retaining retractor was used in both groups. The same electrocautery device and power settings were used in both groups, and no operating microscope was used. The following steps were performed using the same standardized protocol in both groups: (1) posterior exposure and confirmation of target levels; (2) decompression (bilateral complete laminectomy with < 50% medial facetectomy); (3) discectomy and endplate preparation; (4) bilateral interbody cage insertion with local autologous bone graft; (5) No osteotomy or Ponte-like release; (6) rod placement, compression, and final tightening; (7) hemostasis and wound closure with placement of a closed-suction drain.

Conventional PLIF (PLIF group)

Pedicle screws were inserted freehand based on anatomical landmarks. Screw position was confirmed using intraoperative C-arm fluoroscopy according to routine practice.

Robot-assisted PLIF (R-PLIF group)

Robot-guided procedures were performed with the TiRobot surgical system (TINAVI Medical Technologies, Beijing, China). After intraoperative 3D imaging, pedicle screw entry points, trajectories, and implant sizes were planned on the workstation. The robotic arm was then aligned to the planned trajectory to guide drilling/tapping and screw insertion.

To provide procedural granularity relevant to blood loss, decompression-burden and interbody-preparation variables were abstracted from operative records and are summarized together with radiographic stenosis severity in Additional file 1: Table S1. To characterize approach-related exposure and device-specific workflow, the following variables were abstracted from operative records and intraoperative imaging records when available: incision length, retractor type, screw count, mean screw diameter, mean screw length, screw redirection count, screw repositioning count, pedicle breach requiring intraoperative revision, number of intraoperative 3D imaging/O-arm scans, robot registration failure, robot abandonment, and fluoroscopy shots [12]. Robot registration failure was defined as inability to complete image-to-patient registration requiring conversion to a nonrobotic technique. Robot abandonment was defined as discontinuation of robotic guidance after initial setup. Screw repositioning was defined as removal and reinsertion or revision of a screw during the same procedure because of malposition or breach concern. Fluoroscopy shots were not uniformly documented in the retrospective records and were therefore reported as unavailable rather than analyzed quantitatively. Approach-related exposure and device-specific workflow variables are summarized in Additional file 1: Table S2.

Perioperative management

Topical hemostatic adjuncts were used only when necessary and followed the same institutional protocol in both groups. All patients received routine perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis (cefuroxime sodium, per institutional protocol). No patient received tranexamic acid (TXA) or any other antifibrinolytic agent during the study period; this reflected a uniform institutional protocol rather than a study-driven intervention, which is important for interpreting between-group differences in blood loss [13]. Therefore, the present study should be interpreted as estimating the association between robot-assisted PLIF and perioperative blood-loss metrics under a no-TXA institutional pathway. This design improves internal comparability between groups because antifibrinolytic exposure was consistent across the cohort, but it also defines an important external-validity boundary: the observed absolute differences in TBL, VBL, HBL, drain output, transfusion, and postoperative anemia may not directly generalize to centers that routinely incorporate TXA into bundled perioperative blood-management or ERAS protocols [5]. A closed-suction drain was routinely placed at wound closure using the same institutional protocol in both groups. The same suction setting was used throughout the cohort, and routine drain stripping was not performed beyond standard nursing care. Drain output was recorded in 24-h intervals. Drain removal was planned when the preceding 24-h output was < 50 mL, and no drain was intentionally removed early for logistical reasons such as discharge planning or bed turnover. For auditability of the blood-loss calculations, we additionally recorded cumulative postoperative drain output, the actual time from wound closure to drain removal, the time of the POD1 hematocrit draw, and confirmed that drain removal occurred after the POD1 hematocrit measurement.

Transfusion followed a center-wide protocol jointly used by the spine service and anesthesiology: red-blood-cell transfusion was initiated if (i) hemoglobin (Hb) < 7 g/dL in hemodynamically stable patients; (ii) Hb < 8 g/dL accompanied by anemia-related symptoms or significant cardiovascular disease; or (iii) ongoing bleeding with hemodynamic instability regardless of Hb. “Autologous transfusion” referred to reinfusion of intraoperative cell-salvaged blood; “allogeneic transfusion” referred to packed red cells. Because transfusions may occur before or after the day-1 hematocrit (Hct) draw, transfusion timing could affect formula-based TBL/HBL estimates; therefore, we only included transfusion volumes administered intraoperatively or postoperatively before the postoperative Hct measurement. These volumes were extracted from medical records (mL); when only PRBC units were documented, volumes were converted using 200 mL per unit (institutional standard). The frequency of PRBC unit-to-volume conversion is summarized in Additional file 1: Table S3, Panel A. Transfusion outcomes were prespecified as exploratory endpoints and are reported accordingly.

Outcomes and definitions

The primary outcome was total blood loss (TBL), prespecified as the most comprehensive quantitative measure of overall perioperative blood-loss burden. Key secondary outcomes were visible blood loss (VBL) and hidden blood loss (HBL), because they represent the two direct quantitative components of the primary blood-loss construct and are measured on the same continuous scale. Exploratory outcomes included the intraoperative blood-loss rate (Q-value), transfusion, postoperative anemia, operative time, and length of hospital stay (LOS). Transfusion and postoperative anemia were considered exploratory, although clinically important, because they are downstream outcomes influenced not only by blood loss itself but also by clinical judgment, symptoms, comorbidity, institutional transfusion thresholds, and the timing of postoperative assessment. All outcomes were analyzed in the matched cohort.

Postoperative anemia was defined according to World Health Organization haemoglobin cutoffs [14] (Hb < 12 g/dL in women and < 13 g/dL in men) or patients who required postoperative RBC transfusion.

Postoperative Hct was measured on postoperative day 1 (within 24 h) and used in all calculations. We selected POD1 as the primary postoperative time point because it was the only standardized and consistently available hematocrit measurement across the cohort, thereby reducing measurement heterogeneity and missing-data bias. Although later time points (e.g., POD2 or nadir Hct) may better capture ongoing postoperative hemodilution in some patients, these values were not uniformly available in this retrospective cohort and their use could have introduced additional selection bias. Accordingly, POD1 Hct was used as a pragmatic standardized time point for formula-based blood-loss estimation rather than as a direct measure of true occult blood loss. Visible blood loss was defined as: VBL (mL) = intraoperative blood loss + postoperative drain output.

To reflect perioperative replacement, hidden blood loss was calculated as:

HBL (mL) = TBL − VBL + transfusion volume. For the purpose of the formula-based HBL calculation, autologous cell-salvaged blood reinfusion and allogeneic PRBC transfusion were both included in the transfusion-volume term if administered before the POD1 Hct measurement; however, they were also reported separately as exploratory outcomes because their physiologic implications may differ. When PRBCs were recorded only in units, volume was converted using 200 mL per unit according to the institutional standard [2].

The Q-value was defined a priori as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating index of intraoperative bleeding normalized to operative time: Q-value = IBL (mL) / operative time (min).

Propensity score matching and statistical analysis

To reduce selection bias due to non-random assignment of surgical approach, we performed 1:1 nearest-neighbor propensity score matching (PSM) without replacement. Covariates were prespecified based on clinical relevance, prior evidence for perioperative blood loss and treatment selection, and a directed acyclic graph–style clinical rationale. We prioritized baseline or pre-exposure variables that could influence both surgical approach selection and perioperative blood loss. ASA class captured baseline physiologic risk, and BMD reflected bone quality that may affect instrumentation difficulty and pedicle-tract bleeding. Because perioperative pathways and enhanced recovery practices may evolve over time, index year was additionally included to partially account for temporal changes in perioperative workflow and accumulated surgeon/platform experience, although residual era-related and proficiency-related confounding cannot be fully excluded.

A propensity score (PS) for receiving robot-assisted PLIF was estimated using multivariable logistic regression including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), diabetes mellitus, hypertension, smoking status, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) class (I–III), number of fused levels (1 / 2 / ≥3), platelet count, INR, APTT, albumin, baseline hemoglobin and hematocrit, preoperative anemia optimization, bone mineral density (BMD), and index year. Fused levels were enforced as an exact-match constraint, and nearest-neighbor matching was performed within a caliper of 0.2 SD of the logit of the propensity score. Post-exposure variables, including operative time, transfusion, drain duration, drain output, and perioperative fluid balance, were not included because they may be mediators or components of the outcome construct.

Common support was assessed by visual inspection of propensity score distributions and the overlap of score ranges between groups. Patients falling outside the common support region were excluded from the matching pool (n = 58 in the full cohort). Among those within the overlap region, additional patients were not retained if no eligible match was available under the prespecified exact-match and caliper constraints (n = 138). Ultimately, 102 robot-assisted cases were successfully matched 1:1 to 102 conventional PLIF controls.

As a post hoc sensitivity analysis, pair-clustered linear regression models were fitted for TBL, VBL, and HBL after additional adjustment for available radiographic stenosis severity, decompression-burden variables, interbody-preparation characteristics, and retrievable blood-loss determinants. The corresponding model results are provided in Additional file 1: Table S10. To assess potential selection bias from excluding intraoperative CSF-leak cases, we descriptively compared excluded R-PLIF and conventional PLIF patients with respect to baseline characteristics, fused levels, radiographic severity, decompression burden. Given the small number of CSF-leak cases, this analysis was considered descriptive only.

Discharge Hct was available in a subset of matched patients. Accordingly, we performed an exploratory discharge-Hct sensitivity analysis restricted to complete matched pairs in which discharge Hct was available for both the R-PLIF and PLIF patients. TBL was recalculated using discharge Hct in Gross’s formula, and HBL was recalculated as discharge-Hct-derived TBL − VBL + transfusion volume administered before the discharge Hct measurement when available. Because discharge Hct was not protocolized and was influenced by discharge timing and clinical status, this analysis was considered exploratory.

Outcome analyses were conducted in the matched cohort and accounted for the matched-pair structure. Continuous outcomes were compared using paired t tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, as appropriate; categorical outcomes were compared using McNemar’s test. The prespecified approach × fused-level interaction for TBL was assessed using a linear model with robust standard errors clustered by matched pair. The primary endpoint was tested at a two-sided α = 0.05 without multiplicity adjustment. Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate control at 5% was applied only to the key secondary outcomes, VBL and HBL. Other outcomes were interpreted as exploratory. Sensitivity analyses included alternative PRBC conversion factors of 180, 200, and 250 mL per unit. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R (version 4.3.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). We followed the STROBE recommendations for reporting observational studies.

Results

Patient demographics and baseline characteristics

As shown in Figs. 1 and 479 consecutive patients were screened. Intraoperative CSF leakage occurred in 6.1% (11/181) of R-PLIF cases and 5.4% (16/298) of conventional PLIF cases (χ² = 0.106, P = 0.745). These cases were excluded from matching and outcome analyses per the prespecified criteria. CSF-leak events were rare and occurred across multiple index years without an obvious concentration in any single year. Among the 400 eligible patients entered into propensity score estimation (132 R-PLIF and 268 conventional PLIF), there were no missing data in any baseline covariates used in the propensity score model (Additional file 1: Table S7). After applying 1:1 PSM incorporating index year of surgery, 102 robot-assisted PLIF cases were matched to 102 conventional PLIF controls. Thus, the final matched cohort comprised 204 of 400 patients (51.0%), whereas 196 patients (49.0%) were not retained in the matched analysis. Specifically, 58 patients fell outside the region of common support and 138 additional patients were not retained because no eligible 1:1 match was available under the prespecified exact-match and caliper constraints (Additional file 1: Table S4). Covariate balance improved substantially after matching (Fig. 3; Table 1). Pre-match standardized mean differences (SMDs) ranged from 0.01 to 0.25, whereas all post-match absolute SMDs were < 0.10 (range, 0.00–0.08), indicating adequate overall balance. Among the prespecified baseline covariates, the largest residual imbalances were observed for age and preoperative hemoglobin (absolute SMD = 0.08 for both), while all remaining covariates showed smaller imbalances. Additional matching diagnostics, including pre- and post-match variance ratios, are summarized in Additional file 1: Table S8. For continuous baseline covariates, variance ratios improved from 0.670 to 1.149 before matching to 0.819–1.132 after matching. Balance was also re-evaluated within the matched 1-level, 2-level, and ≥ 3-level strata relevant to the interaction analysis (Additional file 1: Table S6).

Primary outcome

In the matched cohort, total blood loss (TBL) was significantly lower in the R-PLIF group than in the PLIF group (500.5 ± 276.6 mL vs. 629.0 ± 293.0 mL), yielding a mean difference (R − P) of − 128.4 mL with a 95% confidence interval of − 148.7 to − 108.1 mL (P < 0.001). Because TBL was the prespecified primary endpoint, this comparison was not subjected to multiplicity adjustment.

Secondary and exploratory outcomes

Findings for key secondary outcomes were consistent with the primary analysis. Visible blood loss (VBL) was lower with R-PLIF (308.1 ± 146.6 mL) than with PLIF (401.4 ± 173.8 mL), corresponding to a mean difference (R − P) of − 93.2 mL (P < 0.001; BH-adjusted q < 0.001). Hidden blood loss (HBL) was likewise reduced in the R-PLIF group (224.8 ± 175.0 mL vs. 282.5 ± 194.9 mL; MD − 57.8 mL; P = 0.001; BH-adjusted q = 0.001) (Table 2). The intraoperative blood-loss rate (Q, mL/min) was significantly lower in the R-PLIF group than in the PLIF group (1.0 ± 1.1 vs. 1.4 ± 1.2; MD − 0.4 mL/min; P < 0.001). In contrast, operative time did not differ between groups (135.8 ± 19.7 vs. 135.8 ± 22.0 min; P = 1.000), indicating that lower intraoperative bleeding in the robotic group occurred despite a similar overall procedure duration.

When stratified by the number of fused levels, HBL was consistently lower in the R-PLIF group and the between-group difference was largest in two-level fusions. Discussion

Main findings

In this propensity score–matched cohort of patients undergoing PLIF for lumbar spinal stenosis, robot-assisted PLIF was associated with statistically lower estimated perioperative blood-loss metrics compared with conventional PLIF. The mean TBL difference was approximately 128 mL; although statistically robust, this study did not define an a priori threshold for a clinically meaningful difference in relation to transfusion, postoperative anemia, or mobilization. Therefore, the magnitude of this difference should be interpreted cautiously as a difference in estimated perioperative blood-loss metrics rather than as definitive evidence of clinically meaningful benefit. The linear model further showed a significant interaction between surgical approach and number of fused levels, suggesting that the observed between-group difference was larger in multilevel than in single-level fusions. Although the absolute TBL difference appeared larger in the 2-level and ≥ 3-level strata than in the 1-level stratum, the ≥ 3-level subgroup included only 20 matched pairs and had greater residual imbalance than the other strata. Therefore, these findings should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence that robotic assistance provides a larger benefit in multilevel fusion. The results mainly suggest that the association between R-PLIF and lower blood-loss estimates may be more pronounced in more extensive procedures, but this requires confirmation in larger cohorts with adequate multilevel representation.

Mechanisms and comparison with previous studies

Our findings are directionally consistent with Asada et al. [9], who reported reduced intraoperative blood loss and length of stay with robot-navigated pedicle screw insertion in a propensity score–matched cohort. However, both studies remain observational; therefore, the available evidence should be interpreted as supporting an association between robotic/navigation assistance and reduced perioperative blood-loss metrics, rather than proving a causal blood-sparing effect. Two plausible mechanisms may explain the observed association between robot-assisted PLIF and lower blood-loss metrics. First, robotic guidance may reduce repeated pedicle probing, screw redirection, and screw-related revision by standardizing the entry point and trajectory of pedicle screws, thereby decreasing cancellous oozing from screw tracts [17, 18]. Second, a more predictable instrumentation workflow may reduce unplanned manipulation during screw placement, which is consistent with the lower intraoperative blood loss and lower Q-value in the robotic group [19, 20]. Because both groups underwent the same posterior midline approach, bilateral subperiosteal exposure, and retractor type, this finding should not be interpreted as a reduced-exposure or Wiltse-like advantage of robotic PLIF; moreover, because phase-specific blood loss was not recorded, this mechanism remains inferential rather than directly proven. Importantly, the Q-value should not be viewed as more clinically relevant than transfusion or postoperative anemia; rather, it is presented only as a descriptive intraoperative index, whereas transfusion and anemia remain the more patient-centered downstream consequences of perioperative blood loss. Notably, total operative time was similar between groups in the matched cohort. This finding suggests that the lower estimated blood-loss metrics observed in the R-PLIF group were not primarily attributable to shorter overall operative time. Rather, they may be related to differences in instrumentation workflow within a comparable total case duration. At the same time, existing reports on robotic spine surgery often focus on screw accuracy and complication rates, and only sparsely report hidden blood loss [21, 22]; our findings complement the current evidence base by suggesting that, within a PLIF-only matched cohort, robot-assisted surgery is associated with lower estimated TBL and formula-derived HBL, outcomes that have been less directly addressed in prior robotic lumbar fusion studies. Differences across studies may also reflect variation in postoperative drain-management practice, including drain-removal thresholds, drain duration, and other workflow details, in addition to the routine use of TXA. Because postoperative drain output is part of VBL and enters indirectly into the calculation of HBL, measured between-group differences in VBL/HBL may be partly protocol-sensitive. In the present study, drain management followed the same institutional protocol in both groups; nevertheless, some residual practice-level variation inherent to retrospective perioperative care cannot be completely excluded. Furthermore, an interpretation boundary is the uniform absence of TXA in the present cohort. This feature improves internal validity because antifibrinolytic exposure was identical between groups, but it limits external generalizability to contemporary centers using bundled blood-management or ERAS pathways. Modern perioperative blood-management protocols for spine surgery often combine restrictive transfusion thresholds, goal-directed fluid management, TXA, autologous transfusion, standardized drain management, and early postoperative monitoring. Therefore, the present study should be interpreted as estimating the association between robotic assistance and blood-loss metrics under a no-TXA institutional pathway, rather than as quantifying the incremental value of robotics on top of a full pharmacologic blood-conservation bundle. In centers where intravenous and/or topical TXA is routinely administered, both visible bleeding and postoperative oozing/drain output may be reduced in both surgical approaches; consequently, the absolute levels of TBL, VBL, HBL, transfusion, and postoperative anemia, as well as the between-group differences observed here, may differ and could be smaller.

Interpretation of drain output, VBL, and HBL

Drain management affects interpretation because postoperative drain output is included in VBL and indirectly influences formula-derived HBL. In this cohort, no drain was removed before the POD1 Hct draw; however, VBL used cumulative drain output until drain removal, whereas TBL was calculated using POD1 Hct. Therefore, VBL and HBL should be interpreted as protocol-defined estimates rather than perfectly time-synchronized physiologic compartments.

Clinical implications

The matched analysis retained 204 of 400 eligible patients and therefore estimates the association between surgical approach and blood-loss outcomes in the matched-overlap population rather than in all eligible PLIF patients. Non-retained patients were more likely to have lower BMD, lower baseline Hb/Hct, diabetes, ASA ≥ 3, and ≥ 3-level fusion. Therefore, the results may be less generalizable to patients with less matchable high-risk profiles or more complex multilevel disease. This trade-off improved internal covariate balance but narrowed the target population of inference. The fused-level subgroup analysis should be interpreted cautiously. Although the primary and key secondary analyses showed lower TBL, VBL, and formula-derived HBL in the R-PLIF group, these blood-loss metrics should not be interpreted in isolation as definitive evidence of clinical benefit. Recent PLIF blood-management literature has similarly emphasized that hidden blood loss should be interpreted together with transfusion requirements, postoperative anemia, and recovery-related outcomes rather than as a standalone endpoint [23]. Transfusion, postoperative anemia, and LOS are clinically important downstream outcomes, but they were prespecified as exploratory because they may be influenced by factors beyond surgical bleeding itself, including baseline hemoglobin, symptoms, cardiovascular comorbidity, transfusion thresholds, mobilization, discharge practice, and institutional workflow. In the present matched cohort, these exploratory clinical endpoints were directionally favorable for R-PLIF, including lower postoperative anemia and shorter LOS; however, they should be interpreted as supportive and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Because the interaction analysis showed a larger effect in 2-level and ≥ 3-level fusions, robotic assistance may be associated with larger estimated blood-loss differences in multilevel PLIF. This may be particularly relevant for older patients, those with preoperative anemia, or those with limited cardiopulmonary reserve. In settings where TXA is not routinely used—as was the case in our institution—robot-assisted PLIF may represent a potential technique-based component associated with lower perioperative blood-loss estimates [24, 25]. However, this should not be interpreted as a substitute for contemporary blood-management bundles. Rather, robotic workflow and pharmacologic/ERAS blood-conservation strategies should be regarded as potentially complementary, and the incremental value of robotic assistance in centers already using routine TXA requires prospective evaluation. Nevertheless, the observation that HBL remained a substantial proportion of TBL in both groups underscores that standard postoperative blood-management protocols (drain removal criteria, early Hb monitoring, and, where available, antifibrinolytic regimens) should not be relaxed simply because a robotic system was used. Rather, robotic surgery and perioperative blood-management pathways should be viewed as complementary: robotic assistance may be associated with lower baseline surgical bleeding metrics, whereas optimized drains and pharmacologic measures remain necessary to address the residual hidden component.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, despite 1:1 PSM, exact matching on fused levels, good post-match covariate balance, and sensitivity analyses with additional radiographic and procedural variables, residual selection bias and unmeasured confounding cannot be excluded. The matched cohort also represented a reduced overlap population, which may limit generalizability to higher-risk or more complex patients. Second, radiographic and operative-record variables may not fully capture intraoperative bleeding complexity, such as epidural venous bleeding, cancellous bone removal, adhesions, or surgeon judgment during decompression. Exclusion of intraoperative CSF-leak cases improved comparability of hematocrit-derived estimates but may have removed a small high-complexity subgroup. Third, all surgeries were performed by one senior surgeon using a single robotic platform; therefore, surgeon-specific technique, platform workflow, and proficiency effects may limit external validity. Fourth, no patient received TXA, so the absolute effect sizes may differ in centers using routine TXA or ERAS blood-management pathways. Fifth, TBL and HBL were formula-derived using POD1 Hct rather than directly measured; lack of standardized POD2/nadir Hct and detailed fluid-balance data may bias absolute estimates. Finally, this study assessed short-term perioperative outcomes only and was not designed to define a minimal clinically important difference for TBL or HBL. Thus, whether the approximately 128-mL mean TBL difference translates into durable clinical benefit remains uncertain. Future prospective, multicenter studies with protocolized blood-management strategies and standardized reporting of HBL are warranted to confirm these findings and to determine whether reduced perioperative blood loss leads to improved recovery and longer-term outcomes.

Conclusions

In this propensity score–matched retrospective cohort, robot-assisted PLIF was associated with lower estimated TBL, VBL, and formula-derived HBL than conventional PLIF, with larger observed differences in multilevel fusions. However, causality cannot be established, residual unmeasured confounding may persist, and the clinical importance of the approximately 128-mL mean TBL difference remains uncertain.
