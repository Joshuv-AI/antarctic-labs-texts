A putative auxin efflux carrier FgAec1 contributes to the pathogenicity of Fusarium graminearum
Authors: Su Hu, Haibin Zhao, Qin Wu, Qiantao Jiang, Jian Ma, Pengfei Qi, Guoyue Chen, Youliang Zheng, Yuming Wei, Qiang Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAuxin is crucial for plant growth and influences plant–pathogen interactions. Pathogen-produced indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) is crucial for pathogenicity; however, the role of plant-derived or pathogen-derived IAA in regulating plant defense responses remains poorly understood.ResultsIn this study, two putative auxin efflux carrier (AEC) proteins, FgAec1 and FgAec2, were identified in Fusarium graminearum. To study the effects of the pathogen-derived auxins on Fusarium head blight (FHB), knockout mutants of FgAec1 or FgAec2 showed no effect on vegetative growth or conidia morphology. However, the Δfgaec1 mutant (but not the Δfgaec2 mutant) exhibited reduced growth and development in wheat spikes, indicating that FgAec1 is a key pathogenic factor. Deletion of FgAec1 reduced auxin efflux during the wheat–F. graminearum interaction, whereas exogenous auxin restored the pathogenicity of Δfgaec1 in wheat spikes. Knockout of FgAec1 enhanced the salicylic acid (SA)-mediated defense signaling pathway in response to F. graminearum. Additionally, deletion of a nitrilase FgNIT2 gene, involved in IAA synthesis in F. graminearum, reduced fungal pathogenicity and affected the expression of the SA-responsive genes in wheat treated with F. graminearum. The SA-deficient taics-AB mutant plant inoculated with Δfgaec1 demonstrated that a reduction in the auxin pool may increase the plant’s resistance to the Δfgaec1 strain by disrupting the balance of auxin–SA crosstalk.ConclusionsThese results indicate that FgAec1 may act as a putative AEC, regulating auxin transport in F. graminearum and mediating F. graminearum–wheat interactions. These findings provide new insights into the role of auxin biosynthesis in F. graminearum in suppressing host plant defenses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12915-025-02438-x.Keywords: Fusarium graminearum, Wheat, Auxin efflux carrier, Salicylic acid, Plant defenses

Background

As sessile organisms, plants experience many forms of stress, from extreme climates to microbial pathogens. Phytohormones promote homeostasis under various stresses and are essential for regulating growth and defense responses to facilitate plant survival and persistence [1]. Generally, phytohormones are endogenous low-molecular-weight signaling molecules that act as central regulators. They regulate developmental transitions throughout the plant lifecycle, from cell differentiation to reproductive processes, including flowering, fruit maturation, and programmed organ abscission. Concurrently, these hormonal networks interact with stress-responsive secondary metabolism via spatially and temporally coordinated signaling cascades, enabling plants to adapt to environmental challenges [2, 3]. Salicylic acid (SA), a phenolic plant hormone, regulates photosynthesis, respiration, growth [4], and productivity while also playing a significant role in plant defense against various stresses. Notably, pathogen infection is frequently accompanied by SA accumulation. Several studies have shown that plant hormones exert additive but independent effects, such as antagonistic interactions between auxin and SA [5, 6]. For example, exogenous application of SA or methyl salicylate has been found to positively regulate Fusarium head blight (FHB) resistance in wheat [7]. Unlike other phytohormones, auxin transport is characterized by polarity, which is primarily mediated by PIN family proteins [8]. By influencing both synthesis and metabolic pathways, PIN proteins establish auxin concentration gradients in response to external stimuli [9]. Auxin, primarily in the form of indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), plays a key role in gravitropism and phototropism. It also directly or indirectly regulates plant disease resistance through auxin biosynthesis, transport, and signal transduction pathways [10, 11]. Overexpression of YUCCA1, a key enzyme involved in the plant auxin biosynthesis pathway, affects host resistance to Pseudomonas syringae [12]. Silencing Triticum aestivum transport inhibitor response 1 (TaTIR1), an auxin receptor that interacts with AUX/IAA proteins, repressors of auxin gene expression, enhances plant resistance to the FHB, indicating that auxin signaling pathways negatively regulate wheat resistance to Fusarium graminearum [13, 14].

Recent evidence suggests that plants and pathogens can both synthesize hormones that influence plant–pathogen interactions. For example, high levels of IAA content have been reported in a liquid culture medium containing Magnaporthe oryzae and Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae (Xoo) [15, 16]. Xoo synthesizes auxin via nitrilases to suppress reactive oxygen species production and defense gene expression in rice. Moreover, pathogen-derived IAA promotes cell wall extension and relaxation, thereby facilitating the progression of infection [17]. However, P. syringae produces the phytotoxin coronatine, which has a structure and function similar to those of jasmonic acid (JA) and JA–isoleucine conjugates; it helps the pathogen overcome plant defense responses [18, 19]. For instance, the biotrophic pathogen Ustilago maydis synthesizes IAA and employs fungal effector proteins to disrupt auxin signaling [20, 21]. The effector Nkd1 binds maize TOPLESS/TOPLESS-related co-repressors, elevating IAA levels in infected maize tissue. This binding suppresses jasmonate and auxin signaling while activating SA-mediated defenses. The phytoplasma effector tengu-su inducer (TENGU) can inhibit and inactivate auxin pathways in plants, causing plant diseases [22]. The AvrRpt2 effector of P. syringae promotes auxin signal transduction pathways to facilitate pathogen virulence by degrading plant AUX/IAA transcriptional repressor proteins through the ubiquitin–proteasome system [23, 24]. The auxin efflux carrier (AEC) family comprises proteins localized to the plasma membrane or endoplasmic reticulum [25, 26]. It includes PIN auxin transporters, structurally similar PIN-LIKE (PILS) transporters, and various bacterial and fungal homologs [27, 28]. Overall, these findings indicate that pathogens can regulate plant auxin metabolic pathways by secreting virulence proteins and use pathogen-produced auxin to regulate plant immunity, promoting disease susceptibility.

Similar to many other pathogens, F. graminearum, a major FHB pathogen, possesses tryptophan-dependent tryptamine and indole-3-acetonitrile (IAN) pathways. It was the first pathogen identified to convert the IAN substrate into IAA through the F. graminearum nitrilase pathway (FgNIT2) to complete IAA synthesis [29]. For example, higher auxin levels have been found in susceptible wheat cultivars subjected to the F. graminearum–wheat interaction. However, it remains unclear whether IAA accumulation in wheat spikes during infection originates from the host or from F. graminearum, as the fungus has been shown to produce IAA in vitro [30]. Thus, the role of induced IAA accumulation in mediating wheat resistance to F. graminearum is complex and requires further investigation. In this study, we found that FgAec1 may act as an AEC, regulating wheat susceptibility to F. graminearum. The Δfgaec1 mutant exhibited reduced growth and development of Fusarium in wheat spikes, whereas exogenous auxin restored its pathogenicity. FgAec1 knockout enhanced the induction of the SA-mediated defense signaling pathway in response to F. graminearum. FgAec1-regulated pathogen-derived auxin may suppress SA defenses, thereby increasing FHB in SA-deficient mutant taics-AB plants when inoculated with Δfgaec1. These results indicate that FgAec1 may act as an AEC, regulating auxin transport in F. graminearum and mediating its interaction with wheat.

Results

Identification of AEC proteins in F. graminearum

To evaluate the role of AEC proteins in the pathogenicity of F. graminearum, an InterPro search for proteins containing the membrane transport PIN-like domain (IPR004776) identified two candidates, designated as FgAec1 and FgAec2. Both proteins contained 10 transmembrane helices, similar to PIN2 and PIN3 in Arabidopsis thaliana. Phylogenetic analysis of fungal AEC proteins revealed that FgAec1 and FgAec2 clustered separately, each grouping with Fusarium species, suggesting their conservation within the genus (Fig. 1, Additional file 1: Fig. S1). Although FgAec1 and FgAec2 share similar domains, their distant positions on the phylogenetic tree and genetic differences indicate that they may serve distinct functions.

Knockdown of FgAec1 does not affect vegetative growth and conidia morphology

To investigate f FgAec1 and FgAec2 expression during F. graminearum infection of wheat spikes, we harvested wheat spikes inoculated with F. graminearum at 12, 24, 48, 96, and 120 h post inoculation (hpi). For both genes, we assessed relative expression levels in vegetative hyphae and conidia. As shown in Fig. S2, FgAec1 expression was significantly upregulated at 12 hpi, followed by a progressive decline in expression throughout the subsequent infection stages. However, FgAec2 expression of FgAec2 was substantially upregulated in vegetative hyphae relative to conidia, and its expression peaked at 24 hpi during wheat spike infection (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). Next, to investigate the role of AEC proteins under various stress conditions, we generated deletant mutants for each FgAec gene (Δfgaec1-1, Δfgaec1-7, Δfgaec2-1, and Δfgaec2-3), in which the target genes were replaced with the hygromycin-resistance gene (Additional file 1: Fig. S3). We first assessed the tolerance of the two mutants to cell wall (Congo red), hyperosmotic (NaCl), and oxidative stresses (H2O2). No differences in hyphal growth or colony morphology were observed between PH-1 and the two mutants under NaCl, H2O2, or Congo red stress (Additional file 1: Fig. S4). To determine whether the knockdown of AEC genes affects conidia growth and development, we assessed the conidial morphology and conidiation of PH-1 and the two mutants. No differences in conidial morphology or production were observed among the strains (Additional file 1: Fig. S5). Additionally, spore germination showed no significant changes (Additional file 1: Fig. S5). These results indicate that the knockdown of AEC genes does not significantly influence fungal asexual reproduction.

FgAec1 is essential for full virulence of F. graminearum

To further examine the function of the two AEC genes in F. graminearum virulence, conidial suspensions of the mutants were inoculated onto wheat coleoptiles. Compared with the PH-1 strain, wheat coleoptiles inoculated with the Δfgaec1-1 mutant developed significantly smaller lesions (Fig. 2A and B). The relative fungal biomass was also reduced in wheat coleoptiles inoculated with the Δfgaec1-1 mutant compared with that in the control (Fig. 2C). Next, we conducted the same experiment on wheat leaves and found that the Δfgaec1-1 mutant produced shorter lesions than either the Δfgaec2-1 mutant or the PH-1 strain (Fig. 2D and E). Additionally, wheat leaves inoculated with the Δfgaec1-1 mutant contained less fungal biomass (Fig. 2F). In a repeated experiment on wheat heads, the Δfgaec1-1 mutant showed significantly reduced pathogenicity compared with the PH-1 strain, whereas the Δfgaec2-1 mutant did not (Fig. 2G and H). The disease index of the Δfgaec1-1 mutant was reduced by 70% compared with that of the PH-1 strain and the Δfgaec2-1 mutant. Fungal biomass was measured to evaluate the development of F. graminearum in wheat spikes, and was found to be lower in wheat spikes inoculated with the Δfgaec1-1 mutant (Fig. 2I). These results were further confirmed by inoculation of two independent Δfgaec1-1, Δfgaec1-7, Δfgaec2-1, and Δfgaec2-3 strains onto wheat coleoptiles and spikes (Additional file 1: Fig. S6). We also generated FgAec1 complementation constructs and introduced them into the Δfgaec1 mutant. When inoculated onto wheat heads, the resulting Δfgaec1-1/FgAec1 and Δfgaec1-7/FgAec1 transformants restored normal virulence (Fig. 3A, Additional file 1: Fig. S7). The disease index and fungal biomass of the Δfgaec1/FgAec1 strain were increased by 70%, compared with those of the Δfgaec1 mutant (Fig. 3B and C). In summary, these findings indicate that FgAec1 is essential for the full virulence of F. graminearum.

FgAec1 is primarily localized in the plasma membrane of F. graminearum

Considering the putative transmembrane domain of FgAec1, we speculated that it functions as a membrane-localized protein. To determine its subcellular localization, we generated the FgAec1:GFP construct driven by the gpdA promoter and transformed it into F. graminearum. Confocal microscopy revealed green fluorescence distributed throughout the entire conidial cell (Additional file 1: Fig. S8A). Western blots of proteins isolated from the same conidia showed normal expression levels as measured using an anti-GFP antibody (Additional file 1: Fig. S8B). To further determine its subcellular localization, full-length FgAec1 tagged with GFP was expressed in N. benthamiana leaves using Agrobacterium-mediated transient expression. The GFP signal of FgAec1-GFP was localized to the plasma membrane, as evidenced by colocalization with the RFP signal of a plasma membrane marker (Additional file 1: Fig. S8C).

Knockout of FgAec1 influences auxin efflux

To explore the function of FgAec1 in auxin efflux, we measured auxin concentrations in the liquid medium of the Δfgaec1-1 mutant using a highly sensitive enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The Δfgaec1-1 mutant exhibited lower auxin levels in the liquid than with the Δfgaec2-1 or wild-type (WT) strains (Fig. 4A). To investigate the effect of FgAec1 on auxin efflux during F. graminearum infection, we examined the auxin content in inoculated wheat spikes. The results showed lower auxin levels at 24 and 48 hpi in wheat spikes inoculated with the Δfgaec1-1 mutant than in those inoculated with PH-1 or Δfgaec2-1 (Fig. 4B). Next, we tested whether the virulence defect of FgAec1 is related to IAA deficiency. Based on AtPIN3 [31], we introduced point mutations into the FgAec1 amino acid sequence at residues predicted to bind IAA (called FgAec1m), replacing the conserved auxin transport residues with alanine to generate FgAec1m (Additional file 1: Fig. S1A). This construct was transformed into the Δfgaec1-1 and Δfgaec1-7 mutants to assess FgAec1m complementation (Fig. S8A). When the Δfgaec1-1/FgAec1m or Δfgaec1-7/FgAec1m transformants were inoculated onto wheat heads, virulence was not restored (Additional file 1: Fig. S7B). Furthermore, neither the disease index nor the fungal biomass of these transformants was recovered relative to the WT strain (Additional file 1: Fig. S7C–S7D). These findings indicate that FgAec1 deletion might affect auxin efflux during wheat–F. graminearum interaction.

FgAec1-mediated auxin efflux in F. graminearum facilitates wheat susceptibility to FHB

To determine whether FgAec1-mediated auxin efflux affects plant defense, we first examined the role of exogenous IAA treatment on wheat leaves. Wheat leaves were treated with varying exogenous auxin concentrations and then inoculated with PH-1, Δfgaec1-1, or Δfgaec1-1/FgAec1. When the second leaves of wheat cultivar SM482 were inoculated with PH-1 or Δfgaec1-1 and treated with increasing concentrations of exogenous auxin, the size of the disease lesions gradually increased in a concentration-dependent manner (Additional file 1: Fig. S9A). When 100–150 μM IAA was sprayed onto wheat leaves inoculated with the Δfgaec1-1 mutant, lesion size was comparable to that on leaves inoculated with PH-1 treated with 50 μM IAA, indicating partial recovery of Δfgaec1-1 pathogenicity (Additional file 1: Fig. S9B). Additionally, the same experiment on wheat spikes showed that auxin treatment increased FHB symptom development in spikes inoculated with Δfgaec1-1 compared with those without IAA treatment (Fig. 5A). Similarly, the disease index of the Δfgaec1-1 mutant increased by 70% after auxin treatment compared with that of the untreated control (Fig. 5B). After auxin treatment, more fungal biomass was observed on wheat spikes inoculated with the Δfgaec1-1 mutant than on the untreated control (Fig. 5C). To exclude the effect of exogenous auxin on the growth and development of F. graminearum, we first assessed the hyphal growth rate and colony morphology in a complete medium with varying exogenous auxin concentrations. No differences in hyphal growth rate or colony morphology were observed between PH-1 and the two mutants under exogenous auxin stress (Additional file 1: Fig. S10). In summary, these results indicate that exogenous auxin can partially restore the pathogenicity of the Δfgaec1 mutant without affecting its vegetative growth.

Disruption of the auxin–SA crosstalk by FgAec1 enhances wheat susceptibility to FHB

To further explore whether FgAec1-mediated auxin efflux affects host auxin signaling, we first measured the transcript levels of TaTIR1, a crucial auxin receptor involved in disease resistance. TaTIR1 transcript levels were significantly lower in wheat spikes inoculated with the Δfgaec1-1 mutant than in those infected with the PH-1 strain (Additional file 1: Fig. S11). The role of SA signaling in mediating defense responses that reduce wheat FHB is well established. To investigate the effects of FgAec1 knockout, we analyzed the transcript levels of defense-related genes in SA-dependent pathways in wheat. Compared with the control, FgAec1 knockout led to the upregulation of the SA marker genes NPR1 and PR1 at 12, 24, 48, and 72 hpi (Fig. 6). In addition, the expression of isochorismate synthase (TaICS) was significantly increased at 12, 24, and 72 hpi (Fig. 6). We also generated Δfgnit2-1, Δfgnit2-2 (FGRAMPH1_01T07647) mutants, which are defective in IAA synthesis, to examine the SA-mediated plant immune response (Additional file 1: Fig. S12A-12B). Our results revealed that the Δfgnit2-1, Δfgnit2-2 mutant showed significantly lower pathogenicity than the PH-1 strain (Additional file 1: Fig. S12C). Moreover, the disease index of the Δfgnit2-1, Δfgnit2-2 mutant was 70% lower than that of the PH-1 strain (Additional file 1: Fig. S12D). Compared with the control, spikes inoculated with the Δfgnit2-1 mutant showed significantly higher expression of the SA-responsive genes TaPR1, TaNPR1, and TaICS (Additional file 1: Fig. S12E).

To further investigate the role of FgAec1-mediated auxin efflux in SA signaling, we examined how FgAec1-mediated suppression of SA defenses affects wheat FHB using the SA-deficient taics-AB mutant line from hexaploid Cadenza TILLING mutants. Although FgAec1 knockout significantly reduced FHB symptoms when the Δfgaec1-1 mutant was inoculated into WT plants, severe FHB symptoms were observed in taics-AB mutant plants (Fig. 7A). The taics-AB mutant plants exhibited a significantly higher number of infected spikelets and increased fungal biomass accumulation compared with those of WT plants when these plants were inoculated with the Δfgaec1-1 strain (Fig. 7B and 7C). These results demonstrate that a reduced auxin pool during plant infection may disrupt auxin–SA crosstalk, making plants infected with ΔfgAec1-1 more resistant to the pathogen.

Discussion

Phytohormones are characterized by low production and high efficiency, and their homeostasis is crucial for maintaining normal plant growth and development. Under combined pathogen infection stress, plants undergo various hormonal and metabolic changes. Numerous studies have shown that most phytohormones, including SA and JA, play key roles in plant–microbe interactions [1]. The auxin biosynthesis and metabolism pathways directly regulate almost all growth and development processes [32]. Pathogen-produced IAA is crucial for pathogenicity; however, the role of plant-derived or pathogen-derived IAA in regulating plant defense responses remains poorly understood. In this study, we identified two AECs, FgAec1 and FgAec2, in F. graminearum, which are associated with auxin synthesis. The Δfgaec1 mutant showed a significant reduction in pathogenicity and might be impaired in auxin efflux compared with the PH-1 strain and the Δfgaec2 mutant. Based on these results, we infer that FgAec1 might have a key role in regulating auxin efflux in F. graminearum. Despite homology with plant AEC transporters, FgAec1 did not exhibit functional equivalence, although it may transport other compounds. Auxin biosynthesis and metabolism are crucial for fungal mycelial growth. However, knockout of FgAec1 did not affect fungal growth or development in the culture medium. No significant differences were observed in the mutant sensitivity to auxin stress, despite growth repression in media with specific auxin concentrations. In our study, wheat spikes, leaves, and coleoptiles inoculated with the Δfgaec1 mutant exhibited reduced severity of F. graminearum infection, indicating that FgAec1 plays a crucial role in pathogen infection. A previous study found that the wheat vesicular trafficking system component MIN7, which is involved in auxin transport, was upregulated during infection with FHB [33]. Based on these results, we speculate that the difference in the pathogenicity of F. graminearum is caused by a reduction in plant defense rather than defects in pathogen growth or development.

Auxin has long been recognized for its role in plant responses to various stresses, in addition to its involvement in plant growth and development [17]. Several hemibiotrophic, biotrophic, and necrotrophic pathogenic bacteria and fungi, such as U. maydis, Fusarium oxysporum, and M. oryzae, can biosynthesize IAA, which functions similarly 0to endogenous auxin in plants [34, 35]. Although exogenous auxin treatment has been reported to reduce disease severity and yield loss in barley heads infected with Fusarium culmorum [30], our results do not support the hypothesis that auxin treatment reduces FHB severity. This discrepancy between our findings and those of other studies may be explained by different experimental conditions, including different reagent concentrations or fungal strains. Free IAA levels increase in plants infected with fungal pathogens, such as Puccinia graminis and Ustilago maydis and bacterial pathogens such as P. syringae and Ralstonia solanacearum [36, 37]. Furthermore, elevated IAA levels in A. thaliana reduce host resistance to pathogens such as P. syringae and F. oxysporum [17, 38]. Similarly, different plants may show varying responses to IAA concentration depending on whether they are infected or not. In addition, pathogen infection regulates various hormone signaling pathways that involve numerous regulatory interactions [39, 40]. Thus, treatment with a specific auxin concentration enhanced FHB and restored Δfgaec1 pathogenicity consistent with a previous report showing that auxin increases wheat susceptibility to FHB. The auxin receptor TaTIR1 functions in plant stress responses, and its knockdown has been shown to reduce FHB severity [14, 41], suggesting a link between auxin accumulation and increased FHB severity. Consistent with these findings, our results showed that TaTIR1 expression was significantly downregulated in wheat spikes inoculated with Δfgaec1 compared with the control. This further supports the involvement of FgAec1 in the host–pathogen interaction system.

Phytohormones, as key molecules involved in regulatory processes, are not entirely independent but are part of a complex regulatory network with intricate crosstalk [42, 43]. In most cases, SA antagonizes the auxin signaling pathway and its accumulation inhibits pathogen infection [44]. The expression of the basal defense-elicited miRNA miR393 suppresses auxin signaling in Arabidopsis, leading to an increased accumulation of indole glucosinolates, which have antimicrobial properties. In auxin signaling pathways, GH3.5, an auxin-responsive gene encoding an auxin-conjugating enzyme, modulates auxin homeostasis and indirectly affects SA activity through catalysis [45]. Additionally, intricate interactions exist in hormone biosynthesis. The accumulation of tryptophan, a key precursor in auxin biosynthesis, activates chorismate mutase, an intermediate in SA production, ultimately leading to increased SA concentrations [46, 47]. The levels of SA, an essential component, increase rapidly in response to both abiotic and biotic stresses. In this study, wheat spikes inoculated with Δfgaec1, which lacks a putative auxin efflux function, showed significantly higher expression of the SA defense-related genes NPR1 and PR1 than those inoculated with PH-1. NPR1, a well-known SA receptor, links SA signaling transduction with PR1 gene expression, both of which are involved in systemic acquired resistance and enhance pathogen resistance [48, 49]. The pathogenicity of the Δfgaec1 mutant returned to normal levels when inoculated into the taics-AB mutant line, which is deficient in SA biosynthesis. Based on our findings, we speculate that FgAec1 knockout disrupts auxin–SA crosstalk, resulting in plant resistance to Δfgaec1. In conclusion, we identified a putative AEC, FgAec1, which may promote the release of pathogen-derived IAA into wheat, disrupting auxin–SA crosstalk and ultimately affecting wheat resistance to FHB.

Conclusions

In this study, we investigated the roles of FgAec1 by constructing a deletion mutant and its functional corresponding mutant. Knockout of FgAec1, a putative AEC, reduced the pathogenicity of F. graminearum while promoting the expression of SA defense-related genes. Additionally, deletion of a nitrilase FgNIT2 gene, involved in IAA synthesis in F. graminearum, reduced fungal pathogenicity and activated the expression of the SA-responsive genes in wheat treated with F. graminearum. Overall, our results reveal that FgAec1 may regulate auxin efflux in F. graminearum and mediate its interaction with wheat, offering a biologically relevant perspective on auxin–SA dynamics in a host–pathogen system.

Methods

Biological materials and culture conditions

In this study, F. graminearum PH-1 was used as the WT strain and the parental strain for transformation experiments. PH-1, Δfgaec1, and Δfgaec2 strains were routinely cultured on potato dextrose agar (PDA) plates at 25 °C. Agrobacterium tumefaciens AGL1 and GV3101 were cultured in Luria–Bertani (LB) medium containing kanamycin (50 µg/mL) and rifampin (100 µg/mL) at 28 °C. Escherichia coli DH5α was cultured in LB medium containing kanamycin (50 µg/mL) at 37 °C. To produce macroconidia, F. graminearum was cultured in carboxymethylcellulose (CMC) medium at 25 °C, and fungal mycelia were grown in liquid yeast extract peptone dextrose (YEPD) medium at 25 °C. Wheat cultivars—i.e., SM482, Cadenza, and the taics-AB mutant from hexaploid Cadenza TILLING mutants, were grown at 20–25 °C for the experiments.

Sequence analysis

The open reading frames of FgAce1 and FgAce1 were amplified from cDNA of wheat heads infected with PH-1. The cDNA was isolated using an RNA purification kit. After sequencing, transmembrane structures were predicted using TMHMM (http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/TMHMM/). Sequence alignments were performed using DNAMAN version 6. Protein sequences homologous to auxin efflux carriers were obtained from BLASTX, and their phylogenetic relationships were analyzed using the neighbor-joining (NJ) method in MEGA version 5. The resulting NJ tree was generated using a Poisson substitution model with uniform evolutionary rates across sites. For this tree, Arabidopsis thaliana auxin transporters were designated as the outgroups to root the topology. Branch support was evaluated through 1000 bootstrap replicates, with values ≥ 70% considered indicative of robust clade stability.

Stress treatments: abiotic and hormone stress

Next, we assessed the effects of abiotic and hormonal stresses on mycelial radial growth using PDA plates supplemented either with 9 mM H2O2, 1 M NaCl, or 0.5 mM Congo red for abiotic stress and 50, 100, or 150 μM IAA for hormonal stress [50]. Each plate was inoculated with a 5-mm mycelial agar block at the center and incubated at 28 °C for 96 h in the dark. Each treatment was performed with five biological replicates.

Transformation of F. graminearum and pathogenicity assays

Gene replacement constructs were generated using the split-marker approach. Homologous recombinant fragments containing the 5ʹ and 3ʹ flanking sequences of FgAec1, FgAec2, and FgNit2 were inserted into a Prf-HU2 vector and fused with the hygromycin phosphotransferase gene. For each target gene, two independent deletant strains were obtained from separate transformation events to ensure reproducibility. These strains are designated as Δfgaec1-1 and Δfgaec1-7 (for FgAec1), Δfgaec2-1 and Δfgaec2-3 (for FgAec2), and Δfgnit2-1 and Δfgnit2-2 (for FgNit2) [50, 51]. Next, A. tumefaciens carrying constructs Prf-HU2:FgAec1 or Prf-HU2:FgAec2 was co-cultured with PH-1. Subsequently, positive strains were identified using specific primers (Additional file 2: Table S1). To generate the complementation strain, the entire FgAEC1 gene, including its native promoter, was cloned into the pFL2 vector and transformed into A. tumefaciens. This was then co-cultured with the Δfgaec1-1 and Δfgaec1-7 mutants. The resulting transformants were screened using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with specific primers. For pathogenicity assays, conidia from F. graminearum mutants and PH-1 were collected from 5-day-old CMC cultures and resuspended at a density of 105 spores/mL in distilled water. The flowering wheat heads of cultivar SM482 were inoculated with 10 μL of conidial suspension at the fifth spikelet, and the FHB phenotype was assessed 14 days post-inoculation (dpi) by calculating the disease index (ratio of infected to total spikelets). For the wheat leaves inoculated with F. graminearum, third leaves of similar size were cut into 3-cm segments. A wound was made at the center of each segment, and 2 μL of conidial suspension was applied to the wound site [14]. For wheat coleoptiles inoculated with F. graminearum, wheat cultivar SM482 seedlings were grown at 22 °C under a 16-h light/8-h dark cycle. Approximately 10 μL of the conidial suspension was inoculated onto wheat coleoptiles [52]. The size of the disease lesion was photographed and analyzed at 7 dpi with F. graminearum.

IAA quantification and exogenous IAA treatment

For exogenous IAA treatment, we first dissolved IAA powder (Cat: SI8340, Solarbio) in ethanol and added deionized distilled water (ddH2O) to obtain various IAA solution concentrations. The second leaves of wheat cultivar SM482 and wheat spikelets were sprayed with 50, 100, or 150 μM IAA 12 h before conidial suspension inoculation [14]. The control groups were treated with distilled water. Measurements were taken from 4–6 leaves and wheat heads per replicate, with three biological replicates for each treatment. For IAA measurement, we inoculated 10 μL of a 105 spores/mL suspension into 50 mL of first-stage medium (15.0 g, carboxymethylcellulose sodium; 1.0 g, NH4NO3; 1.0 g, yeast extract; 0.5 g, MgSO₄·7H₂O; 1.0 g KH₂PO₄; and 1000 mL ddH2O) and incubated in 250 mL flasks at 28 °C and 200 rpm in the dark for 72 h to grow mycelia. Mycelia were filtered using sterile gauze, washed with distilled water, and harvested via vacuum filtration. Subsequently, 0.1 g of mycelia was transferred to 50 mL of the second-stage medium (4.0 g, potato infusion powder; 20.0 g dextrose; and 1000 mL ddH2O). Samples of the medium were collected after 12, 24, 48, and 72 h for ELISA. Infected wheat heads were collected at the same indicated time points to quantify the free total IAA content using an ELISA kit (Cat: ml077231, Mlbio). All procedures followed the manufacturer’s protocol, with one modification: the incubation time was extended to 60 min. The absorbance of IAA standard samples was measured at 405 nm to obtain a standard curve, which was then used to determine the IAA concentrations of all samples.

Nucleic acid extraction and qPCR assay

We extracted DNA from tissue samples using a CTAB-based DNA extraction protocol [53]. Briefly, ground samples were mixed with extraction buffer (2% CTAB, 100 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 20 mM EDTA, pH 8.0, 1.4 M NaCl; 1% β-mercaptoethanol) and incubated at 65 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, samples were mixed with chloroform–isoamyl alcohol (24:1, v/v), vortexing vigorously, and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min. Next, the aqueous phase was collected, mixed with an equal volume of isopropanol, and precipitated at − 20 °C for 2 h. The DNA pellets were washed twice with 75% ethanol, air-dried at room temperature, and resuspended in 50 μL nuclease-free water. Total genomic DNA was extracted from wheat heads infected with F. graminearum at 5 dpi and analyzed via qPCR using primers specific for wheat TaEF and TaGAPDH as well as F. graminearum FgActin and FgGAPDH. Biomass was determined by absolute quantification using double-standard curves. Wheat and F. graminearum DNA were diluted in a gradient series (10 × , 20 × , 50 × , 100 × , 200 × , 500 × , and 1000 ×) to generate double-standard curves [54]. Finally, the results of three independent biological replicates were used to estimate the ratio of wheat and F. graminearum biomass.

For RNA extraction, we first collected wheat spikelet samples at 0, 12, 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 hpi with F. graminearum. Next, these samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C. Total RNA was then extracted using the MiniBEST RNA Universal Extraction Kit (Takara Bio Inc.) and cDNA was synthesized using the Evo M-MLV RT Premix (Takara Bio Inc.). TaEF, TaGAPDH, FgActin, and FgGAPDH were used as internal controls for wheat and F. graminearum, respectively. qRT-PCR was conducted on a CFX Connect Real-time instrument (Bio-Rad, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Three biological replicates were prepared for each sample. All qPCR amplifications were performed in 20 μL reaction mixtures containing 10 μL of ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q711-02), 10 pmol primers, and 2 μL of diluted cDNA (1:10). Amplifications were performed under the following conditions: preheating at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles at 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s to determine the cycle threshold. Melt curves were obtained using the following melt protocol: 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 1 min, and 95 °C for 15 s. Data were analyzed using CFX Manager (Bio-Rad, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Conidiation assays

For conidiation assays, PH-1, Δfgaec1, Δfgaec2, and Δfgaec1/FgAec1 strains were routinely cultured on PDA plates for 3–4 days. Three plugs from the edge of each strain were transferred into 100 mL of CMC liquid medium at 25 °C for 5 days. Conidia were collected by filtering through three layers of gauze and washed with distilled water. The number of conidia was determined using a microscopic hemocytometer. Conidial morphology was observed using an Olympus microscope, and 100 conidia were measured per test strain. Measurements were taken from three biological replicates, each consisting of 100 conidia. For germination assays, fresh fungal conidia were harvested from CMC liquid medium and grown in YEPD liquid medium at 25 °C. Germination was observed under a microscope at 6, 9, and 12 h. Each time point was assessed in triplicate, with three 10 × 10 fields examined per replicate.

Subcellular localization of FgAec1 in F. graminearum and N. benthamiana

The FgAec1 gene was amplified from F. graminearum cDNA and independently cloned into two vectors: the Prf-HU2-eGFP vector for fungal expression and the pCAMBIA1302 vector, which carries a GFP tag, for plant expression. The resulting Prf-HU2-eGFP-FgAec1 construct was transformed into A. tumefaciens AGL-1 for fungal transformation, while the pCAMBIA1302-FgAec1 construct was introduced into A. tumefaciens GV3101 for Nicotiana benthamiana transformation. Fluorescence in conidia and N. benthamiana leaf cells was observed by confocal microscopy (Leica STELLARIS STED/EM CPD300; ocular: 10 × ; objective: 10 ×) using laser excitation at 488 nm with emission at 512–527 nm.

Statistical analyses

All data analyses were performed using GraphPad 8.0 (GraphPad, MA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. The experiments included two technical replicates. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0. Differences between experimental and control group means were assessed using the two-tailed Student’s t-tests or one-way ANOVA.
