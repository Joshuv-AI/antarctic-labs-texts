Internal neurolysis for trigeminal neuralgia without apparent vascular compression: Clinical outcomes and intraoperative electrophysiological conduction changes
Authors: Keisuke Onoda, Jumpei Kato, Tomoyuki Naito, Tomihiro Wakamiya, Masahiro Indou, Tatsuya Tanaka, Takashi Agari, Takashi Sugawara, Kazuaki Shimoji, Eiichi Suehiro, Kimihiro Nakahara, Hiroshi Itokawa, Akira Matsuno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Microvascular decompression (MVD) is the standard surgical treatment for classical trigeminal neuralgia (TN) with evident neurovascular compression. Internal neurolysis (IN) offers an alternative microsurgical technique. However, the physiological basis of symptom relief remains unclear. This study analyzed surgical outcomes and intraoperative conduction changes in patients without apparent vascular compression.Methods: Among 158 patients who underwent MVD for TN between February 2022 and March 2025, 14 had no definite vascular compression on preoperative 3D Magnetic resonance cisternography/angiogram-fusion imaging or intraoperative inspection and underwent IN. Conduction changes were classified into four patterns: (1) shortened latency with increased amplitude, (2) unchanged latency with increased amplitude, (3) unchanged latency and amplitude, and (4) recovery from unmeasurable to measurable conduction.Results: The mean age was 64.9 years with an equal sex distribution. The right and left trigeminal nerves were affected in 10 and 4 patients, respectively. Nine patients had typical TN, and five had atypical features. Five patients had recurrent pain after previous MVD. Excellent outcomes (Barrow Neurological Institute [BNI] I–II) were achieved in 11 of 14 patients (78.6%), while 3 patients (21.4%) had partial pain relief (BNI III). Typical TN patients more frequently demonstrated conduction patterns 1 or 4, whereas atypical TN patients more often exhibited patterns 2 or 3.Conclusion: IN was associated with favorable clinical outcomes and intraoperative electrophysiological improvement in patients with TN without apparent vascular compression. These findings provide physiological observations accompanying clinical improvement following IN.

INTRODUCTION

Trigeminal neuralgia (TN) is characterized by brief, paroxysmal, electric shock–like facial pain in the distribution of the trigeminal nerve.[1] In classical TN, neurovascular compression (NVC) at the root entry zone (REZ) is commonly identified and treated with microvascular decompression (MVD), which remains the gold-standard surgical treatment.[2]

However, a subset of patients with medically refractory TN shows no definite vascular compression on high-resolution magnetic resonance (MR) imaging or during intraoperative exploration. These cases are often referred to as non-NVC TN.[3,4] The optimal surgical strategy for these patients remains uncertain.

Internal neurolysis (IN), also known as “nerve combing,” is a microsurgical technique involving longitudinal separation of trigeminal nerve fascicles under high magnification.[1,3] Previous reports have described favorable outcomes of IN in patients without identifiable vascular compression.[1,3,5,6] However, the physiological mechanisms underlying symptom relief remain unclear, and intraoperative electrophysiological changes in trigeminal nerve conduction have not been well characterized.[7,8]

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate clinical outcomes and intraoperative electrophysiological changes following IN in patients with non-NVC TN.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient population

Between February 2022 and March 2025, 158 consecutive patients underwent retrosigmoid MVD for TN at our institution. Among them, 14 patients showed no apparent vascular compression on preoperative 3D MR cisternography/angiography fusion imaging or during intraoperative inspection and were included in this study.

Baseline demographic and clinical data were collected retrospectively. All patients had a minimum follow-up of 6 months. Pain severity was evaluated using the Barrow Neurological Institute (BNI) pain intensity score before and after surgery.

Typical TN was defined as paroxysmal electric shock–like pain without a continuous component, whereas atypical TN included a continuous or burning pain component.[2-5]

Surgical procedure

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia with continuous auditory brainstem response monitoring. After retrosigmoid craniotomy, the trigeminal REZ was exposed through careful arachnoid dissection. Perineural adhesions were gently released. When no offending vessel was identified, IN was performed.

IN consisted of three to four longitudinal incisions along the axis of the trigeminal nerve under high magnification. Each incision involved <50% of the nerve thickness, and fascicular transection was avoided. Hemostasis was achieved and the dura was closed in a standard fashion.[1-3]

Intraoperative electrophysiological monitoring

Intraoperative monitoring of the trigeminal nerve was performed by recording compound nerve action potentials (CNAPs). Electrical stimulation (0.2–0.3 mA) was applied to the trigeminal REZ using a monopolar stimulation holder with a 1.0-mm disposable probe (Nihon Kohden, Japan). CNAPs were recorded from the supraorbital region corresponding to the frontal notch, representing the peripheral portion of the ophthalmic (V1) branch.

As a control, stimulation near Meckel’s cave was also performed to confirm conduction in an area unaffected by IN. Electrophysiological data were recorded using the Neuromaster G1 system (Nihon Kohden, Japan). Baseline latency and peak-to-peak amplitude were measured before IN and repeated immediately after the procedure.

RESULTS

IN was performed in 14 patients with TN without identifiable NVC.

Intraoperative trigeminal nerve conduction monitoring revealed four electrophysiological response patterns following superficial epineurial incision [Table 1]. Most patients demonstrated improvement in at least one electrophysiological parameter, including latency shortening, amplitude increase, or recovery of previously absent conduction.

DISCUSSION

The present study demonstrated that superficial epineurial incision during IN was associated with measurable intraoperative electrophysiological changes in most patients with TN without identifiable NVC.

These findings are consistent with the hypothesis that increased intraneural pressure at the epineurial level may contribute to conduction impairment in a subset of patients with non-NVC TN. Previous studies have reported favorable outcomes of IN in such patients, although the physiological basis has remained unclear. The intraoperative conduction changes observed in the present series suggest that partial mechanical release at the epineurial level may improve trigeminal nerve conduction.

In particular, improvement in amplitude without latency shortening may reflect recruitment of previously dysfunctional but viable axons. Conversion from unmeasurable to measurable conduction may indicate release of severe conduction block.

The concept of epineurial compartment syndrome has been discussed in peripheral nerve compression disorders. It is biologically plausible that similar intraneural pressure elevation could occur in TN even in the absence of macroscopic vascular compression.[9]

However, direct measurement of intraneural pressure was not performed. Further investigations incorporating physiological monitoring or advanced imaging may help clarify the underlying mechanisms.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study was retrospective and conducted at a single institution with a small sample size. Second, intraoperative electrophysiological assessment was not blinded. Third, long-term follow-up remains limited.

Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as preliminary physiological observations rather than definitive mechanistic evidence. Further prospective studies are required to confirm these findings and to clarify the role of IN in patients without NVC.

CONCLUSION

Superficial epineurial incision during IN was associated with intraoperative improvement in trigeminal nerve conduction parameters in most patients without identifiable NVC.

These observations support the hypothesis that intraneural pressure at the epineurial level may contribute to TN in selected cases without vascular compression. IN with superficial epineurial release may represent a potential surgical option in carefully selected patients, although further validation is required.
