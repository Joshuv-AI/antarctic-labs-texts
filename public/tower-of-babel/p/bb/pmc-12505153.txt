Ventilator-induced diaphragmatic dysfunction: pathophysiology, monitoring and advances in potential treatment and prevention
Authors: Wei Fu, Lili Guan, Qi Liu, Zhefan Xie, Junting You, Rongchang Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Mechanical ventilation is a life-saving method for those critically ill patients with acute or chronic respiratory failure without assistance. However, even short-term mechanical ventilation can lead to alterations in the fibrous structure and reduced contraction force of the diaphragm, which is defined as ventilator-induced diaphragmatic dysfunction (VIDD). This condition is associated with various risks of adverse clinical outcomes. Research on mechanical ventilation-related respiratory mechanics helps us to understand the macroscopic myotrauma mechanisms of VIDD. Ongoing clinical trials into comprehensive management strategies for lung- and diaphragm-protective ventilation are continually refining respiratory therapy protocols in clinical practice. Although the cellular and molecular mechanisms are not fully defined, pathways involving mitochondrial oxidative stress have been identified as key contributors to disease progression, leading to both accelerated proteolysis and depressed protein synthesis. Additionally, research on the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway, lysosomal autophagy, calpain, caspase-3 and dysfunction of the ryanodine receptor-1 pathway is enhancing our understanding of the downstream mechanisms involved. Promising interventions based on these findings have yielded hopeful results in animal models for preventing VIDD. This review summarises the epidemiology and pathophysiological mechanisms of VIDD and advances in potential treatment and prevention.

Introduction

Mechanical ventilation (MV) is a vital life support method for critically ill patients with primary or secondary conditions linked to neuromuscular diseases, respiratory failure, severe infections, cardiac or respiratory arrest, major surgeries or serious trauma. However, even short-term MV (animal studies >12 h, human biopsies >18 h) can lead to alterations in the fibrous structure and reduced contraction force of the diaphragm, defined as ventilator-induced diaphragmatic dysfunction (VIDD) [1]. Research indicates that VIDD is a critical risk factor for numerous adverse clinical outcomes, such as challenges in weaning from ventilation, prolonged ventilation duration, extended intensive care unit (ICU) stays and complications related to acute respiratory failure [2]. WEAN-SAFE, a multicentre observational study, showed that 35% of patients on MV faced weaning failure within 90 days [3]. Another multicentre observational study, the WIND study, showed that 10% of ventilated patients faced difficult weaning, and 9% had prolonged weaning with an increased death rate [4]. Diaphragmatic weakness is present in up to 80% of patients with difficult weaning [5]. Despite numerous studies investigating the pathogenesis of VIDD, clear conclusions remain elusive. This review summarises the epidemiology, pathophysiological characteristics and major mechanisms of VIDD, as well as advances in potential treatment and prevention. Definitions of terminology

Prevalence

Demoule et al. [6] found that diaphragmatic weakness was present within 24 h of intubation at the time of admission to the ICU. Marchioni et al. [7] found that diaphragmatic dysfunction occurred in approximately 28% of patients on noninvasive ventilation (NIV), and that diaphragmatic dysfunction was associated with NIV failure. A study by Duiverman et al. [8] monitored rib diaphragm activity in hypercapnic patients using surface electromyography (EMG) in awake patients with COPD and found that diaphragm electromyography (EMGdi) activity was significantly reduced by NIV compared to unassisted breathing, especially when high inspiratory pressures were used.

In a study by Dres et al. [5], diaphragmatic dysfunction was found to be twice as likely (63%) as peripheral muscle weakness (34%) in spontaneous breathing trials (SBTs) in patients on MV for >24 h, and the two coexisted in 21% of patients. Compared to ICU-acquired weakness (ICU-AW), which mainly affects limb muscles during ICU admissions, VIDD mainly affects the diaphragm after MV. Although both ICU-AW and VIDD can manifest as respiratory muscle dysfunction, we can distinguish between the two based on their distinct characteristics in clinical settings (table 2). Interestingly, while the diaphragm and limb muscles are both skeletal muscles, they appear to differ significantly in their susceptibility to MV and bed rest, with the diaphragm being particularly sensitive. This also implies that there may be different pathophysiological mechanisms beyond pure inactivity between ICU-AW and VIDD.

The pathophysiological characteristics of VIDD

Decrease in diaphragm contractile force

Decreased diaphragmatic contractile force is the earliest and most prominent feature of VIDD. In isolated tissues and live animals, measuring the contractile force of the diaphragm after electrical stimulation is a commonly used method to quantify diaphragmatic muscle strength. For isolated muscle fibres, normalising the force generated by the diaphragm to the muscle cross-sectional area (CSA) is a standard index used to assess the magnitude of muscle strength. It refers to the maximum isometric contraction force generated by isolated diaphragm bundles under a super-maximal stimulation sequence (160 Hz) sustained for 500 ms, divided by the CSA of the muscle bundle. Research by Powers et al. [9] has shown that MV can lead to a decrease in the unit area contractile force of isolated diaphragm muscle bundles in rats at stimulation frequencies of 15–160 Hz. Moreover, this decrease in contractile force is time-dependent, with a reduction of 18% and 48% after 12 and 24 h of ventilation, respectively. In the study by Radell et al. [10], intravenous phrenic nerve stimulation was applied to mechanically ventilated pigs for five consecutive days at fixed frequencies of 20, 30, 40, 50 and 100 Hz. Compared to the values measured on the first day, the diaphragmatic pressure on the fifth day decreased by approximately 20% at all tested frequencies, accompanied by a 30% reduction in the amplitude of the evoked compound muscle action potential.

In clinical trials, diaphragm contractility can be assessed indirectly through EMGdi and diaphragm ultrasound, in addition to phrenic nerve stimulation (PNS). In the experiment by Jaber et al. [11], the airway occlusion pressure generated by diaphragmatic contraction during PNS measured in the patient group undergoing MV gradually decreased during ventilation, with a mean decrease of 32±6% after 6 days. In observations by Sklar et al. [12] and Coiffard et al. [13], diaphragm contractility was very low or absent (EMGdi <5 μv) in a majority of patients (51% of study time) over the first 48–72 h of MV [12, 13]. In the study of Hoshino et al. [14], there was a progressive reduction in end-inspiratory thickness of the diaphragm (−30%) and diaphragm thickening fraction (−25%) from baseline to the first 24 h measured by diaphragm ultrasound. A study by Kim et al. [15] confirmed that approximately 29% of patients on MV manifest clinically significant diaphragm dysfunction as evaluated by bedside diaphragmatic ultrasound, defined as movement of the diaphragm in the direction of the longitudinal axis of the body <10 mm or paradoxical movement during inspiration.

Additionally, studies have indicated that the decrease in diaphragm contractile force is associated with reduced concentrations of myofibrillar proteins, structural abnormalities in the diaphragm, impaired excitation–contraction coupling and super-relaxed (SRX) state myosin [16–18].

Diaphragm muscle fibre atrophy

Shanely et al. [19] found that 18 h of MV led to a significant decrease in the weight of the diaphragm and the diaphragm weight/body weight ratio in ventilated rats, while there was no significant change in peripheral muscles when compared to the soleus muscle. A pathological analysis of ventilated mouse using immunofluorescence staining under the microscope showed a significant reduction in the CSA of type I and II skeletal muscle fibres, with type II muscle fibre alterations being more noticeable (about −24% to −30%). Multiple animal studies have shown that just 12 h of controlled mechanical ventilation (CMV) results in a 10–30% reduction in the CSA of all septal fibre types (i.e. type I, type IIa and type IIx/b) in rats [20, 21]. Similar to the results of preclinical studies, in human diaphragm specimen biopsies compared to the control group (lung cancer surgical patients), the CSA of slow muscle fibres and fast muscle fibres in MV (CMV >18 h) decreased by 57% and 53%, respectively [1].

Some evidence of diaphragm atrophy comes from continuous monitoring of noninvasive ultrasound in critically ill patients. In an observational study by Grosu et al. [22], a series of ultrasound measurements was used to assess the thickness of the diaphragm muscle. The decrease in diaphragm thickness occurred within 48 h after the initiation of MV, and the degree of atrophy showed a linear relationship with the duration of MV, with an average atrophy rate increasing by 6% per day. It is worth noting that both pressure support ventilation and CMV can cause diaphragm fibre atrophy, although the atrophy occurs at a relatively slower rate with pressure support ventilation [23].

Ultrastructure changes of the diaphragm muscle fibres

Changes in the ultrastructure of diaphragm muscle fibres related to MV include decreased titin concentration, disordered sarcomeric fibre arrangement, increased lipid vacuoles in the myoplasm and altered mitochondrial morphology. Electron microscopy images of diaphragm fibres from patients exposed to MV for over 72 h reveal myofibril damage consistent with ultrastructural injury, with the extent of myofibril disarray correlating to the duration of MV exposure [11].

Titin is a giant filamentous cytoskeletal protein that spans half of a sarcomere in muscle fibres, serving several key functions: 1) stabilising the sarcomere structure; 2) promoting passive force generation within muscle fibres; 3) acting as a mechanical sensor; and 4) functioning as a tunable elastic element. MV reduces phosphorylation levels of filamin in rodent diaphragm and decreases filamin abundance in human diaphragm; this reduction can lead to decreased force generation in diaphragm fibres [24, 25]. Importantly, the study by van den Berg et al. [26] investigated the impact of titin stiffness on diaphragm weakness induced by MV. Using genetically engineered mouse models, including Rbm20ΔRRM mice expressing a lower stiffness titin isoform and TtnΔIAjxn and TtnΔ112–158 mice with higher stiffness titin isoforms, the research revealed that Rbm20ΔRRM mice maintained diaphragm contractility during MV, while wild-type mice and those with stiffer titin isoforms demonstrated significant diaphragm weakness. This finding highlights the critical role of titin's elastic properties in the diaphragm's adaptation to MV, suggesting that the diaphragm's response to MV can be modified by adjusting the elasticity of muscle proteins. There is a need for further research into the role of titin in this process.

Another significant feature of the microstructural changes in diaphragm is that the myonuclear domain, i.e. the ratio of muscle fibre cytoplasmic volume to the number of myonuclei, remains unchanged during MV exposure. The multinucleated muscle nuclei are located at the periphery of skeletal muscle fibres to ensure even distribution of gene products. A study on rats demonstrated that 12 h of MV exposure leads to a loss of myonuclei in diaphragm fibres, while the CSA of both type I and type II muscle fibres significantly decreases. Notably, the parallel changes in CSA and myonuclei number do not alter the myonuclear domain of diaphragm muscle [27].

Additionally, changes in diaphragm microstructure can be manifested by increased lipid accumulation, changes in mitochondrial dynamics, production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), activation of the autophagic system and aggregation of large numbers of lysosomes and autophagic vesicles, which will be described in more detail in the section on cellular molecular mechanisms below.

Pathophysiological mechanisms of diaphragmatic myotrauma

MV is a key factor in the development of VIDD. For the diaphragm, the ventilator is a double-edged sword. Inactivity in completely passive ventilation can lead to diaphragmatic atrophy due to excessive decompensation. Conversely, inadequate and inappropriate ventilator assistance can lead to diaphragmatic injury.

Existing research shows that the main macro-mechanisms of VIDD include over-assistance myotrauma, under-assistance myotrauma, eccentric myotrauma and expiratory myotrauma. Excessive ventilator pressure support suppresses respiratory drive and inspiratory effort, leading to over-assistance injury and exacerbating diaphragmatic disuse atrophy [1]. When MV support fails to meet physiological demands, the respiratory drive increases and muscles contract under excessive load, leading to acute muscle inflammation and injury and weakness, resulting in insufficient assistance and concentric muscle damage [28, 29]. Dyspnoea progresses further, with significant recruitment of accessory muscles, paradoxical breathing and patient–ventilator asynchrony. During expiration, the diaphragm undergoes intense contraction, triggering eccentric injury [30–32]. Finally, in patients on MV with high positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) levels, increased end-expiratory lung volume causes caudal displacement of the diaphragm, leading to shortening of diaphragmatic fibre length. Prolonged shortening induces longitudinal atrophy, i.e. a reduction in the number of sarcomeres in series. As a result, during weaning, the diaphragm fibres are overstretched to the upper limit of sarcomere length, impairing their ability to generate effective tension and causing expiratory myotrauma [33].

Although NIV usually allows the patient to breathe spontaneously and the diaphragm to maintain a degree of mobility that theoretically makes VIDD less likely to occur, it should be noted that VIDD can still occur if the parameters are not set appropriately, resulting in complete unloading or overloading of the respiratory muscles or patient–ventilator asynchrony. NIV-induced diaphragm dysfunction deserves attention as part of the broader spectrum of VIDD.

The cellular and molecular mechanisms of VIDD

VIDD is a progressive, time-dependent pathological process centred on mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress injury, primarily characterised by an imbalance in protein synthesis and catabolism, as well as diaphragm SRX state myosin. In the following sections, we will introduce the aetiological mechanisms of VIDD from these four perspectives.

Mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress injury

MV and diaphragm mitochondrial dysfunction

Mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress injury are central to the development and progression of VIDD. MV is linked to several changes in the oxidative respiratory function of diaphragm mitochondria. These include 1) defects in the mitochondrial respiratory chain, characterised by intrinsic enzyme dysfunction of cytochrome c oxidase [34] and inhibited activity of electron transport chain (ETC) complexes II, III and IV [35]; 2) widespread downregulation of genes related to mitochondrial biogenesis (e.g. mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFam), transcription factor B1 (TFB1) and transcription factor B2 (TFB2)); 3) mitochondrial genomic damage; and 4) signs of metabolic oversupply, including increased lipid accumulation in muscle fibres [34].

MV and ROS increase

As the primary executor of oxidative stress injury, ventilator-induced increases in ROS release are upstream signals necessary for diaphragmatic atrophy and dysfunction during MV. Research shows that MV increases ROS production from at least three sources: NADPH, xanthine oxidase and mitochondria [36–38]. Notably, the increase in ROS from mitochondria is a key factor in diaphragm injury (figure 1) [39]. Electron leakage of ETC complex I/III

Under hypoxic conditions, metabolic stress or excess reducing substrate load (e.g. elevated NADH/NAD+ ratio), the ETC experiences electron flow obstruction. In such states, molecular oxygen (O2) undergoes single-electron reduction at the flavin mononucleotide site of complex I or during the Q cycle of complex III (ubiquinol-cytochrome c reductase), generating superoxide anions (O2−). These ROS are subsequently released into the cytosol via mitochondrial voltage-dependent anion channels or permeabilisation of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore. This aberrant ROS efflux significantly exacerbates intracellular oxidative stress, triggering downstream pathological cascades [40, 41].

Lipid accumulation/metabolic overload

Studies have shown that CMV leads to diaphragm hypoxia or decreased blood flow, induced mammalian target protein of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway activation, and increased adipogenesis and lipogenesis [42]. Increased triglycerides in diaphragm fibres provide excess energy substrates, exceeding the ATP demands, leading to an accumulation of electron donors (e.g. NADH and flavin adenine dinucleotide (FADH2)) generated by β-oxidation and the tricarboxylic acid cycle [34]. The electron flow will surpass the capacity of the ETC, leading to electron leakage and the formation of large amounts of ROS.

Forkhead box O1-autophagy induces mitochondrial dysfunction

MV induces massive ROS generation, which activates three transcription factors: Fos, forkhead box O1 (FOXO1) and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3). FOXO1 induces enhanced autophagy by regulating autophagy-related (ATG) gene expression and transcription (microtubule-associated protein 1A/1B-light chain 3 (LC-3) and cathepsin L) and selectively degrades antioxidant proteins such as catalase, leading to further ROS production. Concurrently, Fos, FOXO1 and STAT3 synergistically enhance Bcl2-interacting mediator of cell death (Bim) gene expression. Bim protein interacts with mitochondrial anti-apoptotic protein on the mitochondrial membrane, triggering mitochondrial membrane potential collapse and increased permeability. This pathological cascade facilitates cytochrome c release from mitochondria into the cytosol, establishing a self-reinforcing cycle that intensifies oxidative damage through ROS overproduction and mitochondrial dysfunction [43, 44].

Increased mitochondrial fission

MV induces abnormal mitochondrial dynamics, i.e. increased fission, in mouse diaphragmatic mitochondria. Mitochondrial fission is primarily mediated by dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1), while fusion is regulated by mitofusins 1 and 2 and optic atrophy 1. Drp1 binding to mitochondrial receptor fission, mitochondrial 1 (FIS-1) increases fission and ROS production [45, 46].

Calpain pathway

MV induces excessive mitochondrial ROS production, which simultaneously inhibits plasma membrane Ca2+-ATPase activity. This disturbance in cytosolic Ca2+ metabolism leads to intracellular calcium accumulation, which triggers activation of calcium-dependent proteases (e.g. μ-calpain and m-calpain). Concurrently, ROS or Ca2+ overloading downregulates the expression of calpastatin, the endogenous inhibitor of calpain, thereby further enhancing calpain protease activity. Activated calpains disrupt mitochondrial membrane integrity via cleavage of cytoskeletal proteins, leading to ETC dysfunction. This structural damage exacerbates mitochondrial ROS overproduction, creating a self-amplifying loop that perpetuates oxidative stress and cellular injury [40, 47, 48].

Mitochondrial dysfunction induced by Janus kinase/STAT3 signalling

In the diaphragms of both humans and rats undergoing MV, ROS (e.g. H2O2) contribute to the phosphorylation of STAT3 in the nucleus through activation of tyrosine kinases (e.g. Janus kinase (JAK)), which regulates the expression of Bim, the mitochondrial uncoupling protein UCP and Cox5a; destabilises the mitochondrial membrane; and affects mitochondrial function. At the same time, phosphorylated STAT3 accumulates in the nucleus and then enters the cytoplasmic mitochondria, where it binds to and alters the function of ETC complex I (NDUFS3 subunit), leading to electron leakage and ROS generation.

Simultaneously, ROS induces interleukin (IL)-6 and IL-24 secretion and enhances JAK–STAT3 activation through membrane JAK–receptor coupling, and the bidirectional regulation creates a vicious cycle that amplifies oxidative stress injury [49]. Notably, STAT3 activation is absent in limb skeletal muscles (e.g. quadriceps) of ventilated patients and rodents, indicating that this signalling response is specific to the diaphragm [50].

Mitochondrial oxidative stress and diaphragm dysfunction

Mitochondrial oxidative stress affects diaphragm contraction in many ways: first, the free radicals generated can influence post-translational modifications of muscle proteins (e.g. oxidation), altering their structure and function, which affects cross-bridge dynamics and reduces the calcium sensitivity of myofilaments [40]. Second, oxidative stress induces a metabolic shift, decreasing mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation while increasing glycolysis, ultimately reducing muscle energy supply [51]. In individuals on MV, many rate-limiting glycolytic enzymes, such as phosphofructokinase, are significantly upregulated [52]. This accelerated glycolysis generates more intermediate metabolites that convert to fatty acids, while impaired mitochondrial function hampers fatty acid breakdown, exacerbating oxidative stress damage [53]. Third, excessive mitochondrial ROS production oxidises the calcium release type 1 ryanodine receptor (RyR1) and sarcomeric proteins, causing calcium leakage from RyR1 and altering excitation–contraction coupling, leading to MV-associated diaphragmatic weakness [54, 55].

Moreover, oxidative stress is essential for activating major proteolytic systems in diaphragm during MV. It accelerates protein hydrolysis and promotes atrophy of diaphragm muscle fibres by upregulating gene expression of key proteins involved in autophagy and the ubiquitin–proteasome system [43, 47], as well as activating proteolytic pathways like the 20S proteasome [56, 57], calpain [47, 48, 58] and caspase-3 [59].

Decreased protein synthesis

The protein balance in skeletal muscle fibres is primarily determined by the rates of protein synthesis and degradation, with muscle contraction activity playing a significant role in influencing protein synthesis [60]. During periods of inactivity, reduced protein transcription, increased degradation of transcripts (such as mRNA stability) and decreased translation lead to a lower protein synthesis rate. Research by Shanely et al. [61] showed that 6 h of MV can result in a 30% decrease in the muscle protein synthesis rate and a 65% decrease in myosin heavy chain synthesis, without altering the mRNA levels of diaphragm type I and IIx myosin heavy chains [61]. Thus, the early reduction of diaphragm muscle proteins during CMV may be linked to the regulation of protein translation.

The phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt/mTOR pathway is crucial for mediating protein synthesis in skeletal muscle, and this pathway is downregulated during prolonged inactivity [62, 63]. Prolonged MV reduces insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) levels in diaphragm and Akt phosphorylation [27, 64]. Studies show that 12 h of CMV significantly reduces the activation of AKT (phosphorylated AKT) in the diaphragm muscle. The decrease in AKT activation leads to insufficient activation of mTOR, which results in a reduction in the phosphorylation of the 70 kDa ribosomal protein S6 kinase and 4E-binding protein-1. This impedes the translational process of protein synthesis, ultimately leading to a significant decrease in the rate of diaphragm protein synthesis.

Acceleration of protein degradation

Shanely et al. [19] first reported that 18 h of CMV promotes the activation of proteases and increased diaphragm protein degradation. Since then, a substantial amount of evidence has shown that receiving CMV for more than 12 h can lead to accelerated diaphragm protein degradation, resulting in diaphragm atrophy. Its main mechanisms of action involve the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway (UPP), the calpain pathway, the caspase-3 pathway, the autophagy-lysosomal pathway (ALP) and dysfunction of the RyR1 pathway (figure 2). Prolonged muscle inactivity can trigger oxidative stress signalling events that further activate these systems, leading to the accelerated degradation of undamaged proteins and muscle atrophy.

UPP

The UPP is an important route for the degradation of damaged myofibrillar proteins in muscle [65]. The UPP is an ATP-dependent protein degradation system that binds ubiquitin to target proteins via ubiquitin-activating enzyme E1, ubiquitin conjugating enzyme E2 and ubiquitin ligase enzyme E3, and is recognised and degraded by the 26S proteasome when the target protein binds at least four ubiquitin molecules [66]. The high specificity of protein degradation mainly relies on the regulation of ubiquitin ligase E3 [67].

MV can inhibit the IGF-1/PI3K/Akt pathway, activate FOXO transcription factor, upregulate skeletal muscle-specific ubiquitin ligase E3 (muscle RING-finger protein 1 (MuRF-1) and atrogin 1/muscle atrophy F-box (MaFbx)) and enhance the proteolytic action of the UPP [68]. In a study by Ribeiro et al. [69], a decrease in diaphragm contractile force and muscle atrophy was observed 12 h after phrenic nerve injury. The use of the small molecule MuRF-1 regulator MyoMed-205 prevented protease activation and muscle fibre atrophy after diaphragm disuse. At the same time, MyoMed-205 alleviated damage by interacting with pathways that reduce excessive oxidative stress through the activation of FOXO1, inhibit MuRF2, and increase the levels of phosphorylated (Ser473) Akt protein, among others. In the study of Hooijman et al. [70], MuRF-1 knockout prevented diaphragmatic contractile weakness in MV mice.

However, in the study by Smuder et al. [71], the use of the highly selective UPP inhibitor epoxomicin did not prevent diaphragm fibre atrophy and contractile dysfunction induced by 12 h of MV, nor did it affect diaphragmatic oxidative injury, activation of calpain and caspase-3, or increased expression of E3 ligases MuRF-1 and atrogin-1/MaFbx. Agten et al. [72] also found that the use of the 26S proteasome inhibitor bortezomib only partially improved diaphragm contractile dysfunction, but could not prevent diaphragm atrophy.

There are a number of possible reasons for the above contradictory results. First, the UPP may have a limited effect on the development of VIDD within 12 h after MV starts. Second, the degradation of diaphragm proteins is the result of multiple pathways, including the UPP, calpain and caspase-3. The UPP cannot degrade myofibrillar protein directly; it needs to be released from the myofibrillar lattice by calpain and/or caspase-3 before it can be degraded. Long-term MV leads to the oxidation of actin and myosin in the diaphragm, which greatly increases the sensitivity of actin and myosin and other skeletal muscle fibrous protein to degradation by calpain and caspase-3 [59, 73]. Third, potential off-target effects of proteasome inhibitors may play a role. Although the role of the UPP in VIDD still cannot be completely disproved, clinical translation is not promising given the high side effects of UPP inhibitors (e.g. epoxomicin, bortezomib), and their posterior position of action in the pathogenesis, so future studies should focus more on more upstream pathogenic pathways [73].

Calpain pathway

Calpain is a calcium ion-dependent cysteine protease that exists in the cytoplasm and hydrolyses thiol proteins. It can degrade structural proteins in skeletal muscle, such as nebulin, actin, troponin and tropomyosin [74]. The increase in its activity and the change in its potential substrates may cause the early degradation of muscle after an increase in contraction activity. These early degradation changes can lead to alterations in the microstructure, such as the disruption of the myofibril apparatus (including the disappearance of the Z line) and an increase in sarcolemma permeability [75].

Long-term MV can lead to increased activity of the calpain 20S proteasome and increased protein degradation. The release rate of tyrosine, an in vitro protein degradation marker of MV isolated diaphragm, decreased significantly after the use of calpain and 20S proteasome inhibitor [19]. Calpain perpetuates its proteolytic cascade by degrading its endogenous inhibitor, calpastatin, thereby establishing a self-reinforcing loop that exacerbates diaphragmatic net protein loss through synergistic proteolysis and suppressed anabolism [76]. The protease inhibitor leupeptin can selectively inhibit calpain activity, prevent excessive lysosomal cleavage of cytoskeletal proteins, maintain the structural integrity of diaphragm cells, and thus improve the contraction function of diaphragm and reduce diaphragm atrophy [77].

Activation of calpain also facilitates other protein hydrolysis systems. Calpain may indirectly upregulate ubiquitin ligase E3 (MuRF-1 and atrogin-1) via the ATK–FOXO signalling axis, thereby enhancing the activity of the UPP and promoting ubiquitination-dependent protein degradation [48]. It also can process BH3-interacting domain death agonist (Bid) into active proapoptotic factor truncated Bid (tBid), initiating mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilisation and cytochrome c release. This sequentially activates caspase-9 and caspase-3 cascades, culminating in myonuclear apoptosis [76].

Additionally, calpain has a dual effect on protein homeostasis, both inhibiting synthesis and promoting catabolism. Active calpain can block signalling that promotes protein synthesis by inhibiting mTOR activation [78]. Another study showed that calpain proteolytically cleaves glutaminyl-tRNA synthetase, impairing aminoacylation and blocking amino acid transfer to nascent polypeptide chains, thereby inhibiting protein synthesis [48].

Caspase-3 pathway

Caspase is a member of the IL-1β converting enzyme family, playing a crucial role in the activation and execution of apoptosis, as well as in regulating inflammation. Caspase-2 is a major apoptosis initiator that activates downstream effectors, while caspase-3 is a key executor of apoptosis. Caspase-3 can cleave actin complexes, generating monomeric actin and actin fragments, which are then degraded by ubiquitin proteasomes [79]. In a denervation-induced muscle atrophy animal model, caspase-3 is activated, leading to muscle cell death through mitochondrial pathways and promoting muscle atrophy [80].

MV induces massive ROS generation, which triggers mitochondrial depolarisation and increased outer membrane permeability. Cytochrome c is released into the cytosol to bind apoptotic protease activator-1, forming the apoptosome complex, which in turn initiates the intrinsic apoptotic pathway of caspase-9 and activates caspase-3, triggering the apoptotic cascade reaction and inducing cell death. This process accelerates programmed cell death, thereby suppressing protein synthesis and promoting muscle atrophy [44]. Studies have shown that inhibition of caspase-3 activity with DEVD-CHO can reduce the loss of type I and IIa diaphragm fibre areas after 12 h of MV treatment [20].

Concurrently, in the signalling network of VIDD, crosstalk between calpain, caspase-3 and the UPP also amplifies the cascading response to diaphragmatic injury. Caspase-3 degrades the endogenous inhibitor calpastatin, relieving its suppression of calpain. Activated calpain cleaves Bid to generate the proapoptotic fragment active tBid, which further amplifies caspase-3 autocatalytic processing. This reciprocal activation establishes a self-reinforcing feedback loop, synergistically driving actomyosin complex degradation and exacerbating muscle wasting [76]. Furthermore, activation of caspase-3 is associated with the dissociation of myofibrils from the lattice, which cleaves myofibril-associated proteins, causing structural damage to the myofibrils and providing substrates for UPP-mediated protein degradation [81].

Excessive activation of the ALP

The ALP is the fourth protein degradation pathway involved in skeleton muscle catabolism. In limb muscles, autophagy is constitutively active and is strongly induced during fasting, oxidative stress and denervation, leading to significant muscle protein degradation.

Increased oxidative stress and activation of FOXO1 transcription factor may be important initiating factors in the VIDD autophagy pathway [82]. MV-induced oxidative stress and abnormal mitochondrial energy metabolism lead to activation of FOXO transcription factors (e.g. FOXO1/3), which induce ATG genes (e.g. LC3-II, BCL2-interacting protein 3 (BNIP3)), significantly increasing autophagosome formation and driving lysosome-dependent protein degradation [83, 84], and activates the ALP to remove damaged mitochondria (mitochondrial autophagy) [53]. Early fragmentation of intermyofibrillar mitochondria may suggest an early, excessive activation of the ALP in the pathophysiology of VIDD [53]. Meanwhile, ROS released from mitochondria further amplifies the FOXO-mediated autophagy and proteasome pathway, forming a vicious cycle and exacerbating protein imbalance [83].

In human diaphragm biopsy specimens following prolonged MV treatment, CMV increases the mRNA levels of autophagy-related proteins (cathepsin L (CTSL-1), beclin 1 (BECN-1), LC-3, GABA type A receptor-associated protein-like 1 (GABARAPL-1), BNIP-3, ATG-4B, autophagy and beclin 1 regulator 1 (AMBRA-1), phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase catalytic subunit type 3 (PI3KC3) and UV radiation resistance-associated (UVRAG)), promoting the accumulation of proteins like BECN-1, ATG-5 and ATG-7, thereby activating the ALP. Electron microscopy reveals double-membrane autophagosomes in mitochondria [82]. In severely ill patients, autophagic vacuole formation and impaired mitophagy in skeletal muscle tissues result in compromised protein clearance due to cell damage [85].

However, in studies by Moroz et al. [86] and Azuelos et al. [87], pretreatment with the antioxidant N-acetylcysteine and the autophagy inducer rapamycin increased autophagic activity but improved diaphragmatic contractile function. Pauly et al. [88] used an activator of AMP-activated protein kinase to induce autophagy in mdx mice (a model of Duchenne muscular dystrophy) and observed reduced dysfunctional mitochondria, along with improved diaphragm muscle strength. Thus, the question of whether autophagy is a driving factor of pathological injury or a compensatory protective mechanism triggered by increased oxidative stress remains a topic of debate.

Structural remodelling and dysfunction of RyR1

In normally innervated skeletal muscle, cytosolic calcium levels are tightly regulated by muscle electrical activity. Voltage-driven conformational changes in L-type calcium channels on the T-tubules induce remodelling of ryanodine receptors on the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR), facilitating rapid calcium release that triggers actin–myosin cross-bridge formation and muscle contraction. After contraction, cytosolic calcium is rapidly returned to the SR via the SR Ca2+-ATPase pump. In the diaphragm disuse animal model induced by VIDD, calcium leakage from the SR can be observed [89].

RyR1 is a homologous tetrameric macromolecular protein complex consisting of four RyR1 monomers (approximately 565 kDa each), along with kinases, phosphatases (PP1), phosphodiesterases (PDE4D3) and calmodulin [89]. Factors such as oxidative stress and excessive catecholamine release can lead to RyR1 remodelling, impairing Ca2+ handling and causing abnormal Ca2+ leak from the SR, resulting in contractile dysfunction associated with heart failure, chronic muscle fatigue, muscular dystrophy and ageing [90–92].

In both human and animal models, MV rapidly remodels RyR1 on the SR membrane through oxidation, S-nitrosylation and Ser-2844 phosphorylation, disrupting the stability of the receptor complex and leading to calcium leakage [93, 94]. Calcium leakage from the resting SR during MV causes a decrease in Ca2+ reserves in the SR, which could not effectively trigger actin–myosin cross-bridge formation during muscle excitation, leading to decreased diaphragm contraction; at the same time, Ca2+ leakage could activate Ca2+-dependent proteolytic enzymes (e.g. caspase, calpain) and Ca2+-dependent regulation, degrading cytoskeletal proteins and ultimately leading to muscle fibre atrophy [94]. The cell-permeable and orally active benzothiazole drug rS107 can prevent the depletion of calstabin-1 in the RyR1 macromolecular complex and mitigate the loss of diaphragm contractile strength induced by MV. However, blocking calcium release from the SR with an antagonist of RyR1 opening, azumolene, does not prevent the release of ROS, protease activation or fibre atrophy in diaphragm from CMV in rats [95], suggesting that RyR1 remodelling may be a contributory mechanism rather than a primary one in VIDD development.

SRX state myosin

The SRX state refers to a specific conformation of myosin, a motor protein critical for muscle contraction. In this energy-sparing state, myosin heads are sequestered away from the actin filament and are instead bound to each other and the myosin filament backbone. This conformation renders them unavailable to interact with actin and generate force, characterised by an ultraslow ATP turnover rate. It is distinct from the disordered relaxed state and the active cycling state [96].

Studies show increased SRX myosin populations in diaphragm biopsies from ventilated ICU patients compared with those from controls (slow-twitch: 68% versus 58%; fast-twitch: 65% versus 54%), correlating with reduced contractile force and calcium sensitivity. This phenomenon is diaphragm-specific, given that quadriceps muscles from the same patients showed no increase in SRX state myosin, highlighting the role of ventilator-induced unloading. In vitro, troponin activators restore force generation by enhancing calcium sensitivity, promoting myosin recruitment from the SRX state [18]. Thus, SRX state myosin represents a key molecular mechanism in diaphragm contractile dysfunction.

However, from a biological perspective, SRX state myosin is an adaptive and protective response to MV, characterised by low energy consumption and the prevention of muscle damage. Therefore, SRX state myosin has shown potential as both a protective and pathogenic factor in VIDD. More comprehensive research is required to clarify its role. Understanding the dose-response relationship and the influence of different ventilation strategies will provide deeper insights into how SRX state myosin contributes to muscle dysfunction and whether it can be leveraged for therapeutic benefit.

MV

With the in-depth study of MV-related injuries, many experts and scholars have proposed the concept of lung- and diaphragm-protective ventilation. At its core, lung- and diaphragm-protective ventilation seeks to optimise the patient's respiratory work, while maintaining the patient's normal physiological homeostasis, protecting the lungs and diaphragm to avoid secondary injuries. The main principle in lung-protective ventilation is to limit overall or regional stress and strain, while diaphragm-protective ventilation is guided by maintaining a moderate respiratory effort to promote moderate diaphragmatic activity as much as possible. We will combine the recommendations of previous experts and our clinical practice experience to divide the whole process of MV into three periods: initiation, optimisation and weaning, and give the corresponding clinical recommendations for each period (figure 3).

Initiation phrase

Lung- and diaphragm-protective ventilation is started at the initiation of MV, and this guiding principle will be applied throughout the whole procedure. When conflicts arise, the principle of lung-protective ventilation must be prioritised over diaphragm protection [110]. According to the patient's respiratory physiological status and support needs, a reasonable respiratory support strategy will be selected.

Optimisation phase

Management of the primary cause of respiratory failure is the core element of the optimisation phase. It mainly includes control of systemic or local inflammation, optimisation of haemodynamic parameters, improvement of neurological status and enhancement of organ function support.

Optimisation of the respiratory support strategy is a second focus of this phase. It mainly comprises monitoring respiratory effort and optimising the respiratory treatment parameters.

Monitoring respiratory effort

Monitoring respiratory effort enables timely detection of potential lung and diaphragm injury risks associated with current respiratory therapy protocols, facilitating optimisation of ventilator settings or sedation strategies.

Current research recommends several indicators for quantifying spontaneous breathing effort, including oesophageal pressure swing, transdiaphragmatic pressure, airway occlusion pressure (P0.1, the pressure generated against an occluded airway at 0.1 s after the onset of inspiration), expiratory occlusion pressure (Pocc), root mean square of diaphragmatic electromyography and the diaphragm thickening fraction (TFdi) in diaphragm ultrasound. Monitoring tools for respiratory drive and effort

EMGdi: diaphragm electromyography; MV: mechanical ventilation; P0.1: pressure generated against an occluded airway at 0.1 s after the onset of inspiration; PEEP: positive end-expiratory pressure; Pmus: pressure generated by all the respiratory muscles; Pocc: expiratory occlusion pressure; TFdi: diaphragm thickening fraction.

Of these, P0.1 and Pocc are commonly used clinical parameters at the bedside available on many modern ventilators for automatic detection of respiratory drive and respiratory efforts. P0.1 reflects the patient's central respiratory drive by measuring the drop in airway pressure during the first 100 ms of an occluded inspiratory effort. Its features of noninvasive and automatic measurements that are unaffected by respiratory mechanics and moderate muscle weakness make it a very practical monitoring tool for assessing respiratory drive [115]. P0.1≤1.0 cmH2O (sensitivity 100%, specificity 92%) and >3.5 cmH2O (sensitivity 80%, specificity 77%) are specific for detecting low and high inspiratory drive, respectively [113]. Pocc is the negative deflection of airway pressure during a whole breath end-expiratory occlusion. It is more stable and accurate than P0.1 for detecting high respiratory effort [116] and can be used to estimate muscle effort and lung stress [120].

Diaphragm ultrasound is a bedside imaging technique that allows direct assessment of the degree of diaphragm atrophy and contractile function by measuring diaphragm thickness and movement. To quantify diaphragmatic function, various parameters can be evaluated using diaphragmatic ultrasound. The most commonly assessed variables include the TFdi and diaphragmatic excursion (EXdi). TFdi is calculated by measuring diaphragmatic thickness at end-inspiration and end-expiration, with the lower limit of normal being 20% [121]. EXdi refers to the measurement of the diaphragm's movement during respiration, with a lower limit of normal for movement during deep breathing of 3.2 cm for women and 4.1 cm for men [122]. In study by Alam et al. [119], TFdi (area under the curve (AUC) 0.69, sensitivity 61.1%, specificity 84.6%, cut-off 22.33%) and EXdi (AUC 0.83, sensitivity 77.8%, specificity 84.6%, cut-off 11.43 mm) are good noninvasive predictors of weaning success or failure [119].

Factors affecting respiratory effort include not only arterial hypoxaemia and arterial potential of hydrogen/carbon dioxide tension stimuli, but also fever, pain, anxiety, pulmonary oedema or inflammation stimulating peripheral lung receptors. For the first two factors, respiratory effort can be reduced by optimising respiratory therapy protocols. For the others, symptomatic treatment must be combined with primary disease management to correct the underlying causes.

Optimising the respiratory treatment parameters

Target 1: maintain appropriate respiratory drive

Respiratory demands vary among patients. Monitoring respiratory effort helps determine whether the patient's respiratory demand matches the level of ventilator support. For patients with high respiratory drive, reducing ventilator assistance (e.g. pressure or tidal volume settings) can avoid barotrauma and under-assistance diaphragm myotrauma. Conversely, for patients with low respiratory effort, increasing pressure or tidal volume assistance improves oxygen delivery, reduces inspiratory work and prevents spontaneous lung or diaphragm injury due to excessive respiratory load. Current evidence suggests that maintaining inspiratory effort levels consistent with quiet resting breathing may prevent diaphragm atrophy and load-induced injury [110], while thresholds for excessive or insufficient respiratory effort are summarised in table 4.

Target 2: ensure synchronised respiratory cycles

Patient–ventilator asynchrony increases lung stress, strain and injurious eccentric load-induced diaphragm injury. Common dyssynchrony patterns like reverse triggering and premature cycling exacerbate post-inspiratory loading, leading to eccentric diaphragm damage and weakened contractility [13].

Reverse triggering primarily occurs in deeply sedated patients with suppressed spontaneous breathing, in whom ventilator inflation triggers delayed diaphragm contractions. This phenomenon can cause breath stacking, abrupt increases in lung strain and eccentric diaphragm contractions. Neuromuscular blocking agents (NMBAs) may mitigate this effect [123].

Premature cycling arises in patients with high respiratory drive and low respiratory system time constants. When neural inspiratory time exceeds mechanical inspiratory time, diaphragmatic eccentric contractions occur during prolonged expiration [124]. Adjustments such as increasing ventilator inspiratory time, using decelerating flow waveforms in volume-assist-control mode or reducing cycling thresholds in pressure support mode can improve synchrony [125].

During the expiratory period, although also present in healthy people, avoidance of excessive expiratory braking may also reduce the risk of eccentric injury in the diaphragm. The use of the appropriate PEEP reduces the likelihood of its occurrence [31]. A proportional ventilation mode, such as proportional assist ventilation or neurally adjusted ventilatory assist (NAVA), which can adjust the delivery pressure in proportion to the patient's respiratory effort during inspiration, can improve diaphragm contraction efficiency and reduce asynchrony, thus facilitating protective ventilation of the lung and diaphragm [125, 126].

Target 3: optimise sedation and analgesia

Balanced sedation and analgesia ensure patient comfort while avoiding respiratory over-suppression. Guidelines published in 2018 for managing agitation/sedation in the ICU suggest that pain management for adult ICU patients should be guided by routine pain assessment, and pain should be treated before a sedative agent is considered (good practice statement) [100]. Light sedation (versus deep sedation) was recommended for critically ill adults under MV (conditional recommendation, low certainty) [100]. The effects of common sedatives and analgesics on respiratory function are summarised in table 5. In our clinical practice, deeper sedation may be necessary in patients with excessive drive due to pain or anxiety after excluding other factors. Conversely, reducing sedation early in recovery may promote respiratory muscle exercise and reduce the risk of atrophy.

Target 4: short-term reasonable neuromuscular blockade

Complete neuromuscular blockade is a commonly used clinical therapy that can improve patient–ventilator synchrony and facilitate lung-protective ventilation. However, its prolonged use carries the risk of VIDD and ICU-AW. It is particularly indicated for patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) who have a strong spontaneous respiratory drive that remains refractory to sedative medications.

NMBAs gained widespread prominence following the landmark 2010 randomised controlled trial by Papazian et al. [127], which showed improved outcomes in severe ARDS. However, numerous subsequent studies have continuously revealed potential adverse effects and uncertainty in clinical outcomes [128, 129]. The operational utility of NMBAs in ARDS management remains a subject of significant divergence in major guidelines, reflecting ongoing controversy in the field. Specifically, the 2024 American Thoracic Society guidelines provide a conditional recommendation for the use of NMBAs in patients with early severe ARDS (defined as arterial oxygen tension/inspiratory oxygen fraction ratio <100 mmHg and within 48 h of ARDS onset; conditional recommendation, low certainty) [103]. In contrast, the 2023 European Society of Intensive Care Medicine guidelines strongly recommend against the routine use of continuous infusions of NMBAs (strong recommendation, moderate certainty) [99]. A rapid practice guideline of NMBAs in ARDS recommends that intermittent NMBAs combined with judicious deep sedation should be used only in patients with moderate/severe ARDS who require NMBAs to promote lung-protective ventilation. Infusion of NMBAs for up to 48 h is preferred over intermittent NMBAs for patients who require continuous deep sedation and neuromuscular blockade to promote lung-protective ventilation (suggestion, low certainty) [104].

The significant divergence between major international guidelines for the use of NMBAs in ARDS reflects the need for highly individualised clinical patient selection and the concern over potential side effects. Low-dose neuromuscular blockers (partial neuromuscular blockers) are an interesting compromise between complete paralysis and intense respiratory effort, especially when respiratory effort is not adequately titrated to ventilatory support or sedation. Titration of a tidal volume of >8 mL·kg−1 in NAVA ventilated patients to <6 mL·kg−1 and maintenance of diaphragmatic activity have been verified as feasible in some studies [130, 131]. However, partial neuromuscular blockers do not reduce the respiratory drive, which may increase the symptom of dyspnoea, and should be supplemented with a careful sedative drug regimen. In the future, larger clinical trials are needed to verify the protective effect and safety of lungs and diaphragm.

Target 5: diaphragm pacing

PNS can directly control diaphragm activity when there is a loss of respiratory drive, helping maintain adequate diaphragm contraction. Animal studies have shown that PNS reduces diaphragm atrophy, minimises injury and prevents fibre damage, providing the biological foundation for using PNS to prevent diaphragm dysfunction [132]. Based on this, some animal studies have demonstrated that combining PNS with MV can significantly reduce transpulmonary pressure, lower the rate of atelectasis and improve ventilation homogeneity, achieving dual protection for both lung and diaphragm [133].

Despite satisfactory results in animal experiments, relevant evidence from human experiments remains limited. In a phase 1 clinical trial by Morris et al. [134], bilateral diaphragm pacing was performed using transjugular phrenic nerve electrodes in patients without respiratory drive, with the aim of maintaining median EMGdi equal to or above the target minimum (sufficient to keep Pocc≤−5 cmH2O). The study found that within the first 24 h of treatment, temporary PNS significantly shortened the duration of diaphragm inactivity. These findings provide proof of concept for the physiological feasibility of on-demand continuous PNS to prevent diaphragm inactivity during acute respiratory failure when spontaneous respiratory drive is lost.

Dres et al. [135] conducted a multicentre randomised controlled trial involving 112 patients who had failed more than two SBTs and had undergone invasive MV for ≥4 days. The trial compared patients receiving bilateral PNS with those receiving standard care, and assessed diaphragm function and outcomes. Results showed that PNS significantly increased maximal inspiratory pressure and right-side diaphragm contractile thickening fraction without increasing major safety issues. However, it did not significantly affect weaning rates, duration of MV or 30-day survival. This study has limitations, including a small sample size, heterogeneous patient population, open-label design and suboptimal stimulation dose. Future research should involve larger, well-designed randomised controlled trials, optimised stimulation protocols and combined interventions to optimise diaphragm function and weaning outcomes.

Recently, a phenomenon called diaphragm SRX state (also “diaphragm hibernation”) has been raising important physiological concerns around PNS. If this state is considered a protective, energy-sparing state in the early phase of MV, diaphragm activation using pacemakers may actually have detrimental effects on the diaphragm (and other organs), which may be similar to the pacemaker-induced cardiomyopathy observed in cardiac patients [136].

Although PNS has shown encouraging results in animal experiments and some early-phase, small-scale clinical trials, its role in acute critical illness or as a routine strategy for MV weaning in the broader ICU population remains investigational until more reliable evidence emerges and physiological concerns are adequately addressed.

Weaning phase

Patients should begin the weaning phase as soon as clinical stability is achieved. 2017 weaning guidelines suggests managing acutely hospitalised adults who have been on MV for >24 h with a ventilator liberation protocol (conditional recommendation, low certainty) [105]. A standardised weaning protocol facilitates the management of weaning patients [137, 138].

Several randomised trials have shown that daily use of SBTs to identify patients ready to wean safely and effectively reduces the time to extubate compared with methods that gradually reduce ventilatory support (e.g. systematic reduction of inspiratory pressure during pressure support ventilation or reduction of mandatory breath frequency during synchronised intermittent command ventilation). Daily spontaneous awakening trials (i.e. interruption of sedatives) with daily SBTs significantly reduces the duration of MV and improves patient prognosis [106]. Weaning guidelines suggests that, for acutely hospitalised patients ventilated for >24 h, the initial SBTs should be conducted with inspiratory pressure augmentation (5–8 cmH2O; conditional recommendation, moderate-quality evidence) [105]. Patients who can tolerate a 30–120 min SBT trial may be considered for continued extubation readiness, and patients who fail SBTs should have the reason for SBT failure determined and corrected before reattempting SBTs. It has been demonstrated that diaphragm ultrasound can successfully predict weaning failure by continuously monitoring diaphragm excursions (sensitivity 83%, specificity 68%, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve 0.78), providing a new objective basis for clinical decision-making [139].

A cuff-leak test is performed in adults on MV who meet the criteria for extubation and who are considered at high risk of upper airway obstruction (conditional recommendation, very low certainty) [140]. It is recommended that systemic steroids be administered at least 4 h prior to extubation in adults who fail a balloon leak test but are ready for extubation (conditional recommendation, moderate certainty) [141].

Inspiratory muscle training (IMT) is the most commonly performed targeted physical therapy intervention, akin to “weight training for the inspiratory muscles”, and is primarily aimed at strengthening and improving the endurance of the diaphragm and accessory inspiratory muscles. Inspiratory pressure threshold devices are generally recommended for IMT owing to their accuracy and ability to provide consistent loads. Although there is no consensus on the specific implementation process of IMT, multiple studies and systematic reviews consistently demonstrate that IMT is effective in enhancing respiratory muscle strength and endurance in critically ill patients [142, 143]. However, there is significant conflicting evidence in the literature regarding the direct impact of IMT on weaning [144–146]. This implies that, while IMT is physiologically effective in strengthening muscle efforts, its translation into the ultimate clinical outcome of weaning success may be confounded by other variables. Therefore, in clinical practice, IMT should be considered an important component of a comprehensive weaning protocol, rather than a standalone solution, and future research needs to address these methodological complexities to provide clearer guidance on its precise role in extubation success.

Clinical drugs

Cellular, molecular and transcriptional studies have revealed the pathogenesis of VIDD and provided numerous potential pharmacological targets for intervention. A summary of key pharmacological interventions for VIDD is presented in table 6. Theophylline is widely used as a second-line treatment for patients with poorly controlled asthma or COPD. In addition to relaxing airway smooth muscles and its anti-inflammatory effects that improve ventilation, theophylline also stimulates the respiratory neural network and increases the activity of respiratory muscles such as the intercostal muscle, diaphragm and abdominal muscle [148, 149]. Aminophylline can reduce oxidative stress, apoptosis and protein metabolism to improve VIDD by regulating the IGF-1–FOXO1–MuRF-1 pathway [150]. A randomised controlled trial by Kim et al. [151] demonstrated that low-dose theophylline (median dose, 200 mg·day−1) significantly improved respiratory movement in patients who required MV for at least 72 h, met the criteria for a SBT and had ultrasound evidence. The encouraging finding that low-dose theophylline significantly improves diaphragm muscle movement suggests the need for larger clinical trials to determine whether theophylline is beneficial during MV weaning.

The renin–angiotensin system (RAS) consists of two signalling pathways: the classical axis and the non-classical axis. The classical RAS signalling pathway is mediated through angiotensin (Ang) II or mechanical activation of the Ang II type 1 receptor on the cell membrane, triggering a signalling cascade that leads to the activation of NADPH oxidase 2 and increased mitochondrial ROS production, resulting in protease activation and muscle fibre atrophy [152]. In contrast, the non-classical RAS pathway counteracts the effects of the classical pathway [153]. This antagonistic effect occurs through the binding of Ang-1–7 to the Mas receptor, a G-protein-coupled receptor. Ang II infusion can induce skeletal and diaphragm muscle atrophy [154]. While angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, such as enalapril, can prevent elevated plasma Ang II levels, they do not prevent diaphragm oxidative stress or muscle weakness caused by MV [155]. Additionally, Ang-1–7 has been shown to protect the muscles of animals on MV from atrophy caused by prolonged inactivity or high circulating levels of Ang II [156]. Furthermore, the use of Ang receptor blockers like losartan can reduce oxidative stress and diaphragm weakness induced by MV [155]. More clinical trials are needed to further verify the protective effects of these drugs on diaphragm in human.

Levosimendan is primarily used as a calcium sensitiser in patients with heart failure, but its ability to improves neuromechanical efficiency and contractile function of the human diaphragm has been demonstrated in several studies [157, 158]. Furthermore, Roesthuis et al. [159] demonstrated that legomena did not improve diaphragm contractile efficiency, but increased tidal volume and minute ventilation and lowered arterial carbon dioxide in patients weaning from MV. Wu et al. [160] conducted a single-blind randomised controlled trial on the use of levosimendan for sepsis-related diaphragm dysfunction. The study included 44 patients with VIDD who failed the SBTs, randomly assigning them to either the levosimendan or placebo group. The results showed that patients in the levosimendan group experienced an increase in tidal volume, a reduction in rapid shallow breathing index and end-tidal carbon dioxide levels, and a significant improvement in diaphragm mobility observed through bedside ultrasound. Unfortunately, there was no significant difference in the weaning success rate between the two groups.

Experimental pharmacological preparations

Mitochondrial oxidative damage is a core aspect of VIDD, so antioxidant therapy is an important target for pharmacological research. Moroz et al. [86] pretreated mechanically ventilated mice with the antioxidant N-acetylcysteine, which improved diaphragmatic contractile function, attenuated protein ubiquitination, downregulated E3 ligase and autophagy gene expression, and reversed the controlled ventilation-induced decrease in microRNA expression. However, it did not improve diaphragmatic fibre atrophy [87, 161]. In a single site and small sample clinical trial by Howe et al. [147], enterally administered N-acetylcysteine combination (vitamin C 1000 mg plus vitamin E 1000 IU plus N-acetylcysteine 400 mg) decreased the duration of MV. Treatment of rats on MV with antioxidants, such as trolox, and the mitochondria-targeted antioxidant peptide SS31, can significantly alleviate mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress and protease activation in diaphragm, preventing the reduction in specific force and muscle fibre atrophy measured in MV animal models [56, 57]. The mitochondria-localised antioxidant peroxiredoxin-3, when overexpressed in transgenic mice, has been shown to protect against progression to VIDD [34].

Inhibition of the JAK/STAT pathway using Rigel inhibitor R548, which targets the phosphorylation of STAT3 at tyrosine 705 and serine 727, eliminated the accumulation of phosphorylated STAT3 in mitochondria, reversed pathological changes in mitochondrial function, reduced oxidative stress in diaphragm and maintained normal diaphragm contractility. This inhibitor also weakened the activation of key proteolytic pathways in diaphragm, thereby mitigating atrophy [49]. Ruxolitinib is a drug that acts at another site of the pathway; it specifically binds to JAK1 and JAK2, reduces STAT3 phosphorylation levels, and decreases mitochondria-related damage and oxidative stress inflammatory response. It also regulates post-translational modification of myosin, maintains diaphragm muscle strength, reduces diaphragm atrophy and improves survival rate [162].

P110, the mitochondrial fission inhibitor, binds to Drp-1 receptor FIS-1 and reduces mitochondrial fission, mitochondrial ROS production, RyR1 oxidation and SR calcium leak, and thereby prevents diaphragm dysfunction induced by MV [55].

BGP-15 (n-(2-hydroxy-3-(1-piperidinyl) propoxy)-3-pyridine carboximidamide dihydrochloride), a chaperone co-inducer of heat shock protein 72, has shown promise in modulating membrane rafts, inhibiting poly(adenosine 5′-diphosphate)-ribose polymerase 1 and improving mitochondrial efficiency, making it a potential therapeutic candidate for VIDD [49].

The emergence of these novel therapeutic targets signifies a strategic shift in VIDD drug development, from broad symptomatic relief to precise interventions targeting cellular and molecular dysfunctions. This shift stems from a deeper understanding of its complex pathophysiology, moving beyond simply “disuse atrophy” to recognising the intricate interplay of molecular events. Meanwhile, the highly interconnected nature of these molecular biology pathways makes the development of targeted drugs uncertain. Future scientific research will face crucial obstacles and urgent needs in organising these complex pathways, creating a map of molecular interactions based on causal inference, and creating multi-pathway co-targeted medications.

Conclusions

To explore the pathogenic mechanisms of the condition VIDD, many researchers have actively engaged in comprehensive studies. Research on MV-related respiratory mechanics helps us understand the macroscopic myotrauma mechanisms of VIDD. Ongoing clinical trials on comprehensive management strategies of lung- and diaphragm-protective ventilation, such as those encompassing elements like low tidal volume ventilation, monitoring respiratory effort, optimisation of sedatives and analgesia and diaphragmatic pacing, are continually refining respiratory therapy protocols in clinical practice. Although the exact cellular and molecular mechanisms of VIDD are not fully defined, pathways involving mitochondrial oxidative stress have been identified as crucial in disease progression. Additionally, research into genetic pathways and transcription factors like JAK/STAT3/Smad3 and FOXOs, along with diaphragmatic mitochondrial dynamics, is enhancing our understanding of significant upstream signals. Promising interventions based on microscopic cellular molecular pathway findings have yielded hopeful results in animal models for preventing VIDD. We believe that the development of VIDD is a result of the complex interplay between multiple mechanisms and a vast signalling network. Therefore, sustained, collaborative and innovative research is urgently needed to bridge the gap between basic science and clinical practice, translating scientific understanding of VIDD into effective therapeutic solutions, thereby improving outcomes for patients on MV.
