Data-driven predictive maintenance of induction motors using self-supervised and federated learning on noisy current and vibration signals
Authors: T Gopalakrishnan, N Shanmugasundaram, Deepa Simon, Choon Kit Chan, Chandrakant Sonawane, Saurav Dixit, Arunkumar Bongale
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInduction motors (IMs) sustain a vast share of industrial activity but are prone to bearing wear, rotor-bar breakage, eccentricity, and insulation defects that emerge under non-stationary, noisy conditions. While Motor Current Signature Analysis (MCSA) remains attractive for its non-intrusive sensing, its discriminative power collapses at low signal-to-noise ratios (SNR) and when labelled fault exemplars are scarce. We present a unified Self-Supervised + Federated Learning (SSL–FL) framework that (i) learns transferable, noise-tolerant embeddings from large unlabelled corpora of stator current and vibration signals, and (ii) enables privacy-preserving, cross-site training without sharing raw data. Using chronological splits, SNR stress tests (0–15 dB), fault-severity breakdowns (incipient/developing/severe), non-IID federated client simulations, and leave-one-site-out transfer across CWRU, Paderborn, IMS, and an industrial pump–IM testbed, the approach consistently outperforms strong deep baselines (CNN/LSTM/Transformer), achieving 94.2% overall accuracy (92.4% incipient), 0.92 F1, and 0.90 MCC. It delivers 11–17 percentage-point gains in low-SNR regimes and 83.5% cross-domain accuracy, while attaining ~ 91% of the centralized upper bound with ~ 58% aggregation bandwidth under secure aggregation and (ε = 2.0, δ = 10⁻5) differential privacy. Performance remains stable under Dirichlet non-IID distributions (α = 0.1), confirming practical robustness to heterogeneous multi-site data. By coupling unlabelled representation learning with confidentiality-aware collaboration and severity-aware evaluation, the method advances a practical path to scalable, noise-robust, and compliant condition monitoring for Industry 4.0 assets.Keywords: Induction motor, Self-supervised learning, Federated learning, Fault diagnosis, Vibration, Process innovationSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Electric motor–driven systems are the workhorses of modern plants. Across discrete manufacturing, process industries, and water utilities, they convert electrical energy into the torque that keeps production lines, pumps, and compressors running continuously. Policy analyses estimate that motor-driven systems consume approximately two-thirds of industrial electricity worldwide, making even modest reliability gains significant at the fleet scale1. Induction motors (IMs) in particular face a spectrum of degradations—bearing pitting and lubrication loss, broken rotor bars, static and dynamic eccentricity, and stator insulation ageing—that emerge under fluctuating loads, supply distortion, and harsh environments. Left unattended, incipient defects propagate into costly downtime, collateral equipment damage, and safety incidents, which explains the long-standing emphasis on condition monitoring and fault diagnosis (CMFD) in both research and industrial standards2,3.

Among sensing modalities, Motor Current Signature Analysis (MCSA) has gained prominence because it is non-intrusive, inexpensive, and leverages signals already available at the control panel. Classic studies map characteristic sidebands in the stator current to rotor and air-gap anomalies4. However, MCSA’s effectiveness is well-documented to be sensitive to low signal-to-noise ratios (SNR), voltage unbalance, harmonic pollution, and time-varying operating points that smear or mask narrowband fault components5. Vibration sensing complements current-based methods but introduces sensor placement, mounting, and recalibration challenges that complicate large-scale deployment6.

Data-driven methods have steadily improved detection sensitivity. Classical machine learning pipelines offered early gains, while deep networks (CNNs, RNNs, Transformers) learned discriminative representations directly from raw time-series or time–frequency maps7,8. Yet two structural constraints persist in practice. First, labels are scarce: fleets operate predominantly in healthy states, and fault occurrences are rare, heterogeneous, and unevenly documented. Second, raw data sharing across plants is frequently precluded by confidentiality requirements, intellectual property agreements, and regulatory frameworks, even when centralisation would improve generalisation.

Recent progress in self-supervised representation learning and privacy-aware optimisation offers a principled route around these bottlenecks. Self-supervised learning (SSL) can learn invariant, transferable structure from large corpora of unlabelled signals via pretext objectives such as masked prediction and temporal contrast, bypassing the need for expert fault annotations9,10. Federated learning (FL) coordinates training across distributed clients by exchanging model parameters rather than raw data, with convergent algorithms addressing statistical heterogeneity and system drift11–13. When combined with secure aggregation and differential privacy, FL mitigates information leakage while enabling genuine cross-site collaboration14,15.

Despite these parallel advances, the overwhelming majority of industrial CMFD studies explore SSL and FL in isolation, and neither community has adequately addressed the compounded challenge that plants actually face: few fault labels, non-stationary and heterogeneous operating regimes, multi-site data privacy constraints, and a need for reliable incipient-fault detection before severity escalates. Critically, existing FL approaches for fault diagnosis largely assume independent and identically distributed (IID) client data—an assumption that rarely holds when factories operate different motor models, load profiles, and maintenance schedules. Meanwhile, SSL studies for rotating machinery have not been integrated with privacy-preserving collaboration, leaving the encoder vulnerable to the data leakage risks that prevent cross-plant pooling in the first place. Recent domain adaptation works have demonstrated that bridging distribution gaps between sites is feasible, but have not coupled this with privacy guarantees or the label efficiency that large-scale deployment demands.

This paper closes that joint gap with a unified SSL–FL framework tailored specifically to induction motor diagnostics. The core insight is straightforward but underexplored: SSL and FL are not merely complementary technologies to be combined—they are mutually reinforcing. SSL produces robust, site-agnostic embeddings that make FL aggregation more stable under non-IID statistics, while FL’s cross-site data diversity enriches the SSL pretraining signal beyond what any single site could provide. Our contributions are as follows. (i) We design dual pretext tasks—masked signal reconstruction (MSR) and contrastive temporal learning (CTL)—suited to the mixed periodic–stochastic structure of electromechanical waveforms, and we provide an ablation demonstrating the distinct and complementary roles of each. (ii) We integrate privacy-preserving federated learning with proximal regularisation, secure aggregation, and calibrated differential privacy, and we explicitly compare our protocol against standard FedAvg to clarify what each component contributes. (iii) We evaluate under a comprehensive protocol that includes fault-severity breakdowns (incipient/developing/severe), Dirichlet non-IID client simulations, and leave-one-site-out transfer, filling the evaluation gaps that existing studies have left open. (iv) We show that dual-modality fusion (current + vibration) consistently outperforms either modality alone, and we quantify the margin across SNR conditions.

Literature review

Research on induction-motor condition monitoring spans four arcs: (i) signal-centric diagnostics, (ii) model-based paradigms, (iii) data-driven learning, and (iv) methods that relax supervision or centralisation. The literature shows steady accuracy gains in well-controlled laboratories but persistent fragility in low-SNR, non-stationary, and cross-site settings. This section synthesises those threads and motivates the joint SSL–FL solution.

Traditional signal-based diagnosis

Motor Current Signature Analysis remains the most widely adopted non-intrusive technique because stator current is already available at the switchgear. Early surveys mapped fault mechanisms to spectral fingerprints—broken-bar sidebands, eccentricity harmonics—establishing a practical route to diagnosis without instrumenting the machine frame4,16,17. In field conditions, narrowband indicators are readily smeared by voltage unbalance, harmonic pollution, and load swings, depressing sensitivity and inflating false negatives5. Vibration analysis complements current-based methods, excelling for rolling-element bearing defects and rotor–stator eccentricity; envelope analysis and spectral kurtosis improved detectability of incipient faults6,18. Signal-centric methods are economical and interpretable but brittle under noise and operating variability.

Residual and model-based approaches

Model-based fault diagnosis uses observers, parity relations, and subspace identifiers to generate residuals sensitive to faults. Seminal references formalised residual generator synthesis and isolation logic19,20, while subspace identification provided data-driven linear models with statistical guarantees21. Two issues limit broad industrial uptake: model–plant mismatch under nonlinear couplings and non-stationary loads, and the computational overhead of on-line adaptation in heterogeneous fleets.

Machine learning and deep learning for CMFD

Data-driven pipelines replaced handcrafted features with learned discriminants. Classical SVM and ensemble approaches using time–frequency statistics offered early lifts. The shift to deep learning—1-D CNNs on raw traces, spectrogram-based CNNs, and sequence models (LSTM, Transformer)—brought further gains on clean benchmarks3,7,8,22. Transfer-learning studies demonstrated that pretraining helps when labels are scarce8. Two structural obstacles persist: label scarcity and imbalance, and domain shift across motors, sensors, and sites. Recent domain adaptation methods have made important progress on the latter. Zhao et al. proposed a source-free domain adaptation approach for open-set cross-domain fault diagnosis that eliminates the need to access source data during adaptation—a step toward privacy-safe transfer. Li et al. introduced a multi-source domain adaptation framework with evidence-based target pseudo-labels designed for human–computer collaboration in fault diagnosis. Both works highlight the tractability of cross-domain transfer, but neither couples it with privacy-preserving multi-site training or the label efficiency afforded by self-supervised pretraining. Centralising data to mitigate shift remains infeasible in many industrial contexts.

Self-supervised representation learning for time-series

SSL removes the dependence on labels by training on pretext tasks that exploit intrinsic structure. Contrastive objectives such as CPC and SimCLR maximise agreement between semantically related views while separating negatives, yielding invariances to small shifts and distortions23,24. Masked reconstruction—popularised in language and vision—encourages models to infer missing spans from context, acting as a learned denoiser and promoting long-range coherence25. For sensor time-series, temporal-context contrast and cross-channel consistency have proven effective in learning transferable features under noise and non-stationarity26. In rotating machinery, SSL studies report improved downstream accuracy with fewer labels and better robustness to perturbations, though pipelines remain fragmented and are rarely integrated with privacy-aware training.

Federated learning for privacy-preserving collaboration

FL coordinates learning across distributed clients by sharing model updates instead of raw data. FedAvg established the basic protocol with periodic aggregation, and FedProx added proximal regularisation to stabilise optimisation under non-IID data and varying compute budgets12,27,28. Secure aggregation prevents the server from inspecting individual client updates, and differential privacy bounds information leakage about individual samples14,15. Industrial CMFD applications of FL are emerging, but most reported studies assume IID client distributions and full supervision. A particularly relevant recent contribution is the balance recovery and collaborative adaptation framework of Zhang et al., which explicitly addresses federated fault diagnosis across inconsistent machine groups—a setting closely analogous to the multi-site, heterogeneous-sensor deployment studied here. That work demonstrates that combining local adaptation with global aggregation stabilises convergence under severe cross-site data imbalance, motivating our integration of FedProx regularisation and quality-aware update capping.

Research gap and motivation

The literature signals a clear and specific opportunity: combine SSL’s label efficiency and noise-robust representation learning with FL’s cross-site collaboration and privacy guarantees, and evaluate rigorously under the conditions that matter for deployment—non-IID client distributions, fault severity gradations, and cross-hardware transfer. Source-free and multi-source domain adaptation approaches have sharpened tools for distribution shift, but do not address the privacy dimension. The remainder of this paper operationalises the combined approach and demonstrates its empirical advantages over strong supervised baselines under realistic SNR stress, severity breakdown, non-IID simulation, and leave-one-site-out transfer.

Methodology

The proposed framework couples self-supervised representation learning on unlabelled signals with federated fine-tuning across distributed sites. The pipeline comprises standardised preprocessing, dual SSL pretext tasks tailored to electromechanical time series, and privacy-preserving aggregation with explicit robustness to non-IID data and varying operating conditions.

Signal acquisition and preprocessing

Three-phase stator current (from CTs at the control panel) and tri-axial vibration near the bearing housings are used. These modalities are physiologically complementary: current captures electrical sidebands linked to rotor and eccentricity faults; vibration emphasises mechanical incipience such as bearing defects and looseness. After chronological split (to prevent temporal leakage), all channels are resampled to 10 kHz, segmented into 1.0 s windows (10 000 samples), and z-score normalised using training-only statistics. Band-pass filters reflect modality characteristics (10–1000 Hz for vibration; 50 Hz ± harmonics for current). SSL-only augmentations include Gaussian noise injection, time-warping, amplitude jitter (≤ 1%), and frequency masking (≤ 5% bandwidth) to encourage invariance to realistic disturbances. STFT spectrograms and wavelet-packet decompositions are computed as supplementary views to probe the benefit of time–frequency pretraining (Fig. 1 and Table 1).

Self-supervised pretext tasks

Two complementary objectives are designed to reflect the mixed periodic–stochastic structure of IM signals.

Masked signal reconstruction (MSR)

Masked signal reconstruction (MSR)

Random temporal spans (15% of raw-time tokens) or time–frequency patches (15% of STFT tiles) are masked and the model reconstructs the missing content from surrounding context. This objective encourages long-range temporal coherence and acts as a learned denoiser—a property directly relevant to low-SNR operating conditions25. The reconstruction loss is mean squared error over masked positions. Crucially, because MSR forces the encoder to interpolate across masked spans using periodic waveform structure, it implicitly learns to represent fault harmonics and sidebands as coherent, predictable patterns—precisely the cues that supervised classifiers need but cannot easily learn from sparse labelled data.

(b)Contrastive temporal learning (CTL)

Contrastive temporal learning (CTL)

Positive pairs are constructed from temporally adjacent windows of the same machine and run (stride 0.5 s), on the reasoning that mechanical state changes slowly relative to the window length. Negative pairs are drawn from non-overlapping periods or different machines. An InfoNCE contrastive loss with temperature τ = 0.2 maximises agreement for positives while pushing apart negatives23,24. This yields invariance to short-term amplitude fluctuations, band-limited noise, and minor load jitter—the nuisance variations that cause supervised models to overfit to site-specific artefacts. Where MSR promotes local spectral coherence, CTL promotes global temporal consistency; the two objectives are therefore genuinely complementary, as the ablation in Section “Ablation: pretext tasks and model components” confirms.

Second, a quality-aware update cap is applied: clients whose round-average SNR falls below a configurable threshold (set to 5 dB in the experiments) have their update weight dampened to prevent low-quality signals from dominating early aggregation rounds. This is a practical consideration that arises directly from the multi-site industrial setting and is absent from standard FedAvg or FedProx formulations.

A non-trivial complication in distributed industrial deployments is that operating conditions vary not only across sites but also within sites over time—motors cycle through different load points, start-up and run-down phases, and scheduled maintenance states within a single working day. This within-site non-stationarity affects the client data distribution in ways that standard FL analyses, which typically assume a fixed local distribution, do not capture. In the present framework, three mechanisms address this. The SSL pretraining augmentation pipeline—specifically time-warping and amplitude jitter—is designed to produce embeddings invariant to the short-timescale amplitude and phase fluctuations associated with load cycling. The 200 FL communication rounds with partial participation (3 out of 5 clients per round) ensure that the global model is exposed to heterogeneous operating-condition snapshots across rounds rather than converging on any single site’s instantaneous distribution. Finally, the partial unfreezing strategy (last Transformer block plus classification head) allows the fine-tuned model to adapt its top-level representations to site-specific operating-condition patterns while retaining the low-level invariances established during SSL. Fine-tuning and classifier

The SSL encoder is partially unfrozen (last Transformer block plus head) and coupled to a lightweight classifier (global average pooling → two FC layers with dropout → softmax). Class imbalance is addressed with per-class inverse-frequency weights; a focal-loss variant is tested in the ablation. Partial unfreezing adapts to site-specific sensor characteristics while retaining the global invariances established during SSL pretraining.

Evaluation protocol

We assess: (i) in-distribution accuracy (Accuracy, F1, MCC, AUC) with fault-severity breakdown (incipient, developing, severe); (ii) noise robustness via additive noise sweeps at 0–15 dB SNR; (iii) cross-domain generalisation via leave-one-site-out; (iv) non-IID robustness via Dirichlet(α) client partition simulation; and (v) federated efficiency as accuracy versus uplink bytes per round. All splits are strictly chronological; hyperparameters are tuned on validation only.

Experimental setup

Datasets

Four corpora are used. The CWRU Bearing Dataset includes inner/outer race and ball defects seeded at three severity levels (0.007, 0.014, 0.021 inch diameter) under multiple loads, with both current and vibration. The Paderborn University (PU) dataset contains healthy and faulty drives (bearing, rotor, stator) with high-rate current and vibration across diverse machine units. The IMS/NASA dataset provides run-to-failure vibration series capturing natural degradation. An industrial 3.7 kW pump–IM testbed provides controlled misalignment, looseness, and eccentricity logged via PXI-based acquisition. Fault severity is labelled as incipient (early), developing (intermediate), or severe (late-stage) based on either seeded defect size (CWRU), run fraction (IMS), or measured vibration amplitude (industrial testbed).

Preprocessing and augmentations

Signals are resampled to 10 kHz after splitting (to prevent leakage), segmented into 1.0 s windows, and per-channel z-score normalised using training-only statistics. STFT (Hann, 1024-point FFT, 50% hop) and wavelet-packet maps (db8, level 4) are computed as supplementary views. SSL augmentations (Gaussian noise, time-warp, amplitude jitter ≤ 1%, frequency masking ≤ 5% bandwidth) are applied only during pretraining. Leakage-prevention rules follow Table 3.

SSL pretraining details

Hybrid 1-D CNN + Transformer encoder (embedding dim 128; 4 encoder blocks; 8 attention heads). Pretext tasks: MSR (15% mask ratio) and CTL (temperature τ = 0.2, positive stride 0.5 s). Optimizer AdamW (lr = 1 × 10⁻4, weight-decay 0.01), batch 64, cosine decay, early stopping on unlabelled validation. More than 500 h of unlabelled operation used; raw signals remain on-premises.

Federated training protocol

Five clients: CWRU, PU, and three industrial-testbed partitions. 200 communication rounds with partial participation (3/5 clients per round). Local fine-tuning epochs E = 3 per round; FedAvg aggregation with FedProx proximal term μ = 0.01. Update clipping C = 1.0; Gaussian DP calibrated to ε = 2.0, δ = 10⁻5; secure aggregation hides individual updates. Implementation uses Flower; clients run on V100-class GPUs.

Real industrial deployments rarely satisfy the IID assumption: different plants run different fault prevalences, motor sizes, and load cycles. To quantify resilience to statistical heterogeneity, we simulate non-IID client distributions using the Dirichlet partition model with concentration parameters α ∈ {1.0 (near-IID), 0.5 (moderate), 0.1 (severe non-IID)}. At α = 0.1, individual clients may hold fewer than 5% of instances for some fault classes, closely replicating the situation in which one plant has predominantly healthy data while another has a high bearing-fault rate. Table 9 in Section “Results and discussion” reports accuracy and F1 under each setting alongside the IID baseline. As expected, performance declines under severe non-IID, but the SSL–FL gap over the supervised federated baseline widens, suggesting that the noise-tolerant SSL embeddings provide a stabilising effect when client data are most heterogeneous.

Results and discussion

Overall performance

Across all datasets and chronological splits, SSL–FL achieves 94.2% overall accuracy, 0.92 macro-F1, and 0.90 MCC, exceeding the strongest centralised baseline (Transformer without SSL/FL: 89.1%/0.86/0.84). Gains concentrate on incipient faults and mixed-load regimes where narrowband sidebands blur and purely supervised models overfit to site-specific nuisances. Misclassifications for SSL–FL are dominated by early bearing wear versus mild eccentricity—classes with overlapping harmonic and envelope cues at 1.0 s windows—whereas baselines frequently confuse rotor-bar defects with healthy high-load runs. The full severity breakdown is presented in Section “Fault severity analysis”.

Noise robustness

Under additive noise sweeps from 15 dB down to 0 dB SNR, SSL–FL’s macro-F1 degrades by only ~ 4 pp, while FFT-MCSA and SVM lose ~ 35 pp and ~ 22 pp respectively; deep baselines drop 9–12 pp. The SSL–FL line remains comparatively flat, reflecting mask-based denoising (MSR) and contrastive invariances (CTL). This matches field intuition: sideband pickers collapse when peaks smear; representation learning remains stable when trained to predict and contrast through realistic perturbations (Fig. 3).

Robustness under noise.

Cross-domain generalisation

Leave-one-site-out transfer yields 83.5% accuracy versus 74.6% for the Transformer baseline. FedAvg with proximal regularisation curbs client drift under non-IID data, while partial unfreezing permits light site adaptation. This narrows the cross-site gap without centralising raw streams—a meaningful result given the confidentiality constraints typical of multi-plant deployments.

Privacy, communication efficiency, and operating conditions

With secure aggregation and DP (ε = 2.0, δ = 10⁻5), performance remains within ~ 0.7 pp of the non-DP version, while the system reaches ~ 91% of centralised accuracy using ~ 58% of uplink bandwidth—an attractive trade-off for multi-plant deployments. The impact of operating condition changes in distributed scenarios deserves explicit discussion. In practice, different rounds of FL aggregation capture different operating-condition snapshots: motors at Site A may be running under full load during one aggregation window and at half load during the next, while Site B may be undergoing a maintenance cycle. The partial-participation scheduling (3/5 clients per round) was specifically chosen to prevent any single operating regime from dominating the global model. Cross-round diversity in client participation, combined with the operating-condition-invariant SSL embeddings, means the model is continuously exposed to variation in load state during training—not merely as a nuisance to be suppressed, but as a regulariser that promotes generalisation. Table 7 (non-IID results) further shows that performance is maintained even when the Dirichlet concentration parameter drops to α = 0.1, which, in practice, emulates a scenario where one client is operating exclusively under peak-load conditions while others see only partial-load or transient data.

The cited federated framework of Zhang et al. handles inconsistent machine groups by combining local balance recovery with global collaborative adaptation—an approach that complements the present work’s use of proximal regularisation and SSL embeddings. A direct integration of their rebalancing strategy with our SSL pretraining stage is a natural direction for future work, particularly for deployments where some client sites have severe class imbalance.

Ablation: pretext tasks and model components

Table 5 extends the ablation presented in the original submission to include per-pretext-task rows (MSR alone and CTL alone) and individual component contributions. Several observations are noteworthy. First, MSR and CTL individually each contribute positive but distinct gains: MSR alone adds approximately 2.1 pp over the supervised baseline, while CTL alone adds approximately 1.8 pp; combined, they add 3.9 pp, confirming that the two objectives are indeed complementary rather than redundant. The MSR gain is concentrated in low-SNR conditions (where its denoising effect is most active), while the CTL gain is more uniform across SNR levels but larger in cross-site transfer (where its invariance to operating-condition shift is most relevant). This decomposition is consistent with the mechanistic argument made in Section “Self-supervised pretext tasks”: reconstruction promotes local spectral coherence, contrast promotes global temporal consistency, and the two together produce embeddings that are robust along both dimensions simultaneously. Second, adding FL to SSL further improves cross-site accuracy by approximately 2 pp, independent of the pretext combination, confirming that representation learning and cross-site collaboration are mutually reinforcing. Third, applying DP incurs a cost of only 0.6 pp, which is within the noise of the five-seed standard deviation, indicating that the privacy budget is not the binding constraint on model performance in this setting.

Fault severity analysis

A recurring concern in industrial fault diagnosis is that published accuracy figures can obscure the performance distribution across fault severity levels. A system that achieves 94% overall by correctly classifying obvious severe faults while missing early incipient faults provides little practical maintenance value. Table 5 breaks down SSL–FL performance across three severity levels—incipient (early-stage, low-amplitude fault signature), developing (clearly progressed, moderate amplitude), and severe (late-stage, dominant signature)—and compares against the strongest supervised baseline (Transformer) (Fig. 4).

Leave-one-site-out transfer.

The results reveal the expected severity gradient: incipient detection is the hardest task for both approaches. SSL–FL achieves 92.4% accuracy at incipient severity versus 83.7% for the supervised Transformer—an 8.7 pp advantage that is larger than the 5.1 pp overall gap. This disproportionate benefit at the incipient level is consistent with the denoising effect of MSR pretraining: early faults produce low-amplitude sidebands that are easily masked by noise, and the reconstruction objective explicitly trains the encoder to recover structure from partially corrupted observations. At severe severity, both models perform well (SSL–FL: 98.1%; Transformer: 94.8%), and the gap narrows, reflecting that dominant fault signatures are detectable even without SSL enrichment. The bearing and eccentricity fault classes show the largest incipient-stage gap, consistent with the known difficulty of distinguishing early bearing wear from healthy high-load current at short window lengths (Table 6).

Single-modality versus multimodal fusion

Reviewer 1 asked for explicit verification of the performance difference between single-modality and dual-modality operation. Table 7 reports accuracy and F1 for three configurations: current signal only, vibration signal only, and the combined current + vibration fusion used throughout the main experiments. The comparison is conducted under identical SSL pretraining and FL fine-tuning conditions, with the only difference being which input channels are available.

Current-only SSL–FL achieves 89.7% accuracy, reflecting the strength of MCSA-derived sideband features for rotor-bar and eccentricity faults but its weaker response to early bearing defects whose primary signature is mechanical rather than electrical. Vibration-only SSL–FL achieves 91.3%, outperforming current-only for bearing and looseness faults but falling behind for rotor-bar defects where current sidebands are more discriminative. The dual-modality fusion achieves 94.2%, a 4.5 pp improvement over the best single-modality result—a gap that is statistically significant (paired t-test p < 0.01 across five seeds) and consistent across all four datasets. The benefit of fusion is most pronounced in the incipient regime (5.3 pp advantage over the best single modality at incipient severity), confirming the complementarity argument made in Section “Signal acquisition and preprocessing”: current and vibration encode the same underlying fault state through different physical pathways, and models that exploit both pathways are more robust to the ambiguities that arise when either pathway alone is weak (Tables 8 and 9).

Reproducibility, ethics, and privacy

For reproducibility, deterministic chronological splits (Table 2) are released alongside a locked YAML configuration file containing all preprocessing and training parameters. Each experiment is run with five random seeds; mean ± std and paired t-tests (α = 0.05) are reported throughout. A unified metric script computes accuracy, macro-F1, MCC, AUC, and ΔF1@SNR; hashed best-validation checkpoints are archived. Training uses PyTorch on V100-class GPUs with Flower for FL orchestration.

On privacy, federated aggregation (FedAvg/FedProx), secure aggregation, and client-side differential privacy (ε = 2.0, δ = 10⁻5 with gradient clipping) protect against inversion and membership inference attacks; all raw signals remain on-premises with pseudonymised logs. Responsible deployment includes recalibration under drift, interpretability via MCSA spectra cross-checks, equity monitoring across sites, and scheduling aggregation around maintenance windows.

Conclusion

We introduced a unified Self-Supervised + Federated Learning (SSL–FL) framework for induction-motor fault diagnosis that targets four chronic barriers to industrial deployment simultaneously: label scarcity, site heterogeneity and non-IID data, operating-condition variability, and data confidentiality. By pairing masked signal reconstruction with temporal contrast pretraining, the encoder learns noise-tolerant, transferable representations from large pools of unlabelled current and vibration data without requiring fault annotations. Federated fine-tuning with FedAvg + proximal regularisation, quality-aware update capping, secure aggregation, and differential privacy enables cross-plant collaboration without sharing raw signals.

The evaluation demonstrates: 94.2% overall accuracy with 92.4% at incipient severity—an 8.7 pp advantage over the supervised baseline at the hardest detection stage; stable performance down to Dirichlet(α = 0.1) non-IID client distributions; 4.5 pp absolute benefit from dual-modality fusion over the best single modality; and clear decomposition of the MSR and CTL contributions in the ablation. The method reaches ~ 91% of centralised accuracy at ~ 58% uplink bandwidth under differential privacy constraints.

Future directions include extension to multi-modal fusion with acoustic and thermal signals, adaptive windows for fast transients, personalised FL that boosts smaller or noisier sites while preserving global robustness, and direct integration with the balance-recovery adaptation strategy of Zhang et al. for scenarios with severe cross-site class imbalance.
