Narrative strategies in wild-mushroom-poisoning prevention videos: focus-group study among Chinese university students
Authors: Yu Chen, Xinjie Zhao, Yanxi Ding, Jing Xu, Si Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWild mushroom poisoning represents a significant global public health challenge, with China experiencing particularly high incidence rates. Despite continuous prevention campaigns by government and health authorities, existing educational videos demonstrate variable effectiveness and lack culturally adapted strategies for diverse audiences. Specifically, evidence on which narrative strategies engage young audiences across high-tradition and low-tradition cultural contexts remains limited.ObjectiveThis qualitative study aimed to identify effective narrative characteristics in wild mushroom poisoning prevention videos and explore how cultural backgrounds influence university students’ reception of prevention messages, thereby providing evidence-based recommendations for targeted educational content development.MethodsWe conducted four focus group discussions at a university in Fujian Province, recruiting 33 university students through purposive sampling stratified by geographic origin and sex, including students from Yunnan Province (n=9) and other provinces (n=24). Six prevention videos representing different narrative approaches were presented for discussion. Data were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis framework. Two coders independently double-coded a 30% subsample (Cohen’s κ=0.81; 95% CI 0.77 to 0.85), and findings were verified through a two-stage member-checking process.ResultsThree major themes emerged from the analysis: (1) authenticity and consequence visualisation as persuasion mechanisms—participants emphasised the impact of real cases and mortality data; (2) cultural traditions as moderators of risk perception—8 of 9 Yunnan participants rejected complete-avoidance messaging and endorsed harm-reduction strategies, whereas all 24 non-Yunnan participants endorsed regulatory and avoidance approaches; (3) digital media expectations and content preferences—participants expressed preferences for short-form, interactive content adapted to social media platforms. Cultural background significantly influenced acceptance of prevention strategies, with Yunnan participants favouring risk reduction over complete avoidance approaches.ConclusionEffective wild mushroom poisoning prevention videos must consider audiences’ cultural contexts, media consumption patterns and cognitive characteristics. We recommend differentiated narrative strategies: emphasising scientific foraging methods and risk identification for regions with strong mushroom traditions while highlighting severe consequences for regions without such traditions. Video production should integrate authentic cases, innovative formats and platform-specific characteristics for optimal engagement.

Introduction

Wild mushroom poisoning constitutes a persistent global public health concern. The WHO recognises mushroom poisoning as a significant cause of foodborne illness, with annual cases numbering in the thousands globally and mortality rates ranging from 1% to 3% in developed countries to >10% in regions with limited healthcare access.1 Europe reports approximately 10 000 cases annually, with notable concentrations in Eastern European countries where mushroom foraging remains culturally embedded.2 North America experiences roughly 5000 reported cases per year, though actual numbers likely exceed official statistics because of under-reporting of mild cases.3 A recent bibliometric analysis indicates that publications on mushroom poisoning have increased markedly since 2010, with a continuing geographic concentration of clinical case series in East Asia.4

China faces particularly acute challenges regarding wild mushroom poisoning, accounting for an estimated 30% of global cases. The country’s vast territory encompasses diverse ecosystems supporting over 900 edible species alongside numerous toxic varieties, creating complex identification challenges for consumers documented across China and South East Asia more broadly.5 6 Yunnan Province, possessing 90% of China’s wild mushroom biodiversity, exemplifies this public health paradox: the region’s rich mycological heritage supports significant economic activity and cultural traditions while generating the nation’s highest poisoning rates.7 In 2024, China reported 599 poisoning incidents involving 1486 patients and resulting in 13 deaths, with a mortality rate of 0.87%.8 Among these, 116 incidents were reported in Sichuan Province, 99 in Yunnan, 99 in Hunan, 45 in Guizhou and 52 in Chongqing.8 This represents a persistent public health burden despite decades of prevention efforts.7

The complexity of mushroom poisoning prevention stems from multiple intersecting factors. Misidentification remains the primary cause, as many toxic species closely resemble edible varieties, with visual differences often subtle and variable depending on growth conditions, maturity and environmental factors.9 Cultural practices further complicate prevention efforts, as mushroom foraging and consumption represent deeply embedded traditions in many societies, carrying social, economic and identity-related significance that transcends simple nutritional considerations. Traditional knowledge systems, while valuable, often incorporate misconceptions about toxicity identification and neutralisation methods, creating false confidence that increases risk exposure.7

Current prevention strategies predominantly rely on educational campaigns utilising various media formats, yet evidence regarding their effectiveness remains limited and contradictory. Traditional approaches emphasising complete avoidance of wild mushrooms encounter resistance in communities where foraging constitutes cultural practice, while technical identification guides often prove too complex for general public comprehension.10 The emergence of digital media platforms presents both opportunities and challenges for health communication, as younger demographics increasingly obtain health information through social media channels characterised by brief, visually oriented content that differs markedly from traditional educational formats.11

University students represent a particularly relevant population for examining wild mushroom poisoning prevention communication. This demographic experiences heightened vulnerability during the transition to independent living, when dietary autonomy increases dramatically while parental supervision diminishes.10 As ‘digital natives’ (a term used here to denote students who have grown up with digital media), university students exemplify contemporary media consumption patterns, predominantly accessing information through smartphones and social platforms rather than traditional channels. Furthermore, Chinese university students’ diverse geographic origins create natural comparison groups for examining cultural influences on health communication reception, as students from mushroom-endemic regions bring different knowledge frameworks and risk perceptions compared with those from areas without foraging traditions.

The theoretical foundation for understanding health communication effectiveness draws from multiple disciplines. Message framing, derived from prospect theory and subsequently applied to health contexts, demonstrates that information presentation significantly influences risk perception and behavioural intentions.12 Narrative persuasion theory suggests that story-based communication can overcome resistance to health messages by reducing counter-arguing and increasing emotional engagement.13 Cultural health capital concepts illuminate how socially transmitted knowledge and practices influence health behaviour adoption, particularly relevant when traditional practices conflict with biomedical recommendations.14 The extended theory of reasoned action further integrates perceived benefits and self-efficacy, revealing predictors of consumers’ willingness toward wild mushroom consumption.7

Given these complexities, this study addresses critical gaps in understanding how wild mushroom poisoning prevention videos achieve or fail to achieve their intended effects among young adults. Specifically, we sought to identify narrative characteristics that enhance message effectiveness, examine how cultural backgrounds shape information reception and risk perception and explore how digital media expectations influence engagement with prevention content. Although Chinese literature has addressed mushroom-poisoning epidemiology and predictors of consumption willingness, no published qualitative study has compared how audiences from high-tradition and low-tradition regions interpret the same set of prevention videos. Through qualitative exploration of university students’ responses to diverse prevention videos, we aimed to generate evidence-based recommendations for developing culturally appropriate, platform-adapted educational materials that resonate with contemporary audiences while maintaining scientific accuracy.

Methods

Study design and theoretical approach

We used a qualitative descriptive design15 within a constructivist–interpretivist paradigm. This qualitative study employed focus group methodology to explore university students’ perceptions and responses to wild mushroom poisoning prevention videos. Focus groups facilitate exploration of collective sense-making processes and social dynamics that influence health behaviour, making them particularly suitable for understanding culturally embedded health practices.16 Our analytical approach drew insights from multiple theoretical perspectives, including framing theory,17 narrative persuasion models18 and cultural adaptation frameworks,19 while maintaining the inductive orientation characteristic of qualitative inquiry.

Research team and reflexivity

The research team comprised specialists in health communication, public health and qualitative methodology. The primary investigator (first author) holds doctoral training in health communication with extensive experience conducting focus groups in Chinese contexts. Two co-investigators contributed expertise in food safety and behavioural psychology. Throughout data collection and analysis, we maintained reflexive practices through regular team debriefings, analytical memos and peer review of emerging interpretations. We acknowledged our positions as urban-based academics potentially distant from rural foraging traditions, consciously examining how these perspectives might influence data interpretation. For example, in an early analytic meeting, one coder had framed Yunnan participants’ continued consumption of hallucinogenic Boletus species as risk-taking despite knowledge; in a peer-debriefing session, the team agreed this framing imported a biomedical-deficit lens onto a practice that participants described in terms of community bonding, and the interpretation was reframed as calculated risk management within a culturally embedded practice.

Ethical considerations

This study received ethical approval from the China National Centre for Food Safety Risk Assessment Ethics Committee (Registration: 2021–028). This committee, affiliated with the National Health Commission of China, served as the institutional review board responsible for the ethical oversight of this study. All participants provided written informed consent after receiving comprehensive information about study purposes, procedures and data handling. We ensured voluntary participation, confidentiality protection and participants’ right to withdraw without consequence. Participants received modest compensation (100 RMB) for their time and transportation costs.

Participant recruitment and sampling

Purposive sampling strategies were employed to ensure diverse perspectives regarding wild mushroom consumption and cultural backgrounds. Recruitment occurred between September and October 2024 at a comprehensive university in Fujian Province, selected for its diverse student population representing multiple Chinese provinces. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) current undergraduate enrollment, (2) age 18–24 years and (3) voluntary participation with signed informed consent. Yunnan-origin students were specifically recruited to enable comparison between high-tradition and low-tradition contexts regarding wild mushroom consumption.

Recruitment utilised multiple channels including academic department announcements, student organisation networks and snowball sampling from initial participants. Sample size determination followed established guidelines for focus group research, with recruitment continuing until meaning saturation was achieved.20 Of the 47 students approached, 38 expressed interest and 33 attended the scheduled focus groups; the 5 non-attendees cited scheduling conflicts (n=3), illness (n=1) or no longer wishing to participate (n=1). We ultimately recruited 33 participants distributed across four focus groups (table 1).

Focus group composition

Groups were stratified by geographic origin and sex to facilitate comfortable discussion dynamics. The strategic grouping aimed to balance within-group homogeneity to encourage open discussion with between-group heterogeneity to capture diverse perspectives. This composition strategy enabled exploration of both cultural and sex influences on message reception while maintaining comfortable discussion environments. Saturation assessment based on Hennink, Kaiser and Marconi’s20 code-saturation criterion indicated that after the third group (male students) discussion, code saturation was achieved (approximately 90% of themes identified, repetition rate>95%). The fourth group (female group B) only reinforced existing meanings without new information emergence (meaning saturation achieved), ensuring the dataset adequately represented cultural (Yunnan vs non-Yunnan) and sex diversity without requiring additional groups to avoid redundancy (diminishing returns). The Yunnan group was of mixed sex for two reasons: the small Yunnan-origin population at this Fujian university (12 eligible students, 9 consented) made within-Yunnan sex stratification logistically infeasible without compromising sample size. Pilot discussions indicated that for participants with shared cultural foraging traditions, group cohesion derived more strongly from regional identity than from sex, whereas for non-Yunnan participants, sex emerged as the more salient axis of variation, justifying sex stratification.

Video selection and characteristics

Six different types of wild mushroom poisoning prevention videos were selected from short video platforms as stimulus materials, representing different content framing strategies currently employed. Selection criteria included recent production (2019–2024), public availability and distinct narrative strategies. Table 2 presents detailed video characteristics. The six final videos were selected from a candidate pool of 27 identified through searches of Yunnan Center for Disease Control and Prevention (YNCDC), provincial market supervision bureau and CDC accounts on Douyin, Bilibili, WeChat Video and YouTube. Two team members (SC and YC) independently classified candidates using Hornik and Yanovitzky’s typology21 of public health communication strategies, and we then purposively selected one exemplar of each strategy plus one international comparator. The 21 candidate videos that were not selected were excluded for the following reasons: duplication of an already-selected narrative strategy (n=12), production date earlier than 2019 (n=5), restricted access or geo-blocking at the time of stimulus preparation (n=3) and duration >5 min, which would have exceeded the focus-group time budget for stimulus screening (n=1).

Data collection procedures

Focus group discussions convened in November 2024 in a comfortable campus meeting room equipped with audio-visual facilities. Each session lasted 90–120 min following a semi-structured protocol developed in consultation with three experts (a senior epidemiologist, a Yunnan-based ethnomycologist and a health-communication scholar) and pilot-tested with four students at the same university who were not included in the analytic sample. The primary investigator facilitated all sessions using probing questions to deepen responses while avoiding leading participants toward predetermined conclusions. A research assistant managed recordings and took observational notes regarding non-verbal behaviours and group dynamics. All sessions were digitally audio-recorded with participant consent.

Data management and preparation

All sessions were digitally audio-recorded with participant consent and professionally transcribed verbatim in Chinese, producing approximately 50 000 Chinese characters of text. Transcription accuracy was verified through random sampling checks by team members. Chinese language was retained for analysis to preserve linguistic nuances, translating only selected quotations for manuscript preparation. Transcripts were anonymised using participant codes (P1–P33) with group identifiers.

Data analysis

Analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s six-phase reflexive thematic-analysis framework,22 23 using NVivo V.12 for data management and analysis. The analytical process comprised (1) data familiarisation through repeated reading of transcripts and viewing session videos, (2) initial code generation identifying meaningful data segments relevant to video effectiveness perceptions, (3) theme searching through grouping related codes into potential themes, (4) theme reviewing to ensure internal coherence and external distinctiveness, (5) theme defining and naming to establish the final thematic framework and (6) report production selecting compelling extract examples.

Two researchers independently coded transcripts to enhance reliability, achieving substantial agreement. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. Member checking with selected participants validated our interpretations. Specifically, two coders independently coded a 30% random subsample after initial code-list stabilisation, and Cohen’s κ was computed across 47 codes per segment, yielding κ=0.81 (95% CI 0.77 to 0.85), corresponding to substantial agreement. Of 1247 coded segments, 138 (11.1%) initially showed disagreement, 121 were resolved through dyadic discussion and 17 required wider-team adjudication, which was logged in an audit-trail spreadsheet. To examine geographic and sex differences analytically, a Yunnan-versus-non-Yunnan comparison matrix was constructed in which each code was tabulated by group and sex. Member checking was conducted in two stages: at stage 1, the first author returned a Chinese-language summary of preliminary themes to all 33 participants by email or WeChat, with 11 responding and confirming alignment. At stage 2, an hour-long video-call session with eight purposively selected participants discussed the integrative interpretation, and two specific points of feedback were incorporated into the results.

Quality assurance

Multiple strategies were employed to ensure methodological rigour. Credibility was enhanced through prolonged engagement with data, peer debriefing sessions and member checking with selected participants. Dependability was addressed through detailed audit trails documenting analytical decisions. Confirmability was supported by reflexive journaling and team discussion of potential biases. Transferability was facilitated through thick description of context and participant characteristics. Reporting follows the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR); the completed SRQR checklist is provided as online supplemental file 2.

Results

Participant characteristics

As shown in table 3, the study recruited 33 university students, with females comprising 72.7% (n=24) and 27.3% from Yunnan Province (n=9). Nearly half of the participants had wild mushroom consumption experience (48.5%), and over one-third had foraging experience (36.4%). The top three media usage and information acquisition channels were short video platforms (97.0%), domestic social media (78.8%) and news websites (45.5%). Notably, artificial intelligence (AI) language models as information acquisition channels ranked fourth alongside traditional television media (30.3%). Table 3 presents comprehensive demographic and behavioural characteristics, revealing marked contrasts between Yunnan and non-Yunnan participants. All Yunnan participants had wild mushroom consumption experience, nearly 90% had foraging experience (88.9%) and 55.6% had consumed hallucinogenic wild mushrooms (‘seeing hand blues’), whereas non-Yunnan participants had no hallucinogenic mushroom consumption experience, only four participants had foraging experience and only seven had consumed wild mushrooms.

The stark contrasts in mushroom-related experiences between groups provided rich comparative material for examining cultural influence on message reception. All Yunnan participants reported family traditions involving wild mushrooms, whereas only approximately one-fifth of non-Yunnan participants shared this background. This experiential divide shaped substantively different interpretive frameworks for processing prevention messages.

Thematic analysis results

Analysis revealed three interconnected major themes characterising participants’ responses to prevention videos and broader prevention strategies.

Three principal themes emerged from the data, each comprising four to five subthemes that are summarised in online supplemental table S1 with anchor quotations and group attribution.

Theme 1: authenticity and consequence visualisation as persuasion mechanisms

Participants consistently emphasised authenticity as the primary determinant of message credibility and impact. Real footage of poisoning victims, grieving families and medical emergencies generated visceral responses that exceeded reactions to stylised or fictional content. Documentary-style presentations incorporating news footage and survivor testimonials were perceived as most compelling, particularly when featuring local cases that participants could verify independently.

The presentation of mortality statistics and clinical consequences served as powerful persuasion elements, though responses varied by cultural background. Non-Yunnan participants expressed shock on learning specific death rates, with many acknowledging previous underestimation of poisoning severity. One participant (P27, non-Yunnan male) reflected, “Seeing ‘40 poisoned, 4 dead’ made this real for me. I thought people just got stomach aches, not that families lost loved ones”. Yunnan participants, while familiar with poisoning risks, reported that explicit visualisation of symptoms and treatment procedures reinforced the gravity of consequences beyond their community-transmitted knowledge. A non-Yunnan female participant (P19) made the same point and explicitly contrasted news clips with stylised content: “It is the real news clips, the actual numbers—‘so-and-so dead’—that frighten me. The cartoon ones I just laugh at; the news ones stay with me”. A Yunnan female participant (P9) connected the prevention video directly to her own past experience: “I had once eaten a hallucinogenic Boletus species (locally known as jianshouqing) with the family elders, drinking together. At the time, I felt dizzy and just thought it was the alcohol—only later did I realise it might have been the mushroom. The video showing exactly that scenario, with someone collapsed and on a respirator, made me think about it differently”.

Authority source credibility emerged as a critical factor influencing message acceptance. Videos featuring recognisable medical experts, government officials or institutional affiliations received greater attention and trust compared with anonymous or entertainment-oriented presentations. Participants noted that official endorsements signalled information importance and accuracy, though some expressed scepticism about government sources’ tendency toward overstatement. The Australian-produced video generated interesting discussions about international perspectives lending objectivity while potentially lacking local relevance.

Visual presentation of poisoning mechanisms through animation or expert explanation helped participants understand toxicology beyond folk beliefs. Several participants mentioned that understanding how toxins affect neurological and organ systems made risks more tangible than simple warnings. However, overly technical presentations risked losing audience attention, suggesting optimal balance between scientific accuracy and accessibility.

Theme 2: cultural traditions as moderators of risk perception and behavioral intentions

Cultural background profoundly shaped how participants interpreted and responded to prevention messages, with distinct patterns emerging between Yunnan and non-Yunnan participants. Yunnan participants demonstrated sophisticated risk awareness coupled with deep cultural investment in mushroom traditions, creating complex negotiation between safety concerns and identity maintenance. Their discussions revealed multi-generational knowledge transmission systems, seasonal rituals around mushroom collection and community-based risk management strategies that prevention videos rarely acknowledged.

One Yunnan participant (P5) articulated this tension: “Telling us never to eat wild mushrooms is like telling Sichuanese never to eat spicy food. It’s woven into who we are. We need practical guidance for safer consumption, not abstinence messages that we’ll simply ignore”. This sentiment echoed throughout Yunnan participants’ discussions, who viewed complete avoidance as culturally impossible and practically unenforceable. They described family mushroom hunting as bonding experiences transcending mere food procurement, encompassing environmental knowledge, intergenerational relationships and regional pride.

Traditional knowledge systems created both protective and risk factors. Yunnan participants possessed extensive taxonomic knowledge enabling accurate identification of many species, yet also harboured misconceptions about toxicity neutralisation methods passed through generations. One participant (P3) described the cognitive dissonance: “My grandmother identifies 50 mushroom species by smell alone and swears garlic prevents poisoning. The videos say she’s wrong, but she’s been doing this for 70 years. How do I reconcile that?” A Yunnan female participant (P7) emphasised the bonding and intergenerational dimensions of foraging beyond consumption: “In our season, they go up the mountain whether they will eat or not—picking is the point. It is the going up, with family, that matters; the eating is downstream”.

Non-Yunnan participants exhibited more straightforward risk avoidance orientations, viewing wild mushrooms as unnecessary dangers easily avoided through commercial alternatives. However, they acknowledged social pressures that might override safety concerns, particularly in professional or romantic contexts where refusing offered foods could cause offence. Urban participants particularly expressed curiosity about wild mushroom consumption as exotic culinary experiences, potentially increasing vulnerability during travel or social dining situations.

The groups diverged markedly in their responses to prevention strategies. Yunnan participants favoured harm reduction approaches acknowledging continued consumption while minimising risks through education about identification, preparation and early symptom recognition. Non-Yunnan participants supported stricter regulatory approaches including market controls, restaurant restrictions and public warnings, viewing these as appropriate protections for uninformed consumers. Within these contrasting positions, a Yunnan female participant (P22) made the structural argument: “Set up regulated points and times for selling wild mushrooms. People go up the mountain to forage partly because they don’t know where else to buy. Block that gap—combine restraint with a legitimate route”. A non-Yunnan female participant (P25) was more explicit about the regulatory case: “Strengthen market inspection so that the toxic ones don’t make it to the stalls in the first place. Most consumers can’t tell—and shouldn’t have to”.

Theme 3: digital media expectations and content preferences shape engagement patterns

Participants’ media consumption habits profoundly influenced their reception of prevention videos, with consistent preferences emerging for brief, visually engaging content optimised for mobile viewing. Current attention economics, shaped by platforms like TikTok and BiliBili (China’s equivalent to YouTube), created expectations for immediate engagement, narrative efficiency and production quality that most prevention videos failed to meet. Participants reported typically scrolling past videos exceeding 60 s unless immediately captivated, suggesting traditional public health videos’ length and pacing were misaligned with contemporary viewing behaviours.

Production quality emerged as a credibility signal, with participants interpreting poor audio, outdated graphics or amateur editing as indicators of information irrelevance. Several participants noted that government-produced videos’ aesthetic lag behind commercial content created perception gaps where important information appeared less valuable than entertainment content. One participant (P22, non-Yunnan female) observed: “If they can’t invest in decent production, why should I invest my attention? Quality signals whether they seriously want to reach young people”.

Participants expressed strong preferences for interactive and participatory elements enabling active engagement rather than passive consumption. Suggestions included augmented reality applications for real-time mushroom identification, gamified learning modules testing knowledge retention and social media challenges encouraging prevention behaviour sharing. The static, didactic nature of most prevention videos contradicted participants’ expectations for two-way communication and personalised experiences characteristic of digital natives’ media interactions. A non-Yunnan female participant (P15) suggested gamification: “Make it like a small game—H5, click, level up, that kind of immersion. Then, we are not only receiving; we are doing”.

Platform-specific adaptations emerged as crucial considerations. Participants described different expectations across platforms: TikTok demanded ultra-brief, visually striking content; Bilibili supported longer-form educational content with entertaining elements; WeChat (China’s dominant social media platform) required shareability and social relevance. Videos attempting platform-neutral approaches failed to optimise for any specific context, reducing overall effectiveness. Participants from non-Yunnan provinces emphasised that prevention content must feel proximate to be impactful; one participant (P24) was explicit: “Each region should have a different message. Showing us a Yunnan-made video here in Fujian feels distant. Use our own places, our own cases, and we feel the danger is close”. Yunnan participants suggested local adaptation including dialect versions for older relatives; P4 noted: “The people we most need to reach—older relatives in mushroom-foraging villages—listen to dialect on their phones, not standard Mandarin”.

Influencer collaboration represented a consistently mentioned strategy for enhancing reach and credibility. Participants suggested partnering with food bloggers, travel vloggers and regional culture ambassadors who possessed established audiences and authentic voices. They contrasted organic influencer content integration with obviously scripted government messaging, noting that authenticity perceptions strongly influenced message acceptance. A non-Yunnan male participant (P27) recommended: “Partner with the food-content creators on Bilibili—they already have the audience, the format, the trust. Government-only voices feel staged”

The entertainment–education balance proved particularly contentious. While some participants appreciated creative approaches like song parodies, others found forced entertainment elements patronising or distracting from serious health information. Age-inappropriate content, such as cartoon animations for adult audiences, generated particular criticism for underestimating viewer sophistication.

Discussion

This qualitative investigation revealed complex dynamics underlying university students’ reception of wild mushroom poisoning prevention videos, illuminating how authenticity, cultural context and media expectations intersect to influence message effectiveness. Our findings extend current understanding of health communication in culturally embedded risk contexts while highlighting critical considerations for developing prevention materials suited to contemporary digital media environments.

The paramount importance of authenticity in driving message impact aligns with extensive literature on narrative persuasion in health contexts. Green and Brock’s transportation theory suggests that immersion in realistic narratives reduces critical processing and counter-arguing, facilitating attitude change through emotional engagement rather than analytical evaluation. Our participants’ strong responses to documentary footage and survivor testimonials exemplify this mechanism, where authentic human experiences transcended statistical abstractions to create memorable, emotionally resonant impressions.18 This finding resonates with the meta-analysis by Shen et al., demonstrating narrative formats’ superiority over statistical evidence for health behaviour influence, particularly when narratives feature culturally similar protagonists experiencing realistic consequences.13

However, our results complicate straightforward applications of fear appeal theories to mushroom poisoning prevention. While fear appeals can motivate protective behaviours under specific conditions, their effectiveness depends critically on audience characteristics, message design and behavioural feasibility.24 Yunnan participants’ responses suggested that high fear messages without actionable alternatives might trigger defensive reactions or message rejection when complete risk avoidance conflicts with cultural practices. This parallels findings from HIV prevention research in sub-Saharan Africa, where abstinence-only messages proved less effective than harm reduction approaches acknowledging continued risk behaviours within cultural contexts.25

The profound influence of cultural background on message reception extends Shim’s cultural health capital framework to food safety contexts. Yunnan participants’ mushroom-related knowledge constituted valuable cultural capital within their communities, creating identity investments that pure biomedical messaging threatened. Their preference for risk reduction over risk elimination strategies mirrors patterns observed in other culturally embedded health behaviours, from alcohol consumption in European wine regions to coca leaf use in Andean communities, where complete cessation messages encounter resistance while harm minimisation approaches achieve greater acceptance.26 27

The tension between traditional ecological knowledge and biomedical recommendations raises important questions about knowledge hierarchy in health communication. While scientific accuracy remains paramount for preventing poisoning, dismissing traditional knowledge systems risks alienating communities whose cooperation is essential for prevention success. Our findings suggest that effective messaging should acknowledge traditional knowledge’s value while respectfully correcting specific misconceptions, an approach successfully employed in integrating traditional and biomedical healing systems in various global health contexts.28

Digital media expectations challenge traditional health communication paradigms premised on captive audiences consuming complete messages. The attention economy governing social media platforms rewards brevity, visual impact and entertainment value over comprehensive information delivery.29 Our participants’ preferences for sub-60-second content reflect broader transformations in information processing shaped by continuous partial attention and rapid content switching characteristic of mobile media consumption.30 This presents ethical dilemmas for health communicators balancing engagement with accuracy, as simplification risks losing crucial nuance while comprehensive presentations risk losing audiences entirely.

The platform-specific expectations identified by participants underscore media ecology’s importance in shaping message reception. Each platform’s affordances, algorithms and cultural norms create distinct communication contexts requiring tailored approaches rather than simple content republication.31 TikTok’s algorithmic prioritisation of watch-time completion rewards front-loaded engagement, while Bilibili’s commentary culture encourages longer-form content enabling community discussion. WeChat’s social sharing dynamics privilege content that enhances sharer social capital through utility or entertainment value. These platform logics fundamentally shape what content succeeds, demanding health communicators develop platform literacy alongside subject expertise. Practically, our findings imply that a platform-optimised Douyin prevention video should target a duration of approximately 45 s, open with documentary-style footage in the first 3 s, present a verified mortality figure between 5 and 8 s, feature one verifiable local case and close with a clear call to action linking to a poison-information hotline.

Our findings’ implications extend beyond mushroom poisoning to broader food safety communication challenges. Food risks embedded in cultural practices, from raw milk consumption to fermented foods, require culturally sensitive approaches acknowledging tradition while promoting safety. The differentiated strategies suggested by our analysis—emphasising risk reduction for high-tradition populations versus risk avoidance for low-tradition groups—provide frameworks applicable across diverse food safety contexts. Moreover, digital media transformation necessitates fundamental reconsideration of health communication strategies developed for broadcast media environments, demanding new competencies in platform dynamics, influencer partnerships and interactive content creation.

Several limitations warrant consideration when interpreting our findings. The exclusive focus on university students, while providing insights into educated young adults’ perspectives, cannot represent broader population perspectives, particularly rural communities where mushroom foraging remains economically important. Focus group methodology, despite facilitating rich discussions, may suppress minority viewpoints through conformity pressures. The cross-sectional design prevents assessment of whether expressed intentions translate to actual behaviour change. Additionally, the specific videos selected, while representative of common approaches, cannot encompass all possible narrative strategies. The sample also skews female (72.7%), which limits transferability to male audiences and rural foraging populations among whom poisoning incidence is highest. Compared with ethnographic fieldwork in foraging communities, focus groups capture stated rather than enacted practice; future work pairing focus groups with ethnographic fieldwork in Yunnan rural foraging communities would substantially extend transferability. Social desirability bias is plausible because our topic is officially sanctioned safety messaging, and we use the term ‘meaning saturation’ loosely without claiming formal theory development in the Glaser–Strauss sense.

Future research should explore several directions emerging from our findings. Experimental designs testing specific message elements’ causal effects would complement our qualitative insights with quantitative evidence about relative effectiveness. Longitudinal studies tracking actual behaviour change following exposure to different message types would validate whether expressed preferences predict real-world outcomes. Expanding participant diversity to include rural residents, older adults and children would illuminate age and urbanisation effects on message reception. Development and evaluation of platform-optimised interventions incorporating our recommendations would provide practical evidence about implementation feasibility and effectiveness. Finally, exploring technology-enhanced prevention tools like AI-powered identification apps or virtual reality–based education modules could pioneer next-generation approaches suited to digital natives’ expectations.

Conclusions

This qualitative investigation illuminated the complex interplay between authenticity, cultural context and digital media expectations in shaping university students’ reception of wild mushroom poisoning prevention videos. Our findings revealed that effective prevention communication requires a sophisticated balance between scientific accuracy and cultural sensitivity, comprehensive information and platform-optimised brevity, serious health messaging and engaging presentation formats.

The research contributes several key insights to health communication scholarship and practice. First, authenticity through real cases and credible sources emerged as fundamental to message impact, suggesting that prevention campaigns should prioritise documentary evidence over fictional narratives. Second, cultural background profoundly influences risk perception and strategy acceptance, necessitating differentiated approaches that respect traditional knowledge while correcting dangerous misconceptions. Third, digital media transformation demands fundamental reconsideration of health communication strategies, embracing platform-specific optimisation, influencer partnerships and interactive technologies that align with contemporary consumption patterns.

For practitioners developing mushroom poisoning prevention materials, we recommend adopting culturally differentiated strategies that acknowledge audience diversity. High-tradition regions require harm reduction approaches emphasising safer practices within continued consumption contexts, whereas low-tradition regions benefit from clear risk avoidance messages emphasising danger severity. All materials should integrate authentic cases with appropriate emotional resonance, optimise length and style for intended platforms and leverage influencer partnerships to extend reach and credibility. Investment in production quality signals message importance and institutional commitment, while interactive elements transform passive recipients into engaged participants. Where AR or AI-based identification tools are considered, they should be evaluated against rigorous performance benchmarks specific to regional flora, display calibrated uncertainty estimates rather than a binary ‘safe/unsafe’ verdict and be coupled with conservative defaults to mitigate the risk of misclassification of visually similar Amanita and Galerina species.

As China advances toward Health China 2030 objectives, addressing preventable poisonings through effective communication remains crucial. Our findings suggest that success requires moving beyond traditional didactic approaches toward culturally adapted, digitally native strategies that meet audiences within their existing media ecosystems. This transformation demands new competencies among health communicators, institutional support for innovative approaches and continuous adaptation to rapidly evolving media landscapes.

The insights generated through this research extend beyond mushroom poisoning to inform broader food safety and health communication efforts. As global food systems become increasingly complex and cultural food practices intersect with public health imperatives, understanding how different populations receive and respond to risk messages becomes ever more critical. By illuminating these dynamics within the specific context of wild mushroom poisoning prevention, this study contributes to building more effective, equitable and culturally responsive health communication practices suited to our interconnected yet diverse world.
