The intersection of cervicovaginal microbiota and cervical length in predicting adverse perinatal outcomes at preterm birth risk
Authors: Gulcan Okutucu, Atakan Tanacan, Nazli Bayraktar, Burcu Bozkurt Ozdal, Eda Busra Babayigit, Bedia Dinc, Ozgur Kara, Dilek Sahin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo evaluate the cervicovaginal (CV) microbiota in pregnant women with conditions increasing the risk of preterm labor and to assess the relationship between pathogenic microorganism growth in CV cultures and composite adverse perinatal outcomes (CAPO).MethodsA total of 665 CV culture results from pregnant women hospitalized for threatened preterm labor (TPL), preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM), or cervical insufficiency (CIS) without other obstetric complications were included. Culture results were divided into three groups according to the dominant microorganism type: normal (Lactobacillus spp. dominated), candidal, and bacterial cervicovaginal flora (CVF). CAPO were defined as the presence of perinatal mortality alone or the occurrence of at least two of the following neonatal complications: neonatal sepsis, transient tachypnea of the newborn, respiratory distress syndrome, intracranial or intraventricular hemorrhage, and umbilical cord arterial pH < 7.1.ResultsOf the cultures, 431 (64.8%) were normal, and 234 (35.2%) were non-normal CVFs. Indications for hospitalization were TPL (n = 387, 58.2%), CIS (n = 172, 25.9%), and PPROM (n = 106, 15.9%). Bacterial CVFs had the highest rate of preterm birth (p = 0.024) and CAPO (52.5%, p = 0.021), as well as the lowest birth weight (p = 0.011). The optimal cut-off value for cervical length (CxL) to predict CAPO in the presence of non-normal CVFs was determined 28.5 mm, with a sensitivity of 70.7% and specificity of 71.1% (AUC: 0.763, p < 0.001).ConclusionsMonitoring CxL and determining bacterial predominance in CVF in women at risk of preterm delivery may help predict adverse perinatal outcomes and provide insight for better obstetric management and intervention.Keywords: CAPO, Cervicovaginal flora, Culture, Microbiota, Preterm birth

Introduction

Preterm delivery, which refers to births occurring before 37 weeks of gestation [1], is considered to have an incidence of approximately 10% in all live births worldwide [2]. In our country, the rate of premature births is higher and is around 12.9% [3]. Preterm birth is also the leading cause of neonatal mortality and is associated with early multiorgan system diseases and many long-term neurodevelopmental, familial and socioeconomic problems [2]. Most preterm deliveries occur spontaneously, either because of threatened preterm labor (TPL), cervical insufficiency (CIS) or premature rupture of membranes (PPROM). Less commonly, they may be performed as induction of preterm labor or cesarean delivery due to obstetric indications (e.g. placental or maternal-fetal complications) [1, 4].

The term “microbiota” refers to a community of microorganisms that can be classified as commensal, mutualistic, or pathogenic in various anatomical regions and is intimately associated with human health [5, 6]. The presence of Lactobacillus-dominated vaginal microbiota is often considered an indicator of optimal health within the female reproductive system [7, 8]. However, it should be noted that women may also possess a diverse array of vaginal microbiota. A notable risk factor for preterm birth is ascending pathogenic microbial processes such as genitourinary tract and sexually transmitted infections and bacterial vaginosis (BV), in which healthy cervicovaginal (CV) flora is disrupted [8]. Some studies suggest that 40–50% of preterm births occur when pathogenic microorganisms move ascending from the vagina and infect the placenta, fetal membranes, cervix or uterine cavity [1, 8–10].

The aim of this study was to investigate whether the CV microbiota during pregnancy differs between the main obstetric conditions that can cause prematurity, such as TPL, PPROM or CIS, and to evaluate the association between the presence of significant CV pathogenic microorganisms in culture accompanying these obstetric pathologies and compound adverse perinatal outcomes (CAPO).

Materials and methods

Design and study population

A retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted in the Perinatology Clinic of Ankara Bilkent City Hospital between 2020 and 2024. Institutional review board approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Republic of Turkey Ministry of Health Ankara City Hospital (TABED 1-24-546). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

A total of 1006 CV culture results obtained from women who applied to perinatology outpatient clinics within the date range determined for the study were retrospectively screened from the hospital database. Among these data, cases with CV cultures taken for gynecologic reasons, maternal comorbidities (chronic hypertension, pregestational diabetes, malignancies, rheumatologic diseases, etc.), multiple pregnancies, active maternal viral or bacterial infections, or additional obstetric complications that could cause preterm delivery (preeclampsia, fetal growth restriction, moderate or severe polyhydramnios, uterine anomalies or uterine fibroids with intracavitary components at least 5 cm in size, etc.) were excluded from the study. The remaining 665 CV culture results sampled during hospitalization from pregnant women admitted to perinatology outpatient clinics with diagnoses of TPL, PPROM or CIS (history, examination or ultrasound based) without additional obstetric complications were included in the study. (Fig. 1). All CV fluid samples were collected from the posterior vaginal fornix using sterile cotton wool without prior vaginal examination, at least 12 h after the patient had used local antifungal or flora-regulating agents, and before the initiation of clinical treatments such as maternal antibiotics, antenatal steroids or vaginal progesterone. The exclusion of urinary tract infection was confirmed by the absence of microbial growth in urine culture analysis taken at the same time as the CV culture. None of the pregnant women included in the study underwent cerclage or pessary insertion due to CIS before CV sampling. Cervicovaginal fluid samples admitted to the Medical Microbiology and Bacteriology laboratory of our hospital were firstly subjected to gram-stained microscopic examination. The presence of budding yeast and pseudo hyphae structures, clue cells and polymorphonuclear leukocytes (PNL) were examined microscopically. The results were evaluated using the Nugent scoring system [11] for BV. Lactobacillus-dominated and low Nugent score samples were reported as normal CVF [7, 8]. After microscopic examination, the samples were inoculated on 5% sheep blood agar, chocolate agar and sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA). The samples were incubated for 48 h. After incubation, the plates were evaluated for N. gonorrhoeae, H. ducreyi, S. agalactiae, S. pyogenes, L. monocytogenes, C. albicans and other agents. The colonies thought to be causative agents were identified by VITEK-MS (Biomeriux, France) and antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed by VITEK-2 (Biomeriux, France) after identification. In case of growth on SDA medium, yeasts were identified microscopically and reported. The results of CV cultures performed during the patients’ initial clinical visit were evaluated. Following culture results reported as mixed microbial flora due to contamination caused by sampling errors, repeated culture analyses identifying flora organisms or pathogenic agents were used as the basis for evaluation. CV culture results were divided into three groups as bacterial, candidal and normal cervicovaginal flora (CVF) according to the dominant microorganism type.

Flowchart of the study

Gestational week (GW) was confirmed to be consistent with last menstrual period (LMP) by ultrasonographic crown-rump length measurement. Pregnant women included in the study were admitted to the hospital for the first time at the earliest 8th week of gestation according to the LMP. The GWs for CV culture samplings were grouped as first trimester (8–14 weeks), second trimester (14–28 weeks), and third trimester (28–36 weeks). Clinicodemographic characteristics, indications for hospitalization, GW and cervical length (CxL) (mm) at the time of CV culture sampling, CVF characteristics, pregnancy loss (miscarriage) rate and perinatal outcomes of the cases (birth week and weight, preterm birth rate, Apgar scores at 1–5 min, umbilical cord arterial pH, CAPO and neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) hospitalization (day) were recorded. A miscarriage was defined as loss of pregnancy before 20 weeks of gestation or when the estimated fetal weight was less than 500 g [12]. Perinatal mortality was defined as fetal death after 20 weeks of gestation and within the first seven days of the newborn’s life [13]. Deliveries before 37 weeks of gestation were considered preterm birth [1]. CAPO were defined as the presence of perinatal mortality alone or the occurrence of at least two of the following neonatal complications requiring NICU admission: neonatal sepsis, transient tachypnea of the newborn (TTN), respiratory distress syndrome (RDS), intracranial/intraventricular hemorrhage (ICH/IVH), and umbilical cord arterial pH < 7.1 [14].

Pregnant women were divided into three groups according to the indications for hospitalization: TPL, PPROM and CIS. Before 37 weeks of gestation, progression of cervical dilatation and/or maturation caused by regular uterine contractions was defined as TPL [15] and rupture of fetal membranes was defined as PPROM [16]. Cervical insufficiency was defined as the inability of the cervix, due to a functional or structural pathology, to prevent the loss of gestational material or preterm delivery in the absence of uterine contraction [17]. The presence of a history of second trimester pregnancy loss or preterm delivery was defined as history-based CIS, the presence of vaginal or speculum examination findings indicating painless cervical dilatation between 16 and 23 weeks of gestation was defined as examination-based CIS, and the presence of a shortened cervix (CxL < 25 mm) on ultrasonographic evaluation before 24 weeks of gestation was defined as ultrasound (US)-based CIS [17]. The distribution of pregnant women according to CVF characteristics and the indication groups defined above that pose a risk of preterm birth were examined.

Statistical analysis

Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS version 26.0; Chicago, IL, USA) was utilized for data analysis. Median (interquartile range [IQR]) or mean±standard deviation represented continuous variables, while counts (percentages) measured categorical variables. The study assessed the normal distribution of variables through the Kolmogorov– Smirnov test. Two groups’ normally distributed continuous variables were compared using the independent t test, while the Mann–Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed variables. When comparisons were made in more than two groups, one-way ANOVA test was applied if the data fit the normal distribution, and Kruskal-Wallis test was applied if not. Pearson chi-square or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical variables. A bar chart of the distribution of pregnant women according to CVF characteristics and indications for hospitalization was created. The relationship between the CxL at the time of culture sampling and the development of CAPO in pregnancies with non-normal CVF was evaluated using Pearson correlation analysis. Pearson correlation coefficient (r) was calculated. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to estimate optimal cut-off values, maximizing sensitivity and specificity according to the Youden index. The ROC analysis was used to calculate the cut-off value of CxL measured at the time of culture sampling in non-normal CVFs to predict CAPO. A p-value of less than 0.05 was deemed significant across all analyses.

Results

When the results of 665 CV cultures included in the study were analyzed, 431 (64.8%) of them had a normal CVF with a predominance of lactobacilli species, and the remaining 234 (35.2%) had a CVF dominated by pathogenic microorganism species. Candida species were detected in most pregnant women with pathogenic microorganism-dominant CVF (194/234, 82.9%). The most frequently detected candida species were Candida albicans (97/194, 50%) and Candida glabrata (78/194, 40.2%). In the remaining 40 CV cultures where bacterial growth was predominant, the most detected bacteria were Gardnerella vaginalis (13/40, 32.5%) and Escherichia coli (11/40, 27.5%). Discussion

One in three premature babies is born from pregnancies with subclinical intra-amniotic infection [9, 18]. Studies indicate that microorganisms isolated from amniotic fluid are similar to those found in the lower genital tract and therefore the most common route of infections in pregnancy is ascending microbial transmission [9]. Therefore, maintaining a healthy vaginal microbiota in pregnant women has an important role in preventing preterm delivery and many related obstetric and neonatal complications. Indeed, in this study, it was shown that preterm delivery and CAPO rates were the highest and birth weight was the lowest in pregnancies with bacterial dominated CVF. In pregnancies with CVF with candidal or bacterial dominance, the shorter the CxL at the time of culture sampling, the more frequently CAPO occurred. This can be explained by the fact that the shorter route makes it easier and quicker to reach the destination. As another supporting perspective, although bacterial agents were detected by culture sampling at relatively late GWs, the fact that pregnant women with bacterial CVF had the shortest CxL may have facilitated ascending infection and led to worse perinatal outcomes (Table 2).

The identification of a CxL cut-off value of 28.5 mm (AUC: 0.763) in this study offers a novel perspective in evaluating preterm birth risk, particularly in pregnancies with non-normal CVF. While the traditionally accepted threshold for short cervix is < 25 mm, especially before 24 weeks of gestation, as endorsed by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) [19], the moderate sensitivity and specificity (70.7% and 71.1%, respectively) of the 28.5 mm cut-off suggests potential utility as an early-warning parameter rather than a definitive diagnostic value. Integrating microbiota status with anatomical measurements reflects an evolving understanding of preterm birth pathogenesis, where ascending genital tract infections act synergistically with structural cervical insufficiency to precipitate labor [9].

Bacterial vaginosis is a condition characterized by an imbalance in the vaginal microbiota, where protective Lactobacillus species are replaced by anaerobic bacteria such as Gardnerella vaginalis and Atopobium vaginae. This dysbiosis has been strongly linked to preterm birth, largely due to ascending infections and inflammatory responses that can weaken fetal membranes and promote early labor. Recent studies confirm that BV increases the risk of PTB and related adverse perinatal outcomes. Bradshaw et al. [20] and Vamja et al. [21] highlight the role of recurrent BV in promoting vaginal microbial instability and inflammatory signaling. Supportively, the highest CAPO rate was found in the bacterial CVF group in which Gardnerella vaginalis (13/40, 32.5%) growth was most frequently observed in this study. In addition, our study emphasized the predictive role of CxL, identifying a cut-off of 28.5 mm in non-normal CVFs to predict CAPO, an aspect underexplored in previous research. This highlights the added value of integrating microbiological and anatomical markers in evaluating PTB risk among high-risk pregnancies.

Escherichia coli is a gram-negative bacillus that is present in the genital tract of a significant proportion of pregnant women, with prevalence rates ranging from 24 to 31%. These pathogens can colonize the vaginal and endocervical areas of pregnant women, potentially leading to urinary tract, intra-amniotic, or puerperal infections [22]. In a study by Ruhil et al. [23], cervical flora was examined in pregnant women diagnosed with TPL. Normal flora was found in most pregnant women and only 2.07% showed microbial growth in cervical swab culture. In the study, cervical pathogenic microbial growth rates varied between 0.52 and 1.04% and the most frequently detected agents were Escherichia coli, Enterococcus and Klebsiella species, respectively. Similarly, Escherichia coli was one of the bacterial agents frequently detected in CVF in our study, with a detection rate of 1.6% (11/665) in all CV cultures. One reason why this rate was higher in our study may be that not only pregnant women with TPL but also those with PPROM and CIS were included in the study. In a recent study [24], midstream urine samples and vaginal swabs were cultured from 144 pregnant women diagnosed with spontaneous preterm labor, TPL or PPROM. No vaginal infection was detected in any of these women and the most common microbial agent detected in those with urinary tract infection was Escherichia coli with a rate of 35%. We can conclude that Escherichia coli is the most common microbial agent in urinary tract infections and is also an important agent in genital tract infections, as this pathogen is frequently detected in bacterially dominated CVFs. Differently, in our study, in which urinary tract infections were excluded, bacterial and candidal predominant growths were detected in 6% and 29.2% of CV cultures, respectively. One reason why no vaginal infection was detected in the study by Sirisha et al. [24] may be the relatively small sample size.

Group B streptococcus (GBS) or Streptococcus agalactiae is a gram-positive bacterium that is often carried asymptomatically in the vagina or rectum in 20–40% of women. It can cause serious health problems such as preterm labor in pregnant women and neonatal sepsis or death in newborns due to vertical transmission during delivery [25]. The ACOG recommends routine screening for GBS between 36 and 37 weeks of gestation, with positive cases receiving appropriate antibiotics at delivery [26]. Routine GBS screening is not performed in our country and this decision is controversial among local obstetric studies [27, 28] due to the cost-benefit balance. In this study, Streptococcus agalactiae was detected in only four of the pregnant women with bacterial CVF (10%), but half of these pregnant women had preterm birth despite antibiotic therapy and CAPO was observed in one of these babies.

A systematic review of cohort, case-control and randomized controlled trials examining pregnant women with asymptomatic vaginal Candida spp. colonization and assessing the association with adverse pregnancy outcomes found no significant difference in the rates of preterm delivery and other adverse pregnancy outcomes between pregnant women with and without Candida spp. dominant vaginal flora [29]. In another large population study included in this review [30], there was no difference in preterm delivery and low birth weight rate between pregnant women with dominant CV colonization with Candida albicans and pregnant women with normal CVF. Similarly, in this study, no statistically significant difference was found between pregnant women with candidal and normal CVF in terms of week of delivery, birth weight, and preterm delivery rate.

All parts of the human body exposed to the external milieu harbor environmentally diverse microbial communities with a mutualistic relationship. The Lactobacillus spp. dominated vaginal microbiota in healthy women plays an important role in preventing and suppressing various urogenital diseases caused by bacterial, fungal or viral infection [31]. One systematic review reported that women with a vaginal microbiome low in lactobacilli were at higher risk of preterm delivery compared to women with a vaginal flora dominated by Lactobacillus crispatus [32]. Although most of the population in the study consisted of pregnant women with normal CVF, there was no significant increased risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes compared to CVF with pathogenic growth. The relatively higher CAPO rate in pregnant women with normal flora compared to candida-dominant CVF may be due to the problems of premature deliveries associated with underlying obstetric pathologies or the large population size.

An important consideration when interpreting the findings of this study is the potential impact of subsequent clinical interventions-such as cervical cerclage, pessary insertion, or antenatal corticosteroid (ACS) administration-on obstetric and neonatal outcomes. Although CV cultures were obtained prior to any treatment initiation, if interventions like cerclage or pessary were later employed to mechanically support the cervix, they may have modified the trajectory of cervical shortening, potentially delaying delivery and reducing preterm birth-related complications. This could have diluted the observed associations between short CxL and CAPO, particularly in cases where the cervix was < 28.5 mm. Similarly, the administration of ACS following CV sampling, especially in women approaching late preterm gestation, may have attenuated the severity of neonatal complications by accelerating fetal lung maturation and reducing respiratory morbidity-factors directly influencing CAPO components such as RDS or NICU admission [33, 34]. Consequently, these interventions act as post-sampling modifiers, introducing variability that may confound the direct relationship between non-normal CV microbiota, CxL, and perinatal outcomes.

Emerging research underscores the value of combining anatomical, microbiological, and biochemical parameters in the early identification and management of preterm birth risk. Inflammatory markers derived from routine blood counts, such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratios, have recently shown promise in predicting imminent delivery in women presenting with TPL [35]. Incorporating such inflammatory indices alongside CVF profiles and CxL measurements could significantly enhance short-term risk stratification and clinical decision-making. Additionally, advancements in cervical support techniques, including a novel atraumatic forceps-guided pessary insertion method, offer improved safety and reproducibility for managing asymptomatic cervical shortening [36]. If implemented after CV culture sampling, such mechanical interventions may alter the natural course of cervical shortening and potentially improve pregnancy duration and neonatal outcomes -posing a confounding effect on the direct association between short CxL, non-normal microbiota, and perinatal risk observed in this study.

In a study conducted by Ersak et al. [6], the knowledge and attitudes of obstetricians about microbiota were investigated and differences were evaluated according to their experience in the profession. The findings of this study showed that there was a statistically significant difference between obstetricians with < 12 years of experience and obstetricians with ≥ 12 years of experience in their self-assessment of knowledge about microbiota. Physicians with more years of clinical experience perceived to have more knowledge, which may be because these clinicians are more likely to encounter obstetric complications caused by abnormal microbiota-related infective processes. In this study, which we expect to enlighten obstetricians who think that they have insufficient knowledge about microbiota in terms of clinical management and follow-up, we have shown that the presence of non-normal CV microbiota in pregnant women at risk of preterm delivery may adversely affect current obstetric conditions or perinatal outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of the study are that it covered a long period of time, studied a large population, evaluated selected groups with obstetric pathologies that confer a specific risk of preterm delivery, and comprehensively evaluated perinatal outcomes between study groups.

Limitations of the study include its retrospective and single-centre design, the possibility that some cases received local or systemic antibiotic, antifungal or progesterone treatment at advanced GWs, or underwent procedures such as cerclage or cervical pessary, which may have affected perinatal outcomes.

Conclusion

In this study, the importance of CV culture sampling in pregnant women at risk of preterm delivery was emphasized and it was concluded that non-normal CVF may adversely affect obstetric and perinatal outcomes. Remarkably, in CVF with growth of pathogenic agents such as bacteria or Candida species, the CxL measured by obstetric US at the time of culture sampling has an important role in predicting adverse pregnancy outcomes and CAPO in pregnant women at risk of preterm delivery. We believe that the results of this study will contribute to the clinical management of obstetricians in pregnant women at risk of preterm delivery, but there is a need for prospective cohort studies in which homogeneous groups of pregnant women with longer duration and larger populations are evaluated in the long term.
