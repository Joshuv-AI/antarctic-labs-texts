Estrogen promotes glycolysis through the ERα-PFKL axis in chicken hierarchical follicular granulosa cells
Authors: Yu Ji, Mengjia Xin, Caiyun Sun, Shuai Yang, Li Guo, Siyu Li, Junhao Zhu, Qi Zeng, Linyan Huang, Fengyan Meng, Jing Feng, Guixian Bu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oocyte maturation critically relies on metabolic cooperation from the surrounding granulosa cells (GCs), and the energy demands on GCs change dynamically throughout follicular development. Although estrogen is a principal endocrine signal, its precise role and mechanism in regulating chicken follicular GC energy metabolism remain largely unknown. In our study, high-throughput transcriptomic analysis of GCs from hierarchical follicles following 17β-estradiol (E2) treatment identified 2731 differentially expressed transcripts, including 15 genes mapped to the glycolysis/gluconeogenesis pathway. Subsequent functional assays confirmed that the pro-glycolysis effect of E2, including increased glucose uptake, pyruvate production, lactate production, and intracellular ATP level occurred exclusively in hierarchical GCs but not prehierarchical GCs. Consistent with these results, quantitative real-time PCR and western blotting further showed significant upregulation of key enzymes related to glycolysis in hierarchical GCs. Pharmacological activation/inhibition of estrogen receptors combined with Seahorse glycolytic flux analysis showed that ERα is the key receptor mediating E2-induced glycolysis. In addition, glycolytic inhibition by 2-deoxy-D-glucose showed that glycolysis is the primary, though not exclusive, source of E2-promoted ATP production. Dual-luciferase reporter system combined with estrogen response element (ERE) mutation confirmed that E2 can activate the liver-type phosphofructokinase (PFKL) transcription by targeting the ERE (−128/−110) within its promoter. Overexpression and knockdown experiments further confirmed PFKL as a functionally required downstream effector. In summary, these findings establish the E2-ERα-PFKL axis as a mechanism enhancing glycolysis in chicken hierarchical follicular GCs, providing insight into endocrine control of ovarian energy metabolism during avian follicular development.

The ovary is the central female reproductive organ, and its function relies on the integrity and coordinated development of ovarian follicles. In laying hens, ovarian follicles are highly differentiated and exist in a strict hierarchy, which can be categorized into prehierarchical and hierarchical follicles (also called preovulatory follicles) (1). Prehierarchical follicles include small white follicles (SWF), large white follicles (LWF) and small yellow follicles (SYF) (2), while hierarchical follicles are numbered F1 to F6 from largest to smallest (3, 4). The F1 follicle represents the most mature stage and is destined for ovulation in the next cycle (3, 4). Following ovulation, a new follicle is recruited from the prehierarchical pool to enter the hierarchy, a dynamic process known as follicular cyclic recruitment that maintains the hierarchy (3, 4). Crucially, this precise orchestration of follicular growth and recruitment is regulated by a complex network of endocrine factors and is intrinsically linked to ovarian energy balance (3, 5, 6).

Follicles are structurally composed of a central oocyte surrounded by somatic cells, including an inner layer of granulosa cells (GCs) and an outer layer of thecal cells. Within these somatic compartments, particularly GCs, glucose and fatty acids serve as the primary energy sources. Fatty acids are utilized via β-oxidation (FAO), whereas glucose is the primary substrate for glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), and the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway (HBP) in GCs (7). Notably, the oocyte possesses limited intrinsic metabolic capacity and therefore relies on metabolic coupling with GCs (8, 9). This intercellular dependence is functionally supported by extensive gap junctions and the monocarboxylic acid transporters (MCTs) on the oocyte surface, which enable GCs to supply the oocyte with essential substrates, such as pyruvate and amino acids (10, 11). Therefore, maintaining metabolic homeostasis in follicular cells is crucial for determining oocyte quality and ensuring successful embryo development.

Estrogen is a key gonadal steroid hormone that regulates follicular development, maturation, and ovulation (12). Among the various estrogen forms, 17β-estradiol (E2) possesses the highest biological activity and serves as the primary regulator of physiological functions (13). While E2 synthesis is a hallmark of GCs in mammals (14), thecal cells are the primary site of E2 in avian follicles (15). Given the close spatial proximity between GCs and thecal cells, it is highly probable that E2 acts as a paracrine signal to regulate the function of the adjacent GCs. This hypothesis was supported by the documented expression of ESR1 (ERα-encoding gene) and ESR2 (ERβ-encoding gene) in GCs of chicken hierarchical follicles (16). E2 exerts its physiological effects primarily through estrogen receptors (ERs), which mediate both genomic and non-genomic signaling pathways (17). The classic genomic action involves the binding of E2 to nuclear estrogen receptors (ERα or ERβ), forming dimers that interact with estrogen response elements (EREs) on DNA to regulate the transcription of target genes (18). In contrast, the non-genomic action of E2 is mediated through the membrane-bound G protein-coupled estrogen receptor (GPER), which triggers rapid cellular responses within seconds to minutes (19, 20).

Despite the established importance of GCs metabolism in supporting oocyte maturation, the precise endocrine signals orchestrating this metabolic network in avian species remain elucidated. Considering the established role of E2 in modulating energy metabolism in mammals (21, 22, 23, 24), together with the distinctive estrogen secretion pattern in chickens (15, 25, 26, 27), it was hypothesized that E2 might directly regulate energy metabolism in chicken GCs. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the direct regulatory role of E2 on energy metabolism in chicken GCs and elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Results

Expression of E2 receptors in chicken follicular GCs at different developmental stages

To determine whether GCs serve as direct targets for E2, the developmental expression profiles of its receptors in chicken GCs were analyzed across 10 key stages of chicken folliculogenesis (28). As shown in Figure 1, A–C, ESR1 expression was primarily restricted to hierarchical follicles, while its expression was negligible in prehierarchical stages. Conversely, GPER displayed an inverse expression pattern, showing higher levels in prehierarchical follicles and significantly lower levels in hierarchical follicles. ESR2 displayed ubiquitous expression across GCs of all developmental stages, without clear stage-specific differences. Transcriptomic changes of F2 GCs in response to E2

To validate the identity of the isolated primary GCs, immunofluorescence (IF) for Forkhead Box L2 (FOXL2), a well-established granulosa cell marker, was performed. As shown in Figures 2A and S1, most isolated cells from different follicular stages exhibited FOXL2-positive signals, supporting their granulosa cell identity. Considering the substantial expression of all three receptors in GCs at the F2 stage, F2 follicles were selected as the representative stage for the subsequent transcriptomic profiling and downstream mechanistic validation in this study. High-throughput transcriptomic analysis was then performed to investigate the potential cellular functions and regulatory mechanisms of E2. A total of 2731 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 1243 downregulated and 1488 upregulated genes, were identified after E2 treatment (Figs. 2B and S2). The DEGs were subsequently assessed by Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses. GO functional enrichment revealed that most of the DEGs were primarily associated with regulation of cell migration, steroid biosynthetic process and cholesterol metabolic process (Fig. 2C). KEGG pathway analysis demonstrated that several top-ranked enriched pathways among the DEGs were related to the PPAR signaling pathway, biosynthesis of amino acids, carbon metabolism and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis (Fig. 2D). To explore the contribution of glycolysis to folliculogenesis in chickens, the expression patterns of key glycolytic regulators were characterized in chicken GCs across hierarchical stages (28). These included glucose transporter 12 (GLUT12) and glycolytic rate-limiting enzymes such as hexokinase (HK1 and HK2), phosphofructokinase-liver type (PFKL), and pyruvate kinase M (PKM). The expression levels (measured in transcripts per million, TPM) of GLUT12, HK1, HK2, and PFKL showed increasing trends throughout hierarchical follicular development, while only PKM exhibited relatively stable expression across the different stages (Fig. S3). The progressively upregulated expression patterns of these genes during follicular development suggest an important role of glycolysis in folliculogenesis.

Effect of E2 on glycolysis in chicken GCs from SYF and hierarchical follicles

To validate the effect of E2 on GCs glycolysis, the primary GCs from follicles of various sizes (SYF and F1-F6 follicles) were isolated and cultured. Subsequently, glycolysis-related indicators including glucose uptake, pyruvate production, lactate secretion, and intracellular ATP level were examined. As shown in Figure 3, A–D, E2 treatment significantly increased the levels of glucose uptake, pyruvate secretion, lactate secretion, and intracellular ATP concentration in hierarchical GCs, but had no influence on SYF GCs. Subsequently, qRT-PCR was carried out to detect the mRNA levels of key enzymes related to glycolysis. To select the optimal treatment duration, the mRNA levels of key glycolytic regulators (including GLUT12, HK1, HK2, PFKL and PKM) and LDHA (the enzyme responsible for converting pyruvate to lactate) in GCs from F2 follicles were detected after E2 treatment for 3 h, 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h. The results showed that E2 increased the expression of these key glycolytic enzymes as early as 6 h, and this effect persisted up to 24 h (Fig. 4, A–F). Therefore, 24 h was determined as the optimal treatment duration for subsequent functional and molecular analyses. Next, the stage-specificity of E2-modulated glycolytic gene expression was investigated. We observed that E2 significantly upregulated the expression of GLUT12, HK1, HK2, PFKL, PKM and LDHA in hierarchical GCs, with no significant effect on prehierarchical GCs (Fig. 4, G–L). Consistent with the results of qRT-PCR, the protein levels of HK2, PFKL, and PKM were upregulated in hierarchical follicular GCs (Fig. 5). Effect of the agonists and antagonists of E2 receptors on glycolysis in chicken F2 GCs

To identify the key receptor mediating E2-induced glycolysis in GCs, the agonists and antagonists of E2 receptors were used to treat GCs from F2 follicles. Firstly, ER antagonist fulvestrant and GPER antagonist G15 were employed independently to treat the cells for 24 h (Fig. 6A). The stimulatory effects of E2 on glucose uptake, pyruvate release, lactate release and the expression levels of key glycolytic regulators were significantly counteracted when E2 was co-treated with fulvestrant, while co-treatment with G15 failed to produce any significant effect (Fig. 6B and D–K). Likewise, the protein levels of HK2, PFKL and PKM were significantly downregulated when E2 was co-treated with fulvestrant compared to E2 treatment alone (Fig. 6L). However, the intracellular ATP level was elevated both following treatment with fulvestrant and G15 compared to the control group (Fig. 6C). Besides, co-treatment of E2 with either fulvestrant or G15 resulted in a further significant increase in ATP levels compared to the E2 treatment group (Fig. 6C). However, as reported in section 2.4, both ERs agonists and antagonists treatments increased ATP levels, suggesting that ATP production in GCs may be regulated by a more complex metabolic network beyond ERα-mediated glycolytic activity. To distinguish the contribution of glycolysis to E2-regulated ATP production, GCs were treated with E2 in the presence or absence of the glycolytic inhibitor 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG). ATP levels were reduced by 2-DG co-treatment compared with E2 alone but remained higher than those in the 2-DG group (Fig. 8C), indicating that while glycolysis is the major contributor to E2-induced ATP production, E2 also supports ATP generation through glycolysis-independent pathways. These findings provide a possible explanation for the paradoxical ATP increases observed following ERβ/GPER activation and ER antagonist treatments.

Effect of ESR1 knockdown on glycolysis in chicken F2 GCs

To further confirm whether ERα mediated the stimulatory effect of E2 on glycolysis in GCs, specific small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting the transcripts of ESR1 was designed to silence ESR1 expression (Fig. 9A). The interference efficiency of four distinct siESR1 were assessed by qRT-PCR, and siESR1-4 exhibited the highest knockdown efficiency (Fig. 9B). Consequently, siESR1-4 was selected for subsequent experiments. As expected, the E2-induced increases in glucose uptake, pyruvate release, and lactate secretion were significantly decreased after knocking down ESR1 in GCs from F2 follicles (Fig. 9, C–E). Surprisingly, siESR1-4 only significantly decreased the PFKL mRNA level in E2-treated GCs, whereas the mRNA levels of all other key glycolytic regulators showed no significant decrease, with some showing increased expression after siESR1-4 transfection (Fig. 9, F–K). The protein levels of PFKL were also significantly decreased in E2-treated GCs transfected with siESR1-4 (Fig. 9L). Identification of the transcriptional regulation of PFKL by the E2-ERα axis

Prior ERα ChIP-Seq data from chicken liver suggested that PFKL was an estrogen-responsive gene (29). To validate whether the E2-ERα axis regulates PFKL promoter activity, the pGL3-cPFKLp vector containing the promoter region (−504/+18) was constructed and a dual-luciferase reporter assay was performed (Fig. 10A). As shown in Figure 10B, DF-1 cells transfected with pGL3-cPFKLp exhibited significantly enhanced luciferase activity compared to the pGL3-Basic group, confirming the potent promoter activity of the cloned region. Next, luciferase activity was significantly higher in cells co-transfected with pcDNA3.1-ESR1 and pGL3-cPFKLp than in the control group (Fig. 10C). Notably, E2 treatment further stimulated PFKL promoter activity only in the presence of pcDNA3.1-ESR1, while no response was observed in cells transfected with the empty vector (Fig. 10C). Subsequently, the JASPAR database (https://jaspar.elixir.no/) was utilized to predict the EREs within the PFKL promoter (Fig. S4). The mutated construct, pGL3-cPFKLp-M, which carried a mutation at the highest-scoring ERE site (position −128/−110), was successfully constructed (Fig. 10A). After mutating the ERE, pGL3-cPFKLp-M no longer responded to pcDNA3.1-ESR1 co-transfection and E2 stimulation (Fig. 10C). These data demonstrate that the E2-ERα axis activates PFKL promoter activity through an ERE-dependent mechanism and that the predicted ERE at −128/−110 is required for this transcriptional response.

Effect of PFKL overexpression on the expression of glycolytic rate-limiting enzymes in chicken F2 GCs

To confirm the necessity of PFKL in E2-induced glycolytic activation in GCs, we cloned the pcDNA3.1-cPFKL vector containing the complete open reading frame of PFKL and transfected it into GCs from F2 follicles (Fig. 10D). Overexpression of PFKL significantly increased the mRNA levels of GLUT12, HK1, HK2, PKM and LDHA (Fig. 10D). Taken together, these results demonstrate that PFKL is the crucial downstream effector in the E2-ERα signaling pathway, and its transcriptional activation is sufficient to drive the upregulation of key enzymes and transporters in the glycolytic pathway.

Effect of PFKL knockdown on E2-induced glycolytic activity in chicken F2 GCs

To further determine whether PFKL is functionally required for E2-induced glycolytic activation, PFKL was knocked down in GCs from F2 follicles using specific siRNAs. The result of qRT-PCR showed that all four siRNAs reduced PFKL mRNA expression, among which siPFKL-2 and siPFKL-4 were selected for subsequent functional assays (Fig. 11A). Importantly, PFKL knockdown with either siPFKL-2 or siPFKL-4 markedly attenuated E2-induced increases in glucose uptake, pyruvate production, and lactate production (Fig. 11, B–D). Discussion

The development and maturation of the ovarian follicle is a highly energy-demanding and precisely regulated process. While E2 is well-established as a key regulator of energy metabolism across various mammalian cell types (30, 31), and the distinctive E2 secretion pattern in chickens allowed it to regulate the function of adjacent GCs (15, 25, 26, 27), whether E2 could regulate energy metabolism in GCs remained unexplored. In this study, we found that E2 enhanced glycolysis in hierarchical follicular GCs but showed no effect on prehierarchical follicular GCs. The pro-glycolysis effect was mediated by ERα, which activated PFKL transcription through the ERE located at the −128/-110 promoter region. The upregulated transcription of PFKL stimulated the expression of other glycolysis-related enzymes, including GLUT12, HK1, HK2, PKM, and LDHA, ultimately leading to a robust enhancement of glycolytic flux in GCs. Our results not only clarify the role and mechanism of estrogen in glycolysis in chicken GCs for the first time but also provide essential insights into the supportive role of estrogen in metabolism during avian folliculogenesis.

Over the last 2 decades, E2 has been recognized as a key regulator preserving the integrity of energy metabolic homeostasis in mammals (32). However, the specific effects of E2 on GCs metabolism have remained largely unexplored, partly due to its distinct secretion patterns. In mammals, E2 is predominantly synthesized by GCs of pre-ovulatory follicles under the stimulation of follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) (14). Previous studies have mainly focused on its roles in differentiation and endocrine regulation, such as the modulation of progesterone production, impact on melatonin synthesis, and regulation of cell adhesion molecule expression (33, 34, 35). Distinctively, E2 is primarily produced by theca cells of LWF in chickens (15). Research regarding the function of E2 on chicken GCs also remains limited. In primarily cultured F1 GCs, E2 treatment inhibited the conversion of pregnenolone to progesterone (P4) (36), while pretreating chicken GCs with E2 followed by luteinizing hormone (LH) stimulation significantly increased P4 levels (37). These seemingly contradictory results make it difficult to summarize the role of E2 in chicken GCs. In the present study, the KEGG pathway analysis of high-throughput transcriptomic data revealed an enrichment of DEGs in multiple metabolic pathways, providing strong evidence that E2 acted as a regulator of energy metabolism in chicken GCs. Among the metabolic pathways, we focused specifically on glycolysis because it was of great significance for follicular development. In mammals, glucose utilization is inefficient in oocytes owing to the low phosphofructokinase (PFK) activity (38). To compensate for the energy demand of oocytes, GCs possess high PFK activity and express abundant GLUTs, which leads to the secretion of large quantities of pyruvate and lactate (39). These metabolites are then transferred to the oocyte both through gap junctions and via the MCTs on the oocyte surface (40, 41), thereby ensuring a continuous supply of energy substrates required for metabolic homeostasis during follicular development and oocyte maturation. Although direct evidence for the metabolic cooperation is limited in avian species, our data provide compelling support for a conserved mechanism. The TPM values of GLUT12, HK1, HK2, and PFKL progressively increased across the maturation of hierarchical follicles in chickens, underscoring that glycolysis played an equally indispensable role in avian follicular development. Our findings mirror the metabolic patterns observed in mammals, where enhanced glycolysis acts as a critical driver of follicular development (42, 43, 44). Collectively, these parallel findings suggest that the essential physiological role of GCs glycolysis in supporting follicular development is evolutionarily conserved between mammals and chickens.

Our findings reveal that E2 could enhance glycolytic levels in chicken GCs, shedding light on the role of E2 in coordinating the energy metabolism of avian follicular cells. Intriguingly, the pro-glycolysis effect exhibits strict stage specificity, occurring exclusively in hierarchical GCs with no detectable impact on prehierarchical GCs. This observation presented an apparent paradox, as E2 in chickens was primarily synthesized by the thecal cells of LWF (15). One possible reason for this difference could be the expression of ERα. According to the expression profile of ESR1 across key stages of chicken folliculogenesis, ESR1 expression was negligible in prehierarchical stages, which likely limited the ability of E2 to activate glycolysis in prehierarchical follicles. Besides, E2 seemed to undergo a functional switch across follicular stages. Previous studies in chicken GCs highlighted that E2 acts on prehierarchical GCs to reduce proliferation and promote slow maturation by enhancing the expression of inhibin α-and βB-subunit and follistatin (45). This functional divergence is supported by single-cell RNA sequencing of hierarchical and prehierarchical GCs in chickens, which revealed that prehierarchical GCs were mainly associated with cell proliferation and division, while hierarchical GCs were predominantly linked to metabolism and steroid biosynthesis (46). These findings suggest functional differences between hierarchical and prehierarchical GCs, which may explain the stage-specific effects of E2 on glycolysis and other metabolic processes. Finally, the fact that E2 is synthesized by both theca cells and GCs within hierarchical follicles (25, 26, 27) further ensured stronger autocrine/paracrine signaling, potentially facilitating the stage-specific enhancement of glycolysis observed in our study. Importantly, the early onset of the glycolytic response argues against differentiation as its primary cause. E2-induced upregulation of glycolysis-related genes was detectable as early as 6 h. In parallel, preliminary data from our laboratory showed that differentiation markers including StAR, HSD3B1, CYP11A1, FSHR and LHR were not significantly altered by E2 within 6 h under identical treatment conditions (unpublished observations), supporting a direct rather than differentiation-coupled glycolytic response.

The role of the ERs in energy metabolism is both extensive and highly conserved (32). In terms of glucose uptake, E2 is well known to stimulate glucose uptake in various target organs by altering the expression of genes encoding proteins and enzymes involved in glucose metabolism (21, 47, 48). For example, ERα positively regulates GLUT4 expression in skeletal muscle, as ERαKO mice showed significant reductions in GLUT4 protein and mRNA levels (49). In this study, we observed a similar mechanism in the avian ovary, confirming that E2-ERα promoted the glucose uptake and the expression of GLUT12 in chicken hierarchical GCs. Specifically, the E2-induced upregulation of GLUT12 expression aligns with studies in cultured breast cancer cells (50), further establishing GLUT12 as a conserved target for glucose influx in metabolically active cells. Beyond regulating glucose uptake and glycolysis, E2 and its receptors are involved in the broader regulation of cellular energy metabolism, stimulating mitochondrial respiratory activity by inducing the expression of nuclear DNA-encoded mitochondrial respiratory chain proteins (51, 52, 53, 54). Since mitochondria are the primary site for FAO, E2 is essential for supporting this process (24, 55, 56). To illustrate, E2 restores the expression of key metabolic regulators like PPAR-γ, PGC1α and the enzymes for FAO in adipose tissue and skeletal muscle, supporting FAO and lipid homeostasis (24). This coordinated control by E2 over the catabolism of both glucose and fatty acids significantly promotes ATP generation and oxygen consumption. Notably, all ERs agonists (PPT, DPN, and G-1) led to an increase in the intracellular ATP levels in chicken GCs, although only ERα activation was accompanied by enhanced glycolytic flux. These findings raise the possibility that ERβ and GPER may contribute to ATP production through alternative pathways. The 2-DG inhibition experiment further confirmed that ERα-dependent glycolysis is the principal, but not exclusive, source of E2-promoted ATP in GCs, providing a possible explanation of this paradox. Supporting this hypothesis, ERβ has been localized to mitochondria and has been shown to enhance mitochondrial respiratory capacity upon activation by DPN (57, 58), while genetic ablation of ERβ impairs the activity of multiple electron transport chain complexes (59). Furthermore, GPER signaling has been implicated in the regulation of lipid mobilization and fatty acid oxidation, which provides alternative substrates for mitochondrial ATP generation (60, 61). Taken together, these findings suggest that ERβ and GPER may increase ATP production through oxidative metabolism rather than direct activation of glycolytic flux. Similarly, the paradoxical ATP increase observed following ER antagonists’ treatment likely reflects a compensatory metabolic response to disrupted estrogenic signaling, in which GCs activate alternative energy-generating pathways such as mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation or FAO to preserve energy homeostasis. Whether and how ERα, ERβ, and GPER coordinately regulate these complementary metabolic routes in chicken GCs warrants further investigation.

PFK, a key rate-limiting enzyme in glycolysis, exists as three tissue-specific isozymes: PFKM (muscle type), PFKP (platelet type), and PFKL (liver type). In this study, we established PFKL as the key downstream effector of the E2-ERα axis in avian GCs for the first time, shedding light on the species- and tissue-specific regulation of glucose metabolism. This discovery holds important implications for understanding how estrogen influences metabolic pathways across different tissues. In MCF-7 cells, E2 promoted PFKFB3 transcription via ER, which produced fructose-2,6-bisphosphate that allosterically activated PFK-1 and stimulated glucose uptake and the entire glycolytic pathway (62). Similarly, E2 could promote glycolysis through GPER1-mediated activation of the glycolytic enzyme PFKFB3 in endothelial cells (63). However, in chicken GCs, we observe a distinct mechanism in which the E2–ERα axis enhances PFKL promoter activity through an ERE-dependent mechanism. Importantly, PFKL knockdown attenuated E2-induced glucose uptake, pyruvate production, and lactate secretion, further indicating that PFKL is not only a transcriptional target of E2–ERα signaling but also a functional effector required for E2-induced glycolytic activation. Although ESR1 knockdown did not uniformly suppress all glycolysis-related genes, this likely reflects compensatory or PFKL-independent regulatory mechanisms that maintain partial glycolytic gene expression. Thus, the E2–ERα–PFKL axis represents a major route driving glycolytic activation in chicken GCs, rather than the only pathway through which E2 affects glycolysis-related genes. Moreover, while a recent study confirmed that genistein activates the GPER-mediated cAMP/PKA-AMPK signaling pathway to enhance glucose catabolism in broiler chicken hepatocytes (64), our findings highlight that E2 regulates glycolysis specifically in GCs via ERα. This indicates that different estrogen receptor subtypes may be strategically utilized in a tissue-specific manner within the chicken to optimize energy metabolism. GPER appears to play an important role in regulating energy metabolism in the liver, while ERα is responsible for coordinating reproductive energy supply through GCs in the ovary. This proposed tissue-specific allocation of ERs functions warrants further exploration, but it provides a compelling framework for understanding the physiological relevance of the E2-ERα-PFKL axis in the chicken ovary.

In summary, this study establishes a molecular signaling pathway that links the crucial endocrine signal estrogen with glycolysis in the chicken ovary. Our findings demonstrate that E2 promotes glycolysis exclusively in hierarchical follicular GCs by acting through the ERα-PFKL pathway. The transcriptional activation of PFKL serves as the core driving force to enhance the expression of other key enzymes and transporters in the glycolytic pathway and thus boosts glycolytic flux, which contributes to oocyte energy supply and follicular development in the laying hen (Fig. 12). However, several limitations warrant further investigation. Due to the lack of a validated antibody for endogenous chicken ERα in GCs, ERα protein knockdown and ERα binding to the endogenous PFKL promoter could not be directly confirmed. Experimental procedures

Chemicals, antibodies and primers

Unless stated otherwise, all chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, and restriction enzymes were obtained from TransGen Biotech. The pharmacological agents, including E2, PPT, DPN, G-1, Fulvestrant, and G15, were purchased from MedChemExpress. HK2 antibody (Cat#22029-1-AP) was purchased from Proteintech Group. Antibodies against ACTB (Cat#AC026), PFKL (Cat#A7708) and PKM (Cat#A18800) were obtained from ABclonal Technology. All primers used in this study were synthesized by Tsingke Biological Technology Co., Ltd and listed in Table S1.

Animals and granulosa cells culture

Lohmann layers at the active laying stage were purchased from a local commercial company. All layers were reared under the same conditions and had free access to food and water. Chickens were euthanized and the whole ovary was collected in PBS solution buffer at 4 °C. Based on the previously established method (65), GCs from various sizes of follicles (including SYF, F5-6, F4, F3, F2 and F1 follicles) were isolated and digested by type I collagenase (Biosharp, Beijing) at 37 °C for 15 min. The dispersed cells were filtered with 200 mesh cell sieves and then cultured in 48-well plates (Cellpro, Suzhou) with DMEM/F12 medium (BasalMedia, Shanghai) containing 10% (vol/vol) fetal bovine serum (FBS, GeminiBio) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. After 6 h, the medium was removed, and cells were treated with DMEM/F12 medium containing 10 nM E2 for 24 h. All animal experimental protocols employed in this study were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Sichuan Agricultural University.

Immunofluorescence staining

GCs were fixed with 4% PFA, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 10% goat serum. Cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a polyclonal anti-FOXL2 antibody (1:300, Boshide, A01185-1), followed by ABflo 488-conjugated Donkey anti-Rabbit IgG (1:500, Abclonal, AS035) for 60 min at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (2 μg/ml, Solarbio, C0060).

Total RNA extraction and reverse transcription

Primary GCs were plated into a 48-well plate at a density of approximately 1 × 105 cells per well in 200 μl of culture medium. After 24 h of treatment, total RNA was extracted from cells by using RNAzol (Molecular Research Center) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and dissolved in diethylpyrocarbonate (DEPC)-treated H2O. These RNA samples were then used for reverse transcription (RT) based on Moloney murine leukemia virus (MMLV) reverse transcriptase (Takara). Briefly, 1 μg RNA was mixed with oligodeoxythymidine in a total volume of 5 μl, incubated at 70 °C for 10 min, and cooled at 4 °C for 2 min. Thereafter, the first strand buffer containing 0.5 mM of each deoxynucleotide triphosphate (dNTP) and 100 U of reverse transcriptase was added to achieve a final volume of 10 μl. The RT reaction was performed at 42 °C for 90 min. RT-negative controls were performed under the same conditions without the addition of reverse transcriptase.

RNA-seq library construction and sequencing

RNA-seq libraries were prepared following the standard Illumina protocols by Novogene. In brief, mRNA samples (3 samples per group, each sample contributed from different individuals) were purified from total RNA by using poly-A oligo-attached magnetic beads with an integrity value > 8.0. Double-stranded cDNAs were synthesized with random hexamer primers and purified with the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter). Inserts with the expected size were concentrated and subjected to transcriptome analyses. The library quality was assessed on the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system.

Differential expression and functional enrichment analyses

Differential expression analysis between the control and E2 groups was performed using DESeq2, which models RNA-seq count data based on a negative binomial distribution. Raw p values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Genes with an adjusted p value (padj) < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed genes (DEGs), without applying an additional fold-change cutoff.

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of the identified DEGs were performed using the clusterProfiler package (version 3.4.4). For GO enrichment analysis, DEGs were annotated and classified into three categories: biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). GO terms with a Benjamini-Hochberg-adjusted p value of < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. KEGG pathway analysis was performed to identify metabolic and signaling pathways associated with the DEGs, and the top enriched pathways were visualized according to their enrichment significance.

Quantitative real-time PCR assay

To detect the mRNA level of glycolysis-related genes in GCs, qRT-PCR was conducted on the Archimed Quantitative PCR Detection System (RocGene Technologies Co., Ltd, Beijing) in a 20 μl reaction volume using EvaGreen (Biotium). Based on the previously established method (66), the PCR reaction was carried out at 94 °C for denaturation for 2 min, followed by 40 reaction cycles (94 °C for 20 s, 60 °C for 20 s and 72 °C for 20 s) and the fluorescence signal was detected at 72 °C. Melting curve analysis and agarose gel electrophoresis were performed subsequently to validate the specificity of PCR amplification.

Western blotting

Based on our previously established method (67), western blotting was employed to detect the protein levels of HK2, PFKL and PKM in cultured GCs. The cell lysates were resolved by SDS-PAGE and the bands were visualized using Enhanced ECL Chemiluminescent Substrate Kit (Yeasen Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer’s directions. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH). The relative protein levels were calculated as the ratio to that of β-actin and then expressed as the percentage of the control group (without E2 treatment).

Measurement of ATP generation

An ATP assay kit (Beyotime) was used to measure the ATP content in the GCs. Briefly, cell lysates were centrifuged at 12,000g at 4 °C for 5 min, and the supernatant was incubated in the ATP-detection buffer. ATP levels were measured using a luciferase-based assay on a 96-well plate. The ATP concentration in each sample was quantified by averaging the luminescence values of blank, standard, and sample wells, followed by subtraction of the mean blank signal from the corresponding standard and sample readings. All measured ATP concentrations were within the standard curve of the assay.

Determination of lactate release, pyruvate production, and glucose uptake

To detect the lactate and pyruvate secretion and glucose uptake in GCs, the culture media from different treatment groups were collected and measured by the Lactic Acid assay kit (Cat#A019-2-1), Pyruvate assay kit (Cat#A081-1-1), and Glucose assay kit (Cat#A154-1-1) (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) following the manufacturer's instructions, respectively. Glucose uptake was quantified as the net difference between the initial glucose concentration in the fresh culture medium and the remaining glucose concentration in the collected conditioned medium after the treatment.

Plasmid construction

Based on the genomic sequence of chicken PFKL, gene-specific primers were designed. Whole chicken genomic DNA was then used as the template to amplify its 5′-flanking regions using high-fidelity Taq DNA polymerase (TOYOBO). To identify the functional EREs on the PFKL promoter, the JASPAR database (https://jaspar.elixir.no/) was used to predict the potential binding sites within the PFKL promoter, and overlapping PCR approach was conducted to mutate the highest-scoring ERE site (position −128/−110). The amplified PCR products were inserted into the pGL3-Basic vector. The ORFs of chicken ESR1 (NM_205183.2) and PFKL (NM_001396039.1) were cloned and inserted into the pcDNA3.1 vector. All the plasmids were sequenced and determined by Tsingke Biological Technology.

Cell transfection

siRNAs specific for ESR1, siRNAs specific for PFKL and the scrambled control siRNAs (Table S2) were obtained from GenePharma. Transfection of siRNA was performed using siRNA-Mate plus (GenePharma, Shanghai) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To assess the role of PFKL in E2-induced glycolytic activation, primary GCs were cultured in 48-well plates and transfected with the pcDNA3.1-PFKL vector (300 ng/well) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Dual-luciferase reporter assays

DF-1 cells were cultured in DMEM medium (BasalMedia, Shanghai) supplemented with 10% (vol/vol) FBS, 100 U/ml of penicillin G and 100 μg/ml of streptomycin (HyClone) in a 90-mm dish (Corning) and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. As described previously (68), DF-1 cells were seeded into 48-well plates at a density of approximately 5 × 104 cells per well in 200 μl of culture medium. The promoter activities of pGL3-PFKLp were determined in DF-1 cells using the Dual-luciferase Reporter assay (Promega). To evaluate the direct transcriptional effect of E2 on PFKL transcription, cells were co-transfected with pcDNA3.1-ESR1 and either pGL3-PFKLp or pGL3-PFKLp-M and subsequently treated with or without 10 nM E2. Luciferase activity in cells was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity derived from the pRL-TK vector and then expressed as a relative fold increase compared with the control group.

Seahorse analyses

Glycolytic flux in F2 GCs was measured using the Seahorse XF Glycolytic Rate Assay Kit with a Seahorse XF96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Agilent Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, primary F2 GCs were seeded into Seahorse XF96 cell culture microplates at a density of approximately 4 × 104 cells per well and allowed to attach before treatment. Cells were then treated with E2, ERs agonists, or ERs antagonists as described above. Before the assay, the culture medium was replaced with Seahorse assay medium, and cells were incubated at 37 °C in a non-CO2 incubator for approximately 1 h. The sensor cartridge was hydrated and calibrated according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Basal glycolysis and related glycolytic parameters were calculated using Seahorse Wave software.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis based on the collected data was conducted in GraphPad Prism 7 (GraphPad Software), and the mean of replicates (N ≥ 3) was evaluated to be displayed as mean ± SD. To validate our results, all experiments were repeated at least twice.
