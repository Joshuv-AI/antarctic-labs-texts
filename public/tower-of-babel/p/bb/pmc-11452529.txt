Cortical kappa opioid receptors integrate negative affect and sleep disturbance
Authors: Robson C Lillo Vizin, Hisakatsu Ito, Caroline M Kopruszinski, Megumi Ikegami, Daigo Ikegami, Xu Yue, Edita Navratilova, Aubin Moutal, Stephen L Cowen, Frank Porreca
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSleep disruption and negative affect are attendant features of many psychiatric and neurological conditions that are often co-morbid including major depressive disorder, generalized anxiety disorder and chronic pain. Whether there is a causal relationship between negative affect and sleep disruption remains unclear. We therefore asked if mechanisms promoting negative affect can disrupt sleep and whether inhibition of pathological negative affect can normalize disrupted sleep. Signaling at the kappa opioid receptor (KOR) elicits dysphoria in humans and aversive conditioning in animals. We tested the possibility that (a) increased KOR signaling in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), a brain region associated with negative emotions, would be sufficient to promote both aversiveness and sleep disruption and (b) inhibition of KOR signaling would normalize pathological negative affect and sleep disruption induced by chronic pain. Chemogenetic Gi-mediated inhibition of KOR-expressing ACC neurons produced conditioned place aversion (CPA) as well as sleep fragmentation in naïve mice. CRISPR/Cas9 editing of ACC KOR normalized both the negative affect and sleep disruption elicited by pathological chronic pain while maintaining the physiologically critical sensory features of pain. These findings suggest therapeutic utility of KOR antagonists for treatment of disease conditions that are associated with both negative affect and sleep disturbances.Subject terms: Neuroscience, Diseases

Introduction

Neurological and psychiatric disorders are often co-morbid and characterized by negative affect and disrupted sleep. Though repeatedly proposed, causal and/or mechanistic links between sleep disruption and negative emotional states have yet to be established. In this study, we evaluated cortical kappa opioid receptors (KOR) as a potential link between negative affect and sleep disruption.

To date, development of KOR agonists as analgesic drugs has been unsuccessful due to dysphoria and unpleasantness produced by these molecules in human trials. These observations, and others, have now led to the discovery and evaluation of KOR antagonists that are currently in advanced clinical trials for treatment of major depressive disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, anhedonia, and bipolar disorder. Notably, all of these conditions are associated with negative affect and disrupted sleep. For this reason, we hypothesized that endogenous KOR activation could be a common link between sleep disruption and negative affect.

This possibility was tested in the present experiments using a murine model of chronic neuropathic pain. Chronic pain was chosen as a model system for testing the potential link between negative affect and sleep disruption as preclinical studies have shown that KOR activation promotes pain aversiveness and systemic administration of KOR antagonists diminish the negative affective qualities of chronic pain and normalize pain-related sleep disruption [1–4]. As the aversive qualities of chronic pain in humans are thought to be mediated in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and few studies have linked the ACC to sleep disruption in pain [5, 6], we hypothesized that (1) activation of cingulate KOR signaling could elicit both negative affect and sleep disruption in naïve mice and (2) inhibition of KOR signaling could normalize the aversiveness and disrupted sleep associated with pathological chronic pain.

Materials and methods

Animals

Male C57BL/6J mice (8 weeks) or KOR-cre mice (8–10 weeks) were used. KOR-cre mice were generated as previously described and were backcrossed to C57BL/6 background for at least 8 generations. Mice were maintained under a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on at 7 a.m.) in a climate- and humidity-controlled environment. Food and water were available ad libitum. The number of animals per group was based on previous studies [7, 8]. Mice were randomly assigned to the treatment groups and the experiments were conducted in a blinded fashion. The study was conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines, the ethical guidelines of the International Association for the Study of Pain regulations on animal welfare, and the National Institutes of Health guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. The experimental procedures were previously approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Arizona.

Design and in vivo transfection of CRISPR/Cas9 targeting KOR

To delete the KOR, we targeted the second exon of the oprk1 common to all KOR splice variants (ENSMUSG00000025905, gRNA: gTCCCCCATTCAGATCTTCCG, on-score 65.7, off-score 78.8). The indicated gRNA sequence was inserted into the Esp3I restriction site of the pL-CRISPR.EFS.tRFP lentiplasmid (Cat# 57819, Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA) as described before [7]. Plasmids were verified by Sanger sequencing (Eurofins, Louisville, KY, USA).

Intracranial Adeno-Associated Virus (AAV) vector or KOR CRISPR plasmid microinjection

AAV8-hSyn-DIO-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry (150 nL; viral prep #44362-AAV8, Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA) encoding hM4Di-mCherry or KOR CRISPR plasmid (500 nL) was injected bilaterally into the ACC (1.41 mm anterior, 0.25 mm lateral, 0.8 mm ventral relative to Bregma). Experiments were performed at least 4 weeks after AAV injection or 1 week after plasmid injection. hM4D(Gi)-mCherry expression was verify post-hoc.

Partial sciatic nerve ligation surgery

Partial sciatic nerve ligation (PSNL) surgery was performed as described previously [7]. Mice were anesthetized with 2–5% isoflurane. The right sciatic nerve was ligated by a tight ligature with 8–0 silk suture around approximately one-half of the diameter. In sham-operated mice, the nerve was only exposed without ligation. The muscle was sutured with a 5–0 braided suture and the skin was closed with an Auto-clip.

EEG/EMG head-mount surgery

Under 2–5% isoflurane anesthesia, mice were implanted with EEG/EMG electrodes for polysomnographic recordings (Pinnacle Technology, Lawrence, KS, USA) as described previously [7]. Briefly, mice were mounted in a stereotaxic head holder and a 2 cm incision was made down the midline from just behind the eyes to expose the cranium. Dedicated 2 EEG/1 EMG mouse head-mounts (Pinnacle Technology) were implanted with four EEG electrode screws. This was accomplished by drilling four holes with avoidance of visible blood vessels to allow the insertion of electrode screws at 1 mm anterior from bregma and lambda, both 1.5 mm lateral to the midline. Two Teflon-coated stainless-steel wires were placed bilaterally into both trapezius muscles to record EMG. The head-mount was secured to the skull with two anchor screws and covered by dental acrylic. Mice were allowed to recover from the surgery for at least 7 days before recording.

EEG/EMG recording and analysis

Mice were individually placed in clear Plexiglass sleep chamber (241 mm diameter) allocated in a dedicated quiet room and allowed to acclimate to the experimental conditions for at least 3 days. EEG and EMG were recorded through a preamplifier and cable with a low-torque commutator, which allowed the mice unencumbered freedom of movement. Data was collected using Sirenia software (Pinnacle Technology). All data were taken from the EEG electrodes positioned on the frontal lobe. Data were analyzed by in a blinded fashion by SleepSign software (Kissei Comtec, Matsumoto, Nagano, Japan). The vigilance state of every 5-s epoch was automatically classified into wake, rapid-eye movement (REM), or non-REM (NREM) sleep. Wakefulness was defined by a high EMG amplitude, and a low EEG amplitude; REM sleep was defined by a low EMG amplitude, low EEG amplitude, and high theta (θ) wave (5.0–10.0 Hz) activity; and NREM sleep was defined by a low EMG amplitude, high EEG amplitude, and high delta (δ) wave (0.65–4.5 Hz) activity [7]. As a final step, defined sleep-wake stages were examined visually and corrected, if necessary.

von Frey testing

Mechanical allodynia was assessed using calibrated von Frey filaments. Prior evaluation, mice were acclimated in clear Plexiglas chambers (10 cm L × 10 cm W × 20 cm H), on top of a wire mesh stand (0.635 cm² grid), for 2 h. The filament was gently applied until the filament was slightly arched. For PSNL mice, von Frey filaments were applied to the ipsilateral hindpaw, and the withdrawal threshold was determined using the up-down method as previously described [9]. For KOR-cre mice, hindpaw frequency of response to tactile stimulation was performed as previously reported [10]. Von Frey filament of 1 g (Stoelting Co, Wood Dale, IL, USA) was applied 10 times to the hindpaw. Responses were scored after each filament application. Hindpaw responses were characterized by sharp withdrawal of the paw, shaking, and/or licking the paw. Cutaneous allodynia was defined by the increased frequency of response to tactile stimulation. Frequency of response was calculated as [number of positive responses/10 × 100%].

Conditioned place preference (CPP) and conditioned place avoidance (CPA)

A five-day conditioning protocol was used to assess ongoing pain (CPP) and pain avoidance (CPA) [7] as depicted in Fig. S1. On acclimation day (day 1), mice were placed into the CPP/CPA boxes (Panlab, Harvard Apparatus - LE896/76-0376 and LE989/76-0441) for 15 min with free access to all chambers. The next day, mice were placed in the chambers again and baseline (preconditioning) time spent in each chamber across 15 min was analyzed using automated software (Panlab, Harvard Apparatus - PPCWIN). On the following two conditioning days (days 3 and 4), mice received a saline injection (i.p.) in the mornings and were confined in one conditioning chamber for 30 min. Four hours later, mice received gabapentin (30 mg/kg, i.p.) for CPP or CNO (5 mg/kg, i.p.) for CPA and were placed into the opposite chamber for 30 min. Chamber assignment was counterbalanced. On test day (post-conditioning, day 5), mice were placed in the CPP/CPA box and were allowed to explore all chambers freely for 15 min. The time in chambers was recorded to determine chamber preference. CPP difference score was calculated as the test time in drug-paired chamber – baseline time in the drug-paired chamber. CPA score was calculated as the time difference between the CNO-paired and the saline-paired chambers at test time – baseline.

Verification of hM4D(Gi)-mCherry expression

Anesthetized animals were perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde from the left ventricle of the exposed heart. The brain was removed and post-fixed for 4 h with 4% paraformaldehyde and dehydrated with 15% and 30% sucrose for 24 h in each solution. Brains were sectioned coronally at 20 μm thickness and DAPI stained. To evaluate the expression of mCherry, sectioned samples were examined and photographed on an Echo RVL-100-M microscope (San Diego, California, USA) [7]. Animals that did not exhibit bilateral ACC expression of hM4D(Gi)-mCherry were excluded from the study.

Verification of CRISPR/Cas9 editing of KOR

To verify that KOR CRISPR/Cas9 effectively reduced KOR expression in the targeted brain region, we quantified KOR expression in the ACC brain punches using western blotting. Animals were euthanized, the brains were quickly dissected and 2 mm coronal sections at approximately 1.4 mm anterior to bregma containing the ACC were cut on ice using a brain matrix. A 2 mm punch of the ACC was collected and frozen in liquid nitrogen. The tissue was homogenized with lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors, phosphatase inhibitors, and benzonase. Extracted proteins were separated by electrophoresis and transferred to PVDF membranes 0.45 μm (Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). The membranes were blocked with TBST (50 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween 20, pH 7.4), 5% non-fat dry milk, then incubated with the rabbit anti-KOR antibody (Cat# sc-9112, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA USA) and mouse anti-βIII-tubulin antibody (Cat# G7121, Promega, Madison, WI, USA) in TBST, 5% bovine serum albumin. Blots were incubated in Alexa Fluor 555 anti rabbit and Alexa Fluor 647 anti mouse secondary antibodies and fluorescence was detected by the Odyssey Fc Imager (LI-COR, Lincoln, NE, USA).

Statistical analysis

The data follow a normal distribution and were analyzed by Student t test or two-way repeated-measures ANOVA followed by Tukey’s or Sidak’s test. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10. Data are presented as mean ± SEM and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Summary of statistical analysis is reported in Supplementary Table 1.

Results

To evaluate the effect chemogenetic Gi-mediated inhibition of KOR-expressing ACC neurons on pain and sleep, we injected AAV8-hSyn-DIO-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry bilaterally into the ACC of KOR-cre mice (Fig. 1A, B). The assessment of pain and sleep started four weeks post-AVV injection to ensure adequate expression of hM4D(Gi)-mCherry, a Gi-coupled designer receptor (DREADD) activated by the synthetic ligand clozapine-N-oxide (CNO). Post hoc verification of ACC expression was confirmed by the presence of mCherry fluorescence (Fig. 1C). Chemogenetic Gi-mediated inhibition of KOR-expressing ACC neurons with i.p. injection of CNO did not elicit mechanical allodynia (Fig. 1D) but produced CPA in KOR-creHet mice (Fig. 1E and Fig. S2), suggesting selective induction of negative affect without changes in tactile thresholds.

In a separate cohort, after AAV injection, KOR-cre mice received implantation of EEG/EMG head-mounts to allow recording of sleep architecture. Baseline sleep patterns were recorded for a 24-h cycle while the mice had access to water (day 0). Mice exhibited a typical wake/sleep cycle and sleep architecture, spending most of their time awake (~73%) during the dark phase, and asleep (~72%) during the light phase (Fig. S3A–C). We observed no differences in the number of wake episodes, non-rapid eye movement (NREM) episodes, or NREM mean duration between KOR-creHet and KOR-creWT under water-only conditions (Fig. 1F–H). Following the baseline recordings, mice received CNO in their drinking water (approximately 20 mg/kg in 24 h) for a 6-day experimental period. The consumption of water with CNO for KOR-creHet (3.5 ± 0.3 mL) and KOR-creWT (3.2 ± 0.2 mL) mice were not significantly different. Additionally, there was no significant difference observed when comparing the consumption of water with CNO to the consumption when only water was available for both groups (3.3 ± 0.3 mL for KOR-creHet and 3.1 ± 0.1 mL for KOR-creWT). EEG/EMG recordings were conducted on days 4 and 6 post-CNO onset. CNO treatment did not change the wake/sleep cycle or total, NREM or REM sleep during the dark and light phases in KOR-creHet mice as compared to KOR-creWT mice (Fig. S4A–F). However, persistent chemogenetic Gi-mediated inhibition of KOR-expressing ACC neurons in KOR-creHet mice induced sleep fragmentation characterized by an increase in the number of wake and NREM episodes during the light and dark phases (Fig. 1I, J and Fig. S5A–D) and, consequently, shorter mean duration of NREM episodes (Fig. 1K and Fig. S5A–D) on day 4 of CNO treatment as compared to KOR-creWT. Corroborating with these findings, we also observed sleep fragmentation on day 6 of CNO treatment in KOR-creHet compared to KOR-creWT mice as evidenced by increased number of wake and NREM episodes and decrease in the mean duration of NREM bouts (Fig. 1I–K and Fig. S5A–D). These data reveal that KOR-creHet mice were unable to sustain sleep, presenting fragmented sleep after chemogenetic Gi-mediated inhibition of KOR-expressing ACC neurons. Together, the data show that chemogenetic inhibition of KOR-expressing neurons in the ACC is sufficient to promote negative affect and sleep disruption, i.e., sleep fragmentation, in the absence of neuropathic injury.

Previously, we have reported that partial sciatic nerve ligation (PSNL)-induced chronic pain promotes sleep disruption [7]. Thus, we evaluated the involvement of ACC KOR signaling on chronic pain-induced sleep disruption. First, to determine if the negative affect associated with chronic pain was due to ACC KOR signaling, we microinjected a CRISPR/Cas9 plasmid with a guide RNA (gRNA) targeting KOR (i.e., KOR CRISPR) bilaterally into the ACC (Fig. 2A, B) followed by PSNL surgery. Consistent with our previous studies [7, 8], bilateral administration of KOR-targeted CRISPR–Cas9 plasmid into the ACC produced downregulation of 37.8 ± 18.4% of KOR expression (data not shown). After 7–15 days from PSNL, we evaluated mechanical allodynia and CPP to systemic administration of gabapentin. In comparison to baseline measurements, PSNL elicited robust hindpaw allodynia that was not affected by KOR CRISPR editing (Fig. 2C). However, KOR deletion prevented the CPP induced by gabapentin in mice with PSNL injury (Fig. 2D and Fig. S6), suggesting that the ACC KOR signaling mediates the negative affect, but not physiologically critical sensory features, of pain.

For sleep studies, naïve mice were injected with KOR CRISPR (or control) plasmid in the ACC to down-regulate the expression of KOR in this region. Mice were implanted with EEG/EMG head-mounts followed by PSNL (or sham) surgery and sleep was recorded (Fig. 2A, bottom timeline). Representative hypnograms are depicted in Fig. S7A–D. The amount of total and NREM was not significantly influenced by PSNL surgery (Fig. 2E, F). However, PSNL-induced chronic pain notably reduced REM sleep, especially during the light phase when mice are predominantly asleep (Fig. 2G and Fig. S7C). Downregulation of KOR into the ACC partially recovered PSNL-induced decrease in REM sleep (Fig. 2G and Fig. S7D). Importantly, in sham-operated mice, KOR CRISPR did not alter the amount of total, NREM, and REM sleep (Fig. 2E–G). Further, compared to sham, PSNL-induced chronic pain promoted robust increase in the number of wake and NREM episodes (Fig. 2H, I and Fig. S7A, C), as well as reduction in the mean duration of NREM bouts (Fig. 2J and Fig. S7A, C) during either the light or dark phases, reflecting sleep fragmentation. ACC KOR CRISPR editing significantly reduced the number of wake and NREM episodes (Fig. 2H, I and Fig. S7D) and, consequently, increased the mean duration of NREM bouts (Fig. 2J and Fig. S7D) in PSNL-operated mice, indicating that deletion of KOR into the ACC normalized chronic pain-induced sleep fragmentation. In sham-operated mice, the number of wake and NREM episodes (Fig. 2H, I and Fig. S7A, B) nor the mean duration of NREM bouts (Fig. 2J and Fig. S7A, B) were affected by KOR CRISPR injection.

Discussion

In the present study we showed that (a) chemogenetic Gi-mediated inhibition of KOR-expressing ACC neurons can produce aversiveness in uninjured mice along with fragmented sleep and (b) that negative affect, reduction in REM sleep and fragmented sleep observed in a model of chronic neuropathic pain was normalized by CRISPR/Cas9 downregulation of ACC KOR. Our data reveal previously unknown roles of ACC dynorphin/KOR signaling mediating negative affect and sleep disruption under both physiological and pathological conditions. The ACC is an association cortex with established links to negative emotional states in humans including aversiveness and reward. In early studies, cingulotomy was reported to improve emotional states in patients with psychiatric conditions including attention and compulsive disorders as well as the negative affect of chronic pain. Data from these cases are consistent with the conclusion that the cingulate cortex is critical for disrupted emotion in a variety of pathological conditions. The ACC has also been associated with the increase in pain induced by sleep restriction [5]. Furthermore, pyramidal neurons in the ACC play a crucial role in the development of chronic pain-induced insomnia [11]. Thus, the ACC appears to play a pivotal role in the detrimental vicious spiral of pain and sleep disruption. Previous studies, however, evaluated only the physiological sensory features of pain, without considering the negative emotional component evaluated here. Futher, potential involvement of KOR/dynorphin cingulate circuits and a possible causal link between negative emotional conditions and sleep disruption have not previously been established.

Consistent with previous pharmacological studies, we found that selective chemogenetic Gi-mediated inhibition of ACC neurons expressing KOR is sufficient to produce aversive states that could be detected in naïve mice with learning paradigms. Critically, sustained activation of these ACC KOR circuits also disrupted sleep in uninjured mice. Sleep disruption was characterized largely by fragmentation with increased numbers of wake episodes and shorter periods of non-REM sleep. These findings suggest that activation of ACC KOR signaling is sufficient for both negative affect and sleep disruption but do not establish causality. For this reason, we evaluated the role of ACC KOR in mice with pathologically-induced negative affect and sleep disruption resulting from chronic pain.

Many patients with chronic pain report sleep disturbance [12, 13] that has been linked to pain severity [14]. The relative hazard ratio of sleep disturbances in patients with chronic pain is two to five times higher than in healthy individuals [15–19]. Conversely, patients with insomnia disorders have high comorbidity with chronic pain besides higher sensitivity to nociceptive stimulation than those without insomnia [20]. A vicious cycle therefore exists between chronic pain and sleep where pain can disrupt sleep while sleep disruption provides an even stronger enhancement of pain. Human studies suggest that fragmented sleep is especially important in influencing pain thresholds [21]. We previously reported that chronic pain produced by PSNL was associated with negative affect and sleep disruption that could be normalized by systemic administration of KOR antagonists [7]. These findings led us to suggest that chronic pain-induced sleep disruption is adaptive in promoting increased vigilance that can assure survival in the setting of injury [7]. However, accumulating sleep debt may then become maladaptive resulting in reinforcing the negative emotional component of pain and promoting chronic pain.

In agreement with these findings [7], we observed that PSNL-induced chronic pain promoted reduction in REM sleep, fragmented sleep, and persistent negative affect demonstrated by rewarding behavior (i.e., CPP) to gabapentin, a first-line drug used clinically in patients with chronic pain. Critically, CRISPR/Cas9 editing of ACC KOR expression inhibited both chronic-pain related negative affect and sleep disruption. This finding suggests that activation of ACC KOR signaling is also necessary for promoting negative emotion, reduction in REM sleep, and fragmented sleep and provides a causal link between engagement of ACC KOR circuits and these behavioral effects. Importantly, CRISPR/Cas9 editing of ACC KOR did not affect the sensory qualities of pain as allodynia in PSNL mice was preserved.

Collectively, we suggest that ACC KOR signaling can serve as an integrating mechanism of both negative affect and sleep disruption. An intriguing observation is that clearance of toxic cellular byproducts by the glymphatic system only occurs during non-REM sleep [22]. Moreover, REM sleep, the stage of sleep in which vivid dreams occur, is implicated in processing and consolidation of emotional memories [23]. Persistent sleep disturbance could therefore promote a wide variety of disorders including those characterized by negative affect such as depression, anxiety disorders, and chronic pain. Inhibition of KOR signaling in pathological states may therefore represent a therapeutic strategy that can stabilize emotional states and normalize sleep disruption in many diverse conditions beyond chronic pain. KOR antagonists including aticaprant and navacaprant are currently in advanced clinical trials for the treatment of depression, anxiety disorders, and anhedonia. Whether these drugs could also normalize sleep disruption can be assessed in future clinical studies.
