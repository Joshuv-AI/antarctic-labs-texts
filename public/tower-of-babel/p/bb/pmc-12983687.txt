Two distinct healthcare utilisation trajectories prior to a COPD diagnosis in a multi-ethnic Asian population: a comparison of transient and chronic users
Authors: Sheryl Hui-Xian Ng, Pei Yee Tiew, Sanjay Haresh Chotirmall, Wenjia Chen, John Abisheganaden
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) imposes substantial clinical and economic burdens. Early detection can allow for monitoring and timely treatment to slow its progression. Tracking of patient trajectories prior to their diagnosis can inform the timing and targeting of interventions. We aimed to identify pre-diagnosis healthcare utilisation patterns in a COPD cohort and profile the associated subgroups.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients with a new inpatient or specialist outpatient clinic (SOC) diagnosis of COPD from 2018 to 2019 in a regional health system in Singapore. Their healthcare utilisation, expenditure and diagnoses from the 3 years prior to diagnosis were extracted. Patients were classified into subgroups with different expenditure and utilisation patterns using Bayesian mixture modelling and compared against a propensity score–matched non-respiratory control group.Results1171 patients with COPD were matched to a control and classified as either chronic (n=688) or transient healthcare users (n=483) prior to a first COPD diagnosis. Chronic users had increasing utilisation over time across all settings, recording multiple SOC visits (median, 25th–75th percentile: 10, 5–20). Transient users had low utilisation throughout, reporting fewer SOC visits than controls (transient: 0, 0–1, control: 2, 0–9). The prevalence of hypertension or hyperlipidaemia was >50% in chronic users, >30% in controls and <30% in transient users.ConclusionEarly detection strategies should focus on case-finding among patients with known risk factors in SOCs for referral to respiratory care and outreach to socially disadvantaged communities to facilitate timely access to healthcare.Keywords: COPD epidemiology

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) affected 213 million people worldwide in 2021.1 Within the health system, screening, diagnosis and management of COPD poses a substantial burden, notably higher among Asia-Pacific countries.2 Inpatient costs of advanced COPD are particularly high, with approximately one-third of patients readmitted within 3 months of discharge and nearly 20% experiencing deterioration and death within a year.3 As COPD is not curable or reversible in this stage, early identification and intervention are critical to slow disease progression through monitoring and timely treatment. Several diagnostic tools have been proposed as an alternative to traditional spirometry for facilitating early detection of COPD, such as handheld devices, screening questionnaires and risk prediction models.4 However, these strategies on their own do not provide information on when and how to intervene on high-risk patients and must be integrated meaningfully as part of a care pathway for clinical utility. A randomised trial that identified patients with undiagnosed COPD through questionnaires for immediate referral to either a pulmonologist or primary care practitioner showed improvements in symptoms and disease-specific quality at 1 year for both groups, demonstrating the potential impact of a care pathway.5

To develop such a pathway for early detection, tracking of patient trajectories over time prior to diagnosis could provide valuable in-depth insight into optimal junctures for deployment of existing case-finding or predictive tools. Jones et al reported that 85% of patients with COPD in the UK seen from 1990 to 2009 had presented to primary care for a lower respiratory consultation or chest radiograph in the 5 years prior to diagnosis.6 Similarly, Johnson et al found that patients with COPD seen from 1996 to 2015 in British Columbia, Canada, had interacted with pharmacists, primary care and specialists up to 5 years prior to diagnosis and were more likely to do so than patients without COPD within the final 2 years.7 By identifying prior encounters of patients with relevant risk factors in their pre-disease period, subsequent efforts can be focused in these settings to identify and intervene on patients earlier in their disease trajectory.

Further, given the salience of heterogeneity in COPD, accounting for the possibility of variation in such trajectories would be pertinent to ensure eventual interventions are relevant and targeted. Given the established impact of comorbidity-related phenotypes on outcomes among patients, exploration of their roles in the pre-diagnosis phase would be highly relevant. A systematic review of COPD phenotypes identified using machine learning in 2020 reported groups with concurrent cardiovascular disease, diabetes or depression with differentiated mortality risk.8 Tiew et al identified five phenotypes within a South-East Asian Chinese cohort, of which the groups with cardiovascular disease and a history of tuberculosis were associated with increased mortality risk.9 As no clear subgroups have been established in pre-COPD, identifying subgroups with varying characteristics and medical history within this pre-diagnosis stage would provide novel insight into the optimal approaches to identify and intervene on different profiles of patients.

In addition to the knowledge gaps identified, pre-diagnosis trajectories have not been explored thus far in any Asian populations, despite distinct differences in risk factors driving COPD between Western and Asian populations.10 Hence, we aimed to identify distinctive patterns of pre-diagnosis healthcare utilisation trends among a newly diagnosed COPD cohort in Singapore with the application of Bayesian mixture modelling and profile the patient subgroups associated with each pattern in terms of socio-demographics and medical history. A non-respiratory control was included to contrast these utilisation patterns of patients with COPD against those of the general patient cohort. Our findings will add new knowledge of pre-diagnosis profiles in a multi-ethnic Asian cohort and inform the development of interventions specific to characteristics of the subgroups for targeted intervention.

Methods

Study design

A retrospective cohort study was conducted to identify distinct patterns of healthcare expenditure trajectories in a 3-year period preceding a diagnosis of COPD (online supplemental Appendix 1), leveraging an administrative database of a regional health system (RHS) in Singapore.

Setting and data source

The public healthcare system in Singapore is organised into three RHSs, each consisting of acute and community hospitals, national specialty centres and clinics providing primary care.11 The National Healthcare Group (NHG) RHS includes two acute hospitals (Tan Tock Seng Hospital (TTSH) and Khoo Teck Puat Hospital (KTPH)), a psychiatric hospital (Institute of Mental Health), as well as six polyclinics (outpatient clinics providing primary care). The NHG RHS also serves as the regional health manager of all residents in the central and north regions of the city-state, providing care to approximately 1.5 million residents, or a quarter of the resident population.12 To support health profiling of these residents, the Population Health Data Mart (PHDM) was a data warehouse set up with the objective to link de-identified individual-level medical, social, lifestyle and economic data of all residents seen by institutions and providers under NHG.13

Variables extracted

Data were extracted from the PHDM for the 2015–2019 period. Information on patients’ socio-demographic characteristics was extracted, and smoking history and survival status were recorded. Socio-demographic characteristics included patients’ age, sex, ethnicity, marital status and income level, measured by the use of subsidies under the Community Health Assist Scheme (CHAS). CHAS provides additional subsidy for chronic disease treatment for citizens with declared household monthly income below a pre-determined threshold, that is, below the bottom 20th percentile of the resident household income,14 15 thereby serving as a proxy indicator of whether a patient resides in a low-income household. As smoking history assessments were often recorded for smoking cessation monitoring, patients who did not have a smoking history assessed were assumed to be non-smokers.

Data on patients’ healthcare encounters, in terms of volume, expenditure and associated diagnosis codes across inpatient admissions (IP), specialist outpatient clinic (SOC) visits, emergency department (ED) attendances and polyclinic visits, were also extracted. Inpatient admissions included both emergency and elective admissions, as well as day procedures. SOC and polyclinic visits reported were consultation visits to medical and allied health professionals, excluding visits solely for diagnostic and radiological investigations. Healthcare expenditure extracted was the bill to the patient before government subsidy to reflect the extent of resources dedicated for treatment in that particular encounter.

Study population

Patients with a new diagnosis of COPD in TTSH and KTPH from January 2017 to December 2018 were identified. This study period was selected to circumvent the impact of COVID-19 on healthcare utilisation trends, and we opted to focus on patients diagnosed in the hospitals where they would likely access spirometry for confirmation of COPD. A validated algorithm, which previously recorded a sensitivity of 85.0% and specificity of 78.4%, was applied to identify patients with COPD from administrative databases (International Classification of Disease, 10th version (ICD-10): J41.xx, J43.xx and J44.xx). Under this algorithm, patients with COPD were identified as those with at least one IP or a SOC consultation for COPD.16 Additionally, they were required not to have any history of COPD or asthma (ICD-10: J45) within 5 years prior to the visit date to ensure they were newly diagnosed with COPD.

Subsequently, a non-respiratory control group was identified from a random sample of patients who were seen in the two hospitals during the same period and were not diagnosed with COPD, asthma or any respiratory condition. Propensity score matching was performed to match patients without respiratory problems to patients with COPD, through nearest-neighbour matching with a calliper distance based on sex, age at visit and visit setting.17 If there were multiple control patients with the same matching distance for a patient with COPD, one control patient was randomly selected. To align reference dates for examination of pre-diagnosis trends, the date of COPD diagnosis during the study period was used as the index visit date for patients with COPD, whereas the date of the control visit where matching was successful was used as the index date for the control patient.

Statistical analysis

For longitudinal trends of healthcare encounters, information on all healthcare encounters within the hospitals and in primary care that occurred up to 3 years prior to a patient’s index visit was extracted. This was informed by observations in Lewis et al, where>90% of COPD diagnoses were recorded in this period among patients admitted for exacerbations, and additional extension of the lookback period added minimal incremental information.18 The number of inpatient admissions, SOC visits, ED attendances and polyclinic visits was described cumulatively for the 3-year period, as well as the final quarter, or 90-day interval, before diagnosis.

To accurately classify patients with COPD into groups based on their pre-diagnosis healthcare utilisation trends, accounting for patient-level random variations, a robust, Bayesian mixture modelling (BMM) approach was adopted. The BMM allows for joint modelling of multiple outcomes over time, by assuming a common correlation within each patient over repeated measures and across the outcomes, and clustering of patients into groups based on this correlation.19 This approach has successfully identified children with distinct patterns of high early-life healthcare utilisation that predispose them to an asthma diagnosis in Canada.20

Outcomes modelled were the probability of inpatient admission, probability of ED attendance, number of SOC visits, number of polyclinic visits and healthcare expenditure incurred across these visits for each patient. To determine model specifications in terms of fixed and random effects for each outcome as input for the BMM, generalised linear mixed effect models were first fitted and compared based on their Akaike information criterion value. The probabilities of inpatient admission and ED attendance were modelled with a logit link, and the number of SOC and polyclinic visits was modelled with a Poisson distribution with a log link. The healthcare expenditure data first underwent the Box–Cox transformation to mitigate its skewness,21 before it was modelled with a Gaussian distribution with identity link.

Estimation of parameters for the BMM was conducted using Markov chain Monte Carlo simulations over 4000 iterations after a burn-in of 2000 iterations and thinning every 200 iterations. The best model was identified to minimise the penalised expected deviance value, and models corresponding to 2–4 groups were compared with identifying the best model. Patients were assigned to groups based on the higher posterior probability of membership in each group, and the posterior mean estimate for each outcome was reported. To ensure robustness of trends found, sensitivity analyses were conducted by repeating the BMM incorporating patients with COPD who were not matched to a control, and subgroup analyses were conducted by performing the BMM for the ethnic majority and minority groups separately.

After identifying groups within patients with COPD, the above-mentioned socio-demographics, diagnoses and healthcare utilisation measures were profiled and compared across the COPD and control groups. Survival status was reflected as 1-year and 5-year mortality rates from the index date. Diagnosis codes associated with each visit were grouped into comorbidities based on the broad disease chapters within the ICD-10 system (online supplemental Appendix 2). Smoking history was augmented to include patients’ prior diagnosis of tobacco use disorder. Additionally, respiratory comorbidities were identified and grouped based on the Agency for Healthcare Quality Clinical Classification software.22 Comparisons of socio-demographic and comorbidity profiles between the COPD and control groups were conducted using Wilcoxon rank-sum test and Fisher’s exact tests for continuous and categorical characteristics, respectively. To account for potential multiple testing arising from the large number of hypothesis tests performed, Bonferroni’s correction was used to correct the threshold for statistical significance to 0.001. All statistical analyses were conducted using R and RStudio, with the BMM performed using the package mixAK.23

Results

Cohort characteristics

From 2018 to 2019, 1552 patients were diagnosed with COPD, of which 1171 (75.5%) were successfully matched to a control patient without respiratory problem. The 381 patients with COPD who were unmatched were inpatients, older and mostly male, and a larger proportion died within 1 year (online supplemental Appendix 3 and 4). After matching, the COPD and control groups demonstrated similar distributions of age, sex, ethnicity, marital status and income level; however, the proportion of patients with known smoking history and the 1-year mortality rate were higher in the COPD group (smoking history: COPD, 45.5%; control, 14.3%, p<0.001; 1-year mortality: COPD, 14.7%; controls, 6.5%, p<0.001) (online supplemental Appendix 5).

Comparisons of model specifications for each outcome are detailed in online supplemental Appendix 6. The probabilities of inpatient admission and ED attendance were modelled with variations in the final quarter before diagnosis as a fixed effect, as well as a random intercept. The number of SOC and polyclinic visits was modelled with a random intercept and random slope for the effect of time. The healthcare expenditure accounted for the variation in the final quarter as a fixed effect and contained a random intercept and slope for time effect.

Two distinctive healthcare utilisation paths to chronic obstructive pulmonary disease

With these model specifications, the best-fitting BMM identified two groups within the COPD cohort, as this classification reported the lowest deviance value (figure 1, online supplemental Appendix 7). The first group of 483 patients (41.2%), representing transient healthcare users (‘transient users’), on average had low utilisation across all care settings over the 3-year period. The estimated probability of admission or ED attendance, as well as the number of visits to the SOC or polyclinic, was near zero (range over 12 quarters: probability of admission, 0.01–0.04; probability of ED attendance, 0.02–0.11; SOC, 0.0004–0.07 visits; polyclinic, 0.001–0.03 visits; expenditure, S$0.49–3.45).

COPD, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

In contrast, the second group of 688 (58.8%) patients, representing chronic healthcare users (‘chronic users’), had some predicted level of utilisation across all settings, with a slow increase over time (range over 11 quarters: probability of admission, 0.07–0.15; probability of ED attendance, 0.06–0.13; SOC, 0.44–0.93 visits; polyclinic, 0.21–0.36 visits; expenditure, S$ 21.86–$105.88). These increased distinctly in the final 3-month period (estimate (95% credible interval): probability of admission, 0.26 (0.22 to 0.31); probability of ED attendance, 0.31 (0.26 to 0.36); SOC, 1.00 (1.00 to 1.00) visits; polyclinic, 0.38 (0.38 to 0.38); expenditure, S$511.29 (S$ 408.93 to 639.76)).

Sensitivity analyses incorporating unmatched patients with COPD showed similarities in observed healthcare utilisation and expenditure trends between the COPD cohort and the 381 patients without a matched control, as well as in estimated posterior mean trajectories from the BMMs on the 1552 identified patients and the analysed 1171 patients (online supplemental Appendix 8). Subgroup analyses of ethnic majority and minority groups replicated the classification into acute and chronic user groups within each subgroup, with both groups exhibiting similar posterior mean trajectories (online supplemental Appendix 9).

In the 3 years prior to COPD diagnosis, the proportion of chronic users seeking care at every setting was higher than in transient users and control patients (table 1, p<0.001). Almost all chronic users had at least one SOC visit for any condition (94.2%), and most (75.1%) had at least one inpatient admission. In contrast, only a third of transient users visited either setting (inpatient, 30.4%; SOC, 35.0%), which was less frequent in comparison to controls (inpatient, 39.2%; SOC, 61.4%). Chronic users also had multiple inpatient, SOC and polyclinic visits (median, IQR: inpatient, 2, 1–4; SOC, 10, 5–20; polyclinic, 6, 0–14). In the final 3 months prior to diagnosis, almost two in three chronic users had an SOC visit, higher than the proportion in transient users and controls (63.4% vs 21.9% and 26.3%, both p<0.001). In contrast, transient users had few visits across all settings over the 3 years (all 0, 0–1 visits), which were fewer than in controls (inpatient, 0, 0–1; ED, 0, 0–1; SOC, 2, 0–9; polyclinic, 1, 0–10, all p<0.001). In the final 3 months, the proportion of transient users who had a polyclinic visit remained lower than among control patients (transient, 15.7%; control, 32.2%, p<0.001), but the proportion with an inpatient admission was higher (12.8% vs 6.5%, p<0.001).

Comparing demographic profiles in the two pre-chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and control groups

Comparing multimorbidity patterns in the two pre-chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and control groups

Examining the prevalence of comorbidity in the 3 years prior to diagnosis, chronic users generally had higher comorbidity than transient users and control patients across most categories reported (figure 2, p<0.001). Endocrine, nutritional and metabolic (ENM) conditions were the most prevalent, with 73.0% of chronic users having a history of such a condition, whereas only 18.8% of transient users and 46.9% of control patients did so. The most common ENM condition was hyperlipidaemia, where more than half of the chronic users were diagnosed (chronic, 54.5%; transient, 7.5%; control, 37.2%) (online supplemental appendix 10). >7 in 10 chronic users had a circulatory condition, whereas only 2 in 10 transient users and 4 in 10 control patients did so (chronic, 70.8%; transient, 20.9%; control, 45.8%). The most common circulatory condition was hypertension, where 6 in 10 chronic users had a diagnosis (chronic, 60.2%; transient, 12.2%; control, 37.8%) (online supplemental appendix 10).

Discussion

Among patients who were diagnosed with COPD in two tertiary hospitals in Singapore, two distinct trajectories of healthcare utilisation trajectories were identified over the 3 years prior to their diagnosis. Chronic users, accounting for 58.8% of the cohort, tended to be older, ever married and known smokers. They often had substantial healthcare utilisation over the 3-year period before an increase in the final 3 months, and half of them had pre-existing chronic conditions such as hypertension and hyperlipidaemia. In contrast, transient users, accounting for the remaining 41.2%, were more likely to be younger and living in a low-income household. Most of these patients had no utilisation in the same 3-year period, with less than a quarter recording a visit in the final 3 months prior to diagnosis. Comorbidity prevalence was also low in this group. Nonetheless, both groups had similar mortality rates of 15% at 1 year from diagnosis.

More than half of the patients with COPD identified were chronic users, and these patients often had multiple SOC visits for various conditions over the 3 years prior to COPD diagnosis. As alluded to in similar studies of pre-diagnosis patterns,6 7 identifying visits where these patients are likely to seek care could facilitate case-finding efforts. Patients attending SOC consultations related to smoking or ENM conditions can be prioritised for administration of case-finding questionnaires,4 enabling early detection and referral to respiratory care with minimal incremental cost. In contrast, only a third of transient users ever had a SOC visit or inpatient admission in the 3 years prior to diagnosis, and they were less likely to have other comorbid conditions, even in comparison with the non-COPD control group. As 7 in 10 of these transient users did not have any history of respiratory conditions, their first presenting visit to the hospital would already be for an established diagnosis of COPD, highlighting the importance of outreach through community and social resources for this group.

Moreover, transient users had lower primary care utilisation than controls, and a larger proportion of them were categorised as having low income based on household income, in comparison with both chronic users and controls. In Singapore, lower socio-economic status, proxied by residence in public rental housing, has been associated with poorer health outcomes, arising from various drivers including a lower propensity to participate in preventive health screening and a higher likelihood of utilising hospitals for acute care. This suggests that a subset of transient users may be living with disease but are presenting at the hospitals late, only when they experience an exacerbation. Case-finding in environments where individuals may not seek timely care due to social factors could help the health systems detect patients at a less severe stage of disease and allow patients to access care earlier. Healthier SG, a reform of the healthcare system in Singapore in 2020 to shift from curative to preventive care, would facilitate the connection of residents to primary care and community resources, thereby increasing the likelihood of identifying at-risk residents earlier.24

Most chronic users also had a history of chronic conditions. The high prevalence of ENM and circulatory conditions seen aligns with findings of a systematic review on metabolic-related conditions in COPD.25 With 1 in 10 in Singapore being diabetic,26 identifying and intervening on respiratory risk factors among patients with a history of metabolic diseases could have an appreciable impact. Further, Chen et al reported that comorbidity-attributable excess costs were the highest for circulatory diseases,27 highlighting the substantial cost impact of timely intervention to prevent progression of COPD for these patients with concurrent disease.

Specific to respiratory disease, one in four chronic users had either a history of pneumonia or LRD prior to COPD, far lower than the estimate by Jones et al.6 However, as a history of respiratory conditions has been associated with poorer outcomes after diagnosis, such as severe exacerbations and mortality,28 29 patients with this risk factor at point of diagnosis must nonetheless be monitored and managed appropriately. Notably, as having a visit for any COPD subtype in the 5 years prior, including chronic bronchitis, was an exclusion criterion for the COPD group, we were unable to assess the full extent of preluding respiratory conditions in patients in either group. In addition, while Tiew et al reported on five phenotypes among Southeast Asian patients with COPD, including one with a history of tuberculosis,9 we did not see a substantial number of patients with established tuberculosis, potentially due to the scope of this study being constrained to the 3 years immediately before COPD, instead of lifetime diagnoses.

Our inclusion of a non-respiratory control group spotlighted distinctive features of patients with COPD in comparison with other patient groups presenting at the hospitals. By matching patients without respiratory problems to the COPD cohort patients on age, sex and visit setting, we were able to create a comparator group with similar demographic profile who were presenting with conditions other than COPD. While we unsurprisingly identified a subgroup of patients with COPD who had an elevated comorbidity burden relative to the controls, we also detected a second subgroup who incurred less healthcare utilisation, illustrating the peculiarity of these transient users. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that there are caveats in anchoring our comparisons against such a hospital control group, where almost half recorded prior metabolic or circulatory conditions, and one in five were deceased within 5 years. Furthermore, this remains a pragmatic reference point for our comparisons with the COPD cohort, as it would be impracticable to describe definitively an average patient without respiratory condition presenting to the hospital.

Strengths of this study include the use of validated diagnostic criteria to facilitate comparability with other studies, as well as a propensity score–matched control cohort to contextualise the elevated comorbidity and healthcare utilisation among patients with COPD in comparison to a general patient cohort. Our findings would be generalisable to other health systems where diagnosis of COPD remains sited within the hospital, and case detection is low in the primary care setting due to lack of spirometry use. However, this study also has limitations. We were unable to incorporate information on visits to private hospitals, other RHS and private practitioners in primary care, and the mortality data reported was based on deaths known to the health system. However, the public system accounts for 80% of all inpatient admissions nationwide,11 and the NHG RHS alone accounts for a quarter of Singapore’s population.12 We defined newly diagnosed patients as those without a COPD diagnosis in the 5 years prior, though we acknowledge the possibility that patients could have been diagnosed prior to this study period. The smoking history reported in this study reflects those who had recorded interventions for smoking and may be an under-estimation of the true extent of smoking prevalence in the cohort. Furthermore, with the lack of spirometry data in this study, we are unable to verify whether all patients had spirometry-confirmed diagnoses and acknowledge the possibility that this may not be true for a subset of patients. Nonetheless, as the diagnostic criteria employed in this study had high sensitivity and specificity in identifying patients with COPD from health administrative data,16 the impact of misclassification due to lack of spirometry would be minimal on our findings. We were also unable to stage the disease as per the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) criteria and hence cannot verify if patients have lung impairment comparable to the pre-COPD archetype. Subsequent work to compare the pre-diagnosis healthcare utilisation patterns across GOLD stages and other measures of lung function would be invaluable to validate the classification of pre-diagnosis groups proposed in this study. Lastly, while we have incorporated Bonferroni’s correction to account for multiple hypothesis testing, we nonetheless acknowledge the possibility of a heightened type I error in detecting between-cluster differences.

Conclusion

We identified two distinct groups of patients with COPD in a multi-ethnic Asian population based on their healthcare utilisation patterns in the past 3 years prior to the first COPD diagnosis. One group demonstrated high comorbidity and frequent healthcare use over the 3 years leading up to diagnosis, whereas the other only presented to the health system in months before the diagnosis. The first subgroup should be prioritised for targeted case-finding strategies in outpatient settings; community outreach to the second subgroup could ensure contact of these patients with the healthcare system, facilitating timelier access to care. Insights from this study can complement existing predictive tools for COPD diagnosis and support their integration into clinical care pathways for early detection. Future work will explore incorporating trajectory-based information to enhance the predictive performance and clinical utility of early detection tools.
