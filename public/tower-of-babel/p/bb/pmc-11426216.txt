Down syndrome and associated atrioventricular septal defects in a nationwide Norwegian cohort: Prevalence, time trends, and outcomes
Authors: Cecilie Bryn Nordklev, Ola Gjesdal, Nina Gunnes, Henrik Holmstrøm, Nils‐Halvdan Morken, Siri Vangen, Vasilis Sitras
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe prevalence of Down syndrome (DS) is approximately 1 per 1000 births and is influenced by increasing maternal age over the last few decades. DS is strongly associated with congenital heart defects (CHDs), especially atrioventricular septal defect (AVSD). Our objectives were to investigate the prevalence of live‐born infants with DS having a severe CHD in the Norwegian population over the last 20 years and compare outcomes in infants with AVSD with and without DS.Material and MethodsInformation on all births from January 1, 2000 to December 31, 2019 was obtained from the Medical Birth Registry of Norway. We also obtained data on all infants with severe CHDs in Norway registered in Oslo University Hospital's Clinical Registry for Congenital Heart Defects during 2000–2019 and accessed individual‐level patient data from the electronic hospital records of selected cases. Infants with AVSD and DS were compared to infants with AVSD without chromosomal defects. Crude and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) of infant mortality and need for surgery during the first year of life, with associated 95% confidence intervals (CIs), were estimated by logistic regression.ResultsA total of 1 177 926 infants were live‐born in Norway during the study period. Among these, 1456 (0.1%) had DS. The prevalence of infants with DS having a severe CHDs was relatively stable, with a mean of 17 cases per year. The most common CHD associated with DS was AVSD (44.4%). Infants with AVSD and DS were more likely to have cardiac intervention during their first year of life compared to infants with AVSD without chromosomal defects (adjusted OR [aOR]: 2.52; 95% CI 1.27, 4.98). However, we observed no difference in infant mortality during first year of life between the two groups (aOR: 1.08; 95% CI 0.43, 2.70).ConclusionsThe prevalence of live‐born infants with severe CHDs and DS has been stable in Norway across 20 years. Infants with AVSD and DS did not have higher risk of mortality during their first year of life compared to infants with AVSD without chromosomal defects, despite a higher risk of operative intervention.

INTRODUCTION

Down syndrome (DS) is the most common chromosomal disorder among live births, with a prevalence of 0.16%. 1 It is associated with cognitive impairment in addition to physical comorbidities. 2 Moreover, it is strongly associated with congenital heart defects (CHDs), in particular atrioventricular septal defect (AVSD). 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Despite an increasing proportion of pregnancy terminations due to DS, the prevalence of live births with DS remains unchanged in Europe. 6 However, some countries, such as France, Italy, and Denmark, have reported decreasing rates of live‐born infants with DS, as low as 0.05%, due to nationwide comprehensive antenatal screening programs. 1 , 8 Interestingly, Bergström et al. 9 have reported a decrease in complex CHDs among infants with DS in Sweden, presumably due to increased prenatal detection rates leading to termination of pregnancy. From a clinical point of view, it is important to assess the associations between CHD and morbidity and mortality in infants with DS to identify fetuses with severe phenotypes at higher risk of early death.

Since 1986, the Norwegian antenatal screening program has included one second‐trimester ultrasonography examination following international standards. 10 , 11 In 2004, the updated Norwegian Biotechnology Act opened up opportunities for more extensive prenatal screening for trisomies for women older than 38 years, by first trimester combined ultrasound and biochemical screening (CUB). 12 In 2018, noninvasive prenatal testing by free‐fetal DNA was used as contingency test to CUB for women older than 38 years. These changes in the screening policy led to higher prenatal detection and increased rates of termination of pregnancies with DS. 13 Therefore, we hypothesized that CHDs might be less common among live births with DS due to improved antenatal screening in Norway. The Medical Birth Registry of Norway (MBRN) and Oslo University Hospital's Clinical Registry for Congenital Heart Defects provided data that enabled investigation of prevalence of DS and associated CHDs in live births in Norway during a 20‐year period. Our aims were to investigate the prevalence, time trends, specific cardiac phenotypes, and outcomes of CHDs among infants with DS.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

We conducted a population‐based cohort study based on two comprehensive health registries. Data on all pregnancies from January 1, 2000 to December 31, 2019 were obtained from the national MBRN. 14 The MBRN contains information on all births from 12 weeks of gestation, including abortions requiring approval. We used the MBRN to obtain information on the total number of live births and prevalence of DS during the study period (2000–2019). In addition, data describing live‐born children with severe CHDs were retrieved from Oslo University Hospital's Clinical Registry for Congenital Heart Defects. Oslo University Hospital serves as a national center for treatment of children with severe CHDs in Norway. All CHDs are classified according to the International Pediatric and Congenital Cardiac Code. 15 Transposition of the great arteries, atrioventricular septal defects, tetralogy of Fallot, hypoplastic right heart syndrome, hypoplastic left heart syndrome, and coarctation of the aorta were classified as severe CHDs. Moreover, clinical data of all patients who have been referred to the Department of Pediatric Cardiology at Oslo University Hospital are entered into the registry. The registry is regularly updated with reports from other hospitals and information on deaths from the Norwegian Population Registry. Furthermore, the first author of the present study (CBN) reviewed all medical records of the infants included in this study, and the following four potential confounders were identified and recorded: maternal age, birthweight, gestational age at birth, and whether or not the diagnosis was known prenatally. Infants with AVSD and other chromosomal abnormalities except DS were excluded. The World Health Organization's fetal growth calculator was used for calculating the percentile of fetal weight according to gestational age and sex. 16

We applied logistic regression to estimate crude and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) of infant mortality (i.e., death within the first year of life) and need for cardiac intervention during the first year of life, with associated 95% confidence intervals (CIs), for infants with both AVSD and DS compared to infants with AVSD but without chromosomal defects. Linear regression was applied to estimate the difference in mean number of cardiac interventions during the first year of life between the two groups. Intervention was defined as either surgical or via transcatheter.

Data were analyzed using Stata SE 18 software (Stata Corp., 2023. Stata Statistical Software: Release 18. College Station, TX: Stata Corp LLC). Time trends were analyzed using the Joinpoint Regression Program (version 5.1.0; SEER software, National Cancer Institute, USA) and are presented as the average annual percent changes (AAPC) with 95% CIs. Results are reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) recommendations. 17

RESULTS

A total of 1 177 926 infants were born alive in Norway during the study period, and among these 1456 (0.1%) had DS. CHDs were found in 702 (48.2%) of the infants with DS (Table 1) compared to an overall prevalence of 0.7% in the general Norwegian birth cohort (MBRN). The prevalence of DS with severe CHDs was relatively stable (mean: 17 cases per year; range: 10–29 cases per year).

DISCUSSION

In this national cohort, we found that infants with DS had increased risk of CHDs, in particular, AVSD. However, the mortality of infants with AVSD and DS was comparable to infants with AVSD but without chromosomal defects, although infants with AVSD and DS needed more cardiac interventions. We identified 1456 children with DS among more than one million live births in Norway over a 20‐year period, corresponding to a prevalence of 12.4 per 10 000 live births, which is comparable to previous estimates for Europe. 3 , 4 , 6 , 18 Almost half of the infants with DS had CHDs (48.2%), with AVSD being by far the most common CHD (44.4%) with a mean annual prevalence of 2.65 per 10 000 live births which remained stable during the study period. This proportion is higher than in a Swedish study that showed a decline in AVSD rate from 46% to 30% among DS infants during the period 1992–2012. 9 The European Surveillance of Congenital Anomalies Central Register which includes data from 20 European countries has reported a CHD prevalence of 43% and an AVSD prevalence of 32% in live‐born DS infants in the period 2000–2010. 7

Prenatal screening is regulated by the Norwegian Biotechnology Act, which has been updated twice during the last two decades in accordance with developments in ultrasonography and genetic prenatal testing techniques. Despite these changes, namely, offering prenatal screening to more pregnant women and the fact that maternal age has increased over the years, the prevalence of live births with DS, with and without CHDs, remained fairly stable throughout the study period. 19 In particular, the prenatal detection rate of DS was 68% of all live births in Norway in 2018. 13 Also, the prenatal detection rate of severe CHDs increased to almost 50% of live‐born children with severe CHD in 2020, as compared to 7% in 2006. 20 , 21 Interestingly, Berg et al. found that co‐occurrence of DS might lead to earlier detection of CHDs in pregnancy. 22 In the present study, the overall prenatal detection of AVSD was surprisingly low at 22.6% of all live births, regardless of being accompanied by DS or not; however, it should be noted that the proportion of missing data regarding prenatal detection in our cohort was relatively high (Table 2). Nevertheless, we found a statistically significant increase in the prenatal detection rate in both groups during the study period (data not shown), probably due to systematic training in sequential fetal cardiac examination and better ultrasonography screening techniques.

We aimed to investigate the associations between DS diagnosis and outcomes of morbidity and mortality among infants with AVSD born in Norway in 2000–2019. In this connection, we manually reviewed all medical records of the infants included in this study with regard to prematurity, birthweight, and sex. Nevertheless, there were considerable missing observations in our database in order to make conclusions regarding these factors (Table 2). We found that more than one‐fourth of the infants with AVSD were SGA (85 of 311), regardless of DS or not. A meta‐analysis by Ghanchi et al. published in 2020 showed a pooled proportion of 20% SGA newborns with any type of CHD. 23 However, the SGA proportion in the subgroup of infants with AVSD ranged between 25% and 32%, 24 , 25 , 26 which is consistent with our findings. Furthermore, we found that 18.1% of the infants with AVSD (62 out of 343) were born prematurely (i.e., before 37 weeks of gestation), irrespective of whether they had DS or not. This proportion is much higher than the overall proportion of 5.3% preterm births in Norway. 27 Our finding is in accordance with reports from England showing an overall proportion of 16% prematurity in live‐born infants with CHD and 22% prematurity in live‐born infants with AVSD. 28 We did not find any gender difference, in contrast to previous studies that have shown that DS females were more commonly affected by AVSD than DS males. 5 , 7

The odds of cardiac interventions during the first year of life in infants with AVSD and DS were more than 2.5 times higher than in infants with AVSD but without chromosomal defects. However, we found no statistically significant difference in infant mortality. AVSD is characterized by the presence of a common or partially separate atrioventricular orifice combined with a variable degree of defects of the atrial and ventricular septa. Although AVSD is not a duct‐dependent CHD, infants with complete defects need surveillance in the neonatal period. Due to increased risk of congestive heart failure and pulmonary vascular disease, surgical repair is usually performed within the first year of life. In Norway, the standard surgical treatment is a two‐patch technique performed at 3–6 months of age. Other possible surgical techniques include a classical or modified single‐patch technique or a no‐patch technique. 29 The difference in the proportion of cardiac interventions between infants with DS and infants without chromosomal abnormalities might be due to two factors. Firstly, infants with DS often have a complete AVSD leading to heart failure during early neonatal period. On the contrary, infants without DS more often have a partial AVSD that can remain asymptomatic for several years without the need for intervention. 30 Secondly, newborns with typical physical characteristics of DS are routinely examined with echocardiography, leading to earlier diagnosis of AVSD compared to infants without DS.

Clinical outcomes have improved over the past two decades, including mortality rates following cardiac surgery in individuals with DS of all ages. This probably reflects changing attitudes toward treatment of congenital malformations in children with DS. 31 In fact, infants with DS, operated for AVSD, have better survival than those without DS. 32 , 33 , 34 Interestingly, the absence of DS has been shown to be a possible risk factor for significant left atrioventricular valve regurgitation after AVSD repair. 35 Subtle differences in cardiac structure may explain the variations in cardiac surgical outcomes between patients with and without DS. A prospective cohort study including 147 children who underwent AVSD repair found that children without chromosomal defects were more likely to have a dysplastic atrioventricular valve which is a risk factor for reoperation. Remarkably, even after accounting for the presence of a dysplastic atrioventricular valve, individuals with a DS diagnosis still exhibited a lower 30‐day mortality following AVSD repair. 30 In a similar German study, infants with DS had a lower risk of reoperation. 36 Another possible explanation for the improved outcomes observed in children with DS compared to children without DS is that they might have a higher resistance to oxidative stress, which is commonly associated with ischemia–reperfusion injury and cardiopulmonary bypass. 37

The main strength of our study was the use of a national population‐based cohort of participants with severe CHDs and access to individual‐level patient data. The use of national health registries and medical records ensured inclusion of nearly all AVSD cases in Norway. However, this study also had some limitations, including the lack of linkage between the two health registries, which might have resolved the issue of considerable missing data regarding maternal age, prematurity, and SGA in our study sample (Table 2). Furthermore, some infants with CHDs who were born at smaller hospitals without a prenatal diagnosis and who died before referral to Oslo University Hospital might be missing from the Oslo University Hospital's Clinical Registry for Congenital Heart Defects. Children with DS and less severe CHDs that do not require interventions might also be left out of the registry. We did not find a statistically significant difference in infant mortality of infants with AVSD, with and without DS. However, we had no information on the exact proportions of prenatal diagnoses and pregnancy terminations in this study. Unfortunately, our study design does not include prenatal and postnatal phenotypes of AVSD. The termination rate was probably higher in pregnancies involving fetuses with more severe phenotypes, for example, a common atrioventricular canal with insufficient valve and unbalanced four‐chamber view. In a previous study from Norway, Wik et al. found that the 2‐year mortality rate among live‐born infants with severe CHD was 10%, and approximately half of these infants were offered palliative care. In particular, unbalanced AVSD with severe valve dysplasia was a group that was not offered surgery, indicating that AVSD can be difficult to treat. 30

CONCLUSION

In this population‐based cohort study, the rate of live‐born children with DS and AVSD has remained stable during the last 20 years. Notably, infants with DS and AVSD needed more cardiac interventions during the first year of life, but they did not seem to have increased risk of mortality compared to infants with AVSD without chromosomal defects. This information is important for the counseling of expectant parents of a child with a prenatal diagnosis of AVSD with DS.
