Regulatory role of N-nitroso compounds and lncRNA NEAT1 in endoplasmic reticulum stress and malignant transformation of gastric epithelial cells
Authors: Xing Liu, Yichun Zhao, Yueyue Zhou, Xihuan Zou, Ruobing Chen, Yuting Peng, Meng Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the early oncogenic transformation of gastric epithelial cells (GES-1) and adenocarcinoma cells (AGS) following long-term exposure to N-nitroso compounds (NOCs), using N-methyl-N’-nitro-N-nitrosoguanidine (MNNG) as a model carcinogen. Prolonged MNNG treatment enhanced proliferation, migration, invasion, and anchorage-independent growth. These effects were accompanied by persistent activation of endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) and autophagy, characterized by elevated PERK, eIF2α, ATF4, CHOP, Beclin-1, and LC3-II, and reduced p62. Long non-coding RNA nuclear-enriched abundant transcript 1 (NEAT1) was markedly upregulated and acted as a competing endogenous RNA, sponging microRNA-329-3p (miR-329-3p) to derepress ATF4. Silencing NEAT1 or restoring miR-329-3p expression suppressed ERS, autophagy, and malignant traits. These findings establish a NEAT1/miR-329-3p/ATF4 signaling axis that links stress response to early gastric epithelial transformation, offering a potential target for intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-03954-0.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is a major global health challenge. In 2020, it accounted for 5.2% of all new cancer cases and 7.7% of cancer-related deaths worldwide, ranking fifth in incidence and fourth in mortality [1], and imposing a substantial economic burden [2]. The incidence of GC varies markedly across regions, with higher rates in East Asia (e.g., Japan, China, Korea), Eastern Europe, and parts of South America countries. In contrast, the prevalence remains relatively low in North America and Western Europe [1, 3, 4]. Prognosis remains relatively poor, with global 5-year age-standardized net survival rates ranging from 20 to 40% [5]. Survival outcomes differ significantly across countries: over 60% in Japan and Korea, but only 35.9% in China [6]. Despite recent declines in both incidence and mortality in China, GC remains one of the most common and deadly malignancies [7, 8].

GC is a multifactorial disease influenced by dietary habits, Helicobacter pylori infection, underlying medical conditions, and genetic predisposition [9–11]. Among dietary risk factors, N-nitroso compounds (NOCs) have been identified as major contributors to gastric carcinogenesis [12]. The International Agency for Research on Cancer has classified NOCs as Group 1 carcinogens, with evidence indicating that both dietary exposure and endogenous synthesis contribute to their role in elevating GC risk [13]. Under acidic gastric conditions, excess nitrates react with amines to form carcinogenic nitrosamines, which have been shown to promote gastric epithelial proliferation and malignant transformation [14–16]. Experimental studies in animal models further support the tumorigenic potential of NOCs across multiple organs, including the stomach [17]. N-methyl-N’-nitro-N-nitrosoguanidine (MNNG), a prototypical NOC and monofunctional alkylating agent, is widely used to induce GC models and investigate underlying mechanisms of tumorigenesis [18, 19]. However, the regulatory roles of endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) and autophagy in MNNG-induced gastric epithelial transformation remain poorly understood.

The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is a key organelle involved in protein synthesis and folding, lipid metabolism, and calcium storage, thus playing an essential role in maintaining cellular homeostasis [20–22]. When protein folding capacity is exceeded, unfolded proteins accumulate and trigger a protective signaling cascade known as the unfolded protein response (UPR) [23]. UPR is mediated by three ER stress sensors: inositol-requiring enzyme 1 (IRE1), protein kinase R-like ER kinase (PERK), and activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6), which collectively act to restore ER function by reducing protein load, enhancing folding capacity, and promoting degradation of misfolded proteins [24, 25].

However, under persistent or severe ER stress, the UPR shifts from pro-survival to pro-apoptotic programs, contributing to pathogenesis including cancer [26, 27]. Activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4), a key member of the ATF/CREB transcription factor family, serves as a pivotal downstream effector of the PERK signaling pathway [28, 29]. Upon ER stress, ATF4 is upregulated and regulates genes involved in amino acid metabolism, redox balance, apoptosis, and autophagy [30, 31]. In cancer, ATF4 expression is frequently elevated and has been implicated in regulating tumor cell proliferation, migration, and survival under stress conditions [32–34]. ATF4 facilitates tumor development and resistance to treatment by regulating cellular adaptive responses, highlighting its potential as a promising target for therapeutic strategies [35, 36].

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are emerging as critical regulators in cancer [37, 38]. NEAT1, a well-characterized lncRNA, functions as a competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) by binding miRNAs, including the tumor-suppressive miR-329-3p [39]. miR-329-3p is downregulated in GC and targets key factors in the PERK-eIF2α-ATF4 pathway, a central axis of the ER stress response [31]. NEAT1 is frequently upregulated in tumors and promotes proliferation, migration, and invasion [40, 41]. However, its role in regulating ER stress and autophagy in gastric epithelial cells under N-nitroso compound (NOC) exposure remains unclear.

Despite increasing evidence implicating NEAT1 in cancer progression, its role in mediating endoplasmic reticulum stress and autophagy in gastric epithelial cells under N-nitroso compound (NOC) exposure remains unclear. This study aim to investigate the regulatory function of the NEAT1/miR-329-3p/ATF4 signaling axis in NOC-induced malignant transformation of gastric epithelial cells. By integrating molecular and functional assays, this work seeks to elucidate the mechanistic link between non-coding RNA regulation and ER stress-driven gastric carcinogenesis.

Materials and methods

Modeling and monitoring the malignant transformation process of GES-1 induced by MNNG

GES-1 and AGS cell lines were used as experimental models and maintained in high-glucose DMEM enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) under standard conditions (5% CO2, 37 °C). To induce malignant transformation, GES-1 cells at 70–80% confluence were treated with 1 µmol/L MNNG, a representative N-nitroso compound (NOC), and designated as the first-generation treated cells (MNNG-1). Cells were continuously cultured and passaged every 3–4 days. At passages 20, 25, 30, and 35 (MNNG-20, MNNG-25, MNNG-30, and MNNG-35), cells were harvested for phenotypic and molecular analyses to monitor malignant transformation.

Assessment of cell viability under MNNG exposure using cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

To evaluate cell viability, MNNG-treated and untreated GES-1 and AGS cells were subjected to the CCK-8 assay. Cells at passages 20, 25, 30, and 35 were harvested upon reaching ~ 80% confluence, digested with trypsin, and resuspended in complete medium. After counting, cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 103 cells per well in 100 µL medium. Edge wells were filled with PBS to minimize evaporation effects. Following overnight incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well. After 1.5 h of incubation, absorbance was recorded at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Soft agar colony formation assay

To evaluate MNNG-induced malignant transformation, GES-1 and AGS cells from MNNG-treated and control groups at various passages were collected and digested with trypsin to prepare single-cell suspensions. After cell counting and density adjustment, 1.4% agar was mixed with complete medium to form the bottom layer in 30 mm culture dishes. For the upper layer, 1 × 104 cells were mixed with 0.7% agar-medium solution and overlaid onto the solidified base layer. Cultures were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 14 days. Fresh medium was added every 3–5 days to prevent drying. Colony formation was assessed under an inverted microscope and photographed when visible colonies appeared.

Wound healing assay

To assess the effect of MNNG on cell migration, a wound healing assay was performed on GES-1 and AGS cells at passages 20, 25, 30, and 35. Cells from MNNG-treated and control groups were collected at ~ 80% confluence, digested with trypsin, and seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells/well in 2 mL complete medium. After 24 h of incubation, a uniform scratch was created in the cell monolayer using a sterile 10 µL pipette tip. Non-adherent cells were gently removed by rinsing with PBS, and the remaining cells were cultured in serum-free medium. Scratch closure was observed and imaged at 0 h and 24 h. Wound areas were quantified utilizing ImageJ software, and the percentage of wound closure was calculated to evaluate migration capacity.

Transwell migration assay

Transwell assays were performed to evaluate the migration ability of GES-1 and AGS cells following MNNG exposure. Cells from passages 20, 25, 30, and 35 (MNNG-treated and controls) were serum-starved for 24 h to eliminate serum effects. After trypsinization, cells were resuspended in serum-free medium and adjusted to the appropriate density. A total of 200 µL of cell suspension was seeded into the upper compartment of a Transwell insert with an 8 μm pore membrane, while 600 µL of culture medium supplemented with 20% FBS was placed in the lower chamber to serve as a chemoattractant. After 24 h incubation, non-migrated cells on the upper surface were removed. The membranes were washed with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Migrated cells were imaged and quantified under an inverted microscope.

Transwell invasion assay

A Transwell invasion assay was used to assess the invasive capacity of GES-1 and AGS cells following MNNG exposure. Cells from MNNG-treated and control groups at passages 20, 25, 30, and 35 were serum-starved for 24 h. Matrigel was thawed at 4 °C and diluted at a ratio of 1:8 using serum-free medium. The diluted matrix was then applied to the upper compartments of Transwell inserts and incubated at 37 °C for 1–3 h to allow for gel polymerization. Subsequently, 2 × 10⁴ cells suspended in serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chambers, while the lower chambers were filled with complete medium containing 20% FBS to provide a chemoattractant gradient. After a 24-hour incubation period, non-invading cells remaining on the upper surface were gently removed. The membranes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and visualized using an inverted microscope. Invasive cells were counted in 3–5 randomly selected fields using ImageJ, and mean values were used for quantitative analysis.

Quantification of RNA expression by quantification of RNA expression by RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent following standard protocols. Briefly, cells were harvested, lysed in 1 mL TRIzol, and phase-separated with chloroform. The aqueous phase was collected, and RNA was precipitated with isopropanol, washed with 75% ethanol, and dissolved in DEPC-treated water. RNA purity and concentration were assessed using a spectrophotometer (OD260/OD280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0). cDNA synthesis for lncRNA, mRNA, and miRNA was performed using specific reverse transcription kits. QRT-PCR was conducted using appropriate primer sets (Table 1), with GAPDH and U6 as internal controls. All reactions were performed in RNase-free conditions. Relative gene expression was calculated utilizing the 2−ΔΔCT method.

Western blot

GES-1 and AGS cells from control and MNNG-treated groups at 70–80% confluence were rinsed twice with ice-cold PBS and lysed in 200 µL RIPA buffer supplemented with 1% PMSF on ice for 30 min with gentle vortexing every 10 min. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and supernatants were collected for protein quantification using a BSA assay at 562 nm. Equal protein amounts were mixed with 5× loading buffer, denatured at 100 °C for 10 min, and stored at − 80 °C. Samples were resolved on SDS-PAGE gels (prepared according to standard protocols) and transferred onto methanol-activated PVDF membranes using wet transfer (110 mA, 1.5 h, on ice). Membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature (RT), followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Primary antibodies included: p-PERK (CST, 3179), PERK (CST, 3192), p-eIF2α (CST, 9721), eIF2α (CST, 9722), ATF4 (CST, 11815), CHOP (CST, 2895), β-actin (CST, 4967), Beclin-1 (CST, 3738), LC3B (CST, 2775), and p62 (CST, 5114). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at RT. Bands were detected utilizing enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents in the darkroom and quantified by ImageJ software based on band intensity.

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence was performed to localize LC3 protein in GES-1, AGS, MNNG-treated GES-1, and AGS cells. Cells in logarithmic growth phase were seeded onto sterile glass coverslips in culture dishes and grown to 70–80% confluence. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at RT, followed by PBS washes. Permeabilization was achieved using 0.2% Triton X-100 for 20 min, and cells were blocked with 2% BSA for 1 h at RT in a humidified, light-protected chamber. Cells were incubated with primary antibody against LC3 overnight at 4 °C, washed, and then incubated with fluorescent secondary antibody for 1 h at RT in the dark. Nuclei were stained with DAPI for 10 min. After washing, coverslips were mounted with antifade reagent. Fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope under darkroom conditions.

Transfection of NEAT1 and miR-329-3p in GES-1 and AGS cells

To investigate the regulatory roles of NEAT1 and miR-329-3p in cell behavior, GES-1 and AGS cells were transfected with corresponding siRNAs and miRNA inhibitors. Seven experimental groups were established: control (GES-1 or AGS), MNNG-35, miR-329-3p inhibitor, miR-NC, si-NEAT1, si-NC, and co-transfection group (si-NEAT1 + miR-329-3p inhibitor). At 70–80% confluence, cells plated at 4 × 10⁵ per well in 6-well plates were incubated in serum-free medium for 4–6 h. Transfection complexes were prepared by separately diluting siRNAs/miRNA inhibitors and transfection reagents in serum-free medium, then combining and incubating at RT for 10–15 min. The mixtures were added to each well and gently shaken to promote uniform distribution. After 48 h of incubation at 37 °C, cells were collected for downstream molecular analyses.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

To evaluate the interaction between NEAT1 and miR-329-3p, a dual-luciferase reporter assay was conducted in MNNG-35 cells. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 5 × 103 cells/well and incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2. At 60–70% confluence, cells were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 with one of the following constructs: pmirGLO-NEAT1-WT, pmirGLO-NEAT1-MUT, or pmirGLO-NC, in combination with either miR-329-3p mimic or miR-NC. After 6 h of transfection, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium. Following 48 h of incubation, cells were washed with PBS, lysed, and centrifuged to collect the supernatant. Firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were sequentially measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Renilla luciferase served as the internal control. Relative luciferase activity was calculated as the ratio of Firefly to Renilla luminescence.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Two-group comparisons were assessed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests, while one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied for multiple group comparisons. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To investigate the regulatory network of lncRNA NEAT1, bioinformatic predictions of miRNA interactions were conducted using StarBase, TargetScan, and miRWalk databases. miR-329-3p was identified as a key candidate via intersecting results. GEPIA (based on TCGA and GTEx data) was employed to assess the expression differences of NEAT1, miR-329-3p, and ATF4 between GC and normal tissues, as well as their prognostic value through survival curve analysis. Potential binding sites between NEAT1 and miR-329-3p, and between miR-329-3p and ATF4, were predicted using StarBase. All experiments, including CCK-8, soft agar colony formation, wound healing, Transwell migration and invasion, RT-PCR, and Western blot, were performed in biological triplicates. ImageJ was used for quantitative analysis of migration/invasion and Western blot band intensity. The study was designed to comprehensively assess the molecular mechanism underlying MNNG-induced malignant transformation in GES-1 cells, with a focus on the NEAT1/miR-329-3p/ATF4 signaling axis.

Results

Multifaceted biological assessment of GES-1 malignant transformation induced by MNNG exposure

To evaluate the biological effects of prolonged MNNG exposure, cell proliferation, colony formation, wound healing, and invasion were assessed in GES-1 and AGS cells. In the CCK-8 assay, the proliferation of MNNG-treated GES-1 cells was comparable to controls at passage 20 but significantly increased from passage 25 onward (p < 0.05), with the most pronounced growth observed at passages 30 and 35 (Fig. 1A-D; Fig. S1A-D). Soft agar assays revealed a marked increase in colony numbers in the MNNG-treated groups starting from passage 25, with a progressive rise at later passages (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1E-F; Fig. S1E-F). In wound healing assays, MNNG-treated GES-1 cells showed enhanced migration from passage 25, evidenced by accelerated wound closure and reduced scratch area over time (p < 0.05)V Transwell invasion assays demonstrated significantly increased invasive cell numbers in the MNNG-treated groups starting from passage 25, with further elevation at passages 30 and 35 (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1I-K; Fig. S1I-K). Collectively, these results indicate that extended low-dose MNNG exposure induces a passage-dependent enhancement of malignant characteristics in GES-1 cells.

Dynamic expression profiling of ER Stress-Related genes and proteins in GES-1 cells following Long-Term MNNG exposure

The mRNA expression levels of ER stress markers GRP78, PERK, eIF2α, ATF4, and CHOP were significantly increased in GES-1-MNNG and AGS-MNNG cells from passage 20 to 35, as measured by RT-qPCR. Expression levels rose progressively with continued MNNG exposure compared to controls (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2A-E, Fig. S2A-E). Protein expression of the same markers, assessed by Western blot, also showed a consistent upward trend over passages, confirming transcriptional results (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2F-J, Fig. S2F-J).

Alterations in LC3 distribution and Autophagy-Related protein expression during MNNG-Induced malignant transformation of GES-1 cells

Autophagic activity was assessed in GES-1 and AGS cells following extended exposure to MNNG. Immunofluorescence staining revealed increased LC3 puncta accumulation in MNNG-treated cells at passage 35, with more intense and aggregated cytoplasmic fluorescence compared to controls (Fig. 3A; Fig. S3A). Western blot analysis showed elevated expression of Beclin-1 and LC3-II/LC3-I starting from passage 25 in both cell lines. A gradual decrease in p62 protein levels was observed from passage 30 onward (Fig. 3B-E; Fig. S3B-E).

Application and validation analysis of NEAT1 expression levels in prognostic assessment of GC

GEPIA analysis revealed significantly higher NEAT1 expression in GC tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 4A). Kaplan-Meier survival curves showed that elevated NEAT1 levels were associated with shorter overall survival in GC patients (Fig. 4B). To validate these findings, RT-qPCR was carried out to measure NEAT1 expression in GES-1, AGS, and their MNNG-treated derivatives. NEAT1 expression was markedly increased in MNNG-treated GES-1 and AGS cells starting from passage 25, with further elevation at later passages (Fig. 4C-D). These experimental data were consistent with the bioinformatics analysis results.

The CeRNA function of LncRNA NEAT1 in GC cell malignant transformation and its mutual regulation with miR-329-3p analysis

Candidate miRNAs potentially interacting with both NEAT1 and ATF4 were identified via intersection analysis of miRWalk, TargetScan, and StarBase databases (Fig. 5A). Among 10 overlapping miRNAs, miR-329-3p was selected for further investigation [42, 43]. RT-qPCR analysis demonstrated a progressive decline in miR-329-3p expression in GES-1 cells with continued MNNG exposure, reaching a statistically significant reduction by passage 20 (p < 0.05; Fig. 5B). Binding sites between NEAT1 and miR-329-3p were predicted using StarBase. Dual-luciferase assays in MNNG-35 GES-1 cells revealed that miR-329-3p mimics significantly suppressed luciferase activity of NEAT1-WT reporters, but not NEAT1-MUT constructs (Fig. 5C-D), confirming a specific interaction between NEAT1 and miR-329-3p.

Progressive upregulation of ATF4 in GC cells and its validation as a direct target of miR-329-3p

Quantitative PCR analysis revealed a progressive increase in ATF4 mRNA expression in both GES-1 and AGS cells upon prolonged MNNG exposure, with significant upregulation observed from passage 20 onward (Fig. 6A) (p < 0.05). StarBase analysis predicted a conserved miR-329-3p binding site within the 3’ UTR of ATF4. To validate this interaction, dual-luciferase reporter assays were conducted in GES-1 cells at passage 35 using constructs containing either the wild-type (WT) or mutant (MUT) ATF4 3’ UTR. Co-transfection with miR-329-3p mimics led to a significant reduction in luciferase activity in the ATF4-WT group, while the MUT construct showed no appreciable change (Fig. 6B-C), confirming ATF4 as a direct target of miR-329-3p.

Correlation analysis of LncRNA NEAT1, miR-329-3p, and ATF4 expression in MNNG-Induced malignant transformation of GES-1

Quantitative PCR and correlation analyses revealed a significant inverse association between miR-329-3p and ATF4 expression levels in GES-1 cells following MNNG exposure (Fig. 7A). A negative correlation was also observed between NEAT1 and miR-329-3p (Fig. 7B), while NEAT1 and ATF4 exhibited a positive correlation (Fig. 7C). Similar patterns were confirmed in MNNG-treated AGS cells. These results suggest a coordinated expression pattern among NEAT1, miR-329-3p, and ATF4 during the process of malignant transformation.

NEAT1 regulates endoplasmic reticulum stress and autophagy in GC cells through the miR-329-3p/ATF4 pathway

In MNNG-treated GES-1 and AGS cells (passage 35), NEAT1 knockdown led to a significant upregulation of miR-329-3p and a concomitant reduction in ATF4 expression at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 10A and D, Fig. S5A-S5D). Co-transfection with miR-329-3p inhibitor restored ATF4 expression. Expression levels of ER stress markers—PERK, eIF2α, ATF4, and CHOP—were reduced upon NEAT1 silencing, and this effect was reversed by miR-329-3p inhibition (Fig. 10E and I, Fig. S5E-S5I). For autophagy-related proteins, NEAT1 knockdown led to reduced Beclin-1 and LC3-II/I levels and increased P62 expression, all of which were restored by miR-329-3p inhibition (Fig. 10J and M, Fig. S5J-S5M). These results indicate that NEAT1 regulates ER stress and autophagy through the miR-329-3p/ATF4 axis in transformed gastric epithelial cells.

Discussion

NOCs are recognized environmental carcinogens closely associated with the development of GC, yet the molecular mechanisms underlying the tumorigenic effects remain inadequately defined [44]. This study examined the regulatory relationship between NOCs-induced endoplasmic reticulum stress and lncRNA NEAT1 in gastric epithelial cells. Results demonstrated that prolonged exposure to MNNG, a representative NOC, significantly enhanced endoplasmic reticulum stress and autophagic activity, accompanied by increased expression of NEAT1. Further analysis revealed that NEAT1 promotes malignant transformation by modulating the miR-329-3p/ATF4 axis, implicating its functional involvement in stress response pathways during carcinogenesis. These results enhance our understanding of cellular adaptations to NOC exposure and suggest potential molecular targets for therapeutic intervention in GC.

NEAT1 has been shown to regulate ERS and autophagy through diverse miRNA pathways and to promote malignant progression in various cancers [45]. However, most previous studies have been conducted in fully transformed cancer cells, lacking insights into the dynamic events during early carcinogenesis. Using an MNNG-induced transformation model, our study systematically constructed the NEAT1/miR-329-3p/ATF4 axis, showing that NEAT1 functions as a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) by sponging miR-329-3p, thereby relieving its inhibitory effect on ATF4. This derepression activates the PERK/eIF2α/ATF4 arm of the unfolded protein response and upregulates Beclin-1 and LC3-II, promoting autophagic signaling and driving malignant transformation. miR-329-3p, previously identified as a tumor suppressor in multiple cancer types [43], exhibited an inverse correlation with ATF4 expression in our analysis. Given that ATF4 mediates ERS-induced autophagy [46], our findings emphasize its central role in gastric epithelial cell transformation. While previous work has linked lncRNAs to stress-response networks [47, 48], our results extend this understanding to the early stages of tumorigenesis, establishing NEAT1 as a potential intervention node.

In the gastric epithelial transformation model, NOC exposure led to sustained activation of ERS and autophagy. MNNG-treated GES-1 cells exhibited increased proliferation, migration, and invasion, coinciding with elevated expression of ERS markers (p-PERK, p-eIF2α, ATF4, CHOP) and autophagy proteins (Beclin-1, LC3-II/LC3-I), and decreased P62, suggesting a progressive and coordinated upregulation of these pathways. Previous studies have shown that ATF4 mediates ERS-induced autophagy [49] and that VacA triggers autophagic cell death in GC via ATF4 [50]. While ERS and autophagy have been implicated in various cancers, their specific roles in early transformation of gastric epithelial cells remain incompletely characterized [51–53].

Further investigation demonstrates that NEAT1 plays a critical regulatory role in this process. By sponging miR-329-3p, NEAT1 releases its repression on ATF4, amplifying ER stress signals and promoting downstream autophagic responses. Silencing NEAT1 significantly reduced expression of ATF4 and downstream stress and autophagy markers, confirming the functional loop of the NEAT1/miR-329-3p/ATF4 axis. This aligns with previous reports showing NEAT1-mediated UPR–miRNA networks in tumor progression [54]. Notably, ERS and autophagy may initially serve protective roles during early transformation but can shift to tumor-promoting functions upon persistent activation [55, 56]. Our study constructs and validates a complete regulatory axis by which NEAT1 controls ERS–autophagy crosstalk during NOC-induced carcinogenesis, offering potential targets for early intervention.

Through an integrated approach combining immunofluorescence, Western blotting, and RT-qPCR, this study systematically defines the dynamic changes in the NEAT1/miR-329-3p/ATF4 pathway during gastric epithelial transformation. Aberrant expression of NEAT1 and its downstream axis represents not only a mechanistic hallmark but also a promising early biomarker for GC. Notably, NEAT1 knockdown significantly disrupted both autophagy and ER stress signals, suggesting that targeting this pathway may effectively hinder tumor progression. However, this regulatory network is highly complex and may involve additional miRNAs or transcriptional factors [57–59]. Thus, future research should incorporate in vivo models and clinical tissue validation to confirm the function of NEAT1 and explore its relevance across other tumor types, enhancing its translational potential as a therapeutic target.

Although this study delineates the NEAT1/miR-329-3p/ATF4 axis in NOC-induced malignant transformation, several limitations remain. The findings are based on long-term cell line exposure models, which lack physiological components such as immune interactions, stromal context, and vascular dynamics. Moreover, the potential cross-talk of this axis with other oncogenic pathways (e.g., mTOR, NF-κB, HIF-1α) remains unexplored. Clinical validation using patient-derived tissues is needed to assess its diagnostic and prognostic relevance. Additionally, the role of this axis in chemotherapy response, immune evasion, or metabolic rewiring warrants further investigation.

Future studies should incorporate in vivo models (e.g., MNNG-induced mouse GC) and gene editing tools (e.g., CRISPR/Cas9) to establish the causal role of NEAT1 in gastric tumorigenesis. Large-scale clinical analysis of NEAT1, miR-329-3p, and ATF4 across tumor stages and molecular subtypes will help define their utility as biomarkers. Therapeutically, siRNA or antisense oligonucleotides targeting NEAT1, or miR-329-3p mimics, could offer novel approaches to modulate ERS-autophagy pathways and enhance sensitivity to standard therapies. Given NEAT1’s recurrent upregulation across cancers, pan-cancer analyses are warranted to evaluate its conserved function and druggability. Ultimately, elucidating the NEAT1-ERS-autophagy axis could pave the way toward multi-pathway precision interventions in GC.

Consistent with previous research, our results indicate that lncRNA, as a non-coding RNA, participates in cellular stress responses. However, this mechanism is being proposed for the first time in the malignant transformation of GC cells [47, 48]. The pivotal role played by NEAT1 in the malignant transformation of GC is unveiled in this molecular mechanism, providing crucial leads for identifying new potential therapeutic targets [47, 48, 60]. This study not only fills a knowledge gap in the mechanism of malignant transformation of GC cells but also offers a novel perspective for future therapeutic strategy research. However, additional investigations are needed to clarify the precise role of NEAT1 in GC progression and to further evaluate its potential as a therapeutic target. This finding strongly supports further exploration of the role of NEAT1 in the development of GC, bringing new insights for future related research and clinical applications.

Our results indicate that NOCs exposure significantly induces endoplasmic reticulum stress in GES-1 cells, subsequently triggering an autophagic process. While the roles of endoplasmic reticulum stress and autophagy in cancer cells have been widely studied, their specific mechanisms in the malignant transformation of GES-1 remain unclear [51–53]. Specifically, following MNNG exposure, the expression of endoplasmic reticulum stress-related genes such as P-PERK, P-eIF2α, ATF4, and CHOP significantly increases, and the levels of autophagy-related factors such as Beclin1, LC3II/LC3I, and P62 correspondingly rise, suggesting that endoplasmic reticulum stress and autophagy play crucial roles in the malignant transformation of GC cells.

The combination of various experimental techniques such as immunofluorescence, immunoblotting, and RT-PCR enables us to systematically analyze the dynamic changes of the lncRNA NEAT1/miR-329-3p/ATF4 signaling pathway in the malignant transformation process, providing a solid foundation for understanding the complex cellular response mechanisms.

The aberrant expression of NEAT1 and the miR-329-3p/ATF4 signaling pathway can serve as biomarkers for the progression of GC. Interventions targeting this pathway may emerge as a new therapeutic strategy. Specifically, interventions targeting NEAT1 or miR-329-3p may effectively suppress the malignant transformation and invasive ability of GC cells by regulating endoplasmic reticulum stress and autophagy processes. Future clinical studies should further validate the feasibility and effectiveness of these molecular targets, offering more precise treatment options for GC patients.

Furthermore, the regulatory mechanism of the signaling pathway is not simple, involving complex interactions that require in-depth research to confirm the relationships between NEAT1 and other signaling molecules [57–59]. Hence, further experimental research, especially utilizing animal models and clinical samples, is needed to validate our findings comprehensively and gain a deeper understanding of the functionality of NEAT1. In future studies, exploring the potential roles of NEAT1 in other types of cancers may enhance our understanding of its involvement in tumorigenesis. These efforts will deepen our knowledge of the signaling pathways that NEAT1 participates in, laying a foundation for studying new therapeutic strategies.

The role and clinical significance of the NEAT1/miR-329-3p/ATF4 signaling pathway in GC. Through these in-depth studies, we aim to provide theoretical and practical guidance for early diagnosis and personalized treatment of GC. With persistent efforts, significant breakthroughs in the treatment and management of GC may be achieved, offering more effective medical services to patients.

Conclusion

This study identifies a critical regulatory axis in which NEAT1 promotes gastric epithelial cell transformation under chronic NOC exposure by modulating the miR-329-3p/ATF4 pathway (Fig. 11). Sustained activation of ER stress and autophagy accompanies this process, with increased expression of PERK, eIF2α, ATF4, CHOP, Beclin-1, and LC3-II, and suppression of P62. Functional disruption of NEAT1 leads to impaired proliferation, migration, and invasion, while co-suppression of miR-329-3p partially restores these malignant traits, confirming the specificity of the regulatory cascade. This mechanism underscores the dynamic interplay between non-coding RNA regulation and stress-adaptive signaling in the early stages of gastric carcinogenesis. The NEAT1/miR-329-3p/ATF4 axis may serve as a novel molecular target for early diagnosis or intervention. Further validation in animal models and patient-derived tissues is essential to determine its clinical relevance and therapeutic feasibility.
