Effect of the fine structure of starch on the gelatinization and retrogradation characteristics of Sorghum varieties
Authors: Xi Deng, Lijuan Gong, Yongyi Wang, Peiyao Yuan, Kangquan Qiao, Shuai Qin, Fangyong Fan, Ning Gan, Yushong Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGelatinization and retrogradation of sorghum are important factors influencing liquor quality. In this study, japonica and waxy sorghum were used as the research objects to elucidate the effects of fine structural changes in different sorghum varieties on their gelatinization and retrogradation characteristics, as well as their physicochemical properties. Changes in the fine structure of starch change its crystallinity and ordering, subsequently changing its gelatinization, retrogradation, and thermal properties. Compared with japonica sorghum (SD,DB), waxy sorghum (XK32,XK35) has a longer average amylopectin chain length (21.51–21.70 DP vs. 21.06–21.22 DP) and a more stable crystal structure (relative crystallinity: 33.18 %–33.20 % vs. 23.14 %–23.45 %). Furthermore, waxy sorghum exhibits higher gelatinization enthalpy (ΔH: 12.64–13.64 J/g vs. 11.02–11.66 J/g) and peak temperature (Tp: 74.90–75.10 °C vs. 72.60–74.00 °C), while showing lower setback (SB: 540.33–712.67 cP vs. 1738.33–1872.67 cP), indicating it is easier to disintegrate and difficult to regenerate after gelatinization. The findings of this study can enhance our understanding of the molecular changes in different starch varieties and provide basic data support for studying the effects of starch structure on grain cooking quality.Keywords: Sorghum starch, Branched-chain length distribution, Crystallinity, Fine structure, Gelatinization and retrogradation characteristics

Introduction

Sorghum is the main raw material for liquor brewing and has been used in China for more than 700 years. Its quality directly influences the liquor quality (Monkhan et al., 2021). Sorghum is composed mainly of starch (amylose and amylopectin), protein, fat, and tannins, with starch being the most significant, accounting for >70 % of the grain's dry weight (Jinsong et al., 2021). The gelatinization of sorghum starch facilitates microbial metabolism and enhances aroma production, which is closely related to the wine-making quality of sorghum (Yaoyao, 2023). Recent research on brewing sorghum has focused on breeding new varieties (Syahruddin et al., 2021), analyzing the grain's physical and chemical properties (Fei et al., 2022), and refining liquor-making processes (Rui-Rui et al., 2022), aiming at the physical and chemical properties of different varieties of sorghum starch. However, there have been few comprehensive studies on how cooking quality and starch fine structure affect the gelatinization properties of sorghum starch, and systematic analyses remain lacking. General sorghum varieties contain amylose and amylopectin starch molecules, whereas waxy sorghum varieties contain only a small amount of amylose starch molecules. The fine structure and amylopectin-to-amylose ratio significantly affect the physicochemical properties of starch, influencing saccharification, gelatinization, fermentation efficiency, and ultimately, liquor quality. This study examined the starch composition and gelatinization characteristics of four sorghum varieties (Qingqing et al., 2015; Zhenbing et al., 2025). Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and multiple linear regression analysis were used to elucidate how the fine structure of starch and its physicochemical properties affect the gelatinization process of sorghum. This study's results provide basic data on the effect of starch structure on grain cooking quality and provide a theoretical basis for variety selection and sorghum breeding in the brewing industry.

Materials and methods

Materials

Two waxy (Xike 32 [XK32] and Xike 35 [XK35]) and two japonica (Shangdang [SD] and Debang (Elkhalifa et al., 2004) sorghum varieties were selected for this study. Among them, XK32 and XK35 are both specialized sorghum varieties for brewing produced in Sichuan Province, while SD represents Shanxi sorghum and DB is imported sorghum from Australia. These four sorghum varieties exhibit distinct characteristics. By conducting a comprehensive comparative study on these two Japonica sorghum and two waxy sorghum varieties, the limitations of single-variety research can be avoided. Among the four types of sorghum starch, DB had the highest crude starch content, significantly different from the other varieties. The amylopectin content and amylopectin-to-amylose ratio in waxy sorghum varieties XK32 and XK35 were significantly higher than those in japonica sorghum. (Table 1).

Extraction of starch

Sorghum starch was extracted using a slightly modified wet grinding method (Jing et al., 2022). A 200 g sorghum sample was washed with clean water and then soaked in 400 mL 0.2 % NaOH (w/v) solution at 4 °C for 24 h. After soaking, the sample was washed with clean water to remove any insoluble impurities and floating particles before being soaked in 400 mL of 6 % NaOH solution and heated in a water bath (Model HH-2; Changzhou Putian Instrument Manufacturing Co., Ltd., Changzhou, Jiangsu, China) at 55 °C for 20 min. After the sample was taken out, clear water was then used to knead and wash the seed coat repeatedly, turning the sample white. Then, using a tissue crusher (Model TQ-300; Yongkang Tianqi Shengshi Industry and Trade Co., Ltd., Yongkang, Zhejiang, China), it is ground into a slurry and filtered through a 100-mesh sieve, followed by a 200-mesh sieve. The pH was adjusted to neutral, and the slurry was centrifuged at 4500 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the gray-white non-starch substance on the upper layer was removed. The remaining lower starch was placed in a Petri dish and then dried in an oven at 40 °C overnight. Afterward, it was crushed and passed through an 80-mesh sieve to obtain a sorghum starch sample, which was dried in a sealed bag for further use. The yield of extracted sorghum starch was 68.2 %–72.5 % (calculated as the ratio of dried starch mass to raw sorghum mass), and the starch purity was determined by the anthrone‑sulfuric acid method to be 92.3 %–94.1 %, meeting the requirements for subsequent experiments.

Determination of amylopectin chain length distribution

Referring to previous research methods (Nishi et al., 2001; Wenzhi et al., 2015; Zhitong et al., 2018), approximately 10 mg of starch was weighed and resuspended in 5 mL of water, followed by boiling water bath for 60 min with intermittent vortex mixing. Then, 50 μL of sodium acetate (0.6 M, pH 4.4), 10 μL of NaN3 (2 % w/v) and 10 μL of isoamylase (1400 U) were added, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. A 0.5 % (w/v) sodium borohydride solution was added, and the mixture was vortexed thoroughly and left to stand for 20 h. 600 μL of the mixture was transferred to a centrifuge tube and dried by nitrogen blowing at room temperature. The dried sample was dissolved in 30 μL of 1 M NaOH for 60 min, then diluted by adding 570 μL of water, centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was collected for injection.

Amylopectin chain length was determined using a Thermo ICS5000 ion chromatographic system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to analyze and detect starch. According to a previous research method, approximately 10 mg of starch was re-suspended in 5 mL of water, subjected to a boiling water bath for 60 min with intermittent vortex mixing, and then treated with 50 μL sodium acetate (0.6 M, PH 4.4), NaN3 (10 μL camera, 2 % w/w), and 10 μL isoamylase (1400 U). The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, followed by treatment with 0.5 % (w/w) sodium borohydride solution, mixed well, and left to react for 20 h. Next, 600 μL of the solution was dried with nitrogen at room temperature. The dried sample was then dissolved in 30 μL of 1 M NaOH for 60 min, diluted with 570 μL of water, and centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was collected and injected into the chromatographic system for analysis. A Dionex “CarboPac” PA200 column (250 mm × 4.0 mm, 10 μm) was used with an injection volume of 5 μL. The mobile phases were 0.2 M NaOH (A) and 0.2 M NaOH/0.2 M NaAC (B), with a column temperature of 30 °C. The components were analyzed using an electrochemical detector. The flow rate was 0.4 mL/min; elution gradient: 0 min A pedicle B (90:10 V/V), 10 min A pedicle B (90:10 V/V), 30 min A scarlet B (40:60 V/V), 50 min A pedicle B (40:60 V/V), 50.1 min A pedicle B (90:10 V/V), and 60 min A pedicle B (90:10 V/V).

Starch crystal structure

The determination of X-ray diffraction (XRD, Model D2 PHASER; Bruker Corporation, Billerica, MA, USA) of sorghum seeds was carried out according to the method of Gao S et al. (Shanshan et al., 2019) slight modifications, and the diffraction patterns of starch from different sorghum varieties were drawn by X-ray diffractometer.The crystalline and non-crystalline regions of sorghum starch were analyzed using Jade 5.0 (Jian et al., 2023). The X-ray diffraction conditions were as follows: the scanning speed was 2°/min, the step width was 0.02°, the range was 5–50°, the target was Cuka, the X-ray wavelength was 1.5406 Å, the Ni filter plate the graphite monochromator, the voltage was 40 kV, and the current was 30 mA. The percentage of relative crystallinity was calculated by dividing the peak area of crystallization by the total area.

Ordered starch structure

The surface structures of the different varieties of sorghum starch were analyzed using Fourier transform infrared (FTIR, Model VERTEX 70; Bruker Corporation, Billerica, MA, USA) spectrometry following the method of Johnson et al. (Johnson et al., 2023; Kizil et al., 2002). The dried starch samples were first ground into fine powder in an agate mortar. Subsequently, an appropriate amount of potassium bromide was added, and the mixture was ground to uniformity before being pressed into pellets for scanning. The scanning range was set as follows: wavenumber, 400–4000 cm−1; resolution, 2 cm−1; and scanning times 24 times.

Microstructure of starch granules

The microstructure of the sorghum starch granules was examined using a scanning electron microscope (Model VEGA3; TESCAN ORSAY HOLDING, a.s., Brno, Czech Republic). A conductive adhesive was fixed on the sample table, and a proper amount of sample powder was evenly coated onto a conductive adhesive with toothpicks. A 20-nm gold powder was sprayed using a gold spray instrument and then magnified using a scanning electron microscope with <15 kV acceleration voltage and 100 V working voltage. The microstructure of the sorghum starch particles was observed and photographed.

Solubility and swelling power of starch

The solubility and swelling power of starch were determined using a slightly modified version of Mao Xiang's method (Xiang et al., 2020). A 0.15-g starch sample (dry weight, M1) was placed in a test tube containing 10 mL of distilled water and equilibrated at room temperature for 5 min. The tube was then placed in an 80 °C water bath with continuous stirring for 30 min. After cooling to room temperature, the sample was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 20 min (Model DS-HXT9; Shanghai Hengke Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The supernatant was transferred into a constant-weight aluminum box and then dried at 105 °C to constant weight. The precipitate mass and the drying to a constant supernatant mass correspond to the values of solubility and swelling force at different temperatures.

Thermal properties of starch

The gelatinization thermal properties of starch were determined using a differential scanning calorimeter (Model DSC 200F3; NETZSCH-Gerätebau GmbH, Selb, Germany). Approximately 3 mg of room-temperature-dried sorghum starch was mixed with deionized water at a ratio of 3:1 in an aluminum crucible, sealed, and equilibrated for 2 h before being determined. The scanning temperature range was 20 °C–100 °C at a heating rate is 10 °C/min, with a sealed empty aluminum crucible serving as the blank control.

Starch viscosity properties

Starch gelatinization viscosity was determined using a rapid viscosity analyzer (Model RVA4500; Perten Instruments, Hägersten, Sweden). A 3.00 g ± 0.1 g starch sample was placed in an aluminum can, mixed with 25.0 g ± 0.1 mL of ultra-pure water, and stirred uniformly. The sample was pre-stirred at 960 rpm for 10 s before being maintained at 50 °C for 1 min, with a constant spin rate of 160 rpm. The temperature was then increased from 50 °C to 95 °C at a rate of 12 °C/min, maintained at 95 °C for 2.5 min, then cooled back to 50 °C at 12 °C/min, and maintained at 50 °C for 2 min. Each sample was tested in triplicate, and viscosity-related parameters were recorded and analyzed.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was performed in triplicate, and the results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Data were processed and analyzed using SPSS Statistics 27 and plotted using Origin Jing et al., 2022 software. Principal component analysis (PCA) was employed to analyze the correlations among the data, followed by multiple linear regression analysis to identify the key factors influencing SB and BD.

The amylopectin chain lengths are divided into four types: short chain (Fa), medium chain (Fb1), medium and long chain (Fb2), and long chain (Fb3), with Fa and Fb1 being the most abundant (Gomand et al., 2009; Wenfang et al., 2017). The proportion and distribution of chain lengths among the four types of sorghum varieties differ . Notably, the Fb2 and average chain length showed a tendency to be higher in the two waxy sorghum varieties than in the japonica sorghum, whereas Fa showed the opposite trend (Luyao et al., 2024). The average chain length of waxy sorghum was significantly longer than that of japonica sorghum, with Fb2 ranging from 12.19 to 14.22. Additionally, the chain length of Fa is 21.98–26.88, with japonica sorghum having significantly higher Fa content than waxy sorghum.

Crystal structure of starch

The X-ray diffraction patterns of all the samples (Fig. 2) show strong diffraction peaks at about 15.7°, 17.2°, 18.1°, and 23.1° of 2θ, indicating a typical A-type crystal structure (Dan et al., 2022). The starch structure is compact with high thermal stability (Jian et al., 2023). This A-type structure stands out for its compact and highly organized starch arrangement, which endows it with remarkable thermal stability (Kangyi et al., 2021).

X-ray diffraction(XRD) pattern indicates a relative crystallinity (RC) range of 23.14 %–34.20 %(Table 3), with waxy sorghum showing significantly higher RC values than japonica sorghum. Waxy sorghum's higher RC (33.18 %–33.20 %) is due to the high amylopectin content (95.86 %–96.31 %), which promotes the formation of double helices. In contrast, japonica sorghum has lower amylopectin content (79.43 %–79.46 %) and contains more amylose. Amylose molecules can interfere with the ordered arrangement of amylopectin clusters, thereby reducing crystallinity (Hyun-Jung et al., 2011).

The infrared absorption spectra of the four sorghum starches were similar, and the absorption peaks caused by the bending vibration of the 995 cm−1 bond, Cmuro O and Cmure C bond, H—O—H bond, H—C—H bond, and Omurh bond appeared at the wavenumber of 995 cm−1, 1150 cm−1, 1650 cm−1, 2930 cm−1, and 3380 cm−1, respectively (Govindaraju et al., 2025; Jidong et al., 2018; Zhenbing et al., 2025). The peak height at 1047 cm−1 increases with the rise in crystallinity. The peak at 1022 cm−1 represents the vibration mode of molecules in the amorphous region, and its peak height decreases as crystallinity increases. Therefore, the peaks at 1047 cm−1 and 1022 cm−1 are sensitive to the crystalline and amorphous structures in starch, and the absorbance ratio at wavenumbers 1047/1022 cm−1 (OD value) can be used to reflect the relative proportion of ordered crystalline regions to amorphous regions in starch molecules. In this study, waxy sorghum had higher OD values (XK32 = 1.1645 ± 0.01, XK35 = 1.1537 ± 0.01) compared to japonica sorghum (SD = 1.1456 ± 0.02, DB = 1.1535 ± 0.01), indicating a higher degree of short-range order, which is consistent with the higher RC values obtained from XRD. This positive correlation suggests that the amylopectin-dominated structure in waxy sorghum is more conducive to forming hierarchical ordered structures from short-range to long-range (Jian et al., 2023). However, it should be noted that a high short-range order does not always guarantee high long-range crystallinity, as the extent and perfection of double-helix aggregation also influence the final crystalline structure (Zhenbing et al., 2025). This shows that the amylopectin/amylose ratio dictates the crystallinity and structural order of starch.

Morphology of sorghum starch granules(SEM)

All sorghum starch granules exhibit irregular polyhedron shapes with varying sizes(Fig. 3). They are unevenly arranged and display honeycomb-like structures and depressions. Microscopically, the particle sizes of the four types of sorghum starch granules ranged from 5 μm to 20 μm. Microscopic measurements revealed that, compared with waxy sorghum, japonica sorghum granules tend to be slightly larger and more irregular. The surface of SD starch granules was smoother than that of DB starch, whereas most of the SD starch granules were spherical. Japonica sorghum's slightly larger granules may be related to its higher amylose content, as amylose can promote granule aggregation during biosynthesis (Lindeboom et al., 2004).

Swelling power and solubility of starch

Swelling power refers to the ability of starch molecules to retain water during heating, with values ranging from 6.51 to 69.35 g/g (Table 3). The starch swelling power of the two waxy sorghum varieties was significantly higher than that of the two japonica sorghum varieties. The reason can be as follows: first, the starch fine structure affects the change in the expansion coefficient, as the presence of amylose inhibits the expansion of starch particles and maintains the integrity of the expanded granules (Hyun-Jung et al., 2011); second, the size of the starch particles significantly influences starch swelling, as a larger average particle size equates to lesser swelling power(Qingqing et al., 2015). The low amylose content (inhibiting granule expansion) and smaller particle size (increasing water absorption area) of the two waxy sorghum varieties increased its swelling power.

Thermal properties of starch

Enthalpy Change (ΔH) reflects the heat energy absorbed during gelatinization as the starch crystal melts, breaking the hydrogen bonds within the double-helical structure (Yongzhi et al., 2017). The Peak Temperature (Tp) of starch reflects the energy needed to destroy the starch crystal structure. The higher ΔH, Tp and Crystallization Temperature (Tc) of waxy sorghum indicate that its crystal structure is more stable, requiring more energy to melt (Table 4). This is because longer amylopectin chains (Fb2, Fb3) form more double helices, which is in line with Zhao et al. (Luyao et al., 2024; Xiangli et al., 2014; Zhao et al., 2021) who reported that waxy sorghum has higher gelatinization temperature.

Starch gelatinization and retrogradation

Starches derived from different sorghum varieties exhibited significant differences in their pasting and retrogradation characteristics. The gelatinization curve is presented in Fig. 4, and the gelatinization parameters are summarized in Table 5. During starch pasting, when the system temperature exceeds the Pasting temperature (PTM) value, that is, around 200 s, the starch crystals break down, start absorbing water and swelling, causing the viscosity to rise rapidly and eventually reach its peak viscosity (PV) (Jingwei et al., 2023). The viscosity of all four sorghum varieties exceeded 200 s and increased continuously with the extension of time. Around 230 s, SD reached the peak first, followed by two japonica sorghum varieties which peaked at approximately 250 s, while DB did not reach its peak until about 270 s. After reaching the peak, the viscosity of the four sorghum varieties all decreased. Subsequently, the starch viscosity showed a certain degree of increase, among which the japonica sorghum exhibited a more significant increase during the temperature decrease process.

PV reflects the degree of expansion of starch particles or the ability to combine water. The higher PV value means the starch particles have a stronger absorption and expansion capability, signifying a more complete pasting process (Bhat & Riar, 2018). The PV values for XK32 and XK35 were notably higher compared to the other two samples. This shows that XK32 and XK35 have a strong ability to bind with water, resulting in higher swelling power. Setback (SB) represents the difference between the final viscosity and the minimum viscosity, serving as an indicator of the stability and retrogradation properties of the starch paste once it has cooled (Elkhalifa et al., 2004). Although waxy sorghum has higher crystallinity, its PV values (5251.33–5366.00 cP) are only slightly higher than japonica sorghum (4722.00–5124.67 cP) because its high amylopectin content promotes granule swelling, offsetting the delayed gelatinization caused by high crystallinity. This is different from the general rule that high crystallinity leads to significantly higher PV, which may be due to the unique balance between amylopectin content and crystallinity in sorghum starch.

The SB values of SD and DB were significantly higher than those of waxy sorghum, which were easier to regenerate and difficult to decompose and utilize by microorganisms (Fei et al., 2023). Final viscosity (FV) indicates the starch's ability to form a sticky paste after heating and cooling. Some studies have suggested that starch with a high amylopectin content leads to higher PV and lower FV and SB (Lindeboom et al., 2004; Xiaomei, 2024), corresponding to the present study. Breakdown (BD) represents the shear resistance and stability of the starch paste. The larger the disintegration value, the more obvious the decrease in the viscosity of the starch paste during cooling, the weaker the shear resistance, and the worse the stability. The BD values of XK32 and XK35 were significantly higher than those of the other samples, indicating that waxy sorghum had poorer thermal stability and easier saccharification and gelatinization than japonica sorghum, which was beneficial to microbial utilization.

Principal component analysis(PCA) of the physicochemical properties and gelatinization and retrogradation characteristics of sorghum starch

The differences and similarities between the four sorghum varieties were visually analyzed using a PCA diagram. The first two principal components accounted for 90.6 % of the total variance, with the first and second principal components accounting for 60.0 % and 30.6 % of the total variance, respectively (Fig. 5). The results show that the fine structure, gelatinization, and retrogradation characteristics differed significantly between the japonica sorghum and waxy sorghum starch. Close parameters indicate a positive correlation, whereas opposite parameters indicate a negative correlation. PC1 was related to Fb2, PV, Fa, and Tp, whereas PC2 was related to Δ H, Tc–To, Tc, and Fb3. The axis prediction load score of PCA confirmed that a higher FV and amylopectin content, coupled with a lower PV, results in greater starch retrogradation. Compared with the waxy sorghum varieties XK32 and XK35, the indexes of the two japonica sorghum varieties SD and DB were highly similar, mainly located in the positive region of PC2, while XK35 exhibited more variation than XK32, indicating that XK35 was more affected by starch structure.

Conclusion

This study's results provide clear information regarding the effects of the fine molecular structure and physicochemical properties of starch in four different sorghum varieties. The starch granule of the japonica sorghum variety (SD, DB) tended to be slightly larger than that of the waxy sorghum variety (XK32, XK35). The crystallinity of waxy sorghum was higher than that of japonica sorghum, and its average chain length was also longer. The higher the amylopectin content and the lower the proportion of the short chains of amylopectin, the higher the starch's crystallinity. This, in turn, significantly affected the solubility and swelling power of starch, which were lower in the japonica sorghum than in the waxy sorghum. Increased crystallinity made the starch structure more compact, as evidenced by the higher Tp (74.90–75.10 °C) and ΔH (12.64–13.64 J/g) of waxy sorghum (high RC: 33.18 %–33.20 %) compared to japonica sorghum (low RC: 23.14 %–23.45 %, Tp: 72.60–74.00 °C, ΔH: 11.02–11.66 J/g). Higher crystallinity requires more energy to break the double-helical structure, making gelatinization more difficult, which is supported by the DSC results. Higher crystallinity of Fb2 and Fb3 results in lower starch retrogradation capacity. In terms of thermal properties, ΔH, Tp, and Tc were lower in japonica sorghum than in waxy sorghum. Regarding gelatinization and retrogradation characteristics, japonica sorghum had a higher disintegration value than waxy sorghum. The study revealed that increased proportions of branched chains and medium-to-long chains, crystallinity (RC), order degree (OD), and average chain length increases ΔH, Tp, Tc, solubility, and disintegration value (BD). Conversely, a smaller proportion of short branched chains resulted in reduced FV, TV, and SB values. This study analyzed the structure of amylopectin in different sorghum varieties and identified key factors affecting starch gelatinization and retrogradation. The findings of this study provide basic data for understanding the effects of starch structure on grain cooking quality.
