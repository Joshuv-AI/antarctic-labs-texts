Mutational scanning reveals substrate-assisted autoregulation of the WNT destruction complex
Authors: Murugesh Padmanarayana, Saira Sakalas, Parijat Sarkar, Mengxiao Ma, Ethan R. Garvin, Ethan Lee, Steven M. Corsello, Sebastian Guettler, Ganesh V. Pusapati, Rajat Rohatgi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The β-catenin destruction complex (BDC) regulates WNT–β-catenin signaling and is a prime therapeutic target in colorectal cancer, yet its biochemical complexity has hindered mechanistic understanding. We mapped the sequence–function landscape of the BDC using tiled base editor screens across its components CTNNB1, AXIN1, APC and GSK3B. Amongst ~150 previously unreported mutations that affected WNT signaling, we discovered gain-of-function and separation-of-function alleles that reveal mechanisms of complex assembly, including a β-catenin region regulating TCF/LEF transcription factor binding. Critically, we found that the AXIN1–β-catenin interface controls signaling flux through the oncogenic BDC found in APC-mutant cancers. In cells expressing truncated APC, β-catenin itself scaffolds BDC assembly, establishing a substrate-assisted autoregulatory mechanism. This architecture represents an unexploited therapeutic vulnerability: strengthening the AXIN1–β-catenin interaction restores destruction complex function and impairs the growth of colorectal cancer cells. Our mutational resource provides a foundation for mechanistic understanding and therapeutic targeting of the WNT pathway.

Main

The WNT–β-catenin pathway is a cell–cell communication system that coordinates tissue development and homeostasis1. Unchecked WNT–β-catenin signaling is a principal cause of human cancer morbidity and mortality, yet we lack an approved agent targeting this pathway in any cancer2. Truncating mutations in the tumor suppressor gene adenomatous polyposis coli (APC) lead to ligand-independent, oncogenic signaling that drives >80% of human colorectal cancer (CRC)—the second leading cause of cancer deaths3,4. APC is a component of the BDC—a multiprotein complex that limits signaling by promoting the degradation of β-catenin5–7. The BDC is composed of two scaffolding proteins (APC and AXIN1), two kinases (CK1α and GSK3β) and β-catenin. In the absence of WNTs, the BDC keeps signaling off by promoting the sequential phosphorylation of β-catenin by CK1α and GSK3β, marking it for ubiquitination and degradation8 (Fig. 1a). Three decades of structural and biochemical studies have mapped several overlapping and seemingly redundant protein–protein interactions (PPIs) between BDC components5,6,10 (Fig. 1b). β-Catenin contains a structured central armadillo (ARM) domain composed of 12 repeats and flexible N- and C-terminal segments. β-Catenin engages AXIN1, APC and the TCF/LEF family of transcription factors in a groove that extends along the ARM superhelix. AXIN1 binds at ARM repeats 3–4, whereas APC and the TCF/LEF proteins bind a longer extent of this groove. APC can bind to β-catenin through four 15 amino acid (aa; 15R A–D) and six 20-aa (20R 1–7, excluding 20R2) peptide motifs. Phosphorylation of the 20R repeats by CK1α and GSK3β enhances their affinities for β-catenin by ~1,000-fold11,12. APC contains three SAMP motifs that allow it to interact independently with an N-terminal RGS domain in AXIN113. AXIN1 recruits the kinases CK1α and GSK3β into the BDC complex, bringing them into the proximity of their substrates β-catenin and APC14–16. Other proteins also occupy the same β-catenin groove: TCF/LEF family transcription factors, cadherins and ICAT (inhibitor of β-catenin and TCF4)6.

The complex biochemistry of the BDC has presented obstacles to both mechanistic understanding and therapeutic targeting. Here we report adenine base editor (ABE) and cytosine base editor (CBE) screens to evaluate the WNT signaling phenotype of mutations introduced across endogenous gene loci encoding BDC components17–21. These screens enabled in-cell functional annotation of BDC PPI interfaces identified by previous structural and biochemical studies. We also identified classes of mutations that are highly informative in sequence–function studies: activating mutations, separation-of-function mutations and mutations that selectively tune the activity of the oncogenic BDC.

Results

Tiled base editor screens targeting destruction complex genes

We used promiscuous PAM-flexible (NG-PAM Cas9n) ABEs and CBEs (NG-ABE8e and NG-BE3) to generate transition mutations across all exons of endogenous APC, AXIN1, CTNNB1 (encoding β-catenin) and GSK3B (encoding GSK3β) in a pooled format19–22. Our ABE and CBE libraries contain single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) that are predicted to generate missense, splice-site, nonsense and silent mutations covering 80–90% of aa residues encoded by each gene (Extended Data Fig. 1a,b and Supplementary Table 1). Mutagenesis was conducted in human haploid cells (eHAP1) stably expressing our fluorescent transcriptional reporter of WNT–β-catenin signaling strength23 (7TS; Fig. 1c). Pooled ABE and CBE mutant cell libraries were exposed to the ligand Wnt-3a and cells with 7TS fluorescence intensities in the top 10% or bottom 15% of the population were isolated by fluorescence activated cell sorting (FACS) (Fig. 1d). Two z-scores for each sgRNA (for enrichment in the top 10% and bottom 15% gates) were calculated based on a distribution of ~280 nontargeting control (NTC) sgRNAs (Supplementary Table 1). Screen design, reproducibility, z-score distributions, and false positive/negative rates are described in Extended Data Fig. 1c–g, Supplementary Note and Supplementary Fig. 1.

Our screens identified many known functional sites in the BDC, including the phosphodegron in β-catenin and the tankyrase, β-catenin and Dishevelled (DVL) binding sites in AXIN1 (Extended Data Figs. 2 and 3). Reassuringly, the most statistically significant sgRNAs in our screen encode mutations that drive oncogenic WNT–β-catenin signaling in human cancer4,24. More than 80% of sporadic and familial CRC cases are driven by truncating mutations that map to a ~250-aa segment in the central intrinsically disordered region (IDR) of APC known as the mutation cluster region (MCR)25–31 (Fig. 1e). Mutant APCmcr alleles in human cancer produce a truncated protein lacking all three SAMP repeats that mediate direct interaction of APC with AXIN1 (Fig. 1e), forming a compromised, oncogenic BDC that cannot fully suppress β-catenin abundance10,32,33. Mirroring oncogenic APCmcr driver alleles, several sgRNAs predicted to introduce nonsense mutations in APC before the first SAMP repeat were enriched in the top 10% gate (Fig. 1e). Remarkably, nonsense mutations in APC after the first SAMP repeat were not enriched, highlighting the low false positive rate of the screens and their ability to recapitulate human CRC genetics (Extended Data Fig. 1g).

The most common oncogenic β-catenin mutations in human cancers disrupt an N-terminal degron motif (32DSGIHSGATTAPS45) sequentially phosphorylated by two AXIN1-bound kinases, CK1α and GSK3β8,33,34 (Fig. 1f). GSK3β phosphorylation of Ser33 and Ser37 generates a high-affinity binding site (32DpSGφXpS37) for the SCFβ-TrCP E3 ligase, which ubiquitylates β-catenin and earmarks it for proteasomal degradation35. Most (~60%) of the 31 overlapping sgRNAs predicted to target residues in this phosphodegron were enriched in the top 10% sorted population with a z-score > 2, consistent with their expected effect of causing constitutive, high-level WNT–β-catenin signaling (Fig. 1f). More generally, pathogenic β-catenin missense variants in ClinVar were enriched as screen hits versus other variant classes (Supplementary Note and Supplementary Table 2).

An x–y plot of sgRNA z-scores for enrichment in the bottom 15% gate (x axis) versus top 10% gate (y axis) identified sgRNAs predicted to make mutations in many regions known to be critical for BDC function and regulation (Fig. 1g). We used a multistep approach to validate missense mutations selected for deeper mechanistic analysis (Extended Data Fig. 4a and Supplementary Table 3). Most validated missense mutations mapped to β-catenin and AXIN1 (Extended Data Fig. 4b–e). Mutations in APC (Extended Data Fig. 3a–d) and GSK3β (Extended Data Fig. 3e–h) had overall lower z-scores in the screen and smaller effects on signaling in validation tests (Extended Data Fig. 4f,g). The redundancy of APC interactions (Fig. 1b) makes them difficult to disrupt with point mutations, consistent with only nonsense mutations being found as drivers in CRC24. Functional redundancy between GSK3β and GSK3α in HAP1 cells probably explains the absence of GSK3B hits36. We therefore focused on mechanistic analyses of β-catenin and AXIN1.

The sequence–function landscape of β-catenin

Mapping top 10% and bottom 15% enrichment z-scores (above a cutoff of 2) onto a structural model37,38 revealed that our screen identified missense mutations in β-catenin located at known interaction interfaces with β-TrCP, BCL9, AXIN1, APC and the TCF/LEF transcription factors (Fig. 2a and Extended Data Fig. 2a–d). These mutations alter β-catenin residues that make direct contacts with the partner protein (defined as a distance <5 Å) as well as nearby residues that probably influence the interaction by means of allosteric effects (Extended Data Fig. 5a–d). Overall, our screen and subsequent directed analysis in two different cell lines provided functional data on endogenous mutations at ~185 residues in β-catenin (Fig. 2b and Extended Data Fig. 6a,b). In addition to identifying several residues implicated previously in β-catenin function, our screens also identified unexpected mutations that either increase or decrease WNT–β-catenin signaling strength (but cannot be predicted by previous structural or biochemical studies). To illustrate how scanning mutagenesis can functionally annotate a structurally characterized protein interaction interface, we focused on β-catenin site 4, which forms an extended groove along the ARM superhelix between repeats 5 and 10 (Fig. 2c). The groove in site 4 can bind (in a mutually exclusive manner) to APC 20R and 15R repeats, TCF/LEF proteins, ICAT and E-cadherin39–46. Our screen identified a column of mutations along one face of a helix that frames the site 4 groove: K435G/M436V, R469H, C466Y/A467T and E462K (Fig. 2c). K435 is a well-characterized ‘charge button’ residue critical for several β-catenin interactions6. Indeed, sgRNAs targeting β-catenin K435 or the nearby basic residue R469 reduced signaling strength in response to WNTs or the GSK3β inhibitor CHIR99021, consistent with their roles in anchoring β-catenin to TCF439,40 (Fig. 2d). Notably, R469H is a ClinVar variant of uncertain significance associated with severe intellectual disability and progressive spastic diplegia (Supplementary Table 2). Mutations at β-catenin C466/A467, implicated in nonpolar contacts with both APC and TCF447,48, also reduced signaling strength in response to WNTs (Fig. 2e). Biolayer interferometry (BLI) binding assays using purified proteins confirmed that R469H and C466Y/A467T reduced β-catenin’s affinity for TCF4 by more than tenfold (Fig. 2f).

We also identified β-catenin mutations in this crowded PPI region that had effects only on specific interaction partners. E462K unexpectedly enhanced signaling strength in cells (Fig. 2g), suggesting selectively impaired interactions with the negative regulator APC. In binding assays with purified proteins, E462K did not alter β-catenin-TCF4 affinity (Fig. 2f). E462 probably forms an electrostatic interaction with K1030 in APC 15R1, although the K1030 side chain is disordered in the crystal structure42 (Fig. 2c). The corresponding TCF4 residue (K22) points in the opposite direction and cannot make a salt bridge with β-catenin E462, explaining why β-catenin E462K is a separation-of-function mutation that can discriminate between APC and TCF/LEF binding.

Gain- and loss-of-function mutations in β-catenin

Several mutations in sites 4–6 identified in our screen targeted residues not previously implicated in β-catenin function or PPIs (sgRNAs highlighted in magenta lettering in Fig. 2b, individual mutations shown in Extended Data Fig. 6a). Mechanistic analysis was conducted in pooled HEK293T cell lines following base editor and sgRNA introduction. Mutant allele frequencies were measured by Illumina sequencing to identify the dominant β-catenin mutation (Supplementary Fig. 2).

In β-catenin site 4 (ARM 5–10), three mutations (L497P/L498P, E571K and I579T/L580P; Fig. 3a) reduced Wnt-3a-induced activation of both the 7TS reporter (Fig. 3b) and transcription of the endogenous target gene AXIN2 (Extended Data Fig. 7a), without altering β-catenin abundance or signal-induced accumulation (Fig. 3c and Extended Data Fig. 7b). To measure in-cell PPIs, β-catenin was immunoprecipitated from extracts of untreated cells or cells treated with Wnt-3a or CHIR99021 (Fig. 3c). These loss-of-function mutations did not impair the interaction of β-catenin with AXIN1, APC or E-cadherin, confirming ARM domain integrity and ruling out global misfolding as the basis for the signaling defect. However, they decreased selectively the interaction between β-catenin and several TCF/LEF transcriptional activators. Notably, E571K inhibited Wnt-3a-driven signaling but had little effect on CHIR99021-induced signaling (Fig. 3b). Consistent with activity assays, the interaction between β-catenin E571K and TCF/LEF proteins was partially restored by CHIR99021 (Fig. 3c). In vitro interaction assays (BLI) demonstrated that β-catenin E571K is a separation-of-function mutant: it has an around sevenfold reduced affinity for TCF4 but unaltered affinity for ICAT, a negative regulator that binds in the same region (Fig. 3d). We next turned to mutations in site 5 (ARM11–12) that both decreased (Fig. 4a and Extended Data Fig. 7c) and increased (Fig. 4b) WNT ligand sensitivity without substantially altering β-catenin abundance or signal-induced accumulation (Fig. 4c and Extended Data Fig. 7d). The only exception was β-catenin L621P, which showed a modest decrease in abundance but still accumulated upon signal activation. The reduction-of-function L621P and D624N/E626K/E629K mutations reduced the association between β-catenin and TCF/LEF proteins in cell lysates. In purified binding assays, D624N/E626K/E629K reduced the affinity of β-catenin for both TCF4 and ICAT by around tenfold (Fig. 4d). In comparison, both β-catenin mutations (C619Y/E620K and L643S/P;L644P) that increased Wnt-3a potency also enhanced association with TCF4 in cell extracts (Fig. 4c). The C619 and E620 residues are located at the end of the β-catenin ARM superhelix that interacts with a helical bundle of the negative regulator ICAT (Fig. 4e). Introduction of a bulky residue in C619Y or a charge reversal in E620K could disrupt this interface. In a purified binding assay, C619Y/E620K did not change β-catenin-TCF4 affinity but profoundly impaired the β-catenin-ICAT interaction (Fig. 4d). As ICAT and TCF4 bind to β-catenin in a mutually exclusive manner, the enhanced association of β-catenin C619Y/E620K with TCF/LEF proteins (Fig. 4c) probably results from the impaired association with ICAT or other negative regulators41,44,49. The second activating mutation (β-catenin L643S/P;L644P) is not at the direct interface with ICAT (Fig. 4f) but could potentially affect the interaction allosterically. This example highlights the ability of base-editing screens to identify separation-of-function mutations at a common PPI interface, especially useful in the analysis of multifunctional proteins such as β-catenin.

The isolation of bidirectional mutations in β-catenin ARM10–12 nominates this region as a largely unexplored regulatory site for the association of β-catenin with TCF/LEF transcription factors. Several of these mutations also reduced the constitutively high WNT reporter activity in CRC cell lines carrying the APCmcr oncoprotein (which cannot bind AXIN1 directly) (Extended Data Fig. 7e–h), suggesting that small molecules that bind in ARM10–12 could disrupt the β-catenin-TCF4 interaction in CRC.

The sequence–function landscape of AXIN1

As AXIN1 is a negative regulator, gain-of-function mutations (Fig. 5a, blue bars) suppress WNT–β-catenin signaling and loss-of-function mutations elevate signaling (Fig. 5a, red bars). Gain-of-function mutations in AXIN1 were considerably more common than those identified in β-catenin and were enriched in its C-terminal region, including its domain present in DVL and AXIN (DAX) domain (Fig. 5a (blue bars) and Extended Data Fig. 2e–h). As with β-catenin, sgRNAs predicted to make mutations at known AXIN1 interaction interfaces were top hits in our screens (labeled in Fig. 5a). Among the bottom 15% hits, several mapped to the known interface between AXIN1 and tankyrase enzymes (Fig. 5c and Extended Data Fig. 2f). Conversely, mutations at the interface between the AXIN1 RGS domain and the SAMP motifs on APC (Fig. 5d and Extended Data Fig. 2g) were highly enriched in the top 10% gate, consistent with disrupting a critical PPI in the BDC13.

Mutations in AXIN1 can repair the oncogenic BDC

This observation presented an opportunity to probe the AXIN1-β-catenin interaction in cells by tuning its affinity. Our goal was to compare the impact of these mutations on AXIN1-β-catenin affinity in vitro to their effect on BDC integrity in cells, WNT–β-catenin signaling and the proliferation of WNT-addicted CRC cells. To further magnify affinity differences, we combined V475I with L479W, identified in a phage display campaign for high-affinity β-catenin-binding stapled peptides53,54 (Fig. 6c). The V475I/L479W double mutant AXIN1 CBD peptide showed a 25-fold increase in β-catenin affinity over the wild-type CBD (40 nM versus 1 μM) (Fig. 6d and Extended Data Fig. 9b). The increased affinity was driven almost completely by a ~20-fold decrease in the dissociation rate constant (koff) of β-catenin from the AXIN1 V475I/L479W CBD. To disrupt the AXIN1–β-catenin interaction, we combined three mutations (L471A/D472A/H474A) in the AXIN1 CBD55 and established that this variant showed no detectable binding to purified β-catenin (Fig. 6c,d and Extended Data Fig. 9b). We hereafter refer to these affinity-tuned variants as affinity enhancing (AE) (AXIN1AE: V475I/L479W) or affinity reducing (AR) (AXIN1AR: L471A/D472A/H474A), distinct from wild-type AXIN1 (AXIN1WT).

To understand the consequences of increasing or decreasing the lifetime of the AXIN1-β-catenin complex in cells, we introduced the AE and AR mutations at the endogenous AXIN1 locus in two isogenic eHAP1 cell lines: one carrying an unmodified APC allele and another with an oncogenic APCmcr allele (predicted to express an APCmcr protein truncated at amino acid 1,337 or APC 1,337*) (Supplementary Fig. 3). In cell lines expressing full-length APC, all three AXIN1 variants associated with β-catenin and APC to a comparable extent under no or low Wnt-3a conditions (Fig. 7a and Extended Data Fig. 9c). As AXIN1AR has no measurable direct affinity for β-catenin (Fig. 6d), it probably associates with β-catenin indirectly through APC10,56. Under saturating Wnt-3a or CHIR99021 (both maximally inhibiting GSK3β and activating signaling), AXIN1AE pulled down much greater amounts of both β-catenin and APC than AXIN1WT (Fig. 7a and Extended Data Fig. 9c). In APCmcr cells expressing truncated APC (APC 1337*) lacking all three AXIN1-binding SAMP repeats, AXIN1AE bound much greater amounts of β-catenin and APC compared to AXIN1WT under all conditions tested. Conversely, AXIN1AR association with both β-catenin and APC was abolished. Thus, in the absence of the RGS-SAMP mediated interaction between AXIN1 and APCmcr, β-catenin can bridge the two proteins by binding simultaneously to APCmcr (through its 15R A–D repeats) and AXIN1 (through its CBD)10,56. Reconstitution with purified proteins confirmed β-catenin as the linchpin of the oncogenic BDC10: APC variants truncated before the SAMP repeats associated only with AXIN1 in the presence of β-catenin (Extended Data Fig. 10). Further truncations of APC that remove the 15R repeats abolished β-catenin-mediated bridging.

We observed a notable difference in WNT–β-catenin signaling behavior, measured by AXIN2 mRNA abundance, between cell lines expressing full-length APC and APC 1337*. With full-length APC, basal and Wnt-3a-induced signaling was comparable across all three AXIN1 variants. In APCmcr cells, however, AXIN1AR led to further increases in AXIN2 mRNA and nuclear β-catenin abundance, whereas the AXIN1AE had the opposite effect (Fig. 7b and Extended Data Fig. 9d). Thus, cells with oncogenic APCmcr were more sensitive to changes in AXIN1-β-catenin affinity than cells with wild-type APC.

To test this concept in a cancer cell line, we inducibly expressed AXIN1WT, AXIN1AE or AXIN1AR in SW480—a human CRC cell line with a mutant APC allele encoding a protein truncated at residue 1,338. AXIN1AE showed enhanced association with β-catenin, whereas AXIN1AR failed to associate with β-catenin (consistent with HAP1 results) (Fig. 7c). β-Catenin phosphorylated at its N-terminal degron motif (and thus marked for degradation) was considerably more abundant in cells expressing AXIN1AE, showing that enhancing AXIN1-β-catenin affinity can improve the activity of the oncogenic BDC complex (Fig. 7c). Accordingly, AXIN2 mRNA was suppressed to a greater extent in AXIN1AE-expressing cells than AXIN1WT cells at a range of protein abundances (Fig. 7d,e). AXIN1AR was unable to suppress signaling at all concentrations tested.

To test the functional impact of increasing AXIN1-β-catenin affinity on cell proliferation, we expressed the affinity-tuned AXIN1 alleles in three CRC cell lines carrying APCmcr alleles whose growth depends on WNT signaling57 (Fig. 7f). As expected, AXIN1WT partially suppressed colony formation and proliferation of all three cell lines52. However, AXIN1AE was much more effective in suppressing both colony formation and proliferation in every case, despite comparable expression. As a control experiment, AXIN1AE and AXIN1WT were indistinguishable in a CRC cell line with full-length APC (Fig. 7f, RKO). Together these data indicate that the AXIN1-β-catenin interaction is a rate-limiting step in oncogenic WNT–β-catenin signaling that can be manipulated to increase or decrease the activity of the impaired BDC found in cells that carry APCmcr alleles.

Discussion

A longstanding, unresolved challenge in WNT signaling is understanding the biochemical differences between the wild-type, ligand-regulated BDC and the oncogenic BDC, hobbled by truncating mutations in APC. Human cancer genetics first revealed that oncogenic WNT–β-catenin signaling is not driven by the complete loss of APC function28,58. Instead, at least one APC allele in both sporadic and familial CRC cases is truncated such that it loses all three SAMP repeats required for its interaction with AXIN1 but retains at least one of the repeats that interacts with β-catenin (Fig. 1b and Extended Data Fig. 7e). This produces an intermediate level of ligand-independent WNT–β-catenin signaling that drives tumorigenesis but avoids senescence or cell death58,59.

Building on previous models for BDC function5,43,47,55,56 and the data in this study, we propose that BDC assembly and regulation are fundamentally different in cells expressing full-length APC and APCmcr. With full-length APC, increasing or even abolishing AXIN1-β-catenin affinity had very little effect on BDC assembly or target gene induction under basal conditions. These data are inconsistent with a model in which β-catenin first binds AXIN1, is phosphorylated by GSK3β and is then handed-off to APC for ubiquitination and degradation55. Our data are more consistent with the view43,47 that phosphorylation of APC 20R repeats (p20R) gates the β-catenin-AXIN1 interaction because both APC p20R and the AXIN1 CBD bind to an overlapping site in a groove in β-catenin (ARM 3–4; Fig. 8a). In the absence of Wnt-3a, APC 20R repeats are phosphorylated by GSK3β, increasing drastically their β-catenin affinities (Kd = ~1 nM) to levels well above those of either AXIN1WT (Kd = ~1 μM) or AXIN1AE (Kd = ~40 nM). Thus, β-catenin cannot bind to AXIN1 directly; however, it can still be recruited into the BDC by APC p20R and phosphorylated by the AXIN1 kinases (Fig. 8b). When GSK3β is inhibited by Wnt-3a or CHIR99021, APC phosphorylation drops, reducing the affinities of its 20R repeats for β-catenin (by 300- to 1,000-fold)11,12. The ARM 3–4 binding site on β-catenin (Fig. 8a) is now available to interact with AXIN1 (Fig. 8c), making AXIN1–β-catenin affinity relevant: AXIN1AE pulls down much more β-catenin and APC than AXIN1WT under Wnt-3a or CHIR99021 (Fig. 7a). In APCmcr cells, AXIN1-β-catenin affinity becomes a critical determinant of BDC assembly: APCmcr cannot bind directly to AXIN1 because it has lost its SAMP motifs and, as a consequence, its remaining 20R repeats cannot be phosphorylated by GSK3β to generate a high-affinity β-catenin-binding site (Fig. 8d). Indeed, point mutations at the interface between AXIN1 CBD and β-catenin ARM 3–4 (AXIN1AR) abolished the association of AXIN1 with both β-catenin and APCmcr (Fig. 7a). Our data support the model that β-catenin serves as the linchpin of the oncogenic BDC56, bridging AXIN1, which binds to ARM 3–4 through its CBD, and APCmcr, which binds to the extended ARM5-9 region through its 15R repeats (Fig. 8d).

We propose that β-catenin becomes both a substrate and a key assembly factor for the oncogenic BDC, thus promoting its own degradation and limiting its own abundance to achieve the ‘just right’ signaling flux necessary for CRC development. This mode of autoregulation is conceptually analogous to substrate-assisted catalysis: steady-state β-catenin abundance in CRC cells is self-regulated by its own availability and critically dependent on its affinities for AXIN1 and APCmcr. Supporting this idea, increasing or decreasing AXIN1-β-catenin affinity has a corresponding effect on the abundance of phosphorylated β-catenin—the immediate product of the reaction catalyzed by the BDC (Fig. 7c). With full-length APC, β-catenin is simply recruited as a substrate to the BDC, which is optimized to suppress basal signaling by minimizing β-catenin abundance (Fig. 8b).

The substrate-assisted model of the oncogenic BDC has therapeutic implications. Small molecules that slow first-order dissociation of β-catenin from AXIN1 or APCmcr (mimicking the AE alleles identified in our screens) may partially repair the activity of the oncogenic BDC (without substantially affecting native BDC). Conversely, drugs that disrupt these interactions should superactivate WNT–β-catenin signaling—also a detrimental outcome given the just right model for CRC development58,59. Substrate affinity and substrate turnover can be competing properties that would need to be balanced in any such strategy. If the affinity between β-catenin and the oncogenic BDC is too high, product inhibition may limit BDC activity if phosphorylated β-catenin cannot be efficiently ubiquitinated or extracted for proteasomal degradation.

We finish by noting that we were able to conduct in-depth analyses of only a small fraction of the base-editing screen dataset in this study. Even in this limited analysis, we identified informative (but rare) gain-of-function and separation-of-function alleles, along with putative regulatory sites (the AXIN1 CBD and the ARM 11–12 region of β-catenin) where distinct mutations have opposite effects on signaling strength (Supplementary Note). Even at known interfaces, our work shows that the functional impact of mutations at endogenous genes can be difficult to predict from structural proximity or in vitro binding alone. Most importantly, base-editing screens allowed us to design precise affinity-tuned alleles of AXIN1 at endogenous loci to determine how changes in affinity at a single PPI interface in a multiprotein complex influence assembly, signaling output and cancer cell proliferation.

Methods

Reagents and antibodies

Wnt-3a conditioned medium was prepared in house23. Recombinant human R-Spondin 3 (R&D Systems, 3500-RS-025) and CHIR99021 (Cayman Chemical, 13122) were obtained commercially. All primary and secondary antibodies, with vendors, cat. nos., clones (where applicable), and dilutions used for immunoblotting and immunofluorescence, are listed in Supplementary Table 4.

Cell lines and culture

HAP1 (near-haploid) and eHAP1 (haploid) human cell lines62,63 were a gift from J. Carette (Stanford University); HEK293T (ATCC CRL-3216), RKO (ATCC CRL-2577), DLD-1 (ATCC CCL-221), SW480 (ATCC CCL-228), HT29 (ATCC HTB-38) and LoVo (ATCC CCL-229) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). None of the cell lines used in this study are listed in the International Cell Line Authentication Committee database of commonly misidentified cell lines. ATCC lines were authenticated by the vendor by short tandem repeat profiling before distribution and were used within 25 passages of receipt without further independent short tandem repeat authentication. HAP1 and eHAP1 ploidy was verified every fifth passage by propidium iodide flow cytometry64. All lines were confirmed mycoplasma-negative (InvivoGen rep-mys-10) and used between passages 3 and 25. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere in the media listed in Supplementary Note. Subculturing every 3 days used 0.05% or 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA (Thermo Fisher, cat. nos. 25300062 or 25200072) depending on adherence.

Generation of WNT transcriptional reporter cell lines

Clonal fluorescent WNT reporter lines were generated in eHAP1, HEK293T, RKO and DLD-1 cells. The lentiviral plasmid 7xTcf-eGFP (7TG; modified from a published construct65 by digestion with BsrGI and SalI to remove the SV40-PuroR cassette, yielding a selection-marker-free backbone compatible with CRISPR base editor plasmids) was used for RKO and DLD-1; 7xTcf-mScarlet (7TS; enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP) replaced by mScarlet) was used for eHAP1 and HEK293T. Lentivirus was produced in HEK293T cells by triple transfection of reporter plasmid with psPAX2 (Addgene, cat. no. 12260) and pMD2.G (Addgene, cat. no. 12259) using polyethylenimine (PEI; Polysciences, cat. no. 23966-2). Recipient lines were transduced (multiplicity of infection (MOI) = ~0.5; 8 µg ml−1 polybrene) and, 48 h later, treated with 50% Wnt-3a conditioned medium for 24 h; reporter-positive single cells were sorted on an SH800S cell sorter (Sony Biotechnology) into 96-well plates. Of ~24 expanded clones per parent line, those exhibiting ≥100-fold reporter induction by 24 h Wnt-3a treatment were retained. Reporter fluorescence was quantified by flow cytometry using a 488 nm laser with 525/50 nm filter (eGFP) or a 561 nm laser with 600/60 nm filter (mScarlet). The sequential gating strategy applied universally across all experiments is detailed in Supplementary Fig. 4.

Tiled CRISPR base-editing sgRNA library construction

Tiled sgRNA libraries against CTNNB1 (ENST00000349496), APC (ENST00000257430), AXIN1 (ENST00000262320) and GSK3B (ENST00000264235) were designed using the Beagle tool (Broad Institute Genetic Perturbation Platform) and filtered to remove BsmBI sites. The design extended each guide ~20 nt into adjacent introns at intron–exon junctions to enable splice-site mutations. Each library included ~280 NTC guides; nonsense mutations were generated only by the CBE library. BsmBI overhangs were appended to each 20-nt protospacer for Golden Gate cloning into pRDA 429 (ABE8e Cas9-NG; Addgene, 179098) and pRDA 336 (BE3.9 Cas9-NG; Addgene, 179095). Subpool-specific PCR primers (Supplementary Table 5) were used to amplify each gene/editor pool from CustomArray oligonucleotide pools before Golden Gate ligation (Esp3I, T4 DNA ligase) and electroporation into Stbl4 cells (Invitrogen C751003) at 30 °C; plasmid DNA was prepared with the HiSpeed Plasmid Maxi Kit (Qiagen, cat. no. 12662). Plasmid libraries met established quality benchmarks66: >70% perfectly matching guides (observed 80–96%), <0.5% undetected guides (0–0.003% per gene) and skew ratio <10. PCR amplification and Golden Gate ligation conditions are in the Supplementary Note.

Mutant library generation, FACS-based phenotype enrichment and sgRNA sequencing

Lentivirus for pooled libraries was produced in HEK293T cells by PEI cotransfection of pMD2.G, psPAX2 and the library plasmid (5 µg, 50 µg and 40 µg respectively per 225 cm2 flask of 20 × 106 cells); supernatant was collected at 48 h. Lentivirus was titered on eHAP1-7TS cells to identify the dose yielding 30–50% transduction efficiency (MOI = ~0.35–0.70). For each of two biological replicates, mutant library cells were generated by spinfection (640g, 2 h; 8 µg ml−1 polybrene) at MOI = ~0.5 followed by 7-day puromycin selection (5 µg ml−1). Library guide representation in pDNA and transduced cells was confirmed by Illumina sequencing against the same quality control criteria as for the plasmid library. For each replicate, library cells were treated with 50% Wnt-3a conditioned medium for 24 h, and the top 10% and bottom 15% of the reporter-fluorescent population were collected by FACS on an SH800S sorter; unsorted cells were retained for log fold change normalization. Genomic DNA was extracted with the Mag-Bind Blood and Tissue HDQ Kit (Omega BioTek, M6393); integrated sgRNA cassettes were amplified from 10 µg gDNA in 100-µl reactions with 2× NEBNext Master Mix and indexed P5/P7 primers (Supplementary Table 5) and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 550 with 20% PhiX spike-in. PCR cycling parameters are in the Supplementary Note.

Screen analysis

Analysis was performed in R 4.3.2. FASTQ files were generated with bcl2fastq v2.20.0; guide RNA reads were extracted with QuasR v1.36.0 PreprocessReads67 and aligned with the Subread algorithm in Rsubread v2.16.268 against a custom reference of all library guides. Counts were assembled into a matrix69 and normalized to log2(reads-per-million (RPM) + 1). sgRNAs with log2RPM in the unsorted sample falling outside 1.5× the interquartile range from the first and third quartiles were filtered as low- or high-representation outliers. For each guide, log2 fold change (LFC) between sorted and unsorted populations was computed; z-scores were derived using the means and standard deviations of NTC LFCs21. One-sided P values were calculated against the upper tail of the standard normal, separately for top- and bottom-gate enrichment, and adjusted by the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Predicted edits within the N3–N10 window were categorized (missense, nonsense, splice-site, silent) by parsing library annotations using custom R functions; outputs are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Code is on GitHub (‘Code availability’).

Validation of base editor gRNA hits

sgRNAs with z-score ≥ 2 (370 in total) were cloned individually into pRDA 429 or pRDA 336 and tested in two reporter lines, eHAP1-7TS and HEK293T-7TS (Extended Data Fig. 4 provides a summary). In eHAP1-7TS cells, sgRNA constructs were delivered by lentiviral transduction at MOI = ~0.5 with spinfection and 5–7 days of puromycin selection; reporter fluorescence was measured by flow cytometry after 24 h ±50% Wnt-3a conditioned medium. In HEK293T-7TS cells, plasmid was delivered by PEI transfection (1 µg per well of 3 × 105 cells) followed by 5–6 days of puromycin selection and the same Wnt-3a readout. Owing to the scale of the initial validation, each measurement was performed once per cell line. sgRNAs producing ≥50% reduction (bottom-gate hits) or ≥10% elevation (top-gate hits) of reporter fluorescence in both cell lines were advanced for at least three independent replicate characterizations. To probe the dependence of selected sgRNA effects on APC status, advanced sgRNAs were also delivered by lentiviral transduction (MOI = ~0.5; 8 µg ml−1 polybrene; 5–7-day puromycin selection at 5 µg ml−1) into the APCmcr reporter line RKO-7TG 1320* and into DLD-1-7TG.

Mutant cell line generation using FACS-enrichment followed by amplicon deep sequencing

For mechanistic follow-up, HEK293T-7TS cells were transduced with individual sgRNAs as above. To enrich activating mutations, cells were treated with 5% Wnt-3a conditioned medium plus 10 ng ml−1 RSPO3 for 24 h and the highest-mScarlet peak was sorted (~1 × 106 cells); to enrich inhibiting mutations, cells were treated with 25% Wnt-3a plus 10 ng ml−1 RSPO3 and the lowest-mScarlet peak was sorted. Sorted populations were expanded and frozen. Mutational composition was determined by two-step amplicon sequencing of the target region (Supplementary Table 5 lists primers and demultiplexing barcodes). Allele frequencies were called with CRISPResso2 v2.2.1470. Detailed PCR conditions are in the Supplementary Note.

APCmcr truncations and AXIN1 precise genome editing

Endogenous APCmcr truncations were introduced into RKO and HAP1 cells by CRISPR–Cas9. RKO cells were nucleofected with the Cell Line Nucleofector Kit R (Lonza VCA-1001; Amaxa Nucleofector II, program A-024) using 1 µg of pSpCas9(BB)-2A-GFP (Addgene, cat. no. 48138) encoding sgRNA APC sg1320 (5′-ACTGCTGGAACTTCGCTCAC-3′). HAP1 cells were electroporated (NEPA21 Electro-Kinetic transfection system; 190 V poring pulse, 5 ms, default settings otherwise) with pre-assembled SpCas9 ribonucleoprotein (RNP; 100 pmol Cas9, 120 pmol sgRNA) targeting APC sg1336 (5′-ACCAAATCCAGCAGACTGCA-3′). Recombinant SpCas9 expression and purification, sgRNA in vitro transcription and RNP assembly71 are described in the Supplementary Note. Live single cells were sorted into 96-well plates 48–72 h after electroporation; clones were validated by Sanger sequencing of the targeted locus and by immunoblotting (truncation size for APC).

For precise genome editing of AXIN1, single-stranded oligonucleotide (ssODN) HDR templates carrying L471A/D472A/H474A or L479W mutations were designed with the IDT CRISPR homology-directed repair (HDR) Design Tool (sequences in Supplementary Table 5). The L471A/D472A/H474A template included a silent PstI site (designed with the molbiotools silent-mutagenesis tool) for restriction-based screening; an allele-specific forward primer (Supplementary Table 5) was used to screen for L479W. RNP (100 pmol) and ssODN (100 pmol) were co-electroporated into wild-type APC and APCmcr-truncated HAP1 cells with 1 µM HDR Enhancer v2 (IDT 10007910). Targeting sequences were 5′-GGAGAACCCTGAGAGCATCC-3′ (L471A/D472A/H474A) and 5′-CAGCGTGTGCTGAGGACACC-3′ (L479W). Putative-positive clones were screened by PstI digestion of a PCR product spanning the locus or by allele-specific PCR, and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The AXIN1 V475I/L479W double mutant line was generated from an L479W clone by lentiviral CBE (pRDA 336) targeting 5′-CTGTACGTGCTCGTCCAGGA-3′ followed by single-cell sorting and Sanger confirmation.

Stable expression of FLAG-AXIN1 variants

We used stable expression (instead of genome editing at the endogenous locus) to introduce AXIN1AE and AXIN1AR alleles in all cell lines except for eHAP1 because engineering these alleles requires several genome edits that are challenging to install at the at least two AXIN1 alleles present in most cell lines. Doxycycline (Dox)-inducible FLAG-AXIN1 (wild-type, AR L471A/D472A/H474A and AE V475I/L479W) lines in SW480 were generated using the PiggyBac transposon system: cells (3 × 105 per six-well plate) were cotransfected with 1 µg PB-TAC-ERP2 (Addgene, cat. no. 80478) and 1 µg Super PiggyBac Transposase plasmid using X-tremeGENE DNA 9 (Sigma, cat. no. 6365787001) and selected for 5 days in 5 µg ml−1 puromycin. For constitutive expression in SW480, RKO, DLD-1, LoVo and HT29 cells, the same FLAG-AXIN1 ORFs and an mNeonGreen vector control were cloned into pRDA 336 with the Cas9-NG sequence replaced (P2A-puromycin retained); lentivirus was produced and transduced as for reporter lines (MOI = ~0.5; 8 µg ml−1 polybrene; 5-day puromycin selection).

Immunoblotting

Cells were harvested by scraping and lysed under conditions matched to the target. For soluble (nonphosphorylated, active) β-catenin, cells were lysed in hypotonic buffer (10 mM Tris pH 7.5, 10 mM KCl, 5 mM EDTA) supplemented with protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo, cat. no. 78442) and 1 µM bortezomib, then subjected to a single liquid-nitrogen freeze–thaw cycle56. For AXIN1, cells were lysed in cell lysis buffer with ionic and nonionic detergents (50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS, 0.1% deoxycholate, 10% glycerol) with inhibitors and vortexed for 30 min at 4 °C. Lysates were cleared at 20,000g for 30 min at 4 °C; protein was quantified by BCA (Thermo, 23225). Total protein (20–30 µg) was resolved on 4–12% NuPAGE Bis-Tris gels and transferred to nitrocellulose. Membranes were blocked in LI-COR Odyssey blocking buffer (β-catenin) or 5% nonfat milk in TBS-T (AXIN1), probed overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody (Supplementary Table 4), washed and detected with IRDye 800CW secondary antibody (Odyssey CLx imager; LI-COR) or horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody (SuperSignal West Pico PLUS, Thermo, cat. no. 34580; film capture).

Co-immunoprecipitation

Cells were collected for co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) by scraping and lysed in cell lysis buffer with nonionic detergent (50 mM Tris pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 10% glycerol) supplemented with protease/phosphatase inhibitors, 1 µM bortezomib, 2 mM MgCl2 and 50 U ml−1 benzonase (1 h on a nutator at 4 °C), then cleared at 20,000g for 30 min at 4 °C. For endogenous β-catenin or AXIN1, 3–4 mg of cleared lysate was precleared with Dynabeads Protein A/G (Thermo 10002D and 10004D; 1 h, 4 °C) before overnight incubation with antibody-conjugated beads (2 µg antibody per IP; Protein G beads for β-catenin, Protein A beads for AXIN1). For FLAG-AXIN1, anti-FLAG M2 magnetic beads (Sigma, cat. no. M8823) were used directly without preclearing. Beads were washed three times with cell lysis buffer with nonionic detergent lacking inhibitors, and immunocomplexes were eluted in 2× Laemmli buffer for immunoblotting.

Immunofluorescence and image analysis

Cells grown on glass coverslips were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS (10 min, room temperature), permeabilized and blocked in BPB (PBS pH 7.4, 1% donkey serum, 1% BSA, 0.1% Triton X-100; 30 min, room temperature) and stained with primary antibody against active β-catenin (1 h, room temperature; Supplementary Table 5) followed by Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti-rabbit (45 min, dark). Coverslips were mounted in ProLong Diamond Antifade with DAPI (Invitrogen P36962). Z-stacks (0.5 µm steps) were acquired on a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope (×63 oil objective) and maximum-intensity projections were analyzed in CellProfiler v4.2.672. Nuclear masks were defined by automated DAPI thresholding; cytoplasmic masks were generated by radial expansion of nuclear boundaries with neighbor exclusion. Mean fluorescence intensity was extracted per compartment.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol (Thermo, cat. no. 15596026); RNA concentration and purity were measured on a Synergy H1 microplate reader (BioTek). RNA (250 ng) was reverse-transcribed with iScript Reverse Transcription Supermix (Bio-Rad, cat. no. 1708841). Quantitative PCR with reverse transcription (RT-qPCR) was performed on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time System (Agilent) with SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Selleck, cat. no. B21703); product specificity was verified by melt-curve analysis. Target gene expression was normalized to glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH). Primers for AXIN2 and GAPDH are listed in Supplementary Table 5.

Recombinant protein expression and purification

Recombinant SpCas9 was expressed from pMJ915 (Addgene, 69090) in Rosetta 2(DE3)pLysS Escherichia coli (Sigma, cat. no. 71403) and purified by Ni-NTA affinity chromatography, TEV cleavage, ion-exchange chromatography (HiTrap SP HP) and size-exclusion chromatography (HiLoad 16/60 Superdex 200) as described in the Supplementary Note. Glutathione S-transferase (GST)-tagged β-catenin binders (AXIN1 aa Cys435–His498; TCF4 aa Met1–Glu53; ICAT aa Met1–Gln81) were expressed in E. coli, lysed in B-PER (Thermo, 90079), captured on glutathione resin, eluted with reduced glutathione and buffer-exchanged for storage. GST-bdSUMO-tagged full-length β-catenin was purified analogously, with on-resin cleavage by 200 nM bdSENP1 (1 h, 4 °C) and final size-exclusion chromatography. Buffer compositions and purification protocols are provided in the Supplementary Note. Protein concentrations were determined by A280. Recombinant AXIN1, β-catenin and APC variants used in reconstituted complex pull-downs were expressed in Sf9 cells using a baculovirus system10.

In vitro pulldown assays

For reconstituted APC–AXIN1–β-catenin complexes10, equimolar prey (APC variants, β-catenin) and AXIN1-dStrepII bait were incubated 20 min at room temperature in binding buffer (50 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 1 mM Tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine). Complexes were captured on packed StrepTactin Sepharose (IBA Lifesciences; 60 min, 4 °C, with rotation) in a final volume of 500 µl, washed three times with 1 ml binding buffer (5 min per wash), eluted in 1.5× SDS sample buffer, and analyzed by SDS–PAGE with Coomassie staining. For GST-AXIN1 CBD–β-catenin pull-downs, 1 µM GST-AXIN1 CBD was incubated with 0.01–2 µM full-length β-catenin (30 min, 22 °C) in binding buffer (20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Triton X-100, 2 mM DTT) before capture on glutathione magnetic agarose (Thermo, 78601), washing, elution in 2× Laemmli buffer and immunoblotting with antibodies against GST and β-catenin (10% of the elution loaded).

Biolayer interferometry

Kinetic binding assays were performed on an Octet RED96 system (ForteBio/Sartorius) at 30 °C with 1,000 rpm shaking in 96-well black flat-bottom polypropylene microplates (Greiner, cat. no. 655209). Octet buffer was 30 mM sodium phosphate pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 5% glycerol, 0.05% Tween-20, and 0.1% BSA. GST biosensors were pre-equilibrated in Octet buffer for 10 min, then loaded with 50 nM GST-tagged ligand (GST-AXIN1 variants, GST-ICAT or GST-TCF4) within the linear BLI range to avoid surface crowding. After loading, sensors were baselined in Octet buffer and dipped into analyte: 500 nM full-length β-catenin for GST-ICAT and 1 µM full-length β-catenin for GST-AXIN1 and GST-TCF4. Association was monitored for 20–25 s and dissociation for 100 s. Buffer-only reference sensors were included to correct for drift and baseline fluctuations. Reference-subtracted sensorgrams were globally fit to a 1:1 binding model in ForteBio Data Analysis Software v10.0; the association phase was modeled with pseudo-first-order kinetics (analyte excess of ~10–60×) and the dissociation phase with first-order decay. Kd = koff/kon; reported Kd values are means of at least three independent experiments.

Cell viability and proliferation assays

After lentiviral transduction and 5-day puromycin selection, RKO, DLD-1, LoVo and HT29 cells expressing FLAG-AXIN1 variants or mNeonGreen control were assayed by two orthogonal approaches. RKO cells were cultured in EMEM (ATCC, 30-2003) and DLD-1 cells in RPMI-1640 (ATCC, 30-2001), both with 10% FBS and 50 U ml−1 penicillin–streptomycin. For colony formation, 25,000 cells per well were seeded in triplicate in six-well plates; 8 days later, cultures were fixed in 8% paraformaldehyde (20 min, room temperature), stained with 0.05% crystal violet (1 h, room temperature), washed, dried and scanned. For continuous proliferation monitoring, 5,000 cells per well were seeded in pentaplicate in 96-well plates and imaged every 3 h for 120 h on the Incucyte S3 Live-Cell Analysis System (Sartorius; phase-contrast, ×10 magnification, two fields per well); confluence was quantified using the Incucyte AI confluence tool with fluorescence channels disabled. Note that we did not test SW480 cells as they did not show strong WNT-dependent cell proliferation (and are not represented in DepMap).

Residue-level overlap of ClinVar missense variants and base-editing screens

ClinVar missense variants for CTNNB1 and AXIN1 were retrieved and curated by excluding entries annotated as ‘Indels’ or with conflicting germline classifications. Germline classifications were collapsed into Pathogenic (pathogenic, likely pathogenic or pathogenic/likely pathogenic), Uncertain Significance or Benign (benign, likely benign or benign/likely benign). Curated ClinVar entries were merged with ABE/CBE screen hits (top or bottom gate; z-score ≥ 2.0) by residue number. Two overlap levels were defined: residue-level (matching residue number; green annotation in Supplementary Table 2) and exact substitution (matching residue and amino acid change; red annotation). Validation results from individual sgRNA characterization were used to assess reproducibility of overlap calls across cell lines. Enrichment of pathogenic versus uncertain-significance variants among screen hits was tested with a two-sided Fisher’s exact test.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size. Sample sizes were chosen on the basis of previous experience with each assay and what is standard for the field. Unless otherwise stated, all experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates and all n values cited in the legends refer to biological replicates. Technical replicates were not counted as independent samples for statistical analysis. These sample sizes were deemed sufficient to ensure rigorous statistical testing because our in vitro systems use targeted genetic and biochemical perturbations that yield highly reproducible effect sizes with low intra-group variance. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during data collection or analysis, as blinding is not relevant or standard practice for in vitro assays where data collection relies on automated readouts (for example, flow cytometry, sequencing and chemiluminescence). Experiments were not randomized. Comparisons were made between defined genotypes or treatment conditions processed in parallel within the same experiment. Covariates of concern (cell line, passage, batch of conditioned medium) were controlled by within-experiment matching. No data were excluded from analysis except (1) sgRNAs failing the pre-defined IQR-based representation filter described in ‘Screen analysis’ and (2) CRISPR-edited clones that failed Sanger sequencing or restriction-digest validation. All replication attempts were successful unless explicitly noted in figure legends. For all representative images shown in the manuscript (including the immunoblots in Figs. 3c, 4c and 7a,c–f and Extended Data Figs. 6b, 7b,d, 9b,c and 10), the experiments were performed independently three to four times with similar results.

For two-group comparisons within a single treatment condition (untreated, Wnt-3a or CHIR99021), significance was assessed by multiple unpaired Student’s t-tests with Welch’s correction; P value rejection thresholds were adjusted by the two-stage step-up method of Benjamini, Krieger and Yekutieli to control the false discovery rate at 0.01. For multigroup comparisons within a single treatment condition, significance was assessed by Brown–Forsythe and Welch analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s T3 multiple-comparison test. Exact P values for key comparisons are reported in figure panels. Quantitative data were visualized in GraphPad Prism v10 and R v4.3.2. Structural figures were generated in UCSF ChimeraX v1.8 with PDB codes given in the respective figure legends. Immunoblot panels were processed in Adobe Photoshop (chemiluminescence) or Fiji v2.14.0 (LI-COR data); figures were assembled in Adobe Illustrator. Data are presented as the mean of independent biological replicates with all individual data points overlaid; error bars and n values are defined in figure legends. Further details on study design and reproducibility are provided in the Supplementary Note and ‘Reporting Summary.’
