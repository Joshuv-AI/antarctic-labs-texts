Dyadic coping profiles and emotional distress in Chinese infertile couples
Authors: Junrui Chen, Sushan Qian, Yujiao Li, Yuting Wu, Jing Zhang, Yanhui Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfertility is a shared stressor that affects both partners, yet heterogeneity in dyadic coping patterns and their associations with emotional distress remain insufficiently understood. This cross-sectional study enrolled 251 infertile couples from reproductive clinics in Hengyang, China. Participants completed the Dyadic Coping Inventory, Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire, Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7, and Patient Health Questionnaire-9. Latent profile analysis identified four dyadic coping profiles: Low Interaction and Mutual Disengagement (31.6%), Wife-Active and Husband-Supported (6.7%), Husband-Active and Wife-Passive (38.4%), and High Interaction and Mutual Engagement (23.3%). Mediation analyses showed that more adaptive illness perceptions were associated with lower anxiety and depression. Indirect associations via illness perception were observed for wives across adaptive profiles and for husbands in specific profiles, with cross-partner effects present only for anxiety. The findings indicate that dyadic coping among infertile couples is heterogeneous and differentially associated with emotional adjustment in wives and husbands. Longitudinal studies are needed to confirm these associations.Keywords: Infertile couples, Dyadic coping, Illness perception, Anxiety, Depression, Latent profile analysisSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Infertility is defined as the failure to achieve pregnancy after 12 months or more of regular unprotected sexual intercourse1. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) 2023 report, the global lifetime prevalence of infertility (the proportion of people who have ever experienced infertility in their lifetime) is estimated to be 17.5%, and the 12-month period prevalence is estimated to be 12.6%, indicating that infertility affects approximately one in six adults worldwide. Regionally, the highest lifetime prevalence is observed in the Western Pacific Region (23.2%), while the highest period prevalence is found in the African Region (16.4%)2. In China, a large population-based study reported a 12-month infertility prevalence of 15.5% among couples of childbearing age3. According to the Lancet Commission on women‘s and children’s health in China, this prevalence increased from approximately 12.0% in 2007 to 15.5% in 2010, and further rose to about 18.0% by 20204. The rising trend, exacerbated by delayed marriage and childbearing, indicates a continuously increasing number of affected individuals each year in China2,5. Beyond its physiological impact, infertility imposes serious psychological and relational distress on couples.

Infertile couples experience greater psychological stress and higher rates of anxiety and depression compared with fertile couples. Studies have reported that the prevalence of depression ranges from 21.0% to 52.2% in infertile women and from 14.0% to 23.6% in men6,7. Approximately 45.0% of infertile couples report moderate to severe psychological distress, with wives typically exhibiting higher levels than husbands8. Financial burdens, repeated medical procedures, and treatment failures further intensify emotional strain. Consequently, infertility functions as a dyadic stressor, affecting both partners simultaneously and influencing not only individual emotions but also relationship quality and treatment outcomes9,10.

Over time, research on coping with infertility has shifted from an individual focus to a couple-oriented perspective, introducing the concept of dyadic coping. Dyadic coping refers to the joint efforts of partners to manage stress and maintain emotional balance in the face of adversity11. It can take both positive and negative forms12, with differing coping styles exerting distinct effects upon psychological well-being. Positive dyadic coping enhances mutual support, communication, and interdependence between partners, thereby improving relationship quality, psychological well-being, and treatment outcomes13,14. Empirical studies further demonstrate a negative correlation between supportive dyadic coping and anxiety and depression, indicating that higher levels of such coping are associated with significantly reduced psychological distress15. Among infertile couples, increased engagement in positive dyadic coping is linked to lower levels of anxiety and depression in both partners, whereas negative dyadic coping is correlated with elevated emotional distress in dyadic contexts16. These findings highlight the pivotal role of dyadic coping in moderating emotional health, particularly in mitigating and preventing anxiety and depression in couples experiencing infertility.

Anxiety and depression are key psychological outcomes associated with infertility and play a bidirectional role in couples’ adjustment. They not only reflect emotional strain but also influence physiological processes related to fertility. One study indicated that marital adjustment capacity within couples can predict levels of anxiety and depression in both partners, while such negative emotions may further impede fertility through physiological mechanisms17. For instance, anxiety and depression have been shown to suppress the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis, leading to amenorrhea or anovulation18. Emotional stress may also trigger fallopian tube spasms or vaginismus, thereby reducing pregnancy rates19. In males, anxiety and depression may reduce semen quality. Higher levels of these symptoms are linked to lower sperm concentration, poorer motility, and decreased total sperm count20,21. They are also associated with sexual dysfunction22,23. Furthermore, high levels of psychological distress have been consistently linked to an increased risk of infertility24,25. These findings highlight the importance of understanding how couples cope with infertility and how their cognitive and emotional processes interact to shape mental health outcomes.

Most existing studies have adopted variable-centered approaches (e.g., correlation and regression analyses) to examine the relationship between dyadic coping and emotional distress13,14,26,27. Although these approaches have shown that positive dyadic coping is associated with lower anxiety and depression, they assume that the study sample is drawn from a homogeneous population and focus on average effects across all couples. However, this assumption overlooks the potential heterogeneity in how couples organize and enact their coping efforts. In reality, couples may differ qualitatively in their patterns of stress communication, support provision, and joint problem-solving27. For example, some couples may display high mutual engagement across all positive coping dimensions, whereas others may show an asymmetric pattern where one partner is active and the other passive or disengaged. Such qualitative differences cannot be captured by variable-centered methods, thereby limiting the precision of conclusions and the development of targeted interventions.

Furthermore, the mechanisms through which dyadic coping influences anxiety and depression remain underexplored. The cognitive-behavioral model posits that cognitive appraisals play a crucial role in linking external stressors to emotional responses28; however, few studies have directly examined the mediating role of illness perception in the context of infertility. Illness perception refers to an individual’s cognitive and emotional appraisal of the health threat posed by infertility, encompassing beliefs about consequences, timeline, personal control, treatment control, and emotional responses such as worry and distress29. Within the cognitive-behavioral framework, negative or catastrophic illness perceptions (e.g., viewing infertility as an uncontrollable and life-ruining condition) are hypothesized to trigger maladaptive coping behaviors and, in turn, lead to higher levels of anxiety and depression27. Conversely, more adaptive illness perceptions (e.g., seeing infertility as a manageable challenge) are thought to facilitate constructive dyadic coping and better emotional outcomes30. Despite this theoretical rationale, whether illness perception mediates the relationship between dyadic coping patterns and anxiety/depression has not been systematically examined among infertile couples.

Various couple-based psychosocial interventions have been developed to improve coping and emotional well-being, including couple-based cognitive behavioral therapy, mindfulness-based therapy, emotionally focused couple therapy, behavioral couple therapy, and psychoeducational programs31,32. Theoretical and empirical evidence suggests that components of cognitive representations of the disease and its treatment (i.e., illness perception) may be associated with individual psychological adjustment33–35. Based on this, emotion regulation interventions focusing on cognitive reappraisal have been shown to effectively improve mental health36,37. However, most existing interventions are designed for all couples regardless of their coping styles, and their effects may vary across different subtypes. Consequently, cognitive factors such as illness perception are often overlooked in their critical role in the psychological outcomes of infertile couples.

To address the above research gaps, this study introduces two major innovations. First, latent profile analysis (LPA) is applied to classify infertile couples. LPA is a person-centered method that does not assume population homogeneity and identifies latent subgroups based on response patterns across multiple indicators38. Using the dimensions of stressful communication, supportive coping, delegated coping, common coping, and negative coping from the Dyadic Coping Inventory as indicators, this study employs LPA to reveal naturally occurring heterogeneous coping profiles among couples. On this basis, illness perception is incorporated as a potential mediator within the analytical framework to explore its associative pathways between the identified dyadic coping profiles and anxiety and depression. This integrated framework has not been previously reported in the literature.

Based on the above innovations and the cross-sectional study design, the following hypotheses are proposed: (1) dyadic coping among infertile couples exhibits identifiable heterogeneous profiles; (2) different dyadic coping profiles are differentially associated with levels of anxiety and depression, and these associations are mediational in nature with illness perception; and (3) the patterns of these associations differ between wives and husbands. Latent profile analysis and relative mediation analysis will be used to test these hypotheses, thereby providing a descriptive basis for profile-sensitive psychosocial support.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study employed a cross-sectional design to conduct a descriptive correlational investigation. All procedures were performed in accordance with STROBE guidelines and relevant regulations.

From December 2024 to March 2025, women receiving infertility treatment and their spouses were recruited using a convenience sampling method from the reproductive medicine clinics of two tertiary hospitals in Hengyang City, China. All participants were heterosexual married couples. In this study, “patient” refers to the wife receiving infertility-related clinical care, whereas “spouse” refers to her husband. Although infertility etiologies included female factors, male factors, and unexplained infertility, the distinction between “patient” and “spouse” was based on participants’ clinical and dyadic roles rather than the attribution of infertility etiology. Potential participants were approached by research assistants in the clinic waiting areas, who briefly explained the purpose of the study and assessed eligibility using a short screening checklist based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria described below. The inclusion criteria for patients were as follows: (1) Patients with a clear diagnosis of infertility; (2) Patients undergoing infertility treatment; (3) Individuals with consciousness, basic language expression, and comprehension; (4) Those who provided informed consent and voluntarily participated in the study. For spouses: (1) Legal spouse of the patient; (2) Individuals with consciousness, basic language expression, and comprehension; (3) Those who provided informed consent and voluntarily participated in the study. The exclusion criteria for both patients and their spouses were as follows: (1) Individuals with a history of psychological disorders, psychiatric illnesses, serious complications, or other severe illnesses; (2) Individuals with significant cognitive impairment, confusion, or speech difficulties that hinder cooperation; (3) Those who have suffered other major life events (for example, bereavement, separations from the workforce, and so forth) or traumatic events (for example, natural disasters and man-made disasters).

Sample size

Sample size calculation was performed using a multi-pronged approach. A post hoc power analysis was conducted with G*Power (v. 3.1) software for multiple linear regression. Assuming a medium effect size (f2 = 0.15), 18 predictors, and a significance level of α = 0.05, with an effective sample size of N = 251, the achieved power (1 − β) was 0.982, indicating that the sample was sufficient to detect the hypothesized medium effects. For the Actor-Partner Interdependence Model (APIM), an a-priori power analysis using APIMPowerR software was performed. Based on the effect size reported by Richard et al. (mean = 0.21)39 and referring to parameter settings in previous dyadic studies40 (e.g., Li et al.), the actor effect was set at 0.25 (small-to-medium) and the partner effect was set at 0.20. The calculation showed that at least 185 couples were required to achieve 80% statistical power (β = 0.20) to detect both actor and partner effects. Furthermore, according to Fritz and others41, the minimum sample size for structural equation modeling (SEM) should be no less than 200 participants. Considering statistical power, model stability, and survey feasibility, the target sample size was set at 250–300 couples.

Data collection

Eligible couples who agreed to participate completed the questionnaires independently in a private area of the clinic, without discussing their responses with each other. Each member self-administered the paper-and-pencil questionnaire; for participants with lower literacy, trained research assistants read the questions face-to-face and recorded the answers. The research assistants checked the questionnaires on site for completeness. Of the 300 couples who initially consented, 49 were excluded during or after data collection for the following reasons: incomplete questionnaires from one or both partners (n = 36), failure to complete the questionnaires separately (n = 5), withdrawal of consent (n = 4), and meeting exclusion criteria after further review (n = 4). The final analytical sample consisted of 251 couples, yielding an effective response rate of 83.67%. There were no missing data in the final analytical sample, as incomplete or logically inconsistent responses had been excluded.

The study received ethical approval from the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of the University of South China (HLSC075) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Measurements

A general information questionnaire and four self-reported questionnaires were administered.

General information questionnaire

Self-reported demographic characteristics included the patients’ and spouses’ ages, residence, occupation, personal monthly income, education level, duration of marriage, duration of treatment, number of children, and cause of infertility.

Dyadic coping inventory (DCI)

The Chinese version of the Dyadic Coping Inventory (DCI), originally developed by Bodenmann42 in 2008, and validated among Chinese couples by Xu et al.43, is a reliable and effective tool for assessing stressful communication and dyadic coping behaviors. The scale comprises 37-item covering six dimensions: stressful communication, supportive coping, negative coping, delegated coping, common coping, and quality appraisal. According to standard scoring procedures, items in the negative coping dimension were reverse-coded, and the quality appraisal subscale was excluded from the total score and thus not used in subsequent analyses. The total score thus ranges from 35 to 175, with higher scores indicating better dyadic coping ability. The DCI has been extensively used in couples coping with chronic illnesses and demonstrates sound reliability and validity44–46. In the present study, Cronbach’s α coefficients for patients were 0.959 for the total scale and 0.911, 0.892, 0.695, 0.903, and 0.919 for the five dimensions, respectively; for spouses, the corresponding coefficients were 0.942 for the total scale and 0.841, 0.841, 0.620, 0.885, and 0.805 for the dimensions.

Brief illness perception questionnaire (BIPQ)

BIPQ was developed by Broadbent in 200647, the Chinese version was tested for reliability and validity by Mei48. The BIPQ consists of 9-item. Items 1 to 8 are rated on an 11-point scale ranging from 0 (“no perception”) to 10 (“extremely strong perception”), measuring cognitive illness representations (five items: consequences, timeline, personal control, treatment control, and identity), emotional representations (two items: concern and emotions), and illness comprehensibility (one item). Item 9 is an open-ended question asking participants to list the three most important causes of their condition. Items 3 (personal control), 4 (treatment control), and 7 (illness comprehensibility) were reverse-coded when calculating the total score. The total score is the sum of the first eight items (range: 0–80), with higher scores indicating a more threatening or negative illness perception. The open-ended item is not included in the total score. In the present study, Cronbach’s α coefficients for patients and spouses were 0.739 and 0.707, respectively.

Generalized anxiety disorder (GAD-7)

The Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-Item Scale (GAD-7), developed by Spitzer et al.49 in 2006, consists of seven items assessing anxiety symptoms over the past two weeks. Responses are rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = not at all, 3 = nearly every day), with total scores ranging from 0 to 21. Higher scores indicate more severe anxiety. The GAD-7 has been widely validated in infertile populations in China as well as in international contexts50,51. Its suitability for self-report makes it particularly practical in clinical and research settings. In the present study, Cronbach’s α coefficients for patients and spouses were 0.838 and 0.687, respectively.

Patient health questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9)

The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) is a self-administered scale used to assess depressive symptoms based on 9-item52. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 27, with higher scores indicating greater severity of depression. The PHQ-9 has been extensively validated among individuals with infertility in both China and other countries53. In the present study, Cronbach’s α coefficients for patients and spouses were 0.826 and 0.683, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Latent profile analysis (LPA) was performed using Mplus 8.3 to identify distinct dyadic coping profiles among infertile couples. Raw subscale scores of the five DCI dimensions (stressful communication, supportive coping, delegated coping, common coping, and negative coping) were used as profile indicators. Because all DCI dimensions were derived from the same instrument and had comparable scoring structures, raw subscale scores rather than standardized scores were used in the latent profile analysis. Model fit was evaluated using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), adjusted BIC (aBIC), entropy, the Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMR-LRT), and the bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT)54. Lower AIC, BIC, and aBIC values indicated better model fit; entropy values closer to 1 indicated higher classification accuracy55. A significant p-value (p ≤ 0.05) for the LMR-LRT and BLRT suggested that a k-profile model was superior to a (k − 1)-profile model56. The final model was selected by considering fit indices, parsimony, and a minimum class proportion of at least 5%57.

After determining the optimal LPA model, subsequent analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0 and AMOS 28.0. Descriptive statistics were calculated for demographic and clinical variables. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess normality of continuous variables. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and non-normally distributed data as median (interquartile range). Levene’s test was used to evaluate homogeneity of variance. Because incomplete questionnaires had been excluded from the final analytical sample, no missing data required further handling. Group comparisons were performed using the identified latent profiles as the grouping variable. Categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square tests. For continuous variables, normally distributed variables were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with partial η² reported as the effect size; non-normally distributed variables were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis H test. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the LSD procedure to maintain statistical power given the relatively unequal subgroup sizes, particularly for the small “Wife-Active and Husband-Supported” class. In addition, Bonferroni-adjusted sensitivity analyses were conducted, and the overall patterns of results remained generally consistent.

Common method bias was assessed using Harman’s single-factor test. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to examine bivariate relationships among the study variables. To test the mediating role of illness perception, structural equation modeling was performed within the framework of the Actor-Partner Interdependence Mediation Model (APIMeM). The model was saturated (degrees of freedom = 0), with all possible paths being estimated; therefore, model fit indices are not applicable. The four latent profiles were dummy-coded with the “Low Interaction and Mutual Disengagement” group as the reference. In the subsequent APIMeM analyses, each couple was assigned to the latent class for which they had the highest posterior probability (i.e., most likely class membership), which was then treated as an observed grouping variable. We acknowledge that this approach does not fully propagate classification uncertainty. According to previous literature58, age, monthly household income, education level, and duration of infertility treatment are associated with anxiety and depression; therefore, these variables were included as covariates in the regression models. Bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals (95%, 5,000 resamples) were calculated for indirect effects, and unstandardized indirect effects were reported. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Common method bias test

Harman’s single-factor test was employed to evaluate the likelihood of a common method bias. It was found that the first unrotated factor accounted for 20.71% of the total variance, which is below the critical threshold of 40%59, suggesting no significant common method bias in the present study.

Participant characteristics

The participants’ demographic details are reported in (Table 1). Patients were 31.27 ± 4.38 years old on average, while their spouses were 32.93 ± 4.69 years old on average. The majority of patients and their spouses were childless (76.9%, n = 193), were employed (64.9%, n = 163; 80.5%, n = 202), and had a college degree or above (62.2%, n = 156; 67.3%, n = 169). Nearly one-third of infertile couples had an average monthly household income below 5,000 ¥ (34.7%, n = 87), and the majority lived in urban regions (75.3%, n = 189). About half of the couples had experienced infertility for less than three years (50.2%, n = 126), and 41.0% had been married for under three years (n = 103). Half of the causes of infertility (51.8%, n = 130) were female factors.

LPA

The results of the latent profile analysis (LPA) for dyadic coping among infertile couples are presented in (Table 2) and (Fig. 1). As the number of profiles increased, the information criteria (AIC, BIC, and aBIC) progressively decreased; however, the magnitude of improvement diminished substantially after the four-profile solution. The four-profile model demonstrated the highest entropy value (0.93), indicating superior classification accuracy. In addition, the Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMR-LRT) indicated that the four-profile model provided a significantly better fit than the three-profile model (p < 0.001), whereas the five-profile model did not significantly improve model fit compared with the four-profile model (p = 0.229). Although the five- and six-profile solutions yielded slightly lower information-criterion values, both models contained relatively small profiles (approximately 6% of the sample), suggesting limited stability and reduced potential for replication. Furthermore, the additional profiles identified in the five- and six-profile solutions did not demonstrate conceptually distinct coping patterns beyond those observed in the four-profile solution. Therefore, considering model fit, classification accuracy, parsimony, profile size, and substantive interpretability, the four-profile solution was retained as the optimal model.

Based on the pattern of scores across the dyadic coping dimensions of the DCI (stressful communication, supportive coping, negative coping, delegated coping, and common coping), we identified four distinct types using latent profile analysis: Low Interaction and Mutual Disengagement (Class 1, 31.6%, n = 80), characterized by the lowest scores on all positive coping dimensions and relatively high levels of negative coping; Wife-Active and Husband-Supported (Class 2, 6.7%, n = 17), characterized by high wife engagement and predominantly supportive coping from husbands despite low stress communication; Husband-Active and Wife-Passive (Class 3, 38.4%, n = 95), characterized by active husband involvement and relatively low wife engagement; and High Interaction and Mutual Engagement (Class 4, 23.3%, n = 59), characterized by the highest scores on all positive coping dimensions. Although Class 2 had a modest sample size (n = 17), it demonstrated good classification stability. As shown in the boxplot of maximum posterior probabilities (Fig. 2), Class 2 achieved a median maximum posterior probability of 1.000 and an interquartile range of 0.01, indicating near-perfect classification certainty. Its average posterior probability (AvePP) was 0.982, well above the recommended threshold of 0.80. Thus, although the sample size of Class 2 is modest, the high classification certainty provides support for the stability of subsequent mediation analyses involving this class.

Associations between dyadic coping profiles and emotional distress

Table 3 presents a comparison of subscale scores across the four classes, revealing significant differences in dyadic coping dimensions among the identified subgroups. As shown in (Table 4), a one-way ANOVA was conducted to examine differences in anxiety and depression scores across the four dyadic coping profiles, with the dyadic coping subgroup as the independent variable and total anxiety/depression scores as the dependent variables. Significant group differences were observed among patients for both anxiety (F = 27.999, p < 0.001) and depression (F = 18.982, p < 0.001).

Post hoc comparisons using the LSD procedure indicated that patients in the Low Interaction and Mutual Disengagement profile reported significantly higher anxiety and depression scores than those in the other three profiles. In addition, patients in the Husband-Active and Wife-Passive profile showed higher anxiety and depression scores than those in the High Interaction and Mutual Engagement profile. Bonferroni-adjusted sensitivity analyses demonstrated generally consistent patterns of findings, although several subgroup comparisons were attenuated after conservative correction. No significant differences were observed between patients in the Wife-Active and Husband-Supported profile and either the Husband-Active and Wife-Passive or High Interaction and Mutual Engagement profiles.

Among spouses, anxiety scores differed significantly across dyadic coping profiles (F = 35.057, p < 0.001). Post hoc comparisons showed that anxiety was highest in the Low Interaction and Mutual Disengagement group, significantly exceeding all other groups. Spouses in the Wife-Active and Husband-Supported group also exhibited higher anxiety than both the Husband-Active and Wife-Passive and High Interaction and Mutual Engagement groups. Bonferroni-adjusted analyses yielded comparable overall patterns, and the difference between spouses in the Wife-Active and Husband-Supported and High Interaction and Mutual Engagement profiles remained statistically significant after correction (adjusted p = 0.015).

For depression, scores also varied significantly among spouses (F = 12.194, p < 0.001). According to the LSD comparisons, spouses in both the Low Interaction and Mutual Disengagement and Wife-Active and Husband-Supported groups reported higher depression levels than those in the other two groups. Bonferroni-adjusted analyses showed generally similar trends, with the primary differences between the Low Interaction and Mutual Disengagement profile and the more adaptive coping profiles remaining statistically significant. For the anxiety model, actor effects showed that both wives’ and husbands’ illness perceptions were positively associated with their own anxiety levels. Regarding partner effects, wives’ illness perception was positively associated with husbands’ anxiety, whereas the corresponding path from husbands’ illness perception to wives’ anxiety was not statistically supported. Concerning indirect associations, wives’ illness perception was associated with the relationship between dyadic coping profiles and wives’ anxiety across all three adaptive profiles (Class 2: B = − 1.011, 95% CI [− 1.869 to − 0.462]; Class 3: B = − 0.925, 95% CI [− 1.517 to − 0.494]; Class 4: B = − 1.224, 95% CI [− 2.091 to − 0.629]). For husbands, illness perception-related indirect associations with their own anxiety were observed in the Husband-Active and Wife-Passive profile (Class 3: B = − 0.942, 95% CI [− 1.403 to − 0.591]) and the High Interaction and Mutual Engagement profile (Class 4: B = − 0.895, 95% CI [− 1.375 to − 0.537]), but not in the Wife-Active and Husband-Supported profile (Class 2). In addition, indirect partner associations from wives’ illness perception to husbands’ anxiety were observed across all three adaptive profiles (Class 2: B = − 0.367, 95% CI [− 0.779 to − 0.107]; Class 3: B = − 0.336, 95% CI [− 0.637 to − 0.091]; Class 4: B = − 0.444, 95% CI [− 0.861 to − 0.133]). No significant indirect partner associations from husbands’ illness perception to wives’ anxiety were identified.

For the depression model, actor effects similarly showed that both wives’ and husbands’ illness perceptions were positively associated with their own depression levels. Partner effects were not observed. Regarding indirect associations, wives’ illness perception was associated with the relationship between dyadic coping profiles and wives’ depression across all three adaptive profiles (Class 2: B = − 1.062, 95% CI [− 1.994 to − 0.493]; Class 3: B = − 0.972, 95% CI [− 1.595 to − 0.517]; Class 4: B = − 1.285, 95% CI [− 2.196 to − 0.632]). For husbands, illness perception-related indirect associations with their own depression were observed in Class 3 (B = − 0.789, 95% CI [− 1.267 to − 0.415]) and Class 4 (B = − 0.749, 95% CI [− 1.237 to − 0.380]), whereas no such association was observed in Class 2. Unlike the anxiety model, no indirect partner associations were identified in the depression model, as all confidence intervals for cross-partner pathways included zero.

Overall, the APIMeM findings suggest that illness perception was primarily associated with emotional distress through actor pathways. Wives’ illness perception demonstrated relatively consistent indirect associations across multiple dyadic coping profiles for both anxiety and depression, whereas husbands’ illness perception showed a more profile-specific pattern. Cross-partner indirect associations were observed only in the anxiety model, where wives’ illness perception was associated with husbands’ anxiety, while no comparable associations were identified for depression.

Discussion

Heterogeneity in dyadic coping patterns

This study identified four distinct dyadic coping patterns among infertile couples: the Low Interaction and Mutual Disengagement group, the Wife-Active and Husband-Supported group, the Husband-Active and Wife-Passive group, and the High Interaction and Mutual Engagement group. Significant differences in dyadic coping scores across these profiles showed pronounced heterogeneity in how patients (wives) and spouses (husbands) were positioned within infertility-related coping processes. The Husband-Active and Wife-Passive group accounted for the largest proportion and was characterized by relatively higher engagement in positive dyadic coping among husbands and comparatively lower engagement among wives. The Low Interaction and Mutual Disengagement group accounted for the second largest proportion, with both wives and husbands showing relatively low levels of communication, cooperation, and coordinated coping. In the High Interaction and Mutual Engagement group, both wives and husbands showed high levels of positive dyadic coping, reflecting a reciprocal and collaborative coping configuration. The Wife-Active and Husband-Supported group accounted for the smallest proportion and was characterized by greater initiative among wives and primarily supportive responses from husbands.

These findings are broadly consistent with previous studies on heterogeneity in couple coping patterns16,27. They suggest that dyadic coping among infertile couples should not be understood solely in terms of overall coping levels, but also in relation to the distribution of roles and reciprocity between partners. In the context of infertility, medical evaluation and treatment are often more directly connected to wives’ bodily experiences and treatment trajectories60, which may contribute to asymmetries in coping involvement within couples. A person-centered approach provides a more nuanced description of how couples organize their responses to infertility-related stress.

Dyadic coping profiles and emotional distress: role-differentiated associations

Different dyadic coping patterns showed role-differentiated associations with anxiety and depression among wives and husbands. This finding is broadly consistent with previous studies reporting gender-differentiated emotional associations within couple relationships27,61,62, although the present study did not directly assess the sociocultural or psychological mechanisms underlying these differences. Specifically, compared with the Low Interaction and Mutual Disengagement profile, the Wife-Active and Husband-Supported, Husband-Active and Wife-Passive, and High Interaction and Mutual Engagement profiles were consistently associated with lower levels of anxiety and depression among wives.

By contrast, the pattern among husbands was less consistent. The Husband-Active and Wife-Passive group and the High Interaction and Mutual Engagement group showed more stable associations with lower anxiety and depression among husbands, whereas the associations between the Wife-Active and Husband-Supported profile and husbands’ emotional distress, particularly depression, were comparatively weaker. These findings may suggest that the associations between dyadic coping patterns and emotional distress differ across husbands, although this interpretation remains tentative and requires further replication.

Consistent with previous research63, the High Interaction and Mutual Engagement group showed the most consistently lower levels of emotional distress in both wives and husbands. This pattern is also broadly in line with dyadic coping theory emphasizing reciprocity, mutual engagement, and collaborative coping processes64. However, these findings should be interpreted as cross-sectional associations rather than evidence of causal relationships. Therefore, the present results should not be interpreted as indicating that specific dyadic coping patterns directly lead to particular emotional outcomes.

Illness perception as a dyadic correlate of emotional distress

The APIMeM analyses further showed that illness perception-related indirect associations were present between dyadic coping profiles and emotional distress, with distinct patterns observed between wives and husbands.

Among wives, illness perception demonstrated significant actor indirect associations across multiple dyadic coping profiles, including the Wife-Active and Husband-Supported, Husband-Active and Wife-Passive, and High Interaction and Mutual Engagement profiles. These findings suggest that more adaptive dyadic interaction patterns were associated with lower perceived illness threat and lower emotional distress33,60. Within the context of infertility, medical evaluation and treatment procedures are often more directly connected to wives’ bodily experiences and treatment processes. This clinical context may help explain some of the differential association patterns observed in the present study, although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear and require further investigation33. In the present study, illness perception-related indirect associations involving wives were relatively consistent across several dyadic coping profiles, suggesting that wives’ cognitive appraisal of infertility may be more stably associated with their own anxiety and depression.

Among husbands, illness perception-related indirect associations appeared to be more context-specific. More evident indirect associations between illness perception and emotional distress were observed in the Husband-Active and Wife-Passive profile and the High Interaction and Mutual Engagement profile, whereas relatively weaker associations were observed in the Wife-Active and Husband-Supported profile, particularly in the depression model where statistical significance was not reached. This observed pattern shows descriptive consistency with previous studies examining associations among dyadic coping, illness perception, and emotional distress65,66. However, because the present study did not directly assess gender-role beliefs, perceived agency, role strain, masculine norms, infertility-related stigma, or related sociocultural and psychological variables, the specific mechanisms underlying these differential association patterns cannot be determined. Future studies incorporating direct assessments of these variables may help further clarify the potential sources of these gender-differentiated associations67,68.

In addition, different partner indirect association patterns were observed between the anxiety and depression models. In the anxiety model, a significant partner indirect association from wives’ illness perception to husbands’ anxiety was identified, suggesting the possible presence of cross-partner cognitive-emotional associations within the anxiety model. In contrast, no comparable partner indirect associations were observed in the depression model, where associations appeared to remain primarily at the individual level. These findings may indicate that anxiety and depression differ in their dyadic association patterns within couples. However, given the cross-sectional nature of the present study, these findings should be considered exploratory and require longitudinal investigation to further clarify the temporal dynamics of these dyadic processes.

From a practical perspective, the observed indirect association patterns may provide preliminary directions for future profile-sensitive psychosocial support research among infertile couples. For example, future studies may further explore the potential utility of digital and mobile health technologies for supporting cognitive restructuring targeting maladaptive illness perceptions69. App-based support modules may also be explored as potential approaches for developing more tailored psychosocial support according to couples’ dyadic interaction characteristics, particularly for couples with limited access to face-to-face psychosocial services. Nevertheless, the present findings should not be interpreted as evidence for clinical intervention effectiveness. The four dyadic coping profiles identified in this study have not been validated as clinical classifications, and no intervention was tested in the present research. Further replication and validation in larger, multicenter, and longitudinal samples are required before these findings can inform profile-sensitive screening approaches or couple-based psychosocial interventions.

Practical contributions

The present findings may provide preliminary directions for future psychosocial assessment research and couple-oriented supportive care in infertility. First, the results suggest that future research may benefit from considering not only the overall level of dyadic coping, but also the distribution and reciprocity of coping roles between wives and husbands. In particular, the Low Interaction and Mutual Disengagement profile was associated with relatively higher emotional distress and lower coordinated coping, suggesting that limited mutual engagement may warrant further attention in future psychosocial assessment studies.

Second, the findings highlight the potential relevance of mutual engagement within couples. The High Interaction and Mutual Engagement profile was consistently associated with lower anxiety and depression levels in both wives and husbands. Although the present study does not establish causal relationships, this pattern may support further investigation into whether reciprocal involvement in infertility-related communication, emotional support, and shared coping processes is associated with more adaptive emotional adjustment among couples.

Third, illness perception may represent a potentially relevant domain for future psychosocial screening research. Wives’ illness perception showed relatively stable associations with their own anxiety and depression, whereas husbands’ illness perception appeared to demonstrate more context-specific associations depending on dyadic coping configurations. Future studies may therefore further explore how couples’ cognitive appraisals of infertility, including perceived threat, controllability, and emotional meaning, relate to emotional distress within dyadic contexts.

In addition, the observed profile-specific association patterns may offer preliminary implications for future couple-oriented psychosocial assessment and supportive care research in infertility. The findings suggest that future studies may further examine whether profile-sensitive approaches are relevant to couples’ emotional experiences, dyadic interaction characteristics, and illness perception patterns. However, these practical implications should still be considered exploratory at this stage. Further validation in larger, multicenter, and longitudinal studies is needed before their potential clinical application can be considered.

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Although data were analyzed within an APIMeM framework, simultaneous collection prevents determining temporal precedence. Therefore, the mediation analyses should be interpreted as statistical indirect associations rather than evidence of causal or temporal pathways. Longitudinal designs (e.g., multi-wave or diary studies) are needed to establish directionality. Second, the treatment of latent class membership as an observed variable in the mediation analyses does not fully account for classification uncertainty. Although the four-class solution demonstrated high entropy (0.93) and the smallest class (Class 2) showed excellent average posterior probability (0.982), we did not propagate classification error into the APIMeM models. This may lead to slightly underestimated standard errors. Future studies with larger samples, particularly for the Wife-Active and Husband-Supported profile, should apply a three-step or BCH correction method to formally incorporate classification uncertainty. Accordingly, the APIMeM findings should be interpreted with caution, particularly for the Wife-Active and Husband-Supported profile, which contained the smallest number of couples. Third, regarding the latent profile analysis itself, raw DCI subscale scores were used as profile indicators. Although all indicators were derived from the same instrument and had comparable scoring structures, differences in score ranges or variances across DCI dimensions may have influenced the relative contribution of individual indicators to profile formation. Therefore, the identified profiles should be interpreted with some caution. Future studies are encouraged to examine the stability, robustness, and replicability of these profiles using standardized indicators or alternative scaling approaches. Fourth, common method bias may affect results. All variables were self-reported from two tertiary hospitals. The Harman single-factor test (first unrotated factor explained 20.71% of variance, < 40%) alone cannot rule out method bias. Future studies should incorporate multi-source data (e.g., clinical interviews, observational measures) or marker variables, and collect multi-center data to enhance objectivity. Fifth, gender-role beliefs were not measured. The two asymmetric patterns (“wife-active, husband-supportive” and “husband-active, wife-passive”) may reflect unmeasured gender norms. Future research should directly assess gender-role beliefs (e.g., masculinity/femininity scales). Sixth, the reliability of the GAD-7 and PHQ-9 for spouses was slightly lower (Cronbach’s α = 0.687 and 0.683, respectively) than the conventional threshold of 0.70, which may have attenuated the observed associations for husbands; nonetheless, both scales have been well validated in infertile populations and remain suitable for exploratory analyses. Finally, we examined only illness perception as a mediator. Other psychological or relational mediators should be explored to build a more comprehensive framework.

Conclusion

This study found that illness perception was differentially associated with dyadic coping patterns and emotional distress in infertile couples, with gender-differentiated patterns. Patients’ (wives’) emotional adjustment was consistently associated with their own illness perception, whereas for spouses (husbands) such associations depended on specific coping role configurations. These findings suggest that cognitive appraisals and role-specific coping positions are associated with emotional outcomes, but the underlying sociocultural and psychological mechanisms remain to be investigated. Using a person-centered, partner-specific perspective, this study describes heterogeneity in couples’ psychological adjustment. Although causal inference is limited, the results may help generate hypotheses for future pattern-sensitive psychosocial support and longitudinal research. These findings should be considered exploratory and await replication in future studies.
