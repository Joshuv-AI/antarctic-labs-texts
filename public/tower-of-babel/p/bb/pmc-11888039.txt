Incidence and long-term patient reported outcome measures of patients with radiological cauda equina compression but without clinical features of cauda equina syndrome
Authors: Hamzah Alsheikh Soleiman, Mary Solou, Andreas K. Demetriades
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Cauda equina syndrome (CES) is a rare spinal emergency. Paradoxically, in the British NHS, suspected CES requiring an emergency magnetic resonance imaging constitutes one of the commonest reasons for acute referrals to neurosurgery/spine surgery. Further acute referrals also occur with the reverse scenario, when radiological evidence of cauda equina compression is not accompanied by the clinical red flags of the syndrome. The understanding of CES, particularly in cases with radiological compression but lacking clinical symptoms, remains limited. This study aims to explore the incidence and the long-term outcomes of such patients, addressing a significant knowledge gap.Methods:A single-center, retrospective and cohort study was conducted, analyzing suspected CES referrals managed between August 2013 and November 2014. Patients with radiological cauda equina compression but without clinical CES features were identified. A structured questionnaire assessed long-term patient reported outcomes.Results:Out of 344 suspected CES referrals, 38 patients (11%) had radiological CES of degenerative cause without clinical symptoms. From those with radiological cauda equina compression but no features of CES, 14 were assessed for long-term results and the majority still underwent surgery (64%), either emergent (35.7%) or elective (28.6%). The long-term patient reported outcomes of the operated patients revealed that 66.7% were fully ambulant, 77.8% maintained an intact sexual function, and 88.9% were free of urinary symptoms. However, no matter the management, chronic pain persisted in 85.7%.Conclusion:This study highlights the existence of patients who are referred urgently with radiological cauda equina compression but who lack clinical CES symptoms. Despite surgical interventions, chronic pain remains a significant issue. Further research, including multi-centered studies, is warranted to enhance our understanding of this condition and its nuanced subpopulations.

INTRODUCTION

In adults, the spinal cord concludes around T12-L2, transitioning to the cauda equina, critical for lower body functions.[123] Susceptible to injury possibly due to hypovascularity, cauda equina compression most commonly by disc herniation can lead to cauda equina syndrome (CES), characterized by bowel, bladder, sexual dysfunction, with motor, sensory, and autonomic changes.[14567] It is a rare syndrome and a spinal emergency, with a challenging clinicoradiological diagnosis and varied clinical presentations.[89101112] Recent evidence synthesis showed that the incidence of CES is approximately 1:100,000 in asymptomatic patients per year, only 0.27% in patients with low-back pain and only 18.9% in patients with signs and symptoms consistent with CES.[89101112] Despite its rarity, paradoxically in the British NHS, suspected CES requiring an emergency magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) often constitutes the commonest reason for acute referrals to spine surgery. CES can lead to lifelong debilitation and socioeconomic strain, with litigation affecting around 10% of cases.[811] Timely diagnosis is crucial, yet challenges arise due to variations in clinical and radiological correlation, complicating treatment decisions.[13141516171819]

While a negative MRI can lead to dismissing emergency CES referrals, the reverse with radiological evidence of cauda equina compression but without clinical symptoms can also occur. Little information surrounds this subtype of potential CES, and anxiety over the possible lifelong debilitation can lead to defensive medicine with unnecessary and potentially unnecessarily hurried and dangerous operations by concerned physicians.

Aim of the study

This study aimed to address the aforementioned knowledge gap: To explore the entity of radiological cauda equina compression without indicative clinical features of CES and the long-term outcomes of such patients under different management strategies, as treated pragmatically.

METHODS

A single-center, retrospective cohort study was undertaken to explore the incidence and the long-term outcomes of patients with confirmed radiological cauda equina compression but without any clinical symptoms of CES. For the purposes of this analysis and after Caldicott approval, the electronic database of the regional neurosurgical center of the Western General Hospital, Edinburgh, was searched to identify suspected CES referrals managed between August 2013 and November 2014, as well as follow-up records until July 2016. We previously reported the clinical features and outcome of scan-negative and scan-positive cases.[17] To estimate the long-term outcome, a questionnaire was created and performed through telephone sessions as well. As a purely retrospective, noninterventional study, formal ethical approval was not required.

Definitions

First, a definition of CES had to be decided upon and the one selected for the purposes of this study was the definition given by Fraser et al.[13] This paper was chosen as it included the most extensive and well-researched literature review regarding the different definitions and presentations of CES. Fraser et al. state that for a diagnosis of CES to be made at least one of the following must be present: (1) Bladder and/or bowel dysfunction, (2) Reduced sensation in the saddle area, (3) sexual dysfunction; with possible neurological deficit in the lower limb (motor/sensory loss, reflex change); and a radiological confirmation.

Second, radiological cauda equina compression was defined as an over 75% occlusion of the spinal canal or an absence of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) visible around the cauda equina, as previously used in work reported by members of our group and others.[182021]

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

To obtain the cohort group patients, the electronic dataset of the regional neurosurgical center of the Western General Hospital, Edinburgh was searched for suspected CES referrals on dates between August 2013 and November 2014 and patients were deemed eligible for inclusion if (1) aged over 18 years old, (2) with no symptoms of CES as described above, (3) but diagnosed with radiological cauda equine – over 75% occlusion of the spinal canal or absence of CSF around the cauda equina. All included patients should be available to answer the questionnaires assessing long-term outcome (4).

Excluded patients included those (1) who met the foretold symptoms of CES definition; (2) without radiological signs of cauda equina compression in their diagnostic imaging studies as previously described; and (3) with cauda equina compression secondary to tumors, trauma, infection or listhesis. The latter excluding criteria were applied due to their small numbers, as these would not allow conclusions from a heterogeneous group. Patients who were not retrieved or were unwilling to answer the follow-up questionnaire were also excluded (4).

Data collection

All patients were clinically assessed, and the electronic documentation was completed in real time by healthcare staff, including junior doctors, consultants, as well as advanced nurse and physiotherapy practitioners. Demographic and clinical data were reviewed to identify information on gender (female or male), age (in years), level of spinal cord compression (T12, L1, L2, L3, L4, and L5), possible cause of compression (disc herniation, soft tissue, or facet hypertrophy), percentage of compression as measured by neuroradiology in mm3 surface area of cross-section across the most stenosed area compared to the nearest healthy canal cross-sectional surface area, management type (surgical or conservative), and the time from referral to treatment.

Further to the data found in the electronic records, patients were called and answered a structured questionnaire to investigate the main symptoms that may develop as a consequence of CES and particularly their long-term clinical status.

The structured questionnaire consisted of five sections, each one relating to specific possible sequelae: (1) current symptoms, outpatient referrals after their last follow-up, and their current employment status; (2) pain effect on daily life as assessed by the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI); (3) bladder function and its impact on patient’s life as assessed by the short form incontinence questionnaire; (4) effect of altered bowel function on everyday life as assessed by the neurogenic bowel dysfunction (NBD) Score; and (5) sexual function as assessed by the Arizona Sexual Experience Scale [Supplementary Material 1].

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the incidence of patients with radiological cauda equina compression but without any clinical symptoms of CES.

Secondary outcomes comprised the long-term outcome of such patients in terms of mobility, bladder and bowel function, sexual function, quality of life, and association of outcome with the treatment choice.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was done using simple biostatistical apps for statistical calculations and comparisons. DATAtab, an Online Statistics Calculator was used for data analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to present the main demographic data. Frequencies were generally reported in percentages (%) and in numbers. Because of the small size of the population, special statistic tools for example to assess heterogeneity were not possible to be utilized to avoid any possible bias. P =0.05 was statistically significant. Microsoft® Excel was used for charts and tables.

RESULTS

Selection process

Of the 344 suspected CES referrals identified in our database between August 2013 and November 2014, 285 were excluded for not meeting this study’s inclusion criteria, specifically the requirement of absence of CES symptoms [Figure 1]. Further ten cases were excluded as they were noncompliant with radiological compression criteria. Finally, 11 patients had cauda equina compression secondary to tumors, trauma, infection, or listhesis and hence were also excluded. This left 38 patients with radiological only cauda equina compression but no clinical features of the syndrome [Figure 2]. Further 24 were excluded due to the inability/unavailability to provide patient reported outcomes. The study focused on 14 patients, aiming to investigate the long-term outcomes of this neurosurgical entity.

Pain and mobility

In the long-term pain outcomes, only two patients confirmed pain freedom; both had been managed electively. Regarding mobility, 71.4% of patients were fully ambulatory. Among those treated electively, approximately half (50%) experienced severe residual back and leg pain and were using walking support. In the emergent treatment group, three patients (60%) experienced resolution of back pain, but four (80%) had residual leg pain. Four out of five emergently treated patients had no further mobility restriction. In the conservatively managed group, all five patients had some residual back or leg pain or nonaffected leg pain of various severity, with 80% being fully ambulatory despite the pain.

Impact of pain on daily activities

The impact of pain on activities of daily living (ADLs) in patients with radiological-only cauda equina compression is illustrated in Figure 4a. Personal care and sexual life mainly remained intact for over 75% of patients, with <20% reporting severe difficulties in sexual life. Walking, traveling, social life, sleeping, and sitting were >50% intact. Lifting weights, standing, and pain intensity were the most affected ADLs. Summing up these results, the ODI for emergently managed patients showed 20% without disability, 60% minimal, and 20% moderate disability [Figure 4b]. In electively operated patients, 50% were intact, but 50% had severe disability. In the conservative treatment group, 80% had minimal disability, with only one patient (20%) reporting severe disability.

Figure 4c depicts the employment status of patients with radiological-only cauda equina compression, with the majority being retired (35.8%, 5 patients) and 28.6% (four patients) unable to work due to disability. In total, 35% of patients sought further advice on CES and 29% needed physiotherapy [Figure 4d].

Figure 5a, based on the follow-up questionnaire, shows that 14.3% of patients reported suffering from uncontrolled bladder function and 7.1% having some slight incontinence. It seemed that those patients who were treated emergently remained intact, while those who had elective surgical management (25%) and conservative management (40%) reported the onset of subsequent urinary issues [Figure 5b]. DISCUSSION

Main primary and secondary findings

Out of the 344 patients referred to the tertiary neurosciences center with suspected CES, only 11% had radiological cauda equina compression without any clinical CES features, and 4% were assessed for long-term results. Within this group, the most common level of spinal stenosis was L4/5 (64%) and in about half the cases compression was due to disc herniation. The mean percentage of radiological cauda equina compression was 79.9% (95% CI: 75.2–84.51). The majority of patients underwent surgery; 35.7% emergent and 28.6% elective surgery. Worthy of note is that 35.7% of cases were treated conservatively. As far as the long-term outcome is concerned, only 14.2% of patients were pain free and 71.4% were fully ambulatory. Sexual life and personal care were the least affected ADLs and remained intact in 75% of patients. From those cases treated emergently, no one reported a severe disability due to residual pain while 50% of the elective surgery and 20% of the conservative cases had severe disability. Regarding bladder and bowel function, 78.6% were free of urinary symptoms and 85.7% had minor or no bowel malfunction. Only 14.3% of patients reported a significantly affected sexual life. Finally, 28.6% of patients could not return to work due to disability.

Symptomatology and radiology

In contemporary literature and clinical practice, there is an increasing focus on subsets of CES presentation and more specifically when suspected CES cases do not completely meet the clinical criteria.[22] Accurate and timely diagnosis of CES and treatment choice remains challenging due to the variable suspected CES presentations and the limited data surrounding its prognosis.[23] Limited knowledge exists on only radiological cauda equina compression with no CES symptomatology and its long-term outcome, which was the subject of this study. However, literature data for CES in general show a similar incidence to our 11% incidence of radiologically confirmed cauda equina compression.[2324]

As expected, the surgical intervention rates for clinical CES are appropriately quite high, such as 98.2%.[23] It is interesting, therefore, that in our cohort where there was radiological only cauda equina compression and in the absence of the clinical syndrome-the respective operative rate was 64.3%. The variation observed in those treated conservatively or surgically, whether emergent or electively, reflects the variation in decision making by the consultant on call.

In our study, the main symptoms of the radiologically driven referrals (i.e., radiological cauda equina compression and spinal stenosis but no CES symptoms) were 64.3% sciatica, 14.3% leg weakness, and 14.3% footdrop. Literature suggests that saddle sensory deficits are closely related to radiological CES signs.[232425] Further to sensory loss, Angus et al. reported that bilateral leg pain and bilateral absent ankle or knee jerk reflexes may also be indicative of radiologically confirmed CES.[23] In previous work, we have reported that a previous history of lumbar spine surgery was not a predictive factor associated with CES but mostly with a more clinically suspected CES.[26]

Radiological compromise of the spinal canal

The percentage of spinal canal compression as observed on radiological sequences which might be indicative of surgical treatment is still unclear when patients have no clinical CES. Thus, other imaging criteria can be taken into account. For instance, Andreisek et al. suggested that central stenosis, nerve root compression in the lateral recess or inside the foramina should lead the patients to a decompressive operation.[27] In our study, the percentage of canal stenosis ranged from 62% to 94%, with a mean value of 79.9% and 95% CI 75.2–84.51. The 50% of disc prolapse and the preponderance of the L4/5 level in our study are findings consistent with those of Ahad et al. and Hussain et al., whose results show that disc herniation is the most common cause of CES and that the most commonly affected level is L4/5.[2829] However, another study by Korse et al. reported that the L5/S1 level was the most common level affected in their series of CES patients.[30]

Long-term outcomes

There is a paucity of literature on the long-term outcome of CES, and even more so when particularly referring to cases of radiological-only cauda equina compression. Studies by Barker et al. and our group (Hazelwood et al.) show that in terms of pain, chronic back pain and leg pain are symptoms that persist and disturb the quality of life in the majority of CES patients.[1131] A lower visual analog scale score for leg pain is reported in cases where patients underwent an operation for a large disc prolapse.[31]

As far as bladder function is concerned, Barker et al. reported that a third of CES patients have long-term moderate-to-severe bladder dysfunction and that only 20% remained intact.[31] A rate of 38% of urinary symptoms at follow-up were described by Korse et al.[30] Seidel et al. calculated a 10% risk for residual bladder malfunction.[32] Women seem to be more sensitive to urinary incontinence based on the study of McCarthy et al.[33] Furthermore, in a previous study from our department (Hazelwood et al. 2021), we reported bladder incontinence at around 76%, which is significantly different from the results of the current study that showed 78.6% intact bladder function, with only a limited number of patients reporting urinary dysfunction.[11] This supports the fact that CES is a clinico-radiological diagnosis; and that radiological only cauda equina compression, with no CES features, is different.

Further support to this distinction comes when assessing bowel function outcomes. Both the Barker et al. and Hazelwood et al. studies reported a 13% dysfunction as assessed by NBD score, while in this present study we report a smaller percentage of 7% with only a minor impact in bowel functionality.[1131] A higher rate of defecation dysfunction is reported by the study of Korse et al.(43%).[30]

Limitations

It is pertinent to acknowledge the retrospective and single-center design of this study, as well as the questionnaire method of data collection. While the actual sample size is small, it derives from a large number of referrals for suspected CES-from which we previously reported the clinical features and outcome of scan-negative and scan-positive cases[17]-and is therefore representative of the pragmatic experience at a tertiary center. There may well have been a variation in the decision of when to operate or not, based on the admitting consultant’s decision, as is common in the British setting. The limited number of patients does not permit a greater in complexity analysis of long-term outcomes nor a multivariate analysis of associated factors. However, we believe that overall this study provides an incentive for further and larger scale studies on the so far rather unexplored topic of radiological only cauda equina compression.

CONCLUSION

This study focused on patients with radiological cauda equina compression lacking clinical features of CES. Such patients comprised 11% of a large cohort referred to the emergency neurosurgery service with suspected CES. Nearly two-thirds underwent surgery, either urgent or elective nonetheless. Our study sheds light on the long-term outcomes of this rather unexplored subset, emphasizing its link to persistent chronic pain irrespective of treatment modality and the emergence of subsequent sphincter dysfunction when not treated urgently. Our data are supportive of further exploration through well-powered multi-centered studies to facilitate an improved understanding of this devastating condition and such nuanced subpopulations.
