Dynamic Interplay between Prefrontal Theta and Beta Bursts Facilitates Flexible Learning
Authors: Hahyeon Park, Haseong Kim, Eunyoung Yeo, Alan Jung Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The ability to cope with changing environments is critical for healthy functioning, yet this flexibility is impaired in many neuropsychiatric disorders. However, neural mechanisms underlying flexible behavior remain elusive. Here, we report that oscillatory dynamics in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) support learning to flexibly overcome established behavioral bias. Mice performed a delayed non-match-to-sample task that required trial-by-trial adjustment of arm choice strategy despite persistent arm bias. Decoding analysis of delay-period local field potentials (LFPs) and single-unit activities revealed evolving neural representations across trials as mice adapted to the task. Notably, mPFC neurons modulated by theta (4~12 Hz) bursts selectively encoded upcoming choice information after acquiring the new rule. In contrast, beta (12~30 Hz) bursts correlated with perseverative behavior and appeared to inhibit theta-modulated neuronal firing in mice showing adaptive behavior. These theta and beta bursts were temporally separated over the delay period, reflecting a dynamic gating mechanism. Thus, beta bursts shape neuronal ensembles that are modulated by theta bursts to facilitate flexible learning. This dynamic interaction provides a mechanistic basis for cognitive flexibility and provides insights into cognitive rigidity seen in neuropsychiatric disorders such as schizophrenia and autism.

INTRODUCTION

The ability to update actions in response to changing environments is crucial for survival, and this flexibility is often impaired in many psychiatric disorders such as schizophrenia[1, 2]. The mPFC plays a key role in adaptive control by encoding associations among context, events, and adaptive responses[3], and its dysfunction underlies cognitive deficits in schizophrenia [4]. During the delay period, when future action is determined based on past experience, the mPFC maintains task-relevant information necessary for upcoming behavior after mastering task rules [5]. However, the contribution of the delay-period mPFC activity to flexible updating of task contingencies remains elusive.

LFP oscillations reflect the synchronized activity of neurons that encode task-relevant information [6]. Specifically, theta and beta frequency oscillations are crucial for memory processing in the mPFC. Theta oscillations have emerged as a principal substrate for memory encoding. In humans and rodents, theta power increases when there is a conflict with expected outcomes, implicating its role in flexible learning [7, 8]. Moreover, delay-period theta bursts appear during memory retention and predict better memory performance [9, 10]. These findings highlight a crucial role for theta in memory-guided behavior. It should be noted, however, that previous studies are based on experiments with subjects who have already mastered the task rule. In other words, it has yet to be determined how delay-period theta orchestrates behavior when the task structure is obscure. Thus, investigating the dynamics of delay-period theta over the course of learning conflicting rules is essential to determine the principles underlying adaptive memory-guided behavior. Additionally, beta activity in the mPFC supports delay-period memory retention by maintaining established task rules and suppressing interference from irrelevant information [11-13]. Moreover, beta bursting decreases when task-relevant information is retrieved, reflecting its role in gating information content [14]. These findings suggest that elevated beta activity may act to stabilize established memory representations, at the expense of flexible updating when new information becomes relevant. Thus, if theta activity enables flexible encoding of task-relevant information, resolving the interplay between theta and beta rhythms is essential for understanding the neural mechanisms of cognitive flexibility.

Here, we investigated how frequency band-specific dynamics orchestrate information updating when previously learned contingencies become irrelevant. We adopted a behavioral paradigm in which mice are trained to modify already acquired task representations once the task rule is switched [15, 16]. Over the course of training, we tracked delay-period activities in the mPFC and compared the evolution of neural representations between groups of mice that either rapidly adapted to the new rule or remained behaviorally rigid. By focusing on the dynamic phase of learning rather than the stable maintenance of already acquired representations, our approach provides a mechanistic framework to understand how mPFC flexibly reorganizes frequency band–specific activity to support adaptive behavior.

We found that theta and beta bursts are temporally exclusive, with beta activity gating theta activity. Decoding analyses demonstrated that theta bursts facilitate information updating, whereas beta bursts encode perseverative behavior and serve an inhibitory function. Together, these findings offer novel insights into the role of the mPFC in adapting to dynamic environments.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study analyzed a subset of data that were not assessed in previous publications [15, 16].

Subjects

Three-month-old male C57BL/6J mice (Jackson Labs) were housed with lights on at 7 am and off at 7 pm. Food and water were available ad libitum. Pairs of mice were separately housed in a homecage divided in half by a perforated plastic divider to prevent social isolation and protect head implants. Mice were randomly assigned to each experimental group. All procedures followed the NIH Guidelines and were approved by Columbia University and the New York State Psychiatric Institute Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees (IACUC).

Surgical procedures

Mice were anesthetized at 2% and maintained at 0.8% isoflurane on a heating pad. Carprofen (0.15 ml) and dexamethasone (0.05 ml) were subcutaneously administered at the beginning of the surgery. Mice were allowed to fully recover for four weeks before behavioral experiments.

Drive implant

A bundle of thirteen tungsten wire stereotrodes (13 μm diameter) was implanted into the mPFC (targeting prelimbic/ infralimbic cortex, layer II/III;1.8 mm anterior to, 0.3 mm lateral to, 2.1 mm below bregma). Reference and ground wires were screwed into the skull overlying the olfactory bulb and cerebellum, respectively. The stereotrodes, reference, and ground wires were connected to a 36-channel electrode interface board (EIB, Neuralynx). Dental cement was used to fix the screws and EIB on the skull.

Histology

Electrode placements were confirmed after completing behavioral experiments by inducing electrolytic lesions. Mice were anesthetized using a ketamine/xylazine mixture, and lesions were created by passing a 50 μA current through an electrode for 20 seconds. Then, transcardial perfusion was performed using 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS, and the brains were collected. After overnight fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4°C, the brains were cryoprotected in 30% phosphate-buffered sucrose at 4°C for three days. Finally, the brains were sectioned at 40 μm using a cryostat and mounted with DAPI Fluoromount-G mounting medium (Southern Biotech, Cat. #: 0100-20). Only recordings from validated electrode placements were analyzed.

Behavior

Food restriction started three days before the first day of free choice session to maintain weight at ~85% of pre-restriction weight until the end of the experiments. To acclimate animals to experimenters, all mice were gently handled for 3 minutes per day over three consecutive days before free choice sessions. The circular arena was 50 cm in diameter and 25 cm in height. The custom-built automated T-maze had arms 10 cm wide and 15 cm high, with a center arm 55 cm in length and goal arms 32 cm in length. The dimension of the start box was 10 cm by 9 cm. Because exposure to a brightly lit open arena induces anxiety-like behavior, all experiments were performed in the dark to minimize nonspecific anxiety-related effects on neural activity [17].

On days 1~3, all mice underwent 30-min free choice sessions in the T-maze, in which mice were allowed to freely obtain rewards (condensed milk diluted 1:3 in deionized water). After receiving a reward in one of the two goal arms, mice were required to return to the start box to consume a reward to initiate another trial. By the last day of free choice sessions, mice exhibited a strong arm bias, selecting one arm approximately 90% of trials [15]. In addition, the familiar-exposed group was given 30 min of exploration in the circular arena 1 hour before each free choice session to have mice be familiarized to the arena.

On day 4, the novel and familiar groups were exposed to the circular arena for 10 minutes. Then, 1 hour later, they underwent 40 trials of delayed non-match-to-sample flexible choice training. The 1-hour interval was chosen because novelty effects typically persist for about 2 hours [18-20]. Control mice were transferred directly from their home cages to flexible choice training without prior arena exposure. Each flexible choice trial consisted of three phases: 1. sample phase, during which mice were guided to one goal arm for a reward; 2. delay phase, a 60-second stay in the start box; and 3. choice phase, in which mice were required to select the arm opposite to the sample arm to obtain the reward (Fig. 1A).

The delayed non-match-to-sample flexible choice task used in this study fundamentally differs from the conventional delayed non-match-to-sample working memory paradigm [21, 22]. Classical working memory tasks include shaping sessions where mice are guided to alternate arms, followed by repeated daily training until mice master the delayed non-match-to-sample rule. Thus, working memory is assessed in animals that have already acquired the rule. In contrast, flexible choice training omitted both shaping and extensive training. After free-choice sessions, mice developed a strong individual arm bias, which they had to overcome to obtain a reward in the flexible choice task. Both goal arms had the same sensory features (color, scent, brightness, reward amount), and the orientation of the maze was held constant. The side of the arm bias varied across animals, indicating that it was internally generated, not driven by external cues.

Behavior analysis

Learning curve analysis quantified trial-wise acquisition of the flexible choice rule by calculating performance score accounting for arm bias. The arm bias was quantified as the dominant arm choice during the first 10 trials. For each subject, a logistic regression model estimated the probability of a correct performance at trial t by including the effect of the current trial and the arm bias. To account for ambiguous trials, in which mice had a correct response by following their bias, the bias term (Biast) was added.

Here, 1 is the indicator function assigning 1 if the choice at trial i is the biased arm and 0 otherwise. This logistic model assures that a correct response reflects genuine learning rather than choice bias. The bias-corrected learning curve was then smoothed and fit to a sigmoidal function.

Perseverative error analysis quantified the persistence of incorrect responses by choosing the biased arm. Individual trial-wise perseverative error rates were then calculated, smoothed, and modeled by fitting sigmoid functions that described the decay of these errors over time.

Neural data analysis

Neurophysiological recordings were performed during flexible choice training using a Digital Lynx system (Neuralynx, Bozeman, MT). Local field potentials (LFPs) and spikes were band-pass filtered (1~1,000 Hz, and 600~6,000 Hz, each) and collected at 2 kHz and 32 kHz, respectively. Klustakwik (Ken Harris) was used to identify single units. Specifically, single units were initially clustered using the first two principal components (peak and energy). Then, clusters were accepted by visual inspection of waveform, isolation distance, inter-spike interval, and L-ratio.

Data collected during the delay period of flexible choice training were analyzed. The first 20 seconds of the 60-second delay were excluded to avoid any signals related to reward port reaching and reward consumption in the start box. Data analysis was performed using custom-written MATLAB scripts. Each electrode bundle consisted of 13 stereotrodes. To account for impedance variability across electrodes and animals, raw LFP data were normalized to the root mean square of the voltage signal of the entire session. Power scalogram was calculated using the wavelet method (MATLAB wavelet toolbox). Single units were included for analysis if they had more than 50 spikes per trial for at least 20 of the 40 total trials (Control: 50 units, Familiar: 47 units, Novel: 47 units). Since the number of units for each mouse varied, analyses were performed on total number of units for each group. Also, all decoding analyses incorporated behavioral choices across all trials for each mouse.

To detect delay-period burst events, power time series of normalized LFPs were extracted using the Hilbert transform. Bursts were defined when power was above the 70th percentile of the power time series across all trials and lasted longer than 3 cycles.

The temporal relationship between theta and beta burst events was measured by quantifying how much bursts in the two bands mutually exclude each other.

Mutual Exclusivity Index=Exclusive theta burst time+Exclusive beta burst timeTotal burst time theta & beta

The mutual exclusivity index ranges from 0 to 1, where a value of 1 indicates complete temporal exclusivity while a value of 0 corresponds to complete overlap between theta and beta burst events.

Neuronal population decoding was performed to examine how neurons modulated by theta or beta burst activity encode behavioral outcomes. First, for each cell and trial, modulated cells were determined by calculating differences between firing rates inside and outside bursting events. Then, a permutation test was performed by shuffling spike events 1,000 times to generate distributions of rate differences under the null hypothesis of no relationship between bursts and spikes. Statistical significance was determined by comparing the observed rate differences with the shuffled distribution. Cells were positively or negatively modulated if the observed firing rate during bursting was greater or lower than that of outside bursting. Next, the identified modulated cells underwent population decoding. Firing rates inside and outside burst events, positive or negative modulation direction, and behavioral labels for sample or choice phases were considered. Firing rates were z-scored to normalize differences across cells. A logistic regression classifier (MATLAB function, fitclinear) was trained to decode behavioral data using a 5-fold cross-validation. Decoding accuracy was averaged across folds to obtain the observed classification performance. Statistical significance was evaluated using a permutation test with 1,000 random shuffles of behavioral labels or firing patterns. For each shuffle, cross-validated accuracy was recalculated to form a null distribution of decoding accuracies. The p-value was computed as the proportion of permutations yielding accuracy equal to or exceeding the observed accuracy.

Population principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to visualize and compare burst-related ensemble dynamics underlying distinct behavioral outcomes. For each trial and burst-modulated cell, firing rates inside and outside burst events were z-scored across trials. Then, PCA was conducted, and PCA projections were averaged across cells separately for each behavioral condition, resulting in mean trajectory curves in the 2D PCA space for each behavioral outcome. To assess differences in PCA trajectories between behavioral outcomes, the Euclidean distance between the mean PCA trajectory of different behavioral outcomes (e.g., right arm vs. left arm) was computed across time and averaged to yield a root mean square (RMS) trajectory difference:

Dglobal=1T∑t=1Tm0t-m1t2

where m0(t) and m1(t) are the PCA mean vectors at time t for each behavioral outcome. For statistical significance, permutation distributions of RMS trajectory differences were generated by shuffling behavioral labels 1,000 times. The permutation p-value was defined as the fraction of permutations yielding an RMS trajectory difference greater than the observed value.

Logistic regression analysis between beta burst events and behavioral conditions was performed to decode their relationship. Behavioral conditions include biased arm choice, perseverative error, performance (correct or incorrect), and choice switch (trial-to-trial change in choice). A generalized linear model (GLM) was fit to determine coefficients and standard errors (MATLAB function, fitglm), thereby predicting each behavioral variable from beta burst events.

Statistics

Graphpad Prism 9 or MATLAB was used for statistical analysis. All statistical tests were two-tailed. Differences were considered statistically significant when p<0.05.

RESULTS

Novelty exposure enhances learning to overcome perseverative behavior

To evaluate the progress of adaptive learning, we developed behavioral procedures that require mice to overcome previously learned strategies when the rule for reward has changed. For three days, mice underwent free-choice sessions, in which they could freely choose one of the two arms of a T-maze to obtain a reward. The following day, they performed flexible choice training. Here, the reward was only available in the arm opposite to the sample arm in a delayed non-match-to-sample paradigm (Fig. 1A). We previously reported that mice develop a strong arm bias, consistently choosing one specific arm after free choice ses sions. Notably, regardless of the arm bias, mice exposed to a novel arena 1-hour before flexible choice training acquired the delayed non-match-to-sample rule more rapidly than those exposed to a familiar arena or left unexposed (control) [15]. In this study, we re-visited these findings with advanced mathematical approaches.

To reliably evaluate the learning curve during flexible choice training, it is important to account for false correct trials in which the goal arm coincided with the biased arm. The previous study addressed this issue by incorporating the bias level observed on the last day of free choice session as a fixed term in a logistic regression model. This method assumed that mice maintained the same level of arm bias established during free choice sessions throughout flexible choice training [15]. Thus, this approach does not capture trial-by-trial fluctuations in performance during flexible choice training. To obtain a more precise estimate of the learning curve, we developed a new model that calculates the probability of a correct response on each trial based on arm bias observed in past trials during flexible choice training (see Methods). During the first 10 trials of flexible choice training, mice exhibited a strong arm bias (Fig. 1B; Control: 91±1.8%, Familiar: 85.5±4.7%, Novel: 85±4.6%). Then, we evaluated the learning curve and found that the novel group gradually outperformed the familiar and control groups (Fig. 1C). To determine whether this effect reflected learning to overcome arm bias, we quantified perseverative errors, defined as the tendency to persist with the biased arm choice. Indeed, the novel group displayed a more rapid decay in perseverative error rates, compared with the other groups (Fig. 1D). These findings assure that prior novelty exposure promotes the ability to update previously acquired information. Thus, comparing the novel group with the familiar and control groups would serve as an ideal platform to unveil neural dynamics underlying flexible learning.

Delay period theta bursts orchestrate task encoding with learning

Delay period activity in the mPFC encodes task-specific information [3]. To investigate how the encoding of past sample and prospective choice information in the mPFC evolves during the delay period of flexible choice training, we first analyzed oscillatory activity patterns. Because neurons in layer 2/3 of prelimbic and infralimbic cortices contribute to flexible learning, recordings were made in these areas [15]. All groups of mice exhibited prominent burst activities in theta and beta frequency bands (Fig. 2A~C). Notably, theta and beta burst events were mutually exclusive in time (Fig. 2D~L). The number of theta bursts, beta bursts, and their mutual exclusivity were similar between all groups, with beta burst frequency gradually decreasing over the 40 trials of flexible choice training (Fig. 2M~O).

We next examined whether burst events modulate the activity of mPFC neurons. In each trial, neuronal firing rates were increased (positively modulated), decreased (negatively modulated), or unaffected during theta burst events (Fig. 3A). The direction of modulation of each neuron varied across trials. To determine if these theta-modulated neurons encode task-specific information, we employed two independent approaches. First, because arm choice information is binary (left or right), we used a binary decoder and found that modulated neurons only in the novel group successfully classified arm choice (Fig. 3B~E). This effect was unseen when neuronal firing patterns were shuffled (Fig. 3F~I). Second, we performed PCA on theta-modulated neurons. Since the learning curve of the novel group diverged from the others around trial 20, we compared the PCA trajectories between the early phase (before trial 20) and the late phase (after trial 20). Consistent with the decoding results, only modulated neurons in the novel group exhibited distinct PCA trajectories between arm choices in the late phase, when mice learned the flexible choice rule (Fig. 3J~L).

On the other hand, we did not find concrete evidence that theta-modulated mPFC neurons encode information about the sample arm presented before the delay period (Fig. 4). Additionally, when the same decoder and PCA approaches were applied to beta-modulated mPFC neurons, neither choice nor sample arm information was clearly classified in all groups (Figs. 5, 6). Collectively, these findings suggest that theta-modulated mPFC neurons underlie facilitated learning in the novel group.

Delay period beta bursts encode arm bias and gate theta activity

Delay period beta bursts encode already acquired task rules [23]. Therefore, we examined whether delay period beta bursts encode the arm bias acquired from free choice sessions. In all groups, the number of beta bursts positively correlated with the probability of perseverative error and biased am choice (Fig. 7A, B, E, F, I, J). This suggests that beta bursts indeed encode the established arm bias from free choice sessions. This finding is not a non-specific artifact of the decreasing number of beta bursts over trials (Fig. 2N). Beta burst counts were negatively correlated with the probability of choice switching in consecutive trials only in the control and familiar groups (Fig. 7B, C, G, K). In contrast, task performance was negatively correlated with beta burst counts only in the novel group (Fig. 7B, D, H, L). Thus, beta bursts encode the established arm bias, and this encoding is specific to the degree of learning to overcome the bias.

Theta and beta bursts are temporally exclusive (Fig. 2G~L, O), with theta bursts linked to task-specific choice information (Fig. 3B~L) and beta bursts encoding task-nonspecific bias information (Fig. 7A~L). Therefore, we postulated that beta burst activity gates the firing of theta-modulated neurons encoding task-specific information, thereby facilitating adaptive behavior. Indeed, we found that the firing rate of theta-modulated neurons was suppressed during beta burst events relative to non-bursting periods in the novel group, but not in the control and familiar groups (Fig. 7L, M). Overall, these findings demonstrate that delay-period beta bursts encode established arm bias and filter theta-modulated task-specific neuronal activity to support flexible choice behavior.

DISCUSSION

This study revealed medial prefrontal mechanisms supporting flexible decision-making by dissecting oscillatory dynamics during the delay period, when future choices are computed based on past experiences. Specifically, we found that effective inhibitory control exerted by beta bursts on neurons modulated by periodic theta activity is crucial for overcoming behavioral rigidity. This study captured the dynamic interaction between theta and beta bursts over the course of memory updating, distinguishing it from conventional working memory studies on stable memory maintenance during delay periods.

We found that mPFC neurons modulated by theta bursts encode arm choice after mice learned to overcome previously established arm bias. This is in line with human and rodent studies that theta activities in the mPFC are essential for the encoding of task-specific information [7, 8, 24-26]. However, these works focused on steady-state or event-related theta power after the subjects learned the task structure. On the other hand, the present study highlighted periodic theta burst events during the progressive learning process. A unique aspect of our findings is that theta burst encoding emerges as animals adapt to the new rule. This suggests a dynamic shift in network function over the course of learning. Future experiments with closed-loop optogenetic manipulations could causally test whether the disruption of theta bursts during delay impairs the acquisition of new rules, but not the maintenance of already encoded information (e.g., arm bias).

Our study reported that beta bursts during the delay period encode the strength of perseverative behavior rather than task-specific information. Moreover, the gating of theta-modulated neuronal firing by beta bursts is crucial for overcoming perseverative behavior, as shown in the novel group. In humans and primates, beta activity has been proposed to protect established rules in working memory and oppose rapid updates [11, 12, 23, 27]. Our findings not only support this view but also extend it; intermittent beta bursts shape the firing patterns of neurons that participate in task-specific information updating. Although there is evidence supporting this notion [6], this dynamic was not apparent in previous studies using highly trained animals performing under stable rules. Closed-loop optogenetic manipulations could confirm the role of beta bursts in balancing between the maintenance and update of information.

The present work neither found prominent gamma bursting during the delay period nor any significant evidence that gamma bursts encode task-relevant information (data not shown). This seems contradictory to the literature showing that gamma bursts encode specific items held in working memory during the delay period [23, 27-29]. These studies relied on well-trained subjects required to maintain sensory information during a brief few seconds of delays. In contrast, our study focused on progressive updating of task rules during a longer (1-minute) delay. These findings suggest that gamma bursts may mediate short maintenance of specific sensory information, rather than the continuous updating of task contingencies during longer delays. Future studies using sensory task designs with variable delay lengths could confirm this speculation.

The interplay between theta and beta burst activities in flexible learning is a largely unexplored area. Given the evidence that reduced theta and increased beta activity are linked to cognitive inflexibility in neuropsychiatric disorders such as schizophrenia [12, 30], our study opened a new avenue for investigating cognitive flexibility in health and disease.
