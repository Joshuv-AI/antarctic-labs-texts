Advances in oncolytic virus delivery strategies and challenges in clinical translation
Authors: Yu Guo, Chunyao Huang, Lihua Xu, Yujing Xuan, Pengju Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oncolytic viruses (OVs) represent an emerging cancer therapeutic modality with dual functions of direct tumor cell lysis and activation of systemic antitumor immunity. However, the systemic delivery of OVs remains a significant challenge owing to immune barriers and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). Current delivery strategies can be broadly categorized into naked virus delivery and vector-based delivery. Each platform presents distinct advantages in enhancing delivery efficiency and antitumor immune activation, yet both share common challenges, including immune clearance and delivery stability. In this review, we provide a comprehensive summary of the advantages and limitations associated with these different delivery strategies. In summary, combining OVs with advanced delivery systems holds great promise for advancing personalized and precise cancer therapies. Future research should focus on the development of multifunctional platforms, the optimization of combination treatment regimens, and the establishment of regulatory frameworks to accelerate the clinical translation of OV-based therapies.

Introduction

Oncolytic viruses (OVs) are capable of selectively infecting and lysing tumor cells while simultaneously activating the host's antitumor immune response. In recent years, OVs have emerged as a focus in cancer therapy research. In 2005, China approved the first oncolytic virus drug “Oncorine” (H101, an adenovirus) for the treatment of head and neck cancers [1,2]. Subsequently, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and Japan's regulatory authorities approved T-VEC (talimogene laherparepvec) and Delytact, respectively [3]. These regulatory approvals mark a significant milestone in the transition of OVs from experimental research to clinical application.

As of 2025, over 186 clinical trials of oncolytic virotherapy involving nearly 7000 patients have been conducted globally [4]. A meta-analysis of 36 randomized Phase II–III trials demonstrated that OV-based regimens significantly improved the objective response rate (ORR) by nearly threefold (OR = 2.77) and reduced the risk of death by 16% (OS HR = 0.84) compared with standard therapy [5]. However, the overall ORR of OVs across all solid tumor types remains only 29%, with a complete response (CR) rate of just 11% [6], and no new OV product has gained multinational regulatory approval in nearly a decade. The clinical translation of OVs faces numerous challenges. Among these, inefficient delivery has emerged as a critical bottleneck limiting therapeutic efficacy. Accumulating evidence indicates that the delivery strategy plays a pivotal role in shaping the in vivo distribution, infectivity, and immunomodulatory effects of OVs [7]. Different delivery approaches not only determine the amount of virus that successfully reaches the tumor site, but also influence its ability to replicate, spread within the tumor microenvironment (TME), and interact with the host immune system. Therefore, the rational optimization of OV delivery methods has become a central focus in both basic and translational research.

This review systematically summarizes the current mainstream delivery strategies for OVs, which can be broadly categorized into naked virus delivery and vector-based delivery. Overview of naked virus delivery

Naked virus delivery refers to the direct administration of virus particles without the use of external carriers. As one of the foundational strategies in oncolytic virotherapy, this approach exploits the inherent ability of OVs to selectively infect and lyse tumor cells. Depending on the route of administration, naked virus delivery can be classified into several modalities, including intratumoral injection, intravenous infusion, local perfusion, and intrathecal injection.

Intratumoral injection

Intratumoral injection is currently the most commonly employed administration route for OVs. All OVs approved for clinical use to date utilize intratumoral injection as their primary delivery method (Table 1). Efficacy

The efficacy of intratumoral administration has been validated in numerous preclinical studies and clinical trials, particularly in treating locally accessible tumors, such as melanoma, head and neck cancer, and liver cancer. For instance, in an open-label, randomized Phase III trial (NCT00769704), 295 patients with unresectable melanoma received intratumoral injections of T-VEC. Compared with the control group receiving subcutaneous GM-CSF, T-VEC significantly improved the durable response rate (16.3% vs. 2.1%; unadjusted odds ratio = 8.9; p < 0.001), with a complete response rate of 10.8% [9]. Subsequent studies have further confirmed T-VEC's potential efficacy across multiple tumor types [10].

Combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) further enhances the therapeutic efficacy of OVs. A Phase I/IIa trial (NCT03071094) assessed JX-594 in combination with nivolumab as a first-line treatment for advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), yielding a 33.3% ORR (12 patients, range: 9.9%–65.1%). Moreover, a meta-analysis by Zou et al. reported an overall response rate of up to 45% when OVs were combined with ICIs in the treatment of advanced melanoma [11] Combination therapies have the potential to enhance synergistic antitumor effects, overcome resistance to monotherapies, and reduce the required doses of chemo- or radiotherapy, thereby expanding the therapeutic window and improving safety.

It is worth noting that OVs exert antitumor effects not only at the site of injection but also systemically through mechanisms such as the abscopal effect and bystander effect. Riikka et al. developed an oncolytic adenovirus (oAd) encoding human TNF-α and interleukin-2 (IL-2), and in a bilateral tumor-bearing mouse model, they observed significant tumor suppression, immune cell infiltration at the injected sites, and even regression of uninjected tumors—indicating the generation of a systemic antitumor response [12]. Similar systemic effects have been observed with NDV, vesiculovirus, and HSV-1 models [[13], [14], [15]]. Local injection of Newcastle disease virus (NDV), for example, increases the infiltration of CD8+ T cells and NK cells in distant tumors, along with an upregulation of inflammatory mediators such as IFN-γ and IL-12 [16]. This “remote immune activation” offers a theoretical foundation for treating multifocal or micrometastatic disease. Furthermore, combining OVs with ICIs, immunogenic chemotherapy, IFNAR blockade, or hyperthermia has been shown to enhance these abscopal effects [17,18].

Safety

Intratumoral injection is generally well-tolerated. Most treatment-related adverse events are mild (CTCAE grade 1–2) and include systemic symptoms or local injection site reactions such as fatigue, chills, and fever. Serious toxicities are rare [19]. In a retrospective study of 113 patients receiving image-guided intratumoral T-VEC injections, only one patient (1%) experienced a mild adverse event, further supporting the approach's favorable safety profile [20].

Limitations

Despite its clear advantages, intratumoral injection also presents several limitations. First, solid tumors often exhibit challenging biophysical characteristics such as low pH, dense cellular architecture, elevated interstitial fluid pressure (5-40 mmHg) [21], and a dense extracellular matrix (ECM) rich in fibroblasts, hyaluronic acid, and collagen [22]. These features collectively impede the intratumoral spread of OVs. As a result, OVs administered by local injection may fail to distribute evenly throughout the tumor, may reflux into the vasculature or lymphatic system, and may exhibit shortened retention times, all of which compromise therapeutic efficacy and the extent of infection. Second, the hypoxic and acidic tumor microenvironment can further hinder viral replication and activity [23]. Furthermore, intratumoral injection procedures require advanced imaging guidance and specialized expertise, increasing procedural complexity and thereby limiting the clinical applicability. For metastatic or multifocal tumors, intratumoral injection becomes technically challenging and provides limited coverage. Finally, to maintain therapeutic efficacy, repeated injections are often necessary. This not only reduces patient compliance but also increases the risk of complications such as bleeding or tumor seeding along the needle tract during repeated punctures [24].

Intravenous/systemic injection

Although intratumoral injection can induce abscopal effects and systemically activate antitumor immunity, such responses are not consistently observed or may be insufficiently robust. In contrast, intravenous injection of naked OVs relies on the intrinsic biological properties of the virus to disseminate and target tumors throughout the body, offering the potential for simultaneous control of both primary and metastatic lesions. Furthermore, it is simple to administer, allows for systemic distribution and repeatable dosing, and holds strong translational potential. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that systemic delivery enables OVs to “home” to tumor cells and activate CD8+ T cells and natural killer (NK) cells, while also inducing the expression of proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-6 [25,26].

Intravenous delivery of OVs has demonstrated significant antitumor efficacy in animal models. For instance, a single IV dose of Seneca Valley Virus-001 (SVV-001) induced complete tumor regression in murine models of neuroendocrine tumors [30]. In glioblastoma and medulloblastoma models, SVV-001 was capable of crossing the blood-brain barrier, infecting tumor tissues, and significantly prolonging survival [31]. However, clinical trial results for IV-delivered OVs have been mixed. Although SVV-001 led to complete responses in murine models, a Phase I trial in patients with neuroendocrine tumors failed to produce objective tumor responses per RECIST criteria. Most patients maintained stable disease (SD), though improvements in carcinoid syndrome and significant PET response were noted [32]. Similar findings were observed in a Phase I study of ICOVIR-5 for metastatic melanoma and REOLYSIN (reovirus type 3 Dearing) for brain tumors. While intravenous delivery successfully delivered viruses to tumor sites, no substantial clinical benefit was observed [27,33]. In contrast to these negative results, in a study of CG7870 (oAds) for hormone-refractory metastatic prostate cancer, patients experienced a 25–49% decline in PSA levels [34]. And in a trial of systemic Onyx-015 for colorectal cancer, 8% patients achieved partial responses (PR), and 46% had SD [35]. These findings underscore the limited efficacy of OVs as monotherapies in clinical settings.

To address the limitations of single-agent therapies, particularly in terms of efficacy and resistance, combination strategies are emerging as a promising avenue for enhancing the overall therapeutic potential. By leveraging the complementary mechanisms of different treatments, combination approaches aim to maximize antitumor effects and minimize resistance. Additionally, OVs combined with radiotherapy, chemotherapy, small-molecule inhibitors, or immunotherapy have exhibited promising tumor control in preclinical models and clinical trials. OVs have demonstrated promising therapeutic effects in combination with radiotherapy and small molecule inhibitors in mouse tumor models. [36,37]. Additionally, OV infection has been reported to upregulate PD-L1 expression on tumor cells, providing a theoretical basis for combining OVs with immune checkpoint inhibitors. [38,39]. In several clinical trials, intravenous delivery of OVs, such as VV GL-ONC1 in combination with cisplatin and radiotherapy, has demonstrated improved 1-year and 2-year progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) rates. [40]. Similarly, the combination of REOLYSIN with chemotherapy in advanced cancer patients also showed promising response rates. Although the complete response rate was low, a portion of patients achieved stable disease, providing new insights for further therapeutic combinations [26,41]. These results suggest that OVs can potentiate antitumor immune responses and work synergistically with conventional therapies to improve tumor control.

Safety

Multiple Phase I/II clinical trials have shown that intravenous administration of OVs is generally safe. Most adverse events are mild to moderate, commonly presenting as flu-like symptoms such as fever, fatigue, chills, nausea, and vomiting, typically requiring no specific medical intervention [32,35]. For instance, in a study evaluating the oncolytic adenovirus ONYX-015 (H101) for the treatment of advanced non-small cell lung cancer, no dose-limiting toxicity (DLT) was observed at doses as high as 2 × 1013 viral particles (vp) [42]. Other viruses, including vaccinia virus (VV), herpes simplex virus (HSV), NDV, and reovirus, have also been evaluated through intravenous delivery in multiple Phase I/II trials with noDLTs reported [[43], [44], [45]] further supporting the safety and feasibility of systemic OV delivery.

Although the overall risk remains low, treatment-related adverse events, including rare fatal cases, have been reported in isolated studies. High-dose intravenous administration may trigger excessive immune responses, leading to severe hepatotoxicity, vascular complications, and cytokine storms. For instance, in a phase I trial of CG7870 for castration-resistant metastatic prostate cancer, three patients experienced grade 3 adverse events, including fatigue, elevated transaminases, and increased D-dimer levels, indicating potential coagulation-related toxicity [34]. Other reported risks include viral exanthema, neutropenia, elevated inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α), hypotension, and lactic acidosis. A notable case is Jesse Gelsinger, who died from multi-organ failure following adenoviral gene therapy, highlighting the risks of viral vectors and prompting stricter regulatory oversight [46,47].

Biodistribution and tumor targeting specificity

Intravenous administration of OVs enables systemic dissemination, with viral particles initially accumulating in highly perfused organs like the liver, spleen, and lungs. However, viral replication and sustained transgene expression are primarily confined to tumor tissues. The persistence of OVs in circulation is generally brief, and the duration depends on both the viral vector and the host's immune status. In a Phase II clinical trial, Fong et al. observed that dl1520 (adenovirus) remained detectable in patients' blood and tumor tissues several days after administration, indicating active replication [48]. Similarly, biodistribution studies of VV JX-594 and ONYX-015 showed replication-competent viruses were largely confined to tumors. [40,49,50]. Other vectors such as reovirus, NDV, and HSV also display greater replication activity in tumors than in normal tissues [26,[51], [52], [53]]. These findings suggest that the TME provides a permissive niche for OV replication, likely due to its immunosuppressive nature and defective antiviral signaling pathways. Such tumor-selective permissiveness greatly reduces the systemic toxicity and expands the therapeutic window of OVs.

Limitation

Despite the advantages of intravenous delivery in terms of convenience and systemic reach, it remains constrained by several biological barriers. One of the most critical limitations is the rapid clearance of viral particles from the bloodstream due to neutralizing antibodies, complement activation, antiviral cytokines, tissue-resident macrophages, and nonspecific uptake by organs such as the lungs, liver, and spleen. These mechanisms significantly reduce the effective viral dose before the OVs reach the tumor site [54]. Additionally, OVs may be impeded by physical barriers in the TME, including abnormal vascular structures, dense stromal matrices, elevated interstitial fluid pressure, and tightly packed tumor architecture, all of which hinder efficient viral penetration and intratumoral spread [55,56]. Furthermore, intravenous administration may induce systemic toxicities, especially at high doses. Acute inflammatory reactions—including chills, fever, and liver enzyme abnormalities—have been reported [57,58], limiting the ability to escalate viral dosage for therapeutic gain.

In addition to the aforementioned limitations, intravenous administration has not consistently yielded superior antitumor efficacy in every clinical trial. For example, a Phase III trial of Pexa-Vec combined with sorafenib (NCT02562755) in HCC failed to demonstrate superior efficacy compared to sorafenib alone [29], highlighting the importance of optimizing therapeutic combinations and patient selection strategies. This underscores that rational combination approaches should go beyond simple additive use of agents and instead be grounded in a comprehensive understanding of OV mechanisms, particularly their capacity to remodel the tumor microenvironment and regulate oncogenic signaling pathways. Mechanism-driven combination strategies offer the potential to maximize synergistic effects, minimize toxicity, and ultimately enhance patient benefit.

Local perfusion

The administration of OVs into biological cavities, such as the peritoneal cavity, thoracic cavity, or bladder, represents an effective delivery strategy. This approach achieves higher concentrations in the target area, enhancing antitumor efficacy while reducing systemic toxicity. Intraperitoneal, intrathoracic, and intravesical perfusion are being actively explored as routes of administration. Among these, CG0070 is the most developed and clinically translatable example for intravesical perfusion, showing promising efficacy and safety in patients with BCG-unresponsive non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC), making it a strong candidate for conservative bladder cancer treatment [59]. In contrast, studies on intrathoracic and intraperitoneal perfusion are still primarily at the preclinical stage. For example, CF33 demonstrated potent antitumor activity in a murine gastric cancer peritoneal metastasis model [60]. Additionally, two clinical trials using HSV and measles virus (MV) for intrapleural perfusion in treating malignant pleural mesothelioma are ongoing (NCT01503177, NCT01721018), but clinical data have not yet been published.

Although studies have shown that OVs can be delivered via intravesical perfusion with controllable acute toxicity [28], there is still a lack of data on long-term bladder wall structural changes and persistent lower urinary tract dysfunction. Given the high frequency of bladder irritative side effects in conventional intravesical therapies (chemotherapy/BCG/other immunotherapies) [61,62], future studies should include long-term follow-up to comprehensively evaluate the safety and urinary function impact of OV perfusion therapy. Furthermore, although the Phase I study of GL-ONC1 intraperitoneal perfusion in treating peritoneal metastases did not observe tumor shedding [63], given the delivery method and virus type, local perfusion requires caution regarding viral shedding and environmental transmission risks.

Intrathecal injection

Due to the presence of the blood-brain barrier (BBB), intravenous delivery of OVs has difficulty effectively entering the central nervous system (CNS). In contrast, intrathecal injection bypasses the BBB, directly delivering OVs into the ventricles or cerebrospinal fluid, thereby allowing for localized viral replication and tumor lysis within the CNS. This makes it a promising strategy for treating CNS tumors. However, current research on intrathecal injection remains largely limited to preclinical models. For example, intrathecal or intracerebral injection of recombinant OVs into rat models of breast cancer brain metastasis and meningeal metastasis has shown stronger tumor control effects in the experimental groups [64,65].

In studies with rats and primates, intrathecal injection has resulted in symptoms such as lethargy, conjunctival congestion, hydrocephalus, viral meningitis, and demyelination [66,67]. Although current data is limited, especially regarding long-term safety, neurotoxicity, viral shedding, and environmental safety, systematic reports on these aspects are lacking. Overview of vector-based delivery

Beyond conventional naked virus infusion strategies, a variety of auxiliary delivery approaches have been developed in recent years to enhance the stability and tumor-targeting efficiency of systemic oncolytic virotherapy. These strategies can be broadly categorized into two major types: cell-based carriers and nanomaterial-based delivery systems. Cell-based carriers—including stem cells, immune cells, and tumor cells—can actively migrate toward tumor sites by exploiting their innate chemotactic properties or immune evasion capabilities, thereby serving as vehicles for viral delivery. Nanomaterial-based systems, on the other hand, include inorganic nanoparticles (such as iron-, manganese-, and carbon-based particles) and organic polymeric systems (based on hydrogels, polyethylene glycol, or chitosan). These materials not only exhibit favorable biocompatibility but can also be engineered with surface modifications to achieve targeted tumor delivery. Collectively, these strategies show great promise in preserving viral activity, enhancing tumor specificity, and modulating the interactions between the virus and the host immune system.

Cell-based delivery

Systemic delivery of OVs faces significant challenges due to rapid clearance by the innate immune system, including neutralization by antibodies, complement activation, and sequestration by the reticuloendothelial system. Cell-based carrier strategies involve “camouflaging” the virus within or on the surface of host cells, enabling immune evasion and targeted delivery.

Encapsulating OVs within cellular carriers provides a form of “hitchhiking” transport, facilitating stealthy delivery while shielding viral particles from neutralizing antibodies. Notably, certain cell types—such as mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), T cells, and neural stem cells (NSCs)—exhibit inherent tumor tropism, which enhances the targeting precision of OVs and improves their biodistribution and pharmacokinetics. Stem cells

Stem cells—particularly MSCs and neural stem cells NSCs—have garnered significant attention as delivery vehicles for OVs due to their low immunogenicity and strong tumor tropism [68]. MSCs are known to possess inherent migratory capacity toward tumor tissues in vivo. This migration is influenced by multiple factors, including cell culture conditions, chemokine gradients, the inflammatory status of the tumor microenvironment, and tissue oxygenation [69,70]. Several signaling axes have been implicated in guiding MSCs’ homing to tumors, including SDF-1/CXCR4, SCF/c-Kit, and VEGF/VEGFR pathways [69,71,72]. Modulating these pathways has been shown to enhance the tumor-targeting properties of MSCs [73,74].

The use of MSCs as delivery vehicles for OVs has shown varying degrees of success in preclinical models of glioblastoma, melanoma, ovarian cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, lymphocytic leukemia, and pancreatic cancer [69,[75], [76], [77], [78]]. Youjin Na et al. utilized bone marrow–derived MSCs to deliver oncolytic adenoviruses (oAds) and demonstrated significantly enhanced viral replication within pancreatic tumors, leading to robust antitumor effects in preclinical models [79]. Similar safety and efficacy were observed in liver cancer models as well [78]. MSCs have been preliminarily explored as carriers for OVs in clinical trials (Table 4). NSCs have also been explored as carriers for targeting central nervous system tumors, with studies showing that the amount of virus released was significantly higher than that from MSCs, resulting in superior efficacy in the treatment of intracranial tumors [81,82]. However, current research on NSC cell carriers mostly focuses on intratumoral or intracranial injection. Ahmed et al. loaded the oAds CRAd-S-pk7 into NSCs and administered them via intratumoral injection. The combination significantly inhibited tumor growth, enhanced tumor tropism, and extended the median survival time in treated mice compared to virus alone, strongly supporting the use of NSCs as OVs carriers [83]. In clinical trials using NSCs to deliver adenoviruses, except for one patient who developed grade 3 viral meningitis due to accidental injection into the lateral ventricle, no treatment-related deaths were reported (NCT03072134) [84].

Despite promising data, some concerns remain regarding the safety of stem cell-based delivery systems. First, viral loading into stem cells may impair their proliferative capacity and increase genomic instability in primary cells. In addition, the number of viral particles that a single MSC can carry is inherently limited, and tumor-targeted modifications often reduce the replication efficiency of OVs within MSCs, thus restricting viral amplification and release in vivo. Second, tumor cells have been shown to stimulate MSCs to secrete chemokines such as CCL5, which in turn promotes epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), thereby enhancing tumor cell invasion and metastasis [85,86]. Third, MSCs are known to suppress both innate and adaptive immune responses by interfering with the activation of dendritic cells and macrophages, leading to downregulation of co-stimulatory molecules such as CD80 and CD86, ultimately weakening T cell activation [87,88]. Thus, using MSCs as OV carriers may exacerbate local immunosuppression and compromise the antitumor efficacy of OVs. Fourth, MSCs may differentiate into cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) within the tumor microenvironment [89], which secrete immunosuppressive and pro-tumorigenic factors including FGF2, TGF-β, and IL-10 [90], potentially promoting immunosuppression, angiogenesis, and tumor progression. Finally, the clinical translation of stem cell-based delivery is limited by challenges in sourcing cells from clinical-grade specimens, manufacturing variability, and batch-to-batch inconsistency—factors that complicate scalability and regulatory approval.

Immune cells

T cells

T cells offer several unique advantages as platforms for OV delivery, especially when genetically engineered as chimeric antigen receptor (CAR-T) or T-cell receptor (TCR-T) cells. Their ability to circulate systemically and recognize tumor-specific antigens makes them ideal carriers. When combined with OVs, these engineered T cells can synergistically enhance therapeutic outcomes.

Recent studies have demonstrated that CAR-T cells can effectively deliver oncolytic herpes simplex virus (oHSV) to tumor lesions. This approach enhances T cell infiltration, promotes intratumoral viral replication, suppresses tumor growth, and significantly prolongs survival in mouse models [[91], [92], [93]]. In addition, OVs can be passively adsorbed onto the surface of T cells without entering the cells. This allows the virus to be transported without compromising T cell immune function. At the same time, surface-bound viruses are shielded from immune recognition and neutralization, thereby enhancing delivery efficiency [94]. This combined strategy has shown promise in both hematologic and solid tumor models. For example, CD8+ central memory T cells were successfully used to load vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) and deliver it to tumor sites, demonstrating potent therapeutic effects in a NSG mouse model of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [95]. Furthermore, CAR-T cells maintained their biological function for at least 3 days following HSV-1 exposure and effectively mediated viral delivery in a glioblastoma model [93]. These findings highlight the synergistic potential of combining the innate cytotoxic function of T cells with the oncolytic effects of viruses, positioning T cells as one of the most promising cell-based delivery platforms for OVs.

However, T cells are not ideal hosts for OVs, as OVs replicate poorly within them, and some viruses (such as NDV) cannot replicate in T lymphocytes. In studies involving intravenous injection of oncolytic measles virus-infected T cells, only 1-2% of T cells were able to migrate to the tumor site [96], limiting the overall efficacy of OVs. Additionally, various mechanisms within the TME of “cold” tumors inhibit T cell infiltration [97], meaning that these tumors may not benefit from T cell-based delivery. Finally, the activation status of T cells may influence virus release. Activated T cells not only migrate to the tumor but also enhance their secretion capabilities [98], which could result in premature virus release before reaching the tumor, leading to off-target effects.

Monocytes and macrophages

Monocytes and their differentiated progeny, macrophages, represent another promising class of cellular carriers for OVs, owing to their intrinsic migratory capacity and tolerance within the TME. Monocytes constitute approximately 10% of circulating white blood cells and can be readily obtained from patients’ peripheral blood, making them an accessible delivery vehicle [99]. Importantly, monocytes serve as precursors of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) in various cancers and can be actively recruited into tumor parenchyma via distinct chemokine axes without inducing resistance typically seen in adoptive monocyte therapy. Reale et al. demonstrated that primary human monocytes loaded with oHSV-1 could migrate toward multiple tumor cell types and successfully home to head and neck tumors in a chorioallantoic membrane model [100].

Furthermore, the hypoxic and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, which restricts the infiltration and viability of many cell types, is readily tolerated by monocytes and macrophages. These cells can survive and support OVs replication under such conditions, thereby facilitating viral dissemination within tumors. For example, Muthana et al. engineered monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs) armed with OVs; upon reaching severely hypoxic regions, these cells activated adenoviral replication and exhibited selective cytotoxicity in tumor tissues only [101]. Additionally, certain OVs—such as VSV—can reprogram macrophages toward an M1 phenotype, enhancing their antigen-presenting capacity and further bolstering antitumor immune activation [102].

Notably, immortalized macrophage cell lines fail to accurately replicate the biological characteristics of bona fide TAMs and lack the capacity to infiltrate pancreatic tumor tissue [103], underscoring the necessity of using primary myeloid cells. Additionally, many myeloid cells possess immunosuppressive properties, which may counteract the therapeutic effects of OVs. Furthermore, macrophages themselves can engulf viruses and induce their degradation or interfere with viral replication, which may lead to premature inactivation or clearance of OVs and a consequent reduction in overall treatment efficacy [102,104].

Dendritic cells

Dendritic cells (DCs) possess potent antigen-presenting capabilities and have demonstrated the ability to deliver OVs even in the presence of neutralizing antibodies. Unlike T cells, which typically carry viruses via surface adsorption, DCs can internalize viral particles, thereby shielding them from immune detection and antibody-mediated neutralization [105,106]. Ilett et al. reported that mature DCs loaded with OVs elicited robust antitumor immune responses and achieved durable tumor clearance [102,106], highlighting their dual role as both antigen carriers and viral delivery vehicles. These properties make DCs a highly attractive platform for combination immunovirotherapy. The efficacy of DCs as carriers for OVs is closely related to their maturation status. When mature DCs (mDCs) are used to deliver recombinant reovirus, they are capable of completely eliminating metastatic tumors, whereas immature DCs (iDCs) can only partially clear early-stage tumors, demonstrating a weaker therapeutic potency [106].

Similar to macrophages and monocytes, DCs, due to their potent antigen-presenting capability, face the risk of prematurely recognizing and clearing OVs when used as carriers, thereby limiting viral dissemination in the body. This inherent antiviral response may overshadow or attenuate the antitumor immune effects. In addition, immature DCs often exhibit immunosuppressive properties and require strong activation signals to exert effective antitumor activity [107]. More importantly, DCs are highly susceptible to the influence of cytokines and metabolic factors within the TME, which can lead to their functional inactivation or conversion into tolerogenic regulatory DCs, ultimately impairing their efficacy in delivering OVs [108].

Mast cells

Mast cells (MCs) are key immune cells involved in allergic reactions, capable of secreting a variety of cytokines, chemokines, and immunoglobulins. They also possess a natural tendency to migrate, and their presence in the TME is substantial, playing an important role in tumor development [109,110]. Recent studies have demonstrated that by employing tumor-associated antigens as “allergens”, MCs can be driven to actively accumulate at tumor sites in vivo, triggering a “tumor allergic reaction” that leads to the recognition and immune activation of tumor tissue. Furthermore, sensitized mast cells have been utilized as carriers for OVs, allowing for the precise delivery of OVs to tumor sites. In multiple mouse models, mast cell-mediated OV delivery resulted in a strong anti-tumor immune response [111].

However, evidence also suggests that MCs may play a dual role in tumor initiation and progression. Studies have shown that MCs are involved in the development of tumors [110]. Specifically, Sijin Cheng et al. found that TNF+ MCs exert anti-tumor effects, while VEGFA+ MCs may promote tumor growth [112]. This highlights the necessity for careful selection of MCs as carriers for OVs. Additionally, within the TME, MCs release various angiogenic factors such as VEGF and FGF2, which provide vascular support for tumor growth [113]. Therefore, although mast cells have shown significant potential as OV carriers in preclinical models, their complex role in the tumor microenvironment requires further investigation to ensure their safety and efficacy in clinical applications.

Neutrophils

Neutrophils are the most abundant white blood cells in the body, and are responsible for innate immune responses, possessing capabilities for rapid chemotaxis, phagocytosis of pathogens, and release of inflammatory mediators. Current research divides neutrophils in the TME into N1 and N2 types. N1 neutrophils release pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and are considered an anti-tumor phenotype, while N2 neutrophils promote tumor formation, suppress T cell function, and are associated with tumor progression, often considered an immune-suppressive phenotype [114]. Jessica et al. observed that systemic injection of OVs significantly increased the number of neutrophils in the TME and directly eliminated tumor cells through TNF-α release, while assisting the spread of OVs [115]. Later, Xu et al. used bioorthogonal reactions to deliver OVs via neutrophils, significantly increasing OV accumulation in tumors, alleviating CD8+ T cell exhaustion, and enhancing anti-tumor effects [116].

Neutrophils enhance the efficacy of OVs through the secretion of various inflammatory cytokines. However, this process also involves neutrophil activation and degranulation, which may compromise the stability of the therapeutic effect [117]. Additionally, artificial intervention or improper modification may trigger potential immune rejection responses. Finally, challenges in cell collection, preservation, and large-scale production remain major bottlenecks for clinical translation.

Red blood cells

Red blood cells (RBCs) are the most abundant cell type in the blood and can be easily obtained from peripheral blood. Their small volume, large surface area, and lack of nucleus and organelles make both their internal cavity and membrane surface available for loading OVs [118]. Additionally, mature RBCs do not express major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules but do express CD47, which binds to the SIRPα receptor on macrophages to deliver a “don't eat me” signal, effectively preventing phagocytosis and conferring high immune tolerance and biocompatibility [119]. RBCs can be loaded with therapeutic agents either internally or on their surface and are increasingly recognized as a novel drug delivery platform. Liu et al. developed an RBC–based OV therapy strategy called EleOVt, in which OVs were electrostatically assembled onto the RBC surface with nearly 100% efficiency. The CD47 signal on RBCs helped the viruses evade immune clearance and prolonged their circulation time [120]. Compared to naked viruses, RBC-loaded OVs significantly reduced virus-associated hepatotoxicity. During passage through pulmonary capillaries, RBCs experience shear stress that triggers the release of the carried viruses, thereby enhancing the targeted delivery of OVs to lung metastases [120]. Additionally, the shear stress generated by high-intensity focused ultrasound can induce RBC carriers to release OVs within a short period, enabling precise delivery and on-demand release [121].

Currently, research on using RBCs to deliver OVs remains limited. RBCs lack tumor tropism and are unable to actively migrate toward tumor sites. Abnormal vascular architecture and high interstitial pressure can hinder RBC infiltration into the tumor core. Moreover, RBCs lack phagocytic and endocytic capabilities, making the controlled release of OVs at the target site a significant technical challenge [122,123]. Unlike macrophages or T cells, RBCs do not possess the ability to synergize with OVs in enhancing antitumor immunity. As a result, achieving comparable therapeutic efficacy may require higher viral loading, thereby increasing the risk of dose-dependent toxic side effects [124]. Finally, the process of loading OVs onto RBCs may alter their membrane structure and deformability, potentially impairing their ability to circulate through the microvasculature and compromising their in vivo stability [125].

Tumor cells

Although controversial, some studies have explored the use of inactivated tumor cells as carriers for OVs. Tumor cells possess inherent “homing” capabilities, enabling them to return to tumor sites via self-recognition mechanisms. This phenomenon, termed “homotypic tropism,” has been utilized to achieve targeted delivery of OVs back into tumor tissues [126]. Raykov et al. validated the concept of tumor cells as OV carriers, highlighting their organotropism and ability to respond to tumor microenvironmental cues—particularly in delivering OVs to pulmonary metastases [127]. Moreover, tumor cells are naturally permissive to many OVs and can efficiently support viral replication and release. For instance, H-1 parvovirus–infected Morris hepatoma cells, after γ-irradiation inactivation, lost proliferative capacity but retained the ability to produce and release active virus. These inactivated carrier cells were capable of surviving for up to six days in the lungs and continued releasing virus throughout this period [128].

In addition to delivering progeny virus directly to tumors for lysis, these tumor cell–based carriers may act as TAAs themselves, thereby inducing specific antitumor immune responses and functioning as therapeutic cancer vaccines [127]. Using patient-derived tumor cells for OV loading also supports personalized, tumor-specific therapeutic strategies [129].

However, this approach remains in the early exploratory stage. Compared to immune cells, tumor cells are generally larger in size and tend to become trapped in the pulmonary capillary beds following intravenous injection [130], which favors lung-targeted applications but limits homing to non-pulmonary tumors. In addition, when tumor cells are used as carriers, treatments such as irradiation or cryo-shock should be applied to eliminate their proliferative capacity and prevent the risk of new metastatic growth, while simultaneously preserving the cells' structural integrity and their inherent tropism toward tumor sites at the same time [131,132]. What's more, technical complexity, uncertain long-term safety, difficulties in standardization, and potential clinical immunogenic risks pose substantial challenges to clinical translation [129].

Cell-based carriers offer an innovative strategy for the delivery of OVs, combining advantages such as immune shielding, enhanced tumor targeting, and functional synergy. Various cell types have demonstrated potential feasibility and achieved promising outcomes in preclinical studies. However, these approaches still face several common technical and biological challenges, including low viral loading efficiency, difficulties in precisely controlling the biological behavior of the carriers, operational complexity, and the risk of allogeneic immune rejection. Moreover, large-scale manufacturing and quality control of cell-based carriers remain major obstacles, which currently limit their broader clinical translation and application [129].

Nanomaterial-based vectors

In recent years, owing to the excellent biocompatibility, controlled release properties, and tumor-targeting potential, artificially constructed nanocarrier systems have become one of the promising platforms for OV delivery. Encapsulation or surface modification of OVs using nanomaterials can partially shield viral epitopes, reducing immune recognition and clearance, thus enhancing viral stability and circulation half-life [133]. Nanoparticles also exhibit strong tumor retention due to enhanced permeability and retention effects in solid tumors [134]. Surface modification (e.g., charge, hydrophilicity, particle size) can optimize tumor targeting and penetration [135]. Moreover, stimuli-responsive nanomaterials enable controlled OV release in the tumor microenvironment, boosting viral infectivity and replication efficiency [136,137].

Nanomaterial-Based Vectors not only accommodate OVs but can also co-deliver chemotherapeutic agents, immunomodulators, or fluorescent imaging probes, enabling real-time virus tracking, controlled release, and combinatorial therapies [138]. Such multifunctional platforms offer a new perspective on the clinical translation of OVs through tailored, context-specific therapeutic strategies. A diverse range of nanomaterials has been employed in the construction of OV delivery platforms, which can be broadly categorized into inorganic, organic, and biologically derived materials.

Inorganic nanoparticles

Inorganic nanocarriers include iron-based nanoparticles, manganese-based nanoparticles, and carbon-based materials, mesoporous silica, and gold nanostructures (e.g., nanoshells/rods), among others. These materials often possess intrinsic magnetic or photothermal properties, enabling synergistic applications with photothermal or hyperthermia-based therapies [139,140].

Extensive in vitro and in vivo studies have validated the superior performance of inorganic nanomaterials in OV delivery. For instance, Li et al. developed a bimetallic copper–manganese ion-encapsulated adenovirus system (OA@CuMnCs), which significantly enhanced viral stability and triggered immune pathway activation. Copper ions suppressed PD-L1 expression, mitigating local immunosuppression, while manganese ions reacted with intracellular hydrogen peroxide to generate oxygen, alleviating tumor hypoxia and promoting viral replication. These synergistic effects enhanced DC maturation and antitumor immune responses, providing a promising immunotherapeutic strategy for colorectal cancer, particularly for metastatic disease [141]. Furthermore, the released Mn2+ enhances the T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) signal, enabling precise delineation of tumor boundaries through T1-mode MRI. In addition, the increased O2 levels improve photoacoustic imaging (PAI) performance, endowing the system with bimodal imaging capability and providing real-time monitoring information for therapy [142,143]. Carbon nanotubes also have emerged as promising inorganic nanocarriers for platinum-based drug delivery due to their high drug-loading capacity and versatile surface functionalization, although their long-term biocompatibility and potential toxicity remain under active investigation [144].

In addition to serving as carriers for viral delivery, certain nanoparticles possess intrinsic photothermal, photosensitizing, or magnetothermal properties, which can be leveraged to enhance therapeutic efficacy through combination with photodynamic therapy (PDT) or gene therapy. Iron-based magnetic nanoparticles, for instance, can be directed to tumor sites under an external magnetic field, enabling spatially controlled viral delivery. Photosensitive nanoparticles can generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) upon near-infrared (NIR) irradiation, thereby promoting tumor cell apoptosis and facilitating viral release [[145], [146], [147]]. These combinatorial strategies effectively extend OV therapy into a multimodal, stimuli-responsive “multiplatform-based” approach.

Organic nanoparticles

Organic nanomaterials primarily encompass hydrogel-related polymer nanoparticles, PEG-related polymer nanoparticles, and chitosan-based polymeric carriers. These systems can be fabricated through techniques such as emulsification, nanoprecipitation, and microfluidics, and are characterized by high drug-loading capacity and favorable pharmacokinetic profiles [148].

Badrinath et al. employed PLGA nanofibers to encapsulate vaccinia virus, achieving effective intratumoral accumulation and cytotoxicity in a colorectal cancer model, which led to increased apoptosis and reduced tumor burden [149]. In another example, coupling PEG-modified oligopeptide-modified poly (β-amino ester) (OM-pBAEs) with adenovirus significantly enhances the immune evasion ability and oncolytic activity of adenovirus in pancreatic cancer models [150]. Deng et al. introduced a redox-responsive nanohydrogel delivery platform that allows tumor-specific release of encapsulated viruses. This system maintained viral integrity and activity under reductive conditions and demonstrated potent oncolytic effects [151]. Encapsulated oncolytic NDV in polymeric thiolated chitosan nanoparticles and surface decoration with hyaluronic acid (HA) not only enable active targeting of CD44-overexpressing cervical cancer cells, but also allow sustained release of the virus in the TME, thereby enhancing the bioavailability of the virus [152].

Biologically derived nanoparticles

Biologically derived nanocarriers, including cell membranes, lipid nanoparticles, and extracellular vesicles, offer innate advantages such as low immunogenicity and enhanced tumor-targeting capability [153], are gradually emerging as a strong candidate carrier for OV delivery.

Cell membranes

A cell membrane–coated delivery system derived from NSCs and glioblastoma (GBM) cells not only enabled tumor-specific infection and immune evasion but also exhibited a remarkable ability to traverse the blood–brain barrier and inhibit tumor growth [154]. Chen et al. developed an oncolytic virus–T cell chimera (ONCOTECH) delivery system designed to enhance the tumor-targeting specificity and therapeutic safety of OVs. In this system, B16-OVA tumor cell membranes were used to cloak an oncolytic adenovirus, forming a membrane-enveloped virus (M@eOA). The membrane was further engineered to express MHC-I–OVA complexes that specifically bind to OT-1 CD8+ T cells, thereby anchoring the virus to form a T cell–virus chimera (T-M@eOA). Experimental results demonstrated that T-M@eOA exhibited efficient homing to B16-OVA tumor cells and displayed potent antitumor activity in both in vitro and in vivo models. Moreover, no off-target genome editing events were detected, indicating high tumor specificity and safety [155]. In another study, cloaking oVV with RBC membranes accelerated its infection of cancer cells and conferred responsiveness to acidic microenvironments, potentially improving its adaptability and therapeutic efficacy within tumor tissues [156].

Lipid nanoparticles

Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), which possess a phospholipid bilayer structure similar to the cell membrane, can enable efficient drug delivery by mimicking natural membrane fusion or endocytosis mechanisms [138]. Research into their application as carriers for OVs dates back to the 1990s, with studies demonstrating that LNPs can encapsulate OVs and mediate in vivo delivery via endocytosis. Katsuyuki et al. developed a liposome-based delivery system encapsulating oAd plasmid DNA (Lipo-pTS), which exhibited significant antitumor effects in the HCT116 colorectal cancer model. Notably, Lipo-pTS maintained potent cytotoxicity even in the presence of anti-adenovirus neutralizing antibodies (AdNAbs), indicating favorable circulatory stability and resistance to immune clearance, thereby highlighting its potential as a platform for systemic administration [157]. In addition to delivering viral genomes, LNPs can also carry intact viral particles. Iscaro et al. employed CCL2-coated liposomes to encapsulate oncolytic adenoviruses, enabling preferential uptake by circulating monocytes expressing CCR2. Leveraging the natural homing capacity of these cells, the system successfully delivered viruses to tumor tissues, thereby reducing the required intravenous dose and enhancing antitumor efficacy [158]. In this regard, emerging strategies such as mitochondria-targeted liposomes exemplify a promising direction for next-generation nanocarrier design—shifting the goal from tumor-level to organelle-level precision—though their clinical translation remains at an early conceptual stage [159].

Extracellular vesicles

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are membrane-bound nanoparticles secreted by various cell types, characterized by immune tolerance, stability in the circulatory system, and the ability to traverse biological barriers to reach distant organs such as the brain [160,161]. They can carry a variety of bioactive cargos, including nucleic acids, proteins, and viral particles [162], making them highly promising for OV delivery. EVs can be broadly classified into natural EVs and engineered EVs. Regarding natural EVs, Magurine et al. demonstrated that cells infected with adeno-associated virus (AAV) can release EVs containing AAV particles [163]. Sarri et al. further found that adenovirus-infected tumor cells can secrete EVs containing viral DNA and proteins but lacking complete capsid structures; these EVs remain infectious and can evade clearance by neutralizing antibodies in the immune system [164]. Feng et al. utilized a capsid dual-modified oncolytic adenovirus (CRAd) to infect NSCs, producing “oncolytic extracellular vesicles” (CRAdEVs) with enhanced tumor-targeting infection and cytotoxicity [165]. Additionally, EVs can enable the combined delivery of OVs and chemotherapeutic agents, such as paclitaxel, significantly improving antitumor efficacy in murine models [166]. Bacterial outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) have attracted increasing attention in recent years. OMVs can not only serve as carriers but also retain the antigenicity of the donor bacteria, thereby eliciting immune responses in host cells [167].

However, natural EVs suffer from limitations, including low viral loading efficiency and poor controllability, which hinder clinical translation. Engineered EVs, generated through genetic modification or chemical functionalization, can overcome these challenges by improving loading efficiency, enhancing targeting specificity, and boosting therapeutic outcomes. For example, Lv et al. developed an oncolytic adenovirus delivery platform (OA@BCMNs) that employs in vivo and in vitro genetic membrane engineering to display targeting ligands on bioengineered nanovesicles, achieving efficient and specific OV delivery with promising therapeutic benefits [168]. In another example, engineered E. coli OMVs “hitchhike” on neutrophils and leverage the postoperative local inflammatory chemotactic gradient to home to the tumor resection site. At the lesion, the OMVs release anti-CD47 antibodies to block the “don't eat me” signal, promote TAM polarization toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, and induce the release of more reactive oxygen species, thereby triggering multiple anti-tumor immune responses and effectively suppressing postoperative tumor recurrence and metastasis. Despite the many advantages of nanoparticle-mediated OV delivery, most current work remains at the preclinical stage and faces several limitations that hinder clinical translation. First, nanoparticles may form a “protein corona” in circulation or be sequestered by the mononuclear phagocyte system [138,170], compromising both delivery efficiency and viral activity. Second, the long-term biocompatibility of certain nanomaterials—particularly heavy metal-based oxides—remains unclear, and toxicity profiles observed in animal models may not fully predict human responses. Third, many delivery platforms still suffer from structural complexity and inconsistent release profiles. The fabrication of such nanocarriers typically involves multiple chemical or physical steps, often lacking standardized protocols, Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) -grade materials, and robust quality control measures, all of which severely constrain scalability and clinical translation [146,171].

Beyond these scientific challenges, the increasing complexity of nanomaterial-based delivery systems raises distinct ethical considerations. First, the sophisticated manufacturing processes required for nanocarrier production introduce risks of batch-to-batch variability and undetected impurities, which, if not rigorously controlled, could compromise patient safety in clinical translation. Second, the prolonged tissue retention and unknown environmental fate of certain nanomaterials pose potential long-term risks to subjects and the environment, necessitating more comprehensive risk-benefit assessments and informed consent protocols. Third, the high cost and technical barriers to GMP-compliant nanocarrier production may exacerbate disparities in access to advanced virotherapies, raising ethical concerns regarding justice. We therefore advocate that ethical risk assessment be integrated early in the development pipeline, alongside technical optimization, to guide responsible clinical translation of nanocarrier-based oncolytic virotherapy.

In summary, naked virus, cell-based carrier, and nanomaterial carrier delivery strategies each possess distinct advantages and limitations with respect to tumor specificity, immune compatibility, translational potential, and regulatory feasibility. Oncolytic virus delivery optimization methods

Although current OV delivery methods have shown potential in several clinical trials, challenges such as low viral delivery efficiency, immune evasion, and insufficient tumor specificity still remain. Selecting an appropriate OVs and modifying it, elucidating immune evasion mechanisms, and adopting multimodal combination therapies can further improve the delivery efficiency and therapeutic outcomes of OVs.

Choosing the appropriate virus

The type of virus directly affects its spreading ability, infection efficiency, and immune response intensity within the TME. Based on the virus genome type, OVs can be broadly categorized into DNA viruses and RNA viruses (Table 6), each with distinct advantages. DNA viruses (such as adenoviruses, herpes simplex viruses, and vaccinia viruses) typically have larger genome capacities and higher genetic stability, making them more suitable for viral vector modification and long-term therapeutic applications [172]. In contrast, RNA viruses (such as enteroviruses, measles viruses, and coxsackieviruses) exhibit rapid replication and potent immune-stimulating effects, enabling them to kill tumor cells more quickly. However, RNA viruses generally have poorer tumor specificity, are more prone to mutations, and can be cleared by the immune system. Virus vector modification and enhancement

Virus vector modification is a critical component in optimizing OV delivery systems. Genetic engineering of virus vectors can significantly improve their targeting specificity, stability, and immune evasion capabilities within tumor tissues. By introducing tumor-specific ligands (such as antibodies or tumor-associated antigens) on the virus surface, the virus's ability to specifically recognize and infect tumor cells can be enhanced. For example, integrating single-chain antibodies (scFv) onto the surface of oHSV enables targeted infection of EGFR-expressing tumor cells [174]. The use of specific ligands not only increases the affinity of the virus for tumor cells but also reduces toxicity to normal tissues, thereby improving the safety of the treatment. Moreover, the DNA damage response (DDR) can directly influence OV replication [175], and tumor-specific DDR alterations may therefore inform vector design. For instance, cancer-associated mutations or post-translational modifications of poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase 2 (PARP2) can affect its catalytic activity [176], potentially leading to differential OV replication efficiency across tumors. A deeper understanding of such tumor-specific DDR alterations may guide the engineering of OVs with selective replication capacity. Additionally, immune evasion can be further enhanced by encoding immune-modulating factors within the virus vector. This strategy can extend the virus's retention in the TME and improve therapeutic outcomes. For instance, oncolytic vaccinia virus carrying PD-L1 and GM-CSF can systemically suppress PD-L1-mediated immune suppression and activate tumor-specific T cell responses [177]. Furthermore, the development of an artificial protein corona on the virus surface has been proposed as an effective strategy for enhancing delivery. By constructing an artificial protein layer on the virus, the immune recognition in the bloodstream can be weakened, leading to a significant increase in the virus's circulation time and delivery efficiency, especially with intravenous injections [178].

Multimodal combination therapy

Monotherapy with OVs often has limited efficacy in clinical applications; therefore, multimodal combination therapy has become a key strategy to improve the therapeutic effects of OVs. Clinical and preclinical evidence suggests that combining OVs with immune checkpoint inhibitors can significantly improve objective response rates and enhance the immune activity within the TME [[179], [180], [181]]. Combining CAR-T cell therapy or mRNA vaccine with OVs can enhance T cell infiltration and function, thus increasing the success rate of immunotherapy for solid tumors [182]. Additionally, combining OVs with radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and other traditional therapies has demonstrated synergistic effects, particularly in promoting immune responses and disrupting tumor barriers. Several clinical trials are currently exploring the combination of OVs with traditional treatments.

The interplay between OVs and the host DDR can also generate therapeutic synergies. Certain OVs, such as adenoviruses, can directly inhibit DDR [183], thereby sensitizing tumor cells to radiotherapy. Conversely, DDR inhibitors can amplify OV-induced DNA damage: the ATR inhibitor combined with the HSV-tk-encoding adenoviral vector CAN-2409 significantly increased γ-H2AX expression and DNA double-strand breaks, and prolonged survival in a glioblastoma model [184]; inhibitors of DNA-dependent protein kinase (DNA-PK) or ataxia telangiectasia mutated (ATM) combined with coxsackievirus have also demonstrated synergistic effects in non-small cell lung cancer models [185].

Pretreatment with cytokines has been shown to improve delivery efficiency in murine tumor models [186], and normalization of tumor vasculature has facilitated more effective OVs penetration and immune cell infiltration into the tumor core [187,188]. Kottke et al. demonstrated that depletion of regulatory T cells (Tregs) combined with IL-2 administration enhanced intratumoral viral distribution, suggesting that reshaping the immune environment may promote more effective delivery [189]. However, broad Treg depletion can carry the risk of triggering systemic inflammation, emphasizing the need for targeted and balanced approaches.

Additionally, nanocarriers can simultaneously encapsulate OVs and therapeutic agents, such as small molecule inhibitors, and deliver them to the tumor site. For example, some studies have used hydrogel carriers to co-encapsulate OVs and anti-CAF drugs, employing a differential release strategy to achieve efficient therapeutic effects in tumors enriched with stromal components [190].

Limitations of current clinical trials

Delivery failure vs. tumor resistance

In completed clinical trials of oncolytic virotherapy, many have been discontinued due to a lack of objective responses. However, the published literature often fails to clearly elucidate the mechanistic basis for this insufficient efficacy. A critical distinction must be drawn between delivery failure—where the therapeutic virus cannot reach the target tumor bed in sufficient titers—and oncolytic resistance—where the virus successfully reaches the tumor but fails to produce significant antitumor effects due to intrinsic or adaptive resistance mechanisms within the TME. To clarify this distinction, we systematically reviewed representative clinical trials and categorized them according to the strength of evidence for intratumoral viral delivery: Level I evidence is defined by post-treatment tumor biopsies confirming the presence of viral proteins or nucleic acids via immunohistochemistry, in situ hybridization, or qPCR; Level II evidence is inferred from local tumor responses (e.g., injection-site necrosis) without molecular confirmation; Level III indicates that no post-treatment biopsies were included in the trial protocol, leaving the delivery status unknown. Our analysis reveals that even in trials with Level I evidence (e.g., TILT-123, Enadenotucirev), complete response rates remained limited (Table 7). This suggests that the primary hurdle may shift toward immune editing or antiviral interferon responses within the TME. Importantly, not all subjects had detectable viral delivery even within these positive-evidence trials—indicating that delivery failure persisted in a subset of patients and highlighting the continued need to optimize delivery strategies. Long-term safety and commercialization considerations

While the preceding sections have provided a detailed account of the safety profiles of various OV delivery strategies in clinical trials, several longer-term challenges inherent to these replication-competent biological agents warrant dedicated discussion.

First, viral shedding is a central concern in the regulatory evaluation of OVs. As replication-competent agents, OVs can be shed from treated subjects via excreta, saliva, or skin lesions, posing a potential risk to close contacts and the environment. However, systematic shedding monitoring remains inadequately implemented in published clinical trials—a published analysis of clinical trial registries found that only 42.4% of trials reported viral shedding data, while 57.6% provided no explicit information on shedding [199]. Shedding patterns also vary substantially across virus platforms: shedding has been observed in subjects treated with naturally replication-competent coxsackievirus and SVV, whereas no shedding was detected with the recombinant attenuated chimeric poliovirus PVS-RIPO [[199], [200], [201]]. Even among oncolytic vectors derived from HSV, shedding profiles differ markedly—viral genome was detected in shedding samples from subjects treated with T-VEC and OrienX010, whereas no evidence of shedding was observed for OH2, G47Δ, HF10, or HSV176 [199,202]. We therefore recommend that standardized shedding monitoring protocols be incorporated into early-phase clinical trials to support environmental risk assessment.

Second, viral persistence may carry risks of delayed toxicity or genomic integration. Particularly for certain retroviral or DNA virus platforms, the long-term retention of viral genomes in host cells raises theoretical concerns of insertional mutagenesis, as highlighted by adverse events caused by enhancer-mediated proto-oncogene activation in early gene therapy trials [203]. This underscores the need for controllable replication systems, such as molecular switches, to further improve the safety profile of oncolytic virotherapy.

Third, the robustness of GMP manufacturing processes is critical for the successful commercialization of oncolytic virus products. Beyond patient-specific risks, weaknesses in GMP processes can lead to batch-to-batch inconsistency, residual impurities, or loss of viral potency, all of which pose long-term safety and supply risks for clinical application. Specifically, the structural complexity of virus particles demands sophisticated purification strategies, including the effective separation of infectious particles from non-infectious empty capsids and the removal of residual host cell proteins and nucleic acids [204,205]. Ensuring batch-to-batch consistency at scale while maintaining product quality and potency remains a key Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls (CMC) bottleneck for the field.

Conclusion and outlook

As a therapeutic modality that combines direct oncolysis with robust immune activation, OVs hold great promise in the treatment of cancer. However, the delivery of OVs remains a significant challenge due to various immunological barriers and the complex TME. The therapeutic outcome of OVs administration often reflects a dynamic balance between antiviral and antitumor immunity. Maximizing the antitumor efficacy of OVs while maintaining safety requires not only sophisticated genetic engineering to enhance tumor selectivity, but also optimized delivery strategies that play a crucial role in determining therapeutic success.

This review has summarized the current major OV delivery approaches, including naked virus delivery and vector-based delivery. Each method has its own strengths in modulating antitumor immunity, yet all face common challenges such as inconsistent delivery efficiency and clearance by the host immune system. Furthermore, the absence of standardized manufacturing protocols, GMP-compliant preparation systems, and unified evaluation and regulatory frameworks [10,24] remains a major bottleneck limiting clinical translation.

It is worth noting that a phenomenon of “pseudoprogression"—characterized by transient tumor enlargement followed by rapid regression—has been observed in multiple murine models and clinical studies following OV administration [206]. For example, in a clinical trial of Onyx-015, eight patients exhibited a temporary increase in tumor volume by 10–48% post-injection, which was subsequently followed by extensive tumor necrosis and regression [35]. However, according to RECIST criteria, these changes were misclassified as progressive disease (PD), leading to premature discontinuation of therapy. Similar observations have been reported in preclinical murine models [206]. These findings suggest that conventional RECIST criteria may underestimate the delayed therapeutic benefits mediated by OV-induced immune responses. Thus, future clinical assessment should incorporate immune-related response criteria (iRECIST), functional imaging modalities, biomarker-based evaluations, and multi-timepoint dynamic monitoring, among others, to more accurately capture treatment efficacy. Furthermore, as noted above, the majority of clinical trials have not distinguished whether failure results from inadequate delivery efficiency or intrinsic tumor cell resistance. Trial endpoints are typically designed to focus solely on objective response rate or progression-free survival. This “one-size-fits-all” approach to defining failure may lead to two distinct problems. First, it risks prematurely terminating promising candidates that could have been rescued by optimizing delivery strategies. Second, for agents that have already overcome the delivery bottleneck, the lack of mechanistic distinction deprives them of a clear rationale to guide combination with immunomodulatory therapies. We therefore recommend that, wherever clinically feasible, post-treatment tumor biopsies be incorporated into early-phase trial protocols—particularly those involving intravenous administration—to more clearly delineate the causes of treatment failure.

Intravenous administration is expected to become an important trend for delivering OVs, with cell membrane–coated delivery strategies attracting attention due to their inherent immune camouflage and targeting potential. Notably, certain viruses, such as VV, are naturally enveloped and can produce multiple types of viral particles, including intracellular mature virus (IMV), intracellular enveloped virus (IEV), cell-associated enveloped virus (CEV), and extracellular enveloped virus (EEV) [207]. Among these, EEV plays a pivotal role in long-distance spread within the host by acquiring components of the host cell membrane to achieve immune evasion, thereby effectively avoiding clearance by antibodies and complement. However, EEVs account for only ∼1% of progeny virions [208]. Studies have shown that genetic modifications can increase the production and release of EEVs; for example, point mutations in A34R significantly enhance EEV yield [209,210], while mutations in B5R and A33R have also been reported to promote EEV release [211]. In contrast, IMV is the most abundant particle type, enclosed by a single membrane, and is massively released upon host cell lysis but is more susceptible to neutralization by antibodies and complement. By integrating human CD55 protein into the IMV membrane, complement-mediated lysis and antibody neutralization are effectively prevented, thereby prolonging the virus's survival time [212]. In the future, strategies to enhance EEV generation and release, while modifying IMV to reduce its immunogenicity, may be key to improving the systemic delivery efficiency and safety of VV.

Systemic intravenous delivery of OVs often leads to their accumulation in the lungs, which can be advantageous for treating primary or metastatic lung tumors. However, OV delivered via cell carriers or nanoparticle-based vectors are typically too large to effectively reach non-pulmonary tumors. Consequently, arterial administration has emerged as an important alternative. Introducing OVs directly into the arterial circulation supplying the tumor can increase local viral concentration, enhance tumor penetration, and bypass certain barriers encountered in the systemic circulation. Arterial infusion of OVs has been preliminarily explored in models of liver metastases and glioblastoma [25,213]. Although clear therapeutic efficacy has yet to be established, combining arterial delivery with cell or nanoparticle carriers represents a promising strategy to maximize intratumoral OV distribution and therapeutic outcomes, which remains difficult to achieve with conventional intravenous administration.

In conclusion, OVs in combination with advanced delivery systems are driving cancer immunotherapy into a new era of multifunctional, individualized, and precision treatment. The development of integrated platforms that combine therapy, imaging, and immune modulation, and immune modulation to improve the delivery efficiency, therapeutic efficacy, and safety of OVs, along with the exploration of effective combination strategies, enhanced regulatory oversight, and the establishment of standardized GMP-grade manufacturing systems, is expected to accelerate the bench-to-bedside translation of oncolytic virotherapy.
