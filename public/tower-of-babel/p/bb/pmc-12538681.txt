Mechanisms of ultrasound-synergized polyphenol enhancing the foaming properties of ovalbumin: Focus on interfacial behavior and microstructure
Authors: Lan Liu, Pei Zhang, Wenjie Yan, Yingmei Wu, Guofeng Jin, Hongbo Song, Fengping An, Yingqun Wu, Xin Li, Peng Luo, Qun Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Ovalbumin (OVA), as a key protein in egg white (accounting for approximately 54% of total egg white proteins and with an isoelectric point of 4.5), is a monomeric phosphoglycoprotein [1]. It is composed of 385 amino acids, among which over 50% are hydrophobic groups, and it also contains an appropriate proportion of hydrophilic groups. Therefore, it can interact during stirring process, rapidly adsorb at gas-liquid interface and form a viscoelastic interfacial film, promoting foam formation and stability [2]. The foaming properties of OVA are widely utilized in food processing applications, including baked goods, confectionery, dairy products, and meat products [3]. The ideal fluffy texture and structure can be endowed to the product through the formation and stability of foam. However, the foaming ability (FA) and foam stability (FS) of native OVA are limited by external environmental factors (such as pH value, temperature and ionic strength), so it is necessary to find novel approaches to improve the foaming properties of OVA.

Ultrasound, as a green and efficient physical processing technology, alters the structure of protein through cavitation and mechanical effects, thereby improving the functional properties of proteins [4]. Previous studies reported that egg white treated with ultrasound at 360 W had lower viscosity and surface tension, making them easier to adhere to the gas-liquid interface and undergo molecular rearrangement, with foaming properties 4.9 times higher than the control group [5]. Zhang et al. [6] found that depolymerization effect of ultrasound reduced the particle size of egg white protein, enhanced solubility and made the conformation more flexible. The results of ultrasound-assisted pH shift treatment of perilla protein isolate demonstrated that the particle size of protein aggregates was obviously reduced, while the FA and emulsifying properties were improved [7]. Tian et al. [8] reported the FS of egg white increased by 36.99% after combined treatment with ultrasound and flaxseed gum.

Polyphenolic compounds, which contain aromatic ring structures and abundant hydroxyl groups, have a strong affinity for proteins and have attracted much attention as green and safe crosslinking agents. Chen et al. [9] observed that polyphenols induced casein aggregation and structural changes, and FS was remarkably enhanced. Tian et al. [10] prepared conjugated substances of polyphenols (epigallocatechin gallate, procyanidine, apple polyphenol and puerari) and whey protein isolate by the alkali method. The results reflected that conjugates had more excellent foaming and emulsifying property. Sun et al. [11] coupled polyphenols (caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid, epigallocatechin gallate, catechin, and quercetin) with egg white protein using the ultrasound-assisted radical method. It was found that ultrasound accelerated the interaction between proteins and polyphenols by enhancing the generation of hydroxyl radicals. The antioxidant activity and emulsifying property of proteins after coupling were significantly enhanced, and the in vitro digestion efficiency of proteins was also improved. Chang et al. [12] reported that ferulic acid modification of OVA reduced protein aggregation, altered protein structure and molecular flexibility, and enhanced foaming properties.

In conclusion, ultrasound has been widely utilized as an auxiliary method in various protein modifications. Polyphenols are considered as effective modified substances for improving the foaming property or thermal stability of proteins. However, the potential for ultrasound-synergized polyphenol treatment to further improve the foaming properties of OVA has not been fully elucidated and warrants further investigation. Therefore, this study focuses on OVA and investigates the effects of ultrasound-synergized polyphenol treatment on its foaming properties. From the perspectives of protein particle size, surface tension, microstructure and polyphenol-protein interactions, the mechanism of improved foaming properties was fully revealed. The aim to enhance the foaming property of OVA and provide valuable references and data support for improving its application in the food industry.

Materials and methods

Materials

OVA (BR, 80%), protocatechuic aldehyde (PA) (purity≥98%) and syringic acid (SA) (purity≥98%) were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Folin phenol, Nile blue A and potassium bromide (analytical grade) were all analytical grade and obtained from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of samples

The OVA solution (0.5%, w/v) was subjected to ultrasonic treatment at 120 W for 5 min using a probe ultrasonicator (VOSHIN-1500C, Woxin Instruments Co., Ltd., Wuxi, China). The ultrasound conditions are obtained from the preliminary test results. Then the samples were cooled in an ice water bath and designated as ultrasound-treated OVA (UOVA). Various amounts of PA or SA were weighed and added to the OVA solution to achieve polyphenol mass ratios of 0.05%, 0.1%, and 0.15%, respectively. The concentration of polyphenols was also set based on the preliminary experimental results. These mixtures were subsequently subjected to the same ultrasonic treatment. The obtained samples were labeled as OVA treated with ultrasound-synergized PA (UPOVA-0.05, UPOVA-0.1, UPOVA-0.15) and ultrasound-synergized SA (USOVA-0.05, USOVA-0.1, USOVA-0.15), respectively.

Polyphenol binding capacity

Polyphenol binding was quantified using the Folin Ciocalteu method [13]. Briefly, UPOVA or USOVA was mixed with Folin Ciocalteu reagent at a ratio of 1:5 and reacted in the dark for 5 min. Then, Na2CO3 solution (15%, w/v) was added and reacted in the dark for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 760 nm using a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA). Quantification was performed based on standard calibration curves prepared with PA and SA.

SDS-PAGE

SDS-PAGE was performed following a previously described method [14] with modifications. A 5% stacking gel and 12% separating gel were prepared. Samples were mixed with loading buffer (3:1 ratio), heated at 100°C for 10 min, and centrifuged at 10,000×g for 10 min (H1650R, Xiangyi Instrument Co., Ltd., Changsha, China). The supernatants were loaded onto gels and subjected to electrophoresis at 80 V for 20 min, followed by 120 V for 1 h (JY600E, Junyi Dongfang Electrophoresis Co., Beijing, China). Finally, gels were stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250, destained with a decolorizing solution and imaged using a gel imager (Alliance 4.7, HuaYue Enterprise Holding Ltd., Guangzhou, China)[15].

Measurement of physicochemical properties

Rheological measurement

Apparent viscosity was measured using an HR20 rheometer (TA Instruments, USA) with a 40-mm parallel plate at 25°C. Shear rates ranged from 0.01 to 10 s-1 [16].

Particle size and ζ-potential

The concentration of OVA samples was adjusted to 0.1 mg/mL and balanced in the chamber of the Malvern laser particle size analyzer (ZS90, Malvern Instruments, Worcestershire, UK) to equilibrate for 120 s to determine the particle size, PDI and ζ-potential of the sample [17,18].

Solubility and surface hydrophobicity

The protein concentration of samples before and after centrifugation was determined by the BCA method, and solubility was the ratio after centrifugation to before centrifugation [19]. The surface hydrophobicity was determined by the bromophenol blue method [20]. A 200 μL solution of bromophenol blue (1 mg/mL) was thoroughly mixed with 1 mL of protein sample solution, centrifuged at 8,000×g for 15 min. The supernatant was diluted and measured for absorbance at 595 nm, denoted as A. Deionized water was used as the blank sample, and the absorbance was recorded as A1. The surface hydrophobicity of the sample was determined by the amount of bromophenol blue bound and calculated using formula (1).(1)Surfacehydrophobicity(μg)=200×A1-AA1

Thermal stability

The freeze-dried powder sample (approximately 6 mg) was evenly distributed in an aluminum crucible and compressed using a tablet press [21]. Subsequently, it was placed in a DSC (DSC250, TA Instrument-Waters Ltd., Delaware, USA) chamber and the heat flow curve changes within 20-120°C were measured at a rate of 10°C/min.

Foaming properties

FA and FS

The measurement method was referred to Hammershøj et al. [22] with slight modifications. Briefly, 40 mL of different samples were homogenized at 8,000 rpm for 2 min (T25, IKA Works GmbH & Co. KG, Staufen, Germany), and the foam volumes at 0 min and 30 min were observed and recorded in a 100 mL graduated cylinder. The formulas for calculating FA and FS were as follows:(2)FA=V0V×100%(3)FS=V30V0×100%

where V0 is the foam volume at 0 min, V is the volume of the liquid in initial stage, V30 is the volume of foam after standing for 30 min.

Microscopic observation

Based on the method proposed by Wu et al. [23] and with slight modifications. The microscopic morphology of foams in different samples was observed using an optical microscope (DMZL-431088, Xiamen Jingyi Xingye Technology Co., Ltd., Xiamen, China) with the magnification of objective lens being 5 times.

Surface tension

The change in surface tension at the gas-liquid interface was measured using a contact angle measuring instrument (OCA25, Dataphysics Instruments, Filderstadt, Germany) [24]. The sample was placed in a 1 mL syringe, and a 10 μL sample droplet was squeezed out of the syringe. The droplet was maintained at the gas-liquid interface for 700 s, and the changes in surface tension were measured.

Structural characterization

FT-IR

The freeze-dried powder of sample was mixed with KBr in a ratio of 1:100, thoroughly ground and then pressed into thin slices [25]. The thin slices were placed in a FT-IR spectrometer (VERTEX70, Bruker Instruments Co., Ltd., Saarbrucken, Germany) for spectral scanning. The scanning range was 4,000-400 cm-1 and the number of scans was 32 times.

Fluorescence spectroscopy

The fluorescence intensity of OVA samples with a concentration of 0.1 mg/mL was determined by a fluorescence spectrophotometer (RF-5301, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). Among them, the excitation wavelength is 280 nm, slit width is 5 nm, and fluorescence intensity within the wavelength of 300 to 500 nm was measured [26].

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was performed using Autodock Vina program (v18.3.23) on the YASARA v17.4.17 software. The three-dimensional structure of OVA was obtained from RCSB PDB protein database (PDB ID: 1UHG), while the three-dimensional structures of CA (CID: 8768) and SA (CID: 10742) were obtained from the PubChem database. Specifically, OVA was placed in a three-dimensional spatial grid box with a size of 119.3×119.3×119.3Å for multiple docking operations, and ultimately the optimal docking conformation of PA or SA with OVA was screened out. The Discovery Studio 4.5 Visualizer program was used to analyze the interaction forces between the receptor and ligand, generating two-dimensional and three-dimensional structural diagrams [27].

Observation by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM)

The 500 μL of sample with a protein concentration of 0.1 mg/mL was stained with 20 μL of Nile blue solution (0.1%, wt) in the dark for 30 min [28]. The microstructure of samples was observed by CLSM (Leica TCS SP8X DLS, Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).

Statistical analysis

Three batches of experimental samples were prepared and subjected to three parallel tests. The experimental results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Analysis of variance was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 27, with p < 0.05 indicating a statistically significant difference. Graphs were plotted using Origin 2023.

Results and discussion

Analysis of polyphenol binding capacity

The degree of protein-polyphenol binding was determined by quantifying the polyphenol content (Fig. 1A). The results reflected that binding capacity of polyphenols increased outstandingly with rising polyphenol concentration. Maximum binding values of 0.67 mg/g for UPOVA and 0.77 mg/g for USOVA were observed at a 0.15% polyphenol concentration. Notably, USOVA exhibited a higher polyphenol content than UPOVA under equivalent ultrasound conditions. Liu et al. [29] reported that polyphenols with higher molecular weights exhibit greater affinity and binding constants toward proteins. SA has a higher molecular weight than PA, and thus has a higher binding amount with OVA. Mao et al. [30] further highlighted the importance of polyphenol surface characteristics when investigating interactions between blackcurrant polyphenols and milk proteins. The hydrophobicity and surface charge of polyphenols might further affect their functional properties, possibly explaining why SA has a higher affinity for OVA. SDS-PAGE

Reducing SDS-PAGE was employed to evaluate the effects of interactions between polyphenols and OVA on protein molecular weight (Fig. 1B). The predominant band at 48 kDa corresponded to OVA with 80% purity. Comparison of samples before and after ultrasonic treatment revealed no changes in the OVA bands, indicating that ultrasound did not induce covalent cleavage or cross-linking of the proteins [31]. In contrast, the addition of PA and SA induced an upward band shift in the bands of UPOVA and USOVA, with a more pronounced shift observed in USOVA. This shift aligned with the amount of polyphenol bound and suggested a stronger affinity between SA and OVA. The interactions between polyphenols and proteins in this article were non-covalent. Therefore, band shifts observed here might be different from those reported for covalent grafting reactions between sugars or polyphenols and proteins in previous studies [23,32]. Additionally, the bands of UPOVA-0.15 and USOVA-0.15 appeared fainter, implying the aggregation of some macromolecules that were retained in the gel. This phenomenon might be attributed to the formation of complexes between polyphenols and proteins or protein cross-linking induced by polyphenols [32,33].

Analysis of physicochemical properties

Apparent viscosity

Apparent viscosity of proteins is jointly influenced by protein-water and protein-protein interactions. These interactions not only determine apparent properties of proteins but are also related to the processing and application of proteins [10]. As shown in Fig. 2A, the apparent viscosity of all samples declined with enhancement of shear rate, confirming that the dispersed OVA samples exhibited pseudoplastic fluid behavior [34]. Under steady-state rheological conditions, free molecules in the dispersion became entangled due to their asymmetric structures and disordered arrangements, requiring greater shear force to disrupt these interactions. With growth of shear rate, protein molecules undergo stretching and rearrangement and gradually become ordered, reducing the resistance and entanglement between molecules, thereby shear thinning occurs [10]. Incorporation of polyphenols (UPOVA, USOVA) made the viscosity of system was improved compared to OVA and UOVA, which might indicate cross-linking between proteins and polyphenols [35]. As polyphenol concentration increased, the apparent viscosities of UPOVA and USOVA first rose and then declined. This suggested that moderate polyphenol levels promote molecular cross-linking, whereas excessive polyphenols might induce steric hindrance, inhibit cross-linking and reduce apparent viscosity. Additionally, USOVA consistently exhibited higher apparent viscosity than UPOVA. Apparent viscosity was also closely related to molecular conformation and molecular weight [36]. Particle size and ζ-potential analysis

Particle size of proteins can affect interfacial and functional properties [8]. As shown in Fig. 2B, the particle size of UOVA was significantly smaller than that of OVA, decreasing by 42.2% from 373.8 nm. This might be correlated to the fact that the cavitation effect of ultrasound caused intense collisions among protein molecules, thereby destroying the protein structure under shear force, which was consistent with the fact that UOVA has a lower apparent viscosity. The addition of PA or SA increased UOVA particle size to varying degrees, with USOVA exhibiting a notably larger particle size (approximately 3.5 times that of UOVA). This suggested that SA possesses a higher molecular affinity and aggregation degree with OVA, potentially explaining the higher apparent viscosity of USOVA which could be the reason why USOVA had a higher apparent viscosity. Furthermore, Li et al. [37] reported that the number and position of hydroxyl groups (hydrogen bond donors) on polyphenols profoundly affect hydrogen bond interactions between polyphenols and proteins. Thus, the observed changes in particle size might also be associated with variations in hydrogen bonds.

ζ-potential serves as a critical indicator of system stability, reflecting the charging condition of protein solution [38]. The higher absolute value of ζ- potential corresponds to stronger electrostatic repulsion between protein particles, thereby reducing protein aggregation. As illustrated in Fig. 2C, all systems exhibited negative surface charges except for USOVA, which displayed positive ζ-potential values. Liu et al. [39] modified the lactoferrin system with EGCG to make it more acidic and exhibit a positive charge. Notably, the absolute value of ζ-potential of UOVA increased by 10.9% compared to native OVA, reflecting that ultrasonic treatment might change the protein structure, enhance electrostatic repulsion and reduce particle size, thereby promoting system stability. Conversely, both UPOVA and USOVA exhibited decreased absolute ζ-potential values, which might be related to the protein structural rearrangement caused by polyphenol adhesion. This modification exposed more positively charged residues on the protein surface, reducing the absolute ζ-potential and affecting complex stability [40]. Additionally, aggregates formed by polyphenols and proteins might bury charged groups, further decreasing the absolute ζ-potential. Importantly, UPOVA, which exhibited smaller particle size, maintained a higher absolute ζ-potential, underscoring the role of reduced particle dimensions in stabilizing colloidal systems.

Solubility and surface hydrophobicity

Solubility reflects protein dispersibility in solution and indirectly correlates with aggregation state, which is closely linked to the functional characteristics of proteins. As shown in Fig. 2D, the solubility of UOVA was outstandingly higher than that of native OVA. This increase might result from ultrasound-induced disruption of protein structure, which reduced particle size and enhanced protein-water interactions. Ren et al. [41] reported that soy protein isolate treated by ultrasound displayed a higher solubility. The addition of PA and SA remarkably increased the solubility of system, illustrating that ultrasound promoted OVA depolymerization and enhanced the accessibility of internal protein binding sites. Moreover, the incorporation of polyphenols might introduce additional polar hydroxyl sites, significantly improving protein solubility [37]. However, at a polyphenol concentration of 0.15%, the solubility of UPOVA and USOVA either plateaued or declined, indicating that excessive PA or SA may coat OVA and alter its charge state, leading to protein aggregation and reduced solubility [42]. Notably, USOVA consistently exhibited higher solubility than UPOVA, possibly due to a lower extent of hydrogen bonds that mitigates aggregate formation. Alrosan et al. [43] found that hydrogen bonds formed between trehalose and lentil protein would reduce the aggregation degree of protein and increase its solubility.

Surface hydrophobicity serves as an indicator of the exposure level of hydrophobic groups within proteins and reflects changes in protein structure. Since the foaming properties of proteins depend on the balance between surface hydrophobicity and hydrophilicity, a comprehensive investigation of surface hydrophobicity is crucial for understanding protein foaming behavior. As elucidated in Fig. 2E, the lower surface hydrophobicity of UOVA compared to native OVA suggested that ultrasound induced protein unfolding, exposing more hydrophilic groups on the protein surface and thereby reducing surface hydrophobicity [44]. On the other hand, it might be related to the fact that ultrasonic treatment promoted the formation of soluble aggregates, with hydrophobic groups sequestered within these aggregates. Polyphenols conjugation markedly increased the surface hydrophobicity of OVA, implying that the interaction between polyphenols and OVA resulted in a looser protein structure and greater exposure of internal hydrophobic groups [45]. Crucially, USOVA exhibited higher surface hydrophobicity than UPOVA at equivalent concentrations, which might be related to enhance adsorption efficiency at gas-liquid interface and directly influence foaming properties.

Thermal stability

The thermal denaturation of proteins can disrupt their structure and chemical interactions, which collectively maintain the structural integrity of protein molecules [11]. The effect of ultrasound combined with polyphenols on the thermal stability of OVA was investigated by DSC, as manifested in Fig. 3. The heat flow curves of all OVA samples presented characteristic endothermic peaks. Ultrasound slightly decreased the thermal denaturation temperature (Td) of OVA, which might be related to the structural and conformational changes of proteins caused by ultrasound [46]. The addition of PA and SA increased the Td of UPOVA and USOVA by 1.55°C and 3.53°C respectively compared to UOVA, demonstrating that PA or SA enhanced thermal stability of OVA. Furthermore, changes in enthalpy (ΔH) were considered indicative of protein structural order and the strength of hydrophobic interactions [47]. The ΔH values of UPOVA and USOVA were 2.06 and 1.28 times higher than that of OVA, suggesting that ultrasound-synergized polyphenols might improve the order degree of OVA or elevate the hydrophobic interaction to improve thermal stability of OVA. Notably, UPOVA exhibited a higher ΔH, whereas USOVA showed a higher Td. This discrepancy might be related to differing interaction modes between polyphenols and OVA, warranting further investigation in subsequent sections.Fig. 3Changes in thermal stability of OVA after ultrasound-synergized polyphenol treatment.

Analysis of foaming properties

FA and FS

Foaming property, as a critical characteristic of OVA, directly influences the taste and texture of food and holds significant application value in the food industry. FA refers to the adsorption capacity of proteins at gas-liquid interface [34]. As shown in Fig. 4A, the FA in UOVA decreased by 3.65% compared to native OVA. This might be due to the fact that ultrasonic treatment promoted the soluble aggregation of OVA, reduced the surface hydrophobicity and decreased the hydrophobic region of protein molecules adsorbed at the air-water interface, which had a negative impact on foam formation [44]. Additionally, with increasing polyphenol concentration, the foamability (FA) of the UPOVA and USOVA groups increased by 11.54%, 27.50% and 24.20%, 18.02%, 34.33% and 34.51%, respectively, compared to the OVA group. This can be attributed to enhanced solubility and surface hydrophobicity following polyphenol binding. The initially compact and stable protein structure, characterized by limited hydrophobic regions, became more extended, facilitating protein adsorption at the gas-liquid interface and thereby improving FA. Comparing UPOVA and USOVA at equivalent concentration revealed consistently higher FA for USOVA. This difference might arise from USOVA exposing more hydrophobic groups, enabling more efficient protein transfer to the gas-liquid interface [48]. Wang et al. [45] reported that hydrophobic groups distributed on the protein surface effectively lower the energy barrier at the gas-liquid interface, allowing OVA to rapidly migrate into the gas phase. Additionally, these groups accelerate adsorption and rearrangement of OVA at the gas-liquid interface, resulting in improvement of FA.Fig. 4Changes in FA (A), FS (B), microscopic foam (C) and surface tension (D) of OVA after ultrasound-synergized polyphenol treatment.

Changes in FA (A), FS (B), microscopic foam (C) and surface tension (D) of OVA after ultrasound-synergized polyphenol treatment.

FS is the ability of stable foam to resist gravity, which is influenced by protein concentration, hydration, and molecular interactions [34]. In Fig. 4B, UOVA has the highest FS (it increased by 9.19% compared with OVA), which might be attributed to the fact that ultrasound unfolded protein structure, promoting rapid adsorption at the gas-liquid interface and the formation of a viscoelastic film, thus enhancing foam stability [49]. However, the addition of polyphenols slightly decreased FS, potentially due to enhanced FA of OVA in the presence of PA and SA, which led to greater foam generation but reduced the thickness of liquid film. These factors collectively contributed to a reduction in FS [23]. Nevertheless, the overall FS of treated groups was higher than that of OVA. This improvement might be attributed to the ultrasound-synergized polyphenol treatment enhancing the viscoelasticity and surface hydrophobicity of OVA, which in turn reinforced the rigidity and thickness of foam layer, thereby resulting in better FS.

Microscopic observation

The microscopic morphology of the foam was examined using optical microscope, revealing distinct differences in foam size and distribution among the samples (Fig. 4C). It was found that there was little difference in the size of foams before and after ultrasound, while the interfacial film thickened. In addition, the higher net charge around the bubbles was conducive to the increase of molecular repulsive force, which can effectively prevent the aggregation of bubbles [8]. This phenomenon might be the reason why the higher FS of UOVA mentioned above (it increased by 9.19% compared with OVA). Systems containing polyphenols exhibited denser foam, smaller size and more uniform distribution, suggesting that polyphenols might promote protein adsorption at gas-liquid interface. At the polyphenol concentrations of 0.1% and 0.15%, foams of UPOVA and USOVA both presented denser foam and more uniform distribution. Specifically, compared with OVA, the FA improved by 27.50% for UPOVA-0.1 and 34.33% for USOVA-0.1, while the FS increased by 5.52% and 2.24%, respectively. At equivalent polyphenol concentrations, USOVA foam appeared denser with smaller volume, consistent with its superior FA. These microscopic observations confirmed that ultrasound-synergized polyphenols effectively enhanced the foaming properties of OVA.

Surface tension

The surface tension of protein molecules reflects their adsorption behavior at gas-liquid interface and is closely related to foaming properties [50]. As shown in Fig. 4D, the surface tension of all samples initially declined rapidly before gradually stabilizing. The initially downward trend (0-100 s) corresponds to the rapid adsorption of protein molecules onto gas-liquid interface [51]. From 100 to 700 s, the surface tension curve gradually plateaued, indicating that the proteins adsorbed at the interface had reached dynamic adsorption equilibrium. This stabilization might be related to the saturation of adsorbed proteins at interface, which created an energy barrier that limited further adsorption and rearrangement [8]. Comparative analysis revealed that the surface tension of both UPOVA and USOVA was lower than that of the control. This effect might be attributed to polyphenol binding, which promoted protein unfolding and increased surface hydrophobicity. Consequently, proteins became more prone to unfolding at the two-phase interface and rapidly adsorbed onto the gas-liquid interface, thereby enhancing the FA of UPOVA and USOVA. This mechanism might also explain the superior FA observed in USOVA. However, excessive polyphenol content can induce protein aggregation and reduce solubility, resulting in a certain degree increase in surface tension.

Structural characterization analysis

FT-IR

FT-IR qualitatively evaluates the interaction states and binding relationships between protein molecules in samples, such as the formation and disruption of chemical bonds. In Fig. 5A, the region around 3300 cm-1 primarily corresponded to the stretching vibrations of N-H and O-H, suggesting the presence of intermolecular and intramolecular hydrogen bonds within OVA molecules. The bands between 2,800-3,000 cm-1 are attributed to symmetric and asymmetric stretching vibrations of -CH3 and-CH2 groups. The partial blue shift observed in these peaks after ultrasound-synergized polyphenols indicated potential alterations in the polarity and structure of protein molecules [52]. The bands at 1655, 1543, and 1295 cm-1 corresponded to amide I, amide II, and amide III bands, respectively. A comparison between OVA and UOVA revealed no significant shifts in peak positions following sonication, further confirming that ultrasound did not induce the formation or cleavage of chemical bonds. However, the addition of PA and SA resulted in the emergence of new peaks in their respective complexes. Specifically, the UPOVA complex exhibited new peaks at 1595 cm-1 and 1295 cm-1, while the USOVA complex showed new peaks at 1383 cm-1 and 1236 cm-1. It was worth noting that the number of USOVA peak positions continued to increase with enhancement of SA content, and new peaks appear at 1329, 1240, and 1219 cm-1. These peaks predominantly distributed in the amide II and amide III regions, implying that PA and OVA promoted the formation of complexes through N-H and C-N interactions, while SA formed complexes through N-H, C-H, and glycine CH2 interactions [53]. Furthermore, both polyphenols induced a new peak near 1115 cm-1, with peak intensity increasing with concentration, suggesting that changes in hydrogen bonds were involved in complex formation [54].Fig. 5Changes in the FT-IR spectra (A), secondary structure content (B), endogenous fluorescence spectra (C) and molecular docking models (D) of OVA after ultrasound-synergized polyphenol treatment.

Further analysis of amide I band (1600-1700 cm-1) reveals changes in the secondary structure of OVA (Fig. 5B). Ultrasound-synergized polyphenols reduced β-sheet content while significantly rising β-turn content. Generally, α-helix and β-sheet structures represents ordered conformations, whereas β-turn and random coil denote disordered structures. An increase in β-sheet content is often associated with protein aggregation [55]. Therefore, the observed changes suggested that polyphenols might confer new spatial functionalities to OVA. Meanwhile, ultrasound-synergized polyphenol likely induced unfolding of OVA molecular conformation, rendering the structure more extended and disordered. Previous study reported that a reduction in β-sheet content correlated with the unfolding and exposure of hydrophobic regions within OVA [56], which might account for the enhanced surface hydrophobicity and FA observed in earlier experiments.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking can simulate the docking situation of proteins and polyphenols at the lowest binding energy and achieve visualization, further analyzing the binding mode of PA or SA and OVA from the molecular perspective. As depicted in Fig. 5C, PA inserted into a hydrophobic pocket of OVA formed by four amino acid residues. Among these, LYS92, SER98, and SER100 established hydrogen bonds with PA, indicating that hydrogen bonds played a crucial role in stabilizing of the PA-OVA complex. The interaction between SA and OVA involved 16 key amino acid residues, with van der Waals forces and carbon-hydrogen bonds identified as the primary binding interactions. This was consistent with the results of FT-IR showing that the binding modes of PA or SA with OVA were both related to hydrogen bonds. Zhang et al. [57] reported the importance of hydrogen bonds and van der Waals forces in the interaction between OVA and isoquercitrin. Although van der Waals forces were relatively weak, they provided essential support for maintaining the spatial stability of the complex. These interactions promoted the formation of stable complexes, which might also be the reason why USOVA-0.1 had a higher Td in the thermal stability mentioned above. Additionally, the residues PRO93 interacted with SA via hydrophobic interactions, with bond distances of approximately 4.79 Å. Notably, residues PHE99 and SER98 on OVA formed both polar hydrogen bonds and carbon-hydrogen bonds with SA. Comparing the interaction modes of two polyphenols with OVA, it was found that UPOVA possessed more polar hydrogen atoms and exhibited higher affinity at the air/water interface. This property hindered intermolecular interactions, thereby conferring superior FS to UPOVA [58]. This difference in molecular structure might precisely be the essential reason for disparity in functional characteristics.

Endogenous fluorescence spectroscopy

Certain chromophores within proteins are highly sensitive to changes in their polar microenvironment, changes in protein conformation might modify the microenvironment of these chromophores and affect fluorescence intensity. Therefore, changes in the spatial conformation of proteins can be characterized by measuring fluorescence spectra. fluorescence spectroscopy can be employed to characterize changes in protein spatial conformation. As shown in Fig. 5D, ultrasonic treatment reduced the fluorescence intensity of OVA, which might be due to the cavitation effect of ultrasonic waves destroying the protein structure, leading to an increase in the number of chromophores exposed to polar environments [49]. Notably, the addition of PA caused a blue shift in the emission maximum (from 320 nm to 315 nm), which might imply that CA changed the conformation of OVA. The polarity around tryptophan residues was reduced, and the resulting complex is more hydrophobic [59], which was in keeping with the surface hydrophobicity results mentioned above. As the concentration of polyphenol increased, the fluorescence intensity showed a decreasing trend, which might indicate that fluorescent groups became buried within a more polar environment after sufficient interaction between polyphenols and protein [60]. The polar environment might favor the unfolding of internal hydrophobic interactions within proteins, with more hydrophobic groups exposed on the molecular surface. This result aligned with the surface hydrophobicity and FT-IR findings and contributed to the improvement in FA.

Microstructure

Conclusion

This study comparatively investigated the impact of ultrasound-synergized polyphenols (PA, SA) on the foaming properties of OVA. Polyphenol binding capacity and SDS-PAGE results provided indirect evidence for the formation of non-covalent complexes between polyphenols and OVA, with the higher molecular weight SA exhibiting a stronger affinity for OVA. Ultrasound promoted the interaction between polyphenol and OVA, enhancing particle size and apparent viscosity, as well as improved solubility and surface hydrophobicity. These changes make the complexes to adsorb more efficiently at the air-water interface, thereby improving FA. Microscopic foam indicated that 0.1% concentration of SA was more conducive to formation of uniform and dense foam. Structural analyses revealed that ultrasound-synergized polyphenol treatment facilitated the unfolding of OVA, leading to a more extended and disordered structure. PA primarily interacted with OVA through hydrogen bonds, which increased polarity and decreased fluorescence intensity. In contrast, SA, with its higher molecular weight, predominantly interacted with OVA via weaker polar forces such as van der Waals interactions and carbon-hydrogen bonds. These interactions promoted formation of soluble aggregates and contributed to the structural stability of proteins, ultimately enhancing its foaming properties. In summary, ultrasound-synergized polyphenol treatment enhanced the adsorption of OVA at air-water interface, thereby improving its foaming characteristics. Moreover, differences in the molecular weights and interaction with proteins resulted in different complex structures, leading to varying foaming properties. This study primarily focused on the improvement of OVA foaming properties through ultrasound-synergized polyphenols and provides references. Future research directions may explore the application of OVA foaming properties or further investigate other characteristics.
