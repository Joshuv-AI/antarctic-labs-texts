Nucleic acid binding affinity and antioxidant activity of N-m-Tolyl-4-Chlorophenoxyacetohydroxamicacid
Authors: Rubi Khilari, Sohilkhan Chauhan, Mamta Tripathi, Rama Pande, Mohammed S Alqahtani, Rabbani Syed, Mudassar Shahid, Devashish Das, Avijit Sarkar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractHydroxamic acids represent a group of weak organic acids, both naturally occurring and synthetically derived, characterized by the general formula RC(= O)N(R’OH). In this study, we investigated the binding behavior of N-m-tolyl-4-chlorophenoxyaceto hydroxamic acid with calf thymus DNA (ct-DNA) and torula yeast RNA (t-RNA) through a combination of techniques including UV–visible spectroscopy, fluorescence emission analysis, viscometry, and computational simulations using AutoDock4 software. Our findings reveal that the mode of binding between the compound and the nucleic acids is consistent with intercalation. Competitive binding experiments demonstrated that the complex competes effectively with ethidium bromide (EB) for binding to ct-DNA/t-RNA, displacing EB from its binding sites. Additionally, the introduction of the compound into the DNA-EB system resulted in a quenching of fluorescence emission peaks. Analysis of absorption spectra indicated a red shift and hypochromic shift when the compound interacted with DNA, further supporting the intercalative binding mode. The calculated binding constant (Kb) value for the compound is 6.62 × 104 M−1 and 5.40 × 103 M−1 indicating a strong interaction with ct-DNA and t-RNA respectively. We determined the Stern–Volmer constants for ct-DNA and t-RNA as 9.96 × 104 M−1 and 8.13 × 105 M−1, respectively. The binding free energy values for ct-DNA/t-RNA were calculated to be − 3.741 × 107 and − 5.425 × 108 kcal/mol, respectively. Viscometric studies corroborated the UV results, showing a continuous increase in relative viscosity of ct-DNA/t-RNA solutions with the addition of the optimal hydroxamic acid concentration. Furthermore, we assessed the antioxidant activity of the compound using DPPH-radical scavenging and β-carotene linoleic acid assays. Gel electrophoresis results demonstrated the compound's remarkable efficacy in preventing DNA damage. Collectively, all experimental evidence supports the conclusion that N-m-tolyl-4-chlorophenoxyaceto hydroxamic acid binds to ct-DNA/t-RNA through an intercalative mechanism, which is consistent with our molecular docking simulations.Keywords: Hydroxamic acid, Interaction, Ct-DNA /t-RNA, BindingSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques, Chemical biology, Drug discovery

Introduction

The chemistry and biochemistry of hydroxamic acids and their derivatives have attracted considerable attention, due to their pharmacological, toxicological, and pathological properties1. Hydroxamic acids generally have low toxicities and have a wide spectrum of activities in all types of biological systems, such as acting as growth factors1, tumor inhibitors2, antimicrobial agents3, antituberculosis4, antileukemic agents5, key pharmacophores in many important chemotherapeutic agents6, and pigments and cell-division factors. Several of them have been advanced into human clinical trials as pharmaceutical drugs for the treatment of several diseases7. The mode and knowledge of DNA binding interaction and antioxidant activity of some N-aryl hydroxamic acids derivatives have been reported8,9.

The investigations based on drug-DNA/RNA interactions help to understand the action mechanisms of some anti-tumor and anti-viral drugs but also help to design new nucleic acid-targeted drugs and to screen these drugs in vitro10. This emerging field of research has attracted researchers and has gained much attention in recent years11–16. Various techniques, namely gel electrophoresis17, foot printing technique18, X-ray crystallography19, structural modeling20, spectroscopy, and especially fluorescence spectroscopy21,22, have been employed to study the interactions of some anticancer drugs with nucleic acids.

Antioxidant is defined as "a substance that, when present at low concentrations compared with those of an oxidizable substrate, significantly delays or prevents oxidation of that substrate"23. The antioxidant activity nature of hydroxamic acid functionality –NOH.C = O is due to the presence of oxygen and nitrogen atoms in the core functional group. The reported hydroxyl urea and alginic acid, structurally similar to hydroxamic acid, are already used as antioxidants7,24. PBHA of this series has been reported to show an intercalating mode of interaction with ct-DNA and with t-RNA25,26.

In the present investigation, the interaction between a hydroxamic acid derivative, namely N-m-tolyl-4-chlorophenoxyaceto hydroxamic acid, was studied for ct-DNA/t-RNA binding properties and its antioxidant activity. The binding mode of ct-DNA/t-RNA with this ligand is complemented through viscometric studies. The comparative study on the interaction between such complexes with ct-DNA and t-RNA should be useful in the development of nucleic acids-based molecular probes and new therapeutic reagents for some diseases such as AIDS and SARS, which are caused by viruses27.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

N-m-tolyl-4-chlorophenoxyaceto hydroxamic acid was synthesized following a previously reported procedure28. The purity of the synthesized compound was confirmed by assessing its melting point and elemental analysis, which matched the reported values29.

For DNA-binding experiments, a buffer consisting of 50 mM NaCl and 5 mM Tris–HCl was used at room temperature. The pH was maintained at 7.4 by adding 0.01 M HCl using standard protocols. A solution of calf thymus DNA (ct-DNA) in the buffer was prepared to achieve a UV-absorbance ratio at 260–280 nm of 1.8–1.9, ensuring the DNA sample was free of protein contamination30. RNA-binding experiments were conducted in citrate–phosphate (CP) buffer containing 1 mM [Na+], adjusted to pH 7.4 with 0.5 mM Na2HPO4, using citric acid31. Stock solutions (0.01 mM) of the experimental compounds for antioxidant studies were prepared in ethanol (Merck) and stored in a cool, dark environment. The compound N-m-tolyl-4-chlorophenoxyacetohydroxamic acid was selected for this study. The DNA target was the octamer sequence d(CCTCGTCC)2, corresponding to PDB ID: 1XRW. The RNA target utilized was the phenylalanine transfer RNA (PHE-t-RNA) sequence GCGGAUUUAGCUCAGUUGGGAGAGCGCCAGACUGAAGAUCUGGAGGUCCUGUGUUCGAUCCACAGAAUUCGCACCA, corresponding to PDB ID: 1EHZ.All chemicals utilized were of spectroscopic grade, and doubly distilled deionized water was employed for solution preparations.

Apparatus

Absorbance measurements were carried out using a Biospectrum BL-198 spectrophotometer (Elico) with 1.0 cm quartz cells. Fluorescence spectra were recorded on a Cary Eclipse Fluorescence Spectrophotometer (Varian, USA) equipped with a xenon flash lamp and 1.0 cm quartz cells. pH measurements were conducted using a digital pH meter (Eutech Oakton) with a combined glass-calomel electrode. A Tarsons spin with a microcentrifuge (1.5 ml tube) at 6000 rpm was employed. A submarine gel system was used for gel electrophoresis. All solvents and reagents, obtained from different suppliers, met the highest purity standards required for each specific application.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking simulations were performed to elucidate the binding affinities and interaction mechanisms of N-m-tolyl-4-chlorophenoxyacetohydroxamic acid with DNA and RNA targets. AutoDock4 software was employed for these studies.

For the DNA octamer (PDB ID: 1XRW), which is known for its intercalative binding properties, the docking parameters included a population size of 150, a maximum of 2,500,000 energy evaluations, and a maximum of 27,000 generations. The grid box was defined with dimensions of 58 × 68 × 126 grid points and a grid spacing of 0.375 Å, centered at coordinates (14.719, 20.979, 8.824). For comparative analysis, docking simulations were also performed on the DNA structure 1BNA to investigate potential groove binding modes.

For the PHE-t-RNA monomer (PDB ID: 1EHZ), the parameters were similarly set with a population size of 150, a maximum of 2,500,000 energy evaluations, and a maximum of 27,000 generations. The grid box dimensions were 82 × 56 × 78 grid points, with a grid spacing of 1.0 Å, centered at coordinates (67.402, 49.295, 28.427).

AutoDock Vina was utilized to perform the docking simulations, generating a total of 1,000 docking conformations for both DNA and RNA targets to ensure thorough exploration of potential binding sites. The grid boxes were designed to encompass the entire nucleic acid molecules, with grid center coordinates strategically positioned at the geometric centers of the targets. This setup allowed the compound to fully explore possible binding interactions with the nucleic acids.

Binding mode studies

In addition to groove binding studies using the 1BNA structure, we investigated the potential intercalative binding mode of the compound using the PDB: 1XRW crystal structure. The methodology involved superimposing our compound with the existing ligand in the 1XRW structure, followed by replacing the ligand and performing a redocking simulation at the same position. This approach aligns with the reference by Al-Rashida et al. (2015)49, who explored docking protocols to differentiate between DNA intercalators and groove binders.

Molecular dynamics simulations

For the final selection of the most favorable binding mode, both the groove-bound (1BNA) and intercalated (1XRW) structures were subjected to 100 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulations using Gromacs software. Standard settings were employed, as described in references by Tsuchiya et al. (2023)50 and Bekker et al. (1993)51. These MD simulations provided insights into the stability and dynamics of the compound bound to DNA in both groove and intercalative binding modes.

Hydrogen bond analysis

Hydrogen bonding interactions were analyzed post-docking using an in-house developed script. This script identified hydrogen bonds based on predefined geometric criteria: the distance between donor and acceptor atoms (D..A) was required to be less than 3.5 Å, and the donor-hydrogen-acceptor angle had to be greater than 120°. The script processed the docking output files to extract detailed hydrogen bond information, including the identification of donor and acceptor atoms, the hydrogen atoms involved, and the distances between these atoms. These criteria ensured the identification of significant hydrogen bond interactions contributing to the stability and specificity of the binding poses.

This comprehensive methodological approach enabled the detailed characterization of the binding interactions between N-m-tolyl-4-chlorophenoxyacetohydroxamic acid and its nucleic acid targets, providing valuable insights into its potential as a bioactive compound52–57.

Results and discussion

Measurement of ct-DNA/t-RNA binding properties

Ct-DNA/t-RNA binding experiments

Nucleic acid binding experiments were conducted at a temperature of 25.0 ± 0.2 °C. The concentration of ct-DNA/t-RNA in base pairs was determined by UV absorbance at 260 nm, using the molar extinction coefficient values of 13,200 M-1 cm−1 for ct-DNA and 18,500 M-1 cm−1 for t-RNA at 260 nm56,57. Absorption titration experiments of hydroxamic acid complexes were carried out in a buffer (50 mM NaCl—5 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4) using a fixed complex concentration to which increments of nucleic acid stock solutions were added.

UV- Visible spectroscopic measurements

The interaction of ct-DNA/t-RNA with the hydroxamic acid complex was monitored using the UV–visible absorption titration method. The absorption spectra of N-m-tolyl-4-chlorophenoxyacetohydroxamic acid for both ct-DNA and t-RNA are presented in Figs. 1 and 2 In the case of N-p-tolyl-p-chlorophenoxyaceto hydroxamic acid, the effect of varying concentrations of ct-DNA (25–200 mM) on the electronic spectra resulted in a hypochromic shift. This type of result indicates a strong interaction between hydroxamic acid and ct-DNA. A prominent shift in wavelength (bathochromic shift ≥ 45 nm) suggests an intercalative mode of binding due to π-π stacking interaction. An isosbestic point was observed at 278 nm. The calculated binding constant (Kb) value for the compound is 6.62 × 104 M−1, indicating a strong interaction with ct-DNA. A pronounced hypochromic shift was observed for hydroxamic acid when mixed with increasing concentrations of t-RNA, demonstrating a strong intermolecular association. The negative peak observed in the final spectrum can be attributed due to the formation of a complex between N-m-tolyl-4-chlorophenoxyacetohydroxamic acid and ct-DNA/t-RNA , which could lead to changes in the electronic environment and absorption characteristics. This complex formation might result in a hypochromic effect, where the absorption decreases due to molecular interactions, causing the observed negative peak. Another potential reason could be the saturation of binding sites on t-RNA at higher concentrations, leading to changes in the spectral properties.The binding constant obtained for this interaction was 5.40 × 103 M−1. Fluorescence quenching studies

Fluorescence quenching studies were conducted to confirm the mode of binding. Emission spectra of the complexes were obtained with a maximum appearing at around 465 nm when excited at 265 nm. The fluorescence intensity in Figs. 5 and 6 exhibited continuous decreases upon successive addition of increasing concentrations of ct-DNA/t-RNA (0–125 µl). Ethidium bromide (EB), a classical fluorescent probe, intercalates between the base pairs of ct-DNA/t-RNA. EB bound to ct-DNA/t-RNA results in enhanced emission intensity, which is quenched by the addition of a second molecule, confirming the displacement of EB and indicating an intercalation mode of binding. N-m-tolyl-4-chlorophenoxyaceto hydroxamic acid demonstrated significant quenching of EB bound to nucleic acid58,59, as shown in Figs. 7 and 8. A remarkable decrease in emission intensity maxima at 584 nm, corresponding to EB bound to nucleic acid, was observed. Plots of I0/I versus [Q] were constructed (Fig. 5). In the case of t-RNA, the plots of I0/I versus [Q] exhibited a straight line at lower concentrations but a downward curvature at higher concentrations of hydroxamic acid complexes, indicating that the quenching mechanism is dynamic at low concentration but static at higher concentration. The Stern–Volmer constants for ct-DNA/t-RNA were determined to be 9.96 × 104 M-1 and 8.13 × 105 M-1, respectively. A larger KSV value signifies more effective quenching of fluorescence, indicating a stronger interaction between the ligand and the nucleic acid. This is because a higher KSV value reflects a greater ability of the ligand to quench the fluorescence of the nucleic acid, suggesting that the ligand is more efficiently bound to the nucleic acid.

Determination of Gibb’s free energy change

The binding free energy values for ct-DNA/t-RNA were calculated to be -3.74 × 107 kcal/mol and 5.42 × 108 kcal/mol, respectively, using spectrophotometric and spectrofluorimetric data and the standard Eq. (5). These negative values indicate the spontaneity of the hydroxamic acid ct-DNA/t-RNA interaction. Even though the compound forms a hydrogen bond and exhibits a binding energy of -1.32 kcal/mol with the tRNA, the ligand does not effectively bind to the tRNA. This conclusion is supported by the binding constant (Kb) and Gibb’s Free Energy Change (ΔG) values. Specifically, the Kb value for tRNA is 5.40 × 103 M-1, which is significantly lower than the binding constant observed for ct-DNA (6.62 × 104 M-1). Additionally, the Gibb’s Free Energy Change (ΔG) for tRNA is 5.42 × 108 kcal/mol, indicating a less favourable interaction compared to the ΔG value for ct-DNA, which is -3.74 × 107 kcal/mol. These parameters suggest that, despite the formation of a hydrogen bond and a measurable binding energy, the overall binding affinity and thermodynamic favourability of the ligand for tRNA are insufficient for effective binding.

Molecular docking

Binding energy analysis

The docking studies of N-m-tolyl-4-chlorophenoxyacetohydroxamic acid with the DNA octamer (PDB ID: 1XRW) revealed a detailed binding mode and interaction mechanism.60,61 The compound was found to intercalate at a specific site within the DNA duplex based on a combination of computational modelling and docking simulations. The compound was docked in the DNA duplex, targeting the intercalation site between the base pairs of the sequence d(CCTCGTCC)2 with a binding energy of -8.46 kcal/mol (Fig. 9a). Comparatively, the compound showed a much lower binding energy of -2.49 kcal/mol when bound with 1BNA at the groove side62. This comparison is supported by th1 100 ns simulation. The RMSD of the intercalated compound is much more stable than that of the groove side bound counterpart as shown in supplementary Fig. 1. Similarly, the binding energy of the compound to the PHE-t-RNA monomer (PDB ID: 1EHZ) was found to be -1.32 kcal/mol. This interaction primarily occurred at the acceptor stem of the tRNA, suggesting a meaningful interaction between the compound and the RNA molecule (Fig. 9b).

DNA docking: intercalation vs groove binding

To further understand how our compound interacts with DNA, we ran molecular dynamics (MD) simulations for 100 ns to compare the stability of the compound in both binding modes.63,64 The results were quite revealing. As shown in Figure S1, the RMSD analysis demonstrated that the intercalated complex remained remarkably stable throughout the simulation, indicating a strong and secure interaction between the compound and the DNA bases. In contrast, the groove-bound complex exhibited significant fluctuations in RMSD, suggesting it was far less stable.

Both complexes underwent 100-ns MD simulations, and the results were telling. The Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD) analysis, shown in Figure S1, reveals that the intercalated complex remained remarkably stable throughout the simulation, indicating that the compound stayed securely intercalated between the DNA bases. In contrast, the groove-bound complex exhibited significant fluctuations in RMSD, suggesting a lack of stability.

The comparison between groove binding and intercalation revealed stark differences in stability. In the groove-bound complex, the compound exhibited significant instability, as evident from the early detachment of the compound from the DNA during the simulation. This instability is further highlighted by the Radius of Gyration (Rg) analysis65, shown in Figure S2. The groove-bound complex demonstrated considerable fluctuations in Rg, indicating a lack of structural compactness over time. These variations in Rg align with the instability observed in the RMSD profile, reinforcing the idea that groove binding leads to structural changes and eventual detachment of the compound from the DNA.

In contrast, the intercalated complex showed a much more stable interaction. As illustrated in Figure S3, the compound remained securely intercalated between the DNA bases from the start (0 ns) to the end (100 ns) of the simulation. The minimal deviation in the compound’s position throughout the simulation underscores the stability of this binding mode. This clear contrast between the unstable groove-bound complex and the stable intercalated complex strongly suggests that intercalation is the preferred and more stable binding mode for this compound.

Hydrogen bonding interactions

Hydrogen bonding plays a critical role in stabilizing the complex formed between the compound and its nucleic acid targets. In the DNA, the intercalation site of the compound was identified at the 5-C4G5-3 base-pair step (Fig. 9a). The aromatic ring system of the compound aligned parallel to the adjacent base pairs, with the long axis of the compound oriented to match the long axes of the DNA base pairs. Significant hydrophobic interactions occur between residue DC at position 4 of chain A and the compound, with a close contact distance of 3.88 Å. This interaction helps stabilize the compound within the DNA duplex.

Additionally, pi-stacking interactions play a crucial role in the stabilization of the compound. For example, residue DC at position 4 of chain A shows a T-type pi-stacking interaction with the compound, with a centroid distance of 5.19 Å. Similarly, residue DG at position 5 of chain A forms a parallel pi-stacking interaction with the compound, with a centroid distance of 3.94 Å. Another parallel pi-stacking interaction is observed between the same residue DG at position 5 of chain A, with a slightly shorter centroid distance of 3.55 Å. Finally, residue DG at position 5 of chain B also engages in a parallel pi-stacking interaction with the compound, with a centroid distance of 3.63 Å. Overall, these hydrophobic and pi-stacking interactions collectively contribute to the stable binding and intercalation of the N-m-tolyl-4-chlorophenoxyacetohydroxamic acid compound within the DNA duplex, highlighting its potential mechanism of action at the molecular level.This alignment facilitated efficient penetration and stable intercalation within the base stack. These interactions contributed to the stable binding of the compound within the DNA duplex, elucidating its potential mechanism of action.

The results from the 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation further reinforce the stability of this interaction. The H-bond analysis, as shown in the figure S4, reveals that the intercalated complex consistently maintained a higher number of hydrogen bonds throughout the simulation compared to the groove-bound complex. In the intercalated mode, the compound engaged in one to three hydrogen bonds consistently, contributing significantly to its stability within the DNA duplex. On the other hand, the groove-bound complex exhibited fewer hydrogen bonds and showed a noticeable decrease in hydrogen bonding interactions over time, particularly after the 60 ns mark, further underscoring the instability of this binding mode. These findings from the H-bond analysis underscore the critical role of hydrogen bonding in maintaining the structural integrity of the intercalated complex over time, further validating the preference for intercalation as the primary binding mode of the compound with DNA.

For the RNA complex, one hydrogen bond was also identified. This hydrogen bond occurred between the same oxygen atom (O20) of the compound and the oxygen atom (O3') of the cytosine base at position 70 of the RNA sequence. The hydrogen atom involved in this interaction was again H34. The donor–acceptor distance (D..A) in this case was 3.274 Å, and the donor-hydrogen-acceptor distance (D-H..A) was 2.468 Å. Although these distances are slightly longer than those observed in the DNA complex, they still indicate a meaningful hydrogen bond interaction.

Discussion

The docking results and subsequent hydrogen bond analysis underscore the potential of N-m-tolyl-4-chlorophenoxyacetohydroxamic acid as a bioactive compound capable of interacting with both DNA and RNA molecules (Fig. 9). The lower binding energy with DNA suggests a slightly higher affinity towards the A-T rich regions of DNA, while clusters in the C-G regions with higher binding energies indicate possible false positives. The specific interaction observed in the RNA complex at the acceptor stem of the tRNA highlights the compound's ability to bind selectively to different regions of nucleic acid targets. The observed hydrogen bonds in both complexes not only stabilize the compound within the binding sites but also contribute to the specificity of these interactions. The binding interactions of N-m-tolyl-4-chlorophenoxyacetohydroxamic acid with DNA and RNA targets can be contextualized by considering the principles outlined in previous studies. Warner et al. (2018) highlights the significance of identifying appropriate target structures in disease-causing RNAs with high information content and suitable ligand-binding pockets66. This principle is essential for understanding the specificity and efficacy of small-molecule drugs targeting RNA. In our study, the interactions of N-m-tolyl-4-chlorophenoxyacetohydroxamic acid with the phenylalanine transfer RNA (PHE-t-RNA) suggest that the compound can specifically bind to RNA regions that possess favorable binding pockets, thereby supporting the idea of targeted RNA interactions.

Similarly, the study by Rohs et al. (2005) underscores the importance of drug-DNA interactions in various therapeutic applications, including anticancer, antiviral, and antibacterial therapies67. Understanding the binding-site transitions and modes of drug docking to DNA is crucial for elucidating the mechanisms of action of bioactive compounds. Our findings, which show a binding energy of -8.46 kcal/mol for the interaction between N-m-tolyl-4-chlorophenoxyacetohydroxamic acid and the DNA octamer, align with these principles by demonstrating the compound’s ability to favorably interact with G-C rich regions of DNA.

The molecular dynamics simulations further corroborate these findings by providing dynamic insights into the stability of these interactions over time. The consistent hydrogen bonding observed in the intercalated DNA complex, as revealed by the 100 ns simulation, emphasizes the robust nature of the binding mode. The stability of the intercalated complex, supported by both RMSD and Rg analyses, aligns with the principles of drug-DNA interactions, reinforcing the potential therapeutic applications of N-m-tolyl-4-chlorophenoxyacetohydroxamic acid.

Hermann and Westhof (1999) emphasize targeting specific three-dimensional structural elements of RNA, such as bulges, hairpins, and junctions, to achieve selectivity in drug binding68. This approach is pertinent to our observation of the compound’s interaction with the acceptor stem of the PHE-t-RNA. By focusing on distinct RNA regions, we can better understand how the compound achieves its selective binding, thereby enhancing the precision of RNA-targeted drug design.

Furthermore, Li and Kang (2023) discuss the potential of targeting RNA-binding proteins with small molecules, particularly through the use of bifunctional molecules69. This perspective broadens our understanding of how small molecules, like N-m-tolyl-4-chlorophenoxyacetohydroxamic acid, can modulate RNA functions and contribute to innovative drug development strategies. Our study’s insights into the compound’s binding interactions with both DNA and RNA targets suggest potential avenues for exploring its broader applications in modulating nucleic acid functions.

In conclusion, integrating insights from studies on drug binding to DNA and RNA, such as those by Warner et al. (2018), Rohs et al. (2005), Hermann and Westhof (1999), and Li and Kang (2023), enriches the discussion on the binding interactions of N-m-tolyl-4-chlorophenoxyacetohydroxamic acid with nucleic acid targets. These references provide a robust foundation for comprehensively understanding the mechanisms underlying drug-nucleic acid interactions and offer valuable perspectives for further exploration in drug discovery and development.

Conclusion

In vitro ct-DNA/t-RNA profiling of N-m-tolyl-4-chlorophenoxyaceto hydroxamic acid complexes was conducted using optical (absorption, fluorescence) and hydrodynamic methods. The in vitro DNA binding studies of the complex, as revealed by absorption, fluorescence, and viscosity measurements, indicated binding via an intercalative mode to the ct-DNA double helix. The intercalation binding of hydroxamic acid to DNA was supported by the isosbestic point, bathochromic and hypochromic effects in the absorption spectra, a decrease in EB fluorescence quenching, and a significant increase in the relative viscosity of the ct-DNA, all of which complemented the intercalation mode of binding.

The analysis of binding data, both from spectrophotometric and fluorimetric studies, indicated that N-m-tolyl-4-chlorophenoxyaceto hydroxamic acid binds with t-RNA. The viscosity of t-RNA increased more rapidly with increasing compound concentration, suggesting stronger binding of the compound with t-RNA. The compound demonstrated significant antioxidant activity, exhibiting excellent radical scavenging abilities and prevention of heat-induced oxidation of linoleic acid for DPPH free radical scavenging and the β-carotene linoleic acid assay, respectively. All results support the conclusion that the intercalation binding mechanism is followed by N-m-tolyl-4-chlorophenoxyaceto hydroxamic acid when binding to ct-DNA/t-RNA, which has implications for the design of new drugs. In conclusion, this comprehensive analysis provides a detailed understanding of the binding interactions between N-m-tolyl-4-chlorophenoxyacetohydroxamic acid and nucleic acid targets. These findings pave the way for further investigations into the potential biological applications of this compound, particularly in the context of its nucleic acid-binding properties.
