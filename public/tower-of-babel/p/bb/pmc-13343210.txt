Kinetic and Spatial Resolution by dSTORM of miniSOG‐Mediated Proximity Labeling in Mammalian Cells
Authors: Benoît Arnould, Alexandria L Quillin, Aastha, Tatiana F Flores, Jennifer M Heemstra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe mini Singlet Oxygen Generator (miniSOG) is a genetically encoded flavin‐binding protein that enables photocatalytic proximity labeling upon blue‐light illumination. Despite its widespread adoption for proteomic and transcriptomic applications, key operational parameters such as labeling kinetics and effective spatial reach have remained undefined in cellular environments. Here, we implement a microscope‐based irradiation platform coupled with single‐molecule localization microscopy to quantitatively characterize miniSOG activity in fixed mammalian cells. We identify two illumination‐dependent patterns: a productive window at intermediate power yielding sustained signal accumulation, and photoinactivation at higher power. Super‐resolution imaging reveals that the effective labeling diameter expands with illumination time, from ∼70 nm at early illumination to ∼180 nm after 30 min, consistent across cytoskeletal and nuclear contexts and sensitive to singlet oxygen quenching. These results provide the first in situ quantification of miniSOG's kinetic and spatial capture parameters, and demonstrate that the effective proximity of labeling can be tuned to meet the needs of varying biological applications. Our findings establish a framework for rigorous experimental design and ensure that miniSOG‐based proteomic and transcriptomic studies are interpreted within spatially meaningful constraints.Keywords: dSTORM, miniSOG, photocatalysis, proteins, proximity labeling, RNA

Introduction

Precise mapping of molecular interactions within living cells is essential to understanding cellular machinery, signaling, and organization. In particular, many aspects of cellular physiology emerge from transient interactions between proteins, nucleic acids, and metabolites. Despite this importance, capturing and characterizing these dynamic interactions at the nanometer spatial scale remains a profound challenge. Proximity labeling (PL) strategies have transformed this landscape by enabling the covalent tagging of molecules near a genetically targeted catalyst, thereby recording local molecular neighborhoods with high spatial specificity. Enzyme‐based PL systems, such as BioID [1] and APEX [2, 3], have been invaluable for proteome and transcriptome mapping. However, they come with significant drawbacks. BioID is limited to proteomics studies, and APEX relies on exogenous cofactors that inherently stress cells, impairing robust biological data [4]. Additionally, the requirement for an exogenous diffusible reagent constrains precise temporal control.

Mini Singlet Oxygen Generator (miniSOG), a 106‐residue monomeric flavin mononucleotide (FMN)‐binding protein, offers a compelling alternative for spatiotemporally controlled PL. Upon blue light illumination, miniSOG generates a reactive oxygen species, singlet oxygen (1O2), that modifies residues of proximal biomolecules and enables subsequent functionalization with a nucleophilic probe. The 1O2 generated by miniSOG primarily reacts with guanosine nucleobases in DNA or RNA [5] (Figure 1a), and histidine residues in proteins, enabling use for mapping of both nucleic acids and proteins in the cellular environment [6]. MiniSOG has been used in a range of applications, including correlated light and electron microscopy [7], transcriptional profiling (e.g., CAP‐seq [5]), and the recently reported RinID proteomics workflow that achieves turn‐on kinetics with high subcellular specificity and minute‐level temporal resolution [6].

Nevertheless, several key parameters underpinning data interpretability of miniSOG experiments remain poorly defined. Of particular interest in PL is the effective labeling diameter of miniSOG in the complex and crowded milieu of mammalian cells, yet this has not been quantified. Diffusion theory prediction based on the lifetime of 1O2 in aqueous solution has been used to approximate this diameter, but does not account for molecular crowding, oxygen gradients, quenchers, or protein density in cellulo [8]. Moreover, early biochemical reports and more recent adaptations such as RinID [6] suggest that labeling efficiency is strongly dependent on illumination regime but provide no direct measure of nanoscale labeling range. Therefore, “proximity” labeling remains an elusive term, lacking a robust quantitative definition of spatial parameters in cellular environments.

In the absence of a quantitative definition of labeling radius, miniSOG results can be easily misinterpreted. Biomolecules captured tens or hundreds of nanometers away may be erroneously considered direct interactors, while true neighbors outside the effective labeling zone may be overlooked. This uncertainty blurs the boundary between true molecular partners and random bystanders, confusing network inference from proteomic and transcriptomic datasets. Here, we address this challenge by presenting a comprehensive, quantitative characterization of miniSOG‐mediated labeling in mammalian cells. By combining high‐precision spatial imaging with calibrated blue‐light illumination and time‐resolved biochemical capture of oxidized neighbors, we define both the kinetic regimes and the nanometer‐scale labeling diameter of miniSOG in situ. Our results reveal that the diameter of effective labeling expands with illumination time and power from 70 to ∼200 nm. These metrics enable customization of PL experiments and provide valuable guidelines for deploying miniSOG‐based methods in proteomic, transcriptomic, and interactome mapping studies, enabling rigorous quantitative interpretation of the “proximity” in PL.

Results and Discussion

Controlled Illumination Workflow

Conventional lamp‐based setups are often used for miniSOG activation and employ a desk lamp with blue‐light bulbs. While this offers a cost‐effective option, the associated challenges include poorly defined emission spectra and large, uneven illumination. Especially notable is that subtle differences in distance or angle between the lamp and the sample can cause several‐fold variations in photon flux. Thus, to characterize miniSOG‐mediated labeling with high accuracy and reproducibility, we first implemented an automated widefield fluorescence microscope as an automated irradiation platform. The nano‐positioning stage of the microscope allowed for fully automated conditions, screening with precise light delivery, and a square‐shaped illumination profile (Figure 1b).

MiniSOG Labeling Kinetics Over Time and Power

To quantify the temporal dynamics of miniSOG‐mediated proximity labeling, we exposed fixed HEK293T cells expressing cytosolic miniSOG–mScarlet to graded 440 nm light doses in the presence of propargylamine, followed by Cy5‐azide under copper(I)‐catalyzed azide–alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC) conditions. Fluorescent signal intensities were normalized to transfection efficiency and fit to single‐exponential growth models. Labeling efficiency displayed a pronounced dependence on excitation power (Figure 1c,d). At moderate irradiances (50–70 mW cm−2), labeling accumulated steadily throughout the 30 min illumination period, yielding the highest endpoint intensities. This is consistent with continuous generation and capture of photo‐oxidized intermediates over this timeframe. By contrast, at higher irradiances (>130 mW cm−2), labeling rapidly approached saturation within the first 5–10 min, after which no further signal increase was detected. Despite the faster onset, these high‐power conditions ultimately produced lower total labeling compared to the productive intermediate regime. Low powers (<30 mW cm−2) showed a low yet measurable signal, indicating subthreshold singlet oxygen generation.

Together, these results delineate two operational regimes for miniSOG‐mediated photolabeling in cells. In the productive regime (≤70 mW cm−2), labeling proceeds gradually with sustained accumulation. In the saturating regime (≥130 mW cm−2), rapid signal onset is counterbalanced by early plateauing, suggesting photobleaching, chromophore inactivation, or local depletion of reactive nucleophiles as limiting factors. These findings highlight that intermediate light doses yield the most efficient and reproducible labeling outcomes, offering critical parameters for designing miniSOG‐based proteomic or transcriptomic mapping experiments.

Illumination‐dependent shifts have been subject to previous explorations, in which miniSOG crystals display excited‐state dynamics in vitro [9]. Under prolonged blue‐light exposure, photodegradation of FMN into lumichrome and oxidation of nearby residues reconfigure the balance between protein quenching and oxygen quenching, enhancing 1O2 generation over time. We were not able to resolve this distinct labeling efficiency enhancement in our own experiments, likely due to our drastically different experimental design. However, our data converge on the principle that miniSOG does not display a linear photon‐to‐1O2 generation relationship, and these specific irradiation regimes that result in nonlinear relationships need to be taken into account when extracting quantitative length data from miniSOG capture experiments.

MiniSOG Labeling Diameter Is Diffraction‐Limited

Having defined the kinetic regimes of miniSOG photolabeling, we next sought to resolve the effective nanoscale labeling diameter in fixed mammalian cells. To this end, HEK293T cells expressing a miniSOG‐vimentin fusion protein were irradiated with blue light (18 mW cm−2 for 20 min). This condition ensured increasing labeling with time, while avoiding premature saturation (Figure S1), yielding a robust signal confined to vimentin filaments to enable quantitative profiling.

Analysis of line‐scan intensity profiles across individual filaments yielded an average full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 619 ± 64 nm (SD, n = 9) when imaged by conventional spinning‐disk confocal microscopy (Figure 2a–c). This apparent width is dominated by the microscope resolution and labeling spread. To explore this diameter further, we implemented direct stochastic optical reconstruction microscopy (dSTORM), which resolved the same structures with substantially narrower distributions, yielding an average FWHM of 125 ± 40 nm (SD, n = 10, Figures 2d,e and S2). The dramatic reduction in apparent labeling diameter when moving from standard confocal microscopy to a single‐molecule approach demonstrates that miniSOG‐dependent labeling remains confined to a sub‐diffraction spatial scale.

Spatial Resolution of the Labeling Diameter

To further explore the effective labeling diameter of miniSOG across the time parameter, we applied dSTORM imaging to quantify the distribution of Cy5 adducts relative to miniSOG irradiation time. To robustly characterize this labeling diameter, we implemented a filament averaging approach. For each straightened filament section (∼2 to 4 µm length), we plotted the mean pixel intensity for every column of the image matrix, generating a cross‐sectional profile along the filament width. The averaged profile was fit to a Gaussian function to determine the FWHM of the labeling distribution (Figure 3a). As expected, labeling intensity and apparent diameter are time‐dependent (Figure 3b). Irradiation for 5 min resulted in a signal confined to a small nanometric region, with this diameter broadening upon increasing irradiation time.

To confirm that the observed expansion was not specific to a single protein or cell type, we extended our analysis beyond HEK293T vimentin filaments to include HeLa cells and a nuclear envelope protein model, LaminB1 (Figure 3c). Both constructs yielded clear Cy5 labeling under our dSTORM protocol, enabling quantitative analysis across cytoskeletal and nuclear contexts (Figure 3c). As a control for reactive oxygen species dependency, labeling was repeated in the presence of increasing concentrations of sodium azide, a singlet oxygen quencher. Inhibition was dose‐dependent, as the apparent diameter decreased from ∼134 nm (±40 nm, SD, n = 25) at 1 mM NaN3 to ∼68 nm at 10 mM (±17 SD, n = 22) (Figure S3), While NaN3 quenching strongly supports a dominant role for 1O2 in miniSOG‐mediated labeling, minor contributions emerge from additional reactive oxygen species such as superoxide generated through flavin photochemistry processes [10]. We note that, in addition to 1O2–driven oxidation chemistry, propargylamine may also undergo minor side reactions with endogenous electrophilic species, including carbonyl‐containing biomolecules and metabolites. Although such contributions are expected to be minor under the conditions used, they may contribute to background labeling independent of singlet oxygen diffusion. We were pleased to observe that, across cells and protein models, means of observed labeling diameters and the impact of increased irradiation time are highly consistent (Figure 3d). Averaging across protein and cell models, diameters spanned from 72 nm (±18 nm, SD, n = 35) at 5 min, 90 nm (±28 nm, SD, n = 37) at 10 min, 132 nm (±47 nm, SD, n = 32) at 20 min, and up to 187 nm (±54, SD, n = 40) at 30 min (Figure 3d,e). Collectively, these results define the operational parameters of miniSOG in fixed mammalian cells and will guide future miniSOG‐based biological exploration.

Finally, we adapted our approach to explore the effective labeling diameter in living cells, as current PL workflows are commonly performed before fixation. For these experiments, we transfected a vimentin‐miniSOG construct into HEK293T cells and then irradiated the cells in the presence of propargyl amine for defined time periods. Finally, cells were fixed to allow for fluorophore conjugation before dSTORM imaging (Figure 4a). Encouragingly, we observed very similar labeling diameters, spanning from 85 nm (±13, SD, n = 16) at 5 min, 94 nm (±15, SD, n = 25) at 10 min, 134 nm (±24, SD, n = 25) at 20 min, and 176 nm (±34, SD, n = 29) at 30 min (Figure 4b). Plotting of labeling diameters obtained with fixed versus live‐cell experiments for the vimentin construct in HEK293T cells (Figure 4c) yielded a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.9978, strongly suggesting that measurements obtained in fixed cells closely recapitulate the in cellulo context.

Such a strong correlation was somewhat unexpected, as live cells are dynamic systems that contain endogenous quenchers such as glutathione and exhibit heterogeneous oxygen partial pressures and molecular crowding. In particular, the lifetime and diffusion of 1O2 are highly sensitive to its local environment. While we cannot fully explain the strong similarity in labeling radius between live and fixed cells, we hypothesize that paraformaldehyde fixation preserves much of the intracellular ultrastructural organization and macromolecular density, thereby maintaining major steric constraints and diffusion barriers. In this context, the effective diffusion range of 1O2 may remain within a similar nanometric regime after fixation, particularly given its intrinsically short lifetime. Consequently, its effective diffusion length may already be close to the lower physical limit imposed by rapid local quenching, thereby reducing observable differences between live and fixed cells. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that our observations are limited to protein structures that do not freely diffuse in cells and therefore may not fully generalize to all molecular targets.

These findings refine the mechanistic understanding of miniSOG as a genetically encoded photocatalyst. Since its introduction as a tool for correlative light and electron microscopy [7], miniSOG has been applied in diverse contexts, including proteomic and transcriptional profiling [5, 6, 8]. Yet, despite broad adoption, quantitative benchmarks for the effective labeling diameter in crowded cellular environments have been lacking. Prior estimates of singlet oxygen diffusion length in aqueous solution (lifetime ∼0.6 μs, corresponding to a diffusion length ∼70 nm) [11] did not account for the high density of biomolecules in cells and the resulting crowded environment. Oakley et al. recently established a super‐resolution framework to measure labeling radii of PL approaches, reporting 54 ± 12 nm for μMap carbene chemistry, 80 ± 28 nm for novel aryl‐azide probes, and 269 ± 41 nm for peroxidase‐based phenoxy radicals [12]. It is crucial to note that these numbers include the length added by antibody tags, which spatially displace the reactive core and artificially increase the apparent labeling diameter from 20 to 40 nm [13]. In this context, miniSOG emerges as an intermediate‐resolution photocatalyst, with an operational labeling range that overlaps with azide‐based μMap variants but remains significantly narrower than classical peroxidase systems. Moreover, due to its small size, miniSOG mitigates the loss in resolution that is commonly experienced with antibody‐dependent methods. Notably, the upper limit of ∼180 nm we observe under extended irradiation places miniSOG within the size scale of sub‐organelle structures and protein assemblies that are poorly captured by either ultra‐confined (carbene‐based) or highly diffuse (peroxidase‐based) probes. Thus, miniSOG uniquely occupies a spatial window that enables interrogation of mesoscale molecular neighborhoods and offers the user a capture length tunable over ∼100 nm upon irradiation modulation.

Conclusion

This work establishes quantitative benchmarks for both the illumination‐dependent kinetics and the nanoscale labeling radius of miniSOG in fixed mammalian cells. Controlled blue‐light exposure reveals distinct catalytic regimes, while dSTORM imaging defines an illumination‐tunable labeling diameter of ∼70–180 nm. These parameters refine the mechanistic understanding of miniSOG as an optogenetic photocatalyst and provide actionable spatial limits for interpreting proteomic and transcriptomic proximity‐labeling datasets. The robustness of these measurements across constructs and cell types suggests that the quantitative standards described here are broadly applicable. Together, these findings offer a practical framework for experimental design and ensure that miniSOG‐based neighborhood mapping is grounded in spatially meaningful biochemical constraints.
