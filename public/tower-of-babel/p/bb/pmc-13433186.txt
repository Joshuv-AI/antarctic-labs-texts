Multimodality imaging-guided diagnosis of aortic annular avulsion with right coronary sinus pseudoaneurysm and aorto–left ventricular communication: a case report
Authors: Lin Gui, Jia-Guang Song, Mei Zhu, Hui-Hui Wang, Cheng-Wei Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAortic annular avulsion with a right coronary sinus (RCS) pseudoaneurysm is a rare, life-threatening cardiac pathology. Multimodality imaging is crucial for diagnosis and surgical planning.Case presentationA 42-year-old woman presented with progressive exertional dyspnea following prior coronary angiography (CAG). Transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) revealed a right coronary sinus pseudoaneurysm and a large interventricular septal dissecting aneurysm (DAIS) communicating with the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT). Cardiac computed tomography angiography (CCTA) delineated the pseudoaneurysm and its relationship to the coronary arteries, and cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) supported septal myocardial involvement. Transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) further characterized annular disruption and deformation of the right coronary cusp (RCC). The patient subsequently underwent bovine pericardial patch repair and aortic valve replacement. At the 3-month postoperative follow-up, recovery was favorable, with a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 55%; however, pseudoaneurysm recurrence was detected at 6 months. The patient declined reoperation and died 8 months after the initial surgery.ConclusionWe report an exceptionally rare adult case of aortic annular avulsion with a right coronary sinus pseudoaneurysm and aorto–left ventricular communication. Integrated multimodality imaging facilitated diagnostic refinement and guided operative planning, underscoring the need for reinforced root/annular repair and structured mid-term imaging surveillance.

Introduction

Aortic annular avulsion is an uncommon but catastrophic form of aortic root disruption, and its coexistence with a right coronary sinus (RCS) pseudoaneurysm is particularly rare and potentially fatal (1, 2). A majority of the reported cases occur in the context of infective endocarditis or prior surgical trauma (3, 4). However, when annular disruption is accompanied by interventricular septal dissection and aorto–left ventricular communication, diagnosis becomes challenging and is frequently misinterpreted as isolated valvular disease or infection (5). Pathophysiologically, a pseudoaneurysm represents a contained focal rupture confined by adventitia and perivascular fibrous tissue rather than a true three-layered dilatation, thereby creating a pulsatile cavity that remains in communication with the aortic lumen and may extend toward adjacent structures (6, 7).

In suspected root-septal pathology, multimodality imaging provides complementary anatomic and functional information that can refine the differential and inform operative strategy. TTE rapidly screens for abnormal anatomy and hemodynamic consequences, while TEE refines annular and cusp assessments. CCTA defines the three-dimensional lesion extent and its proximity to the coronary arteries, and CMR adds tissue characterization when septal involvement is considered. Together, these modalities can provide a coherent preoperative roadmap for defining the extent of the lesion, coronary relationships, and septal involvement.

Accordingly, we report an adult case with no evidence of infective endocarditis in which multimodality imaging enabled preoperative delineation and operative correlation of a rare root-septal phenotype.

Case description

A 42-year-old woman presented to our hospital with progressive exertional dyspnea following a 4-month history of chest tightness accompanied by tooth pain 4 months. Two months before admission, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) and coronary angiography (CAG) performed at a local hospital were unremarkable (Figures 1B,C). Despite these findings, her symptoms of exertional dyspnea progressively worsened over the subsequent 2 months, prompting presentation to our hospital (Figure 1A). She had no significant family or past medical history. Physical examination revealed a grade III biphasic murmur that was clearly heard over the aortic area. The remainder of the physical examination was unremarkable. Electrocardiography revealed a complete right bundle branch block.

(A) Timeline of symptoms, diagnostic work-up, and interventions. (B) TTE performed 2 months before admission showed mild aortic valve regurgitation. (C) Coronary angiography (CAG) 2 months before admission was unremarkable.

On admission, TTE exhibited left ventricular dilation, prolapse of the RCC, and a 1.2-cm pseudoaneurysm in the right sinus of Valsalva (Figures 2A,B). CCTA further delineated the three-dimensional extent of the pseudoaneurysm and aorto–left ventricular communication. No evidence of coronary compression was identified (Figure 2C, Supplementary Figure 1A). CMR T1 mapping demonstrated linear and patchy T1 elevation within the mid-ventricular interventricular septum (Supplementary Figure 1B). Laboratory testing revealed an elevated B-type natriuretic peptide level of 5,829 pg/mL. Evaluation for infective endocarditis did not indicate an infectious etiology, with four sets of blood cultures remaining negative. The patient subsequently underwent median sternotomy with thoracoscopic assistance, followed by bovine pericardial patch reconstruction of the RCS wall and LVOT, together with aortic valve replacement with a 21-mm St. Jude Regent mechanical prosthesis under cardiopulmonary bypass. Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) provided high-resolution delineation of the annulus and cusp morphology (Supplementary Video S1). Multiplanar reconstruction (MPR) of the TEE dataset demonstrated deformed morphology of the RCC that appeared compressed relative to the non-coronary cusp (NCC) and the left coronary cusp (LCC; Figure 2D). Direct intraoperative inspection corroborated the deformed morphology of the RCC (Figure 2E).

Intraoperative examination confirmed the diagnosis of aortic annular avulsion and pseudoaneurysm of the RCS. The ventricular septal fragment and the avulsed right coronary annulus were resected, and the RCS wall and LVOT were reconstructed using a bovine pericardial patch. Aortic valve replacement was performed after reconstructing the right coronary annulus. Histopathological examination of the aortic valve showed no evidence of infective endocarditis (Figure 2F).

The early postoperative course was initially favorable. Perioperative antimicrobial prophylaxis with cefazolin was administered in accordance with institutional practice. The patient received guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure, including spironolactone, furosemide, and metoprolol, with adjunctive myocardial support (creatine phosphate sodium) and dose adjustments according to clinical status.

At the 3-month follow-up, TEE and CCTA showed no significant abnormalities, with an LVEF of 55% (Figures 3A,B). However, at the 6-month follow-up, imaging demonstrated re-dehiscence at the RCS wall/LVOT suture line, resulting in a recurrent pseudoaneurysm adjacent to the reconstructed aortic root, with an LVEF of 47% (Figure 3C). Unlike the initial lesion, the recurrent cavity did not communicate with the left ventricle. The patient declined reoperation and died 8 months after initial surgery.

Discussion

This case illustrates an exceptionally rare adult root–septal phenotype: aortic annular avulsion with a right coronary sinus (RCS) pseudoaneurysm, septal propagation (DAIS), and aorto–left ventricular communication. Aortic annular avulsion and RCS pseudoaneurysm are most associated with infective endocarditis or prior surgical/iatrogenic trauma (8, 9). In this case, repeated blood cultures and histopathological examination showed no evidence of infective endocarditis. Together with the absence of clinical and intraoperative features of infection, these findings made infective endocarditis highly unlikely, although culture-negative infection cannot be fully excluded. Although the lesion was not detected on TTE and CAG performed 2 months earlier, this temporal relationship does not establish causality. Nevertheless, the possibility that coronary angiography served as a procedural trigger cannot be entirely excluded. The morphological alteration of the right coronary cusp observed in this case may indicate an underlying structural vulnerability, although this remains hypothesis generating. In this case, the high-pressure flow within the pseudoaneurysm did not induce pericardial tamponade or acute right coronary artery compression. Instead, it propagated along the septal dissection plane (DAIS) with the development of communication physiology, contributing to progressive left-sided heart failure.

Multimodality imaging provides complementary anatomical, functional, and tissue information throughout the diagnostic process (10). TTE enables rapid recognition of abnormal anatomy and hemodynamic consequences (11). TEE provides superior visualization of the annular–cusp morphology (12). CCTA delineated avulsion extent and coronary relationships (13), and CMR T1 mapping demonstrated septal myocardial involvement and tissue remodeling (14). Together, these imaging techniques provided incremental diagnostic information for characterizing complex root–septal pathology (15).

Surgical treatment focused on restoration of annular continuity, reconstruction of the RCS wall and LVOT using a bovine pericardial patch, and an aortic valve replacement. These procedures aim to restore structural integrity, eliminate abnormal communication, and reduce the risk of recurrent instability (16, 17).

Compared with the case reported by Zhou et al. (18), in which the patient underwent aortic root replacement, our patient was treated with root-preserving repair given the localized nature of the lesion and the preserved integrity of the remaining aortic root. The recurrence observed at 6 months, characterized by localized RCS/LVOT suture-line dehiscence, without recurrent aorto-left ventricular communication suggests that long-term durability of localized repair may remain challenging in the presence of annular tissue fragility.

The postoperative course is instructive: Early imaging improvements did not guarantee durability (19). The recurrence/re-dehiscence of the RCS/LVOT suture line without clinical or pathological evidence of endocarditis may represent localized repair-site failure at the root/annulus–patch interface in the setting of compromised tissue quality or stress concentration rather than infection.

While sharing clinical and imaging features with prior adult reports (20–22), the present case is distinguished by the integration of operative correlation, tissue assessment, and longitudinal follow-up in the absence of infective endocarditis.

As expected, this report is limited by its single-patient nature and the constraints of retrospective data. Nevertheless, it offers a transferable framework for diagnosing and managing similar root-septal phenotypes. Integrated multimodality imaging enabled imaging-driven reconstruction and stepwise operative planning, while the observed recurrence underscores the importance of durable root/annular stabilization and a deliberate mid-term imaging checkpoint (e.g., ~6 months).
