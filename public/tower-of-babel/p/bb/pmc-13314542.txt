Variable Aβ tracer uptake in hyperostosis frontalis interna: implications for brain Aβ PET/CT interpretation
Authors: Joseph M. Ryan, Hina J. Shah, Michael P. McCormick, Alberto Serrano‐Pozo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONHyperostosis frontalis interna (HFI) is a relatively common incidental finding on head computed tomography (CT). We report two patients with HFI identified on amyloid‐beta (Aβ) positron emission tomography (PET)/CT who demonstrated distinct patterns of tracer uptake within the affected calvarium.METHODSBoth patients underwent 18F‐florbetaben PET/CT imaging to assess eligibility for Aβ‐targeting therapy.RESULTSPatient 1 was an 85‐year‐old woman with amnestic mild cognitive impairment whose 18F‐florbetaben PET/CT scan revealed a Centiloid value of 59 and marked tracer uptake within the calvarium affected by HFI. Patient 2 was a 76‐year‐old woman with a Centiloid value of 82 who exhibited HFI on CT but no 18F‐florbetaben uptake within the HFI region.DISCUSSIONOff‐target tracer retention within the calvarium may represent a source of visual and quantitative misinterpretation in Aβ and tau PET scans in patients with HFI, particularly older women. Careful visual inspection and adjustment of automated quantification masks are essential to avoid inaccurate Centiloid and standardized uptake value ratio measurements.

INTRODUCTION

Brain amyloid‐beta (Aβ) positron emission tomography (PET)/computed tomography (CT) imaging has emerged as a pivotal tool in the diagnosis and management of Alzheimer's disease (AD) and other neurodegenerative disorders. By enabling in vivo visualization of Aβ plaque deposition, this imaging modality aids in early diagnosis and enhances the stratification of patient management strategies. 1 , 2 , 3 Importantly, it also helps differentiate AD from other causes of dementia, thereby informing therapeutic decisions.

Although brain Aβ PET imaging is primarily focused on cerebral Aβ burden, incidental findings on PET/CT imaging are not uncommon. Increased Aβ tracer uptake has been described in extracerebral structures including the scalp, skull, salivary and parotid glands, as well as in extra‐ and intracranial masses, such as meningiomas. 3 , 4 , 5

Frontal calvarial hyperostosis (also known as hyperostosis frontalis interna [HFI]) is an incidental finding often associated with cerebral parenchymal atrophy but also potentially linked to conditions like Paget's disease, hyperparathyroidism, and other metabolic disorders. 6 Cerebral atrophy is commonly observed in patients with AD undergoing brain Aβ PET imaging. 4 This report highlights two cases of HFI with differing patterns of Aβ tracer uptake, providing new insights into this phenomenon.

CASE DESCRIPTION

Patient 1: An 85‐year‐old female patient presenting for baseline Aβ PET imaging with 18F‐florbetaben prior to initiating Aβ‐targeting therapy with donanemab. 7 , 8 Imaging revealed moderate to frequent cerebral cortical neuritic Aβ plaque deposition (Centiloid value of 59), alongside notable Aβ tracer uptake within the HFI region (Figure 1). The HFI region had not changed in volume or morphology compared to a prior 18F‐fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) PET/CT performed 6 years earlier (not shown), which showed no significant calvarial FDG uptake.

Marked Aβ tracer uptake in calvarial hyperostosis in patient 1. Representative axial (A) and sagittal (B) slices showing distribution of the Aβ tracer 18F‐florbetaben on fused PET/CT (left) and PET (right) images. Aβ, amyloid‐beta; CT, computed tomography; PET, positron emission tomography.

Patient 2: A 76‐year‐old female patient undergoing baseline Aβ imaging with 18F‐florbetaben prior to initiating Aβ‐targeting therapy with lecanemab. Imaging demonstrated moderate to frequent cerebral cortical neuritic Aβ plaque deposition (Centiloid value of 82); however, no tracer uptake was observed within the HFI region (Figure 2).

Lack of significant Aβ tracer uptake in calvarial hyperostosis in Patient 2. Representative axial slices showing distribution of the Aβ tracer 18F‐florbetaben on fused PET/CT (left) and PET (right) images. β, amyloid‐beta; CT, computed tomography; PET, positron emission tomography.

Of note, both patients underwent imaging with the same radiotracer (18F‐florbetaben, brand name: Neuraceq) 3 , 9 and were administered similar activities (8.4 mCi and 8.3 mCi, respectively). Radiotracer uptake times were also comparable at 73 minutes for Patient 1 and 68 minutes for Patient 2.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: Amyloid‐beta (Aβ) positron emission tomography (PET) imaging is commonly used to detect cerebral Aβ deposition in Alzheimer's disease. Prior literature has described various sources of extracerebral Aβ tracer uptake, and that hyperostosis frontalis interna (HFI) can demonstrate uptake variability. However, reports describing this phenomenon and its implications for interpretation remain limited and, specifically, the potential for misinterpretation during routine clinical assessment has not been well characterized. Interpretation: In this report, we present two patients with differing amounts of 18F‐florbetaben uptake within HFI. These findings highlight the potential for extracerebral tracer uptake to mimic or obscure adjacent cortical uptake and emphasize the importance of careful anatomic correlation to avoid misinterpretation, especially when automated imaging quantification is used. Future directions: Improved recognition and characterization of extracerebral Aβ tracer uptake is essential to ensure accurate interpretation of brain Aβ PET imaging. Further investigation may help refine interpretation guidelines and improve diagnostic confidence.

DISCUSSION

Hyperostosis frontalis interna (HFI) is generally considered an incidental finding characterized by excessive bone deposition and remodeling of the frontal calvarial inner table. 6 Estimates of prevalence in the general population range from 5% to 12%, although women (especially older adults) are more frequently affected than men, with prevalence in post‐menopausal women approaching ≈ 50%. 10 The etiology remains unclear, with the only definitive epidemiologic associations being female sex, older age, and post‐menopausal status. 6 , 10

These two cases reveal an intriguing differential pattern of Aβ tracer uptake in HFI. In Patient 1, uptake within the hyperostotic region may suggest a potential pathophysiological link, possibly reflecting localized changes in the bone or adjacent tissues. In contrast, Patient 2 showed no such uptake, raising questions regarding the heterogeneity of Aβ deposition in HFI. Aβ tracer uptake is frequently observed in extracranial locations on brain Aβ PET/CT scans, including salivary glands and the base of the skull. 4 Tracer uptake in the rest of the calvaria is not typically as prominent as that seen in the skull base but can be variably present. However, given the unclear etiology of HFI, the possible mechanisms responsible for calvarial Aβ (or tau) radiotracer uptake are not well understood.

The calvarial uptake of the Aβ radiotracers (18F‐florbetapir [FBP], 18F‐florbetaben [FBB], 11C‐Pittsburgh compound B [PiB]) and the tau radiotracer 18F‐flortaucipir (FTP) tends to follow a similar demographic pattern as HFI, with uptake most commonly seen in women, as well as in patients with lower calvarial bone density, which tends to associate with older age. 11 , 12 Recent work by Flores et al. reported that 16% of patients undergoing FTP scans show high signal in the skull, 82% of whom were women. Interestingly, while HFI was highly associated with increased calvarial tracer binding, only 21% of women with elevated calvarial binding were diagnosed with HFI. 11

Calvarial tracer binding has been attributed to spontaneous defluorination of the 18F‐based radiotracers, however the high tracer radiochemical purity and the fact that high calvarial tracer binding can be seen with non–18F‐based tracers such as 11C‐PiB suggest additional underlying mechanisms. There is some evidence that clearance of cerebrospinal fluid Aβ may occur via perivascular and lymphatic pathways in the skull base; 13 , 14 however, whether the degree of calvarial uptake outside of the skull base correlates with the efficiency of Aβ clearance is not known. Despite the lack of clearly delineated mechanisms, efforts to further explore this phenomenon are ongoing. Of note, both patients were of the same sex, had similar age (85 vs. 76), presented with the same indication (i.e., clinically suspected AD, pre‐Aβ‐targeting therapy baseline imaging), and had studies performed with nearly identical parameters (e.g., same radiotracer, similar dose and uptake times, etc.). Accordingly, the observed differential uptake appears to be driven by factors other than just demographic and technical variables. Since both patients were elderly, post‐menopausal women, and these represent the patient population with the highest prevalence of both HFI and calvarial Aβ tracer binding, the observed differential uptake may be clinically relevant. There were apparent differences in degree of hyperostosis burden, with Patient 1 exhibiting a greater volume of hyperostosis than Patient 2. However, the stability of HFI over time in Patient 1, demonstrated by comparison to an FDG PET/CT scan performed 6 years earlier (not shown), suggests that the observed uptake is not due to ongoing/evolving or subacute osseous changes.

Patient 2 subsequently had a follow‐up brain Aβ PET/CT imaging 1 year after initiating lecanemab treatment, which showed slightly decreased cerebral Aβ uptake, suggesting partial treatment response (Centiloid value of 63, compared to 82 at baseline), as well as continued absence of calvarial uptake. Patient 1 had not yet had 1‐year follow‐up imaging performed, which limits assessment of stability and degree of calvarial Aβ tracer signal change across different time points. Recent work suggests that skull binding of the tau PET radiotracer FTP tends to be individual specific, associated with female sex, and stable in degree of uptake at different time points. 11 As more patients undergo brain Aβ PET imaging and are treated with Aβ‐targeting therapies over longer time periods, it will be interesting to determine whether changes in patterns of extracerebral tracer uptake occur, and to what extent there is correlation with an individual's therapeutic response and duration of treatment. Future work exploring longitudinal Aβ tracer uptake on patients with positive calvarial binding, as in our Patient 1, may provide additional insights, for example whether calvarial Aβ tracer uptake changes over time and/or diminishes with treatment, or whether it remains stable as seen with FTP. 11

Regardless of the underlying mechanisms, differential Aβ tracer binding in the skull (or indeed any extra‐cerebral sites, but especially those adjacent to the cerebral cortex) is an important potential interpretation pitfall for radiologists, underscoring the need for careful interpretation of Aβ PET imaging, particularly in patients with atypical findings such as HFI. Incidental tracer‐specific uptake patterns may also have implications for the accuracy of automated quantification workflows, which rely on image segmentation and activity quantification. 3 , 4 , 15 If hyperostotic regions are included in tracer quantification without physician oversight, there is a risk of overestimating disease burden. Accordingly, the radiologist or other interpreting physician should always perform visual assessment of the images separate from automated segmentation and quantitative assessment. Furthermore, it is critical to diligently scrutinize the results of segmentation before database comparison can be confidently performed. Specifically, if regions of extracerebral uptake (such as tracer‐avid HFI) are included in the masked boundaries, the regions of interest must be adjusted to exclude the skull or other non‐cerebral structures. If, for example, tracer‐avid HFI is included in the masked boundaries used for database comparison, the standardized uptake value ratios and Centiloid values could be erroneously elevated, risking false‐positive interpretations. Awareness of this potential pitfall is therefore essential to competent and reliable reporting of brain Aβ PET/CT imaging studies.

CONCLUSION

Brain Aβ PET/CT imaging continues to play a crucial role in the diagnostic landscape of AD and other neurodegenerative disorders. 15 , 16 These two cases highlight the importance of recognizing and accurately characterizing atypical findings, such as differential Aβ tracer uptake in extracerebral locations and conditions such as HFI. Understanding incidental uptake patterns can refine imaging interpretation, improve diagnostic accuracy, and enrich our knowledge of the potential interplay between neurodegenerative diseases and skeletal changes. Continued investigation of these phenomena will enhance diagnostic capabilities and ultimately improve patient outcomes.
