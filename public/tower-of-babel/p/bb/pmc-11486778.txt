The character of Lake Kishkensor contamination in the area of underground nuclear explosions at the Semipalatinsk Test Site territory
Authors: Almira Aidarkhanova, Lyubov Timonova, Assan Aidarkhanov, Valeriy Monayenko, Alibek Iskenov, Sergey Subbotin, Sergey Pronin, Natalya Belykh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The timely identification of areas where man-made radionuclides migrate through water streams is highly important for the territory of the former Semipalatinsk Test Site since the aquatic environment is currently a source of secondary contamination of environment. This article presents research findings on radioactively contaminated Lake Kishkensor located at the Semipalatinsk Test Site territory. As a result of this research, a new locally contaminated spot was discovered in the vicinity of the “Balapan” test site. Lake Kishkensor was found to be contaminated with man-made 3H and 90Sr. The 3H activity concentration in surface waters reached 430 kBq/L, and the concentration of 90Sr reached 100 Bq/L. In the sediments, the 3H activity concentration reached 700 kBq/kg, while that of 90Sr and 239+240Pu reached 310 Bq/kg and 250 Bq/kg, respectively. Ground water was found to be a source of surface water and sediment contamination. The monitoring results showed that the contamination level of the lake largely depended on the season and the inflow of contaminated ground water.

Introduction

To date, the consequences of nuclear explosions at the Semipalatinsk Test Site (STS) have been well studied. All significant areas of radioactive contamination have been identified, and the content of radionuclides in soil (Timonova et al., 2015, 2020b; Krivitskiy et al., 2022; Panitskiy et al., 2023), plants (Larionova et al., 2017, 2018, 2021), air (Lyakhova et al., 2012; Turchenko et al., 2023), surface water (Aidarkhanova et al., 2018, 2022), and underground water (Gorbunova & Subbotin, 2012; Subbotin et al., 2013) has been explored.

The “Balapan” test site, located in the southeastern part of the STS, was used for underground nuclear explosions in boreholes. Previously, it was believed that the main objects with high contents of man-made radionuclides at this site are the “Atomic” lake (Aktayev et al., 2019; Subbotin et al., 2020; Timonova et al., 2020a) and the Shagan River (Aidarkhanov et al., 2013; Aktayev et al., 2024).

However, in the area of the “Balapan” test site, there are a number of natural lakes. Some of them periodically produced on the surface of steppe saline soils after a spring flood or short-term rainfall flood. In the summer, these lakes may dry completely. Lake Kishkensor, 1500 × 1000 m in size, located close to the western boundary of the “Balapan” test site (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Location of Lake Kishkensor in the vicinity of the “Balapan” site

Location of Lake Kishkensor in the vicinity of the “Balapan” site

In 2016, water contamination was detected at Lake Kishkensor with man-made 3H and 90Sr. The 3H activity concentration varied from 0.2 to 5 kBq/L, and the 90Sr concentration ranged from 1 to 5 Bq/L (Information report, 2016). The presence of these radionuclides in the water of Lake Kishkensor proved to be rather unexpected.

Radionuclide 3H is one of the most common man-made radionuclides at the STS territory. During the nuclear tests at the STS, a large amount of 3H was formed (about millions Bq), which, despite the short half-life of 3H (T1/2 = 12 years) and the closure of the STS more than 30 years ago, the 3H concentration in environmental components can reach several hundred thousand Bq (Timonova et al., 2024; Turchenko et al., 2024). Due to its high migration capacity, 3H is omnipresent and is present not only in water, soil, and plants, but also in the air in the form of gaseous compounds and tritiated water vapor (HTO), which leads to widespread distribution through the water cycle and the food chain (Marchenko et al., 2020; Feng et al., 2023).

Presumably, Lake Kishkensor could have become contaminated in two ways:Due to radioactive fallout during air and aboveground STS tests.Due to the entry of contaminated ground waters into the daylight surface from the direction of the “Balapan” test site.

The likelihood of Lake Kishkensor contamination as a result of radioactive fallout is attributed to the fact that more than 100 aboveground and air nuclear tests were conducted at the “Experimental Field” site. Owing to this, elevated concentrations of man-made radionuclides could have been produced on the daylight surface of the study area (Nuclear tests in the USSR, 1997). According to comprehensive research findings (The report, 2018), the 137Cs activity concentration in topsoil samples from the northwestern part of the “Balapan” test site was on the order of 30–60 Bq/kg, that of 90Sr was 9–200 Bq/kg, and that of 239+240Pu was 4–620 Bq/kg.

The possible contamination due to the entry of ground water is attributed to the fact that two hydrogeological complexes exist at the “Balapan” test site. The first includes pore waters occurring in local hydrogeological basins. Waters reach 50 m deep. The second is incorporated in the regional hygrogeological system. These data include fracture waters from the Paleozoic basement and waters from the Mesozoic weathering crust. The thickness of the aquifer reaches 150 m. Fracture waters are artesian with a head of up to 80 m. These conditions predetermine ground water movement through impact zones of tectonic faults that excessively fracture and are more permeable to ground water (Subbotin et al., 2011).

Thus, to determine the pattern and mechanism of ecosystem contamination of Lake Kishkensor with man-made radionuclides, the following research was conducted:The study of the distribution of man-made radionuclides in soils and sedimentsThe evaluation of the ground water contamination level (ground and fracture waters)The study of water exchange processesThe monitoring of changes in ground water contamination with radionuclides

Materials and research techniques

Sediments and soils (2016)

For research into man-made radionuclides in sediments and soils, the inundable zone between the rim and the boundary of the surface waterflood was outlined on the map. The sediment sampling points were located at nodes of a 150 × 150-m rectangular grid. The soil sampling points were located at the nodes of a 250 × 250 m grid. The sampling scheme is depicted in Fig. 2.Fig. 2The sampling scheme for sediments and soils at Lake Kishkensor

The sampling scheme for sediments and soils at Lake Kishkensor

Sixty sediments and 48 soil samples were collected at the selected locations. Samples were taken using a special metal tool (scoop). This tool allows to take samples of the surface layer to a depth of 0–5 cm. One sample was taken from each point.

For research into the deep distribution of radionuclides in sediments, 8 sampling points were identified (Fig. 2). Sediments were taken from drained areas, near water manifestations. Studying the deep distribution of radionuclides was carried out in the form of an unbroken column using a cylindrical sampler. The sampler consists of two halves, thanks to which samples can be easily extracted. Sampler dimensions: internal diameter is 5 cm; column height is 20 cm. A metal flap is provided at the headboard of the sampler to prevent the sample from falling out during sampling. The selection of the sediment column was carried out pointwise in an open place without vegetation and visual destruction of the surface layer. Three layers of 20 cm were selected at each point (0–20 cm, 20–40 cm, 40–60 cm).

Ground waters (2016)

Research into ground waters was undertaken in locations where ground and fracture water travel. For this purpose, 9 hydrogeological wells were drilled. Points were arranged at nearby “warfare” boreholes in the adjacent territory (Fig. 3). Ten liters of water were taken from each drilled well.Fig. 3Research areas of ground waters

Research areas of ground waters

Auger-type boreholes (SK-31, SK-27, SK-32, SK-39, SK-34, SK-35, and SK-37) are used for sampling ground water. Air- hammer boreholes (SKP-1/16 and SKP-2/16) are intended for use in researching fracture-vein waters. The depths of the boreholes ranged from − 1.5 to 16.5 m.

Surface waters (2018–2020)

Surface waters were sampled with a polyethylene water intake container. The volume of each sample was 10 L. During the field work and monitoring observations, surface waters were traced only in the southern part of the lake.

Study of water exchange processes

The use of an isotope hydrology technique is currently a direct method for obtaining information on the conditions under which various types of waters are produced. The method is based on the study of the ratio of stable isotopes of hydrogen (2H/1H) and oxygen (18O/16O) (For, 1989, Vasilchuk & Kotlyakov, 2000, Craig, 1961). A laboratory analysis to determine the ratios of stable isotopes 2H/1H and 18O/16O in water samples was carried out to study water exchange processes (Toktaganov et al., 2018).

Laboratory analysis

The 3H, 137Cs, 90Sr, and 239+240Pu contents and the ratios of stable isotopes 2H/1H and 18O/16O were determined in samples collected in accordance with established guidelines and instructions using calibrated equipment.

Water

To determine 3H, water was filtered through filter paper to remove coarse suspended matter. Then, an aliquot of 20 ml was taken, and the distillation process was carried out at a temperature of 105 °C until dry salts were formed. An aliquot of 5 ml was taken from the resulting distillate and mixed with an “Ultima Gold” scintillator. The resulting sample was shaken to a homogeneous medium and transferred for 3H analysis by liquid scintillation spectrometry on a “TRI-CARB 2900 TR” β-spectrometer from “PerkinElmer” (USA) (ISO 9698: 2019). The minimum detectable concentration (MDC) 3H in water is 0.006 kBq/L.

For the determination of 239+240Pu, 90Sr, and 137Cs, water samples with a volume of 10 L were filtered to remove coarse suspended matter and organic residues through a double layer of paper filter. The water sample filtrate was preserved by adding nitric or hydrochloric acids to pH < 2.

The water samples preparation with the 10 L volume was carried out by concentration using the sequential co-precipitation method (Measurement Procedure, 2012). To control the chemical yield of radionuclides 239+240Pu, 90Sr, and 137Cs, isotopic tracers 242Pu, 85Sr, and 134Cs were added to the water sample.

At the first stage, 239+240Pu co-precipitation with ferric (III) hydroxide was carried out: for this, 2 ml of a 20% solution of ferric (III) chloride as a carrier and 20% solution NaOH were added to the water sample to pH = 7. The resulting precipitate was left to settle for at least 12 h and filtered. The precipitate was then dissolved in 7.5 M nitric acid and sent for radiochemical analysis to determine the 239+240Pu concentration. For the separate of plutonium isotopes, the strongly basic anion exchange resin AB17-8 was used. A spectrometric source was prepared by electroplating plutonium isotopes on a steel plate at a current of 1.4 A for 1 h. To determine 239+240Pu, α-spectrometric measurement of the prepared sample was carried out on an “Alpha Analyst 7200” α-spectrometer. Chemical yield of 239+240Pu was determined with 242Pu tracer. MDC of Pu isotopes is 0.0002 Bq/L.

At the second stage, 90Sr was co-precipitated with calcium carbonate: for this purpose, a 20% solution Na2CO3 was added to the filtrate obtained after 239+240Pu precipitation to pH = 9–10. In this case, with the Lake Kishkensor water, the addition of the carrier of a stable Ca2+ was not required, since this water has a high mineralization degree about 30 g/l, which was previously determined using a “Mettler Toledo Seven Compact LtS 230-Ki” conductometer. The resulting precipitate is settled for at least 12 h and filtered, dissolved in 6 M hydrochloric acid, and radiochemically analyzed. Radiochemical separation of strontium isotopes consists of precipitation with various carriers, then accumulation of daughter 90Y for at least 14 days, its separation, and determination of the 90Sr concentration by daughter 90Y. The 90Sr concentration was determined by the liquid scintillation method on a “TRI-CARB 2900 TR” β-spectrometer. Chemical yield of 90Sr was determined with 85Sr tracer. MDC 90Sr in water is 0.01 Bq/L.

At the third stage, 137Cs was co-precipitated with copper hexacyanoferrate: for this purpose, a cocktail containing 8 ml of 0.25 M solution K4[Fe(CN)6] and 10 ml of 0.5 M solution CuSO4 in an acidic medium was added to the filtrate obtained after 90Sr precipitation. The resulting precipitate was left to settle for at least 12 h and filtered, then dried, packed, and γ-spectrometrically measured. To determine the 137Cs concentration in water, a γ-spectrometer from “ORTEC” (USA) with a semiconductor detector “GEM25P4-70,” with a relative efficiency of gamma radiation registration of 25%, with a crystal of ultra-pure germanium, analyzer was used pulses “DSPEC-502 TM” (USA) and with the software package “MAESTRO”. MDC 137Cs in water is 0.01 Bq/L.

The ratio of stable isotopes 2H/1H and 18O/16O was measured using an “LGR 912–0008” laser spectrometer. The international standard “VSMOW-2” (Vienna Standard Mean Ocean Water, IAEA) was used as calibration standards. The precision of the 2H/1H and 18O/16O measurements was ± 1 ‰ and ± 0.5 ‰, respectively.

Sediments and soil

Preparation of sediments for determination of 3H was carried out as follows (Serzhanova et al., 2018). A sample weighing 200 g was taken from the selected sample of sediments. The sample was placed in a round-bottomed flask. The flask was installed in a heating mantle for distillation. The distillation process of sediments was carried out at a temperature of 105 °C until the formation of distillate completely ceased. An aliquot of 5 ml was taken from the resulting distillate. An “Ultima Gold” scintillator was added to the aliquot, shaken, and transferred for β-spectrometric analysis. MDC 3H is 0.1 Bq/kg.

Determination of the 137Cs, 90Sr, and 239+240Pu content in sediments and soil was carried out using standard procedure (Krivitskiy et al., 2022). The determination of 137Cs content in sediments and soil was carried out by γ-spectrometric method (MP 5.06.001.98 RK, 1998). First, large inclusions (stones, roots) were removed from each sediment sample. Next, the samples were dried in an oven until completely dry. The dried samples were sifted through a sieve with a mesh size of 2 mm. Then, using the quartering method, a sample weighing 300–400 g was selected and transferred for gamma spectrometric analysis of 137Cs. MDC 137Cs is 1 Bq/kg.

Radiochemical isolation of 239+240Pu from sediment and soil samples (MP 06–7-98, 1998) includes the following steps: introduction of the 242Pu isotopic label; complete acid decomposition of the sample; isolation and radiochemical purification of Pu isotopes; and obtaining an α-source by electrolytic deposition. The specific activity of 239+240Pu in sediment samples was measured using an “Alpha Analyst 7200” α-spectrometer. MDC 239+240Pu is 0.1 Bq/kg.

Radiochemical separation of 90Sr from sediment and soil samples (MP 06–7-98, 1998) includes the following steps: introduction of the 85Sr isotopic label; complete acid decomposition of the sample; isolation and radiochemical purification of Sr isotopes; and 2-week accumulation of 90Y, its isolation and measurement of activity. The specific activity of 90Sr in sediment samples was measured using a “Quantulus 1220” β-spectrometer. MDC 90Sr is 0.7 Bq/kg.

The radionuclide determinations were submitted to quality checks. One test and one “blank” sample were added to each batch of samples analyzed (10 samples each batch). The test sample was selected randomly from the set of samples included in the batch, while the “blank” sample was prepared in advance from samples collected from territories of “background” content of technogenic radionuclides. The test and “blank” samples were analyzed simultaneously with all the rest of samples. The test sample was intended to control the quality and repeatability of the analytical results, while the “blank” sample was used to control hypothetic sample cross-contaminations.

The study of water exchange processes

To determine how contaminated waters of Lake Kishkensor formed, the ratios of stable isotopes 2H/1H and 18O/16O in the waters sampled were determined. To analyze the conditions for water formation, the measurement results are plotted on the δ18O–δ2H diagram with the Local Meteoric Water Line (LMWL), which is built on the basis of the Global Meteoric Water Line, taking into account stable isotopes in atmospheric precipitation of the researching region. The results are given in Fig. 6.Fig. 6Stable isotope ratio analysis

Stable isotope ratio analysis

Between June (point 21.06.2017) and July (point 11.07.2017), markedly greater concentrations of isotopes are noted from − 111.4 to − 68.8‰ for δ2H and from − 17.6 to − 6.0‰ for δ18O. Thereafter, in August (point 28.08.2017), the concentration of isotopes became lower, down to − 90.7 ‰ for δ2H and − 11.5 ‰ for δ18O. These changes indicate that the waters sampled in June are characterized by residual waters produced by snowmelt because the region of the isotope ratio distribution corresponds to the atmospheric fallout in the winter. Next, an essentially heavier concentration in June is related to the evaporation process during which the residual water concentrates heavier isotopes. In August, water stable isotope ratios are supposed to be at heavier concentrations corresponding to the atmospheric fallout in August. Nevertheless, the concentrations in stable isotopes of surface waters are observed to decrease again in August. Such a change in the ratio of water isotopic composition is related to the discharge of relatively light ground waters into surface waters.

Variations in the concentrations of man-made radionuclides in surface waters

As observed, 3H and 90Sr activity concentrations, depending on the sampling season, vary widely. The 3H content in 2018 varied from 110 to 430 kBq/L, in 2019 from 100 to 200 kBq/L, and in 2020 from 175 to 190 kBq/L. The highest values of 3H were observed in spring and autumn.

The 90Sr concentration ranged from 8 to 100 Bq/L in 2018, 10 to 17 Bq/L in 2019, and 3 to 14 Bq/L in 2020. No quantitative values of 137Cs and 239−240Pu were registered.

Discussion

3H content

Elevated concentrations of 3H on the sediments surface are localized in the southern lake part, pointing to a place where contaminated ground water enters. This is proven by the vertical distribution of 3H, which to increase with depth at the points with the highest content.

Research into ground waters showed that the 3H activity concentration in fracture waters did not exceed the 3H content in surface or ground waters. Hence, 3H toward Lake Kishkensor does not migrate through channels of geological faults from “warfare” borehole 1366. Moreover, the relatively shallow occurrence of ground water (1–2.5 m) and the highest content of 3H therein point to its entry as a result of ground water level rise.

90Sr content

In most cases, the 90Sr concentration on the sediments surface is ubiquitously distributed at levels ranging from 1 to 90 Bq/kg. In the vertical distribution, 90Sr is notable for elevated values in the top layers and for minima in the lower layers (40–60 cm deep). All of these findings indicate a surface source of 90Sr formation. Moreover, 90Sr maxima were detected at two points in the southern part of the lake (230 and 310 Bq/kg), which indicates that contamination occurred due to the entry of ground water. The entry through ground waters is proven by the presence of 90Sr quantitative values in ground waters of the southern part (up to 4 Bq/kg) and elevated values in surface water (up to 100 Bq/kg). And the 90Sr intake with radioactive fallout has been proven by its presence at the level of 9–200 Bq/kg in this region (The report, 2018).

Monitoring observations showed that depending on the sampling season, the 3H and 90Sr contents in surface water may essentially vary. For instance, 3H maxima reached 430 kBq/kg and up to 100 Bq/kg for 90Sr. This finding suggested that the lake contamination level largely depends on the season and the level of contaminated ground water inflow.

Research findings on the stable isotope ratio revealed that winter precipitation is a source of ground water recharge because it seeps into cavities in “warfare” boreholes during snowmelt, thereby increasing the ground water level. Consequently, as a result of this process, contaminated ground water enters the lake surface.

The mechanism of ground water contamination appears as follows. The planting depths of charges during nuclear testing in boreholes are well below the level of the regional ground water aquifer (270 to 1200 m) (Israel et al., 2000). After underground nuclear tests were conducted, deformation zones were produced in the rock mass consisting of voids and crushed rock. Radioactive products are localized on the surfaces of cracks and rock fragments. Through systems of man-made fissures, ground water enters deformation zones. After they reach a cavity, they dissolve radionuclides and carry them back to the phreatic aquifer, after which they migrate and discharge onto the daylight surface.

137Cs and 239+240Pu content

The contents of 137Cs and 239+240Pu in sediments are ubiquitous without being locally confined to certain locations. When researching the vertical distribution, quantitative values of 137Cs and 239+240Pu in sediments are only registered in the 0–20 cm layer of topsoil. No content of 137Cs and 239+240Pu was detected in ground and surface waters. Thus, the production of 137Cs and 239+240Pu on the surface of Lake Kishkensor is attributed to radioactive fallout after nuclear explosion at the STS territory.

According to the results of aquatic environment researches near the Fukushima Nuclear Power Plant (NPP), where the radiation accident occurred in 2011, 3H concentrations at coastal stations located near the coast of Fukushima ranged from 90 to 175 kBq/L and decreased from 67 to 83 kBq/L at stations located 12–16 km from the coast, and only in some river mouths and ports, the 3H concentration was about 200–500 kBq/L in 2014–2018 (Aoyama et al., 2021). Thus, the 3H concentration in the Lake Kishkensor water is at the same level and in some cases higher than in the water near the Fukushima NPP.

More than 30 years after the Chernobyl nuclear accident, the research (Querfeld et al., 2018) showed that radionuclides have remained in the water in easily detectable quantities. The highest contamination in water was found in the water from the canal to the NPP at 4.1 Bq/L for 90Sr and 8.0 Bq/L for 137Cs, followed by the cooling pond and the Pripyat River bay water. The 90Sr concentration in water generally decreased with increasing distance from the accident site. 3H levels remained below detection limits. These values for 90Sr are much lower than those obtained from the study of the water of Lake Kishkensor. But the 137Cs concentration in the Lake Kishkensor water is below the detection limit, unlike the waters of the Chernobyl exclusion zone.

According to researches of the East Ural radioactive trace (EURT) lakes (Levina et al., 2008; Smagin, 2023) formed as a result of the Kyshtym accident, the 90Sr concentration in the water of most lakes does not exceed 1 Bq/L; only in the Lake Large Ighish water, the 90Sr concentration was 6.1 Bq/L, and the maximum concentration was recorded in the Lake Uruskul water at the level of 103 Bq/L (according to 2005 data). The average values of 90Sr concentration in the Lake Kishkensor water were about 13 Bq/L, which is higher than the concentration of this radionuclide in the water of the EURT lakes, with the exception of Lake Uruskul. However, in November 2018, the 90Sr concentration in the Lake Kishkensor water was recorded at 100 Bq/L, which is at the same level as the concentration in the Lake Uruskul water.

Conclusions

Based upon comprehensive research, the surface waters and sediments of Lake Kishkensor were found to be contaminated with the man-made radionuclides 3H and 90Sr. The main contamination is localized in sediments. Lake Kishkensor contamination occurred due to the outflow of contaminated ground water onto the daylight surface on the southern side.

The contents of 137Cs and 239+240Pu in the sediments and soils correspond to local radioactive fallout during nuclear explosion at the STS territory. No migration of these radionuclides to Lake Kishkensor was observed.

To control of radiological situation change and potential transfer of man-made radionuclides through ground waters, it is necessary to continue monitoring observations.
