Brodalumab as a Second-Line Therapy in Psoriasis: Rapid Response and Unique Mechanism of Action After Biologic Failure
Authors: Lawrence Green, April Armstrong, George Han, Abby Jacobson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This narrative review evaluates the clinical evidence supporting brodalumab as a second-line therapy against moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis in adults who have experienced prior systemic treatment failure. Despite advances in biologic therapy, treatment failure remains a substantial challenge, with patients often discontinuing initial biologic treatment owing to inadequate response. Here, we analyzed data from clinical trials, real-world studies, and observational research examining brodalumab’s efficacy in treatment-experienced populations. Unlike other biologics that target specific interleukin 17 (IL-17) cytokines, brodalumab blocks the IL-17 receptor A and prevents signaling from multiple IL-17 family members, potentially explaining its effectiveness in treatment-resistant cases. The evidence presented here demonstrates rapid Psoriasis Area and Severity Index response rates, as well as durable efficacy of brodalumab in patients after a failed treatment with previous biologic therapies. This narrative review synthesizes the current evidence base for brodalumab’s role specifically in the second-line setting, providing clinicians with a comprehensive assessment of its efficacy in treatment-resistant psoriasis.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Despite significant advances in biologic therapy against psoriasis, challenges such as treatment failure persist, with up to 47% of patients discontinuing their initial biologic treatment within 3 years, primarily because of inadequate response [1–3]. Patients treated with biologic therapy may initially experience nonresponse to treatment (primary failure) or may initially respond to treatment but experience subsequent loss of response (secondary failure) [4]. This treatment failure pattern has economic implications, with switchers experiencing greater annual all-cause healthcare costs compared to nonswitchers (mean difference, $10,120), and adds substantial burden to healthcare systems through additional monitoring and clinical visits [5].

The impact of treatment failure is particularly pronounced in certain patient populations, with younger patients who are biologic experienced having stronger expectations of rapid therapeutic response [6]. Moreover, the psychologic impact of treatment failure can negatively affect patient adherence and outcomes [7], highlighting the need for effective second-line options. This pattern of treatment failure and high expectations highlights a critical unmet need in psoriasis management—effective second-line treatment options that can deliver reliable efficacy in biologic-experienced patients.

With an expanding number of biologics being developed for plaque psoriasis, patients may be treated with therapies spanning several classes, such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin (IL)–12/23, IL-23, and IL-17 inhibitors [8]. For moderate-to-severe psoriasis, common first-line therapies may include oral deucravacitinib or injectable anti-TNF (in cases of comorbid conditions such as psoriatic arthritis or inflammatory bowel disease), anti–IL-23, or anti–IL-17 biologics [9–11]. Known for their rapid onset of action [12], IL-17 inhibitors are often selected for patients seeking immediate symptom relief. Additionally, most anti-IL-17 agents demonstrate high Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) response rates and are effective in treating difficult-to-manage areas affected by psoriasis, such as the scalp and nails [4]. IL-23 inhibitors are also efficacious and require a low injection frequency, potentially improving patient convenience and adherence. While there are many differences among these therapies, especially regarding their relative contraindications, safety profiles, and frequencies of injection, a common feature of the aforementioned biologics is that they target inflammatory pathways at the level of the soluble and membrane-bound cytokines themselves rather than the receptors [4]. Therefore, an alternative mechanism to target pathways activated in psoriasis is an important feature of a second-line therapy. Furthermore, the ideal second-line therapy should demonstrate not only effectiveness after prior biologic failure but also rapid onset of action to meet patient expectations and minimize patient burden. Understanding the characteristics that define an optimal second-line agent is crucial for clinical decision-making and treatment success.

Brodalumab is a human interleukin-17 receptor A (IL-17RA) antagonist indicated for the treatment of moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis in adult patients who are candidates for systemic therapy or phototherapy and have failed to respond or have lost response to other systemic therapies [13]. The IL-17R family contributes to the pathophysiology of psoriasis by inducing IL-17 production by T cells, proinflammatory cytokine and chemokine production by keratinocytes, and a positive-feedback loop of inflammation [14, 15]. The IL-17 family of cytokines contains six separate forms of IL-17, A through F, each with its unique function and role in the pathogenesis of psoriasis [14]. Unlike other anti–IL-17 biologics that specifically target the IL-17A or IL-17A/F cytokines, brodalumab is the only biologic approved for the treatment of psoriasis that binds to the IL-17A receptor, effectively blocking signaling from multiple IL-17 family members, including IL-17A, IL-17C, IL-17E, IL-17F, and IL-17A/F [2, 14, 15]. This comprehensive receptor-level blockade is hypothesized to contribute to its effectiveness in patients who have experienced failure of other targeted therapies, including IL-17 cytokine inhibitors.

The 2019 joint American Academy of Dermatology-National Psoriasis Foundation guidelines for biologic treatment of psoriasis highlight a critical gap: limited data exist to guide treatment-switching decisions, and changing therapies does not guarantee better outcomes [4]. This narrative review addresses the knowledge gap by examining brodalumab’s potential as a second-line therapy against moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis, discussing its unique mechanism of action, rapid onset of response, and efficacy in biologic-experienced patients. Understanding these characteristics is crucial for clinicians to make informed decisions about therapeutic options for patients who have experienced treatment failure with other biologics. This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Mechanism of Action of Brodalumab

The evolution of psoriasis treatment has progressed to targeted approaches, with the IL-17 pathway now recognized as a central therapeutic target that is directly responsible for the keratinocyte hyperactivation, which is the hallmark of plaque psoriasis [14]. After biologic therapy failure, clinicians may choose to switch to a drug within the same class or from another class with a different mechanism of action to increase the likelihood of treatment response [2, 16, 17].

Brodalumab’s unique receptor-targeting mechanism provides a rationale for its use after the failure of first-line treatments, including IL-17 agents. Unlike other biologics that target specific cytokines (e.g., secukinumab and ixekizumab, which target IL-17A, or bimekizumab, which targets IL-17A and IL-17F), brodalumab blocks the IL-17 RA itself, thereby preventing signaling from multiple IL-17 family members, including IL-17A, IL-17C, IL-17E, IL-17F, and IL-17A/F (Fig. 1) [13, 18–21].Fig. 1Mechanisms of action of biologics for the treatment of psoriasis [13, 18–21]. Mechanistic studies provide evidence supporting this comprehensive blockade approach. Compared directly to ixekizumab, brodalumab showed superior ability to normalize gene expression responses in keratinocytes induced by multiple IL-17 cytokines, demonstrating the comprehensive nature of its receptor-blocking mechanism [22]. This broad inhibition of IL-17 signaling pathways may explain brodalumab’s clinical effectiveness in patients who have experienced treatment failure with other biologics, including IL-17 inhibitors [23].

Rapid Onset of Response

For patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis who have experienced treatment failure, rapid response time is crucial not only for symptom relief but also for maintaining treatment adherence and confidence in the new therapy [6]. The psychologic impact of treatment failure and dissatisfaction creates an urgent need for second-line therapies that can demonstrate early efficacy [6, 24].

Brodalumab has demonstrated rapid onset of action across multiple efficacy measures. In phase 3 trials (N = 3712) comparing brodalumab to ustekinumab, brodalumab showed significantly faster response times, with median time to PASI 90 of 6.4 versus 12.1 weeks (P < 0.001) and to PASI 100 of 12.4 weeks for brodalumab versus ustekinumab, which did not reach this milestone (P < 0.001) [25]. Importantly for patient quality of life, brodalumab showed early improvements in symptoms by week 2, with significantly more patients achieving improvements in pruritus (36% vs 17%, P < 0.0001) and pain (62% vs 44%, P < 0.01) compared to ustekinumab [26, 27].

A systematic review of IL-17 and IL-23 agents revealed brodalumab’s superior speed of response among six evaluated therapies [12]. The analysis demonstrated that 50% of patients achieved PASI 90 at 6.2 weeks for brodalumab versus 7.4 weeks for ixekizumab, 9.4 weeks for risankizumab, 10.2 weeks for guselkumab, and 16.3 weeks for secukinumab (Fig. 2A) [12]. PASI 100 was achieved in 25% of patients at 6.9 weeks for brodalumab versus 8.1, 9.7, 12.8, and 15.1 weeks for ixekizumab, risankizumab, guselkumab, and secukinumab, respectively (Fig. 2B) [12]. These findings are reinforced by an additional systematic review showing that, among all evaluated biologics, brodalumab along with ixekizumab achieved the highest early response rates (PASI 75, 90, and 100) at week 2 and alone at week 4 [28]. Recent real-world evidence further supports these findings, with a retrospective study of 1057 patients treated with different biologics (secukinumab, n = 256; ixekizumab, n = 189; brodalumab, n = 203; guselkumab, n = 74; risankizumab, n = 236; tildrakizumab, n = 99), showing superior PASI 90 achievement (66%) at week 16 with brodalumab compared to other IL-23 and IL-17A inhibitors (P < 0.001) [29]. High levels of PASI 90 and 100 correlate with patient satisfaction [30]; thus, the faster achievement of these endpoints with brodalumab suggests it may better meet patient expectations for rapid, complete skin clearance. Rapid response should ideally be followed by durable efficacy to reduce or prevent treatment switching. An international, observational study of 1981 patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis who initiated or switched biologic treatment (Psoriasis Study of Health Outcomes) evaluated the durability of eight biologic treatments (ixekizumab, n = 532; secukinumab, n = 241; brodalumab, n = 64; tildrakizumab, n = 95; guselkumab, n = 303; risankizumab, n = 259; adalimumab, n = 284; ustekinumab, n = 127) [31]. Of patients who achieved PASI 90 and 100 after 12 weeks of treatment, 34% (22/64) and 19% (12/64) of brodalumab-treated patients maintained PASI 90 and 100 at month 12, respectively. These rates were similar to those of ixekizumab (37% [198/532] and 20% [105/532], respectively), which had the highest durability rates of all biologics in this study. However, comparisons of PASI rates between biologics should be interpreted considering nonuniform sample sizes of each therapeutic. Collectively, this evidence demonstrates that brodalumab offers a faster therapeutic response compared to other biologics, while maintaining similar durability.

Proven Efficacy Following Prior Biologic Failure: Clinical and Real-World Evidence

Clinical Trials and Post Hoc Analyses

In the phase 3 AMAGINE-2/-3 clinical trials of brodalumab, 27% (n = 334) of the 1236 participants who received brodalumab 210 mg during the induction period had prior exposure to biologics, 89% of which were TNF inhibitors [32]. Of participants with prior biologic exposure treated with brodalumab, 45% (n = 150) had experienced failure of a prior biologic [32]. At week 12 of brodalumab treatment, 63% of participants with prior biologic failure achieved PASI 90 and 32% achieved PASI 100, demonstrating statistical superiority of brodalumab versus ustekinumab (P < 0.001 and P < 0.003, respectively; Fig. 3) [32]. Additionally, a greater proportion of participants with prior biologic failure achieved Dermatology Life Quality Index score of 0/1 and Psoriasis Symptom Inventory score of ≤ 8 with brodalumab versus ustekinumab. Post hoc analyses have demonstrated the long-term efficacy and safety of brodalumab in subgroups regardless of prior exposure and response to prior biologics (e.g., TNF inhibitors, adalimumab) and with 1, 2, or ≥ 3 prior biologic therapies [33–36]. Additionally, participants enrolled in AMAGINE-2/-3 who experienced inadequate response (defined as a single Static Physician's Global Assessment [sPGA] score of ≥ 3 or persistent sPGA score of 2 over ≥ 4 weeks) to ustekinumab could switch to brodalumab at week 16 or continue ustekinumab [37]. Participants who switched to brodalumab had higher PASI 90 and 100 rates at week 52 (58% and 36%, respectively) than participants who continued ustekinumab (26% and 5%, respectively), despite the ustekinumab group potentially having more responders because only participants with poor responses were allowed to switch to brodalumab. Greater improvements in quality of life and psoriasis symptoms were also observed at week 52 in participants who switched to brodalumab versus in those who continued ustekinumab after inadequate response [37]. The rate of treatment-emergent adverse events was similar between patients who switched to brodalumab and those who continued ustekinumab treatment (377.3 vs 389.9 adverse events/100 patient-years).

Open-Label and Real-World Studies

Additionally, open-label studies and real-world analyses demonstrated brodalumab’s efficacy in patients who had experienced treatment failure of IL-17 inhibitors. While the AMAGINE trials excluded patients with prior IL-17 exposure, two phase 4, open-label, multicenter studies investigated brodalumab in patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis after a failed treatment with other biologics. A Canadian study (NCT04149587) included 251 participants after treatment failure with IL-17, IL-23, IL-12/23, or TNF biologics; overall rates of PASI 90 and 100 at week 16 were 54% and 40%, respectively [38]. A US study (NCT03403036) focused specifically on participants who experienced treatment failure of prior IL-17A biologics (N = 39) [39]. Figure 4 shows PASI rates stratified by prior biologic type that failed in both studies; generally, rates were similar in participants who experienced anti–IL-17 biologic failure (Canadian and US studies) and across biologic classes in the Canadian study [38, 39]. The Canadian and US studies reported adverse event rates of 65% and 15% and serious adverse event rates of 4% and 0%, respectively. Three additional open-label or real-world studies evaluated PASI 90 and 100 responses at 16 weeks in patients treated with brodalumab after failure of IL-17 inhibitors (secukinumab, ixekizumab, or both) [40–42]. An open-label study of 39 patients switching to brodalumab after secukinumab, ixekizumab, or both had failed showed that 44% and 28% achieved PASI 90 and 100, respectively, at week 16 (last observation carried forward) [40]. Six adverse events were reported; none were serious or believed to be related to brodalumab [40]. In a smaller study of 16 patients who experienced secukinumab failure, 75% achieved PASI 100 at week 16 with brodalumab [41]. In a retrospective study of 47 patients who switched to brodalumab after secukinumab, ixekizumab, or both had failed (primarily because of nonresponse), 47% and 42% achieved PASI 90 and 100 at week 16, respectively [42]. Brodalumab was efficacious regardless of the number of prior biologics used, with PASI 90 achievement ranging from 33 to 67% across patients who had used 1–6 previous biologics [42]. Taken together, the evidence presented here demonstrates brodalumab’s efficacy in patients with prior biologic exposure, while real-world studies further validate its effectiveness across patients who have experienced treatment failure of multiple biologics or IL-17 inhibitors, further supporting its use as a second-line option.

Safety Considerations

The safety profile of brodalumab was established by a pooled safety population across controlled clinical trials and open-label extension studies, of which one-third of patients had prior biologic exposure [13]. The most common adverse events (≥ 1%) were arthralgia, headache, fatigue, diarrhea, oropharyngeal pain, nausea, myalgia, injection site reactions, influenza, neutropenia, and tinea infections.

Similar to other biologics that inhibit IL-17, brodalumab may reactivate or exacerbate inflammatory bowel disease and increase risk of infection, specifically Candida infections [4]. As such, brodalumab is contradicted in patients with Crohn’s disease, and brodalumab should be discontinued if Crohn’s disease develops [13]. The risk-to-benefit ratio of brodalumab use in patients with chronic or a history of recurrent infection should be carefully considered, and patients should be instructed to take proper precautions if infection occurs. Furthermore, although a causal relationship between brodalumab use and increased risk of suicidal ideation and behavior has not been established, the US prescribing information for brodalumab contains a boxed warning for suicidal ideation and behavior [13].

In the US, brodalumab is only available through a Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategies (REMS) program [13]. Recently, a panel of psoriasis experts initiated a call to action to eliminate the REMS program for brodalumab, citing nonexclusion of patients with depression, suicidality, and substance abuse from pivotal trials and exceptionally low rates of suicide in US pharmacovigilance and worldwide postmarketing data (including data from countries without REMS programs) [43]. The panel concluded that brodalumab has been incorrectly associated with suicidality and is underutilized because of this monitoring program.

Discussion

Clinical evidence supports brodalumab’s potential as a second-line therapy, demonstrating both rapid onset of action and sustained efficacy in biologic-experienced patients. Notably, similar response rates were observed in both biologic-naive and -experienced patients. Real-world evidence further supports these findings, showing substantial improvement in patients who experienced treatment failure of prior TNF, IL-12/23, IL-23, or IL-17 therapies [38, 39].

The present narrative review contributes to current knowledge by consolidating evidence from clinical trials, post hoc analyses, and real-world studies in biologic-experienced populations. Despite frequent treatment switching, definitive guidance on therapeutic sequencing after biologic failure is lacking, with the timely and costly trial-and-error strategy predominating prescribing behaviors [4, 24, 44]. Collectively, the reviewed data demonstrate that brodalumab not only maintains high efficacy against psoriasis that persisted after a first-line biologic agent but also achieves the most rapid skin clearance among currently approved biologics. This rapid response may be particularly valuable in restoring patient confidence and supporting treatment adherence. By integrating mechanistic and clinical evidence, this review underscores the distinctive role of brodalumab in the psoriasis treatment algorithm and offers a framework for therapy selection when a rapid, durable response is required.

Based on available data, switching to brodalumab after inadequate response to other biologics offers a rational and strategic alternative owing to its distinct mechanism of action. For example, while IL-23 inhibitors modulate the upstream cytokine pathway that drives IL-17 production [14], brodalumab acts downstream by directly blocking the IL-17 receptor. This receptor-level blockade disrupts the signaling of multiple IL-17 cytokines and provides broader suppression of inflammatory mediators that drive psoriasis. By acting on a downstream immunologic target, brodalumab is hypothesized to overcome incomplete suppression of IL-17, which may contribute to an inadequate treatment response with IL-23 inhibition [14], thereby increasing the likelihood of achieving disease control. Moreover, the efficacy of brodalumab in patients who have previously failed other IL-17 inhibitors highlights its potential as a second-line agent, even for prior use of IL-17 inhibitors. Furthermore, rapid therapeutic response meets patient expectations, especially in those who are frustrated by prior treatment failure.

Reviewed evidence should be interpreted considering methodologic limitations. Head-to-head prospective trials of multiple biologic therapies in patients with prior biologic failure that compare time to onset of effect are lacking. Although real-world studies are valuable for continued monitoring of effectiveness and safety, these studies have heterogenous patient populations that compromise comparisons. Systematic reviews are also limited by heterogeneity across studies and reporting biases. Additionally, we acknowledge that industry funding and inclusion of an industry-affiliated co-author may influence the interpretation of the reviewed evidence. Readers should consider this context when evaluating our conclusions, although we have attempted to present a balanced assessment of the available literature.

Future studies should investigate the mechanism of primary and secondary treatment failure in nonresponders, with the goal of aligning the pathways driving recalcitrant disease with the appropriate treatment options.

Conclusions

Collectively, the evidence supports brodalumab as a viable treatment for patients after a single prior biologic has failed or lost efficacy, while also offering long-term skin clearance for patients with a history of multiple biologic drug failures. The broad blockade of IL-17 cytokine activity and a demonstrated therapeutic effect in patients with inadequate response to one or more prior biologics, including those targeting specific IL-17 family members, support the use of brodalumab for second-line therapy.
