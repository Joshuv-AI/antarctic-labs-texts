Altered Topology of Brain Structural Network in Patients With Migraine Without Aura: A Structural MRI Study
Authors: Zi‐Min Cao, Jing‐Nan Jia, Guo‐Yun Liu, Ze‐Qiu Yang, Jing‐Shi Zhang, An‐qi Shi, Yi‐Chao Chen, Jian‐Wei Huo, Ya‐Nan Zhang, Ni Liu, Chao‐Qun Yan, Jun Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMigraine is a neurovascular disease associated with significant morbidity and disability, but its underlying pathophysiology remains elusive. Functional alterations within the brain are frequently observed in individuals with migraine, whereas structural changes are less frequently documented. This study was primarily designed to investigate topological abnormalities in brain structural networks in patients with migraine using structural magnetic resonance imaging.MethodsGraph theoretical analysis was used to compare global and regional topological properties of grey matter structural networks in 37 migraine patients and 44 age‐, gender‐, and education‐matched healthy controls. Structural correlation networks were constructed for both groups on the basis of measurements of grey matter volume.ResultsA statistically significant difference was observed in the scores of Self‐rating Anxiety Scales (SAS) and Self‐rating Depression Scales (SDS) between migraine patients and healthy controls. The brain networks of patients exhibited significantly increased path length, decreased clustering coefficient, and small‐worldness at the global level. At the regional level, brain regions with changes in node degree/betweenness centrality in migraine patients were predominantly located in the left cuneus, the left fusiform gyrus, the left precuneus, the right precentral gyrus, the right middle frontal gyrus, and the bilateral lingual gyrus.ConclusionThe findings of this research indicate that the topological organisation is less efficient in individuals who experience migraine. This may provide new insight into the pathogenesis of migraine from a structural perspective. Trial Registration: Chinese Clinical Trial Registry, ChiCTR2000033995SignificanceThe present study addresses the central pathogenesis of migraine and shows at a structural level that global brain properties are altered in migraine patients and that regional properties of specific brain regions also show abnormalities. This provides new ideas and an objective basis for the future diagnosis and treatment of migraine.Keywords: graph theoretical analysis, grey matter, migraine, structural MRI

Introduction

Migraine is a very widespread primary headache, with an estimated global 1‐year prevalence of 15% of the general population (Feigin et al. 2019). As a chronic neurovascular disorder with episodic manifestations, migraine has an enormous socioeconomic impact on individuals and society (Ferrari et al. 2022). It is listed by the World Health Organization as the sixth most disabling disease in the world and the most disabling of all neurological diseases (Hay et al. 2017). Migraine attacks are often accompanied by nausea, vomiting, photophobia and phonophobia (International Headache Society 2018). The mechanism of migraine is still unclear, and diagnosis relies mainly on the analysis of clinical features, with no objective diagnostic methods.

In recent years, several neuroimaging studies have attempted to investigate the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of migraine (Dai et al. 2021; Messina et al. 2022; Younis et al. 2021). Previous studies, including our own, have identified abnormalities related to migraine in local brain regions and functional connectivity between various brain regions using resting‐state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs‐fMRI) (Cao et al. 2022; Chen et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2024a). Migraine may result from disrupted connections between brain regions rather than isolated regional brain abnormalities (Schwedt et al. 2015).

MRI‐based structural analysis has also been used to study the pathogenesis of migraine (Jin et al. 2013). Voxel‐based morphometry (VBM) and cortical thickness analysis studies have shown that migraine patients present with reductions in grey matter (GM) volume and thickness in brain regions including the frontal lobe, temporal lobe and cerebellum, indicating that morphological alterations in the brain are a common occurrence in migraine patients (Bonanno et al. 2020; Mehnert and May 2019).

Previous research has shown that coordinated changes in the structural morphology of the brain, such as synchronised variations in cortical features between regions, differ across diseases and can be used as an effective approach to infer large‐scale structural brain networks reflecting anatomical connectivity (Liu et al. 2024b; Monteiro et al. 2024). Graph theoretical analysis (GTA) is an ideal tool for examining structural covariance networks in the brain, as it can quantify the topology of brain networks (Bullmore and Sporns 2009). It was used to quantify the structural network of the brain by using nodes (brain regions) and edges (connections) (John 2017). This method has revealed changes in the structural network associated with diseases such as cirrhosis without hepatic encephalopathy, developmental dyslexia and Alzheimer's disease (Bai et al. 2012; Liu et al. 2015; Lv et al. 2015). Studying the altered structural network of the migraine brain using the GTA approach will provide a better understanding of the neuropathological mechanisms of migraine.

In this study, we compared the interconnectedness of structural brain networks in patients with migraine without aura (Mwoa) and healthy controls (HCs) using GM volumes and GTA. Our hypothesis is that Mwoa patients exhibit alterations in topological properties—such as network efficiency, clustering coefficient, and path length—in structurally connected brain networks, reflecting disrupted brain network organisation. Additionally, we compared between‐group differences in centrality and central distribution to further elucidate the brain regions most strongly associated with migraine.

Methods

Participants

Thirty‐seven right‐handed patients with Mwoa were enrolled from the outpatient department of Dongzhimen Hospital Affiliated to Beijing University of Chinese Medicine. Patients were required to meet the following criteria for inclusion in the study: (i) 18–65 years of age; (ii) migraine without aura according to the International Classification for Headache Disorders (ICHD‐III, beta version); (iii) free of migraine headache for at least 72 h prior to scanning; (iv) age ≤ 50 years at first occurrence; (v) have suffered from migraine for at least 1 year or more; (vi) no use of any prophylactic treatments (including oral and injectable treatments) in the past month. The exclusion criteria included: (i) headache attacks > 10 per month; (ii) use of preventive treatments, anti‐epileptic, or anti‐depressant medication in the past month; (iii) pregnancy or breastfeeding; (iv) any other type of headache; (v) abuse or dependence on drugs/alcohol; (vi) headache due to other diseases; (vii) inability to scan.

A total of 44 right‐handed HCs were recruited by post from local communities. The HCs were matched to the patients for age, sex, and education level. Inclusion criteria for healthy controls were: (i) no history of migraine; (ii) no history of mental illness or chronic systemic disease; (iii) 18–65 years of age; (iv) no MRI contraindications. Exclusion criteria were: (i) headache due to primary disease; (ii) taken any preventive medicines in the last three months; (iii) abuse or dependence on drugs/alcohol; (iv) pregnancy or breastfeeding; (v) participating in other clinical trials; (vi) MRI contraindications; (vii) neurological disorders, bleeding disorders, immunodeficiency, or allergies.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Dongzhimen Hospital Affiliated to Beijing University of Chinese Medicine. An informed consent form was signed by all participants. They were asked to complete a questionnaire consisting of demographic data, Self‐rating Depression Scales (SDS) and Self‐rating Anxiety Scales (SAS). In addition, the patients were asked to complete visual analogue scales (VAS) and the Migraine‐Specific Quality of Life Questionnaire (MSQ, including Emotion function, Preventive, and Restrictive).

MRI Data Acquisition

All participants were scanned on a Siemens 3.0‐Tesla scanner (Skyra, Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) in the radiology department of the Beijing Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine affiliated to Capital Medical University. During the scanning process, sound‐insulation earplugs were used to reduce noise interference, and sponge plugs were used to reduce head movement. The three‐dimensional T1‐weighted (3DT1) images were acquired using a magnetisation‐prepared rapid gradient‐echo imaging sequence [192 sagittal slices, repetition time = 2300 ms, echo time = 2.98 ms, field of view = 240 × 240 mm, flip angle = 8°, acquisition matrix = 256 × 256].

Structural Data Preprocessing

All 3DT1 images were carried out using Statistical Parametric Mapping 12 (SPM 12, http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12). The preprocessing pipeline included the following steps: First, the original 3DT1 images were imported into SPM12, and manual or automatic reorientation was performed to ensure that the images conformed to the standard anatomical orientation. Next, the images were segmented into grey matter, white matter and cerebrospinal fluid tissue classes using the ‘Segment’ function in SPM12, generating corresponding tissue probability maps. Subsequently, based on the segmentation results, non‐linear deformation fields were applied to spatially normalise the images to the standard MNI space to achieve inter‐subject spatial alignment. During normalisation, modulation was applied to preserve the local tissue volume information. Finally, the normalised images were smoothed using an isotropic Gaussian kernel with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 8 mm to improve the signal‐to‐noise ratio and satisfy the assumptions of subsequent statistical analyses.

Network Construction

The entire brain network was segmented into 90 regions of interest (ROIs) according to the Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) template. The correlation matrices were constructed based on pair‐wise ROI correlations. The structural correlation between the mean GM volumes for two ROIs, ‘i’ and ‘j’, within a group, was defined as the Pearson correlation coefficients (r ij ). The age and sex of all subjects were used as covariates. A 90 × 90 correlation matrix R was then constructed for each group by collecting the structural correlations of each pair of ROIs between migraine patients and healthy subjects. Based on the matrix R, the matrix A was further generated by thresholding the r ij to a value of 0 or 1. The thresholds were defined as a series of network densities (Dmin–Dmax) over which the network topologies of the Mwoa patients and HCs were compared. In this study, we calculated a density range of 0.14–0.30 and an interval of 0.01 for the investigation of network properties. The Dmin was finally determined to be 0.14, above which neither network is fragmented (i.e., the network contains nodes that are not connected to any other node). The observed Dmax was chosen to be 0.30 to allow efficient estimation of the network properties while minimising the number of pseudo‐edges. In addition, the matrix A was considered an undirected graph G, where N, K, D, i and j represented the total number of ROIs (i.e., 90), the number of edges, the proportion of surviving connections at a given density threshold, and a random pair of ROIs from the 90 brain regions.

Global Network Properties

We used graph theoretical analysis to quantify the topological properties of the brain structure network. The characteristic path length L and the clustering coefficient C are two basic metrics. L of a given graph G was defined as the average of the shortest path lengths between two generic nodes across all the nodes. L ij is the length of the shortest path between nodes i and j. A shorter path length indicates stronger integration between brain regions, whereas a longer path length reflects weaker integration. Thus, L as a metric of network integration essentially reflects the ability of the network to integrate information at the global level.

The clustering coefficient of a node i (C i ) measures the number of edges in its nearest neighbours, and C is defined as the average of the clustering coefficients C i of all 90 nodes. G i is a subgraph of the entire network, consisting of the nearest neighbours of node i. Therefore, C is a measure of network isolation that reflects the efficiency of the network in dealing with information locally. Biologically, a higher clustering coefficient suggests stronger local specialisation and segregation, supporting efficient localised information processing in the brain. After comparing it with the random network, we further calculated the normalised path length λ and the normalised clustering coefficient γ to better summarise the topology of the brain network. λ typically should be slightly greater than 1, indicating efficient global integration. γ is generally > 1, reflecting stronger local clustering compared to random networks. On the basis of λ and γ, the small‐world index σ of the brain network is calculated and provides a more direct measure of how the brain is organised in the small‐world.

Regional Network Properties

In this study, two node network measures of normalised intermediate centrality b i and normalised degree k i were used to summarise and quantify the regional network properties. B i denotes the total number of shortest paths that pass through node i, K i denotes the number of edges between node i and others. The b i essentially measures the effect of a node on the flow of information between itself and other nodes, while k i measures the interaction of a node with the network as a whole. The b i reflects the node's regulatory role in the brain's local information flow, reflecting local signalling and mediating functions; k i reflects the node's interaction strength in the whole brain network, reflecting its role as a global hub or key regulator.

Hub Identification

In the present study, we used both b i and k i to define two sets of network hubs. Network hubs here are defined as nodes with higher values (at least 2 SDs greater than the mean b i /k i ) and are considered to be brain regions that play an important role in information transfer and integration in whole‐brain communication.

Statistical Analysis

Demographic and scale data were analysed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) for Windows v20.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). The distribution of continuous variables (age, education level) was tested with a two‐sample t test. The chi‐squared test was used to test for gender differences between Mwoa patients and healthy controls. The correlation between Mwoa patients' clinical data (e.g., VAS, SAS) and brain regions exhibiting topological abnormalities was analysed using Pearson correlation analysis. p < 0.05 was considered significant for all analyses.

Between‐Group Network Comparisons

Differences in global and regional topological measures (λ, γ, σ, b i and k i ) between the Mwoa patients and HCs were investigated using non‐parametric permutation tests in the Graph Analysis Toolbox software. A p value of 0.05 (two‐tailed) was considered to be statistically significant. The global network properties were compared within the density range of 0.20–0.30. Between‐group differences in regional network properties were compared using area under the curve (AUC) analysis with a density range of 0.22–0.30. The p‐values obtained were rigorously corrected using the Benjamini‐Hochberg method (BH) of False Discovery Rate (FDR).

Global Network Properties Changes

0.22–0.3 is the final density range of interest for global properties, where the network is unfragmented and biologically meaningful in the migraine and HC groups. As presented in Figure 1, HCs showed an effective small‐world topology (λ > 1.02, γ > 1.2, σ > 1.1), while the network of Mwoa patients showed an altered topology (increased λ, decreased γ and σ). There were no significant differences in the characteristic path lengths of the network between the two groups (Figure 1d), while within the density range of 0.24–0.28, the clustering coefficients were significantly lower in the Mwoa patients' networks (Figure 1e), resulting in lower small‐worldness compared to the HCs within the same density range.

Regional Network Properties and Hubs Changes

The AUC analysis of regional network properties revealed changes in a number of brain regions b i and k i (p < 0.05, Figure 2a,b). Compared with HCs, Mwoa patients had increased b i values in the left cuneus and fusiform gyrus and significantly lower b i values in the right lingual gyrus and precentral gyrus (Figure 2c). In addition to this, the k i values in Mwoa patients were increased in the right middle frontal gyrus and the left precuneus and decreased in the left lingual gyrus (Figure 2c). In Mwoa patients, SAS scores were positively correlated with ki values in the left precuneus (r = 0.273, p = 0.029). However, patients' VAS, SDS, MSQE, MSQP, and MSQR scores showed no significant correlation with brain regions exhibiting topological abnormalities (p > 0.05).

According to b i and k i , four network hubs were identified in HCs, namely the right precentral gyrus, the right middle frontal gyrus, the left cuneus, and the left fusiform gyrus (Figure 3a,c). In contrast, seven hubs were identified in Mwoa patients: the left cuneus, the left fusiform gyrus, the left precuneus, the right precentral gyrus, the right middle frontal gyrus, and the bilateral lingual gyrus (Figure 3b,d). Overall, a greater number of hubs were detected in the Mwoa patients than in the HCs, indicating a loss of associated hubs and the emergence of additional unassociated hubs.

Discussion

In this study, by comparing with HCs, we identified topological changes in the structural network of migraine patients. The principal findings are as follows: (1) The overall topological organisation of the GM volume network is disrupted in migraine patients compared to HCs, manifesting as small‐worldness alterations; (2) Brain areas with altered nodal degree/betweenness centrality were predominantly observed in association cortices in Mwoa patients; (3) Different patterns of hub distribution were observed in the Mwoa patients versus the HCs based on nodal betweenness and nodal degree. In conclusion, the results demonstrate that the topological organisation of structural networks constructed from GM volumes in Mwoa patients is less optimal than that of networks corresponding to HCs, indicating a reduced capacity to integrate information between different brain regions.

GTA is increasingly recognised as a valuable tool for describing the human brain and characterising clinical syndromes (Qingbao et al. 2018). The global topological properties in GTA exhibit small‐worldness architecture, a key organising principle of the complex human brain (Bullmore and Sporns 2009). Path length and clustering coefficient quantify GM network integration, reflecting efficient communication (Jennifer et al. 2013). Global properties assess information transmission efficiency, while regional properties indicate fault tolerance (Linquan et al. 2021). Additionally, communication efficiency among a node's first neighbours can be evaluated when the node is removed (Yiheng et al. 2019). Shorter path length suggests stronger integration between brain regions, while longer path length indicates weaker integration (John 2017). This study confirmed the presence of small‐worldness in healthy brains, consistent with prior findings. Conversely, Mwoa patients showed increased path length and decreased clustering coefficient and small‐worldness, suggesting sparser inter‐nodal organisation compared to controls (Kejie et al. 2024). These findings in Mwoa may indicate that the inter‐nodal organisation throughout the brain tends to be sparser in migraine patients compared to healthy controls. These results align with observations in chronic migraine but differ from findings in women with migraine (DeSouza et al. 2020; Jixin et al. 2012), possibly due to variability in disease course. Overall, structural brain alterations in migraine patients reduce global information transmission capacity but enhance functionality in specific hubs related to nociceptive transmission and affective cognition, potentially facilitating rapid pain‐related information transfer and contributing to migraine pathophysiology.

The Default Mode Network (DMN) is a group of interconnected brain regions that exhibit strong and steady metabolic activity when the brain is at rest (Greicius et al. 2003). Existing literature has shown that alterations in the DMN serve as a transdiagnostic factor across chronic pain conditions and are associated with pain severity, particularly pain duration (Marwan et al. 2014). However, in our study, we did not observe a significant correlation between VAS scores and the DMN in Mwoa patients. This discrepancy may be due to several reasons: firstly, the VAS measures pain intensity at a single time point and may not fully capture the chronic or cumulative impact of pain that is reflected in DMN changes; secondly, individual variability in pain perception and coping mechanisms could weaken the association; lastly, the relatively small sample size or methodological differences may have limited our ability to detect subtle correlations. Future studies with longitudinal designs and larger cohorts may provide deeper insights into the relationship between DMN alterations and pain characteristics in migraine. The extant clinical studies scarcely mention structural abnormalities in the DMN regions, with the majority focusing on abnormalities in DMN functional connectivity. Patients with menstrual migraine without aura exhibit reduced effective connectivity between the precuneus and insula (Huang et al. 2024), which leads to impaired information integration and transmission in the precuneus and subsequent DMN dysfunction. At the same time, there is a significant difference in GM volume in the precuneus between chronic and episodic migraine (Castro Zamparella et al. 2024). In addition, the underlying mechanism of anxiety and depression symptoms in adults may be related to an increase in the GM volumes of bilateral precuneus (Zhengxinyue et al. 2023). Depression and anxiety are strongly associated with DMN abnormalities, whereas migraine frequently accompanies anxiety or depressive symptoms and vice versa (Lantéri‐Minet et al. 2005; Nie et al. 2023; Smitherman et al. 2020). The present study revealed a significant increase in SAS and SDS scores in Mwoa. Meanwhile, the precuneus has also been identified as one of the hubs associated with Mwoa. Notably, there is a positive correlation between the precuneus and the SAS, which is responsible for modulating the level of arousal to external stimuli and attentional state, and facilitating the brain's rapid response to environmental stimuli. The precuneus, as a key node in the DMN, is not only involved in internal cognitive activities, but also works in coordination with the SAS to maintain the balance of arousal and consciousness. This positive correlation suggests that the precuneus plays a bridging role in internal and external information integration and arousal regulation, which may further explain the co‐morbid mechanism of precuneus physiological abnormalities and dysregulation of the arousal system in diseases such as migraine and mood disorders.

The frontal lobe plays a critical role in regulating physical movement, language and expression, attention, emotional processing, planning and control, as well as thinking and judgement (Catani 2019). Previous studies have shown that the frontal lobe is the brain region most often exhibiting structural abnormalities in individuals with migraine (Schmitz et al. 2008; Schwedt and Dodick 2009). Among these, the middle frontal gyrus (MFG) appears to be crucial in Mwoa by modulating nociceptive processing. It is involved in both the sensory and cognitive components of migraine symptoms and is linked to pain intensity (Russo et al. 2012). Research indicates that patients with Mwoa show a significant reduction in GMV in the MFG, with this reduction negatively correlating with disease duration (Zhijian et al. 2022). This process is believed to be regulated by the DMN, which modulates both pain intensity and frequency. Our findings align with those of previous studies, which have suggested that the MFG plays a role in modulating nociception in Mwoa patients. Changes in the structure of the MFG may affect an individual's pain tolerance and increase the frequency and severity of migraine attacks. Future studies may concentrate on the relationship between the structural modifications of MFG and the symptoms associated with migraine.

The precentral gyrus, situated within the motor cortex, is responsible for controlling limb movements and coordinating fine motor skills (Evan et al. 2023). Altered activation patterns in the precentral gyrus have been observed in individuals with episodic migraine (Todd et al. 2014), which may be linked to abnormal pain perception and processing in these patients. Significant reductions in GMV have been observed in the left precentral gyrus of migraine sufferers. Additionally, the onset of the disease has been associated with notable GM reductions in cortical regions involved in the pain circuit (Walter et al. 2008). These changes may impair the brain's ability to process and modulate nociceptive signals, potentially contributing to the onset of migraine pain. Therefore, it can be hypothesised that the dysfunction observed in migraine patients is related to structural abnormalities in the precentral gyrus, which may underlie the symptoms associated with the condition.

The identification of alterations in brain network topology in this study holds significant clinical implications for migraine. These network changes may serve as potential biomarkers for assessing migraine severity or predicting treatment response, thereby enabling more personalised and timely interventions. Understanding the specific patterns of network disruption provides valuable insights into the underlying neurobiological mechanisms of migraine, which can guide the development of targeted therapeutic strategies. For instance, neuromodulation techniques or cognitive therapies aimed at restoring network balance could be designed based on these findings to improve patient outcomes. The study's findings indicate that individuals suffering from migraine exhibit increased path length, decreased clustering coefficient, and small‐worldness. These observations suggest a disruption in efficient information processing within the brain network. Consequently, enhancing brain network connectivity and restoring information transmission efficiency may emerge as a novel therapeutic approach for addressing migraine. Moreover, by linking our computational network analyses with patients' clinical prognosis and treatment pathways, we strive to offer interpretations that are not only scientifically robust but also biologically meaningful and clinically accessible. This integrative approach has the potential to bridge the gap between advanced neuroimaging research and practical clinical application, ultimately benefiting both clinicians in tailoring treatment strategies and patients in achieving better symptom management and quality of life.

It is important to note that this study is not without limitations, which require further consideration. Firstly, previous studies have shown that regions of the DMN are associated with chronic pain, including pain severity and pain duration. However, our study did not collect data on the duration of pain in migraine patients. We plan to improve the collection of such relevant data in future research. Secondly, the number of patients included in this study was not identical to that of the HCs. Nevertheless, the discrepancy in the number of subjects between the two groups was minimal. It is recommended that future studies include as many participants as possible to minimise the potential for bias in the results due to differences in sample size. In addition, due to the limited duration of the study, only patients with Mwoa were included, and other types of migraine should be the subject of further investigation. Moreover, this was a cross‐sectional study; future research should therefore employ longitudinal methods in order to more fully elucidate the neural correlates of Mwoa.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that the topological organisation is less efficient in the context of migraine. This provides a new framework for understanding the mechanisms that underlie the pathogenesis of migraine, offering novel avenues for the development of therapeutic and preventive strategies.
