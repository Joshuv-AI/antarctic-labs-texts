Dialectical behavior therapy skills training for African American pregnant women with ACEs: a pilot study of feasibility, acceptability, and mental health barriers and attitudes
Authors: Abigail Powers, Imani Belton, Rebecca Lipschutz, Emma Lathan, Yara Mekawi, Shimarith Wallace, Ahana Narayanan, Briana Woods-Jaeger
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Toxic stress contributes to socioeconomic and racial health disparities that persist across generations. Developing and implementing prenatal interventions that reduce toxic stress and associated comorbidities is warranted. Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) Skills Training group interventions have demonstrated efficacy for depression and posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and are scalable in diverse settings. Implementing DBT skills group interventions for African American pregnant women with adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and current depression or PTSD symptoms is novel and has the potential to interrupt the intergenerational cycle of toxic stress by improving maternal mental health. Yet, much remains to be understood about barriers that may impact feasibility.Objective: This mixed-method pilot randomized controlled trial (RCT) examined barriers and attitudes toward mental health treatment, feasibility, and acceptability of an adapted 8-week DBT Skills Training group for mothers-to-be (DBTMTB) compared to waitlist control (WLC) for African American pregnant women with a history of expanded ACEs and current depression or PTSD symptoms (n = 30; n = 15 for DBTMTB, n = 15 for WLC).Methods: The study was delivered in a prenatal clinic of an urban public hospital or virtually. Pre-treatment quantitative and qualitative measures of treatment barriers and attitudes toward mental health treatment were obtained. Feasibility was measured through treatment and study retention rates. Acceptability was measured through qualitative responses on intervention acceptability.Results: We found a high level of barriers identified by women; stigma and time constraints were endorsed most often. Approximately half of women were receiving professional help and many reported stigma about asking others for help. Feasibility (treatment retention) was poor; only 26.7% (n = 4) of participants randomized to DBTMTB completed the intervention (≥6 sessions). Acceptability among treatment completers was high.Conclusions: Poor completion rates suggest challenges to feasibility and acceptability of DBTMTB in this population that must be addressed.

Introduction

Toxic stress, or the cumulative effects of stress and trauma from prolonged activation of stress response systems, is related to a wide range of adverse health outcomes (e.g. depression, anxiety, cardiovascular disease) (Shonkoff et al., 2021). Importantly, toxic stress can be transmitted across generations, resulting in life-long health impacts for mothers and their children (Zhang et al., 2023). Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), or exposure to abuse, neglect, or household dysfunction before the age of 18, are common within the United States (∼62%; Aslam et al., 2024) and increase risk for toxic stress. African American women with limited socioeconomic resources show similar rates of ACEs compared to their White counterparts (Aslam et al., 2024), but also must navigate additional stressors, such as living in dangerous neighbourhoods, lack of adequate and accessible public services, poverty, and racial discrimination, contributing to heightened levels of toxic stress and directly impacting racial health disparities (Thornton et al., 2016). As evidenced by profound disparities in maternal morbidity and mortality for African American women (Adams et al., 2024; Admon et al., 2018), pregnancy is a time of increased vulnerability for African American women. Mental health interventions during pregnancy have the potential to prevent the intergenerational transmission of toxic stress and associated health disparities for African American women with low socioeconomic resources. Trauma-related psychological disorders in pregnancy increase risk for adverse maternal health and birth-related outcomes, which disproportionately affect African American women (Brown et al., 2021; Trost et al., 2022). Thus, using transdiagnostic treatments to address mental health symptoms among African American women with a history of ACEs may be of value.

Emotion dysregulation is a transdiagnostic risk factor for the development and maintenance of a wide range of mental health problems, including trauma-related conditions (e.g. PTSD, depression) (Beauchaine & Cicchetti, 2019). Emotion dysregulation is also related to numerous clinically relevant outcomes in African American adults and their children (e.g. parenting behaviour, PTSD; Powers et al., 2020; Powers et al., 2021) and mediates the relationship between experiences of racism and PTSD symptoms (Mekawi et al., 2020). Interventions that promote emotion regulation strategies for coping with the negative impacts of mental health and social determinants of health are needed to address disparities in chronic stress experienced by African American pregnant women. Importantly, while exposure-based treatments (e.g. Prolonged Exposure Therapy, Cognitive Processing Therapy) for PTSD are considered the gold standard (McLean et al., 2022), there is growing evidence that non-trauma focused treatments that address transdiagnostic processes (e.g. emotion dysregulation) are also effective for addressing PTSD symptoms (Hoppen et al., 2023) and may be a helpful option for trauma-exposed African American women given that mental health stigma, valid mistrust of the medical system, and hesitation to approach trauma memories may be present (Misra et al., 2021; Ward et al., 2013).

One viable transdiagnostic approach that directly targets emotion regulation and can improve overall health is Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) Skills Training, a group format mindfulness-based intervention with demonstrated efficacy for increasing emotion regulation and reducing depression and PTSD symptoms (Neacsiu et al., 2014; Valentine et al., 2015). DBT Skills Training has been adapted for trauma-exposed populations (Iverson et al., 2009). Additionally, recent data in a majority White (87% White, 0% African American) sample has shown the effectiveness of a 7-week perinatal DBT group on improving emotion regulation (Agako et al., 2023). Using a community-based participatory research (CBPR) approach, our team also developed an adapted DBT Skills Training group for parents with trauma histories and/or mental health problems (Woods-Jaeger, Cho, et al., 2018; Woods-Jaeger, Sexton, et al., 2018). Through an iterative process that included piloting the intervention and gathering feedback from parent partners, a strong need for earlier intervention in pregnancy was identified. This led our team to develop a prenatal DBT Skills Training group intervention for African American women. Limited data exists for DBT Skills Training in pregnancy and we built upon our previous community partnered iterative intervention development to pilot DBT Skills Training for mothers-to-be (DBTMTB).

This pilot randomized controlled trial (RCT) examined psychological treatment attitudes, experiences, behaviours, and barriers, as well as initial feasibility and acceptability of an adapted 8-week DBTMTB group intervention for low socioeconomic resourced African American pregnant women with a history of expanded ACEs and current depression and/or PTSD symptoms compared to waitlist control (WLC). We first obtained pre-treatment quantitative and qualitative feedback from participants regarding treatment barriers and attitudes toward mental health treatment. Then, to assess feasibility, we measured both study and treatment retention rates. We hypothesized retention rates would be greater than 65% (given prior studies showing 63–75% retention, Agako et al., 2023; Wilson & Donachie, 2018). To assess DBTMTB acceptability, we obtained qualitative responses on intervention acceptability for those that were randomized to the DBTMTB intervention.

Procedure

Pregnant women were recruited between April 2019 and June 2022 from the obstetrics clinic of an urban safety net hospital during routine obstetrics care. They were invited to participate in a pilot RCT (NCT03938350) for an 8-week DBTMTB intervention. Women were not actively seeking behavioural health resources and were identified based on medical appointments for prenatal care or through participation in a longitudinal study of pregnant women (observational) already ongoing in the laboratory. Inclusion criteria for the study were self-identification as Black or African American, in first or second trimester of pregnancy, presence of four or more expanded ACEs, and positive screen for posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) or depression (see measures below). Exclusion criteria were presence of intellectual disability or actively displaying psychotic symptoms.

If eligible for participation based on initial screen and indicated willingness to participate, a member of the research team obtained written or electronic consent and conducted a baseline assessment by phone or HIPAA-compliant Zoom technology. Informed consent was obtained by all participants and the study was approved by the university’s institutional review board and the hospital’s research oversight committee in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. At the conclusion of the pre-assessment, participants were randomized to DBTMTB or treatment-as-usual (i.e. WLC). Then, participants engaged in a post-assessment at the end of the 8-week intervention or WLC condition. Assessments lasted approximately one hour, and participants were compensated $20. WLC participants were eligible for the intervention after study completion.

The study was temporarily halted in March 2020 when the COVID-19 pandemic began. Recruitment using a fully virtual mode of delivery for the intervention approved by the university IRB resumed in September 2020, and the study was completed including all data collection in September 2022. Measures

Trauma exposure was assessed with the Expanded ACE Questionnaire (Cronholm et al., 2015) and the Traumatic Events Inventory (Gillespie et al., 2009). The Expanded ACE Questionnaire is a validated 21-item questionnaire with expanded items from the original ACE questionnaire to include other stressors experienced growing up in an urban community: experiencing racism, witnessing violence, unsafe neighbourhood, experiencing bullying, and foster care. An ACE score ≥ 4 was used to designate being at risk of intergenerational transmission of toxic stress for study eligibility for practical consistency with prior research using the original 10-item ACEs showing a tipping point of four or more ACEs on adverse health consequences (Hughes et al., 2017). Of note, the cutoff of 4 has not yet been validated for the expanded ACEs. The Traumatic Events Inventory (TEI) assesses lifetime exposure to traumatic events (Gillespie et al., 2009). Eighteen types of traumas are assessed, and for each traumatic event reported, participants indicated frequency on a scale of 0 (1 time) to 8 (greater than 20 times). The TEI has been validated in a trauma-exposed civilian urban sample (Mekawi et al., 2021). The TEI was obtained at pre-assessment.

Psychological symptoms were screened for inclusion with the Patient Health Questionnaire (Kroencke et al., 2001; cutoff ≥ 9 for moderate-to-severe depression) and the Primary Care PTSD Screen, 5-item (Prins et al., 2016; cutoff ≥ 3 for probable PTSD). The Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) is a 9-item self-report screener for depression symptoms obtained at the screening visit and post-assessment. The PHQ-9 has been validated for use in prenatal populations (Sidebottom et al., 2012) and was chosen because it is used as the screener for postpartum depression in the setting where the study took place. Internal consistency in the sample was adequate (α = .69). The Primary Care PTSD Screen (PC-PTSD-5) is a 5-item self-report measure of PTSD symptoms obtained at the screening visit. The PC-PTSD-5 has been validated in a predominantly African American sample of adults utilizing medical care at the same urban public hospital (Lathan et al., 2023). Internal consistency in the sample was good (α = .84). PTSD symptoms were also assessed using the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5; Blevins et al., 2015). PCL-5 is a 20-item self-report measure of DSM-5 PTSD symptoms that uses a 5-point scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 5 (extremely). Higher scores indicate higher levels of symptoms, and a cutoff of 33 indicates probable PTSD. The PCL-5 has been validated in many samples (Blevins et al., 2015; Bovin et al., 2016) including a Black civilian sample from the same public hospital setting (Mekawi et al., 2022). Internal consistency in the sample was high (α = .92). The PCL-5 was obtained at pre- and post-assessment.

Trait mindfulness was measured using the Five Factor Mindfulness Questionnaire (FFMQ; Baer et al., 2006). FFMQ is a validated 39-item self-report measure that assesses five facets of trait mindfulness: (1) observing, (2) describing, (3) acting with awareness, (4) nonjudgment, and (5) nonreacting. Participants responded to statements on a Likert-type scale from 1 (never or very rarely true) to 5 (very often or always true). FFMQ total score was calculated based on summation of scores; higher scores suggest higher levels of mindfulness. The FFMQ has been validated in an African American sample (Watson-Singleton et al., 2018). Internal consistency in the sample was high (α = .94). The FFMQ was obtained at pre- and post-assessment.

Emotion dysregulation was evaluated using the Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS; Gratz & Roemer, 2004). DERS is a validated 36-item self-report measure that assesses difficulties in emotion regulation across six dimensions: (1) nonacceptance of emotional responses, (2) difficulties engaging in goal-directed behaviour, (3) impulse control difficulties in the presence of strong emotions, (4) lack of emotional awareness, (5) limited access to emotion regulation strategies, and (6) lack of emotional clarity. Participants responded to statements based on a Likert-type scale from 1 (almost never) to 5 (almost always). The DERS total score was calculated based on summation of scores; higher scores indicate higher levels of emotion dysregulation. The DERS has been validated in a sample of Black women (Mekawi et al., 2020). Internal consistency in the sample was high (α = .92). The DERS was obtained at pre- and post-assessment.

Treatment barriers were assessed using the Perceived Barriers to Psychological Treatments (PBPT; Mohr et al., 2006). PBPT is a validated self-report measure of barriers to participating in psychological interventions (Mohr et al., 2006). Individual items correspond with eight subscales: stigma (e.g. ‘concerns about being judged’), lack of motivation (e.g. ‘difficulty motivating self’), emotional concerns (e.g. ‘being seen while emotional’), negative evaluations of therapy (e.g. ‘bad experience with counselors’), misfit of therapy to needs (e.g. ‘attending counseling would feel self-indulgent’), time constraints (e.g. ‘interference with daily responsibilities’), participation restriction (e.g. ‘problems with transportation’), and availability of services (e.g. ‘don’t know how to find a counselor/therapist). For each item, participants ‘rate the degree to which different kinds of problems might get in the way of seeking therapy or counseling.’ Items are scored on a Likert scale from 1 (not difficult at all) to 5 (impossible). A prior validation study showed the mean score of overall barriers from the PBPT was 43.74 (Mohr et a., 2010). For this study, to be considered a significant barrier, an individual item must have been rated as moderately difficult (3), extremely difficult (4), or impossible (5). This was obtained at pre-assessment.

To understand general acceptability toward mental health treatment, qualitative interviews were conducted pre-treatment with all participants to understand aspects of mental health stigma (i.e. feelings on mental health and feelings on help-seeking), past experiences with mental health professionals, and what forms of mental health coping/support participants currently use. To understand acceptability of DBTMTB, qualitative questions regarding intervention satisfaction and acceptability was obtained at post-assessment for any participants that were randomized to DBTMTB and able to be contacted. We chose not to audio record the pre-intervention interviews to promote participant comfort and openness when discussing sensitive topics related to trauma and mental health. Instead, interviewers wrote participant responses in real time, which allowed for respectful, rapport-building conversations while minimizing potential concerns about privacy and confidentiality.

Interventions

WLC

WLC reflected treatment-as-usual including routine prenatal care with any mental health assessment, social work involvement or mental health service provision based on clinician referral or self-referral. At the time of the study, depression and PTSD were not being screened systematically during prenatal visits. There also was not an embedded mental health service provider or social worker in the clinic. If a patient showed distress during their prenatal appointment or asked for mental health support, they were referred to the hospital’s outpatient behavioural health clinic where patients would go on a first-come first-serve basis to initiate care (rather than a direct referral). Although not directly measured for this study, prior work by our team in this setting suggests that PTSD and depression diagnoses often go undiscussed in prenatal visits and behavioural health treatment engagement is limited, particularly for PTSD (Powers et al., 2020).

DBTMTB

We used a community-based participatory research (CBPR) approach led by the senior author of this study to develop a 90-minute 8-week DBTMTB prenatal intervention focused on mindfulness, distress tolerance, and emotion regulation skills. This was adapted from the full DBT Skills Training (Linehan, 2020), which includes four modules usually taught for 6–8 weeks each (i.e. Mindfulness, Interpersonal Effectiveness, Distress Tolerance, Emotion Regulation). Adaptations were informed by our prior CBPR with Black mothers (Woods-Jaeger, Cho, et al., 2018; Woods-Jaeger, Sexton, et al., 2018) and our Community Action Board. Table 2 provides an overview of intervention content. Session content followed Linehan’s DBT Skills Training structure; content changes focused on the inclusion of specific pregnancy and parenting applications of DBT skills. These adaptations aimed to create opportunities for participants to practice applying skills to manage chronic stressors related to pregnancy and parenting. They specifically addressed priorities identified in our formative work, including skills that support parenting goals to improve children’s lives and provide nurturing and support, while also helping parents address challenges such as their own trauma histories and mental health concerns (Woods-Jaeger, Cho, et al., 2018; Woods-Jaeger, Sexton, et al., 2018). We focused largely on mindfulness and emotion regulation DBT skills based on parents’ feedback that these skills were appropriate, feasible and acceptable (Woods-Jaeger, Sexton, et al., 2018) and taught skills within a framework of dialectics, emphasizing consistency, not rigidity. We supported pregnant women to employ skills based on their ‘wise mind,’ knowing that different situations call for different skills, and we emphasized the importance of mindfulness to assess and respond most effectively in the moment. Building on dialectical principles emphasized in standard DBT, we taught women that when confronted with stressful situations in pregnancy and as a parent, it is useful to focus on the ideas that: (1) You are doing the best you can at this moment, and you can consistently work to be the person and parent you want to be; and (2) You can accept that you will make mistakes and strive to improve your skills and take responsibility for effectively handling difficult situations. We also: (1) provided normalization and validation of their exposure to chronic stressors; (2) allowed women to benefit directly from observing and participating in solution generation for problems related to emotion dysregulation and pregnancy worries and concerns; and (3) enhanced social supports for pregnant women in a setting that they access for their prenatal care. Supervision also focused on trauma-specific and cultural considerations, ensuring content was appropriately tailored to our sample (e.g. how mindfulness skills can pair with spiritual practice). Two trained clinicians supervised by a licensed and board-certified clinician led the 8-week DBT sessions. When possible, Black or African American women served as clinicians (two out of four cohorts). To encourage treatment retention, groups were run with rolling admission and facilitators offered make up phone sessions to ensure participants who miss a session can immediately engage in the next session without concern of missed content. Phone sessions were not used by any participants despite being offered. Participants were considered completers if they attended 6 or more sessions. Group size cap was set at 6 individuals at outset; however, due to recruitment challenges, group size was small throughout, and each cohort only included 2 individuals (due to missed visits or differences in start date, at times participants engaged in the session content individually with facilitators; one group included 3 participants because a WLC person (post-study) was present). There was a total of 4 groups (1 in person, 3 virtual). For two of the four completers, the majority of the sessions were completed alone. Weekly skills rating surveys were collected at the beginning of each session. The study was designed for delivery in a prenatal clinic to promote accessibility and reduce potential stigma in mental health service engagement (transitioned to virtual due to COVID-19 pandemic).

Data analytic approach

Exploratory analysis of qualitative pre-treatment data was conducted using an iterative thematic coding approach. Two coders independently reviewed responses to questions and created codes; a separate coder (primary author) reviewed the codes and addressed disagreement to establish the final codebook. Then, two separate coders without knowledge of study participants or randomization coded all responses; any disagreement was discussed together with the primary author to determine the final code. Quantitative analyses examined descriptive statistics on barriers to treatment and mental health outcomes as well as relevant outcome measures (PTSD symptoms, depression symptoms, emotion dysregulation, trait mindfulness). Feasibility was measured based on treatment completion as well as retention levels for the overall study. Acceptability was evaluated based on post-treatment interviews with treatment completers. Given limited sample size for the qualitative post-treatment interview, the first author of the paper summarized these qualitative results based on available data without establishing specific themes due to lack of saturation.

When asked about what other sources of support are available to them in the community (besides mental health services), nearly half of women (43.3%, n = 13) reported no alternative networks and support in their community. Few women mentioned community health professionals and therapists. Discussion

This mixed-method study examined psychological treatment attitudes, experiences, behaviours, and barriers and evaluated initial feasibility and acceptability of DBTMTB, an adapted 8-week DBT Skills Training group, in a small sample of trauma-exposed African American pregnant women utilizing care in an urban public hospital obstetrics clinic. Although prior studies have examined DBT Skills Training groups for pregnant women with emotion dysregulation and a broad range of psychological disorders (Agako et al., 2023; Wilson & Donachie, 2018), this is the first to have an explicit focus on trauma- and ACEs-exposed pregnant African American women with PTSD or depression. Feasibility, as measured by study and intervention retention rates, was low and fell far below our expected level, driven by the poor completion rates of women randomized to DBTMTB condition at only 20%. Study retention in the WLC condition was greater than the hypothesized 65%, suggesting feasibility of future trials in this population if identified barriers in this sample can be adequately addressed. It is clear by the drastic differences in completion rates between DBTMTB and WLC that something substantial needs to shift to truly evaluate the potential benefits of such interventions for pregnant African American women.

While the completion rates for virtual DBTMTB were higher than for in-person, both were poor, suggesting virtual delivery alone does not address engagement barriers for this sample. Due to the limited sample size of DBTMTB women who completed the intervention and subsequent interviews across both modes of delivery, it is difficult to concretely suggest primary barriers to engagement. Nonetheless, the complex challenges and modifications to daily life presented by COVID-19 may have disrupted the availability of otherwise interested women. For example, there were 21 women that expressed interest in the intervention and scheduled participation but did not attend; attempting to make an additional obligation (albeit virtual) during the pandemic may have proven to be particularly challenging. It is possible that privacy within the home to engage in the intervention was another issue for mothers during the COVID-19 pandemic. Acceptability among study completers was high, but high levels of dropout suggest the need to adapt DBTMTB and better understand engagement barriers. Particular attention may be given to DBTMTB content and intervention structure to ensure that those enrolled in the intervention remain available, interested and engaged. Results from this pilot trial highlight implementation challenges that can inform future larger scale or adapted trials.

Pre-treatment quantitative and qualitative feedback from participants showed high levels of reported barriers to psychological treatment by pregnant women, the most prevalent being stigma, lack of motivation, emotional concerns and time constraints. PBPT results showed higher levels of perceived barriers compared to prior research with a randomly selected diverse sample of medical patients (Mohr et al., 2010). Despite frequent endorsement of barriers, many women reported a generally positive or neutral attitude toward professional help-seeking, and several women expressed a desire for care, which may be expected given their interest in the DBTMTB treatment study. Specifically, the pre-intervention survey highlighted half of women held positive sentiments towards getting help from mental health professionals, and more than half sought care from mental health professionals. This reflects that help-seeking attitudes and behaviours vary among the women and multiple approaches may be necessary to address unmet mental health needs. Half of the women approached for the RCT indicated they were not interested, and so the current study findings may not generalize to help-seeking attitudes among African American women that declined participation.

There are many potential contributors to the variety in help-seeking behaviours and engagement illustrated by this study. Only one-third of women reported positive prior experiences with mental health professionals, suggesting a gap in receiving adequate mental health services (among those who desired it), as well as a gap in satisfaction among those who had previously received mental health care. This is in line with prior research showing that some African American pregnant women found mental health resources to be generally available, but access to those resources was limited by having to individually seek out and navigate coordination of that care (Ash et al., 2025). It is unclear if lack of cultural competency or direct experiences with racism during mental healthcare engagement were factors in dissatisfaction and should be explored in future research. Several well-documented barriers to care were highlighted in the current study, such as insurance, cost, and transportation (Webb et al., 2023). Conversely, several women in the current study reported no barriers – a response that was often seen among those who had positive sentiments and/or experiences with mental health professionals. Almost half of mothers reported feelings of distrust and a lack of comfort related to help-seeking behaviour, with several noting how their help-seeking behaviour depended on the person. Prior research highlights how African American women have felt hindered in seeking care by the ‘Strong Black Woman’ stereotype and not wanting to show ‘weakness’; mental health stigma and uncertainty around seeking mental health care from certain providers are also factors impacting help-seeking (Ash et al., 2025). This finding has broader implications regarding who decides to engage with mental health care as well as how mental health care is delivered, pointing to the importance of both mental health intervention content and delivery, particularly who delivers these interventions.

While many mothers reported not knowing about alternative sources of support in their community to cope with toxic stress and trauma, others frequently noted a variety of community resources, including churches, community members, and neighbours. This supports previous research demonstrating the advantage of spirituality and religiosity as integrated components within mental health care among African Americans (Kaufman et al., 2020) as well as the potential to engage community members or lay health providers in providing mental health supports (Shelton et al., 2017; Woods-Jaeger et al., 2021). Pre-intervention results specifically highlighted that participants valued and leaned on religious elements to cope in times of stress, emphasizing the potential to build relationships between mental health providers and local religious community organizations. In addition, emphasizing synergistic elements of religiosity and mindfulness within the intervention (e.g. highlighting how mindfulness could integrate into active spiritual or religious practices) may help women feel more comfortable and connect more with intervention material and practices. More than half of women reported receiving support from family and/or friends who they could trust in times of stress. Recognizing that valid mistrust and a lack of comfort are large barriers to help-seeking among African American women (Jaiswal & Halkitis, 2019), a more communal model of care integrating existing social ties, or a peer-based model may prove advantageous in the future; special attention may also be given to how to effectively embed the intervention into community settings. While racial concordance and racism were not explicitly mentioned by the women in the study as barriers to care, previous research has found that these factors can impact provider-patient trust, perceptions and satisfaction with the provision of care among African American pregnant women (Chambers et al., 2022; OjiNjideka Hemphill et al., 2023; Teal et al., 2024). This study intentionally included Black or African American women clinicians as facilitators of the DBT sessions, however, they were not able to lead all cohorts due to lack of availability of Black or African American clinicians. Future interventions may consider ways to increase Black or African American clinician facilitation.

The poor feasibility for treatment completion found in our sample was a surprise and does not fit with prior studies in pregnant samples where retention rates were 63–75% (Agako et al., 2023; Wilson & Donachie, 2018). There are many factors that could have contributed to low study and intervention retention rates. The sample of women in the current study were recruited from a public hospital serving majority Medicaid or uninsured patients with limited socioeconomic resources and so many women were navigating other challenges (e.g. precarious housing, financial instability), which would understandably take priority over intervention participation. Additionally, most women were single and had additional children and thus were juggling childcare without other caregivers to share duties with, again potentially impacting the ability to attend sessions regularly. Our sample was very different from the samples from both prior prenatal DBT skills group intervention studies who were majority (or entirely) White and married (Agako et al., 2023; Wilson & Donachie, 2018) and may not have faced the same resource challenges. We also only offered the intervention at a single time on a week-day morning because of clinician availability and this limited who could engage. Having greater flexibility in when sessions were offered (e.g. offering sessions at multiple times during the week), choosing locations where mothers may already access childcare services (e.g. childcare centres) or have access to childcare could be facilitated (e.g. study-provided childcare, study providing compensation for childcare) could help reduce some of these barriers. A convenient or commonly frequented location could also help reduce the burden of transportation, which was often cited as a barrier to care. Additionally, we proposed a group in the RCT because it is more scalable and can provide greater access, however, the results of the study made clear that groups may not always be feasible if recruitment levels are low and offering the content 1:1 could aid with the need for flexibility. In this study, 1:1 sessions still resulted in a positive experience for the mothers that did participate, demonstrating that future trials may consider mixed delivery models (group vs. 1:1) or flexibility and patient choice to understand the best fit for pregnant mothers. Finally, given that stigma and prior negative experiences or lack of trust for healthcare providers were common barriers among our participants, it is possible that those got in the way of active engagement. Trust building may be necessary prior to treatment to ensure treatment retention and promote treatment success; incorporation of peer providers or community liaisons to deliver the intervention or facilitate the referral process may be helpful to that end.

Post-intervention participants expressed their overall satisfaction and the beneficial nature of the DBT intervention. Mothers found mindfulness activities helpful, as it allowed them to take time to focus on their emotions and helped relieve symptoms of anxiety and depression, in line with previous mindfulness literature among African American pregnant women (Abatemarco et al., 2021). Further impacts extended to an increase in intrapersonal emotional acceptance and self-compassion, supporting previous research demonstrating how mindfulness practices decrease depressive symptoms and increase self-compassion among pregnant women (Matvienko-Sikar et al., 2016). Participants also expressed how intervention skills increased their comfort with emotional regulation and expression, allowing them to better communicate their feelings in both personal and professional relationships. It was interesting to see the endorsement of improvements in relationships even without the inclusion of interpersonal effectiveness skills, highlighting the potential benefits of emotion regulation and mindfulness skills in the relational domain. Our findings suggest emotion regulation and mindfulness are valuable psychological factors to measure in such a treatment context and larger scale trials are needed to fully understand the benefits of DBTMTB on emotion regulation and mindfulness, as well as psychological symptoms. There remains limited research examining changes in mental health problems or transdiagnostic targets following DBT interventions for pregnant or postpartum women but results so far are promising (for recent review see Hellberg et al., 2024). Due to the limited sample size in the present study, we could not test symptom change and additional research in larger samples is needed to further understand the benefits of this intervention for various mental health outcomes among pregnant women.

The strengths of this study include the mixed-method design, the evaluation of a culturally adapted brief DBT Skills Training group intervention that was developed through a CBPR approach, and the inclusion of an understudied and vulnerable population. However, there are several limitations. The sample size was very small, making it difficult to fully establish feasibility and acceptability and limiting analysis capabilities for post-intervention qualitative data from the women in DBTMTB. Additionally, qualitative data was not recorded so full transcripts were not available, limiting the level of qualitative analysis that could be conducted. Due to the limited sample size, the group size was small as well and for some participants, content was delivered individually versus in the planned group context, limiting the valuable learning and connecting that can occur in groups. However, delivery of individual DBTMTB may have its own benefits, and future research should explore this. It remains unclear why participants did not utilize makeup phone sessions despite their availability and future trials should explore strategies to boost such engagement in the future. External factors, including a limited budget for the scale of the study and COVID-related delays, also likely affected the outcomes. Finally, engagement in behavioural health services among the WLC group was not measured during the study so it was impossible to characterize treatment as usual for this group.

Overall, this study highlights several barriers and challenges to mental healthcare access and engagement for African American women in pregnancy as well as factors that could increase feasibility, acceptability, and ultimately efficacy of DBT interventions for this population to be examined in future studies. Future larger scale trials must be conducted before a true determination can be made on the value of DBTMTB for pregnant African American women and their children; utilization of peer-led approaches to provide mental health care for this population may be particularly valuable to break down barriers surrounding medical mistrust and mental health stigma for African American women.
