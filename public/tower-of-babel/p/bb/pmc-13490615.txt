Arsenic exposure promotes retinal pigment epithelium dysfunction and oxidative stress via NF-κB pathway
Authors: Lan Zhong, Yumei Tao, Liming Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAge-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a leading cause of blindness in older adults and is closely linked to dysfunction of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). Arsenic (As), a ubiquitous environmental toxicant present in drinking water and food, has been implicated in multiple chronic diseases. However, its role in AMD remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the effects of As exposure on AMD-related retinal injury and to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms using in vitro and in vivo experimental models. ARPE-19 cells were treated with sodium iodate (NaIO3) to establish an AMD-like injury model and then exposed to 1 µM As to assess cell viability, apoptosis, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, autophagy-related changes and epithelial barrier integrity. Evaluation of NF-κB signaling pathway activation using molecular biology techniques. In vivo, a NaIO3-induced mouse model of AMD was established and combined with As exposure at 25 ppm to to validate retinal damage, oxidative stress, mitochondrial impairment, and barrier dysfunction. ShRNA-mediated silencing of NF-κB was further used to confirm pathway involvement. As exposure significantly aggravated NaIO3-induced injury in ARPE-19 cells and mouse retinas. In vitro, As reduced cell viability and enhanced apoptosis, inflammatory responses, reactive oxygen species accumulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, autophagy dysregulation, and epithelial barrier disruption. In vivo, As exacerbated retinal structural damage, increased oxidative stress, impaired mitochondrial function, and further reduced the expression of tight junction proteins in NaIO3-treated mice. Mechanistically, these effects were accompanied by sustained activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway. ShRNA-mediated silencing of NF-κB partially reversed As-induced retinal damage in vivo. Our study found that As exposure exacerbated AMD-like retinal degeneration via NF-κB pathway. These findings identified As as a potentially modifiable environmental risk factor for AMD and suggested that individuals in As-endemic regions may benefit from targeted retinal screening. Introduction

Arsenic (As) and its compounds are ubiquitous in the natural environment and are closely associated with human activities. Human exposure occurs predominantly through contaminated drinking water, food, and air. As a naturally occurring element, As is environmentally persistent and resistant to degradation. Once absorbed into the body, it can accumulate in keratin-rich tissues such as hair and nails. Chronic exposure to As disrupts endocrine homeostasis and exerts toxic effects on multiple organ systems1. Extensive toxicological and epidemiological evidence has demonstrated that long-term As exposure poses serious threats to human health, particularly through its strong association with the development of various malignancies, including skin, bladder, and lung cancers2. In addition, As exposure has been significantly linked to an increased risk of chronic diseases such as cardiovascular disorders and diabetes. Population-based studies indicate that drinking water and dietary sources, especially rice and seafood, constitute the principal routes of As exposure. Contamination of drinking water, in particular, facilitates As accumulation and biomagnification within the food chain, thereby posing substantial risks to public health3.

Among the diverse adverse health outcomes associated with As exposure, ocular diseases have garnered increasing attention. Although the relationship between As exposure and systemic conditions such as cancer, cardiovascular disease, and diabetes has been extensively investigated, relatively limited research has addressed its impact on ocular health. Existing evidence, derived primarily from in vitro experiments, animal models, and postmortem tissue analyses, suggests that As may exert detrimental effects on the retina and optic nerve. However, causal associations between As exposure and ocular diseases in human populations remain to be conclusively established4. Recent studies have suggested a potential link between As exposure and retinal disorders, particularly age-related macular degeneration (AMD). AMD is a chronic, age-associated ocular disease characterized by blurred vision, central visual field defects, and progressive visual decline, predominantly affecting individuals over 50 years of age5. The pathogenesis of AMD is multifactorial, involving both genetic susceptibility and environmental influences, with environmental pollution recognized as an important modifiable risk factor6. Epidemiological analyses have demonstrated that mixed exposure to As and other heavy metals is significantly associated with an elevated risk of AMD. Studies based on data from the U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and the Korean National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) have reported positive associations between As exposure and increased AMD risk7,8. Recent studies have further substantiated the association between As and retinal pathology. Zhylkibayev et al. demonstrated that dose-dependent As accumulation was detected in ocular tissues, and As concentrations in ocular tissues and the lens were significantly higher than those in organs such as the liver, lungs, heart, spleen, and brain9. Moreover, a comprehensive review by Aschner et al. systematically summarized epidemiological evidence linking toxic metal exposure, including As, to retinal pathology, and highlighted that As exposure may cause retinal damage through oxidative stress, apoptosis, mitochondrial dysfunction, and retinal inflammation4.

At the tissue level, investigations of donor eyes have revealed significantly elevated concentrations of As and other heavy metals in the choroid–retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) complex and neural retina of patients with AMD10–12. Mechanistically, As is known to induce the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby triggering mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammatory responses, and apoptotic pathways. These processes collectively contribute to oxidative stress-mediated retinal degeneration and may accelerate the onset and progression of AMD13.

To establish a reliable model of oxidative stress-driven RPE injury relevant to AMD, sodium iodate (NaIO3) was selected in this study. NaIO3 is a well-established oxidative stress inducer that has been extensively utilized in preclinical AMD research. Mechanistically, NaIO3 selectively targets the RPE, generating reactive oxygen species that trigger mitochondrial dysfunction, RPE degeneration, and subsequent photoreceptor loss14. Notably, recent studies have validated that NaIO₃ administration robustly recapitulates the cardinal features of dry AMD, including RPE atrophy, outer nuclear layer thinning, complement activation, and visual functional deficits, across multiple species including mice and non-human primates15–17. Moreover, the NaIO₃ model has been widely adopted in high-throughput screening contexts due to its well-controlled, reproducible, and cost-effective nature, enabling systematic dissection of oxidative and inflammatory pathways implicated in AMD pathogenesis18.

Despite emerging epidemiological evidence suggesting an association between As exposure and AMD, a comprehensive mechanistic framework explaining this relationship remains lacking. Further studies are urgently needed to validate these findings and elucidate the underlying biological mechanisms. A deeper understanding of the role of As exposure in AMD pathogenesis will not only advance our knowledge of the environmental etiology of AMD but also provide a theoretical basis for early diagnosis and targeted intervention strategies. Accordingly, the present study aims to systematically investigate the effects of As exposure on AMD-related retinal injury through toxicological experiments and to elucidate the potential molecular mechanisms involved.

Methods

Cell experiments

Cell culture and treatment

The human retinal pigment epithelial cell line ARPE-19 was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). Cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 12634010) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, A5670401) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 15140122) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2. The NC group received no treatment. The NIO group was treated with 1 mM sodium iodate (NaIO3, Sigma-Aldrich, S4007) to establish an in vitro AMD-like injury model. Co-exposure to As or exposure to As alone groups treated with sodium arsenite (NaAsO2; Sigma-Aldrich) at 1 µM for 24 h.

Cell viability and cytotoxicity assessment

Cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Abcam, ab228554). ARPE-19 cells were seeded into 96-well plates and treated as described. Following treatment, CCK-8 reagent (Glpbio Technology Inc., GK10001) was added to each well and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability was expressed relative to the control group. For dose-response experiments, ARPE-19 cells were treated with NaAsO2 at concentrations ranging from 0 to 100 µM. For time-course experiments, cells were treated with 1 µM NaAsO2 for 0–24 h. Based on the results, 1 µM NaAsO2 for 24 h was selected for all subsequent in vitro experiments.

Apoptosis analysis

Cell apoptosis was analyzed using an Annexin V-FITC/propidium iodide (PI) apoptosis detection kit combined with flow cytometry. After treatment, cells were collected and resuspended in Annexin V binding buffer (Elabscience Biotechnology, E-CK-A258), followed by staining with Annexin V-FITC (Elabscience Biotechnology, E-CK-A211) and PI (Elabscience Biotechnology, E-CK-A165) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were incubated in the dark and subsequently analyzed by flow cytometry. The proportions of viable, early apoptotic, late apoptotic, and necrotic cells were quantified using FlowJo software.

Measurement of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS)

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using the fluorescent probe 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA, Beyotime, S0036S). Treated cells were incubated with 10 µM DCFH-DA at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark, washed with PBS. Fluorescence images were captured using the fluorescence microscope under identical exposure settings for all groups.

We also measured RNS levels using the assay kit (BestBio, BB-462112). After treating the cells in groups, the culture medium was discarded and the cells were washed with PBS. The O53 probe was diluted 1:1000 with the diluent, added to the cells, and incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. After incubation, the supernatant was discarded, and the cells were washed three times with PBS. Corresponding images were obtained using a fluorescence microscope.

ATP and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assays

Intracellular ATP levels were determined using a commercial ATP assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A22007) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. In brief, cells were lysed with the provided lysis buffer, centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min, and the supernatant was mixed with the luciferase substrate. Luminescence was measured using a microplate reader. ATP content was normalized to total protein concentration.

Cellular injury was evaluated by measuring LDH release into the culture medium using an LDH cytotoxicity detection kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 88953). After treatment, 50 µL of culture supernatant was mixed with 50 µL of reaction mixture and incubated for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader.

Evaluation of epithelial barrier function

ARPE-19 cells were seeded onto Transwell inserts (6.5 mm diameter, 0.4 μm pore size, PET membrane; Corning) at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well) and cultured until a confluent monolayer was established. After treatment, transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) was measured using an EVOM2 epithelial voltohmmeter.

Barrier permeability was assessed using a fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-dextran (4 kDa; Thermo Fisher Scientific/Invitrogen™, D-713) permeability assay. FITC-dextran was added to the apical chamber, and after incubation, medium from the basolateral chamber was collected. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a fluorescence microplate reader to evaluate paracellular permeability.

Immunofluorescence assay

ARPE-19 cells grown on glass coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% normal goat serum. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against ZO-1, LC3B, or TOMM20. After washing, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488- or 555-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1 µg/mL). Coverslips were mounted and images were captured using a confocal microscope.

Animal experiments

Animals and experimental model

Male C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old) were randomly divided into different groups. The AMD-like model was established by administering a single intravenous tail injection of NaIO3 (35 mg/kg) to mice. For the mice in the As-treated group, regardless of whether they received the NaIO3 injection, NaAsO2 was added to their drinking water at 25 ppm for 6 consecutive weeks to induce As exposure. Previous study has shown that exposure to 0-250 ppm of NaAsO2 in drinking water for 4 weeks can lead to a dose-dependent increase in As levels in ocular tissues19. At the end of the experiment, all mice were deeply anesthetized with 3–5% isofluran and maintained at 1.5-2.0%. Once complete loss of pedal reflex was confirmed, euthanasia was performed by cervical dislocation. Death was confirmed by the absence of heartbeat and respiration before tissue collection. All animal procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University (KYLL 20240136) and were conducted in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines and other relevant guidelines and regulations.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

At the end of the experimental period, mouse eyes were enucleated and fixed for 24 h at 4 °C to preserve retinal morphology. After fixation, eyes were dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin wax. Serial Sect. (5 μm thickness) were cut using a rotary microtome through the entire eye. The collected sections were stained with Harris hematoxylin for 5 min, rinsed in running tap water for 10 min, differentiated in 1% acid alcohol, blued in Scott’s tap water substitute, and counterstained with 1% eosin Y for 2 min. Stained sections were examined under a light microscope (Olympus BX53). And the outer plexiform layer (OPL), inner nuclear layer (INL), outer nuclear layer (ONL), RPE thickness were measured.

Western blotting (WB)

Total protein was extracted from cultured cells or retinal tissues using RIPA lysis buffer (Bosterbio, AR0102-100). Protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Thermo Scientific, 23225). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 88585).

Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk (Elabscience, E-BC-R337) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Bax (Thermo Fisher Scientific/Invitrogen™, MA5-14003), Bcl-2 (Abcam, ab182858), cleaved caspase-3 (Abcam, ab32042), IL-6 (Abcam, ab216492), IL-1β (Abcam, ab254360), TNF-α (Antibodies-Online, AA 86–150), COX-2 (Abcam, ab179800), NOX4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, PA5-72816), SOD (Abcam, ab238052), and GPx (Prosci, 43–088), among others. After washing with TBST (G-Biosciences, 786–1742), membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Aviva Systems Biology, OPPA00841) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 34577) and quantified using ImageJ software. β-actin or GAPDH served as the internal loading control.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Cell culture supernatants or retinal tissue lysates were collected for ELISA analysis. Commercial ELISA kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific/Invitrogen™, 88-7066-88) specific for IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, COX-2, and related inflammatory mediators were used according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and cytokine concentrations were calculated based on standard curves.

mRNA expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific/Invitrogen™, 15596026), and RNA purity and concentration were determined spectrophotometrically. One microgram of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, K1691).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using SYBR™ Green Universal Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 4344463) and gene-specific primers (primer sequences are provided in the original table). Amplification was conducted on a real-time PCR detection system following standard cycling conditions. β-actin was used as the reference gene, and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method.

AAV-shRNA-mediated intraocular knockdown of NF-κB p65

To achieve stable intraocular knockdown of NF-κB p65 (Rela), a recombinant adeno-associated virus carrying short hairpin RNA targeting mouse Rela was constructed. The AAV serotype used was AAV9. The viral titer was 1 × 109 vg/eye, delivered in a volume of 1 µL per eye. Briefly, shRNA oligonucleotides targeting murine Rela were designed and cloned into an AAV expression vector under the control of the U6 promoter, while a fluorescent reporter driven by a separate promoter was included to monitor transduction efficiency. A scrambled shRNA sequence with no homology to the mouse genome was used as the negative control. Mice were anesthetized and pupils were dilated before surgery. Subretinal injection was performed using a Hamilton syringe fitted with a fine glass micropipette. AAV-shp65 was delivered into the subretinal space of one eye at a volume of 1–2 µL per eye, while avoiding lens injury and excessive retinal detachment. After injection, topical antibiotic ointment was applied to prevent infection.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were independently repeated at least three times. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test, whereas comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Statistical analyses and graphical visualizations were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 and R software (version 4.2.2). A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

As exposure exacerbates NaIO3-induced apoptosis, inflammatory response, and oxidative stress damage in ARPE-19 cells in a dose-dependent and time-dependent manner

We systematically evaluated the aggravating effects of As exposure on NaIO3-induced injury in ARPE-19 cells. CCK-8 assay demonstrated that As exposure significantly reduced cell viability in a dose-dependent manner (0-100 µM) across concentrations ranging from 0 to 100 µM (Fig. 1A). Based on these results, the concentration of 1 µM was selected for time-related analysis. As shown in Fig. 1B, exposure to 1 µM NaAsO2 led to a time-dependent decrease in cell viability over 24 h. Consequently, 1 µM NaAsO2 for 24 h was used in all subsequent experiments. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that, compared with the group treated with NaIO3 alone (NIO group), the group treated with NaIO3 and exposure to As (NIO + As group) exhibited a significantly higher proportion of apoptosis cells (Fig. 1C). Consistently, WB analysis showed that As exposure combined with NaIO3 treatment (NIO + As group) compared to NIO group upregulated the pro-apoptotic proteins Bax and cleaved caspase-3, while downregulating the anti-apoptotic protein BCL-2 (Fig. 1D-E). As exposure further enhanced NaIO3-induced elevated expression of inflammatory mediators, including IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and COX-2 (Fig. 1F). Furthermore, the results from the NC + As group compared to the NC group indicated that As exposure exhibits mild toxicity to normal cells. But when combined with NaIO3, it significantly promoted oxidative stress compared to the NaIO3 group, including the significant increase in NOX4 levels and further suppression of the expression of the antioxidant enzymes SOD and GPx (Fig. 1G). Figure 1H and I showed that total intracellular ROS and RNS levels were significantly higher in the group treated with NaIO3 in combination with As (NIO + As group) than in the NaIO₃-only group (NIO group).

As exposure aggravates NaIO3-induced barrier dysfunction and downregulates tight junction proteins in ARPE-19 cells

To evaluate the impact of As exposure on NaIO3-induced barrier function in ARPE-19 cells, this study employed analysis including TEER measurements, dextran permeability testing, WB analysis, and immunofluorescence staining. TEER measurements revealed that NaIO3 treatment significantly reduced monolayer resistance values compared to NC group. While As exposure alone had a minor effect (NC + As group compared to NC group), combined treatment with NaIO3 further impaired barrier function compared to NIO group (Fig. 2A). Similar results were observed in the dextran permeability assays (Fig. 2B). Additionally, compared to the NC group, the expression of tight junction proteins were downregulated in normal cells treated with NaIO3 (NIO group). Co-exposure to As further intensified this effect (Fig. 2C-D). Immunofluorescence staining for ZO-1 yielded similar results (Fig. 2E). In NC group, ZO-1 exhibited continuous distribution along the cell membrane. As treatment alone caused mild signal attenuation and discontinuity (NC + As group). NaIO3 treatment resulted in fragmented, punctate ZO-1 distribution with enlarged intercellular gaps (NIO group). And the NIO + As group showed the most severe loss of ZO-1 membrane localization.

As exposure further promotes mitochondrial dysfunction in NaIO3-induced ARPE-19 cells

WB results (Fig. 3A) revealed that compared to NIO group, combined As exposure with NaIO3 treatment further altered mitochondrial dynamics and the expression of autophagy-related proteins. This was reflected in the significant upregulation of DRP1, p62, PARKIN and PINK1, while the expression of the mitochondrial outer membrane translocase TOMM20 and OPA1 were markedly downregulated. Immunofluorescence analysis (LC3 and TOMM20) provided similar evidence (Fig. 3B). Compared to the group treated with NaIO3 alone (NIO group), in NIO + As group, punctate aggregates of the autophagy marker protein LC3 were more pronounced, with significantly enhanced levels (yellow signal) of TOMM20, suggesting changes in mitochondria-associated autophagic structures.

As exposure exacerbates NaIO3-induced disruption of retinal barrier in mice and activates the NF-κB signaling pathway

HE staining revealed that NaIO3 treatment caused disorganization of the outer retinal layers in mice compared to NC group, with thinning observed in layers such as the ONL. But As co-exposure (AMD + As group) further exacerbated these histological alterations (Fig. 4A). Quantitative analysis of retinal layers demonstrated significantly reduced thickness in the ONL, INL, OPL, RPE in AMD group, while the AMD + As group exhibited greater reduction in layer thickness (Fig. 4B-C). Also, expression of tight junction proteins ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin5 were downregulated after NaIO3 treatment (AMD group vs. NC group), reaching the lowest levels in AMD + As group (Fig. 4D-E). Moreover, phosphorylated p65, p50, and IκB levels increased in AMD group and further elevated in AMD + As group, while total protein levels showed no significant change (Fig. 4F-G).

As exposure promotes oxidative stress, impairing mitochondrial function in vivo

Results of oxidative stress level detection (Fig. 5A, B) showed that NaIO3 treatment significantly elevated ROS levels compared to NC group in multiple ocular tissues including the mouse cornea, vitreous, and retina. Combined As exposure (AMD + As group) further increased ROS levels, particularly in the vitreous and retina, where ROS fluorescence intensity was significantly enhanced compared to AMD group. Results in Fig. 5B indicated that NaIO3 treatment significantly reduced ATP content and increased LDH release in retinal tissue. The AMD + As group further exacerbated this change compared to the AMD group. WB analysis (Fig. 5C-D) showed that AMD + As group further enhanced DRP1, PINK1, PARKIN and P62 expression compared to AMD group.

As exposure exacerbates NaIO3-induced oxidative stress and barrier function damage in mice by modulating the NF-κB signaling pathway

ROS levels in RPE tissue was higher in AMD group than in NC group, and combined As exposure (AMD + As group vs. AMD group) further exacerbated ROS accumulation, which was reversed by application of sh-p65 (Fig. 6A). Additionally, As exposure (AMD + As group vs. AMD group) significantly reduced the activity of key antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, GSH, and CAT (Fig. 6B). However, these abnormalities were markedly alleviated upon addition of sh-p65. Flow cytometry revealed that As co-exposure (AMD + As group vs. AMD group) significantly increased the proportion of both early and late apoptotic cells induced by NaIO3 (Fig. 6C). Concurrent inhibition of the NF-κB pathway, however, also significantly suppressed apoptosis. Furthermore, NaIO3 treatment and As co-exposure (AMD + As group vs. AMD group) activated the NF-κB pathway, but the levels of key NF-κB pathway proteins were reversed following sh-p65 intervention (Fig. 6D-E).

WB results showed that in NC group, tight junction proteins exhibited stable high expression (Fig. 7A-F). In AMD group, levels of these proteins were significantly reduced. Mild downregulation was also observed in NC + As group. In the combined NaIO3 and As exposure group (AMD + As group), tight junction protein expression reached the lowest. Notably, shRNA-mediated knockdown of p65 led to marked recovery in barrier function protein expression. HE staining (Fig. 7G-J) revealed that retinal layers in NC group exhibited clear, well-organized structures with normal ONL and INL thickness. In contrast, AMD group showed markedly thinned layers, disorganized RPE cell morphology, vacuolar degeneration, and cell detachment. Although the NC + As group did not exhibit severe morphological damage, the NaIO3 and As co-exposed group (AMD + As group) showed the most pronounced structural disruption. Extensive thinning occurred across multiple retinal layers, with significant reductions in ONL and INL thickness. The RPE layer exhibited marked detachment and fragmentation, indicating that As exposure significantly exacerbates tissue damage associated with AMD lesions. However, following NF-κB pathway intervention, in the AMD + As+sh-p65 group, retinal layer thicknesses significantly recovered, and RPE layer structure remained relatively intact. This indicates that inhibiting the NF-κB pathway partially reverses the retinal structural degeneration exacerbated by As exposure.

Discussion

This study systematically elucidates the role and mechanisms of the environmental contaminant As in the initiation and progression of AMD. We establish, for the first time, an integrated evidence chain spanning epidemiological association to molecular mechanistic validation, demonstrating that As exposure markedly exacerbates AMD-like pathological injury20.

Mechanistically, As disrupts RPE homeostasis through multiple convergent pathways. First, As significantly amplifies oxidative stress, as evidenced by elevated intracellular ROS, upregulation of the pro-oxidant enzyme NOX4, and downregulation of antioxidant defenses (e.g., SOD and GPx)21. Sustained ROS accumulation promotes lipid peroxidation, DNA damage, and mitochondrial dysfunction in RPE cells, processes widely regarded as central drivers of AMD pathogenesis22. Second, As provokes a robust inflammatory response, characterized by markedly increased expression of pro-inflammatory mediators such as IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β23. Notably, we confirm persistent activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway in this context; pharmacological inhibition of NF-κB effectively mitigated apoptosis, oxidative stress, and barrier impairment induced by combined As and NaIO3 exposure, indicating that NF-κB is a critical mediator of As toxicity in RPE cells24–26. Recent studies have shown that As-induced ROS generation leads to NF-κB signaling activation27, and that the IKK complex plays a key protective role in countering As toxicity by maintaining cellular antioxidant capacity28. However, the toxicity of As to RPE cells and its effects on the NF-κB signaling pathway remains to be elucidated. Third, As impairs mitochondrial quality control, leading to loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, reduced ATP production, and suppression of key mitophagic processes, thereby promoting the accumulation of damaged mitochondria28. Finally, As compromises the epithelial barrier function of the RPE, as reflected by reduced expression of tight junction proteins (ZO-1 and occludin), decreased transepithelial electrical resistance, and increased permeability-features consistent with the blood-retinal barrier disruption observed clinically in AMD29.

In vivo experiments further corroborated these findings. In NaIO₃-induced AMD model mice, As exposure intensified structural disruption (outer nuclear layer thinning and reduced tight junction protein expression) and functional loss of the outer retinal barrier, accompanied by aberrant mitochondrial morphology and ROS accumulation. Moreover, shRNA-mediated knockdown of NF-κB partially reversed these injuries, further underscoring the central role of this pathway. The NaIO₃-induced oxidative stress model was selected because it selectively damages the RPE via reactive oxygen species and reproducibly recapitulates key dry AMD features, including outer nuclear layer thinning and barrier dysfunction, as validated by recent studies30,31. Although the model is inherently acute, its well-controlled and high-throughput nature makes it a widely accepted platform for dissecting oxidative and inflammatory pathways in AMD.

The present study provided important insights. It is the first to systematically demonstrate that As, an emerging environmental risk factor, accelerated AMD progression through promoting oxidative stress, inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and barrier breakdown. From a public health perspective, strengthened environmental As surveillance and interventions aimed at drinking-water purification are warranted. Supplementation with antioxidants or mitochondrial-protective agents may offer benefit for populations at risk of As exposure32,33.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, we did not directly measure arsenic concentrations in RPE or retinal tissues. Direct quantification by ICP-MS in our model would have provided stronger evidence of local target tissue exposure. Second, our study was not designed to conform to OECD regulatory toxicology guidelines, as the primary objective was mechanistic hypothesis testing rather than safety assessment. Consequently, we did not include multiple dose groups or comprehensive clinical pathology endpoints. Third, only a single arsenic concentration (25 ppm) was tested in vivo, which precludes assessment of dose-dependent effects in the mouse model. Although our in vitro data established a dose-response relationship, future studies examining a range of arsenic concentrations (e.g., 5, 10, and 25 ppm) would be valuable to determine whether the observed retinal effects exhibit dose-dependence in vivo. In addition, the NaIO3-induced model does not fully recapitulate the chronic progression and complex genetic background of human AMD34. While our study demonstrated that NF-κB p65 knockdown partially rescued As-exacerbated retinal damage, we acknowledged that RNA interference approaches carried the risk of off-target effects. Future studies using the CRISPR-Cas9-based knockout approach, would provide more definitive evidence. Despite these limitations, the consistency between our in vitro and in vivo findings, along with the positive rescue effect of NF-κB silencing, supports the conclusion that arsenic exacerbates AMD-like retinal injury through NF-κB pathway activation. Future studies incorporating tissue arsenic quantification, multiple dose levels, and extended exposure durations are warranted to further validate these findings.

Conclusion

This study systematically investigated the mechanisms by which As exposure modulates NaIO₃-induced injury in ARPE-19 cells and in a mouse model of AMD, with a particular emphasis on oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, mitochondrial dysfunction, and disruption of epithelial barrier integrity. Our findings demonstrate that As exposure markedly exacerbated NaIO₃-induced RPE damage, as evidenced by reduced cell viability, increased apoptotic rate, elevated oxidative stress, and upregulated expression of inflammatory mediators. Mechanistically, As aggravated retinal structural and functional impairment by activating the NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby promoting oxidative stress, loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, apoptosis, and downregulation of tight junction proteins. Collectively, these results indicate that As, as an environmental toxicant, can accelerate AMD progression through multiple convergent pathways, and its contribution to macular degeneration and related ocular disorders is likely closely linked to oxidative stress and inflammation. This work provides a mechanistic rationale supporting a pathogenic role of As in AMD and offers experimental evidence to inform future prevention and therapeutic strategies targeting environmentally associated retinal degeneration.
