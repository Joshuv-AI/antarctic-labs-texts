Home-Court Advantage and the Associations of Travel and Jet Lag with Team Performance in the Women’s National Basketball Association (WNBA)
Authors: Josh Leota, Dean J. Miller, Mark É. Czeisler, Jesse Cook, Luis Mascaro, Tim D. Smithies, Ayden Nelthropp, Elise R. Facer-Childs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTravel-related fatigue and circadian disruption (i.e., jet lag) are well-established factors limiting performance in elite men’s sport and are recognized contributors to home advantage. Yet evidence from women’s professional leagues remains scarce.AimThis study examined temporal trends in home-court advantage and assessed how cumulative travel burden and directional jet lag relate to performance in the Women’s National Basketball Association (WNBA).MethodsGame location, timing, and performance data from 3489 regular season WNBA games spanning 18 seasons (2007–2024) were analyzed using mixed-effects models adjusted for game time, team and opponent strength, and season. We quantified cumulative travel distance over the preceding seven days and directional jet lag based on eastward or westward travel adjusted for circadian resynchronization.ResultsHome-court advantage declined significantly over time (β = − 0.67, p = .002), from 64.3% in 2009 to 52.3% in 2024, coinciding with improved league-wide travel conditions. Greater recent travel distance was associated with worse performance among away teams (β = –0.04, p = .020), primarily driven by increased points conceded (β = 0.04, p = .018). Eastward jet lag was significantly associated with worse home team performance (β = –0.15, p = .049), with no such associations observed for westward jet lag or among away teams (all p ≥ .399).ConclusionThese findings provide the first large-scale evidence linking travel and circadian disruption to performance in the WNBA. Although home-court advantage has declined, travel-related challenges persist. Incorporating circadian principles into travel and recovery protocols may help improve performance, particularly as game schedules grow more congested with league expansion.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40798-026-01067-0.

Introduction

Frequent travel across time zones is a defining characteristic of North American professional team sports. Although numerous studies have examined the impact of rapid travel on performance in professional men’s leagues, such as the National Basketball Association (NBA) [1–4], National Football League (NFL) [5, 6], Major League Baseball (MLB) [7–9], and National Hockey League (NHL) [10, 11], research in women’s leagues remains critically understudied, with only limited work examining schedule load [12]. In particular, the impacts of travel direction and distance on team performance have not been systematically studied in the Women’s National Basketball Association (WNBA).

The WNBA was founded in 1996 as an NBA-backed effort to establish a sustainable professional women’s basketball league and featured eight teams in a 28-game regular season in its inaugural season. The league underwent cycles of expansion and contraction of teams and games before landing on a stable 12-team model by the 2010s (and a 13th franchise in 2025) alongside progressive increases in regular season games. The regular season typically runs from mid-May through mid-September involving multiple games per week before playoffs extending into October, aligning with a summer schedule that avoids overlap with the NBA. Absent lucrative media rights deals like the NBA, WNBA franchises operated on relatively smaller operational budgets, combatted in part by a 2022 $75 million capital campaign leading to initiatives including the 2024 league-wide chartered flights program [13].

WNBA athletes face highly demanding schedules that require consistent peak performance and rapid recovery, often under suboptimal travel conditions with repeated logistical disruptions [14]. Indeed, in a 2023 survey of WNBA athletes, most players voted travel as the biggest issue facing the league [15]. Existing research, predominantly conducted in males, indicates cumulative travel fatigue and circadian misalignment (i.e., jet lag) can adversely impact athlete physiology [16] and performance via impaired physical capacity [17, 18] and cognitive function [18].

Travel fatigue and jet lag are common yet distinct consequences of rapid transmeridian travel [19–21]. Travel fatigue is a non-specific cumulative disturbance that can arise after long duration travel, is independent of the number of time zones crossed, and is characterized by lethargy, changes in mood, and reduced motivation [22, 23]. Travel fatigue arises from the physical and psychological demands of travel, including prolonged uncomfortable seating conditions, psychological stress, reduced oxygen quality, dehydration, and physical inactivity [24, 25]. In contrast, jet lag reflects transient circadian disruption following rapid travel across time zones [26]. Human physiology and behavior are governed by a wide range of endogenously driven circadian rhythms that synchronize to the 24-h environment via the light–dark cycle [27], among other circadian cues. Travel across time zones can desynchronize internal circadian rhythms from the external 24-h environment, causing disruptions to athlete performance and wellbeing [28]. This desynchronosis—or jet lag—persists until internal rhythms entrain to the new 24-h environment, which generally occurs at a rate of 1-h per day [29]. For North American leagues like the WNBA, teams are based at varying longitudes, requiring frequent eastward and westward travel and, therefore, frequent circadian desynchrony.

Home-court advantage ([30] for a historical view, see [31]), the tendency for home teams to outperform visiting teams, is a multifactorial phenomenon, with contributing drivers including partisan crowd support, venue familiarity, and travel-related fatigue and circadian misalignment caused by the visiting teams’ travel demands [32–35]. However, existing literature has again primarily focused on male athletes, overlooking potential sex-specific physiological and psychological responses to travel-related stressors. This omission of female-focused research is problematic because females are physiologically distinct and are unlikely to respond identically to travel fatigue or jet lag compared to male athletes, in part due to sex-based differences in sleep and circadian regulation [36–38]. For example, females exhibit an earlier circadian phase angle of entrainment [39] and a shorter intrinsic circadian period [40] relative to males (e.g., 35% of females have average intrinsic circadian periods shorter than 24 h, compared to 14% of males), indicating a circadian system engineered to facilitate phase advances more than delays. In an evolving evidence base, there are also reports of sex differences in light responsiveness and photic resetting [41, 42], which have further implications for entrainment capacity. For travel, these differences suggest that females may, on average, adapt more readily to eastward (phase-advance) travel and males to westward (phase-delay) travel, though direct evidence remains limited and interindividual variability is substantial.

Beyond the implications of sex differences in biology, there are important structural and cultural sex differences in professional sports leagues. For example, whereas the NBA features fairly consistently timed evening games that reinforce stable, delay-biased circadian entrainment (> 90% games start between 6:00 p.m. and 9:30 p.m. local time [3]), the WNBA schedule exhibits substantially greater day-to-day variability in game timing, including a higher proportion of daytime contests (only 72% of games start between 6:00 p.m. and 9:30 p.m. local time). Increased game-to-game variability in game timing combined with more constrained travel logistics predisposes WNBA teams to more irregular light exposure and behavioral timing, introducing competing phase-shifting signals. Consequently, observed differences in jet lag responses between leagues may relate to schedule- and environment-dependent entrainment dynamics as much as sex differences in physiology.

Compounding this issue, WNBA players have historically faced under-resourced and suboptimal travel conditions, including travel via commercial flights, potentially exacerbating fatigue and circadian disruption. Further, collecting data related to circadian rhythmicity in athletes remains logistically and methodologically challenging [38, 43], highlighting the need for innovative research in this area.

Aims of the Study

This study presents the first large-scale analysis of home-court advantage, travel demands, and directional jet lag in the WNBA. Based on 18 years of regular season WNBA games (2007–2024), we aimed to (1) describe temporal trends in home-court advantage; (2) examine the association between recent cumulative travel distance and team performance; and (3) examine the association between directional jet lag and team performance.

Materials and Methods

Regular season game data from 18 WNBA seasons (2007–2024) were compiled from ESPN.com using the open-access R package wehoop (v2.1.0) [44]. To verify the accuracy of the publicly available data, we randomly selected 10 games and cross-referenced the date, venue, location, and score against an independent source (the official WNBA website, https://stats.wnba.com). All data from sampled games were consistent. Games from the Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) impacted 2020 “Wubble” season were excluded from travel and jet lag analyses due to the absence of travel between games [45]. Prior seasons were not included due to variations in game structure (the WNBA transitioned from two 20-min halves to four 10-min quarters) and to avoid the compositional disruption caused by the Charlotte Sting dispersal draft prior to the 2007 season. Games representing the WNBA Commissioner’s Cup Final were also excluded due to selection bias toward stronger teams, their playoff-like nature, and their neutral-site locations. Game statistics were missing for four regular season games from the data source and therefore were individually inspected. Two of the four games were missing due to venue changes within the same city; in these cases, box score data were manually retrieved from the official WNBA website (https://stats.wnba.com/scores) and added to the dataset. The remaining two games were excluded from analysis due to exceptional circumstances. One was postponed due to poor air quality impacting outdoor conditions in New York City and rescheduled for a later date, with no official box score recorded for the original fixture [46]. The other was a 2018 forfeit, in which the Las Vegas Aces were unable to compete against the Washington Mystics after more than 25 h of commercial travel delays and cancellations, which ultimately resulted in the players unanimously voting not to play and forfeiture of the game [14]. A total of 3489 regular season games (M = 205.24 per season, SD = 14.60) met the inclusion criteria for the travel and jet lag analyses. This study did not require ethical approval from an institutional review board as all data were obtained from publicly available records and human participants were not directly recruited or involved.

Travel Predictors and Game Outcome Variables

Travel Distance

To quantify travel demands for each WNBA team, we calculated the cumulative travel distance (in miles) over the seven days preceding each regular season game. Game schedules and team box scores were accessed using the wehoop R package (v2.1.0) [44], with location data linked to each team’s home venue. Games were geocoded using U.S.A. city coordinates and team-specific stadium locations.

The Haversine formula (i.e., great-circle distance between two points on a sphere) was used to calculate the distance, in miles, between the latitudes and longitudes of each team's current and previous game locations using the geosphere R package (v1.5.18) [47]. These travel distances were summed within a rolling 7-day window, producing the primary travel distance predictor variable (i.e., total miles traveled over the past seven days).

Directional Jet Lag

To estimate potential travel-induced circadian misalignment, directional jet lag was defined as the number of time zones traveled after accounting for a resynchronization rate of 1-h per day (see Fig. 1), as athletes have demonstrated return to baseline performance following sufficient circadian adjustment [3, 8]. Specifically, eastward and westward travel were coded based on the difference in time zone codes between the current and previous game locations. The number of days between games was subtracted from the time zone difference to estimate potential remaining misalignment after resynchronization. If the resulting value was 1 h or more, the game was coded as having eastward or westward jet lag, depending on the direction of travel; otherwise, the game was coded as no jet lag. Team Performance

Team performance was measured using points differential, calculated as total points scored minus total points conceded. This continuous outcome captures game-level variation in performance more sensitively than binary outcomes (e.g., win vs. loss), which may obscure meaningful differences in team performance. When significant points differential associations were observed, additional models were run to test whether these relationships were primarily driven by team points scored (i.e., changes in offense), team points conceded (i.e., changes in defense), or a combination of both.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed in R version 4.3.3 [49]. To examine Aim 1, which explored historical trends in home-court advantage across WNBA regular seasons, we calculated average home win percentage (i.e., the proportion of games won by the home team) and average home points differential (i.e., average game total points scored minus total points conceded) for each season from 2007 to 2024. These values were aggregated at the season level and visualized using line plots to examine temporal changes.

To address Aims 2 and 3, we fitted multilevel mixed-effects linear models using the lme4 package (v1.1.36) [50]. The primary outcome was team points differential and the primary predictors were cumulative distance traveled over the past seven days, modeled continuously, and directional jet lag, modeled as a three-level factor (westward vs. eastward vs. no jet lag). Both predictors were included in the same model to account for potential shared variance and to isolate the unique contribution of each travel-related factor. Game time (in local decimal hours; e.g., 18:30 = 18.5) and season-long win percentage differential (team win percentage minus opponent win percentage) were included as covariates to account for time-of-day effects [51] and team (and opponent) strength, respectively [1, 52]. Given that schedule congestion has been associated with game performance in prior work [12, 52, 53], we included a covariate that quantified the number of games played in the prior 10 calendar days. We also included the team’s prior game point differential to adjust for potential autocorrelation in team performance.

To adjust for clustering and repeated measures, we included two random intercepts in the model: team identifier and opponent identifier, each nested within season. Results

Trends in WNBA Home Advantage

The WNBA regular season home winning percentage (β = −0.67, 95% CI [−1.06, −0.28], t = − 3.60, p = 0.002, R2 = 0.45) and average home points differential (β = −0.63, 95% CI [–1.04, –0.22], t = − 3.23, p = 0.005, R2 = 0.40) declined significantly over the 18-season observation period (Table 2 and Fig. 2A–B). From 2007 through 2017, home teams won ≥ 55% of games in 10 of 11 (91%) seasons, peaking in 2009 at 64.3%. In contrast, from 2018 through 2024, home teams won ≥ 55% of games in just one of seven (14%) seasons, reaching a low of 51.7% in 2023. Notably, home winning percentage was exactly 50.0% in the pandemic-impacted 2020 season which saw all games played at a neutral venue. Home points differential followed a similar downward trend, peaking in 2009 at a + 4.0 points per game advantage and declined to just + 0.8 points per game advantage by 2024. Discussion

Here, we present the first large-scale, longitudinal analysis of home advantage, travel demands, and directional jet lag in the WNBA. Using data from over 3400 regular season games spanning 18 seasons (2007–2024), we showed that home-court advantage in the WNBA has declined over time, with the decline more pronounced and consistent in the most recent five seasons (2020–2024). Additionally, we observed differential dynamics for home and away teams regarding associations between game performance and travel-related factors (distance and jet lag). Specifically, greater travel distance in the week preceding a game was significantly associated with worse points differential for away but not home teams. This finding was seemingly driven by opponents scoring more points as cumulative travel distance increased, which may reflect degraded defensive performance, or other in-game performance consequences of travel-related impairment. Further, we observed significantly worse home (but not away) team performance when experiencing eastward (but not westward) jet lag. These findings in the WNBA build upon the existing literature related to travel and professional sport performance, which has predominantly been performed in the context of male professional sports [54]. Importantly, the associations observed are generally consistent with previous work performed in male athletes.

Trends in WNBA Home Advantage

Home-court advantage in the WNBA declined steadily from 2009 to 2024, consistent with attenuation of home-court advantages over time observed in other professional sports leagues [55, 56]. One potential explanation is that improvements in travel conditions and the broader professionalization of sports leagues have reduced the physical and psychological burdens traditionally associated with playing on the road [54, 57]. Travel conditions in the WNBA have changed significantly since the league’s first season in 1997. For example, under the WNBA’s 2020 collective bargaining agreement, players were guaranteed upgraded travel provisions, including comfort or economy plus seats on airlines and individual hotel rooms when travelling [58]. More recently, beginning with the 2024 season, the WNBA implemented full-time charter flights for all teams, driven in part by concerns over player health and safety [13]. Consistent with these improved travel conditions, home-court advantage from 2021 to 2024 averaged 53.0%, only one-third of what it was from the 2007 to 2019. These travel and accommodation advancements may have partly contributed to the reduced home-court advantage across the most recent seasons observed in the current study.

Home winning percentage and home points differential exhibited biannual fluctuations, with higher-magnitude differences in odd calendar years particularly between 2009 and 2019. Systematic differences intrinsic to the WNBA league scheduling are unlikely to account for these fluctuations, as teams hosted and visited an approximately equal number of games per season and team performance varied over time. One explanation could be Olympic and FIBA World Championship tournament years, which occur selectively on even calendar years. Star WNBA player participation in these tournaments may result in more league parity due to scheduling impacts (a month-long hiatus on Olympic years and earlier season in FIBA World Championship years) and star player absences (from practices and team events, if not games). The fluctuations appear diminished during 2021 to 2024, suggesting that the same or co-occurring factors associated with the decline in home-court advantage have also influenced these fluctuations. Moreover, fluctuations may be reduced as a result of smaller-magnitude home-court advantages.

The pandemic-disrupted 2020 WNBA season, which took place in a single-site “Wubble” environment in Bradenton, Florida, altered multiple aspects of home versus away team dynamics. Living in the Bubble and effectively eliminating travel minimized potential jet lag effects, and no home-court advantage was observed. Similar patterns were observed in the NBA during the same period, where the removal of travel was linked to the elimination of home-court advantage [59, 60]. However, the removal of in-person fans during these seasons also removed the potential influence of home crowd support, making it difficult to disentangle the specific effects of travel from crowd support on home-court advantage [33]. In-person fans returned in the 2021 WNBA season (except for Indiana Fever home games), but travel was reduced amid the pandemic by implementing two-game mini-series [61]. During that season, home-court advantage remained relatively low (53.9%), suggesting a reduced travel burden may have continued to dampen the typical benefits associated with playing at home.

Travel Distance and WNBA Team Performance

Greater accumulated travel distance across the week preceding a game was associated with worse points differential for away (but not home) teams. This finding suggests that cumulative travel is associated with adverse effects on team performance, consistent with prior studies linking travel fatigue to reduced athletic performance [62, 63]. The association was driven by an increase in points conceded by away teams, suggesting travel fatigue may disproportionately impair defensive, rather than offensive, performance. However, alternative in-game factors could have been affected, contributing to more points conceded. For example, a team may make more offensive turnovers or commit more fouls. As such, this association may not only reflect degraded defensive performance, but rather overall game performance that translates into more points conceded.

This defense-specific relationship is consistent with the cognitive and psychological demands of defensive play. Successful defensive execution relies on rapid and reactive decision-making in response to dynamic offensive actions, potentially making it more sensitive to impairments in attention and choice reaction time caused by travel fatigue and poor sleep [22, 64]. In contrast, offensive sets are often more structured and rehearsed and, at times, directed by the sideline coaching staff, potentially buffering them from the same performance declines.

Away teams in our study often faced suboptimal travel conditions relative to male professional sports leagues. In August 2018, the Las Vegas Aces forfeited their game against the Washington Mystics after enduring over 25 h of travel delays and cancelled commercial flights. The decision to forfeit was made on the grounds of player safety, with players and coaches citing excessive travel fatigue and increased injury risk [14]. Earlier collective bargaining agreements prohibited chartered flights as it was deemed an unfair competitive advantage. In 2022, the New York Liberty were fined $500,000, the most in league history, and risked termination for chartering flights for their players in the 2021 season [65]. In 2024, in response to ongoing concerns about player safety, and amid the league’s growing popularity, the WNBA announced a $25 million per year investment to fund full-time charter flights for all teams [13].

Directional Jet Lag and WNBA Team Performance

Home teams had a significantly worse points differential when playing with eastward jet lag compared to no jet lag. The magnitude of this difference (−1.86 points per game) is twice as large as the difference between the 6th (−2.68, Indiana Fever) and 9th (−3.30, Phoenix Mercury) best points differentials during the 2024 WNBA regular season. There was no association when playing with westward jet lag, nor when playing on the road. These findings are consistent with previous research that demonstrated eastward- and home-specific associations with impaired performance in the NBA [3] and MLB [8].

Human circadian rhythms can be shifted earlier through a phase advance or later through a phase delay [66]. As the average human endogenous circadian period is slightly longer than 24 h, humans have a natural tendency to delay rather than advance [67, 68]. As a result, westward travel, which typically requires a phase delay, is generally easier to adapt to than eastward travel, which typically requires a more difficult phase advance [69]. The performance decline observed in the current study may reflect biological challenges associated with phase advancing the circadian clock following rapid eastward travel [23, 70]. Such phase advances require athletes to fall asleep and wake up earlier than their homeostatic and circadian drives for sleep would otherwise signal, resulting in a mismatch between internal physiological rhythms and external cues (e.g., light). This circadian misalignment can disrupt sleep, reduce alertness, and negatively impact performance and motivation, particularly within 72 h of arriving in the new time zone [17]. Eastward travel disadvantage is not unique to basketball, with similar effects being observed in college football [71], international soccer [72], and elite female skeleton athletes [73].

Despite shorter intrinsic circadian periods and photic response profiles that would theoretically favor more efficient phase advances in females, eastward travel and home-game contexts are consistently associated with impaired performance across both the WNBA and NBA. These findings suggest that shared structural and environment-driven factors such as travel logistics, schedule congestion and variability, and behavioral constraints may outweigh potential sex-based differences in circadian physiology.

We did not observe an association between eastward or westward travel and performance among away teams, consistent with previous work [3, 8]. One explanation may be that away teams benefit from more structured environments during travel as compared to at home. Team management can proactively manage sleep–wake schedules, training loads, meal timings, and recovery protocols, which may help alleviate potential jet lag symptoms [21, 23, 74]. Away teams may purposefully maintain alignment with their home time zone during short trips—scheduling meals, light exposure, physical activity, and sleep accordingly—to minimize circadian disruption (i.e., “time zone maintenance”). In contrast, players returning to their home time zones may experience greater disruptions to their routines as they depart for their individual homes and attend to family and social obligations.

Practical Applications

These findings have practical applications for athletes and support staff. Away teams that accumulate greater travel distance in the week preceding a game showed worse defensive performance. Practitioners could consider circadian-informed scheduling of training loads, light exposure, and sleep timing to facilitate adaptation prior to competition [21]. Similar considerations should be made for home teams travelling eastward in the days preceding a home game.

At the league level, these findings emphasize the value of schedule design that minimizes consecutive cross-time zone travel, particularly for away fixtures, and supports the continued implementation of travel provisions such as charter flights introduced under the 2024 collective bargaining agreement. Importantly, these recommendations should be interpreted in the context of the broader home-court advantage landscape; travel and jet lag are likely contributors to, rather than sole determinants of, home-court advantage, and the effect sizes observed here are modest. Individualized, circadian-informed travel protocols represent an evidence-based avenue for mitigating performance decrements in a league where travel demands remain substantial.

Study Strengths

This research draws on nearly two decades of WNBA regular season game data (2007–2024) to present the most comprehensive longitudinal analysis of travel, jet lag, and team performance in the women’s professional basketball to date. Second, unlike the vast majority of sports and exercise science research which centers on men athletes, this study addressed an important gap by focusing on professional women athletes, a critically underrepresented population in the field [75]. Third, by using multilevel mixed-effects models with teams and opponents nested within seasons and covariate adjustment for game time, team relative win rate, recent schedule congestion, and prior game performance, our analysis accounted for structural changes in the league over time and adjusted for variation in contextual factors such as competition timing and team strength.

Study Limitations and Future Directions

Consistent with established approaches in chronobiology and sports science and in an effort to improve reproducibility and comparisons with published literature, we estimated travel fatigue and circadian misalignment via directional jet lag at the team level via generalizations about travel time and individual circadian biology. This approach, featuring a uniform 1-h per day resynchronization rate, does not account for inter- and intra-individual heterogeneity in circadian phase, circadian sensitivity, and sleep–wake behavior, which have been shown to moderate performance responses to travel [76, 77]. Our team-level estimates should thus be interpreted as approximations of average circadian misalignment rather than exact individual-level risk. Future research should incorporate player-level data (e.g., continuous sleep–wake monitoring, menstrual cycle and circadian phase assessments) to better quantify this association. Additionally, future research should explore whether travel fatigue and circadian disruption affect professional WNBA players’ health and wellbeing beyond on-court performance. Emerging evidence suggests that female athletes may be particularly vulnerable to poor physical and mental health outcomes when exposed to disrupted sleep, irregular sleep patterns, and circadian misalignment [38, 78]. Given the WNBA’s condensed travel schedules and comparatively limited support resources, players may disproportionately face psychological challenges relative to athletes in better-funded professional sports leagues.

This study focused on relationships between travel predictors and global indicators of game performance (e.g., total point differential, points scored, and points conceded), which reflect only a subset of basketball performance outcomes. Future research examining relationships between travel-related dynamics and more granular features of basketball performance, such as free throw percentage, turnovers, steals, personal fouls, and rebounding rate, may provide unique insight into the impact of travel on specific components of basketball performance. Indeed, examples of such granularity exist in studies examining the association between travel and performance in male basketball competitions [1–3, 60, 79].

Travel distance and jet lag estimates were calculated based on game locations only, as team travel itineraries are not publicly available due to privacy considerations. Therefore, our travel and jet lag estimates are underpinned by the conservative assumption that teams departed for their next game destination immediately following each game. To the extent that teams departed later, arrived closer to tip-off, and therefore spent less time in the destination time zone before the game, our estimates of travel demands and circadian resynchronization time may underestimate the true impact of travel and circadian disruption. Team training venues and practice schedules are also not publicly available, and it is possible that some travel occurred between training and competition venues that is not captured in our estimates. Future research working directly with teams could integrate travel itineraries and training data to refine these estimates. Although our models adjusted for opponent and team strength, recent schedule congestion, and travel demands as independent covariates, residual contextual confounding may remain, and findings reported here should be interpreted as adjusted average associations. Finally, although we adjusted for serial dependence by including each team’s prior-game points differential, we did not model a formal residual autocorrelation structure. Future research could extend these findings via alternative modeling frameworks, such as logistic mixed-effects models with binary win/loss outcomes, explicit autocorrelation structures, or lagged-effect specifications.

Conclusion

The present study is the first to examine over 3,400 regular season WNBA games from 2007–2024 to describe long-term trends in home-court advantage and examine associations between travel and jet lag on team performance. Home-court advantage has declined, although travel-related factors remain associated with team performance. Greater travel distance in the week preceding a game was associated with poorer away team performance, while eastward jet lag was associated with poorer home team performance, revealing that teams face distinct travel-related challenges when playing at home and as visitors. As the WNBA expands and with expectations for an increasingly demanding schedule [80], there remains an important need for more research in this population and league initiatives that incorporate sleep and circadian biology into scheduling as well as teamwide and athlete-specific strategies to mitigate travel fatigue and jet lag.
