The emerging role of ERphagy/reticulophagy in pathogen invasion
Authors: Damian Gatica, Daniel J Klionsky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn recent years ERphagy, the selective autophagic degradation of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), has emerged as a key selective autophagic pathway involved not only in the recycling of the ER, but also in preventing the replication of viruses and bacteria. The mechanisms by which ERphagy achieves this do not seem immediately related to canonical xenophagy pathways and could provide a new avenue for therapeutic targets to combat pathogenic infections. Macroautophagy/autophagy is a critical recycling pathway that maintains cellular homeostasis by promoting the clearance and lysosomal degradation of damaged and/or superfluous cytosolic components, including proteins, and organelles; this process is also used for the removal of invasive pathogens [1]. Autophagy selectivity is achieved by specific protein receptors that act as adaptors between the cargo selected for degradation and the expanding sequestering membrane, the phagophore, that matures into an autophagosome [2]. Xenophagy is the term used for the selective autophagic degradation of intracellular pathogens, such as viruses (in a process also termed virophagy) and bacteria, allowing the cell to respond to microbial invasion. Nevertheless, in recent years ERphagy, the selective autophagic degradation of the ER, has emerged as a key selective autophagic pathway involved in preventing the replication of viruses and bacteria inside the cell and promoting their clearance. Furthermore, multiple reports have highlighted how pathogens seek to block or highjack this pathway to promote their survival, indicating that ERphagy could be a promising therapeutic target to combat intracellular microbes.

During ERphagy specific portions of the ER can be targeted for degradation promoting ER homeostasis, regulating ER morphology and size, and clearing ER lumenal misfolded protein aggregates that can be toxic for the cell [3]. Similar to other selective autophagic pathways, ERphagy utilizes specialized receptors, including RETREG1/FAM134B, RETREG2, RETREG3, TEX264, CCPG1, RTN3L, ATL3 and SEC62 to regulate this process [4]. These specialized receptors are involved in the response against various viruses and bacteria, emphasizing the role that ERphagy plays in the replication cycle of these pathogens. For instance, ERphagy through its protein receptor RETREG1, has been implicated in the cellular response against different viruses, including HIV [5], SARS-CoV-2 [6], Ebola [7], Zika and dengue [8]. The ER constitutes a main source of membranes and protein synthesis machinery for viruses; thus, by controlling ERphagy, viruses can prevent the degradation of viral structural proteins and adapt the ER morphology to their own needs. For example, SARS-CoV-2, through its ORF8 protein, highjacks the ERphagy receptors RETREG1 and ATL3, preventing ERphagy and increasing viral replication [6]. Similarly, Zika and dengue virus utilize their viral proteases to cleave RETREG1, preventing the ERphagy-mediated degradation of viral proteins [8]. Additionally, other reports have emphasized the role of ERphagy and RETREG1 as a limiting factor in Ebola replication [7] and neurocognitive impairment in children infected with HIV [5]. ERphagy is also involved in the degradation of the innate immune adapter STING1, a key factor in the type I interferon (IFN-I) response to virus infection. Both RETREG1 [9] and TEX264 [10] are upregulated during HSV-1 infection, which results in ERphagy-mediated STING1 degradation and decreased IFN-I production in infected cells. Other ERphagy receptors such as SEC62 are also implicated in restricting positive-strand RNA virus infection through ERphagy induction in plants [11], indicating a conserved response mechanism to viral infection involving ERphagy.

Pathogenic bacteria that have ER remodeling capabilities are also involved in targeting ERphagy receptors to promote their intracellular replication. ER fragmentation and remodeling triggered by Legionella pneumophila infection, involves the phosphoribosyl-linked ubiquitination and recruitment of RETREG1, RETREG2, RETREG3 and TEX264 to generate Legionella replicating vacuoles [12]. Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium, known to interact with the ER and actively change endomembrane morphology through the formation of filaments, can inhibit ERphagy by means of the Salmonella effector SopF, preventing RETREG1 activation and increasing bacterial burden [13]. Consistently, the ER has long been proposed as the locus for the formation of autophagosomes involved in antibacterial autophagy [14]. Several studies have now proposed that RETREG1-mediated ERphagy activation protects against lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced sepsis in a myocardial injury model in mice by reducing inflammation and apoptosis [15]; and in dendritic cells by relieving ER stress and preventing ferroptosis [16]. Moreover, ERphagy activation through RETREG1 induction is determinant in dendritic cell maturation and correct immune function when confronted with LPS-induced sepsis [17,18].

Whether these reports give a full picture of the role and implications of ERphagy in pathogenic restriction, the full scope of its functions and prospective therapeutic applications remains an open question. Multiple important issues remain to be addressed if we are to fully understand and modulate ERphagy to combat pathogenic infections. Among these, probably the most important one would be to elucidate a possible direct crosstalk between ERphagy and xenophagy. Future studies will be necessary to answer this and other questions to fully realize the undeveloped potential of ERphagy as an innate immune response against pathogens.
