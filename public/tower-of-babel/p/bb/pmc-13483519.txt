Identification of risk factors for postpartum pelvic floor dysfunction in a Chinese population: a retrospective study
Authors: Yunfang Yan, Xiaohua Pan, Wenjie Lv, Xiufang Shuai, Yunxia Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo identify risk factors for postpartum pelvic floor dysfunction (PFD) diagnosed at 6 weeks after delivery in a Chinese population.MethodsThis retrospective study examined data from 828 postpartum women in China, collected from January 2020 to August 2021. Multivariate analyses were employed to identify factors associated with six outcomes of PFD, including anterior vaginal wall prolapse, posterior vaginal wall prolapse, uterine prolapse, stress urinary incontinence (SUI), and impaired type I and type II muscle fiber strength.ResultsA series of factors are considered to be associated with PFD.Cesarean delivery significantly reduced severe anterior vaginal wall prolapse risk (OR=0.426, P＜0.001). Age, overweight (BMI ≥24.9 kg/m²), macrosomia (birth weight ≥4000g), forceps-assisted delivery, and constipation significantly increased risks of anterior and posterior vaginal wall prolapse, uterine prolapse, and SUI (all OR>1, P<0.05). Twin pregnancy reduced type I (OR=0.430, P=0.013) and type II (OR=0.385, P=0.009) muscle fiber strength but increased risks of anterior vaginal wall prolapse, uterine prolapse, and SUI. Higher parity (two or more) was correlated with higher risks of anterior (OR=2.402, P<0.001) and posterior vaginal wall prolapse (OR=2.977, P=0.0003) and SUI (OR=1.726, P=0.001). Heavy physical labor was strongly associated with posterior vaginal wall prolapse (OR=6.285, P=0.008), uterine prolapse (OR=5.264, P=0.009), and SUI (OR=3.987, P=0.024). Underweight status (BMI<18.5 kg/m²) significantly increased risks of impaired type I (OR=0.173, P<0.001) and type II muscle fiber strength (OR=0.197, P=0.0003).ConclusionThis study identified significant PFD risk factors in a Chinese population, highlighting the importance of targeted prenatal, perinatal, and postpartum care to mitigate these risks. In addition to uncontrollable factors like mode of delivery, age, and parity, interventions targeting weight gain and fetal macrosomia during pregnancy and appropriate assisted delivery could mitigate the risks of PFD. Educational programs for pregnant women should emphasize a proper diet and lifestyle. For women with chronic constipation, clinical treatment should be carried out as soon as possible to avoid further aggravating the damage to the pelvic floor muscles.Keywords: Pelvic floor dysfunction, Postpartum, Risk factors, Pelvic organ prolapse, Stress urinary incontinence

Introduction

The female pelvic floor is a complex support system, consisting of different layers of muscles, fascia, ligaments, and nerves, which interact to maintain the normal position of the pelvic floor organs and support sexual function [1]. When these supporting tissues are weakened, pelvic floor dysfunction (PFD) occurs, leading to changes in the position and function of the pelvic organs [2]. PFD is a common and prevalent condition in women, mainly including pelvic organ prolapse (POP), stress urinary incontinence (SUI), fecal incontinence and sexual dysfunction. The prevalence of the four components of PFD ranges from 20.9% to 67.7% (while 60.2% of the 824 participants had PFD, 67.7% reported signs of bowel dysfunction. Urinary dysfunction, prolapse, and sexual dysfunction were present in 44.1%, 67.7%, and 55.4% of the participants, respectively) [3].

PFD has long-term impacts on women. Untreated SUI can severely affect the lives of women due to its uncontrollable characteristics, often referred to as “social cancer”. Women with PFD often experience psychological challenges which, in turn, result in sexual dysfunction, fatigue and sleep disorders [4]. Moreover, women who experience postpartum urinary leakage and the related difficulties in daily life are more likely to show reluctance or a decreased frequency of sexual intercourse [5].

Previous studies have identified multiple risk factors for PFD, including age, constipation, obesity, menopause, poor lifestyle choices, as well as pregnancy and childbirth-related factors, among which pregnancy and childbirth are recognized as independent risk factors that can significantly increase the incidence of postpartum pelvic floor trauma and the risk of PFD [6–9]. These influencing factors are also high-risk factors for POP [10, 11].

Despite the identification of these relevant risk factors, the impact of delivery mode on pelvic floor function remains controversial, studies on the effects of maternal lifestyle and habits on PFD are limited, and the research on these risk factors is still insufficient. However, the research on how these factors affect, as well as the extent of these impacts, remains incomplete. We need to further explore how these factors cause different types of PFD, different parts of POP, and different stages. In-depth research and analysis on PFD are still necessary.

This study was conducted to explore the risk factors affecting PFD in a more comprehensive and in-depth manner. In our study, we attempt to perform a risk factor analysis for different prognostic outcomes of POP based on retrospectively collected single-center date. This study sought to identify factors associated with poor prognosis, with the goal of guiding in clinical practice.

Methods

Study design

This study is a retrospective, single-center, case-control study conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University. Data were collected between January 2020 and August 2021. The research adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki (revised in 2013) and received approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University (PJ 2024-11-40). Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the need for individual consent was waived.

Detailed patient histories, pelvic organ prolapse quantification (POP-Q) stages, accompanying symptoms, delivery methods and parity, fetal factors, delivery assistance factors, pelvic floor electromyography (Glazer surface EMG evaluation), treatment plans, and follow-up data (including telephone follow-up and/or outpatient follow-up) were recorded in the PFD registry at the Clinical Center for PFD. Specifically, we utilized the POP-Q staging for pre-, mid-, and post-pelvic organ prolapse, assessed the severity of SUI, and evaluated the strength of muscles using electrophysiological assessments to conduct a thorough and integrated study on the risk factors for the occurrence of PFD. Patients with missing data were excluded from the analysis.

Conventionally, POP is quantified clinically by using the International Continence Society POP-Q system [12]. This publication represents the joint consensus of the International Continence Society (ICS), American Urogynecologic Society (AUGS), and Society of Gynecologic Surgeons (SGS), and is the globally accepted international standard for POP-Q staging [13].

Patient positioning (supine lithotomy position with maximal Valsalva maneuver; standing position if prolapse was not maximized in lithotomy). The six anatomic points (Aa, Ba, C, D, Ap, Bp) and three linear measurements (gh, pb, TVL) relative to the hymen The standardized staging criteria based on the most distal prolapse. Ultrasonographic assessment yields negative values when prolapse extends below the symphysis pubis (SP), whereas positive measurements are obtained using the ICS POP-Q system. For instance, bladder descent of 5 cm below the SP (− 5 cm) corresponds roughly to a Ba value of + 3 [14]. The hiatal area during Valsalva maneuver was quantified from three-dimensional rendered volumes, as documented in a previous report [15]. All measurements were derived from volume datasets acquired during maximal Valsalva maneuver in both supine and standing positions. The diagnosis of levator avulsion was established on multiplanar or tomographic imaging in accordance with previously reported criteria [16].

Subjects

The study included a total of 828 postpartum women first diagnosed with PFD at the Pelvic Floor Rehabilitation Center in our outpatient clinic, between 42 days and 2 months postpartum. The sample consisted of 828 women who delivered at the obstetrics department of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University.

Diagnosis was based on the 2020 Chinese guidelines for POP diagnosis and treatment, using the Pelvic Organ Prolapse Quantitation (POP-Q) system. The inclusion criteria required delivery at our hospital, postpartum pelvic floor screening after 42 days, without clinical signs or evidence of vaginal infection, without severe perinatal complications, and informed consent obtained from patients. Exclusion criteria included a history of urinary incontinence before pregnancy, previous pelvic surgeries, or a history of chronic cough.

Observational indicators

Data collection included variables such as patient age, method of delivery, parity, fetal factors, height, weight, BMI, presence of diastasis recti, pubic symphysis separation, use of forceps during delivery, incidence of constipation, and engagement in heavy physical labor. Each of these factors was meticulously recorded and subjected to statistical analysis to identify potential risks associated with pelvic organ prolapse (POP).

The evaluation of outcomes included the measurement of contraction strength for type I and type II muscle fibers, both graded on scales of 0–2 and 3–5, respectively. Additional outcomes recorded included incidences of SUI, categorized as mild or moderate to severe, and the stages of pelvic organ prolapse according to the Pelvic Organ Prolapse Quantification (POP-Q) system. Specifically, anterior vaginal wall prolapse, posterior vaginal wall prolapse, and uterine prolapse were classified as stage 0 or I, and stage II and above [17].

The contraction strength of Type I (slow-twitch) and Type II (fast-twitch) muscle fibers was graded on a 0–5 scale. For Type I fibers, grading was based on the duration of maximal voluntary contraction: 0 = no contraction (0 s), 1 = 1 s, 2 = 2 s, 3 = 3 s, 4 = 4 s, and 5 ≥ 5 s. For Type II fibers, grading was based on the number of rapid repeated contractions: 0 = no contraction (0 times), 1 = 1 time, 2 = 2 times, 3 = 3 times, 4 = 4 times, and 5 ≥ 5 times. Strength grades 0–2 were defined as decreased muscle function, while grades 3–5 were considered normal [18].

Rectal muscle diastasis, or diastasis recti abdominis (DRA), describes an abnormal separation of the bilateral rectus abdominis muscles at the midline linea alba [19]. Previous studies measured inter-recti distance (IRD) by palpation, using a gap of ≥ 2 fingerbreadths as the diagnostic criterion for DRA [20].

One limitation of this study is the relatively occupational or daily activities involving intense physical exertion, such as frequent lifting, carrying, pushing, or pulling of heavy objects, prolonged standing, squatting, or strenuous household labor, which substantially increase intra-abdominal pressure. According to U.S. Social Security Administration [21], heavy work involves lifting no more than 100 pounds at a time with frequent lifting or carrying of objects weighing up to 50 pounds.

Constipation is generally defined as a disorder characterized by persistent difficult or seemingly incomplete defecation, and/or infrequent bowel movements (once every 3 to 4 d or less) in the absence of alarm symptoms or secondary causes [22].

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software. Quantitative data with a normal distribution are presented as means ± standard deviations (± SD), and with between-group comparisons were performed using independent samples t-tests. Categorical data were analyzed using chi-square tests. To identify risk factors for POP, a two-step analysis was performed. First, a univariate analysis was conducted, followed by a multivariate unconditional binary logistic regression analysis to account for potential confounders. Statistical significance was set at a P value of <0.05.

Discussion

In this study, PFD was evaluated through both clinical diagnosis and measurement of pelvic surface electromyography. We conducted a comprehensive and integrated study on risk factors for PFD, evaluating the POP-Q staging for anterior vaginal wall prolapse, posterior vaginal wall prolapse, uterine prolapse, the severity of SUI and the strength of pelvic muscles, in order to identify different lesions, types and clinical symptoms as our prognostic outcomes. Unlike previous studies, we identified additional factors associated with PFD beyond age, mode of delivery, BMI and parity. Specifically, constipation during and after pregnancy, neonatal birth weight, twin pregnancy, forceps-assisted delivery, and heavy physical labor were found to be significant factors influencing PFD outcomes. Additionally, some accompanied symptoms like rectus abdominis diastasis and symphysis pubis diastasis may also have association with muscle fiber contraction strength.

PFD is a functional disorder that is prevalent among women. Many patients experience clinical symptoms such as vaginal bulging, urinary incontinence, voiding dysfunction, defecation dysfunction, and sexual dysfunction, severely reducing their quality of life, increasing their economic burdens, and causing various psychological issues [23]. Due to a lack of effective prevention and treatment, even with effective rehabilitation, 40% to 50% of women still develop POP. Previous studies have revealed multiple risk factors for PFD, however, these studies often use randomized controlled trial cohorts, excluding younger individuals, those with immediate postpartum symptoms, and chronic cases commonly seen in clinical practice [24]. Thus, the practical value and reproducibility of these risk analysis and predictive models in clinical practice are debatable. Several demographic variables were found to be associated with PFD. In our study, age, constipation, neonatal birth weight, twin pregnancy, forceps-assisted delivery, and heavy physical labor are emerged as significant factors influencing the risk of PFD, including various outcomes like anterior vaginal wall prolapse, posterior vaginal wall prolapse, uterine prolapse and SUI.

Previous studies have confirmed that PFM and abdominal wall muscles are closely associated with postpartum pelvic floor function. Along with the diaphragm, these muscle groups work synergistically to regulate intra-abdominal pressure and maintain core stability [25]. PFM tends to manage increased intra-abdominal pressure with excessive tightness, known as elevated muscle tone, limiting its ability to contract and relax and ultimately contributing to PFD [26]. Our study demonstrated that diastasis recti abdominis may lead to decreased contractile strength of both type I and type II muscle fibers, as well as an increased incidence and severity of SUI. Consistent with our findings, Manrong et al. reported that DRA is prevalent among postpartum women and is associated with increased rectus abdominis separation and levator ani muscle hiatus, exacerbated by risk factors such as advanced age, higher BMI, multiple births, and cesarean delivery [27, 28]. These conditions correlate with weakened PFM and increased pain, underscoring the importance of targeted postpartum rehabilitation.

To ensure a consistent study population and uniform follow-up duration, we set a fixed follow-up time at 6 weeks postpartum. While this single time-point assessment is common in the literature [29, 30], pelvic floor recovery is a dynamic process, and studies with serial assessments have demonstrated that postpartum pelvic floor structure and function can evolve over time [31, 32]. Handa et al. reported that only one-third of primiparous women exhibited POP stage ≥ II at 6–12 months after first delivery [29], and the variability in reported prevalence rates across studies [29, 33] underscores the critical influence of follow-up timing on study findings.

Another study identified parity and constipation as potential risk factors for pelvic floor disorders [34]. In our research, we found that constipation during pregnancy was associated with almost all the outcomes, including anterior, posterior, uterine prolapse and SUI. Constipation could exert pressure on the internal pelvic organs and muscles during defecation [35]. Additionally, parity was recognized as a factor influencing pelvic floor function [36]. However, several ultrasound-based studies reported that PFD is not significantly associated with the number of pregnancies and deliveries, and structural deformations were unrelated to parity [37, 38]. A cross-sectional study of Gao et al. [39] included 845 women and tell us, in addition to uncontrollable factors like mode of delivery, age, and parity, interventions targeting weight gain and constipation during pregnancy and vaginal flora could mitigate therisks of PFD. Educational programs for pregnant women should emphasize a proper diet and lifestyle. Therefore, further investigation is needed to clarify the impact of parity on pelvic floor function.

Although our study demonstrated that the incidence and severity of prolapse at 6 weeks postpartum were lower in women who underwent cesarean section than in those with vaginal delivery, cesarean section cannot completely prevent PFD. This highlights the need for in-depth exploration of the underlying molecular mechanisms, including changes in pelvic anatomical structure, mechanical stress stimulation, collagen degradation, and inflammatory response. The structural integrity of pelvic floor supporting tissues depend on normal fibroblast function and extracellular matrix (ECM) homeostasis, with collagen I/III and elastin as critical structural proteins [40]. Abnormal mechanical stress may contribute to PFD via multiple pathways, such as collagen metabolism, ECM remodeling, cellular senescence, oxidative stress, apoptosis, mitochondrial dysfunction, and elastic fiber processes [41, 42], though the specific mechanism remains unclear.

One study explored that as weight increased, there was a risk of exceeding the endurance contraction amplitude threshold of the pelvic floor muscles and may potentially lead to PFD [43]. In our study, we found that when BMI exceeded to 24.9 kg/m², there was a risk of affecting pelvic floor function and causing PFD, including anterior, posterior, uterine prolapse, SUI and type I/II muscle fiber contraction strength. It can be explained that overweight (BMI ≥ 24.9 kg/m²), twin pregancy and fetal macrosomia can exert sustainable pressure on the pelvic organs and pelvic floor muscles during pregnancy and delivery. Sahin et al. [44] found that urinary incontinence was observed in 56.1% of the 549 pregnant women, with mixed urinary incontinence being most common in the third trimester. Consistent with our findings, the mean BMI of pregnant women with SUI was significantly higher than that of continent women, and the severity and bother scores were also higher in the incontinent group (p = 0.001).

We also found that the mode of delivery, whether vaginal or cesarean, played a significant role in PFD. In our research, cesarean delivery was found to reduce the risk of anterior vaginal wall prolapse compared to vaginal delivery. During pregnancy, the enlarged uterus moved the center of gravity forward, compressing the tissues of the pelvic floor [45]. During vaginal delivery, the pelvic floor muscles, ligaments and fascia were subjected to extreme stretching and damage, which sometimes result in permanent nerve injury [46]. Therefore, muscle fiber strength was more likely to be abnormal in women who delivered vaginally, increasing their risk of developing PFD [47]. One study reported a higher cumulative incidence of SUI and overactive bladder in women who had vaginal deliveries compared to those who underwent cesarean Sect [48]. However, cesarean delivery did not completely prevent the development of PFD. Another study found that cesarean section led to abnormal resting muscle strength due to altered anatomical position between bladder and uterus, which weakened the fascia supporting the bladder neck [49]. As our study showed, women who had cesarean deliveries had a significantly reduced risk of severe anterior vaginal wall prolapse, but not that obvious in posterior vaginal wall and uterine prolapse, which aligns with similar conclusions with this research. However, the stress response following a cesarean section resulted in elevated inflammatory and oxidative stress markers, ultimately contributing to PFD. Therefore, it is necessary to monitor postpartum pelvic floor function of the parturient, whether vaginal or cesarean delivery.

Although our data confirm that cesarean delivery is associated with a reduced risk of severe anterior compartment prolapse, caution is warranted against simply recommending cesarean delivery for asymptomatic women solely for the prevention of PFD. In clinical practice, the choice of delivery mode involves a complex trade-off between maternal and neonatal risks. While elective cesarean delivery may reduce the risk of pelvic floor injury, it is associated with increased risks of maternal hemorrhage, infection, abnormal placental implantation in subsequent pregnancies, and neonatal respiratory complications [50, 51]. Therefore, the protective effect observed in our study must be balanced against the well-documented risks of operative delivery. For women at high risk of PFD, such as those with connective tissue disorders or a family history of severe prolapse, our findings provide important data for shared decision-making, facilitating a more comprehensive discussion of the benefits and risks associated with planned cesarean delivery versus attempted vaginal delivery.

With advancements in pelvic floor rehabilitation technology, treatments such as neuromuscular electrical stimulation and Kegel exercises have been widely used for POP and SUI patients, including postpartum patients, to prevent and delay the progression of PFD [52]. Previous randomized controlled trials have shown that minimally invasive pelvic floor treatments are associated with a reduced incidence of POP-Q stage III and IV POP when compared to observation management [53]. Additional conservative measures, such as pelvic floor physical therapy, would be advantageous in some of the pelvic symptoms of prolapse and, to a lesser degree, have a beneficial impact on the anatomic extent of a vaginal Bulge [54]. Therefore, there is a significant demand for surgical decision support tools in clinical practice for postpartum patients undergoing pelvic floor rehabilitation.

Factors such as age, mode of delivery, parity, fetal factors (including birth weight and multiple gestations), maternal weight, forceps-assisted delivery, constipation, and heavy physical labor were included in our risk factor analysis as potential risk factors [55]. These variables have also been identified as high-risk factors in previous analyses and can be easily obtained from the electronic medical records of hospitalized and outpatient patients, enhancing the usability of our predictive model. Previous risk factor analyses did not separate the stages of prolapse into anterior, middle, and posterior compartments for comparison. In our study, we separately compared the prolapse stages and found differences in risk factors between different prolapse sites. Moreover, previous studies rarely used different muscle strengths as outcomes. Our study analysed the risk factors for type I and type II muscle strength separately.

PFD in women is caused primarily by pregnancy, childbirth, and ageing [56, 57]. Pregnancy and childbirth factors are critical in the development of POP and SUI [58, 59]. Pregnancy, childbirth, and other processes associated with elevated intra-abdominal and pelvic pressure are recognized as independent risk factors for the development and progression of POP [56]. Studies have reported that the risk of POP among primiparous women is twice that of nulliparous women [57], and up to 52% of women after their first vaginal delivery present with anterior vaginal wall prolapse of varying degrees [58]. Additionally, difficult labor and forceps delivery can damage pelvic support structures, thereby contributing to POP [59]. During pregnancy, the increasing size of the foetus and uterus increases the abdominal pressure, compressing the pelvic floor muscles and supporting structures. The prolonged use of abdominal pressure, compression of the pelvic floor muscles, and vaginal dilation during the second stage of labor can cause varying degrees of muscle fiber stretching and damage, leading to changes at the cellular level, such as collagen alteration, apoptosis, and oxidative stress, resulting in irreversible damage.

The impact of delivery mode on pelvic floor function remains controversial. Previous studies have suggested that vaginal delivery can cause tears in the pubocervical fascia and overstretching of pelvic floor neuromuscular tissues, thereby impairing pelvic floor support structures and forming the pathological basis of postpartum POP [60]. Other evidence indicates that during the second and third trimesters, the growing uterus and fetal head exert continuous downward and anterior pressure on the pelvic cavity. Such sustained stress may damage PFM, ligaments, and fascia, leading to a relatively high POP risk even in women undergoing cesarean section, which is also associated with BMI, birth weight, and trial of labor. Nevertheless, recent clinical research has questioned this traditional understanding. Not all women with multiple pregnancies and deliveries develop POP, and some only show mild prolapse. Conversely, some women with only one delivery or cesarean section may develop severe POP requiring early surgical treatment. These findings imply that the pathogenesis of POP involves multiple pathophysiological and molecular mechanisms. Given the ongoing debate, this study carries important clinical implications.

In this study, the overall incidence of POP-Q stage II or higher anterior POP was approximately 27.6%, that of POP-Q stage I POP was approximately 71.6%, and the overall postpartum incidence of SUI was 23.5%, similar to the incidence rates reported in the literature. Our results indicate that older age, higher BMI, neonatal weight ≥ 4000 g (fetal macrosomia), multiple gestations, vaginal delivery, forceps-assisted delivery, constipation, and heavy physical labor are independent risk factors for early PFD in postpartum women, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies. As women age, oestrogen levels decrease significantly, leading to increased collagen degradation, reduced collagen content, and weakened muscle and ligament strength, which may contribute to PFD development [61, 62]. Increased weight adds more pressure to the pelvic floor, causing the pelvic floor muscles and other structures to be in a prolonged state of tension, weakening supportive structures. Multiple pregnancies and deliveries cause significant damage to the pelvic floor muscles, increasing the risk of PFD. Increased gestational age, multiple gestations, and fetal weight increase mechanical pressure, leading to denervation of the pelvic floor muscles, nerve tissue, and urethral sphincter, further increasing the risk of SUI [63, 64]. Macrosomia and forceps-assisted delivery also contribute to mechanical stretching and nerve damage, often accompanied by prolonged labor and long-term pressure from the foetal head on the pelvic floor tissues, increasing PFD risk. Forceps-assisted delivery results in direct tension in and places pressure on the pelvic floor muscles and vaginal tissues, causing abnormal changes in type I and type II muscle fibers [65, 66]. The high OR between forceps delivery and POP-Q ≥ II stage in our study is highly consistent with the results of previous key studies [67, 68]. These studies confirm that operative vaginal delivery is one of the strongest risk factors for pelvic floor injury, and the underlying mechanism may be related to rapid overstretching or even avulsion of the levator ani muscles. This further strengthens the biomechanical plausibility of this association.

Constipation and heavy physical labor cause long-term increased abdominal pressure, which are risk factors for abnormal fatigue in pelvic floor type I and II muscle fiber strength, increasing the risk of PFD. Constipation is a chronic condition affecting many individuals worldwide [69]. In affected individuals, inappropriate contraction or failed relaxation of the puborectal muscle and external anal sphincter during defecation—termed pelvic floor dyssynergia—represents a form of maladaptive learning [70].

Although the high OR for constipation in our research is consistent with the pathophysiological hypothesis of “chronic increased intra-abdominal pressure leading to pudendal neuropathy and weakened connective tissue” as described in [71], the cross-sectional design of our study precludes the establishment of a causal relationship. There may be a bidirectional relationship between constipation and prolapse (severe prolapse may also exacerbate constipation symptoms), which provides a promising new perspective for future longitudinal studies.

Limitations

Like any population-based observational study, our study has some limitations. First, our model is derived from the outpatient data of postpartum patients over 42 days from our PFD Clinical Centre, excluding hospitalized patients in gynaecology and urology departments, which may restrict the generalizability of our findings to women with more severe PFD. Second, the follow-up period was relatively short and variable; since postpartum visits were voluntary, the majority of asymptomatic patients did not undergo systematic assessments beyond six months, potentially introducing selection bias. Third, in line with previous studies [72], POP was assessed using the POP-Q system, which is standardized, reproducible and widely used clinically, but remains partly examiner-dependent and does not fully capture underlying anatomical changes or symptom burden. Finally, due to the retrospective design, some obstetric variables, such as fetal head circumference and the duration of the second stage of labor, were not included in the multivariable analysis, and residual confounding cannot be excluded. Future prospective studies with standardized longitudinal follow-up extending from late pregnancy to 1–2 years postpartum and more comprehensive obstetric data are warranted to validate our findings.

To identify risk factors for postpartum PFD in a Chinese population can help us establish a predictive model with active predictive performance, leading to the early identification and efficient screening of high-risk PFD patients and result in timely intervention. These findings provide a reference for personalized clinical diagnosis, treatment, and disease prevention, with significant practical application value and importance for improving women’s reproductive health. This study employed a dual approach to evaluate pelvic floor muscle function, utilizing sensitive electrophysiology alongside clinical-specific diagnosis. The research was relatively comprehensive, utilizing sensitive electrophysiology alongside clinical-specific diagnosis, yet it also had many limitations. It was conducted as a retrospective study, lacking further intervention and follow-up on pelvic function in postpartum women. Factors such as fetal head circumference and the duration of the second stage of labor can be included for more precise predictions. Further research is needed to determine whether POP or SUI may develop later as a result.

Conclusion

Although POP and SUI were less common among postpartum women, but there were higher occurrences of abnormal electrophysiological values at each stage, and they also severely impact women’s life quality and cause significant distress. Currently, there are few predictive models for PFD. In addition to uncontrollable factors like age, mode of delivery and parity, interventions targeting weight gain, BMI, fetal weight, constipation and heavy physical labor during and after pregnancy could mitigate the risks of PFD. Educational programs for pregnant women should provide guidance on a proper diet and lifestyle. To avoid forceps during delivery and timely treatment for women with forceps-assisted delivery should be initiated promptly to prevent exacerbating damage to the pelvic floor muscles and reduce the incidence of PFD among postpartum women. This study identified several significant risk factors for PFD in a Chinese population. These findings emphasize the crucial role of comprehensive prenatal, perinatal, and postpartum management, as well as targeted interventions in mitigating the risk factors associated with PFD in postpartum women.
