Procedural outcome and safety of percutaneous patent ductus arteriosus occlusion in adults
Authors: Abdul Malik Sheikh, Asma Kanwal, Hina Sattar, Tauqeer Akbar, Syed Najam Hyder, Tayyaba Saher
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Percutaneous device closure of patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) in infants and children is a frequently performed procedure. However, transcatheter closure is rare in adults, especially in developed countries, as most ducts are typically closed in childhood. PDA in adults poses unique challenges due to age-related comorbidities and some morphological changes in PDA that occur with advanced age. This study analyzes the safety and procedural success of adult PDA transcatheter closure.Materials and Methods:A retrospective single-center study analyzed 184 adult PDA device closures between July 2015 and July 2024. The study examined patient demographics, clinical aspects, procedural details, and immediate hospital outcomes. Outcome parameters focused on procedural success, complications, residual shunt, and improvement in symptoms.Results:Out of 184 patients, 183 adults with a mean age of 27 ± 13 years and a mean weight of 52 ± 16.5 kg underwent PDA device occlusion. Most patients were referred for evaluation of a murmur (n = 116, 63.3%). The most common comorbidity was type 2 diabetes mellitus (n = 20, 10.9%). The mean PDA diameter at its narrowest end was 6.17 ± 4.56 mm (1.2–17 mm). In total, 6.55% of patients exhibited duct calcification, and 2.73% presented with a PDA aneurysm. Severe pulmonary hypertension was present in 24 patients. Transcatheter intervention achieved a 100% success rate at 24 h postprocedure, with no residual flows at the 6-month follow-up. There was no mortality, device embolization, or aortic/pulmonary artery stenosis.Conclusions:Percutaneous device closure is a safe and successful treatment option for PDA in adults.

INTRODUCTION

The ductus arteriosus, vital in fetal circulation, typically closes within the first 48 h after birth in most term infants.[1] However, in preterm neonates, closure may be delayed for weeks or months. Some individuals retain patency of this structure into late childhood or adult life,[2] leading to complications such as pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), Eisenmenger syndrome, infective endocarditis, arrhythmia, and coronary steal syndrome. Regardless of age, percutaneous closure of the patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) is the recommended treatment method because of its safety and effectiveness.[3] This procedure is frequently performed before the patient reaches adulthood, despite disagreements over the need for intervention.[4]

Before choosing an intervention, adult patients should be evaluated for PAH and left-to-right shunting, according to the recent American Heart Association guidelines.[5] The hemodynamic importance of an adult PDA requires a thorough clinical examination, echocardiography, and cardiac catheterization.[5] Recommendations for closure in adults are based on parameters such as left atrial and ventricular volume overload, pulmonary artery pressures, and pulmonary vascular resistance. If there is left atrial and ventricular volume overload, the pulmonary artery pressure is <50% of the aortic pressure, and the pulmonary vascular resistance is less than one-third of the systemic vascular resistance (Class I), the duct should be closed. PDA occlusion may be considered if pulmonary artery pressure is between 50% and 75% of aortic pressure, and pulmonary vascular resistance is between one-third and two-thirds of systemic vascular resistance (Class IIb).[5] The procedural complexity of adult PDA device occlusion arises from certain pathological alterations, including tortuosity, calcifications, aneurysms, and friability. Although PDA occlusion is often carried out in infants, newer technologies and more experience have allowed the treatment of neonates and preterm newborns. Data on adult PDA device occlusion remain sparse, despite the growing popularity of early intervention in childhood. Most published data focused on young patients and utilized a single type of duct occluder. This study reports our experience with adult percutaneous duct closure in a resource-constrained medical setup.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

A retrospective single-center study conducted from July 2015 to July 2024 at the Rawalpindi Institute of Cardiology, Pakistan, included patients older than 16 years with a hemodynamically significant duct. Patients with associated lesions, clinically silent ducts, and Eisenmenger syndrome were excluded from the study. The hospital’s ethical committee approved the study, and prior written consent was secured from all patients.

Implantation techniques

Procedures, predominantly performed under local anesthesia (89%), involved femoral artery and vein access in most cases. In five cases, only the venous route was used. Heparin (50–100 IU/Kg) and antibiotic prophylaxis (intravenous ceftriaxone) were administered at the start of the procedure. Aortic and pulmonary artery pressures were measured. An angiogram was performed on lateral and/or right anterior oblique projections. Images were analyzed for the shape, narrowest point, ampulla, and duct length according to Krichenko classification. Sizing balloons were used in some cases to outline the ductus for morphological and measurement purposes. Balloon occlusion (Osypka AG, Rheinfelden, Germany) was also performed in hypertensive ductus to assess the reduction of pulmonary artery pressures (n = 24). In hypertensive ducts, pressure recordings were repeated after giving 100% oxygen for 10 min. Oximetry and shunt calculations were performed in 3 of 24 hypertensive ducts where pulmonary artery pressures were near systemic pressure. The procedure was only continued if the pulmonary artery pressure fell below two-thirds of the systemic pressure in hypertensive ducts. None of the patients had acute vasodilator testing using nitric oxide or other approved pharmacological agents. A 5Fr or 6Fr MPA2 (Cordis) catheter was used to cross the PDA from the pulmonary end, aided by a straight tip 0.035” hydrophilic wire. An extra-stiff wire was used to place the delivery sheath in the descending aorta. When the PDA could not be crossed from the venous side in 4.9% of patients (n = 9) due to shape, size, and unusual attachment of the duct at the pulmonic end, it was crossed from the arterial side to create an arteriovenous loop and advance a delivery system from the venous access. The selection of the device was based on duct size, shape, pulmonary artery pressure, and the availability of the device. The device was oversized, 3 mm larger than the narrowest duct diameter in normotensive ducts, but by 5 mm in hypertensive ducts. The position of the device was assessed using transthoracic echocardiography and angiography before its release. A computed tomographic angiogram was not performed in any of the patients.

Follow-up protocol

Patients underwent clinical examination, electrocardiography, and transthoracic echocardiography assessments before discharge and at 1, 3, and 6 months postprocedure. Pulmonary hypertension was assessed using tricuspid and pulmonary regurgitation jets. None of the patients underwent a repeat catheterization to assess residual PAH. Left ventricular function was evaluated using M-mode measurements.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as means, medians, and standard deviations, whereas categorical variables were presented as percentages.

RESULTS

Of the 184 patients, 183 underwent PDA device occlusion, achieving a 100% success rate at 24 h postprocedure. One patient was referred for surgery as the duct was too large for occluder devices. Procedures were often performed under local anesthesia (89%). Whenever pulmonary hypertension in 20 adults warranted a hemodynamic assessment on 100% oxygen, intubation anesthesia was used. The mean age was 27 ± 13 years, and the mean weight of patients was 52 ± 16.5 kg [Table 1]. The presence of a murmur was the most common reason for referral in 116 patients (63.39%), followed by symptomatic presentation in the remaining patients. Symptoms included shortness of breath in 29 patients (15.84%), palpitation in 20 patients (10.92%), and fatigue in 15 patients (8.19%). One patient had infective endocarditis, treated before the intervention, and two patients had heart failure. Type 2 diabetes mellitus was present in 20 (10.92%) patients, and systemic hypertension in 13 (7.10%) patients. The rarer comorbidities in these adult patients included mild coronary atherosclerotic disease in two patients, respiratory disease in three patients, renal diseases in five patients, liver disease in four patients, and dyslipidemia in 6 patients. According to duct morphology, Krichenko type A was the most common type (93.44%). Three patients had type B ducts, four had type C ducts, and five had type D ducts. The mean diameter of the narrowest part of the PDA was 6.17 ± 4.56 mm (1.2–17 mm). Mean pulmonary artery pressures were 32 ± 23.39 mmHg. Severe PAH was present in 24 patients. In these hypertensive ducts, the mean pulmonary artery and aortic pressures in air were 60 ± 14 mmHg and 98 ± 15 mmHg, respectively. After administering 100% oxygen for 10 min and simultaneous balloon occlusion, mean pulmonary artery and aortic pressures changed to 39 ± 20 mmHg and 88 ± 13 mmHg, respectively. Calcification of the PDA was found in 6.55% (n = 12) of patients, and a PDA aneurysm was present in 2.73% (n = 5) of patients.

The procedural details are summarized in Table 2. Standard and long-shank Occlutech duct occluders were the most commonly used devices in 119 patients (65.02%) and five patients (2.73%), respectively. The other devices were Amplatzer duct occluders in six patients (3.27%), Memopart devices in 30 patients (16.39%), Konar multifunction occluders in 10 patients (5.46%), ventricular septal occluders in 6 patients (3.27%), atrial septal occluder in one patient, postmyocardial infarction ventricular septal occluder device in three patients (1.63%), and coils in 3 patients (1.63%). The Occlutech device with a long shank was used in aneurysmal ducts, while the device was slightly oversized in calcified ducts. PDA could not be crossed from the venous side in 4.91% of patients (n = 9); therefore, it was crossed from the arterial side and snared to make an arteriovenous loop. Patients in whom an arteriovenous loop was made included three aneurysmal and three calcified ducts. The mean procedure time was 45 min, and the fluoroscopy time was 6 min. The success rate of transcatheter intervention was 100% at 24 h postprocedure. Complications were rare, with one case of femoral arterial thrombotic occlusion (0.54%). The average postprocedure hospital stay time was 24 h (range: 12–72 h). Left ventricle function worsened transiently in 13.11% (n = 24) patients, with full recovery in all these patients within 3 months. These patients were treated with diuretics and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors. Pulmonary hypertension regressed in all patients with hypertensive ducts within 6 months (n = 24). Fourteen patients were lost to follow-up within 6 months. No mortality, device embolization, residual leaks, or aortic/pulmonary artery stenosis were reported at the 6-month follow-up.

DISCUSSION

PDA in adults is a rare condition in clinical practice, as most children undergo closure immediately after their diagnosis.[45] Studies of PDA closure in the adult population are rare. These studies were conducted over an extended period of time using fewer designs of devices.[67891011] One large study from Canada analyzed their 17-year experience obtained from 141 adult patients.[12] During the study duration, we performed 1189 PDA device occlusions; 184 (15.47%) of those cases involved adults. The delay in the early diagnosis of congenital heart diseases in Pakistan is the reason behind the delay in intervention.[13] This observation could be linked to socioeconomic conditions, education status, and remote access to the appropriate medical facilities. The prevalence of unrepaired congenital heart defects is higher in middle- and low-income nations compared to high-income countries.[14] The delayed diagnosis of PDA leads to anatomical and histological changes, including aneurysmal dilatation and ductal calcification. It also exposes the patients to the common adult comorbidities such as diabetes and hypertension. The results of the current study demonstrate that, despite the different anatomical changes, PDA device closure is safe and effective, even in adults. Our results were similar to those of a Saudi Arabian study, which demonstrated procedural safety and efficacy.[6]

Acute procedural complications of percutaneous PDA occlusion include vascular injury, device embolization, residual shunting, arrhythmia, allergic reactions, thromboembolic phenomenon, and aortic/pulmonary obstruction. Infective endocarditis, device-related thrombosis, pericardial effusion, aortic dissection, device erosion or migration, persistent pulmonary hypertension, and heart failure are some of the postprocedural complications. Jang et al. reported acute complications in 8.5% of patients.[15] Another study reported an 11% rate of procedure-related acute complications in adult patients.[6] The only complication in our study was femoral thrombosis, which was resolved with treatment in one patient. We used heparin and streptokinase serially to regain pulse within 24 h. PDA device occlusion may prolong procedural time in adults due to age-related morphological changes that necessitate the establishment of an arteriovenous loop, as observed in 9 patients in our study. Despite these procedural complexities, the fluoroscopic and procedural durations remained the same, as shown in the literature.[6]

Pulmonary hypertension is a well-established sequel of large ducts in grown-up patients. Various factors are involved in the outcome of pulmonary hypertension after ligation or occlusion of hypertensive ducts. Pulmonary hypertension may or may not regress after the occlusion of the ducts.[69] Twenty-four of our patients had hypertensive ducts. We evaluated pulmonary hypertension according to guidelines before occlusion.[5] We occluded all the hypertensive ducts in this cohort. After the duct occlusion, we advised the use of dual pulmonary vasodilators in all these patients. Pulmonary hypertension regressed in all the patients on follow-up echocardiography. Literature review shows transient deterioration of left ventricular function with complete recovery within 3 months after occlusion of large ducts in adults.[16] Our observation was the same when left ventricular systolic function worsened in 24 patients. We used diuretics and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors in these patients to promote improvement in left ventricular function. Our patients regained good systolic function within 3 months, as assessed by serial echocardiography.

Various devices have been used to treat transcatheter occlusion of PDA after the first successful procedure reported by Portsmann in 1967.[10] A rapid evolution of PDA occlusion devices has been observed, driven by procedural ease and associated benefits.[11] The aortic retention skirt, made of an expandable nitinol wireframe, is a typical feature of PDA occlusion devices. The nitinol body of most devices (Amplatzer, Lifetech, and Memopart) is broader at the aortic end, but the Occlutech device has a wider pulmonic end (reverse shank). Specific PDA devices, such as the long shank Occlutech duct occluder, feature a long nickel body that provides an advantage in aneurysmal ducts in our cohort. While specific devices, such as the ADO II and Konar-MFO, feature two retention discs, the majority of the duct occluders only have one aortic retention disk. Early reports of PDA devices utilized Amplatzer devices (ADO I and ADO II).[678911] Later, other devices, such as Konar-MFOs, Chinese devices, and Occlutech duct occluders, were also used.[1718] In our institution, we have employed a variety of PDA occlusion devices to accomplish the procedure. Our choice of a particular device was based on morphology, anatomical variations, and age-related complications. It is pertinent to mention that we used double retention disc devices in hypertensive ducts. Despite our limited resources, we used a wide array of occlusion devices in accordance with PDA requirements. Our results, based on large patient numbers, are comparable to previous reports from developed countries.[678911]

CONCLUSIONS

Percutaneous device closure is a successful and safe therapeutic option for PDA closure, even in adult patients. Challenges specific to adults, including altered duct morphology, pulmonary hypertension, and postprocedural afterload mismatch, warrant careful consideration when selecting PDA device occlusion strategies.
