Long-term relief of CRPS-associated limb dystonia with ketamine infusion
Authors: Abdulrahman AlZahrani, Danielle Weber-Adrian, May C Ong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryComplex regional pain syndrome (CRPS) with associated dystonia is a severely debilitating condition often refractory to conventional management. We present a case report of three patients with chronic, treatment-refractory CRPS-related limb dystonia. Patients were treated with multiday intravenous ketamine infusions (target dose 30 mg/hour) every 3–6 months, supplemented with oral ketamine troches. This treatment protocol resulted in the complete resolution of dystonia in two patients and significant functional improvement in the third. The treatment was well-tolerated, with transient and manageable adverse events including nausea and hypertension. While these findings are limited due to the small sample size and lack of randomisation, this case report presents a potentially promising therapeutic approach for the treatment of CRPS-related motor complications in carefully selected patients with appropriate monitoring.

Background

Complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS) is a neuropathic condition characterised by ongoing pain disproportionate to any inciting event.1 It is accompanied by a constellation of sensory, vasomotor, sudomotor, trophic and motor changes. A particularly debilitating motor manifestation is fixed dystonia, an involuntary sustained muscle contraction resulting in abnormal posturing, which is thought to be secondary to maladaptive neuroplasticity.2 CRPS-related dystonia has been historically difficult to manage due to its complex pathophysiology and limited response to conventional treatments like physiotherapy, oral medications (baclofen and benzodiazepines) and nerve blocks.3 4 Ketamine, a non-competitive N-methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist originally developed as an anaesthetic in 1962, has gained recognition as a treatment for conditions like treatment-resistant depression and, more recently, CRPS.57 A meta-analysis on ketamine infusion for CRPS found an average immediate pain reduction of 69%, with common side effects including hypertension, nausea, anxiety and insomnia.7 This case report aims to describe the clinical outcomes of ketamine infusion on CRPS-related dystonia in three patients with long-standing, treatment-refractory disease.

Case presentation

Case 1: A woman in her late 50s presented with a long-standing history of CRPS affecting the left lower limb, which developed following a fall approximately 15 years prior to initiation of ketamine infusion therapy. Her symptoms included severe burning and lancinating pain, marked allodynia, flushing, swelling and trophic skin changes. Shortly after the initial injury, she developed dystonic posturing of the left ankle, characterised by a fixed inversion with internal rotation. Multiple treatments had been attempted prior to referral, including physiotherapy with range-of-motion exercises and desensitisation therapy, which provided limited benefit. Pharmacological therapies included oral gabapentin (up to 1800 mg/day), pregabalin (up to 300 mg/day) and amitriptyline (50 mg/day), all of which resulted in minimal pain relief. Topical lidocaine and capsaicin creams were ineffective. Interventional treatments included three lumbar sympathetic blocks, which provided only transient benefit. Oral baclofen (up to 60 mg/day) was trialled for dystonia without improvement, and botulinum toxin injections to the left ankle invertor muscles were similarly ineffective. Prior to her first ketamine admission, the patient required an electric wheelchair, a cane and a walker for ambulation (figure 1A and D and table 1).

Case 2: A woman in her early 70s developed CRPS affecting the left foot following complications related to spinal cord stimulator implantation. The condition progressed to a fixed dystonic foot drop accompanied by severe mechanical hyperalgesia and allodynia, resulting in significant impairment of ambulation. Previous management included removal of the malfunctioning spinal cord stimulator and physiotherapy with gait training and use of an ankle–foot orthosis, both of which provided limited benefit. Pharmacological treatments included gabapentin (1200 mg/day), which offered partial pain relief, and duloxetine (60 mg/day), which was ineffective. She underwent four lumbar sympathetic blocks with only transient benefit. Trials of oral baclofen (30 mg/day) and diazepam (10 mg/day) did not result in improvement of dystonia. Prior to ketamine therapy, the patient required a walking boot and additional mobility aids for ambulation (figure 1B, E and F and table 1).

Case 3: A man in his mid-40s developed CRPS with a fixed flexion–inversion dystonia of the left hand and wrist following thoracic outlet surgery. His presentation was notable for severe allodynia and prominent discolouration of the left hand and arm. Several treatments were attempted prior to ketamine therapy, including a spinal cord stimulator trial, which provided no benefit and was therefore not implanted. He underwent five stellate ganglion blocks with only transient benefit and three brachial plexus blocks without improvement. An intravenous lidocaine infusion (5 mg/kg over 60 min) resulted in minimal benefit. Pharmacological therapies included gabapentin (up to 2400 mg/day) with minimal pain relief and pregabalin (300 mg/day) without additional benefit. Oral baclofen was escalated to 75 mg/day without improvement in dystonia, and botulinum toxin injections to the wrist and finger flexors were ineffective. Occupational therapy with splinting was attempted but not tolerated due to pain. Prior to ketamine treatment, the patient had severely limited hand function and was unable to tolerate a splint (figure 1C and G; table 1).

In all cases, the patients presented to our specialist unit with long-standing, debilitating dystonia that had failed to respond to multiple previous therapies.

Investigations

The diagnosis of CRPS in all three patients was established clinically based on the Budapest criteria. The clinical diagnosis of dystonia was made after ruling out primary and secondary causes of dystonia. Baseline laboratory tests, including complete blood count, electrolytes and liver function tests, were conducted before each ketamine infusion cycle and were monitored throughout the admission. The Brief Pain Inventory (BPI), Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and Generalised Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) were used to quantitatively assess pain, mood and function before and after each treatment cycle.

Differential diagnosis

The primary diagnostic consideration was to ensure the fixed dystonia was a manifestation of CRPS and not a conversion disorder or other movement disorder. The presence of clear preceding nerve injury or trauma, coupled with the constellation of sensory, vasomotor and sudomotor signs fulfilling the Budapest criteria for CRPS, strongly supported the diagnosis of CRPS-related dystonia. The consistent co-localisation of pain, sensory changes and dystonia in the affected limb further supported the diagnosis.

Treatment

All three patients were admitted to our institution for repeated intravenous ketamine infusions lasting between 7 and 14 days. The infusions began at a rate of 5 mg/hour and were gradually increased to a target of 30 mg/hour as tolerated (table 2). To maintain the therapeutic effects between hospital admissions, the patients were prescribed sublingual ketamine troches (50–100 mg, 2–4 times daily, up to a maximum of 400 mg/day). This ketamine treatment was paired with a comprehensive physiotherapy programme that included range-of-motion exercises, hot wax therapy and splinting of the affected limb.

Outcome and follow-up

All patients demonstrated significant and sustained improvement over their respective treatment periods (5, 3 and 2 years).

Case 1: Following the first admission, the patient’s left leg swelling, rubor and allodynia resolved completely. After the third admission, she achieved near-normal foot alignment and walked with a boot. Although her dystonia (left foot inversion and plantar flexion) initially recurred between infusions, it eventually resolved completely after 3 years of interval ketamine treatments. For the past 2 years, the resolution has persisted for over a year between infusions. The patient now walks unaided, drives, gardens and has normal activity engagement and range of motion (figure 1A and D and table 1).

Quantitative outcomes

Across all three cases, ketamine infusion was associated with a substantial reduction in subjective CRPS pain severity, as measured by the Numerical Rating Scale, from admission to discharge (figure 2A). Improvements in pain-related interference with daily activities were observed in cases 1 and 2 (figure 2B). Anxiety scores, assessed using the GAD-7 scale, remained unchanged in all patients (figure 2C), whereas depressive symptoms, measured by the PHQ-9, showed a clinically meaningful improvement only in case 3 (figure 2D). Given the small number of observations per patient, paired comparisons of questionnaire scores may be subject to interpretation errors. No correction for multiple comparisons was applied, which increases the risk of type I error. Power calculations were not feasible given the exploratory nature of this case report and the absence of prior effect size estimates in the literature.

Adverse events and safety monitoring

Ketamine infusions were generally well tolerated. Common adverse effects included nausea, which was managed with ondansetron (4–8 mg) and dimenhydrinate (25–50 mg), constipation treated with lactulose or polyethylene glycol and transient hypertension controlled with low-dose clonidine (0.1 mg two times per day) and amlodipine (5 mg daily). Some patients experienced transient drowsiness, which resolved with dose adjustment or following completion of the infusion. Laboratory monitoring identified transient elevations in alanine aminotransferase in cases 1 and 2, with peak values approximately 1.5–2 times the upper limit of normal; these resolved spontaneously without interruption of treatment. No clinically significant changes were observed in renal function, complete blood count or electrolyte levels. Urinalysis showed no evidence of ketamine-associated cystitis in any patient during the follow-up period. There were no notable changes in sleep, mood or affect during or between admissions. No patient reported symptoms suggestive of psychological dependence, and sublingual ketamine doses remained stable over prolonged follow-up (table 1).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first case report to describe long-term functional resolution or substantial improvement of CRPS-associated fixed limb dystonia through interval intravenous ketamine infusions supplemented with sublingual ketamine troches. This report details three patients with refractory CRPS-related dystonia who demonstrated clinically meaningful improvements following treatment. The cases illustrate both the potential benefits and the complexities of this therapeutic approach for a challenging condition while highlighting important considerations regarding patient selection, treatment protocols, safety monitoring and interpretation of outcomes. Published literature on ketamine for CRPS has primarily focused on pain outcomes, with relatively few reports specifically addressing motor manifestations such as dystonia. Dystonia is the most prevalent movement disorder observed in CRPS and occurs more frequently in individuals with a younger age at onset (mean age 33.4 vs 44 years) and those with multilimb involvement.8 The timing of dystonia onset is variable, occurring within 1 week in 26% of cases, between 1 month and 1 year in 47%, and beyond 1 year in 27%, including a small subset (3%) with onset more than 5 years after the initial CRPS symptoms.8 Upper extremity involvement is also associated with a higher risk of developing dystonia.8

The pathogenesis of CRPS-related dystonia is multifactorial and may include genetic predisposition (eg, human leucocyte antigen (HLA)-B62 and HLA-DQ8 polymorphisms), post-traumatic small fibre neuropathy, central sensitisation and neuroinflammation.1 9 CRPS-related dystonia is believed to involve maladaptive neuroplastic changes within spinal circuits, particularly disinhibition of nociceptive withdrawal reflexes mediated by C-fibre and Aδ-fibre. These pathways are modulated by neuropeptides such as substance P and calcitonin gene-related peptide, which contribute to central sensitisation and heightened spinal excitability.1015 This is further supported by studies demonstrating reduced presynaptic GABAergic inhibition in CRPS-related dystonia,16 the clinical effectiveness of intrathecal baclofen (a GABA-B agonist) in CRPS dystonia,17 and cortical as well as spinal disinhibition on neurophysiological testing.18

Ketamine’s therapeutic effect may involve NMDA receptor antagonism, which modulates nociceptive signalling and synaptic plasticity, producing both rapid and sustained analgesia.1922 Additionally, ketamine’s immunomodulatory effects and indirect interaction with catecholaminergic systems could contribute to its therapeutic role by promoting anti-inflammatory cytokine expression.23 Psychological factors in chronic pain and dystonia may also be addressed by ketamine’s metabolite (2R,6R)-hydroxynorketamine, which activates alpha-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazoleproprionic acid receptors and pathways linked to depression reversal.24 25

A key finding in this case report is the sustained benefit of low-dose ketamine troches (50–100 mg, 2–4 times daily) between infusions. This aligns with prior research showing stable ketamine dosing over 31.3 months in neuropathic pain patients, with mild side effects (eg, drowsiness and dizziness) and no significant renal or hepatic toxicity at doses below 1 g/day.26 27 In this study, transient drowsiness was the only significant adverse event. Overall, interval ketamine infusions supplemented with troches provided lasting improvement or resolution of dystonia in all three patients. Infusion-related adverse events (nausea, constipation and hypertension) were manageable with adjunct medications.

The variability in treatment response observed across our three patients deserves careful consideration. While all patients demonstrated initial improvements, the magnitude of response ranged from moderate to substantial, and the durability of benefit varied considerably. The third patient’s need for repeated infusion series to maintain improvements highlights a potential limitation of ketamine therapy and raises questions about optimal maintenance strategies. Factors that might influence treatment response could include disease duration, severity of dystonia, presence of fixed contractures, concurrent psychological factors and individual pharmacogenetic differences in ketamine metabolism and receptor sensitivity. Identifying predictors of treatment response would be valuable for patient selection but requires larger systematic studies.

Several limitations must be explicitly acknowledged. The observational nature and absence of a control group preclude definitive conclusions regarding causality, as improvements could be attributed to natural disease fluctuation, placebo effects or concurrent therapies. The small sample size limits generalisability, while the lack of standardised, validated outcome measures for CRPS-related dystonia makes it difficult to objectively quantify improvements or compare results across studies. Additionally, the follow-up period remains relatively short for assessing long-term safety, and the variability in dosing protocols prevents the identification of optimal treatment parameters. Potential publication bias, the lack of blinding and the absence of a cost-effectiveness analysis further limit the findings. Finally, the specialised setting and equipment required for safe administration, along with inconsistent insurance coverage, may present significant barriers to patient access. Despite these limitations, these cases provide valuable real-world clinical data suggesting that intravenous ketamine may be a potential option for carefully selected refractory patients. Future research should prioritise randomised controlled trials with appropriate blinding and standardised protocols to definitively establish safety, efficacy and optimal dosing regimens.

In conclusion, this case report contributes to the limited literature on ketamine therapy for CRPS-related dystonia by providing detailed documentation of treatment protocols, outcomes and safety considerations in three patients with refractory disease. While the results are encouraging and suggest potential benefit, the observational nature of the data, small sample size and various methodological limitations necessitate cautious interpretation. These findings provide preliminary clinical insights that warrant further validation through randomised controlled trials. However, the data suggest that intravenous ketamine infusions, in carefully selected cases, may serve as an off-label treatment option for refractory CRPS-related dystonia cases that have failed conventional therapies, as illustrated in the management pathway in figure 3. Clinicians considering ketamine therapy for CRPS-related dystonia should engage in thorough informed consent discussions, implement appropriate safety monitoring and recognise the current limitations of the evidence.

Patient’s perspective

Case 1 (written by the patient, edited for clarity by the authors):

For over a decade, I lived with constant burning pain and an uncontrollable inward twisting of my foot. My leg would swell, turn red and I couldn’t put weight on it. I used a wheelchair or walker just to get around the house. Every treatment I tried either failed or only helped for a short time. When my doctors suggested ketamine infusion, I was nervous. But after the first treatment, I noticed a huge difference. The swelling and pain calmed down, and with each admission, my foot started returning to a more normal position. Today, I can walk unassisted, garden, drive and move freely. The change has been incredible, and I’ve gotten part of my life back.

Case 2 (written by the patient, edited for clarity by the authors):

My left foot became stuck in a bent position after complications from a device implant. It felt like walking on knives—every step was torture. I couldn’t sleep properly and feared leaving my home. I had given up hope until I tried ketamine infusion. After just one session, I could touch my foot again without agony. The infusions gave me months of relief at a time. I now look forward to walking and spending time with family without constant pain. I feel stronger physically and mentally.

Case 3 (written by the patient, edited for clarity by the authors):

Following surgery, I lost control of my hand and wrist, which remained clenched for years. This was more than just pain—it meant losing much of my independence. Simple things like opening a bottle, writing or shaking someone’s hand became impossible. I tried numerous treatments, including nerve blocks and stimulators, but nothing worked. By the time ketamine was suggested, I was doubtful it could make a difference. During the infusions, however, I began noticing changes: the burning pain eased, and I gradually regained some movement in my wrist and hand. My hand movements have improved, though they are still limited. I am not cured, and I will not be able to return to work, but the treatment has reduced my pain and given me some functional improvement.
