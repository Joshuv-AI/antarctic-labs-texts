External Validation of a Prognostic Model for Outcome After Mild Traumatic Brain Injury at 6 Months Post Injury
Authors: Robin van Pinxteren, Marilien C. Marzolla, Melloney L. M. Wijenberg, Caroline M. van Heugten, Frits H. M. van Osch, Joukje van der Naalt, Jacoba M. Spikman, Marieke E. Timmerman, Jeroen J. Roor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundWhile most people fully recover after mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI), a substantial minority experience persistent symptoms and incomplete recovery. This may be prevented by early interventions specifically targeting patients at risk of incomplete recovery. The UPFRONT‐model was developed to identify patients at risk of poor functional outcome. This study aimed to externally validate the UPFRONT‐model in an independent sample.MethodsA prospective, longitudinal, multicenter cohort study with 126 mTBI patients recruited from emergency and neurology departments (ED) of six hospitals in the Netherlands was performed. Predictors in the UPFRONT‐model included educational level, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), neck pain at injury, alcohol intoxication, Post Traumatic Amnesia (PTA), pre‐injury mental health, anxiety, depression, coping, and the number and severity of post‐traumatic complaints. Functional recovery at 6 months was assessed using the Glasgow Outcome Scale Extended (GOS‐E). The external validity of the UPRFONT model was assessed with measures of calibration and discrimination.ResultsThe model showed acceptable discriminative ability (AUC = 0.74), comparable to the development sample (AUC = 0.77). However, calibration revealed systematic underestimation of recovery (predicted: 46%; observed: 75%), with a calibration intercept of 1.52 and a slope of 0.70. Despite differences in some predictor effects, psychological variables were robust and consistent across samples.ConclusionThe UPFRONT‐model demonstrated solid discriminative performance in an external cohort, but tended to underestimate the likelihood of complete recovery. Further validation and optimization are needed before clinical implementation. The model holds promise for early identification of at‐risk patients, enabling targeted interventions following mTBI.

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is one of the most prevalent neurological disorders and poses a substantial challenge to public health [1, 2]. Based on acute injury characteristic criteria, approximately 70%–90% of all TBIs are considered to be ‘mild’ (mTBI) [3]. Although the majority of patients with mTBI show rapid and complete recovery without a need for intervention, mTBI can lead to persistent symptoms that limit daily functioning [4, 5, 6] diminishing quality of life [7]. Several studies suggest that early interventions specifically targeting patients at risk of incomplete recovery after mTBI may improve outcomes [6, 8, 9, 10, 11]. To do this, a well‐performing prediction model is needed to timely identify patients who are at risk for incomplete recovery [10, 12].

In recognition of the limitations of defining TBI severity based on the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) alone, a multidimensional framework was recently proposed [13]. This framework incorporates four pillars: clinical findings, imaging findings, biomarkers, and modifiers (e.g., pre‐injury mental health). This proposition is in line with empirical finings, as previous studies have shown that injury severity alone (i.e., Computed Tomography (CT)‐characteristics or trauma mechanisms) explain incomplete recovery to a very limited extent at most [10, 14, 15, 16]. In several studies, it was found that psychological factors such as more initial psychological distress play a more prominent role in recovery after mTBI [4, 10, 11]. Within their systematic review, Silverberg and colleagues (2015), in which multivariable prognostic models were investigated (k = 29; n = 6939), found psychological factors, such as preinjury mental health and early postinjury anxiety, to be the most robust prognostic factors for outcome after mTBI [17].

In a prospective multicentre cohort study on mTBI (UPFRONT‐study) such an integrated multifactorial approach was used, by combining demographics (i.e., age, sex, and level of education), injury severity (i.e., CT abnormalities, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)‐score) and psychological factors (i.e., anxiety, depression, post‐traumatic stress symptoms, coping, and the number and severity of post‐traumatic complaints) [10]. Emotional distress, post‐traumatic complaints, and maladaptive coping measured at 2 weeks after injury, in combination with preinjury mental health problems, were the strongest predictors for incomplete functional recovery (i.e., a maximal score on the Glasgow Outcome Scale Extended [GOSE; [18, 19]]) at 6 months following mTBI [10]. This UPFRONT‐model demonstrated a good predictive value (AUC = 0.79, with optimism 0.02; Nagelkerke R2 = 0.32) and an accurate estimation of the overall probability (bias‐corrected calibration intercept = 0.04) of complete recovery, indicating its potential utility in clinical practice [10].

While a promising prediction tool, the UPFRONT‐model needs to be validated externally to assess the models' reproducibility [20] and external validity [21]. The use of incorrect prediction models can be misleading, and if clinical decisions are based on incorrect prediction models, this could have adverse effect on various patient outcomes [20]. In a study aiming to validate mTBI prediction models, the UPFRONT‐model was included [22]. Yet, crucial predictors, such as coping style and neck pain, were missing. Not surprisingly, the discriminative ability of the model without these crucial predictors was low, precluding assessing calibration‐in‐the‐large. Therefore, we aimed to externally validate the UPFRONT‐model, using all its predictors. We investigated how the UPFRONT‐model performs (i.e., calibration and discrimination) in predicting functional outcome following mTBI after 6 months, in an independent, comparable, patient cohort. External validation supports clinical application, enabling interventions to specifically target patients at risk for incomplete recovery after mTBI.

Method

Design

Data used for the current study were collected as part of a larger data collection effort for a project investigating mTBI and fear avoidance behavior [23]. For this prospective, longitudinal, multicenter cohort study, ethical approval was received from the medical ethics committee of Maastricht University and Maastricht University Medical Centre (METC 16–4‐209) and local research and ethics committees of participating hospitals. All participants gave written informed consent.

Participants

Participants were consecutively recruited from emergency and neurology departments in six hospitals in the south of the Netherlands between March 2017 and August 2019 (follow‐up until August 2020). Inclusion criteria were: mTBI diagnosis by their treating healthcare professional (either ED physician or neurologist) using the following criteria: a history of impact to the head; a GCS score between 13 and 15 at 30 min after the impact or later at hospital admission, and in case of a GCS score of 15, at least one of the three following: loss of consciousness (LOC) with a maximum duration of 30 min and posttraumatic amnesia (PTA) with a duration not exceeding 24 h, other transient neurological signs such as vomiting; aged between 18 and 76 years old; fluent in Dutch; able to be followed‐up over the course of 1 year; able and willing to provide informed consent. Exclusion criteria were as follows: a history of neurological disease or injury such as (m)TBI, epilepsy, stroke, neurodegenerative disease, brain tumor, encephalopathy, neurovascular abnormality, or multiple sclerosis; a history of psychiatric disorders for which hospitalization was needed; under the influence of illicit substances at the time of injury or a history of drug addiction; active use of psychoactive medication known for or reported by the patient to cause cognitive (side) effects before the injury.

Selection criteria for both the validation (i.e., current sample) and development (i.e., the UPFRONT sample) samples are detailed in Table S1.

Procedure

Eligible patients were informed about the study by their healthcare provider. With permission, researchers contacted them to schedule the first assessment within 2 weeks post‐injury (T1). After signing the informed consent, the first questionnaires were completed (T1). Participants received a notification for follow‐ups: after 3 months (T2), 6 months (T3), and 12 months (T4). To match the development sample, only data from T1 (demographic information and predictor variables) and T3 (outcome variable, GOS‐E) were used. Participants had 4 weeks to complete the questionnaires after each time point, with reminders sent if needed. Injury‐related variables were retrieved from the hospital database as part of the consent procedure.

Measures

Injury Severity

The GCS score ranging from 13 to 15, presence of PTA > 1 h (yes/no), CT‐abnormalities (yes/no), LOC (yes/no) were assessed at the ED.

Demographics

Demographic information (age, gender, education, and pre‐injury mental health) was assessed with a demographic questionnaire. Educational level was based on the highest completed level of education and divided according to the 7‐point Verhage classification [24]. Pre‐injury mental health problems (yes/no) were defined by a psychological disorder necessitating psychological treatment and/or use of psychoactive medication.

Physical Conditions

Alcohol intoxication on the day of the injury (yes/no) was assessed at the ED. Neck pain intensity was measured with a 100 mm VAS [25]. The left end of the VAS represented ‘no pain’ and the right end ‘most severe pain imaginable’. Scores were dichotomized into the absence (0) or presence (1) of neck pain (any indication of pain on the VAS, regardless of intensity, was scored as presence).

Psychological Factors

Anxiety (HADS‐A) and depression (HADS‐D) were assessed with the Dutch version of the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale [HADS; [26]], which includes 14 questions rated on a four‐point Likert scale (0–3), yielding subscale scores (0–21), with a cutoff score of 8 or higher suggesting the presence of either depression or anxiety. It is a valid and reliable measure for screening depression and anxiety in patients with TBI [27].

The avoidant and passive coping styles were assessed with the Utrecht Coping List [UCL;28], a valid and reliable measure commonly used in brain injury populations [28]. Each subscale includes 15 items. Higher total scores (1–5) indicate greater use compared to a historical group based on age and sex.

Post‐Traumatic Complaints

The number and severity of post‐concussion symptoms were assessed with the Rivermead Post‐Concussion Symptoms Questionnaire [RPQ; [29]], a valid and reliable measure in TBI populations [30]. The 16 items are rated on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from 0 ‘not experienced at all’ to 4 ‘a severe problem’ in comparison to before the injury. Higher scores (range 0–64) indicate more post‐concussion symptoms. For the calibration and discrimination analyses, RPQ scores are used to predict the HISC scores using the regression formula: −0.47 + (0.5 × total RPQ score). This formula is estimated based on data collected as part of a project investigating a matched care approach for mTBI patients. The sample included 78 mTBI patients who filled out both the RPQ and the HISC at 2 weeks post‐injury. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were the same as in the validation sample. Data collection occurred between January 2023 and May 2024, with patients recruited following visits to the Emergency Department or Neurology Department of VieCuri Medical Center. The study protocol of this matched care project was approved by the Ethics Review Committee Psychology and Neuroscience at Maastricht University (ERCPN‐256_105_08_2022).

Functional Recovery

Functional recovery was assessed with the GOS‐E [GOS‐E;18], an eight‐point scale where higher scores indicate a better global outcome. The GOS‐E is the most widely used and accepted instrument in neurotrauma research for assessing patient outcomes after TBI [31]. Outcome was dichotomized into complete (GOS‐E = 8) and incomplete recovery (GOS‐E ≤ 7) [10].

Minor differences in variable operationalization existed between the validation sample and the development sample (see Table S2). For further details on the UPFRONT‐study methodology, see van der Naalt and colleagues (2017) [10].

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted with the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software (SPSS), version 26.0. An alpha level of 0.05 was used for all statistical tests.

Missing Data Analyses

If patients had missing data for one or more of the predictors or the GOS‐E at 6 months, they were excluded. Differences in group characteristics between included and excluded patients of the validation sample were assessed with two‐sample t‐tests or chi‐square tests, as deemed appropriate.

Baseline Demographics, Clinical Characteristics and Outcome

Patients' baseline characteristics were described by mean, standard deviation (SD), and range for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables.

Comparison Validation Sample and Development Sample

Differences between the validation and the development sample on the relevant predictors were assessed using two‐sample t‐tests or chi‐square tests, as deemed appropriate. Effect sizes were quantified using Cohen's d for continuous variables and Cramér's V for categorical variables. Thresholds for small, medium, and large effects were 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 for d, and 0.1, 0.3, and 0.5 for V, respectively.

Exploratory Logistic Regression Analysis

To compare the estimated regression coefficients of the predictors in the development sample with those in the validation sample, a multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed with functional recovery as the outcome variable (GOS‐E = 8, yes/no), and using the predictor variables that were included in the UPFRONT‐model (collected at the same time point as in the development sample, i.e., within 2 weeks after the injury): education level (1–7), presence of pre‐injury mental health problems (yes/no), GCS (13–15), PTA > 1 h (yes/no), alcohol consumption on the day of the injury (yes/no), neck pain (yes/no), HADS‐A, HADS‐D, RPQ, UCL avoidant, and UCL passive. This allowed us to assess the consistency in effect directions and the relative importance of predictors across both samples. We assessed associations between the predictors and the outcome using the odds ratios (OR) and corresponding 95% confidence interval.

Calibration and Discrimination

The external validity of the UPFRONT‐model was assessed by applying this model to our sample data and considering measures of calibration and discrimination. Calibration assesses the accuracy of risk estimates; i.e., how well the absolute predicted risks correspond to the observed risks on a group level [20, 32]. For calibration, first the predicted risk for each individual was calculated based on the UPFRONT‐model, using the reported estimated equation [10]. Then three measures of calibration, namely calibration‐in‐the‐large, calibration plot and calibration slope, and a measure of discrimination were considered.

Calibration‐in‐the‐large involves evaluating how well a predictive model's estimated probabilities align with the true probabilities of events occurring [20, 32]. This was evaluated by calculating the average predicted probability across all individuals in our sample and comparing it to the actual observed rate of complete recovery in the sample. The calibration intercept expresses calibration‐in‐the‐large and has a target value of 0; values below 0 indicate over‐estimation, whereas values above zero indicate under‐estimation [32].

A calibration plot was computed to compare the predicted probabilities with the observed probabilities. The plot was computed by dividing the data into 10 groups based on predicted probability. The 10% of patients with the lowest predicted probability are grouped together. Within this group, the average predicted probability and proportion of patients who experience the outcome (observed probability) are computed. This is repeated for subsequent tenths of the patient sample. A line at 45 degrees at the population level would indicate perfect calibration [20, 21, 22, 32].

The calibration slope assesses the spread of the estimated risks and was determined in a logistic regression model with the logit of the model predictions as the only predictor [20]. An ideal calibration slope is 1 at the population level [20, 32]. If the slope is less than 1, it indicates that the estimated probabilities are overestimated (slope < 1; “overfit”) [20, 32]. If the slope is more than 1, it means the associations between predictors and outcomes are underestimated (slope > 1; “underfit”) [20, 32].

Discrimination is a relative measure of the ability to classify patients with a poor versus a good outcome based on a prognostic model and is assessed by the area under the operator receiver characteristic curve (AUC), which is equal to the concordance‐index in logistic regression models [20, 32]. The discriminative capacity is perfect when the AUC is 1.0; there is no discriminative capacity when the AUC is 0.5, which is equivalent to chance [20, 21, 22]. The ROC‐curve was plotted with the linear predictor and the actual observed outcomes. An AUC of 0.7 ≤ AUC < 0.8 is usually considered acceptable, 0.8 ≤ AUC < 0.9 good, and ≥ 0.9 is excellent or outstanding [20, 33].

Results

Missing Data Analysis

In total, 185 patients with mTBI were included. Fifty‐nine patients with missing data for one or more of the predictors or GOS‐E at 6 months, were excluded. A flowchart representing participant numbers at every stage is presented in Figure S1. From 126 (68.1%) data were used for further analysis. The excluded participants (n = 59) were significantly younger (M = 44.6, SD = 18.6) than included participants (M = 50.8, SD = 16.5), p < 0.05. There were neither significant differences between these two groups for sex, level of education, LOC, PTA > 1 h, anxiety, depression, or post‐traumatic complaints at T1, nor significant differences between these two groups in complete recovery after 6 months.

Comparison Validation Sample and Development Sample

Patients in the validation sample differed significantly from the development sample on the following variables: they had higher GCS scores, were less likely to have experienced LOC, were less likely to be intoxicated with alcohol on the day of injury, more frequently reported neck pain, reported lower levels of depressive symptoms, demonstrated a less passive coping style, and reported post‐traumatic complaints more often; further, a greater proportion of patients in the validation sample showed complete recovery at 6 months post‐injury. The effect size for the difference in GCS score was medium; all the other effect sizes were small. For a comparison of the characteristics between the validation and development samples, see Table S3.

Exploratory Logistic Regression

Logistic regression analysis showed that the model including all variables from the UPFRONT‐model was significant (χ2 = 33.282, p < 0.01). For most predictors (i.e., all except for alcohol intoxication, education level, GCS, and PTA > 1 h) the direction of the effect (positive or negative) was consistent across both samples. For most predictors, there were differences in significance and effect size between the validation and the development sample. For alcohol intoxication, GCS, education level, and PTA > 1 h these differences were substantial (i.e., difference in OR > 0.5). Anxiety, post‐traumatic complaints, passive coping style, and pre‐injury mental health appear robust and consistent across the two samples. For a comparison of the estimated regression coefficients of the individual predictors in the validation sample with those in the development sample, see Table S4.

The predicted proportion of complete recovery was 0.46, whereas the observed proportion was 0.75. In addition, the calibration intercept was 1.52, which is substantially higher than 0 and deviant from the calibration intercept in the development sample (i.e., 0.04). These results indicate that the percentage of complete recovery was underestimated. The calibration plot (Figure 1) shows that the observed proportion of complete recovery is higher than the predicted probability of complete recovery in each 10% of participants.

Calibration plot. Calibration plot which shows the relation between the predicted probability (x‐axis) and the observed proportion of events (y‐axis). The dotted line at 45 degrees represents perfect calibration, where predicted probabilities match observed outcomes exactly. The plot includes 10 data points, each representing a decile of the study population sorted by predicted probability. For each decile, the average predicted probability and the corresponding observed proportion of complete recovery are calculated.

The calibration slope is below one (0.70) and lower than the calibration slope in the development sample (i.e., 0.78), which indicates that the estimated probabilities are too extreme (i.e., too high for patients with high probability of complete recovery and too low for patients with a low probability of complete recovery).

The model discriminates to an acceptable extent between incomplete and complete recovery (AUC = 0.74) in the validation sample. This is similar to the optimism‐corrected AUC of 0.77 in the development sample (i.e., apparent AUC = 0.79, optimism = 0.02).

Discussion

In this study, we used an independent (external) patient sample to examine the performance of the complete UPFRONT‐model for predicting functional recovery at 6 months after mTBI. The ability of the UPFRONT‐model to distinguish between complete and incomplete recovery in the validation sample was acceptable (AUC 0.74) and comparable to the discriminative power in the development sample (optimism‐corrected AUC 0.77) [10]. The calibration results show that the model tends to underestimate the proportion of patients with complete recovery. This is reflected in the calibration intercept of 1.52, indicating systematic underprediction, in contrast to the near‐perfect calibration in the development sample (intercept = 0.04) [10]. In addition, the calibration slope of 0.70 (comparable to the 0.78 in the development sample) suggests that predictions are too extreme—overestimating probabilities for patients with a high chance of recovery and underestimating them for those with a low chance [10]. Logistic regression analysis showed that the number and severity of post‐concussion symptoms (RPQ) and the psychological factors anxiety, pre‐injury mental health, and coping style appear robust and consistent predictors across samples. The other predictors were not significant in the validation sample, which may be due to the limited size of the validation sample.

A first limitation of the study is the relatively low sample size in our sample (i.e., N = 671 in the development sample vs. N = 126 in the validation sample). Additionally, it should be noted that the validation sample appears to consist of a milder group of patients and had a narrower age range. A “restriction of range,” together with the smaller sample size, may have led to a more limited variation in predictor variable scores, which in turn may explain why certain predictors did not reach significance in the validation sample. It is important to note that for external validation of the prediction model the two cohorts do not necessarily need to be identical, provided that both come from the target population, the population for whom the model is intended to make predictions. This is the case in our study. Nevertheless, further validation and optimization are needed to validate the prediction model for the complete range of severity for which the model is intended, and thus particularly for cases with (in medical terms) more severe mTBI.

A third possible limitation of this study is that some patients had to be excluded because of missing data. Excluded patients were younger than patients from whom predictor and outcome scores were available at the 6‐month timepoint. However, they did not differ on any other variable, and most importantly, there were no significant differences between these two groups regarding recovery at 6 months. Therefore, we believe that the exclusion of these patients is unlikely to have introduced significant bias or affected the overall validity of the results. A last possible limitation is that there were small differences in the operationalization of certain variables between the development sample and the validation sample. For instance, post‐traumatic complaints were assessed using the RPQ, whereas in the development sample the Head Injury Symptom Checklist (HISC) was used. However, it is important to highlight that these two measures are fundamentally similar, as the HISC is derived from the RPQ for the assessment of post‐traumatic complaints [34]. Additionally, neck pain was measured with a VAS‐scale within the first two weeks after the injury, whereas in the development sample this was categorized as either absent or present and assessed at the day of the injury. The use of the VAS‐scale may have prompted participants to report more often a little pain and can explain why significantly more patients in the validation sample reported neck pain. These discrepancies arise from the fact that the data utilized in the current study were not originally collected to address this specific research question but were part of a larger data collection effort focused on investigating mTBI and fear‐avoidance behavior [23]. Lastly, a limitation of this study is that there was no patient information on race or ethnicity data limiting the generalizability of the results to diverse groups. A key strength of this study is the comprehensive inclusion of all predictors from the original UPFRONT‐model without exclusion. This approach allows for a thorough assessment of the model's performance in a new clinical sample. While the primary aim of the study was to externally validate the UPFRONT‐model for functional recovery following mTBI, our study is, to our knowledge, the first to do so using the full set of predictors identified in the original development study. This provides valuable insight into the model's generalizability and represents an important step toward its potential clinical application.

Van der Naalt and colleagues (2017) [10] suggested that the UPFRONT‐model would facilitate a two‐step approach to identify patients who are at risk of incomplete recovery, since indiscriminate treatment of all patients with mTBI would not be beneficial [10, 35]. This early identification could be done at discharge from the ED, using the model with ED variables alongside a short questionnaire at 2 weeks in which the psychological variables are collected. At‐risk patients could then be contacted for telephonic counseling to support their recovery. Before this promising prognostic model can be implemented in clinical practice, several actions must be taken. First, the model should be extended if the underestimation of the likelihood of complete recovery can be reduced and should then be further cross‐validated. Subsequently, the use of the resulting model to support clinical decision making can be considered.

Conclusion

In conclusion, in this study the UPFRONT‐model for the prediction of outcome after mTBI was successfully validated in an external patient sample. Further optimization of the predictive performance and cross‐validation is needed to improve the model. This study marks a significant step toward integrating the UPFRONT‐model into clinical practice, ultimately enhancing patient outcomes following mTBI.
