Nanocorona-engineered gold nanostars orchestrate photothermal augmented immunotherapy and durable cancer vaccination
Authors: Jun Young Park, Su Hyun Lim, Gayeon Kim, Jun-Young Park, Byung-Gil Lee, Sung Jean Park, Youngki Lee, Dongwoo Khang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Protein coronas inevitably cloak nanoparticles in vivo, yet their therapeutic potential remains largely untapped. Here we engineer a single-protein nanocorona by adsorbing immunoglobulin G onto PEGylated gold nanostars and reveal a dual-mode antitumor strategy that couples innate and adaptive immunity. Nanocorona formation remodels β-sheets and α-helices secondary structure in IgG and reprograms tumor-associated macrophages toward an M1 phenotype, releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines. Leveraging the photothermal property of the AuS, near-infrared irradiation synergistically induces immunogenic cell death, lowers interstitial fluid pressure, and drives deep infiltration of cytotoxic T lymphocytes while depleting regulatory T cells. In syngeneic CT26 tumor models, the combined nanocorona and photothermal regimen with anti-PD-L1 destroys established tumors significantly and confers complete protection on rechallenge, indicating vaccine-like immune memory. Comprehensive hematology and organ histology show negligible systemic toxicity. These findings position single-protein nanocoronas as stand-alone immunotherapeutics and provide a generalizable blueprint for converting photothermal nanomaterials into multifunctional cancer vaccines.Graphical Abstract Anticancer immunotherapy strategy combining nanocorona and photothermal therapy. This study presents an innovative cancer immunotherapy using gold nanostars coated with immunoglobulin G. The conformational change in the protein structure upon coating triggers immune cell activation in the tumor microenvironment. Combined with photothermal therapy and immune checkpoint inhibition, this approach enhances both innate and adaptive immune responses, leading to increased immune cell infiltration and effective tumor suppression without additional anticancer drugs.

Introduction

The modulation of the response of protein coronas surrounding nanoparticles (NPs) has been a significant topic in clinical applications [1–3]. NPs are rapidly coated with plasma proteins when administered in vivo, forming a protein known as the nanocorona [4]. The formation of a nanocorona is an unpredictable bio-safety issue because the composition and structure of the corona can change significantly depending on the characteristics of the NPs and the biological environment they encounter [5–8]. Thus, there are concerns about the unintended adverse effects of the protein corona, as it is challenging to predict the impact on the behavior of NPs in actual biological systems.

Despite extensive studies on nanocoronas, it is difficult to analyze adsorbed proteins on NPs because the plasma protein corona is highly transient and undergoes rapid changes within a short timeframe [9, 10]. Moreover, interactions between the protein corona and NPs can induce structural changes in proteins [11, 12]. Therefore, investigating nanocoronas composed of a single type of protein is valuable to improve understanding of the immunological impact of complex protein coronas.

In previous studies, a protein corona was artificially formed on NPs by precoating them with specific proteins [13–15]. Precoating proteins enhances their stability and controls cellular uptake, thereby reducing unintended consequences. While these findings highlight the potential of precoating, the interaction between NPs and surrounding proteins further induces additional therapeutic effects. For example, interaction with NPs alters the secondary structure of the surrounding proteins, which triggers an immune response in vitro [16, 17]. For this reason, there is a demand for further research regarding the inherent therapeutic potential of nanocoronas caused by conformational changes in proteins. The current study extends this concept by exploring the nanocorona on gold nanostars (AuS) and its therapeutic application in anticancer therapy.

Although the nanocorona can induce immune responses by itself, effective immunotherapy must sufficiently resolve the immunosuppressive conditions in the tumor microenvironment (TME) [18–21]. The TME is composed of cancer cells and a heterogeneous collection of infiltrating and resident immune cells, secreted factors, and the extracellular matrix (ECM) [22, 23]. Among these, immune cells play a significant role in immune regulation and are capable of inducing activation and suppression of the immune response. For instance, M1 macrophage polarization, natural killer (NK) cell distribution, and T cell infiltration in the TME are associated with a favorable prognosis for tumor treatment [24–26]. However, in typical solid tumors, the immune cell distribution is biased toward M2 macrophages and regulatory T cells (Treg), which limits the efficacy of immunotherapy [27–29].

Likewise, the T cell population is associated with a wide range of immune responses. Differentiation into effector T cells (CD69+, CD62L−, Teff), including cytotoxic T cells (CD8+ T cells, CTLs), can rapidly inhibit tumor growth and sustain an inhibitory effect [30, 31]. However, CTLs face several challenges in the TME, which hinder their effectiveness [32–34]. CTLs are mainly located around the tumor boundary in an immune-excluded state and do not infiltrate tumors. Additionally, CTLs in the TME are often exhausted, losing their functionality and ability to destroy tumors. Therefore, for comprehensive treatment of solid tumors, activated CD8+ T cells must be uniformly distributed throughout the tumor [35, 36]. These findings highlight the importance of immune cell distribution and the activation status in the TME, as they significantly influence tumor therapy outcomes [37–39]. To mitigate these obstacles, the development of a multimodal therapy that induces a favorable immune cell population and facilitates their infiltration into the tumor is crucial.

Photothermal therapy (PTT) is an innovative cancer therapy that utilizes photothermal agents, such as indocyanine green and gold nanoparticles, which convert absorbed light energy into heat, thereby inducing thermal ablation of tumor tissues [40–42]. PTT is notable for its ability to directly kill tumor cells, destroy the ECM, reduce interstitial fluid pressure (IFP), and enhance blood perfusion. Furthermore, the hyperthermia-induced destruction of cancer cells can trigger an inflammatory response, promoting the recruitment of immune cells and the secretion of various chemokines. These immune-modulating effects, in conjunction with the release of tumor-specific antigens, significantly enhance the infiltration and activation of T cells, thereby improving their antitumor efficacy [43, 44]. Additionally, PTT offers several advantages, including minimally invasive procedures, short treatment durations, and rapid recovery times, which have contributed to its growing popularity in antitumor therapy [45, 46]. The strategy of applying PTT for the nanocorona, therefore, not only offers the ability to directly kill tumor cells but also modulates the TME to facilitate an effective immune response, which makes it a promising approach to the comprehensive treatment of solid tumors.

In this study, AuS was artificially coated with immunoglobulin G (IgG), which induced the activation of immune cells in the TME (Fig. 1). Obtained results demonstrate the first use of nanocoronas for antitumor therapy in animal models without additional anticancer drugs. The results showed that nano-corona can induce innate immune response, but to activate adaptive immune response, both high temperature and release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) were simultaneously required which was induced by employing PTT. Moreover, PTT not only directly eliminated tumor cells, but also reduced tumor-dense structure and IFP, which resulted in increased blood flow and promoted the infiltration of immune cells without blood toxicity. The immune evasion protein (PD-L1) that hinders the immune response of T cells, was neutralized by immune checkpoint inhibitor (anti-PD-L1) administration and the results demonstrated that combination therapy improved tumor suppression ability of AuS-IgG. Specifically, it increased the M1/M2 macrophage ratio, NK cell distribution, and improved the effector/naive ratio of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in TME, which contributed to tumor growth suppression the most effectively. Notably, treatment prevented tumor recurrence, suggesting a potential vaccination effect, which could have significant clinical impact for invasive cancer. In conclusion, these findings suggest that nano-corona has the potential to be novel immune therapy that reinforce the anti-cancer strategy to overcome TME.

Results

Physical characterization

NPs surrounded by a corona can increase the innate immune response through conformational changes in the protein structure [16, 17]. Therefore, as its therapeutic effects require no additional drugs, it could be advantageous to develop an artificial nanocorona for anticancer therapy. Building upon previous research, this study modeled the encapsulation of the most abundant immunoglobulins in blood, IgG, and human serum albumin (HSA) onto AuS to formulate a hard corona (Fig. 2a and S1a). To determine the optimal protein conjugation ratio, Tnfa levels were measured and the immune response reached a plateau at 1:3 ratio (Figure S2). However, the 1:1 ratio was selected as optimal because it yielded the highest loading efficiency while maintaining a desirable particle size (Table S1). Therefore, further experiments were conducted using AuS-IgG with 1:1 ratio. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirmed the visualization of proteins surrounding AuS, and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) confirmed the colocalization of Au (gold, yellow) and S (sulfur, red), indicating the PEGylation of AuS (Fig. 2b and S1b-c). Furthermore, to evaluate the potential of PTT, AuS-IgG heating using near-infrared (NIR) laser successfully increased the delta T (ΔT) to 15 °C within 5 min, confirming that AuS-IgG was NIR laser-responsive (Fig. 2c). Photoactivation of AuS-IgG by NIR laser irradiation was detected using a thermal-imaging infrared camera (Fig. 2d).

Successful conjugation of IgG onto AuS was confirmed by changes in particle size and surface charge. Dynamic light scattering analysis showed the hydrodynamic size increased from 156.74 ± 7.62 nm for AuS to 224.67 ± 8.06 nm for AuS-IgG (Fig. 2e). Correspondingly, the zeta potential at pH 7.2 shifted from a negative − 13.11 ± 1.35 mV to a near neutral 0.42 ± 0.78 mV (Fig. 2f). This change resulted in a surface charge similar to that of free IgG, indicating sufficient protein coating on the AuS surface. Identical trend was consistently observed in control experiments using HSA and obtained results supported the evidence of altered physiochemical surface properties by surrounded protein corona formation (Figure S1d-e).

In addition, to identify changes in the secondary structure of the corona, proteins conjugated to AuS or heat-denatured proteins as model proteins of denatured structures were subjected to circular dichroism (CD) measurements, and their secondary structures were analyzed by CD secondary structure retrieval (CDSSR) (Fig. 2g-h and S1f-g).The CDSSR revealed that the stranded form (beta sheet components) of IgG protein decreased from 47.2% to 13.1%, and unordered form (unordered region) increased from 14.7% to 32.3% in AuS-IgG conjugate. In addition, HSA also exhibited structural changes upon adsorption onto AuS characterized by a decrease in helical content and a concomitant increase in β-strand content (Figure S1d-e and Table S2). Notably, these conformational changes in AuS-IgG were quite different from those observed in heat-shocked IgG, and exceeded the structural alterations reported in previous studies involving protein adsorption onto molybdenum disulfide nanosheets [17, 47]. Furthermore, the protein release profile showed that approximately 20% of the protein dissociated from the conjugate within 24 h, and these released proteins still retained meaningful order of conformational change (Figure S3). Therefore, these findings suggest that the secondary structure of the protein is altered upon binding to AuS and may influence the protein function.

In conclusion, plasma proteins such as IgG and HSA artificially bound to NPs were prepared to mimic a hard corona. As secondary structural changes in the bound proteins were identified, subsequent cellular responses can be examined, particularly focusing on immune stimulation within the cold TME.

Release of pro-inflammatory cytokines from macrophages by nano-corona: in vitro and in vivo analysis

To understand the interaction of the nanocorona with different macrophage phenotypes, cellular internalization and uptake pathways of the nanocorona in various macrophage types were investigated. Macrophages can be classified into three main types: M0, M1, and M2. In the TME, the macrophages are predominantly M2 macrophages, which suppress intratumoral inflammation and immune reactions, thereby supporting tumor growth [48]. Conversely, activation of M1 macrophages in the TME is considered the primary immune gateway for tumor suppression. Confocal microscopy and fluorescence activated cell sorting (FACS) analyses were performed after administration of the nanocorona to M0 macrophages (J774A.1 cells), lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated J774A.1 cells (M1 polarization), and IL4-stimulated J774A.1 cells (M2 polarization) (Figure S4a-b). More than 80% of macrophages absorbed nanocorona after 2 h. To identify the activated uptake channels for the nanocorona, the known intracellular inhibitors genistein (GEN), 5-(N-ethyl-N-isopropyl)amiloride (EIPA), and chlorpromazine (CPZ) were used. The results showed that the uptake of nanocorona was suppressed by less than 20%, whereas CPZ inhibited uptake by 40–45%, indicating that nanocorona is primarily internalized through the clathrin-mediated pathway regardless of macrophage polarization status (Figure S4a).

To further elucidate the influence of macrophages on tumor cells, both in vitro and in vivo experiments were conducted (Fig. 3). First, cell cycle analysis was performed on CT26 cells co-cultured with J774A.1 cells to determine the effect of macrophages activated by AuS-IgG on tumors (Fig. 3a and Figure S5a). When treated with AuS-IgG, most tumor cells were distributed in the G/G0 phase, and an increase in the number of apoptotic cells was observed. In addition, macrophages treated with nanocorona released inflammatory cytokines (Tnfa, Il1b, and Il6), whereas native proteins that did not undergo structural changes remained inactive (Fig. 3b). The data indicate that the AuS-IgG-induced M1 polarization of macrophages can directly inhibit cancer cell proliferation through the secretion of inflammatory cytokines. The results were also consistent in AuS-HSA treatment (Figure S6). These findings are consistent with those of previous studies demonstrating that conformational changes in proteins can trigger immune responses [8, 16, 17].

Activated macrophages were found to not only suppress cancer cell proliferation but also mediate T cell activation. To demonstrate these effects, an additional in vitro experiment was performed to evaluate the adaptive immune response to AuS-IgG. Briefly, bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were seeded at the basolateral side, and CT26 cells were seeded at the apical side of the transwell. Subsequently, IgG, AuS, and AuS-IgG were treated with or without NIR laser irradiation (Figure S5b). After treatment, transwell and CT26 cells were removed, and additional red fluorescent protein (RFP)-expressing CT26 cells and T cells were seeded (Figure S5c). The presence of RFP-positive apoptotic CT26 cells was analyzed after 48 h, and the apoptosis rates observed were as follows: control, 34.9%; IgG, 22.7%; AuS, 35.5%; AuS + PTT, 42.2%; AuS-IgG, 37.3%; and AuS-IgG + PTT, 61.9% (Fig. 3c). These results demonstrated that the combination of AuS-IgG and PTT was more effective in inducing apoptosis which was due to the interaction between M1 macrophages and T cells stimulated by AuS-IgG and further enhanced by PTT (Fig. 3d and Figure S5d). This could be explained by the release of antigens and DAMPs from PTT-treated tumor cells, which stimulated the BMDM to induce stronger anticancer responses in T cells than in non-irradiated groups (Figure S7a-b). These findings suggest that the induction of an adaptive immune response is more effective when high temperatures and DAMPs release are employed through PTT.

To evaluate the efficacy in the immunosuppressed TME, a CT26 animal tumor model was established, and IgG, AuS, and AuS-IgG were administered when the tumor size reached 50 mm3 to verify the activation of macrophages (Fig. 3e). The results demonstrated that the levels of inflammatory cytokines (Tnfa, Il1b, and Il6) in the tumor tissue were significantly increased by AuS-IgG, whereas Arg1, an M2 macrophage phenotype gene, was decreased, indicating the repolarization of M2 macrophages into M1 macrophages (Fig. 3f-g). This shift toward an M1 macrophage phenotype is consistent with the observed increase in pro-inflammatory cytokine release, and suggests a potential mechanism of the nanocorona-induced antitumor effects. Additionally, an increase in the Ifng gene, a marker predominantly associated with adaptive immune responses, was observed in the tumor tissue of the treated group (Fig. 3h). This suggests that the nanocorona not only stimulates innate immunity through macrophage activation, but also potentially triggers adaptive immune responses, further enhancing its antitumor potential [49, 50].

In conclusion, AuS-IgG induced both innate and adaptive immune responses through M1 macrophage polarization and T cell activation, and these effects were significantly enhanced by PTT.

Nanocorona induces pro-inflammatory transcriptomic reprogramming in macrophages

To elucidate the impact of nanocorona on macrophage activation at the transcriptomic level, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis was performed on J774A.1 macrophages following treatment with nanocorona (Figure S8a). Principal component analysis revealed a distinct clustering of the nanocorona group, separating it from both the control and simple mixture of AuS and IgG (AuS + IgG (mixture)) in principal component 1 (59.4%) (Fig. 4a). This indicates that nanocorona can induce significant alteration in the overall gene expression profile of macrophages, which was not observed in simple mixture. Further analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was conducted by comparing the nanocorona and AuS + IgG (mixture). A volcano plot showed that nanocorona treatment led to a substantial number of DEGs, with 292 genes upregulated and 318 genes downregulated (Fig. 4b). Furthermore, to specifically investigate the nature of this immune activation, expression of key M1 and M2 macrophage markers were visualized (Fig. 4c). The heatmap revealed a distinct polarization pattern in the nanocorona-treated group, characterized by a strong upregulation of pro-inflammatory M1 markers, including Tnf, Il1b, Il6, Il12b, Cxcl9, Cxcl10, Nos2, Cd86, Cd64a, and Stat1 [51, 52]. Concurrently, anti-inflammatory M2 markers such as Arg1 and Il10 were downregulated compared to the control and mixture groups. Notably, the AuS + IgG (mixture) exhibited no DEGs when compared to control group, underscoring the unique impact of the nanocorona (Figure S8b).

To identify the biological pathways modulated by nanocorona treatment, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed (Fig. 4d). Analysis of the hallmark gene sets revealed significant enrichment of several pathways crucial for M1 macrophage polarization and pro-inflammatory responses. These include interferon-α response, TNF-α signaling via NF-KB, interferon-γ response, inflammatory response, IL-6/JAK/STAT3 signaling, and complement pathways. The upregulation of these pathways strongly indicates that the nanocorona primes macrophages towards an M1-like activated state, consistent with in vitro results demonstrating its anti-tumorigenic potential (Fig. 3). Furthermore, GSEA of biological process gene ontology (GO) terms provided insights into the induced immune responses (Fig. 4e). Several GO terms such as natural killer cell activation, T cell activation involved in immune response, and T cell proliferation showed positive enrichment scores, indicating their upregulation by nanocorona treatment. Conversely, GO terms related to cell cycle progression exhibited negative values. These findings collectively suggest that the nanocorona not only promotes a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype in macrophages but also induce a broader immune response involving activation and proliferation of other key immune cells like NK cells and T cells.

In summary, obtained RNA-seq results demonstrate that the nanocorona significantly reprograms the transcriptome of macrophages, driving them towards a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype.

Enhanced antitumor effects of the Nanocorona combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors in the tumor model

The in vitro results indicated that AuS-IgG can induce both innate and adaptive immune responses, leading to antitumor effects. However, in the TME, immunosuppressive conditions inhibit the efficacy of AuS-IgG-induced immunotherapy. The immune evasion mechanism involving PD-L1 is a well-known factor that suppresses T cell responses in the TME [53–55]. To counteract this, anti-PD-L1 was applied along with AuS-IgG treatment, and experiments were designed to assess the effect of combination therapy on tumor suppression. CT26 cells (5 × 106) were injected subcutaneously into mice to establish an in vivo tumor model. The tumor-bearing mice were sorted when the tumor volume reached 50 mm3. The nanocorona with a protein concentration of 5 mg/kg was injected every other day (EOD) (Fig. 5a). A significant decrease in tumor volume was observed in the AuS-IgG + anti-PD-L1 combination therapy group compared to the other groups (Fig. 5b-c). One out of five mice treated with AuS-IgG + anti-PD-L1 exhibited complete tumor regression, which was not observed in the other groups. In contrast, the group treated with AuS-IgG alone exhibited mild therapeutic effects, likely because of the absence of anti-PD-L1 to overcome the immunosuppressive nature of the TME. Moreover, AuS-IgG did not achieve an efficacy comparable to that of the anti-PD-L1 only group, highlighting the importance of addressing the immunosuppressive TME. Tumor sections confirmed the therapeutic efficacy through hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assays (Fig. 5d-e). Although a marked reduction in tumor size was observed, the lack of PTT likely resulted in the absence of a strong apoptotic signal and significant changes in histochemical analysis.

In a previous study, repeated and prolonged administration of NPs surrounded by a protein corona significantly increased the levels of neutrophils, NK cells, and CD8+ T cells in mouse spleens [16]. Additionally, the population of effector T helper cells (CD4+) increased, whereas that of naïve CD4+ T cells decreased. Based on this, the immune cell population was analyzed using flow cytometry (Fig. 5f-i). The NK cell population, which was 7.2% in healthy control mice, was reduced to 1.6% in tumor-bearing mice. However, treatment with AuS-IgG and anti-PD-L1 successfully restored the cell population to 3.6% (Fig. 5f). In the tumor animal model, the M1/M2 macrophage phenotype ratio was downregulated to 0.96, which significantly increased to 7.92 in the AuS-IgG-treated group, comparable to 13.39 in normal mice (Fig. 5g). The effect on adaptive immune cells was assessed by evaluating the effector-to-naïve ratios of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Fig. 5h-i). In tumor-bearing mice, a low ratio of CD4+/CD8+ effector T cells to CD4+/CD8+ naïve T cells (0.10/0.03) like that in normal mice (0.10/0.12). In contrast, the AuS-IgG + anti-PD-L1 group demonstrated an antitumor environment with an effector/naïve CD4+ T cell increasing to 0.65 and CD8+ T cells to 0.83, showing the induction of antitumor efficacy by the nanocorona. Similar mild effects on the CD4+ T cell ratio were confirmed in AuS-IgG-treated group without anti-PD-L1 (0.34/0.83). These results indicate that nanocoronas can reprogram immune cell populations in the TME and stimulate a robust T cell response, which holds great promise for future immunotherapeutic applications. Moreover, by inducing both innate and adaptive immune responses, nanocoronas could be used as novel adjuvants, offering a more effective approach to cancer immunotherapy.

Photothermal therapy enhances Nanocorona mediated tumor suppression

Despite the significant reduction in tumor size observed in the anti-PD-L1 combination therapy group, there was no significant increase in apoptotic signals. The in vitro experiments have demonstrated that PTT can directly kill tumor cells and release DAMPs, thereby enhancing adaptive immune responses and improving therapeutic outcomes (Figure S7) [56]. To assess the effects of PTT in combination with AuS-IgG, the same experimental design was followed by NIR laser treatment (Fig. 6a). A substantial reduction in tumor volume was noted in the AuS-IgG + anti-PD-L1 + PTT combination therapy group compared to the other groups (Fig. 6b-c and S9). Remarkably, three out of five mice in this group achieved complete tumor regression, highlighting the enhanced therapeutic effect over the non-PTT-treated groups. As expected, the AuS-IgG and AuS + anti-PD-L1 groups also exhibited therapeutic benefits, but not to the same extent as the AuS-IgG + anti-PD-L1 + PTT group. Histological analysis through H&E staining and TUNEL assay also confirmed the therapeutic efficacy (Fig. 6d-e). The AuS with NIR irradiation group demonstrated a strong TUNEL signal, indicating extensive apoptosis, which was not observed in the non-PTT-treated groups. This suggests that PTT, by directly inducing tumor cell death, significantly amplifies the antitumor effects observed in Sect. 2.5, where the absence of PTT limits apoptosis despite immune activation. This implied that PTT induced significant tumor cell death, and the subsequent release of DAMPs further stimulated immune cell activation, which was consistent with preivious findings (Fig. 3 and S7).

Further immunomodulatory effects of the PTT combination therapy were observed (Fig. 6f-i). The decrease in the NK cell population was sufficiently recovered by AuS-IgG + anti-PD-L1 + PTT, bringing the cell count back to the level of normal mice (Fig. 6f and S10). The M1/M2 macrophage phenotype ratio, which was initially reduced in the tumor animal model, significantly increased to 28.30 in the AuS-IgG + anti-PD-L1 + PTT-treated group, which was higher than that in normal mice (Fig. 6g, S11, and S12). In comparison, the non-PTT groups in Sect. 2.5 showed only moderate increases in the M1/M2 ratio, underscoring the additional influence of PTT on macrophage polarization. The effect of the PTT combination therapy on adaptive immune cells was also analyzed (Fig. 6h-i, S13, and S14). The AuS-IgG + anti-PD-L1 + PTT group demonstrated a shift toward an antitumor environment, with the ratio of effector-to-naïve CD4+/CD8+ T cells increased to 1.88 and 1.41, respectively. These values were notably higher than those observed in Sect. 2.5 (0.65/0.83), where the absence of PTT resulted in lower effector T cell ratios, further indicating the effectiveness of PTT in reprogramming the TME. Furthermore, the AuS-IgG + anti-PD-L1 + PTT treatment group exhibited the most significant downregulation of Treg cells compared with the other groups (Figure S15). While the AuS-IgG group without PTT also exhibited significant antitumor immune responses, the addition of PTT led to a substantial enhancement of these effects, indicating that PTT significantly boosted the efficacy of the nanocorona. In conclusion, these findings confirmed that combining PTT with AuS-IgG represents an optimal strategy for cancer treatment in vivo by significantly modulating the adaptive immune system in the TME, thereby achieving greater tumor suppression and immune reprogramming.

Enhanced anticancer efficacy: deep tumor infiltration of T cells by PTT and Nanocorona

Tumor tissues are characterized by IFP and an immunosuppressive TME, which leads to the peripheral distribution of CTLs and other immune cells (Fig. 7a). While the therapeutic effects of combining AuS-IgG with anti-PD-L1 were demonstrated (Fig. 5), the addition of PTT resulted in significantly enhanced overall tumor treatment efficacy (Fig. 6). To elucidate the underlying mechanism of the adaptive immune response, the distribution of T cells within the tumor tissue was analyzed (Fig. 7b-d). Immunohistochemistry revealed that T cells remained within the blood vessels in the saline-treated group, which might be due to high IFP levels (Fig. 7b-c and S16). However, in the AuS-IgG + anti-PD-L1 + PTT group, most T cells were able to detach from blood vessels and infiltrate deep into tumor tissues, with a higher overall population of T cells within the tumor tissue. Although the AuS-IgG + anti-PD-L1 group without PTT also showed some degree of T cell migration toward the tumor, the effect was less than that observed in the PTT combination therapy group. Additionally, the combination therapy of AuS-IgG and PTT facilitated the distribution of T cells toward the central region of the tumor rather than the peripheral region (Fig. 7d and S17). These results are consistent with those of other studies that utilized PTT to induce vasodilation and increase T cell distribution [57, 58]. Additionally, the data visually and quantitatively showed that T cells detached from blood vessels. In conclusion, combination therapy with a nanocorona, anti-PD-L1, and PTT can overcome the immunosuppressive TME and enhance the therapeutic efficacy of T cells by promoting their infiltration.

Tumor recurrence Inhibition via systemic antitumor immunotherapy of AuS-IgG

To investigate the potential of AuS-IgG to induce a systemic immune response, a re-challenge experiment was conducted in CT26 colon cancer-bearing mice. NPs modified with immune checkpoint inhibitors and NIR irradiation can activate robust immune responses within the TME and prevent long-term tumor recurrence [59–61]. These approaches require the use of additional adjuvants or coatings with tumor cell membranes to enhance their efficacy. Such modifications raise concerns regarding biocompatibility and potential adverse effects. In contrast, the nanocorona formed through simple protein secondary structure modifications not only induces an immune response without adjuvants but also ensures inherent biocompatibility.

We analyzed the nanocorona-induced immune response in preventing tumor recurrence. Two days after primary tumor eradication, surviving mice were re-challenged with CT26 in the contralateral flank (Fig. 8a). Briefly, mice with primary CT26 tumors had previously been treated with combination therapy through intratumoral AuS-IgG + anti-PD-L1 injection, and NIR irradiation showed strong resistance to tumor re-challenge (Fig. 8b). Specifically, two out of the three re-challenged mice completely rejected the re-challenge, demonstrating that the systemic immune response against CT26 tumors was sustained. Furthermore, compared with the saline-treated group, the triple combination treatment (i.e., AuS-IgG, anti-PD-L1, and laser hyperthermia) altered the immunosuppressive TME. This was evidenced by the increased infiltration of tumor-killing effector CD4+/CD8+ T cells (Fig. 8c-f and S18). These findings suggest that AuS-IgG, when used in combination with immune checkpoint blockers and PTT, not only induces a potent neoadjuvant effect but also establishes a long-lasting systemic vaccine that effectively prevents tumor recurrence. In conclusion, the nanocorona holds significant promise as an effective strategy for the treatment of high-grade invasive cancers, particularly those with a high risk of recurrence.

Blood and organ toxicity induced by nanocorona: evidence of recovery from immuno-suppression

The systemic toxicity of the nanocorona was evaluated by examining the organs and blood to ensure toxicological evaluation in tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 9a). Notably, there was no significant change in the overall weight of the animals across treatment groups, indicating negligible systemic toxicity (Fig. 9b). Organ weights remained consistent with those of controls, except for the spleen, where an increase was observed in animals with larger tumors. This suggests a potential correlation between tumor progression and the immune response. For instance, splenic enlargement or splenomegaly can be indicative of an activated immune system in response to tumor antigens (Fig. 9c) [62]. Animals that exhibited reduced tumor size had spleen weights similar to those of the control group, suggesting that effective tumor suppression correlated with the normalization of spleen size.

Hematologic analysis revealed no significant alterations in RBC count, platelet count (PLT), hemoglobin (HGB), alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), or blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels, indicating that liver and kidney functions remained within normal ranges. However, an increase in white blood cell (WBC) and neutrophil counts was observed in animals with larger tumors, potentially reflecting an inflammatory or immune response to tumor progression (Fig. 9d) [63, 64]. In contrast, WBC and neutrophil counts in animals with reduced tumors recovered to within normal limits, suggesting effective tumor management. Recovery after nanocorona treatment indicates a reduction in tumor-associated inflammation, often characterized by elevated levels of these cells [65]. Conversely, lymphocyte reduction in tumor-bearing animals is often a sign of tumor-induced immunosuppression, in which the TME actively inhibits lymphocyte function to evade immune surveillance [66]. This immunosuppressive environment allows tumors to grow while evading the natural immune responses of the body. However, nanocorona treatment reversed the immunosuppression by restoring lymphocyte counts and functionality to normal levels (Fig. 9d). This finding suggests the restoration of tumor-induced immunological disruptions and the immune cell balance by the nanocorona, which is consistent with other studies and previous data (Figs. 5f-i and 6f-i) [67]. In conclusion, nanocorona treatment effectively managed tumor progression with minimal systemic toxicity, while reversing the immunosuppression induced by tumors. The recovery of immune activity, as evidenced by the normalized levels of lymphocytes, neutrophils, and WBCs, highlights the potential of the nanocorona as a promising therapeutic approach in cancer.

Discussion and conclusion

The strategy of precoating NPs with proteins to form a nanocorona has been utilized since the 1980 s, either to increase the circulation time in vivo or to evade immune cells [68, 69]. More recently, several studies have shown that artificial nanocorona engineering can induce immune responses [16, 17]. However, there has been no successful evidence for applying nanocorona systems to actual immuno-therapeutic strategies in cancer.

This study is the first to use nanocoronas to trigger an immune response as an anticancer therapeutic strategy. Interestingly, the nanocorona did not incorporate any additional chemotherapeutic agents or adjuvants into the NPs. Instead, AuNPs, which inherently lack therapeutic effects, are coated with immunoglobulin G (IgG) or HSA. These proteins were chosen based on their abundance in the blood. Despite the lack of therapeutic efficacy of the individual components, the interaction between NPs and proteins induces structural changes that trigger both innate and adaptive immune responses. Building on previous research, this study also demonstrated that nanocorona formation, particularly with IgG, could strongly induce immune responses due to alterations in the secondary structure of proteins. By applying this concept to AuS, these engineered nanocoronas could be used as novel adjuvants for future cancer therapies. Furthermore, this study demonstrated enhanced immune activation through combination therapy with anti-PD-L1 and PTT, using AuS as the photothermal agent. PTT induced vascular expansion and IFP reduction, which facilitated the escape of activated T cells from the vasculature and their effective infiltration into the tumor core, as shown in confocal microscopy images. Moreover, the treatment showed promise as a tumor vaccine, providing long-term protection against cancer recurrence. The efficacy of the nanocorona combination therapy was also demonstrated by complete tumor regression in three of five treated mice. Additionally, the nanocorona treatment functions as a cancer vaccine, providing long-term protection against cancer recurrence.

This artificially engineered nanocorona contributes to the field of nanomedicine by expanding the boundaries of NPs engineering and its therapeutic potential. Importantly, although this study utilized only AuS, the principles demonstrated herein can be extended to other materials. Therefore, the successful development of nanocorona systems not only highlights their immediate potential but also suggests a new field for anticancer nanomedicine. Future research should focus on elucidating the precise mechanisms by which protein conformational changes are induced upon nanocorona formation. Understanding mechanism of secondary structural changes in proteins triggering immune responses is crucial for optimizing nanocorona systems. For instance, a precedent for this mechanism was established by previous study which demonstrated that NPs-induced conformational unfolding of the fibrinogen was critical for its interaction with the macrophage Mac-1 receptor [70]. The conformational change of the protein subsequently activated the NF-κB signaling pathway, leading to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α.

Moreover, by designing a nanocorona with a single type of protein, this study addresses the main challenge of protein–corona research. The formation of a protein corona is highly dynamic because of the Vroman effect, in which proteins with higher mobility initially bind to the nanoparticle surface. Over time, they are displaced by proteins with a greater affinity, leading to the formation of a stable hard corona. This dynamic behavior complicates the prediction and control of nanoparticle interactions in biological systems. However, focusing on a single-protein nanocorona simplified this problem and enabled the investigation of the secondary structure of proteins and the associated results. The insights gathered from this controlled single-protein nanocorona system will provide a broader perspective for the design of drug delivery systems in actual in vivo environments in future research.

In contrast, although the AuS-IgG used in this study effectively induced innate immunity, it required the assistance of PTT to achieving adaptive immune responses and efficient tumor suppression. PTT plays a crucial role in reducing IFP and dilating blood vessels, which induces the widespread infiltration of T cells throughout the tumor. The direct tumor cell death and DAMPs release resulting from this process provide additional benefits for T cell activation. Future strategies could administer therapeutic agents onto the nanocorona to induce a synergistic impact or optimize the potential of the nanocorona system itself, capable of eliciting immune responses without the need for additional PTT. In addition, the system did not propose cell- or tissue-targeting abilities or mechanisms to evade the reticuloendothelial system (RES) and was restricted to intratumoral injection. To overcome these limitations, follow-up studies should focus on the development of nanocorona platforms with diverse proteins and compositions to provide various modalities. Such platforms could optimize pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics and enable targeted delivery to specific tissues via various routes of administration. Recent advances in deep learning and artificial intelligence offer possibilities for accelerating the development of nanocorona platforms [71]. With the ability to analyze vast datasets of protein corona interactions, researchers could uncover hidden patterns and relationships that might be difficult to determine through traditional methods. This approach may predict how different proteins interact with various nanoparticle surfaces, ultimately aiding in the design of more effective and biocompatible nanocorona platforms.

We demonstrated the potential of engineered nanocorona systems as a novel cancer therapy platform that effectively induces immune responses without the need for additional chemotherapeutic agents.

Methods

Synthesis of the Nanocorona

AuS-PEG was synthesized using the seeded growth technique [72]. Gold seed solutions and citrate-stabilized AuNPs were synthesized according to a previously established protocol. Briefly, the reaction mixture was injected into deionized water under vigorous stirring, followed by the addition of 25 mM HAuCl4 (5209258, Sigma, St. Louis, MO) and 1 N hydrogen chloride (OCI Company, Seoul, South Korea). A solution containing 3 mM of AgNO3 (209139, Sigma, St. Louis, MO), 100 mM of ascorbic acid, and 5 mg/mL of O-(3-carboxypropyl)-O’-[2-(3-mercaptopropionylamino)ethyl]-polyethylene glycol solution (712515, Sigma, St. Louis, MO) was added to the gold seed solution and the reaction proceeded for 2 h. The AuS-PEG solution was concentrated using Amicon filter (Amicon YM-50, 100 kDa, Millipore, Burlington, MA) by centrifugation at 3,000 rpm for 10 min at room temperature (RT). The AuS-PEG solution was redispersed in 2-morpholinoethanesulfonic acid monohydrate buffer (MES) (M3671, Sigma, St. Louis, MO) (50 mM, pH 8.0). The AuS-PEG solution was washed thrice with MES buffer prior to protein conjugation. To form the nanocorona, IgG (I4506, Sigma, St. Louis, MO) and HSA (A1887, Sigma, St. Louis, MO) were both prepared at 5 mg/mL in MES buffer. The AuS-PEG and protein mixtures were incubated at 4 °C for 48 h with gentle shaking. The nanocorona (AuS-IgG and AuS-HSA) in MES buffer was washed three times with MES buffer to remove unbound proteins. The purified nanocorona complexes were suspended in phosphate buffered solution (PBS) (10010023, Gibco, Waltham, MA) and stored until physical characterization.

CD spectroscopy

CD spectra were obtained using a Chirascan CD spectrometer (Chirascan V100, Applied Photophysics, Randalls, UK). The samples were prepared at a concentration of 0.2 ~ 1 mg/mL in 25 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). The data were collected at RT between the wavelength range of 190–260 nm using a 1-nm quartz cuvette with a sample volume of 300 µL. The spectral data were acquired using a 1-nm bandwidth, with an allocation of 1 s acquisition per data point. The analysis was repeated three times and the spectra were averaged. Secondary structures were analyzed using CDPro software and the CDSSTR algorithm.

Dynamic light scattering

The sample was prepared at a concentration of 100 µg/mL gold in deionized water. For particle size analysis, 1 mL of the sample was placed in a quartz cuvette (163390, Anton Paar, Graz, Austria) and measured using dynamic light scattering (Litesizer 500, Anton Paar, Graz, Austria). Zeta potential measurements were conducted with a 0.5 mL of the sample in an Omega cuvette (225288, Anton Paar, Graz, Austria) for zeta potential analysis. Each parameter was measured three times and the average value was analyzed.

Nanocorona quantification

The amount of AuS in the immune corona was determined by measuring absorbance using UV–vis spectrophotometry (Libra S50, Biochrom, Cambridge, UK). The Au concentration in the synthesized nanocorona was determined using a calibration curve generated from a standard solution measured at a wavelength of 780 nm. The protein conjugated to AuS in the nanocorona was quantified using the Bradford reagent (B6916, Merck, Rahway, NJ), and a calibration curve was constructed with a standard protein solution, followed by a second curve with a standard solution containing 0.01 µg/mL of AuS. The protein concentration in the nanocorona was measured at 595 nm by using an established calibration curve. Infrared camera

To investigate the photothermal temperature stability of AuS-IgG, the solution (Au: 2.5 mg/mL protein 500 µg/mL) was irradiated with a 1 kW laser at 808 nm. Temperature was measured every minute using an infrared camera (TG165, Teledyne FLIR LLC, Wilsonville, OR).

Electron microscopy

The morphologies, crystallinities, and EDS mapping of the AuS-PEG, AuS-IgG, and AuS-HSA complexes were analyzed using TEM, high-resolution TEM, elemental mapping, and high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM imaging, conducted using an TEM (FEI Tecnai G2 F30 S-TWIN, FEI-Philips, Hillsboro, OR) operated at 300 kV. For the TEM measurements, the nanocorona was immersed in deionized water and dried overnight. It was then placed on a carbon-coated copper grid (CF200-Cu, Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA) and imaged by TEM.

Cell culture

The J774A.1 (TIB-67, ATCC, Manassas, VA) and CT26 (CRL-2638, ATCC, Manassas, VA) cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. J774A.1 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (11965092, Gibco, Waltham, MA) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (12483020, Gibco, Waltham, MA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (15140122, Gibco, Waltham, MA) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. CT26 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 (11875093, Gibco, Waltham, MA) medium under identical conditions. To investigate the various macrophage phenotypes, M1 polarization was induced by stimulating J774A.1 cells with 50 ng/mL LPS (L5293, Sigma, St. Louis, MO), and M2 polarization was induced using 20 ng/mL IL-4 (214-14-20UG, Gibco, Waltham, MA) [73]. The BMDM were collected from BALB/c mice as previously described [74]. Briefly, muscles located near the lower limb base were removed from the sacrificed mice and the hip joint was subsequently exposed. The femur was isolated by cutting the bases of the femur and knee joints. Bone marrow cells were collected by flushing with 2 mL of PBS using a 23-G syringe. The BMDMs were resuspended, filtered through a 100 μm cell strainer, and centrifuged to extract only cells. BMDMs were cultured in bone marrow culture medium at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 7 days. Bone marrow culture medium consisted of 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 1 ng/mL of M-CSF-1 (PMC2044, Gibco, Waltham, MA).

Two hours before the experiments, the medium was replaced with an FBS-free bone marrow culture medium. T cells were collected from BALB/c mice according to a previously published procedure [75]. Splenocytes were collected from the spleens of sacrificed BALB/c mice by passing through a cell strainer. RBCs were removed using RBC lysis buffer (555899, BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ) and magnetically associated cell separation (MACS) (19851, Stemcell, Newark, CA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The isolated T cells were resuspended in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 20 mM HEPES (15630, Gibco, Waltham, MA), 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 50 ug/mL Gentamicin (15750-060, Gibco, Waltham, MA), 100 mM sodium pyruvate (11360, Gibco, Waltham, MA), 500 μm 2-mercaptoethanol (21985023, Gibco, Waltham, MA), and 1 mM non-essential amino acid (NEAA, 11140, Gibco, Waltham, MA). T cells were activated using ImmunoCult™ Mouse T Cell Activator Kit (100–1572, Stemcell, Newark, CA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The T cell suspension was transferred to a 24-well plate and cultured at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 2–3 days, to monitor proliferation. Once T cell proliferation was confirmed, 50 µg/mL of interleukin-2 (#78081, Stemcell, Newark, CA) was added to promote further growth. To generate RFP-expressing CT26 (CT26RFP) cells, CT26 cells were seeded in six-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and incubated overnight. The following day, the medium was replaced with a mixture of 1 mL of lentivirus supernatant (1 × 105 viral particles per well) encoding RFP genes (#GlowCell-15p-1, Biosettia, San Diego, CA) and 2 mL of total medium. After 24 h, the virus-containing medium was discarded and the cells were cultured in fresh culture medium for another 72 h. To remove non-transduced cells, the culture medium was supplemented with 1 mg/mL puromycin. After the initial selection of non-transduced cells, the cells were cultured in 0.3 mg/mL puromycin-containing medium for a total of 1 month to ensure reliable selection.

Uptake pathway

To investigate the uptake pathway, AuS-IgG was synthesized and then reacted with 500 µL of Streptavidin-Alexa Fluor™ 488 Conjugate (S11223, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA) for 24 h, followed by removal of unbound Streptavidin-Alexa Fluor™ 488, in accordance with a previously published study [76]. Upon differentiation of J774A.1 cells as described in Sect. 4.7, the uptake pathway was inhibited by selective inhibition of each channel [77]. To selectively inhibit cellular uptake pathways, cells were treated at 37 °C for 30 min with pathway-specific inhibitors: 5-(N-ethyl-N-isopropyl) amiloride (EIPA) (A3085, Merck, Rahway, NJ) at 25 µM (7.49 µg/mL) for macropinocytosis, chlorpromazine (CPZ) (C8138, Merck, Rahway, NJ) at 20 µM (6.38 µg/mL) for clathrin-mediated endocytosis, and genistein (GEN) (G6649, Merck, Rahway, NJ) at 200 µM (54.05 µg/mL) for caveolin-mediated endocytosis. Thereafter, the cells were treated with 500 ng/mL Alexa488-AuS-IgG based on the protein concentration. After 2 h of incubation, the cells were fixed with 4% PFA (P2031, Biosesang, Yongin, South Korea) for 30 min at RT. The samples were washed three times with PBS and stained with a 1:1000 dilution of DAPI (D3571, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA) from a 1 mg/mL solution. A mounting medium containing phalloidin (H-1600-10, Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA) was used, and the samples were analyzed using EVOS 7000 (evos7000, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA), Celleste 6.0, (AMEO4942, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA), and FACs (LSR2, BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ).

qRT-PCR

For in vitro analysis, J774A.1 cells were treated with free proteins (IgG), PEGylated AuS, and nanocorona complexes (AuS-IgG and AuS-HSA), incubated for 2 h, and subsequently stimulated with 50 ng/mL LPS for 12 h. For the in vivo study, mice were treated when the tumor volume reached 50 mm3. Tumors were injected with the drug at a dose of 5 mg/kg (protein basis), and anti-PD-L1 (804722F1B, Bio X Cell, Lebanon, NH) was administered intraperitoneally at 5 mg/kg every 2 days, for a total of three injections. The tumor tissue was homogenized using a tissue homogenizer (TH-02, Omni International, Kennesaw, GA). Both the in vitro and in vivo samples were prepared for RNA extraction.

Total RNA was isolated from the cells and spleen tissues using QIAzol lysis reagent (79306, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and purified using the RNeasy Mini Kit (74104, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Following DNase I treatment, cDNA was synthesized from 1,000 ng of RNA using an iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (1708890 Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). Reverse transcription conditions were set at 45 °C for 60 min, followed by denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min. For qPCR, each reaction mixture contained 2 µL of cDNA, 1 µL of each forward and reverse primer (0.4 µM), 12.5 µL of iQ SYBR Green supermix, and 9.5 µL of dH2O, for a final volume of 25 µL per tube. The primer sequences used for qPCR are listed in Table S3 and S4. Gene expression was calculated using the comparative Ct method (2 − ΔCt), normalized to glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) levels, and analyzed with a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (CFX384, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA).

RNA sequencing

RNA was extracted as previously described, and its quality was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with an RNA 6000 Nanochip (Agilent Technologies, Amstelveen, Netherlands). The RNA was quantified using an ND 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). Samples, including LPS-stimulated J774A.1, AuS, IgG, AuS-IgG (Conjugation), and AuS + IgG (mixture), were prepared for sequencing using the QuantSeq 3’ mRNA-Seq Library Prep Kit (Lexogen, Inc., Wien, Austria) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. High-throughput sequencing was performed using the NextSeq 500 platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA) with single-end 75 bp reads. Differentially expressed genes were identified by a two-fold or greater change in expression levels. Gene enrichment calculations were performed using GSEA at the Broad Institute (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp). Reads obtained from QuantSeq 3’ mRNA-Seq were aligned using Bowtie2, with indices generated from genome assembly or representative transcript sequences for both genome and transcriptome alignment [78]. Transcriptome assembly, abundance estimation, and differential gene expression were determined from the alignment files using BedTools [79]. The quantile normalization method was applied to the read count data using EdgeR (R Development Core Team, 2016) in R, along with the Bioconductor software [80]. Gene classifications were identified using the DAVID (http://david.abcc.ncifcrf.gov/) and MEDLINE (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) databases, and data analysis and visualization were conducted using ExDEGA (eBiogen Inc., Seoul, South Korea).

MTT assay

J774A.1 cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2, and seeded at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well in 96-well plates. The cells were irradiated in a time-dependent manner using a 1 kW near-infrared (NIR) laser (808 nm). After a 24-h incubation, the cells were treated with 100 µL of MTT solution (0793-5G, Ameresco, Framingham, MA). Following 2 h incubation with MTT, 100 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (D2680-010, GenDEPOT, Baker, TX) was added to dissolve intracellular formazan crystals. The absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader (UVM 340, Biochrom, Cambridge, UK), and the signal intensity was expressed as a percentage relative to the control.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

CT26 cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cells were seeded at a density of 3 × 10⁴ cells per well in a 24-well plate. The cells were then irradiated with a 1-kW NIR laser (808 nm) for 5 min. Each well was divided into five equal sections and the laser was directed at each section for 5 min to ensure uniform exposure across the entire well. After irradiation, the medium was collected, and the cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 500 g for 10 min at 4 °C. HMGB-1 levels in the supernatant were quantified using an ELISA kit (NBP2-62767, Bio-Techne, Minneapolis, MN) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Evaluation of T cell anticancer efficacy

A six-well transwell plate (CLS3451, Corning, Corning, NY) with a 0.4-µm polyester membrane was used for the co-culture experiment. BMDM cells were seeded on the basolateral side at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL in 1 mL of RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. CT26 cells were seeded in the apical compartment of a transwell plate at a density of 5 × 105 cells/mL. After 24 h of co-culture, the apical compartment was treated with AuS, IgG, or AuS-IgG at a protein concentration of 500 µg/mL(Au: 2.5 mg/ml) for 6 h to the upper chamber.

Following treatment, the CT26 cells on the apical side were exposed to NIR laser irradiation for 3 min to induce PTT. After laser exposure, the transwell insert containing the CT26 cells was removed. CT26RFP cells and activated T cells were then added to the basolateral compartment where the BMDM were seeded. The co-culture was maintained for 48 h. To assess tumor cell apoptosis, cells from the basolateral compartment were harvested after 48 h and stained with Annexin V-Pacific blue (A35122, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA). Flow cytometry analysis was performed using an LSR II, and the data were processed using FlowJo software.

Animal test

BALB/c mice (female, 4–6 weeks old, and 16–20 g) were obtained from Orient Bio (Seoul, South Korea). Upon shipment, the mice were housed in a temperature- and humidity-controlled, specific pathogen-free environment with a 12 h light/dark cycle (lights on at 6:30 AM) for a period of 7–14 days. During this period, mice were allowed to acclimatize. The Gachon University Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals was followed for all animal experiments (LCDI-2021-0054). The CT-26 tumor-bearing mice were randomized when the tumor volume reached 50 mm3. Mice were intratumorally injected with free IgG, AuS-IgG (at a protein concentration of 5 mg/kg), and AuS (at a Au concentration of 25 mg/kg). An anti-PDL1 was administered via intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 5 mg/kg. Injections were administered every 2 days, and the tumor size was measured with calipers every 2 days. NIR laser irradiation was performed for 3 minutes on the day after the third and fourth injections. The mice were sacrificed on day 14.

In the tumor recurrence model, mice were administered 1 × 106 cells, 1.5 cm from the initial tumor injection site on day 10. The tumor volume was measured at regular intervals, and the mice were sacrificed on day 30.

Flow cytometry analysis of innate and adaptive immune cells

This procedure was adapted from a previously published study to analyze the phenotype of immune cells induced by the nanocorona [16]. Briefly, the spleen membrane was removed from sacrificed mice using a strainer (CLS352350, Corning, Corning, NY). Red blood cells were lysed with RBC Lysis Buffer (00–4333-57, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA), and the remaining cells were suspended in FACS Stain Buffer (554656, BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ). Cells were then incubated with anti-mouse CD16/CD32 (2.4G2), (553141, BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ) for 15 min at 4 °C to block the Fc receptor. Following blocking, cells were stained for 30 min at 4 °C with specific antibodies against different immune cell types. For the M2 macrophages, intracellular staining was performed (554714; BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ). The antibodies used were as follows: anti-CD335 for NK cells (12–3351-82, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA), anti-F4/80 for macrophages (11–4801-85, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA), anti-CD86 for M1 phenotype (12–0869-42, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA), anti-CD206 for M2 phenotype (565250, BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ), anti-CD3e for T cells (11–0031–85, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA), anti-CD4 for CD4+ T cells (100432, BioLegend, California USA, 120041-82, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA), anti-CD8a for CD8+ T cells (45–0081–82, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA), anti-CD62L for Tnaive cells (104435, BioLegend, San Diego, CA), anti-CD69 Teff cells (561240, BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ), and anti-FOXP3 for Treg cells (560401, BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ). Flow cytometry analysis was performed using an LSR II, and the data were processed using FlowJo software.

Histology

To assess the histological characteristics of the tumors, the tissues were paraffin-fixed, sectioned at 4 μm, and stained with H&E for histopathological analysis. Cell death was assessed using a DeadEnd fluorometric TUNEL system (G3250, Promega, Fitchburg, WI). Immunofluorescence (IF) staining was performed for immune cell localization using the following antibodies anti-CD3e: T cells (ab110899, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-CD8 for CD8+ T cells (ab217344, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-FOXP3 for Treg cells (ab215206, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), and anti-CD31 for vascular endothelial cells (ab7388, Abcam, Cambridge, UK).

Toxicological evaluation

After sacrificing the mice, blood samples were collected from the orbital veins. Blood samples were placed in labeled vials containing heparin (5 units/mL) and transferred to a refrigerator at 4 °C for hematologic analysis of peripheral circulating blood cells. In the case of serum, blood was coagulated at RT and centrifuged at 3,000 × g in RT for 10 min to separate the supernatant. The levels of ALT, AST, Crea, and BUN were measured using a blood biochemical analyzer (BS220, Mindray, Shenzen, china).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software (GraphPad Software). After analyzing the treatment effects using one-way analysis of variance, Tukey’s multiple comparison test was performed.
