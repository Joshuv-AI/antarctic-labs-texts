Genomic signatures of subterranean lifestyle and ecological adaptation in Myospalax
Authors: Na Wan, Jiaqi Chen, Muli Jiang, Mengzhuo Wu, Haolin Li, Mozhi Wen, Wei Shen, Xiaolong Liang, Xiaojie Yang, Yijing Liu, Yige Gao, Eviatar Nevo, Kexin Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMammals exhibit diverse adaptations to varied habitats, yet the genomic mechanisms underlying these transitions remain incompletely understood. Here, we present a comparative genomic, epigenomic, and three-dimensional chromatin analysis of two closely related zokor species, Myospalax aspalax and Myospalax psilurus, which inhabit arid and humid subterranean environments, respectively.ResultsWe identified subterranean lineage-specific positively selected and rapidly evolving genes enriched for DNA repair, hypoxia response, blood vessel development, and fructose metabolism. M. psilurus showed a notable loss of olfactory receptor genes, expansion of pheromone-related gene families, and widespread activation of a specific ERVK transposable element family, accompanied by elevated DNA methylation, suggesting enhanced epigenetic defenses and ecological specialization. In contrast, M. aspalax showed selection in kidney and lipid metabolism genes. Large chromosomal inversions (> 1 Mb) overlapped islands of high genetic divergence (high-FST or high Dxy) and were enriched for sensory, reproductive, and DNA repair genes. Although chromatin compartments remained largely stable, large inversions disrupted topologically associating domains and preferentially occurred in regions characterized by longer chromatin loops. Inversion breakpoints were flanked by inverted repeats and segmental duplications—features characteristic of the formation mechanism of non-allelic homologous recombination, suggesting that both the spatial proximity and sequence architecture of these regions may predispose them to large inversions.ConclusionsOverall, our results illuminate the structural, regulatory, and evolutionary mechanisms underlying ecological divergence and highlight how genome architecture contributes to adaptive evolution in subterranean mammals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12915-025-02431-4.Keywords: Subterranean, Hygrothermal, Transposable element, DNA methylation, Large inversion, Chromatin contacts, Inverted repeats, Non-allelic homologous recombination

Main text

Background

Life on Earth is characterized by an astonishing diversity of adaptations, enabling organisms to thrive in a myriad of ecological niches [1]. The colonization of novel environments invariably imposes unique selective pressures, driving rapid evolutionary changes at morphological, physiological, and molecular levels [2–5]. One striking example is the transition of several mammalian lineages from surface-dwelling to subterranean lifestyles [6–9]. This shift poses several challenges, including perpetual darkness [10], hypoxia [11], and limited food availability [12]. Similarly, transitions between distinct climatic zones, such as from arid to humid regions [13, 14], necessitate profound physiological and metabolic adjustments to maintain homeostasis in the face of varying water availability and temperature fluctuations [15]. Understanding the genomic underpinnings of these diverse adaptations is paramount to unraveling the fundamental principles of evolutionary biology and how life persists in ecologically challenging settings.

Upon colonizing a novel environment, populations must undergo coordinated transformations to persist and reproduce under new selective pressures [16]. Over successive generations, individuals harboring phenotypic or genotypic variants that confer higher fitness in the new habitat tend to increase in frequency through natural selection, while maladaptive variants are progressively eliminated [17]. These adaptive responses may arise through the emergence of novel genes [18] or regulatory elements derived from transposable elements (TEs), or through point mutations that fine-tune protein function [19], while large sequence mutations, including structural variants (SVs) [20] and TEs [21], can dramatically alter gene regulation by affecting gene dosage, disrupting or establishing regulatory elements, or reconfiguring chromatin landscapes [22], influencing adaptation. In particular, large inversions contribute to genome evolution and local adaptation by suppressing recombination [23, 24]. Their breakpoints are frequently embedded within large (> 1 kb), highly identical segmental duplications (SDs) [25] or inverted repeats (IRs), with the IRs specifically promoting inversion formation via non-allelic homologous recombination (NAHR) [26]. Additionally, large inversions have been shown to reshape the three-dimensional (3D) architecture of chromosomes, inducing chromatin compartment switches [2], reorganizing topologically associating domains (TADs) [27], altering chromatin loops [28]. Advances in high-throughput sequencing and integrative genomic technologies now enable comprehensive detection of these multilayered genomic changes, offering unprecedented insights into the molecular foundations of ecological adaptation and the emergence of reproductive barriers during speciation [29].

Zokors (genus Myospalax) are subterranean rodents [30] endemic to the semiarid and semi-humid steppes of China. Within this genus, Myospalax aspalax and Myospalax psilurus are closely related species that exhibit distinct geographical distributions and ecological preferences, despite partial habitat overlap in northeastern China (Fig. 2A) [31]. M. aspalax inhabits semi-dry prairies under a temperate continental climate with 200 ~ 400 mm annual precipitation, while M. psilurus occupies semi-humid forest-steppe regions with a temperate monsoon climate and 400 ~ 800 mm precipitation (Fig. 1B) [32]. These climatic differences are directly linked to their distributions across China’s second (1000 ~ 2000 m) and third (500 ~ 1000 m) topographic steps, separated by the Greater Khingan Mountains, which block the southeast monsoon from reaching the western part of the second step (Fig. 1B) [33]. This environmental gradient, contrasting aridity and humidity, has shaped the genomic architecture and adaptive paths of these two species, making them an excellent model for studying genetic adaptation to contrasting environments.

In this study, we present a comprehensive comparative genomic analysis of M. aspalax and M. psilurus to address two central questions: (1) what are the genomic signatures that underlie the evolution of subterranean traits in Myospalax species, and (2) how have distinct ecological pressures, particularly differences in precipitation, shaped species-specific genetic divergence and adaptive strategies between M. aspalax and M. psilurus? To address these questions, we first applied comparative genomics to explore the molecular basis of underground adaptation, as well as the distinct adaptations of M. aspalax and M. psilurus to arid and humid environments. We then integrated population resequencing, genomes, and Hi-C data to assess genetic divergence, compare transposable element landscapes, identify large chromosomal inversions, and evaluate their impact on 3D genome architecture and their association with highly differentiated genomic islands. Finally, we investigated the breakpoint features and formation mechanisms of these inversions.

Results

Genome evolution revealing subterranean adaptation

To elucidate the evolutionary history and adaptive mechanisms within subterranean lineages, we constructed a phylogenetic tree based on 1,082,849 concatenated four-fold degenerate sites derived from 5178 high-confidence orthologous genes across 12 rodent species (Fig. 1B, Additional file 2: Table S1). Based on this tree, we applied the branch-site model to the 5178 orthologous genes to detect positive selection at specific amino acid sites, identifying a total of 436 positively selected genes (PSGs) taking all subterranean lineages as the foreground branch. These genes showed significant enrichment in functions related to DNA repair, blood vessel development, hemopoiesis, immune system maturation, glucose metabolism, and glycolysis (Additional file 1: Fig. S1A), all of which are critical for adaptation to subterranean environments. Within the DNA repair pathway, several key canonical genes were highlighted: ataxia telangiectasia mutated (Atm), ATR-interacting protein (Atrip), and minichromosome maintenance protein 2 (Mcm2). In the blood vessel development and hemopoiesis pathways, a number of hallmark genes were also identified, such as bone morphogenetic protein receptor type II (Bmpr2), NADPH oxidase 1 (Nox1), and erythropoietin receptor (Epor).

Moreover, we used the branch model and identified 702 rapidly evolving genes (REGs) in subterranean lineage, and they were enriched in visual perception, fructose metabolism, skeletal muscle organ development, germ cell development, cardiac muscle morphogenesis, heart contraction, and pathways of neurodegeneration (Additional file 1: Fig. S1B). Numerous key genes associated with lens and retina development(Gnat1, Gnat2, Gngt1, Rs1, Pde6a, Pde6g, Cryba2, Crybb2, Crybb3, and Crygs) and genes involved in fructose metabolism (Aldob [34], Akr1b1 [35], Sord [36] and Glyctk [37]) both showed signs of accelerated evolution. Additionally, the distribution of K2P values across all orthologous genes revealed significantly higher values in above-ground species compared to subterranean species (Fig. 1C), which is confirmed by the significantly lower rate of synonymous substitutions per site per generation observed in subterranean species (Additional file 1: Fig. S1C), suggesting potential evolutionary constraints or selective pressures associated with subterranean adaptation.

Beyond protein-coding genes, we identified 6548 rodent-specific conserved elements longer than 20 bp, with over 80% located in intergenic and intronic regions (Additional file 1: Fig. S1D). These elements had an average length of 28.5 bp, and only 15 exceeded 100 bp (Additional file 1: Fig. S1E). In addition, we detected 172 subterranean-specific accelerated conserved non-coding elements, which are positioned significantly closer to genes than expected by chance (Additional file 1: Fig. S1F, p-value = 0.000999). Genes associated with these conserved non-coding elements (CNEs) were significantly enriched in pathways related to blood circulation, neurogenetics, cancer-related pathways and regulation of cell cycle (Additional file 1: Fig. S1G). Notably, we identified a 29-bp CNE located 1.8 kb upstream of Wnt family member 7B (Wnt7b) (Fig. 1D), a gene known to be involved in the regulation of angiogenesis and programmed cell death of vascular endothelial cells [38]. Another significant finding was a 92-bp CNE located 3 kb upstream of hyaluronidase 1 (Hyal1), a gene involved in tumor suppression and resistance through regulation of cell proliferation and apoptosis [39] (Fig. 1E).

Genomic signatures of divergent adaptation in M. aspalax and M. psilurus

To investigate the genomic divergence and species-specific adaptations between M. aspalax and M. psilurus, we first estimated their divergence time based on the same phylogenetic tree constructed from four-fold degenerate sites. The results suggest a divergence time of approximately 2.94 million years ago (Mya) (95% CI, 1.8 ~ 4.6 Mya) (Fig. 1B), which was close to the split time of ~ 2.8 Mya (95% CI, 2.37–4.59 Mya) inferred by ∂a∂i pipeline in our previous study [31]. To investigate the genetic basis of local environmental adaptation, we identified 373 candidate REGs and 102 PSGs with M. aspalax designated as the foreground branch, and 331 candidate REGs and 84 PSGs with M. psilurus as the foreground branch, respectively (Fig. 1B). Several rapidly evolving genes in M. aspalax are involved in kidney development, like laminin beta2 (Lamb2) and platelet-derived growth factor receptor beta (Pdgfrb), which may contribute to enhanced water retention in arid habitats (Fig. 2B). Among the PSGs, a notable enrichment was observed in genes linked to lipid metabolism, such as insulin receptor substrate-1 (Irs-1) and SREBP-cleavage activating protein (Scap), suggesting adaptations to nutrient limitation in dry environments. In contrast, M. psilurus, which occupies more humid habitats, exhibited 331 candidate REGs and 84 PSGs enriched in pathways related to renal water balance mediated by aquaporins, the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, taste perception, DNA damage response, and germ cell development (Fig. 2B). Among them, taste receptor type 2 member 1 (Tas2r1), a core gene in bitter taste transduction [12], was detected to undergo rapid evolution; DNA (cytosine-5)-methyltransferase 3 alpha (Dnmt3a), encoding a DNA methyltransferase, is involved in DNA methylation [40] and is subject to positive selection in M. psilurus.

Genetic divergence of M. aspalax and M. psilurus

To further characterize the genetic differentiation and population structure between M. aspalax and M. psilurus, we performed a comprehensive population genomic analysis. A neighbor-joining phylogenetic tree constructed based on single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), clearly resolved all 12 M. aspalax individuals and all 14 M. psilurus individuals into two well-supported, species-specific monophyletic clades (Fig. 2C), indicating pronounced genome-wide divergence. This phylogenetic evidence was corroborated by principal component analysis (PCA), where the first two principal components explained 86.11 and 3.69% of the total genomic variance, respectively, revealing strong population structure aligned with species boundaries (Fig. 2D). Measures of genetic diversity provided additional insights into the demographic histories of the two species. Our analysis revealed that M. aspalax harbors higher nucleotide diversity compared to M. psilurus (Fig. 2E), suggesting a historically larger effective population size or less severe population bottlenecks, consistent with previous demographic inferences [31]. Consistently, linkage disequilibrium (LD) decayed more rapidly in M. aspalax, indicative of higher recombination rates or a greater effective population size (Fig. 2F). In parallel, we analyzed copy number variation (CNV) across 26 individuals, encompassing M. aspalax and M. psilurus. We identified 1044 genes exhibiting significant differences in copy number between the two species. Specifically, M. psilurus showed 587 significant gene gains and 457 gene losses relative to M. aspalax (Fig. 2G). Notably, a total of 22 olfactory receptor genes exhibited significant copy number loss in M. psilurus (Fig. 2G), whereas only 10 genes showed significant copy number gain (Fig. 2H). Interestingly, the Ora1 gene, which is involved in the detection of pheromones related to reproduction [42], displayed the most pronounced copy number variation in M. psilurus. This highlights a potential role for chemosensory genes in reproductive isolation and species recognition. Furthermore, 28 olfactory and 2 taste receptor genes (Tas2r134 and Tas2r40 genes) exhibited significantly greater genetic differentiation between M. aspalax and M. psilurus compared to genome-wide background levels (Fig. 2I, t-test: p-value < 2.2e − 16), implicating these sensory pathways as key targets of ecological adaptation and drivers of species divergence.

TE composition and expression divergence in M. aspalax and M. psilurus

To investigate the genomic underpinnings of ecological adaptation between M. aspalax and M. psilurus, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of TE composition, expression profiles, structural features, and epigenetic regulation. M. aspalax exhibited a slightly higher proportion of LINE elements (21.69%), SINE transposons (14.63%), and LTR elements (10.31%) relative to M. psilurus (19.23%, 12.68% and 9.31% respectively) (Fig. 3A). Transcriptomic profiling of heart, lung, liver, kidney, and flesh tissues showed that numerous TE families are differentially expressed between species (Fig. 3B and Additional file 1: Fig. S2A-D). Despite lower overall TE content, M. psilurus exhibited extensively higher transcriptional activation than M. aspalax (Additional file 1: Fig. S2E). Notably, two ERVK-derived families, MP_RND-5_FAMILY-237 and MP_RND-1_FAMILY-133, consistently ranked among the top 10 upregulated TEs across all five surveyed tissues-heart, lung, liver, kidney, and muscle (Fig. 3B and Additional file 1: Fig. S2A-D), indicating broad and coordinated expression. Genes located within 2 kb of the most highly upregulated TE families in M. psilurus displayed higher inter-tissue overlap compared to genes near downregulated TEs. Specifically, a greater proportion of upregulated TE-linked genes were shared across ≥ 2, ≥ 3, ≥ 4, and all 5 tissues, suggesting that these genes are more consistently co-regulated across tissues and may play broader roles in the species’ environmental adaptation (Fig. 3C). Enrichment analysis of these genes revealed significant involvement in processes such as renal water homeostasis via aquaporins, ferroptosis, kidney development, mitochondrial DNA metabolism, and immune responses (Fig. 3D), implicating TEs in transcriptional networks relevant to adaptation in humid environments. Of the two top shared upregulated families, only MP_RND-5_FAMILY-237 retained intact group-specific antigen (gag), protease (pro), and polymerase (pol) domains, along with canonical long terminal repeats (LTRs) at both ends, indicating potential for retrotranspositional activity [43] (Fig. 3E, Additional file 1: Figs. S2F and S2G). Copies of this family were significantly longer in M. psilurus than in M. aspalax (Fig. 3F), suggesting a more recent amplification, as reflected by its smaller Kimura distance in M. psilurus (4.33% vs. 27.94%). Interestingly, a 6482-bp full-length insertion of MP_RND-5_FAMILY-237 was identified in the third intron of the Morc1 gene, which encodes a known regulator of TE silencing via DNA methylation [44]. M. psilurus consistently exhibited higher levels of DNA methylation compared to M. aspalax (Fig. 3G, Additional file 1: Figs. S3A and S3B). Consistent with TE activation, M. psilurus exhibited globally elevated DNA methylation levels compared to M. aspalax, particularly around transcription start sites (Additional file 1: Fig. S3C), across major TE classes (Additional file 1: Fig. S3D), and specifically at MP_RND-5_FAMILY-237 loci (Fig. 3G). This suggests a more robust epigenetic defense system in M. psilurus, potentially as an adaptive response to increased viral or exogenous element exposure in humid environments.

Large inversions overlapping with highly diverged islands

Comparative genome analysis identified 24,755 high-confidence SVs (> 500 bp) differentiating the two species, including 5253 insertions, 5206 deletions, 1976 duplications, 545 inversions, and 734 translocations, along with a set of complex rearrangements (Additional file 1: Fig. S4). Among them, we detected 40 large balanced inversions exceeding 1 Mb in length, of 12 of these spanning more than 10 Mb (Fig. 4A). To further investigate the evolutionary significance of the identified large chromosomal inversions, we examined their overlap with genomic regions exhibiting high genetic differentiation between M. aspalax and M. psilurus. These regions, often referred to as “differentiation islands”, are characterized by elevated FST (relative divergence) and Dxy (absolute divergence) values, indicating reduced gene flow and increased genetic divergence. We found that large chromosomal inversions significantly overlapped with high-FST islands (top 5%) (32/40, odds ratio = 1.11, p-value = 3.9 × 10⁻⁶). A similar pattern was observed for high-Dxy islands (top 5%) (32/40, odds ratio = 1.11, p-value = 4.1 × 10⁻⁶). In total, 32 large inversions intersected with both high-FST and high-Dxy islands (Additional file 2: Table S2), indicating that large inversions may serve as barriers to gene flow and thereby contribute to reproductive isolation. Genes located within these highly differentiated inversions were significantly enriched in biological processes related to spermatogenesis, male gamete generation, male genitalia development, single fertilization, monocarboxylic acid metabolic process, carbohydrate catabolic process, histone modification, chromatin organization, and regulation of phosphorylation (Fig. 4B). Notably, several genes within specific inversions (INV2108, INV2128, and INV2515) were enriched in pathways associated with nucleotide excision repair, DNA damage checkpoint signaling, and double-strand break repair. These genes include Ercc1, Lig1, Xrcc1, Trex1, Nek11, Topbp1, Mcm4, and Ube2v2. The enrichment of DNA repair-related genes within these inversions suggests a potential link between large inversions and genome instability. Additionally, genes such as Rtp1 and Rtp2 within INV2296 are involved in the sensory perception of bitter taste.

Impacts of large inversions on 3D chromatin structure

We next elucidated the effect of large inversions on 3D chromatin structure. We generated high-resolution Hi-C contact maps for both M. aspalax and M. psilurus, obtaining 409,605,081 and 493,301,206 validated Hi-C contacts, respectively. At a 100 kb resolution, we inferred ~ 40.79% and ~ 44.06% bins across the whole genome as compartment A bins in M. aspalax and M. psilurus genome, respectively. Consistent with established principles of chromatin organization, compartment A shows positive correlation with Guanine Cytosine content (M. aspalax: Spearman’s r = 0.5, p-value < 2.20e − 16, M. psilurus: Spearman’s r = 0.48, p-value < 2.20e − 16) (Additional file 1: Figs. S5A and S5B). We found that about 90% of background genome and large inversion regions in M. aspalax have the same compartment type as corresponding homologous regions in M. psilurus, indicating large inversions have little impact on chromatin compartment status (Fig. 4C).

Based on a finer resolution of 40 kb, we investigated TAD features (body and boundary) conservation between M. aspalax and M. psilurus. Genomic coordinates of 87.7% of TAD bodies and 89.1% of TAD boundaries were successfully lifted over between two species. For annotated TADs of M. aspalax, we found 52.2% TAD bodies and 42.3% TAD boundaries were conserved with M. psilurus, which covered 49.6% of M. aspalax genome. After examining corresponding TAD features of large inversion region, we found that the number of shared TAD bodies and TAD boundaries is lower in large inversions than in the whole genome, which suggests large inversions may contribute to TAD rearrangement (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, we explored the loop features conservation between M. aspalax and M. psilurus based on a resolution of 20 kb. In M. aspalax, 35.2% of loops are inter-chromosome and 64.8% of loops are intra-chromosomal, with most loops (42%) anchored between non-gene regions (Additional file 1: Figs. S5C and S5D). Conversely, M. psilurus shows a higher proportion of intra-chromosomal loops at 88.2% and only 11.8% are inter-chromosome loops (Additional file 1: Figs. S5C and S5D). The length of loops within large inversions was longer than that of loops in background regions, suggesting that 3D chromatin proximity may facilitate inversion formation (Fig. 4E).

Mechanisms and constraints of large inversions

Of all large inversions, six inversions were located near chromosome termini (< 1 Mb from the end), and 10 had predicted breakpoints within 1.5 Mb of putative interstitial telomeric sequences (Fig. 4A). This preferential localization near telomeric regions suggests that these areas might be hotspots for chromosomal rearrangements, possibly due to their repetitive nature. To investigate the formation mechanisms of large balanced inversions, we validated their breakpoints using PacBio HiFi subreads and the contig-level genome assembly. Among 80 predicted breakpoints, 92.5% (74/80) were confirmed using HiFi reads (Additional file 1: Fig. S6), and 63.75% (51/80) were validated with the contig-level genome (Additional file 1: Fig. S7). To further investigate the structural and functional characteristics of large chromosomal inversions, we investigated IRs and SDs near breakpoints. For IRs, we only focused on the 38 breakpoints from 19 large inversions that were validated by both methods. A consistent feature observed across all 19 large inversions was the presence of flanking IRs within 500 kb of both breakpoints (Fig. 5A, left and Additional file 1: Fig. S8). These repeats displayed a skewed length distribution, with most ranging from 300 bp to 320 kb (Fig. 5A, right), consistent with a potential role for NAHR in mediating inversion formation. A representative example from chromosome 7 illustrated the presence of nearly symmetric inverted repeat sequences flanking both breakpoints within a 500-kb window (Fig. 5B). For SDs, we focused on 77 inversion breakpoints validated by at least one method. SDs were significantly enriched near inversion breakpoints compared to 10,000 random 1-Mb genomic regions (Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, p-value < 2.22e − 16) (Fig. 5C), as further exemplified by a region on chromosome 3 (Fig. 5D). These results suggest that the formation of large inversions may be facilitated by repeat-mediated mechanisms such as NAHR. SNPs analysis revealed a relatively low proportion of loss-of-function (LoF) variants within inversion regions (Fig. 5E), suggesting purifying selection may actively eliminate highly deleterious mutations within these structurally constrained regions. Moreover, the obvious enrichment of inversion breakpoints at the 0 Mb position relative to TAD boundaries indicates that large inversions preferentially occur at or near TAD boundaries (Fig. 5F). Together, these results indicate that while large inversions are frequently flanked by structural features that may promote their formation, such as IRs and SDs, they tend to avoid carrying a high deleterious mutation load and disrupting intact TADs, potentially facilitating their maintenance in natural populations.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive genomic framework for understanding the adaptive evolution of subterranean rodents, with a special focus on the divergent adaptations of M. aspalax and M. psilurus to distinct hygrothermal environments. By integrating comparative genomics, epigenomics, and 3D genome architecture, we uncover multilayered genomic changes, ranging from gene sequence evolution and regulatory element dynamics to structural variation, that collectively shape adaptation to extreme underground and hygrothermal environments. Our work provides new insights into how selection, demography, and genome architecture interact to facilitate evolutionary transitions in mammals.

The subterranean lineage showed strong signatures of positive selection and accelerated evolution in genes related to DNA repair, erythropoiesis, angiogenesis, metabolism, immune system development, germ cell development, cardiac contraction, and neurodegeneration-associated pathways, highlighting the critical challenges of life in hypoxic subterranean environments [45]. Canonical DNA damage response genes such as Atm, Atrip, and Mcm2 showed evidence of coordinated evolution, potentially enhancing genome stability under chronic oxidative stress. Atm and Atr function as primary sensors of DNA damage, triggering repair signaling pathways [46]. Atrip may modulate Atr activity, contributing to checkpoint control [47], while Mcm2 appears to maintain replication fork stability and facilitate DNA repair downstream of Atr [48]. This integrated system may represent an adaptive mechanism for maintaining genomic integrity under chronic oxidative stress associated with hypoxic subterranean environments. Simultaneously, adaptive evolution in Bmpr2, Nox1, and Epor suggests a molecular basis for vascular remodeling and oxygen transport, both of which are critical under oxygen-limited conditions. Bmpr2 is involved in maintaining vascular homeostasis by regulating pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cell proliferation and apoptosis [49]; Nox1 promotes angiogenesis, thereby enhancing oxygen delivery capacity [50], while Epor facilitates erythropoiesis and increases red blood cell size to improve oxygen transport under hypoxic stress [51] Several PSGs identified in this study, including Epor [52], Nitric Oxide Synthase 1 (Nos1) [2], Period Circadian Regulator 2 (Per2) [53], and Heat Shock Protein 90 Alpha Family Class B Member 1 (Hsp90ab1) [54], have previously been shown to play critical roles in subterranean Spalacidae species, providing functional validation for our findings. In addition, Eospalax cansus utilizes both glucose- and fructose-driven glycolysis to sustain energy production under hypoxic stress [55]. Blind mole rats maintain diverse naive T cells and innate-like B cells, representing an energy-efficient immune strategy in resource-limited underground habitats [56]. Eospalax baileyi exhibits seasonal testicular regression leading to spermatogenic arrest [57] and cardiac hypertrophy that enhances blood transport efficiency [58], whereas in the Damaraland mole rat, visual and auditory functions have degenerated [59]. Collectively, these findings support the enrichment of pathways related to glucose metabolism, glycolysis, immune system maturation, germ cell development, cardiac muscle morphogenesis, cardiac contraction, and neurodegeneration in subterranean lineage. Crucially, our analysis extends beyond protein-coding sequences to CNEs, revealing accelerated evolution in regulatory regions near genes involved in angiogenesis, neurodevelopment, and cancer-related pathways [39]. This underscores the importance of regulatory evolution in shaping adaptive phenotypes and provides a more nuanced view of the molecular innovations supporting subterranean life.

Within the Myospalax genus, M. aspalax and M. psilurus exemplify divergent adaptive strategies in response to contrasting hygrothermal environments [32]. In the arid-adapted M. aspalax, selection has favored genes involved in kidney development and lipid metabolism, consistent with enhanced water retention and energy efficiency. For example, Lamb2 is a vital component of the glomerular basement membrane, essential for maintaining the glomerular filtration barrier [60]. Likewise, Pdgfrb-deficient mice lack mesangial cell recruitment and fail to form mature glomeruli [61]. In terms of lipid metabolism, depletion of Irs-1 reduces basal β-oxidation, a central pathway for fatty acid energy production [62]. Moreover, Scap facilitates the transport of SREBP from the endoplasmic reticulum to the Golgi, where it activates lipid metabolism genes such as the low-density lipoprotein receptor [63]. Conversely, M. psilurus, adapted to humid habitats, exhibits divergence in aquaporin-mediated water regulation, taste receptor pathways (e.g., Tas2r1), and epigenetic machinery (e.g., Dnmt3a), possibly reflecting adaptations to moist environments rich in plant secondary compounds and pathogens [18, 64, 65]. Notable loss of olfactory receptor genes in M. psilurus may reflect reduced olfactory reliance in humid habitats, while in arid environments, enhanced sensitivity to bitter and salty compounds may aid in locating water sources and avoiding toxic plants [66]. A notable copy number gain of the pheromone-related gene Ora1 in M. psilurus suggests that it could influence mating preferences, reinforcing prezygotic barriers and contributing to lineage divergence under distinct ecological conditions [42]. Remarkably, M. psilurus also displays widespread transcriptional activation of specific ERVK-family transposable elements and elevated DNA methylation levels. These epigenomic patterns suggest a reinforced defense against transposon proliferation, potentially shaped by viral pressures in humid conditions [65]. The co-regulation of nearby genes across tissues further implies a broader role for transposable elements in modulating environmental responsiveness and gene networks during adaptation [44]. While further functional validation is needed, these patterns provide insights into how environmental gradients may drive local adaptation and species divergence in subterranean rodents.

The role of large chromosomal inversions in shaping species divergence was particularly striking [67]. Most of the large inversions identified between the two species overlapped with genomic islands of high FST and Dxy values. This strong co-localization provides compelling evidence that these structural variants are not merely random occurrences but are actively involved in potential reproductive barriers [68]. These regions are enriched for genes involved in spermatogenesis, chromatin remodeling, and DNA repair, supporting the hypothesis that inversions act as “supergenes”, capturing adaptive haplotypes and limiting recombination between diverging species [69]. These structural variants may play a pivotal role in both ecological adaptation and prezygotic isolation. The colocalization of large inversions with regions of high genetic differentiation further supports the hypothesis that these structural variants act as barriers to gene flow, promoting divergence between the two species [70]. Regarding seven non-overlapping inversions: they may be relatively young and thus have not yet accumulated sufficient sequence divergence to be detected as highly differentiated [71]; and/or they may be largely homogeneous and permit genetic exchange, thereby reducing their capacity to generate pronounced genetic differentiation. We have revised the text to clarify these points [72]. Our Hi-C-based 3D chromatin analysis reveals that while most large inversions do not disrupt global compartment identity, they do modulate TAD boundaries and chromatin loops, particularly within inversion regions. This suggests that inversions may exert regulatory consequences through altering genome topology without disrupting large-scale chromatin organization [27]. These findings are consistent with recent evidence that large inversions can modulate 3D genome organization and gene regulation without necessarily altering global chromatin compartment status [73].

Our findings provide genomic insights into the mechanisms that drive the formation and evolutionary persistence of large inversions. The enrichment of IRs and SDs near inversion breakpoints (Fig. 5A–D) strongly implicates ectopic recombination-particularly NAHR—as the predominant mechanism of inversion genesis. This is consistent with well-documented NAHR-driven rearrangements observed in human and deer mouse genomes [25, 72]. Furthermore, breakpoints tend to localize near telomeric and centromeric regions, which often harbor an excess of SDs and are less subject to purifying selection compared to inversions occurring in other genomic regions [74]. Importantly, the absence of significant mutational burden (Fig. 5E), combined with avoiding disrupting intact TADs, suggests that purifying selection efficiently eliminated deleterious alleles within these rearrangements [75].

In conclusion, our study provides a comprehensive genomic framework for understanding the adaptive evolution of subterranean rodents, with a special focus on the divergent adaptations of M. aspalax and M. psilurus to distinct hygrothermal environments. We highlight the importance of sequence evolution, transposable elements and structural variation, particularly large chromosomal inversions, in shaping species divergence and adaptation. This research not only contributes significantly to our understanding of Myospalax evolution but also provides broader insights into the genomic basis of adaptation to extreme environments and the role of structural variants in driving evolutionary diversification. Future research could focus on functional validation of candidate genes and structural variants, as well as exploring the epigenetic modifications associated with these adaptations, to further deepen our understanding of these fascinating subterranean mammals.

Conclusions

Our study reveals how genomic, epigenetic, and 3D chromatin features contribute to ecological divergence between two subterranean zokor species. We identify species-specific adaptations in metabolic, sensory, and DNA repair genes, alongside transposable element activation and DNA methylation changes. Notably, over 40 large chromosomal inversions, often overlapping with divergence islands, disrupt TADs and occur in regions with longer chromatin loops. Flanked by inverted repeats and segmental duplications, these inversions likely arose via non-allelic homologous recombination, underscoring the role of genome architecture in subterranean adaptation.

Methods

Whole genome alignments

We used conserved genome synteny methodology to establish a high-confidence orthologous gene set that included sequences from 7 superterranean rodent species (Jaculus jaculus: GCF_020740685.1, Cricetulus griseus: GCF_000223135.1, Mesocricetus auratus: GCF_017639785.1, Peromyscus maniculatus bairdii: GCF_003704035.1, Rattus norvegicus: GCF_015227675.2, Castor canadensis: GCF_001984765.1, Cavia porcellus: GCF_000151735.1) and 5 subterranean rodent species (Rhizomys pruinosus: GCA_009823505.1, Spalax galili: GCF_000622305.1, Eospalax fontanierii: GCA_035773235.1, Myospalax aspalax: GCA_049190835.1 and Myospalax psilurus: GCA_049190895.1). Taking T2T-CHM13 v2.0 as reference, we first built pairwise alignments between human and these 12 query species using lastz pipeline [76] with parameters suitable for placental mammal alignment (K = 2400, L = 2000, Y = 9400, H = 2000). After building chains with axtchain, we applied RepeatFiller to remove any repeat masking from the unaligned regions and detect novel alignments between repetitive regions. Then we used chainCleaner with parameters “-LRfoldThreshold 2.5 -doPairs -LRfoldThresholdPairs = 10 -maxPairDistance = 10,000 -maxSuspectScore = 100,000 -minBrokenChainScore = 75,000” to improve alignment specificity. Pairwise alignment chains were converted into alignment nets using a modified version of chainNet that computes real scores of partial nets. Nets were filtered using NetFilterNonNested.perl with parameters “-doUCSCSynFilter -keepSynNetsWithScore 5000 -keepInvNetsWithScore 5000”. For the Chinese hamster, Prairie deer mouse, American beaver, Guinea pig, Hoary bamboo rat, and Upper Galilee mountains blind mole rat that have genome assemblies with contig N50 < 500 kb, we just required that nets have a score ≥ 100,000 and kept inversion or syntenic nets with scores ≥ 3000 [76]. Finally, we used the filtered nets to build a human-referenced multiple genome alignment with MULTIZ v20190527 [77].

Four-fold degenerate sites tree construction

The high-confidence orthologous genes were defined as gene groups among several related species, which retain the same evolutionary origin rather than being paralogs generated by lineage-specific duplication. They generally exhibit high sequence similarity, similar functions, and are retained as one-to-one orthologs across all species. To identify the high-confidence orthologous genes among all 13 species and to minimize the effects of annotation, sequencing and assembly errors, pseudogenes, non-orthologous alignments, and non-conserved gene structures on subsequent evolutionary rate analyses, a series of rigorous filtering criteria were adopted: (1) CDS containing premature stop codons were excluded; (2) CDS shorter than 150 bp were excluded; (3) genes with Ks values (synonymous substitution per synonymous site) greater than two were excluded. After these filtering steps, a total of 5178 genes were retained and 1,082,849 four-fold degenerate sites were extracted using our in-house Perl scripts and concatenated for constructing a maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree by RAxML v1.2.2 [78] using the default parameters.

Divergence time estimation

To estimate divergence times, we applied the MCMCTree program from the PAML [79] v4.8 package, using a burn-in of 5,000,000 iterations and conducting two independent runs to ensure convergence, which was verified using Tracer v1.4 [80]. To calibrate the divergence time, we used the first fossil time of > 23 Mya when the divergence between Myospalacinae and Rhizomyinae occurred [30], the second one > 22 Mya when Spalacinae split from the Spalacidae [81], and the third one from 42 to 49 Mya when Spalacidae diverged from Muridae [82].

Identification of REGs

To estimate the lineage-specific evolutionary rate for each branch, the codeml program in the PAML package v4.8 [79] with the free-ratio model (model = 1) was run for each ortholog. The branch model in PAML was used, with the null model (model = 0) assuming that all branches have been evolving at the same rate and the alternative model (model = 2) allowing the foreground branch to evolve under a different rate. The tree topology constructed by four-fold degenerate sites above was used as the prior tree topology. A likelihood ratio test (LRT) with df = 1 was used between alternative model and null model for each ortholog gene. Chi-square statistics were calcultaed, and genes with p-value less than 0.05 and a higher ω value for the foreground than the background branches were considered as evolving with a significantly faster rate in the foreground branch. We identified REGs at the ancestral branch of whole subterranean lineage, the ancestral branch of Myospalacinae lineage, the ancestral branch Myospalax lineage, Myospalax aspalax branch and Myospalax psilurus branch. Then we conducted KEGG and GO enrichment for REGs using Metascape (https://metascape.org).

Identification of PSGs

We used the same orthologous genes and tree topology as for RSGs to identify PSGs. Positive selection signals on genes along specific lineages were detected using the PAML branch-site model. LRT was likewise conducted to compare a model that allowed sites to be under positive selection (fix omega = 0, omega = 1.1) on the foreground branch with the null model (fix omega = 1, omega = 1) in which sites could evolve either neutrally and under purifying selection. Genes with p-value less than 0.05 were treated as candidates that underwent positive selection. We identified PSGs at the same branches as RSGs. Then we also conducted KEGG and GO enrichment for PSGs using Metascape.

Estimation of evolutionary distances based on orthologous genes

Based on the 5178 high-confidence one-to-one orthologous genes identified from whole-genome alignments across 13 species, we estimated pairwise evolutionary distances using DiStats v1.0 [83] with the parameter –distance = k2p. The analysis excluded the reference species (Homo sapiens) and was thus conducted among the remaining 12 species, consisting of 7 aboveground and 5 subterranean species. The K2P model was used to measure nucleotide substitution distances, as it accounts for differences in the rates of transitions and transversions between DNA sequences. For each orthologous gene, a custom script count_k2p.py (https://github.com/aaannaw/Myospalax-CFi/blob/main/K2P/count_k2p.py) is designed to process the output k2p files generated by the DiStats software. Specifically, for each gene, it iterates through the pairwise K2P distances between one species and all the remaining species, accumulates these values, and then calculates the average. The resulting value represents the average K2P distance between that species and all others for each gene. Finally, the distribution of average K2P distances per species was visualized using boxplots generated by the ggplot2 package in R, providing a comparative overview of sequence divergence levels among the analyzed taxa.

Identification of CNEs

The reference of multiple genome alignment of 12 rodent species above was converted to M. aspalax by “maf project” command. We first used phyloFit (default parameters) from PHAST v1.7 package [84] to predict non-conserved models based on the four-fold degenerate sites and then applied phastCons [85] to predict non-conserved and conserved models for alignments of 12 rodent species based on the estimated non-conserved models above. PhyloBoot [84] was utilized to construct mean conserved and mean non-conserved models. PhastCons was again used to predict highly conserved elements in rodents and to generate a conservative score (phastCons –most-conserved -score –target-coverage 0.3 –expected-length 45). In addition, elements overlapping with exon region and aligned to Homo sapiens were excluded and phyloP [84] was applied to detect conserved elements (altsubscale > 1) that had accelerated evolution in subterranean branch. KEGG and GO enrichment analyses for CNE-related genes, defined as the closest gene to each CNE, were also conducted using Metascape.

CNV analysis between two species

To investigate gene CNV differences between M. aspalax and M. psilurus, we analyzed whole-genome resequencing data from 26 individuals across both species. Short-read DNA sequencing data (PRJNA982286, 12 M. aspalax individuals and 14 M. psilurus individuals) were aligned to the M. aspalax reference genome using BWA-MEM v2.21 [86], and the resulting SAM files were sorted and converted to BAM format using SAMtools v1.6 [87]. GATK v4.1.4.1 [88] was employed to remove PCR duplicates via the MarkDuplicates module. We then used Control-FREEC [89] to detect CNV regions in each individual by predicting coordinates of copy number alterations. Candidate CNV regions were intersected with gene annotation data to assign copy number values to each gene across samples. To quantify interspecies copy number divergence for each gene, we calculated the Vst statistic, defined as Vst = (Vt-Vs)/Vt, where Vt represents the total variance of gene copy number across all individuals, and Vs is the average within-species variance, weighted by species sample sizes. Vst values range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater divergence in copy number between species. To assess the statistical significance of observed Vst values, we performed permutation testing by randomly shuffling species labels 1000 times. For each permutation, Vst values were recalculated to generate a null distribution. Genes with observed Vst values within the top 1% of this distribution were considered to exhibit significant interspecies CNV divergence (empirical p-value < 0.01). Finally, to visualize interspecies copy number variation in olfactory receptor genes with significant divergence, we used the ggplot2 package in R to generate heatmaps based on copy number values.

SNP identification

Based on the deduplicated BAM files obtained from the Copy Number Variation Analysis Between Two Species section, SNP calling was performed for each individual using GATK v4.1.4.1 [88] HaplotypeCaller. To improve the reliability of variant detection, we applied a series of stringent filters: (1) indels with quality scores below 3 were excluded; (2) multi-allelic SNPs were removed; (3) SNPs within 5 base pairs of any indel were filtered out; (4) variants with genotype quality (GQ) scores below 10 were discarded; and (5) SNPs with read depths less than one-third or more than three times the mean coverage were excluded. Subsequently, joint genotyping across individuals was carried out using GATK’s GenotypeGVCFs, producing two variant sets: one including all sites using the -all-sites option, and another containing only variant sites. The resulting multi-sample VCF files were further filtered using VariantFiltration with the following criteria: QD < 2.0 || FS > 200.0 || ReadPosRankSum < −20.0 || MQ < 40.0 || MQRankSum < −12.5. Final filtering was conducted using VCFtools v0.1.16 [90], with thresholds set to minor allele frequency > 0.05, minimum depth ≥ 5, maximum depth ≤ 150, minimum genotype quality ≥ 20, Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium p-value ≥ 0.001, and missing rate ≤ 5%.

Calculation of population genetic parameters

Based on the SNPs identified in the “SNP identification” section, we performed analyses of population genetic diversity and linkage. To evaluate LD patterns, we used PopLDdecay v3.40 [91] to compute pairwise LD statistics within populations. The LD decay results were then visualized using the Plot_MultiPop.pl script provided by PopLDdecay. In addition, nucleotide diversity (π) was calculated for each population using VCFtools v0.1.16 [90], providing a measure of genetic variation within populations. Additionally, we examined population genetic differentiation in olfactory receptor and taste receptor genes between M. aspalax and M. psilurus. Using the gene annotation file of M. aspalax, we extracted the genomic coordinates of annotated OR and Tas2r genes. These coordinates were then used with VCFtools v0.1.16 [90] via the –bed option to calculate Fst values between the two species for each receptor gene. To further assess whether these sensory receptor genes evolved under natural selection, we performed a comparative analysis using putatively neutral noncoding regions. We randomly selected an equal number of noncoding regions from the genome that were located at least 200 kb away from any annotated gene. These control regions were chosen to match the average length of the OR and Tas2r genes. Fst values for these neutral regions were also calculated using VCFtools v0.1.16 [90], allowing for comparison with the receptor gene results.

TE identification and annotation

We identified TEs in M. aspalax and M. psilurus using a multi-step pipeline. First, we applied RepeatModeler v1.0.11 [92] (with the -LTRStruct option) separately to each genome to construct species-specific repeat libraries. The resulting consensus sequence files for each species were then used as input for the classic de novo Earl Grey v5.8 [93] run (earlGreyLibConstruct) to perform de novo TE detection, consensus generation, and library improvement through the BEAT process. Next, we combined the high-quality repeat libraries generated from both species and removed redundant sequences using CD-HIT v4.8.1–2019-0228 (https://sites.google.com/view/cd-hit) with parameters -aS 0.8 -c 0.8. The resulting non-redundant, high-quality repeat library was then employed to run the final annotation and defragmentation steps for each species using earlGreyAnnotationOnly [93].

Transcriptional quantification of TEs

To quantify the expression of identified TEs based on bulk RNA-seq data, we analyzed five tissues (heart, liver, lung, kidney, and muscle) from M. aspalax and M. psilurus. First, raw RNA-seq reads were quality-filtered using Trimmomatic [94], and the resulting high-quality reads were aligned to the respective reference genomes of each species using HISAT2 v2.2.1 [95] with the parameters: –min-intronlen 20, –max-intronlen 600,000, –no-mixed, and –no-discordant. Next, we used makeTEgtf.pl [96] to convert the TE annotations (in GFF format), obtained from the Transposable Element Identification and Annotation step, into GTF format. TE expression levels were then quantified using TEcount v2.2.3 [96], which aggregates counts from different copies of a TE subfamily based on the GTF annotation. For each tissue, we used multiJoin.pl (https://github.com/agordon/bin_scripts/tree/master/scripts) to aggregate TE counts from both species into a single count table. Differential expression analysis between the two species for each TE family in the same tissue was performed using DESeq2 (https://github.com/thelovelab/DESeq2). Finally, ggplot2 in R was used to generate volcano plots visualizing the differential expression of TE families between M. aspalax and M. psilurus.

Manual curation of transposable elements

To assist in the manual curation of TEs, we used TE-Aid v1.0.0 [97]. First, consensus sequences of TEs were extracted using SeqKit v2.6.0 [98]. Each TE consensus sequence, along with the corresponding species-specific reference genome, was then used as input for TE-Aid analysis. TE-Aid generates multiple visual outputs to facilitate manual inspection, including (1) Genomic hits with divergence to the consensus; (2) Genomic coverage plots of each consensus sequence; (3) A self dot-plot showing internal sequence structure; and (4) A structural analysis.

DNA methylation analysis based on HiFi reads

We used CCS (https://github.com/PacificBiosciences/ccs) to convert raw PacBio subreads of both M. aspalax and M. psilurus into HiFi reads. The –hifi-kinetics parameter was enabled to retain base modification kinetics information. Next, we used Jasmine v2.4.0 (https://github.com/pacificbiosciences/jasmine) to process the resulting HiFi_reads.bam files in order to identify and extract methylation signals embedded in the HiFi reads. This produced methylation-annotated BAM files named methylation.hifi_reads.bam. These annotated BAM files were then aligned to each species’ reference genome using pbmm2 v1.16.99 (https://github.com/PacificBiosciences/pbmm2) with the –sort parameter to ensure sorted alignment outputs. Finally, we applied the aligned_bam_to_cpg_scores tool from the pb-CpG-tools v3.0.0 (https://github.com/PacificBiosciences/pb-CpG-tools) package to generate site-specific methylation probability scores based on the aligned BAM files. The –model parameter was specified to define the appropriate prediction model for methylation calling.

Identification of differentially methylated regions (DMRs)

To identify DMRs between M. aspalax and M. psilurus, we first generated pairwise genome alignment chains using the make_chains.py [99] script from HillerLab, allowing us to establish syntenic regions between the two genomes. Next, we clustered CpG sites from M. aspalax into candidate methylated regions using the combine_CpG_sites.py script from DMRfinder v0.3 [100]. These regions were then mapped onto the M. psilurus genome using liftOver v1.3.0 [101] with default parameters to identify homologous regions in the second species. To determine whether the lifted regions correspond to methylated regions in M. psilurus, we intersected the lifted coordinates with M. psilurus CpG site data using BEDTools v2.30.0 [102]. Only regions containing at least three methylated CpG sites were retained as M. psilurus methylation regions. Finally, we used the findDMRs.r script from DMRfinder v0.3 [100] to identify regions with statistically significant differential methylation between the two species based on the homologous CpG clusters.

Methylation profiling around transcriptional start sites and transposable elements

To analyze DNA methylation patterns around gene transcriptional start sites and TEs, we used gene annotation files and methylation site data for both M. aspalax and M. psilurus. For each gene, we identified the TSS based on strand orientation: for genes annotated on the positive strand, the TSS was defined as the coordinate in the fourth column of the GFF file; for genes on the negative strand, the TSS was taken from the fifth column. We then defined TSS-flanking regions as ± 3 kb around each TSS and used BEDTools v2.30.0 intersect to extract methylation sites within these regions. The methylation levels in these ± 3 kb regions were visualized using R. For TE methylation analysis, we extracted genomic coordinates of TEs from the GFF files and converted them to BED format. These coordinates were intersected with the species-specific methylation site data using BEDTools v2.30.0, and the average methylation level for each TE was calculated.

Structural variation detection between two chromosome-level genomes

Based on two chromosome-level genomes for M. aspalax and M. psilurus, we removed scaffolds that were not anchored to pseudo-chromosomes and used SyRI v1.6 [103] to identify SVs between each pair of homologous chromosomes of the two genomes. Due to the chromosome fission event and inconsistent karyotype number between the two species, we first applied chroder (https://schneebergerlab.github.io/syri/scaforder.html) to merge Chromosome 1 and Chromosome 32 of M. psilurus and adjust chromosome ID and positive and negative strands taking M. aspalax as reference. Then genome-wide alignments were built using MUMmer v4.0.0 [104] between adjusted M. psilurus genome and M. aspalax genome. SyRI was then conducted to identify structural variation based on the alignments with default parameters. Finally, the identified syntenic regions and SVs were visualized with the plotsr script based on the output of SyRI [103].

Validation for large inversion breakpoints

Based on 40 large inversions identified by SyRI [103], we verified breakpoints utilizing both de novo draft genome and PacBio HiFi raw reads. For the approach based on de novo draft genome, we first aligned the contig-level genome of M. psilurus to chromosome-level genome of M. aspalax with MUMmer v4.0.0 [104] and filtered out the alignments less than 4 kb. Then we localized breakpoints as follows: (i) When a Contig spanned the entire inversion region and the Contig was aligned to the reference genome in the opposite direction, breakpoints could be recognized. (ii) When a Contig spanned only a portion of the inversion and its alignment to the reference genome is in the opposite direction to that of the remaining inversions, the inversion breakpoint could also be identified [72]. For the approach based on PacBio HiFi raw reads, Minimap2 v2.24 [105] was utilized to align HiFi raw reads of two genomes with M. aspalax reference genome and IGV (https://igv.org) was used to visualize. Breakpoints could be identified when reads coverage of M. psilurus was significantly lower than that of M. aspalax [106].

IRs and SDs

Based on the soft-masked reference genome, we used SEDEF v1.1 [107] to identify IRs and SDs near large inversion breakpoints. IRs are those identified by SEDEF in opposite directions between two breakpoints (near 500 kb); SDs are those identified by SEDEF within a single breakpoint (near 500 kb) that are more than 1 kb in length, with greater than 70% similarity and less than 70% masked regions. To compare the SD density of breakpoints with that of random genome-wide regions, we randomly selected 10,000 regions from the whole genome and counted the SD density within 500 kb of them and implemented Kolmogorov–Smirnov test for SD density between breakpoints and random regions. Finally, we used R to draw dot plots to show IRs and SDs.

Genetic load of large inversions

To explore whether inversions enrich for deleterious mutations, SnpEff v5.2 [108] was used to annotate SNPs in the inversion region and whole genome region for each species separately. Then we counted the proportion of SNPs annotated as loss-of-function type. Wilcoxon test was implemented to test whether the proportions in the inverted region and the whole genome were significantly different.

Generation of normalized chromatin interaction matrices

We employed HiC-Pro v3.10 [109] to generate chromatin contact matrices using chromosome-level genome assemblies and Hi-C sequencing data. Initially, Hi-C reads were aligned to the reference genome using Bowtie2 v2.4.4 [110]. We filtered out unmapped read pairs, low-quality alignments, multiply mapped reads, and singleton reads, retaining only uniquely mapped read pairs that aligned to the expected restriction enzyme sites (MboI). Subsequently, we excluded three categories of invalid interaction pairs-Dangling Ends, Self-Circles, and Dumped Pairs-to ensure data accuracy. These valid interactions were used to construct raw contact matrices at multiple resolutions, including 1 Mb, 100 kb, 40 kb, 20 kb, and 10 kb. Finally, the raw interaction matrices were normalized using the Iterative Correction and Eigenvector decomposition method to correct for technical biases and ensure comparability across bins.

Constructing homology bin

The MAF file for the genome alignments of M. aspalax and M. psilurus described above was converted into “psl” and “chain” file using “mafToPsl” and “axtChain” of the UCSC Genome Browser tool [101]. Then liftOver v1.3.0 [101] was applied to obtain the homology bins between the two genomes.

A (active)/B (inactive) compartment conversion

Cwold-dekker pipeline (https://github.com/dekkerlab/cworld-dekker) was first applied to identify compartment type for each bin. Principal component analysis was performed for normalized interaction matrix at 100 kb resolution and the first principal component value typically was used to identify compartments A/B. We further corrected identified compartment types based on A compartment characterized by higher gene density and GC content. When homologous bins between the two genomes exhibited different compartment types, then these bins are considered to be compartmentally changed regions, and conversely when the compartment types are the same, then they are considered to be compartmentally stable regions.

Comparison of topologically associated domains

The insulation score approach [111] was used to identify TADs for 40 kb resolution contact matrix. Then BEDTools v2.30.0 [102] was applied to recognize TADs intersected with homologous bin between the two genomes. We identified as shared TADs when TADs located in homologous bins was intersected for more than 70% of their respective lengths, with remaining TADs being species-specific TADs.
