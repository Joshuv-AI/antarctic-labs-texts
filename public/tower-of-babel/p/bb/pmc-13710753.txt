Fermentative iron reduction by a psychrotolerant Clostridium-dominant consortium enriched from Antarctic penguin-impacted soils
Authors: Xin Wang, Hongyan Wang, Xuhao Wang, Hengyi Liao, Jiaojiao Yang, Huijuan Jin, Erik Hoffnagle, Min Ki Jeon, Yiru Cui, Xiuying Li, Xiaodong Liu, Xin Chen, Li Liao, Yiran Dong, Lisi Jiang, Zongming Xiu, Yi Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microbial iron cycling regulates nutrient availability and redox balance in global ecosystems, yet its pathways remain underexplored in ice-free Antarctic terrestrial ecosystems. This study reports the enrichment of a psychrotolerant microbial consortium from penguin-impacted soils on Beaufort Island, Antarctica, capable of reducing Fe(III) to Fe(II) at 4 °C via an anaerobic (likely fermentative) iron-reducing pathway. The consortium was dominated by Clostridium sensu stricto 13 and completely reduced 230 mg L-1 Fe(III) citrate within three months and drove the biogenic formation of magnetite (Fe3O4). Metagenomic binning yielded four high-quality Clostridium genomes harboring multiple hydrogenases and cold-shock proteins (csp), revealing genomic strategies for energy conservation and psychrotolerance. Hydrogen production was strongly suppressed in the presence of Fe(III) citrate, indicating an intimate coupling of fermentation-derived electron flow to Fe(III) reduction. Our findings reveal a previously unrecognized low-temperature iron reduction mechanism and highlight the ecological significance of anaerobic (likely fermentative) iron reducers in ornithogenic soils—microhabitats enriched in organic matter and metals by penguin guano. This work expands the known diversity of Fe(III)-reducing microorganisms, demonstrates their role in magnetite biomineralization under extreme conditions, and provides insights into microbial modulation of iron speciation in Antarctic ornithogenic soils.

Introduction

Iron (Fe), the most abundant transition metal on Earth, plays a crucial role in biogeochemical cycles, particularly in the coupled biogeochemical cycles of carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur in terrestrial and marine ecosystems1,2. Due to its multiple oxidation states and redox properties, Fe serves as both an electron donor and acceptor in diverse microbial metabolisms, fundamentally influencing redox dynamics, organic matter turnover, and nutrient availability3,4. The cycling of Fe in natural environments is shaped by complex interactions between both abiotic processes, such as mineral weathering and precipitation5, and biotic processes, including microbial oxidation and reduction6, and biological mobilization1. In marine ecosystems, Fe often acts as a limiting nutrient for primary production, especially in high-nutrient low-chlorophyll (HNLC) regions such as the Southern Ocean7. Iron inputs to these waters arise from a combination of sources, including aeolian dust, glacial meltwater, upwelling of deep waters, and biological vectors such as seabirds, whose guano deposits contribute substantial amounts of bioavailable Fe to coastal and terrestrial ecosystems8–11. Understanding the drivers of Fe speciation and bioavailability is thus critical for predicting ecosystem responses to the Antarctic environment.

Microorganisms are central to Fe transformations, with Fe(III)-reducing bacteria (IRB) occupying a key position in the redox network of sediments and soils12. IRB fall into two functional groups: dissimilatory IRB (DIRB), which conserve energy by coupling Fe(III) reduction to cellular growth13, and fermentative IRB (FIRB), which reduce Fe(III) incidentally during substrate fermentation without direct energy conservation14. Respiratory Fe(III) reducers such as Geobacter spp. and Shewanella spp. have been widely studied as model organisms for metal respiration, extracellular electron transfer, and bioelectrochemical applications15–17. In contrast, FIRB, including species of Clostridium and Bacillus, are less well characterized, particularly in natural ecosystems18. These organisms mineralize organic substrates such as sugars, amino acids, and short-chain organic acids generating hydrogen and other fermentation products, while simultaneously reducing Fe(III) as an incidental side reaction18. Recent studies have revealed that FIRB can synthesize magnetite nanoparticles under controlled laboratory conditions, highlighting their potential ecological and applied relevance19.

Despite significant advances in geomicrobiology, the role of fermentative Fe(III) reducers in extreme ecosystems remains underexplored20. Polar regions, particularly Antarctica, present unique opportunities to investigate Fe cycling under extreme conditions characterized by low temperatures21, strong seasonality in light and nutrient fluxes22, and limited anthropogenic disturbance23. Despite these harsh conditions, IRB have been detected in various Antarctic habitats, particularly in pristine soils and sediments20,24,25.

Ornithogenic soils, formed by prolonged deposition of penguin guano, represent a distinctive and biogeochemically dynamic habitat in Antarctica26–28. Penguin colonies enrich soils with high levels of organic matter, nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, and trace metals, including Fe, while simultaneously modifying pH, moisture content, and redox potential27,29,30. Guano inputs create unique geochemical conditions of ornithogenic soils as well as microenvironments with high reducing capacity, potentially favoring the growth of anaerobic fermenters and metal-reducing bacteria31. These soils thus serve as natural “hotspots” for Fe cycling and may function as reservoirs for microbial communities adapted to organic rich, cold, and redox-stratified environments32. The microbial communities of ornithogenic soils are distinct from those of surrounding barren soils, shaped by continual inoculation from penguin gut microbiota and by the selective pressures imposed by high nutrient loads and periodic freeze thaw cycles33.

While considerable attention has been paid to DIRB in polar environments, the role and diversity of FIRB in these ecosystems34, particularly in ornithogenic soils, remain poorly understood. This knowledge gap is particularly significant given that FIRB may be better adapted to the extreme conditions of Antarctic environments compared to their respiratory counterparts. Furthermore, low-temperature anaerobic iron reduction may represent an important but overlooked pathway in the biogeochemical cycling of Fe in polar regions35, potentially influencing nutrient availability and ecosystem functioning. The secondary Fe minerals produced during microbial Fe(III) reduction may further impact phosphorus and metal mobility, contributing to the ecological dynamics of these unique environments36. Understanding these anaerobic processes is crucial for expanding our knowledge of microbial adaptations to extreme environments.

Here, we investigate the occurrence and characteristics of psychrotolerant FIRB in ornithogenic soils from Beaufort Island, Antarctica. Using a combination of anaerobic (likely fermentative) enrichment cultures, 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing, metagenomic binning, and mineralogical analyses, we characterize a Clostridium-dominated consortium capable of reducing Fe(III) to Fe(II) and producing magnetite at 4 °C. We further explore the relationship between substrate fermentation, hydrogen metabolism, and Fe(III) reduction, and examine the genomic features underlying cold adaptation and electron transfer capability. Our study highlights the functional role of anaerobic (likely fermentative) Clostridium in Antarctic iron cycling. These findings reveal the ecological distribution and physiological diversity of FIRB and provide new insight into microbial contributions to Fe bioavailability and biogeochemical cycling in polar ecosystems.

Results

Fe(III) reduction and magnetite formation under anoxic conditions

Anoxic microcosms established with Beaufort Island ornithogenic soils exhibited robust Fe(III)-reducing activity when supplied with lactate and peptone as electron donors and organic substrates. Initial enrichments completely reduced 230 mg L−1 Fe(III) to Fe(II) within three months. Successive transfers markedly accelerated reduction kinetics: by the second transfer, complete Fe(III) reduction occurred within 80 days, and by the fifth transfer, 125 mg L−1 Fe(III) was fully reduced in just 25 days (Fig. 1A, B). The culture medium underwent a color change every 10 days from yellow to green and finally to black, consistent with Fe(II)-bearing mineral formation (Fig. 1E). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis revealed abundant rod-shaped bacterial cells, approximately 1–2 μm in length, often forming aggregates closely associated with Fe(III) mineral particles (Fig. 1D and Fig. S3). The intimate cell–mineral contact suggests close association that may facilitate electron transfer during Fe(III) reduction (Fig. S3). At the phylum level, the sediment microcosms were dominated by Proteobacteria (35.19%) and Bacteroidetes (29.33%), followed by Firmicutes (22.50%) and Actinobacteria (10.31%) (Fig. 2A). Minor phyla included Chloroflexi (1.22%), Gemmatimonadetes (0.58%), Patescibacteria (0.45%), and Deinococcus-Thermus (0.30%). Through successive transfers, a significant shift in community composition was observed, with Firmicutes becoming increasingly dominant. The relative abundance of Firmicutes increased dramatically from 22.50% in sediment to 83.34% in the Transfer 2 enrichment culture (T2), and further to 98.95% in the Transfer 5 enrichment culture (T5), maintaining high abundance (97.38%) in T11. By comparison, other phyla showed substantial decreases, with Proteobacteria declining from 35.19% to nearly undetectable levels. At the genus level (Fig. 2B), more pronounced community shifts were observed during the enrichment process. Initially, Marinobacter (13.79%) was the dominant genus in the sediment microcosms, while Clostridium sensu stricto 13 represented only 0.13%. However, the enrichment process led to significant community restructuring. Clostridium sensu stricto 13 emerged as the predominant genus, increasing dramatically from 0.13% to 34.31% in T2, and further to 98.95% in T5, maintaining dominance (93.33%) through T11. This divergence does not reflect the in situ community but reveals the cold-adapted anaerobic (likely fermentative) iron-reducing functional group that can be activated from the native reservoir. Notably, Tissierella showed temporary dominance (43.15%) in T2 but disappeared in subsequent transfers. Similarly, Proteiniphilum peaked at 15.84% in T2 before declining to negligible levels. Other genera, including Sedimentibacter (3.15%), Hydrogenoanaerobacterium (0.98%), and Desulfosporosinus (0.87%), remained at low abundances throughout the enrichment process.

Low temperature selects for Clostridium dominance

Temperature exerted a strong selective effect on the enrichment cultures (Fig. 2C). At 10 °C, the microbial community exhibited higher diversity, with Clostridium sensu stricto 13 accounting for 63% of the total population, followed by Acinetobacter (19%), Proteiniclasticum (10%), Dehalobacter (3%), and Thermus (2%). In contrast, lowering the temperature to 4 °C acted as a powerful selective pressure, causing a dramatic shift in which Clostridium sensu stricto 13 became almost the sole member of the community (99% relative abundance). Substrate type further influenced community composition but preserved Clostridium dominance. Lactate- and peptone- amended cultures contained 92–96% Clostridium sensu stricto 13, with minor co-occurrence of Proteiniclasticum, Sedimentibacter, and Alkalibacter (<4%) (Fig. 2C). These findings suggest that Clostridium can metabolize diverse organic substrates and is favored under cold, anoxic, iron-reducing conditions, although this represents an inference based on enrichment patterns rather than direct competition assays. The observed increase in Clostridium abundance likely reflects environmental filtering under the imposed incubation conditions rather than measured competitive superiority.

Fe(III) reduction suppresses anaerobic hydrogen production

To elucidate the relationship between fermentative metabolism and Fe(III) reduction, we quantified hydrogen production under varying substrate concentrations in the presence and absence of Fe(III) (Fig. 3A). In lactate-amended cultures, H2 production increased progressively with lactate concentrations (2.5–10 mM), regardless of Fe(III) availability. However, the presence of Fe(III) consistently suppressed H2 accumulation by 54–60%, with production ranging from 28 ± 7 to 49 ± 4 nmol L−1 compared to 71 ± 9 to 116 ± 4 nmol L−1 in Fe(III)-free controls. This inhibitory effect was even more pronounced in peptone-amended cultures. In Fe(III)-amended bottles, H2 production remained low and nearly constant (42 ± 5 to 47 ± 5 nmol L−1) across peptone concentrations (0.125–0.5 g L−1), whereas in Fe(III)-free controls, H2 production increased dramatically, from 267 ± 64 to 579 ± 57 nmolL−1. These results suggest that Fe(III) may function as an important preferential electron sink that reduces the electrons available for H2 formation, which could influence hydrogen-related metabolism in Clostridium. However, this interpretation is based on observed patterns in Fe(III) reduction and H2 dynamics rather than direct measurement of intracellular electron flow. Further physiological assays would be required to directly confirm Fe(III) as a preferential electron sink at the cellular level. The observed suppression of H2 production in the presence of Fe(III) aligns with the established ecological principle that Fe(III) reduction outcompetes other anaerobic (likely fermentative) pathways for reducing equivalents. Genomic insights into cold adaptation and metabolism

Metagenomic binning yielded four draft genomes of Clostridium species (Fig. S2), designated as Antarctic1, Antarctic2, Antarctic3, and Antarctic4, with genome completeness ranging from 82% to over 98% and contamination levels below 4%, as assessed by CheckM. The genome sizes of these strains range from 3.56 to 5.31 Mb, with G + C contents between 30.90 and 31.24% (Table S2). PATRIC annotation predicted that strain Antarctic1 contains 4442 total genes, including 4173 protein-coding sequences (CDS) and 102 RNA genes. Strain Antarctic2 harbors 3455 total genes (3253 CDS and 77 RNAs), while strain Antarctic3 possesses 4393 total genes (4116 CDS and 62 RNAs), and strain Antarctic4 contains 5278 total genes (4986 CDS and 74 RNAs). Key genomic features included multiple hydrogenase (hyd) genes (2–4 copies), critical for anaerobic (likely fermentative) hydrogen production, with Antarctic4 having the highest count (4 copies), followed by Antarctic2 (3 copies), and Antarctic1 and Antarctic3 (2 copies each) (Fig. 3B). Csp genes, essential for psychrophilic adaptation, were also prevalent, with Antarctic4 encoding 4 copies, Antarctic2 encoding 3, and Antarctic1 and Antarctic3 encoding 2 each. These csp genes may support membrane fluidity, transcription, translation, and protein folding at low temperatures, explaining the strains’ success in Antarctic conditions37,38. Consistent with this, a Csp phylogeny built from Antarctic Clostridium isolates and references (Fig. 4, Table S4 and S5) placed the Antarctic and Arctic sequences in multiple interspersed clades rather than a single cluster39,40; notably, the four copies in Antarctic4 fall into at least two subclades, indicating lineage-specific expansions and/or horizontal acquisition with ensuing paralog diversification under cold selection. Metabolic annotation using the DRAM pipeline elucidated the functional potential of these metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs). The genomes encoded diverse pathways for carbon, nitrogen, sulfur, and iron cycling (Fig. S4). Genes for anaerobic (likely fermentative) respiration and Fe(III) reduction, including multiheme cytochromes, hydrogenases, and sulfur reductases, underscored the community’s capacity for metal reduction and energy conservation in anoxic environments. Abundant genes for anaerobic (likely fermentative) metabolism, such as those for acetate and propionate production, aligned with the dominance of fermentative taxa like Clostridium sensu stricto 13. Additionally, genes associated with stress response, nutrient acquisition, and environmental adaptation highlighted the consortia’s resilience in the nutrient-rich, cold, and anoxic conditions of Beaufort Island’s ornithogenic soils. Discussion

Microbial Fe(III) reduction in cold terrestrial environments represents a critical but understudied component of global biogeochemical cycles41. This study advances our understanding of polar biogeochemistry by uncovering three key, interlinked discoveries. First, we report the successful enrichment and cultivation of a Clostridium-dominated consortium from Antarctic ornithogenic soils that mediates fermentative Fe(III) reduction at psychrophilic temperatures (4 °C). Second, we show that this process suppresses, rather than stimulates, fermentative hydrogen production revealing a distinct regulatory pattern compared to mesophilic Clostridium species42. Third, we demonstrate that low temperature itself acts as a strong ecological filter, selecting for the dominance of these fermentative Fe(III)-reducing Clostridium strains. Together, these findings reveal a previously unrecognized cryptic iron cycle in Antarctic soils, driven by cold-adapted fermenters with distinct metabolic regulation, and establish a new ecological principle for microbial community assembly under extreme cold and anoxic conditions.

Our work demonstrates a previously overlooked pathway of fermentative iron reduction in Antarctic soils. Although diverse Clostridium species have been detected in Antarctic habitats and several psychrotolerant isolates have been characterized40,43,44, their role in iron cycling has remained unresolved. Previous studies enriched cold-tolerant Fe(III)-reducing communities from Antarctic subglacial sediments, but these were dominated by other taxa such as Desulfosporosinus45. By successfully cultivating a Clostridium-dominated consortium under fermentative, Fe(III)-reducing conditions at 4 °C, we provide the first direct functional evidence for this metabolic process in Antarctic soils. This is particularly significant for ornithogenic soils, which are enriched with organic matter and metals by penguin guano and represent natural hotspots for anaerobic (likely fermentative) microbial activity46. Furthermore, while our study focuses on microbial FIR, we cannot entirely exclude the contribution of secondary abiotic processes. Trace sulfate present in the mineral salts medium or sulfur-containing amino acids from peptone may be converted to reduced sulfur species. These products can abiotically reduce Fe(III), thereby reinforcing the overall iron cycle in these ornithogenic soils. However, given the near-complete dominance of Clostridium (>98%) and the rapid acceleration of reduction kinetics during successive transfers, FIR remains the primary driver of iron transformation in this system.

Unlike previously reported iron-reducing bacteria such as Geobacter, which employ respiratory Fe(III) reduction47, our Antarctic Clostridium strains employ a fermentative pathway. For instance, fermentative Clostridium sp. LQ25 utilizes flavins as electron shuttles for Fe(III) and Cr(VI) bioreduction, differing from respiratory bacterial mechanisms48. Similar Clostridium fermentative iron reduction pathways have been reported at temperate conditions (25 °C)49, but our findings provide evidence of their occurrence at temperatures as low as 4 °C. The main differences between temperate and low-temperature systems are therefore quantitative and ecological, including lower reaction rates, shifts in community composition, and the presence of cold-adaptation traits (e.g., cold-shock proteins, membrane modifications) rather than a fundamentally different metabolic route. Additionally, while most psychrophilic iron reducers are marine sediment-derived50, our terrestrial Antarctic ornithogenic soils represent a novel environment for such processes. Our results therefore highlight ornithogenic soils as an understudied terrestrial setting where psychrotolerant Fe(III) reduction operates, potentially mediated by taxa that ultimately originated from marine environments but now function within a guano-influenced soil ecosystem.

The unique conditions of ornithogenic soils offer distinct insights into Antarctic iron cycling. Clostridium spp. are well-studied for their resilience and metabolic versatility in extreme conditions50, and ferment at low temperatures51,52. The sampled Beaufort Island soils, shaped by prolonged penguin activities, represent microenvironments enriched in organic matter, iron, and unique pH conditions resulting from penguin diet and excreta53. Penguin guano, primarily derived from Antarctic krill (Euphausia superba), significantly enriches soil iron content, creating localized anaerobic microsites over time27,54. Soil compaction and pH shifts induced by penguin activities further enhance microbial accessibility and iron bioavailability.

An intriguing possibility is that the Clostridium isolates originated from penguin gut microbiota, introduced into soils via fecal deposition. Previous studies have identified diverse Clostridium species in the gastrointestinal tracts of penguins, potentially playing roles in nutrient cycling and soil microbial community structuring55–57. Thus, Clostridium enrichment in ornithogenic soils may reflect continuous microbial inoculation from penguin gut microbiota. Initially, Clostridium was not dominant in the original soil samples; however, selective pressure from the laboratory enrichment conditions likely promoted their dominance. This suggests a competitive advantage under specific anaerobic (likely fermentative), iron-rich, and cold conditions, possibly due to efficient hydrogen production and fermentative iron reduction capabilities. Such selective enrichment highlights Clostridium’s ecological adaptability and underscores potential shifts in microbial community structures in response to environmental disturbances.

An unexpected finding of this work is the marked suppression of H2 production when soluble Fe(III) citrate is introduced, despite its rapid abiotic and biotic reduction to Fe(II). This result contrasts with mesophilic Clostridium systems (e.g., C. pasteurianum, C. acetobutylicum), where low concentrations of bioavailable Fe(II) rather than Fe(III) have been shown to stimulate hydrogenogenesis by supporting ferredoxin-dependent electron transfer58,59. The regulatory role of iron in microbial fermentation is profoundly dictated by its speciation and bioavailability. In mesophilic systems, insoluble Fe(III) oxyhydroxides (e.g., ferrihydrite) are often observed to stimulate H2 production by acting as weak electron sinks that relieve ferredoxin redox bottlenecks without overwhelming the proton reduction pathway60. However, the behavior of Fe(III) is strongly dependent on its chemical form: insoluble Fe(III) (e.g., ferrihydrite) provides minimal bioavailable Fe(III) and can weakly buffer acidification, often exhibiting neutral or mildly positive effects on H2 yields, whereas soluble Fe(III) complexes (e.g., Fe(III)-citrate) are highly redox-active and can outcompete protons for reducing equivalents. These results reflect the general tendency for Fe(III) reduction to outcompete hydrogen formation for reducing equivalents, potentially reinforced by additional electron sinks such as reduced sulfur species61. The choice of Fe(III)-citrate was intended to maximize bioavailability at psychrotolerant temperatures and facilitate the characterization of the fermentative functional group. However, we recognize that this soluble form does not represent the insoluble (hydro)oxide minerals (e.g., ferrihydrite, hematite) typically encountered in situ. The microbial response to Fe(III) is profoundly dictated by its chemical speciation. Insoluble phases like ferrihydrite provide minimal bioavailable iron and may weakly buffer acidification, often leading to negligible H2 suppression. In contrast, our results with psychrotolerant Clostridium demonstrate that the introduction of highly soluble Fe(III)-citrate (4 mM) dramatically reshapes metabolic bifurcation. In this scenario, the accessible Fe(III) pool serves as a preferential electron sink, diverting the flow of reducing equivalents away from H2 formation. This resulted in a 54–60% suppression of hydrogen production observed in our system. Such suppression reflects a metabolic optimization strategy where the consortium prioritizes the more thermodynamically favorable reduction of Fe(III) over the production of a diffusible byproduct (H2) under the extreme energy limitations of a 4 °C environment. While these findings demonstrate the significant metabolic capacity of the Clostridium consortium, they also highlight the necessity for future studies to examine reduction kinetics with natural mineral phases to fully resolve threshold behaviors in polar soils.

Our observations point to a clear contrast between the effects of insoluble and soluble iron phases. Ferrihydrite, being largely insoluble and poorly accessible, acts only as a modest electron sink and therefore does not substantially compete with proton reduction, enabling H₂-generating pathways to dominate. In sharp contrast, soluble Fe(III)–citrate introduces a highly available electron acceptor that draws reducing equivalents away from H2 formation, shifts ferredoxin-linked bifurcation pathways, and induces broader changes in community metabolism. The resulting electron redistribution leads to decreased H2 output. These results emphasize that the physiological and ecological impacts of iron depend strongly on its solubility, accessibility, and redox speciation. This redirection is fundamentally driven by thermodynamic advantages; the reduction of Fe(III) complexes generally provides a greater free energy yield per electron transferred compared to the reduction of protons to H2. This metabolic strategy likely reflects an adaptation to the energy limitations of the Antarctic environment, where maximizing electron efficiency is critical for survival. Microbial communities in ornithogenic soils of Antarctica represent unique ecosystems shaped by the interaction between extreme environmental conditions and penguin activities29. Our results provide experimental evidence that temperature acts as a decisive ecological filter shaping iron-reducing microbial guilds. While enrichments incubated at 10 °C supported a more diverse consortium, incubation at 4 °C drove near-complete dominance of Clostridium sensu stricto 13 (> 99% relative abundance). These strains demonstrate sophisticated genomic adaptations that enable survival and metabolic activities in cold environments while contributing to local biogeochemical cycles. Metagenomic analysis identified key genes linked to cold adaptation62. The presence of multiple cold-shock protein genes in the recovered genomes offers a molecular basis for this selection, supporting the maintenance of transcription, translation, and protein folding under near-freezing conditions. Such cold-adaptation mechanisms are critical for maintaining microbial activity at consistently low Antarctic temperatures63,64. These findings establish a new ecological principle for Antarctic soils: temperature not only limits metabolic rates but also actively structures the functional guilds that govern key iron biogeochemical processes.

Microbial Fe(III) reduction observed in this study resulted in the formation of magnetite (Fe3O4), indicating the potential role of Clostridium in biomineralization processes. The production of magnetite is significant because it immobilizes iron in a relatively stable but redox-active mineral phase, thereby shaping the long-term geochemical stability and redox dynamics of Antarctic soils. Although magnetite formation under anaerobic (likely fermentative) conditions has been well documented for classical respiratory iron-reducing bacteria such as Shewanella and Geobacter16,65, our results demonstrate that psychrophilic Clostridium strains can mediate the same process at near-freezing temperatures. This finding demonstrates that biomineralization can occur via fermentative iron reduction in this extreme environment, suggesting a potential pathway for iron speciation in polar soils. Furthermore, magnetite biomineralization has far-reaching implications for nutrient cycling: it sequesters iron while maintaining it in a redox-active form, potentially influencing phosphorus retention, trace metal mobility, and the timing of Fe release to surrounding waters66. Because Fe is a limiting nutrient for primary production in the Southern Ocean, microbial iron cycling in ornithogenic soils may have a disproportionate impact on coastal nutrient fluxes and, consequently, on regional carbon sequestration67. Seasonal freeze–thaw cycles further modulate redox conditions, organic matter availability, and microbial activity68, potentially driving pulsed iron mobilization69. As climate change alters penguin colony size and distribution, the balance of organic inputs, soil redox dynamics, and microbial iron transformation is likely to shift, with cascading effects on Southern Ocean productivity and polar biogeochemical cycles70. Understanding these processes is therefore critical for polar environments.

Conclusion

Our findings reveal that psychrotolerant Clostridium not only drive fermentative Fe(III) reduction but also mediate magnetite biomineralization under near-freezing conditions, revealing an easily overlooked pathway for iron transformation in Antarctic soils. By linking penguin derived organic inputs to specific microbial metabolic shifts and mineral formation, this study reveals a localized iron cycle that influences Fe speciation in these nutrient-rich microhabitats. These results provide a more refined understanding of how microbial functional groups respond to the unique geochemical conditions created by penguin colonization. While the broader implications for regional nutrient fluxes remain to be quantified, our work establishes that fermentative iron reduction is an active and potentially significant component of the biogeochemical landscape in ice-free Antarctic terrestrial ecosystems.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Analytical grade chemicals and reagents were used in this study, unless otherwise specified. Fe(III) citrate (CAS number: 3522-50-7, C6H5FeO7, ≥99.7%), sodium DL-Lactate (CAS number: 72-17-3, C3H5NaO3, 60% aqueous solution), and peptone (CAS number: 73049-73-7, FMB Grade) were purchased from Biotech. All other chemicals were obtained from Sigma–Aldrich and were of analytical or higher grade.

Microcosms and transfer cultures

Sampling sites were selected on Beaufort Island (166° 58’ 23.6” E, 76° 58’ 23.6” S) in Antarctica in 2010, with a sampling depth of 17.5 cm. Samples were taken from a small catchment area near active emperor penguin nesting sites. From the bottom to the top of the profile, brown clay gradually transitions into black, fine-grained ornithogenic soil, with one site identified along this gradient at depths of 0.5 cm, 5 cm, 8 cm, 10 cm, and 17.5 cm71. After collection, all samples were immediately frozen and stored at −80 °C for long-term preservation, and experimental work began in 2021 upon receipt of the archived material (details are provided in Text S2). Unless otherwise specified, all cultivations were performed in 120 mL glass serum bottles containing 80 mL of a bicarbonate-buffered (30 mM, pH 7.2) reduced mineral salts medium (details in Text S1), with a 40 mL N2/CO2 (80/20, v/v) headspace72,73. Triplicate microcosm bottles were inoculated with approximately 5 g of homogenized Antarctic soil inside an anaerobic chamber (Coy Laboratory, Ann Arbor, MI). Blue butyl rubber stoppers were then placed on top and secured with aluminum crimps. Each bottle received 10 mM sodium lactate as a carbon source and 0.5 g L−1 peptone as a nitrogen source. Additionally, approximately 4 mM Fe(III) citrate was added to each bottle in the first enrichment culture, and approximately 2 mM Fe(III) citrate was added in the fifth transfer. Lactate and peptone were added to initiate and standardize enrichment cultivation and to enable physiological characterization at 4 °C, rather than to simulate natural substrate availability. Solid-free enrichment cultures were obtained by transferring 3% (v/v) of the culture to fresh mineral salts medium amended with Fe(III) citrate, peptone, and sodium lactate. To account for potential abiotic reactions, triplicate negative controls were prepared in the same manner but were autoclaved and incubated under identical conditions. All bottles were incubated without agitation at 4 °C in the dark.

DNA Extraction and 16S rRNA Gene Amplicon Sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from ~0.5 g sediment slurry or 1 mL of Fe(III)-reducing cultures using the TIANamp Soil DNA Kit following the manufacturer’s instructions74. Cells were collected on 0.22-µm mixed cellulose membrane filters by vacuum filtration. DNA concentrations were quantified using a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer (Invitrogen, USA). The V3-V4 regions of 16S rRNA genes were amplified using barcoded primers. PCR was performed in 25 μL reactions containing 25 ng template DNA, 12.5 μL PCR Premix, and 2.5 μL of each primer75. The PCR conditions were: initial denaturation at 98 °C for 30 s; 32 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 54 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s; followed by final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. PCR products were verified by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis. Negative controls using ultrapure water were included to monitor potential contamination. The PCR products were purified using AMPure XT beads (Beckman Coulter Genomics, USA) and quantified by Qubit. Library quality was assessed using Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, USA) and Illumina Library Quantification Kit (Kapa Biosciences, USA). Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq PE250 platform at LC-Bio. Raw reads were processed using FLASH for paired-end merging and fqtrim (v0.94) for quality filtering. Chimeric sequences were removed using Vsearch (v2.3.4). Feature tables were generated using DADA2. Taxonomic classification was performed against the SILVA database. Alpha diversity indices (Chao1, Observed species, Goods coverage, Shannon, and Simpson) were calculated using QIIME2. Beta diversity analysis and visualization were performed using QIIME2 and R (v3.5.2).

Metagenome sequencing and analysis

DNA was extracted from enrichment cultures pelleted at 13,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C using the SDS method76,77. DNA quality was verified by gel electrophoresis and quantified using Qubit® 2.0 (Thermo Scientific). Metagenomic sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw reads were filtered using Readfq (V8) and assembled using JGI Metagenome Assembly Pipeline (Fig. S2)78. Taxonomic classification was conducted using Kaiju v1.7.379, and metagenomic contigs were binned using Maxbin2 v2.2.480. The quality of metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) was assessed using CheckM81. Taxonomic classification was performed using GTDB-Tk and TYGS. All four MAGs showed high completeness (>82%) and low contamination (<4%) (Table S1), meeting the standards for high-quality draft genomes. Genomes were annotated with DRAM (Distilled and Refined Annotation of Metabolism); genes called by Prodigal; DIAMOND/HMMER against KEGG/UniRef/Pfam/dbCAN; DRAM-distill summarized KEGG module completeness and presence/absence for ETC, N/S metabolism, methanogenesis/methanotrophy, photosynthesis, and SCFA/alcohol conversions; CAZy from dbCAN; the heatmap (Fig. S4) shows per-genome fractions and boolean presence.

Effects of nutrient concentrations on Fe(III) reduction

The influence of carbon and nitrogen sources on Fe(III) reduction was investigated using different concentrations of sodium lactate (2.5, 5.0, and 10.0 mM) and peptone (0.125, 0.25, and 0.5 g L−1). The experiments were conducted in triplicate using media containing 4 mM Fe(III) citrate and inoculated with 3% enrichment culture. Cultures were incubated statically at 4 °C in the dark, and hydrogen was monitored after complete Fe(III) reduction.

Analytical methods

Total Fe and Fe(II) levels were periodically monitored throughout the study. Fe(II) was determined spectrophotometrically using the ferrozine assay at 562 nm on a UV spectrophotometer (UH5300, Hitachi High-Tech, Japan)82. Fe(II) concentrations were determined using standard curves with high linearity (R² > 0.99, Fig. S1), ensuring accurate quantification throughout the experiment. The Fe(III)-reduction rate was calculated based on the change in Fe(III) concentration over the period where it decreased linearly, confirmed by at least three measurements. For iron valence analysis, 1 mL aliquots of culture fluid were collected at regular intervals using disposable syringes. Each sample was immediately acidified with 50 µL of concentrated HCl and then frozen for preservation. Hydrogen concentrations were measured via gas chromatography using a Peak Performer Gas Analyzer (Model PP1 910-100, Peak Laboratories, CA, USA), equipped with a reducing compound photometer (RCP).

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) and X-ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis

The morphology of enriched Fe(III)-reducing bacteria and their association with Fe(III) minerals were observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Samples were freshly centrifuged from enrichment cultures and immediately fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (SPI, cat. no. 02607-BA) at 4 °C for 12 to 24 h. Fixed samples were washed three times with 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.0) for 15 min each. Dehydration was performed through a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95% for 15 min each), followed by two treatments in 100% ethanol for 20 min each. Samples were then dried using a Quorum critical point dryer (Quorum K850). Dried specimens were mounted on aluminum stubs using conductive carbon adhesive and sputter-coated with platinum for approximately 120 s using a Hitachi MC1000 ion sputter coater. SEM imaging was conducted on a Hitachi Regulus 8100 SEM at an accelerating voltage suitable for biological samples (typically 3 kV) under high vacuum mode. Images were acquired at magnifications ranging from ×10,000 to ×50,000. Scale bars correspond to 5.00 μm for lower magnification images and 1.00 μm for higher magnifications. Mineralogical characterization of iron mineral precipitates formed during microbial Fe(III) reduction was performed using X-ray diffraction (XRD). Samples containing precipitates were collected by centrifugation, and the solids were dried under vacuum at room temperature until constant weight. The dried samples were finely ground and evenly mounted on a low-background sample holder. XRD patterns were recorded on a Rigaku SmartLab SE diffractometer (Japan) equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source (λ = 1.5406 Å). The scans were conducted over a 2θ range of 10° to 80° at a scanning rate of 2° per minute, with operating conditions set at 40 kV and 40 mA. Phase identification was performed by comparing diffraction patterns with standard reference data from the Powder Diffraction File (PDF) database.

Statistical analysis and reproducibility

All experiments were conducted using biologically independent triplicate microcosms unless otherwise stated. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), as indicated in the figure legends. No formal statistical comparisons were performed unless explicitly stated; trends are described based on reproducible patterns observed across independent experiments.
