Emerging diagnostic modalities in microbial keratitis: Beyond culture and smear
Authors: Victoria Vought, Rita Vought, Hassaam S. Choudhry, Mohammad H. Dastjerdi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microbial keratitis (MK) is a significant cause of corneal blindness worldwide, necessitating accurate and timely diagnosis for effective treatment. Traditional diagnostic techniques, such as corneal scraping for staining and culture, have limitations in sensitivity, specificity, and result time. This review analyzed peer-reviewed PubMed articles to explore emerging diagnostic modalities that address these challenges, focusing on molecular diagnostics, imaging techniques, and artificial intelligence (AI). Polymerase chain reaction and next-generation sequencing offer rapid and sensitive detection of the causative pathogen, although limitations include cost and false positives. Imaging technologies, such as in vivo confocal microscopy and anterior segment optical coherence tomography, allow for visualization of corneal pathology and may be useful for some etiologies. The emergence of AI with these imaging techniques has demonstrated significant success in classifying pathogens, which can be used to tailor antimicrobial treatment. The integration of these advanced diagnostic tools into clinical practice has the potential to significantly improve the management and outcomes of MK by enabling precise and rapid pathogen identification, guiding more effective treatment strategies, and ultimately reducing the morbidity associated with this condition. As these technologies continue to evolve and become more accessible, they are poised to transform the diagnostic approach to MK.

INTRODUCTION

Microbial keratitis (MK) is a common etiology of corneal opacity and causes inflammation and damage to the cornea through infection by bacteria, fungi, viruses, or protozoa such as Acanthamoeba. It is a significant cause of corneal blindness globally.[1] The incidence of MK varies significantly based on region, climate, and socioeconomic factors, with estimates ranging from 0.5 to 79.9 cases/10,000 individuals/year.[2] MK is diagnosed clinically with the presence of a corneal infiltrate on slit lamp examination. Patient history may also identify the presence of risk factors such as recent trauma, contact lens use with water exposure, or continuous wear. Patients often report a rapid onset of ocular pain, photophobia, and possibly decreased vision. Although those with small (<2 mm) peripheral ulcers without suspicious features may not require additional diagnostic testing, other cases require additional laboratory diagnostics. Successful treatment of MK is dependent on accurate and timely identification of the causative organisms. Corneal scraping for stain and culture remains the gold standard diagnostic test for MK. Although various stains, including Gram, Giemsa, potassium hydroxide, and periodic acid–Schiff, provide quick results, sensitivity and specificity remain low. Culture also has a variable positivity rate,[3] especially in those already treated with topical antimicrobials. A 2022 meta-analysis found an overall positive culture rate of 47% for bacterial keratitis.[4] Staining and culture exhibit varying positivity rates in fungal keratitis, depending on the type of selective culture media used.[5] In addition, culture can require significant time to result,[3] with an incubation period of at least 1–2 days for bacteria and 5–14 days for fungi.[3] Improving efficiency and identification of the causative organism in MK can have significant benefits in guiding the management of afflicted patients. This review explores the various new methods to address the diagnostic challenges presented in MK.

Methods

A comprehensive search of the literature was conducted using the PubMed database in July 2025 to identify relevant studies for this narrative review. The initial search included all articles published within the last 10 years (2015–2025). A combination of medical subject headings and free-text terms was utilized to collect studies. The primary search terms included: “microbial keratitis,” “infectious keratitis,” “diagnostics,” “diagnostic techniques and procedures,” “polymerase chain reaction,” “metagenomics,” “whole genome sequencing,” “proteomics,” “non-culture methods,” “molecular diagnostics,” “imaging techniques,” “advanced diagnostics,” and “artificial intelligence.” Boolean operators (AND; OR) were used to refine the search results, yielding 683 studies. Relevant studies that were peer-reviewed and available with full text in English were examined.

Molecular diagnostic testing

Polymerase chain reaction

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR), a technique that amplifies specific DNA sequences, has been utilized in the diagnosis of MK since the early 2000s.[67] PCR only requires a small sample of DNA, which is fragmented and amplified with predetermined primers to identify target sequences. A wide range of PCR techniques has been utilized in MK,[8] including methods that can detect many different pathogens at once, such as broad-range and multiplex PCR.[9] The use of pathogen-specific, pan-bacterial, and pan-fungal primers can be used, as well as primers targeting antimicrobial resistance. PCR has been demonstrated to assist in tailoring antimicrobial therapy to decrease the risk of future resistance and better treat the infection at hand.[10] Given its rapid identification of organisms and wide ability to identify bacteria, protozoa, and fungi, PCR has been widely adopted as an adjunct in MK diagnostics.

When compared to the gold standard culture, PCR has demonstrated overall higher sensitivity and comparably high specificity in diagnosing infectious keratitis.[8] For bacterial pathogens, this benefit can be variable; for instance, Somerville et al. reported a significant increase in the detection of pathogenic bacteria with the addition of PCR to culture.[11] Wagner et al. reviewed almost 500 bacterial keratitis samples and found 94% sensitivity and 100% specificity with PCR among bacterial pathogens.[12] Shimizu et al. highlighted the importance of quantifying bacterial DNA levels in diagnosing bacterial MK and reported 63.5% and 67.5% sensitivity and specificity of PCR, respectively, compared to 51.8% and 77.2% for conventional culture.[13] In comparison, Tuft et al. showed that corneal culture and PCR alone both exhibit high rates of negative results and frequent discordance and highlighted that these tests are complementary rather than interchangeable. Culture demonstrated higher positivity rates for bacterial keratitis (51.6% vs. 6.5% for PCR) and fungal keratitis (28.3% vs. 15.0% for PCR), while PCR showed greater sensitivity for Acanthamoeba (40.5% vs. 19.0% for culture).[14] Similarly, a 2022 evaluation of 70 bacterial keratitis cases found a PCR sensitivity of 25.0% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 14.7–37.9) compared to 95.6% (95% CI: 87.6–99.1) using conventional culture.[15]

Hammoudeh et al.[16] also reported a 73.5% (95% CI: 59.0–84.6) sensitivity for bacterial PCR versus 87.5 (95% CI: 72.4–95.3) using culture. In Acanthamoeba keratitis, PCR has also shown high sensitivity compared to conventional culture. Hoffman et al.[15] reported sensitivities for PCR and culture as 63.6% (95% CI: 48.3%–76.6%) and 36.5% (95%CI: 21.9%–51.2%), respectively. Yera et al. examined the use of different PCR primers and found variable sensitivities ranging from 73.3% to 86.7%, with an increase to 100% when used together (compared to 66.7% sensitivity in culture).[17] Therefore, the considerable variability in sensitivity and specificity for PCR suggests that it may be best utilized as a complementary diagnostic tool alongside culture rather than a replacement.

Because of PCR’s high sensitivity, it can also have a high false positive rate in the presence of dead or contaminant species in some cases. Although traditional PCR required physicians to identify the most likely causative pathogens for analysis, the newer generation of PCR can broadly analyze a wide range of microbes. This poses a challenge for physicians to decide which of the identified species may be the actual cause of the disease. Wong et al. underscored the importance of clinico-microbiological correlation of PCR to avoid false positives, such as in patients who had positive Acanthamoeba PCR but resolution without antiacanthamoebic treatment.[18] Given the significant consequences of undertreating MK, physicians are likely to treat in the setting of a positive result. However, the possibility of over-treating a false positive, along with the potential ocular toxicity, must still be considered.[18]

In fungal keratitis, reported PCR sensitivities range from 30.8% to 98.0%, compared to culture sensitivities of 41.7–71.4%.[815] PCR can serve as a useful adjunct to culture, especially in culture-negative keratitis.[16] Behera and Srigyan reported a noteworthy increase in fungal pathogen detection following the addition of PCR in culture-negative cases.[19] Todokoro et al. also demonstrated the use of PCR to rapidly differentiate clinically similar-appearing pathologies: Fungal keratitis and herpes simplex endotheliitis with feathery infiltration, highlighting the benefit of rapid diagnostics in improving outcomes for affected eyes.[20]

PCR has also been highlighted in its use in the diagnosis of atypical pathogens, such as mycobacteria and Nocardia. Specialized PCR systems developed for this use can identify these organisms that are fastidious and difficult to culture using traditional methods; however, these systems are expensive and not easily accessible.[21] Shrestha et al. validated PCR for mycobacteria using the LightCycle system, correctly categorizing mycobacteria tuberculosis and nontuberculous mycobacteria.[22] In a study by Dhiman et al.,[23] early diagnosis of nontuberculous mycobacterial keratitis was achieved in 62% of cases (10 out of 16) within 24–48 h using PCR-based DNA sequencing of the heat shock protein 65 (Hsp65) gene. The sensitivity and specificity of PCR in identifying Nocardia were found to be 88% and 76%, respectively, and PCR-based hsp65 gene sequencing has also been demonstrated to isolate ocular Nocardiosis.[24]

Metagenomic next-generation sequencing

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) has been applied to MK diagnostics and addresses some of the limitations present in other molecular diagnostic techniques. NGS consists of either targeted amplicon sequencing (TAS) or microbial whole-genome sequencing (MWGS), where TAS is more efficient and cost-effective. In Multiplex PCR-based NGS, a type of TAS, specific genomic regions or smaller genomes can be targeted that are prepared with multiplex PCR. TAS can be used to identify common pathogens, but may miss atypical or rare infections. In contrast with MWGS, the metagenomics approach allows for the simultaneous sequencing of hundreds to thousands of genes, and all of the microbes present within a sample can potentially be identified.[25]

NGS has been previously demonstrated to have high sensitivity,[26] especially in the setting of mixed infections,[27] given its ability to sequence all types of organisms without restriction. In a study of 114 patients with infectious keratitis, NGS had a positivity rate of 83.9% compared to 47.3% in secretion culture.[27] In evaluations of bacterial keratitis, NGS had a sensitivity of 61% and a specificity of 94% using DNA sequencing, as well as 100% sensitivity and specificity using RNA sequencing. The same study also examined fungal keratitis and found 65% sensitivity and 100% specificity using DNA sequencing, as well as 100% sensitivity and specificity with RNA.[26]

Another benefit of NGS is that its sensitivity is not affected by prior antimicrobial use, which frequently lowers culture yield.[3] A case series of three infected patients highlighted that multiplex PCR-based TGS was useful in the identification and management of superinfection keratitis.[28] Case reports of capnocytophaga keratitis, a rare and aggressive fastidious infection, were also accurately identified with NGS.[29] Although the cost of NGS is significantly higher than conventional culture with a similar processing time, NGS returns results faster in atypical organisms that may be difficult to identify. While the cost of NGS is anticipated to decrease over time, it still holds great potential value in the identification of fastidious pathogens.[30]

Of note, similar to PCR, diagnostic yield in the setting of other negative results must be confirmed by some other laboratory methods to avoid false positives.[30]

One major limitation of NGS is the presence of contaminants. For instance, a 2018 study found that 96% of reads generated were human, 0.3% were known vectors or contaminants, 2.4% could not be classified, and only 1.7% were microbial sequences.[31] Another consideration in the implementation of NGS is the presence of logistic barriers for routine clinical use. The high cost required for implementation, ranging up to hundreds of United States Dollars per specimen.[332] In addition, the need for bioinformatics support and infrastructure, including the appropriate facilities and staff with expertise to interpret nonspecific results, may limit it.

Proteomics

Several proteomic approaches, including enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, antibody arrays, and Western blot, have been piloted alongside traditional molecular testing for MK.[33] The majority of data collected via these approaches is focused on gaining insight into the pathogenesis of MK, allowing for a clearer understanding of inflammatory mediators in this disease process and subsequent changes in management that can be made.[3334] However, in the future, this information may be used in diagnostics as well. For instance, identification of the pathogen proteome in conjunction with mass spectrometry may be useful in MK diagnostics.[35]

Mass spectrometry

Mass spectrometry is another molecular technique that utilizes mass-to-charge ratios to quantitatively analyze specimens. This can be used to identify pathogens in MK as well,[8] classically for bacterial keratitis, but as an emerging technology in fungal keratitis. After the initial cost of purchase, which can be a significant barrier to its adoption in widespread clinical practice, mass spectrometry has low recurrent costs.[3637] Although it still depends on the initial culture of the specimen, mass spectrometry can be performed within minutes,[37] compared to a much longer wait time for traditional sensitivity testing. Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry is one of the most frequently used methods for identifying microbes. Newer technologies utilize automated techniques to identify pathogens; however, MALDI-TOF has been shown in some studies to have high sensitivity and specificity when compared to gold standard techniques.[37]

Mass spectrometry has been previously demonstrated to be useful in classifying bacterial pathogens by genus,[383940] and in distinguishing Moraxella to the species level.[41] However, among some closely related pathogens with similar mass spectra, mass spectrometry is less accurate in distinguishing organisms.[42] For instance, MALDI-TOF has lower specificity in separating Mycobacterium species.[42] Although robust literature comparing the diagnosis of fungal keratitis using conventional culture and mass spectrometry is still lacking, a comparison of MALDI-TOF to traditional culture and PCR found concordance in Aspergillus identification.[43]

Visualization and imaging in microbial keratitis

In vivo confocal microscopy

Another standard diagnostic technique is in vivo confocal microscopy (IVCM), which was first used in the examination of the cornea in 1985.[44] IVCM is a noninvasive imaging technique that allows for qualitative and quantitative analysis of corneal layers, nerves, and cells.[44] This is preferential for visualization of some blinding organisms, such as fungi and Acanthamoeba, but is limited in the ability to speciate many other pathogens.[25] Because the axial resolution is limited to 5–7 μm, this is not sufficient to detect bacteria.[21] However, both cyst and trophozoite forms of Acanthamoeba can be visualized on IVCM, allowing for IVCM to act as a useful adjunct to corneal culture.[45] In an examination of 125 individuals for A. keratitis, IVCM showed a sensitivity of 90.9% (95% CI: 78.3–97.5%) and a specificity of 90.1% (95% CI: 81.5–95.6%) in cases confirmed with positive culture or smear. However, challenges still remain in differentiating cysts from other findings, including leukocytes, epithelial cells, and debris.[44]

For fungal keratitis, IVCM has reported sensitivities and specificities ranging from 66.7%–94% to 78%–100%, respectively. IVCM can be used to visualize characteristic features of different fungi, including septate hyphae with 45-degree dichotomous branching in Aspergillus, 90° angle branching in Fusarium, and pseudohyphae in Candida.[2146] Therefore, IVCM may serve as a useful adjunct to culture to tailor antimicrobials in select cases where fungal keratitis is suspected.

Anterior segment optical coherence tomography

Anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) was developed in 1994 as a noninvasive modality that provides cross-sectional images of the cornea, including measurements of corneal and stromal thickness.[47] This allows for direct assessment of the depth of pathology and associated corneal edema. In comparison to IVCM, AS-OCT has had increased improvements in resolution and accessibility, resulting in its increased adoption in clinical practice.[47] Although slit-lamp examination is used for initial assessment and follow-up, AS-OCT can provide valuable data,[48] which are useful for both medical and possible surgical management of MK. Some examinations of AS-OCT have found differential progression of MK based on the pathogen type,[47] including a later reduction in stromal thickness in bacterial versus fungal keratitis. Other studies have characterized AS-OCT findings to differentiate between bacterial and fungal keratitis, such as the presence of early localized and diffuse necrotic stromal cystic spaces in fungal MK.[49] Stromal edema and descemet folds visualized on AS-OCT have also been more associated with Gram-positive bacterial keratitis, compared to diffuse infiltrates with stromal thinning in Gram-negative keratitis.[50] AS-OCT can also assist clinicians in the assessment of endothelial plaque, which has implications for surgical indications and pathogen differentiation. Visualization of the posterior corneal plaque without interference of corneal infiltrate on observation can allow for distinction between etiologies, where fungal plaque has an unclear and wavy corneal-plaque interface and bacterial plaque has a clear boundary.[51]

Furthermore, AS-OCT has been used to follow the progression of eyes with A. keratitis;[52] however, it has not been found to have any specific diagnostic advantage. In microsporidia keratitis, AS-OCT has been utilized to differentiate its hyperreflective lesions in the corneal epithelium from adenovirus nummular scars.[53] Although AS-OCT cannot independently replace any other diagnostic testing for MK, it may serve as a helpful adjunct in improving diagnostic accuracy.

Impression cytology

Impression cytology is conducted by applying a cellulose acetate filter to the cornea, which takes up superficial layers of the cornea for analysis. Impression cytology has been studied as an alternative to a blade for corneal scraping in sample collection. Kaye et al. found greater success in isolating microbes using a corneal impression membrane compared to a surgical blade in a study that included bacteria, Acanthamoeba, and fungi.[54]

A study of over 3000 samples, including bacteria, fungi, and Acanthamoeba, found comparable isolation rates between impression cytology and culture, including an increase in isolation rates of some bacterial species.[55] Examinations of specimens obtained from impression cytology in corneas with superficial A. keratitis found amoeba cysts after staining with modified Papanicolaou stain, with some also containing trophozoites.[5456] A disadvantage of impression cytology that remains is the limited depth of sample that can be obtained using this method, which restricts analysis of deeper corneal layers.

Artificial intelligence analysis of microbial keratitis imaging

The incorporation of artificial intelligence (AI) into diagnostic imaging analysis is rapidly expanding. MK is not excluded from this trend, and several studies have piloted the use of deep-learning AI in MK diagnostics, employing a wide range of imaging studies, including slit lamp photography, external photographs, and IVCM.[5758] Some have examined images taken using smartphone devices as well.[59]

The use of deep-learning AI has been examined numerous times,[57] with a highest area under the curve of 0.998, derived from corneal images taken on slit lamp cameras and smartphones with super macro mode.[59] A 2024 meta-analysis of 35 studies found an 86.2% (95% CI: 71.6–93.9) and 96.3% (95% CI: 91.5–98.5) sensitivity and specificity in infectious keratitis when compared to other microbiological exams, respectively.[60] In publications that compared AI to ophthalmologist review, sensitivity and specificity were comparable.[60] An app for iOS, CorneAI, was even piloted in 2024 using images published in the Cornea journal, yielding an AUC of 0.76 (95% CI: 0.67–0.85) for infectious keratitis.[61] AI interpretation of IVCM images has also been piloted to classify keratitis as fungal, Acanthamoeba, or other, with overall high diagnostic accuracy in making these distinctions. However, limitations in the models’ ability to classify heterogeneous samples likely reflect the high levels of expertise required for clinical review.

AI has been developed for different purposes in the analysis of MK. For instance, some models are only designed to differentiate between bacterial, viral, or fungal causes broadly.[62] Others have been developed to make finer differentiations, such as discrimination between filamentous-type and yeast-type fungal infections.[63] Kuo et al. developed algorithms to differentiate Pseudomonas from non-Pseudomonas keratitis in external eye images.[64] Essalat et al. examined models to automate the differentiation of early Acanthamoeba and fungal keratitis.[65] Given the wide range of applications for AI analysis in MK, it may serve as an important supplement in MK diagnosis in the future. Especially in areas with less access to ophthalmic care, AI may assist in the diagnosis and differentiation of pathogens.[66] However, despite these high-performance metrics, the bridge between research algorithmic success and real-world clinical implementation still requires additional development. Validation with regulatory approval in the presence of variable settings and diverse patient populations with improved patient outcomes is still required for adaptation into clinical practice.

CONCLUSION

The diagnostic landscape of MK is rapidly evolving beyond traditional culture and smear techniques, with molecular diagnostics like PCR and NGS offering enhanced sensitivity and specificity. These methods enable rapid identification of a wide range of pathogens, including atypical and fastidious organisms, facilitating timely and targeted treatment. Proteomics and mass spectrometry may contribute to detailed pathogen profiles, while imaging technologies such as IVCM and AS-OCT offer noninvasive insights into corneal pathology. The integration of AI into MK imaging offers exciting opportunities to customize and enhance diagnostic accuracy and accessibility. Although no individual diagnostic technique is superior, these modalities collectively are promising to improve the diagnostic accuracy and outcomes for those affected by MK, ultimately reducing the morbidity associated with this vision-threatening condition.
