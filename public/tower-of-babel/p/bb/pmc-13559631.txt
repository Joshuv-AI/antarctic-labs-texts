Network toxicology and interpretable modeling identify nicotine-associated neuroendocrine targets and a three-gene prognostic signature in breast cancer
Authors: Junyan Weng, Caifeng Ou, Jiamin Yi, Huan Yang, Chiwei Chen, Mei Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNicotine, a major tobacco metabolite and persistent environmental pollutant, is epidemiologically associated with breast cancer risk, yet its non-classical carcinogenic molecular mechanisms and prognostic value remain unclear. This study was designed to systematically elucidate the potential targets and molecular mechanisms underlying the prognostic impact of nicotine on breast cancer, alongside developing an interpretable prognostic risk model.MethodsThis study integrated network toxicology, interpretable modeling, and molecular docking. Potential nicotine targets were intersected with breast cancer differentially expressed genes, and hub genes were identified via protein-protein interaction (PPI) network. A prognostic model was built using Cox regression analysis and interpreted by the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP). The binding potential of nicotine to targets was predicted through in silico molecular docking.ResultsThirty-three nicotine-breast cancer common targets and 21 hub genes were identified. A three-gene prognostic model (TACR1, NPY1R, ADRB1) was constructed via Cox regression based on nicotine-related candidate targets, with the risk score (RS) formula: RS =−0.07 × NPY1R − 0.17 × ADRB1 − 0.19 × TACR1. This model could potentially stratify patients into high- and low-risk subgroups (P<0.001), and the RS may serve as an independent prognostic indicator. SHAP analysis identified TACR1 as the main contributor. Molecular docking computationally predicted favorable binding interactions between nicotine and the three candidate targets, and enrichment analysis pointed to G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) and neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction pathways.ConclusionsThis study computationally prioritized potential nicotine-associated breast cancer targets and constructed a three-gene prognostic model; in silico docking analysis suggested the potential binding of nicotine to these targets. We hypothesize that nicotine might function as a potential neuroendocrine disruptor to modulate breast cancer progression via GPCR signaling pathways. We propose a unique nicotine-associated three-GPCR-gene signature that provides new clues for environmental health risk assessment and prognostic biomarker development in breast cancer, yet further experimental validation is needed.Keywords: Breast cancer, nicotine, predictive model, molecular docking, network toxicology

Highlight box

Key findings

• This study identified 33 common targets and 21 hub genes associated with nicotine-associated breast cancer, established a three-gene (TACR1, NPY1R, ADRB1) prognostic model based on nicotine-associated candidate targets that shows potential to stratify patient risk and acts as an independent prognostic factor, predicted favorable nicotine-target binding potential via in silico molecular docking, and revealed that nicotine may regulate breast cancer progression through G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) and neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction pathways.

What is known and what is new?

• Epidemiological observations suggest nicotine exposure may correlate with unfavorable breast cancer clinical outcomes. Preclinical experiments imply nicotine could promote malignant progression of breast cancer through non-genotoxic routes, whereas its core molecular targets, systematic signaling networks and specialized prognostic biomarkers remain largely unclear.

• We constructed a combined computational pipeline integrating network toxicology, interpretable modeling and molecular docking to explore the potential regulatory effects of nicotine on breast cancer. We identified three candidate nicotine-related GPCR genes and developed a tentative exposure-relevant prognostic signature, together with in silico structural clues indicating possible binding between nicotine and these receptors. This work puts forward a speculative neuroendocrine disruption hypothesis to partially explain nicotine-associated breast cancer progression.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These findings provide new clues for environmental health risk assessment and prognostic biomarker development in breast cancer.

• Prospective clinical cohorts and experimental validation are required to confirm the causal mechanisms and clinical utility of the identified targets and model.

Introduction

Breast cancer is one of the malignant tumors with the highest incidence and mortality rates among women worldwide (1). An analysis of the overall burden of female breast cancer in 185 countries, using the Global Cancer Observatory, has shown that the number of new breast cancer cases and deaths is projected to rise by 38% and 68%, respectively, by 2050 (2). Currently, the etiology of breast cancer remains unclear, as its development involves an interplay between genetic susceptibility and environmental exposure. In addition to well-established risk factors such as ionizing radiation, a high-fat or high-estrogen diet, and alcohol abuse, exposure to environmental pollutants, particularly tobacco smoke, has been indicated by epidemiological studies to have a potential association with breast cancer occurrence, drug resistance, and metastasis (3,4). However, most previous studies have focused on typical carcinogens in tobacco, such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and nitrosamines, while the independent mechanism of action of nicotine, an alkaloid and the primary addictive component of tobacco, in breast cancer lacks a systematic elucidation.

Nicotine, a constituent of tobacco, has been recognized globally as an emerging contaminant of concern due to its widespread use and environmental persistence. Its long-term health risks are generally considered to be confined to addiction and cardiovascular effects. Though it is not a traditional carcinogen that induces DNA mutations, it remains a known trigger for the recurrence, progression, and drug resistance of bladder cancer (5). Nicotine acts as a tumor promoter in the initiation and progression of tumors by activating multiple neurogenic and non-neurogenic receptors [such as nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs), adrenergic receptors, neuropeptide receptors], interfering with intracellular signal transduction, promoting cell proliferation, and creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment (6,7). Recent basic research indicates that nicotine participates in regulating the invasion, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance of breast cancer cells through non-addictive pathways (8,9). A recent study by Kuo et al. (10), integrating clinical data and in vitro and in vivo models, revealed that nicotine enhances tumor invasiveness by upregulating the signaling pathways of the cholinergic receptor CHRNA9 and insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor, and demonstrates that nicotine exposure drives a stem cell-like metastatic phenotype in triple-negative breast cancer cells. However, its precise oncogenic mechanisms, core molecular targets, network regulation, and clinical prognostic significance remain to be elucidated.

Current research investigating the association between nicotine and breast cancer is predominantly confined to in vitro experiments or animal models, with a paucity of systematic studies focusing on target screening and mechanism elucidation based on clinical samples. Meanwhile, conventional statistical models possess inherent limitations in interpretability when elucidating intricate gene-environment interactions. To address these constraints, we adopted an integrated analytical framework combining network toxicology, interpretable modeling and molecular docking, which are conventional computational tools in current cancer research. By applying this mature pipeline to the specific research scenario of nicotine involvement and breast cancer, we constructed a multi-level interaction network of “nicotine-potential targets-breast cancer” to systematically explore the underlying toxicological and oncogenic mechanisms of nicotine. A polygenic prognostic risk model, built upon key targets and integrated with the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) interpretable analytical framework, quantifies the contribution of each gene to individual patient risk prediction and enhances model interpretability, thereby providing a clear rationale for therapeutic outcome prediction (11). Molecular docking is a classic in silico predictive tool for evaluating the interactions between compounds and their targets. By analyzing the binding free energy, binding conformations, and residue interactions, it predicts the feasibility of stable binding between compounds and key targets from physicochemical and spatial structural perspectives, providing structural clues for upstream network predictions and prognostic models.

This study aims to systematically elucidate the molecular network underlying nicotine-associated breast cancer initiation and progression and construct a clinically interpretable prognostic model based on nicotine-related G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) targets (TACR1, NPY1R, ADRB1). It also proposes the mechanistic hypothesis that nicotine may act as a potential neuroendocrine disruptor in breast cancer, providing preliminary bioinformatic insights for subsequent experimental validation, environmental risk assessment, clinical prognostic prediction, and targeted intervention. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0919/rc).

Methods

Collection of nicotine and breast cancer-related target genes

Potential human target genes affected by nicotine and breast cancer-associated genes were systematically collected from six public databases with unified data access on November 16, 2025. Specifically, nicotine-associated target genes were retrieved using the keyword “nicotine” from three sources: SwissTargetPrediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/, version 2019, species: Homo sapiens, confidence threshold: probability ≥0.1), which provides computationally predicted targets based on chemical similarity with known ligands; ChEMBL (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/chembl/, version 33, target confidence score ≥6), containing experimentally supported targets with binding data; and Similarity Ensemble Approach (SEA) (https://sea.bkslab.org/, version 2024-09, species: Homo sapiens, max TC >0.2), offering computationally predicted targets based on ligand structural similarity. Meanwhile, breast cancer-associated genes were collected using the keyword “Breast Cancer” from another three databases: GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/, version 5.26, Relevance score ≥5), which includes both experimentally validated and computationally predicted gene-disease associations; Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD) (https://ctdbase.org/, version 2025-10, inference score ≥10), containing manually curated and inferred associations; and Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) (https://omim.org/) by retaining all gene entries confirmed to be associated with breast cancer. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. After data acquisition, the dplyr package (version 1.1.4) in R software was applied to eliminate duplicate genes from all acquired gene datasets and retain unique gene symbols. Subsequently, a Venn diagram was employed to identify overlapping genes between nicotine targets and breast cancer-associated genes, and the intersecting genes were considered as potential targets of nicotine in breast cancer.

Collection and analysis of breast cancer-related data from the The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data from the breast cancer cohort of TCGA (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) were utilized for this analysis. The processing steps involved extracting normalized gene expression data and clinical phenotype information. Only primary tumor and tumor-adjacent normal tissue samples were included in subsequent analyses. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package, with the criteria for identifying differentially expressed genes (DEGs) set as an adjusted P<0.05 and |log2 fold change (FC)| >1. Raw fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) counts were extracted from TCGA. For each gene, expression values were transformed using log2 (FPKM + 1) prior to differential expression analysis and prognostic modeling.

Construction of protein-protein interaction (PPI) network and screening of hub genes

The common potential target genes of nicotine and breast cancer were submitted to the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/), with the organism restricted to Homo sapiens, and a medium confidence (0.400) set as the required minimum interaction score. The results obtained from the STRING database were then imported into Cytoscape software (version 3.10.4) to evaluate the topological properties of the PPI network (12). In Cytoscape, the cytoHubba plugin was used to calculate the node scores with 11 algorithms, including MCC, DMNC, MNC, Degree, EPC, BottleNeck, EcCentricity, Closeness, Radiality, Betweenness, and Stress. For each algorithm, the top 20 genes with the highest scores were designated as key genes. Subsequently, the key genes identified by the 11 algorithms were intersected, and those consistently present in at least 5 algorithms were defined as hub genes (13).

Functional enrichment analysis of target genes

To clarify the potential targets and underlying biological processes (BP) of nicotine-associated breast cancer, enrichment analysis of the identified hub genes was performed using Gene Ontology (GO) analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis, respectively. GO analysis, encompassing the evaluation of BP, molecular functions (MF), and cellular components (CC), was conducted to reveal the primary biological functions of relevant targets. KEGG enrichment analysis was employed to identify the key pathways associated with potential nicotine-associated breast cancer targets. R packages (org.Hs.eg.db, clusterProfiler, limma, enrichplot) were used to analyze and visualize the top 10 significantly enriched GO categories and KEGG pathways separately. The thresholds for significant enrichment were set at P<0.05 and false discovery rate (FDR) <0.25.

Construction of prognostic model based on nicotine-associated candidate targets and SHAP feature interpretation

To construct a prognostic model based on nicotine-associated candidate targets, the expression data of hub genes and clinical information from the TCGA database were analyzed. Initially, the log2(FPKM + 1)-transformed expression values of the hub genes were used as input for multivariate Cox regression, and risk scores (RSs) were calculated based on the coefficients and expression levels of these genes. The optimal cutoff point for stratifying patients into high- and low-risk groups was determined using the R package survminer. Kaplan-Meier curves and the log-rank test were then employed to evaluate the prognostic significance of the RSs by comparing overall survival between the two groups. Multivariate Cox regression analysis was conducted to determine whether the RS could serve as an independent prognostic factor for breast cancer patients. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was also performed to assess the predictive accuracy of the risk model. Additionally, we assessed model discrimination and calibration via the time-dependent concordance index (C-index) and 1-, 3-, and 5-year calibration curves. A nomogram integrating the three-gene signature and clinical factors was constructed for individualized quantitative prognostic prediction. For external validation, we retrieved gene expression and clinical data from an independent breast cancer cohort, GSE20685 (n=327), from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and time-dependent ROC analysis were applied to evaluate the risk stratification ability and predictive performance of the signature in this external cohort. Then, SHAP analysis was performed to interpret the prognostic model. All SHAP values were calculated from the linear predictor (log-hazard function) of the Cox model, instead of survival probability at a fixed time point (14). SHAP analysis was implemented using the R package shapviz (version 0.9.0). For SHAP approximation, the background distribution was established by randomly sampling 100 observations from the entire training dataset to approximate the marginal distribution of gene features. Censored survival data were preprocessed and analyzed within the Cox proportional hazards framework. SHAP was further adopted to interpret the linear predictor outputs derived from the Cox model. SHAP values were computed to quantify the marginal contribution of each gene feature to model outputs and rank feature importance, thereby identifying key predictive factors associated with patient survival. Of note, SHAP only reflects feature contributions to model predictions and cannot establish biological causal relationships between genes and clinical prognosis.

Molecular docking simulation and binding prediction

To further explore the intermolecular interactions between nicotine and the three core target proteins (TACR1, NPY1R, ADRB1), molecular docking was applied to computationally predict their binding modes and binding affinities using AutoDock Vina (version 1.2.3) (15,16). The three-dimensional structure of nicotine was retrieved from the PubChem Compound Database, and the crystal structures of NPY1R [Protein Data Bank (PDB) ID: 5ZBQ], TACR1 (PDB ID: 6E59), and ADRB1 (PDB ID: 7BTS) were obtained from the PDB. Water molecules and irrelevant non-protein components were removed via PyMOL (version 3.0.3) (17). The protein structures were then imported into AutoDockTools (version 1.5.7), where hydrogen atoms were added, Gasteiger charges were calculated, and AutoDock atom types were assigned. The binding pocket was defined based on the ligand-binding region observed in the corresponding co-crystal structure. Molecular docking was subsequently performed between nicotine and each corresponding target protein using AutoDock Vina. This program adopts an iterated local search algorithm to explore possible ligand-binding conformations and optimize docking poses. The binding free energy (ΔG, kcal/mol) served as the main indicator for binding affinity, with a more negative value indicating stronger and more stable binding. The docking simulation searched for favorable binding conformations within the predefined binding pocket, and the pose with the lowest predicted binding free energy was selected as the representative result and used for subsequent analysis. Finally, the three-dimensional docking complexes were visualized and analyzed using PyMOL (version 3.0.3), and two-dimensional interaction diagrams were generated using LigPlot+ (18). PyMOL displayed the spatial binding conformations and hydrogen-bonding interactions, while LigPlot+ plotted two-dimensional protein-compound interaction maps. Combined, these two visualization methods were applied to characterize the binding modes and key intermolecular interactions between nicotine and the target proteins. Sample size and missing data

The sample size was determined by the TCGA breast cancer cohort. No a priori sample size calculation was performed; however, a post hoc evaluation based on the 10 events per variable (EPV) rule was conducted. The multivariate Cox model included up to 7 candidate variables, and the number of overall survival events was 137, yielding an EPV of 19.6, which is above the recommended minimum of 10, indicating adequate sample size for model development. For the variables used in model construction (TACR1, NPY1R, ADRB1 expression, age, stage, survival status and time), there were no missing data after extraction from TCGA. Thus, complete-case analysis was performed without imputation.

Statistical analysis

Differential expression analysis of breast cancer RNA-seq data was performed using the DESeq2 package in R, with thresholds of adjusted P<0.05 and |log2FC| >1. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were conducted to screen prognostic genes and construct the risk model. Survival curves were plotted using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences between groups were assessed by the log-rank test. Time-dependent ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive accuracy of the prognostic model, with the area under the curve (AUC) calculated. SHAP analysis was applied to quantify the contribution of each gene feature in the model. For molecular docking, binding free energy was used to evaluate the binding stability between nicotine and target proteins. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. All multivariate Cox regression models reported hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and two-sided P values. All analyses were implemented in R software (version 4.5.1) with corresponding packages.

Results

Identification of potential target genes

RNA-seq data of breast cancer were obtained from the TCGA database, encompassing 1,116 primary tumor and 113 tumor-adjacent normal tissue samples. After filtering low-expression genes, a total of 14,590 genes were included for differential expression analysis using the DESeq2 package. Based on the criteria of adjusted P<0.05 and |log2FC| >1, we identified 3,299 DEGs, including 1,837 significantly up-regulated genes and 1,462 significantly down-regulated genes. The top 20 most significantly dysregulated genes were annotated in the plot (Figure 2A), and the top 50 most significantly up-regulated and down-regulated DEGs were visualized separately (Figure 2B). A total of 171 non-redundant human target genes of nicotine were curated from the Swiss Target Prediction, ChEMBL, and SEA databases after duplicate deletion; meanwhile, 10,989 breast cancer-related genes were collected from GeneCards, CTD and OMIM databases following the same deduplication criteria. Through cross-intersection analysis of the TCGA-derived breast cancer DEGs, nicotine target genes and breast cancer-related genes, we identified 33 potential target genes implicated in nicotine-associated breast cancer (Figure 2C). A PPI network was constructed for these 33 genes, followed by network analysis using Cytoscape. In the PPI network, the node size and color gradient (from blue to red) of each target were corresponded to its degree, where targets with higher degree values exhibited larger sizes and deeper red hues (Figure 2D).

Functional enrichment analysis of all potential target genes

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed on the 33 potential target genes separately, with the results sorted in ascending order by adjusted P value. The findings revealed that in the GO BP, the three most enriched processes were “regulation of membrane potential”, “adenylate cyclase-modulating G protein-coupled receptor signaling pathway”, and “positive regulation of MAPK cascade”. For GO CC, the most enriched components were “synaptic membrane”, “presynaptic membrane”, and “postsynaptic membrane”. Among the GO MF, the top three enriched functions were “hormone binding”, “catecholamine binding”, and “G protein-coupled amine receptor activity” (Figure 3A). The top 40 most significantly enriched GO pathways were analyzed using the aPEAR software package and visualized via an enrichment network plot, which revealed that most pathways were significantly associated with “amine transport” (Figure 3B). In the KEGG pathways, the top three enriched pathways were “Neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction”, “Bladder cancer”, and “Calcium signaling pathway” (Figure 3C). Similarly, the aPEAR software package was used to plot an enrichment network for the top 20 most significantly enriched KEGG pathways, which suggested a predominant enrichment in processes associated with “Non-small cell lung cancer” (Figure 3D).

Hub genes identification and prognostic model construction

Through computation with the Hubba-impl-0.1 plugin in Cytoscape, we obtained the top 20 genes from each of the 11 algorithms. By generating an Upset plot, we identified 21 hub genes present simultaneously in at least five algorithms (19,20), namely MMP9, ESR1, MMP1, MAOA, ADRB2, SLC6A4, DRD2, ADRB1, ERBB2, PRKCA, NR3C1, ALDH1A1, DRD4, CNR1, KDR, OPRD1, ADRA2A, TACR1, CYP2A6, PTGS2 and EGFR (Figure 4A). Further enrichment analyses were performed on these 21 hub genes, with the results presented in ascending order of adjusted P value (Figure 4B). Among the GO BP, the top three enriched processes were “regulation of tube diameter”, “blood vessel diameter maintenance”, and “regulation of tube size”. In the GO CC, the most enriched components were “synaptic membrane”, “presynaptic membrane”, and “neuronal dense core vesicle”. For the GO MF, the most enriched functions were “catecholamine binding”, “hormone binding”, and “G protein-coupled amine receptor activity”. In the KEGG pathway, the three most enriched pathways were “Neuroactive ligand signaling”, “Neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction”, and “Hormone signaling”. Prognosis-related genes were screened from the 21 hub genes, and three hub genes—NPY1R, ADRB1, and TACR1—were selected through multivariate Cox regression analysis to construct a nicotine-associated prognostic model. The RS formula was defined as: RS =−0.07 × NPY1R − 0.17 × ADRB1 − 0.19 × TACR1. Specifically, raw expression data of the three genes were obtained as FPKM values from TCGA, and a log2(FPKM+1) transformation was performed prior to model building. All three of these genes served as protective factors, with samples exhibiting higher expression levels of these genes demonstrating better prognosis. Based on the RS calculated by these three hub genes, patients were stratified into low-risk and high-risk groups for survival analysis (Figure 4C). The Kaplan-Meier survival curve revealed that compared with the low-risk group, patients in the high-risk group had an increased risk of death and a significantly poorer overall survival (P<0.0001) (Figure 4D). The multivariate Cox regression analysis incorporating age, gender, tumor stage, and RS revealed that the RS was an independent prognostic factor for overall survival of breast cancer patients (HR =1.580, 95% CI: 1.1123–2.24, P=0.01). Among the covariates, age (HR =1.034, 95% CI: 1.0194–1.05, P<0.001), stage III (HR =3.223, 95% CI: 1.8042–5.76, P<0.001) and stage IV (HR =10.506, 95% CI: 5.1633–21.38, P<0.001) were also significantly associated with poorer prognosis, whereas gender showed no independent prognostic value (P>0.05) (Figure 4E). The time-dependent ROC curve for the RS model yielded AUC values of 0.594, 0.592, and 0.590 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival prediction, respectively, indicating a moderate and statistically significant predictive accuracy (Figure 4F). To further evaluate the discriminatory performance of the prognostic model, we calculated the time-dependent C-index, with a value of 0.750 (95% CI: 0.705–0.796) (Figure S1). Calibration curves for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival indicated acceptable consistency between predicted and actual survival outcomes (Figure S2). We built a nomogram integrating the three-gene signature and clinical factors to enable individualized quantitative survival prediction for breast cancer patients (Figure S3). For external validation, the three-gene signature and corresponding RS formula were applied to an independent breast cancer cohort obtained from the GEO database (GSE20685, n=327). The optimal RS cutoff value of −0.666 was determined using the R package survminer in the TCGA training cohort, and this unified threshold was adopted to stratify all patients into high- and low-risk subgroups in the external cohort. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis suggested that patients in the high-risk group tended to have worse overall survival. Although moderate separation was observed between the two groups, the difference did not reach statistical significance (log-rank P=0.36, Figure S4), possibly due to the limited sample size or incomplete adjustment for molecular and treatment-related confounders in this external dataset. In the external cohort, the time-dependent ROC analysis produced AUC values of 0.505, 0.549, and 0.584 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival prediction, respectively (Figure S5). Overall, the nicotine-associated three-gene prognostic model constructed using the three prognosis-related genes derived from the 21 hub genes in the PPI network may offer a tentative approach for breast cancer prognosis prediction, while its predictive performance remains to be further improved.

SHAP feature interpretation of the prognostic model

The SHAP bar plot illustrated the importance of each gene feature in the three-gene prognostic model based on nicotine-associated candidate targets (Figure 5A). By ranking the gene features in descending order of their mean absolute SHAP values, TACR1 (0.177) exerted the largest overall predictive contribution to the Cox model output across the entire patient cohort, followed by NPY1R (0.174), while ADRB1 (0.142) presented a relatively lower but notable contribution. These results demonstrated that TACR1 expression was the most influential feature for model prediction. Notably, this ranking only reflects the feature importance (contribution weight) of each gene, rather than the direction of their effects on prognostic risk. The SHAP beeswarm plot (Figure 5B) revealed the non-linear relationships between each gene and prognostic risk. Overall, high expression tendencies of all three genes were correlated with lower SHAP values, corresponding to RSs of a favorable prognosis. This distribution trend was consistent with the negative coefficients of each feature gene in the model, further supporting the hypothesis that up-regulated expression of these genes may exert protective effects in prognosis from the perspective of the prognostic model. To better illustrate the contributions of these three genes in model prediction, we presented the SHAP waterfall plot (Figure 5C) and force plot (Figure 5D) for a representative sample from the low-risk group. The baseline prediction value for this sample was E[f(x)] =−0.666, with purple representing negative values and orange representing positive values. The SHAP value of the sample was calculated by integrating the effects of the three gene variables, yielding a relatively low SHAP value [f(x) =−0.724]. For this representative single low-risk patient selected for SHAP visualization, the individual contribution of the three prognostic genes to the RS was as follows: ADRB1 (expression level 1.74) contributed −0.181 and acted as the primary risk-reducing factor in this case; TACR1 (expression level 0.833) contributed +0.0685 and slightly elevated the RS of this individual; NPY1R (expression level 3.76) also produced a mild risk-increasing effect (+0.0555) in this sample. This individual deviation from the overall cohort trend reflects the non-linear effects and patient heterogeneity of gene expression.

Molecular docking simulation and binding prediction

Co-crystal structure-based molecular docking was performed to systematically explore the interactions between nicotine and three core target proteins. PyMOL and LigPlot+ were used to visualize the optimal (lowest-energy) binding conformations of nicotine with each target protein. It is generally accepted (16,21) that a docking energy value lower than −4.25 kcal/mol indicates a certain degree of binding activity, a value lower than −5.0 kcal/mol indicates favorable binding activity, and a value lower than −7.0 kcal/mol indicates strong binding activity. These widely accepted criteria are used for reference only, and these computational thresholds do not represent definitive proof of high ligand-receptor affinity. The results (Figure 6A-6C, Table 1) showed that nicotine bound favorably to the binding pockets of all three proteins. For NPY1R, the binding energy was −9.984 kcal/mol, and nicotine formed three hydrogen bonds with the amino acid residues THR280, ASN283, and ASP287 of the protein. For TACR1, the binding energy was −10.59 kcal/mol, with one hydrogen bond to residue TYR272. For ADRB1, the binding energy was −6.158 kcal/mol, and nicotine established four hydrogen bonds with residues THR1143, SER1228, SER1232, and ASN1363. These hydrogen-bond interactions enhanced protein-ligand affinity, stabilized the docking complexes, and may play vital roles in maintaining protein function. Collectively, these computational results suggest that nicotine may bind stably and spontaneously to the three target proteins. Such interactions may modulate downstream signaling pathways, which is hypothesized to further affect the progression and prognosis of breast cancer.

Discussion

As a ubiquitously existing tobacco metabolite, nicotine is one of the most frequently encountered environmental pollutants, and its long-term exposure is associated with a variety of adverse health outcomes such as lung cancer and cardiovascular diseases. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 4 million cases indicated that breast cancer patients with prolonged exposure to smoking environments after treatment had an elevated risk of developing second primary cancers, with a standardized incidence ratio of 1.25 (P<0.001) (22). In this study, we integrated network toxicology, interpretable modeling, and molecular docking techniques to systematically elucidate the potential targets, molecular networks, and prognostic value of nicotine in breast cancer. Through multiple databases, we screened 33 potential target genes and constructed a PPI network, identifying three hub genes: NPY1R, ADRB1, and TACR1. Based on these hub genes, a three-gene prognostic model based on nicotine-associated candidate targets was constructed and preliminarily evaluated, and the overall feature importance was assessed using SHAP values, which quantify each gene’s contribution to model predictions. Furthermore, molecular docking simulations provided structural clues suggesting potential binding interactions between nicotine and the proteins encoded by these hub genes. Findings from this study may shed light on the molecular mechanisms behind nicotine-associated breast cancer development. Additionally, the integrated application of these conventional computational tools provides a feasible research paradigm for exploring the molecular mechanism of environmental pollutants in tumor progression.

The three hub genes involved in the prognostic model construction—TACR1, NPY1R, and ADRB1—all belong to the GPCR superfamily. Previous studies indicate that air pollutants and cigarette smoke can activate the GPCR signaling pathway, interfere with the regulatory processes of neurotransmitters, hormones and stress signals, and thereby exacerbate pulmonary diseases (23), which is consistent with common scientific knowledge. Tachykinin receptor 1 (TACR1) is predominantly expressed in the central and peripheral nervous systems and is involved in BP such as pain modulation, neurotransmitter release, and immune regulation, with substance P serving as its major endogenous ligand. Recent research reveals that the metastatic process of breast cancer is highly reliant on substance P secreted by sensory neurons. This neuropeptide can induce the release of single-stranded RNA, which then acts on the TLR7 receptors of tumor cells, thus activating a non-canonical, PI3K-dependent pro-metastatic gene expression program (24). This mechanism links neuronal activity to the oncogenic signaling pathways of innate immune receptors within the tumor microenvironment (TME) and is associated with poor prognosis in breast cancer patients. Based on this mechanism, depletion of TACR1 in tumor cells or administration of TACR1 antagonists such as aprepitant may block the pro-metastatic signaling axis between neurons and cancer cells (25), which warrants further clinical evaluation. In the present study, TACR1 was incorporated into the prognostic risk model with a negative Cox coefficient of −0.19, indicating the average trend across all patients: higher TACR1 expression was generally correlated with lower prognostic risk and a favorable overall survival in the whole cohort, which is consistent with the findings of previous drug sensitivity analyses (26). This population-level trend is consistent with the protective role of TACR1. Of note, a small number of individual samples may show inconsistent effects due to gene heterogeneity, as reflected in the SHAP analysis of the representative single patient above. Combined with our in silico docking results, we hypothesize that nicotine may interact with TACR1 via potential binding, and the two isoforms of TACR1 (full-length and truncated) could differentially affect breast cancer development. Existing studies have indicated that the expression of full-length TACR1 is negatively correlated with the initiation and progression of breast cancer (27).

In the context of environmental nicotine exposure, our in silico analysis suggests that elevated expression of neuropeptide Y1 receptor (NPY1R) and adrenergic beta-1 receptor (ADRB1) may correlate with a favorable prognosis in breast cancer patients. Although NPY1R showed a slight risk-increasing effect in one representative individual sample in the SHAP analysis, this individual heterogeneity does not contradict the overall protective trend observed in the entire patient cohort. As the most abundant neuropeptide in the mammalian brain, NPY1R is involved in the regulation of multiple physiological functions, including appetite, pain perception, circadian rhythm and memory, and it can also alleviate anxiety and stress as well as reduce blood pressure (28-36). ADRB1, by contrast, participates in the modulation of myocardial activity, sleep, and body temperature regulation (37-40). It has been reported that in breast cancer, the expression level of NPY1R is significantly higher in the luminal A subtype than in other subtypes, with a gradual decrease in the order of luminal A, luminal B, triple-negative, and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) positive subtypes, suggesting that its high expression may be associated with longer recurrence-free survival and overall survival. Based on our computational predictions, we speculate that nicotine may act on NPY1R through potential binding and further influence the clinical outcome of breast cancer. In vitro cellular experiments demonstrated that the expression of NPY1R is down-regulated in an endocrine-resistant microenvironment, indicating that NPY1R may serve as a biomarker for predicting the sensitivity and efficacy of endocrine therapy in hormone receptor-positive (HR+) breast cancer patients and holds promise to be a novel therapeutic target for estrogen receptor-positive (ER+) breast cancer (41,42). ADRB1 has been suggested in previous studies to be closely associated with the occurrence and progression of breast cancer and to be involved in the construction of multiple prognostic models (24,43,44). Based on our network analysis and docking predictions, we hypothesize that nicotine binds to ADRB1 to modulate breast cancer progression. In prognostic models involving circadian rhythm-related genes or anesthesia-related drug target genes, high ADRB1 expression was correlated with a more favorable prognosis in breast cancer, which is consistent with the findings of the present study. However, several studies have reported that ADRB1 exerts an oncogenic effect or is associated with an unfavorable prognosis. For instance, BRCA1 deficiency in ovarian cancer cells leads to increased ADRB1 expression, which promotes the generation of cAMP; this in turn inhibits DNA damage-induced apoptosis by abrogating p53 accumulation and upregulates the immunosuppressive factor DKK1 to suppress the proliferation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes, ultimately conferring apoptosis resistance in ovarian cancer cells (45). The seemingly contradictory prognostic associations of ADRB1 in breast cancer and ovarian cancer precisely reveal the context dependence and functional pleiotropy of signaling pathways in tumor biology. These discrepancies may be mediated by the following factors. First, the specificity of study populations and exposure backgrounds. Nicotine, as a neuroactive substance, similar to anesthetic drugs such as propofol and midazolam, may remodel the TME through sustained activation or interference with the adrenergic system (46-48). In light of our computational findings, we presume that under specific nicotine exposure conditions, high ADRB1 expression may trigger adaptive or compensatory signaling feedback distinct from the conventional state, thereby potentially inhibiting the progression of malignant tumors. ADRB1 may thus exert divergent effects under different exposure backgrounds. Second, potential functional remodeling of receptors caused by nicotine exposure. GPCR signaling exhibits biphasic activation properties with high complexity and plasticity (49). For example, GPCRs undergo regulatory processes such as desensitization and endocytosis when mediated by the multifunctional receptor proteins β-arrestins (βarrs) (50). Although sustained activation of ADRB1 may promote cell proliferation via cAMP in the short term, it is likely to induce feedback inhibition, receptor desensitization, or activation of apoptosis-related bypass pathways under long-term nicotine stimulation. The protective role of ADRB1 observed in this study may reflect its capacity to exert specific protective functions at distinct disease stages or under particular signaling intensities. Collectively, SHAP analysis quantified the predictive contribution of each gene to the Cox regression model. As an interpretable modeling approach, SHAP merely assesses feature importance for model outputs and cannot establish biological causality linking TACR1, NPY1R and ADRB1 to breast cancer prognosis. The gene ranking derived from SHAP serves as a computational hypothesis, which needs to be verified by follow-up functional experiments.

Through GO and KEGG enrichment analyses, we conducted an in-depth investigation into the molecular mechanisms underlying nicotine-associated breast cancer. The results indicated that 33 potential target genes and 21 hub genes were significantly enriched in a series of highly relevant neural signaling and endocrine regulation pathways. This computational inference suggests that the effects of nicotine on breast cancer may extend beyond conventional cytotoxic or genotoxic mechanisms, and that nicotine could potentially act as a systemic neuroendocrine disruptor interfering with the body’s homeostatic network and contributing to tumorigenesis and progression (51). The GO CC analysis revealed that the targets were significantly enriched in “synaptic membrane”, “presynaptic membrane”, and “postsynaptic membrane” (Figure 3A), indicating that the functional sites of nicotine are primarily localized at the key structural components of neuronal communication. Based on enrichment results, it is speculated that nicotine could potentially interfere with chemical synaptic signal transmission across the blood-brain barrier or via peripheral nerve endings, thereby linking central nervous system activity with abnormalities in the TME (52,53). The GO MF analysis showed a significant enrichment of targets in “catecholamine binding”, “hormone binding” and “G protein-coupled amine receptor activity” (Figure 3A), which clarified the ligand characteristics of the potential targets of nicotine—these are precisely the core molecular switches involved for the body’s stress response, metabolic regulation, and maintenance of homeostasis (54). In silico binding predictions yield computational clues, suggesting that nicotine may disrupt normal neuroendocrine regulatory pathways through a potential mechanism of “molecular mimicry”. Meanwhile, the GO BP analysis revealed that its downstream signaling involves “regulation of membrane potential”, “adenylate cyclase-modulating G protein-coupled receptor signaling pathway”, and “positive regulation of MAPK cascade” (Figure 3A). This constitutes a coherent signaling cascade from the alteration in cellular electrophysiology to the production of second messengers, and subsequently to the activation of pro-proliferative signals, which represents a typical mechanism underlying GPCR-mediated cellular effects. Notably, the enrichment of “regulation of membrane potential” suggests that nicotine may modulate cellular excitability by regulating ion channel activity, which could be related to apoptosis resistance and metabolic reprogramming in tumor cells (55). Finally, the KEGG pathway analysis integrated the multi-pathway effects of nicotine at a systemic level. The most significantly enriched “neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction” pathway is a pivotal finding (Figure 3C), as it encompasses nearly all the core GPCR targets identified in this study and implies the potential of nicotine to simultaneously interfere with multiple neurotransmitter systems (e.g., catecholamine, neuropeptide) (56). The enrichment of the “calcium signaling pathway” (Figure 3C) forms a mechanistic link with the “regulation of membrane potential” result from the GO BP, reflecting the close association between calcium ions as second messengers and the regulation of membrane potential (57). These two pathways together constitute a universal route through which neural signals modulate cellular functions. Furthermore, the enrichment of the “bladder cancer” pathway (Figure 3C) provides important corroborative evidence. It suggests that the nicotine-mediated network identified in this study is not exclusive to breast cancer but may also participate in the pathogenesis of other smoking-related epithelial tumors, such as bladder, lung and pancreatic cancers (58), which enhances the reliability and generalizability of our findings.

Previous nicotine-related breast cancer studies mostly focused on individual genes or isolated pathways, without systematic transcriptome-wide screening of downstream targets (8-10). Existing prognostic models using TACR1, NPY1R or ADRB1 were developed from general tumor, anesthesia or circadian rhythm markers (36,41,43). Our three-gene signature, by contrast, originates from nicotine-associated molecules, offering bioinformatic clues connecting nicotine exposure to breast cancer prognosis. Although GPCR and neuroactive ligand-receptor pathways are well characterized in cancers (23,56), whether nicotine disrupts these pathways by interacting with the three receptors remains underexplored. Accordingly, our innovations lie in target screening, signature construction and mechanistic speculation. First, we systematically identified nicotine-associated hub genes at the transcriptomic network level for breast cancer. Second, we established a three-GPCR-gene prognostic signature specifically based on nicotine-related targets, which differs from existing models constructed using common tumor biomarkers. Third, we put forward a novel hypothesis that nicotine may act as a neuroendocrine disruptor to interfere with GPCR and neuroactive ligand-receptor signaling via binding to TACR1, NPY1R and ADRB1.

Although computational predictions suggest that nicotine may bind to the three GPCRs (TACR1, NPY1R, and ADRB1) to modulate downstream signaling, several alternative explanations should be carefully considered in the absence of experimental validation. First, the canonical primary targets of nicotine are nAChRs. Nevertheless, any regulatory effects of nicotine on the GPCRs predicted here might not result from direct binding but could instead be indirectly mediated through nAChR activation and its subsequent downstream signaling cascades. Second, as reported in various tumors such as bladder and gastric cancers (59-61), the biological functions of GPCRs are highly dependent on the TME. These receptors have been reported to participate in immune modulation, angiogenesis, and tumor invasion within the TME (62,63). Thus, the expression profiles of these GPCRs may partly derive from non-tumor CC within the TME, including neurons, immune cells, and fibroblasts. Therefore, the prognostic value of these GPCRs may reflect the compositional features of the TME rather than cell-autonomous effects of nicotine on breast cancer cells. Third, the expression of GPCRs differs markedly among various breast cancer subtypes and is associated with patient prognosis and drug response. For instance, in triple-negative breast cancer, certain GPCRs have been reported to remodel the TME into an immunosuppressive state, thereby driving tumor progression and metastasis (64). This suggests that our computational findings may also be correlated with the hormone receptor status of breast tumors, warranting further stratified analyses across different breast cancer subtypes. Collectively, while our study generates the hypothesis that nicotine may specifically interact with these three GPCRs, rigorous in vitro and in vivo experiments are required to validate this speculation in future work.

Despite integrating computational and predictive evidence across multiple dimensions, the present study has several limitations. First, all data used in this work were mainly obtained from retrospective analyses of public databases. The TCGA clinical dataset lacks real-world information regarding patients’ smoking status, serum nicotine concentrations and quantitative exposure levels, so we cannot classify patients by actual nicotine exposure. For this reason, the current model is a gene expression-based prognostic model derived from nicotine-associated candidate targets, rather than a model built on actual nicotine exposure stratification. Accordingly, the observed association between gene signatures and clinical prognosis only reflects statistical correlation rather than direct causality. Future work will enroll independent clinical cohorts with detailed nicotine exposure data to externally validate the model and explore the relationships among gene expression, nicotine exposure and clinical prognosis in breast cancer patients. Second, the candidate targets screened via network toxicology and molecular docking are purely computational predictions. Although we validated the docking protocol by co-crystallized ligands, the predicted binding of nicotine to TACR1, NPY1R, and ADRB1 does not constitute proof of direct interaction or functional modulation. To validate these preliminary findings, we will conduct comprehensive follow-up functional experiments. The experimental scheme includes nicotine treatment of breast cancer cell lines with differential TACR1, NPY1R and ADRB1 expression profiles, quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and Western blotting detection to quantify the messenger RNA (mRNA) and protein levels of the three receptors, gene knockdown and pharmacological receptor antagonist assays, functional detection of cAMP and calcium signaling, reporter assays for downstream GPCR signaling, and in vitro binding assays to verify the direct interactions between nicotine and target proteins. Furthermore, additional in vivo animal models will be adopted to systematically clarify the molecular mechanisms by which nicotine drives breast cancer progression through these key targets. Third, this study was solely based on mRNA expression profiles, without addressing protein expression, post-translational modifications or epigenetic regulation. Integrative multi-omics analyses should be conducted in future studies to fully characterize the regulatory network of nicotine action in breast cancer. In addition, our study lacks data regarding breast cancer molecular subtypes, hormone receptor status, tumor grade, and treatment regimens. These variables are key prognostic factors for breast cancer and may confound the associations identified in our analysis. Additionally, we did not assess the prognostic performance of this three-gene model across major molecular subtypes, namely luminal, HER2-positive, and triple-negative breast cancer. Accordingly, we will conduct systematic subtype-stratified analyses and incorporate these clinicopathological variables into model development in future research. No significant difference was observed in the external validation using the GSE20685 cohort, which is likely due to the small sample size and insufficient follow-up time, which lowered statistical power. In follow-up studies, we will enroll large and reliable external cohorts to comprehensively evaluate the robustness and subtype specificity of this prognostic signature.

Conclusions

In summary, using the integrated computational pipeline of network toxicology, interpretable modeling, and molecular docking, this study computationally prioritized TACR1, NPY1R, and ADRB1 as putative functional targets of nicotine in breast cancer, constructed a clinically interpretable three-gene prognostic model based on nicotine-associated candidate targets, with modest predictive performance, and predicted potential structural binding interactions between nicotine and these targets via in silico docking. These findings generate the hypothesis that prolonged low-dose nicotine exposure may induce sustained, low-grade activation or inhibition of these critical neuroendocrine receptors, leading to chronic dysfunction of the stress response system, autonomic nervous system, and immunomodulatory network, ultimately shaping a TME conducive to tumorigenesis, immune evasion and progression. All the above in silico findings generate target-specific novel research hypotheses regarding nicotine-associated breast cancer pathogenesis. Further prospective clinical cohort investigations, together with in vitro and in vivo wet-lab experiments, are essential to validate the causal relationship and clinical application value of our predicted signature and candidate targets.
