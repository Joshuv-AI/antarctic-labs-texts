Sources and environmental occurrence of per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) in the environment: a threat to Central European agriculture
Authors: Wiesław Szulc, Stanisław Gawroński, Dominika Gancarczyk, Witold Szulc, Beata Rutkowska, Małgorzata Majder-Łopatka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) are persistent environmental pollutants of growing concern due to their wide use, resistance to degradation, and potential risks to human health and ecosystems. This study presents a comprehensive assessment of PFAS contamination in soils from a more than century-old long-term fertilisation experiment, fire training grounds, and landfills, as well as their presence in atmospheric precipitation conducted under Central European environmental conditions. Soil, leachate, and precipitation samples were analysed for 54 PFAS compounds using the HPLC-MS/MS method. Elevated concentrations, mainly of short-chain compounds (PFOS, PFOA, PFHxS, PFBA), were detected at sites where AFFF (aqueous film-forming foam) was used (Σ 2537 µg·kg−1) and in landfill leachates (Σ 2325–3664 ng·dm−3). In contrast, soils from the fertilisation experiment without biosolid or sewage application showed PFAS at detection limits. Atmospheric deposition was identified as a potential PFAS source in agricultural areas, with PFBA prevailing in precipitation (14.8 ng·dm−3; over 70% of total PFAS). The results indicate an environmental risk linked to PFAS dispersion, especially near firefighting and landfill sites, highlighting the need for continued monitoring and strategies to limit PFAS mobility in soils.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11356-026-38041-y.

Introduction

Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) are classified, due to their chemical structure, as so-called forever chemicals. They comprise a group of more than 5000 different organic compounds containing an aliphatic chain of varying length (typically C4–C16), in which fluorine is placed within the carbon and hydrogen chain structures, replacing hydrogen atoms fully or partially. The bond between carbon (C) and fluorine (F), due to the high electronegativity of the latter, has proved to be one of the strongest known in chemistry. In addition, the size of the fluorine atom is suitable to fit closely to the carbon chain and protect it from interaction with other atoms. The dissociation energy of the carbon–fluorine bond is 485 kJ mol−1, which means that fluoroalkyl substances are resistant to thermal, chemical, and biological degradation (Brunn et al. 2023; Luo 2007).

PFAS include, among others, perfluorinated surfactants occurring in large quantities in FFFP (film-forming fluoroprotein products) and AFFF (aqueous film-forming foam) used by fire brigades. The major advantage of using perfluorinated surfactants is the formation of an insulating water film, which improves the extinguishing efficiency of foams. The drawback is the negligible biodegradability of the surfactants used. As is widely known, the ability of compounds to undergo biodegradation in the environment depends, among other factors, on their chemical structure. The perfluorinated organic compounds used are based on hydrocarbon structures. It can be assumed that the fundamental bond in the compounds under consideration is the bond between carbon atoms. The C–C bond is relatively long and of relatively low energy, which is why these bonds often break. The dissociation energy of the C–C bond is 358 kJ mol−1 (Majder-Łopatka et al. 2022). Carbon atoms are capable of forming bonds other than C–C. Based on the electron configuration of the carbon atom, it can be observed that it is able to form four bonds (it has four unpaired electrons). This means that apart from C–C bonds, other bonds such as C–H or C–F will also occur. Hydrogen is a substituent that allows the unpaired electrons of the carbon atom to bond. Another substituent occurring in large amounts in PFAS is fluorine. It should be emphasised that fluorine forms very strong bonds with carbon. This is because the length of the C–F bond is relatively small and its energy relatively high. This can be understood in the sense that bacteria would need to generate a large amount of energy to break such a bond, which is not beneficial to them. A further fact confirming that the C–F bond is very strong is that fluorine has the highest electronegativity in the entire periodic table on the Pauling scale. This means that fluorine also has the ability to attract the electrons of the carbon atom when forming a chemical compound with it. Another fact is that fluorine in organic compounds is a substituent and not part of the main chain. During compound breakdown, the chance of the C–F bond breaking is very small. In the environment, it is usually the bonds between carbon atoms that are broken.

Among all PFAS, perfluoroalkyl sulfonic acids (PFSAs) and perfluoroalkyl carboxylic acids (PFCAs) attract the most interest, with perfluorooctane sulfonic acid (PFOS) and perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) being the most extensively studied to date (Ateia et al. 2019). These substances are very widely used in different branches of industry. They have been continuously entering the natural environment since the 1940 s (Wang et al. 2014). It is estimated that globally, between 1951 and 2002, between 2600 and 5000 tonnes of PFAS C4–C14 was released into the environment (Johnson 2022). At the same time, it is predicted that by 2030, a further 5510 tonnes of PFAS C4–C14 may enter the environment (Johnson 2022). The main sources of PFAS in the environment are aqueous film-forming foams (AFFF) used for firefighting, sewage sludge and composts from municipal waste applied in agriculture, plant protection agents, leachates from landfills, wastewater treatment plants and industrial plants producing fluoropolymers and using them in the production of food packaging, water-repellent and stain-resistant clothing, electrical and electronic equipment, and fire extinguishing agents (Costello and Lee 2024; Dasu et al. 2022; D'Ambro et al. 2021; Elgarahy et al. 2024; Glüge et al. 2020; Pepper et al. 2021). In addition to point sources of PFAS in the environment, such as fluorochemical processing plants (FPP), pesticides, wastewater treatment plants (WWTP), fire brigade training grounds, or landfills, these compounds also enter the environment from diffuse, non-point sources with no defined origin or location, usually involving emissions from many sites such as surface runoff, atmospheric deposition, airborne transport of volatile PFAS, and biological and chemical degradation of precursor compounds to persistent PFAS (Benskin et al. 2012; Donley et al. 2024; Elgarahy et al. 2024).

Due to their persistence, PFAS are constantly accumulating in the environment. They are present in all environmental compartments—in water, sediments, air and soil, as well as in living organisms. They are transported through the atmosphere over long distances and occur even in remote regions of the globe such as the Arctic and Antarctica (Del Vento et al. 2012; Xie et al. 2015).

The detection of PFAS in rainwater in recent years at concentrations several times higher than the permissible drinking water standards has been widely reported (Coates and Harrington 2024; Dreyer et al. 2010), with relatively persistent metabolites accounting for a significant proportion of the total PFAS burden (Kali et al. 2025). It has been shown that in precipitation, some PFAS are associated with particulate matter (PM), while gaseous forms remain in the atmosphere and may even slightly increase during the initial phase of precipitation (Li et al. 2024). Similar relationships were obtained in the study by Finnian et al. (2020). High levels of PFOA and PFSA have been reported in stormwater in North Carolina, despite a 20-year ban on their production. The authors consider several potential sources, including transformation and arrival with air from the ocean, and indicate the highest levels of these compounds between July and September in all studied locations (Zhou et al. 2021).

Pronounced seasonal variability in PFAS concentrations in precipitation has been documented, as demonstrated by a field experiment conducted in the Arctic on Svalbard. Precipitation samples were collected in midsummer during a 24-h daylight period and in winter during a 24-h polar night. The concentrations of all measured metabolites increased during continuous solar radiation by a factor of 7.6 to 71, depending on the compound (Hartz et al. 2024). This study provides the first evidence for the possible atmospheric formation of PFOS from precursors.

Many studies indicate that significant amounts of PFAS can accumulate in soil and from there enter further links in the food chain, where they accumulate. Plants have the ability to take up PFAS through both leaves and roots. However, the mechanism of uptake from soil via roots is dominant and better understood than foliar uptake (Xu et al. 2024). The uptake of PFAS by plants from soil depends on many factors, the most important of which are sorption–desorption processes, which determine their transport and mobility, as well as bioavailability and uptake, thus influencing their impact on the environment (Kookana et al. 2023). PFAS accumulated in soil become available to plants after dissolving in pore water (Nassazzi et al. 2023; Xu et al. 2024). It has been shown that PFAS content in plants increases with their content in soil (Lesmeister et al. 2021). More hydrophilic compounds are more readily taken up by plant roots than hydrophobic compounds, which are retained by the soil solid phase (Lesmeister et al. 2021; Nassazzi et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2023). Short-chain compounds are more mobile than long-chain compounds, which undergo sorption in soil (Campos Pereira et al. 2018; McLachlan et al. 2019). At the same time, similar of chain length, PFSAs undergo stronger sorption in soil than PFCAs (Campos Pereira et al. 2018). Soil properties such as organic matter content, cation exchange capacity, or pH can influence PFAS mobility in soil (Nguyen et al. 2020). However, no clear effect of these soil properties on PFAS sorption in soil has been demonstrated so far (Li et al. 2018; Campos Pereira et al. 2018).

PFAS can enter the human body via ingestion, inhalation, and through the skin (Poothong et al. 2020). However, diet is the main source of human exposure to PFAS, with uptake of these substances by crops and their bioaccumulation being key processes in the transfer of PFAS into the terrestrial food chain (Liu et al. 2017).

These compounds accumulate in the body and may cause serious diseases in humans and animals, including weakening of the immune system, thyroid and liver diseases, reproductive system disorders and can lead to kidney and liver cancers (De Silva et al. 2021; Fenton et al. 2021; Schaider et al. 2017). Due to their persistence in the environment and high potential for bioaccumulation, PFOS and PFOA and their salts have been voluntarily phased out and placed on the list of persistent organic pollutants under the Stockholm Convention (UNEP, 2009; 2019).

Despite measures taken in the EU to reduce PFAS concentrations in the environment, pollution by these compounds still poses a major environmental problem and a threat to public health due to their persistence, widespread use, and toxicity at low concentrations. Moghadasi et al. (2023), using machine learning to analyse data from the Map of Forever Pollution in Europe (MFPE), showed that a large part of European soils may potentially be contaminated with PFAS at concentrations exceeding 5000 ng kg−1. The authors indicate that at such a level of soil contamination, these compounds may be rapidly dispersed in the environment. This may lead to excessive PFAS concentrations in percolating soil water (at levels of 2–5 ng dm−3), exceeding current and proposed standards for drinking water in Europe, and also to PFAS entering the food chain from soils contaminated with these substances (Moghadasi et al. (2023). Therefore, identifying potential sources of these substances in the environment and determining the mechanisms of their release are key actions to reduce PFAS emissions to the environment.

Understanding the different pathways by which PFAS enter agricultural soils is crucial to assessing their impact on the environment. Studies by various authors show that the sources of PFAS in agricultural soils are primarily biosolids and irrigation water (Costello and Lee 2024; D'Ambro et al. 2021; Pepper et al. 2021). In Germany, Röhler et al. (2021) identified large-scale contamination of agricultural soils caused by the use of PFAS-contaminated compost. At one of the studied sites, they found 6300 µg kg⁻1 of PFOS and PFOA in the topsoil, which contributed to the contamination of about 1802 ha of farmland (Röhler et al. 2021). Nascimento et al. (2018), on the other hand, pointed to one pesticide—sulfluramid—as a source of PFAS in agricultural soils. Therefore, a comprehensive assessment of PFAS contamination of agricultural soils is necessary to evaluate and mitigate the human health risks associated with their presence in cultivated soils (Biswas et al. 2025; Costello and Lee 2024; Röhler et al. 2021).

PFAS contamination has been increasingly recognised as an important environmental issue in Central Europe, although the intensity of research has remained lower than in North America or Scandinavia. Existing studies nevertheless demonstrate that PFAS are now widely distributed in aquatic systems, soils, sediments, and the atmosphere across European environments. Research conducted in Poland, Germany, the Czech Republic, Austria, and neighbouring countries indicates that the major sources of PFAS include industrial emissions, wastewater treatment plants, firefighting foams, landfill leachates, and diffuse urban pollution (Kurwadkar et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2025; Ng et al. 2022; Paragot et al. 2020; Wei et al. 2025). Due to their exceptional persistence, mobility, and resistance to degradation, PFAS are capable of long-range transport and accumulation in multiple environmental compartments (Liu et al. 2025). Recent European studies have particularly emphasized the growing importance of groundwater contamination because groundwater constitutes a strategic source of drinking water in Central Europe. PFAS occurrence in aquatic environments has been documented in both surface waters and groundwater systems. Studies from Poland demonstrated the presence of PFAS in wastewater-impacted aquatic environments, including the Baltic Sea region and the Vistula River basin, confirming the role of wastewater treatment plants as important emission pathways (Gałęzowska et al. 2024). Monitoring performed within the Polish national groundwater monitoring network also revealed PFAS occurrence in nearly one-fifth of investigated groundwater sites, especially in industrialised and urbanised regions (Kuczyńska 2025). Similar observations have been reported in Germany and other Central European countries, where PFAS migration through the vadose zone into aquifers has become a major environmental concern (Ackerman Grunfeld et al. 2024; Zhao et al. 2025; Göckener et al. 2023).

Soil contamination by PFAS has also become an important topic of environmental research in Europe. Investigations have shown that soils located near industrial facilities, airports, firefighting training areas, and sewage sludge application sites may act as long-term reservoirs of PFAS. European studies indicate that longer-chain PFAS compounds tend to accumulate in soils and sediments, whereas short-chain compounds exhibit higher mobility and leaching potential toward groundwater. Although soil-focused PFAS studies in Central Europe remain relatively limited, recent reviews emphasise increasing concern regarding agricultural soils and urban-industrial areas (Binner et al. 2024; Wei et al. 2025; Moghadasi et al. 2023).

Atmospheric transport represents another important pathway for PFAS dispersion in Europe. Airborne PFAS and atmospheric deposition have been reported even in regions distant from direct industrial sources, indicating the significance of long-range atmospheric transport. Recent environmental assessments demonstrated that volatile PFAS precursors may contribute substantially to secondary contamination of soils and waters through wet and dry deposition processes. This mechanism is particularly important in densely industrialised regions of Central Europe, where atmospheric emissions may redistribute contaminants across transboundary areas (Paragot et al. 2020).

This study presents pilot research on per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) concentrations in soils and identifies potential sources of these substances in Central European environments.

Materials and methods

Study area and site descriptions

The research was conducted at various sites located in central Poland, approximately 250 km from the town of Suchowola, in which 1775 was recognised as the geographical center of Europe (Zombory 2018) (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Location of study sites: Skierniewice, Experimental Station of the Warsaw University of Life Sciences; Pruszków, municipal waste landfill; Suchowola, the geographical center of Europe

Location of study sites: Skierniewice, Experimental Station of the Warsaw University of Life Sciences; Pruszków, municipal waste landfill; Suchowola, the geographical center of Europe

At the Experimental Station of the Warsaw University of Life Sciences in Skierniewice, located about 80 km south-west of Warsaw (51°57′54.3″N 20°09′28.4″E) (Fig. 1), PFAS content in atmospheric precipitation and in agriculturally used soils was analysed depending on different fertilisation regimes. The Experimental Station is the oldest experimental station of its kind in Poland. Ongoing fertilisation experiments have been carried out at this site for over 100 years in an unchanged design. The experiment was established on a Luvisol-type soil (IUSS, 2022). The soil in the 0–30 cm layer contained sand (2–0.05 mm) 76%, silt (0.05–0.002 mm) 15%, and clay (< 0.002 mm) 9%, and was classified as sandy loam in texture. The basic physico-chemical properties of the soil are presented in Table S1. The experiment is carried out in an open crop rotation without legumes, with the following fertilisation applied continuously for over 100 years: 90 kg N ha−1, 26 kg P ha−1, and 91 kg K ha−1. Manure (FYM) is applied once every 4 years at a rate of 20 Mg ha−1. In selected fertilisation combinations, liming is also applied once every 4 years at a rate of 1.6 Mg CaO ha−1.

Soil samples for PFAS determination were collected in autumn 2024 from the following fertilisation treatments: 0 (control without fertilisation), NPK (mineral fertilisation), and NPK + FYM (mineral fertilisation with farmyard manure), in three replications using a soil probe from a depth of 0–20 cm (ISO, 2018). Twenty cores were taken from each plot to make a composite sample. Each plot had dimensions of 4 × 9 m and was separated from the next plot (another treatment) by a strip 1 m wide.

To collect atmospheric precipitation at the Experimental Station in Skierniewice, an existing meteorological station established in 1921 was used. Atmospheric precipitation samples were collected on three dates, namely 17.09.2024, 19.02.2025, and 25.03.2025. The precipitation samples were immediately transferred to PFAS-free plastic containers, cooled, and sent to the ALS accredited laboratory in Prague.

The second site where research was carried out on identifying sources from which PFAS may enter soils was the municipal landfill in Pruszków (52°17′83.9″N 20°77′49.0″E), located near Warsaw. This landfill consists of two areas. The first area is the currently operated landfill, and the second is a landfill closed 10 years ago and reclaimed. The operation of each sector consisted of dumping the collected municipal waste and compacting it. The layer was then covered with a 30-cm layer of soil (ordinary clay with fractions: sand (2–0.05 mm) 41%, silt (0.05–0.002 mm) 39%, and clay (< 0.002 mm) 20%) to reduce odour emissions to the surroundings. In autumn 2024 (17.09.2024), two composite soil samples were collected from each sector using a soil probe from a depth of 0–20 cm in accordance with ISO (2018). In addition, leachate samples were collected from the operating landfill, which are collected by a drainage system and flow into a leachate reservoir. From the reservoir, leachate samples were collected with a dipper (ISO 2023) on three dates: 17.09.2024, 19.02.2025, and 25.03.2025.

The third site from which samples were collected to identify PFAS was a fire brigade training ground located in central Poland. The soil of the training ground is Luvisol-type (IUSS, 2022), with a light loamy sand texture. Soil samples were collected in autumn 2024 from three different places on the training ground where firefighting foam made of fluorinated and protein surfactants had been applied for more than 20 years. Soil samples were taken in three replications using a soil probe from a depth of 0–20 cm in accordance with ISO (2018). Fluorinated surfactants containing AFFF are used to extinguish flammable liquid fires. Typical AFFF concentrates are anionic, cationic, and anionic–cationic substances containing perfluoroalkyl chains of 3 to 14 carbon atoms in length. At the sampling site, fluorinated surfactants were applied systematically at about 100 dm3 per year, provided that the individual application did not exceed 25 L. Initially, foams made from concentrates containing PFOS were applied at the sampling site. Since 2010, extinguishing agents containing more than 10 mg kg−1 of PFOS or its derivatives have not been used. Until the end of 2022, C8 fluorinated surfactants, whose final degradation product is perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), were applied. Until the end of February 2023, the foaming agent concentrates also contained carboxylic acids with a perfluoro group of the formula CnF2n+1, directly bound to another carbon atom (C9–C14—PFCA). According to European Commission (2025), these compounds may be used in firefighting agents until 3 December 2025 for fighting class B fires (hydrocarbon liquids), provided full capture is ensured at the place of use. Currently, only foam made from concentrates of foaming agents containing six perfluorinated carbon atoms or 6:2 telomer is applied at the sampling site. Unfortunately, the exact composition of the foam concentrates is not known. Their safety data sheet only states that it is a fluorinated surfactant. Manufacturers of foam concentrates cite trade secrets and do not disclose the exact composition of the concentrate.

In the PFAS analyses, standard QA/QC procedures consistent with ITRC (2023) guidelines were applied, including field blanks and equipment (rinsate) blanks. For soil samples, equipment blanks were prepared by rinsing sampling tools with PFAS-free water. In addition, collected soil and water samples were placed in PFAS-free containers, refrigerated, and transported to the laboratory within 24 h of sampling. The plastic containers, provided by the ALS laboratory, were verified as PFAS-free. Rigorous quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC) procedures included the use of solvent, procedural, and matrix blanks to ensure that no PFAS contamination occurred during sample preparation or analysis. All blank measurements were required to be below the method detection limits prior to data acceptance. Additional precautions included the avoidance of fluoropolymer materials and the exclusive use of PFAS-free reagents and laboratory ware (ITRC 2023).

Analyses

In soil samples as well as in leachates from the landfill, PFAS content was determined at the ALS accredited laboratory in Prague, Czech Republic, accredited under no. 1163. For determining PFAS content in water/leachate, the analytical method W-PFCLMS02 was applied, in accordance with the standard CZ_SOP_D06_03_197 A (determination of brominated compounds by liquid chromatography with MS/MS detection). For determining PFAS in soil, the analytical method W-PFCLMS02 was applied, in accordance with the standard CZ_SOP_D06_03_197 B (determination of perfluorinated compounds by liquid chromatography with MS/MS detection). The method allows the determination of 54 different PFAS. The majority of analysed PFAS were not quantified, their concentration being lower than the LOQs (limit of quantitation) of the method. However, we have to keep in mind that a concentration below the LOQ does not necessarily imply the absence of the compound, but rather the inability to identify it with the method used.

Preparation of soil samples

Prior to analysis, soil samples are homogenised. Subsequently, 5 g of soil is weighed into disposable 50 mL plastic containers. Using a calibrated automatic pipette, 100 µL of an internal standard (ISTD) solution (50 ng/mL) is added to each container. The sample is left to stand for at least 10 min. Then, 10 mL of an acetonitrile/methanol mixture (1:1, v/v) is added. The tube is closed, shaken, and left to stand for at least 20 min. After this time, the tube is mixed on a rotary shaker for 10 min and then centrifuged at 11,000 rpm at 20 °C for 5 min. Following extraction, 100 µL of the obtained extract is transferred to a 2-mL vial with a cap containing 900 µL of 5 mM ammonium acetate (AmAc). Finally, the vial is closed with a polyethylene cap without a polypropylene membrane and mixed.

Before applying the low-limit method, a preliminary analysis is performed using method W-PFCLMS02 (standard LOQ). This step is intended to minimise the risk of contamination of laboratory equipment and the LC-MS/MS system. Depending on the results obtained, the low-limit method is subsequently applied or the results from W-PFCLMS02 are reported.

Standard-limit methods—drinking water, surface water, groundwater, and wastewater samples—are homogenised by shaking the entire volume prior to sample preparation. The following components are added to a disposable plastic sample container using a calibrated automatic pipette: 120 µL of ISTD solution (10 ng/mL), 3 mL of acetonitrile, 9 mL of the water sample, and 240 µL of 500 mM ammonium acetate. The mixture is thoroughly mixed or, if necessary, centrifuged and then filtered through cellulose filters with a pore size of 0.22 µm. Filtration is performed by discarding the first 8 mL of filtrate, after which the remaining volume is filtered into transparent 2 mL screw-cap vials with non-Teflon caps. The concentration of ISTD in the measured extract corresponds to 0.1 ng/mL of extract. Shaking the sample prior to preparation is also required as a visual check for potentially elevated levels of perfluoroalkyl substances. If a stable foam forms, the sample is prepared after dilution.

Low-limit methods—drinking water, clean surface water, and groundwater samples—are homogenised by shaking the entire volume prior to preparation. The following components are added to a disposable plastic tube using a calibrated automatic pipette: 50 µL of ISTD solution (10 ng/mL) and 50 mL of the water sample. For samples containing a high amount of suspended solids, the sample is first centrifuged (5 min at 10,000 rpm) and then decanted into a new centrifuge tube.

Samples are subsequently prepared using solid-phase extraction (SPE). SPE cartridges are placed on a vacuum manifold connected to a vacuum pump. Reservoirs and fittings are rinsed with methanol prior to use. In conditioning, cartridges are conditioned by sequential application of NH₄OH in methanol, methanol, and MQ water. After the MQ water has passed through, the manifold valves are closed. Fittings and 60 mL reservoirs are attached, and the water sample is carefully poured in. The sample is loaded onto the SPE cartridge at a rate of approximately one drop per second. After loading, the SPE cartridges are dried under vacuum for approximately 30 min.

In elution and concentration, 15-mL plastic tubes (with conical bottoms) are placed under the cartridges for elution. Elution is performed using 6 cm3 NH₄OH with a concentration of 1.25% in methanol. The eluates are evaporated under a stream of nitrogen at 40 °C to a volume of approximately 0.5 mL. The residue is made up to a final volume of 1 mL with 5 mM ammonium acetate (AmAc), mixed, and filtered through a cellulose filter into transparent vials with polyethylene caps without inserts. The final concentration of ISTD in the analysed extract corresponds to 0.5 ng/mL.

Analysis of experimental data

Due to the nature of the data, statistical analysis was restricted to descriptive statistics. All samples (soil, rainwater, and leachate) were collected in duplicate. Mean values and standard deviations (mean ± SD) were calculated to characterise the variability within the dataset.

Results

This over-a-hundred-year experiment with various mineral and organic fertilisations is a valuable research object for tracing the process of PFAS accumulation from the beginning of their appearance in agricultural soil environments with different land use systems. In all treatments, the presence of the entire list of 54 analysed PFAS was recorded, with the NPK + FYM treatment showing 4 compounds above the detection limit, namely perfluorooctane sulfonic acid (PFOS), perfluorooctane sulfonamide (FOSA), 8:2 fluorotelomer sulfonic acid (8:2 FTS), and 6:2 fluorotelomer sulfonic acid (6:2 FTS). In the control treatment, there were two compounds: FOSA and 6:2 FTS. In the NPK treatment, all the substances studied occurred at concentrations below the quantification limit (Table 1). The total content of PFAS in soil under mineral–organic fertilisation was 6.55 µg·kg−1, and in the unfertilised treatment 1.34 µg·kg−1. Among the PFAS identified in soil, the compound occurring in the largest amounts both in the control treatment (0.76 µg·kg−1) and in mineral fertilisation with manure (3.94 µg·kg−1) was 6:2 fluorotelomer sulfonic acid (6:2 FTS). Its content in soil accounted for 57% (control) and 60% (NPK + FYM) of all detected substances, respectively. In addition, 50 PFAS were detected in the soils of all experimental treatments below the limit of quantification (Table S2).

Of all 54 PFAS analysed in water, in precipitation samples collected at the Experimental Station in Skierniewice during the first (autumn) period, 10 compounds were quantified by the applied method (Table 2), while 42 compounds were below the quantification limit. At this sampling date, two compounds—perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOcDA) and perfluorohexadecanoic acid (PFHxDADA)—were not detected in atmospheric precipitation. The results obtained indicate that one of the sources of PFAS in the agricultural environment is atmospheric precipitation regardless of the season.

The research carried out at the municipal landfill in Pruszków clearly indicates that in the soil cover layers over the municipal waste, all the analysed PFAS compounds occurred at levels below the limit of quantification of the applied method (Table S2). This is due to the way the landfill is operated. Municipal waste is delivered to the landfill, dumped, compacted, and then covered weekly with a 30-cm soil layer. The soil brought in shows no PFAS contamination. The landfill poses an environmental hazard as a point source of PFAS contamination in terms of PFAS concentrations in leachates (Table 2). These compounds can enter shallow, surface and deep waters. In leachate samples from the municipal landfill collected in autumn, 12 compounds with a total concentration of 3664.72 ng·dm−3 were quantified. The leachates analysed were dominated by short-chain substances (C4–C8), among which perfluorobutane sulfonic acid (PFBS) occurred in the highest concentration, accounting for 39% of the total PFAS concentration in leachates. Discussion

The results obtained indicate that on agriculturally used lands, organic fertilisers may be a source of PFAS in soil (Table 1). In contrast, the presence of these compounds in the control treatment may point to the role of dry and wet deposition in the transfer of PFAS from the atmosphere to soil. As studies indicate, wet deposition is the main pathway of PFAS removal from the atmosphere and their transfer to the soil environment (Gewurtz et al. 2019; Chen et al. 2019; Shimizu et al. 2021). At the same time, the content of short-chain PFAS in soil decreases under conditions of intensive precipitation due to their high solubility and leaching into the soil profile. The compounds easily leached from soil are PFBA, PFPeA, PFHxA, PFHpA, and PFBS (McLachlan et al. 2019). This may explain the absence of these compounds in the soil of the long-term fertilisation experiment or their presence below the detection limit, since the soil of the experimental field in Skierniewice is characterised by a sandy loam texture, which favours leaching of contaminants into deeper soil layers. The PFAS content in soil in the NPK treatment, in amounts below the detection limit of the applied method, may also be due to greater uptake by plants compared with the control treatment. Yields obtained at the Experimental Station in the NPK treatment are about three times higher than in the unfertilised treatment (Studnicki et al. 2021). Plants have the ability to take up PFAS both through leaves and via the root system from soil. The uptake of these compounds increases with their content in soil, with short-chain compounds being taken up in larger amounts than long-chain compounds (Costello and Lee 2024; Ghisi et al. 2019). The PFAS content in soil under organic fertilisation was higher compared with the control treatment (without fertilisation) and the mineral-only fertilisation treatment. Saliu et al. (2024) showed that mineral fertilisers are free from PFAS contamination, their content in these fertilisers being below the detection limit (not exceeding 0.99 µg kg−1). In contrast, ∑79 PFAS in fresh and unmodified animal manure were between 10.3 and 21.9 µg kg−1 and increased to 59 µg kg−1 in composts produced from animal manure with the addition of humus or peat moss material. Estoppey et al. (2024) showed that the total content of PFOS and PFOA in bio-based fertilisers (BBFs) was < 30 µg kg−1, i.e. lower than the strictest thresholds in force in various EU countries. The calculated predicted concentrations in soils after a single application of BBF fertiliser (e.g. PFOS < 0.05 µg kg−1) were lower than the background levels (PFOS 2.7 µg kg−1) reported in the literature (Estoppey et al. 2024; Sivaram et al. 2022).

The total PFAS concentration in atmospheric precipitation obtained, equal to 20.92 ng dm−3 in autumn and 2.7 ng dm−3 in spring (Table 2), falls within the ranges identified in other countries such as the USA, ranging from 2.28 to 92.42 ng dm−3, or nearby Germany with concentrations from 1.6 to 48.6 ng dm−3 (Coates and Harrington 2024; Dreyer et al. 2010), and indicates very high dynamics of PFAS variability in precipitation, largely consistent with the findings of other authors (Hartz et al. 2024; Kali et al. 2025; Li et al. 2024). In turn, Coates and Harrington (2024) and Wang et al. (2023) highlight the role of secondary emissions (e.g. from soils, waters and sewage) as continuous sources of PFAS in the atmosphere. The results indicate that atmospheric precipitation plays an important role in the transfer of PFAS and their secondary introduction into the aquatic and soil environments. Thus, they constitute an important element of the environmental cycle of these persistent pollutants. In atmospheric precipitation (Table 2), the presence of short-chain perfluoroalkyl acids was mainly recorded, which demonstrates their ability for long-range atmospheric transport and wet redeposition (rain, snow) (Ahrens and Bundschuh 2014; Alam et al. 2024). The compound occurring in the highest concentration in the studied precipitation samples was PFBA, which, as a short-chain compound, is more volatile and more easily transported through the atmosphere, explaining its high content in precipitation—a phenomenon described by, among others, Coates and Harrington (2024), Alam et al. (2024), Pickard et al. (2020), who demonstrated the global extent of PFAS redeposition. Lower concentrations of other compounds such as PFUnDA, PFDoDA, and PFDA confirm the limited volatility and transport capacity of these long-chain compounds in the atmosphere. According to studies by Chen et al. (2018) and Dauchy (2023), long-chain PFAS are mainly deposited near emission sources, whereas lighter forms dominate in precipitation. The level of PFAS concentration in atmospheric precipitation depends on the season, emissions from local sources, distance from urban areas, and meteorological conditions (Coates and Harrington 2024; Olney et al. 2023).

The total PFAS concentration found in leachates from the municipal landfill (Table 2) is very high and in the first sampling period (autumn) exceeded 3600 ng dm−3, while in the second and third periods, it exceeded 2300 ng dm−3. In the winter and spring periods, 55 compounds were present. The short-chain (C4–C8) acid forms of PFAS dominating in leachates from the municipal landfill, such as PFBS, PFOA, PFHxA, and PFPeA, are also identified as the most commonly occurring in landfill leachates by other authors (Liu et al. 2021). Benskin et al. (2012) showed that in Sweden, PFAS concentrations in leachates ranged from several tens to over 1000 ng dm−3, with domination of short-chain sulfonic and carboxylic acids. Similarly, Yan et al. (2015) documented the presence of PFHxA, PFBS, and PFOA in landfill leachates in China at levels comparable to those identified in the present study. The predominance of short-chain PFAS in landfill leachates may be related to the biodegradation of perfluoroalkyl acid (PFAA) precursors, leading to the formation of short-chain PFAAs (Hamid et al. 2018). The high chemical persistence of PFAS means that despite membrane filtration and other leachate treatment methods, PFAS can still migrate into the environment from municipal landfills and contaminate surface and groundwater (Busch et al. 2010). At the same time, these authors showed that leachates contained mainly PFHxA and PFBS at concentrations reaching even 2000 ng dm−3, consistent with the results of the present study. It is also worth noting that older landfills contain different PFAS profiles compared with newer ones, due to differences in industrial materials used. This may also be linked to the cessation of production of long-chain PFAS, particularly PFOA and PFOS, in recent decades in various countries (Wang et al. 2023). Such conclusions were presented, among others, by Lang et al. (2017), analysing leachates in the USA, in which 5:3 fluorotelomer carboxylic acid (FTCA) dominated. It is also considered that PFAS precursor degradation leads to higher concentrations of these compounds at older landfills than at younger ones (Gallen et al. 2017). In addition, PFAS may also be released in gaseous form from landfills, increasing the risk of migration of these pollutants into different environmental compartments (Ye et al. 2024). The high persistence and mobility of PFAS make landfills a significant threat to the natural environment.

In soil samples collected from the area where firefighters carried out activities with foaming agents, the presence of numerous per- and polyfluoroalkyl compounds at high concentrations (total 2537.32 µg kg−1) was confirmed (Table 3). At the same time, the highest concentrations were recorded for sulfonic compounds such as PFOS, FOSA, 6:2 FTS, and 8:2 FTS. Similarly high PFAS concentrations in soils at firefighting sites were reported by Reinikainen et al. The authors also pointed out that the compound occurring in the greatest amount in soil was PFOS (50–96% of the sum of the identified compounds), while FOSA and some other long-chain PFAS compounds (e.g. 8:2 FTSA and PFTrDA) were the next most abundant substances (Høisæter et al. 2019; Reinikainen et al. 2022). The high PFAS content in the soil at the fire brigade training ground clearly indicates that foaming agents systematically used by fire brigade units are the source of these compounds in the soil. These compounds are the main components of fluorinated foams used in extinguishing flammable liquid fires, especially in rescue–firefighting training (Peshoria et al. 2020). Scientific literature has repeatedly shown that from the late 1960 s to the early 2000 s, AFFF based on PFOS and its precursors, such as FOSA, MeFOSA, and EtFOSA, were produced, which slowly degrade into more persistent end metabolites such as PFOA and other shorter-chain PFAS in the environment (Moody and Field 2000; Place and Field 2012). From the early 2000 s to 2016, the use of PFOS-based AFFF was replaced by fluorotelomer-based AFFF such as 6:2 FTS and 8:2 FTS, which, however, can degrade into PFOA and short-chain PFCA in the natural environment (Baduel et al. 2017b). Their significant presence in the studied firefighting site soils (6:2 FTS accounted for 42% of the total content of all identified compounds) (Table 3) confirms the continuous use of modern AFFF, consistent with observations from other rescue training sites (Baduel et al. 2017a). High concentrations of carboxylic acids such as PFOA, PFHxA, and PFNA may indicate the breakdown of fluorotelomer precursors and further PFAS migration in the environment (D’Agostino and Mabury 2014). These compounds are characterised by high chemical persistence and mobility, which favours their movement into deeper soil profiles and, in the longer term, into groundwater (Baduel et al. 2017a). It is worth stressing that the presence of PFDA, PFUnDA, and PFDoDA in soil, at lower but detectable concentrations, confirms their origin from secondary transformations and accumulation in the environment during long-term foam use. These are long-chain compounds that show a greater tendency for sorption in soil, which can lead to their long-term retention (Peshoria et al. 2020). The results obtained indicate the urgent need to monitor firefighting sites for PFAS contamination and the necessity of introducing effective remediation methods, particularly in areas intensively used by emergency services. At the same time, it should be emphasised that more and more countries are deciding to limit or completely ban the use of foams containing PFOS, PFOA, or 6:2 FTS, in line with the Stockholm Convention (UNEP, 2019).

Conclusions

The presence of per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) in various environmental components is widely studied, primarily due to their toxicity to living organisms (Dobrzyńska et al. 2025). When analysing their fate and transport in soil, it is crucial to consider PFAS interactions between soil and other ecosystem elements, such as air or water, yet few studies adopt such an integrated approach (Wang et al. 2023). The research conducted showed that in soils used agriculturally for over 100 years, where neither biosolids nor sewage irrigation are applied, no elevated PFAS levels are observed. In the tested combinations of the more than century-long fertilisation experiment, all analysed forms of PFAS were detected in the soil in unmeasurable amounts. The finding of PFAS in soil contaminated with high concentrations confirms the slow degradation of these compounds, a similar situation with the arrival of these compounds with rainfall in a multi-year experiment suggests that we should monitor these processes in order to identify the possible impact on their presence and activity in cultivated soil. At the same time, it was shown that atmospheric precipitation may be a source of these substances in agriculturally used soils. Under temperate climate conditions, a significant variation in PFAS concentration in precipitation throughout the year was demonstrated. The dominant compound in precipitation was perfluorobutanoic acid (PFBA), which accounted for at least 70% of the total PFAS concentration. The high PFAS concentrations observed in leachates from a municipal landfill, exceeding a total of 2000 ng dm−3, clearly indicate that landfills constitute point sources of these substances and may pose a threat to the environment, including agriculturally used soils. The concentration and composition of PFAS in leachates from the municipal landfill differed depending on the sampling time (autumn, winter, spring); however—as in other studies—the dominant group consisted of short-chain (C4–C8) compounds in acid form. The research conducted confirmed that AFFF are a significant source of PFAS in the environment. Soil at the fire brigade training ground contained the broadest range of PFAS, of which sulfonic substances accounted for 86% of the total content, including significant amounts of three forms of perfluorotelomer sulfonic acid (6:2 FTS, 8:2 FTS, and 10:2 FTS), which readily degrade into more persistent, potentially toxic compounds, mainly shorter-chain PFCAs. This indicates the necessity of monitoring these areas for PFAS contamination and implementing effective remediation methods, particularly in regions intensively used by emergency services, as these areas may contribute to the spread of PFAS in the environment. Future research should focus on integrated, catchment-scale studies combining soil, water, and atmospheric compartments to better understand PFAS cycling in agroecosystems. There is also a clear need for long-term monitoring of deposition, leaching, and transformation processes, and risk assessment frameworks should increasingly incorporate soil–plant transfer mechanisms and potential human exposure via the food chain.
