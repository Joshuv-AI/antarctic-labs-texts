Cost-Effectiveness of Pre-emptive DPYD Genotyping Compared to Standard of Care Among Patients Receiving Fluoropyrimidine-Based Anti-cancer Treatment in Australia
Authors: Sarah Glewis, Mussab Fagery, Senthil Lingaratnam, Sam Harris, Chloe Georgiou, Craig Underhill, Mark Warren, Robert Campbell, Jennifer H Martin, Jeanne Tie, Maarten IJzerman, Marliese Alexander, Michael Michael
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite international evidence demonstrating pre-emptive pharmacogenetics (PGx) screening is cost effective or cost saving in preventing serious or fatal toxicities, it is not routinely adopted in Australia. This study evaluated the cost effectiveness of PGx screening versus standard of care (SOC) among patients with cancer undergoing fluoropyrimidine-based treatment (FP) in Australia.MethodsFrom the Australian healthcare perspective, we developed a cohort-based state transition model in TreeAge Pro 2024. The model used PACIFIC-PGx trial data for the PGx arm and literature-based inputs for the SOC arm. Patients transitioned between four health states (full-dose, reduced-dose, treatment termination or death) over two treatment cycles each corresponding to a standard 3-week period. Outcomes included the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) per quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) gained, per adverse event averted, and per hospitalisation avoided. Deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses (DSA, PSA) evaluated the effects of varying assumptions and the uncertainty associated with input parameters.ResultsPGx screening yielded incremental QALYs of 0.05 at an additional cost of $274.25 AUD (Australian dollars), resulting in an ICER of $6014.5 AUD per QALY gained compared to SOC. DSA showed the model’s outcomes remained robust, with the ICER staying below the specified threshold of $50,000 AUD under a ± 20% variation in input parameters. PSA suggested PGx screening was favourable in 97.6% of iterations.ConclusionThis Australian single-arm study demonstrated that pre-emptive PGx screening prevents severe, fatal FP-related toxicities and hospitalisations and is likely to be cost effective. Our findings suggest the value of PGx screening and warrant implementation and reimbursement within Australian healthcare settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40261-025-01489-w.

Key Points

Pharmacogenetics (PGx) screening is not funded or widely implemented in Australia.Our study suggests that PGx screening can be integrated into routine care to enhance patient outcomes.PGx screening prevents severe and fatal fluoropyrimidine-related toxicities and hospitalisations, and is likely to be cost effective from the Australian healthcare perspective.Our findings suggest the value of the PGx screening and warrant implementation and reimbursement within Australian healthcare settings.

Introduction

Fluoropyrimidines (FPs) (5-fluorouracil (5-FU) and its oral prodrug capecitabine) are widely used in a variety of solid tumours. 5-FU and capecitabine are commonly prescribed as a single agent or in combination with other anti-cancer therapies such FOLFOX (leucovorin, 5-FU and oxaliplatin), FOLFIRI (leucovorin, 5-FU and irinotecan), and CAPOX (oxaliplatin and capecitabine) [1]. Severe toxicities may result in mucosal, digestive or haematological side effects, including death [2, 3]. Fatal toxicity is strongly linked to partial or complete dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase (DPD) deficiency, the enzyme responsible for 5-FU metabolism, encoded by DPYD gene [4].

Pre-emptive DPYD genotyping should be considered prior to FP-based chemotherapy in a wide range of solid cancers such as gastric, colorectal and breast cancers [1]. Evidence for pre-emptive DPYD genotyping has been well established, with a reduction in patient morbidity, mortality and hospitalisation [1, 5–7], without compromising patient survival outcomes [3, 8, 9]. International guidelines to assist clinicians with dosing recommendations based on DPYD genotyping are readily available [10, 11].

Landmark studies from the United Kingdom (UK), United States (US), Canada, Qatar and Europe have shown that pharmacogenetics (PGx) screening is cost effective or cost saving in preventing serious and fatal toxicities [3, 5–7, 12, 13]. However, there are currently no funding reimbursements from the Australian healthcare system for the cost of a PGx service [14]. Hence, the uptake of PGx screening remains limited due to the cost of service being the primary barrier to implementation [14].

To the best of our knowledge, no cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) for PGx screening programs have been conducted in the Australian healthcare settings. To better understand the costs and the benefits of the PGx screening, relative to standard of care, we conducted a cost-effectiveness model from the perspective of the Australian healthcare. This study was built on the clinical claim that PGx testing will identify patients with DPYD variant allele carriers and who received upfront FP genotype-guided dosing, which will lead to a superior safety profile and non-inferior efficacy (based on previously published matched-pair analyses) [8, 9]. While the findings of this study are directly relevant to the Australian context, they address one of the primary barriers to implementing PGx programs in routine oncology care, as identified in surveys of Australian clinicians [14, 15]. These findings will also strengthen global data, enhancing confidence in the cost effectiveness of PGx across diverse healthcare settings and contemporary treatment protocols.

The objectives of this study were to conduct a CEA and a cost-utility analysis (CUA) from a multicentre PGx trial (PACIFIC-PGx, Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry Number: ANZCTR12621000251820) [1, 16]. While CEA compares the costs and health outcomes in terms of severe toxicity and hospitalisation avoided, CUA measures outcomes in terms of quality-adjusted life years (QALYs). Together, CEA and CUA provide a comprehensive overview of the value of a pre-emptive PGx screening program compared to standard of care.

Methods

PACIFIC-PGx Clinical Trial

PACIFIC-PGx was a prospective, multicentre, single-arm clinical trial that enrolled 276 patients between 7 January 2021 and 25 February 2022 at four Australian hospitals, one metropolitan primary site and three regional hospitals [1, 16]. The study population included adults with a cancer diagnosis, planned for FP-based chemotherapy, without prior exposure to FPs [1, 16]. DPYD variants with dosing recommendations by Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC) c.1905+1G>A (*2A), c.1679T>G (*13), c.2846A>T, c.1236G>A, c.557A>G were tested [1, 11]. DPYD screening detected 5% (15/276) intermediate metabolisers (who received 50% upfront FP dose reduction) and 95% (261/276) wild-type (who received full FP dosing) [1]; no poor metabolisers were identified in the study. High-grade toxicities were significantly reduced for DPYD variant allele carriers who received genotype-guided dosing (7%) compared to standard of care (39%; odds ratio (OR) = 0.11, 95% confidence interval (Cl) 0.01–0.97, p = 0.024) [1].

Type of Economic Evaluation

We conducted a model-based analysis and presented findings in accordance with the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards 2022 (CHEERS 2022) [17]. A cohort-level state transition model was developed to estimate the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) per QALY gained as the primary outcome. Secondary analyses calculated the ICER per adverse events averted and per hospitalisation avoided, comparing PGx screening to standard of care. The analysis was conducted over a 6-week time horizon, corresponding to an average of two standard 3-week treatment cycles, to capture the period when severe genetically-driven toxicities are most likely to occur following FP exposure. The evaluation was undertaken from the perspective of the Australian healthcare payer. Discounting was not applied, as the time horizon of the analysis was less than 1 year.

Intervention versus Comparator

The intervention cohort included patients who underwent PGx screening in the PACIFIC-PGx trial [1]. Those identified as DPYD variant allele carriers received genotype-guided dosing from the outset, while individuals with wild-type genotypes received full-dose FP. The comparator group included patients who received standard of care without screening, with risk estimates informed by published literature [2, 18, 19].

Data Estimates

Data estimates related to FP treatment toxicity, treatment-related hospitalisations (grade ≥ 3), treatment-related mortality, reduced dosing, and treatment termination in the PGx intervention arm were extracted from the PACIFIC-PGx trial [1]. For the standard of care arm, toxicity risks were drawn from Froehlich et al. [18], selected for its close alignment with the PACIFIC-PGx trial regarding the duration of follow-up, cancer diagnoses, treatment type and DPYD variants tested [18, 20]. Hospitalisation and mortality were not reported in the Froehlich study. Therefore, hospitalisation rates were estimated based on studies by Lunenberg et al. and Toffoli et al. [19, 21], while mortality estimates were derived from pooled data from 13 studies analysed in the systematic review conducted by Sharma et al. [2].

Model Description

This study employed a decision-analytic framework consisting of a decision tree linked to a four-state cohort-based Markov model. The economic model was developed using TreeAge Pro Healthcare 2024 (TreeAge Software, LLC, Williamstown, MA, USA) [22] (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1, Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)). The analytical structure was branched into two distinct cohorts: the PGx screening and the standard of care. The decision tree then segregated patients into two genetic profiles: wild-type and those carrying deleterious DPYD alleles (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1, ESM).

The model included four health states and utilised a 3-week cycle length corresponding to a standard treatment period. Between these treatment cycles, patient transitions were modelled between various treatment dosages and health states: (1) full dose, (2) reduced dose, (3) treatment termination, or (4) death. Specifically, the PGx screening cohort initiated the model with an upfront DPYD genotyping prior to the commencement of FP chemotherapy. Depending on the genotyping results, patients received a full FP dose or genotype-guided dosing. Conversely, the standard of care group entered the model receiving full FP dose.

Patients undergoing chemotherapy were presumed to be susceptible to varying degrees of treatment-related toxicity, classified into severe (Grade ≥ 3), moderate (Grade 2), or none/minimal (Grade 0–1) toxicity. Those experiencing severe toxicity were further assumed to be at an increased risk of hospitalisation, with potential outcomes of survival or mortality attributed to treatment-related adverse effects (Fig. 1).

The model’s primary aim was to trace patient transitions across the specified health states, thereby quantifying the QALYs accrued through the mitigation of adverse events. The model accounted for the economic implications stemming from both the initial screening and subsequent hospitalisation costs. For a fair comparison with the historical controls [19, 21], which included hospitalisations due to grade 3 or 4 toxicity, we have included only the subset of patients in our PACIFIC-PGx trial who experienced grade 3 or 4 hospitalisations. The detailed model structure is presented in Supplementary Fig. 1 (ESM).

Model Assumptions

To ensure clarity in our economic analysis, we delineated the key assumptions integral to the economic evaluation of the model: (1) Mortality rates: background mortality and cancer-related mortality were not modelled, as they were not anticipated to be influenced by DPYD genotyping, given the clinical assertion of non-inferior effectiveness of PGx screening compared to no screening; however, toxicity-related mortality was incorporated into the model by assuming fatal outcomes among patients experiencing grade 3 or higher toxicity only. (2) Treatment toxicities: patients may encounter varying degrees of toxicities across each treatment cycle. (3) Treatment-related hospitalisation was expected among patients with Grade ≥ 3, whereas patients experiencing Grade 0–2 were not anticipated to require hospitalisation. (4) Screening reliability: the failure rate of the PGx screening due to technical errors, sample rejection or inconclusive DPYD results was assumed to be 0%. (5) Prevalence in genetic variability: the proportion of DPYD intermediate and poor metabolisers within the PGx cohort were sourced from published literature, reflecting prevalence data [1, 7]. The majority of patients in our study were of Caucasian ethnicity [1]. The prevalence of DPYD variants in the PGx screening cohort was 5% for DPYD variant allele carriers and 95% for wild types, similar to the standard of care cohort (3% for DPYD variant allele carriers vs. 97% for wild-type) (Supplementary Table 1, ESM). From the five DPYD variants tested in the program, 3% of patients were carriers of c.1236G>A variant, followed by 1% for c.1905+1G>A and 1% for c.2846A>T. In the PGx screening cohort no patients carried the c.1679T>G or c.557A>G variants [1]. Similar findings were reported for c.1236G>A as the most common variant identified in our cohort, consistent with European data reported [23].

Health-State Utilities

Health utility decrements used in the model were derived from published literature over one treatment cycle value [26–31]. Health-state utilities were estimated for the model health states (grade 0–1, grade 2 and grade ≥ 3 adverse events). Each health state’s utility estimate was estimated based on the total utility decrements resulting from the adverse events experienced with different toxicity grades (Supplementary Table 5, ESM). In the base-case analysis, we assumed that the negative effect of the adverse events on patients’ quality of life (i.e., utility decrement) would last for approximately 4 weeks, consistent with the assumptions adopted in a previous Ontario Health Technology Assessment [7]. The selection of adverse events included in the model was based on the most common toxicities seen/observed in clinical practice and in the published literature (i.e., nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, mucositis, fatigue) [29, 31].

Sensitivity Analyses

One-way deterministic sensitivity analyses were performed to explore the uncertainty on the CEA and CUA for PGx screening versus standard of care by varying all individual parameters by 20% and examining effects on outcomes for all input parameters including transition probabilities, utility estimates and cost data (Supplementary Table 6, ESM).

A probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) was conducted using Monte Carlo simulation to simulate an incremental cost-effectiveness plan to explore uncertainty around the ICER and to estimate the CEA.

Results

Base-Case Analysis (Cost-Utility Analysis)

Comparing PGx screening to standard of care, PGx screening resulted in an incremental cost of $274.25 AUD and incremental effectiveness of 0.05 QALYs relative to standard of care. The ICER was $6014.5 AUD per QALY for PGx screening (Table 2).

Secondary Analysis (Cost-Effectiveness Analysis)

The model shows an incremental difference of 20% reduction in Grade ≥ 3 toxicities and 5% in hospitalisations, favouring PGx screening, with an estimated cost savings of $1499.21 AUD per two treatment cycles primarily driven by the avoidance of hospitalisations and management of severe adverse events. ICER per Grade ≥ 3 averted was $7815.69 AUD and hospitalisations avoided was $27,426.29 AUD (Supplementary Table 7, ESM).

Sensitivity Analysis

One-Way Deterministic Sensitivity Analysis

Results from one-way deterministic sensitivity analysis (DSA) are presented in Fig. 2 and Table 3. The DSA demonstrated that when assuming a 20% variability in input parameters, the ICER remained below the threshold of $50,000 AUD. Notably, the transition probabilities for grade 0–1 toxicity and grade ≥ 3 toxicity in both the PGx screening and the standard of care arms had the most significant impact on the ICER.

A scenario analysis using lower hospitalisation rates from Toffoli et al. [21] for the standard of care arm increased the ICER to $6269.5 AUD per QALY, a 4.2% rise from the base-case value of $6014.5 AUD (Supplementary Table 8, ESM).

Monte Carlo simulation with 100,000 iterations was used in the probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA). The threshold for willingness-to-pay (WTP) was $50,000 AUD per QALY. Discussion

This is the first cost-effectiveness analysis comparing the PGx screening to standard of care from the Australian healthcare payer perspective and one of few with contemporary treatment and supportive care protocols. Our base-case analysis showed that PGx screening has improved health outcomes, with an increase in effectiveness of 0.05 and an ICER value of $6014.5 AUD per QALY. This aligns with the nature of PGx screening, where the primary benefit was harm avoidance rather than direct therapeutic gain. Also, given the short time horizon of the analysis (two chemotherapy cycles), the modest QALY gain was expected and reflects immediate improvements in treatment tolerability and patient safety.

Our probabilistic sensitivity analysis demonstrated that the ICER value was below the $50,000 AUD/QALY threshold in 97.6% model iterations, showing that the PGx screening was favoured and likely cost effective compared to standard of care. The one-way DSA demonstrated that the model’s outcomes remained robust with up to a 20% variability in most input parameters, as the impact on the ICER remains below the specified threshold. However, transition probabilities for toxicity events were the main drivers of results. These estimates draw on a single-arm PGx cohort and a literature-derived comparator from heterogeneous populations rather than randomised head-to-head data. Despite harmonised toxicity definitions, between-study differences and unmeasured confounding limit robustness; thus, these parameters are a material uncertainty.

PGx screening was associated with lower rates of adverse events and hospitalisations, leading to downstream cost savings; the higher incremental cost observed ($274.25 AUD) can be attributed to differences in treatment continuation between the two groups. In the model, patients in the standard care arm experienced higher rates of treatment-related death and early treatment termination after the first cycle due to severe toxicities. In contrast, patients in the PGx arm were more likely to continue to the second treatment cycle, resulting in additional chemotherapy and monitoring costs. The continuation of treatment offsets some of the savings from reduced toxicity in the PGx arm and contributes to the slightly higher overall incremental cost. This strongly emphasises the importance of pre-emptive DPYD screening, enabling patients to safely continue chemotherapy, while delivering improved clinical outcomes and cost effectiveness. Recent studies from the UK, US and Canada have demonstrated that DPYD genotyping is cost effective [6, 7, 32]. The 2022 US study showed improved survival by 0.0038 QALYs with an ICER of $20,506/QALY among patients receiving FP in adjuvant chemotherapy for stage 3 colon cancer, over a 5-year horizon period [6]. The 2023 UK study based its CEA on the assumption of non-inferior treatment efficacy and survival. The Markov model had a lifetime horizon and a cycle length of 2 months. It demonstrated 0.19 additional QALYs and savings of £78,000 per patient over a lifetime with upfront DPYD genotyping in metastatic breast cancer [32]. A Canadian study published by Ontario health based on a literature search of clinical evidence has shown that PGx screening is likely cost effective compared to usual care, and it is likely a cost saving of $714,963 over a 5-year period if DPYD genotyping is implemented prior to FP [7].

Other international studies have demonstrated that PGx screening for the four common DPYD variants prior to FP results in cost savings [3, 5], as the costs associated with treating serious toxicities and hospitalisations outweigh the cost of screening [3, 5]. A 2018 European study assessed the cost implications of reactive versus prospective testing for the four DPYD variants (three variants were the same in our study) in 134 patients over a 3-year period in an Irish private hospital. Patients commenced on FP chemotherapy as first line for colorectal cancer, costing €177 per patient [13]. The cost of testing was similar to our PGx screening when converted to Australian dollars (~$288 vs. $274.25) [33]. The author concluded that the cost of pre-emptive DPYD genotyping was significantly lower in comparison to the cost of hospitalisations for serious toxicity for the DPYD variant allele carriers (s) with an average of €46,412 per case [13]. Consistent findings across various global healthcare settings provide confidence that the data will be applicable to different health system models.

Targeted DPYD genotyping for five variants tested in our study is supported by strong evidence in preventing serious adverse events, hospitalisations and death [3, 34]. However, it is limited to detecting only known DPYD pre-specified variants associated with severe toxicity commonly found in a Caucasian population (except for the variant c.557A>G, which is common in patients with African ancestry) [11] and may not sufficiently capture variant frequencies in other ethnicities such as Indigenous or migrant populations within Australia. In addition, it does not include rare or novel variants that contribute to severe toxicity [1, 3, 34, 35]. Emerging technologies such as next-generation sequencing (NGS), including whole-genome sequencing or whole-exome sequencing, identify novel or rare variants tailored to the individual from different ethnicities [35, 36]. However, these approaches remain challenging for reasons such as complex clinical interpretation, clinical utility uncertainties, and reimbursement processes [37, 38].

In Australia, pre-emptive DPYD genotyping is not currently funded by the Australian government through the Medical Benefits Schedule (MBS). As a result, this test is typically paid for by the healthcare provider, supported by research grants or out of pocket by the patient. The test is available through some commercial laboratories with costs reaching up to $160 AUD [39]. However, In January 2025 the Australian government Medical Services Advisory Committee approved an application for public funding for DPYD genotyping [40]. The application for reimbursement lacked an Australian economic evaluation. From 1 November 2025, DPYD genotyping will be officially listed on the MBS, supporting the safer use of FP-based chemotherapy and making it accessible to all health providers across the country [41]. Our study supports this change by providing evidence to inform the implementation of PGx into routine oncology care, highlighting key areas such as testing capabilities or infrastructure, financial reimbursement and healthcare professionals’ training, all of which were identified as priorities in clinician surveys conducted by our group in Australia [14, 15]. This analysis will also contribute valuable data to support the broader international pre-emptive DPYD genotyping reimbursement decision making.

Limitations

Our study has limitations. (1) Estimates obtained from the literature were unavailable for some of the model parameters, i.e. utility values for different toxicity grades. This lack necessitated the use of proxy data or assumptions, which may introduce uncertainty into the model’s outcomes. (2) Utility estimates were estimated based on the most common adverse events assumed to occur at different grades of toxicity. Utility decrements for those events were then collected from the literature. (3) To align with the trial duration and avoid the uncertainty introduced by extrapolating beyond observed follow-up, we applied a 6-week time horizon. This choice may underestimate longer-term costs and benefits, such as downstream hospitalisations, dose adjustments, and quality-of-life effects extending beyond the first two treatment cycles. However, it is important to note that DPYD-driven serious and potentially fatal toxicities, including hospitalisations, and their subsequent impact on dose adjustments and quality of life, are most likely to occur in the first two cycles of chemotherapy [42, 43]. Accordingly, the benefits estimated in this analysis should be interpreted as applying to the first two treatment cycles. (4) In a memoryless Markov framework, extending disutility beyond the index cycle is not feasible without structural changes (e.g., tunnel states or a semi-Markov model) and additional data on grade-specific recovery trajectories. This simplification may underestimate QALY losses for prolonged recovery after grade ≥ 3 toxicities. (5) The impact of underdosing in DPYD variant allele carriers on treatment effectiveness was not addressed in this economic evaluation, as the short time horizon limited further investigation into addressing the potential long-term effects on survival. However, matched-pair analysis studies have shown no compromise in survival measures in patients who were dose-reduced based on DPYD genotyping [8, 9], but we acknowledge this is an ongoing clinical concern, particularly in the context of shortened curative treatment protocols. (6) Assuming a 0% PGx test failure rate is a limitation; while idealised and not fully reproducible, it reflects the PACIFIC-PGx real-world cohort (standard commercial lab, no errors reported), and a high failure rate is not expected. (7) Although Australia is a multicultural country, most patients enrolled in the PACIFIC-PGx trial were of Caucasian ethnicity [1]. Variation in DPYD allele frequencies and associated toxicities across ethnic groups may limit the generalisability of these findings. Therefore, future research is required to explore the prevalence of DPYD variants from other ethnic cohorts.

Conclusion

Pre-emptive PGx screening prevents severe and fatal FP-related toxicities, reduces hospitalisations, and improves patient safety. Under a WTP of $50,000 AUD per QALY, this approach is likely to be cost effective in the Australian context. This health-economic analysis suggests the PGx feasibility from a resource-use perspective and supports its implementation and funding within the Australian healthcare settings as part of standard clinical care. This analysis adds to the growing global evidence base supporting cost effectiveness in different health-system models.
