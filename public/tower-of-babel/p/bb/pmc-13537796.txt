Combined inulin and glucomannan modulate colonic cytokine balance and mucosal barrier function in a western diet-induced colonic inflammation mouse model
Authors: Bernadette Dian Novita, Steven Christian Angkawijaya, I Gede Putu Adhi Wedharga, Suciatuz Zinanir Rohman, Ferdinand Wiliam Jaya, Daniella Widarma, David Timothy Abraham, Irene Lingkan Parengkuan, Adi Pramono Hendrata, Hendy Wijaya, Yudy Tjahjono, Sianty Dewi, Niluh Suwasanti, Endang Isbandiati Soediono, Suryo Kuncorojakti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA rising worldwide health concern, inflammatory bowel disease is linked to western-style diets that damage intestinal barrier integrity, change gut microbiota, and increase colonic inflammation. In contrast, inulin and glucomannan enhance short-chain fatty acid production and preserve mucosal structure, suggesting a protective role against diet-induced colonic inflammation. This study aimed to investigate whether combined inulin and glucomannan supplementation prevents western diet-induced colonic inflammation by modulating intestinal barrier function and colonic inflammatory cytokine profiles. Over 8 weeks, elicited by a high-fat, high-sucrose (HFHS) diet, colonic inflammation in mice as a model was utilized in a preventative experimental investigation. Mice received an HFHS diet with supplementation of inulin, glucomannan, or their combination. Colonic histopathology was evaluated, and interleukin 6 (IL-6) and IL-10 levels were assessed using immunohistochemistry. Statistical analyses included one-way analysis of variance with appropriate post hoc tests, covariate-adjusted analyses controlling for caloric intake, and correlation analyses between histological and inflammatory parameters. Fiber supplementation reduced HFHS-induced colonic inflammation severity, crypt shortening, and mucosal thinning without affecting caloric intake. While colonic inflammation severity linked favorably with IL-6 and inversely with IL-10 and mucosal integrity, combined inulin–glucomannan supplementation provided the lowest IL-6/IL-10 ratio along with uniquely elevated IL-10 expression, indicating better immunomodulatory effects. Soluble fiber supplementation prevents HFHS-induced colonic inflammation by preserving mucosal architecture and restoring inflammatory cytokine balance, with combined inulin and glucomannan providing additive immunoprotective benefits.

INTRODUCTION

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), which includes Crohn’s disease as well as ulcerative colitis, has become a growing worldwide health problem due to its chronic nature, increasing incidence, and substantial consequences on patients’ quality of life. Between 1990 and 2017, there was a significant increase in disease-related morbidity as the IBD’s age-standardized prevalence increased by almost 6.04%, and the total years lived with disability almost doubled.[1] The burden of IBD is particularly evident in rapidly developing regions such as Asia, where incidence rates continue to rise. The broad adoption of western dietary habits, which are generally typified by high fat and dietary sugar consumption combined with low-fiber consumption and further urbanization, has been strongly associated with this tendency.[2] Such dietary shifts are known to impair intestinal barrier function, disrupt gut microbial balance, and contribute to immune dysregulation, all of which play central roles in the pathogenesis of IBD.[3]

Colitis related to western-style diets arises from interconnected mechanisms, particularly the intestinal’ barrier’s disruption through tight junction proteins’ downregulation along with increased permeability, which facilitates the entry of luminal antigens and lipopolysaccharides, triggering mucosal inflammation.[345] High-fat, high-sucrose (HFHS) diets further induce colitis-like alterations, including crypt distortion, goblet cell depletion, inflammatory infiltration, and reduced crypt depth, ultimately weakening mucosal defense.[67] These alterations are accompanied by a proliferation in pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor alpha and interleukin-6 (IL-6) and a drop in the production of short-chain fatty acid (SCFA), which prevents anti-inflammatory signaling. In contrast, soluble fibers like inulin as well as glucomannan improve gut histomorphometry and mucosal integrity by being fermented into SCFAs such as butyrate, which support epithelial repair, maintain goblet cells, and restore crypt architecture, while also modulating gut microbiota and immune responses.[89] Collectively, these findings suggest that soluble fiber supplementation may represent a promising strategy to attenuate HFHS-induced colonic injury; therefore, this study evaluated the effects of inulin, glucomannan, and their combination on colonic histomorphology and mucosal integrity in mice.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental design

Kemuning Laboratory Animal Supplier (Karanganyar, Indonesia) provided male C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks old; 18–22 g; n = 25) for 1 week of habituation. The mice were kept in standardized, regulated conditions with a 12-h light–dark cycle, 22°C–25°C, 50%–60% moisture level, and two mice per cage. Typical chow and solution were available to them without restriction (ad libitum). The mice were subsequently divided into five groups (n = 5) at random: normal-fat diet (NFD), HFHS diet, and HFHS supplemented with either glucomannan (HGO), inulin (HIO), or both inulin and glucomannan (HIG). The HFHS groups were fed a fat-rich diet (5 kcal/g) with 20% (w/v) sucrose as a drinking solution, whereas the NFD group was fed typical chow (3.8 kcal/g). For 8 weeks, all diets were given unrestrictedly, and daily food and fluid intake was tracked along with weekly body weight measurements.

At study completion, mice were anesthetized (ketamine/xylazine), euthanized, and colonic tissues were collected for histological and immunohistochemical analyses. NFD and HFHS groups served as shared controls within a western diet-induced colonic inflammation model under identical conditions, while this study specifically evaluated the preventive effects of dietary fiber supplementation.[6] The Health Research Ethics Committee, Faculty of Medicine, Widya Mandala Surabaya Catholic University, Surabaya, Indonesia, approved all animal studies in compliance with institutional and national standards (Approval No. 0069/WM12/KEPK/MHS/T/2025).

Colon histological analysis

For histological analysis, colonic specimens were immersed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, paraffin-processed with 5-μm sections, and then treated with hematoxylin and eosin. Microscopic assessment was performed at ×400 using an Olympus CX31 microscope. The severity of colitis was independently evaluated by two blinded observers based on a validated scoring system.[10] Quantitative measurements of crypt length and mucosal thickness were obtained from at least five well-oriented crypts per sample using Image Raster 3 software (Miconos, Yogyakarta, Indonesia).

Colon immunohistochemical analysis

After deparaffinization in xylene and rehydration in graded ethanol, paraffin-infiltrated colon histological sections (5 μm) underwent immunohistochemical examination. Heat-induced epitope reactivation in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) was utilized for antigen recovery following hydrogen peroxide quenching of endogenous peroxidase and Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) washing. Sections were allowed to react with anti-IL-6 or anti-IL-10 antibodies (1:200) for an entire night at 4°C, followed by 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB)-based visualization and hematoxylin counterstaining. Stained sections were examined at ×400 (Olympus CX31), with images captured from at least 10 random high-power fields per sample. The IL-6 and IL-10 quantification was conducted employing FIJI (ImageJ) (Laboratory for Optical and Computational Instrumentation, University of Wisconsin–Madison, Madison, WI, USA) after color deconvolution of the DAB signal, with staining intensity expressed as inverted mean gray value (optical density) to reflect protein expression.

Statistical analyses

GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) were used for the statistical analyses. The substantial level was set at P < 0.05, and the findings were summarized as mean ± standard deviation. The usage of one-way analysis of variance with Tukey’s post hoc test or the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s correction depended on the homogeneity and normality of the data. Total calorie intake was controlled for as a potential confounding factor using analysis of covariance, and relationships were evaluated using Pearson’s or Spearman’s techniques as necessary. Histomorphometric analyses included all animals, whereas immunohistochemical variables (IL-6 and IL-10) were measured in a predefined subset (n = 3 per group) using a complete-case approach, resulting in variable sample sizes and degrees of freedom across analyses. All calculations were based on the actual number of biological replicates included.

RESULTS

Inulin, glucomannan, and their combination mitigate colonic inflammation and mucosal architecture damage

Total colitis scores differed significantly among the groups (P = 0.0001) [Figure 1a-c]. HFHS-fed mice exhibited markedly higher colitis scores than NFD controls (P = 0.0008). Inulin, glucomannan, and combined supplementation significantly reduced colitis scores compared with HFHS-fed mice (HIO: P =0.0008; HGO: P =0.0013; and HIG: P =0.0001), with no significant disparities identified relative to NFD or among the fiber-treated groups. The disease severity in untreated HFHS mice was characterized by marked mucosal inflammation, epithelial damage, and architectural distortion.

Inulin, glucomannan, and their combination modulate colonic cytokine balance

HFHS-fed mice showed markedly elevated IL-6 expression versus NFD controls (P = 0.0001), which was significantly reduced by inulin, glucomannan, and their combination (all P ≤ 0.001) to levels comparable with NFD, with no differences among fiber-treated groups [Figure 2a and b]. Conversely, only the HIG group showed a substantial rise in IL-10 expression relative to the controls and single-fiber groups (all P < 0.015) [Figure 2a and c]. As a result, the HIG group had the lowest IL-6/IL-10 ratio (P ≤ 0.0066 vs. controls), while single fibers similarly decreased the ratio compared with the HFHS group [Figure 2d]. While IL-10 showed inverse correlations with body weight (r = −0.52, P = 0.048), colitis score (r = −0.48, P = 0.069), as well as a direct link with mucosal thickness (r = 0.57, P = 0.027), correlation analysis showed that IL-6 was strongly linked with colitis score (r = 0.83, P < 0.001) and relatively correlated with body weight (r = 0.53, P = 0.043) [Figure 2e].

DISCUSSION

Disruption of colonic barrier integrity contributes to metabolic and inflammatory diseases, making mucosal preservation a key therapeutic target.[11] By utilizing a subchronic western-style diet that combines high-fat pellets with sucrose-enriched drinking water, this study closely mimics human dietary behavior.[712] Soluble fibers were administered via the sucrose solution to ensure consistent intake and palatability, simulating beverage-based fiber consumption. Furthermore, fiber doses were carefully selected to minimize caloric confounding, a methodological refinement that strengthens the validity of diet-induced colonic inflammation models.[1314]

Fiber supplementation reduced HFHS-induced weight gain independent of total calorie intake, consistent with microbiota-mediated metabolic regulation.[1516] Covariate-adjusted analyses confirmed that all fiber interventions reduced colonic inflammation severity independent of energy intake, indicating a direct anti-inflammatory action of inulin and glucomannan on the colonic mucosa. Histomorphometric findings further demonstrated that inulin, either alone or combined with glucomannan, was superior to glucomannan alone in preserving mucosal thickness and crypt architecture. This differential effect may be attributed to enhanced fermentability, SCFA production, and subsequent stimulation of epithelial repair processes.[15] The inverse relationships observed between colitis scores, crypt length, and mucosal thickness underscore that maintaining mucosal architecture is critical for reducing the inflammatory burden in the colon.

The HFHS-induced elevation of IL-6 observed in this study is consistent with its established role in diet-related intestinal inflammation and epithelial injury.[6] The normalization of IL-6 levels across all fiber-supplemented groups supports a mechanism of fermentation-dependent suppression of pro-inflammatory signaling. In contrast, the selective and robust upregulation of IL-10 observed only with combined inulin–glucomannan supplementation suggests that dual-fiber intervention may more effectively promote regulatory immune responses than single-fiber supplementation. This additive potential was further reflected by a pronounced reduction in the IL-6/IL-10 ratio, an inflammatory balance’s integrated marker that is increasingly recognized as a prognostic indicator in chronic colitis models.[1718]

The observed correlations between cytokine expression, colonic inflammation severity, body weight, and mucosal architecture provide additional evidence for a mechanistic link between HFHS-induced metabolic stress, inflammatory cytokine imbalance, and progressive structural damage of the colonic mucosa. Collectively, these findings indicate that the preservation of mucosal architecture is intrinsically linked to inflammatory control.[11] Furthermore, the data suggest that combined inulin–glucomannan supplementation confers additive benefits in maintaining colonic homeostasis under pro-inflammatory dietary conditions.

This study employed a subchronic western-style diet model with rigorous monitoring of calorie and fluid intake, enabling the assessment of calorie-independent effects of soluble fiber supplementation. Fiber delivery via a sucrose solution ensured consistent intake and good palatability, as demonstrated in our previous study.[19] Nevertheless, this study has drawbacks, such as a small sample size, evaluation of the single-dosing regimen, and the lack of molecular investigation of gut microbiota composition. Despite these constraints, the findings provide compelling histological and immunological evidence that soluble fibers – particularly when combined – exert preventive effects against subchronic diet-induced colonic damage.

This study demonstrates that preventive dietary supplementation with soluble fibers mitigates western diet-induced colonic inflammation in mice, with distinct effects on structural and immunological outcomes. The combined administration of inulin and glucomannan did not confer additional structural benefits beyond inulin alone, but uniquely enhanced inflammatory cytokine balance, as reflected by the lowest IL-6/IL-10 ratio. These immunomodulatory effects occurred independently of caloric intake, whereas structural improvements were partially influenced by it.
