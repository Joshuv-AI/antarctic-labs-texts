Ultrasound-guided trans-perineal pudendal nerve block for post-hemorrhoidectomy voiding complications: a prospective randomized double-blinded study
Authors: Alrefaey K. Alrefaey, Shaimaa Ezzat Amin, Fahad S AL Amri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPost-hemorrhoidectomy urinary symptoms are common, with an incidence reaching 50% in some studies. Pudendal nerve block was used to alleviate pain and urinary symptoms after perineal surgeries. This study explored the effect of trans-perineal pudendal nerve block on the patient experience after hemorrhoidectomy, regarding urinary symptoms and analgesic requirements.MethodsThis study included 66 patients randomly allocated into two equal groups. In group P, ultrasound-guided pudendal nerve block was applied after induction of anesthesia and lithotomy position, while no block was applied in group C.ResultsThe incidence of postoperative dysuria was significantly lower in group P (14% in group P, 40% in group C, p 0.028). Also, the mean numerical rating scale score and the frequency of analgesia requirements on the first day after surgery were significantly lower in group P (1.7 ± 1 vs. 0.5 ± 0.8, p 0.00, 3.8 ± 0.31 vs. 2.7 ± 0.59, p 0.00 consecutively).ConclusionsUltrasound-guided trans-perineal pudendal nerve block resulted in a better patient experience after hemorrhoidectomy, regarding urinary symptoms and analgesic requirements, without any noted complications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-025-03458-4.

Background

Hemorrhoidectomy is a common surgical procedure. Despite the assumed simplicity of the procedure, the associated complications can be notably disappointing. Post-hemorrhoidectomy urine retention stands as a common and serious event, with an incidence reaching 50% of patients in some studies [1–4].

The mechanism of post-hemorrhoidectomy retention (PHUR) is multifactorial. The exact mechanism may include autonomic system imbalance or detrusor muscle dysfunction. The risk factors include the type of anesthesia, use of opioids, the surgical technique, female gender, and the volume of the intravenous fluids [5].

Employed strategies to decrease the incidence of PHUR involved comparing different surgical techniques, fluid restriction, sitz bath, and alpha blocker medications [1–3, 6]. Postoperative pain was also associated with a higher incidence of urinary retention after anal surgeries, necessitating adequate analgesic plan [7–9].

Pudendal nerve block (PNB) was successfully used to alleviate pain and urinary symptoms after anal surgeries [7, 8, 10]. PNB was associated with a significantly lower incidence of urinary symptoms and retention in different surgeries involving the perineal region [11–13]. In our study, we applied the trans-perineal approach for ultrasound-guided pudendal nerve block in cases of hemorrhoidectomy under general anesthesia. The applied block was be performed in lithotomy position which is traditionally used for anal surgeries. Also, the ultrasound guidance allowed employing a small local anesthetic (LA) dose, minimizing the possible complications.

This study hypothesizes that US-guided trans-perineal approach is a safe and applicable technique that would decrease the incidence of PHUR (primary outcome). Secondary outcomes included postoperative voiding symptoms, postoperative pain scores, and postoperative analgesia consumption.

Methods

This prospective randomized controlled double-blinded study was conducted in a tertiary hospital in KSA between May 2024 and November 2024. The study design adhered to the principles set in the Helsinki Declaration and to CONSORT guidelines. The study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee at King Khalid University, KSA, on 22−3-2024 (ECM#2024 − 507, Chairperson: Dr. Mohammad Y. Abodahmah) and registered in the UMIN Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN000054259, on 25-4-2024).

The study included adult patients of both genders, suffering from hemorrhoids grade 3–4, planned open hemorrhoidectomy (Milligan-Morgan hemorrhoidectomy) under general anesthesia. Patients with known allergy to bupivacaine, redo surgery, preoperative voiding problems, prostatic hyperplasia, neurological disease affecting voiding control, or the patient’s ability to communicate were excluded from the study. A written informed consent was obtained from all included patients after a detailed explanation of the study protocol. A software-generated randomization list was used to randomize patients into two equal groups: Pudendal block group (Group P; n = 33), Control group (Group C; n = 33). On the day of surgery, an independent physician used opaque sealed envelopes, containing the generated codes, to allocate the patients in each group. The included patents and the physician involved in data collection were blinded to the allocation process.

All patients were subjected to routine preoperative assessment and were educated about the study protocol and outcomes. Anesthesia technique was standardized for all patients. Upon arrival to OR and connection to basic monitors (ECG, NIBP, Spo2), anesthesia was induced using IV Propofol 1–2 mg/kg, IV Fentanyl citrate 0.5 µ/kg, IV Atracurium Besylate 0.25–0.5 mg/kg. Airway was secured and adequate ventilation confirmed by EtCo2 tracing. Anesthesia was maintained using sevoflurane 1–2% in a 40% oxygen-air gas mixture. Patient is safely moved into lithotomy position, and then allocated to the study randomization sequence to receive pudendal nerve block in group P or no block in group C according. After ensuring hemodynamic stability, each of the patients of both groups received 1 g paracetamol and ondansetron 4 mg intravenously. After completion of the surgery, awake extubation was performed after reversal of the muscle relaxant and fulfillment of extubation criteria. Post-operative paracetamol 1 g/6 h was administered intravenously, every 6 h, with rescue analgesia (IV pethidine 0.5 mg/kg when NRS ≥ 4).

Pudendal nerve block (PNB) technique

After induction of anesthesia and putting the patient is in lithotomy position, sterilization and draping of perineal area, the lateral border of the superficial transducer, covered by a sterile drape, is placed over the ischial tuberosity localized by palpation, in an oblique sagittal or transverse position. Both B-mode and color Doppler mode were used for the recognition of the perineal anatomy [Acoustic shadow of the ischial tuberosity (IT), pudendal neurovascular bundle (PNVB), Sacrospinous ligament SSL, Sacrotuberous ligament (STL), superficial transverse perineal muscle (STPM)]. The PNVB is expected to be located just medial to the IT between the SSL and STL, identified as two hyperechoic lines. After LA infiltration, a 25G x 50 mm needle is advanced out-of-plane in an oblique orientation towards the targeted PNVB, and hydrolocalization is used to identify the exact needle tip placement. After gentle aspiration, the local anesthetic mixture (5 ml of bupivacaine 0.25%) was slowly injected. The procedure was then repeated on the other side (Fig. 1). Outcomes definitions and data collection

Data were collected by an independent physician who was blinded to the study allocation process. Demographic data (Age, weight, height), medical history, degree of hemorrhoids, and surgical technique were collected. The incidence of urinary retention (defined as an unproductive urge to urinate, or small volume micturition with persistent sensation of incomplete emptying), dysuria (any discomfort or burning at the urethra and meatus during voiding), and urinary incontinence were recorded for the 1 st 24 h after surgery. Postoperative analgesic requirements, including the frequency of rescue analgesic requirements, NRS score at 0,1,2,6,12, 24 h, or at discharge after surgery. Also, the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) was recorded on the first postoperative day. Any local complications related to the procedure in group P were recorded and followed in the postoperative visits, including local infection, hematoma, or ecchymosis.

Sample size calculation and statistical analysis

Based on the results of a previous study [4]. Urinary retention after hemorrhoidectomy can affect more than 52% of patients. Pudendal nerve block was assumed to decrease the urinary symptoms after hemorrhoidectomy to 25%; a total sample size of 66 was sufficient to achieve a study power of 0.9 with an alpha error of 0.05. G*power software version 3.1.9.7 was used for sample size calculation.

IBM SPSS (USA) version 22 was used for statistical analysis of the collected data. Descriptive data were described as mean ± SD or median, interquartile range (IQR) as appropriate, and compared using an independent sample T-test or Mann-Whitney U test. Nominal data were described as numbers (percentages) and compared using the chi-square test. A P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This study investigated the effect of ultrasound-guided anterior approach of pudendal nerve block on postoperative pain and urinary symptoms after open hemorrhoidectomy under GA. Patients who received pudendal nerve block (group P) showed a significantly lower incidence of postoperative dysuria. The incidence of urinary retention was lower in group P but did not reach statistical significance. Also, pudendal nerve block was associated with a significantly better analgesic profile, in the form of lower NRS scores and lower rescue analgesic requirements.

The pudendal nerve is a mixed nerve originating from the sacral plexus (S2, S3, S4). It runs through the greater sciatic foramen to exit the pelvis and then enter the perineum, through the pudendal canal, accompanied with pudendal blood vessels. The pudendal nerve then divides into three branches (inferior rectal nerve, perineal branch, the dorsal nerve of the penis or clitoris) [14] to supply the skin, organs, and muscles of the perineum. The Inferior rectal branch innervates the perianal skin and lower third of the anal canal, Fig. 2.

PNB has been proposed as an effective analgesic modality for pain after anal surgeries including hemorrhoidectomy [8]. Classically, transvaginal approach was used for pudendal nerve block in obstetric and gynecological procedures. Other techniques emerged to target the pudendal nerve in anal and urologic procedures guided by fluoroscopy or US including perineal, gluteal or perianal approaches [15, 16]. Ultrasound-guided trans-perineal pudendal nerve block utilizes two approaches: the anterior or posterior approach. In our study, the anterior approach was employed to benefit from the already applied lithotomy position and the relative ease of accessing the nerve, increasing the success rate of the block.

The traditional trans-vaginal approach for PNB was associated was a range of complications including intravascular injection, bladder, bowel injury and a high risk for needle stick injuries [17]. However, the safety of US-guided PNB techniques has been reported to be extremely low [18]. These complications included pudendal nerve injury, local hematoma, intravascular injection, local tissue atrophy and weakness, urinary or fecal incontinence. As indicated above, and in accordance to our results, US-guided techniques, allowing for direct visualization of needle advancement and real-time LA spread, would prevent these complications and assure the block safety and efficacy [18–20].

The incidence of urinary symptoms after hemorrhoidectomy ranges from 25–52% [4, 5, 21–23]. Risk factors include female gender, obesity, postoperative opioids, spinal anesthesia, higher grade hemorrhoids, and liberal intravenous fluids administration [5]. While the postoperative pain is the most incriminated factor, other etiologies include detrusor muscle dysfunction, external sphincter hypertonicity associated with pain, anxiety, sympathetic stimulation, distention of the anal canal and perineum [4, 23].

In accordance with our results, the incidence of voiding problems after hemorrhoidectomy was significantly lower in patients who received a pudendal nerve block [8]. Similarly, the incidence of urinary retention was lower when PNB was applied compared to spinal anesthesia alone [24]. A recent meta-analysis assessing the efficacy and safety of PNB after hemorrhoidectomy showed a significantly lower incidence of PHUR when groups receiving PNB were compared to control groups [RR = 0.51, 95% CI (027, 0.97), P = 0.04] [11]. The beneficial effect of PNB on voiding function can be related to the absence of micturition reflex affection and thus the maintenance of normal vesical function. The external urethral sphincter tone is increased after hemorrhoidectomy due to inadequate analgesia and sympathetic overstimulation, a situation that is attenuated by the better analgesia offered by PNB [11, 25, 26].

The dense nerve supply in the perineal area augments the severity of pain after hemorrhoidectomy. Moderate to severe pain after surgical hemorrhoidectomy can affect nearly two-thirds of cases [27, 28]. Several analgesic strategies have been used for pain management after hemorrhoidectomy. The use of pharmacological agents like paracetamol, NSAIDS, Gabapentinoids, and opioids was limited by the long duration and the severe nature of post-hemorrhoidectomy pain, and the drug-related side effects, including gastric ulcers, cardiovascular events, respiratory depression, urinary retention, and pruritus [29]. Local infiltration of drugs like nifedipine, nitroglycerine, metronidazole lignocaine was investigated as an analgesic agent. Additionally, the use of local anesthetic infiltration and perineal blocks in the multimodal protocols was associated with lower pain scores in the early postoperative period, but it cannot be repeated for long-term analgesia [19, 30].

The severity of pain after hemorrhoidectomy is affected by the type of surgical technique, the presence of anal spasm, perioperative analgesic and anesthetic plan,, patient age, and gender [31, 32]. In general, applying local anesthetic techniques, either local infiltration or pudendal nerve block, resulted in lower NRS scores and rescue analgesia requirements with both general or spinal anesthesia [33–35]. In a metanalysis by Xia et al. including 501 patients in 6 studies, PNB either guided by nerve stimulator or US resulted in lower NRS score at different points at the first 48 h after hemorrhoidectomy [8]. These results are comparable to our results where group p showed a statistically significant drop in NRS scores with lower postoperative analgesic requirements. Another review article including 7 randomized studies confirmed the safety and efficacy of PNB to decrease the analgesic medications and alleviate postoperative complications, including PONV and POUR [7]. The lower NRS scores and thus lower use of narcotics, plus decreased surgical stress response with PNB was associated with a lower incidence of PONV after hemorrhoidectomy in more than one study [7, 25].

The local anesthetic and anti-inflammatory effect of LA used as infiltration or in a regional block is known to attenuate the peripheral sensitization effect of inflammatory mediators implicated in acute pain development. This effect will potentiate the inhibitory effect of general anesthesia on the central sensitization process, and subsequently improve patient postoperative pain [36]. This effect is more evident when preemptive blockade of the nociceptive impulse transmission to the CNS is achieved [37].

Limitations of this study include the elimination of high-risk groups for postoperative urinary complications, like patients with prostatic hyperplasia or previous urogenital surgeries. Exclusion of the high-risk patients can obscure the clinical value of the pudendal block in these groups. Also, using only the clinical definition for POUR can underestimate the situation; the ultrasound examination of bladder volume could add value to our results. Unfortunately, using the ultrasound for bladder volume assessment was not feasible due to logistical issues in our center.

Conclusions

US-guided pudendal nerve block resulted in a better patient experience after hemorrhoidectomy, regarding urinary symptoms and analgesic requirements, without any noted serious complications. Future studies are encouraged to investigate the value of PNB in high-risk groups, including patients with preoperative lower urinary problems.
