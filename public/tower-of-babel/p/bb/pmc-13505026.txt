Cross-cultural adaptation and psychometric evaluation of the Daycare Anesthesia Satisfaction questionnaire in Turkish-speaking adults undergoing ambulatory surgery
Authors: Merve Bulun Yediyıldız, Irem Durmus, Hulya Yilmaz Ak, Kubra Taskin, Ayşe Demirden, Gulten Arslan, Kemal Tolga Saraçoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to perform the cross-cultural adaptation of the Daycare Anesthesia Satisfaction (DAS) Questionnaire and to evaluate its psychometric properties in Turkish-speaking adults undergoing ambulatory surgery.MethodsThis methodological cross-sectional study was conducted with 560 patients undergoing elective ambulatory surgery. The adaptation procedure followed the established cross-cultural translation procedures, which included forward translation, back translation, expert panel review and cognitive debriefing. Construct validity was evaluated using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and convergent validity was assessed using composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE) values. Reliability was analyzed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).ResultsThe scale-level content validity index was 0.95. The Turkish-adapted DAS demonstrated excellent internal consistency for the total score (Cronbach’s α = 0.971). Test-retest reliability was excellent (ICC = 0.988, 95% CI: 0.986–0.989). CFA results indicated a mixed fit for the original four-factor structure; while absolute fit indices were acceptable (χ²/df = 3.53, RMSEA = 0.07, SRMR = 0.06), incremental fit indices fell below optimal thresholds (CFI = 0.83, TLI = 0.81). Nevertheless, the CR and AVE values for all factors exceeded the recommended thresholds (> 0.84 and > 0.51), indicating strong convergent validity.ConclusionThe Turkish adapted DAS questionnaire demonstrated excellent reliability and satisfactory content and convergent validity for Turkish-speaking adults undergoing ambulatory surgery. Although CFA provided only partial support for the original four-factor structure, the instrument appears to be a useful tool for assessing patient satisfaction in clinical practice and research. Given that only partial support was obtained for the four-factor structure, this conclusion should be regarded as provisional with respect to structural validity. Further multicentre studies are warranted to confirm the structural validity of the DAS-T and broaden its generalizability.Keywords: Ambulatory surgery, Anesthesia, Patient satisfaction, Surveys and questionnaires, Questionnaire

Introduction

The increasing shift from inpatient surgery to ambulatory surgical care has had a substantial impact on perioperative quality assessment [1, 2]. As the number of procedures performed on a same-day basis continues to increase, conventional indicators such as morbidity and mortality are no longer adequate to comprehensively assess the quality of care [2]. Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), including patient satisfaction, have therefore become increasingly important in evaluating perioperative services, reflecting a broader shift towards patient-centred quality metrics in day-surgery care [3–6].

Despite the recognized importance of satisfaction measurement, there exists an enormous gap in the availability of robust, standardized tools unique to the ambulatory setting as highlighted in earlier reviews— a gap that is particularly pronounced for day-surgery populations, where the perioperative experience differs substantially from that of hospitalised patients [7]. Existing tools, such as the Iowa Satisfaction with Anesthesia Scale (ISAS) or the Evaluation du Vecu de l’Anesthesie (EVAN-G), were either too specific (e.g., focused on monitored care) or too broad (e.g., combining inpatient and outpatient data) to fully capture the unique experience of day surgery patients [7–9]. To address this specific gap, Joselyn et al. have developed and validated the Daycare Anesthesia Satisfaction (DAS) questionnaire for assessing patient satisfaction in the ambulatory anesthesia setting [9, 10].

However, instruments developed in one linguistic and cultural context cannot be assumed to perform equivalently in another. Cross-cultural adaptation is imperative to ensure semantic, conceptual and contextual equivalence prior to the implementation of an instrument in a distinct population; a simple forward translation is therefore insufficient, and a structured, committee-based adaptation process is required [11, 12].

The objective of this study was to cross-culturally adapt the DAS Questionnaire into Turkish and to evaluate its psychometric properties in Turkish-speaking adults undergoing ambulatory surgery.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This methodological, cross-sectional study was conducted at a tertiary-level hospital between July and September 2022. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Kartal Dr. Lütfi Kırdar City Hospital Clinical Research Ethics Committee, Istanbul, Türkiye (Decision No: 2022/514/228/20). Written permission to translate and cross-culturally adapt the scale was provided via email from the original author Dr. Anita Shirley Joselyn. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Sample and setting

Adult patients scheduled for elective ambulatory surgery were considered eligible if they were Turkish-speaking, aged 18–65 years and had an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status of I-III. Patients who were unable to communicate effectively or complete the interview process were excluded.

The sample size was determined according to the commonly recommended rule of at least 10 participants per item for psychometric analysis [13]. Given the 27-item structure of the DAS, a minimum of 270 participants was considered necessary. To improve the robustness of the factor-analytic procedures, 560 patients were ultimately recruited, thereby comfortably exceeding the minimum recommended subject-to-item ratio and enhancing the stability of the parameter estimates.

Data collection tools

Data were collected using a Patient Information Form and DAS questionnaire. Patient Information Form was prepared by the researchers and included participants’ the following demographic and clinical variables: age, gender, education level and ASA physical status classification. The original DAS questionnaire was previously developed by Joselyn et al. and consists of 27 items designed to measure patient satisfaction in the ambulatory setting [9]. The instrument was developed to assess patient satisfaction across its original four dimensions. The four dimensions comprise the following item distributions: preoperative (8 items), intraoperative-physical (7 items), intraoperative-emotional (6 items) and postoperative (6 items). In the original validation study, Joselyn et al. [9] reported satisfactory internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.84) and excellent test-retest reliability (ICC = 0.97), with confirmatory factor analysis supporting the four-factor structure. These psychometric benchmarks served as the reference against which the present Turkish adaptation was evaluated. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (very dissatisfied) to 5 (very satisfied), with higher scores indicating greater satisfaction [9].

Procedure language adaptation

The cross-cultural adaptation process was conducted in accordance with established guidelines [12]. Initially, the original English questionnaire was translated independently into Turkish by four translators. All four forward translators were native Turkish speakers; two had healthcare backgrounds (a physician and a nurse) and two were professional translators without medical expertise, in accordance with established guidelines [12]. These translations were subsequently consolidated into a unified preliminary Turkish version through a consensus meeting involving the research team (two anesthesiologists and one methodologist with expertise in psychometric methods). Secondly, independent back-translations were performed by two native English-speaking translators who had no prior knowledge of the original DAS questionnaire. In conclusion, an expert panel of eight specialists reviewed all versions to assess semantic, idiomatic, experiential, conceptual and cultural equivalence. In accordance with Beaton et al. [12] guidelines, the expert committee reviewed all translation versions simultaneously — including the original instrument, all four forward translations and both back-translations — in order to identify and resolve discrepancies through consensus. The final version was produced based on the feedback received. A subsequent cognitive debriefing phase was then conducted with 15 patients to assess clarity and comprehensibility, and no major revisions were required.

Data collection

The finalized Turkish-adapted DAS (DAS-T) was administered through face-to-face interview. The interview format was deliberately chosen to accommodate the wide range of educational backgrounds in our patient population and to prevent missing data in the immediate postoperative period. All interviews were conducted by a single research physician who had received standardized pre-study training prior to data collection, ensuring consistency throughout the study and eliminating inter-rater variability. The first interview was administered in the surgical ward where the patient had undergone the procedure, prior to discharge, once the patient was fully alert and medically cleared for discharge. To assess the test-retest reliability the questionnaire was re-administered to 270 participants 5–14 days after the first application. In accordance with the sample-size recommendations of Kyriazos, this comparatively large retest subsample was chosen a priori to yield a narrow 95% confidence interval around the ICC and to guard against the imprecision that characterises the smaller retest samples frequently reported in adaptation studies [13].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) software package. CFA was carried out using IBM AMOS 28.0. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Descriptive statistics for demographic and clinical data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), median (Minimum-Maximum) or as frequencies (n) and percentages (%).

Reliability analysis

Internal Consistency was evaluated by calculating Cronbach’s Alpha coefficients for the total scale and its subdimensions. Test-Retest Reliability was assessed by calculating the ICC.

Validity analysis

Construct validity was assessed using CFA to determine the validity of the data’s original 4-factor structure. The goodness-of-fit of the model was assesed through the Chi-square/degrees of freedom ratio ( χ 2 /df ), the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), the Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) and the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). Acceptable model fit was considered to be χ 2 /df ≤ 5, CFI/TLI ≥ 0.90 and RMSEA/SRMR ≤ 0.08. Additionally, convergent validity was assessed by evaluating the CR and the AVE for each factor with recommended thresholds being CR ≥ 0.70 and AVE ≥ 0.50. Discriminant validity was not formally assessed in the present study. Because the original DAS lacks an established competing factor structure or a theoretically related comparator instrument in the existing literature, standard approaches such as the HTMT ratio or the Fornell–Larcker criterion could not be meaningfully applied at this initial stage of cross-cultural adaptation. This omission is explicitly acknowledged as a limitation and is identified as a priority for future multicentre studies once a suitable reference model or comparator instrument becomes available.

Group comparisons and correlations

Since the satisfaction scores were not normally distributed, the Mann-Whitney U test was used for pairwise comparisons and the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for comparisons of more than two groups. The relationship between age and satisfaction scores was analyzed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Reliability analysis

The internal consistency of the DAS-T was excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.971) and similarly, high internal consistency was observed across subscales (α = 0.868–0.925). Test-retest reliability was also excellent for the total score (ICC = 0.988, 95% CI: 0.986–0.989) and subscale scores (ICC range: 0.969–0.983), indicating strong temporal stability, with uniformly narrow 95% confidence intervals that underline the precision of the reliability estimates (Table 2).

Validity analysis

The Scale-Level Content Validity Index (S-CVI) for DAS-T was 0.95, indicating excellent content validity. CFA results showed acceptable absolute fit indices for the original four-factor structure ( χ²/df ratio = 3.53, SRMR = 0.06, RMSEA = 0.07) whereas incremental fit indices were below conventional thresholds (CFI = 0.83, TLI = 0.81), indicating partial support for the original model (Table 3). The standardised factor loadings ranged from 0.58 to 0.89. The CR values ranged from 0.840 to 0.924, and the AVE values ranged from 0.516 to 0.648. These results support convergent validity across all domains; however, the overall CFA findings indicate only partial replication of the original four-factor structure (Table 4).

Descriptive satisfaction scores and group comparisons

The mean total DAS-T score was 54.3 ± 19.6. A significant, weak negative correlation was observed between age and total satisfaction score (Spearman’s r = -0.269, p < 0.001). Subscale scores for all four domains were significantly lower in patients older than 45 years and in patients who had previous day-care anesthesia experience (p < 0.05 for all subscales) (Table 5). In addition total satisfaction scores differed significantly based on education level (Kruskal-Wallis, p < 0.001). Post-hoc analysis showed that patients with higher education levels reported significantly higher overall satisfaction compared to those with lower education levels (Table 5).

Discussion

The objective of the present study was to assess the cross-cultural adaptation and psychometric characteristics of the DAS Questionnaire in Turkish-speaking adults undergoing ambulatory surgery. The findings indicate that the adapted questionnaire has excellent internal consistency, excellent temporal stability and satisfactory content and convergent validity. However, structural validity should be interpreted cautiously, as CFA provided only partial support for the original four-factor structure.

A standout result of our validation is the remarkably high reliability profile. The DAS-T achieved an internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.971) that actually exceeded the results reported in the original study by Joselyn et al. (alpha = 0.84) [9]. This suggests a very high degree of item homogeneity when applied to this population, consistent with a coherent underlying satisfaction construct. At the same time, such a high alpha value should be interpreted with caution, as it may also indicate a degree of item overlap or redundancy. In addition, the elevated satisfaction scores and their relatively narrow distribution raise the possibility of ceiling effects, with some respondents clustering near the upper end of the scale. This pattern may potentially inflate internal-consistency estimates. Furthermore, the test-retest reliability was nearly perfect (ICC = 0.988), surpassing the already impressive ICC of 0.97 found in the original development phase [9]. Although such stability across a large cohort provides confidence that the scale remains consistent over time and across different patient encounters, the very high ICC should also be interpreted in light of possible ceiling effects and restricted score variability. Future psychometric studies should therefore include item-level ceiling analyses and, where indicated, parsimonious item reduction to determine whether some items may be redundant.

With respect to construct validity, the CFA analysis demonstrated a mixed fit profile. While the absolute fit indices (χ²/df, RMSEA and SRMR) demonstrated compatibility with established criteria for global model fit, the incremental fit indices (CFI and TLI) fell below the optimal threshold of 0.90. This pattern of discrepancy between absolute and incremental fit indices is a well-documented phenomenon in the field of adapting complex patient satisfaction and psychological tools across diverse cultural contexts, in which strict incremental indices such as the CFI and TLI are known to be sensitive to model complexity and to the number of items, even when the approximation error remains low [5]. The findings indicate that, while the overall error of approximation is minimal, the original specific factor model was not replicated flawlessly within the current sample. Consequently, while the structural validity cannot be considered unequivocal, the strong convergent validity and excellent internal consistency indicate that the psychometric performance of the instrument remains highly satisfactory and clinically applicable. Accordingly and in line with the partial structural support reflected in the incremental fit indices (CFI = 0.83; TLI = 0.81), we have deliberately refrained from claiming full structural equivalence with the original instrument. The four-factor structure should therefore be regarded as provisional at present and requires confirmation in independent multicentre samples.

A number of possible explanations have been posited to account for this partial structural mismatch. Firstly, it is important to note that satisfaction is a contextual outcome that may vary depending on factors such as language, patients’ expectations, communication, and perioperative care processes. Secondly, the decision to utilize face-to-face interviews as opposed to self-administration might have exerted a substantial influence on the observed response patterns. Thirdly, given the ordinal nature of the DAS, future psychometric studies employing estimators more suitable for categorical data may offer a more precise evaluation of model fit.

Convergent validity results were supportive across all four domains, as all CR values were well above 0.80, and all AVE values were well above 0.50. This finding indicates the presence of sufficient common variance across all items within each domain, despite the suboptimal global fit of the model.

A further consideration warranting attention is the potential influence of the interview-based administration on the reliability estimates reported. It is well established that patient satisfaction measurement in healthcare settings is particularly susceptible to social desirability bias, as patients may feel reluctant to express dissatisfaction when responding in the presence of a healthcare-affiliated interviewer. This phenomenon may partly account for the exceptionally high Cronbach’s alpha (0.971) and ICC (0.988) observed in this study, as well as the elevated mean satisfaction scores. These coefficients should therefore be interpreted with the possibility of item redundancy and ceiling effects in mind, since the elevated and narrowly distributed satisfaction scores suggest that a proportion of respondents clustered near the upper end of the scale, potentially inflating internal-consistency and stability estimates. Future validation studies are encouraged to employ anonymous, self-administered formats to assess whether the reported psychometric values hold under conditions less susceptible to social desirability effects. In addition, future work employing item-level ceiling analysis and, where indicated, parsimonious item reduction is warranted to determine whether some items may be redundant.

Our analysis of demographic variables offered some compelling insights into factors associated with patient satisfaction. We found that younger patients and those with higher education levels reported significantly better experiences. Interestingly, the negative correlation observed between age and satisfaction in the present study appears to differ from some previous reports suggesting that older patients may report higher perioperative satisfaction [14, 15]. This might be explained by the “fast-track” nature of ambulatory surgery; younger, active individuals may appreciate the efficiency of quick discharge, whereas older patients might feel more comfortable with traditional, supportive inpatient care. Additionally, the higher satisfaction scores among university graduates point toward the critical role of health literacy, which shapes patients’ ability to process preoperative information, adhere to discharge instructions, and form realistic expectations of care [16]. Patients who can better navigate preoperative instructions and discharge plans likely feel more empowered and satisfied [17, 18]. This suggests that centers should consider using more accessible communication tools—like simplified visuals or digital alerts—to support patients with lower education levels, thereby helping to reduce satisfaction disparities linked to educational background and to promote more equitable perioperative care.

These group comparison analyses were conducted as a preliminary assessment of known-groups validity, a recognised component of construct validity. The between-group contrasts were specified a priori rather than post hoc, based on prior perioperative-satisfaction research suggesting that age and health literacy-related characteristics may be associated with satisfaction outcomes [5, 14, 16]. Before analysis, we hypothesised that younger patients and those with higher educational attainment, considered a commonly used indicator related to health literacy, would report higher satisfaction. The directionally consistent and statistically significant findings therefore provide preliminary known-groups validity evidence, rather than merely descriptive between-group comparisons. These results suggest that the DAS-T may be sensitive to theoretically meaningful differences between patient subgroups, thereby contributing to the construct validity evidence base for the instrument and supporting its potential utility in clinical and research settings.

The current findings suggest that the Turkish-adapted DAS has the potential to serve as a reliable instrument for evaluating patient satisfaction in ambulatory anesthesia settings. However, further multicenter validation studies are needed to examine its structural validity more thoroughly and to evaluate additional psychometric properties, including discriminant validity, known-groups validity and item-level performance.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the study was conducted in a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other ambulatory surgical settings. Secondly, the questionnaire was administered through face-to-face interviews rather than by self-administration. This approach may have introduced interviewer-related influence or social desirability bias, potentially affecting both satisfaction ratings and reliability estimates. Future studies employing anonymous, self-administered formats are encouraged to determine whether the psychometric values reported herein are maintained under conditions less susceptible to such biases. Thirdly, although the Turkish-adapted questionnaire demonstrated excellent reliability and satisfactory content and convergent validity, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) provided only partial support for the original four-factor structure. Fourthly, discriminant validity was not assessed in the present study because a reference model is not yet available in the DAS literature. Future studies should prioritise this analysis. Fifthly, as data were collected at a single tertiary centre, measurement invariance across demographic subgroups, such as age, sex and educational level, and across institutions could not be examined. Establishing configural, metric and scalar invariance in multicentre samples is therefore an important next step before between-group score comparisons can be made with full confidence. Sixthly, the present cross-sectional design did not permit evaluation of the responsiveness of the DAS-T to change over time or in response to service-level interventions; longitudinal studies are needed to characterise this property. Finally, the potential for item redundancy and ceiling effects implied by the very high internal-consistency and stability coefficients should be formally investigated in future psychometric work. As ambulatory care becomes increasingly prevalent in the post-discharge period, future research should also prioritise the assessment of patient satisfaction following patients’ return to the home environment.

Conclusion

The Turkish-adapted DAS questionnaire exhibited excellent reliability and satisfactory content and convergent validity in Turkish-speaking adults undergoing ambulatory surgery. However, CFA provided only partial support for the original four-factor structure. Consequently, the instrument should be regarded as a promising tool rather than a fully established structural replica of the original version. Further multicenter studies employing more comprehensive psychometric analyses are needed to confirm its structural validity and generalizability. Until such confirmation is obtained, the DAS-T is best characterised as a reliable instrument with supported content and convergent validity, while its four-factor structural validity remains to be fully established.
