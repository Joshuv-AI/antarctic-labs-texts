Implementation science derived from low- and middle-income countries is essential for advancing global health
Authors: Olakunle Alonge, Meredith B Brooks
Published: 2025

Global health is at a crossroads. As global health programs are scaled back and funding grows uncertain, the stakes for ensuring effective, equitable, and sustainable implementation of evidence-informed interventions have never been higher.

Implementation science offers powerful tools to translate evidence into real-world impact—but too often, its methods and frameworks are rooted in high-income settings, where health systems and contexts look nothing like those in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Unless implementation science is adapted and innovated for LMIC realities, we risk deepening inequities and perpetuating epistemic injustice in global health.

The recent collection on “Evolving implementation science concepts and methods in low- and middle-income countries” (https://link.springer.com/collections/ehbifbgffc) sought to spotlight locally driven approaches that confront these challenges head-on. The response was robust—spanning original research, reviews, and commentaries from diverse scholars and practitioners—and revealed four key insights.

First, frameworks from LMICs prioritize health systems realities. Innovative determinant frameworks are emerging that grapple directly with financing, governance, infrastructure, and workforce issues—factors often taken for granted in relatively well-functioning systems, such as those in high-income countries. For example, Dougherty et al. showed how the novel Newborn Essential Solutions and Technologies (NEST360) small and sick newborn care (SSNC) framework illuminated contextual barriers and strategies for scaling medical device coverage across Kenya, Malawi, Nigeria, and Tanzania—complementing, but not replacing, established implementation science frameworks like Implementation Research Logic Model (IRLM) and the Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance (RE-AIM) framework [1]. These insights reinforce that implementation science in LMICs must remain tightly linked to health policy and systems research [2].

Second, strategies developed in LMICs address challenges unique to these settings. They include health systems strengthening strategies for addressing broader health policy and systems challenges that complement traditional implementation strategies, which are more proximal to the implementation settings, as conceptualized by studies from high-income countries [3, 4]. Milenova and colleagues reviewed implementation strategies crucial for sustaining Official Development Assistance-funded programs, which are programs funded by foreign government aid to support development and welfare in LMICs [5]. These strategies mapped to eight components—shared principles and values, resource stability, operational linkages, local champions, staff care and capacity, leadership and multilevel partnerships, flexibility, and monitoring and evaluation; some of which have no direct analog in high-income contexts. Such approaches highlight the necessity of indigenous solutions to sustain interventions beyond donor cycles.

Third, participatory and co-designed methods accelerate evidence uptake. Kuhrt et al.demonstrated in Sierra Leone and Zambia how policy labs, which are collaborative spaces to design, test, and refine policy solutions using evidence and innovation, dismantled hierarchies, fostered trust, and shortened the lag from evidence to action [6]. Their work exemplifies that participatory approaches not only facilitate uptake of evidence to improve health services and outcomes, but they also reduce the time it takes for evidence to be translated to action. Indeed, their example is one of many participatory approaches for implementation that may be indigenous to philosophies from LMIC settings [7], which may not necessarily be well-documented in published literature.

Fourth, adaptation, not abandonment, of existing theories, models, and frameworks can advance implementation science. Studies such as those from Ikemeh et al. and Nelson et al. showed how adapting frameworks like RE-AIM and implementation outcomes for antenatal care and HIV PrEP integration revealed new insights while maintaining rigor [8, 9]. Thoughtful adaptation ensures global knowledge exchange flows bidirectionally.

Despite these advances, critical gaps remain on how to tackle various global health challenges, as defined by the United Nation’s Sustainable Development Goals, using implementation science in LMICs. Few studies explicitly examined health inequities—yet implementation science is uniquely positioned to analyze and disrupt inequitable causal pathways. Integrating systems science and shifting from descriptive frameworks to causal models could help target inequity-reducing strategies and fast-track progress toward global health goals.

The way forward is clear: implementation science must evolve with and from LMICs. It must generate contextually grounded theories, methods, and strategies that address the realities of health systems under strain, sustain gains beyond donor cycles, and confront inequities head-on. Implementation science cannot afford a one-size-fits-all approach built for high-income countries. By investing in and elevating LMIC-led innovation, we not only advance the science—we strengthen the possibility of healthier, more equitable futures worldwide.

In this moment of retreating funds and fragile programs, the imperative is urgent: implementation science for advancing global health in LMICs is not optional. It is the key to sustaining progress, protecting equity, and achieving lasting global health impact.
