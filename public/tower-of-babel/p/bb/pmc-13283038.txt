The effects of Cannabidiol on podocytes in vitro
Authors: Steven Didik, Ameneh Ahrari, Krish Taneja, Ruslan Bohovyk, Vladislav Levchenko, Lashodya V. Dissanayake, Jin O-Uchi, Alexander Staruschenko
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cannabidiol (CBD) is a popular non-psychoactive formulation commonly used to treat pain, anxiety, and nausea. Currently, CBD has little to no regulatory oversight in the United States thus, inconsistencies exist regarding how CBD is produced and controlled. Moreover, little is known about its precise biological impact on cells and tissues, particularly in the kidney. To address this, we utilized human immortalized podocytes treated with varying concentrations of CBD for different durations, and assessed their intracellular calcium changes, mitochondrial bioenergetic changes, and viability. We demonstrate for the first time that prolonged exposure to CBD induces a dose-dependent elevation in intracellular Ca2+ concentration and a decrease in mitochondrial bioenergetics, leading to cell damage and death. These findings identify mitochondrial bioenergetic impairment via calcium dysregulation as a key mechanism underlying CBD-induced podocyte cytotoxicity. Importantly, our results suggest that high-dose, long-term CBD exposure may compromise podocyte health and potentially impair kidney function.

Introduction

Cannabidiol (CBD) is a non-psychoactive compound derived from the Cannabis sativa plant [1]. The majority of commercially and recreationally available CBD is produced from the hemp plant thus making it legal to produce as it contains less than 0.3 % tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) [2]. However, most importantly, the pharmacokinetic profile of CBD, specifically renal side effects, partly due to the overwhelming variety of forms of CBD produced, remains poorly understood. This knowledge gap contributes to challenges in determining proper dosing, optimal preparation methods, and appropriate routes of administration [3]. Thus, the CBD industry remains underregulated, lacking clear federal guidelines [4]. It is speculated that CBD has therapeutic benefits in treating pain, nausea, psychosis, anxiety, and reducing renal inflammation [5,6]. However, human clinical trials have yet to be performed to validate its therapeutic potential [7,8]. It is known that CBD acts as an antagonist, allosterically regulating the endocannabinoid receptor 1 (CB1R) in the kidney [9]. Moreover, our recent data revealed that prolonged activation of cannabinoid receptors can exacerbate hypertension and contribute to kidney injury [10]. CB1R is expressed in numerous cell types in the kidney, including podocytes, proximal tubular cells, distal convoluted tubules, mesangial cells, renal vascular endothelial cells, and interstitial cells [11,12]. It has been demonstrated that increased CB1R signaling in podocytes contributes to the development of diabetic nephropathy and can induce ancillary renal injurious effects [13]. CB1R has also been shown to play a key role in promoting mitochondrial fragmentation in renal proximal tubular cells, leading to impaired organelle function and contributing to tubular injury [14]. Indeed, it has been reported that proximal tubule cell-specific deletion of CB1R in the proximal tubule cells significantly attenuates renal injury [15].

It has been postulated in in vivo studies that CBD may be beneficial to renal health [12]. For example, in a study using mice chronically treated with CBD, transcriptome results revealed beneficial changes in gene expression in the kidney linked to pathways involved in decreased inflammation and improved lipid metabolism [16]. Despite these findings, the precise implications in renal in vitro studies, specifically in podocytes, remain understudied. Here, we examined the effects of CBD at various concentrations and durations on human podocytes in vitro. We found that increasing doses and long-term exposures of CBD to podocytes altered intracellular calcium ([Ca2+]i) levels, mitochondrial bioenergetics, and induced cell death. This study demonstrates, for the first time, a key mechanistic link to which chronic high-dose CBD exposure causes injury to podocytes.

Methods

Cell culture

Human immortalized podocyte cell line AB 8/13 was generously provided by Dr. M. Saleem (University of Bristol) [17]. Cells were cultured as practiced by Ni et al. [18]. Cells were propagated in a humidified atmosphere containing 5 % CO2, at 33 °C using RPMI-1640 medium (#11875093; ThermoFisher Scientific) containing insulin–-transferrin–selenium (#41400045; ThermoFisher Scientific) and 10 % fetal bovine serum (#10437028; ThermoFisher Scientific). Passages 4–15 were used for all experiments. Differentiation was induced by transferring cultures to 37 °C in 5 % CO2 for 10–14 days. Before experimental procedures, cultures were placed in serum-free medium for 3–5 h.

Cannabidiol

CBD was purchased from Cayman Chemical Company (cat#90080). The solid crystalline compound was dissolved in ethanol according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For live cell [Ca2+]i imaging, Seahorse analysis, and cell viability assays, the stock solution was diluted to the desired concentrations in the appropriate experimental solvents, as described in the corresponding Methods sections below.

Live Cell calcium imaging

Cells were seeded in a 96-well black/clear-bottom plate (ThermoFisher Scientific, cat# 165305) at 100 % confluency and loaded with Fluo-4, AM (#20551, AAT Bioquest, 1:1000) at 37 °C for 1 h. Cells were then treated with either vehicle control or CBD (5, 15, or 45 μM), and instantaneous [Ca2+]i levels were recorded at the time of exposure. The changes in fluorescence intensity of Fluo-4 in the single podocytes were monitored by an Olympus microscope equipped with Cellinsight CX7 HCS (ThermoFisher Scientific) and Olympus LUCPlanFL objective lens (N 20×/NA 0.45) at 37 °C. The Peak fluorescent value (F) after stimulation was normalized with those before simulation (F0) in each group and shown as F/F0.

Agilent Seahorse Mito Stress Test

Oxygen consumption rates (OCR) were measured by the Agilent Seahorse XFe96 analyzer at 37 °C. Podocytes were seeded with an optimal confluency (>70 %) in XFe96 cell culture plates (Agilent Technologies, part #103794-100) under normal conditions. Varying concentrations of CBD (1, 5, 15, and 45 μM) were applied to cells for 2 and 24 h as acute and chronic treatments, respectively. Cell culture media after each experimental condition was removed and replaced in each well with 180 μL Seahorse XF base medium (DMEM) mixed with 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 2 mM glutamine and 10 mM glucose solution, warmed to 37 °C (pH 7.4). The Agilent Seahorse Mito Stress test was performed with injections of 2.5 μM oligomycin, 1 μM carbonyl cyanide-p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone (FCCP) and 1 μM rotenone/anti-mycin A, sequentially through ports A–C, respectively. In addition to basal and maximal cellular OCR, ATP-linked respiration was obtained by subtracting the OCR after oligomycin application from baseline cellular OCR.

Cell viability assay

A fluorogenic, DNA-selective and cell-impermeant dye (Nuclear Green DCS1, AAT Bioquest, cat#17550) was employed to quantify the numbers of dead or apoptotic podocytes during CBD treatment (5, 15, and 45 μM CBD). Briefly, podocytes were seeded in a 96-well black/clear bottom plate (ThermoFisher Scientific, cat# 165305) at 100 % confluency, treated with CBD, followed by the Nuclear Green DCS1 staining. One hundred μM H2O2 was used as positive control. Time-lapse images were recorded every hour over 24 h by an Agilent BioTek Cytation C10 Confocal Imaging Reader.

Statistical analysis

All the digital data were transferred to Graph Pad Prism v10 for statistical analysis. All results are shown as mean ± SEM. An unpaired Student’s t-test was performed for two data sets. For multiple comparisons, the Kruskal-Wallis test (live cell calcium imaging) and two-way ANOVA (cell viability), followed by the post hoc tests Dunn’s and Tukey’s (respectively) were performed. The value of p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

High dose CBD treatment results in Alterations to intracellular calcium levels in human podocytes

Podocytes were treated with either vehicle or 5, 15, or 45 μM CBD and instantaneous [Ca2+]i changes were recorded at the time of exposure. Our data illustrates that the application of 45 μM CBD led to a significant elevation of [Ca2+]i fluorescence intensity (ΔF/F0), a direct measurement of [Ca2+]i concentration in podocytes, immediately following application compared to controls (Fig. 1A). For 5 μM concentration, we found no significant differences in [Ca2+]i fluorescence intensity (ΔF/F0) compared to controls. Both 15 μM and 45 μM CBD significantly increased [Ca2+]i ΔF/F0 compared to controls (*p < 0.05 and ***p < 0.001, respectively), with the greatest effect observed at 45 μM (Fig. 1B).

CBD treatment impairs mitochondrial bioenergetics in human podocytes

Prolonged [Ca2+]i elevation triggers excessive mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake (i.e., mitochondrial Ca2+ overload), followed by the depolarization of mitochondrial membrane potential, a decrease in ATP production, acceleration of the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and subsequently activates cell death pathways under various pathological conditions [19]. Therefore, we next performed the Mito Stress Test assay to assess mitochondrial bioenergetics using the Agilent Seahorse XFe96 Pro. OCR was measured in podocytes after both acute and chronic treatment with varying concentrations of CBD for 2 and 24 h, respectively. Acute (2 h) CBD exposure at 5 μM significantly decreased basal, ATP-linked, and maximal respiration compared to control (Fig. 2A–D). Under higher CBD concentrations (15 and 45 μM), basal OCR was less than 20 pmol O2/min/well, below the recommended threshold for OCR measured with the XFe96 analyzer. This indicates a significant decrease in mitochondrial bioenergetics at increased CBD concentrations, and/or the cell density might be significantly lower after high doses of CBD (Fig. 2A). Further, we assessed mitochondrial bioenergetics after chronic exposure (24 h) to varying concentrations (1, 5, and 15 μM) of CBD on podocytes. Prolonged exposure to 1 μM CBD significantly reduced basal, ATP-linked, and maximal respiration in podocytes (Fig. 3A–D). 5 μM and 15 μM CBD caused severe mitochondrial bioenergetic deficits and/or significantly altered the cell density to which OCR measurements could not be obtained (Fig. 3A).

High dose CBD treatment results in human podocyte Cell death after 24 h

Vehicle control, positive control (100 μM H2O2), or 5, 15, and 45 μM CBD treatment were applied to podocytes. Podocytes with respective CBD concentrations were then placed under confocal microscopy for image recording for 24 h. Vehicle-treated controls displayed little to no change in cell death over 24 h (Fig. 4A). 100 μM H2O2-treated podocytes exhibited significant cell death over the course of the 24 h compared to vehicle control (***p < 0.001) as we previously reported [20] (Fig. 4B). CBD-treated podocytes demonstrated a dose-dependent increase in cell death over the course of 24 h when compared to controls (##p < 0.01) (Fig. 4B). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that high concentrations of CBD have a detrimental impact on podocyte viability.

Discussion

Here, we demonstrate that different concentrations of CBD can affect [Ca2+]i homeostasis and mitochondrial bioenergetics in podocytes. There is little knowledge describing the effects of CBD on podocytes; however, studies have been performed regarding the effects of CBD on mitochondrial bioenergetics and Ca2+ signaling pathways on other mammalian cell lines [21–23]. First, our data set of [Ca2+]i responses to CBD suggests that CBD can concentration-dependently activate intracellular pathways associated with increased [Ca2+]i levels, which is significant at ≥ 15 μM and at its highest level at 45 μM. Interestingly, these concentrations have a direct correlation to our cell viability data, where we observed a dose-dependent linear trend in podocyte cell death at these concentrations. Our findings align with other reports strongly suggesting that there is a correlation between increased CBD concentrations and altered [Ca2+]i signaling [24,25]. CBD is known to have a direct modulatory effect on different types of transient receptor potential (TRP) channels located on podocytes, which likely promotes Ca2+ influx at the plasma membrane [26]. In addition to the plasma-membrane ion channels such as TRP, there are several other intracellular ion channels/transporters, including mitochondrial proteins [27,28], that are affected due to CBD exposure [22,29–32]. The effect of CBD could occur through voltage-dependent anion channel 1 (VDAC1), mitochondrial Na+/Ca2+ exchanger (NCLX) or the mitochondrial Ca2+ Uniporter (MCU) [22,27–29,31]. Further, the CBD induced disruption of [Ca2+]i homeostasis may impair mitochondrial Ca2+ buffering capacity through the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) by modulating its proper assembly, as high [Ca2+]i levels are an established stimulation for improper opening and assembly of the mPTP [27,29,31]. This phenomenon induces pathological mitochondrial depolarization and decreases OCR, which then is known to induce apoptotic cell death [29]. This signaling mechanism likely underlies the CBD-induced disruption of [Ca2+]i homeostasis, altered mitochondrial bioenergetics, and increased cell damage and death observed in our study [33,34]. The activation of these intracellular mechanisms suggests that certain mitochondrial proteins may be targets to which CBD-induced disruption of [Ca2+]i homeostasis can alter the bioenergetic profile of cells.

Ca2+, arguably, serves as the most critical intracellular signaling molecule in the kidney [35], and particularly in podocytes [36]. Furthermore, it is essential for the maintenance of proper mitochondrial bioenergetics, particularly involved in ATP production [37]. To further study this, we investigated, for the first time, whether varying doses of CBD can negatively impact mitochondrial bioenergetic activity in podocytes. Our results confirmed that different concentrations of CBD can profoundly impair mitochondrial bioenergetics [38] both acutely and chronically. In both cases, we observed a significant dose-dependent reduction of mitochondrial respiration parameters. Interestingly, after 24 h of CBD exposure, our 5 μM CBD group displayed notable cell viability but diminished mitochondrial activity. This could be due to cells entering a state of metabolic reprogramming, where cells maintain viability through other pathways such as glycolysis versus oxidative phosphorylation [39]. Drummon et al. [21] demonstrated that acute (10 min), low concentrations of CBD in isolated rat brain mitochondria, and the chronic CBD treatment (24 h) on BE(2)-M17 neuroblastoma cells can significantly reduce mitochondrial oxygen consumption rates and ATP production. Further, previous studies in rat cardiac mitochondria have shown that acute exposure to cannabinoids can markedly reduce oxygen consumption rate and decrease mitochondrial membrane potential [40]. These findings align with our data demonstrating that CBD impairs mitochondrial bioenergetics in podocytes.

Under normal conditions, Ca2+ entry into the mitochondria of podocytes is a highly controlled process [41]. In pathophysiological conditions, altered mitochondrial Ca2+ homeostasis can lead to irreversible cell injury [42–45]. This is consistent with our data regarding the significant impairment of mitochondrial bioenergetics we observed with increased [Ca2+]i levels via CBD in podocytes.

It should be noted that some studies illustrate a protective effect of CBD against toxins from mitochondria [30,46,47]. Conversely, in alignment with our findings, many studies report that CBD induces cell death by disrupting [Ca2+]i homeostasis [22,29,31]. Our findings are consistent with other studies, which describe that low concentrations of CBD do not have effects on cell survivability, while higher concentrations have detrimental effects. These results align with the less pronounced decreases in mitochondrial bioenergetics and minimal increases in the [Ca2+]i response in the podocytes. Taken together, our data strongly suggest that CBD, in a dose and time-dependent manner, can affect mitochondrial bioenergetics via altered [Ca2+]i homeostasis, thus contributing to decreased cell viability in podocytes.

Limitations

Although the study provides novel in vitro evidence that high doses of CBD applied to human immortalized podocytes disrupt [Ca2+]i homeostasis and mitochondrial bioenergetics, certain limitations should be mentioned. A primary limitation of this work is that the isolated human podocytes do not fully recapitulate podocytes in vivo. Thus, further in vivo work is needed to confirm our observations. Additionally, podocytes are one component of the glomerular filtration barrier. Future studies should incorporate the effects of CBD on the endothelium and the glomerular basement membrane to provide a comprehensive assessment of CBD’s effect on glomerular function.
