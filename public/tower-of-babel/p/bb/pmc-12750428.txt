Impact of Percutaneous Intercostal Nerve Stimulation Combined With Diaphragm Pacing on Respiratory Function in Cervical Spinal Cord Injury: A Case Report
Authors: Run Peng, Huiming Gong, Xiaoxin Wang, Liang Zhang, Zitong Liu, Yongqi Xie, Shuang Guo, Xinqi Cao, Xinyue Pang, Mingliang Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In the treatment of respiratory impairment because of cervical spinal cord injury, bilateral implantation of a diaphragmatic pacemaker is considered to be the most effective way to enable patients to achieve independent breathing. However, in patients who can only use unilateral diaphragmatic pacemakers, especially adolescent children, the use of unilateral diaphragmatic pacemakers may have an effect on the productive development of such patients, which can lead to thoracic deformities and a decrease in the efficiency of pacing. This study presents a case of a pediatric patient with high cervical spinal cord injury who underwent unilateral implanted diaphragm pacing (IDP) combined with bilateral percutaneous intercostal nerve stimulation. The results demonstrated that intercostal nerve stimulation led to an increase in tidal volume. After 14 days of treatment and follow-up, a further increase in tidal volume was observed. These findings suggest that percutaneous intercostal nerve stimulation may serve as a viable adjunctive therapy for respiratory rehabilitation in patients with cervical spinal cord injury.

Normal respiration involves the participation of the diaphragm and the inspiratory intercostal muscles. The diaphragm is the predominant inspiratory muscle and is innervated by the phrenic nerve (C3-C5).1 The inspiratory intercostal muscles are innervated by the intercostal nerves (T1-T12) and drive thoracic expansion.2 Effective breathing is achieved through the coordinated action of both diaphragmatic and thoracic breathing. Cervical spinal cord injury (CSCI) leads to the flaccid paralysis of most respiratory muscles below the level of injury.3 In patients with high-level spinal cord injuries above C3, complete paralysis of the diaphragm and most of the respiratory muscles results in the patient’s inability to breathe on his own and his dependence on a ventilator. The implanted diaphragm pacer (IDP) becomes the sole option for restoring diaphragmatic inspiratory function to help wean patients off respiratory support.4 However, thoracic rigidity and paralysis and atrophy of the inspiratory intercostal muscles in patients with SCI significantly reduce thoracic compliance and limit thoracic expansion during diaphragmatic contraction, thereby impairing the mechanical efficiency of the diaphragm and overall respiratory function.

Although stimulation techniques such as spinal cord stimulation, trans-spinal magnetic stimulation, intercostal nerve electrical stimulation, and abdominal muscle stimulation have been reported to enhance the recovery of respiratory function after CSCI in both animal and human studies, there are limited clinical observations on the combined use of intercostal and phrenic nerve stimulation to enhance respiratory function in patients with CSCI.5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 This study aimed to preliminarily investigate the effects of percutaneous synchronized electrical stimulation combined with diaphragmatic pacemaker activation of the inspiratory intercostal muscles on respiratory function in patients with high levels of CSCI.

Case report

A 23-year-old man presented to the hospital with dyspnea for over 3 months, with intermittent ventilator use. At the age of 12, he was diagnosed with acute myelitis eight years prior and classified as AIS B at the C2 level according to the American Spinal Injury Association’s Impairment Scale.12 Subsequently, he underwent tracheostomy and required mechanical ventilation, becoming fully ventilator-dependent for 2 years. At age 14, because of respiratory insufficiency, he received a right implanted diaphragm pacemaker (IDP) procedure. After discharge, he relied on the IDP for full-time respiratory support over the following 5 years, while consistently performing home-based rehabilitation exercises for 1-2 hours daily.

Over the past year, he has reported a subjective decline in the efficacy of diaphragmatic pacing in maintaining adequate respiration, accompanied by intermittent episodes of chest tightness. A thoracic deformity was noted, characterized by a flat chest and depression of intercostal spaces, particularly visible in the right fifth to sixth intercostal spaces. No limb deformities were observed. The limb muscle tone was rated at grade 2-3 on the Modified Ashworth Scale, with flexor muscles predominating. The key muscle strength in his limbs was assessed at grade 0, whereas the sternocleidomastoid and trapezius muscle strength were graded at levels 3-4. The sensory function had largely returned to baseline. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee (approval no. 2013LL-003), and written informed consent was obtained from the patient and his guardians.

Methods

Pulmonary function was evaluated using a portable spirometer (Model X1) and its matching mouthpiece (Model X-YZ-01), both manufactured by Xicer (Xiamen) Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the measurements. In a tracheostomized patient, a nose clip was applied, and the patient was instructed to keep his mouth closed during tidal volume measurement. The flow sensor mouthpiece was securely attached to the tracheostomy site, forming a tight seal, which was verified by obtaining consistent and adequate tidal volume waveforms. Both pulmonary function and arterial blood gas analysis were assessed once at admission and again after two weeks of training.

A wireless surface electromyography (sEMG) telemetry system (YW-Wireless, Beijing Changfeng Technology Co., Ltd.) was used to record sEMG activity from various respiratory muscles, including the sternocleidomastoid, trapezius, and third external intercostal (T3 EIC), sixth external intercostal (T6 EIC), as well as the diaphragm, rectus abdominis, and external oblique.13 Electrode placements are detailed in figure 1. The electromyographic responses were amplified (gain × 3000), band-pass filtered between 50 Hz and 750 Hz, and digitized at a sampling rate of 2 kHz using an analog-to-digital converter (YW-Wireless, Beijing Changfeng Technology Co., Ltd.). Intercostal nerve electrical stimulation

An extracorporeal diaphragm pacemaker (EDP-D2, Zhongke Teco) was adapted for intercostal nerve stimulation (fig 2). Positive and negative electrodes (spaced 20 mm apart) were positioned at the intersections of the sixth and eighth intercostal nerves along the midaxillary line, with a grounding reference electrode placed at the xiphoid process. The intercostal nerve electrical stimulation frequency was set at 20 Hz (consistent with the IDP). A low-frequency square-wave pulsed current was applied with a pulse width of 0.3 ms and a pulse train duration of 1 s, using a stimulation intensity of 45 mA per channel. The stimulation protocol consisted of 30-minute daily sessions over 2 weeks.Fig 2The X-ray image shows the correct placement of electrodes.Fig 2

Results

All methods were well tolerated, with no adverse effects reported. The patient’s heart rate ranged from 89 to 96 beats per minute, and arterial blood gas analysis showed SaO₂ levels between 92% and 99%. The sEMG recording of the diaphragm revealed a rhythmic “barrel” waveform. At the same time, the rectus abdominis and external oblique muscles exhibited minimal to no activity (fig 3A). Results indicated that intercostal nerve stimulation at the same frequency as IDP improved intercostal muscle recruitment. However, as indicated by the sEMG data, the contraction of the two muscle groups began to exhibit a gradual loss of synchronization over time (fig 3B). Moreover, on day 14, we also recorded sEMG signals from the respiratory muscles under different intercostal nerve stimulation frequencies (8, 10, 12, and 20 Hz). As shown in figure 3B, coordinated contraction between thoracic and abdominal muscles could not be stably achieved under combined stimulation. The patient could perceive diaphragm activation, and when experiencing feelings of oxygen deprivation, he could voluntarily contract the sternocleidomastoid and trapezius muscles in synchrony with diaphragm activation, thus enhancing his inspiratory volume. When the patient perceives difficulty in breathing, he spontaneously adopts a deep breathing pattern that involves the residual function of the sternocleidomastoid and trapezius muscles to actively assist inspiration. Figure 4A illustrates the changes in tidal volume under diaphragmatic stimulation alone and combined stimulation. Initially, diaphragmatic pacing generated a tidal volume ranging from 124 to 138 mL, which subsequently increased to 161 mL. Compared to diaphragmatic pacing alone, the combined stimulation resulted in a gradual increase in tidal volume, reaching 241 mL, demonstrating a significant difference in tidal volume (VT) (fig 4B).

Discussion

Spinal cord stimulation at the T2 vertebral level can enhance maximal inspiratory volume and activate the paracostal, lateral, and medial intercostal muscles in the upper and midrib cage.14, 15, 16 However, it remains inadequate for prolonged spontaneous ventilation in CSCI patients. Our observations suggest that surface electrode stimulation of the intercostal muscles at T1-T3 levels had minimal effect on inspiratory function, potentially because of limited muscle contraction in this region. In contrast, nerve stimulation at T9-T12 hindered the patient’s inspiratory efforts because it simultaneously activated the abdominal muscles. Consequently, we opted to stimulate the dorsal intercostal nerves at T6-T8, theorizing that contraction of the corresponding intercostal muscles would facilitate chest expansion.17, 18, 19 Additionally, these nerves are located near the diaphragmatic ring and align with the diaphragm’s intercostal nerve distribution.20,21

The increased tidal volume resulting from intercostal nerve stimulation may not be solely because of the contraction of intercostal muscles, but rather due to improved support surrounding the diaphragmatic ring, which may help prevent diaphragmatic collapse.22 However, the stimulation range of intercostal nerves is limited, which restricts the extent of muscle activation and underscores the constraints of current techniques. Additionally, unilateral diaphragm pacing and the patient’s secondary thoracic deformity may further complicate measurement accuracy and functional assessment. This study has several limitations, including stimulation of only 2-3 pairs out of the 12 intercostal nerves, its nature as a case report, and a short observation period. To address these shortcomings, future research should focus on larger-scale trials, as well as exploring closed-loop control systems and personalized stimulation strategies to enhance clinical applicability.

Study limitations

The case study research design puts an inherent limit on the generalizability of findings. Case reports are unable to explore in depth the physiological mechanisms behind the combined stimulation of the intercostal nerves and diaphragm pacemakers. It remains unclear whether the effect is simply a mechanical additive effect or if there is a neural-level synergistic interaction. In the future, it is necessary to conduct more studies to further determine the optimal parameters and physiological mechanisms, to validate the effectiveness of this combined stimulation model, and ensure its applicability to a broader population.

Conclusions

We presented the case of a patient whose pulmonary function from acute neurologic impairment was adversely affected by CSCI. The percutaneous intercostal nerve electrical stimulation resulted in increased tidal volume, which allowed the patient to alleviate the hypoxic condition. The proposed method of percutaneous intercostal nerve combined with diaphragmatic pacemaker stimulation may be considered for the rehabilitation of respiratory dysfunction in patients with CSCI.
