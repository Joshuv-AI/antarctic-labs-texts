Therapeutic vulnerabilities and pan-cancer landscape of BRAF class III mutations in epithelial solid tumors
Authors: Eylül Özgü, Benjamin G Kaplan, Smruthy Sivakumar, Ethan S Sokol, Esranur Aydın, Ünal Metin Tokat, Ashkan Adibi, Ebru Gül Karakoç, Jiancheng Hu, Razelle Kurzrock, Mutlu Demiray
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKinase-impaired class III BRAF mutations have recently received attention as a possible prognostic factor and therapeutic target. Class III BRAF variants differ from class I and class II mutations in terms of mechanism of pathway activation and therapeutic vulnerabilities. Genomic landscape analyses of tumors in large real-world cohorts represent a great opportunity to further characterize tumor-related molecular events and treatment vulnerabilities, however, such data is not yet available for tumors with BRAF class III mutations.MethodsWe investigated the pan-cancer genomic landscape of BRAF class III mutations in 376,302 patients. Patients had comprehensive genomic profiling either by FoundationOne® or FoundationOne®CDx from formalin-fixed, paraffin embedded tissue biopsies. 2 patient cases that harbored BRAF class III mutations who demonstrated dramatic response to anti-EGFR treatment were presented.ResultsBRAF class III mutations are likely to co-occur with RAF1, NRAS and HRAS alterations, while concomitant KRAS alterations were rare. Moreover, we found that alterations that predict resistance to anti-EGFR agents were significantly less common in tumors harboring BRAF class III mutations, which is of great importance as anti-EGFR therapies are a potential targeted treatment option in these tumors.DiscussionOur findings suggest a heterogenous interplay of oncogenic alterations in BRAF class III mutated tumors and have important implications for the molecular mechanisms of carcinogenesis while revealing potential therapeutic vulnerabilities.Highlights Tumors harboring BRAF class III (BRAF vIII) mutations comprise a novel subset with distinct genomic heterogeneity.BRAF vIII mutations may sensitize tumors to anti-EGFR treatments.BRAF vIII alterations show significantly less co-occurrence with alterations that predict resistance to anti-EGFR agents.Rare tumors with limited therapy options should be screened for BRAF vIII mutations as they may benefit from anti-EGFR agents.

Introduction

BRAF is one the most frequently mutated genes in cancer [1]. Most BRAF mutations occur at the hotspot V600 codon, which leads to activation of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway [2]. There is a great effort investigating the potential of targeted treatments in patients harboring BRAF mutations. BRAF codon V600 mutations have been thoroughly studied and randomized clinical trials have shown more than 50% efficacy of BRAF, MEK and the combination of BRAF and MEK inhibitors in melanoma [3–5] as well as in other tumors [6, 7] while the addition of anti-EGFR agent Cetuximab to BRAF and MEK inhibitors provided substantial clinical benefit in BRAF V600E mutant colorectal cancer [8].

BRAF mutations other than those that occur in V600 codon have been studied to a much lesser extent in the context of targeted treatment and clinical decision making. Wan et al. showed that BRAF mutations outside of codon V600 can also be oncogenic drivers [9] and distinct mechanisms for oncogenicity were shown in pre-clinical studies which necessitate different treatment approaches [10, 11]. More recently, differences in dimerization requirement and RAS dependency were identified for different BRAF mutations that result in impairment of kinase activity [12–14].

The overall differences in distinct BRAF mutations resulted in classification of BRAF mutations into three major subtypes: class I, class II and class III [15]. Wild-type BRAF depends on RAS activity and requires dimerization for signal transduction. Class I BRAF mutants are comprised of codon V600 mutations, are RAS independent and signal as lax dimers [16, 17]. Class II BRAF mutations occur outside of the V600 codon, have increased kinase activity and are constitutively active, independent from RAS signaling. Class II BRAF mutants require strict dimerization for signaling, which sets them apart from V600 mutants [14]. RAS independency results in resistance to monotherapy with anti-EGFR in tumors harboring class I and class II BRAF mutations [10]. In contrast to Class I and Class II BRAF mutations, Class III BRAF mutations result in the expression of BRAF proteins that are either kinase dead or have impaired kinase activity. Class III mutant BRAF has higher affinity for RAS, and forms dimers with CRAF. CRAF signaling is increased in these mutants, and the activation depends on the upstream RAS activity [12, 14] (Supplemental Table 1). Unlike BRAF class III mutant tumors, class I and class II harboring tumors are sensitive to the inhibition of BRAF and MEK [18–20].

Class III BRAF mutations result in a weaker ERK activation compared to class I and class II mutations. Less pronounced ERK activation results in impairment of a fundamental negative feedback signaling in MAPK pathway. Insufficient negative feedback inhibition from ERK to receptor tyrosine kinases (such as EGFR) and to RAS leads to increased activity in MAPK signaling compared to wild-type BRAF, which can promote tumorigenesis. This mechanism suggests that kinase impaired or kinase dead BRAF mutations amplify the upstream signaling (Fig. 1b)[14]. Therefore, in class III BRAF mutations, it might be more efficacious to target upstream effector molecules compared to downstream elements due to the disrupted negative feedback signaling [14].

NTA motif of CRAF is not phosphorylated in the inactive state [22]. b Upon ligand binding to RTKs, RAS-GDP is exchanged for RAS-GTP, promoting recruitment of RAF proteins to plasma membrane. Dimerization of RAS-GTP with BRAF induce conformational changes that activate BRAF. Class III mutant BRAF has little to no kinase activity however it retains the ability to dimerize and transactivate CRAF. Transactivation of CRAF by BRAF results in activation of MEK1/2, which phosphorylates ERK1/2. While BRAF class III mutants have low to no kinase activity, they are capable of transactivation of CRAF through heterodimerization [12, 14]. RAF proteins require phosphorylation of their N-terminal acidic (NTA) motif to transactivate other RAF molecules [21]. In normal cells, the BRAF NTA motif is constitutively phosphorylated (referred as activator kinase), while the NTA motif of CRAF is phosphorylated only after its transactivated by other RAF protomers (receiver kinase) (Fig. 1a). Class III BRAF mutants therefore dimerize and transactivate CRAF, which in turn can further promote activation of available RAF proteins, as well as activating MEK1/2 (Fig. 1b) [22].

To date, investigation of response to anti-EGFR agents in patients harboring class III BRAF mutations have been limited by sample size and the lack of profiling data to identify these tumors. In a clinical study, patients with colorectal cancer whose tumors had BRAF class III alterations benefited from the use of anti-EGFR agents, while patients with class II BRAF mutations showed no improvement [23]. However, a subset of patients with BRAF class III mutations do not respond to anti-EGFR treatments, possibly due to the tumor heterogeneity as well as the presence of other genomic alterations that may promote resistance to anti-EGFR agents [24]. In terms of prognosis, several studies have demonstrated that patients harboring BRAF class III mutations have a better prognosis than those with class I or class II mutations. In an analysis of 9643 patients with metastatic CRC, excellent prognosis was observed in patients harboring BRAF alterations other than V600E [25]. BRAF mutations in the codons 594 and 596, which often results in impairment of kinase activity, have been shown to be a good prognostic factor in patients with melanoma [26]. On the other hand, in a pan-cancer study, patients with class II or III BRAF alterations had a worse prognosis compared to patients with Class I BRAF alterations (though the study did not separate class II versus III alterations) [27].

These findings suggest that different classes of BRAF mutations have distinct clinical implications and may require different clinical management. Investigating a total of 376,302 tumors, we found that 0.82% of specimens harbored a BRAF class III mutation. In general, BRAF class III mutations co-occurred infrequently with gene alterations that predict resistance to anti-EGFR therapies such as MET and ERBB2 amplifications across all tumor types. Finally, we present two patients with advanced colorectal cancer and cholangiocarcinoma who benefitted from cetuximab-based therapy. We leveraged a large genomic database from Foundation Medicine to perform a pan-cancer investigation of BRAF class III mutations, unraveling the genomic landscape and assessing the downstream therapeutic implications.

Methods

Comprehensive genomic profiling of cancers

This study examined 376,302 patients with solid tumors tested with comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) using FoundationOne® or FoundationOne®CDx from formalin-fixed, paraffin embedded (FFPE) tissue biopsies. All samples were assessed in Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA)-certified and College of American Pathologists (CAP)-accredited laboratories. Using a minimum of 50 ng of DNA, CGP was performed on hybridization-captured, adaptor ligation-based libraries run on Illumina HiSeq 4000 Systems to a median exon coverage depth of > 500X for select exons and introns of at least 324 cancer-related genes. With samples run on two different assays, the analysis was limited to the 296 genes commonly targeted in both assays. The detection of genomic alterations, including short variants (base substitutions, short insertions, and deletions), copy number alterations (amplifications and homozygous deletions), and select gene fusions or rearrangements was performed as described previously [28, 29]. Tumor mutational burden (TMB, mutations/Mb) was calculated based on 0.8–1.1 Mb of targeted coding regions [30]. Microsatellite instability was determined by analyzing intronic homopolymer repeat loci for length variability and compiled into an overall MSI score [31]. Patient age, sex, and site of biopsy were extracted from accompanying pathology reports or the submitted test requisition form. Approval for this study, including a waiver of informed consent and Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act waiver of authorization, was obtained from the Western Institutional Review Board (Protocol #20152817).

Cohort considerations and statistical analyses

The mutational spectrum of BRAF class III alterations and their co-alterations were examined in the pan-cancer cohort as well as for specific tumor types with at least 50 cases with a BRAF class III alteration. To have a balanced pan-cancer cohort for co-occurrence testing, tumor types were weighted according to the number of samples that contained a BRAF class III alteration. A 1:1 ratio of samples that were positive for a BRAF class III alteration and randomly selected samples without a BRAF class III alteration were used within each tumor type, as part of the pan-tumor cohort. Analyses of co-occurring and mutually exclusive gene alterations with BRAF class III alterations were performed using a Fisher’s exact test with false discovery rate (FDR)-based correction for multiple testing, using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. For the Fisher’s exact test, an odd’s ratio of less than one indicates mutual exclusivity while an odd’s ratio greater than one indicates co-occurrence. Statistical significance was set at an FDR-corrected p < = 0.05. Lower and upper 95% confidence intervals for prevalence of gene alterations and other biomarkers were calculated using a percent point function. All statistical analyses were performed in Python 3.7.6 using scipy packages. Visualizations were produced in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Cohort characteristics

A total of 376,302 cases in the pan-solid tumor cohort were analyzed for class III BRAF mutations; 0.82% (3,094) patients were found to harbor a class III BRAF mutation (Table 1, Fig. 2a). The ratio of male to female patients was 51:49, with a median age of 66 years in the pan-solid tumor cohort.

Prevalence of BRAF class III mutations and the codon-level breakdown across tumor types.

a Prevalence of class III BRAF mutations by cancer type. Lower and upper 95% confidence intervals for the prevalence, calculated using a percent point function, are also denoted. b Stacked bar plot showing the codon-level breakdown of BRAF class III mutations by cancer type.

We analyzed the prevalence of BRAF class III mutations and the breakdown by the altered codon in the pan-solid tumor cohort and 13 solid tumor types (Fig. 2). Additional analyses were performed in low-tumor mutational burden cohorts to account for the possible contribution of passenger mutations in the genomic landscape (supplementary figure S3). Highest prevalence of BRAF class III mutations was found in small intestine carcinoma (n = 2677) with 3.26% of patients harboring BRAF class III mutations. This was followed by melanoma (3%, n = 11,732), colorectal cancer (1.3%, n = 48,944) and non-small cell lung cancer (1.2%, n = 78,396). BRAF class III mutations were least frequent in endometrial cancer (0.4%, n = 12,287), pancreas cancer (0.3%, n = 25,055) and breast cancer (0.2%, n = 41,090).

In the analysis of altered codon frequency, we found that mutations in the codon 594 were the most frequent (0.38%, n = 299,591) (Fig. 2b). Codon 594 was the most frequently altered among BRAF class III altered tumors across multiple tumor types, except NSCLCs where mutations in codon 466 mutations were more common (0.43%, n = 78,396).

Genomic Landscape of tumors harboring BRAF class III mutations

To better understand the mutational spectrum of BRAF class III mutated tumors, the co-alteration landscape, and patterns of other biomarkers in patients harboring BRAF class III mutations were investigated across all solid tumors (Fig. 3). We have also investigated the co-alteration landscape in tumors with low TMB that harbor BRAF class III mutations in order to account for possible passenger mutation status (supplementary figure 3). We found that inactivating NF1 alterations were enriched in patients with BRAF class III mutations compared to samples that lacked BRAF class III mutations (Odds ratio, OR = 2.8, p < 10−5). Following NF1, co-occurring alterations in STK11 and KEAP1 were also found to be enriched in BRAF class III mutated tumors. Other RAS pathway alterations such as activating HRAS, NRAS and RAF1 were also enriched in the BRAF class III mutant cohort, while activating KRAS alterations were found to be mutually exclusive with BRAF class III mutations (Fig. 3). EGFR alterations as well as amplifications on 11q13 (CCND1, FGF3, FGF4, FGF19) were also found to be mutually exclusive with BRAF class III mutations. Alterations in various other driver genes such as ALK and ERBB2 were found to be mutually exclusive with BRAF class III mutations in the balanced cohort (Fig. 3).

Next, we performed detailed analysis of the co-alteration landscape to identify genes frequently co-occurring or mutually exclusive with BRAF class III mutations in cholangiocarcinoma, colorectal cancer, melanoma, and non-small cell lung cancer (Fig. 4a–d). In cholangiocarcinoma, ARID1A and SMAD4 alterations were significantly enriched, while FGFR2 alterations were mutually exclusive with BRAF class III mutations.

Genomic landscape of different tumor types with BRAF class III mutations.

In CRC, alterations in PTEN, CRAF and NRAS were enriched in patients with BRAF class III mutations whereas KRAS alterations were found to be mutually exclusive with BRAF class III mutations. In melanoma, NF1 alterations were significantly enriched in patients with BRAF class III mutations. In contrast to other solid tumor types, KRAS alterations were found to co-occur with BRAF class III mutations in the melanoma cohort. Other RAS pathway members such as HRAS alterations were also enriched in the melanoma cohort with BRAF class III mutations. In non-small cell lung cancer cohort, amongst others, STK11, KEAP1, NF1 and TERT alterations were enriched. In the small intestine cohort, we found that CRAF alterations were significantly co-occurred with BRAF class III mutations as well as SOX9, APC, TERT and MAP2K1 alterations, whereas KRAS alterations were found to be mutually exclusive with BRAF class III mutations in the small intestine cohort.

EGFR inhibitor (EGFRi) common resisters include EGFR T790M as well as amplifications (Amp) in EGFR, MET and ERBB2. In addition to these, the EGFRi possible resister group includes any pathogenic alterations in PIK3CA as well as other BRAF alterations (excluding the vIII alteration).

Additionally, we also investigated patterns of some pre-defined alterations that predict resistance to anti-EGFR agents (Table 2, Supplemental Fig. 1). BRAF class III mutations were generally found to be mutually exclusive of gene alterations that predict resistance to anti-EGFR therapies across all tumor types (Supplemental Fig. 1), with additional tumor-type specific patterns (Supplemental Fig. 2). In colorectal cancers, genomic alterations that predict resistance to anti-EGFR treatments were found to be mutually exclusive with BRAF class III mutations. In NSCLC, possible EGFR inhibitor resistors were found to be mutually exclusive with BRAF class III mutations, while the EGFR T790M mutation and amplifications of EGFR, MET, ERBB2 were not significantly associated with BRAF class III mutations.

Presentations

Case 1

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for treatment and publication of this article. The first patient is a 68-year-old female, who was diagnosed with non-metastatic transverse colon carcinoma in 2018 (Fig. 6a). The patient was operated on a pT4 N1 M0 adenocarcinoma, according to TNM staging system, 8th edition. She received adjuvant chemotherapy with XELOX (capecitabine plus oxaliplatin). After six courses of adjuvant chemotherapy, follow-up was initiated. During the first control, two new lung metastases were detected. One lesion was operated, and another was treated with stereotactic body radiation therapy. After local treatments, FOLFIRI+Cetuximab (fluorouracil, folinic acid, and irinotecan) chemotherapy was started as a metastatic first-line regimen. Due to the chemotherapy related adverse effects (diarrhea, fatigue, infection), patient refused further chemotherapy and was admitted to our clinic for a second opinion in 2019. We suggested comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP); previously, only KRAS and NRAS hot spot tests were performed in the primary tumor tissue.

CGP (FoundationOne®CDx) was performed on metastatic tumor tissue. The patient was microsatellite stable with TMB of 4 mut/Mb. The following mutations were detected in the metastatic tissue: BRAF D594G (Variant allele frequency [VAF]: 9.4%), TP53 splice site 994-40_994-2del39 (VAF: 6.3%), APC R876*, Q1328* (VAF: 10.5%, 5.0%), FBXW7 R689W (VAF: 8.4%) and CTNNB1 N426D (VAF: 2.1%, subclonal). After a virtual molecular tumor board discussion, the patient’s treatment was switched to cetuximab plus trametinib as the patient harbored a class III BRAF D594G mutation.

During follow-up, three-monthly imaging studies were performed, and a radiologic complete response was achieved six months later. A personalized circulating tumor (ct) DNA-based minimal residual disease assay (SignateraTM) was done after one year. Following six negative MRD results and about two years of radiologic complete response, the patient requested treatment-free follow-up. Currently, she does not receive any treatment per her request and is disease-free based on the regular PET scans and MRD analysis (Fig. 6b).

Case 2

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for treatment and publication of this article. Patient 2 is a 50-year-old female who was diagnosed with intrahepatic cholangiocellular carcinoma in August 2020 (Fig. 6c). Tumor was found to be inoperable based on radiological imaging. After biliary stent implementation, she received cisplatin plus gemcitabine. She progressed upon four cycles of the cisplatin gemcitabine treatment and XELOX (capecitabine+oxaliplatine) regimen was initiated. Progression was detected after 3 cycles. She was admitted to our precision cancer center in February 2021. CGP (FoundationOne®CDx) was performed on primary tumor site. Microsatellite stability and TMB could not be determined. The following genomic findings were detected: BRAF F595L (VAF 8.5%) and IDH2 R172W (VAF 8.3%). Due to a detected BRAF class III alteration, she received Cetuximab (500 mg/m2 biweekly) plus trametinib (1 mg/day). Trametinib was discontinued after 6 months due to adverse effects (asthenia, skin toxicities) and she continued to receive Cetuximab as a monotherapy. The first and second controls were done 3 and 6 months later, objective response was achieved. Patient´s performance status was improved (ECOG 1 to 0). Last control was performed in October 2022 and about 20 percent increase in lesion size was observed. Re-biopsy and CGP was suggested to her. In the second CGP, IDH2 R172W (VAF 7.5%) and TP53 splice site 994-1 G > A (VAF 1.4%) mutations were detected. The most important finding was the absence of BRAF class III alteration that was detected in the previous CGP analysis. She continues to receive Cetuximab and is being evaluated for local treatment options such as ablation, operation and stereotactic radiotherapy. Enasidenib is also considered to be combined with Cetuximab.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the genomic landscape of patients diagnosed with cancer harboring BRAF class III mutations. Our findings suggest that BRAF class III mutations define a subset of patients that are sensitive to anti-EGFR agents, or inhibitors of upstream KRAS signaling. Strikingly, patients with rare tumors, such as small intestine carcinoma, may benefit from such treatments, which emphasizes the potential value for comprehensive genomic profiling in the clinic.

We found that KRAS alterations are mutually exclusive with class III BRAF mutations in almost every tumor type, apart from melanoma. However, other RAS members such as NRAS and HRAS were found to be activated in a large set of tumors that harbor class III BRAF mutations. As expected, NF1 alterations were significantly enriched in melanoma. Due to the low basal receptor tyrosine kinase activity in melanoma, RAS activation through genomic alterations is consistent with the previous studies that showed BRAF class III mutations alone do not induce carcinogenesis in melanoma [12]. Notably, high median TMB was detected in melanoma samples harboring BRAF class III mutations (Fig. 5a). The association between BRAF class III mutations and TMB requires further investigations, as high TMB predicts response to immunotherapy in melanoma [32].

In tumors with high intrinsic receptor tyrosine kinase activity, class III BRAF mutations induce tumorigenesis by disruption of ERK mediated negative feedback signaling. Furthermore, class III BRAF mutants show increased affinity for activated RAS, which is associated with elevated heterodimer formation between mutant BRAF and wild-type CRAF. These tumors depend on the activity of the mutant BRAF-CRAF heterodimers [14]. In accordance with this mechanism, we observed that oncogenic CRAF alterations co-occurred with class III BRAF mutations in colorectal and non-small cell lung cancer patients. Activating mutations in CRAF may result in decreased response to anti-EGFR agents while sensitizing tumors to MEK inhibitors [33]. Several studies showed that the most common CRAF mutation, S257X, is sensitive to MEK inhibition [34] which suggests that patients harboring both BRAF class III and CRAF mutations may benefit from combination of anti-EGFR agents with MEK inhibitors. Vertical dual inhibition in RAS pathway was shown to be beneficial in the case of KRAS G12C mutant colorectal cancer, where combination of Panitumumab with Sotorasib and Cetuximab with Adagrasib or Divarasib have shown high disease control rates [35–38]. This scenario further emphasizes the importance of comprehensive genomic profiling, in which markers for resistance to a targeted therapy can also be detected and facilitate strategies towards combinatorial therapies.

We found that alterations indicative of resistance to anti-EGFR treatments were significantly less common in tumors with class III BRAF mutations. This may explain the good prognosis observed in these patients treated with anti-EGFR agents. It should be noted that our study does not stratify patients based on their previous treatment. Patients often acquire novel mutations that result in resistance to the previous treatment. In the context of EGFR targeted therapies, point mutations in EGFR such as T790M are frequently found in patients previously treated with anti-EGFR agents which results in resistance to multiple anti-EGFR targeted therapies [39–41] while being sensitive to Osimertinib [42]. Therefore, treatment history as well as surveillance for possible acquired mutations are critical when designing targeted treatments.

Interestingly, approximately 3.3% of small intestine carcinoma samples harbored a BRAF class III mutation. Small intestine carcinoma is quite rare and fatal; standard testing for BRAF class III mutations may increase the chance to treat these subgroups of patients with anti-EGFR agents. However, it should be noted that MAP2K1, which encodes for MEK1 protein, is often found to be mutated in small intestine carcinoma. It was shown that multiple MAP2K1 mutations interfere and prevent the binding of various MEK inhibitors to MEK1, resulting in drug resistance [43, 44].

In cholangiocarcinoma, FGFR2 alterations were found to be mutually exclusive with class III BRAF mutations. Targeted treatments in cholangiocarcinoma are mostly limited to patients harboring FGFR2 alterations and IDH1-2 mutations [45–47]. Our results suggest that cholangiocarcinoma patients who have negative IHC results for FGFR2 and who do not harbor FGFR2 or IDH alterations based on targeted sequencing panels may be eligible for other targeted therapy options.

Additionally, the choice of anti-EGFR agent is pivotal; while monoclonal antibodies against EGFR blocks the wild-type EGFR, EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as Osimertinib has minimal inhibitory activity for wild-type EGFR [48]. Therefore, as most of the BRAF class III mutant patients are EGFR wild-type, monoclonal anti-EGFR antibodies may show superior activity compared to EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors. The use of EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors instead of anti-EGFR monoclonal antibodies may explain the lack of response in some patients with BRAF class III mutations [18]. As increased EGFR activity is a crucial event in the BRAF class III driven tumorigenesis, EGFR targeting treatments have shown better response rates compared to MEK inhibitors [18, 19]. 2 cases presented in this paper (Fig. 6) demonstrate clinical responses to anti-EGFR containing regimens in two different tumor types harboring class III BRAF mutations. One of the patients received Cetuximab monotherapy with durable response for 15 months. Both patients had impressive response to molecularly guided targeted treatment even after the failure of multiple lines of systemic therapy. These cases emphasize the value of BRAF class III alterations as a potential therapeutic target. It should be noted that, in an analysis of 2,084 metastatic CRC patients, those who harbored class III BRAF mutations had stable disease as best response. However, this study was limited with lack of treatment history and a small cohort of patients harboring a class III BRAF mutation [49]. Concomitant alterations with BRAF class III mutations such as NF1 and CRAF mutations may require combinatorial approaches, and these patients may benefit from use of anti-EGFR monoclonal antibodies in combination with MEK inhibitors.

To date, multiple clinical studies have shown benefit from anti-EGFR treatment in patients diagnosed with cancer and exhibiting class III BRAF mutations [14, 18, 19, 23, 25, 50, 51]. However, these studies have generally comprised smaller cohorts and lacked real-world genomic profiling data. Our study demonstrates genomic heterogeneity in patients harboring BRAF class III mutations, underscoring the utility of genomic profiling in these patients to assess the potential full complement of treatment options [52, 53]. While these patients benefit from anti-EGFR agents, it is crucial to determine the other alterations that may result in non-responsiveness. Further clinical trials are required to evaluate the therapeutic value of BRAF class III alterations as well as its prognostic role in cancer. Our study is limited by lack of large cohort demonstration of response to targeted treatment in this subset of patients. We suggest that classifying BRAF alterations as BRAF V600 versus non-V600 mutations may not be as precise as identifying which of the three subclasses are present, since BRAF class III mutations may distribute and behave differently that other BRAF mutations.
