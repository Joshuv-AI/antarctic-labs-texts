Clinical and demographical aspects of a new neurology outpatient clinic in a developing country: a descriptive retrospective study
Authors: Matheus Ballestero, Beatriz Gabrielle Ishikawa Ducci, Daniel Souza da Silva, Milena Carvalho Libardi, Judith Thomas Tayra, Ricardo Santos de Oliveira
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeurological disorders are among the leading causes of disability-adjusted life years and the second leading cause of death globally, posing a major public health challenge—especially in low- and middle-income countries. This study aimed to identify the clinical and epidemiological profiles of patients seen at a newly established neurology outpatient clinic in Brazil, assess the appropriateness of referrals and describe follow-up times and diagnostic patterns.MethodsThis retrospective study reviewed medical records from 1635 patients treated between May 2017 and June 2022. Data collection involved two phases: first, an electronic system extraction and second, a detailed manual review of each record to ensure accuracy. Diagnoses were coded using the ICD 10 (International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems), and presenting complaints were categorised into 14 clinical groups.ResultsAmong the 1635 patients reviewed, 80% received a neurological diagnosis. The two most frequent conditions were neurovascular diseases (23.4%) and migraine (21.4%). A substantial proportion of patients, 69.5%, presented with at least one comorbidity. Hypertension (40.5%) and diabetes mellitus (18.5%) were the most frequent conditions. Compared with patients with cerebrovascular disorders, those diagnosed with migraine or other headache syndromes had significantly lower odds of having hypertension (OR 0.26; 95% CI 0.14 to 0.48) and diabetes (OR 0.26; 95% CI 0.14 to 0.48). Other diagnostic categories also followed this trend, with consistently lower odds of comorbidities in comparison to cerebrovascular disorders. Additionally, 4.2% of referrals were deemed inappropriate, and of these, 71% were directed to general neurology rather than specialised services.ConclusionThis study reveals a distinct clinical profile of neurological patients that diverges from national and international benchmarks, highlighting the unique demographic and health characteristics of the population served. These results deepen our understanding of both clinical and social needs in neurological care and offer valuable benchmarks for comparable services worldwide. By mapping diagnostic patterns, comorbidity burdens and referral practices, the study aligns with several United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDG). These findings can inform public health strategies, particularly in resource-limited settings, to implement more equitable and efficient neurological care, promote targeted prevention and management of chronic comorbidities, and optimise referral systems. Such action would contribute meaningfully to SDG targets, fostering health equity and advancing sustainable neurological services.Keywords: Epidemiology, Public Health, Epidemiologic Measurements

Introduction

Neurological disorders account for a substantial portion of the global disease burden, causing 11.1 million deaths and ranking as the leading contributor to disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) and years of life lost worldwide in 2021.1

These figures, which have increased over the past three decades, are associated with population growth, population ageing and increased exposure to environmental, metabolic and behavioural risk factors, within the context of a global epidemiological transition from communicable to non-communicable diseases.1 2

Neurological disorders, particularly epilepsy and migraine, constitute a significant share of the disability-adjusted life years lost globally.3 While dementia is a major concern in some regions, neurological disorders collectively represent a significant public health challenge worldwide.3 In low- and middle-income countries, including Brazil, the impact of these diseases is especially pronounced, particularly among the adult population.4

The burden of neurological disorders and stroke-related mortality, along with the associated disabilities, tends to be more pronounced among younger populations in developing regions compared with those in developed countries.1 5 6 While stroke accounts for 40–60% of neurological deaths in high-income nations, this proportion can surpass 70% in lower-income areas.7 The rising burden of neurological disorders in BRICS+ (an expanded group of nations originally founded by Brazil, Russia, India, and China, now including South Africa, Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, the United Arab Emirates, and Indonesia), underscores the need for comprehensive healthcare strategies that take into account regional disparities, as well as social and cultural contexts.8

According to studies conducted in other developing nations, the prevalence of neurological disorders is substantial.8,10 For instance, a report from India identified a rate of 3355 cases per 100 000 population, with the most common conditions being headaches, febrile convulsions, epilepsy, stroke and intellectual disabilities.9 Similarly, research in Saudi Arabia reported a prevalence of 131 neurological disorders per 1000 individuals, where headaches, epilepsy and febrile convulsions were more frequently observed than stroke, dementia and Parkinson’s disease.10

In Brazil, most available data on neurological disorders originate from hospital-based studies, which may not accurately represent the broader population. There is a lack of comprehensive epidemiological research capable of elucidating the true incidence and prevalence of these conditions across different regions of the country.11,14

This study aims to identify the clinical and epidemiological profile of patients treated at a newly established neurology outpatient clinic, assess the appropriateness of referrals and describe patient follow-up times and diagnoses patterns. These data are essential for guiding future research and informing the development of community-based studies, public health strategies and clinical trials aimed at improving neurological care in Brazil and other low- and middle-income countries. By generating context-specific evidence, this study directly contributes to the advancement of Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3 (Good Health and Well-Being), by promoting accessible and quality neurological services; SDG 10 (Reduced Inequalities), by addressing disparities in healthcare access; and SDG 9 (Industry, Innovation and Infrastructure), by supporting the development of innovative and efficient healthcare delivery models.

Methods

All stages of the study—including design, data collection, analysis, interpretation and presentation—were conducted following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist of 22 essential items, covering title and abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion and disclosure of funding.15

Study design and setting

This retrospective study was conducted at the neurology outpatient clinics of the Hospital Professor Dr Horácio Carlos Panepucci, affiliated with the Federal University of São Carlos (UFSCar), Brazil. The hospital functions as a regional referral centre serving a population of approximately 500 000 residents in the São Carlos area.

Participants

This retrospective study included all patients aged ≥18 years who attended the neurology outpatient clinic, between 3 May 2017 and 23 June 2022. Records with missing essential data or duplicates within the electronic medical records system were excluded from the analysis.

The data collection followed a two-stage process. Initially, data were extracted for all medical records (n=1635) from the system, including the number of appointments, number of missed appointments, date of birth, age at the first day of data collection, gender, self-declared race, birthplace, marital status and education level. Additional data were subsequently collected by reviewing medical records. A total of 737 records were selected via simple random sampling. Six incomplete records were discarded, resulting in 731 records for the second part of the analysis (figure 1).

Study flowchart following the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines.

This sampling was conducted both to ensure the representativeness of the patient population and to make the study feasible within the available timeframe, considering the time, resources and complexity of medical record review. Therefore, the largest possible number of records was reviewed under these constraints to maximise the sample size. This simple random sampling method grants each patient an equal probability of being included, thereby ensuring the statistical representativeness of the population.

Variables

Neurological diseases were classified according to the 10th revision of the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (ICD-10) and the ‘presenting complaints’ were grouped as follows: (1) follow-up of previous diagnosis; (2) headache; (3) cognitive impairment; (4) motor abnormalities; (5) impaired consciousness; (6) pain; (7) diseases of other systems and apparatus; (8) dizziness, vertigo and balance disorders; (9) hallucinations; (10) changes in sensitivity; (11) dysphoric mood and behavioural symptoms; (12) taste and smell dysfunctions and other sensory changes; (13) sleep disorders; (14) others.

All diagnoses were established by certified, properly registered with their respective Brazilian Regional Councils of Medicine (CRMs) and holding the official specialist qualification (Registro de Qualificação de Especialista – RQE) in Neurology, following ICD 10 (the International Classification of Diseases) criteria for neurological diseases—based on clinical presentation, neuroimaging findings and complementary laboratory tests when indicated.

The appropriateness of referrals was defined by the absence of clinical or age-related criteria warranting neurology outpatient care, according to the hospital’s Neurology Outpatient Care Protocol and the national Manual of Care Routines for Stroke.16

Data sources

All data were obtained from the institution’s electronic medical records.

Study size

Epidemiological data from 1635 patients were analysed, and the complete medical records of 737 patients were reviewed.

Statistical methods

All data were organised and tabulated using Microsoft Office Excel, a data spreadsheet software. Statistical analysis was then performed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.27 for Windows, a widely used software suite for data management and advanced analytics.

To ensure the validity of formal hypothesis testing, χ2 tests were applied exclusively to the five most frequent diagnostic hypotheses. The remaining diagnostic categories were grouped under a single category (‘other’) to mitigate the issue of sparse data. This approach was adopted to comply with the assumptions of the χ2 test, particularly the requirement that expected cell counts be sufficiently large.

Ethical consideration

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Federal University of São Carlos (CAAE: 5.456.901), which operates under Brazil’s National Committee for Ethics in Research. The research adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, as endorsed by the World Medical Association.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research. This is a retrospective study based solely on the analysis of anonymised medical record data.

Regarding the current status of the investigated patients, the collected data encompassed their disposition: outpatient discharge, continuation of follow-up, death and loss to follow-up. Patients referred to the Neurology Department of HU-UFSC attended an average of 2.25 consultations in the department (SD=2.14). Additionally, they missed an average of 0.3 appointments (SD=0.62). For patients who had at least two appointments in the neurology outpatient clinics, the median follow-up time was 238 days, representing the interval between their first and last appointments.

The majority of patients, 91.5%, attended the General Neurology outpatient clinic, while 8.5% were seen at the Stroke and Cerebrovascular Diseases outpatient clinic. An analysis of 731 medical records revealed that 4.2% of patients received inappropriate referrals. These included patients under 12 years of age, those with primary psychiatric or orthopaedic conditions, neurosurgical cases, individuals without neurological complaints, trauma-related conditions and cases with incomplete medical referral forms. Additionally, referrals to the Stroke and Cerebrovascular Diseases outpatient clinic were considered inappropriate when the patient presented with no clinical suspicion or history of cerebrovascular disease. Among all inappropriate referrals, 71.0% occurred in the General Neurology outpatient clinic, accounting for 3.3% of all appointments in that clinic. Furthermore, 14.5% of the referrals to the Stroke and Cerebrovascular Diseases outpatient clinic were deemed inappropriate. These findings suggest that the limited availability of general neurology care in the region may have contributed to the misguided referral of patients without cerebrovascular-related symptoms or conditions to the specialised Stroke and Cerebrovascular Diseases Service.

The group of patients who received inappropriate referrals (n=31) had a mean age of 42.55 years (SD=20.34), 48.4% were female (n=15) and 51.6% were male (n=16). Regarding race and ethnicity, 22 (71.0%) self-identified as white, 4 (12.9%) as brown (Pardo), 4 (12.9%) as black and 1 (3.2%) as ‘Other’. As for educational attainment, 4 patients had incomplete elementary school, 3 had completed elementary school, 6 had completed high school, 2 had incomplete higher education, 1 had no formal education, and in 15 cases, education level was not recorded.

There was strong evidence of an association between age group and diagnostic hypothesis (χ² test, p<0.001), leading to rejection of the null hypothesis. As shown in table 4, neurovascular diseases and Alzheimer’s disease increased progressively with age, peaking in the ≥60 age group, whereas migraine and epilepsy were more prevalent among younger patients (20 to 45 years). Similarly, a significant association was found between gender and diagnostic hypothesis (χ² test, p<0.001). Table 4 also shows that women had higher frequencies of migraine/headache (27.6% vs 12.9%) and Alzheimer’s disease (13.4% vs 8.5%), while men showed higher prevalence of neurovascular diseases (29.8% vs 18.7%) and Parkinson’s disease (8.5% vs 5.9%).

Discussion

This study pioneered the description of the epidemiology and current status of a newly established neurology outpatient clinic in Brazil, aiming to inform the efforts of other University Hospitals as they create and develop similar services.

In 2015, the United Nations General Assembly adopted the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development to guide public policies and inspire societal actors to promote sustainable development worldwide. The core of this programme is 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) with 169 specific targets, most of them to be achieved by 2030.17

According to the second edition of the WHO Atlas: Country Resources for Neurological Disorders, which maps the availability of neurological services in 122 countries classified by World Bank income level, significant disparities persist in the existence of dedicated neurological health policies, impacting the organisation, coverage and quality of neurological care.18

The findings of this study have important implications for public health, clinical practice and policy, particularly in the context of global health equity and sustainable development. By providing insights into the patterns of neurological diagnoses, comorbidities and referrals in a newly established clinic, this research contributes to SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-Being) by promoting improved healthcare access and outcomes for neurological patients. The study also highlights the need for context-specific approaches to neurological care, considering geographic, social and cultural factors that influence disease burden, which aligns with SDG 10 (Reduced Inequalities) by addressing disparities in healthcare delivery. Furthermore, by serving as a benchmark for other healthcare facilities and informing the development of more efficient referral systems, this study supports SDG 9 (Industry, Innovation and Infrastructure) by fostering improvements in healthcare infrastructure and service models. Strengthening community-based research and public health programmes based on these findings can contribute to the advancement of sustainable and equitable neurological care, particularly in low- and middle-income regions.19

Furthermore, the WHO Atlas underscores the importance of national legislation that protects the rights of individuals with neurological conditions and promotes access to adequate services, including legal safeguards, professional training and structured care pathways.18 It highlights that the absence of such frameworks increases the risk of stigma and socioeconomic exclusion, particularly for individuals with epilepsy and dementia.18 By reporting the frequency of these diagnoses in our population, our study offers an initial step towards recognising their public health significance and the need for rights-based and inclusive policies.

In terms of workforce development, the WHO also emphasises the essential role of neurologists in the training and supervision of nurses, allied health professionals and primary care providers.18 Within the setting of a university hospital, this underscores the strategic value of strengthening outpatient neurology services, not only to improve patient access and continuity of care, but also to enhance the education of future healthcare professionals. The integration of specialised services into teaching environments may thus contribute to reducing gaps in neurological care and building capacity in underserved regions.

Medication use and comorbidities

According to WHO, the global prevalence of hypertension in adults aged 30–79 years was approximately 33.1% in 2019.20 This prevalence was higher in low- and middle-income countries compared with high-income countries.21

In 2014, the National Survey on Access, Use and Promotion of Rational Use of Medicines (PNAUM), which analysed a representative sample of 41 433 Brazilians,22 the self-reported prevalence of arterial hypertension was 23.7%. Among those identified with hypertension, 94% were receiving medication therapy, accounting for 22.2% of the total sample.22 In contrast, the survey conducted at the Neurology Department of HU-UFSCar revealed a higher self-reported prevalence of systemic arterial hypertension at 39.4%, with 38.2% of patients using antihypertensive drugs.

When adjusted for age and sex (table 3), hypertension was significantly less frequent among patients diagnosed with migraine and other headache syndromes (OR 0.26, 95% CI 0.14 to 0.48), epilepsy, epileptic syndromes and status epilepticus (OR 0.20, 95% CI 0.10 to 0.38), Alzheimer’s disease and dementia (OR=0.25, 95% CI 0.13 to 0.48), Parkinson’s disorder, other extrapyramidal and movement disorders (OR 0.26, 95% CI 0.12 to 0.56), when compared with cerebrovascular diseases. Similarly, the GBD 2021 study identified high systolic blood pressure as the risk factor with the largest all-age population attributable fraction and emphasised the role of adequate pharmacological therapy in reducing DALYs associated with stroke in high-income countries.1 23 These findings reinforce the importance of prioritising appropriate hypertension management in our service, particularly given the presence of a specialised outpatient clinic for neurovascular diseases.

Global diabetes prevalence is a significant and growing concern, affecting over half a billion adults.24 The International Diabetes Federation estimated 451 million cases in 2017, projecting a rise to 693 million by 2045.25 While prevalence is higher in urban and high-income areas,26 it is rising fastest in middle-income countries.24 Alarmingly, nearly half of those with diabetes are undiagnosed,25 26 emphasising the urgent need for increased screening and early detection.

In line with the pattern observed for hypertension, the prevalence of diabetes mellitus in our sample (18.5%) was markedly higher than that reported in the general population (6.8% in PNAUM).22 The analysis showed that diabetes was significantly less prevalent across all other diagnostic categories when compared with cerebrovascular diseases (eg, OR 0.26, 95% CI 0.14 to 0.48 for migraine and other headache syndromes; OR 0.20, 95% CI 0.10 to 0.38 for the group of epilepsy; OR 0.25, 95% CI 0.13 to 0.48 for Alzheimer’s disease and dementia). These findings, in the Brazilian context where high fasting plasma glucose accounts for 4.89% (95% CI 0.33 to 12.31) of DALYs attributable to neurological disorders, reinforce the global discussion about the need for targeted screening and investment in preventive strategies aimed at modifiable risk factors such as diabetes.8

Pinpointing a precise global prevalence number for dyslipidaemia is challenging due to variations in diagnostic criteria and screening practices.27

Nevertheless, it is recognised as a widespread condition, especially among individuals with type 2 diabetes28 29 and other risk factors.30 Regional differences exist, as highlighted in studies from China and other parts of Asia.27 31 Given that dyslipidaemia is often asymptomatic, the lack of awareness, treatment and control remains a major concern.32 33 In our study, 7.9% of participants had dyslipidaemia. In contrast, the VIGITEL study reported that 22.6% of respondents self-reported a diagnosis of dyslipidaemia.12

These differences in the self-reported prevalence of arterial hypertension, diabetes and dyslipidaemia between the studies may be attributed to various factors, such as the demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants, as well as the different methodologies and criteria used. It’s important to note that these diseases are risk factors for the main diagnoses made in the neurology outpatient clinics of HU-UFSCar, especially cerebrovascular diseases and dementia syndromes. Therefore, the higher prevalence of these conditions in the current study may be influenced by the clinical profile of patients treated in this outpatient context.

Nevertheless, studies conducted in outpatient internal medicine and neurology clinics found prevalence rates of 13% to 14.6% for diabetes mellitus; 47% to 51.6% for hypertension; and 20.4% to 21% for dyslipidaemia.34 35

Consumption of psychoactive substances

National surveys indicate that the prevalence of adult smokers in Brazil ranges from 9.1% to 14.7%.36 Additionally, data from the NHS show that 26.6% of Brazilians are former smokers,37 compared with 22% in England.38 However, the current study found a higher prevalence of 24.2% among participants who reported current or past smoking.

Assessing the frequency of alcohol consumption in Brazil is complex. The National Survey of Health reported that 26.4% of individuals consume alcoholic beverages at least once a week.39 In contrast, the VIGITEL survey found a lower prevalence of 18.3% for abusive alcohol consumption in the last 30 days, with higher rates observed among men.40 Data from the Oswaldo Cruz Foundation further suggest that 30.1% of the population aged 12–65 years used alcoholic beverages in the 30 days prior to the survey, with binge-drinking estimated at 16.5%.41

The present study faced methodological challenges in defining the pattern of alcohol consumption. While all cases mentioning current or past alcoholism were included, occasional consumption was not considered. This limitation may have led to an underestimation of the alcohol consumption prevalence, which was 16.4%. Comparing alcohol consumption data is complex, as De Boni et al found that prevalences can vary up to 4.3 times when analysing the same data differently.42 Therefore, the prevalence obtained in the current research may underestimate the actual situation, despite including patients with a history of ceased alcoholism.

National data from the 3rd National Survey indicate that the prevalence of illicit drug use in the 12 months prior to the survey was 3.2% among Brazilians.39 In contrast, the current study found a prevalence of 2.6% among patients based on medical record documentation of current or past illicit substance use, although a specific timeframe was not defined.

Worldwide, the consumption of psychoactive substances is a significant public health issue. In 2017, an estimated 271 million people used drugs at least once.43 This figure encompasses both legal substances like alcohol and tobacco, as well as illicit drugs.

Lifestyle factors such as alcohol consumption and smoking did not show statistically significant associations with any specific diagnostic category in the multinomial regression model adjusted for age and sex. ORs for both alcoholism and smoking were close to 1 with wide CIs crossing the null, indicating no significant or consistent pattern. These findings suggest that while substance use remains a relevant public health concern, its distribution among neurological diagnostic groups may be more heterogeneous or underdetected in outpatient records, particularly given the limitations of retrospective data collection.

Main complaints and diagnostic hypotheses

Globally, researchers have investigated the health demand profiles of patients attending neurology clinics.1135 44,51 Regarding the presenting complaints, at the University Hospital of Tarragona in Spain,45 headache was the most frequently reported symptom, affecting 36.5% of patients, followed by focal symptoms and temporary loss of consciousness.

During the analysed period, a significant proportion of patients seen in the neurology department of HU-UFSCar sought the service to continue treatment for previously diagnosed neurological conditions from other outpatient clinic services (29.5%), particularly among those aged ≥60 years. There was a higher prevalence of headache complaints among younger adults (33.9% in the 20–45 age group), while cognitive impairment was more frequent among older adults (16.7%). As demonstrated in table 2, headache and follow-up for neurological conditions appear as the most common complaints, with distinct gender patterns: women presented more frequently with headache (24.2% vs 10.9%) and cognitive impairment (13.0% vs 7.5%), whereas men had a higher proportion of follow-up consultations (35.1% vs 24.4%).

Regarding the main diagnostic hypotheses, in the overall analysis, neurovascular diseases were the most prevalent (23.4%), predominantly affecting individuals aged ≥46 years. Headaches (21.4%) and epilepsy (16.2%) were more common among younger adults (20 to 45 years), while cognitive syndromes were concentrated among those aged ≥60 years (22.9%). Among women, there was a higher proportion of migraine (27.6%) and cognitive syndromes (13.4%), whereas men presented more frequently with neurovascular diseases (29.8%).

A study at a University Hospital in Kumasi, Ghana,44 indicated that strokes (57.1%), epilepsy (19.8%) and convulsive disorders (8.2%) were the most common primary neurological illnesses. The most common diagnoses in Tarragona were non-tumoral headaches (35.8%), anxiety and depression, neuropathies and epilepsy.45 Similarly, a study at a University Hospital in Pakistan46 found that headache disorders affected 18.6% of patients, with vascular diseases (17.4%), nerve and radicular injuries (14.1%), and epilepsy (12.5%) also being prevalent.

In Brazil, studies focusing on physiotherapy neurological appointments indicate a higher incidence of stroke cases.47 48 Among studies in medical clinics, research in the state of Paraná, Brazil,11 revealed that headaches accounted for 36.15% of the most frequent diagnoses, followed by epilepsy (22.24%) and ischaemic stroke (10.66%). In the neurology sector of a General Outpatient Clinic at São José do Rio Preto, Brazil,49 migraine was found in 21.81% of analysed medical records, with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (10.90%) and anxiety disorders (7.27%) also being common. At an outpatient clinic in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, the main neurological syndromes identified were headache (24.8%), dementia (15.9%), cerebrovascular disease (13.4%), movement disorders (12.1%) and radiculopathy (10.8%).35

Ferri-de-Barros50 highlights the difficulties in comparing neuroepidemiological studies due to variations in the inclusion of psychiatric disorders. Their research showed that when considering only primary neurological disorders, the main diagnostic hypotheses were headaches (33.48%), epilepsy (30%) and cerebrovascular disease (7.10%).50 However, when psychiatric disorders were included, the order shifted to headaches (28.54%), epilepsy (25.58%) and mental disorders (14.75%).50

In Burgos, Spain,51 the most common referral causes were headache (26%) and dementia (15%). The most frequent final diagnoses after neurological consultation in that region were also headache (27%) and ‘absence of neurological disease’ (19%).51

The findings regarding migraine and other headache syndromes in our outpatient clinic, particularly the higher prevalence among younger women, align with recent evidence from the international literature.52,56 Primary headache disorders, particularly migraine and tension-type headache, impose a significant burden at the individual level, including the functional and psychosocial impact.52 Headache disorders are reported to be two to three times more prevalent in women than in men.56 Studies have underscored that healthcare structures must be adaptable to local realities and must include multidimensional approaches that incorporate both clinical and social aspects to fully understand the extent of disability caused by headaches.52,54 Our results reinforce the urgent need to recognise the burden of migraine—a condition that has been neglected in health surveys in Brazil55—along with gender-related particularities, and to strengthen specialised neurological services, promote health professional education and implement early management protocols to reduce the clinical and social impact of these disorders in Brazil.

Limitations

The use of medical records as the data source may have introduced recording errors or inconsistencies, potentially affecting the integrity and accuracy of the results. While random and chronological sampling was employed, the sample may not fully represent the entire patient population of the clinic. Additionally, the study’s focus on a single neurology clinic at a specific Brazilian hospital could limit the generalisability of the findings to other populations or geographical contexts. Furthermore, the specific data collection period may not capture changes or trends over time, particularly in the post-COVID-19 pandemic context.

Conclusions

This study offers valuable insights into the epidemiology and health profiles of patients attending a newly established neurology outpatient clinic in Brazil. Our findings reveal significant differences in comorbidities, such as hypertension, diabetes and dyslipidaemia, when compared with national surveys. These disparities likely reflect the unique clinical and social context of this population. The patterns of substance use and neurological complaints observed also align with global trends, emphasising the relevance of these data for other low- and middle-income countries facing similar healthcare challenges.

By documenting these trends, the study contributes to Sustainable Development Goal 3 (Good Health and Well-being). It does this by promoting a better understanding of neurological health needs and supporting the development of more effective and inclusive care strategies. It also advances Goal 10 (Reduced Inequalities) by highlighting disparities in health profiles and access to specialised care, reinforcing the need for equitable health interventions. Moreover, as one of the first analyses from a newly implemented outpatient service, this work supports Goal 9 (Industry, Innovation and Infrastructure) by encouraging innovation in healthcare delivery systems and infrastructure development in underserved regions.

Together, these contributions provide a foundation for future research and policy-making. This aims to strengthen neurological care and reduce health disparities both in Brazil and in similar global contexts.
