The complete mitochondrial DNA sequence of the Pentalagus furnessi (Lagomorpha: Leporidae), an endemic species of Amami Ōshima, Japan
Authors: Miyu Tsubota, Himeyuri Sunahata, Asahi Itoi, Mariko Suzuki, Jun-ichi Takahashi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPentalagus furnessi, an endemic relict rabbit restricted to Amami Ōshima and Tokunoshima Islands, Japan, is threatened by invasive carnivores, habitat loss, and roadkill. We report the first complete mitochondrial genome of P. furnessi obtained by next-generation sequencing, providing baseline data for conservation genetics. The mitochondrial genome (approximately 16 kb) encodes 13 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 22 tRNAs, two rRNAs, and a control region, with an overall A+T content of 62.8%. Start codons ATG/ATT and stop codons TAA/TAG were identified. Phylogenetic analysis using 13 PCGs revealed a distinct lineage, consistent with its monotypic genus status and supporting conservation efforts.Keywords: Mitogenome, rabbit, conservation, rare species, near threatened

Introduction

The family Leporidae consists of 11 genera and approximately 60 species (Matt et al. 2004). Pentalagus furnessi Stone, 1900 (Figure 1), which is endemic to Amami Ōshima and Tokunoshima Islands, belongs to the order Lagomorpha, comprising two families: Ochotonidae (pikas) and Leporidae (rabbits and hares). It is believed that P. furnessi underwent early phylogenetic divergence, leaving it with no closely related species or genera, thus making it a monotypic genus (Yamada et al. 2002). P. furnessi is of considerable evolutionary significance, representing a genetically distinct lineage. The species has significant scientific value but faces severe threats, including the introduction of invasive predators, habitat loss due to deforestation, and road mortality. Reflecting both its ecological vulnerability and scientific importance, it has been designated as a Natural Monument, listed as endangered, and is protected under the Act on Conservation of Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora in Japan. Amami Ōshima Island, located at 28°N and 128°E, has an area of approximately 720 km2. The island features a marine subtropical climate, with an average annual temperature of around 20 °C and an annual precipitation of about 3,000 mm. Amami Ōshima is recognized for its high biodiversity and numerous endemic species, leading to its designation as a UNESCO World Natural Heritage Site (UNESCO 2021). The isolation between Tokunoshima and Amami Ōshima is due to sea-level rise after the last Ice Age, which formed a marine barrier between the two islands.

Effective conservation of endangered species requires understanding their population genetic structure and diversity (Ohnishi et al. 2017). Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) sequences provide essential genetic information for species identification and conservation. The complete mitochondrial genome sequence provides useful information for investigating phylogenetic relationships, assessing population genetic diversity, and understanding family structure, particularly when nuclear markers are unavailable or limited. However, as a single-locus marker with maternal inheritance and susceptibility to selective pressures, mitochondrial DNA does not always reflect the overall species-level relationships. Therefore, conclusions based on mitochondrial data should be interpreted with caution and ideally supported by nuclear genetic evidence or integrative approaches (Ballard and Whitlock 2004; Galtier et al. 2009). Until now, no complete mitochondrial genome data for P. furnessi have been available. Here, we present for the first time the complete mitochondrial genome of P. furnessi from Amami Ōshima Island, which provides a valuable resource for designing PCR primers needed for analyses of genetic diversity and genetic variation.

Materials and methods

The P. furnessi specimen used in this study was a female individual that died in a roadkill incident in Amami Ōshima, Japan on June 2, 2024 (128°91′E, 27°84′N). The carcasses were recovered by the Amami Wildlife Conservation Center of the Ministry of Environment. Cardiac muscle cells obtained through dissection were used for DNA extraction. Total DNA was immediately extracted using the DNA Blood and Tissue Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The specimens were preserved at the National Museum of Nature and Science in Japan (voucher number NSMT-M 86991, https://db.kahaku.go.jp/webmuseum/, togodb_info@kahaku.go.jp).

The entire DNA was sequenced using a DNBSEQ next-generation sequencer (MGI Tech), from which the complete mitochondrial genome was obtained. Quantification of the DNA was performed using Synergy LX (Agilent Technologies) and the QuantiFluor dsDNA System (Promega). Library preparation was performed using the MGIEasy FS DNA Library Prep Set (MGI Tech) with 50 ng of input DNA, 8 min of enzymatic fragmentation, and eight PCR cycles, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The library was quantified using a Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer with a dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), yielding 29.9 ng/µL in a total volume of 30 µL. Library quality assessment was carried out using a Fragment Analyzer and the dsDNA 915 Reagent Kit (Agilent Technologies) to evaluate the quality of the prepared library. Circularized DNA was prepared using the constructed library and the MGIEasy Circularization Kit (MGI Tech) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA nanoball (DNB) preparation was conducted using the DNB Rapid Make Reagent Kit (MGI Tech), following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNB was loaded onto the flow cell using the DNBSEQ-T7 DNB Rapid Load Reagent Kit (MGI Tech) and MGIDL-T7, following the manufacturer’s protocol. Sequencing analysis was performed DNBSEQ-T7 (MGI Tech) under 2 × 150 bp conditions to sequence the prepared DNBs.

Sequencing yielded 19,908,146 paired reads, totaling 5,972,443,800 bp. The quality score (≥Q30) was 88.8%, indicating that the data were obtained with high accuracy. The complete mitochondrial genome of Sylvilagus sp. (accession no. ON456163) was used as the reference sequence. Reads were assembled using the Geneious mapper in Geneious R9 (Biomatters Ltd.) with the sensitivity set to ‘High’; all other parameters were left at their default settings. Complete mitochondrial DNA sequences were annotated using MITOS 2 (Bernt et al. 2013) on the Galaxy web server. For phylogenetic analysis, the 13 mitochondrial protein-coding genes (PCGs) from each of the 14 leporid species were aligned separately for each gene using Genetyx 13 (GENETYX) and then concatenated into a single dataset for each species. The mitochondrial DNA of P. furnessi was successfully assembled with high coverage (Supplementary Figure S1). All sequences were retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database on 20 February 2025. Each of the 13 mitochondrial PCGs of P. furnessi was queried against the NCBI nucleotide database using BLAST with default parameters. The highest-scoring hit within the family Leporidae was recorded for each gene. The maximum likelihood (ML) analysis was performed in IQ-TREE (Nguyen et al. 2015). Sequences were partitioned by gene and codon position, and the optimal substitution model (GTR+I + G) was selected for ML inference. Bootstrap values were calculated with 1000 replicates, and those exceeding 50% are presented.

Results

The mitochondrial genome of P. furnessi from Amami Ōshima is a circular molecule of 16,644 bp (LC861750) that includes 13 PCGs, 22 tRNA genes, two rRNA genes, and a control region (Figure 2). The mitochondrial DNA of P. furnessi was successfully assembled with high coverage (Supplementary Figure S1). The average A + T content in the P. furnessi mitochondrial genome is 62.8%. Start codons ATG and ATT occur in 10 and three genes, respectively, whereas stop codons TAA and TAG occur in 10 and three genes, respectively. The heavy strand (H-strand) encodes 12 PCGs and 13 tRNA genes, whereas the light strand (L-strand) encodes one PCG, nine tRNA genes, and two rRNA genes. BLAST searches of the 13 PCGs showed that the highest sequence identity within Leporidae ranged from 80% (cox1) to 86% (cob), with Oryctolagus cuniculus as the top hit for most genes. Phylogenetic analysis showed Pentalagus furnessi as a monophyletic sister-relationships to Oryctolagus cuniculus, followed by Brachylagus idahoensis, with high bootstrap support (>90) (Figure 3).

Discussion and conclusion

Molecular phylogenetic analysis based on the 13 mitochondrial protein-coding genes (PCGs) placed P. furnessi within the clade of other members of the family Leporidae (Figure 3). This placement is consistent with previous morphological studies and with phylogenetic analyses using short nucleotide sequences (Yamada et al. 2002; Ohnishi et al. 2017). BLAST searches of the 13 PCGs in the mitochondrial genome of P. furnessi showed sequence identities of only 80–86% with its closest relative within Leporidae. The genetic distance between P. furnessi and its sister group, Oryctolagus cuniculus, estimated from the phylogenetic tree, was 0.157—a level commonly observed between different genera (Matt et al. 2004). These findings indicate that the Amami rabbit is genetically highly distinct from other extant leporids, consistent with its long-standing treatment as the sole species of the monotypic genus Pentalagus.
