Improving consistency and feedback in essay-type assessments: evaluation of an assessment-cum-feedback checklist
Authors: Prerna Agarwal, Bharti Bhandari, Vivek Gupta, Radhika Agarwal, Rakesh Kumar Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReliable assessment and meaningful feedback are essential to effective learning in medical education. However, conventional unstructured evaluation of essay-type responses is highly vulnerable to rater bias, inter-rater variability, and nonspecific feedback. To address these limitations, we developed an Assessment-cum-Feedback Checklist to provide a structured, criterion-based approach to scoring and feedback. In this study, we evaluated the checklist’s effectiveness in enhancing the reliability, consistency, and clarity of assessment while exploring student and faculty perceptions of its educational value.MethodsWe used a mixed-methods design. Sixty-two first-year MBBS students and four faculty members (two junior < 5 years’ experience; two senior > 10 years’ experience) participated. Two essay-type questions were assessed independently by all four teachers using both the conventional unstructured method and the checklist-based method. Quantitative analyses included descriptive statistics, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, Levene’s test for equality of variance, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC), and Bland–Altman analysis to compare variability and agreement across methods. Data were analysed using JASP (version 0.18.3.0) at a 5% significance level. Student perceptions were gathered using a structured questionnaire, and faculty perceptions were explored through in-depth interviews. Qualitative data were analysed using QCAmap (2020). Institutional Ethics Committee approval was obtained.ResultsChecklist-based scoring demonstrated lower standard errors, standard deviations, and coefficients of variation, indicating improved precision and reduced subjective variability compared with the conventional method. Mean scores were lower with the checklist, and Bland–Altman analysis showed a negative bias, reflecting greater scoring stringency due to explicit criteria. ICC values increased notably with the checklist—particularly among senior teachers—demonstrating improved inter-rater reliability and tighter limits of agreement. Teachers reported that the checklist enhanced objectivity, reduced bias, clarified performance expectations, and standardized assessment practices. Students expressed strong support, citing improved clarity, transparency, and usefulness of feedback.ConclusionsThe Assessment-cum-Feedback Checklist was associated with measurable improvements in the reliability and consistency of essay-type assessment. Both faculty and students perceived the checklist-based approach to enhance clarity, transparency, and the usefulness of feedback by making assessment criteria explicit. With appropriate faculty orientation and iterative refinement, the checklist represents a promising and potentially adaptable tool for strengthening assessment and feedback practices in constructed-response formats in medical education.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-08892-7.Keywords: Assessments, educational; Checklist; Education, medical; Examinations, graduate records; Formative feedback.

Background

Essay-type questions are fundamentally valuable for assessment for learning in the cognitive domain [1]. They allow considerable flexibility for examiners and examinees across both unstructured formats and structured variants, including modified essay questions. Research highlights their utility in assessing higher-order thinking, including comprehension, critical analysis, synthesis, and knowledge organization [2, 3]. Despite these strengths, essay-type questions are increasingly overshadowed by structured and objective formats, such as multiple-choice and short-answer questions.

Their decline can be attributed to several challenges. Essay assessment is inherently subjective [2] and prone to inter-rater variability [4]. Without clear rubrics or structured checklists, scoring lacks transparency and reliability, thereby diminishing credibility. Providing meaningful feedback poses an additional challenge. Feedback is integral to teaching and learning, as it informs students of their performance and level of competence, helping them identify areas for improvement and adapt their learning strategies. Yet feedback from traditional subjective essay evaluation is often vague and delayed, leaving students dissatisfied with their marks and uncertain about what was expected of them [5]. When feedback lacks accuracy, constructiveness, timeliness, or clear identification of learning needs, it loses its educational value and may contribute to repeated poor performance in subsequent assessments [4, 6, 7]. Consequently, essay-type questions have become summative exercises that assess memory rather than reasoning, forfeiting their potential as formative tools for reflective learning.

We reflected that reviving essay-type assessments requires structured scoring tools and integrated feedback mechanisms to transform these questions from burdensome relics into purposeful instruments of deep learning. Guided by this vision, we developed an ‘Assessment cum Feedback Checklist’ template (Supplementary Material 1), customizable for any essay-type question. This tool deconstructs evaluation into clearly defined, weighted criteria, enabling objective and transparent scoring with an intent of reducing bias and inter-assessor variability. At the same time, its framework provides for structured, individualized feedback, allowing students to recognize learning gaps and focus their improvement efforts. By standardizing assessment and strengthening feedback, this approach may help address limitations associated with essay-type questions and support their continued relevance in contemporary medical education.

The current study evaluates whether this checklist achieves its intended purpose. Our objectives were: (1) to assess essay-type questions using the checklist and compare outcomes with those of traditional subjective, non-structured method of their assessment, and (2) to explore the perceptions of both teachers and students regarding its implementation.

Methods

Study setting, design and data collection

We conducted our experimental mixed methods study in the Department of Physiology among the students of MBBS Phase 1 of the Government Institute of Medical Sciences, Greater Noida from June – July 2024. We obtained approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee (GIMS/IEC/HE/2024/34 dated 19.04.2024) and written informed consent of all the student participants. Data analysis

The data of all students who appeared in the formative assessment and gave feedback on the checklist were included in the study. Any student who appeared in the formative assessment but did not give feedback on the checklist, their data were excluded.

We recorded data in Excel Sheets of Microsoft Office Home and Student 2019 and analysed the quantitative data using JASP version 0.18.3.0 (https://jasp-stats.org/) at a 5% level of significance (p < 0.05), and the qualitative data using QCAmap 2020 (https://www.qcamap.org/ui/en/home).

Teachers’ perceptions about the ‘assessment cum feedback checklist’

We conducted face-to-face, semi-structured in-depth interviews [12–14] to explore teachers’ perceptions of the checklist’s utility for assessment and feedback, its limitations, and suggestions for improvement. The interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim using TurboScribe.ai (https://turboscribe.ai), and subsequently reviewed and corrected for accuracy. Qualitative analysis was conducted using a deductive content analysis approach guided by the interview framework [14]. Two authors independently coded the transcripts and compared coding to ensure consistency and minimize redundancy.

Students’ perceptions about the ‘assessment cum feedback checklist’

We designed a self-administered questionnaire with responses to be rated on a 5-point Likert scale, covering various aspects of student satisfaction with assessment results, and attributes of good feedback, to note their perceptions about the checklist method. All the items were positively worded. We included an open-ended question in it to capture the students’ general opinions about the approach. (Details of its validation and pilot testing are given in Supplementary Material 2).

The mean score was categorised into class intervals: 1.00–1.80: Strongly Disagree, 1.81–2.60: Disagree, 2.61–3.40: Neutral, 3.41–4.20: Agree, and 4.21–5.00: Strongly Agree for understanding overall agreement with an item [15]. We also tabulated the frequencies and percentages of the responses.

Additionally, we calculated descriptive statistics and Spearman’s Rank Correlation coefficients of the items of the questionnaire with each other from the students’ responses to get insights into how students perceived different aspects of the feedback process and plotted a heatmap for the coefficient, given in Supplementary Material 2.

Reflexivity and positionality

The corresponding author developed the Assessment-cum-Feedback Checklist, and four authors also participated as faculty assessors in the study—roles with the potential to influence data collection and interpretation. To address this, we practiced reflexivity throughout the research process. We engaged in ongoing self-reflection regarding our dual roles as educators and researchers and acknowledged potential power dynamics with student participants.

To mitigate bias, we maintained transparency in communicating the study’s purpose and conducted regular peer-debriefing discussions. To minimize the influence of cues and criteria embedded in the checklist, we assessed the essays first using the conventional method, followed by checklist-based assessment. We triangulated the quantitative findings with student responses from the feedback questionnaire, including the open-ended item, and faculty feedback. These measures strengthened the credibility, balance, and ethical rigor of the study.

Teacher feedback on the ‘assessment cum feedback checklist’- analysis of the semi-structured in–depth interviews

The teachers’ feedback (detailed in Supplementary Material 2) provided valuable qualitative insights into the checklist’s impact on teaching practices and its reception among educators:

A. Merits of using the checklist

Reduction of bias

Teachers noted a significant decrease in biasing factors such as handwriting and answer length, making assessments more objective.

Consistency in assessment

Both junior and senior teachers appreciated the checklist for its role in standardizing assessments across different evaluators and sessions.

Utility for less experienced teachers

The junior teachers valued the checklist particularly for providing clear guidelines, which helped them assess more confidently and objectively.

B. Drawbacks of using the checklist

Rigidity and inflexibility

Some teachers expressed concerns about the checklist’s rigidity, noting it could be too stringent for assessing responses that don’t conform to the expected structure.

Complexity in application

Teachers experienced some difficulty in assigning global ratings. They noted that there is a need for more training for using the checklist to effectively integrate the tool into regular teaching practices.

Time consumption

The teachers perceived the preparation and application of the checklist as little time-consuming.

C. Using the checklist for student feedback

Enhancing student learning

Teachers noted that the checklist may help students understand expected content and context in answers and encouraged more precise and structured responses. This is particularly important in medical education, where clear and structured feedback is crucial for guiding student learning.

Directing learning efforts

The checklist facilitated more focused and effective study strategies among students, aiding them in concentrating their efforts on essential aspects of the subject matter.

D. General perception and recommendations about the checklist

Positive reception

Teachers viewed the checklist as an improvement over conventional methods, citing greater objectivity and practical utility.

Standardization and training

They recommended refinements, including increased flexibility and additional faculty training for optimal use. Consensus among teachers suggested the need to standardize checklists to minimize variability in their application and to conduct regular training sessions to ensure all faculty members are proficient in their use.

Development of a checklist bank

Teachers advocated for creating a repository of standardized checklists, which could aid in uniform assessments across various topics and contribute to smoother exam preparations for students.

Discussion

A.The checklist as a tool of assessment

Mean scores awarded by teachers were consistently lower with the checklist-based method (Table 3), a finding corroborated by the negative bias observed in the Bland–Altman analysis (Table 6). Together, these findings suggest greater scoring stringency with the checklist, attributable to the requirement for alignment with explicitly defined assessment criteria.

The checklist-based method also demonstrated lower standard error of the mean and standard deviation (Table 3), indicating greater precision and reduced score dispersion. A lower coefficient of variation and reduced variance on Levene’s test (Tables 3 and 5) reflect improved consistency in applying assessment criteria. Narrower limits of agreement in the Bland–Altman analysis (Table 6), along with higher intraclass correlation coefficients—particularly among senior teachers (Table 5)—further indicate closer inter-rater agreement with the checklist-based approach. Collectively, these results indicate that the checklist provided a more consistent and objective grading framework than the conventional method.

Significant differences in scores awarded by most teachers, demonstrated through the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with large effect sizes in several comparisons (Table 4), suggest that the checklist meaningfully influenced marking practices. The educational relevance of structured assessment tools has been well documented. Watling and Ginsburg [16] noted that structured instruments enhance the clarity and specificity of feedback, while Kite and Phongsavan [17] reported that standards-based rubrics facilitate more consistent grading through explicit performance criteria.

The present findings are consistent with previous research. Jönsson et al. [18] reported that analytic grading—assessing discrete criteria prior to synthesizing a final score—significantly improves inter-rater agreement compared with holistic grading, paralleling the distinction between checklist-based and conventional methods in this study. Wahyuni et al. [19] similarly found that analytic rubrics increased inter-rater reliability by reducing subjective interpretation. In contrast, Reid et al. [20] observed greater consistency with generic rather than domain-specific rubrics in capstone project evaluation, suggesting that broader criteria may sometimes enhance uniformity across evaluators.

Along with these improvements, some inter-rater variability persisted, particularly among junior teachers. This finding aligns with Hasan and Jones [21], who reported greater inconsistency among novice assessors in applying marking guides. Bloxham et al. [22] similarly noted that complete elimination of variability is unlikely, given the inherently interpretive nature of academic judgment and the influence of assessors’ implicit criteria, even among experienced markers.

B. Perceptions of the teachers about the checklist method of assessment and feedback

Teacher feedback from the semi-structured interviews provided interpretive context for the quantitative findings and student perceptions. In contrast to conventional unstructured essay assessment, teachers described the checklist-based approach as supporting more objective and consistent judgment by reducing the influence of non-content-related factors and by clarifying expectations for assessment. This was particularly valued by less experienced teachers, for whom the checklist functioned as a guiding framework for applying criteria more confidently and uniformly.

Beyond assessment, teachers perceived the checklist as a useful adjunct for feedback. They noted that the explicit criteria helped students understand the expectations for high-quality responses, thereby encouraging more precise, structured, and analytically grounded answers. Such clarity is particularly valuable in medical education, where structured feedback is essential for guiding learners toward competent clinical reasoning and academic performance.

At the same time, they acknowledged trade-offs inherent to structured tools, including concerns about rigidity, complexity in assigning global judgments, and the additional time required for preparation and use.

Overall, teachers viewed the checklist as a constructive alternative to conventional essay assessment, while emphasising that its effective use depends on appropriate faculty training, standardisation of application, and periodic refinement. Suggestions such as developing a repository of validated checklists reflect a broader recognition that structured assessment tools require institutional support to balance consistency with flexibility.

Only a limited number of studies have examined teachers’ perspectives on structured assessment tools, but those available offer relevant parallels. Schellekens et al. [23], for example, explored teachers’ perceptions of assessment in higher education through semi-structured interviews. Consistent with the themes identified in our study, their participants emphasized the importance of consistency across assessors, accuracy and clarity in reporting performance, transparent assessment procedures, explicit communication of criteria to students, and the provision of constructive feedback. These convergent findings highlight the potential of our checklist approach to meaningfully strengthen assessment and feedback processes by addressing these key dimensions.

C. Students’ perceptions of the checklist method of assessment and feedback

Student feedback indicated high acceptability of the checklist-based assessment approach, with consistent agreement across items related to clarity, usefulness, and guidance for improvement. Students perceived the explicit criteria as helpful in reducing ambiguity in essay assessment and clarifying performance expectations, particularly in relation to identifying strengths and areas requiring improvement.

Checklist-based feedback was also viewed more favourably than general verbal feedback, reflecting a perceived advantage of structured, written feedback in providing specific and actionable guidance. Positive responses related to motivation, satisfaction with awarded marks, and support for regular use—including across other subjects—further underscore the acceptability of the approach from the learner perspective.

Open-ended responses contextualised these findings, with students describing improved organisation of responses, clearer identification of omissions, and greater focus on key content areas. However, a small number of students raised concerns about the rigidity and time demands of checklist-based assessment, noting potential limitations in accommodating analytical or creative responses, as was observed in the teachers’ feedback as well. Suggestions for refinement—including greater flexibility, exemplar responses, and earlier exposure to the checklist—highlight the need for thoughtful implementation.

The importance of structured and transparent feedback is well established in the literature. Rust et al. [24] demonstrated that clear, organized feedback can standardize grading practices and reduce variability—an outcome that closely aligns with the aims of the Assessment cum Feedback Checklist. Zhao [25] further underscored the importance of making assessment criteria readily accessible to students to ensure that they can meaningfully engage with these criteria and use them to guide their learning and preparation. More recently, Sequeira et al. [26] examined learner preferences for feedback formats and reported a strong preference for text-based feedback due to its precision and ease of reference, reinforcing the value of clarity and specificity in guiding student learning.

The Assessment cum Feedback Checklist was developed as an objective method for evaluating subjective essay-type responses—functioning as an “Objectively Structured Essay Examination”—to enhance the quality of assessment and feedback of essays, while remaining acceptable to both students and faculty. As an innovative essay assessment tool, it performed well across multiple statistical measures, demonstrating its potential to improve the accuracy and consistency of essay evaluations while simultaneously enriching their feedback, and was positively received by both stakeholder groups.

To strengthen the reliability of our findings and increase the robustness of evaluation, we applied the checklist to two distinct essay-type questions, to minimize the influence of outlier performance on any one question and included four teachers with differing levels of experience—two junior (< 5 years) and two senior (> 10 years), for assessor diversity. Teachers at dissimilar stages of their careers bring varied expectations, pedagogical perspectives, and interpretive approaches to evaluation. Incorporating this heterogeneity more accurately reflected the tool’s performance across a realistic range of teaching profiles.

Overall, the results indicate that the checklist has the potential to meaningfully contribute to strengthening assessment and feedback practices for essay-type questions. Its structured design may support students in aligning their responses more closely with expected performance standards by providing clearer expectations and more actionable feedback. These findings are congruent with the principles outlined in Hauer et al.’s [27] “Twelve Tips for Assessing Medical Knowledge with Essays,” which affirm the value of constructed-response formats in fostering clinical understanding, emphasize clarity of expectations, and advocate iterative refinement as part of continuous quality improvement. Moon et al. [28] introduced structured feedback mechanisms using checkbox-based grading for handwritten mathematics examinations. Their findings highlighted substantial improvements in assessment consistency and the clarity of feedback delivered to students. However, they also recommended modifications to accommodate a broader range of valid responses and to prevent the suppression of expressive or analytical writing. These results and insights closely resonate with our findings. And accordingly, by carefully applying the more generic criteria of the checklist, the clarity and specificity of detailed criteria may be balanced with the broader applicability and consistency of generic and holistic rubrics. Faculty training in essay checklist use may enhance its effective utilization. Similarly, having iterative reviews based on feedback from both raters and students to refine the criteria and scoring rubrics, may bring rigor and fairness to essay evaluations to make them reflective of the learning objectives. Concurrent analysis of its impact on learning outcomes and making necessary adjustments may be helpful in enhancing its perceived educational value.

Limitations

Despite the comprehensive approach adopted, several limitations warrant consideration. The checklist was applied to a relatively small cohort of 62 students within a single department, which limits statistical power and restricts generalizability across different institutional and disciplinary contexts. The cross-sectional design precludes assessment of longitudinal effects, and it remains unclear whether checklist-based assessment leads to sustained improvements in performance or assessor reliability over time. In addition, the qualitative findings rely on interpretive analysis, and although reflexivity was maintained throughout the study, the perspectives of the researchers — including the involvement of the principal investigator as the checklist developer — may have influenced interpretation. Furthermore, the overlap between assessor and researcher roles may have introduced unintentional bias, despite conscious efforts to mitigate its impact.

Implications for practice

The findings indicate that checklist-based assessment facilitates a more structured and accountable evaluation process by requiring explicit demonstration of predefined criteria. While this approach was associated with lower scores, it aligns with mastery-oriented assessment principles that prioritize precision and criterion-referenced standards. By reducing inter-assessor variability, the checklist supported greater consistency in essay evaluation—an important consideration in contexts where assessment outcomes inform progression and accreditation. The checklist approach may be adapted for other formats, such as short-answer questions, through appropriate standardization of criteria. Future research is warranted to examine its applicability across diverse settings and to evaluate its longitudinal impact on learning outcomes and assessor reliability.

Conclusions

This study suggests that the Assessment-cum-Feedback Checklist represents a promising approach for evaluating essay-type responses in undergraduate medical education. Within the study context, the checklist was associated with improved objectivity, reduced inter-assessor variability, and clearer, enriched feedback that was acceptable to both students and faculty. The convergence of quantitative findings and stakeholder perceptions underscores the potential of structured assessment frameworks to enhance rigor and transparency while reducing subjectivity in constructed-response formats. However, cautious generalization beyond similar educational settings is warranted. With appropriate faculty training and iterative refinement, checklist-based assessment may offer a scalable strategy for strengthening assessment and feedback practices in medical education.
