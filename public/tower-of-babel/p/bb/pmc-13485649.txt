Focused ultrasound in epilepsy
Authors: Ellen J. Bubrick, Lillian H. Stapelfeld, Parker Kotlarz, Nathan J. McDannold, P. Jason White
Published: 2026

Background

Epilepsy is one of the most common neurologic disorders, affecting 3.5 million Americans, and more than 65 million people worldwide. A third of these patients have drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE) [1] with mesial temporal lobe epilepsy (mTLE) being the most common type. The burden of DRE is high; ongoing seizures can be very debilitating, and are often associated with significant morbidity and mortality [2]. Many of these patients endure ongoing cognitive impairment related to persisting seizures and polypharmacy, which in turn has been associated with low quality of life related to difficulties the disorder poses on education, employment and relationships [3]. Total annual cost estimates for epilepsy in the United States alone exceed $10 billion [4]. Because of the prevalence and severity of the disease, there are high rates of seizure-related injuries (burns, falls), accidental deaths and an elevated suicide rate in this population [3,5]. These patients also have elevated rates (1:150/year) of sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP) [6].

Limitations of Current Treatments

Though the population affected by DRE is large with high morbidity, there is a lack of novel treatments being developed to address this disabling disorder. More than 19 antiseizure medications have been brought to the US market since 1993, but not one has changed the statistic that a third of patients remain drug-resistant [7]. Surgical resection or laser ablation can be very effective treatments for focal epilepsy, but only a portion of patients with DRE are candidates for these options [8,9]. Many are ineligible due to poorly localizable seizure onsets, bilateral or multifocal disease, or unacceptable risks of surgery causing a significant neurologic deficit.

Even when epilepsy surgery may be an option, antero-mesial temporal lobe resection carries a significant risk of cognitive decline, and those with extra-temporal disease have poorer surgical outcomes [10,11]. Seizure recurrence rates, both early and even years after surgery, are problematic, and many patients never regain seizure freedom [10,11]. Invasive electrostimulatory devices such as deep brain stimulation (DBS) and responsive neurostimulation (RNS) are of some benefit in epilepsy, but these require permanent implantation, which is unacceptable to many patients, and are still largely only palliative. Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), though noninvasive and highly effective for some psychiatric disorders, has produced disappointing outcomes in epilepsy. Other options to help reduce seizure frequency and burden in this population are scarce, thus there is a critical unmet need for innovative treatments.

Transcranial focused ultrasound (TUS): An Emerging, Novel Method of Neuromodulation

Focused ultrasound is a novel method of neuromodulation, achieved with acoustic, as opposed to electrical, stimulation. TUS can noninvasively target brain tissue with a spatial distribution of acoustic energy that ensures therapeutic effect only at the geometric focus of the transducer. The physics of focused ultrasound are similar to clinical ultrasound imaging except the ultrasound energy is focused to a specific location with the intent of modulating the neural tissue at the target, instead of spreading energy over an imaging region and having it echo back to create an image. Focused ultrasound has been studied for over 60 years [12]. Its safety and utility have since been extensively analyzed for many medical uses and are widely used in many systemic disorders including uterine fibroids, bone cancer pain, disorders of the prostate and nephrolithiasis.

More recently, the technique is also being employed transcranially to treat neurologic and psychiatric disorders including Alzheimer's dementia, Parkinson's disease, neuropathic pain, addiction, obsessive compulsive disorder, and other psychiatric disorders. Focused ultrasound is generally classified into two categories of high and low intensity. High intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU) causes heating or mechanical damage. Thermal ablation with HIFU produces heating of tissue at the beam focus above the level of thermal necrosis, and is FDA approved for noninvasive thalamotomy to treat essential tremor [13]. HIFU has been transformative in treating tremor, as it can reach deep targets such as the thalamus with thermal ablation [13]. For treating essential tremor, the high intensity ultrasound beam is directed to the ventral intermediate nucleus of the thalamus. The procedure is performed within an MRI, which is used to monitor the heating to ensure accurate targeting and control of the energy deposition and results in a precisely placed thalamotomy which resolves the tremor. Thermal ablation with HIFU is incisionless (requires no craniotomy), but it is a surgical technique, which is not acceptable for the many types of epilepsy that are co-localized to tissue with critical neurologic function.

Due to the high ultrasound absorption of bone, transcranial thermal ablation with HIFU is challenging due to the risk of skull heating. The skull also distorts and defocuses the ultrasound field. To overcome these challenges, a complex system, based on a hemispherical phased array transducer, is necessary. The hemisphere design distributes the energy over a large area to reduce skull heating, and the phased array is used to compensate for the aberrations caused by the irregular skull bone [14,15]. This approach is highly effective for deep brain targets like the thalamus, but as the focus is placed nearer to the skull, the angle between large parts of the transducer and the skull bone increases, leading to less energy transmission [16]. As a result, it is not possible to reach ablative temperatures at the focus without overheating the skull. For this reason, HIFU thermal ablation has not been successfully used as a noninvasive alternative to surgery or laser ablation for many epilepsy focal targets.

When low intensity TUS is used for neuromodulation, the acoustic intensities are orders of magnitude lower than for thermal ablation, thus it does not produce significant heating or result in any tissue damage or permanent anatomical changes [12,[17], [18], [19]]. TUS is noninvasive and painless. The field of TUS for neuromodulation has blossomed considerably in the last decade; numerous exciting studies from many centers have demonstrated its region-specific effects on animals and humans [18,[20], [21], [22], [23]]. TUS could be a unique and promising form of neuromodulatory treatment for DRE when surgery is not favored, not viable, or fails.

Preclinical and Clinical Data Using TUS for Neuromodulation

There is a growing literature in animals and humans demonstrating TUS’ ability to noninvasively, safely and reversibly modulate neural tissue and elicit varying effects by focusing the ultrasound beam onto corresponding brain regions [18]. Studies have also shown TUS’ ability to decrease seizure activity with sustained effects. Overviews of human applications of this emerging technology highlight the safety and tolerability of this technique in humans when performed within established safety parameters [19,20,24,25].

Animal and non-human primates (NHPs)

The neuromodulatory effects of ultrasound have been studied in various regions of the brain in many animals. Responses have been elicited with sonication of motor cortex in rats, as well as with sonicating the thalamus, hippocampus, sensory and visual cortices of rodents and rabbits [[26], [27], [28], [29]]. Suppression of visually evoked potentials in small animals and cats has also been observed [[26], [27], [28], [29]]. Similar findings have been demonstrated in larger animals with a rounded cranial vault and skull thickness more similar to humans including sheep, swine, as well as in NHPs [[30], [31], [32], [33], [34], [35], [36], [37]]. TUS produced stimulus-locked effects on saccade latency when applied to frontal eye fields in macaques, and was also able to modulate discharges in the supplementary eye field, a reciprocally-connected brain region [38]. Yang et al. [34] demonstrated fMRI blood oxygen level dependent (BOLD) activation to the primary somatosensory cortex of macaques by TUS, and Downs et al. [39] observed increased rate and accuracy of decision-making tasks after sonicating the putamen.

Several preclinical studies have demonstrated the ability of TUS to suppress seizures in epilepsy animal models [[40], [41], [42], [43]]. Electrographic seizures were suppressed with sonication and recurrent seizures were reduced in a kainic-acid mTLE mouse model [42]. A different study using the same model in rats demonstrated seizure termination with MR-guided TUS to the hippocampus, and another showed sonication delivered to the thalamus reduced the duration of pentylenetetrazol-induced seizures [43,44].

Sustained effects

There are also studies showing TUS can produce lasting effects on seizure activity. Hakimova et al. saw significantly fewer spontaneous recurrent seizures weeks after status epilepticus in mice treated with TUS, suggesting a neuroprotective effect [42], and Chu et al. [43] showed that TUS can reduce epileptiform activity in rats for almost 2 months. TUS has demonstrated the ability to produce sustained “offline” effects of in several NHP studies as well, lasting for 30 min or more, similar or beyond that of repetitive TMS [32,36,37,45]. Evidence of TUS-induced neuroplasticity has also been demonstrated in humans [46].

Human trials

Numerous ongoing studies in humans are investigating TUS for a range of neurologic and psychiatric conditions including epilepsy, tremor, disorders of consciousness, schizophrenia symptoms, mood regulation, pain syndromes, opioid use disorder, obsessive compulsive disorder, and depression [[17], [18], [19], [20]]. Several studies in human subjects have demonstrated TUS-elicited modulatory effects of auditory, visual, sensory and motor functions [[21], [22], [23],[47], [48], [49], [50], [51], [52], [53], [54]]. Legon et al. delivered TUS to deep neural targets by sonicating through the temporal bone to the sensory thalamus of 40 human subjects. TUS was shown to attenuate P14 thalamic somatosensory evoked potentials and modulate cortical oscillations [55]. A report using TUS to treat patients with coma/severe brain injury showed sonication to the thalamus was associated with increased level of arousal [56]. Mueller et al. [57] showed that TUS could alter EEG oscillations in humans compared to sham, a finding also demonstrated by Lee et al. [58] in patients with depth electrodes.

Clinical Trials of FUS for Epilepsy

HIFU ablation for epilepsy

Given HIFU's ability to noninvasively ablate tissue with exquisite precision, one can imagine how this could be an attractive means to knock out a potential epileptogenic focus. This has been trialed by several groups, though with some challenges. Abe et al. attempted to ablate the left hippocampus in a 36yo woman with mesial temporal lobe epilepsy using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) guided HIFU [59]. Despite delivering 12 sonications, however, ablative temperatures were not achieved and no gliotic lesion was produced. The investigators felt this was due to using less than the required number of transducer elements (only 659 of 1024). Interestingly, the patient did experience significant seizure reduction over 12 months, suggesting perhaps a neuromodulatory effect was obtained with the sub-ablative temperatures used.

Krishna et al. reported a phase I open label trial using HIFU to produce a unilateral anterior thalamotomy for drug-resistant focal epilepsy [60]. The target, the anterior nucleus of the thalamus (ANT), is thought to play a crucial role in seizure propagation clinically resulting in progression from a focal to bilateral tonic-clonic convulsion. Two subjects were enrolled and treated. Feasibility was demonstrated with lesioning to the ANT and seizure reduction was noted (with later recurrence one year post-procedure in one subject), but neurocognitive decline (memory) was noted in both subjects, limiting the use of the technique more widely.

HIFU has also been used to treat hypothalamic hamartoma, a lesion that carries a high surgical resection risk given its location. Galimova et al. reported successful HIFU ablation to a woman with seizures secondary to a hypothalamic hamartoma. The subject experienced immediate and sustained seizure reduction (up to 7 months of follow up) without adverse events [61]. Ricciardi et al. also reported a successful HIFU ablation of a hypothalamic hamartoma followed by an 18 month seizure free period at the time of publication [62].

TUS neuromodulation for epilepsy

Though studies using low intensity TUS for neuromodulation have become increasingly popular in many neuropsychiatric disorders, few had been shown to be safe or effective in epilepsy until recently. A group at UCLA first demonstrated safety using TUS to the temporal lobe in humans with epilepsy. Stern et al. [24] administered TUS as a single session to the anterior temporal lobe in eight subjects with temporal lobe epilepsy who were scheduled to undergo temporal lobectomy a few days later. This unique population provided the opportunity to obtain pathologic specimens from the sonicated regions. The sonicated tissue showed no evidence of gliosis or other histiopathologic injury. This study paved the way for further trials using this technique in epilepsy patients.

Our team completed a pilot study investigating safety and tolerability of TUS in a small number of adults with mTLE [63]. Exposure levels used were at the low end of those shown previously to be safe in human controls and neuromodulation, and were selected to be conservative since this was the first study to serially sonicate hyperexcitable hippocampus in humans with mTLE [[49], [50], [51], [52], [53], [54], [55], [56]]. A treatment protocol including six treatment sessions over three weeks in subjects with mTLE was employed. The pilot trial demonstrated safety in this population, as well as seizure reduction, which averaged ∼50%, varied in duration lasting from several weeks to several months before some subjects then ultimately returned to their baseline seizure frequencies six month post-procedure [63]. A few had more sustained effects, one lasting years. No adverse events were observed. In addition, resting state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) was obtained pre and one month post-TUS and showed improved default mode network organization after treatment, potentially confirming target engagement and neuromodulatory effects of the technique. This was the first trial demonstrating TUS can noninvasively and safely modulate human hippocampus in mTLE.

The only trial using TUS in epilepsy subjects that has obtained direct electroencephalography (EEG) read outs was by investigators in Taiwan led by Lee et al. [58]. In this unique trial, six epilepsy patients undergoing invasive intracranial monitoring with stereoEEG to localize the seizure onset zone were enrolled. TUS was administered to the confirmed seizure onset zone, and the subsequent changes in EEG were reported. The results were variable; some subjects showed a decrease in interictal epileptiform discharges (IEDs) while others showed an increase. Changes in seizure frequency were also variable, but significant changes in EEG spectral power were observed with treatment. A few adverse events were noted, one involving report of transient skull heating which resolved within an hour, the other neurocognitive issues (naming and memory) which resolved within weeks. Though the impact of TUS on IEDs and seizures were variable, this study provided evidence of its neuromodulatory capability.

Additional TUS Applications in Epilepsy and Future Directions- Targeted CNS drug delivery

In addition to HIFU for ablation and TUS for neuromodulation, focused ultrasound can also be used for targeted CNS drug delivery by opening the blood brain barrier [64]. Sonicating specific brain targets after injecting microbubbles peripherally has been shown to result in transient, reversible blood brain barrier opening (BBBO), typically for about 24–48 h. This effect is visualized with demonstration of contrast extravasation at the site on MRI immediately after sonication. This has been demonstrated in clinical applications in ongoing trials, mainly CNS tumor treatments and in Alzheimer's disease [[65], [66], [67], [68], [69], [70], [71]]. The main advantage here is delivery of a therapeutic agent that is normally impermeable to the blood brain barrier, but with precise sonication after microbubble administration, the agent can enter the CNS through the area of the barrier that has been opened. The other advantage is the agent is delivered directly in the vicinity of the lesion where it will have its intended effects, sparing the rest of the brain (and body) from significant exposure to it. This technique has not yet been trialed in patients with epilepsy, but has been successful in reducing seizure activity in rodent models [72,73]. One can imagine how, especially in the case of chemotherapeutic treatments, sparing the rest of the brain and the body from potential side effects would be quite attractive.

A critical aspect of drug development and efficacy for epilepsy is the drug's ability to cross the blood brain barrier and remain in the brain. Many drugs currently used for epilepsy are only partially or sub-optimally permeable to the blood brain barrier, or are highly affected by BBB parameters including efflux transporters, thus patients typically require high doses of the medications to reach a therapeutic effect [74,75]. This often results in significant CNS and systemic side effects and toxicities, however, which can negatively affect quality of life. Side effects at subtherapeutic doses are a common cause for abandoning a medication or under-treating, thus resulting in poor seizure control. If one was able to deliver a smaller amount of drug peripherally, however, and then sonicate the seizure focus, a more targeted CNS delivery of the drug may be accomplished with fewer side effects. An implanted [65] or perhaps a wearable device would likely be required in such a scenario, depending on the duration of the drug's effect in the brain. Using such a technique, even just during a time of increased seizure activity or cluster, may help stabilize a patient without having to increase systemic medication doses and endure the sometimes dangerous, often intolerable side effects that can result.

Another way in which focused ultrasound can enhance targeted CNS drug delivery noninvasively is by a process called uncaging. In this technique, a nanoparticle is coupled to an agent of choice and administered peripherally, then focused ultrasound waves are used to disrupt the bond between the particles, thus “freeing” the therapeutic agent from the nanoparticle in the brain. Here, the drug to be administered may already cross the blood brain barrier, but given it was coupled to a nanoparticle and then uncaged in a specific region of the brain, it can deliver effects to a very focused area, again sparing the rest of the brain and body from unwanted effects. Significantly lower doses of the drug may ultimately be therapeutic with this approach as well.

Airan et al. [76] demonstrated this effect in a rat model using PTZ-induced seizures. They used propofol as their drug, and uncaged it from a nanoparticle with focused ultrasound to deliver the drug directly to the brain. They saw reduced seizure activity after drug delivery, as well as diminished broadband and theta power on EEG. There was no increase in blood serum levels seen post-sonication, suggesting sparing of the systemic and more widespread CNS effects from high systemic dosing may be an advantage with this technique as well. Clinically, patients in status epilepticus, a life-threatening condition involving unrelenting seizures, often require extremely high doses of antiseizure medications, so high to even require intubation and medically-induced coma at times. These treatments are often required, but are very high risk, and can result in significant morbidity and even mortality. The ability to noninvasively deliver propofol, or another agent, directly to a relentless seizure focus in the brain this way could be transformative to the treatment of this condition.

This concept of TUS-induced uncaging for targeted CNS drug delivery has also been applied to gene therapy. Han et al. [77] used chemo-gene therapy, a combination of chemotherapeutics with nucleic acid, noted to be promising due to both synergistic effect of the two agents and the ability to use reduced chemotherapy dose with equivalent effect. They developed a focused ultrasound-responsive nanocomplex for enhanced delivery of chemo-gene therapy to treat cancer in mice. Gene therapy trials are ongoing in drug-resistant epilepsy. Though considered minimally invasive, they currently still require a burr hole and injection via an intracranial catheter. If proven to be effective against seizures, using focused ultrasound for noninvasive delivery of the product may be a considerably safer approach for this highly innovative treatment.

From an instrumentation standpoint, there is overlap in the hardware platforms that can be adapted across these applications, and devices made for BBB disruption or drug uncaging can be used for TUS for neuromodulation. BBB disruption and drug uncaging typically uses longer ultrasound bursts (5–10 ms) and lower acoustic intensities than TUS. However, as those applications use exogenous ultrasonically-sensitive agents that pose a risk of inertial cavitation and vascular damage at excessive exposure levels, devices for BBB disruption and drug uncaging likely require more sensitive cavitation monitoring than TUS for neuromodulation.

Putative Molecular and Cellular Mechanisms of TUS Neuromodulation in Epilepsy

The molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying TUS-mediated neuromodulation are an active area of investigation. Several non-mutually exclusive mechanisms have been proposed. Mechanosensitive ion channels, in particular those of the TRAAK/TREK subfamily of two-pore domain potassium channels and PIEZO channels, are activated by membrane tension changes induced by the acoustic radiation force and by cavitation microstreaming, leading to altered ion conductance and membrane potential [78,79]. Activation of stretch-sensitive channels that favor potassium efflux would tend to hyperpolarize neurons and reduce excitability, a mechanism of potential relevance to epilepsy suppression. Conversely, non-selective mechanosensitive cation channels could depolarize neurons and increase firing, which may underlie excitatory TUS effects observed at some parameter settings.

At the membrane level, ultrasound-induced bilayer sonophore dynamics, oscillations within the phospholipid bilayer driven by acoustic energy, have been modeled as capable of modulating the kinetics of voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels, offering a unifying biophysical framework that may reconcile the parameter-dependence of inhibitory versus excitatory TUS effects [80]. Intracellular calcium transients triggered by TUS have also been described [81,82], potentially engaging calcium-dependent signaling cascades including those regulating inhibitory interneuron function. There is also evidence of TUS-induced modulation of neurotransmitter release, including effects on GABAergic and glutamatergic transmission [83,84], though the mechanistic linkage between acoustic energy deposition and synaptic vesicle release remains under investigation.

From a therapeutic development perspective, these mechanistic unknowns underscore the need for parameter optimization studies specifically in epileptic brain, and for more mechanistic studies using epilepsy animal models that can probe the cellular effects of TUS in tissue with known pathological changes. An improved mechanistic understanding could ultimately allow rational, disease-state-specific parameter selection, moving the field beyond empirical dosing toward mechanism-informed therapeutic design.

Limitations and Challenges of TUS in Epilepsy

TUS has extensively shown to produce neuromodulatory effects at many brain targets, as referenced above. Direct proof of target engagement with TUS, however, has been less certain, especially for deep targets critical to epilepsy such as the hippocampus. Studies have demonstrated that MR acoustic radiation force imaging (MR-ARFI) can confirm the location of a TUS focus in the brain and even roughly estimate the dose at that target in a few subjects [[85], [86], [87]]. MR-ARFI works by detecting micron-scale tissue displacements produced by the acoustic radiation force of the ultrasound beam. As such, the acoustic intensities required to produce a displacement detectable by MRI have been higher than those typically used for low-intensity TUS neuromodulation. Recent work, however, has demonstrated that displacements can be mapped at exposure levels similar to those used with TUS [85]. These important preliminary trials are promising, but further studies are needed using larger numbers and directed targeting for clear proof of target engagement.

Part of the difficulty in proving target engagement to epileptogenic sites, specifically without real-time MRI during treatment, is the inability to use scalp EEG as a marker of precise targeting. Though studies are ongoing investigating different types of EEGs that may be used in TUS without disrupting the ultrasound beam, currently this ability is lacking. Investigators are therefore left with EEG data only from surrounding scalp EEG leads, which may be too far away to detect any hippocampal or even neocortical temporal lobe signals. The Taiwan group was the only study thus far that sonicated epilepsy patients with intracranial depth electrodes implanted, having placed the transducer over the correlating burr holes [58]. They found that ultrasound did modify the EEG, which revealed significant changes in spectral power, and some changes in seizure frequency were also noted.

Darmani et al. [88] were the first to demonstrate safety in sonicating subjects with implanted DBS, after this was investigated this in an ex-vivo model [89]. In addition to proving safety of sonicating near DBS electrodes, they also demonstrated that TUS to the globus pallidus internus (GPi) modulates local field potentials (LFPs). The electrodes were placed in the GPi in a patient with Parkinson's disease, not in a patient with epilepsy, thus safety in the epilepsy population is still to be determined. A similar study sonicating near an RNS lead to investigate TUS’ neuromodulatory effects would be particularly informative in an epilepsy patient, given the read out of RNS is continuously recording intracranial EEG recording, not just LFPs. The materials of RNS leads are different from those used in DBS and of the depth electrodes used for short-term intracranial recordings, however. Ex-vivo experiments using those materials may pave the way for safety trials in epilepsy patients with RNS.

Another important factor that must be considered is variability in skull properties. In HIFU thermal ablation, the energy needed to reach ablative temperatures varies by an order of magnitude due to this variability [90]. For that application, MR temperature imaging is used to ensure accurate targeting and to titrate the ultrasound exposure. Without real-time feedback from the patient, EEG or imaging, the operator does not know if the target was hit nor if sufficient (or excessive) acoustic intensities were used.

Instead, they must rely on forward acoustic models [91]. While conceptually attractive, the validity of this approach requires careful scrutiny. Forward acoustic models depend critically on accurate skull bone segmentations gleaned from medical imaging and on the assumed values of skull acoustic impedance, attenuation, and speed of sound, all of which vary substantially across individuals and even across skull regions. Further, the spatial resolution of the skull images is insufficient to fully capture the heterogeneity in the trabecular bone, so the estimates will likely have limits on accuracy. While these models are advancing, their validation for use in specific patients has not been established. If the exposure levels needed to produce clinically-relevant neuromodulation are high and approach damage thresholds, the exposures could result in tissue injury.

One approach that could potentially overcome the variability in skull properties uses two opposing transducer arrays that transmit and receive each other across the skull to infer the skull-induced aberrations [92,93]. While this approach is promising, and clinical results have been published using it [94,95], the applicability to epilepsy and other treatments have yet to be established.

In addition to these limitations, as with any new device technology, some fundamental elements require definition including parameters to be used in epilepsy specifically, and in other neuropsychiatric disorders. Ideal parameters for desired effects will likely vary not just by brain region sonicated, but also between disorders. A safe, effective set of sonication parameters may be completely different when treating the hippocampus than the cingulate gyrus, for example. As such, parameters may be different in treating epilepsy versus tremor, etc. In addition to the optimal parameters, even the idea of “dose” requires further definition. In drug trials, the recommended phase II dose is determined in early phase I trials using where safety is monitored while pushing the dose toward the maximum tolerated dose (MTD) where toxicity appears. This is more complicated in device trials, especially using TUS, where that type of dose-related toxicity can mean cavitation (ie necrosis), an unacceptable outcome. In addition, the term “dose” in a transcranial application can mean many things, not just intensity of energy delivered, but volume of tissue treated, duration of treatment, number of treatments, etc.

Further, the neuropathologic basis of epilepsy differs from that of other neuropsychiatric disorders and from normal brain, yet the potential effects of TUS in epileptic tissue are incompletely understood. What has been shown to be an “inhibitory” TUS pulse (such as one that suppresses evoked potentials) in a normal human subject or a patient with a non-epilepsy disorder may result in a completely different, potentially even dangerous result in a patient with epilepsy. For example, the neuropathologic hallmarks of mesial temporal sclerosis are loss of inhibitory interneurons, altered GABA A receptor subunit composition, mossy fiber sprouting, and chronic neuroinflammation. These pathological alterations in the cellular substrate may shift the net effect of TUS in unpredictable ways. A sonication protocol that produces net inhibition in normal hippocampus by preferentially activating hyperpolarizing mechanosensitive channels could, in a hyperexcitable hippocampus with reduced inhibitory tone, fail to suppress, or even paradoxically activate, residual excitatory circuits. Clinically, this could result in the inability of those circuits to shut down a seizure. The same logic applies in reverse: parameters empirically found to be ‘excitatory’ in normal subjects might engage surviving inhibitory interneurons in epileptogenic tissue preferentially, yielding a net anticonvulsant effect resulting clinically in improved seizure control.

And lastly, perhaps the main challenge in bringing TUS to epilepsy patients as a novel treatment for seizures is difficulty with the accuracy and precision in targeting extratemporal, neocortical seizure foci. Neocortical targets, very important in epilepsy as are deeper targets, are difficult to sonicate due to the proximity to the skull, for reasons discussed above. Ongoing studies investigating new devices are attempting to mitigate this difficulty, but none have thus far definitively proven such areas can be successfully sonicated. In summary, TUS is a noninvasive, novel method to achieve neuromodulatory effects and has shown promise as a potential epilepsy treatment. Many challenges and limitations in this early field of study require further investigation.
