CD73high fibroblasts orchestrate keratinocyte inflammation in the psoriasis-associated epithelial immune microenvironment
Authors: Yuzi Tian, Jia Guo, Jinjian Sun, Xiaoye Zhang, Guowei Zhou, Lin Ye, Yan Zhang, Peihua Liu, Junyu Zhou, Chengeng Xiao, Xiaoyun Xie, Yang Xia, Hervé Bachelez, Hong Liu, Xiang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metabolic dysregulation within the epithelial immune microenvironment (EIME) drives chronic inflammatory skin diseases like psoriasis, but the immune mechanisms and downstream consequences remain unclear. Here we perform in-depth metabolomic analysis showing that nucleotide metabolism is enhanced in psoriatic patients, with elevated adenosine levels closely correlating with disease severity. Single-cell and spatial transcriptomics analyses revealed that adenosine is primarily generated from a population of CD73high fibroblasts in psoriatic skin through enhanced metabolic processes and catalytic capability. Adenosine acts as a mediator between fibroblasts and keratinocytes, causing mitochondrial dysfunction and generating oxidative stress, resulting in the release of pro-inflammatory mediators in keratinocytes via ADORA2B. Deletion of Cd73 in fibroblasts, Adora2b in keratinocytes, or the use of pharmacological inhibitors of the pathways involved, reduces epidermal inflammation in the imiquimod- and IL-23A-induced mouse skin inflammation models. Our study thus identifies the CD73high fibroblast subsets as regulators of epithelial inflammation through metabolic microenvironment interactions with keratinocytes, providing proof of principle for therapeutic strategies targeting fibroblast-keratinocyte crosstalk in inflammatory skin diseases.

Introduction

The skin serves as both a physical and immunological barrier, with immune responses concentrated in the epidermis and papillary dermis, forming the epithelial immune microenvironment (EIME)1,2. In chronic skin inflammation, disruptions within the EIME lead to dysregulated immune responses. Psoriasis, a common chronic inflammatory skin disease affecting 2–3% of the global population3,4, is traditionally studied through immune cell functions and the epithelial-immune cell inflammatory loop, such as the interaction between Th17 cells and myeloid-derived dendritic cells driving type 17 EIME1. However, the immune-related role of numerous stromal cells that constitute the skin’s framework has been largely overlooked.

Fibroblasts are the most abundant type of stromal cells in the cutaneous EIME and can be spatially categorized into papillary and reticular fibroblasts. Their heterogeneity and immune functions in diseases like psoriasis remain underexplored. Recent advancements in single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics highlight the significant contribution of fibroblast-keratinocyte interactions to immune responses in the psoriatic EIME, influencing inflammation initiation and progression5–7. Despite these insights, the precise mechanisms and mediators underpinning this critical cellular crosstalk remain elusive.

Metabolic alterations, a hallmark of psoriasis, play a crucial role in shaping the extracellular microenvironment. Skin cells actively metabolize nutrients in their microenvironment to meet the energy demands of hyperproliferation and inflammation8. While metabolites are known to influence immune regulation in various tissue environments9–12, it remains uncertain whether psoriasis exhibits a disease-specific metabolic pattern and how this may influence the crosstalk between fibroblasts and keratinocytes in the disease-specific EIME.

In this study, we highlight a key ATP degradation pathway centered on adenosine in psoriatic patients, with single-cell and spatial transcriptomics uncovering an expanded CD73high fibroblast subset in the papillary dermis. These fibroblasts generate adenosine, which interacts with ADORA2B on keratinocytes, promoting inflammation via the pCREB-HO-1-mitochondrial dysfunction pathway. Deletion of Cd73 in fibroblasts or Adora2b in keratinocytes, or pharmacological inhibition, significantly alleviates disease phenotype and inflammation in the imiquimod- and IL-23A-induced mouse models. These findings broaden our understanding of metabolite-based keratinocyte-fibroblast interactions in psoriatic EIME and provide potential immune-targeted therapeutic strategies for inflammatory skin diseases.

Results

Adenosine signaling was upregulated in psoriatic patients and correlated to disease severity

Metabolites are core intermediates involved in metabolic crosstalk9. To gain insight into the metabolomic alterations in psoriasis, plasma samples were collected from patients, and a comprehensive metabolomics analysis was conducted, covering nearly 600 metabolites across 13 categories (Fig. 1A, patients’ clinical characteristics in S1A). In total, 356 metabolites were detected, with 75 differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) identified between psoriasis and control groups (Fig. S1B). The DEMs exhibited significant enrichment in 127 KEGG pathways. The differential abundance analysis revealed a notable upregulation of nucleotide metabolism in psoriatic patients (Fig. 1B, and exact p values are listed in Supplemental Table 1). Key altered nucleotides/nucleoside included adenosine monophosphate (AMP), 2’-deoxyguanosine-5’-diphosphate (dGDP), adenosine-5’-diphosphate (ADP), adenosine (ADO), and uridine-5’-diphosphate (UDP) (Fig. 1C). Notably, three out of five (ADP, AMP, and ADO) were degradation products of the extracellular ATP mediated by ecto-enzymes (Fig. 1D). To validate these findings, we further expanded the sample size (Fig. 1A, patients’ clinical characteristics in S1C) and conducted nucleotide extraction for HPLC-MS/MS detection of ATP and its extracellular degradation products (ADP, AMP, ADO). Consistent with the metabolomic data, the levels of ATP, ADP, AMP, and adenosine in the plasma of psoriasis patients were significantly elevated in comparison with healthy controls (Figs. 1E and S1D). Subsequent analysis revealed that only the levels of adenosine showed a statistically significant positive correlation with the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) score, a metric assessing the severity and extent of plaque psoriasis (Figs. 1F and S1E). Therefore, our results indicate that adenosine, a downstream degradation product of the nucleotide ATP, may play a pivotal role in psoriasis.

CD73 catalyzes the generation of adenosine and is implicated in the pathogenesis of the psoriatic mouse model

As ecto-5’-nucleotidase (CD73) serves as the direct nucleotide hydrolase generating adenosine, we then wanted to investigate its role in mediating psoriasis pathogenesis. We utilized CD73 knockout mice to establish an IMQ-induced skin inflammation mouse model and compared them with wild-type mice. As expected, adenosine levels in plasma increased in response to IMQ, and CD73 knockout effectively attenuated this rise (Fig. 1G). Meanwhile, we observed that a total absence of CD73 expression significantly ameliorated IMQ-induced skin scaling, erythema, and thickening, resulting in a notable decrease in the PASI score. Pathological analyses further demonstrated a substantial improvement in epidermal thickening in CD73 knockout mice following the IMQ challenge (Fig. 1H–J and S1F). Additionally, IL-17A–producing T cells, such as the Th17 subset, were reduced, and the expression levels of inflammatory factors known to be upregulated in psoriasis (Il-17a, Il-23a, Tnf-α, Cxcl1, S100a8) significantly decreased in CD73 knockout mice comparison with wild-type following IMQ exposure (Fig. S1G–H). These data indicate that enzymes involved in extracellular adenosine generation plays an essential role in the pathogenesis of psoriasis.

Psoriasis promotes expansion of a subgroup of CD73high fibroblast

We then sought to investigate the primary source of CD73 in psoriatic skin tissues. CD73 is expressed on the surface of a variety of cell types, including endothelial cells, subtypes of lymphocytes, and stromal cells13. To obtain an unbiased perspective of the CD73 expression pattern, we analyzed single-cell sequencing data of the skin tissue of psoriatic patients from published sources14. Eight major clusters of skin cells were identified, including keratinocytes, fibroblasts, melanocytes, macrophage and DC cells, endothelial cells, T and NK cells, mast cells, as well as smooth muscle cells (Figs. 1K and S2A). While CD73 expression was generally low across all cell types in healthy controls, it was significantly elevated in fibroblasts of psoriatic patients (Fig. 1L). In addition, upstream ectonucleotidases such as CD39/ENTPD1 were expressed in macrophages and endothelial cells, supporting a dermal extracellular ectonucleotidase network in psoriatic skin (Fig. S2B). Analysis of fibroblast subtypes in psoriatic skin revealed that a paired comparison of lesions and perilesional skin for each patient showed a significant increased percentage of CD73high fibroblasts in lesional skin (Fig. 1M). To gain the localization information of CD73 high cells, we performed spatial transcriptomics on 2 normal skin samples and 6 psoriatic skin samples, and mapped the expression of CD73 on spatial-RNAseq samples. The results demonstrate a significant increase in the expression of CD73 in the dermal layer of psoriatic skin lesions (Figs. 1N middle and S2C middle). Combining single-cell computational-based fibrosis scores, we found a significant elevation in a subset of CD73high fibroblasts in the skin lesions of psoriasis patients, primarily located in close proximity to the epidermis (papillary fibroblast) (Figs. 1N right and S2C right). These data were also validated by immunofluorescence staining in psoriatic and normal skin, showing an increased proportion of papillary CD73high fibroblasts beneath the epidermis (Fig. 1O).

Given that CD73 catalyzes adenosine generation by hydrolyzing ATP, we explored whether CD73high fibroblasts undergo metabolic reprogramming to support autocatalysis. To assess the metabolic changes in CD73high fibroblasts, we computed scores for all 76 active metabolic pathways in CD73high and CD73low fibroblasts15. Remarkably, 61 out of 76 pathways were significantly more active in CD73high fibroblasts (Fig. S2D), including the key ATP generation pathways like oxidative phosphorylation, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, and the citrate cycle (Fig. 1P). Meanwhile, to further characterize the functional state of these cells, we assessed fibroblast activation, inflammatory, and senescence markers. CD73high fibroblasts exhibited significantly higher activation scores, a modestly increased inflammatory score, and no change in senescence markers, indicating that they represent a metabolically enhanced, activated fibroblast population rather than a senescent state (Fig. S2E). Verification through oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and glycolytic proton efflux rate (GlycoPER) in sorted CD73high and CD73low fibroblasts (sorting strategy and specificity validation shown in Figs. S2F and S2G from skin tissue confirmed increased oxidative phosphorylation and glycolysis activity in CD73high fibroblasts (Fig. 1Q, R). In line with enhanced glycolytic flux and mitochondrial activity, the overall ATP generation rate in CD73high fibroblasts was significantly elevated, particularly in mitochondrial-ATP production (Fig. 1S). Finally, to directly evaluate the adenosine-generating ability of CD73high fibroblasts, we added exogenous ATP to the culture medium of both CD73high and CD73low fibroblasts. After a 4-hour incubation, we observed a significantly higher adenosine content in the culture medium of CD73high fibroblasts compared to the CD73low fibroblast group (Fig. 1T). In line with the in vitro findings, in vivo supplementation of ATP modestly aggravated IMQ-induced skin inflammation in a CD73-dependent manner (Fig. S3A–C). Conversely, pharmacological inhibition of extracellular nucleotide release using CBX attenuated disease severity (Fig. S3D–F). Together, these results support a role for dysregulated extracellular nucleotide metabolism in psoriasis, acting through the CD73-dependent adenosine pathway. In summary, our results indicate that psoriasis patients harbor an expanded CD73high fibroblast subpopulation exhibiting active glycolysis and mitochondrial activity, thereby supporting increased adenosine production through enhanced ATP generation and catalytic activity.

Fibroblast-specific deletion of CD73 attenuates skin inflammation in the IMQ- and IL-23-induced skin inflammation mouse models

To further validate that CD73 on fibroblasts plays a major role in psoriasis, we generated fibroblast-specific Cd73 knockout mice. Consistent with the results of globally CD73-knocked-out mice, fibroblast-specific Cd73 knockout mice also showed significant phenotypic improvements in the IMQ-induced psoriasis model. This improvement was evident in reduced scales, redness, thickness in the back skin (Fig. 2A–D). Similarly, following IMQ challenge, adenosine levels were significantly increased in Cd73f/f Col1a2 CreER- mice but markedly decreased in the plasma of Cd73f/f Col1a2 CreER+ (cKO) mice (Fig. 2E). Additionally, skin inflammatory cytokines, neutrophils, and IL-17A–producing T cells (including Th17 cells) were significantly reduced compared with Cd73f/f Col1a2 CreER- (Fig. 2F–I). To further strengthen our conclusion, we established a second mouse model of IL-23A-induced psoriasiform inflammation. IL-23A intradermal injection induced ear thickening in Cd73f/f Col1a2 CreER- but not Cd73f/f Col1a2 CreER+ mice (Fig. 2J, K). Additionally, inflammatory cytokine levels were attenuated in the Cd73f/f Col1a2 CreER+ group (Fig. 2L). CD73high fibroblasts promote keratinocyte pro-inflammatory mediator production via the ADORA2B

Next, we investigate the receptors and cell types responsible for adenosine signaling transduction in psoriasis. We analyzed the expression levels of the four adenosine receptors—A1, A2A, A2B, and A3—in psoriatic skin using transcriptome data. Comparison of lesional skin with perilesional/normal skin groups revealed that ADORA2B was the only receptor significantly upregulated in most datasets (Fig. 3A). Additionally, ADORA2B expression levels correlated with the PASI score in patients with psoriasis (Fig. 3B). sc-RNAseq data showed that ADORA2B expression was predominantly located in keratinocytes (Fig. 3C). Consistent with these observations, analysis of primary human keratinocytes showed minimal expression of ADORA1, ADORA2A, and ADORA3, whereas ADORA2B was markedly higher (Fig. 3D). Moreover, pharmacological inhibition experiments demonstrated that only ADORA2B antagonism consistently reduced pro-inflammatory mediators in keratinocytes (Fig. S4A). Furthermore, spatial transcriptomics data confirmed that ADORA2B was primarily distributed in the epidermis layer of psoriatic patients’ skin (Fig. 3E left and middle, and S4B left and middle). To explore this possibility, we developed two in vitro cell systems: a direct co-culture system and an indirect co-culture system via conditioned medium, to further evaluate their interactions (Fig. 3G). Whether directly co-culturing fibroblasts with keratinocytes (Fig. 3H) or stimulating keratinocytes with conditioned medium from fibroblasts (Fig. 3I), both scenarios significantly upregulated the expression of pro-inflammatory mediators in keratinocytes. Pre-treatment of fibroblasts with the CD73 inhibitor APCP or knocking down ADORA2B in keratinocytes markedly reduced keratinocyte pro-inflammatory mediator production. Compared to the sole inhibition of CD73 or ADORA2B, simultaneous application of the CD73 inhibitor in fibroblasts and ADORA2B knockdown in keratinocytes only marginally or did not further decrease the pro-inflammatory mediator’s levels in keratinocytes (Fig. 3H, I, S4D-F showed the efficiency and specificity of ADORA2B knockdown). Those results indicate that fibroblasts generate soluble factors via CD73 on their cell surface, which act on ADORA2B in keratinocytes, resulting in pro-inflammatory effects. To explore whether this soluble factor is adenosine, we applied adenosine deaminase (ADA), an enzyme that degrades adenosine, to both the co-culture and the conditioned medium system (Fig. 3J). The results showed that ADA significantly reduced fibroblast-induced production of pro-inflammatory mediators in keratinocytes (Fig. 3K, L). Correspondingly, detection of adenosine levels in the supernatant revealed that co-culture of fibroblasts and keratinocytes significantly increased adenosine levels, while ADA almost completely depleted it (Fig. 3M). Finally, we cocultured CD73high and CD73low fibroblasts with keratinocytes, CD73high fibroblast generated more adenosine in the supernatant and strongly amplified the inflammatory response of keratinocytes. In contrast, CD73low fibroblast produced relatively less adenosine and exhibited a weaker pro-inflammatory effect (Fig. 3N, O). Together, our data reveal that fibroblasts generate adenosine via the cell surface CD73, which then acts on the A2B receptors present in keratinocytes, resulting in pro-inflammatory effects.

ADORA2B activation induces mitochondrial dysfunction in keratinocytes

Our results prompted us to investigate how adenosine stimulates the production of pro-inflammatory mediators in keratinocytes following the activation of the ADORA2B. To ensure specificity, we first confirmed that adenosine analogue NECA or the key psoriasis inflammatory stimulus IL-17A did not alter ADORA2A or ADORA2B expression (Fig. S5A), and that ADORA2B siRNA selectively reduced ADORA2B without affecting other adenosine receptors (Fig. S4D–F). Keratinocyte stimulation with NECA, with or without IL-17a, induced pro-inflammatory mediators production, which was effectively abolished by ADORA2B knockdown (Fig. S5B). Meanwhile, we stimulated keratinocytes with the ADORA2B agonist Bay 60-6583, with or without IL-17A, it exhibited a robust proinflammatory effect (Fig. 4A). To investigate downstream signaling pathways regulated by ADORA2B activation, we conducted global gene expression analysis using RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq). By analyzing differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in response to ADORA2B activator and ADORA2B knockdown under immune stimulation, we identified several signal pathways significantly enriched in both groups. These pathways are closely related to oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and oxidative stress, which suggests alterations in mitochondrial function (Fig. 4B). To assess the impact of ADORA2B activation on mitochondrial function, we measured mitochondrial respiration in keratinocytes under ADORA2B activation. Oxygen consumption rates (OCRs), as a readout of mitochondrial respiration, were significantly reduced in the Bay 60-6583-treated group compared to the control group. This reduction included decreased basal respiration as well as spare respiratory capacity (Fig. 4C). The mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm), reflecting impaired mitochondrial function, was also reduced after Bay 60-6583 treatment, as indicated by potentiometric dyes TMRM and JC-1 (Fig. 4D, E). Noteworthy, the impaired mitochondrial respiration after ADORA2B activation was not due to decreased numbers of mitochondria (Fig. S5C). This led us to explore changes in mitochondrial morphology, as the shape and cristae structure can impact its function. Examination of mitochondrial morphology by transmission electron microscopy revealed that normal keratinocytes had dense cristae structures, whereas the majority of ADORA2B agonist-treated cells exhibited loose cristae and appeared swollen (Fig. 4F, G). To further ascertain whether mitochondrial dysfunction induced by adenosine stimulation in keratinocytes primarily occurs via ADORA2B rather than other adenosine receptors, we conducted further experiments using NECA to stimulate cells with or without ADORA2B knockdown. The results showed that adenosine stimulation induces mitochondrial dysfunction in keratinocytes. However, when the ADORA2B was knocked down, NECA stimulation did not further alter mitochondrial function (Fig. S5E–H), indicating that the ADORA2B serves as the primary responsive receptor for adenosine-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction in keratinocytes.

A crucial role of mitochondria is the production of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mROS). Impaired mitochondrial function leads to the accumulation of mROS, subsequently triggering cellular oxidative stress and inflammatory responses. Indeed, ADORA2B activation significantly increased mitochondrial ROS in keratinocytes (Figs. 4H and S5D). Vice versa, ADORA2B silencing attenuated NECA-induced mROS (Fig. S5I–J). To assess whether ADORA2B activation induces the production of pro-inflammatory mediators in keratinocytes through mitochondrial dysfunction mediated mROS accumulation, we utilized the mROS scavenger Mito-Tempo. The results revealed that mROS is the primary contributor to the increased inflammatory response induced by ADORA2B agonists, as co-administration of Mito-Tempo almost completely alleviated this effect (Fig. 4I).

We further validated this in IMQ-induced skin inflammation animal model. We found that ADORA2B agonists can exacerbate the dermatitis, while Mito-Tempo can improve the phenotype. Furthermore, combining Mito-Tempo with ADORA2B agonists can suppress the worsening of the mouse phenotype, as indicated by similar levels of skin thickness, scales, and redness in the Mito-Tempo group and the Bay 60-6583+ Mito-Tempo group (Fig. 4J–M). Taken together, our data indicate that adenosine induces the production of pro-inflammatory mediators in keratinocytes through ADORA2B-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress.

First, we found that ADORA2B activation increased both the transcriptome and protein levels of HO-1 in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5D–F). ADORA2B is G protein-coupled, and its activation leads to an increase in intracellular secondary messengers. Our lab and others have demonstrated its downstream transcriptional effects through cAMP-dependent protein kinase A (PKA)-dependent activation of the transcription factor cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB)16–18. To this end, we assessed the phosphorylation of CREB after ADORA2B agonist treatment. Our results showed that ADORA2B activation significantly increased the phosphorylation of CREB, and knockdown of CREB significantly downregulated Bay 60-6583-induced RNA and protein levels of HO-1 (Fig. 5G–I). To determine whether p-CREB binds to the HO-1 promoter, we analyzed the HO-1 promoter using the UCSC genome website and JASPAR database, identifying a potential p-CREB binding site. Utilizing a luciferase reporter gene driven by a 600 bp HO-1 promoter region (−500 ~ + 100 bp), we confirmed that Bay 60-6583 significantly activates HO-1 promoter activity (Fig. 5J). The direct binding of p-CREB to the promoter of HO-1 was also determined by chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays, the putative p-CREB-binding site (19 to 30 bp) exhibited a significant enrichment after immunoprecipitation with an anti-p-CREB antibody, and the addition of Bay 60-6583 further increase the enrichment abundance (Fig. 5K). In summary, our results indicate that ADORA2B activation induces CREB phosphorylation, and p-CREB enhances HO-1 transcription by binding to its promoter.

ADORA2B activation inducing mitochondrial dysfunction via HO-1

Next, we explore whether and how ADORA2B mediates mitochondrial dysfunction in keratinocytes through HO-1. Mitochondrially localized HO-1 can regulate mitochondrial function by reducing the concentration of mitochondrial heme, which is essential for the function and stability of the mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC)19–22. Therefore, we first explore whether HO-1 is located in mitochondria of keratinocytes. Cell fractionation and immunofluorescence results showed high levels of HO-1 located at mitochondria of keratinocytes, and ADORA2B activation led to an increase in HO-1 in the mitochondria (Figs. 5L, M and S6A). Next, we assayed the heme concentration, Bay 60-6583 treatment indeed reduced the concentration of cellular heme, and suppression of HO-1 largely normalized heme content in keratinocytes (Fig. 5N). Consistent with heme concentration, siRNA against HO-1 restored the NAD + /NADH ratio, which reflects the ETC activity, to the normal range in Bay 60-6583 treated group (Fig. 5O). Finally, we evaluated the effect of HO-1 suppression on ADORA2B activation induced mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress. As expected, the inhibition of HO-1 didn’t affect mitochondrial quantity but restored mitochondrial respiration, Δψm, mROS, and alleviated inflammation in ADORA2B agonist-treated keratinocytes (Figs. 5P–U, and S6B, C). To validate these findings in patients, immunofluorescence staining of psoriatic skin sections revealed a significantly increased number of HO-1⁺ ADORA2B⁺ double-positive keratinocytes in patients compared with healthy controls (Fig. S6D). Together, our results demonstrate that ADORA2B activation induces mitochondrial dysfunction through HO-1.

Discussion

The metabolic network plays a crucial role in the tissue microenvironment, yet most studies on the cutaneous EIME have focused on immune mediators such as cytokines and chemokines. The impact of specific metabolites on signaling pathways within the EIME remains underexplored. In this study, through in-depth metabolomics assessment, we highlight a significant enrichment of nucleotide metabolism in psoriasis patients, particularly noting the elevated levels of adenosine, which correlate with disease severity. Furthermore, we demonstrate that adenosine acts as a critical mediator, promoting the interplay between keratinocytes and fibroblasts and shaping the epithelial immune microenvironment in psoriasis.

As an exceptionally heterogeneous cell type, fibroblasts exhibit various subtypes with significant variability in phenotype and function23–26. Honglin et al. reported that Tnc+ fibroblasts promote aberrant neurite outgrowth and psoriasiform skin inflammation by secreting the extracellular matrix protein tenascin-C27. Gudjonsson et al. demonstrated that the SFRP2+ fibroblast subset influences myeloid cells, dendritic cells, neutrophils, Tc17 cells, and keratinocytes in psoriasis by producing chemokines5. In the current study, we show that psoriasis expands a subtype of CD73high fibroblast, which exhibits significantly more active aerobic glycolysis and mitochondrial respiratory activities, thereby enhancing the production and release of ATP. The released ATP further degraded by themself though the highly expressed ecto-5’-nucleotidase CD73 on the cell membrane and promote the production of large amounts of adenosine in the extracellular environment for the signaling transduction to keratinocytes. With the development of the chimeric antigen receptor T against antigen-specific fibroblasts, the identification of CD73high fibroblast subpopulations functioning as pathogenic cell subpopulations boost in psoriatic skin may offer strategies for the precisely targeting the deleterious fibroblast subtypes without perturbing the hemostasis of skin28.

Adenosine signaling acts as a metabolic–immune modulator, exhibiting effects on inflammation, tissue repair, or cellular activation that vary with cell type, receptor usage, and microenvironmental context. In psoriasis, prior studies in keratinocytes have shown that A2A activation promotes proliferation, whereas A2B activation induces cell-cycle arrest, illustrating receptor-specific functional divergence29. Immunologically, adenosine can suppress acute inflammation but promote chronic inflammatory remodeling through distinct receptor pathways30. Consistent with previous reports that ADORA2B shape proinflammatory phenotype in several cell types, such as dendritic cell31, vascular cell32 and pulmonary cell33, our data identify a proinflammatory role for adenosine in chronic skin inflammation through ADORA2B activation in keratinocytes. As CD73 generates extracellular adenosine, our data demonstrate a protective role in a psoriasis-like mouse model. However, global CD73 deficiency has also been reported to exacerbate IMQ-induced skin inflammation34, which may reflect context-dependent effects influenced by factors such as mouse background, IMQ protocol, microbiota composition, or reagent-specific effects—for example, A2A/A2B agonists can act anti-inflammatory in keratinocyte independent of adenosine receptors but via membrane phosphatase activation29. These findings underscore the complexity of adenosine signaling and the need for caution in its study.

Translational, the ADORA2B antagonist and CD73 inhibitors, including small molecule compounds and monoclonal antibodies, are currently being evaluated in clinical trials for tumor immunotherapy35,36. In our IMQ-induced mouse model, pharmacological inhibition or genetic deletion of ADORA2B or CD73 each significantly attenuated skin inflammation, with a comparable to IL-17A blockade. Interestingly, combining these interventions did not further enhance efficacy, suggesting that CD73 and ADORA2B may operate along the same pathogenic axis in psoriasis. This contrasts with observations in tumor models, where dual blockade can produce synergistic effects, highlighting context-dependent roles of this pathway37. These findings support a therapeutic strategy targeting both CD73 and ADORA2B to modulate fibroblast-keratinocyte crosstalk in inflammatory diseases, though IMQ itself may act via adenosine receptors, potentially affecting the observed outcomes38.

Mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress are widely acknowledged as pivotal factors in the pathophysiology of psoriasis39,40. However, the current understanding of the primary molecular modulators of oxidative stress and the signaling pathways maintaining oxidative stress status remains limited in psoriasis. This study addresses this knowledge gap by revealing that adenosine-ADORA2B-p-CREB induced HO-1 expression serves as an essential mediator implicated in mitochondrial dysfunction and the oxidative stress status in keratinocytes. Consistent with other studies reported HO-1’s pro-inflammatory functions and its association with detrimental outcomes in diseases such as cancer, obesity, and chronic infection19,20,41–51, our study indicates that a large amount of ADORA2B activation-induced HO-1 is located in the mitochondria of keratinocytes, associated with significant variations in the mitochondrial heme pool, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress. However, the underlying mechanism of HO-1 enriched in mitochondria remains to be further investigated.

In summary, our study revealed a metabolic disturbance centered on adenosine in nucleotide metabolism in psoriasis. Through sc-RNAseq, spatial transcriptomics, and in vivo and in vitro experiments, we identified CD73high fibroblasts as a pivotal subset that catalyzes extracellular adenosine production and mediates crosstalk with keratinocytes, inducing skin inflammation. Together, our study unveils an interaction between fibroblasts and keratinocytes through metabolites in shaping the EIME during the pathogenesis of psoriasis, providing potential therapeutic targets and pathways for treating inflammatory skin diseases.

Methods

Human samples

Skin and blood samples were obtained from patients with psoriasis and healthy controls. Inclusion criteria for psoriatic patients included newly diagnosed and untreated, without any concurrent inflammatory skin diseases. Both psoriatic patients and healthy control subjects were provided written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were the use of subcutaneous and intravenous systemic immunosuppressive drugs. Clinical assessment included evaluation of psoriasis subtype and determination of the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) score.

For blood preparation, samples were collected in tubes containing EDTA, followed by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 10 minutes at 4 °C within 1 hour. Subsequently, plasma aliquots were stored at −80 °C, either with (for adenosine detection) or without (for metabolomic assay) 10 μM pentostatin (adenosine deaminase inhibitor, Tocris) and 10 μm dipyridamole (an inhibitor of equilibrative nucleoside transportors, Selleck), until analysis.

All experiments were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients, and the study received approval from the ethics review committees of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (No. 202308636).

Metabolomic analyses

We performed the 600MRM analysis (Biotree, Shanghai, China) with UHPLC-QTRAP-MS/MS. For metabolite extraction, 250 μl of ddH2O was added to 50 μl of plasma, followed by the addition of 1200 μl of a methanol: acetonitrile (1:1) solution. After thorough vortex, the sample was ultrasonicated in an ice-water bath for 15 minutes and incubated at −4 °C for 2 hours. The sample was then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm, 4 °C for 15 minutes, and the supernatant was transferred into a new EP tube, which was then concentrated by centrifugation (8 hours) until dry. Subsequently, the dried powder was dissolved in 120 μl of acetonitrile: water (6:4, v: v) and ultrasonicated in an ice-water bath for 30 seconds. Centrifuge at 12,000 rpm, 4 °C for 15 minutes. Carefully transfer the supernatant for analysis. A mixture of standard solutions was served as QC samples. A series of calibration standard solutions were then prepared by stepwise dilution of this mixed standard solution.

ACQUITY UPLC H-Class (Waters) ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system was used for the chromatographic separation of target compounds (in total 600 metabolites) on the Waters Atlantis Premier BEH ZHILIC Column (1.7 μm, 2.1 mm × 150 mm). The mobile phase A consisted of ultrapure water and acetonitrile in an 8:2 ratio, with 10 mmol/L ammonium formate. Mobile phase B comprised acetonitrile and ultrapure water in a 9:1 ratio, with 10 mmol/L ammonium formate. The pH of both phases was adjusted to 9 with ammonium hydroxide. The sample tray temperature was set at 8 °C, and the injection volume was 1 μL. SCIEX 6500 QTRAP+ triple quadrupole mass spectrometer equipped with the IonDrive Turbo V ESI ion source was employed for mass spectrometric analysis using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode. The ion source parameters were set as follows: Curtain Gas = 35 psi, IonSpray Voltage = +5000 V/-4500V, Temperature = 500 °C, Ion Source Gas 1 = 50 psi, Ion Source Gas 2 = 50 psi. All mass spectrometry data acquisition and quantitative analysis of target compounds in this project were conducted using SCIEX Analyst Work Station Software (1.7.2) and Data Driven Flow (v-1.0.1).

Adenosine/ATP/ADP/AMP quantification

The concentration of plasma adenosine/ATP/ADP/AMP and cell supernatants adenosine were measured using HPLC/MS/MS. In brief, 100 μl of plasma was utilized to extract nucleotides by adding 0.6 M cold perchloric acid. After centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 10 minutes, 142 μl of the supernatant was sequentially neutralized by 3 M KHCO3/3.6 N KOH. Subsequently, 1.425 μl of 1.8 M ammonium dihydrogen phosphate (pH 5.1) and 3.3 μl of phosphoric acid (30%) were added. The sample was then centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 10 minutes, and the supernatant was used for HPLC/MS/MS analysis (6470 triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer; Agilent Technologies). For cell supernatants, 300 μL of 80% methanol was added to 100 μL of supernatants. The samples were vortexed for 30 seconds and then incubated on ice for 5 minutes. After centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 20 minutes, 350 μL of supernatant was collected, and water was added to adjust the methanol content to 53%. Finally, the supernatant was used for HPLC/MS/MS analysis (6470 triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer; Agilent Technologies).

Chromatographic analyses were conducted using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) equipment, consisting of Agilent 1290 infinity UHPLC high-pressure binary gradient pump. Separations were performed using a 4.6 × 50 mm BEH C18 column with a particle size of 2.5 μm (Waters, XBridge Premier, MA, USA). The mobile phase consisted of (X) water with 0.3% formic acid and (Y) acetonitrile. The binary gradient elution (X: Y proportion, v/v), at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min, comprised 97:3 from 0 to 5 min; switching to 50:50 from 5 to 7 min; maintained for 10 min. This system was coupled to a mass spectrometer with triple-quadrupole mass analyzers and an electrospray interface, operating in positive mode (ESI+). The temperatures of the source block were set at 100 °C, and desolvation gas was set at 350 °C. Ion detection was performed in the multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode, monitoring the transitions of the m/z 268 precursor ion to the m/z 136 product for adenosine (268.1– > 119.0), 508.0– > 410.0 for ATP, 428.1– > 348.1 for ADP, and 348.1– > 97.0 for AMP. Raw data files generated by LS/MS/MS were processed with MassHunter profile software (Agilent Technologies). The standard curve was constructed using a serial dilution of adenosine (3624/50; Tocris Bioscience), ATP (HY-B2176; Express), ADP (A801493; MACKLIN), and AMP (A59067; Innochem).

Experimental animals

All mice were maintained within the core animal facility, and housed under a 12 h light/dark cycle with free access to standard rodent chow and water under specific pathogen-free condition. Mice were maintained under specific-pathogen-free conditions. All experimental procedures involved were performed according to protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (No. 2022020480).

Six to eight-week-old C57BL/6 female WT and BALB/c WT mice were purchased from SLAC, Changsha. Nt5e KO mice were a kind gift from Dr. Yang Xia (University of Texas Health Science Center at Houston17,52). Nt5e-Flox mice (Cat. NO. NM-CKO-210057) and Adora2b-Flox (Cat. NO. NM-CKO-210055) mice were developed on a C57BL/6 J background and obtained from the Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. Homologous recombination principles were applied to introduce flox modifications to the Nt5e and Adora2b genes using a method involving homologous recombination in fertilized eggs. In brief, Cas9 mRNA and gRNA were obtained through in vitro transcription. A donor vector for homologous recombination was constructed using In-Fusion cloning, comprising a 3.0 kb 5’ homologous arm, a 0.7 kb (Nt5e) and 1.5 kb (Adora2b) flox region, and a 3.0 kb 3’ homologous arm. Cas9 mRNA, gRNA (Supplemental Table 2), and the donor vector were microinjected into fertilized eggs of C57BL/6 J mice to generate mice of the F0 generation. Nt5ef/f Col1a2 CreER and Adora2bf/f K14 Cre mice were obtained by crossing the Nt5ef/f mice with Col1a2 CreER (Cat. No. C001248, Caygen) mice and Adora2bf/f mice with K14 Cre (Cat. No. NM-KI-190024, Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc.) mice, respectively. Nt5ef/f and Adora2bf/f mice were used as control. The genotype of all mice was determined through genotype identification. To induce Cre expression in Nt5ef/f Col1a2 CreER mice, Nt5ef/f Col1a2 CreER mice and Nt5ef/f control mice were injected intraperitoneally with tamoxifen at a dose of 75 mg/kg/day for 5 consecutive days.

Skin inflammation mouse models

To establish IMQ-induced psoriasiform dermatitis, the mice were shaved and topically applied with 62.5 mg of IMQ cream (5%) (Cat# 120503, Sichuan Mingxin Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.) on the shaved back skin for 5 consecutive days. For treatment with the pannexin-channel inhibitor carbenoxolone (CBX), intraperitoneal administration of 10 mg/kg CBX (Cat# HY-B1588, MedChemExpress) was performed once a day during the application of IMQ in mice. For treatment with ATP (Cat#HY-B0345A, MedChemExpress), each mouse was injected with 1 mg ATP in a solution of DPBS which was corrected to a PH6.2 with 1 M NaOH. For treatment with the mito-ROS scavenger mito-Tempo and ADORA2B agonist Bay-60-6583, intraperitoneal administration of 5 mg/kg mito-Tempo (Cat# HY-112879, MedChemExpress) and/or 10 mg/kg Bay60-6538 (Cat# 4472, Tocris Bioscience) was performed once a day during the application of IMQ in mice. For treatment with the CD73 inhibitor APCP and ADORA2B inhibitor PSB1115, intraperitoneal administration of 10 mg/kg APCP (Cat# 3633, Tocris Bioscience) or/and 10 mg/kg PSB1115 (Cat# 2009, Tocris Bioscience) was conducted once a day during the application of IMQ in mice. For treatment with the IL-17A antibody, intraperitoneal administration of 100 μg/mouse aIL-17A (Cat# A2120, Selleck) was conducted every two other days during the application of IMQ in mice. PASI scores were evaluated based on the severity of thickness, erythema, and scales (0, none; 1, mild; 2, moderate; 3, marked; 4, severe). Daily information on body weight and PASI scores were recorded.

To establish IL-23-induced psoriasiform dermatitis, mice were injected intradermally with 25 μL PBS containing 500 ng recombinant mouse IL-23a (Cat# HY-P70709, MedChemExpress) in the right ear every other day for 16 days. For treatment with the CD73 inhibitor APCP and ADORA2B inhibitor PSB1115, intraperitoneal administration of 10 mg/kg APCP (Cat# 3633, Tocris Bioscience) or 10 mg/kg PSB1115 (Cat# 2009, Tocris Bioscience) was conducted once a day during the application of IL-23.

Primary cell culture

The keratinocytes and fibroblasts used in this study were human primary keratinocytes and human primary fibroblasts isolated from normal foreskin tissues donated by circumcision patients (informed consent was obtained). Foreskin was incubated in 2 mg/ml dispase II (D4693, Sigma Aldrich) at 4 °C overnight. The epidermis and dermis were gently separated and washed using cold DPBS. For the isolation of human primary keratinocytes, the epidermis was digested with trypsin at 37 °C for 4 min. The digestion was neutralized with DMEM medium containing 10% FBS. After being washed twice with DPBS, the cells were re-suspended in KGM Keratinocyte Growth Medium BulletKit (192060, Lonza) and cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO2 on collagen I (A10483-01, Gibco) -coated plates.

For the isolation of human primary fibroblasts, the dermis was cut into pieces and digested in 3 mg/ml Collagenase Ⅳ (Sigma#V900893) at 37 °C for 2 hours, followed by filtration through 40 μM cell strainers (BD Falcon#352340). After being washed twice with DPBS, the cells were re-suspended in DMEM/F-12 (1:1) (CGM105.05; CellMax) containing 10% FBS and 25 mM HEPES buffer (15630080; Gibco). Cells from the third to eighth passages were used for subsequent experiments. Flow-cytometric validation confirmed that the isolated cultures consisted predominantly of fibroblasts (Fig. S2H, >95% CD45−CD31−vimentin⁺), with negligible immune or endothelial contamination.

Cell treatment

For co-culture cell studies, 3.5 × 105 primary human keratinocytes were seeded into a 12-well plate and gene knockdown was performed using si-ADRA2B or a negative control. Subsequently, 1.5 × 105 primary human fibroblasts, either pretreated or not with 100 μM APCP, were added and co-cultured with keratinocytes for 24 hours.

In the condition medium treatment study, fibroblasts were treated with or without 100 μM APCP for 6 hours. The supernatant was then collected for treating keratinocytes for an additional 24 hours.

For ADA treatment study, under direct contact conditions, 3.5 × 105 primary human keratinocytes were seeded overnight in a 12-well plate, followed by the addition of 1.5 × 105 primary human fibroblasts and 4 U/ml ADA for 6 hours. Under condition medium treatment conditions, the culture medium from fibroblasts was collected and treated with or without ADA (4 U/ml) for 1 hour. Then, the medium was added to the seeded keratinocytes for 6 hours.

In other keratinocyte studies, Bay60-6583 was added to the cell culture medium at concentrations of 5 μM or 10 μM for 24 hours. NECA (35920-39-9, Sigma) was added to the cell culture medium at a concentration of 10 μM for 24 hours. DPCPX (HY-100937, MedChemExpress) was added to the cell culture medium at a concentration of 50 μM for 24 hours. PSB1115 (HY-103182, MedChemExpress) and MRS 1523 (HY-121119, MedChemExpress) were added to the cell culture medium at a concentration of 10 μM for 24 hours. Mito-Tempo was added to the cell culture medium at a concentration of 10 μM for 24 hours.

Quantitative RT-PCR

RNA was extracted using MagZol reagent (R4801-01; Magen). After quantifying the RNA with a nucleic acid concentration analyzer (NanoDrop, ThermoFisher Scientific), cDNAs were synthesized using reverse transcriptase and HiScript Q RT SuperMix (ThermoFisher Scientific, #K1622). Real-time quantitative PCR was conducted to assess the expression of target molecules. Evaluation of mRNA expression utilized SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (B21703; Bimake), with β-Actin employed as an internal control. The standard 2-ΔΔCt method was applied to determine the relative gene expression compared to the internal control. The primer sequences were listed in Supplemental Table 3.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed using RIPA lysis buffer (P0013C; Beyotime) containing protease and phosphorylated protease inhibitors. Specifically, mitochondrial and cytoplasmic proteins were isolated using the Mitochondria Isolation Kit (HY-K1060; MedChemExpress). Subsequently, 10 to 30 μg of protein per well was separated by 10%-12% SDS-polyacrylamide gels. The proteins were then transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% BSA or 5% milk, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against ADORA2B (YT0131; ImmunoWay); HMOX1 (10701-1-AP; Proteintech Group); Phospho-CREB (9198; Cell Signaling Technology). Following TBST washes, goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (ABclonal, AS003) or goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (ABclonal, AS014) was applied. Immunoreactions were visualized using an NcmECL Ultra kit (P10300; NCM Biotech). Image acquisition and intensity quantification were performed using the Odyssey® Fc Imaging System (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA).

Flow cytometry

For flow cytometry analysis of mouse skin cells, mouse skin was digested with dispase (2 mg/ml), Collagenase IV (3 mg/ml), and DNase I at 37 °C for 2 hours with gentle shaking at 200 rpm. Digested tissues were then filtered through 40 μM cell strainers (BD Falcon#352340). Single-cell suspensions were washed twice with PBS buffer. After stimulation with a cell stimulation cocktail (plus protein transport inhibitors) (00-4975-03; eBioscience) for 5 hours to induce cytokine production for subsequent intracellular detection, cells were stained with Zombie AquaTM Fixable Viability Kit (423102; Biolegend; 1:500) to identify cell viability, followed by staining with antibodies against the following: PerCP/Cyanine5.5 anti-mouse CD4 (100434; Biolegend; 1:100)/BB700 Rat Anti-Mouse CD4 RM4-5 (566407, BD Biosciences; 1:100), APC/Cyanine7 anti-mouse CD45 (103116; Biolegend; 1:100), APC anti-mouse CD3 (100236; Biolegend; 1:100), Brilliant Violet 605™ anti-mouse TCR γ/δ (118129, Biolegend; 1:100), and BV711 Rat Anti-Mouse Ly6G (563979; BD Biosciences; 1:100). For further intracellular staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized using the Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (00-5523-00; eBioscience) for 30 min at 4 °C, then stained with intracellular PE anti-mouse IL-17A (506904; Biolegend; 1:100). Stained cells were analyzed using the FACS LSRFortessa X20 flow cytometry system (BD Biosciences), and the data were processed using Flow Jo Software (version 10.4).

For CD73high and CD73low fibroblast sorting, fibroblasts were stained with antibody against FITC-anti-human CD73(344016; Biolegend). The sorting was performed using a BD FACSymphony S6 or BD FACSMelody system. The sorting strategy is shown in Fig S2F.

Public dataset collection and analysis

Gene expression microarray or RNA-seq data of psoriasis and its cohort information were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (accession numbers: GSE67785, GSE26866, GSE11903, GSE51440, GSE14905, GSE85034, GSE6710, GSE41662, GSE117239, GSE30999, GSE69967, GSE106992, GSE41663, GSE136757, GSE67853, GSE34248, GSE54456, GSE79704, GSE53431, GSE83582, GSE52471). The data were analyzed using R (version 4.2.1) and R Bioconductor packages.

Single-cell RNA-sequencing analysis

Data from single-cell RNA-sequencing were obtained from the GEO database (GEO number: GSE173706). The R package Seurat (v4.3.0) was used to analyze and visualize the entire single-cell RNA sequencing data. Briefly, only cells with >200 and <6000 detected genes, of which <25% were mitochondrial genes, were kept for subsequent analysis. Data normalization, feature selection, dimensionality reduction, and automated cell clustering were performed stepwise. The expression count data for genes were normalized as log10 (Counts Per Million) (CPM) values. Clustered cells were annotated by matching cell cluster gene signatures with cell-type specific markers. High-NT5E cells were defined as cells with counts >0. The metabolic profiles were evaluated between different groups using the R package scMetabolism15 by calculating pathway scores with default settings.

Visium CytAssist spatial transcriptome sequencing

Freshly collected healthy skin (2 samples) and psoriatic skin (6 samples) tissues were divided into blocks of appropriate size and immediately fixed in 4% FPA for paraffin embedding. The sections were attached to anti-slide slides according to 10x Genomics Protocol. Deparaffinization, HE staining, decrosslinking of the sections were performed according to 10x protocol (CG000520). Probe hybridization, probe release and library construction were performed using the Visium CytAssist Spatial Gene Expression for FFPE kit from 10x Genomics (PN-1000520 for Human, 6.5 mm) according to 10x protocol (CG000495). The DNA libraries were subjected to high-throughput sequencing with the PE-150 model. Space Ranger software (10x Genomics) was used to process sequencing output and the histology images. The reads were aligned to the human genome (hg38). Seurat was then used to analyze the expression matrix, and SCTransform function was used to scale the expression data. Robust Cell Type Decomposition (RCTD) was used to identify cell composition in all spots of ST data based on single-cell RNA-seq data (GSE173706). The high/low groups of NT5E expression and fibroblast were defined through Jenks Natural Breaks classification. Spatial colocalization is determined by Chi-square test (P < 0.05).

Bulk RNA sequencing and data processing

Total RNA was extracted using an RNA kit. Quality control, library preparation, and sequencing were performed by Novogene Company. The RNA library was generated using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA, Catalog #: E7530L) following the manufacturer’s recommendations and sequenced on a Nova 6000 platform (Illumina) to generate paired-end 150 bp reads. Raw data analysis was performed as previously described54. Briefly, the quality control of the sequencing reads from each sample was assessed with FastQC. Human reference cDNA sequences were downloaded from the Ensembl database (GRCh38 https://ftp.ensembl.org/pub/release-97/fasta/homo_sapiens/cdna/). The reference was indexed with Salmon, and gene expression quantification was performed with Salmon in its nonalignment-based mode. Differential expression analysis was performed with the DEseq2 package in R. The log2 fold change was calculated by default settings with the DESeq2 package. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) are defined as absolute log2 fold change > 0.2 in expression level and false discovery rate <0.05. Gene Ontology pathway enrichment analyses were performed with the cluster Profiler R package.

Metabolomic profiling

The collected plasma sample were handed over to Biotree Company for metabolomic profiling. LS-MS/MS was performed the 600MRM analysis (Biotree, Shanghai, China). Raw data files generated by LC-MS/MS were processed with SCIEX Analyst Work Station Software (1.7.2), metabolites quantification was analyzed with Data Driven Flow (v-1.0.1). Metabolites were considered to have significant differences between the psoriasis and healthy groups if absolute log2 fold change > 0.3 and adjust p < 0.05. The DA score was performed with the ggplot2 package in R. The components of the metabolic pathways used in the analysis were annotated using the KEGG database.

Cell transfections

Human primary keratinocytes at 80% confluence were transfected using Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX (13778150; ThermoFisher Scientific) in Keratinocyte Growth Medium for 48 hours, following the manufacturer’s protocol. The transfection efficiency was assessed at both the protein and mRNA levels.

Seahorse metabolic assay

Sorted fibroblasts or keratinocytes (2 × 104) were seeded into XFe96 cell culture microplates (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). On the testing day, cells were rinsed and pre-incubated with XF RPMI medium supplemented with 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 2 mM L-glutamine, and 10 mM glucose without CO2. For the glycolytic rate assay (103344-100; Agilent), 5 μM Rot/AA was added to port A, and 500 mM 2-DG was added to port B following the recommended 10x concentrations. In the XF Cell Mito Stress Test (103015-100; Agilent), 15 μM oligomycin was added to port A, 10 μM FCCP to port B, and 5 μM Rot/AA to port C, all following the recommended 10x concentrations. For the real-time ATP rate assay (103592-100; Agilent), 15 μM oligomycin was added to port A, and 5 μM Rot/AA was added to port B following the recommended 10x concentrations. Automated stimulation was performed as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Seahorse XFp Wave software was employed for data analysis. The real-time ATP rate, OCR, and GlycoPER were normalized by cell counts quantified using the BioTek Cytation C10 system.

Multiplex IHC staining protocol

Opal 7-colour kit (PerkinElmer, NEL811001KT) was used for multiplex IHC. FFPE sections were dewaxed and rehydrated. In first round antigen was retrieved with a microwave (citrate buffer pH 6) at 100–150 mW for 20 min. Slides were cooled to room temperature (RT), washed with TBS (3 times, 5 min). Slides were washed and blocked with blocking buffer (PerkinElmer) for 10 min. Primary antibody Vimentin (ab8978, Abcam,1:400; dye Opal 570) or HMOX1 (10701-1-AP, Proteintech Group, 1:300; dye Opal 520), or ADORA2B (YT0131, ImmunoWay,1:300; Opal 520) was incubated at RT for 1 hour. Slides were washed and an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (PerkinElmer, NEF812001EA) was incubated at RT for 10 min. TSA dye (1:100) was applied for 10 min after washes. This was repeated using the following antibodies, CD73 (ab115289, Abcam, 1:100, dye Opal 690) or TOM20 (ab283317, Abcam,1:300, dye Opal 690), HMOX1 (10701-1-AP, Proteintech Group, 1:300; dye Opal 690). The second rounds antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer (pH 6) buffer using a microwave (100–150 mW, 15 min). Nuclei were stained with DAPI (PerkinElmer) and mounted with medium (Servicebio). The slides were scanned and captured using the Vectra® Polaris™ Automated Quantitative Pathology Imaging System (PerkinElmer) with fluorescent imaging mode.

Immunofluorescence staining

Human primary keratinocytes were seeded on a chamber slide (177402; Thermo Scientific) to allow attachment. Cells were fixed with 100% methanol for 5 min and permeabilized in 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min. After being blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h, cells were incubated with primary antibodies: HMOX1 (10701-1-AP; Proteintech Group), TOM20 (ab283317; Abcam). Then, secondary antibodies (A21206, A21203; Invitrogen) were applied for 1 h at room temperature. DAPI (Abcam) was used to stain nuclei. A commercial structured illumination microscope (HIS-SIM) was used to acquire and reconstruct the cell images. The procedure for Hessian imaging was performed by following the previous report55. A commercial structured illumination microscope (HIS-SIM) was used to acquire and reconstruct the cell images. To further improve the resolution and contrast in reconstructed images, sparse deconvolution was used by following the previous report56.

Transmission electron microscopy

The cell pellets were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde (A17876, Alfa Aesar) in 0.1 M phosphate buffer at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, the samples were sent to the TEM laboratory at the Pathology Department of Xiangya Hospital. In brief, the samples were incubated for 1 hour in 1% osmium tetroxide, followed by three washes at 10-minute intervals with phosphate buffer. After dehydration in a graded series of 50%, 70%, and 90%, 100%, 100% acetone at 10–15 minutes intervals for each step, the samples were soaked and embedded in a 1:1 mixture of acetone and resin for 12 hours. The sample resin solidification process involved polymerization overnight at 37 °C and then for 12 hours at 60 °C. Ultrathin sections of 50- to 100-nm thickness were made with an ultramicrotome and a diamond knife. After double staining with 3% uranyl acetate and lead nitrate, the specimens were examined and photographed using a Hitachi HT-7700 electron microscope. Mitochondrial morphology was scored as normal or abnormal. Abnormal mitochondria were defined as those with a loose cristae structure and a brighter appearance. All mitochondria in images taken at 10kx magnification were scored, and the percentages of abnormal mitochondria were calculated.

Mitochondrial membrane potential

Mitochondrial membrane potential was evaluated by staining cells with tetramethylrhodamine, methyl ester (TMRM) (HY-D0984A; MedChemExpress) and JC-1 Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit (HY-K0601; MedChemExpress).

In brief, for TMRM staining, cells were trypsinzed after treatment and the pellet was resuspended in fresh medium containing 1 μM TMRM, incubated at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark in a CO2 incubator. The medium containing the stain was then removed and the cells were washed twice with D-PBS. The excitation and emission wavelengths for TMRM staining were 550 nm and 570 nm respectively. The fluorescence was measured by flowcytometry (BD LSRFortessa).

For JC-1 staining, 104 human primary keratinocytes per well were seed into black 96-well plates (655090; Greiner). After treatment, cells were incubated with 2 μM JC-1 at 37 °C, 5% CO2, for 15 minutes. The medium containing the stain was then removed and the cells were washed with D-PBS. In healthy cells with high ΔΨm, JC-1 forms complexes known as J-aggregates. While in cells with low ΔΨm, JC-1 remains in the monomeric form. When excited at 510 nm, JC-1 monomers emit a green fluorescence with a maximum at ~527 nm. Aggregates of JC-1 emit an orange-red fluorescence with a maximum at ~590 nm. The fluorescence microplate reader (BioTek Cytation C10; Agilent) was used to detected the aggregates and monomers JC-1 (excitation wave:510 nm or 570 nm).

MitoTracker green detection

Cells were stained with MitoTracker green to assess mitochondrial mass. Keratinocytes were collected by trypsinization and stained with 100 nM of MitoTracker™ Green for 15 min at 37 °C in the dark in a CO2 incubator. The stain containing medium was then removed and the cells were detected by flow cytometry after being washed twice with D-PBS.

Mitochondrial oxidative stress

Cells were stained with MitoSOX™ to assess mitochondrial superoxide generation. Keratinocytes were collected by trypsinization and stained with 2.5 µM of MitoSOX™ for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark in a CO2 incubator. The stain containing medium was then removed and the cells were washed twice with D-PBS. Red fluorescence was measured using a spectrofluorometer (BioTek Cytation C10, Agilent; excitation: 510 nm, emission: 605 nm) or flow cytometry.

Luciferase reporter assays

The HO-1 promoter fragment (600 bp) was synthesized by Sangon Biotech (China) and cloned into the pGL3 vector (Cat# E1751, Promega). Keratinocytes were co-transfected with the HO-1 promoter-reporter and Renilla PRLTK plasmid using TurboFect (R0532, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Twenty-four hours after transfection, Bay60-6538 was added for an additional twenty-four hours. The HO-1 promoter reporter activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Cat# E1960, Promega) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

ChIP assays were performed using the SimpleChIP Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit with Magnetic Beads (Cat# 9003S, Cell Signaling Technology) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Phospho-CREB antibody (9198; Cell Signaling Technology) was used to enrich the protein/DNA complexes. The relative abundance of HMOX1 gene promoter sequences in the chromatin immunoprecipitation reaction was verified by using qPCR with specific primers for the HMOX1 promoter region: forward, 5’-AGTCAACGCCTGCCTCCTCT-3’; reverse, 5’- GGGTTGCGGACGCTCCATC-3’.

NAD+/NADH measurements

A total of 3 × 105 human primary keratinocytes were seeded into a 10 cm dish. The NAD+/NADH levels were assayed using a commercial kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol (ab65348; Abcam).

Heme measurements

Heme content (protoporphyrin IX fluorescence) was measured based on the autofluorescence of protoporphyrin IX after eliminating the heme iron with oxalic acid at 95 °C, following previously published protocols57,58. Specifically, 2 × 106 human primary keratinocytes were collected and resuspended in 200 µL of 1 M oxalic acid, then heated at 95 °C for 30 minutes. After cooling at 4 °C for 5 minutes, the tubes were briefly vortexed and centrifuged for 2 minutes at 21,000 × g. The remaining porphyrin was excited at 400 nm, and emission was measured at 662 nm.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 9 software (GraphPad). Normality of human data pools was tested with the Pearson-D’Agostino normality test. As for nonnormality distribution, Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare 2 groups. For the correlation between PASI scores and adenosine/ATP/ADP/AMP concentration, as well as the correlation between PASI scores and ADORA2B expression in GEO datasets, Spearman rank-order correlation was used due to the non-normal distribution of both datasets. For other in vivo and in vitro studies, when the sample number was too small for normality detection, the one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used to compare multiple groups. An unpaired two-sided t test was used to compare two groups. P value less than 0.05 was considered significant for all experiments.
