A cell and transcriptome atlas of human arterial vasculature
Authors: Quanyi Zhao, Albert Pedroza, Disha Sharma, Wenduo Gu, Alex Dalal, Chad Weldy, William Jackson, Daniel Yuhang Li, Yana Ryan, Trieu Nguyen, Rohan Shad, Brian T Palmisano, João P Monteiro, Matthew Worssam, Alexa Berezowitz, Meghana Iyer, Huitong Shi, Ramendra Kundu, Lasemahang Limbu, Juyong Brian Kim, Anshul Kundaje, Michael Fischbein, Robert Wirka, Thomas Quertermous, Paul Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryArterial segments show differing disease propensities, yet mechanisms remain unknown. We compiled a transcriptomic and spatial atlas of healthy human arterial cells across multiple segments to understand these differences. Arteries demonstrated a stereotyped pattern of cell-specific, segmental heterogeneity not captured by common marker genes. Arterial identities are encoded in fibroblast and smooth muscle cell (SMC) transcriptomes. Differentially expressed genes enrich for disease loci. Fibroblast gene expression enriches for a disproportionate number of disease loci, highlighting an underrecognized role for fibroblasts in disease risk. Cells of different segments cluster more by embryonic origin than anatomy. Global analysis of disease regulons in fibroblasts and SMCs identified developmental transcription factors that persist into adulthood, suggesting a functional role of these factors in disease. Lastly, the heterogeneity of non-coding transcriptomes rivals that of protein-coding transcriptomes. Differentially expressed lncRNAs enrich for genetic signals for vascular diseases, suggesting a role for lncRNAs in vascular disease.Keywords: single-cell, vascular development, vascular cell populations, arterial segments, non-coding RNA, vascular diseases, fibroblast, aneurysm, transcription factors

Introduction

Human vascular diseases are the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide.1 Different segments of the human aorta and its branches have seemingly identical morphology and structures, yet they have distinct propensity for different vascular diseases.2,3,4 Atherosclerotic disease frequently influences coronaries and carotids, while relatively sparing the descending thoracic aorta.5,6 Hereditary aneurysmal diseases frequently affect the aortic root and ascending aorta but not other aortic segments.7,8 Environmental triggers such as tobacco and methamphetamine exposure disproportionally bring on pathologies in the infra-renal segments of the aorta and pulmonary arteries, respectively.9,10 Even inflammatory or infectious aortopathies, such as Takayasu’s vasculitis, Kawasaki’s diseases, or syphilis aortitis, have well documented segmental pref.11,12,13. While the differences in disease propensity are well documented, the underlying causal mechanisms are less clear. Prevalent hypotheses include differential exposure to life-long hemodynamic forces, along with distinct cellular lineages from different embryonic origins.14,15 Understanding the cellular and molecular basis for the differences between these arteries may open the door to therapies for a variety of vascular diseases.

Lineage-tracing data from animal models suggest a distinct pattern of embryonic origins for the different arteries.15,16,17,18 The coronary artery is thought to derive from several sources.19,20 The endothelium from coronary arteries can be traced to two distinct origins, and they instruct the surrounding mesenchyme, which consists of cells originating from the proepicardial organ, to turn into smooth muscle cells (SMCs).21,22 The ascending aorta is made of at least two populations of embryonic progenitor cells, arising from both the second heart field and the neural crest.21,23 The two populations of cells mix in a distinct pattern, ending before the arch, where most SMCs are thought to come from neural crest cells of the 4th pharyngeal arch. The carotid artery arises from the 3rd pharyngeal arch. The pulmonary artery (PA) arises from the 6th pharyngeal arch, the thoracic descending aorta is thought to arise from the somitic mesoderm, and splenic mesoderm thought to be responsible for the iliac and femoral arteries. These distinct developmental origins have been postulated to be causal for risk related to various diseases, as different segments have differential expression of selected genes in bulk RNA isolation.24,25 Transplantation experiments have suggested arterial segments retain the disease propensities of their original site, suggesting that the intrinsic behavior of segments accounts for their disease propensity.26,27 However, whether the same embryological developmental pattern exists in humans is unknown. In addition, the extent to which developmental origin influences cellular gene expression and behavior of adult vessels remains elusive.

To gain insight into these fundamental observations on differential arterial disease risk, we generated a human arterial cellular atlas spanning multiple segments of the arterial circulation.

Results

To generate a baseline human vascular cellular atlas simultaneously from multiple arterial sites from the same person, we collaborated with the organ donor network to collect arterial tissues from organ donors. Up to 9 distinct arterial sites (ascending thoracic aorta [ASC], aortic arch [ARCH], descending thoracic aorta [DESC], infra-renal abdominal aorta [INFRARENAL], iliac artery [ILIAC], coronary artery [RCA], carotid artery [CAROTID], PA, and aortic root [ROOT]) were chosen to be evaluated due to their different embryonic origins and disease propensities. Available segments were collected using identical surgical techniques by a dedicated team of cardiothoracic surgeons to minimalize variability (Figure 1A) and processed identically from 6 different patients. Tissues were dissociated into single cells using an enzymatic digestion optimized to overcome the dense vascular connective tissue (See STAR Methods).28,29,30 Not all sites were collected from each donor as certain segments were retained along with organs utilized for patient care. A total of 187,958 cells were obtained.

The cell type identities were then identified first via unsupervised low-dimensional clustering. To avoid over-clustering, we initiated the clustering using very low resolution. Visualization of the combined scRNA data revealed the presence of all the expected major cell types in human arteries, namely endothelial cells (ECs), SMCs, fibroblasts, microvascular cells, macrophages, and lymphocytes (Figures 1B and 1C and S1A). The largest component of the variance in the dataset came from markers of different cell types, though the data contained a small level of expected inter-person variation (Figure S1B). The resultant data are presented here (Figures 1C and S1C) and will be used as the main dimensional-reduction framework for data visualization.

Cells of the human arteries

We proceeded to identify each specific populations found in our scRNA-seq data. Cellular populations in the vessels are often defined by positive staining of one or two marker genes (i.e., CDH5 for ECs), which relies on the intrinsic assumption of “marker genes” being specific, and can fail to capture unexpected heterogeneity.17,31,32,33,34 This had led to significant debate regarding the presence and identity of certain cell types, such as vascular mesenchymal stem cells, endothelial progenitor cells, adventitial progenitor cells, pericytes, fibromyocytes, modulated SMCs, and fibroblasts.17,28,31,32,33,34,35 Discrete populations of cells should have distinct transcriptomic signatures that can be identified a priori utilizing only gene expression data. Thus, we started with a conservative clustering resolution to identify principal cell types. We then identified cluster specific gene markers and contrasted them with the canonical “cell type markers” classically utilized to identify SMCs, ECs, immune cells, fibroblast, and pericytes (Figure 1B) to contrast the specificity of historical and empirically determined cell type markers. This preliminary analysis demonstrated high fidelity of canonical endothelial (CDH5 and VWF) and immune cell markers (CD3D and CD68) but poor specificity of several frequently utilized “pericyte” (RGS5, CSPG4, and PDGFRB) and “fibroblast” markers (COL1A1, FSP1/S100A4, and PDGFRA), with robust expression seen in multiple populations (Figure S1A). To further confirm the fidelity of different cell clusters, we mapped each clusters spatially with Slideseq (Figures 1D and S1D). Each of these distinct clusters have spatially distinct locations, with fibroblasts being found near exclusively in the adventitia, whereas two clusters expressing canonical SMC markers (MYH11) are naturally divided into two populations that spans intima/media (termed “smooth muscle cells” in Figure 1C) and adventitia (termed “microvascular cells” in Figure 1C). Each of the clusters are represented in all the donor and site samples (Figures S1E and S1F). The relative proportion of each population of cells across the different arterial segments appears mostly constant, with SMCs making up 30%, fibroblasts (20%), endo (16%), microvascular cells (14%), and macrophage (18%; Figure 1E; Table S1). No sites had statistically significantly more of one cell type.

SMCs of great arteries

Histologically, the largest population of cells in the great arteries are SMCs. These cells make up the majority of the arterial wall28,35,36 and accounts for ∼50% (47%–54%) of the cells identified on spatial transcriptomics. While existing scRNA datasets of arteries in the past have demonstrated inflammatory cells being the most numerous populations,28,37,38 our optimized digestion protocol led to SMCs as by far the most numerous cell type, matching histological observations. SMCs were classically identified using the expression of mature smooth muscle contractile apparatus genes such as SMC heavy chain (MYH11) or calponin 1 (CNN1; Figures 2A and 2B). Visualizing the population using several canonical SMC marker genes revealed a continuous gradient of SMC maturity in healthy vessels, as marked by a gradient of expression of each of these “SMC-specific” markers. SMCs exist in a distinct gradient of hierarchical cell states, MYOCD+>CNN1+>MYH11+>ACTA2+>TAGLN+ population (Figure 2A). It is important to note that a subset of commonly applied smooth muscle markers (smooth muscle actin/ACTA2 and transgelin/TAGLN/SM22α) are expressed quite broadly, including very robust expression of ACTA2 and TAGLN in fibroblasts and a subset of ECs. This has implications regarding lineage-tracing models using Tagln or Acta2 loci as the driver for Cre-recombinase lineage labeling and perhaps explains the distinct results obtained using different Cre-recombinase in the existing literature.39

The high depth of sequencing allowed for the identification of two large transcriptomically distinct populations (labeled “SMC” and “microvascular” in Figure 1C). Both populations express canonical SMC markers MYH11/CNN1 (Figure 2A). However, they both consist of subgroup of a more “contractile” population and a more “phenotypically modulated” population whereby the contractile markers are lost. The presence of MYH11-negative cells clustering with smooth muscle populations, rather than fibroblast or other cell types, may indicate phenotypic modulation of SMCs, a process well documented in murine lineage tracing models. Alternatively, it could be a mathematical artifact from gene-expression-based clustering. To distinguish the two possibilities, we leveraged murine scRNAseq data that contained Myh11-Cre lineage tracing28 to identify SMC and non-SMC progenies. Mapping the transcriptomes of these SMC progenies onto the human scRNAseq data revealed that both the “SMC” and “Microvascular” clusters correspond to murine Myh11-lineage traced cells, including those lacking contractile SMC gene expression (Figure S2A). This suggests that cellular lineage relationships may be captured through unbiased clustering methods. We subsequently performed more detailed analysis of the two SMC populations.

Staining for specific contractile SMC marker CNN1 revealed positive cells in both the media and small vessels in adventitia (Figure 2B). We therefore hypothesize that these two populations of SMCs reflect medial (larger cluster) and microvascular SMCs (smaller cluster). To better understand this observed heterogeneity of these two distinct populations of cells expressing canonical “smooth muscle markers,” we performed differentially expressed (DE) gene analysis across these two populations, which revealed 587 significantly DE genes (Figures 2D and S2B; Table S2). Microvascular mural cells/pericytes were classically identified using the expression of markers RGS5 and NG2/CSPG4.33,40 Interestingly, these “canonical pericyte markers” are not confined to one transcriptomically distinct population but rather mark non-overlapping cell populations (Figures 2C and S1A), suggesting that cells previously categorized as pericytes in different studies using these markers may represent a heterogeneous population of cells, including subsets of medial SMCs. Markers more specific to the smaller population of SMCs (microvascular population) include COX4I2, IGFBP4, and OR51E1. Leveraging RNA in situ hybridization and the spatial transcriptome with Slideseq for markers unique for the smaller SMC population confirmed that the smaller population of cells expressing contractile markers resides exclusively in the adventitia (Figures 2F and S2E) and, therefore, represent mural cells of the microvasculature. While these cells do express some of the classical “pericyte” markers, due to the potential imprecise nature of these markers as described above, we will refer to this population of cells as “adventitial microvasculature” cells. While both of these clusters are MYH11+ cells in the vascular wall, these two populations of SMCs have very distinct biology. Pathway analysis demonstrated that these two populations have distinct biological functions (Figures 2E and S2C). For example, these two populations of SMCs from the same segment have distinct patterns of adrenergic receptor expression with alpha adrenergic receptor 2A (ADRA2A; Figure 2F) found only in the microvascular population, whereas ADRA2C is found more in medial SMCs (Figure S2D).

Given the diversity of transcriptome in the microvascular population, we performed further sub-clustering of this population into two distinct clusters. These two clusters express unique markers, including CCL21 and OR51E1 (Figures S2F and S2G; Table S2). Spatially, both of these clusters reside in the adventitia (Figures 2F and S2H). Pathway analysis revealed that these two populations of microvascular cells likely also have distinct function. These data suggest that two or more populations of microvascular cells exist in the adventitia with distinct function.

We then turn our attention to the largest SMC cluster, the most abundant cell type in the arteries that maps to the vascular media and intima. This population of SMCs can be further divided into two subclusters, whereby one subcluster expresses high levels of mature SMC markers, and another subcluster, where the expression of contractile genes such as MYH11 and CNN1 are low/absent (Table S2). To identify where these cells were located, we performed spatial transcriptomic profiling using Slideseq in conjunction with RNAscope for specific markers. Consistent with previous work,28,41 among the most specifically expressed genes in these less contractile SMC subclusters were TNFRSF11B and FN1. These less contractile SMCs localized to the intima (Figures 2G and 2H; S2I). Leveraging existing murine scRNA-seq data with lineage tracing, we found that the less contractile subcluster of SMCs transcriptomically map to “phenotypic modulated SMCs” in Myh11 lineage-tracing models. Previously identified markers for murine-modulated SMCs28 are found in the same intimal cluster (Figure S2J), including rare cells that express “chondrogenic SMC” markers such as IBSP1 and HAPLN1 (Figure S2K). Applying lineage inference tool using pseudotime, a similar de-differentiation timeline can be constructed using human medial/intimal SMCs (Figure S2L). These findings suggest that the majority of the cells that makes up the human healthy arterial intima shares significant transcriptomic similarity to phenotypically modulated SMCs observed in murine models (Figure S2A). The human data, in conjunction with Myh11-cre SMC lineage tracing in murine models,28,41 lend further evidence to the notion that arterial intima, which is present in humans but not mice, is likely derived from smooth muscles, arising from medial SMCs in a process analogous to neointimal formation in murine atherosclerosis and intimal hyperplasia models.

Adventitial fibroblasts in human arteries

The second most abundant population of cells isolated in our dataset are adventitial fibroblasts, which form a continuum in the transcriptomic space with SMCs. This population of cells do not express contractile SMC markers (Figures 1C and 2A) but do express high levels of extracellular matrix components and modifiers (Figure 3A). Marker gene RNAscope studies for this population, along with spatial transcriptomics, localized this population exclusively to the adventitia (Figures 3B and 1D). Based on marker gene expression, we termed this population “vascular adventitial fibroblast” (AdvFib).

Commonly used canonical fibroblast marker genes,31,42 including fibroblast specific protein (FSP1/S100A4), PDGFRA, COL1A1, and GLI1, were expressed relatively diffusely in several different populations of non-fibroblast cells (Figure 3C). This likely explains part of the heterogeneity of results that comes from different fibroblast lineage tracing/manipulation Cre models in murine studies.43,44,45 Using scRNA-seq data, we additionally identified PI16, SFRP2, and MFAP5 as the most specific marker genes for this adventitial fibroblast population (Figures 3B–3D; Table S3). Of note, genes highly expressed specifically in the adventitial fibroblast population were enriched for genes known to cause genetic aortopathies,46,47,48 including FBN1, TGFBR2, LOX, FBLN1, and MFAP5 (Figure 3E). This suggests a potentially prominent role in the aortopathies.

While it has been described that the adventitial fibroblast is a heterogeneous population, they appeared to be relatively homogenous in the transcriptomic space within the same vascular segments. There were additional ECs and other microvascular cells that are in the adventitia, which makes it more heterogeneous than other layers, but the fibroblasts themselves within each segment appeared to be mostly one large continuous population across a spectrum. However, there is significant heterogeneity across the adventitial fibroblasts across different vascular beds (Figure S3A). Focusing on the transcriptomic heterogeneity across AdvFib, three distinct clusters highlighted by different biological processes can be found (Figures S3B and S3C). Pathway analysis revealed particular sensitivity of each of these clusters to different signaling pathway (Figure S3D). One cluster has enrichment in fibroblast growth factor and pro-migration signal (we termed “FGF-avid”), another has higher TGFB pathway and ossification signals (we termed “TGFB-avid”), and the third has enrichment for BMP signals and microfilaments (we termed “BMP-avid”) cluster (Figures S3C–S3E). The “TGFB-avid” fibroblasts are particularly enriched segments with the neural crest origin, whereas the “BMP-avid” cluster is particularly enriched from segments with the paraxial/splenic mesodermal origin (Figure S3A).

ECs

EC heterogeneity across different types of vessels and organs has been well described.34,49,50 Even at a specific arterial site, there is evidence of different populations of ECs in murine aorta.34 Well-established markers for ECs (CDH5, VWF, and CD31) fall within two large distinct clusters (Figures 4A and S4A). A single layer of ECs is found in the intimal surface of the arteries, but many more are found in the adventitia associated with the adventitial micro-vasculature. In our data, the microvascular ECs appear to be the most abundant population of ECs (Figure 4B). The biggest determinant of the transcriptome of the ECs appears to be the spatial location (intima/adventitia) these cells occupy (Figure S4B). The larger of the two clusters constitutes the majority of ECs (“endo1”) and can be further subdivided into additional subclusters (Figure 4C). One subcluster expresses markers associated with venous/capillary endothelium (i.e., ACKR1), whereas another subcluster expresses HEY1, a Notch signaling mediator associated with arterial fate51 (Figure 4D). Consistent with existing literature, the cluster that expresses ACKR1 is found exclusively in the microvasculature in the adventitia, whereas the cluster that expresses HEY1 is found both in the arterioles in the adventitia and on the luminal surfaces (Figures 4E and S4C). Arterial ECs have been implicated in endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndoMT), whereby these cells start to express mesenchymal genes.52,53 We did find rare EC populations that express TAGLN and ACTA2, but the total incidence (0.57% total cells) is below the expected doublet rate. While these cells are not flagged by computational doublet removal tools,54 we cannot entirely eliminate that these may simply be doublets (Figure S4D). Lastly, using existing markers for lymphatic ECs (PROX1 and LYVE1),55,56 we can observe a small population of lymphatic ECs as well (Figure 4F).

The second cluster of ECs (“endo2”) are notably distinct from the remaining ECs. This population of cells express different HOX genes compared to the other endothelial population within the same segment, suggesting probable distinct origins (Figure 4G). This EC population expresses many unique disease-related markers, including ITLN1 and NRG157,58,59 (Figure 4H). The endo2 population is found in all vascular territories and accounts for a ∼10% of the total endothelial population without significant variance in prevalence across different vascular beds (Figure S4E). Interestingly, one of the most specific markers, ITLN1, is upregulated after large vascular ischemic events.60,61,62 RNAscope using ITLN1 revealed that this population exists exclusively on the luminal surface as a distinct rare sub-population (Figures 4I and 4J). Marker genes for this population appears distinct from the different endothelial populations reported in murine aortic ECs34,50 but can be readily found in other human artery datasets.37,63 DE gene comparison between endo1/endo2 (Figure S4F; Table S4) revealed that they likely have distinct functions and disease association (Figures 4K and S4G). These findings suggest this endo2 population may be uniquely involved in human vascular diseases.

Macrophages and lymphocytes

Emerging evidence suggests that inflammatory cells play an important part in vascular homeostasis.11,32,63,64,65 Our data further supports this notion, as even though these donor samples were obtained from relatively healthy vessels without overt diseases, there is a significant number of macrophages and lymphocytes present in all the vascular beds. There is a trend toward a slight increase in the total fraction of immune cells in the atherosclerosis prone arterial beds (coronary, infra-renal aorta; Figure S5A), which does not reach statistical significance. There was no significant inter-person difference in the proportion of immune cells (Figure S1E). The majority of macrophages and lymphocytes are found in the adventitia. Clusters of B and T cells can also occasionally be found in organized centers of what appears to be tertiary lymphoid organs that abut the adventitia (Figure 1D). Cell-to-cell crosstalk inference using CellChat66 demonstrates significant crosstalk between the adventitial fibroblast, smooth muscle, and the immune cell populations (Figure S5B). These findings suggest a potential role for resident vascular mural cells in coordinating the vascular immune response.

Determinants of arterial identity

To understand differences between arterial segments, we first tried to determine whether there was a consistent relative composition of different cell types within different arteries. With identical digestion protocols, the relative proportions of cell populations were mostly similar across different arterial beds (Figure S6A). There is a slight increase in inflammatory cells in the vascular beds that are more prone to atherosclerosis (Figure 5A), However, there were no large differences in cellular composition across arterial segments.

Visualization of dataset using UMAP accentuates the drivers of the variance within the dataset.67,68 Thus, visualization of the data in this manner enables us to get a sense of relative heterogeneity of each population of cells across the different vascular beds. Simply visualizing this dataset coded by segmental origins, it is readily apparent that there is significant heterogeneity between adventitial fibroblasts and SMCs among the different vascular beds. However, there were relatively fewer differences between the microvascular, inflammatory, and endothelial populations across different vessel beds, as demonstrated on the UMAP (Figure 5B). Because dimensional reduction such as UMAP can distort true distances between populations,67,69 we repeated the analysis by looking at the number of DE genes between the ascending and descending arteries and between the coronary and descending pairs (Figures S6B and S6C), as well as the mean Euclidean distance70 between the average global gene expression of each cell population across the vascular beds (Figures S6D and S6E). Consistent with the UMAP visualization, all of these analyses revealed fewer differences between ECs and macrophages across different vascular beds compared to fibroblasts and SMCs. Interestingly, the same analysis comparing endo1 and endo2 populations showed much smaller difference between endo1 but more difference in endo2 (Figure S6E). These data suggest that the main determinant of arterial identities, at least transcriptomically, appears to be held mostly by fibroblasts, SMCs, and possibly, the endo2 population and much less so in the remaining ECs, microvasculature, and inflammatory cells.

Prominent roles of embryonic origin in adult vascular identity

We next investigated the main differences between SMCs and fibroblasts from different vascular beds. Examination of the DE genes across SMCs and fibroblasts of different vascular beds revealed a large number of developmentally important transcription factors,28,71,72,73,74,75,76 including HOX family genes, ISL1, MEF2C, TCF21, HAND2, and GATA4 (Figure 5C). We hypothesize, therefore, that embryonic origins are the dominant factor in vascular identity. We regenerated a UMAP visualization focusing on the top transcriptomic variances that exist in each individual population. This UMAP readily revealed distinct transcriptomic sub-clusters of fibroblasts and SMCs (Figures S3B; S7A). Consistent with our hypothesis, the proximity of different populations of fibroblasts by either Euclidean distance or visualization in the UMAP space appears to correspond to each segment’s presumed embryonic origin reported in animal models (Figures 5D and 5E and S7B). Cells from arteries of different caliber and flow with similar embryonic origins (i.e., aortic arch and ascending aorta/carotid arteries) cluster closer together than their connected counterparts (i.e., DESC vs. ARCH). This suggests that embryonic origins of each of these arterial segments play a dominant role in adult gene expression. Curiously, this developmental pattern is less preserved in the microvasculature and endo1, suggesting less influence of embryonic origin in these cells (Figures S7C, S7D, and S7E).

To unbiasedly determine if these lowly expressed developmental master regulators continue to play an important role in adult gene expression, we investigated whether the binding sites for these transcription factors are enriched in DE genes between segments compared to other variable genes in the dataset, focusing on the ascending and descending thoracic aorta as the main test case. Transcription factors ISL1, HAND2, and MEF2C are expressed in the second heart field and neural crest cells, which give rise to the ascending aorta, whereas HOX genes are expressed in the somitic mesoderm, which gives rise to the descending thoracic aorta (Figures 5F and S7F). Consistent with these transcription factors playing an important role, we saw a very strong enrichment of ISL1, HAND2, MEF2C, and HOX binding sites in the regulatory regions of DE genes in fibroblasts and SMCs across these vessel beds of distinct embryonic origin (Figures 5G and S7G; Table S5). These data suggest that the embryonic origin plays a critical role in arterial identity and their global gene expression in adults.

Adult vascular HOX gene expression patterns

While HOX genes' roles are best elucidated in development, as one of the most potent cell fate and patterning signals,71,77 their role in adult tissue identity and disease is less well understood. It has been shown that different arterial segments express different HOX genes by bulk RNA-seq.25 Given the impressive enrichment of HOX gene-binding motifs in regulatory regions of most DE genes, we leveraged our dataset to better understand HOX gene expression across the different cell types within each segment of the aorta.

Adult HOX gene expression patterns from the same vessel segment are conserved across donors and mirror their embryonic origin (Figures 5H and S8A). Descending thoracic aorta, abdominal aorta, and iliac arteries each expresses distinct subsets of HOX genes (Figure S8B). However, within the different cell types of a segment, each cell type expresses a different subset of HOX genes. For example, in descending thoracic aorta, HOXD8 is most highly expressed only by endo1, HOX A4/A5 predominantly by endo2, HOXA3 in SMCs, and HOX A5/B7 in fibroblasts. While smooth muscles and microvasculature are transcriptionally and phenotypically similar and AdvFib and microvasculature are geographically close, they express very distinct HOX genes patterns (Figures 5H and S8C). On the other hand, adventitial fibroblast HOX gene expression is similar to that of the medial SMCs. This potentially reflects that these two populations of cells (medial SMCs and AdvFib) have more closely shared embryonic origins. As further evidence that the HOX genes likely play an important role in adult arterial identity, we clustered the cells and segments based on HOX genes alone. Hierarchical clustering of SMCs and fibroblasts based on HOX genes alone mirrors the hierarchical clustering of whole transcriptomes and readily reflects each segment’s developmental origin (Figures 5I and 5J). While ECs from different segments have distinct origins, ECs from neighboring segments are less heterogeneous compared to SMCs/fibroblasts (Figure S8D). Heterogeneity in global gene expression mirrors heterogeneity in HOX gene expression patterns. The distinct pattern of HOX gene expression along with enrichment in HOX binding motifs in DE genes suggests critical roles for HOX genes in cell-type-specific adult segment-specific gene expression.

Enrichment of vascular disease rare and common variant genetic signals

Our original hypothesis was that transcriptomic differences between these vascular beds are important for differences in disease propensities. To determine if these identified transcriptomic differences play a role in adult diseases, we then asked whether the differentially regulated genes were enriched in human signals of genetic diseases. We focused our attention on the difference between ascending and descending thoracic aortic segments, which have a known strong propensity for aortic aneurysms.78,79 While these disease segments are subject to changes in flow, there was relatively few differences between the global EC expression of cells from these two vessel beds (Figure S9A; Table S6). However, there were significantly more transcriptomic differences between fibroblasts and SMCs from these different connected arterial segments. Most notably, there were large numbers of differentially regulated genes in the fibroblast population that have known roles in inherited aortopathies.80 A number of mendelian aortopathies involve haploinsufficiency of critical ECM components, including fibrilin 1 (FBN1), lysyl oxidase (LOX), microfibril-associated protein 4 (MFAP4), microfibril-associated protein 5 (MFAP5), and collagen 3A1 (COL3A1). Loss of function alleles in these genes are associated with thoracic aortic aneurysms, with a strong preference for affecting the root and ascending aorta. Interestingly, the expression of each of these genes is significantly lower in the ascending than the descending aorta (Figures 6A and S9A). Closer examination revealed that differences in expression were seen nearly exclusively in the adventitial fibroblasts, but not in SMCs (Figure 6B). Conversely, PRKG1 is a gene whose gain of function is known to increase the risk of aneurysms,81,82,83 which also has higher expression in the disease prone region (Figure 6C).

To determine if other arterial segment specific vascular traits showed the segregation of genetic signals, we subsequently performed the same analysis on common genetic variants associated with human aortic diameter. Most GWAS lead variants are located in non-coding regions of the genome, likely exerting its effect on gene dosages. Thus, GWAS “hits” enrich for genes whose dose are likely to contribute to pathology. We leveraged recently published GWAS on the aortic diameter84 and asked how often GWAS genes for aortic diameter are DE in different populations of cells in aneurysm prone (i.e., ascending) vs. resistant (i.e., descending) segments. For those within the non-coding region of the genome, we used the putative target gene suggested in the original manuscript. We then asked how often these dose sensitive genes were DE at the individual cell level in the ascending and descending aorta. After Bonferroni correction for multiple hypothesis testing, our analysis revealed that 31 diameter-sensitive genes are DE in ascending vs. descending fibroblasts and that 18 diameter-sensitive genes are DE in SMCs, and 5, 3, and 4 genes are DE in microvasculature, macrophage, and ECs, respectively (Table S7). These analyses suggest a large proportion of segment-specific modifiers of aortic propensity for aneurysm act through fibroblasts and SMCs (Figures S9B and S9C). These findings confirm the known role of SMCs in aneurysms, while suggesting a prominent unrecognized role of AdvFib.

We subsequently performed the same analysis in atherosclerotic disease and observed a similar pattern. Coronary and carotid arteries are known to be more prone to atherosclerosis, whereas the descending thoracic aorta is less so.85 Thus, these vessel beds are used for the atherosclerosis disease propensity comparisons to identify causal cells and causal genes for the reasons for the differences in disease propensity. A significant subset of GWAS genes for coronary artery disease (Figures 6D and 6E and S9D and S9E) are DE in the different population of cells in the vessel wall. Our analysis revealed 45 coronary artery disease (CAD) GWAS genes DE in fibroblasts, 30 in SMCs, 11 in the microvasculature, 15 in endo1, 7 in endo2, and 27 in macrophages (Table S8), each enriching for distinct signaling pathways (Figure 6F). These analyses suggest that segment specific differences in vascular disease propensities appear to be encoded in fibroblasts and SMCs.

Sex-specific differences

There are differences in the susceptibility for vascular diseases between different biological sex.86,87,88,89 Coronary artery disease and Marfan syndrome, for example, are known to have worse vascular manifestation in men,90,91 whereas Takayasu arteritis or spontaneous coronary artery dissections are more common in women.92,93 The underlying cause of these sex-specific differences is poorly understood. While many of the causes of the sex-specific differences may be related to numerous systemic and other confounding factors, this dataset offers an opportunity to explore potential differences between male and female arteries. We performed differential expression analysis comparing different cellular populations in our dataset. This analysis revealed different cell-type-specific transcriptomes between male and female sex. The analysis revealed 119, 176, 201, 79, 196, 281, and 172 sex-specific DE genes between SMCs, fibroblasts, macrophages, endo1, microvasculature, endo2, and lymphocytes, respectively (Table S9), with >90% of them outside of sex chromosomes. Unlike cross-segmental identities that appears to lie more in fibroblasts and SMCs, sex-specific differences were seen across all cell types and were more profound in ECs and immune cells. This parallels the observations of sex-specific differences observed in the incidence of vasculitis and vascular endothelial dysfunction. Pathway analysis revealed distinct biological process enrichments (Table S10). Different cell-type DE gene overlap with OMIM data revealed distinct vascular disease associations with known sex preferences (i.e., SMCs association with Ehler-Danlos, and EC association with macular degeneration; Table S11).

Cell-type- and segment-specific non-coding transcriptome

While existing analysis of scRNA-seq data has mostly focused on protein coding genes, there is an increasing recognition that non-coding transcripts, such as long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), play an important role in health and disease.94,95,96 Taking advantage of the fact that a significant portion of lncRNAs is poly-adenylated and thus captured by the 10x platform, we asked whether lncRNAs also follow segmental and cell-type-specific expression by aligning these sequencing data to a library of known lncRNAs. Given the existing workflow does not allow for strand-specific capture, one cannot readily determine if a read is from the coding or antisense strand of a transcript. To remove any potential confounding reads from protein coding genes, we leveraged a reference lncRNA transcriptome that eliminated any segments of overlap with any protein-coding mRNAs. We will refer to this as the sc-lncRNA transcriptome.

This dataset contains 53,247 unique non-coding transcripts that are found in the vascular cells (compared to 18,088 protein-coding genes). Despite our stringent filtering, on average, ∼1,601 (median: 1,284) non-coding transcripts were detected in each cell, encoding an average of ∼397 (medianL 323) lncRNAs per cell. This represents ∼10% in the number of genes or reads compared to the protein-coding genome. Differential gene expression analysis of these data comparing cells across different segments found that a significant portion of the sc-lncRNA transcriptome also exhibited cell-type- and segment-specific expression (Figures 7A and 7B).

Employing these data, we investigated how much of the cell-type-specific identities are encoded in this limited subset of the non-coding transcriptome. To accomplish this, we performed unbiased clustering of the sc-lncRNA transcriptome, along with UMAP-based visualization. Utilizing no protein-coding gene data, analyzing sc-lncRNA transcriptome alone, standard unbiased clustering readily segregate the dataset into cell-type-specific clusters that recapitulate those identified using the entire transcriptome (Figure 7C), correctly identifying specific cell type with high accuracy using sc-lncRNA data alone without any protein-coding transcripts(Figure S10A). Hierarchical clustering based on sc-lncRNA data itself in SMCs and fibroblasts could recapitulate the developmental origin (Figure 7D). This suggests that a significant portion of the identities of each cell types, behavior, and embryological origin are embedded within the non-coding transcriptome.

The majority of common variants that are identified in humans that carry heritable risk of vascular diseases lie within the non-coding region of the genome.97,98,99 One hypothesis has been that some of these non-coding regions of the genome may overlap with functional lncRNAs. We asked whether these vascular lncRNAs enriched for genome-wide significant GWAS SNPs for different vascular conditions. Interestingly, there appears to be a significant enrichment of GWAS SNPs for coronary artery diseases that lie within the exons of lncRNAs compared to expected (18 lncRNAs with 158 MVP SNPs; Figure 7E; Table S12). We subsequently intersected the DE lncRNAs across segments within major cell clusters with the loci from the GWAS catalog, revealing a significant intersection of these lncRNAs with loci associated with CAD and heart disease (Figures S10B and S10C).98 Many of these GWAS loci-associated lncRNA have distinct expression between intimal and medial SMC populations. Intimal SMCs express a higher number of lncRNA transcripts than medial SMCs (Figure S10D). Beyond GWAS loci, among lncRNA previously associated with altered vascular disease risk,100 a significant portion of them also demonstrate distinct intimal/medial SMC expression levels (Figure S10E). These findings provide potential functional context for lncRNAs implicated in vascular diseases.

Discussion

By performing cellular transcriptomic and spatial localization of donor arteries across a variety of arterial segments, we created a resource for the vascular and genetics research community to better understand the cellular composition and distribution of gene expression programs across human arteries to better link causal genes to causal cells. We contribute to the existing literature of single-cell characterization of human arteries by improving the digestion of arterial tissue to obtain high-quality single-cell RNA-seq data matching the cellular populations and ratios observed by histology.28,30 These techniques also enabled us to achieve deeper characterization of vascular cell transcriptomes, including lowly expressed transcription factors, to better elucidate their biology. We hope that this dataset can serve as a useful reference to aid the cardiovascular research community in future single-cell characterization of vascular conditions.

Utilizing entire cellular transcriptomes instead of specific marker genes to define cellular heterogeneity, this dataset both validates and challenges existing approaches to study vascular diseases in murine and cellular models. One immediate benefit of this approach is the ability to identify specific markers empirically in an unbiased manner for populations of cells. These cell-type-specific markers can be utilized to enable other vascular discoveries, including generating more specific murine Cre-lox models to understand individual cell populations. Furthermore, our data can help reconcile seemingly paradoxical findings of existing cellular and lineage-tracing models. For example, our analyses revealed surprising heterogeneity and limitations of many commonly used vascular population markers, which may explain some of the contradictory published findings from animal models using different “marker” genes.39 This specific issue is best studied in the vascular SMC field, in which heterogeneous findings using different “SMC-specific” Cre-recombinase models have been well documented.39 While Acta2 and Tagln-Cre were previously frequently employed to study SMC lineage tracing and gene perturbation, meticulous studies by multiple groups have documented its activation in non-SMCs, including fibroblasts101,102 and ECs.103 While the non-SMC labeling was previously attributed to “leaky” expression of these Cre alleles, our scRNA-seq data suggest that the issue may be underappreciated endogenous expression of these genes. Indeed, more specific SMCs and vascular SMC Cres (Myh11 and Itga8104) have been recently widely utilized due to their specificity, which is also recapitulated in the scRNA-seq data. What remains most concerning is the poor specificity of fibroblast and microvascular/pericyte markers, which is marked in our scRNA-seq data as multiple populations. More dedicated markers and reagents for these populations may be needed to optimally study their biology. Similarly, by combining single-cell RNA-seq data with complementary spatial transcriptomic techniques, we were able to localize transcriptomically unique populations that reside in different parts of the vessel wall. This includes the identification of multiple “smooth muscle marker”-positive populations and multiple subsets of ECs with distinct transcriptomes and localizations. This extensive heterogeneity that exists in healthy vessels may help explain conflicting findings that sometimes occur from in vitro studies of primary human arterial cells, as in vitro “arterial SMC” or “arterial endothelial” cells using canonical markers may be isolated from one or multiple populations of cells. Our dataset may also be used to deconvolute the population(s) of vascular ECs or SMCs.105,106

One of our main goals with this dataset was to identify underlying differences in the biology and disease propensities across different human arterial segments. Current understanding of such differences mainly attributes them to flow characteristics and hemodynamics.107,108,109 Indeed, turbulent flow, laminar flow, and static flow are known to create significant alterations in transcriptomic profiles of ECs that are relevant to diseases.110,111 On the other hand, numerous pieces of evidence also exist, suggesting that developmental influences may alter vascular disease propensity112,113,114,115. This nature vs. nurture debate of vascular disease remains unsettled. Our analysis of healthy vessels, subjected to decades of differential flow patterns, reveals relatively few differences between the transcriptomes of ECs across different vascular segments. However, adventitial fibroblasts and medial SMCs are clearly responsible for the main transcriptomic differences across different vascular beds. DE genes in these populations appear to be regulated by developmentally critical master regulators and enrich for both common and rare variants of vascular diseases. These findings suggest that developmental origins of these vessels play a large role in different disease propensities. Developmental master regulators may therefore possess key pathogenic roles. Given the clinical observation of strong associations of different arterial segments for diseases within the same person,5,79,116,117 the modulation of these developmental master regulators’ function may be sufficient to alter disease development or progression.

One of the largest populations of cells identified from this dataset is the adventitial fibroblast population. This population of cells carries remarkable heterogeneity across vascular segments with different disease predilections, including a large number of genetic signals for aneurysmal diseases and atherosclerosis. Yet, this population of cells has traditionally not been the focus of pathogenesis of vascular diseases. Further evaluation of this population of cells and their roles in vascular pathologies is warranted.

Analysis of the human endothelial population reveals significant heterogeneity, consistent with distinct developmental origins and early arterial/venous/lymphatic endothelial fate commitment demonstrated in animal models.14,15,18,34,50,55,56 This study identifies a distinct population of relatively rare ECs located at the luminal surface of the great arteries that is not observed in murine arteries. Given the enrichment of this population of cells for distinct disease-related transcriptomic programs, further dedicated studies of this population of cells may yield distinct insight into their pathophysiological function.

Murine disease models with SMC lineage tracing have identified “de-differentiated” and “phenotypically modulated” SMCs as key pathogenic cells in a variety of vascular conditions.17,28,35,36 The importance of this population of cells is well established through a variety of lineage-tracing and manipulation models of different diseases.28,41,118,119 However, in macroscopically healthy vessels, de-differentiated SMC subset is universally present across all vascular segments and populates the intima. This population of cells maps to modulated SMCs in murine models. This potentially reflects the presence of subclinical diseases in all these donors given years of relative hyperlipidemia due to exposure to a western diet.120 Alternatively, another explanation is that SMC phenotypic modulation itself is part of the normal physiological spectrum and not necessarily pathologic. Instead, the specific ways that seemingly similar SMC populations phenotypically modulate in different ways may govern the quality and burden of disease. This notion is further supported by the fact that there appears to be larger global transcriptomic differences in the intimal SMC population across the vascular beds and stronger enrichment for genetic signals of vascular disease than medial SMCs (Figures S9D and S9E).

Lastly, while the focus of pathogenic factors for vascular disease is often on protein-coding genes,84,97,98,99 the analysis of non-protein-coding transcriptomes of the vascular beds reveals surprisingly rich information and heterogeneity. Global analysis of lncRNAs alone can readily identify the main vascular wall cell types, and DE lncRNAs across vascular beds disproportionally enrich for non-coding variants that influence vascular disease risk. While the analysis is preliminary, the enrichment of genetic signals suggests that these DE lncRNAs may contain critical regulators of disease risk. This finding adds on to growing literature of pathogenic lncRNA in different human cardiovascular diseases100 and points to new potential disease-modifying lncRNAs that may be targeted for cardiovascular diseases.

Limitations of the study

Availability and cost created constraints on the total number of donors included in this study, which limited the power to detect subtle differences. While deceased donors’ arteries are without overt diseases, they may not truly be representative of healthy arteries. The technology utilized does not capture non-polyA-capped RNA, and the heterogeneity captured does not capture post-translational modifications, which likely also contribute to segmental differences.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human subjects

We collaborated with the organ donor network to collect arterial tissues from organ donors, some for single cell capture and other for histology. The metadata, along with details on the specific tissues collected and the assays performed, are provided in Table S13. Up to 9 distinct arterial sites (ascending thoracic aorta (ASC), aortic arch (ARCH), descending thoracic aorta (DESC), infra-renal abdominal aorta (INFRARENAL), iliac artery (ILIAC), coronary artery (RCA), carotid artery (CAROTID), pulmonary artery (PA), and aortic root (ROOT)) were chosen to be evaluated due to their different embryonic origins and disease propensities. Tissues were dissociated into single cells using an enzymatic digestion approach we developed to overcome the dense vascular connective tissue.

Method details

Human tissue collection

Human artery samples were collected at the time of tissue collection in collaboration with Donor Network West. The protocol was thoroughly reviewed by both Donor Network West’s research protocol as well as Stanford University IRB. To ensure we can capture coronary artery and aortic root alongside other sites, deceased donors whose hearts do not have an active clinical match and thus were donated to research were prioritized. The goal for each tissue collection was aimed to capture as many sites as possible, though the number of captures varied on each collection as arterial segments were first prioritized to optimize for donated organ’s transplant success. Segments that were preferred to be retained by transplant surgeon’s needs were left with transplanted organs, and remaining available segments were collected without additional skin/superficial incisions being performed, per research ethics and etiquette as respect to our organ donors. Fresh arterial tissue was collected and kept in cold Hank’s buffered saline solution (HBSS, Sigma). For each donor identical sites were collected, as indicated by the blue circles in Figure 1A, with identical locations excised in identical fashion, proposing avoiding segments right at bifurcation sites to remove potential confounders. For consistency, we always targeted middle segments of the RCA, proximal segment of the R-pulmonary artery, aortic root, ascending aorta at >2–3 cm above the coronary cusp, aortic arch at the middle of the minor curvature, descending aorta at 2–3 cm below the insertion of the subclavian artery, 1 cm below the renal artery for infra-renal segments of the aorta. For the carotid artery, the R BCA was retracted proximally to reveal the proximal carotid and subclavian, and the carotid then resected, 1-2cm past the bifurcation was generally collected by this mean. R Iliac artery was retracted proximally from the abdominal cavity leveraging existing incisions, and care was also made to ensure segments utilized were 1-2cm past the bifurcation sites.

Human artery digestion

Tissue was dissected to include all vascular layers and divided into four 50mg pieces. Samples were then placed in digestion solution containing Liberase TM (10.4U/mL working concentration in HBSS, Sigma-Aldrich 05401127001) and elastase (8U/mL, Sigma E7885 in HBSS) and finely minced on ice using iris scissors. The resulting mixture was then transferred into a 2.0mL Eppendorf tube and subjected to constant motion at 650RPM in a heated Eppendorf Thermomixer (Model 5382) with distilled water inserted into the heating block to improve heat exchange. The digestion mixture is checked at 30 min/45 min, and pipetted vigorously x3 if there is evidence of clumps of undigested cells. Following 60 min of digestion at 37°C, digestates were vigorously pipetted x10, and then filtered through a 70uM strainer, centrifuged at 500 g × 10 min in V-bottom plates to enhance cell collection, and resuspended with fresh cold HBSS. Samples were sorted with FACS using Calcein green (ThermoFisher) for live/dead discrimination and used fresh for single cell capture and library preparation.

Histology

For detection of mRNA in situ, RNAscope was performed on slides of fixed frozen human arteries as instructed by the manufacturer. Briefly, 8 μm slides were immersed in distilled water to get rid of optimal cutting temperature compound (OCT). Hydrogen Peroxide was then applied to the slides for 5 min to remove background peroxidase activity. Slides were then immersed in 1x Target Retrieval buffer at 100°C for 5 min. Following dehydration in 100% ethanol, Protease Plus reagents were applied onto the sections at 40°C for 30 min. Sections were then incubated with probes for different cell types or a negative control probe for 2 h at 40°C. Subsequent amplification and signal detection steps were performed as per manufacturer instructions. 50% Gill’s hematoxylin was used for nuclei counterstaining. The images shown were representative of at least 3 arteries from different donors.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

All single cell capture and library preparation were performed per 10x Genomics instruction. Cells were loaded into a 10x Genomics microfluidics chip and encapsulated with barcoded oligo-dT-containing gel beads using the 10x Genomics Chromium controller according to the manufacturer’s instructions (v3 GEX). Single-cell libraries were then constructed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Single Index Kit T Set A). Libraries from individual samples were multiplexed into one lane before sequencing on an Illumina platform (NovaSeq 6000) with targeted depth of 80,000 reads per cell for RNA.

Analysis of single-cell sequencing data

Fastq files from each segment and patient were aligned to the reference genome hg38 individually using CellRanger v3.1.0 (10x Genomics). Individual datasets were aggregated using the CellRanger aggr command without subsampling normalization. The aggregated dataset was then analyzed using the R package Seurat V4.4.0. The dataset was trimmed of cells expressing fewer than 1000 genes, and genes expressed in fewer than 50 cells. The number of genes, number of unique molecular identifiers and percentage of mitochondrial genes were examined to identify outliers. As an unusually high number of genes can result from a ‘doublet’ event, in which two different cell types are captured together with the same barcoded bead, cells with >8000 genes were discarded. Cells containing >7.5% mitochondrial genes were presumed to be of poor quality and were also discarded. The gene expression values then underwent library-size normalization and normalized using the established Single-Cell-Transform (SCTransform) function in Seurat. For ease of visualization and to focus on biology that exists between vascular beds instead of inter-person variations, we performed batch correction between different donors using Single-cell-transform (SCT)-based integration. Principal component analysis was used for dimensionality reduction, followed by clustering in principle component analysis space using a graph-based clustering approach via Louvain algorithm. UMAP was then used for two-dimensional visualization of the resulting clusters. No expression imputation was performed. Analysis, visualization and quantification of gene expression and generation of gene module scores were performed using Seurat’s built-in functions such as “FeaturePlot”, “VlnPlot”, “AddModuleScore”, and “FindMarkers.” The processed Seurat object was converted to SingleCellExperiment format and imported to monocle, followed by cell clustering and DDRTree visualization. orderCells was be used to interpret the topology-preserving single-cell embeddings and developmental trajectories.

Spatial slide-seq

Slide-seqV2 spatial single cell RNA sequencing was performed according to manufacturer’s protocol. Fresh frozen human coronary artery and aorta samples in OCT were taken to a −20°C cryostat for 20 min before cutting into 10 μm sections. These sections were melted onto Curio Seeker Indexed tiles with spatially barcoded beads. The tile was then incubated in hybridization buffer at room temperature for 15 min, washed, and further incubated in RT Reaction Mix at room temperature for 10 min, then at 52°C for 30 min. OCT was cleared with Tissue Clearing Mix at 37°C for 30 min. Beads were mechanically dissociated and washed. A sequencing library was prepared with 600 pg of cDNA using the Nextera XT Library Prep Kit. The tagmented library was indexed with Seeker Dual Indexing Primer Kit and a PCR program. The library was purified with SPRI select beads and eluted in 10 μL water. Quantification and quality were assessed using the Agilent Bioanalyzer High Sensitivity DNA chip. The final library was sequenced on Illumina Novaseq 6000 with a target depth of 300 million reads.

Analysis of spatial slide-seq data

Fastq files were aligned to hg38 by curioseeker pipeline and then the Seurat RDS object was generated for each slide. The objects were imported into R environment for further analyzing, respectively. The dataset was trimmed of cells with number of unique molecular identifiers less than 100. The gene expression values then underwent library-size normalization and normalized using established Single-Cell-Transform (SCTransform) function in Seurat. To facilitate cell-type annotation of the Slide-seq dataset, we are leveraging our processed single-cell RNA-seq dataset. Robust Cell Type Decomposition was utilized to integrate this reference with the spot-level data from spatial transcriptomic sequencing under the Seurat V5.1.0 framework. Analysis, visualization and quantification of gene expression and generation of gene module scores were performed using Seurat’s built-in function such as “SpatialDimPlot”, “SpatialFeaturePlot”, and “AddMetaData”.

Motif and functional analyses

Gene ontology of differentially expressed gene list was analyzed by DAVID (https://david.ncifcrf.gov). KEGG pathways and biological processes enrichment analyses were carried out using default settings. We extracted 500 base pairs upstream of the transcription start site (TSS) of differentially expressed genes to define their promoter regions for motif analysis. Known TRANSFAC motifs and de novo motifs were identified using the findMotifsGenome.pl script from the HOMER suite. To perform position weight matrix (PWM) scanning, we utilized the PWMScore tool. Full-length motifs for Isl1 (MA1608.1), HAND2 (MA1638.1), MEF2C (MA0497.1), HOXA (MA0911.1), and HOXB (MA1499.1) were retrieved from the JASPAR database (https://jaspar2020.genereg.net/).

The smooth muscle modulation score was calculated using previously identified markers of modulated smooth muscle cells (SMCs) in both murine and human models. The top cluster marker genes were selected to compute the scores using the Seurat AddModuleScore function. The genes used included: FN1, LUM, TNFRSF11B, COL15A1, IGFBP2, ANXA2, COL14A1, COL12A1, IGFBP6, MMP2, COL1A1, COL3A1, COL1A2, COL4A2, COL5A1, COL4A1, COL5A2, SERPINH1, FMOD, ECM1, CSF1, CLEC11A, LRRC17, FSTL3, COL1A1, TWSG1, MGP, COL5A2, CDH11, SOST, GPM6B, and PDLIM7. Similarly, the extracellular matrix (ECM) score was computed using genes previously associated with extracellular functions as reported in the Gene Ontology database. These genes included: VIT, PDGFRA, COL14A1, MMP2, FBLN1, TNFRSF11B, NPNT, FBLN2, COL1A1, ADAMTS5, COL3A1, CCDC80, PRDX4, COL1A2, CRISPLD2, ABI3BP, MMP23B, COL5A2, ITGA8, TGFBI, MATN2, SNED1, TNXB, ITGB2, FN1, NID1, NPNT, LYVE1, FBLN5, SGCE, COL3A1, BCAM, ITGA8, ZYX, ITGBL1, ITGA7, ITGA6, SVEP1, and CD34.

Correlation analyses

For differentially expressed genes or lncRNA correlations between cell types or/and segments, we extracted the average expression levels in each cell type or segment. The Pearson correlation and the hierarchical clustering based on Euclidean distance were then performed with those average values. Complete method was used in Pearson correlations and clustering. The average expression values in each segment or cell type were considered two series of paired variables to calculate the joint variability as their covariances using the built-in cov function in R.

GWAS enrichment analyses

The aortic diameter GWAS data was obtained from a right heart structure study Pirruccello et al.84 Differentially expressed genes were matched by official gene names with the single nearest genes at each locus in this study. Million Veteran Program (MVP) CAD lead SNPs were retrieved from dbGap, and the nearest genes on either side of each locus were considered as the potential associated genes. The intersection of GWAS loci and lncRNA was defined as the SNPs located within any of the exons of the lncRNA transcripts. Direct overlap of transcript or genes and GWAS Catalog SNPs was performed with GWASanalytics script (https://github.com/zhaoshuoxp/GWASanalytics). The calculation criteria of this script were described previously Kim et al.128. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the R package gprofiler2. To further validate the GWAS enrichment and overlaps at single-cell resolution, we also utilized scRDS Zhang et al.129.

Cell-cell communications

We utilized R package CellChat Jin et al.66 to identify the cell-cell interactions from our scRNA-seq data. Briefly, the Seurat object was transfer to cellchat and the ligand-receptor interactions or signaling pathways were identified with built-in CellChatDB.human reference database. The over-expressed genes in each cell type were then computed and used as probe to find the correlated pathways. The ligand-receptor pairs were defined as the interaction numbers while the expression levels of the over-expressed genes were defined as interaction strength or weight.

Lnc-RNA analyses

For lncRNA alignment, we downloaded the reference human lncRNA from LncBook 2.0 (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/lncbook/). To remove any potential confounding reads from protein coding genes, we eliminated any lncRNA that have at least 1 bp overlaps of the exon with any protein coding mRNAs, resulting in 53560 total reference features. Given that lncRNAs is only a small portion of the total expressed RNAs, we combined the lncRNA with protein-coding genes to generate a master reference GTF file for cell identification in CellRanger running and expression level normalization in Seurat. After normalizing for library size, we extracted the cells with matching indices from the canonical reference-aligned Seurat object, focusing solely on the lncRNA features. Due to the relatively low nFeature numbers per cell, we directly used library size-normalized expressions instead of performing SCTransform. Similar to the whole transcriptome analyses, CCA integration was performed to correct the batch effect across patients. We defined differentially expressed lncRNAs as those positively expressed in each arterial segment within a given cell cluster, identified using the FindAllMarkers function in Seurat. The resulting lncRNAs from each cluster were merged into a master list for downstream analyses.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Differentially expressed genes in the scRNA-Seq data were identified using single-cell level pseudobulk DESeq2 test (FindMarkers with “DESeq2”) to reduce the potential batch effects. Given that the lncRNA-only expression matrix has less coverage of the whole transcriptome, we used the Wilcoxon Rank-Sum test to identify differentially expressed lncRNAs. The gender-specific differentially expressed genes were identified by individual-level aggregated pseudobulk DESeq2 test due to the high variance across samples. For motifs and gene enrichment analyses, we used the cumulative binomial distribution test. For overlapping of genomic regions or gene sets, we used Fisher’s exact test. The raw p values were corrected by Bonferroni or Benjamini-Hochberg Procedure and corrected p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All error bars represent standard error of the mean (SE). Number of stars for the p values in the graphs: ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗p < 0.05.
