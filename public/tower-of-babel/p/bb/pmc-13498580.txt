Biomass ash as supplementary cementitious material: microstructural mechanism underlying cement paste strength evolution
Authors: Shaoqiang Guo, Huimei Zhang, Chao Yuan, Yuzhang Bi, Xiujian Gao, Weihang Hua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn order to achieve the effective utilization of biomass ash (BA) as a potential cementitious component, the present work explores the influence of BA, employed as a supplementary cementitious material, on the properties of cement paste. Meanwhile, the mechanism by which various BA replacement levels affect the mechanical strength of cement paste is analyzed via a series of microscopic characterization techniques. In this experimental program, BA was applied as a silica-rich admixture, and cement paste specimens were manufactured by substituting Portland cement with BA at mass ratios ranging from 0% to 50%According to the macroscopic test results, the fluidity of the fresh paste exhibits a slight decreasing trend with the elevation of BA substitution content. In terms of strength development, an initial increasing trend is observed, followed by a gradual reduction, and the optimal overall performance is achieved at a 20% replacement level; under the water-to-binder ratio of 0.6, the 28-day compressive strength reaches 36.11 MPa, showing a 29.6% increase compared with the control group (pure cement paste, 27.86 MPa), and the strength remains above 95% of the control group even at a 30% replacement level. Microscopic analytical results verify that BA possesses noticeable pozzolanic reactivity and is capable of participating in partial hydration reactions. During the hydration procedure, the silica-bearing mineral phases in BA are incorporated into the silicate chains and interlayer structures of C–S–H gels. Furthermore, the relative content of silicate phases within C–S–H gels and their polymerization degree are both remarkably enhanced with increasing BA replacement ratios. The impact of BA content on the pore structure of cement paste differs under varying water-to-binder (w/b) ratios, among which gel pores and mesopores are identified as the dominant factors responsible for the evolution of the overall pore system.Keywords: Biomass ash, Supplementary cementitious material, Cement paste, Mechanical properties, HydrationSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

With the acceleration of global industrialization and the transformation of energy structures, biomass energy, as a clean and renewable energy source, has been widely applied. Consequently, the emission of biomass ash (BA) has been increasing year by year. The accumulation of a large amount of solid waste not only occupies land resources, but also its components such as soluble salts and heavy metals are prone to cause soil and water pollution. Realizing the efficient resource utilization of BA has become an important issue in practicing the “dual carbon” goals and ecological environmental protection1–3. Cement is the most widely used basic material in the construction industry, but its production process consumes a large amount of limestone and energy, and emits considerable amounts of carbon dioxide, exacerbating resource shortages and the greenhouse effect. Therefore, the development of new solid waste alternative cementitious materials and the optimization of cementitious material properties have become research hotspots in the fields of materials science and civil engineering4,5. Currently, traditional mineral admixtures (e.g., fly ash, blast furnace slag) are widely used for cement replacement to lower carbon emissions and enhance material performance6,7. Nevertheless, their supply shortages and rising costs limit large-scale applications8,9, driving the exploration of alternative sustainable cementitious materials like biomass ash (BA)10.

BA contains abundant active constituents including SiO2 and Al2O3, thus exhibiting considerable potential pozzolanic reactivity. It can be employed as a supplementary cementitious material to partially replace cement clinker in binder systems. Such an application contributes to the resourceful utilization of solid waste, while lowering the energy consumption and carbon dioxide emissions associated with cement manufacturing, thereby achieving significant environmental and economic advantages11–14. In recent years, scholars at home and abroad have carried out extensive research on the preparation of supplementary cementitious materials by replacing cement with BA, confirming that the incorporation of BA can regulate the microstructure and hydration process of cementitious materials through particle filling effect and pozzolanic reaction, thereby affecting their mechanical properties and durability15–17. Muhammad Nasir Amin et al.18 showed that the chemical composition of wheat straw ash meets the basic requirements of pozzolanic active materials, and after adding 15% wheat straw ash to mortar, its overall performance is slightly improved compared with the reference group. Pandey et al.19 confirmed through experiments that the combined addition of 10% rice straw ash and 5% silica fume in concrete can not only effectively alleviate the strength attenuation and mass loss under acidic environment and carbonation, but also reduce the water absorption and chloride ion penetration ability of the material. The research results of C et al.20 showed that partially replacing cement with an appropriate amount of rice straw ash is helpful to improve the comprehensive performance of concrete. Sehgal Mohit et al.21 pointed out that replacing cement with 10% rice straw ash by equal amount can improve the mechanical strength of mortar, but will prolong the setting time of the system. Zhang Zhengang et al.22 found that when the proportion of BA replacing fly ash reaches 50%, the compressive strength of concrete reaches the peak value. Collectively, existing studies have reached a consensus that an appropriate BA replacement (10–20%) generally improves mechanical performance, but divergences remain regarding the optimal replacement rate and strength attenuation mechanism at high BA contents (> 30%). Furthermore, most previous work focuses on macroscopic performance, with limited in-depth analysis of the synergistic hydration mechanism between BA and cement clinker, as well as the quantitative relationship between hydration products and strength evolution, especially under high replacement rates. Therefore, this paper systematically investigates the influence of BA substitution on paste strength from both macroscopic and microscopic perspectives, and clarifies the underlying regulatory mechanism, which can fill the above research gaps.

To explore how the proportion of biomass ash (BA) substituting cement influences the strength of cement paste, three test groups with distinct water-to-binder (w/b) ratios were designed in this study for concurrent testing. By means of macroscopic and microscopic characterization tests, the correlation between BA content and both the fresh mixing behavior and hardened properties of the paste was comprehensively and systematically analyzed. On the basis of qualitative analysis regarding the phase types and structural features of the paste, combined with quantitative calculation of the content of each phase component, the underlying mechanism through which BA regulates the strength development of cement paste was elaborated in a systematic manner. In the experimental setup, the substitution ratios of BA for cement were set at 0%, 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, and 50% respectively. Additionally, the flowability and mechanical performances of the cement paste were quantitatively evaluated by analyzing the results obtained from fluidity measurements and compressive strength tests. To explore the mechanism by which the replacement rate of BA affects the strength of cement paste, by combining characterization methods such as X-ray diffraction (XRD), thermogravimetric-differential thermal analysis (TG-DTG), FTIR, SEM, and MIP, the composition, content, and evolution law of hydration products were analyzed, the intrinsic correlation between hydration products and mechanical properties was clarified, and the mechanism of BA as a supplementary cementitious material in regulating cement hydration and performance optimization were revealed. This provides theoretical support and experimental basis for the efficient resource utilization of BA solid waste and the preparation of high-performance supplementary cementitious materials.

Materials and preparation

Raw materials

The cement used in the test is of type P·O 32.5, with an apparent density of 3.11 g/cm3, and the fineness of the cement is 334 m2/kg. The apparent density of BA is 1.89 g/cm3. The loss on ignition (LOI) of the used cement and BA is 4.2% and 1.9% respectively. BA was derived from agricultural crop straw (wheat straw), collected from rural areas of Shaanxi Province, China. It was obtained after industrial circulating fluidized bed combustion at 750–850 °C, then naturally cooled, ground and sieved through a 200-mesh sieve for use. Following the completion of the tests, all performance metrics were verified to comply with the relevant standard requirements23. The chemical composition and mass fraction were measured by X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy (XRF), and the results are shown in Table 1. The appearance of BA is light black, and the particle size distribution was measured by a particle size analyzer, as shown in Fig. 1a. The specific surface area of BA is 1124 m2/kg, an effective particle size D10 is 1.57 μm, and a median particle size D50 is 2.46 μm. The BA of SiO2, Al2O3, Fe2O3, and CaO are 71.4%, 1.46%, 1.26%, and 1.22% respectively. The cement has a dark gray appearance, with a specific surface area of 352.5 m2/kg, an effective particle size D10 of 1.40 μm, and a median particle size D50 of 3.57 μm. The contents of SiO2, Al2O3, Fe2O3, and CaO are 23.4%, 4.3%, 2.3%, and 67.5% respectively. The particle size distribution curves of cement and BA show particle size complementarity. The cumulative volume distribution curve of cement is overall biased towards the left small particle size range, and the peak of incremental volume appears at about 1 μm, indicating a higher proportion of fine particles; while the cumulative volume distribution curve of BA shifts to the right large particle size range, with the peak of incremental volume appearing at about 5 μm, reflecting that its particles are overall coarser. This particle size difference allows BA and cement to optimize the packing density of the cementitious system via the particle packing effect when BA and cement are acting synergistically. Among them, the fine particles of BA can fill the gaps between cement particles and refine the pore structure, while the relatively coarse particles of cement can provide skeleton support, providing space for the subsequent exertion of pozzolanic reactions24.

The phase composition of the precursor obtained by X-ray diffractometer (XRD) is shown in Fig. 1b. The XRD pattern of cement has sharp peaks and a small number of peaks, indicating that it is mainly composed of minerals with high crystallinity, and the mineral phase is single and stable, such as calcium carbonate and tricalcium silicate (C3S). The XRD peaks of BA are diffuse and multi-peaked, indicating that its mineral composition is complex, mainly including quartz, anorthite, leucite, etc. The results demonstrate that BA lacks the independent hydration capacity exhibited by cement. Nevertheless, the abundant silicate-bearing minerals within BA possess latent pozzolanic reactivity. Under the alkaline conditions generated during cement hydration, these minerals can react with calcium hydroxide to generate C–S–H gel. In addition, the calcium carbonate present in BA may contribute to the formation of carboaluminate phases at later hydration stages25,26. According to XRF and XRD results, the employed BA is silica‑rich (SiO2 = 71.4%) with low CaO and Al2O3 contents, belonging to the Si‑type biomass ash as classified in the literature27–29. Compared with wood BA (low silica, high CaO) and rice husk BA (ultra‑high silica, low activity), wheat straw BA shows moderate pozzolanic reactivity and suitable particle characteristics for cement substitution. The mineral phases mainly consist of quartz, anorthite, and leucite, which are consistent with typical straw‑derived BA reported in previous studies.

Material design and preparation

To compare the effect of partial replacement of cement with BA on the strength of paste, this study simultaneously selected three different water-to-binder ratios (w/b = 0.6, w/b = 0.7, and w/b = 0.8) for comparative research. The percentages of BA replacing cement were set at 0%, 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, and 50% respectively. The specimen numbers corresponding to w/b = 0.6 are BA0%-0.6, BA10%-0.6, BA20%-0.6, BA30%-0.6, BA40%-0.6, and BA50%-0.6; the specimen numbers corresponding to w/b = 0.7 are BA0%-0.7, BA10%-0.7, BA20%-0.7, BA30%-0.7, BA40%-0.7, and BA50%-0.7. The specimen numbers corresponding to w/b = 0.8 are BA0%-0.8, BA10%-0.8, BA20%-0.8, BA30%-0.8, BA40%-0.8, and BA50%-0.8. This paper studied the water mixing ratio (see Table 2) and the technical route of cement and BA samples (see Fig. 2).

Experimental flow chart.

The production process flow shown in Fig. 2 includes the following consecutive steps: First, the corresponding cement and BA are placed in a planetary mortar mixer for dry mixing at a low speed (140 ± 5 revolutions per minute) for 120 s. After adding one-third of the mixing water, low-speed stirring is carried out for 120 s. The remaining two-thirds of the water is added in two stages, with each stage accompanied by high-speed stirring at 285 ± 10 revolutions per minute for 120 s. The prepared paste is first subjected to a fluidity test, and then the remaining fresh paste is poured into a 40 mm × 40 mm × 40 mm three-dimensional plastic mold. A vibrating table with an amplitude of 0.75 mm is used for vibration for 60 s until the surface bubbles disappear. The specimens are left in the mold for 24 h and then demolded, after which they are transferred to a constant temperature chamber set at (20 ± 1 ℃, relative humidity ≥ 95%) for specific curing.

Samples used for microscopic detection and analysis need to be prepared separately according to the testing requirements. For Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) and Mercury Intrusion Porosimetry (MIP) tests, cube samples of 5 mm × 5 mm × 5 mm need to be cut from the test block to facilitate the observation and detection of pore structures. For X-ray Diffraction (XRD), Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), and Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA), 28-day-old specimens need to be ground into powder for testing.

Test methods

Fluidity test.

Fluidity test.

The flow behavior of cement paste is evaluated based on the elongation ratio of the specimen on a glass plate, with the detailed experimental procedure specified in the corresponding standard27. The frustoconical testing mold has an upper diameter of 36 mm, a base diameter of 60 mm, and a vertical height of 60 mm. For each mix proportion, three parallel specimens were tested, and the final fluidity value was taken as the average of three measurements. The paste fluidity was determined by measuring the maximum spread diameter in two perpendicular directions and taking the arithmetic mean. The elongation rate test diagram is shown in Fig. 3.

Schematic diagram of slurry flow performance test.

(2)Compressive strength test.

Compressive strength test.

After being cured in a standard curing chamber (20 ± 2 ℃, RH ≥ 95%) until the ages of 3 days, 7 days, and 28 days, the specimens were subjected to unconfined compressive strength tests in accordance with GB/T 17671-2021 “Test Method for Strength of Cement Mortar”. Three groups of parallel specimens were selected for each age. A microcomputer-controlled electro-hydraulic servo universal testing machine (range 300 kN, accuracy class 0.5) was used to load at a constant rate of 0.5 mm/min until failure. The peak load was recorded and the compressive strength value was calculated. The final result is the arithmetic mean ± standard deviation.

(3)Phase composition analysis.

Phase composition analysis.

XRD: A Bruker D8 ADVANCE X-ray diffractometer (Cu-Kα radiation, λ = 0.15406 nm, tube voltage 40 kV, tube current 40 mA) was used for crystal structure characterization. The samples were finely ground in an agate mortar to D50<75 μm, and then diffraction patterns in the 10–80°2θ range were collected using a step scanning mode (step size 0.02°, dwell time 0.5s). Combined with the ICDD PDF-4 + database, full pattern fitting was performed using MDI Jade 6.0 software to quantitatively calculate the amorphous phase content and the crystallinity index of hydration products (C–(A)–S–H gel, hydrotalcite, etc.).

(4)Chemical bonding analysis.

Chemical bonding analysis.

FTIR: A Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS50 Fourier transform infrared spectrometer was used to perform transmission mode detection in the 400–4000 cm−1 waveband using the KBr pellet method (sample/KBr = 1:100). The spectral data were collected under the conditions of 32 scan accumulations and 2 cm−1 resolution. Baseline correction, smoothing denoising, and second derivative processing were performed using OMNIC 9.2 software. Emphasis was placed on analyzing the redshift/broadening phenomena of characteristic vibration peaks such as Si-O-Si (~ 970 cm−1) and Al-O-Si (~ 780 cm−1).

(5)Thermogravimetric analysis.

Thermogravimetric analysis.

TG: A thermogravimetric analyzer from Mettler Toledo, Switzerland, was used to study the weight loss of the filler material. This method allows for quantitative comparison of weight loss of different hydrated mineral phases28. Argon was used as the heating medium, with a heating rate of 10 °C/min and a temperature range of 35–1000 °C.

(6)Pore structure analysis.

Pore structure analysis.

MIP: A Quantachrome PoreMaster 33 automatic mercury intrusion porosimeter was used for quantitative characterization of the pore structure. Specimens cured for 28 days were crushed, and 5–10 mm particles were selected, vacuum-dried at 105 °C to constant weight, and then subjected to mercury intrusion testing within a pressure range of 0.0036–227 MPa, with a contact angle of 140° and mercury surface tension of 485 mN/m.

(7)Microscopic morphology characterization.

Microscopic morphology characterization.

SEM: Micro-area analysis was performed using a FEI Quanta 650 field emission environmental scanning electron microscope (accelerating voltage 15 kV, working distance 10 mm) combined with an Oxford X-MaxN 150 energy dispersive spectrometer. The specimens were subjected to vacuum carbon coating after terminating hydration with isopropanol.

For microscopic tests (SEM, MIP, XRD, FTIR, TG), hydration was terminated by immersing 28 days specimens in isopropanol for 48 h, followed by vacuum drying at 60 °C for 24 h to remove residual solvent before testing.

Results and discussion

Macroscopic test results of cement paste

Flowability

Figure 4 shows the influence law of different BA replacement rates on the fluidity of the paste under different water-binder ratios. With the increase of BA replacement rate, the fluidity under various water-cement ratio conditions shows a downward trend. The fluidity of the paste without BA added is the best, with different water-cement ratios being 198 mm, 220 mm, and 247 mm respectively. When the BA replacement rate increases to 20%, for w/b = 0.6, the fluidity decreases to 172 mm and 139 mm in turn; for w/b = 0.7, the fluidity is 207 mm and 172 mm respectively; while for w/b = 0.8, it shows higher fluidity, being 244 mm and 229 mm respectively. It indicates that the higher the water-cement ratio, the smaller the impact of BA replacement rate within 20% on fluidity. When the BA replacement rate exceeds 20%, the fluidity of the slurry drops sharply, with the minimum values being 67 mm, 88 mm, and 142 mm, which are 66%, 60%, and 42.5% lower than those of the BA0 sample respectively. This is consistent with the research results in references28,29. This phenomenon is attributed to the following reasons: the synergistic effect of water-binder ratio and BA replacement rate. The higher the water-binder ratio, the more free water content in the paste, and the stronger the fluidity; when the BA replacement rate increases, the pozzolanic active components such as SiO2 and Al2O3 in BA undergo secondary reactions with cement hydration products. In the initial stage of the reaction, the reactants can improve the dispersion of the paste and enhance its fluidity. However, when the BA replacement rate is too high, the particle gradation and the proportion of active components of BA are unbalanced, resulting in an increase in paste viscosity and a decrease in fluidity30. This law provides a key reference for the alternative application of BA in cement-based materials, and it is necessary to comprehensively consider the matching between the water-binder ratio and the BA replacement rate to optimize the paste fluidity.

Effects of BA replacement ratio and water-to-binder ratio on the fluidity of cement paste.

Mechanical performance

Figure 5 shows the effects of different water-cement ratios and BA replacement rates on the compressive strength of cement paste at 3-day, 7-day, and 28-day curing ages. When the BA replacement rate is 0% (i.e., pure cement paste), the 28-day compressive strengths for w/b ratios of 0.6, 0.7, and 0.8 are 27.86 MPa, 21.56 MPa, and 12.78 MPa, respectively. It can be seen from the figure that under the studied water-cement ratio conditions, the compressive strength at each curing age first increases and then gradually decreases with the increase of BA replacement rate. When the BA replacement rate is 20%, the compressive strength of the cement paste shows a significant inflection point and is slightly higher than that of the pure cement paste. The compressive strengths of different water-cement ratios are almost the same as that of the pure cement paste. After exceeding 20%, the compressive strength gradually decreases. This indicates that an appropriate amount of BA promotes pozzolanic hydration, while an excessive amount of BA inhibits hydration and strength development.

Effect of BA and w/b on the compressive strength of paste.

To efficiently utilize BA solid waste for preparing cement-based supplementary cementitious materials, it is necessary to select the optimal BA replacement rate for cement-based cementitious materials under the premise of achieving the optimal 28-day strength of the paste. When the w/b is 0.6, a 20% BA replacement rate corresponds to a 28-day strength of 36.11 MPa, which is a 29.6% increase compared to pure cement paste; a 30% replacement rate corresponds to a 28-day strength of 28.56 MPa, which is a 2.51% increase compared to pure cement paste. Therefore, when w/b = 0.6, the optimal cement replacement rate is approximately 20%. When w/b = 0.7, a 20% replacement rate corresponds to a 28-day strength of 28.83 MPa, which is a 33.72% increase compared to pure cement paste; a 30% replacement rate corresponds to a 28-day strength of 21.65 MPa, which is a 0.42% increase compared to pure cement paste. When w/b = 0.8, a 30% replacement rate corresponds to a 28-day strength of 14.82 MPa, which is a 15.96% increase compared to pure cement paste. It can be seen that the optimal BA replacement rate for cement when w/b = 0.8 is similar to that when w/b = 0.6 and 0.7, all being approximately 20%.

The change in the strength of solid waste-based cementitious materials is mainly driven by the interactions between the components of solid waste13. When BA is incorporated, the SiO2 content in the material increases significantly, which promotes the dissolution of stable silicon and a small amount of aluminum in the solid waste, making them enter an active state31,32. As chemical bonds break and reorganize, dissociated Si–O participates in complex polymerization reactions of calcium and silicon, ultimately forming C–S–H gel. C–S–H gel exhibits short-range order and long-range disorder, which hinders the formation of stable crystal structures. The resulting three-dimensional aluminosilicate network structure significantly improves the compressive strength of the material33. When the BA replacement rate exceeds 20%, the proportion of cement per unit volume gradually decreases, leading to a slowdown in the pozzolanic reaction rate of BA. In addition, the reaction of BA requires Ca(OH)2 generated by cement hydration as a reactant. Excessive BA will compete with the limited Ca(OH)2 produced by cement hydration, resulting in incomplete reactions of both.

The macroscopic results demonstrate that BA replacement significantly affects the workability and compressive strength of cement paste, with an optimal performance at a 20% replacement rate. To reveal the intrinsic mechanism underlying these macroscopic changes, it is essential to systematically characterize the hydration product evolution and microstructural development. Therefore, multi-scale microscopic tests including XRD, FTIR, TG-DTG, SEM, and MIP were conducted to analyze the phase composition, chemical bonding, thermal behavior, morphology, and pore structure of hardened pastes, aiming to establish a clear correlation between microstructural characteristics and macroscopic mechanical properties.

Microscopic characterization

XRD

All microstructural characterizations (XRD, FTIR, TG-DTG, SEM, MIP) were carried out on specimens cured for 28 days. Therefore, the following analysis focuses on the final hydration products, microstructural morphology, and pore structure, as well as their correlation with the 28 days compressive strength. The XRD patterns of different BA replacement rates and different water-to-binder ratios are shown in Fig. 6. The results in the figure indicate that portlandite, SiO2, CaCO3, and C–S–H gel were detected in all the different samples. The intensity of portlandite showed a systematic and significant decrease with the increase in BA replacement rate. In contrast, the intensity of the main peak of crystalline silica derived from BA increased with the increase in BA content. Ca(OH)2 is one of the main products of cement hydration. The decrease in its peak intensity indicates that the abundant active amorphous SiO2 in BA undergoes a secondary reaction with Ca(OH)2, producing more low-crystallinity products such as C–S–H gel. C–S–H gel helps cementitious materials refine pores, optimize the interfacial transition zone, and thereby improve the mechanical properties of the material. This process consumes Ca(OH)2 in the system, leading to a decrease in its XRD diffraction intensity34. Meanwhile, the enhancement of SiO2 peak intensity directly results from the introduction of BA, among which quartz is an inert crystalline phase that does not participate in the reaction but contributes to the physical filling of the microstructure. The above changes directly confirm the pozzolanic reactivity of BA.

XRD analysis of different (a) BA replacement rates, (b) different w/b ratios.

The XRD patterns with different water-to-binder ratios show similar phase compositions. When the water-to-binder ratio is 0.6, by increasing the particle concentration and the ion supersaturation in the pore solution, the hydration process of cement clinker minerals and the pozzolanic reaction of BA are effectively promoted, which is beneficial to the formation of more abundant C–S–H gel and optimizes the compactness of the microstructure. As the water-to-binder ratio increases from 0.6 to 0.8, the intensity of the characteristic diffraction peak of portlandite shows a systematic decreasing trend, which indicates that under the condition of a high water-to-binder ratio, the degree of cement hydration reaction decreases, resulting in a reduction in the production of the main hydration product Ca(OH)2. At the same time, the characteristic peak of crystalline SiO₂ from BA remains relatively stable, which verifies its phase characteristics as an inert admixture35,36. The increase in the water-to-binder ratio significantly changes the pore solution environment of the system. Excessive free water not only dilutes the cement particle concentration and reduces the hydration reaction rate but also creates channels conducive to CO2 diffusion, which indirectly leads to a relative enhancement of the CaCO3 diffraction signal, reflecting the intensification of carbonation, thus having an adverse impact on the development of mechanical properties and long-term durability of the system.The decrease in portlandite intensity and consumption of Ca(OH)2 confirm that BA actively participates in pozzolanic reactions, producing additional C–S–H gel. The newly formed gel fills microcracks and refines pore structure, directly increasing matrix density and bonding strength. Meanwhile, residual crystalline SiO2 acts as a microaggregate, further enhancing particle packing. These microstructural changes collectively lead to the observed increase in compressive strength at 20% BA replacement and improved overall mechanical performance.

FTIR

Figure 7 shows the FTIR results of paste under different BA replacement rates. The positions and classifications of FTIR characteristic peaks refer to references37–40. Figure 6a show that the characteristic peak at 3645 cm−1 corresponds to the stretching vibration of the O–H bond in Ca(OH)2, which is a characteristic peak of calcium hydroxide (portlandite) generated by cement hydration. As the BA replacement rate increases from 0% to 20%, the intensity of this peak shows a significant weakening trend. The characteristic peak at 3428 cm−1 is attributed to the stretching vibration of the O–H bond in physically adsorbed water and hydration products such as C–S–H gel. This peak is broader in BA samples, which confirms that the pozzolanic reaction consumes Ca(OH)2 produced by cement hydration and generates additional C–S–H gel. The characteristic peak at 1651 cm−1 corresponds to the bending vibration of H–O–H in water molecules, mainly from interlayer water, adsorbed water, and free water in the C–S–H gel. Its intensity exists in all samples but is slightly stronger in the sample with 30% BA replacement rate, which is related to the high water retention capacity brought by the porous structure of BA and the microstructural changes of the system after BA reaction consumes Ca(OH)2. The characteristic peak at 1414 cm−1 corresponds to the asymmetric stretching vibration of the C-O bond in CO32−, mainly from trace carbonates in raw materials or carbonation of hydration products (CaCO3). The peak intensity is enhanced in BA samples, possibly because BA contains a small amount of unburned carbon components or its porous structure is more prone to carbonation. The characteristic peak at 975 cm−1 is the stretching vibration of the Si–O bond in silicate tetrahedrons, which is the main characteristic peak of C–S–H gel41–43. As the BA replacement rate increases, this peak slightly shifts to a higher wave number from 970 cm−1 to 980 cm−1.

FTIR results of paste: (a) different BA replacement rates; (b) different water-to-bonding agent ratios.

As the water-binder ratio increases from 0.6 to 0.8, the intensity of the O–H characteristic peak at 3645 cm−1 attributed to Ca(OH)2 decreases with the increase of w/b, indicating that a high water-binder ratio dilutes the cement concentration, resulting in a reduction in the amount of Ca(OH)2 generated by early hydration. The characteristic peak of C–S–H gel at 3428 cm−1 increases in intensity and broadens with the increase of w/b, reflecting an increase in the content of free water and bound water in the BA-cement system, but it may be accompanied by an increase in porosity44. The intensity of the 1651 cm−1 H–O–H peak increases with the increase of w/b, directly confirming the increase in the content of physically adsorbed water in the system, which is consistent with the expected high porosity structure under a high water-binder ratio. The peak intensity of the C–O stretching vibration at 1414 cm−1, which belongs to carbonate (CO32−), is relatively stronger in the sample with a high water-binder ratio (0.8), indicating that a high porosity environment is more conducive to the carbonation reaction, leading to more Ca(OH)2 being converted into CaCO3. The Si-O stretching vibration peak at 975 cm−1 is the main characteristic of C–S–H gel. Compared with w/b = 0.8, this peak tends to slightly shift to a higher wave number when w/b = 0.6, which usually means that the generated C–S–H gel has a lower Ca/Si ratio and a higher degree of polymerization45–47. This indicates that under a low water-binder ratio, the higher ion concentration promotes more sufficient hydration and pozzolanic reactions, generating a gel with a better structure.The redshift and broadening of the Si–O stretching band indicate higher polymerization and longer silicate chains in C–S–H gel, which forms a denser and more stable network. The reduced O–H peak intensity from Ca(OH)2 further verifies the pozzolanic consumption of portlandite. A more polymerized C–S–H gel with a low Ca/Si ratio enhances interparticle bonding and reduces porosity, which is the microscopic origin of improved compressive strength and optimal performance at 20% BA replacement.

TG/DTG

The TG-DTG curves of BA-cement composite cementitious materials with different water-to-binder ratios show significant differences in their thermal evolution behavior with varying water-to-binder ratios, which can be clearly divided into three key stages: In the C–S–H gel dehydration stage at 0 ~ 200 ℃, the system with w/b = 0.8 exhibits a faster mass loss rate due to its higher initial free water content. Entering the Ca(OH)2 decomposition stage at 200 ~ 600℃, the DTG curves of the high water-to-binder ratio systems fluctuate more significantly, indicating that the removal process of bound water in their hydration products is more active. In the CaCO3 decomposition stage at 600 ~ 1000℃, the system with w/b = 0.8 shows the maximum weight loss step and DTG peak around 700℃ . This phenomenon reveals that the high water-to-binder ratio system has a loose structure and is more likely to absorb CO2 in the air to form calcium carbonate, while the system with w/b = 0.6 has a dense microstructure, which significantly inhibits the carbonation effect, resulting in the highest residual mass48.

Figure 8b shows the TG-DTG biaxial curves of composite cementitious materials with different BA replacement rates, which can be divided into three key stages: At 0–200 °C, all three curves show rapid weight loss, and with the increase of replacement rate (BA = 30%), the initial weight loss rate is faster and the amplitude is larger. This is because the porous structure of BA can adsorb more free water and adsorbed water, which evaporates first during the heating process. It can be seen from the fluctuation of the DTG curve that the mass loss rate peak of the BA = 30% system is the most significant, confirming that the high replacement rate system has a higher initial water content. At 200–600 °C, the weight loss at this stage is mainly due to the further dehydration of C–S–H gel and the decomposition of Ca(OH)2. With the increase of replacement rate, the weight loss amplitude of the curve gradually decreases, which is because the pozzolanic reaction of BA consumes part of calcium hydroxide and generates more C–S–H gel. The fluctuation of the DTG curve around 400 °C also slows down with the increase of replacement rate, indicating that the content of calcium hydroxide in the high replacement rate system is lower. At 600–1000 °C, the significant weight loss step and DTG peak around 700 °C correspond to the decomposition of CaCO349,50. With the increase of replacement rate, the weight loss amplitude and DTG peak gradually increase, which is because the active SiO2 and Al2O3 introduced by BA promote the carbonation reaction, making the high replacement rate system generate more calcium carbonate. The weight loss of the BA = 30% system is the most obvious, reflecting its stronger carbonation tendency. The final residual mass shows the law of BA30% > BA20% > BA0%, which is because the loss on ignition of BA is higher than that of cement, and more organic matter and easily decomposable components in the high replacement rate system are lost at high temperatures, resulting in a lower proportion of residual solids51,52.

SEM

This series of scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images systematically presents the evolution law of the microstructure of supplementary cementitious materials under different BA replacement rates. Under the low-magnification field of view at 50 μm (Fig. 9a–c), when the replacement rate is BA0% (Fig. 9a), the system is mainly composed of cement hydration products, with only a small amount of loose needle-like ettringite (AFt) crystals visible in local areas. As the replacement rate increases to 20% (Fig. 9b), the pozzolanic activity of BA promotes more sufficient hydration reactions. The figure shows a continuous and dense network formed by the interweaving of hydration products, with a significant reduction in structural pores. When the replacement rate further increases to 30% (Fig. 9c), obvious large pores appear in the system, which is due to the local structural looseness caused by the incomplete reaction of excessive BA particles. Under the high-magnification field of view at 10 μm (Fig. 9d–f), the control group with a replacement rate of BA0% (Fig. 9d) is mainly composed of massive calcium carbonate and calcium hydroxide particles, with only a small amount of gel attached to the surface. When the replacement rate is 20% (Fig. 9e), the figure shows a large number of interwoven structures of fibrous calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) gel and needle-rod-shaped AFt crystals. The active silicon and aluminum components of BA fully participate in the reaction, greatly improving the structural compactness. When the replacement rate reaches 30% (Fig. 9f), the amount of gel produced decreases and obvious pores appear, indicating that an excessively high replacement rate will dilute the concentration of cement clinker, inhibit the continuous growth of hydration products, and thus weaken the microstructural stability of the system56. Overall, a 20% BA replacement rate can synergistically optimize the microstructure by promoting the generation of hydration products and particle filling, while a 30% replacement rate causes structural defects due to incomplete reactions. This micro-evolution law is almost consistent with the changing trend of macroscopic mechanical properties57.

Notably, the microstructural evolution directly governs the macroscopic performance. The dense, well-connected C–S–H network at 20% BA replacement minimizes harmful pores and optimizes particle bonding, which macroscopically manifests as the highest compressive strength. In contrast, the loose matrix and abundant large pores at 30% BA replacement reduce effective load transfer and increase porosity, leading to strength deterioration. MIP

According to previous studies58–60, internal pores can be classified into five categories: gel pores (< 10 nm), micropores (10–100 nm), mesoporous capillary pores (100–1000 nm), macrocapillary pores (1000–10,000 nm), and macropores (> 10,000 nm). Figure 11 shows the pore size distribution under different water-binder ratios and different BA replacement rates. The data on porosity and average pore size are summarized in Table 4. Specifically, from the cumulative pore volume distribution in Fig. 11a, it can be seen that as the replacement rate increases from 0% to 20%, the cumulative pore volume of the samples gradually decreases, indicating that an appropriate amount of BA can refine the pore structure; when the replacement rate further increases to 30%, the cumulative pore volume rebounds, revealing that an excessively high replacement rate will cause deterioration of the pore structure. Figure 11b further shows that the peak pore volume of the sample with BA = 20% shifts to the smaller ranges of gel pores and micropores, confirming that the C–S–H gel generated by the pozzolanic reaction of BA has a refining effect on the pore structure; while the sample with BA = 30% shows a new peak in the macropore range, reflecting the deterioration of the pore structure caused by insufficient cement hydration under high replacement rates. These results indicate that a 20% BA replacement rate can optimize the pore structure through the pozzolanic effect and particle filling; however, an excessively high replacement rate leads to a reduction in the proportion of cement clinker, and the porous structure of BA will absorb more water, weakening the hydration reaction, resulting in insufficient generation of C–S–H gel, deterioration of the pore structure, and an increase in the proportion of macropores.

The mechanism of pore structure evolution is that in the low water-binder ratio system, the water content is low, the cement hydration reaction is more sufficient, and the generated C–S–H gel can effectively fill the pores and refine the pore structure; while in the high water-binder ratio system, the excess free water will leave more connected pores after hydration, and the porous structure of BA will also absorb part of the water, further weakening the filling effect of hydration products, leading to the deterioration of the pore structure and an increase in the proportion of macropores61,62.

The BA replacement rate has a significant two-way regulatory effect on the pore structure of composite cementitious materials: at a lower replacement rate, the particle filling effect and pozzolanic reaction of BA can refine the pore structure; but when the replacement rate increases to 30%, the total porosity of the system increases from 36.80% to 50.26%, among which the volume increments of mesoporous capillary pores and macrocapillary pores are the most prominent, increasing from 0.02 mL/g and 0.13 mL/g to 0.19 mL/g and 0.20 mL/g respectively. This deterioration trend is due to the reduction in the proportion of cement clinker under high replacement rates, insufficient hydration reaction leading to a sharp decrease in the generation of C–S–H gel, and at the same time, the porous structure of BA will absorb free water, further weakening the hydration process, ultimately resulting in a significant increase in the proportion of harmful pores. As the water-binder ratio increases from 0.6 to 0.8, the total porosity of the material increases from 31.77% to 46.32%, and the total pore volume increases synchronously. This trend is due to the excess free water introduced by the high water-binder ratio system remaining as connected pores after hydration. From the perspective of pore size distribution, the volume increment of macrocapillary pores is the most prominent, increasing from 0.11 mL/g.

Conclusions

To explore the influence of cement partially replaced by BA on the macroscopic performance of cement-based pastes, a series of fluidity and compressive strength experiments were carried out in this work. The water-to-binder ratio (w/b) of the prepared pastes was controlled at 0.6, 0.7, and 0.8, while the replacement percentage of cement by BA varied from 0% to 50% by mass. In order to reveal the mechanism underlying the effect of BA replacement level on compressive strength, a variety of microstructural characterization approaches were adopted for systematic comparison and analysis. Particular attention was paid to phase composition, relative content, hydration products, and pore structure characteristics. According to the experimental results and analyses mentioned above, the main conclusions obtained in this study are summarized as follows:

The fluidity of cement paste exhibits a gradual declining trend as the BA replacement ratio rises. This phenomenon can be attributed to the considerably stronger physical water adsorption capacity of BA particles compared with cement grains. Both the early-age and later-age compressive strengths of the paste first increase and then decrease with increasing BA substitution, and the strength reaches an optimal value at approximately 20% replacement level. Considering the dual requirements of retaining satisfactory long-term strength and maximizing the utilization of BA as a cement substitute, the optimal replacement proportion is determined to be around 20%.

When BA is employed as a partial replacement for cement, the silicon-bearing mineral phases in BA take part in the hydration process, and silicon atoms are incorporated into both the silicate chains and the interlayer structure of the C–S–H gel. With an increase in BA content, the relative proportion of silicon-rich phases in the C–S–H gel increases noticeably. It is observed that a moderately high BA content contributes to the formation of C–S–H gels with longer average chain lengths and higher polymerization degrees. Such a structure densifies the chemical framework of the hardened paste, strengthens the interfacial bonding between the gel phase and other mineral constituents, and thus enhances the macroscopic mechanical performance of cement pastes with various water-to-binder ratios. These results offer a reasonable interpretation for the mechanical evolution: the long-term strength of the paste is improved rather than weakened when the BA replacement ratio is 20%.

Analyses of pore size distribution, total porosity, and mean pore diameter indicate that both BA replacement ratio and water-to-binder ratio exert significant regulatory effects on the pore structure of cementitious composites. At a BA replacement rate of 20%, the combined effects of particle filling and pozzolanic reaction efficiently refine the pore structure, reduce the average pore size, and maintain a relatively low total porosity. When the replacement ratio rises to 30%, the reduced cement clinker content leads to insufficient hydration; coupled with the porous and water-absorptive nature of BA, the total porosity increases sharply, accompanied by an elevated proportion of macropores and harmful pores, resulting in an obvious degradation of the pore structure. Meanwhile, increasing the water-to-binder ratio from 0.6 to 0.8 significantly raises the cumulative pore volume and overall porosity, driving the pore distribution toward mesopores and large capillary pores that are detrimental to mechanical performance, thus leading to a looser microstructure. In contrast, the system with a lower water-to-binder ratio achieves more sufficient hydration and stronger filling effects of C-S-H gel, forming a compact pore structure dominated by harmless pores.

From an engineering perspective, considering comprehensive factors including mechanical performance, workability, economic benefit, and resource utilization efficiency, the optimal BA replacement range for practical applications is recommended as 15–20%. This range ensures satisfactory workability, significantly enhanced compressive strength (up to 29.6% improvement), and efficient solid waste utilization, balancing performance and cost. It should be noted that this study employed only one type of BA derived from wheat straw under fixed combustion and pre-treatment conditions. Since BA composition and reactivity are highly dependent on biomass type, combustion temperature, atmosphere, and post‑processing, the conclusions obtained in this work may show certain variability when applied to BA from other sources (e.g., wood, rice straw, corn stalk) or different combustion systems.The findings provide a reliable theoretical basis and technical reference for the large-scale engineering application of BA as a sustainable supplementary cementitious material.
