Photophysical model of primary charge separation in green algal photosystem I based on combined analysis of time-resolved absorption and fluorescence spectra
Authors: Wojciech Giera, Krzysztof Gibasiewicz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The mechanism of primary charge separation in photosynthetic reaction centers is still not fully understood. In the case of photosystem I (PSI), the study of this process is particularly difficult due to the integration of the reaction center (RC) with the core antenna and presence of so-called red chlorophylls in the antenna system. Their excitation dynamics overlap spectrally and temporarily with the primary charge separation in RC. Therefore, we performed time-resolved absorption measurements (femtosecond pump-probe technique) for the red chlorophyll-free PSI core from green algae Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, both under reducing (open RC) and oxidizing (closed RC) conditions. Kinetic modeling of the recorded absorption changes indicated the same trapping rate of excitations by open and closed RC. Detailed spectral analysis showed, in turn, that processes related to the primary charge separation can be described by temporal evolution of several main well-defined spectral bands (with different contributions in the case of open and closed RC). We explained and thoroughly discussed their origin in the context of the excitonic coupling between the RC chlorophylls. We also implemented the identical kinetic model to the time-resolved fluorescence data published previously for exactly the same PSI preparation (Giera et al. 2014; Biochim Biophys Acta – Bioenergetics 1837:1756–1768). This combined analysis allowed us to identify different emission properties of the primary state generated in the open and closed RC after excitation trapping. The nature of this state is discussed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11120-025-01176-z.

Introduction

The light-dependent phase of oxygenic photosynthesis is carried out by two pigment-protein complexes embedded in the thylakoid membrane, known as photosystem I (PSI) and photosystem II (PSII) (Blankenship 2002). They cooperate together to perform the light-driven non-cyclic transport of electrons from water molecule to NADP+ resulting in its reduction to NADPH. Electron transport between PSI and PSII is coupled with the proton transport across the thylakoid membrane against the pH gradient. The electrochemical potential generated in this way drives the synthesis of ATP carried out by the ATP synthase which is also located in the thylakoid membrane. The electrons collected in NADPH molecules and the energy stored in ATP are then used to reduce carbon dioxide to sugars in the light-independent phase of photosynthesis.

The overall architecture of the PSI differs between groups of organisms performing oxygenic photosynthesis. Cyanobacterial PSI usually occurs as a trimer (Jordan et al. 2001) whereas algal and plant PSI complexes have a monomeric form but are equipped with additional LHCI antenna system composed of four similar light harvesting subunits in the case of plants (Sheller et al. 2001, Ben-Shem et al. 2003; Amunts et al. 2007; Mazor et al. 2015; Suga et al. 2016; Mazor et al. 2017) and most probably nine or ten subunits in the case of algae (Germano et al. 2002; Kargul et al. 2003; Drop et al. 2011; Su et al. 2019; Suga et al. 2019; see Figure S1A in Supplementary Information). Some cyanobacteria may also possess membrane-embedded light-harvesting complexes (Yeremenko et al. 2004; Bumba et al. 2005; Barber et al. 2006; Akita et al. 2020), that are composed of: (1) iron-stress-inducible A protein (IsiA), (2) prochlorophyte chlorophyll a/b protein (Pcb). The chlorophyll a-binding IsiA protein is expressed in many cyanobacteria under various stress conditions (Vinnemeier et al. 1998; Havaux et al. 2005), especially due to iron starvation (Guikema and Sherman 1983; Burnap et al. 1993; Barber et al. 2006). The Pcb proteins occurs in cyanobacteria that do not contain phycobiliproteins and are often termed prochlorophytes (Barber et al. 2006). Structurally, both IsiA and Pcb are closely related to the CP43 subunit of the PSII (Pakrasi et al. 1985; Laudenbach and Straus 1988; La Roche et al. 1996; Barber et al. 2006).

The protein structure and pigment composition of LHCI-free monomeric PSI (so called PSI core) was found to be very similar in the case of cyanobacteria and plants (Jordan et al. 2001; Ben-Shem et al. 2003; Jensen et al. 2003; Haldrup et al. 2006; Mazor et al. 2015, 2017; Suga et al. 2016). The PSI core binds a large number of cofactors, including ~ 100 chlorophylls a, 2 quinones, 3 iron-sulfur clusters (Fe4S4) and 22 beta-carotenes (Jordan et al. 2001; Ben-Shem et al. 2003; Haldrup et al. 2006; Jensen et al. 2003). Most of the core antenna chlorophylls and carotenoids form a ring-like antenna system directly coupled to the electron transport chain (ETC) located in the central part of the complex (Figure S1A in Supplementary Information). Most of cofactors constituting these two functional groups of PSI core are bound to the same polypeptides and therefore the ETC cannot be separated biochemically from the antenna system.

The PSI ETC is composed of six chlorophylls, two quinones and three iron-sulfur clusters (Ben-Shem et al. 2003; Haldrup et al. 2006; Jensen et al. 2003; Jordan et al. 2001; see Figure S1D in Supplementary Information). Chlorophylls and quinones are organized in two quasi-symmetrical branches (A and B), both active in the electron transfer (Guergova-Kuras et al. 2001; Ramesh et al. 2004; Giera et al. 2009). Their common beginning is a heterodimer of chlorophyll a (ec1B or PB) and chlorophyll a’ (ec1A or PA), traditionally referred to as P700. The next pair of cofactors in each branch are two chlorophylls a: ec2B and ec3A in branch A or ec2A and ec3B in branch B. The first pigment in each pair (ec2A/ec2B) is very often called an accessory chlorophyll (labeled A or Aacc) whereas the second one (ec3A/ec3B) is marked as A0, and it is considered to be the primary electron acceptor. It is still matter of debate which cofactors works as primary electron donors: P700 or accessory chlorophylls (Müller et al. 2003, 2010; Holzwarth et al. 2005; Giera et al. 2010; Russo et al. 2020). The next electron acceptors (A1) following the A0 in both branches are the phylloquinone molecules denoted as PhQA in branch A and PhQB in branch B. Both branches meet together in the iron-sulfur cluster called FX and the final electron acceptors are two other iron-sulfur clusters marked as FA and FB. The ETC cofactors involved directly in the primary charge separation, together with their protein framework, are commonly referred to as reaction center (RC).

Chlorophyll a, dominating the pigment content of PSI, has two main absorption bands: (1) Soret band in the blue spectral region and (2) Qy band in the red part of light spectrum (Papageorgiou 2004). The maximum of the Qy band is observed at ~ 660 nm for chlorophyll a molecules in the organic solvents and is red-shifted by ~ 15 nm in the case of antenna chlorophylls, due to their interaction with the protein matrix. Chlorophyll a exhibits also fluorescence in the Qy band that is Stokes-shifted by another 5–6 nm and in a solution characterized by a lifetime of ~ 6 ns (Blankenship 2002). However, the fluorescence lifetime (and fluorescence quantum yield) of chlorophylls in the PSI is drastically decreased to ~ 20 ps due to very quick quenching by the RC (Holzwarth et al. 2005; Ihalainen et al. 2005a; Giera et al. 2010, 2014; Wientjes et al. 2011). While in the PSI with open RC this quenching is caused by the charge separation process, in the case of PSI with closed RC (with P700 preoxidized to P700+) the exact mechanism is not fully understood. In the literature, two alternative models were proposed: (1) Due to broad absorption band extending into the far-red spectral region, the P700+ accept effectively excitation energy from the antenna chlorophylls and then excited (P700+)* decays quickly by the internal conversion (Byrdin et al. 2000); (2) The primary charge separation still occurs in RC and leads to formation of A+A0– radical pair, however, positive charge present on P700+ promotes fast charge recombination, A+A0– → AA0, and radiationless return to the ground state (Giera et al. 2010, 2014, 2018; Russo et al. 2020).

PSI complexes possess some unique property: they contain a relatively large amount of so-called red/long-wavelength/low-energy chlorophylls absorbing and emitting the light in the spectral region strongly red-shifted compared to the typical antenna chlorophylls (commonly called bulk chlorophylls) (Karapetyan 2006). However, the presence of red chlorophylls in the individual antenna systems and their spectral properties differ between organisms. For example, it was shown that absorption of red chlorophylls in green algal PSI-LHCI reaches maximum at ~ 700 nm (Gibasiewicz et al. 2005) whereas their fluorescence peaks at ~ 715 nm (Giera et al. 2014, 2018). Two factors responsible for this long-wavelength transitions are mentioned in the literature: (1) very strong excitonic interaction between two or more pigments and (2) mixing of the excited states with the charge transfer states. In model calculations, two identical excitonically coupled molecules (monomers) with single excited state each behave like a single supermolecule with two electronic excited states delocalized over the two interacting molecules (van Amerongen et al. 2000; Parson 2007). Energetically, one of the excitonic states is located above the excited state of monomer, whereas the second one is below, and the energy gap between them is determined by the strength of the interaction between pigments (Figure S1C in Supplementary Information). The optical transition involving the lower excitonic state would be responsible for the long-wavelength absorption and emission of red chlorophylls. Excitonic interaction alone, however, cannot explain other specific spectral properties of red chlorophylls: the huge bandwidth, of the order of 200–400 cm− 1 (~ 10–20 nm), and unusually large Stokes’ shift ranging from 160 to 420 cm− 1 (~ 8–22 nm) (Frese at al. 2002, Croce et al. 2007; Gobets et al. 1994; Ihalainen et al. 2000; Rätsep et al. 2000; Pålsson et al. 1996; Hayes et al. 2000; Cometta et al. 2000). Various studies show that the possible origin of these phenomena may be mixing of the lowest exciton state with the charge transfer state of chlorophyll dimer (Frese et al. 2002; Vaitekonis et al. 2005; Romero et al. 2009; Novoderezhkin et al. 2016). The charge transfer states are characterized by a large dipole moment that may strongly interact with the polar protein environment. Strong coupling to the environmental degrees of freedom leads to substantial broadening on the red side of the spectrum and consequently to a relatively large Stokes’ shift (Figure S1C in Supplementary Information).

After light is absorbed and transformed into excited state by one of the antenna chlorophylls, a number of excitation transfer processes take place in PSI on a similar time scale (Figure S1B in Supplementary Information): (1) transfer between bulk and red antenna chlorophylls, (2) transfer from LHCI to PSI core, and finally (3) transfer to RC and trapping due to charge separation. For example, in green algal PSI-LHCI complexes (1) the excitation energy equilibration between bulk and red chlorophylls occurs with a ~ 6-ps lifetime, (2) the average effective time of excitation transfer from LHCI antenna system to PSI core is equal to ~ 12–15 ps and is primarily determined by the time of excitation escape from the red chlorophylls to the bulk pool, (3) the excitation from PSI core bulk chlorophylls is effectively trapped in RC with an average time of ~ 14 ps (Giera et al. 2014). These numbers exemplify that the dynamics of red chlorophylls’ excited states overlap temporarily with the excitation trapping in RC. This makes the time-resolved absorption and emission data difficult to interpret in the context of the primary charge separation in RC, which is also composed of strongly interacting chlorophylls. Therefore, PSI preparations devoid of red chlorophylls would be most appropriate for the studies of charge separation in RC. So far, only the time-resolved emission measurements have been carried out for two such preparations: green algal PSI core (Giera et al. 2010, 2014, 2018) and cyanobacterial red chlorophyll-free mutant (Van Stokkum et al. 2013). The green algal PSI core, unlike its plant and cyanobacterial counterparts, does not contain the typical red chlorophylls (Giera et al. 2014, 2018). The long-wavelength emission observed in the algal PSI-LHCI complexes comes exclusively from the red forms located in the LHCI antennas (Giera et al. 2014, 2018).

In this paper, we present the results of time-resolved absorption measurements performed for the red chlorophyll-free PSI core preparation from the green alga Chlamydomonas reinhardtii with neutral P700. The absence of red chlorophylls in the investigated system allowed us to relate the temporal evolution of the registered absorption changes with the events occurring in the RC during the primary charge separation. Our analysis is not limited to giving a general kinetic diagram of this process. We also attempted to determine the origin of the individual bands that make up the spectra of RC states formed directly after excitation trapping. The interpretation was supported by results of complementary studies performed for PSI cores with P700 chemically preoxidized to P700+ and extended analysis of time-resolved fluorescence data obtained previously for exactly the same preparation (Giera et al. 2014). To understand the absorption changes that occur during the primary charge separation, it is necessary to take into account the exciton interactions in the RC. Therefore, we supplemented our work with a small review of the exciton coupling studies carried out so far for PSI RC and discussed our present results in the context of all these studies.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

The investigated PSI core particles were isolated from the CC2696 strain of the green alga Chlamydomonas reinhardtii (Chlamydomonas Culture Collection at Duke University) carrying a deletion in the chloroplast psbA gene and the DS-521 nuclear mutation. These genetic modifications leads to (1) a complete loss of Photosystem II, (2) a 90% reduction in LHCII content and (3) some minor modifications in the polypeptide composition of LHCI (Owens et al. 1989). Procedures of cell growth, thylakoid isolation, PSI extraction and purification were described previously (Giera et al. 2014). Briefly, the heterotrophically grown CC2696 cells (Lee et al. 1998) were disrupted in the French press and the thylakoids were collected by ultracentrifugation (Chua and Bennoun 1975). The PSI core particles were extracted from thylakoid membranes by detergent (n-dodecyl-β-D-maltoside) treatment and purified by weak anion exchange chromatography (Ramesh and Webber 2004). This purification procedure yields PSI preparations with a Chl a/P700 ratio of 85–95 (Ramesh and Webber 2004) that is very similar to that specified by structural studies for the PSI core of cyanobacteria (96 Chls, Jordan et al. 2001) and higher plants (93 Chls, Ben-Shem et al. 2003). It should be noted that exactly the same algal PSI core preparation has already been thoroughly investigated using ultrafast time-resolved fluorescence spectroscopy (Giera et al. 2014, 2018).

For spectroscopic measurements, the isolated PSI core particles were dissolved in a buffer containing 50 mM Hepes (pH = 7.2), 5 mM MgCl2, 12 mM CaCl2, 20% glycerol (v/v), 1 mM benzamidine, 1 mM PMSF (phenyl-methyl-sulfonyl fluoride), 1 mM EDTA, and 0.03% n-dodecyl-β-D-maltoside. The P700 was kept neutral (open RC) by addition of 80 mM sodium ascorbate and 160 µM phenazine methosulfate (PMS) or oxidized (closed RC) by addition of 12.5 mM K3Fe(CN)6 (potassium ferricyanide). PMS reduced by sodium ascorbate is the most popular reducing agent used to keep PSI open (Giera et al. 2010, 2014; Ihalainen et al. 2005a; Gobets et al. 2001; Engelmann et al. 2006; Slavov et al. 2008; Byrdin et al. 2000; Turconi et al. 1993; Karapetyan et al. 1997; Nuijs et al. 1986). Examples of the use of ferricyanide to the chemical oxidation of P700 can also be found in the literature (Blankenship et al. 1975; Evans et al. 1977; Krawczyk and Maksymiec 1991; Giera et al. 2010, 2014; Schlodder et al. 2014). Finally, the PSI core solutions (with OD at 676 nm equal to ~ 0.6 for 1-mm optical path length) were loaded into 1-mm thick cuvette that was placed on the 2D mechanical motion controller allowing sample movement in horizontal and vertical direction. As a result, the laser beams “scanned” the sample in the Lissajous-like pattern, that prevented exposure of the same sample volume to multiple laser shots and allowed to avoid sample degradation due to excessive irradiation.

Time-resolved absorption spectroscopy

Ultrafast time-resolved absorption experiments were performed using the pump-probe setup working in the Laser Center at the Vrije Universiteit (LCVU) in Amsterdam (described in details by Berera et al. 2009; a simplified scheme is shown in the Figure S2A in Supplementary Information). The 800-nm seed pulses produced by the broadband oscillator (Coherent Vitesse) were amplified by the Ti:sapphire-based regenerative amplifier (Coherent Legend-Ultra Short Pulse) pumped at 527 nm by the Nd:YLF laser (Coherent Evolution). The output from the laser system (40-fs pulses centered at 800 nm with an energy of 2.5 mJ and a repetition rate of 1 kHz) was sent through an optical delay line (consisting of a retroreflector mounted on a high-precision motorized computer-controlled translation stage) to vary the time delay between the pump and probe pulses, and then directed on the crystal for the second harmonic generation (400 nm). After passing through the interference filter (401.9 ± 10 nm), excitation pulses were focused in the sample to a diameter of ~ 300 μm. The energy of single pump pulse was set at 17 nJ for PSI core with open RC and 38 nJ for PSI core with closed RC (in the latter case half of the pump pulse energy was absorbed by potassium ferricyanide). A part of the amplified 800-nm light was transmitted through the calcium fluoride plate to generate a white-light continuum that was used as a broadband probe of the pump-induced absorbance changes. White-light probe pulses were focused on the sample (to a diameter slightly smaller than that of the pump beam) and spatially overlapped with the pump. After passing through the sample, the probe beam was directed into a spectrograph to be spectrally dispersed and then projected on the photodiode array for detection. The diode array signal was read out by a computer after each shot.

The pump-probe experiment was conducted as follows. First, the time delay (t) between the excitation and the probe pulses was fixed. The excitation beam, before reaching the sample, was passing additionally through a mechanical chopper that was synchronized with the regenerative amplifier in such a way that every second excitation pulse was blocked. Consequently, the detector alternately measured the spectral distribution of probe beam intensity (with respect to wavelength λ) for the excited and not excited sample (I(λ,t)e and I(λ,t)n, respectively). The signal received by each diode from 1000 laser shots (500 for excited and 500 form not excited sample) was registered and the average values of I(λ,t)e and I(λ,t)n were used to calculate the absorbance difference spectrum for a given time delay:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta A\left({\lambda,t} \right)= - \log \frac{{I{{\left({\lambda,t} \right)}_e}}}{{I{{\left({\lambda,t} \right)}_n}}}$$\end{document}

After that the delay line was moved to another position (defining the time delay between pump and probe) and the above procedure was repeated. Altogether, we collected absorbance difference spectra at 179 time delays between − 10 ps and 2 ns. Many spectra were registered around the time when pump and probe pulse overlapped temporarily to determine precisely the “time zero” and the instrument response function. We conducted 10 scans between − 10 ps and 2 ns for each sample and averaged the obtained absorbance difference spectra to improve the signal-to-noise ratio.

In general, the signal measured in the pump-probe experiment may contain four different contributions: (1) photobleaching, (2) stimulated emission, (3) excited state absorption, (4) photoproduct absorption. Details regarding the origin and participation of these components in the spectra of absorbance changes can be found in Supplementary Information (Figure S2B and Text S1).

Data analysis

Time-resolved absorbance difference spectra collected for PSI core preparations with open and closed RC were first analyzed globally from 630 to 740 nm with ~ 1.2-nm resolution using the GLOTARAN software (Snellenburg et al. 2012). The purpose of global analysis is to describe the observed time-resolved signal ΔA(λ,t) by the sum of several components with an exponential time evolution, identical to all detection wavelengths (Holzwarth 1996; see Figure S2C in Supplementary Information):2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta A\left({\lambda,t} \right)=\sum\limits_{{j=1}}^{n} {{a_j}} \left(\lambda \right) \cdot \exp \left({ - t/{\tau _j}} \right)$$\end{document}

Each component is characterized by the decay time τj and the spectral distribution of the pre-exponential factor aj(λ), which is commonly called the decay-associated spectrum (DAS).

Global analysis constitute only a mathematical description of the experimental data (in the form of the sum of exponential functions) and the parameters obtained in this way (decay times and decay-associated spectra) are rather used for general inference about the functioning of the investigated system and/or indirect estimation of some physical parameters characterizing the tested object. However, the opposite approach can be used, in which a certain physical model consisting of a certain number of individuals (compartments) and the possible transitions between them is first assumed (see Figure S2C in Supplementary Information), and then the parameters of the model are fitted to the experimental data. Such modeling is called target analysis and provides the spectra of assumed individuals (SAS, species-associated spectra) and the rate constants of transitions between them. We performed such modeling for both the new time-resolved absorption data presented in this paper and the time-resolved fluorescence data for the PSI core published previously (Giera et al. 2014). Theoretical principles related to the global and target analysis can be found in (van Stokkum et al. 2004).

Results

Global analysis

Global analysis of the time-resolved absorption data obtained for PSI core with open RC revealed the existence of five decay components with decay times of 0.2 ps, 4.6 ps, 23 ps, 605 ps and 23 ns. Decay associated spectra (DAS) of all components are shown in Fig. 1B. The conservative spectral shape of 0.2-ps DAS (similar integrated areas under positive and negative parts of spectrum) indicates a very fast equilibration process between the most blue-shifted chlorophylls and the longer-wavelengths chlorophylls of the core antenna system. It is worth emphasizing that this rapid equilibration only occurs within bulk chlorophylls and does not include excitation energy transfer to red chlorophylls that are essentially missing in the algal PSI core. The long-lived component, approximated by the 23-ns lifetime, with (a negative) maximum at around 695 nm has a shape characteristic of PSI RC with photo-oxidized P700, in the literature commonly regarded as the (P700+-P700) difference spectrum (Krabben et al. 2000; Webber and Lubitz 2001; Holzwarth et al. 2006; van Stokkum et al. 2024). The second and third components (with decay times of 4.6 ps and 23 ps, respectively) can be ascribed to the excitation decay due to its trapping in RC and evolution of the initial charge separated states within RC chlorophylls (Müller et al. 2003; Holzwarth et al. 2005; Giera et al. 2010, 2014, 2018; Russo et al. 2020). The negative parts of these spectra reflect the decay of photobleaching and stimulated emission of the excited antenna chlorophylls and, in the case of the 23-ps component, additionally decay of photobleaching of RC chlorophylls involved in the primary charge-separated state (see justification of these assignments below). The positive long-wavelength parts of the 4.6 ps and 23 ps DASes (coinciding spectrally with the negative band of the 23-ns component) can be attributed to the rise of photobleaching due to oxidation of P700 and other possible long-lived photo-induced changes in RC (more details in the Discussion section). The intermediate component, with decay time of 605 ps and relatively low amplitude, originate most probably from some small amount of chlorophyll poorly coupled to the rest of the core antenna system (referred to as uncoupled chlorophylls; see below for more details).

The decay of the time-resolved absorption signal detected for PSI core with closed RC can be well described by only four components with decay times of 0.2 ps, 4.5 ps, 18 ps and 4.7 ns. Decay associated spectra (DAS) of these components are shown in Fig. 1E. The origin of 0.2-ps component is the same as in the case of PSI core with open RC: rapid excitation equilibration within bulk antenna chlorophylls. However, the dynamics in a longer time scale differs from that observed for PSI core with open RC. Since the P700 is now chemically pre-oxidized, we do not observe the signal evolution associated with the formation of a long-lived charge separated state: (1) the long-lived component with maximum at ~ 695 nm is absent, (2) in the spectra of the 4.5-ps and 18-ps components we no longer observe a characteristic pronounced long-wavelength (above 690 nm) positive amplitudes associated with the formation of P700+ (and other possible long-lived photo-induced changes in RC). The excitation decay of uncoupled chlorophylls is now described by a component with decay time of 4.7 ns.

The global analysis showed that the tested PSI preparations contained a small amount of so-called uncoupled chlorophylls, characterized by a much longer excitation decay time (605 ps for PSI core with open RC and 4.7 for PSI with closed RC) than in the case of chlorophylls well coupled to the RC (components with decay times of ~ 4.5 ps and 18/23 ps). The possible origin and dynamics of this long-lived signal was thoroughly discussed in one of our previous publications (Giera et al. 2014; see Chap. 4.2. Origin of the slow fluorescence decay). In the case of our PSI core preparations, this signal may come from free chlorophylls in solution or trace amount of LHCI proteins: free monomers not attached to the PSI core or only weakly bound to the core antenna system. We exclude contamination with LHCII proteins because the PSI core complexes were isolated from a strain with drastically reduced LHCII content (see Materials and methods section). In the above-mentioned cases, the deactivation of the excited state of chlorophylls is mainly achieved by fluorescence and non-radiative decay, however, in the LHCI proteins we also deal with some internal quenching processes (Ihalainen et al. 2005b). Consequently, the excitation decay of uncoupled chlorophylls is described by the entire set of lifetimes, ranging from a few hundred picoseconds to about 5 nanoseconds (Giera et al. 2014; Ihalainen et al. 2005b). However, due to the small contribution of the uncoupled chlorophylls to the total signal of absorbance changes we were able to fit only a single component describing the decay of their excitation (adding additional components leads to numerical artifacts – mutually compensating spectra). The difference between the average lifetimes of the uncoupled chlorophylls’ excitation found for the PSI core preparations with open and closed RC (605 ps vs. 4.7 ns) may be surprising at first glance. However, it is important to remember the limitations of the global analysis. In addition to the above-mentioned small contribution of uncoupled chlorophylls to the total signal, the average lifetime of their excitation falls on the border of the time window (~ 2 ns). In the case of PSI with closed RC, the excited uncoupled chlorophylls are the longest-lived state. In the case of a PSI core with open RC, such a longest-lived individual is a charge separated state modeled with a decay time of ~ 23 ns. The presence of an additional nanosecond component most likely leads to an artificial shortening of the decay time of the uncoupled chlorophylls fitted for PSI core with open RC. Obviously, one can consider the possibility that the 605-ps component describe the very slow excitation equilibration between some weakly bound LHCI proteins and PSI core, followed by the much faster excitation trapping in the RC. However, such equilibration is then a significantly rate-limiting process and has no impact on the fast evolution of the signal coming from the overwhelming majority of correctly coupled systems (decay times of 4.6 ps and 23 ps).

Target analysis

The signals originating from the excited states of chlorophylls and the charge separated states involving RC chlorophylls overlaps spectrally, hence the more precise inference based on the global analysis results is practically not possible. Therefore, target analysis for the obtained absorption time-resolved data was performed. The number of physical states in the models used in target analysis was adjusted to the number of decay components received in the global analysis. And so in the case of PSI core with open RC we applied the following five compartments (Fig. 1A): (1) Bulk – associated with the excited states of antenna chlorophylls before equilibration (the name bulk is used especially for a clear distinction from red chlorophylls, practically absent in the studied PSI core but clearly observed in other PSI preparations); (2) Bulk_eq – associated with the excited states of antenna chlorophylls after energy equilibration; (3) S1 – related to the first detectable state created after trapping the excitation in the RC; (4) S2 – describing the final charge separated state observed in a time scale of the experiment; (5) Unc_Chl – representing the small pool of uncoupled chlorophylls present in the investigated PSI preparation. The rapid equilibration among bulk chlorophylls was described simply as unidirectional transition from not equilibrated state (Bulk) to equilibrated one (Bulk_eq) with fixed rate constant (5 000 ns− 1) being the reciprocal of the decay time of the fastest component in the global analysis (200 fs). Such a sub-picosecond equilibration process has also been observed in some previous studies (Müller et al. 2003; van Stokkum et al. 2024). The transition from Bulk_eq to S1 state was modeled as a reversible reaction (as in many other previous works applying similar models: Müller et al. 2003; Holzwarth et al. 2005; Holzwarth et al. 2006; Slavov et al. 2008; Slavov et al. 2009; Giera et al. 2010; van Stokkum et al. 2013; Russo et al. 2020), whereas formation and decay of S2 state as unidirectional processes. For the record, we mention that there are recent works suggesting irreversible charge separation in PSI RC (van Stokkum et al. 2023; van Stokkum et al. 2024). We assumed that excited states of uncoupled chlorophylls decay independently with the fixed rate constant (1.65 ns− 1) leading to decay time of 605 ps observed in the global analysis. The quite similar model was applied for PSI core with closed RC with only two exceptions (Fig. 1D): (1) S2 state was no longer included in the modeling (because in the global analysis we did not observe the formation of a long-lived charge separated state); (2) the decay rate constant of uncoupled chlorophylls’ excited states was fixed on 0.21 ns− 1 to get the same decay time as observed in the global analysis (4.7 ns).

Figure 1 presents the spectra of particular components (Figs. 1C/F) and transitions rates obtained in target analysis (Figs. 1A/D) for PSI core with open and closed RC (for clarity of the graph, the Unc_Chl spectra are not shown). In both cases the spectrum of the Bulk state is centered at ~ 680 nm whereas the Bulk_eq is shifted to longer wavelength by about 3–4 nm. Interestingly, the forward and backward rate constants in the Bulk_eq-S1 transition are practically the same in the case of open and closed RC and equal to 175 ns− 1 and 29–32 ns− 1 respectively. However, the spectrum of the S1 state depends on the P700 oxidation state. Obviously, the subsequent fate of S1 state is also different for open and closed RC: in the first case, the S1 state evolves into long-lived S2 state with rate constant of 54 ns− 1, whereas in the latter one it simply decays with the rate of 70 ns− 1. Noteworthy, the spectra of the S1 and S2 states found by us for the PSI core with open RC correspond very well with the spectra of the primary and final charge-separated states (respectively) in some previously published models (Müller et al. 2003; van Stokkum et al. 2024).

It is also worth mentioning here that an alternative, more extended model was used in a recent publication by van Stokkum and co-workers to describe the kinetics of excitation transfer and primary charge separation in the PSI core from Chlamydomonas reinhardtii (van Stokkum et al. 2024). It assumes the presence of a certain pool of antenna chlorophylls (~ 10) slowly equilibrating with the rest of the antenna system (lifetime of ~ 2.65 ps). The spectrum of this pool was found to be almost identical to the spectrum of RC chlorophylls (signal maximum at ~ 690 nm). Moreover, according to the discussed alternative model, the equilibration process between bulk and RC chlorophylls occurs on a sub-picosecond time scale (lifetime of ~ 0.28 ps) and is followed by a rapid (rate constant of 900 ns− 1) and practically irreversible primary charge separation.

Spectral analysis

To learn more about the nature of the S1 and S2 states, we conducted a thorough analysis of their spectra. The S1 state spectrum obtained for PSI core with closed RC can be fitted by a sum of five Gaussians (Fig. 2A): the main negative one with a maximum at 685 nm, three smaller negative ones peaking at 674 nm, 702 nm and 727 nm (the last one of a very small size) and the small positive one with a maximum at 654 nm. As the target analysis showed, the kinetics of creating S1 state is basically the same in the PSI with open and closed RC. Moreover, the spectra of this state modeled for the PSI with open and closed RC overlap almost perfectly in the range up to ~ 685 nm (Fig. 2B). Therefore, to decompose the S1 state spectrum found for the open RC, we subtracted from it the properly normalized S1 state spectrum obtained for closed RC (Fig. 2B). Difference spectrum generated this way reaches clearly non-zero values only above 685 nm and can be described by three Gaussians (Fig. 2C): two negative bands peaking at 695 nm and 701 nm and one broad positive band with a maximum at around 752 nm.

The S2 state arising in PSI core with open RC (Fig. 1C) has a spectrum overlapping with DAS of the 23-ns component from the global analysis (Fig. 1B), and reflects the long-lived photo-induced changes in RC, including the effect of oxidizing P700 to P700+ due to charge separation (more details in the Discussion section). Its Gaussian decomposition reveals the presence of six bands (Fig. 2D): two major negative bands peaking at 695 nm and 701 nm along with three minor negative ones at 639 nm, 664 nm and 680 nm, as well as one broad positive band above 700 nm with maximum at 742 nm. However, as one may notice, the 680-nm band is suspiciously narrow and does not match any of the bands previously found in this spectral range for the S1 state. For these reasons, its physical meaning as a stand-alone band is questionable. Still its appearance can be explained in another way: in the region of 650–685 nm, we are actually dealing with two bands with opposite signs (positive and negative), which are slightly shifted relative to each other and, as a result of their overlapping and summing, we obtain this unexpected spectral shape.

To verify the above hypothesis, we decided to compare the S2 state spectrum (Fig. 2E; S2_open RC) with changes in PSI spectra generated by the chemical oxidation of P700 to P700+ (Fig. 2E; RC_chem ox_abs spectrum, obtained as explained in the following paragraph). Preceding the reasoning below we claim that the spectrum of the S2 state consists essentially of two components. One is the P700+-P700 differential signal and the other one is the single negative band centered at 674 nm, exactly the same that was already found for the S1 state (see above). Oppositely, the “RC_chem ox_abs” spectrum represents the P700+-P700 differential signal only (including a possible contribution from electrochromic shift of chlorophylls located close to P700+, that should be similar in both compared spectra).

The P700+-P700 differential signal was calculated as a difference between the appropriately normalized Bulk_eq state spectra of PSI with open and closed RC (see Figure S3 in Supplementary Information), both for time-resolved absorption and, additionally, for fluorescence data (the latter are described in detail in the subsequent section). In this approach, it was inherently assumed that in the Bulk_eq state the excitation is equilibrated over all PSI chlorophylls including P700 in the case of open RC and P700+ in the case of closed RC. It should be emphasized that the differences between the Bulk_eq spectra for open and closed conditions are very small but very meaningful and amazingly similar for absorption and fluorescence. The resulting P700+-P700 difference absorption and fluorescence spectra (RC_chem ox_abs and RC_chem ox_fluor, respectively) are also presented in Fig. 2E. A negative signal in the absorption/fluorescence difference spectra characterizes the spectral regions in which the absorption/fluorescence of PSI is reduced due to the chemical oxidation of P700 to P700+, while a positive signal indicates an absorption/fluorescence increase.

The P700+-P700 difference spectra can now be compared to the S2 state spectrum (see also Fig. 2E), which in turn shows how the absorption properties of PSI with open RC have changed as a result of charge separation (also leading to oxidation of P700 to P700+, but this time due to illumination rather than chemical treatment). This comparison allows us to notice discrepancies in two spectral regions: 660–685 nm and above 705 nm. The first one can be removed by assuming that the 674-nm band built in the S1 state (see Fig. 2A) is not the result of P700 oxidation, and at the same time does not decay during the transition from the S1 to the S2 state. By subtracting this band from the spectrum of the S2 state (see Figure S4A in Supplementary Information), we obtain a modified spectral shape (S2_open RC_mod), which in the range below 705 nm coincides reasonably well with the calculated P700+-P700 difference spectra (Fig. 2E; compare S2_open RC_mod and RC_chem ox_abs). The modified S2 state spectrum can be fitted by a sum of six Gaussians (see Figure S4B in Supplementary Information): two major negative bands peaking at 695 nm and 702 nm, two minor negative ones at 638 nm and 657 nm, and two positive bands with maxima at 672 nm (narrow) and ~ 738 nm (broad). These six bands, along with the negative 674-nm band already created in the S1 state, form an alternative (to that one shown in Fig. 2D) Gaussian decomposition of the S2 state spectrum (Fig. 2F).

It would be worth briefly commenting on the discrepancy of the spectra compared in Fig. 2E in the range above 705 nm. It should be emphasized here that these spectra refer to different physical phenomena: (1) the spectrum of the S2 state (S2_open RC) reflects only photobleaching and photoproduct absorption, (2) the differential fluorescence spectrum (RC_chem ox_fluor) shows changes only in spontaneous emission, (3) the differential absorption spectrum (RC_chem ox_abs) was calculated based on the transient absorption spectra of the Bulk_eq states reflecting both photobleaching/photoproduct absorption and stimulated emission. Hence, discrepancies above 705 nm in the compared spectra may indicate significantly different contributions of P700 absorption and emission in the long-wavelength region using the three different approaches.

Fluorescence decay

In order to determine the emission properties of the S1 state, an additional target analysis was carried out for the previously published time-resolved fluorescence data obtained by the streak camera measurements (Giera et al. 2014). Due to the lower temporal resolution in the fluorescence experiment (~ 3.5 ps), the transition from Bulk to Bulk_eq state was excluded from the “fluorescence” model (Fig. 3A and D). Assuming that the S2 state (as a state with permanently separated charges) is not an emitting state, we also excluded it from the target analysis of the fluorescence data (compare Fig. 3A with 1A). Furthermore, the excitation decay of uncoupled chlorophylls was modeled by two compartments (instead of one) with decay rate constants corresponding to the two longest decay times found in the global analysis of the same fluorescence data (Giera et al. 2014). The rate constants of the transitions between Bulk_eq and S1 states and the decay of S1 state were fixed on the values obtained from the appropriate target analysis of the absorption data (compare Fig. 3A with 1A for PSI core with open RC and Fig. 3D with 1D for PSI core with closed RC). Despite locking these rate constants to specific values, good quality fits were obtained (see kinetic traces shown in Figure S5 in Supplementary Information). Figure 3B and E show the species associated spectra (SAS) modeled for the PSI core with open and closed RC, respectively, by applying the model described above. Obviously, SAS in the case of fluorescence data have only positive values and reflect the wavelength dependence of fluorescence intensity of the individual states included in the model. The fundamental difference between PSI with open and closed RC relates to the S1 state spectrum. In the case of a closed RC, the S1 state is clearly emitting, although its emission spectrum is about half that of the excited antenna chlorophylls (Bulk_eq). In contrast, the emission of the S1 state in the case of open RC is almost completely suppressed. Spectral analysis of the fluorescence S1 spectra reveals the presence of bands similar to those found in the spectral analysis of the absorption data (compare Fig. 3C and F with Fig. 2A). As we can see, the 671 and 685-6 nm bands are drastically reduced in the case of open RC (so much so that in the case of open RC it is not possible to model a separate 671 nm band, instead we observe a slightly wider 686 nm band). Interestingly, the intensity of the 703–706 nm band drops only by ~ 60% after opening the RC.

Discussion

The kinetics of absorption changes related to the primary charge separation in PSI has been extensively studied and discussed in the literature (the most recent and relevant works: Müller et al. 2003; Holzwarth et al. 2006; Müller et al. 2010; Russo et al. 2020; van Stokkum et al. 2024). Therefore, in our discussion, we will focus primarily on the spectral evolution of the recorded signals, in particular on the spectral characteristics of the S1 and S2 states generated after excitation trapping in RC. Gaussian decomposition revealed the multi-band structure of the spectra of these states (Fig. 2). During the discussion, we will describe particular bands by specifying the wavelength of their maximum and the sign of their values: positive as P and negative as N, e.g. 685N.

Figure 4A and B shows the most important bands found by spectral analysis as well as their presence in the individual states. The 685N and 654P bands are characteristic features of the S1 state and decay when transitioning to the S2 state. They are also not generated by chemical oxidation of RC (P700 to P700+). The 674N band is very specific because it occurs in both the S1 and S2 states, but it does not appear in the P700+-P700 difference spectra as a result of chemical oxidation. The 695N and 701N bands are a photoinduced change visible in both the S1 and S2 states as well as constitute the main element of the P700+-P700 difference spectrum upon chemical oxidation. However, it is worth making two additional comments in this regard: (1) The 701N band also appears in the S1 state for closed RC, but its intensity is approximately 1/3 of what we observe for open RC; (2) Both bands, 695N and 701N, in the S1 state have slightly lower intensity than they eventually reach in the S2 state. The longest-wavelength bands, 738P and 752P, appear to be the result only of photoinduced changes occurring in the open RC. Two remarks should also be made here: (1) 752P visible in the S1 state is characterized by much greater intensity than the 738P band recorded for the S2 state. (2) The 738P band is most likely generated as a result of chemical oxidation of RC (explained later in the discussion), however, due to the method of determining our difference spectra, this cannot be shown directly. And finally, the 672P band appears only in the S2 state and in the P700+-P700 difference spectra. It is therefore a clear result of the oxidation of P700 to P700+, either in a photo- or chemically induced process.

The negative bands revealed in the spectral analysis are most likely a consequence of excitonic coupling in PSI RC, involving not only a single pair of chlorophylls, but a much more complex system. It is worth emphasizing that they match very well with the RC excitonic spectra obtained by Gibasiewicz and coworkers (Gibasiewicz et al. 2001, 2003). Figure 4C shows the overlap of a sum of three negative bands (674N, 685N and 695N) and a positive one (654P) with RC excitonic spectrum obtained at 20 K (by direct excitation of RC with 700-nm pulses and recording the absorption changes about 300 fs after excitation; Gibasiewicz et al. 2003). A similar comparison is presented in Fig. 4D, where the sum of the above-mentioned bands (and additionally the sum of these bands and the 701N band) are superimposed on the appropriately normalized RC excitonic spectra obtained at room temperature after excitation at 700, 705 and 710 nm (Gibasiewicz et al. 2001). An almost perfect agreement can be observed between the compared spectra except for the spectral region below ~ 670 nm: in all RC excitonic spectra the positive signal appearing in this range is much larger than in the case of the S1 state in our measurements (the origin of this positive band is explained in the next subsection). In the following two subsections, the S1 state spectra obtained for PSI with closed and open RC will be discussed in detail. In the next step we will turn our attention to the spectrum of the S2 state obtained for PSI with open RC.

S1 state: closed RC

In our modeling, the S1 state represents the first detectable state created after trapping the excitation in the RC. The negative bands are most likely the result of photobleaching of the excitonic states involved in trapping, while the positive bands represent transient absorption of the S1 state, the origin of which we will discuss at the end of this (closed RC) and next section (open RC). Of the negative bands identified in the S1 state, three appear for both open and closed RC: 685N, 674N and 701N (the latter, however, is much smaller in the case of a closed RC). Most likely, their origin is not related to any of the excitonic states involving P700 as it is oxidized in closed RC. Since the assignment of the chlorophyll groups mentioned above to the individual spectral bands is not unambiguous based on the excitonic calculations, let’s look at the experimental verification of individual excitonic bands carried out by Gibasiewicz and co-workers using point mutations next to connecting chlorophylls and A0 (Gibasiewicz et al. 2003). Basically, as a result of 700-nm excitation of PSI core at 20 K, they observed four bands of photobleaching peaking at 667, 675, 683–685 and 695 nm. The last of these bands was insensitive to point mutations and the redox state of the P700 pair. It also disappeared after changing the excitation to 705–710 nm. Therefore, it was found to be independent of the system of excitonically coupled chlorophylls in RC. Of the remaining three, the band at 667 nm had the lowest intensity and we, in turn, were unable to isolate it directly in the spectral analysis of the S1 state spectrum (we will return to this band when discussing the P700+-P700 difference spectrum). However, for the bands at 675 nm and 683 nm, interesting observations were made:

Based on the first observation, it was concluded that the band at 683 nm must be associated with the connecting chlorophylls: the mutation breaks the excitonic coupling of these chlorophylls with RC and their absorption band is not photobleached after selective excitation of RC. It is worth noting, that this mutation may also break the coupling of antenna chlorophyll pairs A38-A39 and B37-B38 with RC and partially reduce their photobleaching bands in the spectrum of the coupled system. According to Byrdin and colleagues, most of the oscillator power of these pairs is concentrated exactly in the 683-685-nm band (Byrdin et al. 2002). Following the above line of thinking, the second observation can also be explained: the mutation at A0 breaks the excitonic coupling in the B branch of ECT, eliminating the chlorophyll A0 and the connecting chlorophyll (and possibly also a pair of antenna chlorophylls B37-B38) from the coupled system. As a result, the excitonic bands associated with the pair of chlorophylls A-A0 and the connecting chlorophylls decrease by half. Observations no. 3 and 4, however, bring perhaps the most surprising conclusions. First, it turns out that the participation of P700 in the excitonic coupling is not necessary to generate photobleaching of the observed excitonic bands (in particular the 675-nm and 683-685-nm bands). Thus, the excitation of a different state in the RC leads to the observed absorption changes. Based on the analysis of the mutation impact, it can be concluded that it is most likely related to the A-A0 pair. Secondly, this state must have a broad absorption band covering the 700–710 nm range, but with a rather low intensity, since it was not observed directly in the experiment.

Based on the excitonic calculations discussed above and experiments on point mutants, we can initially assume that the 675-nm band is associated with RC chlorophyll pairs A-A0 whereas the band 683-nm with connecting chlorophylls A40 and B39. At the same time, we do not exclude the possibility of contributions from the neighboring antenna chlorophyll pairs (A38-A39 and B37-B38) in both of these bands. With this assignment, the issue of the experimentally unidentified lower excitonic level for the A-A0 pairs remains problematic. Excitonic calculations predict the presence of this band at ~ 695 nm, while experiments by Gibasiewicz and colleagues (Gibasiewicz et al. 2003) indicate absorption around 700–710 nm. It may be that the lower excitonic level is characterized by a spectrally broad but rather low absorption band, which is characteristic of charge-transfer state or mixed excited/charge-transfer states (see Introduction section and Figure S1C in Supplementary Information). Excitonic calculations do not take into account the influence of charge transfer on the position and width of the bands. Therefore, we may deal with a situation where the photobleaching of the lower excitonic state of the A-A0 pair is represented in the S1 state spectrum by the 701-nm band possibly together with a small broad band with a maximum at 727 nm (see Fig. 2A). Our previously published time-resolved fluorescence data recorded for the PSI core at 77 K seem to confirm this hypothesis (Giera et al. 2018). DAS of the fastest fluorescence decay component (decay time of ~ 6 ps) obtained in the global analysis contains, in addition to the main short-wavelength band with a maximum at 683 nm, also a low flat band extending from 700 to 750 nm. This long-wavelength fluorescence cannot originate from antenna chlorophylls because, as shown in that work, the decay time of their excited states at 77 K increases dramatically with wavelengths above 675 nm. Hence, the only reasonable explanation seems to be the origin of this long-wavelength fluorescence from the lower excitonic band of the A-A0 pair.

We will now briefly refer to the other two bands in the closed-RC S1 spectrum: 654P and 727N (Figs. 2A and 4). The positive band with a maximum at 654 nm (654P) most likely represents transient absorption of electronically excited chlorophylls to their higher excited states. The positive transient absorption signal from excited excitonically-coupled RC chlorophylls was observed in exactly the same spectral range by Gibasiewicz and co-workers (Gibasiewicz et al. 2001, 2002, 2003), and was interpreted as absorption from the lowest excitonic RC state to the higher (two-exciton) excited states (Gibasiewicz et al. 2002). As can be seen in Fig. 1C and F, similar positive signal is also observed for excited antenna chlorophylls (Bulk and Bulk_eq states), but for slightly shorter wavelengths. As for the small band with a maximum at 727 nm, it may simply represent photobleaching associated with an absorption transition from the first excited vibrational state of the ground electronic state of RC chlorophylls (this state at room temperature can be occupied with a probability of about 0.01 compared to the ground vibrational state). But it may also constitute a long-wavelength part of the spectrum of the lower excitonic state of the A-A0 pair, as we have already noted in the previous paragraph.

At the end of the part of the discussion devoted to the S1 state in PSI with closed RC, let us pay attention to the emissive properties of this state. Target analysis of the fluorescence decay recorded for PSI with closed RC showed that the S1 state exhibits quite strong emissive properties, although its fluorescence intensity is about half that of the antenna chlorophylls represented by the Bulk_eq state (Fig. 3E). A more detailed analysis of the fluorescence S1 state spectrum reveal the presence of the same bands as in the absorption experiment (compare Figs. 2A and 3F). The relative intensity of individual bands in the fluorescence spectrum is similar to that in the absorption spectrum (although not identical: in the fluorescence spectrum the relative share of long-wavelength bands is slightly greater). It can therefore be assumed that in the case of a closed RC all three electronic states represented by the negative bands discussed (Fig. 4A) are occupied and exhibit emission properties.

S1 state: open RC

Detailed spectral analysis of the S1 spectrum revealed for open RC (see section Results: Spectral analysis and Fig. 2) showed that, in addition to the bands observed for the closed RC, two additional bands appear in the case of open RC: one negative and narrow peaking at 695 nm (695N) and the other positive and very wide with a maximum somewhere around 752 nm (752P). Moreover, intensity of the 701N band has increased significantly after the opening of the RC. A comparison of individual bands for RC in the open and closed state is presented in Fig. 4. In the literature, the appearance of a characteristic band with a maximum at 695 and an extended long-wavelength edge (corresponding to the band 701N) is commonly attributed to the photobleaching of P700 resulting from its oxidation in the process of charge separation (Krabben et al. 2000; Webber and Lubitz 2001; Holzwarth et al. 2006; van Stokkum et al. 2024). The disappearance of the 695-nm band and significant reduction of the 701-nm band in the S1 state spectrum after closing the RC, i.e. after chemical pre-oxidation of P700, seem to confirm this simple and intuitive interpretation. Accepting this interpretation, it can be assumed that this P700 excitonic state is only weakly coupled to the rest of the RC. This is because the P700 oxidation, apart from eliminating its own excitonic band, does not generate any changes in the rest of the spectrum. The same hypothesis was put forward by Gibasiewicz and co-workers based on their excitonic calculations and experiments (Gibasiewicz et al. 2001).

We would like to briefly discuss one more hypothesis regarding the origin of the 695-nm and 701-nm bands that is related to the exciton calculation using the extended dipole approximation. According to Byrdin and colleagues (Byrdin et al. 2002), the point dipole approximation (usually applied in the excitonic calculations) must be used with care for distances comparable with or smaller than the extent of the charge distribution. Therefore, for calculation of the spectral properties of the six RC chlorophylls, it may be more reasonable to work with the extended dipole approximation, where one sums over the pairwise Coulomb interaction of four point monopoles instead of two point dipoles. When extended dipole approximation is used instead of the point dipole approximation, the estimated interaction energy within P700 decreases threefold and couplings of P700 chlorophylls to their respective neighbors (the accessory chlorophylls) are increased. Consequently, the coupling energies between neighboring chlorophylls in the RC become comparable. Furthermore, the excited singlet states in the RC, corresponding to the exciton states, are delocalized over the all six RC chlorophylls. Of the six exciton bands calculated by Byrdin and colleagues (Byrdin et al. 2002) using extended dipole approximation, the greatest oscillator strength is demonstrated by 683, 697 and 700 nm. The other three bands with much lower oscillator strength are located at 679, 686 and 694 nm. Interestingly, the spectral positions of the excitonic states calculated in this way almost perfectly correspond to the positions of the bands identified for the S1 state in the open RC (compare the sixth column in Table 1 with Fig. 4). Obviously, the excitation distribution over the individual RC chlorophylls differs from state to state. In the case of the most intriguing 695-nm and 701-nm bands, the first state (695 nm) is located mainly on the chlorophylls A and A0, while the second one (701 nm) on the chlorophylls P700 and A (see Table 1). However, the assumption that PSI RC is a “superaggregate” of six chlorophylls and not three dimers (loosely coupled to each other) shows one major inconsistency with our experimental data: the P700 oxidation should affect all observed excitonic bands, not just knock out two of them (as is the case for the 695-nm and 701-nm bands in the S1 spectra recorded by us).

Another interesting spectral feature distinguishing the S1 state with open RC is the (already mentioned above) broad and relatively flat positive band (designated by us as 752P) extending above 710 nm with a weakly distinct peak around 752 nm. This band has already been suggested to be a spectral indicator of the primary charge separated state in the RC (Müller et al. 2003). Müller and co-workers found that this long-wavelength transient absorption appears with lifetime of ~ 6 ps and then decreases with lifetime of ~ 20 ps, and related its kinetics with the charge separation steps in RC: (1) the faster phase with the development of the primary radical pair state and (2) the slower phase with a secondary electron transfer step. Our time-resolved absorption data confirm their observations: the 752P band appears when S1 state is created and decays during S1→S2 transition. Because it is not observed when the excited state is present in the antenna (Bulk_eq state) or in the closed RC in the S1 state, it can be assigned with great certainty to the charge-separated state in RC. Target and spectral analysis of our time-resolved fluorescence data seem to support this hypothesis to some extent. In the case of open RC in the S1 state, only vestigial emission is observed: compared to the closed RC, (1) the 671-nm band is almost completely extinguished, (2) the 686-nm band is drastically reduced, (3) the long-wavelength bands (maxima at 703–6 nm and 734/747 nm) are reduced by about 60% (compare Fig. 3C and F). At this point, however, the question arises about the nature of this primary charge-separated state in open RCs: are we dealing in the S1 state with completely separated and localized charges, or rather with a charge transfer state mixed with an exciton state (hereinafter referred to as CT-exciton state)? Several facts seem to favor the latter concept:

In the S1 state (in open RC) we observe photobleaching of all bands associated with excitonic coupling in RC, including connecting chlorophylls (A40 and B39) and (most likely) neighboring antenna chlorophyll pairs (A38-A39 and B37-B38), that do not participate directly in charge separation.Some RC fluorescence is still detected, although with very weak intensity. This could indicate a dominant contribution of the charge-separated state in mixing with the excited state.A similar mixing of the charge transfer state with the exciton state was also observed for the PSII RC (Romero et al. 2012, 2014).

The next question is within which pair of RC chlorophylls this CT-exciton state is formed (A-A0 or P700-A, or P700-A0). In our feeling, the primary charge-separated state is A+A0− and only in the next step the positive charge is placed on P700 (for reasoning see next subsection).

S2 state

The S1→S2 transition is accompanied by a number of significant spectral changes: (1) the negative 685-nm band (685N) decays completely, (2) the intensity of the negative 695-nm and 701-nm bands (695N and 701N, respectively) increases by about 20–25%, (3) a new positive band (672P) arises at 672 nm, (4) a broad, intense but flat positive band above 700 nm (752P) disappears and is replaced by a much smaller band with a maximum around 738 nm (738P). Since the S2 state represents in the modeling the final charge separated state (in the time scale of the experiment), we can assume that it primarily includes spectral changes related to photooxidation of P700 to P700+. As we showed in the Results section, the (P700+-P700) difference spectra and the S2 state spectrum do not overlap in some spectral regions. One of the differences results from the fact that in the S2 state we observe a negative 674N band formed already in the S1 state, which is not present in the (P700+-P700) difference spectrum. We therefore constructed a modified spectrum of the S2 state (S2_open RC_mod) by subtracting the 674N band from the original S2 state spectrum (Figure S4A in Supplementary Information and Fig. 2E). Interestingly, the modified spectrum and its component bands (Figure S4B in Supplementary Information) perfectly match the triplet-singlet difference spectrum (P7003-P7001) previously found for P700 (Vrieze et al. 1996; Krabben et al. 2000; Webber and Lubitz 2001). The formation of both difference spectra can be explained in a similar way. In the case of the triplet state transition, the excitonic coupling between the chlorophylls in the P700 pair is lost, resulting in two non-interacting chlorophylls, one in the excited triplet state and one in the ground singlet state. Oxidation of P700 to P700+ also leads to the loss of excitonic coupling in the P700 pair, resulting in one chlorophyll in the ground singlet state and one with an unpaired electron. As previous studies show, the unpaired electron of P700+ and the triplet state are mainly localized on the PB chlorophyll (ec1B) of P700 (Krabben et al. 2000; Webber and Lubitz 2001). The loss of excitonic coupling in both mentioned cases should result in photobleaching of the P700 excitonic bands. In the case of the S2 modified spectrum (S2_open RC_mod spectrum in Fig. 2E), that corresponds well with (P700+-P700) difference spectrum, we observe four such bands (Figure S4B in Supplementary Information): two dominant ones at 695 nm (695N) and 701 nm (701N) and two smaller ones at 638 nm (638N) and 657 nm (657N). There is almost general agreement in the literature that the former two represent photobleaching of the lower excitonic state of P700 (Vrieze et al. 1996; Krabben et al. 2000; Webber and Lubitz 2001; Byrdin et al. 2002; Gibasiewicz et al. 2003). The question of why this excitonic state would be described by two Gaussians remains open. One attempt to explain this fact was to use the extended dipole approximation for excitonic calculations, described by us in subsection S1 state: open RC (see also Table 1). However, the results of this approximation turned out to be inconsistent with our experimental data for closed RC. The second explanation (in our opinion more probable one) is mixing of this excitonic state with a charge transfer state, which leads to the broadening of the spectrum, especially on its long-wavelength side (Vaitekonis et al. 2005). This would explain the asymmetry of the lower P700 excitonic band and the need for fitting with two Gaussians (narrow 695 nm and broad 701 nm). An analogous situation occurs for so-called red chlorophylls in the antenna system (Frese et al. 2002; Vaitekonis et al. 2005; Romero et al. 2009; Novoderezhkin et al. 2016), that has already been discussed in the Introduction section (see also Fig. 1C). The spectral position of the upper P700 excitonic band is still a controversial issue in the literature (Vrieze et al. 1996; Krabben et al. 2000; Webber and Lubitz 2001; Byrdin et al. 2002; Gibasiewicz et al. 2003). The short-wavelength bands (638N and 657N) found by us also cannot be unambiguously assigned to the upper P700 excitonic band, due to their absence in the S1 state for the open RC (photobleaching of the 695-nm and 701-nm bands is already visible there). It is worth noting, however, that the small band with a maximum at 657 nm is separated from the 695-nm band by ~ 850 cm− 1, which corresponds perfectly with the doubled energy of the interaction between chlorophylls in the P700 pair calculated by Gibasiewicz and co-workers (Gibasiewicz et al. 2003). The 638-nm band is in turn separated from the 695-nm band by ~ 1300 cm, which is a value close to the difference between the vibrational levels of chlorophyll a (Rätsep et al. 2009). Therefore, the origin of the 638-nm band could be related to the absorption into the first excited vibrational state of the lower excitonic state of P700.

Perhaps the most interesting feature of the S2 state is the appearance of the positive band with maximum at 672 nm (672P). In the literature, its origin in (P7003-P7001) and (P700+-P700) difference spectra has been generally explained in two ways. The first hypothesis, the most intuitive one, assumes that this band describes the emerging absorption from monomeric chlorophyll in P700, most likely PA, which is “released” from the excitonic interaction due to localization of a triplet/unpaired electron on chlorophyll PB (Den Blanken and Hoff 1983). The second model assumes that the 672P band is due to changes in the absorption of chlorophylls in the immediate vicinity of P700 (for example RC chlorophylls A and A0) resulting from different excitonic interaction of these chlorophylls with P700 in the triplet/oxidized state than in the singlet ground state (Vrieze et al. 1996; Krabben et al. 2000; Webber and Lubitz 2001). Following the latter reasoning, we should expect a difference in the spectrum of the S1 state for the open (P700 in neutral state) and closed RC (P700 oxidized to P700+) around 672 nm. However, as we can see in Fig. 4B, this is not the case, therefore the second hypothesis is much less probable in our opinion. Either way, in the case of charge separation in RC, the appearance of the positive band at 672 nm is associated with the oxidation of P700 to P700+ and most likely location of an unpaired electron (and thus a positive charge) on PB.

In the literature, another positive band peaking at ~ 688–690 nm was reported (not visible in the spectra obtained by us for algal PSI). It was identified in the so-called light-minus-dark difference spectra measured at low temperatures (77 K and/or 5 K) for PSI from both algae and cyanobacteria (Witt et al. 2002, 2003). In the case of the cyanobacterium Synechocystis sp. PCC 6803, this band is also visible at room temperature in the oxidized minus reduced difference spectra of P700 (Nakamura et al. 2011) and the transient absorption spectra of the final charge-separated state (Cherepanov et al. 2020, 2022; van Stokkum et al. 2023; van Stokkum et al. 2024). The origin of this band (similarly to the 672P band) is attributed either to (1) the absorption of monomeric chlorophyll PA within the P700 pair formed after charge localization on chlorophyll PB (Vrieze et al. 1996) or (2) the electrochromic shift of antenna chlorophylls located in the immediate vicinity of RC (Witt et al. 2002, 2003; van Stokkum et al. 2023; van Stokkum et al. 2024). The intensity of this band depends on the temperature (Witt et al. 2002, 2003), which favors the second interpretation (at low temperatures the shielding of the electric field is weakened due to the slowdown of the dielectric relaxation). In contrast, the positive band around 670 nm (672P band in the case of our data) is observed to be the same size regardless of temperature, experimental technique (time-resolved absorption spectra, (P700+-P700) and (P7003-P7001) difference spectra, light-minus-dark difference spectra) or species (algal, cyanobacterial or plant). This also seems to confirm our assumption regarding the origin of this band (absorption by monomeric chlorophyll PA).

Another intriguing fact is the presence of the 674N band in the spectrum of the S2 state (change in absorption caused by photo-oxidation of P700 due to charge separation), while it is absent in the (P700+-P700) difference spectrum (change in absorption caused by chemical oxidation of P700 to P700+). This band is already formed in the S1 state, both in the case of open and closed RC, so it can be concluded that it is insensitive to the redox state of P700. Chemical oxidation of P700 to P700+ does not lead to bleaching of this band in the (P700+-P700) difference spectrum, which confirms the above conclusion. On the other hand, if in the RC we deal with charge separation, i.e. not only with a positive charge located on P700, but also with a negative charge on A1 (more about electron transfer to A1 in the last paragraph of this subsection), then this band remains bleached, that we can see in the spectrum of the S2 state. In contrast, the second large band arising in the S1 state (for both open and closed RC), i.e. 685N, disappears completely during the S1→S2 transition. It seems very likely that RC chlorophylls are more susceptible than antenna chlorophylls to a large electric dipole moment generated by the formation of a charge-separated state. With this assumption, the above observations would constitute further evidence that the 674N band is rather associated with chlorophylls forming the RC (or more precisely the electron transport chain), i.e. the A-A0 pair, while the 685N band originates from photobleaching of chlorophylls in the immediate vicinity of the RC, i.e. the connecting chlorophylls A40 and B39 or the antenna chlorophyll pairs A38-A39 and B37-B38.

The last spectral feature of the S2 state to mention is the broad positive band extending above 720 nm, fitted in our decomposition by a Gaussian with maximum at 738 nm (and therefore named 738P). The transient absorption signal in this spectral range has been observed previously in both the (P700+-P700) and (P7003-P7001) difference spectra (Vrieze et al. 1996; Krabben et al. 2000; Webber and Lubitz 2001). This seems surprising, because different electronic transitions should be responsible for the P700+ and P7003 transient absorption (however, we did not find any publications devoted specifically to this issue). As later studies have shown, this band can extend even beyond 800 nm (Shubin et al. 2008; Nakamura et al. 2011). Moreover, in the case of both P700+ and P7003, it is supposed to be responsible for efficient quenching of red chlorophyll excitation (Schlodder et al. 2005).

At the end of this subsection, we would like to briefly discuss the issue of electron transfer to phylloquinone A1. As shown by previous studies, the positive transient absorption signal at 390 nm originating from reduced A1 builds up with a lifetime of 25–30 ps from the moment of excitation (Giera et al. 2009). We can therefore assume that almost simultaneously to the location of the positive charge on P700, the electron released in the first step of charge separation leaves the RC chlorophylls and transfers to A1. Therefore, the S2 state can be identified with charges completely separated between P700 (positive charge located on PB) and A1 (negative charge).

Photophysical model of PSI RC functioning

Our previous studies on the excitation dynamics in algal PSI (Giera et al. 2010, 2014) were based on time-resolved fluorescence measurements (with streak camera as detection method), which did not allow direct identification of dark states or mixed states that arise in the RC. Time-resolved absorption measurements using the pump-probe technique give such a possibility. Moreover, they are characterized by a much better time resolution (~ 0.1 ps) than the cited fluorescence studies (~ 3.5 ps). Therefore, they provide a more accurate picture of the processes occurring in the PSI core after it absorbs light. In particular, the combined analysis of absorption and fluorescence data allowed us to determine the emission properties of the states generated in the RC immediately after excitation trapping. This in turn enabled a more detailed interpretation of the previous fluorescence data (Giera et al. 2010, 2014).

Figure 5 presents a schematic model of the PSI RC functioning under reducing and oxidizing conditions. As shown by our modeling of time-resolved absorption changes, trapping of PSI antenna excitations in the open or closed RC is described by exactly the same kinetics and takes place in an effective time of ~ 4–5 ps (Figs. 1D-F). However, the state that arises in RC as a result of trapping (S1) has a largely different character in open and closed RC. In the case of closed RC, it shows distinct emission properties (Fig. 3E), and therefore represents RC excited state, reflected in the transient absorption spectrum by three photobleaching bands with maxima at 674, 685 and 701 nm (Fig. 4), and distributed most probably between RC chlorophyll pairs A-A0 and chlorophylls in the immediate vicinity of the RC i.e. the connecting chlorophylls A40 and B39 and antenna chlorophyll pairs A38-A39 and B37-B38. However, many experimental facts (discussed above) indicate that the lower excitonic state of the A-A0 pair may have partially a charge-transfer character. The S1 state formed in a closed RC after excitation trapping decays very quickly in a radiationless process with an average time of ~ 14 ps (Fig. 1D). The reason for such a rapid decay has not yet been clearly identified. If spatial charge separation actually occurs within the A-A0 pair in a closed RC, then their fast recombination may be the channel for decay of the S1 state, as has been proposed earlier in the literature (Giera et al. 2010; Russo et al. 2020). However, since this hypothesis has not been well confirmed so far, we have not included the potential charge separation in a closed RC in Fig. 5.

The state generated in the open RC directly after excitation trapping is poorly emitting (Fig. 3B) and reflects most likely charge transfer state within AA0 pair (A+A0−) mixed with the exciton state (CT-exciton state), and therefore can be considered as a primary charge separated state in PSI RC. Spectrally, it is characterized by the photobleaching bands visible also for the closed RC, and an additional asymmetric photobleaching peaking at 695 nm (described by two Gaussians with maxima at 695 nm and 701 nm), as well as a strong broad positive absorption band of the photoproduct extending far beyond 705 nm (Fig. 4). The first of the mentioned additional bands is associated with the photobleaching of the P700 pair, while the second one can be treated as a spectral indicator of the primary charge-separated state.

Our previous fluorescence studies on the excitation dynamics in algal PSI (Giera et al. 2010; Giera et al. 2014) showed that closing of RC leads to a slower fluorescence decay: the average fluorescence lifetime was extended by about 4 ps for both PSI core and PSI-LHCI complexes (Giera et al. 2014). Originally, the longer fluorescence decay in PSI with closed RC was explained by the slower excitation trapping by closed RC (compared to the open RC). However, the combined analysis of absorption and fluorescence data presented here indicates that the kinetics of excitation trapping by open and closed RCs is the same and the longer fluorescence decay in PSI with closed RC is due to much stronger emission properties of the closed RC compared to the open RC. In the case of PSI with open RC, fluorescence is practically observed only when excitation is present in the antenna system. In the case of PSI with closed RC, fluorescence is observed even after trapping the excitation in the RC, but it then comes from the excited RC chlorophylls.

Our model for the open RC includes a transition from state S1 to the next charge-separated state (S2, the final state observed in the time scale of the experiment) with a characteristic time of ~ 18 ps (Figs. 1A-C). During this time, a positive charge is located on the P700 (mainly on chlorophyll PB), that can be spectrally recognized by the appearance of a characteristic positive transient absorption band at 672 nm (Figs. 2F and 4). Almost simultaneously the electron, released in the first step of charge separation, is transferred from the RC chlorophylls to the phylloquinone A1. Both processes are accompanied by a complete disappearance of the photobleaching at 685 nm (Fig. 4). However, the second large photobleaching band with a maximum at 674 nm, visible in the S1 state for both open and closed RC, does not decay upon transition to the S2 state (Figs. 2F and 4). This is most likely caused by the location of negative charge on the A1 and constitutes the main difference between the S2 state spectrum and the (P700+-P700) difference spectrum (for the latter 674-nm photobleaching band is not observed).

Conclusions

Combined analysis of time-resolved absorption and fluorescence data collected for Photosystem I under reducing and oxidizing conditions allowed us to create a comprehensive photophysical model of the primary processes occurring in the PSI reaction center. The most important findings are as follows:

We have found that the excitation trapping in RC for PSI under reducing (open RC) and oxidizing (closed RC) conditions follows the same kinetics, but the initial states formed in RC are different. The one generated in the open RC reflects mainly the primary radical pair with weak emitting character. In turn, the initial state created in closed RC represents rather excited state with distinct emission properties and (perhaps) weak charge-transfer character. This difference is responsible for the slower fluorescence decay observed for PSI with closed RC compared to PSI with open RC.We have shown that all primary processes occurring in PSI RC after excitation trapping can be described by the temporal evolution of the same four main photobleaching bands peaking at 674, 685, 695 and 701 nm, resulting from excitonic coupling between chlorophylls forming RC.We have identified two positive transient absorption bands constituting spectral indicators of the first two steps of charge separation: (1) the intense broad band extending above 710 nm far into the red is characteristic of the charge transfer state that is formed in the open RC immediately after excitation trapping; (2) the positive band with a maximum at 672 nm is associated with the localization of the positive charge on chlorophyll PB (within the P700 pair) during the second step of charge separation.We have made an intriguing observation regarding the photobleaching band peaking at 674 nm, which is attributed to the higher excitonic level of the A-A0 pair. This band remains photobleached even after complete charge separation and localization of the positive charge on P700 and the negative charge on A1. This indicates the existence of some kind of memory in the RC about the just performed photoinduced electron transfer process. This photophysical memory may result from the formation of a strong electric dipole moment along the ETC or from conformational changes in the surrounding protein matrix following the charge separation process.
