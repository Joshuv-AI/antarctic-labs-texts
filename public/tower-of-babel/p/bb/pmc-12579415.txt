Between evidence and expectation: a retrospective cohort study on chronic endometritis in repeated implantation failure and recurrent pregnancy loss
Authors: Julia Lastinger, Gudrun Schappacher-Tilp, Natalia Palasz, Sabine Enengl, Lea Pichler, Peter Oppelt, Philip Sebastian Trautner, Thomas Ebner, Omar Shebl
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic endometritis (CE) is frequently diagnosed in women with repeated implantation failure (RIF) and recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL), yet the lack of standardized diagnostic criteria and uncertainty about the timing of assessment and optimal treatment lead to open questions regarding its clinical relevance. This study aims to identify clinical risk factors that may guide targeted CE testing and to evaluate an ideal time point in the diagnostic pathway to offer CE assessment in women with RIF and RPL.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, 392 women with RIF (no pregnancy after two or more transfers of good quality blastocysts) and 119 women with RPL (two or more subsequent miscarriages) who underwent endometrial biopsy with CD138 immunohistochemistry between 2016 and 2024 were analyzed. Odds ratios for presumed CE risk factors were calculated and CE prevalence and reproductive outcomes were assessed.ResultsWomen in the RPL group had a higher prevalence of CE compared to the RIF group (39.5 vs. 25.0%, p = 0.004). A history of cesarean delivery was associated with increased CE risk in the RPL group (OR 2.5, 95% CI 1.14–7.84). CE prevalence did not increase with the number of failed embryo transfers in RIF (32.2% after two, 21.3% after three, 24.7% after ≥ 4 transfers; p = 0.349) or miscarriages in RPL (44.2% after two, 51.4% after three, 57.1% after ≥ 4 miscarriages; p = 0.518). When RIF patients treated for CE were compared with those with normal biopsy after both two and three previous embryo transfers, we found no differences in pregnancy outcomes.ConclusionsOur data did not confirm a significant increase in CE prevalence with an increasing number of failed embryo transfers or miscarriages. No relevant differences in the reproductive outcomes of RIF patients with normal biopsies compared with treated CE were found. While prior cesarean delivery may identify a subgroup of RPL patients who could benefit from targeted screening, the overall utility of routine CE testing and treatment remains limited. Standardized diagnostic criteria and further prospective studies are needed to clarify the role of CE in reproductive outcomes, with cautious consideration of uncritical antibiotic treatment.Keywords: Chronic endometritis, Repeated implantation failure, Recurrent pregnancy loss, Endometrial biopsy, Plasma cells, CD138

Background

Chronic endometritis (CE) is a pathology characterized by a persistent inflammatory state in the endometrium [1]. Commonly subclinical or asymptomatic and therefore often unnoticed by both patients and clinicians, increasing focus has been placed on this condition after an association between CE and poor reproductive outcomes has been postulated. CE has been found to have an impact on repeated implantation failure (RIF) in patients undergoing medically assisted reproduction (MAR) and on miscarriages in patients with recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL) [2–4]. CE is diagnosed in 14 to 66% of infertile women suffering from RIF [2–4] and in 9 to 56% of women with RPL [4–7]. The receptive endometrium is crucial for implantation and sustaining pregnancy in both natural conception and assisted reproduction. Factors altering endometrial competence are highly researched to improve both the chances of pregnancy and minimize the risk of pregnancy loss [8].

Studies have shown that chronic endometritis is associated with lower pregnancy rates in patients with recurrent implantation failure compared to healthy controls. After successful treatment of CE, implantation rates are still lower than in non-CE patients [2]. Additionally, an association between CE and unexplained RPL has been suggested [7]. Some researchers have shown an improvement in reproductive outcomes after successful treatment of CE, with a higher percentage of women having a live birth [5, 8, 9]. In another study, patients with cured CE had higher pregnancy and live birth rates than those with persistent CE and those without CE [10]. Other extensive studies and meta-analyses have not detected an impact of CE on reproductive outcomes. A large retrospective cohort study analyzing 1551 endometrial biopsies in a general gynecologic cohort revealed no difference in the prevalence of chronic endometritis between patients with RPL and women without RPL [11]. Another systematic review and meta-analysis could not prove any effect on implantation rate, intrauterine pregnancy rate or live birth rate after treatment for CE [12].

The gold standard in the diagnosis of CE is the immunohistochemical staining of endometrial biopsy samples for CD138, also known as syndecan-1, a glycoprotein expressed on the surface of plasma cells [1, 13, 14]. To date, there is no universal diagnostic criteria for CD138 counts in endometrial biopsy specimens. Studies on the effect of chronic endometritis on reproductive outcomes have used a wide variety of histopathological criteria and CD318 cut-off values [1, 15–17].

Even though some research groups believe that the diagnosis and treatment of CE may have a beneficial effect in women with RIF and RPL, with great heterogeneity among studies, it is unclear who and when to offer CE testing, which diagnostic criteria to apply and which treatment regime to prescribe. Due to the lack of robust evidence, international guidelines do not recommend CE testing and treatment in RIF and RPL patients [18–21].

This study aims to elucidate whether there is an optimal time point in the diagnostic pathway to offer testing for chronic endometritis in women with repeated implantation failure and recurrent pregnancy loss and to provide evidence-based guidance for clinicians during their everyday practice. To identify patient groups who could be preferentially offered CE testing, individual risk factors in the medical history were examined. Furthermore, the prevalence of CE in women with an increasing number of previously failed embryo transfers and miscarriages was compared.

Methods

In this retrospective data analysis, all endometrial biopsies with CD138 + testing performed at a university hospital fertility center between June 10th, 2016, and March 5th, 2024, were included. The indications for CE testing were repeated implantation failure (RIF), defined as failure to conceive after the transfer of two or more good-quality blastocysts [22], and recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL), defined as two or more failed clinical pregnancies [20]. Blastocyst quality was scored according to Gardner et al. [23]. Subgroup analyses were performed on the basis of the number of failed previous embryo transfers and miscarriages.

All endometrial biopsies were obtained during natural cycle. Endometrial biopsy was generally performed in the follicular phase of the menstrual cycle, between day six and ten. The diagnosis of chronic endometritis was confirmed when more than five plasma cells were detected in ten high-power fields via immunohistochemical staining for CD138 [4]. All patients diagnosed with CE were treated with doxycycline 200 mg once daily for 14 days according to the proposed regime by Bouet et al. [4]. Patients with plasma cell counts of up to five per ten high-power fields received no further treatment. The percentage of patients diagnosed with CE was calculated for the RIF and RPL groups according to the number of previously failed embryo transfers and previous miscarriages. The effect of CE treatment on reproductive outcomes of subsequent embryo transfer was assessed if embryo transfer was performed within three months of endometrial biopsy [24]. All patients of the RPL and the RIF group underwent transvaginal ultrasound prior to CE testing to rule out uterine malformations, polyps or intrauterine fibroids. Patients with RPL underwent additional work-up including chromosomal analysis, evaluation of antiphospholipid antibodies and thrombophilia testing. Women with chromosomal aberrations or antiphospholipid syndrome were excluded from the data analysis. We obtained pregnancy rates and outcomes after CE testing was performed. Those outcomes were compared between patients with normal (≤ five plasma cells in ten HPFs) biopsy results and those treated for chronic endometritis. Pregnancy was defined as a positive serum β-hCG test 14 days following embryo transfer. Clinical pregnancy was defined as ultrasonographic visualization of fetal heartbeat. Miscarriages were defined as all pregnancy losses in the first trimester. Medical history was obtained by reviewing data from patient interviews during their clinical visits and from the hospital information system.

The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee of Johannes Kepler University, Linz, Austria (EK 1286/2024, date of first approval January 1 st, 2025) and was performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out using Python Pymer4, version 3.11.8 and IBM SPSS Statistics, version 28.0. The level of significance was set to 0.05.

Descriptive statistical analysis includes n, mean, and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR) in continuous variables, as well as percentages for categorical variables. Normal distribution was evaluated using Kolmogorov-Smirnov Test. Group comparisons were performed using Students’ T-Test for normally distributed metric variables and Mann-Whitney-U Test for non-normally distributed metric or ordinal variables. Categorial data were compared using Fishers’ Exact Test. To estimate the association between potential predictors and the presence of chronic endometritis, Bayesian logistic regression was conducted using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling with the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm. Missing data were handled by complete case analysis, including only patients with available outcome data in each analysis without imputation.

Risk factors for chronic endometritis in repeated implantation failure and recurrent pregnancy loss

To identify subpopulations at risk for chronic endometritis in our cohort, we calculated odds ratios (ORs) for potential predictors of CE. The risk factors included in this analysis are female age, body mass index, gravidity, parity, the number of previous IVF/ICSI treatment cycles, miscarriages, extrauterine pregnancies, the number of cesarean sections, smoking, cycle length and previous pregnancy outcomes. Figure 1 depicts the odds ratios and confidence intervals of the assumed predictors for CE in women with RIF and RPL. None of the assumed predictors were found to have a significant effect on the incidence of CE in women with RIF. In cases with RPL, the same assumed predictors were used to calculate odds ratios for the incidence of CE. In the RPL group, previous cesarean sections were found to increase the odds for CE by 2.5 (95% CI 1.14–7.84). No other predictor was confirmed to have a significant effect on the odds for CE.

Endometrial biopsy after two versus three failed embryo transfers in RIF

Out of 392 patients with RIF in total, 225 (57.4%) patients underwent embryo transfer within three months of endometrial biopsy. Figure 2 depicts endometrial biopsy timing in both groups stratified by the number of previous embryo transfers and miscarriages. No difference was found in the percentage of patients with CE and an increasing number of previous transfers. In patients with two, three or more previous transfers, the percentages of chronic endometritis were 32.2% (n = 19), 21.3% (n = 16) and 24.7% (n = 21), respectively (p = 0.349).

Distribution of endometrial biopsy timing in women with RIF and RPL. Flow distribution of 511 women who underwent endometrial biopsy, categorized by clinical indication (repeated implantation failure [RIF], n = 392; recurrent pregnancy loss [RPL], n = 119). Biopsies were further stratified by timing: after 2, 3, or ≥ 4 embryo transfers (ET) for RIF and after 2, 3, or ≥ 4 miscarriages for RPL

Reproductive outcomes after CE treatment versus normal endometrial biopsy were analyzed separately according to the number of previous embryo transfers in order to evaluate whether to offer CE testing after two or three unsuccessful embryo transfers. A total of 59 patients underwent CE testing after two previous embryo transfers. When patients treated for chronic endometritis were compared with those with normal endometrial biopsy, we detected no differences in pregnancy rate (PR) (27.5% [n = 11] vs. 26.3% [n = 5], p > 0.999), clinical pregnancy rate (CPR) (20.0% [n = 8] vs. 21.1% [n = 4], p > 0.999), miscarriage rate (10.0% [n = 4] vs. 5.3% [n = 1], p > 0.999) or live birth rate (LBR) (17.5% [n = 7] vs. 15.8% [n = 3], p > 0.999).

Seventy-five patients underwent endometrial biopsy after three previous embryo transfers. In those patients, no difference between cases with normal plasma cell count versus cases treated for chronic endometritis in terms of PR (25.5% [n = 15] vs. 43.8% [n = 7], p = 0.215), CPR (15.3% [n = 9] vs. 37.5% [n = 6], p = 0.075), miscarriage rate (10.2% [n = 6] vs. 12.5% [n = 2], p = 0.677) or LBR (13.6% [n = 8] vs. 25.0% [n = 4], p = 0.271) was detected.

Endometrial biopsy after two versus three previous miscarriages in RPL

The percentages of CE detected in endometrial biopsies after two, three or more miscarriages were 44.2% (n = 19), 51.4% (n = 19) and 57.1% (n = 20), respectively. No difference in the percentage of CE according to the number of previous miscarriages was found (p = 0.518).

Similarly, pregnancy rates and pregnancy outcomes were evaluated after CE testing in women with two, three or more previous miscarriages. Forty-three patients (36.1%) underwent endometrial biopsy after two previous miscarriages. When comparing patients with normal endometrial biopsy with those treated for CE after two miscarriages, no differences in PR (20.8% [n = 5] vs. 10.5% [n = 2], p = 0.437), CPR (16.7% [n = 4] vs. 5.3% [n = 1], p = 0.363) or LBR (16.7% [n = 4] vs. 5.3% [n = 1], p = 0.363) were detected. No miscarriages were reported in this patient group.

Thirty-seven patients (31.1%) underwent endometrial biopsy after three miscarriages. Pregnancy outcomes were compared between normal plasma cell count versus treated CE groups. No differences in the PR (0.0% [n = 0] vs. 10.5% [n = 2], p = 0.487) or miscarriage rates (0.0% [n = 0] vs. 5.3% [n = 1], p > 0.999) were found. No clinical pregnancies or live births were reported in this group.

Alternative CD138 thresholds: CE prevalence rates in RIF and RPL using a cut-off of ten and 20 plasma cells/HPF

Assuming that higher plasma cell counts reflect the severity of chronic endometritis in both RIF and RPL patients, CE prevalence should be higher in severe cases of RIF and RPL (indicated by a higher number of previous embryo transfers and miscarriages), we also assessed CE prevalence in these groups with increasing plasma cell thresholds. We calculated new CE prevalence rates in the RIF group according to the number of previous failed embryo transfers with a new cut-off of ≥ ten plasma cells/HPF. After two, three or more previous embryo transfers, CE prevalence rates using a plasma cell count of ≥ ten were 11.9% (n = 7), 14.7% (n = 11) and 9.4% (n = 8), respectively. No significant increase in CE prevalence was detected according to the number of previous embryo transfers after increasing the plasma cell cut-off to ≥ ten/HPF (p = 0.591). After increasing the cut-off to ≥ 20 plasma cells/HPF, a new CE prevalence rate of 8.5% (n = 5), 6.7% (n = 5) and 2.4% (n = 2) after two, three or more previous embryo transfers was calculated. No difference in CE prevalence was detected after two, three or more previous embryo transfers when increasing the cut-off to ≥ 20 plasma cells/HPF (p = 0.243).

Analogously to the RIF group, we calculated new CE prevalence rates using alternative plasma cell thresholds in women with RPL after two, three or more miscarriages. When increasing the cut-off value to ≥ ten plasma cells/HPF, the new CE prevalence rates were 27.9% (n = 12), 37.8% (n = 14) and 40.0% (n = 14), respectively. There was no significant increase in CE prevalence in women with RPL with a higher number of previous miscarriages using a cut-off value of ≥ ten plasma cells/HPF (p = 0.480). After increasing the cut-off to ≥ 20 plasma cells/HPF, CE prevalence was 14.0% (n = 6), 16.2% (n = 6) and 20.0% (n = 7) after two, three or more previous miscarriages. No difference in CE prevalence could be detected when increasing the plasma cell cut-off to ≥ 20 plasma cells/HPF (p = 0.773).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated chronic endometritis in the context of recurrent pregnancy loss and repeated implantation failure. Our findings revealed no clear associations between CE and the number of failed embryo transfers or miscarriages.

Recommendations for CE testing are provided by the two major societies in the field of assisted reproduction. Our data support these recommendations. The European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology (ESHRE) currently does not recommend CE testing in women with unexplained recurrent pregnancy loss [18]. ESHRE does not recommend uNK cell testing in patients with repeated implantation failure. However, in women with RIF, screening for chronic endometritis via plasma cell count in endometrial biopsies can be considered, even though, according to ESHRE, a lack of standardization significantly affects the validity of data on the diagnosis and treatment of CE [19]. The American Society for Reproductive Medicine does not recommend testing for infections or the administration of antibiotics in women with RPL [20] and does not recommend endometrial biopsy for fertility evaluation [21]. These recommendations are supported by two meta-analyses. Cheng et al. did not detect an increase in live birth rates in a general cohort of women with RIF after treatment with CE. Furthermore, no difference in miscarriage rates was found when comparing women with a normal endometrial biopsy result to those treated for CE [25]. In a study published by Kato et al., there was no certain benefit of treatment for CE regarding implantation rate, intrauterine pregnancy rate or live birth rate [12]. Even though pregnancy outcomes in this cohort should be interpreted cautiously due to small sample size in the subgroup analysis, especially in the RPL group, no difference in pregnancy outcomes after treatment of CE in both RPL and RIF were detected. Whether this is due to an effect of CE treatment or another factor independent of CE is not known. A recently published study by Yilmaz et al. found no association between the prevalence of CE and the number of previously failed embryo transfers preceding CE evaluation. The proportion of patients diagnosed with CE was similar when comparing women with and without implantation failure. The authors concluded that CE is of limited clinical relevance in women with implantation failure [26]. This study group performed endometrial biopsy in the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle, while in our center endometrial biopsy is performed during the follicular phase. These differences underscore the methodological variability of studies evaluating CE in infertile patients. Our study results align with the data presented by Yilmaz et al., highlighting that CE prevalence is not correlated with the number of previously failed embryo transfers regardless of the timing of evaluation during the menstrual cycle.

Given the growing interest in personalized medicine, identifying patients at risk for chronic endometritis may help to define subpopulations that could particularly benefit from targeted CE diagnosis and treatment. Risk factors for CE have been inconsistently reported, with some studies reporting fallopian tube obstruction, prior miscarriages, prolonged menstrual bleeding or cesarean scar defects as associated factors [27, 28]. We performed Bayesian logistic regression on previously published or presumed risk factors for CE in both the RIF and RPL groups. The outcome of the last pregnancy before CE testing was accounted for in all patients with a previous pregnancy, a unique feature that has not yet been analyzed to this extent in other studies. In our cohort, none of the presumed determinants were confirmed to increase the odds for CE in women with repeated implantation failure. In women with recurrent pregnancy loss, a history of cesarean section leads to a 2.5-fold increase in the risk for CE. This was the only presumed risk factor confirmed to have an impact on CE risk. Patients with one or more prior cesarean sections who experience subsequent infertility or miscarriages may represent a subgroup that could benefit from chronic endometritis treatment. Although routine CE testing is not generally recommended by societies, these patients might warrant targeted screening and management.

The majority of fertility centers currently adhere to the diagnostic criteria and the treatment procedure proposed by Bouet et al. [4]. However, published studies display a wide variety in the way plasma cells are quantified and, subsequently, how the diagnostic criteria for chronic endometritis are set. With respect to the number of plasma cells detected in endometrial specimens, cutoff values for the diagnosis of CE vary greatly among studies, ranging from one to five plasma cells per high-power field (HPF), ten HPFs, 20 HPFs or the whole section of the sample [4–7, 9, 16, 17]. We hypothesized that the mainly used cut-off value of five plasma cells per high power field might be too low to show the prevalence of severe forms of CE. Women with a higher number of failed embryo transfers and miscarriages could represent a subgroup that might present with a higher number of plasma cells. When increasing plasma cell cut-off values to ten or 20 plasma cells per high power field, there was still no association between CE prevalence and the number of previous embryo transfers and miscarriages.

An overview of plasma cell cut-offs and the time points of testing during the menstrual cycle was published by Liu et al. in 2018, underscoring the heterogeneity among studies [15]. The lack of uniform technical standards and diagnostic criteria for chronic endometritis, along with concerns raised by some authors regarding the accuracy of histopathological assessment, has led to ongoing debate [1, 15, 16, 29]. Some study groups additionally use bacterial cultures when testing for CE [3], others use real-time polymerase chain reaction to screen for pathogens [30], and some perform office hysteroscopy [4, 17, 31]. Concordance rates of histology, hysteroscopy and bacterial culture range from poor to acceptable [4, 9, 30, 31]. Plasma cell counts vary across menstrual phases. When the largest studies on the diagnosis of CE are compared, there are notable differences regarding the timing of assessment during the menstrual cycle. Some authors screen for CE during the follicular phase [3, 4, 9, 32], whereas other studies were performed during the luteal phase [15, 33, 34]. Current cutoff values do not account for this variability, impacting the accuracy and consistency of CE diagnosis. Above all, plasma cells have been identified in endometrial samples of women without fertility issues, raising the critical question of whether complete eradication should be the treatment goal or if establishing a “normal” plasma cell range is more appropriate. A randomized controlled trial revealed no effect of chronic endometritis on IVF/ICSI outcomes in asymptomatic infertile patients with a CE incidence of 2.8%, therefore concluding the CE on reproductive outcomes to be of small relevance [35]. How many or even any plasma cells may be present in a “normal” endometrium sample is currently unknown. The lack of universally accepted diagnostic criteria complicates both diagnosis and treatment.

Treatment practices for CE vary widely, with doxycycline being the most commonly used antibiotic. However, persistent CE is frequently observed [1, 6, 33]. In the era of increasing antimicrobial resistance, the rationale for prolonged antibiotic therapy in patients with CE, particularly without robust evidence for an optimal treatment regimen, is questionable. Experts in the field of microbiology warn about the development of novel bacterial resistance mechanisms following an increase in antibiotic prescriptions [36]. The appropriateness of aggressive treatment strategies for CE in the context of RPL and RIF, where the role of CE is still under debate, needs careful reconsideration.

When investigating possible causes for infertility or miscarriages, a stepwise approach may be useful to prevent possible overdiagnosis and to balance the ratio between benefit for patients and the psychological and financial burden of diagnosis and treatment. The timing of when to offer diagnostic procedures is often unclear. Clinicians need to consider medical evidence and are frequently confronted with patients’ expectations. This dilemma is especially common in the field of medically assisted reproduction [37]. When promising studies on novel diagnostic and treatment methods are published, adaptation into clinical practice is rapidly pursued. In some cases, broad evidence for the application of new methods is lacking, and their use is being questioned thereafter [38]. Many patients are well informed about innovations in the field of assisted reproduction. After several unsuccessful embryo transfers, couples often ask if further testing can be performed to determine why their treatment has not yet been successful [37]. If they are diagnosed with chronic endometritis at this point, they almost certainly question why this examination has not been performed before. It is challenging for clinicians to counsel patients in this situation, and studies to support recommendations on when to perform endometrial biopsy are lacking. Some study groups researching RPL claim to perform CE testing while acknowledging the experimental nature of antibiotic treatment for chronic endometritis [39]. Defining the optimal timing for CE testing within the diagnostic pathway of RIF and RPL would offer evidence-based guidance to clinicians in daily practice. We performed a subgroup analysis in the RIF group to compare the prevalence of CE in women with two, three or more failed embryo transfers. There was no significant difference in the percentage of endometrial biopsies with increasing number of previous embryo transfers. We found a trend toward a lower prevalence of CE in women with the highest number of previous embryo transfers in the RIF group and a trend toward a higher prevalence of CE in women with a greater number of miscarriages, which did not reach statistical significance. Our data did not reveal an increase in CE incidence with a greater number of failed embryo transfers or miscarriages, which questions the direct association between CE and adverse reproductive outcomes in our cohort. Another point of debate in the literature is the observation that live birth rates may be higher in women with cured CE compared to those without CE [10]. This raises the question of potential confounding factors, such as patient selection, differences in treatment adherence or unidentified subclinical pathologies in control groups.

In summary, while the potential role of chronic endometritis in recurrent implantation failure and recurrent pregnancy loss continues to attract clinical interest, our findings do not support a clear association between CE and adverse reproductive outcomes in these populations. The heterogeneity in diagnostic criteria, uncertainties regarding the optimal timing of testing, and lack of robust evidence for treatment efficacy underscore the need for cautious integration of CE screening and treatment into routine practice. In contrast to current practices, with many unanswered questions and a lack of consensus among MAR specialists, some authors suggest that screening for CE should be stopped altogether [40].

Future well-designed, prospective studies with standardized diagnostic approaches are essential to clarify whether targeted testing and treatment for CE can meaningfully improve reproductive outcomes in selected subgroups. Until then, clinicians should carefully weigh current evidence against patient expectations to avoid overdiagnosis and overtreatment while ensuring individualized, evidence-based care.

Our study reports data on a large cohort of patients who underwent testing for chronic endometritis and provides detailed reproductive outcomes, including pregnancy rates, miscarriage rates and live birth rates. Possible limitations include the potential loss of follow-up in the cohort of women with repeated miscarriages who became pregnant spontaneously. When performing subgroup analysis of pregnancy outcomes in the RPL group, sample sizes were considerably small. We did not perform rebiopsy to test for cure of CE after a standard treatment regime of 14 days to avoid cumulative invasive procedures and delay of further treatment.

Conclusion

Our findings highlight the ongoing challenges in diagnosing and managing chronic endometritis within the context of recurrent pregnancy loss and repeated implantation failure. Despite previous studies claiming an association between CE and adverse reproductive outcomes, our data did not confirm a significant increase in CE prevalence with an increasing number of failed embryo transfers or miscarriages. Out of a multitude of presumed risk factors, a history of cesarean section was the only determinant that increased the odds for CE in women with recurrent pregnancy loss but not in women with repeated implantation failure. These patients might represent a subgroup that warrants targeted testing and treatment of CE. There seems to be no ideal time point at which to offer CE testing to patients with RIF and RPL. Future research should prioritize establishing clear diagnostic thresholds, assessing the clinical significance of low-level plasma cell presence and determining the actual benefit of CE treatment on reproductive outcomes via well-designed prospective studies. The emerging issue of antibiotic resistance underscores the need for caution in adopting routine screening and prolonged antibiotic treatment for CE in RPL and RIF patients. Until then, clinicians should critically evaluate the necessity of CE testing and treatment in the management of repeated implantation failure and recurrent pregnancy loss, balancing potential benefits with the risks of overtreatment.
