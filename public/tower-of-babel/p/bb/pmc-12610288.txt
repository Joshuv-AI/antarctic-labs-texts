Differences in microhardness and tensile strength of stainless steel temporary anchorage devices after exposure to fluoride, povidone–iodine, and chitosan solution
Authors: Devina Elita, Haru Setyo Anggani, Nada Ismah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Temporary anchorage devices (TADs) are widely utilized as supplementary anchorage in orthodontic treatment, offering several advantages alongside certain limitations. One such limitation is the potential for mechanical failure, which may occur due to a reduction in corrosion resistance. Recent studies have reported changes in the mechanical properties of TADs – notably decreased corrosion resistance – following exposure to mouthwash during orthodontic treatment. The objective of this study is the evaluation of corrosion resistance of stainless steel (SS) TADs by assessing changes in microhardness and tensile strength following in vitro exposure to mouthwashes. A total of 28 TADs were categorized into four distinct groups differentiated by the type of mouthwash utilized: Fluoride, povidone–iodine, chitosan, or distilled water (control group). Each TAD was placed in a centrifuge tube and immersed in the respective solution for 90 days. Changes in the mechanical aspects of the SS TADs were evaluated through the assessments of microhardness using the Vickers method and tensile strength using a tensile test. The highest microhardness value was observed in the chitosan group (435 VHN), followed by the fluoride group (429.71 VHN), the povidone–iodine group (429.29 VHN), and the distilled water group (420 VHN). Similarly, the highest tensile strength was recorded in the chitosan group (70.20 megapascal [MPa]), followed by the fluoride group (68.87 MPa), the povidone–iodine group (67.19 MPa), and the distilled water group (48.59 MPa). SS TADs exhibited superior mechanical properties after immersion in a chitosan solution, indicating their potential protective effect against corrosion-related degradation.

INTRODUCTION

Temporary Anchorage Devices (TADs) have garnered significant attention in orthodontics due to their role as a supplemental form of skeletal anchorage, effectively addressing many of the limitations associated with conventional anchorage methods.[1] TADs are susceptible to various oral environmental factors that can compromise their integrity, including exposure to mouthwashes.[2]

Orthodontic patients, who often face an elevated risk of dental caries, are frequently advised to incorporate mouth rinses as part of their oral hygiene regimen. Fluoride-based mouthwashes are commonly recommended due to their antibacterial properties and capacity to promote enamel remineralization. However, the release of fluoride ions has been reported to alter the composition and mechanical properties of TAD alloys.[2] Chlorhexidine and povidone–iodine are among the most frequently used mouthwashes, recognized for their substantial antiplaque activity.[3] In addition, Flemingson et al. examined the human gingival fibroblasts’ proliferation following exposure to three oral antiseptics (0.2% chlorhexidine, Listerine®, and 1% povidone–iodine), with povidone–iodine demonstrating the lowest cytotoxicity among the tested products.[34] More recently, chitosan-based mouthwashes have garnered interest due to their therapeutic benefits, including analgesic effects and accelerated wound healing.[5] Notably, Utami et al. reported that 1.5% chitosan exhibited comparable or lower toxicity compared to 0.2% chlorhexidine as well as 0.2% sodium fluoride.[4]

Although stainless steel (SS) is well-regarded for its biocompatibility, mechanical strength, and resistance to fracture, concerns remain regarding potential changes in its mechanical properties when used in TADs exposed to various mouthwashes.[67] To address this issue, this study aimed to evaluate the corrosion resistance of SS by examining changes in microhardness and tensile strength following in vitro exposure to fluoride, povidone–iodine, and chitosan solutions.

The Vickers test is a method for evaluating surface microhardness, utilizing a pyramid-shaped diamond indenter with a square base.[8910] Stress – calculated by dividing the applied load by the material’s cross-sectional area – is defined as the internal force experienced by a material per unit area. Tensile stress refers specifically to a uniaxial stress that acts to elongate a material. In the case of a cylindrical brittle material subjected to compressive loading along its diameter, tensile stress is induced internally.[89]

Corrosion of orthodontic appliances made from SS alloys can be triggered by exposure to the oral environment, particularly saliva.[19] Studies have shown that SS can also corrode in distilled water, with a reported corrosion rate of approximately 0.04763 mm/year.[11] During the corrosion process, metal ions – primarily nickel and chromium – are released from the alloy.[10] The release of these ions accelerates the corrosion of SS, leading to a reduction in structural integrity and increased brittleness of the metal components.[9] Fundamentally, corrosion initiates structural degradation through oxidation, thereby compromising the overall quality of the metal, including key mechanical properties such as microhardness and tensile strength.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Based on the calculation, the resulting value was 6.431; thus, the minimum required sample size per test group, after rounding up, is 7.

Twenty-eight self-drilling SS 316 L TADs (diameter: 2 mm, length: 10 mm; Mico One, Taoyuan, China) were randomly assigned to four groups: Fluoride, povidone– iodine, chitosan, and distilled water (control) [Figure 1]. This study included 0.2% sodium fluoride (Pepsodent Expert Protection Pro Complete, PT Unilever, Indonesia), 1% povidone– iodine (BETADINE®, PT Mahakam Beta Farma, Indonesia), and 1.5% chitosan (KITOBETM, CV EcoShrimp, Indonesia) as mouthwashes. Meanwhile, as the control group, distilled water (Aqua Pro Injection Sterile, PT Ikapharmaindo Putramas, Indonesia) was served [Figure 1b]. Each TAD was weighed using an analytical balance to calculate the appropriate volume of soaking solution, based on a ratio of 1 mL of solution per 0.2 g of TAD weight. Subsequently, the test solutions were prepared and aliquoted into seven centrifuge tubes per group, with each tube containing 1.3 mL of solution.

(a) SS orthodontic mini-implant; (b) four mouthwash groups; (c) SS TADs immersed in the four mouthwash groups

Groups 1, 2, 3, and 4 were randomized and anonymized, then color-coded into GroupsA and B by a supervising researcher [Figure 1c]. One TAD was carefully placed into each centrifuge tube containing the corresponding test solution using sterile tweezers, and the tubes were securely sealed. A total of 28 centrifuge tubes, each containing one TAD immersed in solution, were kept in an incubator for 90 days at 37°C. A 90-day immersion period was selected based on the cumulative exposure time derived from daily mouthwash use, assuming a recommended 6-h retention period per day. Given that TADs are typically used for a duration of 6 months to 1 year, the total exposure time over 1 year amounts to approximately 2160 h, which is equivalent to 90 days.

Following the immersion period, all samples were retrieved using tweezers and rinsed with distilled water to eliminate any surface residue. Subsequently, they underwent three rounds of sonication in a sonicator for a cumulative duration of 15 min at 37°C. Then, at room temperature, each sample would be air-dried and transferred to sterile screw-top tubes for storage.

The testing and data collection procedures were performed by a certified laboratory assistant, who was responsible for operating the equipment and interpreting results according to the researcher’s instructions. For microhardness testing, each SS TAD was securely mounted in the holder of a digital microhardness tester. The surface of each sample was examined under ×40 (Matsuzawa MMT-X3B) to ensure it was free from bubbles, debris, or other surface defects before testing.

For microhardness testing, a force of 500 gf was applied to the heads of the SS TADs for 15 s using a pyramid-shaped diamond indenter [Figure 2]. For tensile strength testing, the SS TADs were positioned in the sample chamber of a Gotech AI-7000 universal testing machine (UTM) [Figure 3]. The UTM was operated at a crosshead speed of 0.5 m/s, applying a uniaxial tensile force until each sample fractured. The maximum force required to induce fracture was recorded as the tensile strength and expressed in megapascals (MPa).

IBM SPSS version 26 (Manufactured by IBM, Armonk, New York, USA) was used to perform descriptive and inferential statistical analyses. To compare the mean values among the experimental groups, a one-way ANOVA test was conducted.

The Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry, Universitas Indonesia (KEPKG FKG UI) has given ethical approval for this study with the ethical clearance code of: 14/Exempt/FKGUI/V/2024.

RESULTS

Due to the procedures being performed by a single operator, an inter-observer reliability test was not conducted in this study. Intraobserver reliability was assessed and found to be high, with Interclass Correlation Coefficient values of 0.98 for microhardness and 0.88 for tensile strength (r > 0.85).

The control group in this study comprised SS TADs immersed in distilled water. This group exhibited the lowest values for both microhardness (420 VHN) and tensile strength (48.59 MPa) when compared to the groups exposed to mouth rinse solutions. These results suggest that distilled water presents the highest corrosion risk to SS TADs within the scope of this study. This finding aligns with a previous study by Yahia, which demonstrated that SS can undergo corrosion even in distilled water.[11]

DISCUSSION

This study focused on these two parameters to evaluate the surface strength of SS TADs following exposure to various mouth rinses. Specifically, the microhardness of SS TADs was assessed after immersion in 0.2% fluoride solution, 1% povidone–iodine, and 1.5% chitosan for 90 days.[12] The highest microhardness value was observed in the chitosan group (435 VHN), followed by the fluoride group (429.71 VHN) and the povidone–iodine group (429.29 VHN). The lowest value was recorded in the control group at 420 VHN. However, statistical analysis revealed no significant differences in microhardness among the groups (P = 0.65). These findings are consistent with previous research, which similarly reported no statistically significant change in the microhardness of SS TADs following exposure to 0.05% fluoride solution for 28 days.[13]

The tensile strength measurements of SS TADs revealed statistically significant differences among the groups exposed to either one of the four mouthwashes. The highest tensile strength was observed in the chitosan group (70.20 MPa), followed by the fluoride group (68.87 MPa) and the povidone–iodine group (67.19 MPa), with the lowest value recorded in the distilled water group (48.59 MPa). These findings contrast with those of Fatimah et al. (2017), who reported no statistically significant differences in the SS orthodontic archwires’ tensile strength after immersion in 0.05% fluoride mouth rinse over 30-, 60-, and 90 min time periods.[10] The discrepancy between the two studies may be attributed to differences in exposure duration and the type of SS alloy tested.[10]

Based on the microhardness and tensile strength test results, the group of SS TADs immersed in chitosan demonstrated the highest values in both parameters. At the same time, immersion in distilled water exhibited the lowest. Statistically, there was insufficient evidence to suggest a significant difference in the microhardness of SS TADs immersed in the various test solutions. However, there was a significant difference in the tensile strength values among the same groups. Although microhardness and tensile strength are typically correlated, this study revealed a deviation from that trend. An increase in microhardness may be associated with greater brittleness of SS, which can reduce the material’s elasticity and contribute to a significant decrease in tensile strength.[14]

After exposure to the chitosan solution, SS TADs demonstrated the highest microhardness and tensile strength compared to all other test groups. Chitosan, which is increasingly recommended for orthodontic patients, has been shown to reduce dental plaque formation and inhibit the growth of Streptococcus mutans. It also exhibits anti-inflammatory properties that contribute to its therapeutic potential.[1516] While previous studies have reported that chitosan mouth rinse, like chlorhexidine and fluoride, can affect the metal’s passivation layer, archwires immersed in chitosan have nonetheless demonstrated superior corrosion resistance compared to those exposed to chlorhexidine and fluoride solutions.[17] Notably, the longer the material remains in the mouth rinse, the more pronounced the damage to the passivation layer.[17] Chitosan functions as an effective corrosion inhibitor primarily due to its polar functional groups: amino (-NH2), hydroxyl (-OH), and acetyl (-COCH3). These groups readily adsorb onto metal surfaces, acting as active sites for electron transfer. This adsorption process displaces surface water molecules, thereby facilitating the formation of a stable protective layer. Furthermore, the presence of nonbonded and π-electrons enables electron donation to the metal, which actively prevents corrosion.[18] These findings align with the present study’s results, which observed a statistically significant increase in the tensile strength of SS TADs immersed in 1.5% chitosan solution compared to the control group, as well as to SS TADs immersed in 0.2% fluoride or 1% povidone–iodine solutions. Fluoride mouth rinses are capable of releasing fluoride ions, which can form hydrofluoric acid (HF) under acidic conditions.[9] This HF then reacts with the chromium oxide (Cr2O3) passive layer present on SS, leading to its degradation and an accelerated release of metal ions. Similarly, the iodine component within povidone–iodine is highly corrosive and can also damage the Cr2O3 layer, thereby promoting the corrosion of orthodontic devices.[19]

The findings of this study should be interpreted with caution, as in vitro models do not fully replicate the complex environment of the oral cavity. This complexity arises from various factors, including variations in salivary pH and temperature. Several studies have demonstrated that low pH can significantly affect corrosion resistance.[2021] Unfortunately, this study did not analyze the solution pH or its direct effect on corrosion resistance. Therefore, there is a need for further research to specifically investigate the influence of pH on microhardness and tensile strength in this context.

CONCLUSION

SS TADs exhibited superior mechanical properties – both in microhardness and tensile strength – following immersion in chitosan solution, compared to fluoride, povidone–iodine, and distilled water; however, the microhardness difference was not statistically significant. Further research is necessary to determine the extent to which fluoride exposure may influence the tensile strength of SS TADs.
