Ethics of nursing in the digital age: perceptions and challenges among Korean nursing students
Authors: Hwa-Mi Yang, Young-Eun Jang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe advancement of digital technologies has brought transformative changes across the healthcare sector, and nursing is no exception. However, existing research has largely overlooked the ethical challenges nursing students face in real-world clinical settings, including dilemmas arising from the integration of artificial intelligence, patient privacy, and digital communication. This study explored nursing students’ perceptions and challenges regarding nursing ethics in the digital age, focusing on the ethical dilemmas they face and their strategies for resolution.MethodsWe conducted two focus group interviews with 13 nursing students from two universities in Seoul, South Korea. Discussions centered on their experiences with ethical conflicts in digital healthcare environments.ResultsNursing students frequently experienced ethical tensions between utilizing digital information and protecting patient privacy, including concerns about photographing EMR screens and using patient data without explicit consent. Many reported confusion in ethical decision-making due to unclear institutional guidelines and insufficient practical guidance. A notable gap existed between theoretical ethics education and its application in digital contexts. Introducing AI in clinical settings further complicates ethical reasoning, raising questions about trust, accountability, and verification of AI-driven decisions. Students also highlighted ethical risks in digital communication, such as improper use of hospital messaging systems and social media, which threaten professionalism and confidentiality. These findings emphasize students’ strong demand for enhanced digital ethics education through case-based learning, simulations, and experiential training.ConclusionsNursing students stressed the need for practical ethics education that bridges the gap between theory and real-world application. This study underscores the importance of mandatory, practice-focused training linked to clinical environments, reinforced patient privacy protections, and ongoing education addressing ethical issues posed by emerging technologies like AI. To develop ethically competent nurses, digital ethics education must adopt a comprehensive, practice-oriented framework grounded in the realities of modern healthcare.Keywords: Digital health, Ethics, Nursing, Qualitative research

Introduction

The advancement of digital technologies has brought transformative changes across the healthcare sector, and the field of nursing is no exception [1]. Digital tools have enhanced the efficiency of nursing practice and play a significant role in improving the quality of healthcare services [2]. The integration of diverse digital technologies—including the internet and social media—into nursing practice has led to the introduction of electronic health records (EHRs), medical robots, artificial intelligence (AI), and telemedicine, thereby redefining the roles of nurses and raising new ethical considerations [3].

Nursing ethics has traditionally provided foundational norms and principles to ensure patient safety and dignity [4]. However, the digital age presents new ethical challenges such as data breaches, misuse of electronic records, and violations of patient privacy [5, 6]. As digital healthcare services continue to expand, the protection of patient privacy and confidentiality has become a more pressing issue [7, 8]. The increasing adoption of digital technologies in healthcare institutions is altering the responsibilities and ethical boundaries of nurses [2].

While EHRs facilitate communication and information exchange among healthcare professionals, the digitization of patient data raises ethical concerns related to data security and confidentiality [9]. Furthermore, as AI-driven medical systems and big data technologies evolve, the scope of medical data utilization has broadened [10]. However, this has also introduced new ethical dilemmas, including concerns over the reliability of AI-based diagnoses and unauthorized use of patient information [10, 11]. The widespread availability of online health information also poses risks, as patients may be misled by biased or inaccurate content, potentially resulting in inappropriate medical decisions [12].

Previous studies have primarily addressed ethical issues in nursing practice, including both traditional professional ethics and those arising from the use of digital healthcare technologies, such as electronic health records, telemedicine platforms, and social media [13, 14]. While recent research on first-year nursing students has examined ethical issues in cyberspace, including online communication and social media use, it has largely overlooked ethical challenges encountered in real-world clinical settings [15]. Emerging studies have also addressed artificial intelligence (AI) ethics in nursing, showing that students’ AI ethics awareness is significantly associated with digital literacy and moral sensitivity [16, 17]. However, these studies are primarily quantitative and focus on correlations, providing limited insight into how students perceive and navigate ethical challenges in practice.

To address these emerging ethical challenges effectively, nursing students require targeted education that fosters ethical values, moral sensitivity, and critical thinking. Developing bioethical awareness enhances students’ capacity for ethical decision-making and their ability to provide patient-centered care while using digital tools responsibly [18, 19].

As future healthcare professionals, nursing students must be prepared to utilize digital technologies effectively while simultaneously addressing the ethical dilemmas that may arise. The specific focus of this study is to explore how nursing students perceive ethical challenges in the digital age, the types of dilemmas they encounter in educational and clinical contexts, and the areas of digital nursing ethics that may require further educational attention, such as privacy, confidentiality, informed consent, equity, and accountability. Therefore, this study aims to explore nursing students’ perceptions of nursing ethics in the digital age and to examine the ethical challenges they encounter. A focus group interview (FGI) methodology was employed, as it facilitates in-depth discussions and the collection of diverse perspectives on a given topic [20].

A focus group interview (FGI) methodology was employed because ethical challenges in the digital environment are often shaped by shared experiences, peer interactions, and social contexts rather than by individual perspectives alone. Unlike individual interviews, FGIs enable participants to discuss, compare, and reflect on their experiences collectively, which helps reveal how nursing students interpret and respond to ethical dilemmas in relation to one another. One of the key strengths of FGIs lies in group dynamics, where interactions among participants help uncover nuanced insights beyond simple opinion gathering [21]. Through this approach, the study seeks to gain a deeper understanding of the ethical issues faced by nursing students and to propose educational strategies aligned with the ethical demands of the digital era.

Purpose of the study

This study aims to explore nursing students’ perceptions of nursing ethics in the digital age and the ethical challenges they encounter, using a focus group interview (FGI) approach. The findings are intended to provide foundational insights for enhancing nursing students’ understanding of ethical considerations arising from the integration of digital technologies, as well as their capacity to respond effectively to such issues.

Methods

Study design

A qualitative research design was employed to explore nursing students’ experiences and perceptions of digital ethics education in depth. Data were collected through focus group interviews, enabling the participants to share their experiences.

Sampling and participants’ recruitment

This study involved 13 nursing students enrolled at two universities (D University and S University) located in the metropolitan area of South Korea. Recruitment took place between March 4 and March 10, 2024. All participants had completed an educational program that included courses in Nursing Ethics and Health Informatics. They also had prior clinical practicum experience involving the use of digital technologies. Across both groups, students received instruction on core ethical topics, including patient privacy, principles of nursing ethics, and ethical considerations related to health informatics. To ensure relevance to the study topic, purposive sampling was employed to select students with relevant experiences. Participant selection criteria were defined as follows: (1) enrollment as an undergraduate nursing student, (2) completion of a basic course in nursing ethics, and (3) prior practicum or nursing experience involving the use of digital tools. Exclusion criteria applied to students who had not completed a basic nursing ethics course, had no experience with digital tools in clinical practice, or declined participation in the focus group interviews.

Data collection

Data were collected from March 11 to March 22, 2024, through two face-to-face focus group interviews. A total of 13 nursing students participated, with each group consisting of 6 to 7 students and one interview session per group. A total of two FGIs were conducted, which was deemed sufficient for the study. This decision was based on the observation that no additional themes emerged during the second interview, indicating that data saturation had been achieved. Recruitment notices were distributed through an online platform, and students who expressed their willingness to participate were provided with a detailed participant information sheet that described the purpose, procedures, and methods of participation.

The interviews were conducted in a quiet office of a professor. Before the interviews began, the researcher explained the purpose and procedures of the study in detail and obtained written consent from all participants. Anonymity and confidentiality were strictly maintained, and all sessions were recorded with the participants’ consent. No participants displayed strong emotional reactions during the discussions, and no uncomfortable or distressing conversations occurred. Furthermore, all students completed the interviews, and no one withdrew due to discomfort.

We used a semi-structured interview guide to explore students’ perceptions and experiences of nursing ethics in the digital age. The main interview questions were developed to explore nursing students’ experiences and perceptions of ethical issues in the digital environment, in line with the qualitative research design. The questions were informed by existing literature on nursing ethics and digital technologies [15, 22], and aimed to elicit detailed accounts of participants’ experiences, dilemmas, strategies, and the types of support needed in nursing practice and education to address ethical challenges in the digital age. Each focus group session lasted approximately 60 to 90 min. After each interview, the researcher took additional written notes to record nonverbal expressions and participant responses.

Data analysis

A qualitative content analysis with a descriptive approach was employed to analyze the data [23]. Two researchers independently read each transcript multiple times to become familiar with the content and to generate initial ideas. During this initial phase, analytical memos were recorded to capture emerging insights. Meaningful units were extracted from the text, and open coding was performed while preserving the authenticity of participants’ expressions. Their original words were used whenever possible to preserve the authenticity of participants’ expressions. The codes were derived from the data’s latent meanings and contextual relevance.

The independently generated codes were then reviewed, discussed, and refined through researcher consensus. The finalized codes were organized into preliminary subcategories and subthemes. This iterative process of independent review, coding, consensus-building, and categorization ensured the consistency and clarity of the themes. Through an iterative process of discussion and consensus-building, the coding framework was refined [24]. During this process, some codes were merged or removed, and categories were clarified to ensure clear thematic boundaries. Ultimately, potential subthemes were finalized and appropriately named to reflect the underlying meanings in the data.

Data validation

This study ensured methodological rigor by applying the four trustworthiness criteria proposed by Lincoln and Guba (1985): credibility, transferability, auditability, and confirmability [25]. First, credibility was enhanced by accurately representing participants’ perspectives. To achieve this, interviews were conducted using open-ended questions, and participants’ responses were transcribed verbatim to preserve the richness and authenticity of the data. Second, transferability was addressed by providing detailed descriptions of the participants’ demographic characteristics and the research context, enabling readers to assess the findings’ applicability to other settings. In addition, member checking was conducted by sharing the findings with participants to confirm the accuracy and relevance of the researchers’ interpretations. Ongoing discussions among the research team also contributed to the credibility of the interpretations. Third, auditability was ensured by thoroughly documenting the data collection and analysis procedures. To facilitate transparency and replicability, tables outlining the themes, categories, and illustrative interview excerpts were included, allowing other researchers to trace the analytical process. Finally, confirmability was established by grounding the findings in the data rather than the researchers’ subjective perspectives. The research team sought to minimize personal biases and maintained transparency throughout the study. Peer debriefing and external review were conducted further to enhance the objectivity and credibility of the results.

Background of the researchers

The researchers are nursing faculty members with over five years of clinical experience and a track record of conducting qualitative research and publishing results in peer-reviewed journals. They have actively participated in qualitative research seminars and workshops to continuously enhance their research competencies and to develop deeper insights into nursing phenomena. Additionally, they currently serve as reviewers for qualitative research manuscripts, which further supports methodological rigor and contributes to the trustworthiness of the data analysis in this study.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Daejin University (IRB No. 1040656-202401-HR-01-13) before data collection. All participants were fully informed about the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of their participation, and the measures taken to ensure confidentiality and data protection. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before participation. They were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time without penalty. All data were anonymized, and access was limited to the research team. All interview data collected in this study were anonymized, and electronic files were stored in an encrypted format. Data will be retained for three years following the completion of the study and will then be securely destroyed. Access to all data is restricted to the research team. Handwritten notes and audio recordings were securely stored in a locked cabinet to maintain data confidentiality.

Perceptions of nursing ethics in the digital age

1. Ethical conflicts nursing students face in digital practice environments

Tension Between Information Access and Privacy Protection.Nursing students reported experiencing ethical conflicts between educational needs and privacy regulations during clinical practicum. Some considered photographing EMR screens for learning purposes, but refrained due to institutional policies. Limited access to student EMR accounts led a few to use unattended staff accounts, and some admitted to using patient information in assignments without consent, which caused guilt and internal conflict. These experiences underscore the tension between academic requirements and ethical standards in clinical practice. “To be honest, when I was on practicum, I wanted to take a photo of the EMR screen… but I knew that was not allowed.” (Participant 2). “I remember sneaking over and taking notes when a nurse left their account logged in.” (Participant 6).

2. Ethical issues in nursing practice with the introduction of AI technology

Ethical Concerns and Accountability in the Use of AI in Nursing Practice.Nursing students expressed ethical concerns about the reliability of AI in clinical settings and accountability in cases of error. While acknowledging AI’s growing use, they emphasized the need for cross-verification, noting that AI-generated content can differ from established standards like NANDA. These experiences highlighted the importance of critical evaluation and human oversight in AI-integrated care. “If an AI-recommended treatment plan is wrong, who is responsible?” (Participant 11). “AI tools require double-checking because their answers sometimes don’t match what we’ve learned.” (Participant 1).

It was tiring, but right.” (Participant 1).

Challenges of nursing ethics in the digital age

4. Changes in the role of nursing in the digital age and strategies for response

Evolving Role of Nurses in a Digital Environment.Nursing students acknowledged that as digital technologies become integral to clinical settings, nurses must actively evaluate AI-generated data and interventions. Rather than passive technology users, nurses are considered critical decision-makers who verify information to protect patient safety and uphold care standards. “Scanning barcodes is convenient, but I saw nurses still double-check names and faces. It made me realize we can’t fully rely on technology—we still need manual verification.” (Participant 2). “Even if AI suggests interventions, we must ask if they truly benefit patients. Nurses must remain vigilant, or we risk losing core parts of our role.” (Participant 8). Students also emphasized the need to combine digital competence with ethical awareness, noting that ethical judgment must come from humans, even in high-tech environments. “Technology might help treatment, but ethical decisions must come from humans. That’s why we need to understand how AI works too.” (Participant 2).

5. Strategies for strengthening ethical competencies in nursing education in the digital age.

The Need for Updated Digital Ethics Education.Nursing students pointed out that current ethics education often lags behind the rapid digitalization of healthcare. While confidentiality agreements and general warnings are standard, students noted a lack of structured instruction on digital ethics related to AI, social media, and telemedicine. They emphasized the need for clear, case-based guidance that connects traditional ethical principles, such as autonomy and nonmaleficence, to modern digital issues. “We learn ethics in class, but topics like AI or SNS rarely come up. We need practical instruction that reflects the digital reality.” (Participant 9). “Before practicum, we just signed a confidentiality form. That’s not enough—we need formal education on digital responsibility.” (Participant 12). Students called for repeated education and specific guidelines to reduce the risk of digital misuse, especially given the easy access to patient data.

The Importance of Practical, Experience-Based Learning.Students emphasized that theory-based instruction is not enough. Real-world examples and clinical cases were seen as more effective in shaping ethical judgment. Hearing actual stories—such as disciplinary cases—helped them better understand the consequences of unethical behavior. They also suggested that senior nurses should act as ethical role models and recommended simulation training to enhance digital ethics competency.

Discussion

Practical dilemmas in the digital ethical environment

The rapid advancement of digital technologies has opened new learning opportunities for nursing students while simultaneously introducing ethical challenges. In clinical settings, students face dilemmas related to patient privacy, AI accountability, online confidentiality, and the evolving role of nurses in digital healthcare.

Nursing students are expected to protect patient privacy during clinical training, yet restricted access to electronic medical records (EMR) often complicates their learning. This limitation creates ethical tensions between preserving confidentiality and fulfilling academic requirements. Many students experience inner conflict when using patient data for assignments without explicit consent. Despite understanding ethical principles, they struggle to apply them due to insufficient curricular guidance. Therefore, nursing education must go beyond theoretical instruction and offer practical, experience-based ethical training. This includes clear guidance on EMR use, privacy laws, and obtaining consent, enabling students to navigate real-life ethical dilemmas with confidence [26].

Ethical challenges and responsibilities arising from the integration of artificial intelligence

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) in healthcare raises concerns about trust, accountability, and the human aspect of care. AI-assisted diagnostic tools, while beneficial, may produce inaccurate results, prompting ethical questions about responsibility and transparency [27]. Students emphasize that clinical decisions should remain under human oversight, with AI serving as a supportive tool. Ethical use of AI requires education on algorithmic bias, system limitations, and the potential erosion of the nurse-patient relationship. To prepare students, nursing curricula must include AI ethics, technical training, and frameworks for evaluating the impact of AI on care quality and trust [28].

Nursing students also face challenges in online environments, where casual sharing can inadvertently violate patient confidentiality. Platforms like YouTube or social media, and even internal messaging apps, can compromise ethical boundaries. Despite social media’s professional potential, ethical risks, such as unprofessional behavior, identity confusion, and patient data breaches, remain high [29]. Previous studies have highlighted real incidents in which nurses violated ethical standards through the inappropriate use of social media or internal messaging platforms. These include sharing patient-related information for personal reasons or engaging in unprofessional conversations among colleagues [30]. Students must be equipped with guidelines and ethical training to responsibly manage digital interactions, critically evaluate shared information, and maintain professional conduct.

Evolving roles of nurses and educational strategies

Digital innovation is reshaping the nurse’s role from caregiver to tech facilitator and ethical mediator. Students recognize the growing need to blend technical competence with ethical sensitivity. While AI and digital devices improve efficiency, they cannot replace emotional care [26]. For instance, chatbots or barcode systems may assist care delivery but may reduce personal connection [31]. Thus, nursing education must prepare students to utilize digital tools without compromising the human aspect of care. Ethical reflection and dialogue on these changes are essential to maintain patient-centered care.

As digital technologies continue to evolve, ethical education must keep pace. Nursing students report a lack of structured digital ethics education, particularly regarding data use and privacy. Curricula should incorporate case-based learning, simulations, and ongoing training to bridge the gap between theory and practice. Scenario-based education, such as responding to AI misdiagnoses or privacy breaches, helps build ethical reasoning and decision-making skills. For example, engaging students in ethics-related campaigns or structured discussions allows them to analyze real-world ethical dilemmas and explore appropriate solutions, thereby bridging the gap between theory and practice [27]. Nursing students must be equipped to recognize potential ethical risks associated with the use of digital tools and to respond appropriately in practice [27]. A lack of digital literacy may prevent students from fully understanding issues related to health information technology, such as risks to patient privacy and safety [32]. Therefore, comprehensive training is necessary to ensure that students are well-informed about legal responsibilities, privacy protection, and prohibitions against sharing patient data via social media. More importantly, they must learn how to apply these principles effectively during clinical practice. Additionally, it is crucial to provide nursing students with ongoing support and guidance when they encounter challenging ethical decisions in real-world clinical situations, such as structured debriefing sessions, mentorship from experienced nurses, and access to institutional resources to help them navigate dilemmas related to patient privacy, data security, or AI-assisted clinical decisions.

Contributions and future directions

This study provides a detailed account of the ethical tensions and challenges that nursing students encounter in clinical and educational settings increasingly integrated with digital technologies, thereby addressing a gap in the existing literature. Specifically, it reveals patterns of ethical conflict grounded in real-world scenarios, including the use of artificial intelligence, management of online information, and protection of patient data, which distinguishes this study from prior research. Building on these findings, future research should explore cross-national comparisons of digital ethics education, evaluate the effectiveness of ethics simulation programs, and examine changes in role perceptions among nurses and educators. Furthermore, as AI and health technologies continue to advance rapidly, there is a pressing need to develop updated ethical standards and corresponding educational content. Although this study was conducted within the specific cultural and educational context of South Korea, the underlying issues arising from the intersection of digital technology and ethics are shared globally. Therefore, these findings can serve as a valuable foundation for designing and implementing digital ethics education in diverse international contexts.

Limitations

There are several limitations in our study. The sample consisted primarily of female participants, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. The data used in this study relied on self-reporting, meaning that the results may vary depending on how participants perceive and express their experiences. Nursing students may tend to provide socially desirable responses, and there is a possibility that they did not fully disclose sensitive issues. Since the study was conducted within the specific educational and cultural context of South Korea, the ethical challenges and experiences of nursing students in other countries may differ, limiting the findings’ generalizability. Additionally, digital technology and ethical issues are rapidly evolving fields, and the problems discussed in this study may change over time. In particular, digital ethics issues identified in this study may be reconstructed in new forms as technologies continue to advance.

Conclusion

In the context of rapid digital transformation, nursing students encounter ethical challenges that increasingly transcend traditional clinical boundaries. Issues such as patient privacy in electronic medical records, the ethical use of AI in clinical decision-making, and professional conduct in digital communication require students to possess advanced ethical competencies. These challenges underscore the need for nursing education to move beyond theoretical instruction and adopt structured, scenario-based training that reflects the realities of digital healthcare environments. Nursing curricula must integrate comprehensive digital ethics education to promote ethical decision-making in this landscape, including guidance on the responsible use of AI, data protection, and professionalism in online spaces. Importantly, students should be equipped with technical knowledge, critical thinking skills, and ethical sensitivity necessary to uphold the core values of nursing, such as compassion, accountability, and trust. Strengthening ethical competence is not optional but a fundamental pillar of modern nursing education. It ensures that future nurses are prepared to deliver patient-centered care with integrity and humanity amid the complexities of a digitally driven healthcare system.
