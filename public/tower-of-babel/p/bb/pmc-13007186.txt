Scalable treatment algorithm focused on hypertension management for the University of California
Authors: Sandeep P Kishore, Heather E Martin, Nicki Friedberg, Pranay Narang, Rachael Sak, Samuel A Skootsky, UC Way Hypertension Initiative Medication Use Collaborators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUncontrolled hypertension remains a leading contributor to morbidity and mortality, particularly among underserved populations. To address care variability and disparities in treatment, the University of California Health system developed and implemented the UC Way Hypertension Medication Algorithm across six academic health centres. This standardised, evidence-based protocol was co-developed by a team of multidisciplinary experts in pharmacy, cardiology, primary care and data science, with an emphasis on medication affordability and the use of two-drug fixed dose combinations as first-line therapy. Implementation strategies included stakeholder engagement, clinician education and integration into electronic health records and routine workflows. The algorithm now informs treatment decisions for over 90000 patients with hypertension and has been associated with improvements in hypertension control. The manuscript focuses on the process of algorithm development, adaptation within a complex multi-payer environment and lessons learned in promoting standardisation, clinician uptake and health equity at scale. This model may inform similar efforts across other decentralised health systems seeking to optimise chronic disease management.Keywords: Health Equity, Clinical practice guidelines, Clinical Protocols, Evidence-Based Practice, Evidence-based medicine

Introduction

Uncontrolled hypertension is a major driver of ischaemic heart disease, chronic kidney disease, heart failure, dementia, pregnancy complications and premature death.1 2 Nearly half of US adults (~119.9 million) have stage 1 hypertension and 37 million have stage 2, yet only about half achieve blood pressure (BP) control (<140/90 mm Hg).3,6 The burden falls disproportionately on vulnerable communities: by age 55, 75% of black adults develop hypertension compared with 54% of white men and 40% of white women, and BP control remains lowest among black and Hispanic adults.1 2 These disparities underscore why any system-wide hypertension strategy must show both overall improvement and reductions in long-standing inequities.

Despite substantial differences in patient populations, delivery models and electronic health record (EHR) workflows across University of California Health (UCH), a unified and standardised care process is both feasible and necessary. The UC Way Hypertension Initiative, launched in 2020, convened stakeholders across six academic health centres (serving 9.5 million annual outpatient visits) to address barriers to medication intensification—one of the most important drivers of BP control.7 8 Wide variation in prescribing patterns, guideline adherence and medication coverage made clear that standardised, evidence-based processes are essential to reduce unwarranted variation and improve hypertension control across heterogeneous systems. To achieve this, the medication use subgroup developed the UC Way Hypertension Medication Algorithm, a unified, evidence-based intensification pathway finalised and adopted across all UCH sites in Q4 2023—the first system-wide standardised hypertension protocol across all six academic health centres. Unlike integrated systems with unified formularies, UCH functions as an open network serving diverse populations, payers and formularies, with sites ranging from large urban centres to suburban affiliates and wide variation in demographics, prescribing cultures and coverage. These complexities have historically hindered standardisation, prompting implementation strategies centred on consensus-building, leadership engagement and local workflow adaptation.3 Developing this algorithm across a multi-institutional open system offers lessons for other decentralised health systems seeking to standardise chronic disease care.

Intervention development and algorithm design

The algorithm (figure 1) was developed through a consensus-driven process involving pharmacy, cardiology, population health, data science and primary care. The team synthesised evidence from local and global guidelines (eg, from Kaiser Permanente, Veterans Affairs, WHO, American Heart Association) and conducted a cost-access review of California Medicaid, Medicare and commercial formularies to inform medication selection, after recognising affordability as a key barrier to prescribing options and patient adherence.

Importantly, choosing two drugs, preferably in a single pill or fixed dose combination (FDC), has been shown to achieve BP goals with lower doses in shorter time with less side effects. Thus, FDCs were designated first-line recommendations.4,6 An angiotensin receptor blocker/thiazide combination was designated the preferred initial FDC therapy based on its affordability through insurance plans in California, availability, tolerability and favourable side-effect profile. The algorithm delineates medications and doses in five distinct intensification steps, while allowing clinical flexibility based on patient-specific factors such as comorbidities, contraindications, side effects and costs. BP targets were set at <140/90 mm Hg for all adults and <130/80 mm Hg for those with comorbidities such as diabetes, 10-year risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease >10%, heart failure or chronic kidney disease. These targets were chosen to reconcile variation in guidelines and post-guideline literature, and with consideration for national quality standards like the National Committee for Quality Assurance Healthcare Effectiveness Data and Information Set (<140/90 mm Hg). We note that there are still considerable differences internationally in setting BP targets (eg, in Europe grade 1 hypertension is <140/90 mm Hg and grade 2 is <160/100 mm Hg).7 The algorithm allows flexibility to accommodate special populations (eg, elderly) and patient-specific factors, including comorbidities and tolerability.

Implementation strategies | adapting to local contexts

To support adoption across diverse settings, the subgroup emphasised iterative feedback and stakeholder engagement before finalising the algorithm. Targeted educational sessions for medical directors, clinicians and ancillary prescribers were used to capture input, address misconceptions and increase familiarity. System-wide materials—including an announcement letter, infographics, patient handouts, email templates, a recorded tutorial and a reference slide deck—were disseminated, and the algorithm was integrated into intranet pages, ordering pathways and quality improvement programmes. Provider in-services further increased comfort with the tool. In parallel, leadership endorsements from UC Population Health, chief medical and nursing officers and chief pharmacy officers helped generate broad buy-in and ensure the tool was trusted, relevant and aligned with local workflows. Adoption progress was tracked monthly across four domains: clinical implementation, socialisation activities, electronic health record integration (eg, SmartSets, best practice alert, preferred medication lists) and patient outreach efforts (eg, outreach to eligible patients, utilisation of UC Population Health patient lists, team-based care/referral to clinical pharmacists). Feedback also highlighted the need for flexibility for special populations, such as older adults.

Demonstration of real-world impact

With respect to impact, BP control across UCH has accelerated following the Q4 2023 launch of the UC Way Hypertension Treatment Algorithm. The algorithm is, of course, part of a broader suite of system-level efforts. Overall control improved from 68.5% in mid-2023 to 73.9% in 2025, building on steady gains since 20218. Among black patients, control rates increased from 63.4% in 2023 to 67.3% in 2025, suggesting early reductions in disparities (unpublished data). Confirmatory BP checks, emphasised by the algorithm, improved by 15% from 2023 to 2025. The UCH hypertension cohort includes 90080 patients as of 30 June 2025. The cohort is defined as (1) populations with one primary care provider (PCP) visit in the previous 3 years and a current PCP at UCH; and (2) patients aged 18–85 with at least two ambulatory visits with a diagnosis of hypertension in the year before the measurement period or the first 6 months of the measurement period.

Standardising a therapeutic approach to BP control across a large, decentralised academic health system demonstrates that consensus-driven algorithm development, affordability-focused drug selection and local workflow adaptation are feasible and may inform similar efforts in other decentralised health systems.
