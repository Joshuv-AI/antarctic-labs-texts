Atg5 deficiency alters myofibroblast accumulation and alveolar regeneration in lung fibrosis
Authors: Xue Li, Guiying Xu, Qi Wang, Qingwen Ma, Xue Liu, Hongwei Li, Sisi Wang, Yimeng Liu, Yueyue Jin, Xuan Miao, Zuokuan He, Jinrui Dong, Kuan Li, Jianhai Wang, Yu Li, Qiuyang Zhang, Dianhua Jiang, Junping Wu, Wen Ning, Huaiyong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryLung fibrosis progression is marked by impaired alveolar epithelial regeneration and uncontrolled myofibroblast differentiation, though the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. In this study, we discovered that Atg5 was upregulated in the mesenchymal cells of lungs from both patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and mice with bleomycin (BLM)-induced fibrosis. Selective deletion of Atg5 in lung mesenchymal cells increased the susceptibility of mice to BLM-induced lung fibrosis. Mechanistically, the loss of Atg5 promoted transforming growth factor β 1 (TGF-β1)-induced oxidative stress and cellular senescence in fibroblasts, myofibroblast differentiation, and extracellular matrix deposition. Intriguingly, loss of Atg5 impaired the secretion of tissue repair-favoring factors in fibroblasts, causing defects in the proliferation and differentiation of alveolar type 2 cells. This study showed that Atg5 is essential for alveolar epithelial repair and myofibroblast control in lung fibrosis.Keywords: pulmonary fibrosis, Tbx4, myofibroblast differentiation, alveolar epithelial progenitor cells, organoid cultures

Introduction

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a severe interstitial lung disease characterized by continuous progression, ultimately resulting in irreversible decline in lung function, with an average survival period of approximately 3–5 years after diagnosis (Raghu et al., 2022). Except for nintedanib and pirfenidone, no effective drugs are available to halt the progression of pulmonary fibrosis. However, even these treatments can only slow the rate of lung function decline (Tanner et al., 2023), making lung transplantation the ultimate treatment option. Owing to the limited availability of donor lungs, developing effective therapeutic drugs or treatment strategies that can halt the fibrosis progression and restore normal lung parenchyma is crucial.

Lung fibroblasts are central to fibrosis progression but exhibit significant heterogeneity. In addition to classical fibroblasts, lipofibroblasts, and myofibroblasts, emerging subtypes are continuously being identified (Liu et al., 2022; Mayr et al., 2024). Alveolar epithelium injury and fibroblast activation and differentiation are crucial to the progression of IPF. Pulmonary fibrosis arises from internal and external environmental triggers that damage the lung epithelium. This initiates a cascade of recurrent inflammatory reactions and aberrant repair mechanisms post injury, which drives the proliferation, migration, and transformation of fibroblasts to myofibroblasts in the lung, causing an excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) components, such as collagen, culminating in catastrophic failure of the alveolar architecture and a subsequent decline in respiratory function (El Agha et al., 2017; Geng et al., 2019). During pulmonary fibrosis, fibroblasts are activated and differentiated into myofibroblasts, producing abundant ECM deposition. Concurrently, the senescence of alveolar epithelial cells disrupts reparative processes, impeding type 2 alveolar cells (AT2) from effectively replenishing AT1 cells, causing dysregulated pulmonary surfactant secretion and alveolar collapse (Yao et al., 2021). The intricate pathogenesis of IPF is further complicated by the interplay of diverse cellular actors and signaling pathways, including transforming growth factor β 1 (TGF-β1), Wnt, Notch, and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) (Fortier et al., 2024; Nabhan et al., 2018). Inhibiting myofibroblast activation and promoting the regeneration of alveolar epithelial cells are promising strategies for treating IPF. However, the development of drugs for the two targets remains unsuccessful.

Autophagy is an intrinsic mechanism through which eukaryotic cells restore their internal environment during oxidative stress and tissue damage. A series of autophagy-related proteins (ATGs), including ATG4, ATG5, Beclin-1 (ATG6), and ATG12, constitute the molecular network regulating autophagy (Isakovic et al., 2017). Prominent among these is ATG5, a conserved protein across eukaryotic species that is crucial in the initiation of autophagy and the subsequent formation of LC3B. Inhibition of autophagy accelerates epithelial cell senescence and promotes the activation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts (Araya et al., 2013). Impaired autophagy in AT2 cells aggravates lung fibrosis in mice exposed to bleomycin (BLM) (Tian et al., 2021). We observed that the selective loss of Atg5 in AT2 cells reprogrammed metabolic pathways, causing defects in alveolar epithelial repair and exacerbation of lung fibrosis in mice following BLM treatment (Li et al., 2020). Fibroblasts constitute a vital mesenchymal niche for AT2 cells by providing growth factors, such as hepatocyte growth factor and fibroblast growth factors (FGFs) (Lv et al., 2022; Nabhan et al., 2018). The autophagic state of lung fibroblasts possibly influences their secretory features, activation, and differentiation; however, this remains uninvestigated.

In this study, by analyzing single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) transcriptomic data, we observed the upregulation of autophagy-related genes in mesenchymal cells from human lung samples of patients with IPF and from mouse lung samples subjected to BLM-induced injury. We then established a mouse line with conditional knockout of Atg5 in lung mesenchymal cells and found that Atg5 loss worsened BLM-induced lung fibrosis. Atg5 deficiency promotes TGF-β1-induced myofibroblast differentiation. Organoid cultures showed that the regenerative capacity of mouse AT2 cells was impaired in the absence of mesenchymal Atg5 after BLM.

Results

Elevated Atg5 in lung mesenchymal cells during lung fibrosis

To elucidate the changes in autophagy in human lung fibrosis, we collected raw scRNA-seq data of 38 healthy controls and 74 patients with IPF from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (Figure S1A). Several autophagy-related genes, spanning vesicle nucleation, autophagosome formation, and maturation phases, were predominantly upregulated in the total lung cells of patients with IPF compared to healthy controls (Figure S1B). According to canonical human lung cell markers (Melms et al., 2021; Travaglini et al., 2020) (Figure S1C), we classified all cells into four primary clusters, epithelial, mesenchymal, endothelial, and immune (Figures 1A and 1B). Additionally, human lung cells encompassing 32 cell types or states (Figures S2A and S2B) were analyzed. UMAP visualization and mesenchymal subset analysis showed that ATG5 expression was increased in mesenchymal cells in IPF relative to controls (Figures 1C–1E; Figure S2C). Concurrently, western blot results showed that the protein levels of ATG5 (Figures 1F and 1G) and LC3 (Figures S2D and S2E) were significantly elevated in primary lung fibroblasts from IPF.

In the BLM-induced lung fibrosis mouse model, autophagy levels in lung tissue were increased (Figures S3A and S3B), parallel findings revealed upregulation of autophagy-related genes in the lungs (Figure S3C). Western blot (Figures 1H and 1I) and immunofluorescence staining (Figure S3D) results showed a significant increase in ATG5 expression in the fibrotic lung tissues. Subsequently, scRNA-seq was conducted on total lung cells from mice 2 weeks after BLM or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) treatment. A total of 16,145 cells were categorized into four types, namely epithelial, mesenchymal, endothelial, and immune (Figure 1J; Figure S3E). Atg5 expression was upregulated in mesenchymal cells from mice after BLM injury (Figure 1K). Western blot analysis revealed increased autophagy levels (Figures S3F and S3G) and elevated ATG5 protein expression (Figures 1L and 1M) in primary lung fibroblasts from mice after BLM. Further analysis indicated that Atg5 is upregulated in the mesenchymal cells of the lungs in both human IPF and BLM-induced mouse lung fibrosis (Figure S4). However, it remains uncertain whether Atg5 promotes pulmonary fibrosis or counteracts it through compensatory mechanisms.

Mesenchymal loss of Atg5 aggravates BLM-induced mouse lung fibrosis

T-box gene 4 (TBX4) is a mesenchymal-specific transcription factor essential for lung development, which regulates myofibroblast accumulation (Xie et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2013). scRNA-seq confirmed that Tbx4 was predominantly expressed in the lung mesenchymal cells of human and mouse lung tissues (Figures S5A and S5B). We established Tbx4CreER;Atg5f/f mice and treated them with tamoxifen to induce Atg5 knockout, specifically in lung mesenchymal cells (Figure 2A). Lung section staining showed that Atg5 deletion in the mesenchymal cells of adult mice did not perturb the lung structure or the abundance of epithelial and mesenchymal cells at a steady state (Figures S5C and S5D).

After tamoxifen treatment, Tbx4CreER;Atg5f/f mice exhibited a reduced body weight and a significant increase in mortality during BLM-induced lung injury (Figures 2B and 2C). Histological analysis and quantification of fibrotic lung sections showed that Atg5 loss in the lung mesenchymal cells resulted in the aggravation of lung fibrosis after BLM treatment (Figures 2D–2F). Immunohistochemical staining and quantitative hydroxyproline assays indicated that collagen accumulation was significantly elevated in the lung tissues from Tbx4CreER;Atg5f/f mice after BLM treatment (Figures 2G and 2H). Moreover, the aggravated fibrosis was confirmed by the expression of collagen and fibronectin (Figures 2I–2K). In summary, we believe that the conditional loss of Atg5 in mesenchymal cells aggravated BLM-induced fibrosis.

Atg5 deficiency enhances the accumulation of lung myofibroblasts

To further reveal the underlying mechanisms of the exacerbation of Atg5-deficiency-induced lung fibrosis in mice, we performed scRNA-seq on lung tissues harvested from Tbx4CreER and Tbx4CreER;Atg5f/f mice 2 weeks after BLM treatment. In total, 14,336 lung cells were collected and categorized into four primary cell groups (epithelial, mesenchymal, endothelial, and immune) comprising 27 cell types or states (Figure 3A; Figures S6A and S6B). Within the lung mesenchymal cell population (1,513 cells), fibroblasts, lipofibroblasts, myofibroblasts, mesothelial cells, and smooth muscle cells were identified (Figure 3B). The proportions of Myofibroblast were increased in Tbx4CreER;Atg5f/f mice during BLM-induced lung fibrosis (Figure 3C). With the decreased Atg5 expression in the mesenchymal cells, Tbx4 expression was elevated, suggesting the negative regulation of Tbx4 by autophagy (Figures S6C and S6D). Tbx4 reportedly drives myofibroblast accumulation and contribute to lung fibrosis (Xie et al., 2016). Immunohistochemistry staining revealed that smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) expression was increased in fibrotic region of Tbx4CreER;Atg5f/f mice after BLM treatment (Figure 3D). Consistently, the elevation of TGF-β1 and α-SMA protein expression was confirmed by western blotting (Figures 3E and 3F). Differentiation-associated transcripts (Tsc22d1, Bnip3, and Igfbp3) were upregulated in the fibroblasts from Tbx4CreER;Atg5f/f mice after BLM-induced injury (Figure 3G). Furthermore, the apoptosis scores of myofibroblasts were significantly lower (Figure S6E), and apoptosis-associated transcripts (Ptgis, Ano6, and Serp1) were downregulated in the myofibroblasts from Tbx4CreER;Atg5f/f mice (Figure S6F), indicating that the conditional Atg5 loss in mesenchymal cells caused myofibroblast accumulation.

Atg5 deficiency promotes the differentiation of fibroblasts to myofibroblasts

After TGF-β1 stimulation, primary lung fibroblasts isolated from Tbx4CreER;Atg5f/f mice expressed lower levels of Atg5 mRNA and protein (Figures 4A–4D). Additionally, there were increased expression of Acta2, Col1a1 and Fn1 mRNA, as well as, α-SMA, collagen and fibronectin protein in the lung fibroblasts isolated from Tbx4CreER;Atg5f/f mice (Figures 4B–4D). Single-cell analysis revealed that oxidative stress-associated transcripts (Txnip and Klf9) were upregulated in the fibroblasts from Tbx4CreER;Atg5f/f mice compared to Tbx4CreER mouse lungs after BLM-induced injury (Figure 4E). Furthermore, senescence-associated transcripts (Btg2 and Trp53inp1) induced by oxidative stress were also upregulated in the fibroblasts from Tbx4CreER;Atg5f/f mice (Figure 4F). Consistent with the upregulation of oxidative stress- and senescence-associated transcripts, fibroblasts from Tbx4CreER;Atg5f/f mice exhibited significantly increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, as indicated by elevated DCFH-DA fluorescence intensity (Figures S7A and S7B). In parallel, β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining revealed an increased proportion of senescent fibroblasts in Tbx4CreER;Atg5f/f mice (Figures S7Cand S7D). With TGF-β1 stimulation, lung fibroblasts from Tbx4CreER;Atg5f/f mice showed increased expression of the senescence-associated factors (P53, P16, and P21) (Figures 4G–4I), along with a more pronounced senescence-associated secretory phenotype (Figure 4J). These results indicate that conditional Atg5 loss in mesenchymal cells exacerbates oxidative stress in fibroblasts, leading to cellular senescence and differentiation into myofibroblasts.

Following these observations in mouse lung fibroblasts, we next performed complementary experiments in human lung fibroblasts (HFL-1 cells) under TGF-β1 stimulation. Compared with the control group, siRNA reduced ATG5 mRNA expression and increased the expression of ACTA2, COL1A1, and FN1 (Figures S8A and S8B). Moreover, ATG5 knockdown led to increased ROS accumulation (Figures S8C and S8D) and enhanced expression of the senescence-associated genes (P53, P16, and P21) (Figure S8E). These findings further support a potential role for reduced ATG5 expression in enhancing profibrotic responses in human lung fibroblasts under TGF-β1 stimulation.

Mesenchymal loss of Atg5 leads to the impairment of AT2 cell regenerative functions

Given the role of mesenchymal cells as niches for AT2 cells, alterations in mesenchymal cells may impair the regenerative capacity of AT2 cells. Thus, we performed scRNA-seq to evaluate AT2 cell function in Tbx4CreER;Atg5f/f mice 2 weeks after BLM treatment. A total of 1,760 lung epithelial cells were categorized into eight epithelial subpopulations, including BASCs and AT1, AT2, club, goblet, ciliated, basal, and neuroendocrine cells (Figure 5A; Figure S9A). The proportions of AT1 and AT2 cells to total epithelial cells were reduced in Tbx4CreER;Atg5f/f mice (Figure S9B). Gene ontology (GO) pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in AT2 cells revealed that cytoskeleton-related pathways were predominantly downregulated in Tbx4CreER; Atg5f/f mice after BLM-induced injury (Figure 5B), potentially inhibiting cell proliferation by controlling the activity of specific proteins related to cell stiffness. For example, the expression of cytoskeleton-associated genes were significantly diminished in the AT2 cells from Tbx4CreER;Atg5f/f mice after BLM treatment (Figure 5C). Cfl1, Actg1, and Actb primarily encode actin protein; Myl12b and Myl12a encode myosin; Itgb6, Pfn1, Rhoa, Tmsb10, and Tmsb4x encode proteins associated with the formation of microfilaments (Inoue et al., 2022; Jovanovic et al., 2007; Quilliam et al., 1996; Ramsay et al., 2007; Vabres et al., 2019). This reduction in gene expression aligned with the module scores, which revealed a marked decrease in cellular proliferation and cellular differentiation in AT2 cells with Atg5 deletion following BLM-induced injury (Figures 5D and 5G). In contrast, fatty acid biosynthesis, acetyl-CoA metabolism, cellular respiration, and aging were upregulated in AT2 cells (Figures S9C and S9E). We have previously shown that fatty acid synthesis and oxidative stress-induced aging inhibit AT2 cell proliferation. Upregulation of mitochondrial respiration may reflect regenerative attempts by damaged AT2 cells during BLM-induced lung injury (Birsoy et al., 2015). Immunofluorescent staining of lung tissues indicated a lower ratio of KI67+pro-SPC+ cells (Figures 5E and 5F), a reduced number of SPC+ cells per field and PDPN+ cells (Figures 5H and 5I) in Tbx4CreER;Atg5f/f mice after BLM injury.

Tgf-β1 and Bmp4 expression levels were elevated (Figures 6A and 6B), while Fgf2 and Fgf10 expression levels were diminished in lung fibroblasts isolated from Tbx4CreER;Atg5f/f mice (Figures 6C and 6D). In addition, Hgf mRNA also showed a downward trend but the difference was not statistically significant (Figure 6E). These data indicate that Atg5 loss in lung mesenchymal cells impaired their supportive role in the regenerative repair of AT2 cells following BLM injury. Flow cytometry consistently showed a decreased proportion of AT2 cells (Figures 6F and 6G). Organoid cultures revealed a significant reduction in the organoid-forming efficiency (OFE) of AT2 cells co-cultured with lung fibroblasts isolated from Tbx4CreER;Atg5f/f mice after BLM injury (Figures 6H and 6I), with little changes in diameter (Figure 6J). Furthermore, qPCR and immunofluorescent staining of organoid cultures showed decreased differentiation into AT1 cells in the presence of lung fibroblasts isolated from Tbx4CreER;Atg5f/f mice after BLM injury (Figures 6K–6M). These findings show that mesenchymal Atg5 is essential for AT2 cell proliferation and differentiation in the development of lung fibrosis.

Discussion

In this study, our results show Atg5 is a critical regulator of fibroblast activation and alveolar epithelial regeneration in pulmonary fibrosis. Integrative analysis of human IPF and BLM-injured mouse lungs showed that Atg5 was upregulated in lung mesenchymal cells during fibrosis. Using Tbx4CreER;Atg5f/f mice, we found that mesenchymal Atg5 loss did not disrupt lung structure at baseline, but markedly exacerbated BLM-induced fibrosis, with increased oxidative stress, senescence-associated signaling, myofibroblast accumulation, and impaired epithelial regenerative support (Figure 7). These findings support the interpretation that Atg5 deficiency does not directly initiate spontaneous fibrosis, but instead exacerbates fibrotic progression by modulating injury-responsive pathways.

Schematic model dissecting the role of Atg5 during BLM-induced lung fibrosis in mice

This schematic illustrates how mesenchymal Atg5 regulates fibroblast activation and alveolar epithelial repair during BLM-induced lung fibrosis. Loss of Atg5 in fibroblasts amplifies TGF-β1-driven ROS generation and cellular senescence, promoting myofibroblast accumulation and excessive ECM deposition. Concurrently, Atg5 deficiency disrupts the paracrine niche by increasing TGF-β1/BMP4 and reducing FGF2/FGF10, thereby impairing AT2 cell proliferation and differentiation into AT1 cells. Together, these defects hinder epithelial regeneration and exacerbate fibrotic progression.

Autophagy, a conserved catabolic process, is crucial in the degradation and recycling of damaged cytoplasmic components and organelles via lysosomal pathways, which are induced by oxidative stress. Consistent with a previous report showing autophagy activation in BLM-induced lung fibrosis (Cabrera et al., 2015), our scRNA-seq analyses revealed differential regulation of autophagy-related genes across lung cell types after BLM injury. A similar pattern was observed in publicly available human IPF datasets, in which Atg5, a gene required for autophagosome formation, was upregulated in lung mesenchymal cells in both humans and mice during fibrosis. Spatial transcriptomic analysis of healthy and IPF lungs further showed enrichment of ATG5 in fibrotic regions, where COL1A1 was also increased, whereas SQSTM1 showed a relative decrease. Together with our human scRNA-seq and primary human fibroblast data, these findings support an association between altered autophagy-related signaling and fibrotic remodeling in human IPF. However, direct protein-level characterization of autophagy markers in human fibrotic lungs remains needed. We also note that the human IPF samples analyzed here were derived from lungs obtained at transplantation and therefore likely represent advanced or end-stage disease, whereas the BLM model primarily reflects injury-induced fibrotic remodeling and aberrant repair. Although these settings are not stage-equivalent, altered mesenchymal Atg5 expression was observed in both, supporting the relevance of this pathway across species and disease settings. Future studies will be needed to define how autophagy-related signaling changes across disease initiation, progression, and end-stage remodeling.

Atg5 overexpression protects against fibrotic progression because Atg5 loss enhances TGF-β1-induced myofibroblast differentiation and collagen synthesis. This may explain previous findings, which suggest that TGF-β1 suppresses autophagy during myofibroblast differentiation (Sosulski et al., 2015). Additionally, pirfenidone has been shown to induce autophagy and inhibit myofibroblast differentiation by enhancing PARK2 expression (Kurita et al., 2017). A recent observation of the profibrotic role of lncIAPF has also been linked to the inhibition of autophagy (Zhang et al., 2022). IL-37 is highly expressed in alveolar epithelial cells and macrophages under normal conditions and is reduced in human IPF. IL-37 can enhance autophagy in IPF fibroblasts, downregulates TGF-β1 signaling, inhibits lung myofibroblast differentiation, and reduces collagen deposition in mouse lung fibrosis (Kim et al., 2019). Inhibiting vimentin intermediate filament assembly promotes autophagy in lung fibroblasts and prevents fibrosis in mice (Surolia et al., 2019). The present and other studies demonstrate that mesenchymal autophagy plays a protective role in lung fibrosis and serves as the central point through which various pathways regulate myofibroblast differentiation.

Our previous findings indicate that Atg5 loss in AT2 cells exacerbated BLM-induced lung injury through metabolic reprogramming (Li et al., 2020). Here, we extend this finding by showing that mesenchymal Atg5 also plays a protective role during fibrotic injury through a distinct yet complementary mechanism. Mesenchymal Atg5 deficiency enhances oxidative stress, senescence-associated signaling, and myofibroblast accumulation, while disrupting the fibroblast-derived niche signals required for AT2 cell proliferation and differentiation, suggesting that Atg5-dependent autophagy acts across both epithelial and mesenchymal compartments to preserve alveolar repair and limit fibrotic progression. Consistent with this, mesenchymal Atg5 loss altered AT2 transcriptional programs, with reduced cytoskeleton-associated transcripts and increased lipid metabolism- and mitochondrial respiration-related transcripts, likely reflecting impaired epithelial support by Atg5-deficient fibroblasts. In parallel, Tgf-β1 and Bmp4 were increased, whereas Fgf2 and Fgf10 were decreased in mesenchymal cells, supporting the idea that autophagy helps maintain a fibroblast secretory program that promotes alveolar repair. In this study, fibroblast-dependent regulation of AT2 cells was assessed using a mouse organoid co-culture system, while future human fibroblast-alveolar epithelial co-culture organoid models may provide a more translational platform for validation. Notably, mesenchymal Atg5 deficiency did not simply recapitulate the BLM response, but further reshaped injury-responsive fibroblast, myofibroblast, and AT2 cell states toward a more stress-associated and profibrotic phenotype. Although Wnt, Notch, and MAPK signaling have been implicated in pulmonary fibrosis, these pathways were not among the most significantly enriched programs in our current scRNA-seq analyses, possibly owing to limited cell numbers and context-specific pathway activation. Thus, while we cannot exclude their involvement, our data suggest that the effects of mesenchymal Atg5 deficiency are more directly linked to oxidative stress, cellular senescence, and altered mesenchymal paracrine signaling. A limitation of the current mouse scRNA-seq dataset is that lung cells from three mice per group were pooled prior to library preparation, generating one sequencing library per group. As methodological studies have emphasized, cell-level testing without biological replicate structure can introduce pseudoreplication and inflate statistical significance (Squair et al., 2021; Zimmerman et al., 2021). Accordingly, the mouse scRNA-seq analyses should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating, rather than as formal replicate-aware statistical inference. Importantly, however, the major conclusions of this study are supported by multiple orthogonal validation experiments, including primary fibroblast assays, ROS and senescence measurements, western blotting, and organoid assays.

Atg5 is a pivotal protein in the autophagy process, particularly in the formation of autophagosome membranes. Beyond its established role in autophagy, Atg5 has been recognized for its diverse functions in cellular biology (Codogno and Meijer, 2006). Notably, Atg5 is implicated in regulating programmed cell death pathways, such as apoptosis (Yousefi and Simon, 2007) and ferroptosis (Chen et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2020), as well as in immune regulation, neuroinflammation, and tumor progression (Yang et al., 2018). Within the immune system, Atg5 is involved in modulating both innate and adaptive immunity, influencing processes such as macrophage polarization, cytokine secretion, antigen presentation, and the activation of immune-related cells (Paunovic et al., 2018; Ye et al., 2018). In addition, Atg5 deficiency has been linked to impaired neural progenitor cell survival and delayed neuronal maturation (Xi et al., 2016). In the progression of fibrotic diseases, Atg5 not only regulates myofibroblast differentiation and epithelial cell regeneration and repair, but may also influence host immunity or pulmonary neuronal signaling. Furthermore, Atg5 is associated with the progression of various diseases, including neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease (Honda et al., 2020) and Parkinson’s disease (Ghafouri-Fard et al., 2021), microglial pyroptosis (Huang et al., 2023), and autoimmune conditions like systemic lupus erythematosus (Jarvinen et al., 2012) and systemic sclerosis (Mayes et al., 2014). In pulmonary fibrosis, the role of Atg5 may extend beyond its conventional autophagy-related functions.

In summary, this study highlights the crucial role of Atg5 in preventing the progression of lung fibrosis by influencing myofibroblast differentiation and alveolar stem cell function. Loss of Atg5 in mesenchymal cells enhances oxidative stress, accelerates fibroblast senescence, and disrupts epithelial regeneration, leading to irreversible fibrotic remodeling. Validation of these findings in aged mouse models, as IPF affects older populations, will strengthen the strategy for targeting Atg5 to develop effective IPF treatments. Autophagy is a dynamic process. We focused primarily on the early stages, specifically investigating the impact of Atg5 gene deletion on the progression of pulmonary fibrosis. Future studies should delve into the role of late-stage autophagy in pulmonary fibrosis to provide a more comprehensive understanding.

Experimental model and study participant details

Experimental animals

C57BL/6 J mice (8–12 weeks old, male) were purchased from Sipeifu Biotechnology (Beijing, China). T-box 4 (Tbx4)-CreER mice were a gift from Prof. Ning Wen’s laboratory (Nankai University). Atg5f/f mice were obtained from the RIKEN BioResource Center (BRC). By crossing Tbx4CreER mice with Atg5f/f mice, we generated Tbx4CreER;Atg5f/f mice. Tbx4CreER;Atg5f/f mice were administered tamoxifen (100 mg/kg, intraperitoneally (i. p.)) to induce Atg5 knockout, specifically in mouse lung mesenchymal cells. The experimental mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free animal facility. The animal experiments were performed in strict accordance with the requirements of the Tianjin Haihe Hospital Animal Care and Use Committee (2024HHKT-(A)-01).

BLM-induced mouse lung fibrosis

As previously described (Li et al., 2020), mice were anesthetized and administered BLM (2 U/kg) via intratracheal instillation or PBS. Lung tissues were collected at predetermined time points for subsequent experiments.

Primary human lung fibroblast

Human lung fibroblasts were isolated and cultured as previously described (Geng et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2022). Briefly, lung tissues from explants obtained from patients undergoing lung transplantation, as well as from normal donors, were minced, digested, and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 15% FBS and antibiotic-antimycotic. The fibroblasts were maintained and passaged up to passage 4, ensuring purity and consistent growth with typical fibroblast morphology. Fibroblasts between passages 4 and 8 were used for experiments. All human lung experiments were approved by the Cedars-Sinai Medical Center Institutional Review Board (IRB) and were in accordance with the guidelines outlined by the IRB. Informed consent was obtained from each subject (IRB: Pro00051481).

Primary mouse lung fibroblast

As previously described (Dong et al., 2015), mouse lungs were digested with Collagenase Type 4 (2 mg/kg, Worthington Biochemical Corporation) and neutral protease Dispase II (2 mg/kg, Roche). The isolated cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Passage 2 fibroblasts were used for myofibroblast activation, small-molecule compound screening, or organoid culture. After treatment with TGF-β1, fibroblasts were collected for qPCR or western blotting.

HFL-1 cell

HFL-1 cells were transfected with 100 nM ATG5 siRNA (5′-ATTATTTAAAAATCTCTCACTGTTG-3′) or scrambled RNA (5′-UGGUUUACAUGUCGACUAA-3′) using Lipofectamine 3000 in OptiMEM medium for 6 h. Subsequently, cells were then cultured in low-serum medium containing 0.1% FBS and TGF-β1 for 48 h. After treatment, cells were either subjected to ROS detection or harvested for RNA extraction.

Method details

Lung tissue processing and histological analysis

Lungs were gently inflated with 4% paraformaldehyde to a consistent degree of expansion prior to excision. The left lungs were then immersion-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 4 μm for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson’s trichrome, immunohistochemistry, and immunofluorescence staining. The same procedure was applied to all experimental groups to minimize processing-related variability. The stained sections were visualized using confocal (TCS SP5, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) or fluorescent microscope (IX73, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Right lung tissues were collected for quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), western blot assay, as detailed in our prior publications (Dong et al., 2015; Li et al., 2020). Specific primer sequences, primary and secondary antibodies are provided in supplemental materials (key resources table). Hydroxyproline levels in lung tissues were quantified according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) and organoid cultures

Mouse lung tissues were digested and flow-sorted to obtain AT2 cells. Briefly, mouse lungs tissues were digested with 3 mL of elastase (4 U/mL) and incubated with DNase I (5 mL of 0.5%). Lung cell suspensions were filtered and collected into a 50-mL centrifuge tube. The red blood cells were removed, and the cells were re-suspended in Hank’s Balanced Saline Solution containing 2% FBS, 1% PS, and 10 mmol/L HEPES, followed by incubation with antibodies: CD31, CD34, CD45, CD24, EpCAM, Sca-1, and streptavidin-APC and 7-Amino-actinomycin D. FACS was performed using an Aria III sorter (BD Biosciences) with the EpCAM+Sca-1−CD24− population representing AT2 cells. AT2 cells (2×104 cells per well) were co-cultured with mouse primary lung fibroblasts (2×105 cells per well) in a basic culture medium containing SB431542. On Day 10, the organoid cultures were observed under a microscope, and OFE and diameter were calculated. To facilitate cell differentiation, SB was removed after 10 days. Organoid cultures were collected in TRIzol reagent for qPCR or fixed in O.C.T for immunofluorescence staining.

ROS detection

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using a ROS assay kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the H2DCFDA probe was diluted in serum-free medium to a final concentration of 10 μM and added to the cells, followed by incubation at 37°C for 30 min in the dark. After incubation, cells were washed with PBS, and fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope. Relative fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

SA-β-gal staining

Cellular senescence was assessed using an SA-β-gal staining kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After treatment, cells were fixed with the provided fixation solution and incubated with the staining working solution at 37°C for 24–48 h. Images were captured under a microscope, and the percentage of SA-β-gal-positive cells was calculated.

Single-cell RNA-seq of mouse lung tissues

Total lung cells isolated from Tbx4CreER and Tbx4CreER;Atg5f/f mice were pooled and processed to prepare scRNA-seq libraries, as described previously (Li et al., 2022). These libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform by Novogene. The raw scRNA-seq data have been deposited in the GEO database under accession number GEO: GSE275162. Using Cell Ranger software, the resultant FASTQ files were quantified, filtered, and mapped to official mouse gene symbols according to the Genome Reference Consortium Mouse Reference 38 (GRCm38). The DEGs among the two groups were subjected to further analysis using the FindMarkers function of the Seurat package under the conditions of fold change (FC) > 1.5 and adjusted p-value <0.05. Subsequently, enrichment analysis of the DEGs was conducted using the online tool (v2021) on the DAVID website (https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov/). Additionally, a module scoring analysis was conducted using the AddModuleScore function of the Seurat package. The gene sets used for module scoring analysis were downloaded from the GSEA mouse molecular signature database.

Single-cell RNA-seq of human lung samples

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data of healthy and IPF lung samples were downloaded from the GEO database. Using the R software (version 4.2.1), we performed a comprehensive downstream analysis. Each sample was treated as an individual analytical entity that initiated the construction of the Seurat object. Doublet cells were calculated and removed using the DoubletFinder package (v2.0.3), and low-quality cells were filtered out because of the high percentage of mitochondrial transcripts. Subsequently, the datasets were consolidated into a unified Seurat object for normalization, scaling, dimension reduction, and batch effect removal using the Seurat (v4.2.0) and harmony (v0.1.0) packages. Finally, the cells were clustered unsupervised and annotated (Melms et al., 2021; Travaglini et al., 2020). DEGs were analyzed using the FindMarkers function in the Seurat package, with the selection criteria between two groups set at FC > 1.5 and adjusted p-value <0.05.

Quantification and statistical analysis

For mouse scRNA-seq, lung cells from 3 mice per group were pooled prior to library preparation, generating one sequencing library per group. The mouse scRNA-seq dataset does not support formal replicate-aware statistical inference at the sample level. For human dataset analyses performed at the patient level, the corresponding N values and comparison framework are indicated in the relevant figure legends. Experimental data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Differences between two groups were compared using the Student’s t test. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001).
