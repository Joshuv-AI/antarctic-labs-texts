Age- and sex-related glymphatic differences and hemispheric asymmetry in healthy adults at 7.0T magnetic resonance imaging
Authors: Yumei Yue, Xiaocui Tang, Ting Shen, Boyi Qu, Wen Lv, Wenying Zhang, Shengzhong Feng, Hsin-Yi Lai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe glymphatic system plays a critical role in cerebral waste clearance and has been implicated in neurodegenerative and cerebrovascular disorders. However, normative variations in glymphatic structure and function across age, sex, and hemispheric organization in healthy adults remain incompletely characterized.MethodsUsing ultra-high-field 7.0T MRI, we quantitatively assessed structural and functional glymphatic markers in 80 healthy adults. Perivascular space (PVS) number and volume were measured in the basal ganglia (BG) and midbrain (Mid), and glymphactic function was evaluated using the diffusion tensor imaging-analysis along the perivascular space (DTI-ALPS) index. Participants were stratified by age and sex. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to control for multiple comparisons.ResultsRobust age-related effects were observed. Older participants exhibited significantly greater PVS burden in the BG and Mid and lower right-hemispheric DTI-ALPS indices compared with younger adults. Age correlated positively with PVS number and left BG PVS volume, and negatively with the right DTI-ALPS index. Several sex effects did not survive FDR correction but showed moderate-to-large effect sizes in age-stratified analyses, suggesting potential sex-by-age interactions. Hemispheric analyses revealed a consistent right-dominant PVS burden in the midbrain, evident across age groups, whereas BG PVS burden and DTI-ALPS indices were largely symmetric.ConclusionThese findings demonstrate that glymphatic structure and function in healthy adults are strongly influenced by aging, exhibit age-dependent sex-related trends, and show region-specific hemispheric asymmetry. This study provides high-resolution normative reference data for glymphatic imaging and informs future investigations of early or subclinical glymphatic dysfunction.Keywords: glymphatic system, perivascular space, DTI-ALPS index, age, sex, hemispheric asymmetry

Introduction

The glymphatic system is a macroscopic brian waste clearance pathway that mediates the exchanges of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and interstitial fluid (ISF) and promotes the removal of metabolic waste products such as amyloid-β and α-synuclein (Taoka et al., 2017). This system comprises perivascular spaces (PVSs), glial cells [particularly astrocytic endfeet expressing aquaporin-4 (AQP4)], the cerebral vasculature, and the CSF–ISF compartments (Liu et al., 2021). Impaired glymphatic clearance has been implicated in a broad range of neurological conditions, including neurodegenerative (Taoka et al., 2017; Shen et al., 2021, 2022; Huang et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024), vascular (Tian et al., 2023), and neuroinflammatory disorders (Carotenuto et al., 2022), highlighting its fundamental role in brain homeostasis.

Reliable, safe, and sensitive in vivo assessment methods are essential for characterizing glymphatic function. Early studies using dynamic positron emission tomography (PET) (de Leon et al., 2017) and intrathecal contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (Eide and Ringstad, 2015) have provided foundational insights but were constrained by invasiveness and safety concerns. Recent advances in non-invasive neuroimaging, particularly high-resolution structural MRI and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), now allow quantitative assessments of glymphatic structure and function under physiological conditions (Yue et al., 2024).

Among available MRI markers, PVS burden has emerged as a sensitive structural indicator of glymphatic integrity. Increased PVS number or volume has been broadly linked to aging-related processes (Park et al., 2023; H.G. Kim et al., 2023), vascular risk factors such as hypertension and diabetes, and a spectrum of neurological disorders including Alzheimer’s disease (AD) (Taoka et al., 2017; Huang et al., 2024), Parkinson’s disease (PD) (Donahue et al., 2021; Shen et al., 2021, 2022; Tu et al., 2022), Huntington’s disease (HD) (Li et al., 2023), and multiple sclerosis (Udaka et al., 2002; Cavallari et al., 2018; Li et al., 2020; Jiaerken et al., 2021; Chan et al., 2021; Shin et al., 2021). Complementing these structural assessments, the DTI analysis along the perivascular space (DTI-ALPS) index provides a functional estimate of perivascular fluid transport, with reduced values reported in AD (Taoka et al., 2017), PD (Shen et al., 2022), and idiopathic normal pressure hydrocephalus (Yokota et al., 2019). In healthy populations, emerging neuroimaging studies have further demonstrated age-related increases in MRI-visible PVS burden (Lynch et al., 2023) and age-related reductions in DTI-ALPS indices (Dai et al., 2023; Ozsahin et al., 2024), suggesting progressive alterations in perivascular fluid transport with aging. However, most prior studies have examined structural or functional markers in isolation, relied predominantly on conventional field strengths, or did not systematically address sex-related and hemispheric variability. Consequently, normative patterns of glymphatic structure and function across adulthood, and their interactions with age, sex, and hemispheric organization, remain incompletely understood.

In the present study, we used ultra-high-field 7.0T MRI to simultaneously quantity structural (PVS number and volume) and functional (DTI-ALPS index) markers of the glymphatic system in healthy adults. By stratifying participants by age and sex and examining hemispheric patterns, we aimed to delineate high-resolution normative patterns of glymphatic function across adulthood. We hypothesized that glymphatic function would decline with increasing age and that potential sex-related differences would be detectable even in the absence of overt neurological disease.

Methods

Participants and clinical data collection

Eighty healthy participants were recruited from the Hangzhou community through standardized screening procedures designed to exclude individuals with psychiatric or neurological conditions. Comprehensive demographic and behavior data were collected, including age, sex, educational attainment, and medical history. To evaluate psychological status and sleep-related status, all participants completed the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA), Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD), and Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI).

The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Ethics No. 20 200 908–30). Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants prior to enrollment, in according with the Declaration of Helsinki.

MRI image acquisition

MRI data were acquired using a 7.0T Magnatom research scanner (Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany). The protocol including T2-weighted turbo spin echo (TSE) sequence (voxel size: 0.5 × 0.5 × 2.4 mm, TR = 7000 ms, and TE = 66 ms), a magnetization-prepared two rapid gradient echo (MP2RAGE) sequence (voxel size: 0.7 × 0.7 × 0.7 mm, TR = 5000 ms, TI1/TI2 = 900/2750 ms, TE = 2.3 ms, α1/α2 = 5°/3°), and a multiband echo-planar DTI sequence (voxel size: 1.5 × 1.5 × 1.5 mm, TR = 6000 ms, TE = 71.8 ms, b-values including 0, 1000, and 3000 s/mm2), and a susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) sequence (voxel size: 0.25 × 0.25 × 1 mm, TR = 30 ms and TE = 15 ms).

MRI processing and PVS quantification

PVS quantification was performed in the basal ganglia (BG) and midbrain (Mid). These regions were selected because PVSs in subcortical structures can be reliably visualized on axial T2-weighted images and have been consistently examined in prior aging- and disease-related glymphatic studies (Li et al., 2020; Shen et al., 2021; Foreman et al., 2024). Subcortical PVS alterations, particularly in the basal ganglia and midbrain, have shown stronger associations with aging, small-vessel disease, and PD compared with some white-matter regions (Park et al., 2023), thereby facilitating comparison with the existing literature.

PVSs were defined as linear, ovoid, or round CSF-like structures following the course of perforating arteries (Jimenez-Balado et al., 2018). High-resolution MP2RAGE images were used to visualize the PVS system along with the spatially corresponding lenticulostriate arteries (LAS), with PVSs appearing hypointense similar to CSF and vessels appearing hyperintense (Barisano et al., 2021; Shen et al., 2021). The PVS burden, representing the severity of PVSs, was assessed on axial T2-weighted TSE images in the BG and Mid (Fig. S1 in the Supplementary material). PVS identification and quantification were performed using ITK-SNAP software (v.3.8; www.itksnap.org). For the basal ganglia, PVS quantification was conducted on the axial slice exhibiting the highest PVS density within predefined anatomical boundaries, consistent with established protocols. For the midbrain, PVSs were quantified across the entire midbrain volume. Two board-certified neurologists independently identified and manually delineated all visible PVSs in each region of interest (ROI).

PVS number was obtained by counting the manually labeled PVS structures, while PVS volume was calculated by summing the labeled voxels and converting to cubic millimeters (mm³). Quantification was performed separately for four ROIs: right BG (BG_R), left BG (BG_L), right midbrain (Mid_R), and left midbrain (Mid_L). The resulting PVS number and volume in each region were used as quantitative indices of PVS burden. Total brain volume, defined as the sum of gray and white matter volumes, was estimated from MP2RAGE images using the VBM8 toolbox implemented in SPM8 (Statistical Parametric Mapping; www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm), following standardized voxel-based morphometry pipelines. PVS volume were corrected for total brain volume in subsequent analysis.

DTI-ALPS index calculation

DTI data were preprocessed using FSL (v.6.0.1, FMRIB Software Library: http://www.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl) through the following steps: (i) DICOM-to-NFITI conversion, (ii) topup correction, (iii) eddy current correction, (iv) tensor fitting, and (v) registration to standard space. Color-coded fractional anisotropy (FA) maps and diffusivity maps along the x-, y- and z-axes (D x, Dy, Dz) were generated.

The DTI-ALPS method was applied to evaluate glymphatic system activity (Chen et al., 2021). Diffusivities along the x-axis (Dxproj, Dxassoc) in projection and association fibers reflect water diffusivity parallel to PVSs, while diffusivities along the y- and z-axes (Dyproj, Dzassoc) represent perpendicular diffusion components. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Python (v.3.8.0), and figures were generated using GraphPad Prism (v.8, GraphPad Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Data normality was tested with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were compared using independent-sample t-tests for normally distributed data and Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. Numerical variables were analyzed using Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests. Correlations among the DTI-ALPS index, PVS burden, and psychological characteristics were examined using Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation analysis, depending on data distribution. Multivariate analyses were performed using multivariable linear regression to identify independent predicators of glymphatic function. These results were statistically significant (P < 0.05) and were subsequently corrected for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR) method, with Cohen’s d values calculated concurrently. Inter-rater and intra-class correlation coefficient analyses were performed using the intraclass corr() function from the pingouin library in python.

Multivariate associations of PVS burden and DTI-ALPS index

In multivariate models, age remained an independent predictor of PVS burden across regions. In addition, HAMD scores were independently associated with PVS number in the left BG (Table 2). This association was specific to PVS number, as no significant relationship was observed between HAMD scores and PVS volume. PVS burden in BG showed no significant associations with sex, education years, or other psychological measures, including HAMA and PSQI scores. For PVS burden in Mid, bilateral PVS number was significantly associated with age, while no significant associations were observed with sex, education years, or psychological measures, including HAMD, HAMA, and PSQI scores. For functional glymphatic measures, multivariate analysis indicated that sex remained an independent predictor of the left-hemispheric DTI-ALPS index, while HAMD scores also showed a significant association with the left DTI-ALPS index (Table 2). In contrast, education years, age, and other psychological measures did not demonstrate significant associations with DTI-ALPS indices.

Age-related differences in PVS burden and DTI-ALPS index

The Mann–Whitney U test revealed significant age-related differences in PVS burden in both BG and Mid regions, as well as in DTI-ALPS index values (Fig. 1). The HSO group exhibited a significantly higher number of PVSs than the HSY group in both unilateral and bilateral assessments of BG and Mid regions, as show in Fig. 1A and Fig. S3. Volumetric analyses showed smaller left BG PVS volume in the HSY group (40.01 ± 25.79 mm3) compared with HSO group (52.08 ± 30.03 mm3, FDR-adjusted P < 0.05), and significantly smaller bilateral Mid PVS volumes in the HSY group, as shown in Fig. 1B. No difference in right BG PVS volume was observed between groups. Regarding glymphatic function, the right DTI-ALPS index was significantly higher in the HSY group (1.56 ± 0.19) than in the HSO group (1.46 ± 0.22, FDR-adjusted P < 0.05), while the left DTI-ALPS index showed no group difference (Fig. 1C).

Sex-related differences in PVS burden and DTI-ALPS index

Sex-related differences were initially observed in several glymphatic markers. Females exhibited higher PVS volume in the right BG compared with males (uncorrected P = 0.047; Fig. 2B), while PVS number did not differ by sex. The left DTI-ALPS index was higher than in males (uncorrected P = 0.037; Fig. 2C), while no sex-related difference was detected for the right-hemispheric DTI-ALPS index. No significant sex differences were found in Mid PVS burden. After applying FDR correction for multiple comparisons, these sex-related effects did not remain statistically significant. Specifically, the difference in the right PVS number showed a trend-level effect after FDR correction (FDR-adjusted P = 0.245, Cohen's d = 0.479), and the difference in the left DTI-ALPS index also demonstrated a trend-level effect after FDR correction (FDR-adjusted P = 0.074, Cohen's d = 0.513).

Discussion

In this study, we characterized both structural and functional features of the glymphatic system in healthy adults by combining PVS burden and the DTI-ALPS index. Our findings indicate that glymphatic function progressively declines with age and exhibits variability across sex and hemisphere. Younger participants showed fewer PVSs bilaterally in the BG and Mid, smaller PVS volumes in the left BG and bilateral Mid, and higher right-hemispheric DTI-ALPS index compared with older adults. Correlation analyses further confirmed positive associations between age and PVS number in both BG and Mid regions, and with left BG PVS volume, while the right DTI-ALPS index showed an age-related decline. Sex-related differences were observed in selected glymphatic markers, with age-stratified analyses suggesting subtle, region-specific structural–functional patterns. In addition, a consistent right-dominant PVS burden was identified in the midbrain across the cohort, revealing hemispheric asymmetry in glymphatic organization.

Structural and functional interpretation of glymphatic markers

Regarding the relationship between structural and functional glymphatic markers, PVSs represent the anatomical substrates that facilitate CSF–ISF exchange along cerebral vessels (Iliff et al., 2012). In contrast, the DTI-ALPS index reflects water diffusivity preferentially oriented along perivascular directions and is therefore considered an indirect functional proxy of perivascular fluid transport rather than a direct measure of CSF flow (Chen et al., 2021). From this perspective, increased PVS burden and reduced DTI-ALPS values provide convergent, though not identical, evidence of altered perivascular fluid dynamics. Structural enlargement of PVSs may reflect chronic or cumulative changes in perivascular architecture, whereas reductions in the DTI-ALPS index probably capture functional alterations in fluid transport efficiency at the microstructural level. Accordingly, these two metrics should be interpreted as complementary indicators of glymphatic integrity, capturing distinct but interrelated aspects of glymphatic structure and function rather than implying a direct one-to-one correspondence.

Age-related changes

Our results align with prior reports of age-related glymphatic decline, while extending existing literature by concurrently demonstrating both structural (PVS enlargement) and functional (ALPS reduction) alterations within the same healthy cohort. Previous studies, largely based on conventional field MRI or clinical samples, have often reported isolated findings of PVS dilation (Yakushiji et al., 2014; Wardlaw et al., 2020; Dai et al., 2023) or ALPS reduction (Zhang et al., 2021; Hsiao et al., 2023; Ozsahin et al., 2024). By using 7.0T MRI, the present study enabled high-resolution visualization of subtle perivascular microstructural changes and their functional correlates. Mechanistically, aging-related glymphatic impairment may arise from loss of AQP4 polarization (Kress et al., 2014), vascular stiffening and diminished arterial pulsatility (Zieman et al., 2005; Iliff et al., 2013; Shulyatnikova and Hayden, 2023), and disrupted sleep-related CSF–ISF exchange (Mendelsohn and Larrick, 2013). Collectively, these processes may weaken perivascular flow dynamics and increase vulnerability to neurodegenerative disorders characterized by impaired waste clearance.

Sex differences

Sex-related variability in glymphatic markers was observed in selected regions although several effects were attenuated after correction for multiple comparisons. Consistent with prior reports, females tended to exhibit higher DTI-ALPS indices than males (Zhang et al., 2021; Ozsahin et al., 2024; Satpathi et al., 2025), suggesting potential sex differences in perivascular fluid transport efficiency. In the present study, females showed greater PVS volume in the basal ganglia and higher left-hemispheric DTI-ALPS indices, with age-stratified analyses indicating that these differences were more evident in younger adults. This pattern may reflect a compensatory upregulation of glymphatic transport in females during early adulthood, which diminishes with aging. Prior studies integrating CSF–brain coupling metrics have suggested weaker CSF clearance and accelerated functional decline in women (Han et al., 2021, 2024), potentially contributing to sex differences in susceptibility to neurological and psychiatric disorders. Epidemiologically, females are more frequently affected by AD, rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder (RBD), major depressive disorder (MDD) and migraine, while males more frequently develop PD and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (Peterlin et al., 2011; Zhou et al., 2015; Golden and Voskuhl, 2017; Ferretti et al., 2018; Cerri et al., 2019; Pape and Grose, 2020; Mou et al., 2023). However, other studies have reported null sex effects on DTI-ALPS indices or PVS volume (Giannetto et al., 2020; Dai et al., 2023; J.Y. Kim et al., 2023) indicating the need for larger multi-cohort studies incorporating hormonal and genetic modulators (e.g. estrogen, APOE genotype) to clarify underlying mechanisms. Although several sex-related effects were attenuated after FDR correction, the consistency in effect direction and the presence of moderate-to-large effect sizes suggest that subtle sex-related variability in glymphatic markers may exist in healthy adults. These findings warrant confirmation in larger, adequately powered cohorts.

Hemispheric asymmetry

We observed a consistent right-dominant PVS burden in the Mid, indicating hemispheric asymmetry in glymphatic structure. This hemispheric asymmetry may reflect underlying lateralization in cerebral vascular architecture and metabolic demand. Previous studies have demonstrated a leftward dominance in cerebral vascular structure in adults, characterized by larger arterial calibers, higher blood flow velocities, and increased overall flow volumes in the left hemisphere compared with the right (Jansen van Vuuren et al., 2017). Similar vascular asymmetry has also been reported early in development, with right-handed neonates showing larger diameters and higher blood flow volumes in the left middle cerebral artery trunk (Jansen van Vuuren et al., 2022, 2024). Consistent with this vascular dominance, the left cerebral hemisphere has been shown to exhibit more robust perfusion and nutrient supply (Gur et al., 1982; Willis et al., 2002). Furthermore, hemispheric metabolic asymmetry has been reported, with greater cerebral glucose metabolism in left-hemispheric regions including the medial frontal gyrus, posterior thalamus, lingual gyrus, cuneus, and superior cingulate gyrus (Willis et al., 2002). Collectively, this leftward dominance in vascular structure and metabolic activity may impose asymmetric demands on perivascular clearance pathways, potentially contributing to lateralized glymphatic function. Such an imbalance could render one hemisphere more efficient in fluid exchange and waste clearance, while the contralateral side may be more susceptible to perivascular fluid stagnation or structural dilation. Although this interpretation remains speculative, it provides a biologically plausible framework for the hemispheric differences in PVS burden observed in the present study. Notably, lateralized glymphatic dysfunction has also been reported in PD (Claassen et al., 2016; Shen et al., 2022) and temporal lobe epilepsy (Zhao et al., 2023). In healthy populations, left-hemispheric dominance of glymphatic function has also been suggested (Zhao et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025). Our findings partially align with this pattern, showing less structural impairment on the left side. Such asymmetry may represent a fundamental anatomical bias that modulates susceptibility to lateralized neuropathologies.

Clinical implications

The present findings provide normative reference data for age- and sex-related variability in glymphatic structure and function. Such reference values may facilitate the early detection of subclinical glymphatic impairment and contribute to personalized risk assessment for neurodegenerative or cerebrovascular diseases. Incorporating glymphatic metrics into longitudinal follow-up studies, particularly in peri- and post-menopausal women, may further enhance our understanding of disease vulnerability and inform early preventive strategies.

Strengths and limitations

The use of ultra-high-field 7.0T MRI represents a major strength of this study, providing submillimeter-resolution visualization of fine PVS structures and sensitive assessment of white matter microstructural diffusivity. By intergrating PVS burden and DTI-ALPS indices, we offer the most comprehensive, non-invasive characterization of glymphatic integrity in healthy adults to date. However, several limitations nonetheless need to be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference, and longitudinal studies are required to delineate temporal trajectories of glymphatic change. Second, the sample size limited statistical power for detecting higher-order interactions between age, sex, and hemispheric effects. Third, although the DTI-ALPS index is a useful surrogate for glymphatic function, it remains an indirect measure of fluid transport. Future studies integrating multimodal imaging approaches, such as dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI, functional ultrasound image, or PET, will be important for validation. In addition, while psychological status and sleep quality were assessed using standardized questionnaires, comprehensive cognitive assessments were not included, limiting direct inference regarding cognitive correlates of glymphatic variability. Future investigations incorporating detailed neuropsychological assessments are warranted. Finally, sleep and circadian influences were not objectively measured. Given the strong modulation of glymphatic activity by sleep and age-related changes (Chong et al., 2022; Mander et al., 2017), the integration of objective sleep measures, such as polysomnography or actigraphy will be important for clarifying the sleep–glymphatic interactions.

Conclusion

In summary, both structure and function components of glymphatic system are modulated by age and sex in healthy adults. Aging was consistently associated with increased perivascular space burden and reduced perivascular fluid transport efficiency, indicating progressive glymphatic decline across adulthood. Sex-related differences were observed in selected glymphatic markers, suggesting subtle structural–functional variability between males and females. In addition, hemispheric asymmetry was evident, with a right-dominant structural PVS burden in the midbrain, implying lateralized organization of glymphatic pathways. Together, these findings provide high-resolution normative reference data that may support future investigations of glymphatic dysfunction in neurological and psychiatric disorders.
