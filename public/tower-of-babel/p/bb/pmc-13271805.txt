Gestational Diabetes Mellitus and Risk of Selected Cancers: A Danish National Register-based Cohort Study
Authors: Lana Rashid Flachs Madsen, Ulrik Schiøler Kesmodel, Karoline Kragelund Nielsen, Peter Damm, Jeannet Lauenborg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is the most common medical complication of pregnancy and is associated with type 2 diabetes, which is associated with breast cancer and kidney cancer. However, little is known about the risk of cancer following a GDM diagnosis.ObjectiveThis study aimed to test the hypothesis that a history of GDM associates with increased risk of breast, gynecological, thyroid, and kidney cancer.MethodsNational cohort study, based on Danish registry data. The study population included a group of women exposed to GDM in their pregnancy leading to delivery between 2000 and 2019 or 1978 and 1997 and a 1:10 matched nonexposed group, matched on calendar period of delivery, the woman´s age (birth year), and the woman´s registered region of residence at delivery. Poisson regression adjusting for educational level was conducted.ResultsOf the 314 742 deliveries included, 28 613 deliveries were in the exposed group and 286 129 in the nonexposed. The median follow-up time was 8.1 years. We found no increased risk of the selected cancers combined [adjusted incidence rate ratio (aIRR) 1.05; 95% confidence interval (CI), 0.94-1.18], breast cancer (aIRR 1.02; 95% CI, 0.89-1.17), gynecological cancer (aIRR 1.00; 95% CI, 0.79-1.26), or thyroid cancer (aIRR 1.26; 95% CI, 0.86-1.82). However, an increased risk of kidney cancer was seen in women with previous GDM compared with women without GDM (aIRR 1.92; 95% CI, 1.10-3.33), although it was a very small absolute risk.ConclusionNo elevated risk was found for the selected cancers combined. Women with previous GDM had an increased risk of kidney cancer, a rare cancer form.

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), defined as abnormal glucose intolerance first diagnosed during pregnancy (1), complicates approximately 6% of all Danish pregnancies (2). Globally, the prevalence is reported as between 7% and 28% and increasing, making it the most common medical complication of pregnancy (1, 3-5). After giving birth, the glucose tolerance returns to normal in the majority, but women with previous GDM are at almost 10-fold increased risk of developing diabetes later in life, particularly type 2 diabetes, and at a 2-fold increased risk of cardiovascular diseases (6-9).

Type 2 diabetes is associated with increased risk of morbidity and mortality, eg, from cardiovascular events such as myocardial infarction and heart and renal failure (10-12). In addition to renal failure, type 2 diabetes is associated with kidney cancer, especially in women (13-15). A meta-analysis showed that type 2 diabetes is associated with an increased overall risk of breast cancer, one of the most common cancer diseases among women worldwide (16). However, little is known about GDM and the risk of cancer. A single study on short-term risk among women with previous GDM has shown an increased risk of thyroid cancer and a reduced risk of premenopausal breast cancer compared with women without previous GDM (17). Another short-term follow-up study after GDM found an elevated risk of several cancers, including kidney, breast, and thyroid cancer (18).

Most studies have a follow-up time of less than 10 years after the GDM-affected pregnancy. It is essential to have a longer follow-up period for women with previous GDM, since cancer typically manifests much later in life. A systematic review published in 2022 concluded that evidence on long-term morbidity, such as chronic disease and cancer, after GDM was sparse (19). Furthermore, existing studies are mostly based on case-control designs with self-reported GDM diagnoses and thus are at risk of misclassification (19). In 2024, a Danish register-based nationwide long-term follow-up study found no association between GDM and breast cancer (20). However, a systematic review published the same year on the risk of cancer after GDM showed an increased risk of several cancers after GDM, including breast cancer (21). Hence, the existing evidence is inconsistent, and more studies based on well-defined cohorts and data with high validity are needed.

This study aimed to test the hypothesis that women in Denmark with previous GDM, as compared with women without previous GDM, have an increased long-term risk of being diagnosed with selected cancers, namely breast, gynecological, thyroid, kidney, and urinary tract cancer.

Methods

This is a national cohort study based on Danish registry data. In Denmark, all residents have a unique personal identification number, stored in the Danish Civil Registration System (CRS), which enables interlinkage with all population-based Danish national administrative and medical registries at an individual-based level (22). In this study, we used data from the CRS, the Danish National Patient Register (DNPR) (23, 24), the Danish Medical Birth Register (DMBR) (25), the Danish Cancer Register (DCR) (26), and the Danish Attainment Register (27). All diagnoses and surgical procedures were defined according to the Eighth Revision (1978-1993) of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-8) and the Tenth Revision (from 1994) of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10), used in Denmark (28).

Study Population

Women ≥ 18 years old who were residents of Denmark with ≥1 singleton delivery at gestational length ≥22 completed weeks (live or stillborn) between 1978 and 2019 were included. Women with a registered diagnosis of diabetes mellitus (ICD-8: 250; ICD-10: DE10-DE14) up to 5 years before the delivery date were excluded.

Exposed Group

The exposed group (n = 28 613) comprised 2 mutually exclusive Danish cohorts of women with GDM with a delivery, respectively, between 2000 and 2019 (the national cohort) and 1978 and 1997 (the local cohort) (Fig. 1). Exposure was the first pregnancy complicated with GDM (ICD-10: DO24.4 and a local hospital database), referred to as the index pregnancy. Registry data on GDM for 1998-1999 (ICD-8: 634.74) were considered associated with uncertainty due to a lack of differentiation between pregestational and gestational diabetes and therefore were not included in the study. Due to this temporal gap, an overlap of the 2 cohorts was not possible.

Flowchart of included deliveries according to GDM exposure.

The national cohort (n = 27 744) consisted of all women in Denmark with a delivery between 2000 and 2019 where GDM was coded in the ICD-10 (ICD-10: DO24.4); these women were identified using the CRS, the DNPR, and the DMBR. Before 2000, the ICD-8 code for diabetes mellitus gestationis (ICD-8: 634.74) was applicable, which did not differentiate clearly between GDM and other types of diabetes.

To be able to explore late-life morbidity, we included the local cohort (n = 869), which consisted of women with GDM from the eastern part of Denmark with a delivery between 1978 and 1997. Data on GDM were obtained from a hospital record database at the Center for Pregnant Women with Diabetes, Rigshospitalet, in Copenhagen. The women were screened for GDM and diagnosed as previously described (7, 8). At that time, the center took care of nearly all women with GDM from eastern Denmark, covering approximately half of the Danish population.

Nonexposed Group and Matching

For comparison, a matched nonexposed group of women (n = 222 749) was sampled from the general population using the CRS, DNPR, and DMBR (Fig. 1). The nonexposed group had never been diagnosed with GDM (ICD-8: 634.74, ICD-10: DO24.4) and were not a part of the local cohort, and these women contributed with ≥1 deliveries (n = 286 129). Each delivery in the exposed group was matched with 10 deliveries from nonexposed women. The deliveries were matched on the following 3 criteria:

calendar period of delivery (1978-1989, 1990-1999, 2000-2009, 2010-2019),

the woman’s age (birth year), and

the woman’s registered region of residence at delivery (Capital Region of Denmark, Region Zealand, Region of Southern Denmark, Central Denmark Region, North Denmark Region).

Matching was performed using a sampling strategy in random order with replacement, allowing deliveries in the nonexposed group to serve as a reference for more than 1 delivery in the exposed group to ensure the highest possible comparability and 10 matches for each delivery in the exposed group. Whenever it was not possible to find 10 matches for a delivery in the exposed group, a number less than 10 was accepted.

GDM Screening and Diagnostic Criteria

During the study period, a risk factor-based screening was used in Denmark, and GDM was diagnosed based on an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT).

For the local cohort (1978-1997), GDM was based on a 50-g 3-hour OGTT or 75-g 3-hour OGTT (from 1986) and glucose values >3 SDs above the mean for normal weight nonpregnant women (7). The risk factors used for screening were glucosuria, previous GDM, pregestational overweight [>20% or body mass index (BMI) ≥ 27 kg/m2], family history of diabetes, previous delivery of an infant with birth weight ≥4500 g, and age ≥35 years (7).

For most of the national cohort (2000-2019), GDM was diagnosed according to national clinical guidelines, first implemented in 2004. GDM was based on a 75-g 2-hour OGTT performed at 27 to 30 weeks of gestation and 2-hour plasma glucose values ≥9 mmol/L (29). The risk factors were modified slightly into glucosuria, previous GDM, BMI ≥27 kg/m2, family history of diabetes, and previous delivery of an infant with birth weight ≥4500 g (30). From 2014 onwards, the updated guideline suggested that OGTT be performed at 24 to 28 weeks of gestation, and polycystic ovary syndrome and multiple gestations were added as risk factors (29).

Follow-up

The CRS contains daily updated information on migration and vital status, enabling long-term follow-up on all individuals residing in Denmark (22). Data from the CRS are virtually complete and of high accuracy (22). All women were included from the delivery date and followed up until diagnosed with the studied disease outcomes, emigration, death, or end of follow-up on December 31, 2019, whichever occurred first. Once a woman was included in the exposed group, she remained there, even if she later experienced a pregnancy without GDM. An exposed woman could only contribute with the delivery of the index pregnancy, as opposed to a nonexposed woman, who could contribute with more than 1 delivery as a reference. For both the exposed and the nonexposed group, person-years and disease outcomes were counted from entry date to exit date; thus person-years and follow-up time vary depending on the studied disease outcome.

Outcomes

The DCR contains information on all patients diagnosed with cancer in Denmark from 1943 onwards (26). Cancer outcomes included breast, gynecological (vulvar, vaginal, cervical, uterine, and ovarian), thyroid, kidney, and urinary tract cancer. Uterine cancer is usually referred to as endometrial cancer, which arises from the epithelial lining of the uterine cavity (31). These cancer types were selected a priori based on the biological plausibility and existing epidemiological evidence linking type 2 diabetes—and related metabolic disturbances—to the risk of cancer in these organs. Diagnoses of premalignancy or adenocarcinomas in any organ were not included, nor were cancers related to placenta tissue. Data were obtained from the DCR (28). For each study outcome, only incident cases were considered. According to Danish legislation, publication of fewer than 3 observations in register-based data is prohibited, and therefore those findings are not shown in this paper.

Other Variables and Covariates

The DMBR contains administrative and medical information on all deliveries in Denmark from 1973 (25). From the DMBR, data were obtained on the woman´s age at delivery, parity, pregestational BMI (kg/m2) (from 2004), smoking status during pregnancy (from 1997), delivery date, gestational length at delivery, preterm delivery (delivery <37 completed weeks of gestation), gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, delivery mode, and stillborn infant. These data were extracted as baseline characteristics of the cohorts.

Data on the highest completed education were obtained from the Danish Attainment Register (27). The highest completed education was categorized into 4 categories (basic, short, long, and unknown/missing value).

Statistical Methods

Baseline characteristics were summarized by counts, proportions, medians, and interquartile ranges (IQR). Frequencies were compared by the χ2 test. The incidence rate ratios (IRRs) were estimated using a Poisson regression model. Risk was calculated according to person-years. Person-years were calculated from the delivery date to the date of cancer diagnosis, emigration, death, or end of follow-up on December 31, 2019, whichever occurred first. The models were adjusted for educational level. In addition, sensitivity analyses were carried out, in which additional adjustments for pregestational BMI and smoking status during pregnancy were performed on a subgroup consisting of all deliveries from 2004 to 2019, with existing data on both pregestational BMI and smoking status. Values of BMI <15 and >55 kg/m2 were considered possibly faulty and excluded from the analyses. Smoking status during pregnancy was dichotomous (yes or no). Corrections were not made for multiple testing. E-value was calculated for robustness against unmeasured confounders. Estimated adjusted IRR (aIRR) were presented with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and P-values. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Characteristics of Exposed and Nonexposed Groups at Baseline

A total of 314 742 deliveries were included during the study period: 28 613 deliveries in the exposed group and 286 129 deliveries in the nonexposed group, completed by 222 749 women (Fig. 1). Eleven deliveries in the exposed group could not reach a total number of 10 matches and had to settle with a number between 1 and 9. The characteristics of the exposed and nonexposed groups at baseline are presented in Table 1. Most deliveries (62%) occurred during 2010-2019, 34% during 2000-2009, 1.8% during 1990-1999, and 1.2% during 1978-1989. The distribution of the women's region of residence at delivery was the Capital Region of Denmark (29%), Region Zealand (16%), the Region of Southern Denmark (22%), the Central Denmark Region (22%), and the North Denmark Region (11%).

The women’s median age at delivery was 31.2 (IQR: 27.8, 34.8) years. The median follow-up time was 8.1 years. The exposed group had a higher median pregestational BMI compared with the nonexposed group at 28.0 kg/m2 (IQR: 23.9, 32.5) vs 23.1 kg/m2 (IQR: 20.9, 26.4). IRRs of Cancer According to GDM Exposure Group

Table 2 shows the incidence rates and IRRs for cancer outcomes according to GDM exposure group. The risk of selected cancers combined was not increased in the exposed group compared with the nonexposed group (aIRR 1.05; 95% CI, 0.94-1.18). Likewise, we found no statistically significant increased risk of breast cancer (aIRR 1.02; 95% CI, 0.89-1.17), gynecological cancer (aIRR 1.00; 95% CI, 0.79-1.26), or thyroid cancer (aIRR 1.26; 95% CI, 0.86-1.82). However, women with a history of GDM had a higher relative risk of kidney cancer compared with the nonexposed group (aIRR 1.92; 95% CI, 1.10-3.33). The incidence rate of kidney cancer was 0.94 per 10 000 person-years, and the cumulative incidence was 0.0094%. It was not possible to estimate the risk of urinary tract cancer in this study due to too few observations.

Also in this subgroup, we found an increased crude risk of selected cancers combined (aIRR 1.23; 95% CI, 1.06-1.42), breast cancer (aIRR 1.21; 95% CI, 1.00-1.46), and uterine (endometrial) cancer (aIRR 2.76; 95% CI, 1.27-6.05) among women with a history of GDM, which persisted after adjustments for pregestational BMI and smoking status. Discussion

This study showed that women in Denmark with a history of GDM, as compared with women without a history of GDM, do not have an increased risk of being diagnosed with the selected cancers combined or breast, gynecological, or thyroid cancer within a median follow-up time of 8.1 years. We found a nearly 2-fold increased relative risk of being diagnosed with kidney cancer; however, this is a rare cancer form, and thus the absolute risk was found to be below 0.02%.

Breast Cancer

One of the most studied cancers among women with previous GDM is breast cancer (21), but the results have been inconsistent (19, 21). In our study, the results regarding breast cancer also divert, as our main analysis did not find an increased risk but our sensitivity analyses on a subgroup of deliveries between 2004 and 2019 showed an increased risk of breast cancer in both the crude model and when adjusted for pregestational BMI and smoking status. A meta-analysis from 2020 of 15 observational studies (7 case-control and 8 cohort) by Wang et al studied the association between GDM and incident breast cancer risk (32). The authors concluded that GDM was not associated with the risk of breast cancer in case-control studies (odds ratio 0.88; 95% CI, 0.69-1.12) or cohort studies [relative ratio (RR) 1.08; 95% CI, 0.89-1.32], with 88.6% heterogeneity across the cohort studies. A recent Danish nationwide cohort register-based study by Christensen et al on the long-term risk of breast cancer after GDM with a median follow-up of 12 years found no association [adjusted hazard ratio (HR) 0.96; 95% CI, 0.83-1.12] and no substantial difference after adjusting for pregestational BMI and smoking status in a sensitivity analysis (20). Stratification by age at cancer diagnosis <50 and ≥50 years as a proxy for pre- or postmenopausal breast cancer did not alter their estimates substantially. This population was similar to ours (also based on Danish registry data); however, the study population was not matched at baseline. Instead, adjustments were made for several potential confounders, such as maternal age, parity, preexisting hypertension, preexisting comorbidities, ethnicity, marital status, income, occupation, education, and calendar year of delivery (20). Pace et al conducted a cohort study of 34 294 women with GDM and a matched reference group of an equal number of women without GDM on GDM and the risk of primary incident cancer (33). No association was found for breast cancer (HR 0.93; 95% CI, 0.80-1.09), adjusting for gestational hypertension, preterm delivery, infant size, parity, prior comorbidity, maternal deprivation index, and ethnicity (33). Our main analysis result of no increased risk of incident breast cancer after a history of GDM is aligned with the results of all the aforementioned studies, indicating consistency (20, 32, 33). Unlike Christensen et al (20), however, our sensitivity analyses on a subgroup of deliveries between 2004 and 2019 showed an increased risk of breast cancer in both the crude model (IRR 1.21; 95% CI, 1.00-1.46) and as we further adjusted for pregestational BMI and smoking (Table 3). While the lower 95% CI of 1.00 undoubtedly indicated a small effect on the verge of nonsignificance, a true effect cannot be excluded due to the narrow 95% CI interval shown. The discrepancies between the 2 Danish studies show that even in a very similar population, differences by choice of study design, statistical methods, or analysis of the data can influence the results. In our study, we matched at baseline, which reduces the risk of confounding even before data analysis and is more robust in the case of rare outcomes. Peng et al recently conducted a nationwide cohort study of 990 572 women—47 343 with GDM and the rest nonexposed—with the objective to evaluate the risk of incident cancers in women with prior GDM in Taiwan (18). Based on diagnosis codes from the Ninth Revision of the International Classification of Diseases, they found an increased risk of breast cancer (adjusted HR 1.23; 95% CI, 1.09-1.39) adjusted for age, hypertension, dyslipidemia, liver disease, infertility, and kidney disease but not for obesity or smoking status (18). Fuchs et al undertook a cohort study on nearly 95 000 Israeli women and showed a 2-fold increased risk (RR 2.00; 95% CI, 1.60-2.51); adjustments for confounders were not reported (34). Another Israeli cohort study on a population of nearly 38 000 women found a 1.5-fold increased risk (adjusted RR 1.5; 95% CI, 1.0-2.1) adjusted for age, birth order at first observed birth, social class, ethnic origin, education, and education status with a median follow-up time of 34 years (35). Stratified by age <50 years at diagnosis, they did not find an association with previous GDM; however, at age ≥50 years at diagnosis the adjusted RR was 1.7 (95% CI, 1.1-2.5), indicating an association of previous GDM and postmenopausal breast cancer. This study by Perrin et al has been described as perhaps the only 1 with a sufficiently long follow-up period to cover breast cancer cases diagnosed at postmenopausal ages [19].

Studies with subgroup analyses by region on the association of GDM and breast cancer have previously reported Asian studies as more likely to find an association, as opposed to North American studies, which do not (32). Our study did not adjust for ethnicity; however, our population was based in a European country, and our results are consistent with the mentioned studies conducted in North America and Europe, rather than the Asian studies.

Gynecological, Thyroid, and Kidney Cancer

It is well known that obesity is the strongest nongenetic risk factor for endometrial cancer (36) and that obesity is more prevalent in women with previous GDM (8). Our main analysis did not find an increased risk of uterine (endometrial) cancer (aIRR 1.14; 95% CI, 0.63-2.07), indicating previous GDM itself is likely not a risk factor for endometrial cancer. Our sensitivity analyses, however, found an increased risk in both the crude model (IRR 2.76; 95% CI, 1.27-6.05) and after further adjustments for pregestational BMI and smoking, although due to the small sample sizes, the estimates had wide CIs and are therefore considered imprecise (Table 3). Wartko et al found no increased risk of endometrial cancer after a history of GDM in an American case-control study with a median time of 14 years from delivery to diagnosis, adjusted for pregestational BMI (36), like the previously mentioned Canadian cohort study by Pace et al, conducted in a matched population, with a mean follow-up time of 13 years (33). These results are in line with our main findings. Fuchs et al, however, found a 2.1-fold increased risk of endometrial cancer (odds ratio 2.1; 95% CI, 1.01-4.05); no adjustments for potential confounders were reported, including BMI (34).

A 39% increase in long-term risk of thyroid cancer after a history of GDM had previously been described in 2 cohort studies from Taiwan and Canada, respectively (18, 33). Our study found no significant association in a population almost 10-fold larger than the Canadian study (33). The study by Peng et al was based on a large Taiwanese population, and the discrepancy between our study and the 1 by Peng et al can be explained by different ethnic populations (18). The existing literature is sparse on this association (19), and future studies with large cohorts, longer time to follow-up, and adjustments for relevant confounders are needed to further explore this association.

Kidney cancer is a rare disease, with an absolute risk of incident kidney cancer around 0.01% in women (37). Our main results showed a 2-fold increased risk of kidney cancer among women with previous GDM adjusting for education level in a cohort matched on calendar period of delivery, the woman´s age (birth date), and the woman’s registered region of residence at delivery. The finding persisted in our sensitivity analysis of a subgroup of women that additionally adjusted for pregestational BMI and smoking status. For a rare outcome like kidney cancer, the estimates were, however, considered less precise and certain due to small sample sizes in each group, resulting in the wide 95% CI of 1.10 to 3.33 (Table 2). Nonetheless, our results are aligned with the results of other observational studies. A nationwide cohort study from Taiwan, similarly, found a 2-fold increased risk of incident kidney cancer after GDM during a study period of 11 years (18). The authors’ assessment of adjusted HR was adjusted for age at delivery and comorbidities at baseline (hypertension, dyslipidemia, infertility, liver disease, and kidney disease) but not for BMI (18). Their study is somewhat different from ours in terms of study population and potential confounders, but our main analysis results are very much aligned. Confounding by obesity is well known in studies on the association between diabetes and kidney cancer (15), so pregestational obesity might therefore be a potential confounder in the association between GDM and kidney cancer, since women with previous GDM continue having a high BMI later in life (8). It is, however, crucial to emphasize that kidney cancer is as yet a highly rare cancer form and that a 2-fold increased relative risk corresponds to an absolute risk below 0.02% and is thus likely of limited clinical relevance.

Bias and Study Limitations

This large national population-based cohort study had a study population of almost 300 000 deliveries observed over a study period of 40 years, which ensured representativeness and enabled long-term follow-up. A prominent strength was that it was based on national health registries on demographic data, education level, data on pregnancies and deliveries, and contacts with the health care system based on diagnosis and procedure codes, minimizing selection bias. Data from the Danish health registries are considered valid and of high sensitivity (23), including the DMBR where the GDM diagnosis is found (38), minimizing the risk of information bias. Another strength was the design with a cohort matched on potential, important confounders at baseline, such as calendar period of delivery, the woman´s age (birth year), and the woman´s registered region of residence at delivery. In addition, adjustments were made for education. In sensitivity analyses, additional adjustments were made for pregestational BMI and smoking status during pregnancy as potential confounders without substantial changes to the IRRs.

A limitation of this study was that most women had less than 10 years of follow-up, which may have biased estimates toward the null for cancers with long latency periods, particularly postmenopausal cancers. As a result, the findings of this study primarily reflected the early to mid-term risk of cancer following GDM rather than the lifetime risk of cancer. The smaller proportion of women with long-term follow-up further limited the ability to detect associations for less frequent cancers and cancers typically diagnosed later in life. Another limitation was that adjustments were not made for other potential key confounders, such as parity and ethnicity. Also, we did not have information on some potential confounders, such as diet, physical activity, alcohol consumption, polycystic ovary syndrome, oral contraceptives, genetic factors, and family history of diabetes and cancer. Pregestational BMI was first registered late in 2003 in the DMBR, enabling adjustments here from 2004 onward. Thus, in our study, adjustments for pregestational BMI and smoking were only possible for deliveries between 2004 and 2019. Statistical methods incorporating confounding with these variables in a main model would have excluded a substantial part of our cohort (1978-2003) and augmented selection bias.

Obstetric characteristics, such as preeclampsia and preterm delivery, were not included in the assessments of risk of cancer, as they were viewed as potential intermediates in the pathway between exposure and outcomes and not as potential confounders. Adjusting for these variables could have introduced overadjustment bias, obscuring true associations.

As GDM screening in Denmark is based on risk factors, some women in the nonexposed group may have had an undiagnosed GDM. Such nondifferential misclassification would most likely have biased risk estimates toward the null, potentially underestimating true associations between GDM and cancer. Jensen et al estimated the validity of the risk indicator-based screening model in Denmark and found a sensitivity of 80.6%. The prevalence of women with GDM in the nonrisk group was 0.7% (30).

Additionally, the Danish criteria for GDM screening have changed slightly across the study period; it included polycystic ovary syndrome and multiple gestations in 2014, resulting in more women being screened. Multiple gestations were excluded in this study, so the impact of this change on the associations of this study was considered null or minor.

The observed associations in the subgroup of all deliveries from 2004 to 2019 should be interpreted with caution. The 2004 to 2019 period was the only period in which adjustments for pregestational BMI and smoking were possible, improving confounder control in a subgroup but augmenting selection bias. This subgroup had a shorter median follow-up time (6.6 years), which may have preferentially captured cancers that were already developing or promoted by preexisting metabolic and hormonal disturbances associated with GDM, such as insulin resistance and hyperinsulinemia. These mechanisms may have accelerated the progression or clinical manifestation of malignancies with relatively short latency without necessarily reflecting an increased long-term risk of cancer attributable to GDM itself.

In addition, the 2004 to 2019 subgroup represented a more contemporary population, where diagnostic criteria and coding practices had become more standardized than previously and potentially represent improved data quality. The observed increased risks of selected cancers combined, breast cancer, and endometrial cancer in this subgroup could suggest potential residual confounding in the full cohort. Based on the calculated E-value of 1.76 for selected cancers combined, the observed crude IRR of 1.23 could potentially be explained by an unmeasured confounder that was associated with both GDM and cancer by a risk ratio of 1.76; however, weaker confounding could not do so. Finally, given the limited number of events and multiple subgroup testing, chance findings could not be excluded, increasing the risk of type I errors (false positives).

Interpretation and Generalization

This large, national cohort study based on Danish registry data found that women with a history of GDM are not at increased risk of being diagnosed with the selected cancers combined, compared to women without GDM. Specifically, no associations were found with breast, gynecological, or thyroid cancer. We found a nearly 2-fold increased relative risk of kidney cancer in women with prior GDM, which persisted after adjustments for key confounders. Yet, kidney cancer remains a rare disease, and even a doubled relative risk results in a still very small absolute risk of being diagnosed with kidney cancer.

Given the strengths of this study and the consistency with the results of other observational studies based on hospital records, we consider the results solid. Overall, our results support that GDM is not a strong predictor of future cancer, but certain associations seen in subgroup analyses may warrant further study and longer follow-up times to explore the postmenopausal risk of cancer. These findings emphasize the importance of study design choices, including matching and follow-up length, in creating epidemiological interpretations. For clinical practice, there appears to be no justification for altered cancer surveillance in women with prior GDM based on current evidence.

Conclusion

Women with previous GDM did not have an increased risk of being diagnosed with the selected cancers combined or with breast, gynecological, or thyroid cancer. With respect to kidney cancer, which is a rare cancer form, women in Denmark with previous GDM had an almost 2-fold increased relative risk of incident kidney cancer compared with women without GDM. From a clinical perspective, the findings do not support enhanced cancer surveillance solely based on previous GDM. Based on this study, we suggest observational studies on large, well-defined populations based on hospital records with follow-up times longer than 10 years that are adjusted for pregestational BMI and perhaps ethnicity. Future studies with longer follow-up into postmenopausal age and more detailed metabolic characterization are warranted to further explore potential time-dependent associations.
