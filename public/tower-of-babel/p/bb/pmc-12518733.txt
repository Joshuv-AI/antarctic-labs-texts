Identifying the causal role and therapeutic potential of immune-related genes in bladder cancer: a Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Yonggang Chen, Qingfeng Yu, Xiaomei Jiang, Lin Li, Le Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBladder cancer, a highly aggressive malignancy, necessitates effective therapeutic strategies due to the significant correlation between muscle invasion and prognosis. Despite advancements in immunotherapy, the mechanisms through which immune-related genes influence bladder cancer remain unclear.MethodsWe conducted a comprehensive causal analysis of immune-related genes using GWAS data from the UK Biobank and FinnGen, and immune gene information from InnateDB. Mendelian Randomization (MR) analysis, supported by sensitivity, colocalization, and reverse causality analyses, was performed using eQTLGen Consortium and proteomics GWAS data. We analyzed protein-protein interaction networks with GeneMANIA and validated findings using mRNA expression and clinical survival data from the TCGA database.ResultsThe expression of the GSTM1 gene demonstrated an inverse correlation with cancer risk, suggesting a protective role in disease progression. Our study also identified OLFM4, NTN1, ITPR3, CLU, and CARD11 as genes significantly associated with immune cell composition and bladder cancer survival. Additionally, we uncovered protein interaction networks and key pathways involving these immune-related genes, providing new insights into bladder cancer intervention and treatment.ConclusionThis study elucidates the immune-related pathways influencing bladder cancer, highlighting GSTM1 and other key immune-related genes as potential biomarkers. These discoveries pave the way for innovative therapeutic approaches, advancing personalized medicine strategies in bladder cancer treatment.Keywords: Bladder cancer, Immune-related genes, Mendelian randomization, Colocalization analysis, transcriptomics

Background

Bladder cancer (BC) is the most prevalent malignant tumor in the urinary system, ranking among the most common cancers globally, with approximately 165,000 deaths annually. BC’s complex biological behavior and high recurrence and metastasis rates pose significant challenges in diagnosis and treatment, particularly influenced by muscle invasion. Non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) generally presents with a more favorable prognosis, while muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) is associated with a higher degree of malignancy. The prognosis for MIBC remains limited even with aggressive treatments such as radical cystectomy, underscoring the critical need for the development of novel therapeutic approaches [1, 2].

Quantitative Trait Loci (QTLs) are genetic regions that influence quantitative traits, impacted by multiple genes and environmental factors. Research in QTL maps and identifies genes related to these traits, laying the groundwork for genetic studies and personalized medicine. In the medical field, QTL mapping is instrumental in uncovering the genetic mechanisms behind complex diseases and their effects on drug responses [3, 4]. Recent findings suggest QTLs have significant implications in bladder cancer, particularly in diagnostics and treatment. For example, Liu et al. demonstrated the utility of QTLs in facilitating rapid diagnosis using mRNA-based biomarkers, while a 2024 review in Frontiers highlighted their potential in identifying therapeutic targets and enhancing patient outcomes [5, 6].

Immunotherapy has proven increasingly effective in bladder cancer treatment, largely due to the role of immune-related genes and the tumor microenvironment. Immune checkpoints like PD-L1 and CTLA-4 regulate immune responses, allowing cancer cells to evade detection. Elevated PD-L1 expression has been linked to poorer prognoses in bladder cancer [7–9]. The advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors, including PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 inhibitors, has improved patient survival rates [10]. Tumor-Infiltrating Lymphocytes (TILs), such as T cells, B cells, and natural killer cells, are pivotal for prognosis and the success of immunotherapy. Enhancing TIL infiltration can significantly boost the efficacy of immunotherapy [11].

Mendelian Randomization (MR) employs genetic variants as instrumental variables to investigate causal links between exposures and outcomes, functioning similarly to randomized controlled trials. MR studies on immune-related genes and the immune microenvironment provide new insights into BC’s diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment [12]. This research focuses on exploring personalized immunotherapy strategies to improve survival and quality of life for BC patients [3].

The objective of this study is to uncover immune-related genes that can improve the efficacy and precision of personalized immunotherapy for bladder cancer. Through the application of techniques such as Mendelian Randomization (MR), we aim to determine the causal links between specific immune-related genes and bladder cancer outcomes, which could lead to the development of more targeted and effective therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Data from genome-wide association studies for bladder cancer

The current study leverages summary data from genome-wide association studies (GWAS) of bladder cancer (BC), obtained from the UK Biobank database (www.ukbiobank.ac.uk) and the FinnGen database (https://r10.finngen.fi/) [13, 14]. The UK Biobank dataset, identified by the accession number 20001_1035, comprises 843 cases and 360,298 controls, with a total of 13,791,468 single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) loci included in the analysis. The FinnGen dataset, referenced by the code R10_C3_BLADDER_EXALLC, consists of 2,193 cases and 314,193 controls, encompassing 21,303,877 SNP loci. The overall design of the study and the analytical workflow are illustrated in Fig. 1.

Overall study design and analytical workflow. The analysis included immune gene selection from the InnateDB database, cis-eQTL and pQTL data acquisition, instrumental variable filtering, and Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis to assess causal associations with bladder cancer risk. Subsequent steps included sensitivity analyses, colocalization analysis, protein–protein interaction network construction, differential expression and survival analyses, immune infiltration profiling, and gene set enrichment analysis

Immune-related genes from the InnateDB database

InnateDB is an open-access database that compiles genes, proteins, experimentally validated interactions, and signaling pathways related to the innate immune response in humans, mice, and cattle [15]. This database merges established interactions and pathways from prominent public sources with manually curated data, forming a centralized resource that enhances the breadth of the innate immune interactome. From InnateDB (https://www.innatedb.com/), 1,378 human immune-related genes were retrieved.

Gene selection

Immune-related genes analyzed in this study were selected based on a combination of prior evidence from the literature and genetic association data. Initially, we focused on genes with established roles in immune response or immune-related pathways. These genes were further validated through genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) analyses. In this study, we focused on cis-eQTLs and pQTLs for the following reasons. Cis-eQTLs and pQTLs are more likely to reflect direct effects on gene expression, making them a more reliable proxy for causal relationships. In contrast, trans-eQTLs and pQTLs can introduce more complex, indirect associations that may weaken the validity of the causal inferences. This distinction is important for ensuring the robustness and accuracy of our Mendelian randomization analysis.

Cis-eQTL data for immune-related genes

The eQTLGen Consortium (https://eqtlgen.org/) focuses on identifying the downstream effects of genetic variants linked to various traits. This collaborative effort encompasses 37 datasets, aggregating data from a total of 31,684 individuals [16]. Cis-eQTL (cis-expressed Quantitative Trait Loci) data for the immune-related genes from InnateDB, defined as eQTLs within a 1 Mb region of the gene’s transcription start site, were sourced from the peripheral blood meta-analysis results provided by the eQTLGen Consortium. The cis-eQTL data for immune-related genes were filtered and utilized as exposure variables in Mendelian randomization analysis.

Cis-pQTL data for immune-related genes

The largest publicly available proteomics GWAS to date, employing the SomaScan assay to measure 4,907 proteins (SomaLogic) in 35,559 individuals of European descent, is utilized [17]. Cis-pQTL (cis-protein Quantitative Trait Loci, defined as pQTLs within a 1 Mb region of the transcription start site of protein-coding genes) data for the immune-related genes were obtained from deCODE (https://www.decode.com/summarydata/), and used as exposure variables in Mendelian randomization analysis.

Mendelian randomization analysis

To explore the causal links between immune eQTL genes and bladder cancer, as well as between immune pQTL genes and bladder cancer, we applied the TwoSampleMR R package(Version 0.5.7, https://mrcieu.github.io/TwoSampleMR/index.html) [18]. Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis was conducted to investigate the causal relationship between genetic variants and bladder cancer risk. We primarily employed the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method as the main analytical approach, given its high statistical power under the assumption of valid instruments. To account for potential horizontal pleiotropy, we additionally applied the MR-Egger regression, which provides both causal effect estimates and a test for directional pleiotropy. Heterogeneity among instrumental variables was assessed using Cochran’s Q test, allowing us to evaluate the consistency of causal estimates across SNPs. Together, these complementary methods improve the robustness of our findings by minimizing bias from invalid instruments and pleiotropy. Mendelian Randomization (MR) analysis was performed to investigate the causal relationship between genetic variants and bladder cancer. We employed multiple MR methods, including inverse variance weighted (IVW) and weighted median, to assess the robustness of our findings. IVW was used as the primary method, which provides efficient estimates under the assumption that all instrumental variables are valid. The weighted median method was used as a complementary approach to reduce bias in the presence of invalid instruments. Together, these methods provide more reliable causal estimates by testing the robustness of results under different assumptions.

Selection of QTL exposure instrumental variables (IVs)

For QTL data, the significance level is set at 5 × 10^-8. The F-statistic is calculated for each SNP locus. F = (beta/s.e.)2 [19]. PLINK software (version v1.90) is used to prune for linkage disequilibrium (with an r2 threshold of 0.001 and a window distance of less than 10,000 kb) [20]. Following the exclusion of potentially biased IVs with F-statistics below 10 and those with a minor allele frequency (MAF) under 1%, the remaining IVs were retained for further analysis.

Method selection for causal Estimation

To determine the causal relationships between QTL genes and bladder cancer, the choice of calculation method was dictated by the number of instrumental variables (IVs) per gene. For genes with a single IV (IV = 1), Wald’s ratio method was employed. When there were 2 to 3 IVs (2 < IV ≤ 3), a combination of techniques, including inverse variance weighted (fixed effects), MR-Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weight mode, was used. For scenarios involving more than three IVs (IV >3), inverse variance weighted (multiplicative random effects), MR-Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weight mode were applied [21].

Sensitivity and heterogeneity analysis

Heterogeneity was assessed using the MR Egger and inverse variance weighted method [22, 23]. Additionally, pleiotropy and outliers were analyzed through the MR Egger and MR-PRESSO (MR pleiotropy residual sum and outlier) methods [23, 24]. The intercept P value was examined to determine the presence of heterogeneity, with a non-significant intercept P value (P >0.05) indicating no horizontal pleiotropy.

Detection of reverse causality

The MR Steiger method was employed for the detection of the directionality of MR [12]. If we are uncertain whether a SNP g has a primary effect on trait x or trait y, the Steiger method can infer the direction of causality. Although two-way MR can be used to pinpoint the direction of causality, two-way MR requires knowledge of valid instruments for each trait. Therefore, we utilized the MR Steiger method, which can make inferences using only summary data.

Colocalization analysis

Based on the results obtained from Mendelian randomization, this study employed a coloc approach to explore whether immune-related genes share genetic mutations with bladder cancer. When GWAS signals and eQTLs or pQTLs are found to colocalise, it is inferred that the loci on the GWAS signals may influence phenotypes through biological processes that alter gene expression. The R package coloc (version 5.1.0) was utilized to investigate whether potential target genes and the genetic associations with bladder cancer harbor causal variants, thereby further strengthening the inference of causality [25]. Colocalization Analysis (Coloc Analysis) was performed to assess the shared causal variants between traits. We selected regions 500KB upstream and downstream of each QTL for coloc analysis, using default prior probabilities: P1 = 1 × 10⁻⁴, P2 = 1 × 10⁻⁴, and P12 = 1 × 10⁻⁵. These represent the likelihood that SNPs in the selected regions are associated with QTLs, bladder cancer, or both. The posterior probabilities from the coloc analysis align with one of five hypotheses: PPH0, the SNP is unrelated to either trait; PPH1, related to QTL but not bladder cancer; PPH2, related to bladder cancer but not QTL; PPH3, associated with both but driven by different SNPs; PPH4, associated with both and driven by a common SNP. A significance threshold of 0.8 was set for the posterior probabilities, and the locuscomparer package (Version 1.0.0) was used to visualize the coloc regions [26].

Protein-Protein interaction network analysis

GeneMANIA utilizes a comprehensive set of functional association data to identify genes related to the input gene set, uncovering connections that include protein-protein and protein-DNA interactions, pathways, physiological and biochemical processes, gene and protein expression patterns, structural domains, and phenotypic characteristics [27]. This extensive association data covers interactions among proteins and genes, pathways, co-expression, co-localization, and similarities in protein domains. Using QTL data, we identified a significant gene set and then used it to create a bladder cancer protein network.

Download of bladder cancer transcriptome and clinical data

mRNA expression and clinical survival data for bladder cancer (TCGA-BLCA) were retrieved from the TCGA database(https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) as of the cutoff date of July 1, 2024. The dataset consists of mRNA expression profiles and survival data from 412 bladder cancer samples and 19 corresponding normal tissue samples.

Differential gene screening

The limma package (version 3.48.3) is a tool for differential gene analysis, applicable for the analysis of microarray data and high-throughput sequencing transcriptome data [28]. It is based on the voom algorithm, which estimates the mean and variance of gene expression across different groups using a linear model, thereby facilitating differential analysis. Utilizing R version 4.1.0 (https://www.r-project.org/) and the limma package, we screened for differentially expressed genes between BC tissues and normal tissues, setting the criteria to |logFC| ≥ 1 and FDR ≤ 0.05.

Survival analysis

Using genes that showed significant results from the Mendelian randomizationand significant differential expression between bladder cancer and normal tissues, we employed the online tool Kaplan Meier Plotter (https://kmplot.com/analysis/) to assess the prognostic value of these key genes [29].

Immune infiltration analysis

CIBERSORT, developed by Stanford University, is an immune infiltration analysis tool [30]. CIBERSORT performs deconvolution analysis based on the principle of linear support vector regression, offering a comprehensive range of 22 different immune cell types. Using the BC mRNA data downloaded from the TCGA database, along with the significant results from the Mendelian randomization QTL FDR correction and the differential immune-related genes between bladder cancer and normal tissues, we conducted an immune infiltration analysis for BC.

Gene set enrichment analysis

We performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) using ClusterProfiler to assess the distribution of genes within predefined sets, ranked by their correlation with phenotype. This analysis, applied to significant differential immune-related genes between bladder cancer and normal tissues, aimed to uncover their biological relevance in bladder cancer development [31].

Result

eQTLs of immune-related genes

As described in the methods, we downloaded 1,378 immune-related genes from the InnateDB database(detailed in /data/InnateDB_genes.csv). After filtering the cis-eQTL data from the eQTLGen Consortium for these immune-related genes and applying the instrumental variable threshold filter, we obtained eQTL data for 984 immune-related genes.

Immune gene eQTL and bladder cancer Mendelian randomization

Using the eQTL data of immune-related genes, we performed a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis with bladder cancer as the outcome variable. Instrumental variables for 976 genes were found in the UK Biobank bladder cancer dataset, and for 968 genes in the FinnGen dataset. After FDR correction for the results obtained using different MR models, nine genes passed the significance threshold: HLA-B, NLRP1, ITPR3, CD300A, ARHGAP15, GSTM1, CARD11, CTSH, and CTSK (Fig. 2; Table 1).

Colocalization analysis

Using colocalization approaches, we investigated whether immune-related genes and bladder cancer share common genetic variants. The colocalization analysis identified additional candidate genes with shared causal variants, further strengthening the causal inference. We applied a PPH4 threshold of > 0.8 to define strong evidence of shared causal variants between immune-related genes and bladder cancer. Nevertheless, lower PPH4 values, such as the PLEC value of 0.61, indicate weaker but still possible evidence for colocalization (Fig. 4; Table 7). This step is crucial for enhancing the robustness of our findings and ensuring the reliability of the colocalization results.

Discussion

Integrative analysis and key findings

This study integrates eQTL and pQTL data with Mendelian Randomization (MR) methods, shedding light on the causal roles of immune-related genes in bladder cancer. We identified significant genes, including GSTM1, OLFM4, NTN1, ITPR3, CLU, and CARD11, linked to bladder cancer risk and prognosis. These findings offer fresh insights into the tumor immune microenvironment, potentially guiding future therapeutic strategies.

Immune gene expression and bladder cancer risk

Our eQTL analysis highlights the direct association between the expression of specific immune-related genes and bladder cancer risk. Notably, the upregulation of HLA-B, NLRP1, ITPR3, and CD300A correlates with increased cancer risk, emphasizing their role in immune surveillance and tumor progression. For instance, ITPR3’s involvement in the NF-ĸB/CD44 pathway and NLRP1’s role in inflammasome regulation underscore their significance in cancer biology [32–36]. These findings are complemented by our pQTL analysis, which links elevated levels of MCP-1, B7-H1, and KAP1 proteins to higher cancer risk, while proteins like CD27, NET1, and BECN1 appear to exert protective effects through enhanced anti-tumor immunity [37–40].

Colocalization and protein interaction insights

Colocalization analysis further elucidates the role of GSTM1 in bladder cancer, suggesting that shared genetic variants influence tumor development via immune gene regulation. This finding aligns with the known function of GSTM1 in carcinogen metabolism, which reduces cancer risk [41–43]. Additionally, our PPI network analysis reveals crucial interactions within the tumor microenvironment, highlighting the role of LILRB1 in immune evasion, as its elevated expression is linked to poor prognosis and increased immune infiltration [43, 44].

Gene expression, survival, and immune infiltration

Transcriptomic differential gene expression and survival analyses strengthen the evidence for the involvement of immune-related genes in bladder cancer prognosis. Genes such as OLFM4 and NTN1 are significantly associated with overall survival, indicating their potential as prognostic biomarkers [45–48]. OLFM4’s role in promoting malignancy through STAT3 signaling and NTN1’s impact on the tumor microenvironment underscore their relevance in disease progression and patient outcomes.CIBERSORT analysis provides additional insights into the tumor immune microenvironment, revealing significant changes in immune cell infiltration among bladder cancer patients. The observed variations in T cells, B cells, and macrophages have profound implications for tumor immune surveillance and response to therapy. Specifically, a higher infiltration of CD8 + T cells and M1 macrophages is linked to improved outcomes with immunotherapy, whereas an increased presence of M2 macrophages and regulatory T cells tends to correlate with a less favorable prognosis [49, 50].

Key signaling pathways and clinical implications

Gene set enrichment analysis identifies key signaling pathways, including the cell cycle, immune checkpoint, and TP53 pathways, that are enriched with bladder cancer-related genes. These pathways are critical for understanding tumor immune evasion and progression, and their modulation holds promise for therapeutic intervention [51–53].Clinically, the identification of GSTM1 and other protective genes offers potential as biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Enhancing these genes or mimicking their protective effects could lead to novel immunotherapy approaches. The strong link between B7-H1 (PD-L1) protein levels and increased cancer risk further underscores the potential of PD-L1 inhibitors as a therapeutic strategy. Combining these inhibitors with other targeted therapies could improve treatment outcomes in bladder cancer patients [54–57].

These genes provide critical insights for tailoring personalized treatment strategies. In NMIBC, certain gene expression patterns could help identify high-risk patients, enabling early intervention and potentially reducing disease progression [58]. For MIBC, these genes are closely tied to immune cell infiltration, which may affect the success of immunotherapy. As biomarkers, they could guide personalized therapy by predicting patient responses [59]. Enhancing or mimicking the function of these genes might pave the way for novel therapies, leading to better outcomes [60, 61]. Emerging evidence suggests that GSTM1, OLFM4, and ITPR3 may exert important regulatory effects on the tumor immune microenvironment. GSTM1 has been implicated in modulating oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling pathways, which may indirectly shape immune responses and influence the recruitment of immune cells. OLFM4, as a secreted glycoprotein, has been shown to participate in tumor immune evasion by modulating neutrophil function and altering the balance between pro- and anti-inflammatory signaling. In addition, ITPR3 plays a critical role in intracellular calcium signaling, which is essential for T cell activation and cytokine production, thereby potentially affecting immune cell infiltration and tumor–immune interactions. Together, these mechanisms suggest that the dysregulation of these genes may contribute to an immunosuppressive microenvironment that facilitates tumor progression. Importantly, our findings provide a rationale for considering these genes as potential biomarkers or therapeutic targets, which may help refine personalized immunotherapy strategies and advance precision medicine approaches in bladder cancer.

Limitations

Our study leverages Mendelian Randomization to examine the causal connections between immune-related genes and bladder cancer risk, acknowledging the intrinsic limitations of this approach. Specifically, MR assumes a linear relationship between genetic instruments and exposures, which may not capture complex gene-environment interactions. Another limitation of our study is the lack of qPCR/RT-PCR validation for the key genes (GSTM1, OLFM4, ITPR3). Due to restrictions on laboratory resources and the availability of clinical samples, these experiments could not be performed in the current stage. Future studies with additional tissue collections and molecular validation will be required to further strengthen the biological evidence of our findings. Additionally, focusing on cis-eQTLs may overlook contributions from trans-eQTLs and other regulatory elements. These limitations may impact the comprehensiveness of our genetic landscape analysis, underscoring the importance of further studies to validate our results in different populations and to explore broader biological mechanisms.

Future directions

Further research is needed to validate these findings across diverse populations to enhance their broader applicability. Exploring the tissue-specific roles of these genes is crucial for understanding their impact on bladder cancer in various biological contexts. Experimental studies on NTN1’s role in immune cell migration and its effects on the tumor microenvironment could provide valuable insights.

Furthermore, integrating omics data, including proteomics and metabolomics, could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the immune landscape in bladder cancer. Combining identified biomarkers with existing immunotherapies, such as PD-L1 inhibitors, could enhance treatment efficacy. Exploring the roles of these genes in treatment resistance could guide the creation of more effective therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

This study used Mendelian Randomization (MR) to identify GSTM1, OLFM4, and NTN1 as key genes driving bladder cancer progression, particularly via immune regulation. These findings highlight the promise of these genes as biomarkers and therapeutic targets, emphasizing their potential utility in personalized bladder cancer treatment. These limitations may impact the comprehensiveness of our genetic landscape analysis, underscoring the importance of further studies to validate our results in different populations and to explore broader biological mechanisms.
