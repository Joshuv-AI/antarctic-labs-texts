Docking of millions: accelerating a million-scale virtual screening using deep learning
Authors: Junsu Ha, Juyong Lee, Junsu Ko, Woong-Hee Shin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVirtual screening is a critical step in computer-aided drug discovery. As synthetic databases and reaction schemes expand, the number of synthesizable compounds grows, making efficient screening essential. The success of ultra-large-scale virtual screening hinges on two factors: accuracy in binding affinity prediction and speed. We previously developed AK-Score2 for accurate protein-ligand binding prediction and V-Dock for estimating docking scores. Here, we present Docking of Millions (DoM), an ultra-large-scale virtual screening system integrating both methods. Iterative learning of V-Dock to approximate AK-Score2-based affinities accelerates screening by eliminating the need to dock all compounds, saving time and resources. In benchmarking, screening 5 million compounds against DDR1, c-kit, ASK1, NSD1, CREBBP, and PDE5, DoM consumed only 319 h on average, 12% of full library screening time. Also, it achieved an average retrieval rate of 89% in the top 100 compounds. Inhibition assays identified that 1 out of 27 and 4 out of 31 molecules suppressed ASK1 and DDR1 activity by more than 50% at 10 μM. Further validation of selected compounds identified that the lowest IC50 values are 1.96 μM and 788 nM for ASK1 and DDR1, respectively. These findings demonstrate DoM as a practical and efficient platform for real-world drug discovery.

Introduction

Virtual screening (VS) has become an essential computational technique in the early stages of drug discovery, allowing for the efficient identification of potential lead compounds. The method utilizes a virtual compound library to identify hit candidates by employing molecular similarity with the known binders and/or exploring binding pose and affinity to the target protein. Advances in chemical synthesis and the capabilities of computer storage have led to a rapid expansion in the size of chemical libraries [1–4]. It is also reported that the probability of identifying hit molecules is enhanced with an increase in the size of the library [5–7]. Consequently, there is an increasing inclination toward the utilization of ultra-large-scale library VS. Nevertheless, the computational expense associated with processing such an extensive chemical library persists as a substantial impediment, impeding the efficacy of VS pipelines.

Recent advances in ultra-large VS include deep learning-based methods that explore vast chemical spaces through iterative sampling (Deep Docking [8], MolPAL [9]) and GPU-accelerated docking that speeds up individual calculations (Uni-Dock [10], Vina-GPU [11]). While iterative methods dramatically reduce computational cost by selectively sampling compounds, they may sacrifice chemical diversity. GPU acceleration evaluates entire libraries but remains computationally intensive for million-scale screening.

Successful ultra-large-scale VS depends on two key factors: screening speed and ranking accuracy. While large libraries increase the likelihood of identifying hit molecules, screening becomes impractical if computational time extends to several months. Recent advancements in GPU-accelerated docking algorithms [10–12] have provided new opportunities to address the challenge by significantly reducing computational time, but they still need to be improved. Recently, we developed V-Dock [13], a method that predicts docking scores without performing actual docking simulations. The approach begins by cluster selecting a subset of molecules (seed compounds) from the library, which are then docked to the target protein and characterized using molecular descriptors, including circular fingerprints. A multi-layer perceptron (MLP) model is trained on these descriptors and their corresponding docking scores, enabling rapid score prediction for query compounds based solely on their molecular features. The algorithm demonstrated a high degree of efficacy in predicting AutoDock Vina scores, as evidenced by a Pearson's correlation coefficient of 0.89. The calculation of the binding affinity for a single compound is significantly expedited, with a reduction in processing time from 15.2 s to 0.0595 s. This development paves the way for the application of ultra-large VS.

The second critical factor is ranking accuracy, which depends on precise binding affinity prediction. Wang et al. conducted a comprehensive benchmark of conventional scoring functions [14], such as AutoDock [15] and UCSF DOCK [16]. The analysis revealed that the maximum Pearson’s correlation coefficient between experimentally determined binding affinity and predicted binding affinity is 0.631. To address these limitations, various machine learning and deep learning models have been proposed for binding affinity prediction. Structure-based methods, such as OnionNet-2 [17], AK-Score [18], and PIGNet2 [19], exploit 3D protein–ligand complex information, whereas graph-based approaches, including MMPD-DTA [20], DeepTGIN [21], and AK-Score2 [22], model proteins and ligands in a unified graph representation. Across these methods, several models report correlation coefficients approaching or exceeding 0.8 on standard benchmark datasets, illustrating recent progress in data-driven affinity prediction. We used AK-Score2 [22], an ensemble scoring function that integrates the predicted physical interaction scores from two graph neural network (GNN) models with a conventional AutoDock score, providing a more robust estimate of protein–ligand binding affinity. The method utilizes a GNN for prediction. The success rate for identifying a hit compound from the library within the top 1% was enhanced to 71.9% from 22.8% for AutoDock-GPU, after rescoring the poses generated from the docking program.

In this study, we present Docking of Millions (DoM), an ultra-large-scale VS platform that synergistically integrates V-Dock and AK-Score2 through an iterative learning framework. Unlike training on a limited subset alone—which often yields inadequate predictive accuracy due to insufficient data diversity—DoM employs an active learning strategy that progressively refines the model by incorporating both top-ranked and randomly sampled compounds at each iteration. Training on a limited subset often yields inadequate predictive accuracy due to insufficient data diversity. To address this limitation, we adopted an iterative learning approach that progressively incorporates new docking data into model training. At each iteration, two sets of compounds are selected: the top-ranked compounds that are predicted to have strong binding affinities from the current V-Dock model, and a randomly sampled set from the unexplored chemical space. Subsequent to this, molecular descriptor calculation and docking simulation are conducted for the selected molecules and then incorporated into the training set. This strategy is conceptually analogous to reinforcement learning-guided molecular docking, wherein models iteratively enhance their performance by integrating feedback from experimental or computational validation. AutoDock-GPU is employed for docking pose generation, and the binding affinity of the program’s predicted poses is further predicted by AK-Score2. The best AK-Score2 was selected for the model training. Thus, V-Dock model implemented in DoM learns to predict AK-Score2.

Given that the DoM predicts docking scores and replaces the docking simulation of the full library, it is imperative to retrieve the potent molecules and high-ranked compounds by actual docking. Six targets across the various protein families, discoidin domain receptor tyrosine kinase 1 (DDR1), KIT proto-oncogene receptor tyrosine kinase (c-kit), apoptosis signaling-regulating kinase 1 (ASK1), nuclear receptor binding SET binding protein 1 (NSD1), CREB binding protein (CREBBP), and phosphodiesterase 5 (PDE5), were selected for the benchmark study. The Mcule in-stock library, which contains 5 million commercially available molecules, was screened against the target proteins using DoM. The top-ranked compounds were then compared with the actual full library docking. The efficacy of the iterative refinement process in enhancing predictive accuracy and expediting the identification of protein-targeting ligands was demonstrated, underscoring its potential value in practical drug discovery applications. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that active learning strategies can significantly enhance VS performance [23, 24]. Experimental validation identified ASK1 and DDR1 inhibitors with half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of 1.96 μM and 788 nM, respectively, demonstrating the practical utility of DoM in hit discovery.

Results

Overview of docking of millions workflow

The DoM protocol for large-scale compound library screening, which integrates V-Dock [13], molecular docking, and AK-Score2 [22], is illustrated in Fig. 1. The protocol was initiated by the docking of seed compounds to the given target protein using AutoDock-GPU [12], a widely used tool for docking calculations, i.e. optimized for high-throughput VS. The compound library was partitioned based on molecular weight (MW) and lipophilicity (logP) criteria, following the hierarchical binning strategy employed in the ZINC15 database [25]. Compounds were prefiltered into MW bins with upper limits of 200, 250, 300, 325, 350, 375, 400, 425, 450, and 500 Da, and into logP bins with upper limits of −1, 0, 1, 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, 4, 4.5, and 5. Each combination of MW and logP bins was further subdivided into 100 sub-bins to ensure fine-grained sampling across physicochemical property space. Within each bin, the compound library was clustered using a Tanimoto coefficient threshold of 0.2 with Extended Connectivity Fingerprint (ECFP) with a radius of three to achieve maximal structural diversity. For example, Mcule in-Stock library, which was used throughout this study, is composed of approximately 5 million molecules as of March 2022 and was clustered into 142 000 clusters. The representative molecules, the center of each cluster, were selected to construct seed molecules. The top predicted pose of each seed compound was rescored using AK-Score2, a method that has been shown to improve affinity predictions by incorporating a GNN beyond conventional docking scores.

An overview of the DoM. The process of DoM starts with the selection of seed molecules from the representatives of clusters. The molecules are then docked to the target protein and rescored using AK-Score2. The MLP model is constructed through the learning process of the compound fingerprint and docking score. The docking scores of all compounds are predicted based on the trained model. The top 25 000 molecules from the prediction result and the randomly selected 25 000 molecules are docked and added to improve the model. The process is iterated 10 times to build the final model of DoM. The molecules that are selected and their docking scores are aggregated until the final iteration. The most efficacious compounds were identified through the combined results from 10 iterations.

Then, the SMILES strings of the seed compounds and their predicted binding affinity were employed for V-Dock featurization and subsequent model training. The molecular featurization process involved the calculation of multiple fingerprints and descriptors, including the MACCS key, Morgan circular fingerprint with a radius of 3, and a total of 196 physico-chemical features. This comprehensive set of metrics was then processed using RDKit [26], resulting in the generation of a 1386-dimensional vector. The V-Dock model was trained to predict the AK-Score2 value of a molecule, with the descriptor serving as the input. Next, the trained V-Dock model was implemented on the entire compound library to derive a ranking of molecules according to their predicted affinities.

Since the limited data of seed compounds might not be suitable for covering the full chemical space of the screening library, the initial V-Dock model could be inadequate for screening molecules. To solve this problem, an iterative approach was employed, whereby molecules were systematically incorporated into the training set with the objective of enhancing the efficacy of V-Dock. The top-ranked 25 000 compounds were selected based on a ranking of molecules derived from V-Dock predicted binding affinities. In the event that a compound had been included in previous iterations, the next highest-ranked compound was selected to ensure the maintenance of a consistent selection size. Concurrently, a random selection of 25 000 compounds was made from the remaining compounds. Among the selected molecules, those that had not previously undergone docking were docked to the target protein. The objective of this process was to eliminate redundancies from previous iterations, thereby preventing the duplication of data. The 50 000 molecules that were newly selected were docked to the target protein, rescored by AK-Score2, featurized, and then added to the training data. The new V-Dock model was constructed using the extended training dataset.

By iterating the process, from V-Dock prediction to construct a new V-Dock model by integrating the new molecule data, the model continuously refined its predictive capability while effectively navigating the vast chemical space. In this study, the number of iterations was set at 10. From each iteration, the top 25 000 molecules and their docking scores were accumulated until the final iteration. The selection of the top 10 000 molecules was based on the cumulated molecules. The workflow exhibited a progressive enhancement in predictive performance, as demonstrated by increased accuracy in identifying experimentally validated ligands. Because V-Dock is trained to approximate AK-Score2, retrieval metrics quantify the fidelity with which DoM reproduces AK-Score2-rescored docking rankings, while biological relevance is assessed independently through experimental validation.

To evaluate the robustness of our docking protocol, we systematically assessed the reproducibility of binding pose predictions using AutoDock-GPU combined with AK-Score2 for pose rescoring. Reproducibility was evaluated by performing multiple independent docking runs and calculating the consistency of predicted binding poses. The initial average reproducibility across all target proteins was 0.972 (Fig. 2A). However, reproducibility gradually decreased with successive iterations, reaching 0.953 by the 10th iteration, indicating that pose consistency slightly decreased as the number of docking runs increased. Among the examined proteins, CREBBP demonstrated the lowest reproducibility value of 0.877, whereas PDE5 exhibited reproducibility of 0.928. The low reproducibility of CREBBP stems from its function as a bromodomain engaged in protein–protein interactions. The binding site, where the co-crystallized ligand binds, is located at the bromodomain-histone interface, resulting in a characteristically small and surface-oriented binding pocket designed to accommodate protein substrates rather than small-molecule inhibitors. This protein-accommodating binding environment presents greater challenges for binding affinity prediction than conventional enzyme active sites, as evidenced by CREBBP exhibiting the lowest correlation coefficient across all repeated calculations. [27, 28]. Future improvements could leverage deterministic or ensemble docking strategies specifically tailored for shallow, surface-oriented binding interfaces. The binding pocket of PDE5 is notably expansive, encompassing multiple subsites with significant H-loop conformational changes (up to 35 Å displacement) [29], and exceeds the grid box dimensions employed in our docking protocol. This mismatch between binding pocket size and grid definition may result in incomplete sampling of the binding site during iterative docking runs, potentially affecting prediction accuracy for ligands engaging distant binding regions. As a future solution, increasing the size of the grid box should suffice.

Given that DoM employs the V-Dock model, which utilizes an MLP to predict docking scores, as opposed to conducting an actual docking simulation, reproducing the binding affinity and the compound ranking is therefore essential. In order to assess the reproducibility of the docking scores, the correlation between predictions from V-Dock and actual docking results was measured at each iteration (Fig. 2B). As the iterations progressed, the correlation exhibited an enhancement at almost every step, thereby representing the improvement of the predictive model. It is noteworthy that all V-Dock models at the 10th iteration exhibited Pearson’s correlation coefficient values greater than 0.8, thereby substantiating the efficacy of the iterative training approach. The lower correlation observed in CREBBP can be attributed to the inherent instability of the docking poses, as indicated by the reproducibility analysis.

To further evaluate the effectiveness of our workflow, a comparison was made between the top 100 ranked compounds identified through the actual VS and those prioritized by our iterative workflow. At each iteration, the number of top 100 compounds retrieved in the top 100 DoM predictions was tracked (Fig. 2C). By the 10th iteration, all six protein targets retained at least 80 of the top 100 compounds, with screening against DDR1 surpassing this threshold as early as the third iteration. On average, 89% of the top 100 compounds were successfully identified by the workflow after 10 iterations.

Beyond the top 100 compounds, the analysis was expanded to include larger sets of compounds: the top 1000, 5000, and 10 000 compounds. The detailed results are displayed in Supplementary Data Fig. S1. As with the top 100 result, the retrieval rate increases as DoM iterates. These findings underscore the efficacy and robustness of the deep-learning-driven screening approach employed, demonstrating its potential to expedite hit identification while preserving high accuracy.

The findings collectively demonstrate the efficacy of DoM, a novel deep-learning-augmented VS pipeline, in accelerating drug discovery while maintaining high predictive reliability. The iterative selection approach facilitates the exploration of vast chemical spaces, rendering it a viable alternative to conventional exhaustive docking simulations.

To evaluate the consistency of compound selection across the iterative docking process, we compared the final top-ranked compounds from the complete DoM workflow (10 iterations) with known active compounds (ChEMBL [30] IC50 < 10 μM) present in the Mcule in-stock library. Among the six target proteins, the number of ChEMBL-validated compounds recovered in the top 10 000 varied by approach (Supplementary Table S1). While AutoDock-GPU-based and AK-Score2-based DoM implementations showed some differences in specific targets (e.g. C-kit and ASK1), both approaches successfully identified known actives, demonstrating the robustness of the overall workflow.

Discovery of small-molecule hits for protein targets

To illustrate the efficacy of our workflow, we employed DoM to identify potential inhibitors for DDR1 and ASK1, two kinases of the benchmark. For screening, we employed a two-step method for compound selection. In accordance with the DoM protocol, 10 iterations were completed, and promising compounds were selected from the top 200. From the top 200 molecules, two compounds for ASK1 and one for DDR1 compounds were excluded, because they have already been reported in ChEMBL to have IC50 values below 10 μM. The remaining molecules are clustered based on Tanimoto ECFP6 2048 fingerprint similarity (threshold: 0.7) in order to assess the diverse molecule scaffolds. The highest-ranked compounds from each cluster were selected for experimental validation. In the event that a potent compound was identified during the preliminary screening phase, analogs present within the same cluster were subsequently subjected to further testing. In the initial phase, the objective was to ascertain the chemical space in a diverse manner. Subsequently, a close region was explored from the hits that were initially identified. In this paper, the initial screening of the cluster representative molecule will be referred to as the first round screening, and the screening of the analog molecules will be named the second round screening. All inhibition assay experiments were done by Eurofins KinaseProfiler LeadHunter Assays at 10 μM concentration.

For ASK1, an initial set of 27 compounds was subjected to the first-round screening (Supplementary Data Table S2), which led to the identification of a single compound exhibiting inhibition levels below 50%. To further explore structurally related candidates, two additional analogs of the active compound were selected for the second round assay (Supplementary Data Table S3). Both of these analogs also exhibited inhibition below 50%. Due to the limited availability of compounds, IC50 measurements were conducted for only one of the initially identified active compounds and one of its analogs, yielding IC50 values of 9.2 μM and 1.9 μM, respectively (Table 2).

Ablation study: effect of sampling strategy

To investigate the optimal balance between exploration and exploitation, we retrospectively analyzed the retrieval performance of DoM on the DDR1 target using various sampling ratios. We measured the recovery rate of the top 100, 1000, and 10 000 compounds (defined by full library docking scores) across 10 iterations (Fig. 5). Six specific ratios of top-ranked to random selection were evaluated: 1:0 (pure exploitation), 5:1, 2:1, 1:1 (balanced), 1:2, and 1:5 (exploration-heavy).

Impact of sampling strategy ratios on retrieval rates for DDR1. Recovery rates of the top (A) 100, (B) 1000, and (C) 10 000 compounds from full library docking were measured across 10 iterations. Different lines represent varying ratios of top-ranked (exploitation) to randomly selected (exploration) compounds, ranging from exploitation-heavy (1:0, 5:1) to exploration-heavy (1:5) strategies.

As illustrated in Fig. 5, the recovery rate is positively correlated with the proportion of top-ranked selection. In the top 100 retrieval task (Fig. 5A), the pure exploitation strategy (1:0) achieved a recovery rate of ~91% by the third iteration, significantly outperforming the balanced 1:1 strategy (~85%) used in our main experiments. This trend remains consistent for larger retrieval sets (Top 1000 and Top 10 000), although the overall convergence speed decreases as the retrieval target size increases (Fig. 5B and C).

These results suggest that a greedy selection strategy (1:0) may be more efficient than the balanced approach for rapid convergence, particularly when the initial seed library already provides sufficient chemical diversity. The high performance of pure exploitation implies that the initial random clustering and selection of seed compounds effectively covered the relevant chemical space, allowing subsequent iterations to focus on refining the decision boundary around active regions rather than exploring distant, irrelevant chemical space.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a high-throughput VS tool, DoM, by combining a highly accurate binding affinity prediction, AK-Score2, and an iterative learning of the affinity score by V-Dock. DoM exhibited its capacity to expeditiously identify lead compounds from ultra-large-scale chemical libraries. The provision of a new compound dataset, selected from the high-ranked molecules and random selection, resulted in enhanced performance of the docking score prediction in terms of both the correlation and retrieval rate of the top-ranked compounds. The proposed approach yielded a reduction in computational time of ~90%, while maintaining a high degree of accuracy in the ranking of top candidates. The experimental validation of DDR1 and ASK1 indicates the potential efficacy of this methodology in the initial stages of drug discovery for hit identification.

While our experimental validation was conducted using compounds selected via a balanced sampling approach (1:1), post hoc analysis revealed that pure exploitation (100% top-ranked selection) could further improve retrieval efficiency. This aligns with recent findings in Bayesian optimization-based VS [9], where greedy acquisition performed best for smooth chemical spaces. Future prospective studies should validate whether hits identified through pure exploitation maintain similar experimental success rates.

In the future, we intend to expand this approach to encompass even larger-scale compound libraries, which are expected to contain tens to hundreds of millions of molecules. This expansion will facilitate a more comprehensive evaluation of the scalability and robustness of our workflow in real-world applications. Furthermore, the incorporation of deep learning models for the purpose of adaptive compound selection, in conjunction with the refinement of binding affinity predictions through the implementation of experimental feedback loops, is anticipated to be a pivotal focus of future research endeavors. Also considering conformational flexibility by ensemble screening could also be valuable for improving VS performance [32]. These advancements will further enhance the efficiency and accuracy of VS, thereby accelerate the drug discovery pipeline and facilitating the identification of novel therapeutic candidates.

Methods

Docking library preparation

The docking library was constructed using the Mcule in-Stock database, which was accessed in March 2022. Compounds that violate Lipinski’s Rule of Five were excluded from the library prior to screening by RDKit. Specifically, compounds with an MW exceeding 500 Da, a logP greater than five, more than 10 hydrogen bond acceptors, or more than five hydrogen bond donors were removed. Subsequent to the application of these criteria, a total of 4 984 530 compounds were retained for VS. These compounds were then prepared for docking using Meeko 0.3.3 [33], a preprocessing tool for AutoDock.

The initial V-Dock model was constructed by selecting a seed compound from the screening database. The preclustering of these compounds was executed based on their molecular similarity, employing the ECFP (radius = 3) method with a Tanimoto distance threshold set at 0.2. By utilizing RDKit’s bulk Tanimoto similarity calculations and Butina clustering method, the molecules were clustered into 142 000 clusters. This approach effectively reduced structural redundancy while preserving chemical diversity. This clustering step ensured a more efficient compound selection process for subsequent iterations. The representative compound (the cluster center) constructed the seed compound set.

V-dock model, molecular descriptors, and selection of molecules

The V-Dock method predicts a docking score for a ligand to the target protein, with the objective of accelerating VS and property optimization through the integration of machine learning. The primary innovation of V-Dock is the substitution of conventional docking calculations—which generally necessitate conversion to 3D structures and considerable computational time—with a predictive model that estimates docking scores directly from SMILES or 1D and 2D molecular descriptors. This facilitates large-scale exploration of chemical space for candidate compounds, even in cases where experimental binding data is limited. In V-Dock, an MLP model is trained using fingerprints and physical property descriptors to predict docking energies.

An MLP model was implemented using PyTorch [34] to perform the prediction task. The network architecture consists of two hidden layers with 1024 and 528 neurons, respectively, each followed by an exponential linear unit activation function. Batch normalization was applied to the input layer to stabilize training, and a dropout rate of 0.3 was used to prevent overfitting. The final output layer is a fully connected linear layer that produces the predicted values.

At each iteration, the accumulated data was randomly split into training (80%) and validation (20%) sets. The model was trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001 for a maximum of 500 epochs. To ensure optimal predictive performance, we monitored the validation error throughout the process and selected the model checkpoint that yielded the lowest validation mean squared error, rather than the final epoch’s model.

In DoM, modifications were made to the input features from the original V-Dock workflow. Specifically, the MACCS Keys (166-bit) and Morgan Fingerprints (radius = 3, 1024-bit) were utilized, in conjunction with 196 physicochemical descriptors. The detailed list of descriptors is presented in Supplementary Data Table S6. This process culminated in the generation of a feature vector comprising 1386 elements for each compound. The V-Dock model was iteratively improved by expanding the training set. At each iteration, the docking scores for the entire screening library were predicted using the current model. The two sets of compounds, each comprising 25 000 molecules, were incorporated: one set derived from the top-predicted molecules, and the other set from a random selection. The descriptor calculation and docking simulation were conducted and merged into the training data set for the new V-Dock model. The docking data of the top 25 000 sets were aggregated, and the final compound selection was made from this list.

Docking and virtual screening

Docking calculation for the selected compound during DoM workflow and actual full library screening were carried out using AutoDock-GPU 1.5.3, an open-source, GPU-accelerated docking tool. For the benchmark study, the crystal structures co-crystallized with compounds undergoing clinical trials or already commercialized were selected from PDB [35], with the exception of CREBBP and NSD1. For NSD1, the structure of PDB ID 6KQQ was selected because it has the best resolution (1.8 Å) bound with an inhibitor. For CREBBP, the PDB structure co-crystallized with a selective inhibitor, XDM-CBP [36], was selected for the benchmark (PDB ID: 5NU3). Initially, receptor structures were converted to PDBQT format using AutoDockTools, while ligand structures were processed into PDBQT format using Meeko 0.3.3, which utilizes RDKit for molecular transformations. The binding pocket for each target protein was defined based on the cognate ligand in the crystal structure, with a docking grid centered at the geometric center of the ligand. The docking search space was defined as a cubic box with side lengths no <22.5 Å, following the AutoDock-GPU default search-space expansion procedure, which ensures sufficient room for ligand rotation and translation within the binding site [15, 37].

In order to predict ligand-protein interactions, the nrun parameter was set to 50. The nrun parameter is the one that determines the number of genetic algorithm runs and thus the number of docking poses generated. The docking program utilizes stochastic optimization, which has been demonstrated to increase the probability of identifying correct poses as the number of poses increases, with a root-mean-square-distance threshold of 2 Å [38, 39]. The parameter, 50, was arbitrarily selected to balance between accuracy and computational cost. Subsequent to docking, the AK-Score2 was utilized to re-evaluate all 50 generated poses, and the conformation exhibiting the lowest AK-Score2 value was selected for further analysis.

Experimental validation for selected hits

To validate computational predictions, experimental kinase inhibition assays were performed using Eurofins KinaseProfiler LeadHunter Assays.
