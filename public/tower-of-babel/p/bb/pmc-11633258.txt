Learning points about regression analysis in a study of predictors of insight in obsessive compulsive disorder
Authors: Mirza Jahanzeb Beg, Gaurav Maggu, Bheemsain Tekkalaki, Chittaranjan Andrade
Published: 2024

To the Editor,

Sinha et al.[1] examined factors associated with insight in 40 patients with obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD). They identified metacognition, OCD severity, and associated disability as significant predictors of insight. Their study was discussed in detail in eJournal Club, India,[2] where several concerns were raised regarding their use of regression analysis. We present these concerns with a view to illustrate avoidable errors.

First, independent variables (IVs) selected for exploratory regression should be based on reasonable expectations and not because data are available for these. As examples of IVs that might have been unjustifiably included, why should quality of life (QoL) and disability have been studied for predictive value with insight as the outcome when directionality was probably opposite, with poor insight predicting more severe OCD, poorer QoL, and greater disability?

Second, high interdependence or intercorrelation of IVs violates assumptions of multivariable regression. In this context, if the authors chose to include Yale–Brown Obsessive–Compulsive Scale (YBOCS) obsessions and compulsions subscale scores, they should not have included the YBOCS total scale score.

Third, the authors used stepwise regressions. Stepwise regressions are discouraged because, whereas they may partially address multicollinearity, this advantage may be illusory,[3] and the model may exhibit overfitting and false positive findings.[4]

Fourth, to avoid overfitting, there should be at least 10-20 times as many subjects as the number of independent variables studied. So, with a sample of 40 subjects in their study, not more than 4 IVs should have been selected. The authors chose 11 IVs.

On a parting note, the authors presented a table with R, R2, and adjusted R2 values; only the last-mentioned is relevant because it represents the variance in outcome explained by the IVs after adjusting for the number of IVs in the model.[5]
