Antidiabetic potential of Sapindus trifoliatus fruit ethanolic extract: Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry profiling, molecular docking, and in vitro/in vivo evaluation
Authors: Sarita Rawat, Prashant Kumar Dhakad, Ritu Gilhotra, Kavita Goyal, A. Rekha, Mohit Rana, Rakhi Mishra, Lakshmi Thangavelu, Gaurav Gupta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Diabetes mellitus causes vascular, renal, and ocular complications. In this study, we evaluated an ethanolic extract of Sapindus trifoliatus fruit using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS) profiling, molecular docking, and in vitro and in vivo assays. GC-MS analysis revealed key bioactive phytochemicals, and docking analysis demonstrated strong AKR1C3 binding. Acute toxicity testing confirmed safety at doses up to 5000 mg/kg. In alloxan-induced diabetic mice, extract treatment dose dependently lowered fasting blood glucose and HbA1c levels, restored pancreatic, hepatic, and cardiac histology, and mitigated ocular, renal, and cardiac damage. In in vitro, the extract inhibited α-amylase and α-glucosidase, supporting its antihyperglycemic activity. Compared to previous studies, S. trifoliatus extract showed enhanced efficacy, likely due to its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, underscoring its potential as a plant-based antidiabetic therapy.

INTRODUCTION

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a metabolic disorder characterized by hyperglycemia due to impaired insulin secretion and/or action. Disruption of carbohydrate, lipid, and protein metabolism leads to nephropathy, retinopathy, neuropathy, and cardiovascular diseases. With the rising global prevalence, DM is expected to become a leading cause of death by 2030. Type 2 DM, which accounts for 90% of cases, arises from insulin resistance and is managed with oral hypoglycemic agents or insulin. However, current therapies often fail to prevent complications and can produce adverse effects, prompting the exploration of safer alternatives.[1] Medicinal plants offer diverse benefits, including enhancing insulin release and sensitivity, inhibiting carbohydrate-hydrolyzing enzymes, and providing antioxidant activity. Sapindus trifoliatus (soapnut) contains saponins, flavonoids, and other phytochemicals with anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antidiabetic properties.[2] This study evaluated an ethanolic soapnut extract via gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS) profiling, in silico docking, in vitro enzyme inhibition assays, and in vivo alloxan-induced diabetic mouse models to assess its efficacy, safety, and impact on diabetic complications.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant material

The S. trifoliatus fruits (STFs) were collected from the Uttarakhand Himalayas (June–August) and authenticated by Dr. Geetanjali Mishra, Department of Botany, Government Degree College, Baluwakote.

Extraction of fruits of Sapindus trifoliatus

The fruits were cleaned and shade-dried, and 50 g portions were defatted with petroleum ether. The residue was extracted with ethanol at 55°C–60°C in a Soxhlet extractor and concentrated by rotary evaporation, and the resulting extract was stored in an airtight container.

Phytochemical investigation

Phytochemical screening of the extract was performed to qualitatively determine the phytochemicals that were present.

Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry analysis

Shimadzu QP2010 (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) based on a 30 m wax column GC-MS was used under optimized conditions to conduct qualitative analysis. The compounds were identified by comparing their mass spectra and retention times with standard databases and literature values.[3]

In silico study

Molecular docking simulates ligand–protein binding by preparing the structures, defining the sites, and computing the affinities. Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion, and Toxicity (ADMET) analysis predicts pharmacokinetics and toxicity, guiding early drug discovery using SwissADME/ProTox-II.

Experimental animal

Swiss albino mice (20–25 g) were maintained at a well-regulated temperature, humidity, and 12 h light/dark cycle in Suresh Gyan Vihar University. They received standard feed, water ad libitum, and 1 week of acclimatization. The experiments followed the IAEC and CPCSEA guidelines (approval: SGVU/PH/IAEC/2021/01).

Acute oral toxicity study

Acute oral toxicity in Swiss albino mice was assessed using OECD 423 at 5000 mg/kg, with behavioral and physiological changes monitored over 3 days.[4]

In vivo activity

Effect of extract on blood glucose level in normoglycemic mice

Fasted mice were separated into four groups that were administered distilled water, S. trifoliatus extract (500 mg/kg), or glibenclamide (2 mg/kg), and blood glucose levels (BGLs) were measured at 0, 30, 60, and 120 min.[5]

Effect of extract on blood glucose level in oral glucose-loaded mice

Fourteen-hour-fasted mice in five groups received water, 500 mg/kg STF, glibenclamide, normal glucose (control), or oral glucose, with glycemia measured at 0, 30, 60, and 120 min.

Effect of extract against alloxan-induced diabetes

Diabetes was induced in male Swiss albino mice by intraperitoneal injection of alloxan (120 mg/kg) after overnight fasting. Mice with fasting glucose >200 mg/dL were grouped and treated with saline, glibenclamide (2 mg/kg), or STF extract (250/500 mg/kg) for 20 days.[6]

Measurement of blood glucose level

Fasting BGLs were measured in different times at 0, 5, 10, 15, and 20 days using the glucose oxidase-peroxidase method.

Glycosylated hemoglobin estimation (%HbA1c)

Glycosylated hemoglobin was measured using the Drabinski method with Drabeck’s reagent, with absorbance read at 540 nm and concentration calculated using the standard equation.

Effect on diabetic mice’s eye

Mouse lenses were homogenized in phosphate buffer, protein was quantified colorimetrically, Na/K was quantified by flame photometry, and water content was calculated from wet/dry weights.

Effect of extract on diabetic mice kidney

Erba kits (Erba Lachema s.r.o., Czech Republic, Transasia Bio-Medicals Ltd. (Erba India), Mumbai, India) were used to determine blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, and urine protein levels, while relative kidney weight (%) was calculated as (kidney weight/body weight) × 100.

Effect of ethanolic extract in diabetic mice heart

Hemodynamic parameters (heart rate and diastolic/systolic blood pressure) were assessed using tail cuff plethysmography. Standard biochemical and spectrophotometric analyses were used to determine the heart-to-body weight ratio, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity.[7]

Effect of various treatments on histopathology of pancreas, liver, and heart of alloxan-induced diabetic mice

The pancreas, liver, and heart were snap-frozen in 10% formalin, paraffin-embedded, sectioned at 5 μm, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin for histological observation.

In vitro inhibition of alpha-amylase and alpha-glucosidase

Alpha-amylase and α-glucosidase inhibition assays involved incubating enzymes with substrates and either test extract or acarbose, measuring spectrophotometric absorbance, and calculating percentage inhibition against controls.[8]

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, Inc., Boston, MA, USA); the results (mean ± standard error of mean) were compared using one-way ANOVA and Newman–Keuls tests, with P < 0.05 significance.

RESULTS

Phytochemical analysis

Phytochemical analysis of the ethanolic extract of STF revealed the presence of glycosides, flavonoids, phenols, saponins, steroids, alkaloids, and tannins.

Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry analysis

GC-MS analysis of the ethanolic extract of STF revealed the presence of several constituents [Figure 1]. Major constituents present were 5-hexenoic acid (7.51%), 1,4-diacetyl-3-acetoxymethyl-2,5-monomethylene-l-rhamnitol (16.89%), Urs-12-en-28-al (7.39%), and 4,4,6a, 6b,8a,11,11,14b-Octamethyl-1,4,4a,5,6,6a,6b,7,8,8a,9,10,11,12,12a,14 (6.38%) [Figures 2 and 3].

In silico study

Molecular docking using Schrödinger Maestro 12.8 (Schrödinger, Inc., New York, NY, USA) yielded docking and ΔG-binding scores for the STF constituents against AKR1C3 (PDB: 4YVV). Compounds such as 4,4,6a, 6b,8a,11,11,14b-Octamethylcyclopenta[a] phenanthrene and n-hexadecanoic acid matched glibenclamide scores [Table 1]. Interaction analysis revealed the presence of predominantly hydrogen bonds and salt bridges. ADMET profiling confirmed Lipinski compliance and low toxicity, with minimal risks predicted by ProTox-II [Table 2].

In vivo antidiabetic activity

Acute toxicity studies of STF extract (up to 500 mg/kg) in mice showed minimal weight variation and no mortality, enabling selection of 250 and 500 mg/kg doses. In normoglycemic mice, STF (500 mg/kg) or glibenclamide significantly reduced glucose at 30–120 min (P < 0.05) and improved glucose tolerance [Tables 3 and 4]. In alloxan-induced diabetic mice, both treatments lowered hyperglycemia from day 5 (P < 0.001), reduced HbA1c [Table 5], and normalized ocular lens water, sodium, potassium, and protein levels (P < 0.001) [Figure 4]. Renal biomarkers, cardiac SOD and AST, and hemodynamic parameters were restored [Table 6]. Histopathology showed STF 500 mg/kg preserved pancreatic, hepatic, and cardiac architecture, confirming its potent antidiabetic and organ-protective efficacy [Figures 5-7].

DISCUSSION

The present study demonstrated that the ethanolic extract of STF exerts significant antidiabetic and organ-protective effects in alloxan-induced diabetic mice. Treatment with STF markedly reduced fasting BGL and HbA1c level, matching the efficacy of glibenclamide, and also lowered glucose levels in normoglycemic and oral glucose tolerance models, revealing both antihyperglycemic and glucose-lowering activities.[9] GC-MS profiling identified key phytochemicals, including 2-furancarboxaldehyde, L-cystine, n-hexadecanoic acid, and urs-12-en-28-al. Molecular docking indicated strong binding affinities of these constituents to the AKR1C3 receptor, implicating mechanisms that modulate insulin resistance and diabetes progression. The extract’s rich antioxidant and anti-inflammatory profile likely mitigates oxidative stress and inflammation, which are central to diabetic complications. Organ-specific benefits included normalization of lens protein, electrolytes, and water content, suggesting inhibition of polyol pathway-mediated cataractogenesis and reduction of elevated serum creatinine, urea, and kidney weight, indicating renal protection.[10] Cardioprotective effects were evidenced by decreased AST levels and increased SOD. In addition, STF demonstrated potent in vitro α-amylase and α-glucosidase inhibition, which supports reduced postprandial hyperglycemia.

CONCLUSION

In alloxan-induced diabetic mice, S. trifoliatus extract significantly lowered fasting glucose and HbA1c levels and alleviated renal, ocular, and cardiovascular damage. Rich in antioxidant and anti-inflammatory phytochemicals, including 2-furancarboxaldehyde, 5-(hydroxymethyl) furfural, and L-cystine, it inhibited α-amylase/α-glucosidase, improved lens and renal markers, restored cardiac enzymes, and preserved histology, highlighting its adjunctive therapeutic potential.
