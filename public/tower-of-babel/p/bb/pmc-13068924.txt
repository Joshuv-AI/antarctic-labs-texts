Association of ciltacabtagene autoleucel with immune effector cell-associated enterocolitis: insights from a large national database
Authors: Connor Frey, Mahyar Etminan, Hannah Cherniawsky
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We have read the intriguing article entitled “Immune Effector Cell-Associated Enterocolitis Following Chimeric Antigen Receptor T-Cell Therapy in Multiple Myeloma” by Fortuna and colleagues with great interest [1]. The article provides valuable insight into rare but serious toxicities of CAR T-cell therapies that are not fully characterized in phase 3 trial data. Given the designation of immune effector cell-associated enterocolitis (IEC-EC), this was the first, and largest, case series describing acute severe non-bloody diarrhea and gut inflammation weeks to months after CAR T-cell therapy without infectious cause. Subsequent reports prompted a warning issued from the FDA regarding the risk of IEC-EC in patients treated with ciltacabtagene autoleucel. We were interested in the national reporting of this event within the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) across multiple institutions for all commercial CAR T-cell therapies, aiming to understand if this adverse event is associated with the disease, target antigen, or specific CART construct. Immune-mediated adverse events, most notably cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) are common side effects of CAR T-cell therapy. Both the product and underlying disease contribute to rates of CRS and ICANS, though they can be seen in up to 93% and 87% of patients, respectively. Additionally, unique and unexpected toxicities have been identified in longer term follow-up of patients treated with CAR T-cell therapy [2]. The emergence of these toxicities harkens to the need for ongoing pharmacovigilance, such is the case for IEC-EC.

To address this unmet need, epidemiological data were extracted from the FAERS database, as previously described [3]. To address this unmet need, epidemiological data were extracted from the FAERS database, as previously described [3]. Data from January 1, 2013, through October 13, 2025, were queried using OpenVigil 2.1, which implements automated data cleaning (deduplication, formatting correction, and drug name harmonization). Analyses included only records designating the drug as the primary suspect. Missing demographic and severity data, as well as time-to-onset, were unavailable through OpenVigil and thus excluded. Both serious and non-serious events were analyzed. Cases were identified using the MedDRA Preferred Term (PT) “immune-mediated enterocolitis”. Data were retrieved for all six FDA-approved CAR T-cell therapies. Signal detection employed disproportionality analysis using the reporting odds ratio (ROR)derived from 2 × 2 contingency tables. A signal was considered significant if ROR > 2.0 and the lower 95% confidence interval exceeded 1.0. Sensitivity or subgroup analyses were not feasible due to incomplete demographic data and limited case counts. Only ciltacabtagene autoleucel (anti-BCMA) showed reports of immune-mediated enterocolitis, with 31 cases (ROR 83.65, 95% CI 58.54-119.52) (Table 1). No reports were found for the other CAR T-cell products, including idecabtagene vicleucel. Given that a case of IEC-EC after idecabtagene have been published elsewhere, this underscores FAERS’ limitations, variable report quality, underreporting, and temporal lags, which constrain signal interpretation. FAERS data should therefore be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than quantitative evidence of incidence or causality.

It is important to note that CD19 CAR T-cell therapy has been available for commercial use far longer than anti-BCMA CART, thus lead time bias is not suspected to contribute (Table 1).

The immunological mechanisms underlying IEC-EC after ciltacabtagene autoleucel seem to involve direct infiltration of CAR-T cells into the gut mucosa, specifically the lamina propria and epithelium, as demonstrated by immunofluorescence [1]. This infiltration appears to be a key pathologic features causing mucosal injury characterized by intraepithelial lymphocytosis, villous blunting, and a graft-versus-host disease (GVHD)-like pattern, including epithelial apoptosis and crypt dropout [4]. The pathogenic process is likely multifactorial, involving persistent CAR T-cell activation, cytokine-mediated inflammation, and possible off-tumour, on-target effects against BCMA-expressing non-malignant gut cells [5]. Compared to other immune toxicities such as CRS and ICANS, IEC-EC is more organ-specific and delayed, driven by direct tissue infiltration rather than systemic cytokine effects [5].

The limited evidence available indicates that no single biomarker has been validated to reliably predict which patients are at highest risk for developing IEC-EC after ciltacabtagene autoleucel. However, several candidate biomarkers and mechanistic insights have emerged. CAR T-cell persistence and expansion kinetics have been associated with both therapeutic efficacy and increased risk of immune-mediated toxicities, including organ-specific inflammation [6]. Early peak levels and sustained persistence of CAR T cells post-infusion correlate with these outcomes. High CAR T-cell expansion may be linked to a greater risk of IEC-EC, as observed in other immune effector cell toxicities, though this relationship specifically for enterocolitis remains to be validated. Elevated systemic inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6, IFN-γ, and TNF-α, have been connected to other CAR-T-related toxicities, including CRS and ICANS. Similar cytokine surges might contribute to the pathogenesis of IEC-EC [6, 7]. In the context of BCMA CAR T-cell therapy, cytokine profiles have been shown to predict prolonged hematologic toxicities, indicating that patients with heightened cytokine responses may also be at risk for IEC-EC, although direct evidence is currently lacking [7]. Regarding gut BCMA expression, there is no direct evidence that baseline or induced BCMA expression in gut tissue predicts risk for IEC-EC. The mechanistic hypothesis suggests that off-tumour, on-target effects could occur if BCMA is expressed in the gut mucosa, but this has not been substantiated in clinical biomarker studies.

Importantly, it remains unclear why IEC-EC has been reported at a much higher frequency after ciltacabtagene autoleucel as opposed not idecabtagene vicleucel, which also targets BCMA [8]. The constructs have significant differences in their scFv binding domains. Ciltacabatagene autoleucel contained two scFv binding domains conferring a higher binding avidity with target cells [9]. Thus, is it is possible that the reports of IEC-EC with ciltacabtagene autoleucel but not idecabtagene vicleucel relate to the differences in binding avidity between the constructs. Other possibilities could include expansion kinetics scFv binding domain specific toxicity, as opposed to avidity, or simply higher rates of use of ciltacabtagene autoleucel.

Current management strategies primarily involve systemic corticosteroids as first-line therapy; however, response rates remain suboptimal, with improvement seen in only about 40% of cases [1]. For steroid-refractory patients, biologic agents such as infliximab and vedolizumab have demonstrated efficacy. Treatment protocols are not standardized and are mostly extrapolated from approaches used in immune checkpoint inhibitor-induced colitis and inflammatory bowel disease [10]. The American Society of Clinical Oncology recommends initiating steroids for immune-related adverse events, with escalation to biologics in refractory scenarios [11]. Significant challenges persist due to the lack of prospective studies, absence of validated grading or response criteria, and high risks of severe complications, including bowel perforation and sepsis. Preventative strategies have been used for other CAR T-cell related toxicities, such as steroid to blunt anti-BCMA CAR T-cell expansion in the hopes to prevent movement and neurocognitive treatment-emergent adverse events. It will be interesting to see if such preventative strategies also mitigate development of IEC-EC [12].

The original study, supplemented by the FAERS pharmacovigilance data, provides important preliminary evidence supporting a possible association between IEC-EC and ciltacabtagene autoleucel. The substantial reporting odds ratio observed suggests a strong association that appears unique among the FDA-approved CAR-T products analyzed. Immunopathologic findings corroborate a mechanistic basis involving direct CAR T-cell infiltration and localized mucosal injury. However, given the limitations inherent to retrospective adverse event reporting systems and the scarcity of comprehensive prospective data, these findings warrant cautious interpretation. Reporting bias does need to be considered. For example, one of the cases reported by Banerjee et al. [8] was in a patient treated with idecabtagene vicleucel [8]. However, this does not appear to have been reported to the FDA, which may have occurred for a variety of reasons. In addition to rigorous reporting, further mechanistic and clinical studies are essential to confirm this association, delineate underlying pathways, identify reliable predictive biomarkers, and optimize diagnostic and management strategies for IEC-EC in the context of CAR T-cell therapy.
