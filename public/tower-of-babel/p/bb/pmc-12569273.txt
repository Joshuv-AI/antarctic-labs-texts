A European monsoon-like climate in a warmhouse world
Authors: Nick Van Horebeek, Niels J. de Winter, Michiel Baatsen, Martin Ziegler, Robert P. Speijer, Johan Vellekoop
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The middle Eocene warmhouse period (45 million years ago) featured atmospheric carbon dioxide concentrations equivalent to those projected under high future emission scenarios. Seasonal- to weather timescale climate reconstructions from this period can provide critical insight into the impact of Anthropogenic warming on intra-annual variability in temperature and precipitation. Here, we combine daily-scale reconstructions of the evolution of temperature and the water cycle in western Europe based on stable oxygen and clumped isotope analyses on the fastest-growing gastropod known in the fossil record: Campanile giganteum. Our dataset shows that the middle Eocene of western Europe featured monsoon-like conditions, with seawater temperatures of ~24 °C during mild and wet winters, >30 °C during hot and dry spring and autumn seasons, and ~28 °C during warm and comparatively wet summers. Coupled climate model simulations using the Community Earth System Model indicate these seasonal variations in temperature and precipitation were driven by shifting atmospheric and oceanic circulation regimes over Western Europe, with winds from different directions bringing distinct waters to the region and minimal wind during spring reducing cooling through diminished latent heat flux. Our results highlight that Europe may experience wetter summers with more frequent extreme rainfall events under future high emissions scenarios.

Introduction

Anthropogenic carbon emissions and land-use change are pushing Earth’s climate to a warmer state1. General Circulation Models (GCMs) project a significant increase in the intensity and frequency of extreme rainfall events in monsoon regions under warmer climate scenarios2. In Europe, more frequent and severe extreme weather events have been observed over the past years3,4. However, the large spread in climate model projections, and a lack of direct, seasonal-scale climate data from past warm periods hamper consistent and reliable projections of the impact of warming on the seasonal variability in temperature and precipitation2,5.

One of such warm periods is the Lutetian (middle Eocene; ~41–48 million years ago6). With atmospheric CO2 levels of ~1000 ppmV7, resembling projections for the year 2300 CE under Shared Socioeconomic Pathway (SSP) 5–8.51, and average deep-sea temperatures up to 10 °C warmer than today8, the Lutetian climate is referred to as a “Warmhouse period”9. Together with modelling of future climate scenarios10, the Lutetian analogue presents a way to understand the impact of high global mean temperatures on the seasonal structure of climate. Our knowledge about past warm time intervals such as the Lutetian is mostly based on geochemical proxy records from (micro)fossils in sedimentary archives, which generally yield information about past climate on long (>1000 year) timescales (e.g. ref. 9). While instrumental for our understanding of climate trends through the Cenozoic, these archives do not resolve information about fundamental aspects of climate such as short-term climate feedbacks, seasonality, and weather extremes11,12. This is problematic because the impact of extreme weather events on human society is generally higher than changes in climatic means13.

The occurrence of extreme events is intrinsically linked to the seasonal distribution of temperature and precipitation14. This seasonal impact on the distribution of extreme events is particularly strong in western Europe15. The Western European climate is heavily influenced by the strength of the Westerlies, which transport moisture from the Atlantic Ocean to the continent. In the current climate state, occasional seasonal variability in the strength of these westerly winds, caused by changing temperature differences between the continent and the North Atlantic Ocean16 can cause a phenomenon called the “European Monsoon”. It is characterized by a dry spring season and a wetter summer season brought about by a resurgence of the Westerlies around June16. It differs from the much stronger Indian Monsoon, which results from the seasonal migration of the Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ; ref. 17). Such seasonally contrasting precipitation patterns are uncharacteristic for temperate latitudes in the present day. High-resolution models for European climate predict that Arctic Amplification of warming will result in a weakening of the Westerlies in the spring/summer season, which would counteract the European Monsoon pattern (e.g.,14,15,18) and cause an increase in prolonged dry spells in summer and intense precipitation events in winter in the mid-latitudes19. Yet, it has so far not been verified if the European Monsoon phenomenon indeed ceases under warmer climate states. Idealized simulations of seasonal temperature variability in a warmer world could help understand such monsoon-like climates in higher latitudes. Additionally, assessing the seasonal impact of global warming on European climate requires archives that reliably record seasonal variability during past warmhouse periods. Fossil mollusc shells are excellent archives for deep time palaeoclimatological data with sub-annual resolution, as they can preserve records with a resolution of hours to weeks20–22.

Here we present a unique ultra-high resolution δ18O record containing multiple years of weather-timescale variability in the Eocene in the giant marine gastropod Campanile giganteum (Lamarck, 1804) from the middle Lutetian deposits of Fleury-la-Rivière, in the shallow marine (2–20 m23,24) Paris Basin in France. Recent research has shown that with growth rates up to 60 cm/year along the helix25, far exceeding those of all other fossil molluscs, these giant gastropods have the unique potential to record weather-timescale variability in the Eocene greenhouse world25. Therefore, we generated a high-resolution δ18Oc record and used seasonal-scale clumped isotope analysis to separate the effects of temperature and the water cycle on the δ18Oc values.

We use our proxy results as a benchmark against which to test middle Eocene seasonality outcomes from Community Earth System Model (CESM) version 1.0.5, which simulates middle Eocene climate with a similar palaeogeography and pCO2 (4x pre-industrial; 1120 ppmV) compared to the middle Lutetian26. To assess the variability in weather regimes, we extended the existing equilibrated simulations to generate 30 years of daily output, in addition to 100 years of monthly output. Based on this data-model comparison, we show that the Western European climate featured a strong European Monsoon-like climate during the Lutetian warmhouse.

Results and discussion

Seasonal variability recorded in the oxygen isotope composition of C. giganteum shell carbonate

The stable oxygen isotope (δ18Oc) values in the shell aragonite of C. giganteum vary periodically between −2.5‰ and +1.0‰VPDB in ~4.5 cycles (Fig. 1; Supplementary Fig. 1). The cycles exhibit a platykurtic shape, defined by sharper maxima and more prolonged minima. Assuming a quasi-sinusoidal temperature progression through the year, these sharper peaks are a typical signature of winters, when the growth slows in modern molluscs27,28. This winter slow-down of growth is confirmed by the presence of visible growth breaks at locations in the shell with high δ18Oc values (Fig. 1). We used an internal age model based on the periodic δ18Oc record (following ref. 29), calibrated with growth increment counting (see Methods) to place our δ18Oc measurements on a chronology relative to the annual cycle, assuming ~365 days in a year and defining a year by one cycle in δ18Oc. Our record shows variability on a scale of days superimposed on this seasonal cycle, most notably in the summer (Fig. 1). The amplitude of these sub-seasonal δ18Oc variations (0.3-0.5 ‰) exceeds the variability observed within the same growth increment (see Supplementary Fig. 2), and thus reflects true variability in the growth environment of C. giganteum on a daily scale. Assuming the periodic δ18Oc variability observed in C. giganteum is seasonal, this specimen records roughly 4.5 years of near-daily environmental variability in its shell. Absolute seasonal temperature reconstructions using clumped isotope palaeothermometry

Since the δ18Oc proxy is sensitive to variations in the δ18O of the water (δ18Ow) from which the shell carbonate is precipitated and the temperature of the water30, the δ18Oc variability in Fig. 1 cannot directly be translated to temperature variability. Therefore, we have reconstructed palaeotemperatures independent from δ18Ow by using the carbonate clumped isotope thermometer31 on a selection of samples from each of the four seasons. To define these seasons, we assumed a priori that the maximum δ18Oc values, associated with growth breaks, represent winter, and the minimum δ18Oc values occur in the calendar summer, with spring and autumn seasons in between (see Fig. 2). This age model was independently corroborated by counting visible growth increments on the exterior of the shell (Methods; Supplementary Fig. 3; Supplementary Data file S1), previously established to reflect the semidiurnal tidal cycle25. Clumped isotope analyses yielded water temperatures of 33.2 ± 4.4 °C for spring, 27.9 ± 3.8 °C for summer, 30.3 ± 3.8 °C for autumn and 24.5 ± 3.8 °C for winter (see Fig. 2). These palaeotemperatures corroborate other annual mean temperature reconstructions from the same palaeolatitude, based on middle Eocene oysters and foraminifera (20–30 °C32; 23–32 °C33). However, the timing of maximum temperature here is in spring, whereas a maximum seawater temperature is expected in summer, where the δ18Oc record reaches the lowest values26,33. This finding casts strong doubts on the assumption that δ18Ow remains constant throughout the year, which is common in δ18O-based seasonality reconstructions.

Reconstructions of seasonal water stable isotope variability in the middle Eocene Paris Basin

We reconstructed the δ18Ow value for every season based on our clumped isotope-based temperature reconstructions, the seasonal δ18Oc measurements in C. giganteum, and the relationship between the δ18Oc value of aragonitic shells, δ18Ow and the temperature during shell formation34. Doing so yields δ18Ow values of +1.56 ± 1.00 ‰VSMOW in spring, -0.27 ± 0.87 ‰VSMOW in summer, +0.84 ± 0.87 ‰VSMOW in autumn and +0.94 ± 0.85 ‰VSMOW in winter (see Fig. 2). In modern shallow marine settings, shifts in δ18Ow values of this magnitude are predominantly caused by the balance between evaporation, which removes isotopically light water and raises δ18Ow values, and precipitation, which adds isotopically light freshwater and lowers δ18Ow values35,36. These reconstructions therefore suggest a comparatively dry spring season and a wet summer, with a more moderate autumn and winter season.

Reconstructions suggest a European Monsoon-like climate during the middle Eocene

The high variability observed in the δ18Oc record during the summer season coincides with a drop in temperature and a decrease in δ18Ow. To facilitate a drop in temperature and δ18Ow in the Paris Basin in summer, an influx of cooler water is needed that is lower in δ18Ow. We hypothesize that this decoupling of temperature and δ18Ow from the Lutetian spring season into the summer is the signature of a transition from the hot, dry spring to a warm, wet summer. Recently, seasonally variable fluvial discharge has been suggested for the Lutetian Paris Basin, based on strontium isotope data from bivalve shells37. The increased δ18Oc variability in the Lutetian summer observed in the C. giganteum record suggests that the low-δ18Ow freshwater influx in this season was likely concentrated in intense pulses, which we interpret to reflect an increase in the frequency of convective rainfall events. This suggests that, rather than drier summers and wetter winters, as predicted for a warmer-than-present European climate19, the Lutetian warmhouse climate seems to have featured a seasonal transition from a warm and dry spring to a summer with intense rainfall, akin to an extreme version of the present-day European Monsoon phenomenon. This is in stark contrast with the mild and moderately wet climate with equal distribution of precipitation throughout the year experienced in present-day western Europe38.

Data-model agreement on an intensified middle Eocene European Monsoon-like climate

CESM simulations for the late-middle Eocene (38 Ma) show annual mean air temperatures of 24.7 °C (23.0–26.3 °C) for the Paris Basin region, and 660 mm (440-810 mm) of annual precipitation (25/75 percentile range; Fig. 3). Our model palaeogeography lacks the spatial resolution to resolve the small marine embayment that constitutes the Paris Basin. As a result, no modelled seawater temperatures are available for this basin. Nevertheless, considering the shallow water depths in this embayment24, we regard near-surface air temperatures as the most appropriate analogue for local seawater temperatures. Over land, we see a pronounced seasonal cycle, with monthly mean temperatures ranging between 15.3 °C in January and 36.1 °C in July. The monthly precipitation distribution is relatively flat, with July being the wettest month. Daily precipitation statistics, however, show a pronounced dry period in late spring (May-June) followed by an abrupt shift towards much wetter conditions in early summer (Fig. 3a; Supplementary Fig. 4). During this peak, convective precipitation makes up nearly 100% of all summer precipitation (see Fig. 3b), suggesting a major contribution from short, intense precipitation events. A brief drier interval in autumn is followed by a steady increase in average precipitation as well as daily variability in winter. While the simulated mean annual air temperatures (24.7 ± 1.7 °C) agree with the shallow marine temperatures reconstructed by carbonate clumped thermometry from C. giganteum (28.9 ± 3.3 °C), modelled air temperatures do not replicate the seasonal seawater temperature pattern observed in the data with hot spring and autumn and milder summers (see Fig. 3a). The modelled surface air temperatures clearly reveal higher seasonal contrast than the data, but this may be caused by the fact that C. giganteum grew much slower during the winter and may not have recorded the coldest winter temperatures. Model simulations of past warm climates generally show high seasonal temperature contrast compared to proxy data from the same location and time period. It is a challenge to simulate low equator-to-pole temperature gradients and mild winter temperatures without unrealistically high (>50 °C) summer temperatures in climate models (e.g., ref. 39). Over Eocene Northern Africa, we find a temperature seasonality similar to the proxy record, with an intense monsoonal precipitation pattern, but with a much higher annual temperature (Supplementary Fig. 4; see also Supplementary Fig. 5). Despite our model configuration using a 38 Ma palaeogeography, similar seasonal patterns can be recognized in DeepMIP simulations39 for the early Eocene (50 Ma; Supplementary Fig. 5 and 6). The latter shows an intensification of the hydrological cycle at middle latitudes and a poleward expansion of monsoonal conditions40. DeepMIP simulations generally show wetter conditions over Europe compared to our simulations, as well as cooler temperatures, accompanied by a more pronounced summer precipitation peak, especially over southwestern Europe. Cooler temperatures in the DeepMIP results can be explained by the standard 3xCO2 forcing compared to the 4xPIC (including CH4) used here, making Europe 3–5 °C warmer in our simulations. The lower latitude of our site in 55 Ma versus 38 Ma may also explain the tendency towards more pronounced summer wet seasons. Intense summer precipitation is more pronounced compared to the ensemble mean in the 55 Ma CESM1.2 simulations (ref. 41; see also Supplementary Fig. 7).

A comparison to distant future conditions over Europe is less intuitive, as precipitation changes in summer are dominated by drying over Southern Europe and little change over central Europe (CMIP6 SSP 5.85; see e.g. ref. 42). These results also show a strengthening of westerly winds in winter and overall weaker circulation patterns in summer. In our own extended (2000yr) pre-industrial 4xCO2 simulations, we see small changes in Central Western Europe precipitation as well (see also Supplementary Fig. 8). Despite a tendency towards a converging circulation pattern, the growing influence of upper ridging, elongated areas of relatively high atmospheric pressure, seems to have a more suppressive effect on summer precipitation as noted in CMIP6 simulations. That said, we note that middle Eocene conditions are still much warmer than any future references considered here (12–15 °C globally, versus 3–5 °C at 2100 or 6–8 °C under 4xCO2).

Within the context of middle Eocene conditions, we further test the sensitivity of the climate conditions shown here to (small; 30–38 Ma) changes in the palaeogeography, greenhouse gas levels, and the orbital configuration (Supplementary Fig. 9). None of these external forcings influences the results shown previously in a significant way. We see an overall cooling resulting from lower atmospheric carbon levels, little effect from the palaeogeography, and an increased seasonality with higher Northern Hemisphere summer insolation. While this means that our results are robust under this range of possible Eocene conditions, the role of these in comparison to the present day is likely still substantial, particularly that of the palaeogeography (see e.g. ref. 26,39). While the annual proxy-based temperature is matched only by the deep tropics in the current climate, we find the best match at middle latitudes for the middle Eocene (Supplementary Fig. 10). In both cases, the best match to seasonality is found in the sub-tropics, which is not surprising as these experience the most pronounced monsoonal climates.

Seasonality in Westerly wind strength drives mild summers in the middle Eocene

A closer look at the circulation patterns around western Europe and the associated weather regimes shown in CESM simulations for the middle Eocene helps to interpret the proxy record (Fig. 4). Through winter and early spring, we see persistent westerly to southwesterly winds across the region. These winds mostly disappear in late spring, transitioning into stronger northerly to northeasterly flow in summer to early autumn. A brief pause in prevailing winds is quickly followed by a return of the westerlies in late autumn towards winter, a pattern reminiscent of the modern European Monsoon16. A convergent circulation pattern over much of western central Europe (including the Paris Basin) provides the conditions needed to produce the convective rainfall events which drive variability in our daily climate record (Fig. 4f). A limitation of the model simulation is the absence of a shallow marine environment near the Paris Basin in the modelled palaeogeography. In addition, the horizontal model resolution prevents an adequate point-by-point comparison to the proxy site. This is a common limitation of model-proxy comparisons. We therefore only consider regional averages and larger-scale climatic conditions. We can argue which of the surrounding waters primarily influenced the region considered. Westerly to southwesterly winds likely brought mild waters from the Bay of Biscay region, which at 22.8 °C (December-March; 20.2–24.1 °C) closely match winter seawater temperatures in the proxy record. A quick temperature increase of the shallow waters of the Paris Basin in late spring is promoted by stable conditions and weak winds, with the simulated near-surface air temperatures in May (27.3 °C; 24.7–28.9 °C) and June (32.9 °C; 30.3–35.0 °C) providing the best match to clumped isotope-based late spring seawater temperature reconstructions. In summer, northerly winds bring relatively cool waters from the North Sea region, which by then have warmed to 28.6 °C (July–August; 27.0–30.7 °C) and again match the proxy record well. Stable conditions only briefly return in autumn, but can be matched to the proxy record. Both land-based temperatures of 28.6 °C (September–October; 27.2–30.3 °C) and sea surface temperatures in the Biscay region of 30.4 °C (September-October; 28.9–32.1 °C) fall well within the limits of the proxy-based estimate. In conclusion, we can state that the changing atmospheric circulation regimes are key to understand the temperature seasonality in the Paris Basin in the middle Eocene. Furthermore, the quick transition from dry, stable conditions in spring towards highly variable and much wetter conditions in summer makes a strong case for a European Monsoon-like weather regime in summer, on which both the proxy reconstruction and the model simulation agree.

European Monsoon-like climate under a high emission scenario

Both proxy reconstructions and model simulations support the existence of a European Monsoon-like climate pattern with elevated precipitation in summer in Europe during the high-CO2 middle Eocene. These results may indicate that under higher CO2 conditions, such as those expected by 2300 CE under the SSP5-8.5 IPCC SSP1, the climate in Western Europe could shift towards a more seasonal precipitation regime, strongly controlled by seasonal variations in the strength of the Westerlies, challenging previous knowledge about a warm European climate.

Methods

The C. giganteum specimen is collected in the Banc a Verrains of the Calcaire Grossier Formation in the subterranean galleries of La Cave aux Coquillages, a gallery/museum in Fleury-la-Rivière (France; 49°06’05”N; 3°52’54”E). The Banc a Verrains, also known as the main Campanile horizon24, is dated to approximately 45 Ma, based on correlations by calcareous nannofossil biozonation (NP15) and magnetostratigraphy (Polarity Chron C20r43,44).

After careful removal of the specimen from the matrix using soft preparation tools, powdered samples were acquired along the entire height of the shell using a handheld DREMEL® 3000 rotary drill with a tungsten carbide drill bit with a diameter of 500 μm. Sampling was done on the outer shell along the most adapical (upper) spiral thread, and was carried out by drilling short transects parallel to the growth increments to gather enough powder while preventing time averaging.

Stable oxygen isotope ratios and age model

Stable oxygen isotope ratios were measured using a Thermo Fisher™ Delta V Advantage isotope ratio mass spectrometer coupled to a Thermo Finnigan™ GasBench II through a continuous flow interface at the Department of Earth and Environmental Sciences, KU Leuven. Aliquots of ~50 μg of aragonite powder were reacted with excess anhydrous orthophosphoric acid (H3PO4) in helium-flushed vials to produce CO2, which is sampled from the headspace of the vial with a needle. Two international standards (LSVEC, NBS19) and two in-house standards are measured to correct for instrumental drifts. They are calibrated to the Vienna Pee Dee Belemnite (VPDB) standard.

The growth model ShellChron is used to transform the isotope records from the distance domain into a chronology relative to the seasonal δ18Oc cycle. Assuming the δ18Oc variation in C. giganteum predominantly reflects seasonal variability, this model aligns δ18Oc measured along a sampling transect to a shell chronology, allowing the growth rate to vary seasonally (including potential growth cessations; ref. 29) To corroborate the seasons defined based on the δ18O profile and calibrate the ShellChron age model, visible growth lines were counted of the inferred 3rd year of growth (Supplementary Fig. 3), represented by the 20st to the 25th whorl, and compared to the a priori defined seasons and ShellChron-based ages. The total amount of visible growth increments in this year (704) is almost double the number of days per year (~365), suggesting the increments reflect a semidiurnal cycle with the winter growth cessations concealing several days, that are not reflected as increments. The interval between the growth cessation (assumed to coincide with the shortest day of the year, December 21st) and the midpoint of the intervals sampled for clumped isotopic analyses, respectively for the autumn, winter, spring and summer, cover approximately 138 growth increments before the growth cessation, 25 growth increments before the growth cessation, 284 growth increments after the growth cessation and 427 growth increments after the growth cessation. Based on the number of increments, it can be calculated that the mean of the winter season sample interval occurs around day 342 of the year (mid-December), the mean of the spring season sample interval occurs around day 132 of the year (mid-May), the mean of the summer season sample interval occurs around day 203 of the year (late July), while the mean of the autumn sample interval occurs around day 286 of the year (mid-October), corroborating the seasons defined based on the δ18O profile. In the 3rd year of growth there is an average +33 day offset in the calculated day of year between the age model based on growth increment counting and the ShellChron age model. To correct for this, the output of the ShellChron age model was shifted by +33 days. (Supplementary Data file S1).

Carbonate clumped isotopes

Carbonate clumped isotope measurements (∆47) were performed in the carbonate clumped isotope lab at the Earth Science Department at Utrecht University. A total of 168 replicates were analysed spread over the four seasons based on the shell chronology presented above. Small (75–95 μg) aliquots were reacted with orthophosphoric acid in a Kiel IV carbonate preparation device. The resultant CO2 was cryogenically purified using two liquid nitrogen-cooled cold fingers before being led through a Porapak trap45. The clumped isotope composition was analysed in a Thermo Finnigan MAT 254 PLUS Isotope Ratio Mass Spectrometer using the Long Integration Dual Inlet (LIDI) routine45,46. The results were corrected for intensity-based negative background effects47 and standardized using intensity-matched ETH-1, ETH-2 and ETH-3 community standards48, which were measured in a roughly 1:1 ratio with samples49. Two check standards (IAEA-C2 and Merck50;) were used to quantify long-term instrumental precision (typical standard deviation of 0.04‰).

CESM model runs and data processing

The proxy record presented here is compared to climate model simulations of the middle Eocene using the CESM 1.0.526,53. Using a 38 Ma palaeogegraphy reconstruction, the 4xPIC (i.e., pre-industrial carbon; CO2 and CH4) simulations were shown to be a good match with Bartonian (41.03–37.71 Ma) SST Proxies. The model configuration features CAM4 at ~2° horizontal resolution and 26 hybrid sigma levels and POP2 at ~1 deg resolution and 60 vertical levels for the atmosphere and ocean, respectively. Existing simulations are well equilibrated after ~3500 years, with the following 100 years considered for monthly climatologies used here. For this work, we continued this simulation by a further 30 years to generate the daily output for mean sea level pressure, near surface (2 m) air temperature, and precipitation. Further information on the simulations, as well as additional spin-up and sensitivity experiments, can be found in refs. 26, 53. We also consider early Eocene simulations from the DeepMIP ensemble39 for a further comparison in Supplementary Fig. 5.

Using global output fields, we consider the European region in our analyses and three regions more specifically: Western Europe surrounding the Paris Basin region (37N-47N, 5W-15E), the Bay of Biscay (35N-43N, 7W-GM), and the North Sea (43N-51N, 4W-3E). Statistics are weighted using the surface area and only consider terrestrial cells (land fraction at least 0.5) for Western Europe and marine cells for the Bay of Biscay and North Sea regions, respectively. Sea surface temperatures are taken from the upper level of the ocean model and are therefore the upper 10 m average. Wind speeds are interpolated from hybrid sigma levels onto the 925 hPa level over the Western Europe region, to represent boundary layer winds without too much interaction from the topography. Over the ocean, we use the 975 hPa level for near-surface winds in the absence of topography, while the atmospheric boundary layer is shallower here. For monthly climatologies, we only consider spatial variation within the considered regions in our analyses. For the daily model output, we also include temporal variation to represent synoptic-scale variability as well. Finally, our resulting daily statistics are smoothed using a local linear regression and a 15-day sliding window.
