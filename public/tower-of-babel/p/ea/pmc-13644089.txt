Triglyceride–glucose index normalized by waist circumference and life’s simple 7 to identify high-risk hypertensive patients in primary care
Authors: Ana María Armas-Padrón, Miriam Sicilia-Sosvilla, Ana Perdomo-Ramírez, Pedro Ruiz-Esteban, Armando Torres, Domingo Hernández
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertension affects over one billion adults and remains the leading contributor to global cardiovascular mortality. The triglyceride–glucose index normalized by waist circumference (TyG-WC) is emerging as a surrogate measure of insulin resistance and visceral adiposity, both associated with metabolic disorders and higher cardiovascular risk. Ideal cardiovascular health metrics (CVHM) from the American Heart Association’s Life’s Simple 7 (LS7) are strongly associated with reduced cardiovascular risk. Previous analyses from this cohort also demonstrated significant associations between LS7-derived CVHM and adverse clinical outcomes. However, it remains unclear whether TyG-WC provides additional and complementary prognostic information beyond LS7 in hypertensive patients. Therefore, the prognostic value of assessing the TyG-WC index together with the LS7 score to identify high-risk hypertensive patients in primary care remains largely unexplored.MethodsWe conducted a longitudinal cohort study of 243 hypertensive adults from a primary-care center in Tenerife, Spain. Baseline TyG-WC index, LS7 metrics, anthropometric variables, biochemical parameters, and comorbidities were recorded. Outcomes included hypertension-mediated organ damage (HMOD) and related comorbidities (HRC), all-cause hospitalization, and mortality. Participants were followed for a minimum of two years, with HMOD-HRC, all-cause hospitalization, and all-cause mortality data updated through September 2025. Logistic regression analyses evaluated the independent associations of TyG-WC and CVHM with the adverse composite outcome (HMOD-HRC, hospitalization, and death).ResultsHigher TyG-WC quartiles were associated with the composite outcome: OR 2.23 (Q2), 2.96 (Q3), and 5.12 (Q4) versus Q1. Associations between CVHM scores and clinical outcomes were consistent with previous findings from this cohort. Only 10.7% of participants achieved 4–5 ideal LS7 metrics, whereas 41.2% had 0–1. Higher CVHM scores were also associated with lower body mass index, waist-to-height ratio, fasting glucose, triglycerides, hs-CRP, and with a lower prevalence of diabetes and cardiovascular disease. TyG-WC increased progressively across poorer CVHM categories.ConclusionsElevated TyG-WC independently predicted HMOD-HRC, hospitalization, and all-cause mortality. The associations observed for LS7-derived cardiovascular health metrics were consistent with previous reports from this cohort. Background

Hypertension is one of the most prevalent chronic diseases worldwide, affecting over one billion adults and is the leading modifiable contributor to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. Hypertension-mediated organ damage (HMOD), including left ventricular hypertrophy, chronic kidney disease, peripheral artery disease, and cerebrovascular disease, and hypertension-related comorbidities (HRC), such as overt cardiovascular diseases and chronic kidney disease (CKD) progression, significantly worsen prognosis even when blood pressure is apparently controlled [3].

The triglyceride–glucose index, particularly when normalized by waist circumference (TyG-WC), has emerged as a sensitive surrogate for insulin resistance and visceral adiposity [4, 5]. TyG-derived indices are consistently associated with metabolic disorders [6, 7] and a higher cardiovascular risk [8]. Indeed, a recent systematic review confirmed that higher TyG predicts cardiovascular disease and mortality in the general population [8]. Moreover, recent evidence indicates that both TyG and homeostasis model assessment (HOMA) indices are predictive of new-onset hypertension in middle-aged adults [9], and that a specific TyG threshold can identify hypertensive patients at higher risk of cardiovascular mortality [10]. Additionally, data from Chinese middle-aged and older adults suggest that insulin resistance plays a key role in mediating the relationship between obesity and incident hypertension [11], highlighting the early metabolic contributions to hypertensive disease.

The American Heart Association (AHA) developed the Life’s Simple 7 (LS7) metrics to categorize ideal cardiovascular health (CVH) using seven metrics encompassing health behaviors and health factors [12]. Achieving higher LS7 scores is associated with lower cardiovascular and all-cause mortality, reduced subclinical disease, and lower healthcare expenditures [13]. Hypertensive individuals frequently exhibit underlying metabolic dysfunction, including insulin resistance and central adiposity, which may not be fully captured by LS7 metrics, as these are based on categorical behavioral and clinical components rather than continuous measures of metabolic status.

Previous reports from our group demonstrated significant associations between cardiovascular health metrics (CVHM), inflammation, and comorbidities in hypertensive adults with overweight/obesity [14, 15]. However, these studies primarily focused on LS7-based cardiovascular health metrics and did not incorporate specific markers of insulin resistance or central adiposity. In this context, the TyG-WC has emerged as an accessible surrogate marker of insulin resistance and visceral adiposity. Although CVHM—including the LS7 score—captures key behavioral and clinical components, it may not fully reflect underlying metabolic dysfunction. Although LS7 and TyG-WC partially capture overlapping cardiometabolic domains, they reflect distinct pathophysiological dimensions. Therefore, rather than comparing or integrating these indices into a single model, the present study aimed to evaluate their independent associations with adverse clinical outcomes. In this context, the present study aims to extend previous findings by evaluating the independent association of TyG-WC with morbidity and mortality in hypertensive primary-care patients, and to explore its potential complementary role alongside established cardiovascular health metrics.

We hypothesized that higher TyG-WC and poorer LS7 cardiovascular health would both be independently associated with increased risk of HMOD-HRC, hospitalization, and mortality in hypertensive patients from an urban primary-care center.

Methods

Study population

A total of 243 adult Caucasian patients with a diagnosis of primary hypertension were enrolled at the urban primary healthcare center of La Cuesta (La Laguna, Tenerife, Canary Islands, Spain) between September 2018 and September 2019. During the recruitment period, both prevalent cases (originating from the center itself or referred from other healthcare facilities) and incident hypertensive cases identified during that timeframe were included. Exclusion criteria were individuals under 18 years of age, or those who declined to provide informed consent. Individuals with secondary hypertension were excluded based on routine clinical evaluation, including medical history, physical examination, and complementary testing when clinically indicated, following standard clinical practice guidelines. All participants gave informed consent and were followed for a minimum of 24 months (until 2021), ensuring at least 2 years of follow-up for all patients or until death. Given the one-year recruitment period, the follow-up duration ranged approximately between 2 and 3 years, resulting in a relatively homogeneous follow-up across the cohort. In addition, information regarding HMOD-HRC, all-cause hospitalization, and all-cause mortality was updated through September 2025 for the entire cohort (Fig. 1).

The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of the University Regional Hospital of Malaga and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Baseline data collection

Socio-demographic, lifestyle, and clinical variables were collected using standardized self-reported questionnaires, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), marital status, education level, household income, night-shift work, physical activity, smoking status, alcohol intake, diet quality, salt consumption, and current pharmacological treatment (antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, and glucose-lowering agents) [12, 14, 15] (Supplementary file). Family history of cardiovascular disease, diabetes, hypertension, and cancer was also recorded. Baseline assessments included blood pressure, fasting glucose, serum lipids, HbA1c, hs-CRP, estimated renal function (MDRD), and the albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR). Chronic kidney disease (CKD) followed KDIGO criteria (eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² and/or ACR > 30 mg/g for ≥ 3 months).

Pre-existing HMOD and HRC were documented at baseline and re-evaluated during the scheduled follow-up period in accordance with clinical practice guidelines [2, 16]. HMOD included stroke, left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH), abnormal ankle–brachial index (ABI), peripheral artery disease (PAD), and CKD. HRC included nonfatal atherosclerotic events (coronary artery disease, stroke, symptomatic PAD), atrial fibrillation, heart failure, and CKD progression. LVH and atrial fibrillation were identified by electrocardiography. PAD was defined by an ABI ≤ 0.9 using an automated oscillometric device (MESI) [17] or by clinical history. Coronary artery disease was defined as myocardial infarction or coronary revascularization using standard criteria [18, 19]. Stroke was defined as a persistent neurological deficit lasting ≥ 24 h. Heart failure required compatible symptoms/signs and objective evidence of structural or functional impairment. CKD progression was defined as a ≥ 50% decline in eGFR or transition to end-stage renal disease. All-cause hospitalizations and mortality during follow-up were also recorded. All biochemical and clinical variables, including the TyG-WC index, were collected at baseline and analyzed as predictors of subsequent clinical outcomes.

TyG-WC index

The TyG-WC index was calculated using validated formulae, as previously described [4, 5]. Briefly, fasting plasma glucose (mg/dL) and triglyceride (mg/dL) concentrations were used to compute the TyG index, which was then multiplied by waist circumference (cm) to generate the TyG-WC value. Waist circumference was measured at the midpoint between the lower margin of the last palpable rib and the top of the iliac crest, with participants standing and breathing normally. The TyG-WC index was treated as a continuous variable for descriptive analyses and categorized into quartiles for inferential analyses to examine associations with cardiometabolic outcomes. Higher quartiles of TyG-WC have been considered indicative of greater insulin resistance and metabolic risk [20].

Visceral Adiposity Index (VAI)

The Visceral Adiposity Index (VAI) was calculated using sex-specific equations that incorporate BMI, WC, triglyceride levels, and HDL cholesterol, following the original formulas proposed by Amato et al. [21]. VAI was considered a continuous variable and used as an indirect marker of visceral fat dysfunction and cardiometabolic risk. This approach allowed for a consistent estimation of visceral adiposity across the study population.

Cardiovascular health metrics (Life’s Simple 7)

CVH was assessed using the LS7 score based on seven metrics: BMI, smoking status, diet quality, physical activity, blood pressure, fasting total cholesterol, and fasting glucose [12]. BMI was calculated from measured height and weight; smoking status was categorized as current, former, or never; and physical activity was assessed using standardized questionnaires, including the Baecke instrument or equivalent frequency-based classifications. Diet quality was evaluated using criteria derived from a food frequency questionnaire, with an ideal diet defined as meeting four or five components related to fish, fruit and vegetable consumption, sodium intake, sugar intake, and fiber-to-carbohydrate ratio [12, 22]. Blood pressure was measured three times in a seated position, and the mean of the final two readings was used for analysis. Total cholesterol and fasting glucose were measured by standardized enzymatic and hexokinase-based laboratory methods. Each LS7 metric was categorized into poor, intermediate, or ideal levels following the LS7 scoring framework. Consistent with LS7 guidelines, hypertensive patients on treatment who achieved their therapeutic goals for blood pressure (systolic < 140 mmHg and diastolic < 90 mmHg), total cholesterol (< 200 mg/dL), and fasting glucose (< 126 mg/dL) were classified in the intermediate category. Because this cohort consisted exclusively of hypertensive individuals, the ideal blood pressure category was unattainable for any participant. A global CVH score (0–7) was calculated by allocating one point to each ideal metric and zero points otherwise. Additionally, individuals were grouped according to whether they met 0–1 (poor), 2–3 (intermediate), or 4–5 (ideal) CVHM [12], with only a small number achieving five ideal metrics.

Outcomes

HMOD was defined as the presence of left ventricular hypertrophy, chronic kidney disease, peripheral artery disease, abnormal ankle–brachial index, or stroke. HRC included nonfatal atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (coronary artery disease, stroke, or symptomatic peripheral artery disease), atrial fibrillation, heart failure, and chronic kidney disease progression. For the purposes of this study, a composite outcome (HMOD-HRC) was defined as the occurrence of any of the above conditions.

The primary outcome was a composite endpoint combining global HMOD-HRC, all-cause hospitalization, and all-cause mortality to capture the overall burden of vascular and nonvascular adverse events.

Statistical analysis

The baseline clinical characteristics of patients were assessed based on the quartiles of the TyG-WC index. Individuals were divided into four groups according to the quartile distribution of the TyG-WC index. In addition, patients were also divided into three groups according to LS7 metrics categorization for additional analysis. Continuous variables were expressed as mean±standard deviation or median with interquartile range, depending on their distribution, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Inter-group comparisons across LS7 and TyG-WC categories were performed using one-way ANOVA for continuous variables and the chi-square test (or Fisher’s exact test when appropriate) for categorical variables. Skewed continuous variables were log-transformed using natural logarithm (ln) before analysis when required. Logistic regression models were used to assess the association between TyG-WC quartiles, cumulative LS7 metrics, and the composite endpoint HMOD-HRC, all-cause hospitalization, and all-cause mortality. To assess whether the observed associations were driven by the composite endpoint, additional multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed using HMOD-HRC and all-cause mortality as separate dependent variables. Given the potential collinearity between LS7 components and metabolic variables included in the TyG-WC index, and to minimize the risk of overadjustment, separate models were constructed to assess their independent associations with clinical outcomes. Model 1 included TyG-WC quartiles as the independent variable, whereas Model 2 was performed using CVHM scores. Both models were adjusted for age, sex, and body mass index. Finally, a subgroup analysis stratified according to diabetic status was also performed. The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was used to assess model adequacy. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using SPSS software version 20.0 for Windows (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL).

Results

Baseline clinical characteristics of the study population

Table 1 displays baseline socio-demographic and clinical characteristics as well as CVHM and community risk factors in the whole study sample and stratified by gender. Overall, the mean age was 68.5 ± 13 years and 54% were women. Obesity (BMI > 30 kg/m²) and diabetes were common (50.2% and 41.6%, respectively). Likewise, the mean waist–height ratio was 0.63 ± 0.08. Men had significantly higher fasting glucose and numerically higher triglyceride levels, and consequently they also had a higher TyG-WC index. Additionally, males had a higher family income, were often married and had higher alcohol consumption. Lastly, CVD was more frequent in male patients.

Table 3 shows the clinical characteristics according to the quartile of the TyG-WC index. The TyG-WC averaged 923.5 ± 129 and increased progressively across quartiles. Compared to patients with the lowest TyG-WC index, those with a higher TyG-WC quartile (second, third and fourth) were prone to presenting a greater VAI, elevated systolic blood pressure (SBP), worse glycemic and lipid profiles, higher hs-CRP, and increased diabetes prevalence. Finally, individuals with higher TyG-WC quartiles were more likely to present lower CVHM scores.

Effect of TyG-WC index and cumulative CVHM metrics on clinical outcomes

There were 290 cases of HMOD-HRC in 243 patients, with 225 recorded in the initial analysis and 65 during follow-up. In total, 58 patients presented more than one HMOD-HRC, all-cause hospitalization, and all-cause mortality. Additionally, 31 deaths were recorded up to September 30, 2025: 9 from CVD, 11 from cancer, 4 from infectious complications and 7 from other causes. As expected, patients with HMOD-HRC, all-cause hospitalization, and all-cause mortality were older, had had hypertension for a longer time, with more having diabetes, in addition to a lower GFR and higher ACR as previously reported [14, 15]. Moreover, a higher proportion of ideal CVHM scores was also found in patients without HMOD-HRC, all-cause hospitalization, and all-cause mortality. Lastly, as expected, patients with diabetes were older, had a worse cardiometabolic profile, a higher ACR and a greater TyG-WC index than non-diabetic patients (Supplementary Table 1).

Multivariate binary logistic regression analysis to assess the association between TyG-WC and cumulative scores and the composite endpoint adjusted for confounders is shown in Table 5. Higher TyG-WC quartiles were associated with an increased risk of the composite endpoint (HMOD-HRC, all-cause hospitalization, and all-cause mortality) after adjustment for relevant confounders (Table 5, Model 1a). When HMOD-HRC was analyzed separately, only the highest TyG-WC quartile remained independently associated with the outcome (OR 4.12, 95% CI 1.35–12.57; p = 0.013), whereas the intermediate quartiles showed a similar direction of effect but did not reach statistical significance (Table 6, Model 3). Likewise, when all-cause mortality was analyzed separately, the highest TyG-WC quartile was independently associated with increased all-cause mortality (OR 8.92, 95% CI 1.47–54.09; p = 0.017) (Table 6, Model 4). These findings support the robustness of the association between TyG-WC and adverse outcomes beyond the composite endpoint. When the composite endpoint was analyzed separately in diabetic and non-diabetic patients, the association between TyG-WC and adverse clinical outcomes remained evident. However, the pattern was not strictly monotonic across TyG-WC quartiles in the diabetic subgroup (Table 5, Model 1b), likely reflecting the limited sample size after subgroup stratification. Finally, by binary logistic regression analysis adjusted for confounders, the risk of clinical adverse outcomes increased significantly as the number of ideal CVHM scores decreased, using patients with 4–5 ideal CVHM scores as the reference in the entire cohort (Table 5, Model 2a). When this analysis was performed separately in diabetic and non-diabetic patients, only diabetic patients showed a significantly higher risk as CVHM scores decreased (Table 5, Model 2b). The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test indicated an adequate fit for all multivariable logistic regression models, except for Model 2b (diabetic subgroup), which showed evidence of poorer calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.017).

Discussion

Summary of findings

This primary-care longitudinal study demonstrates that a higher TyG-WC index is independently associated with adverse clinical outcomes in hypertensive patients from a Spanish primary care center. In addition, higher TyG-WC quartiles were associated with an increased risk of adverse clinical outcomes in the overall cohort. Although a progressive increase in risk was observed for the composite endpoint, when HMOD-HRC was analyzed separately, only the highest TyG-WC quartile remained independently associated with this outcome. Our findings were consistent with previous studies, suggesting that the TyG-WC index may serve as an indicator of adverse clinical outcomes in hypertensive patients. However, the association within the diabetic subgroup should be interpreted with caution, as the pattern across TyG-WC quartiles was not strictly monotonic, likely reflecting the limited sample size after subgroup stratification. Likewise, a poorer cardiovascular health assessed by the LS7 metrics was also strongly associated with increased HMOD-HRC, all-cause hospitalization, and all-cause mortality in this population using a multivariate logistic regression adjusted for confounders (Model 2), consistent with prior analyses from the same cohort [14, 15]. Since both fasting glucose and lipid parameters are involved in the LS7 score, we performed two different models to avoid collinearity between the parameters included in the logistic regression models, as it was verified when we jointly included the LS7 score and the TyG-WC index (data not shown).

Comparison with previous studies, including TyG-WC index and CVH metrics

Large prospective cohort studies have consistently documented a positive association between the TyG-WC index and hypertension, and a meta-analysis has confirmed that the TyG index predicts hypertension risk in the general adult population [23–25]. Indeed, longitudinal data further indicate that insulin resistance mediates the relationship between obesity and incident hypertension [11], supporting the role of metabolic dysfunction as an upstream driver of blood pressure elevation.

Beyond hypertension onset, TyG-related indices have shown robust associations with adverse cardiovascular outcomes. Several studies report positive correlations with cardiovascular and all-cause mortality, including among hypertensive patients [9, 10, 25–27]. Accordingly, predictive performance is further enhanced by hs-CRP–TyG combinations for identifying multivessel coronary disease [28], and by longitudinal changes in TyG-WC associated with increased cardiovascular risk [29]. Evidence from the UK Biobank further links TyG-related indices to myocardial infarction and valvular heart disease [30, 31], and cardiovascular risk among individuals with obesity or pre-obesity [32].

In parallel, growing evidence also supports TyG-related indices as practical and robust markers of cardiometabolic risk, even in non-diabetic populations. These indices are strongly associated with metabolic syndrome, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, coronary atherosclerosis, and overall cardiovascular disease [6, 8, 33, 34], with TyG-WC outperforming standard TyG by more accurately capturing central adiposity [5]. Notably, TyG predicts cardiovascular events comparably to classical insulin-resistance markers in healthy adults [35], and higher TyG levels are associated with increased mortality among hypertensive and diabetic individuals [36]. Although TyG-WC was independently associated with all-cause mortality in our cohort, this finding should be interpreted with caution. Because the present study evaluated all-cause rather than cause-specific mortality, the observed association should not be interpreted as evidence of a specific metabolic mechanism underlying every cause of death. Instead, it may reflect the overall prognostic value of TyG-WC in this hypertensive population. Residual confounding related to comorbidity burden, frailty, or treatment intensity cannot be completely excluded.

Taken together, these findings suggest that a higher TyG-WC index may be employed as a risk marker independent of traditional clinical information for adverse outcomes in this population, which might be more evident in patients with worse cardiovascular health. This is consistent with our observations given its independent association with HMOD-HRC and all-cause mortality in our study. Importantly, these associations remained significant when HMOD-HRC and all-cause mortality were analyzed as separate outcomes, suggesting that the observed findings were not solely driven by the use of a composite endpoint. Both triglycerides and glucose are easily accessible and inexpensive and thus the TyG-WC index does not seem to increase overall health care costs.

A notable observation is the very low proportion of individuals achieving ideal LS7 metrics in our study, consistent with previous evidence showing that optimal cardiovascular health remains uncommon in the general adult population [37]. Moreover, lower prevalence of ideal cardiovascular health, including poorer LS7 profiles, has been documented in populations at higher cardiometabolic risk (e.g., individuals with hypertension) [38]. In fact, we observed that individuals with ideal CVHM ([4–5]) showed a better cardiometabolic profile, a lower TyG-WC index and a significant reduction in hs-CRP levels compared to those with a poorer CVHM score, as reported [14]. Of note, a poorer LS7 score was not associated with a higher risk of the composite endpoint in non-diabetic patients, suggesting a potential complementary role of the TyG-WC index in risk stratification within this specific hypertensive population. Given the global escalation of hypertension prevalence [1] and the increasing burden of obesity and metabolic dysfunction, incorporating simple markers such as TyG-WC into routine evaluation may help identify high-risk phenotypes earlier as evidenced by the CVHM score.

Potential pathogenic mechanisms

Central obesity is a better predictor of cardiometabolic risk than general obesity and it more accurately reflects chronic inflammation and insulin resistance [39–42]. TyG may be a reliable surrogate measure of insulin resistance and visceral adiposity [25, 33, 43]. This is consistent with the growing recognition that abdominal obesity, as indicated by waist circumference, is essential in the progression of insulin resistance and metabolic disorders in patients with hypertension and diabetes [44–51]. Although several TyG-related indices predict the risk of adverse clinical outcome in the general population, we chose the TyG-WC because WC is more representative of visceral fat deposition, and increased visceral fat in the abdomen is more related to insulin resistance and metabolic dysfunction, including hepatic steatosis, than subcutaneous fat [6, 43, 52, 53]. Nevertheless, we explored the relationship between the composite outcome and other cardiometabolic indices, including TyG-related indices and the VAI [21, 54], but the strongest association was found with the TyG-WC assessed in our entire population and also when subgroup analysis was performed in patients with and without diabetes. Moreover, insulin resistance is characterized by a low degree of systemic inflammation, which can lead to endothelial dysfunction contributing to atherosclerosis and plaque progression through multiple mechanisms, including changes in classical cardiovascular risk factors and downregulation of insulin signaling pathways [23, 25, 55]. Additionally, visceral fat increases triglyceride production, impairs insulin resistance and promotes systemic inflammation. Thus, it is plausible that a potential systemic inflammatory mechanism appears to mediate these associations [28, 56].

Accordingly, a higher hs-CRP, elevated TyG-WC and poorer LS7 scores in our cohort parallel evidence that TyG-WC-related metabolic dysfunction may be closely tied to inflammatory activation, immune dysregulation, and vascular injury, leading to clinical adverse outcomes in our hypertensive patients. The fact that hs-CRP and ALT were independently associated with TyG-WC in our multiple linear regression analysis and that a higher TyG-WC was observed in our patients with poorer cardiovascular health, support this argument. Together, these findings highlight the clinical relevance of integrating metabolic and inflammatory markers with CVHM assessments and contextualize the metabolic and inflammatory abnormalities observed across worsening TyG-WC quartiles in our hypertensive cohort. Importantly, TyG-WC was also associated in our study with clinical adverse outcomes in both diabetic and non-diabetic patients, highlighting the importance of increased insulin resistance and inflammation secondary to central adiposity contributing to cardiovascular disease and death even prior to diabetes appearance.

Clinical implications

While LS7 represents a comprehensive assessment of cardiovascular health, its implementation in routine primary care may require the collection of multiple behavioral and clinical variables. In contrast, the TyG-WC index is based on routinely available laboratory and anthropometric measurements, making it a simple and accessible tool in daily clinical practice. In this context, TyG-WC may offer a pragmatic complement to established CVHM, particularly in settings where rapid risk stratification is needed.

Overall, our findings suggest that TyG-WC may provide complementary cardiometabolic information alongside LS7 metrics and may help identify hypertensive patients at higher risk of adverse outcomes. As a simple measurement indicator, the TyG-WC and LS7 score may be used more often in daily clinical practice. In other words, the TyG-WC and CVH metrics might be added to routine health check-up programs to screen for the development of serious clinical complications in these patients. Considering recent evidence, the TyG-WC index could also aid in identifying hypertensive patients at higher risk of adverse clinical outcomes even before traditional clinical markers become abnormal, thus offering an opportunity for earlier intervention. Future studies are needed to confirm the relevance of the TyG-WC index and investigate dose-response relationships and determine cut-off values.

Limitations and strengths

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small and the single-center design may limit generalizability. Additionally, participants were generally older, had multiple comorbidities, and were drawn from a Caucasian urban population, which should be considered when interpreting the findings. Furthermore, the group with 4–5 ideal CVHM metrics was relatively small, which may limit statistical power and the stability of the estimates in this subgroup. In particular, only four diabetic patients were classified in the 4–5 CVHM category, resulting in wide confidence intervals in subgroup analyses. A sensitivity analysis using collapsed CVHM categories yielded similar directional findings but with reduced statistical significance, highlighting the limited precision of these estimates (Supplementary Table 2). This limited sample size may also have affected the calibration of the logistic regression model in the diabetic subgroup, as reflected by the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. The loss of statistical significance for the intermediate TyG-WC quartiles when HMOD-HRC was analyzed separately may reflect the lower number of events after disaggregating the composite endpoint and the consequent reduction in statistical power.

Second, some variables were based on self-reported data, and therefore social desirability bias cannot be excluded. Likewise, repeated measurements of TyG-WC and detailed information on treatment adherence or changes during follow-up were not available, as the study was designed to assess baseline predictors of adverse outcomes. Consequently, we were unable to evaluate longitudinal changes in metabolic status or their relationship with clinical outcomes. Lastly, a substantial proportion of participants were receiving lipid-lowering and glucose-lowering therapies at baseline. Therefore, the TyG-WC index should be interpreted as a marker of residual cardiometabolic risk under routine clinical management rather than as a direct measure of untreated insulin resistance.

Despite these limitations, the study also has several strengths. It reflects a real-world primary care setting with detailed phenotyping and longitudinal follow-up. In addition, a sensitivity analysis using a minimally adjusted model (adjusted for age and sex only) showed that the highest TyG-WC quartile remained independently associated with the composite endpoint, supporting the robustness of the main findings (Supplementary Table 3). Moreover, we included relevant contextual variables such as night-shift work, education level, alcohol consumption, and household income, which may influence cardiovascular health metrics and the TyG-WC index.

Conclusions

In this hypertensive primary-care cohort, elevated TyG-WC and poorer cardiovascular health were independently associated with increased HMOD-HRC, cardiovascular mortality and all-cause mortality. TyG-WC may represent a complementary cardiometabolic risk marker alongside LS7 metrics and may help identify hypertensive patients at higher risk of adverse outcomes.
