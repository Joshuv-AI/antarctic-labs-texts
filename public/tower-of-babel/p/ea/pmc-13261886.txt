Real-world experience with tabelecleucel within and beyond the approval label
Authors: Kiavasch Mohammad Nejad Farid, Florian Kocher, Tobias Arnold, Josia Fauser, Elisa Henze, Christian Schultze-Florey, Carmen Diana Herling, Vladan Vučinić, Christian Reicherts, Matthias Stelljes, Raphael Stadelmann, Christof Scheid, Udo Holtick, Stavroula Masouridi-Levrat, Marc Wehrli, Urban Novak, Paul Schnitzler, Chiraz Ben-Salah, Isabelle Krämer, Anita Schmitt, Carsten Müller-Tidow, Thomas Luft, Peter Dreger, Michael Schmitt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Tabelecleucel demonstrates efficacy and safety in the real-world setting within and beyond the approval label.•Tools for response prediction, immune reconstitution surveillance, and consolidation strategies may be useful to enhance response stability.

Introduction

Tabelecleucel (tab-cel) is an off-the-shelf, allogeneic Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)–specific T-cell immunotherapy product targeting and eliminating EBV+ cells in a HLA-restricted manner. Tab-cel has been available in Europe since its approval by the European Medicines Agency in December 2022. Although it is the only approved therapeutic option after frontline rituximab and/or immunochemotherapy for patients with relapsed or refractory (R/R) EBV-associated posttransplantation lymphoproliferative disorder (PTLD), real-world experience with its use remains limited.

In patients with iatrogenic immunosuppression, reduction of immunosuppressive medication (RIS) is usually the mainstay of initial treatment. In a fraction of patients, RIS is not feasible because of the risk of organ rejection or persistent graft-versus-host disease (GVHD). In addition, in the majority of cases, RIS alone is not sufficient for controlling EBV-driven lymphoproliferation. Further, in cases of rituximab failure in allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (alloHCT) recipients or rituximab plus chemotherapy in solid organ transplantation (SOT) recipients, R/R EBV-associated PTLD must be recognized as a life-threatening disease requiring urgent intervention. In these cases, median overall survival (OS) is only 3 weeks in patients previously receiving alloHCT1 and 4 months in patients after SOT.2 In this context, (salvage) chemotherapy is associated with a high rate of treatment-related mortality and offers only limited therapeutic efficacy.1 Tab-cel showed promising efficacy in the interim results of the phase 3 ALLELE trial.3 Updated findings of this study reinforce the efficacy of tab-cel in this vulnerable cohort of 75 patients. Tab-cel treatment achieved an overall response rate (ORR) of 50.7%, with a median duration of response of ∼23 months and a median OS of 18.4 months, with 1-year survival rates of 55.7% for all treated patients and almost 80% among responders.

According to the 5th edition of the World Health Organization classification of hematolymphoid tumors (lymphoid neoplasms),4 a category of immune deficiency/dysregulation (IDD)–associated lymphoproliferative diseases (LPD) has been established. This category includes inborn errors of immunity and iatrogenic/acquired immunodeficiencies, such as those caused by HIV, and is frequently associated with EBV. Although lymphomatoid granulomatosis (LYG) is not classified as an IDD-LPD by the World Health Organization classification of hematolymphoid tumors (lymphoid neoplasms), it similarly arises because of impaired immune surveillance of EBV, with the majority of patients exhibiting signs of immune dysfunction even in the absence of a known (primary) immunodeficiency.5

Our real-world data aim to capture a broad patient population both within and beyond the approval label, despite the rarity of these diseases, including those typically excluded from clinical trials, offering a more representative understanding of EBV-LPD management and tab-cel use. This study provides insights into real-world treatment patterns from different centers in central Europe (in DACH countries, which are Germany, Austria, and Switzerland), contributing to improved clinical decision-making in this low-incidence setting, where the need for additional evidence is critical.

Methods

Patient selection

This study included 11 patients from 9 academic centers in DACH who received at least 1 dose of tab-cel regardless of indication (in-label treatment for PTLD or off-label treatment) between July 2023 and June 2025. This population includes all patients treated in the DACH region up to the data cutoff date. We aimed to assess the efficacy, outcomes, and toxicity of tab-cel in a real-world setting. Diagnoses of EBV-LPD were established at the respective clinical centers. The study was coordinated by the Cellular Immunotherapies Group within the Department of Internal Medicine V of University Hospital Heidelberg. The data cutoff date for survival, safety, and efficacy analyses was 9 July 2025. All data of the patients treated at different institutions were obtained by electronic chart review and from the database of the central ordering platform for tab-cel, Go Cell Therapy (www.go-cell-therapy.com, by Pierre Fabre SA). All patients gave written informed consent to the treatment, including scientific use of their data and its publication. Data use was based on the objective of the postauthorization safety study for tab-cel (EBVOLVE; European Union postauthorization safety number EUPAS1000000113), with site-specific approval from the ethics committee of the Medical Faculty of University Hospital Heidelberg (approval file number: S-349/2024). The present study was performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. Parts of this work have been presented at the 51st Annual Meeting of the EBMT in Florence, Italy, in March 2025, and the 2025 Annual Meeting of the German/Austrian/Swiss Societies for Hematology and Medical Oncology in Cologne, Germany.

Patient evaluation and response assessment

All patients received tab-cel through IV, at a dose of 2 × 106 viable T cells per kilogram of the patient's body weight per infusion, administered weekly for 3 doses per cycle (cycle length of 5 weeks), as outlined in the summary of product characteristics (SmPC). A switch to a tab-cel lot with a different HLA restriction (if available) was considered in the event of an inadequate response to the preceding cycle. Treatment response was assessed by positron emission tomography or computed tomography using the Lugano 2014 criteria,6 albeit computed tomography imaging in PTLD follow-up is considered less sensitive for response assessment. Magnetic resonance imaging was used in cases of central nervous system (CNS) involvement or as a complementary to the other modalities for specific anatomical regions. All survival curves were calculated from the day of first tab-cel infusion, unless otherwise specified. Response assessments were established by local investigators based on their clinical practice. EBV titers were assessed in the peripheral blood (plasma) of the patients using quantitative polymerase chain reaction, as a disease activity marker.

Statistical analysis

Patient and treatment characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were described by absolute and relative frequencies. The endpoints encompassed investigator-assessed event-free survival (EFS), OS, ORR, and safety (specifically the incidence of cytokine release syndrome [CRS], immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome [ICANS], and tumor flare reaction [TFR]). EFS was defined as the time from first tab-cel infusion to disease progression, or change in treatment because of inadequate response or death. OS was defined as the time from first tab-cel infusion to death from any cause. ORR was defined as the percentage of patients who achieved either a complete response (CR), a partial response, or an indeterminate response (with repeated evidence of disease stability). The Kaplan-Meier method was used to assess the EFS and OS, complemented by the Gehan–Breslow–Wilcoxon test for the estimation of median survival rates (since close to 50% of the patients remained alive), whereas the reverse Kaplan-Meier method was applied to estimate the median follow-up. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software Inc; version 10.4.1).

ECOG, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group; LDH, lactate dehydrogenase; MUD, matched unrelated donor; NA, not available.

PTLD occurred in 6 patients after SOT (from post mortem donors), including 2 after lung transplant, 2 after liver transplant, 1 after heart transplant, and 1 patient after heart and kidney transplant. In addition, 2 other patients with PTLD had received alloHCT, of which 1 patient received a peripheral blood stem cell graft (from a matched unrelated donor), and another patient had received a peripheral blood stem cell graft after bone marrow graft failure (both from the same haploidentical donor). The median time from transplantation to PTLD was 6.4 months (range, 2.4-13).

The median Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status score of all patients at the time point of tab-cel treatment initiation was 1 (range, 0-3). Three patients (27%) had received T-cell–depleting conditioning or immunosuppressive induction regimens before transplantation. EBV serostatus was positive in 6 of 8 patients with PTLD (75%) and in all patients with EBV-LPD by the nature of disease entity (in total 9 of 11, 82% of all patients); in 2 cases, introduction of EBV infection occurred in EBV˗ recipients receiving an EBV+ (organ) graft. The maximum EBV titer in peripheral blood at any time point after PTLD/EBV-LPD diagnosis was, in median, was 61 150 IU/mL (range, 874-32 487 500), and the median maximum serum lactate dehydrogenase level was 449 U/L (range, 206-3135). Median total CD3+ T-cell count before tab-cel infusion was 319/μL (range, 27-456), median CD4+ T-cell count was 100/μL (range, 10-274), and median CD8+ T-cell count was 129/μL (range, 13-294). Concurrent viremias (particularly cytomegalovirus reactivation) were present in 7 of 11 patients (64%). Patients’ own EBV-specific T-cell repertoire were not investigated, except in the case of the patient with LYG, in whom the presence of insufficient EBV-directed immunosurveillance was confirmed.

The median number of HLA allele matches between the chosen tab-cel lot and the respective patient was 3 (range, 1-7), and also 3 (range, 1-3) between the tab-cel lot and the alloHCT or SOT donor (in patient who underwent transplantations). The median number of matching HLA alleles in responders was 3 (range, 2-7), compared with 2.5 in primary nonresponders (range, 1-3), with no statistically significant difference (P = .3292). Tab-cel expansion kinetics were not monitored. The median EBV titer in the peripheral blood before the first tab-cel infusion was 15 730 IU/mL (range, 0-263 036). The median number of prior lines of therapy received by patients was 2 (range, 1-5). All patients, with the exception of 1 patient with CD20− plasmablastic lymphoma-type PTLD (10 of 11 patients, 91%), received rituximab as first-line therapy (median number of 5 doses; range, 3-9), and 3 patients had already received rituximab preemptively before histological diagnosis of PTLD/EBV-LPD. All patients with PTLD after SOT and patients with nontransplantation-associated EBV-LPDs (in total 9/11, 82% of all patients) received chemotherapy as second-line of treatment before tab-cel infusion (usually cyclophosphamide/doxorubicin/vincristine/prednisone-like or platinum-based therapy). In addition, 1 patient received the Bruton kinase inhibitor (BTKi) ibrutinib, and 1 patient was treated with brentuximab vedotin.

The median time between initial diagnosis of PTLD/EBV-LPD and first tab-cel infusion was 175 days (range, 48- 2793), and the median time between the diagnosis of R/R PTLD/EBV-LPD and first tab-cel infusion was 43 days (range, 10-88). Overall response was observed in 7 of 11 (64%) patients, 3 of whom achieved a CR. Response was assessed as described in “Methods”. A selection of representative radiological response assessments is displayed in Figure 1. Best response occurred at a median of 31 days (range, 18-97) after the first tab-cel infusion and, therefore, mostly after the first cycle. Out of all patients, 5 patients (45%) achieved undetectable EBV titers, and 3 patients achieved a reduction in EBV titers in the peripheral blood. Alongside the case described in tab-cel–related complications, in which ibrutinib was used to mitigate TFR (patient 4 in Figure 2), cytotoxic chemotherapy was administered in an overlapping fashion in 2 cases (patient 6 in Figure 2 received rituximab, polatuzumab vedotin plus ifosfamide/carboplatin/etoposide, and patient 8 in Figure 2 received bendamustine). This was necessitated by rapid disease progression, with the goal of controlling tumor growth until immune-mediated control could take over. Chemotherapy was administered before tab-cel to preserve cytotoxic T-lymphocyte (CTL) function.

In 2 patients (18%), a switch to another tab-cel lot (as recommended in the SmPC) with a different HLA restriction was performed because of an inadequate response (in 1 case after the first cycle and in the other after the second cycle). In these cases, the lot switch did not lead to improvement in response. In 3 additional cases with suboptimal response along the course of treatment (regardless of initial response), a switch was considered but could not be performed because of unavailability of another suitable lot. In patients with relapsed or primary progressive disease (PD), the median time to progression was 1.8 months (range, 0.9-5.1).

After a median follow-up of 15.1 months, 6 of 11 (55%) patients remained alive. Despite a relatively high relapse and progression rate after tab-cel affecting 7 of 11 (64%) patients and 4 of 7 (57%) responders, as reflected by a median EFS of 97 days (confidence interval, 48-143), the (estimated) median OS was 13.4 months (confidence interval, 4.4-14.7), mainly because of successful salvage treatments or effective consolidation strategies. There was no statistically significant difference in OS between responders and nonresponders (401 vs 395 days; P = .5369), although a tendency toward earlier death was observed in nonresponders. Subsequent therapies were necessary and attempted in 7 of 11 patients (64%), including the use of conventional chemotherapy in 2 cases (without response), brentuximab vedotin followed by anti-CD19 chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy with axicabtagene ciloleucel in 1 case (PD after both), chemoimmunotherapy followed by epcoritamab (achieving partial response), BTKi with rituximab/polatuzumab vedotin followed by consolidating radiotherapy (consolidating CR), alloHCT (with early treatment-related mortality), and 1 case using another noncommercial academic EBV-specific CTL (alloCELL7 used by the colleagues of Hannover Medical School; achieving ongoing disease control). Tab-cel-related complications

CRS (grade 1) was suspected in 2 patients (18%), although there were multiple other potential causative factors present for the patients’ fever. Organ rejection in patients after SOT was not observed. ICANS was suspected in 2 cases, including 1 case occurring on day 10 of the first cycle (grade 1, with an immune effector cell encephalopathy score of 8/10) with dyscalculia and temporal disorientation, which was self-limiting without intervention within 24 hours. In the other case, ICANS (formally at least grade 2) was a differential diagnosis to CNS-PD or tumor inflammation-associated neurotoxicity occurring on the day of first infusion with decreased arousal, making high-dose steroids (dexamethasone and methylprednisolone) and anakinra necessary. GVHD exacerbation was suspected in 1 patient (9%) with preexisting GVHD (after haploidentical alloHCT) but could not be definitively attributed to tab-cel alone. TFR was suspected in 2 patients (18%). In addition, 1 patient developed a massive increase (>20-fold) in EBV viremia in the peripheral blood after the first tab-cel infusion (most likely because of viral shedding) alongside worsening of clinical symptoms due to mass effect of the PTLD manifestation in the pelvic floor, which was counteracted with the addition of a short course of ibrutinib before gradually going into remission. Another patient (LYG with pulmonary involvement) developed worsening of cough and oxygen saturation, possibly related to local tumor pseudoprogression, but once again there were other potential causative factors present for the patient’s clinical symptoms. The mentioned toxicities, besides being questionably associated with tab-cel treatment, were mostly low-grade and manageable without irreversible sequelae. Tab-cel was not postponed or interrupted because of the adverse events.

Discussion

To date, published experience has mainly consisted of comparative studies between the ALLELE trial cohort and a retrospective chart review study (RS002), in which patients received a range of heterogeneous therapies for R/R PTLD,8 or combined cohorts from other studies (eg, the analysis of CNS-PTLD9 studies), all in which the tab-cel–treated group was selected under controlled trial conditions. Given the stringent eligibility criteria of clinical trials, the generalizability of these findings to broader patient populations remains limited. To our knowledge, this is the first analysis to present robust real-world data from a cohort treated with tab-cel outside of the constraints of clinical trials.

Because EBV+ lymphomas/lymphoma-like diseases typically develop as a result of impaired immune surveillance of EBV (even in the absence of an iatrogenic context such as the post-transplant setting), there is a strong rationale for using tab-cel in other EBV-associated IDD-LPDs. This approach aims to restore immune control over EBV-driven lymphoproliferation by introducing a robust, EBV-specific T-cell repertoire. Issues with concurrent viremias observed in our patients reflect the severe degree of immunosuppression in this group. Restoration of effective immunosurveillance is therefore essential, not only to control EBV-driven lymphoproliferation but also to manage other viral reactivations.

The presence of type III EBV latency, characterized by the expression of all latent genes, offers the strongest target spectrum in most IDD-associated LPDs, including patients with inborn errors of immunity.10 This latency profile is commonly observed in PTLD and IDD-LPDs, and is considered highly immunogenic because of the presence of immunodominant viral antigens in the tumor.11 The similar biology and high immunogenicity of IDD-associated LPDs in this regard offer a common therapeutic rationale for the use of CTLs. These diseases therefore represent optimal targets for tab-cel, which especially recognizes and targets this latency profile.12 In comparison, type II latency involves limited viral antigen expression and reduced immunogenicity. Future studies may enable stratification of patients based on predicted responses to immunotherapies informed by EBV latency patterns. In addition, emerging therapeutic strategies such as immune checkpoint inhibition (ICI) and epigenetic modulation may help overcome poor immunogenicity, potentially rendering them more susceptible to immune-based targeting (with the potential risk of GVHD, especially in combination with allogeneic CTLs and/or after alloHCT or organ rejection after SOT).13

The use of (third-party) CTLs in this setting dates back more than 2 decades, with the first report by Haque et al in 2002.14 However, with the recent development of strategies incorporating BTKi15 or antibody-drug conjugates,16 the optimal therapy (sequence) for patients with PTLD (and other EBV-LPDs), particularly in the R/R setting, remains unclear.17 The role of tab-cel as a recently approved immunotherapy has to be refined for optimal integration into the treatment landscape, considering potential obstacles such as the limited availability of multiple HLA-compatible tab-cel lots (if needed), active GVHD, and other tab-cel-related or -unrelated complications.

Recent reviews, such as those by Atallah-Yunes et al,18 have explored emerging therapies for PTLD. Other strategies being investigated include the use of brentuximab vedotin (because the majority of PTLDs express CD3019) in combination with EBV-specific CTLs20 (other than tab-cel; despite concerns about their efficacy in attenuation, as noted in the tab-cel SmPC) or the combination of ICI and chemotherapy in EBV+ DLBCL.21 Given the aggressive course of the disease, rapid intervention is critical in the management of R/R EBV-LPDs, especially PTLD. In many cases, bridging therapy for temporary tumor control or even cytotoxic treatment overlapping/concomitant to CTLs, is indicated to enable completion of the logistical process of tab-cel procurement and to allow for the development of its immunological effects, as responses may take several weeks. Despite short brain-to-vein times (with the shortest time in our cohort being 10 days), combination therapy of tab-cel with other LPD treatment modalities was inevitable in 3 of 11 patients (27%). Therefore, it remains challenging to determine the extent to which the observed response can be attributed solely to the immunological effects of tab-cel. Although this inherent limitation may restrict the ability to draw definitive conclusions about the isolated impact of tab-cel monotherapy, the treatment strategies employed in our patients reflect real-world management of patients with R/R PTLD/IDD-LPD.

Tab-cel has shown the ability to support EBV-specific immune reconstitution; however, sustained control of EBV-driven lymphoproliferation is not consistently achieved, as disease progression or relapse occurred in the majority of patients despite initial responses (including 1 patient with EBV˗ PTLD relapse). However, the survival rate remained surprisingly high, possibly because of successful subsequent treatments. In contrast to earlier reports by Mahadeo et al,3 updated data from the ALLELE trial revealed a progression rate of ∼30% at 12 months,22 which is still lower but more consistent with our real-world observations. The discrepancy between our results and published studies may be due to incomplete adherence to SmPC dosing recommendations, particularly the omission of consolidation cycles of tab-cel after remission induction, driven by cost reimbursement issues, patient preferences, or unavailability of switch lots. The optimal number of treatment cycles is yet to be defined, and it also remains unclear whether tab-cel may, in some cases, serve more effectively as a bridge to consolidation rather than as definitive monotherapy. Successful use of anti-CD19 CAR T-cell therapy has been demonstrated in patients with PTLD23 and other IDD-LPDs,24 whereas alloHCT has already been performed in selected patients with R/R LYG.25 Although alloHCT as consolidation is not feasible in the majority of patients, CAR T-cell therapy, ICI, or T-cell–engaging bispecific antibodies might be effective consolidation strategies for long-term disease control. However, these approaches require caution, as the use of T-cell–depleting regimens in preparative therapies and efficacy of T-cell–based therapies may be hampered because of the immunosuppressed state of transplant recipients.

Although partial HLA matching is often sufficient, clinical outcomes correlate with HLA matching quality.26 Among responders in our cohort, a trend toward higher numbers of HLA allele matches between the chosen tab-cel lot and the respective patient was noted. Prockop et al26 hypothesized that there may be an advantage in selecting CTLs restricted by >1 HLA allele shared by the patient’s disease (in contrast to consideration of the sheer number of matching HLA alleles). In a proportion of patients, for whom a tab-cel lot switch was considered, the switch could not be performed because of the unavailability of an alternative matching lot, which may have impacted outcomes by withholding potential responses that, though hypothetically, might have occurred with a differently matched lot. The Institute for Clinical and Economic Review recommended in an evidence report on the effectiveness and value of tab-cel that the manufacturer should aim to include less common HLA types in tab-cel banks, particularly those prevalent among underrepresented minorities, and that research into expanded clinical applications and the optimal positioning of tab-cel in EBV+ PTLD treatment should be pursued.27

The latter issue is especially important, as the initial response to tab-cel is of decisive significance. Tab-cel expansion kinetics, as a putative early response marker, were not assessed in real-world clinical practice, and it remains unclear whether they correlate with treatment response.3,28 Achieving an early and deep response may provide a therapeutic window for subsequent consolidation strategies, including additional tab-cel cycles or other immunotherapies. In these situations, an already preemptive approach in patients with rituximab-refractory EBV viremia—even without radiological evidence of lymphomatous disease or histological diagnosis of LPD—may be advantageous. The use of tab-cel in patients with high-risk constellations for developing PTLD (eg, those undergoing haploidentical transplantation with bone marrow as the stem cell source and discordant donor/recipient EBV serostatus29) may be considered in earlier lines of therapy, particularly when RIS is not feasible and recovery of the patient’s own T-cell immunity is unlikely. This rationale similarly applies to patients with other EBV-associated IDD-LPDs or EBV-driven nonhematologic malignancies, in whom early intervention with allogeneic CTLs may offer clinical benefit once a suboptimal response to first-line therapy becomes evident, given that intrinsic immunological control over EBV-driven (lympho)proliferation is not expected to recover within a foreseeable timeframe.

The ongoing phase 2 EBVision trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04554914) is evaluating tab-cel across a broader spectrum of EBV-associated malignancies, including EBV-LPDs beyond PTLD and nonlymphomatous diseases such as EBV-associated sarcomas. Prior studies have demonstrated impressive efficacy in (in-label treated) patients with CNS-PTLD (eg, Baiocchi et al9 and Prockop et al30), and Grześk et al31 have reported the successful (off-label) use of tab-cel in rare diseases such as hydroa vacciniforme-like LPD. Our findings already support the feasibility and preliminary efficacy of tab-cel use beyond its current approval label.

As with all retrospective cohort studies, our results should be interpreted in light of inherent limitations, including potential confounding from concomitant therapies and a relatively small sample size, which limits subgroup survival comparisons. The cohort analyzed included all known tab-cel–treated patients in the DACH region until July 2025, representing a meaningful contribution to the limited real-world data in this rare clinical context.

In conclusion, tab-cel, as the first approved treatment for patients with R/R PTLD, demonstrates efficacy and safety in a real-world setting and shows promise for broader application in EBV-associated (IDD-) LPDs. Although promising outcomes have been observed in a subset of patients, further optimization is needed. Future research should prioritize the development of tools for prediction of response to CTL, refinement of HLA matching to enhance antiviral efficacy, and the integration of tab-cel into the broader treatment landscape of EBV-driven diseases, including strategies following tab-cel consolidation.
