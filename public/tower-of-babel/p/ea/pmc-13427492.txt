Multiplex PCR for the differentiation of Simulium asakoae and molecular detection of pathogens in the S. asakoae species group in Thailand
Authors: Kittipat Aupalee, Wichai Srisuka, Hiroyuki Takaoka, Rutchanee Rodpai, Megan Olivia Jones, Kevin Charoenvilaisiri, Jassada Saingamsook, Kanchon Pusawang, Atiporn Saeung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA human-biting black fly, Simulium (Gomphostilbia) asakoae Takaoka & Davies, 1995, together with certain other members of the S. asakoae species group, is of medical and veterinary importance as a vector of various pathogens. However, accurate species identification remains challenging due to the high morphological similarity among related members and the limitations of cox1 barcoding. To address this challenge, this study developed a multiplex PCR assay based on the cox1 gene to differentiate S. asakoae. In addition, diverse pathogens circulating in the S. asakoae species group collected from Chiang Mai Province, northern Thailand, were molecularly detected. The multiplex PCR assay was developed using two allele-specific forward primers combined with universal cox1 primers as an internal control. This assay demonstrated 100% sensitivity and specificity, successfully differentiating S. asakoae from other Thai-related species in the same species group. Pathogen screening of 100 individual specimens and 20 pooled samples of the S. asakoae species group revealed the highest infection rate for Leucocytozoon (14% in individuals and 8.5% minimum infection rate (MIR) in pools), followed by trypanosomatids (10% in individuals and 4% MIR in pools) and filarial nematodes (5% in individuals and 2.5% MIR in pools). Molecular analysis further confirmed the first detection of Leucocytozoon sabrazesi, Trypanosomatidae spp. (including Leptomonas spiculata), Onchocerca sp. Type I, and a possible new Onchocerca sp. in the S. asakoae species group in Thailand. To the best of our knowledge, this study provides the first multiplex PCR assay for the rapid and correct identification of S. asakoae and confirms the circulation of diverse pathogens in the S. asakoae species group, thereby supporting enhanced surveillance and vector control programmes in Thailand.Keywords: Black fly, DNA barcoding, Filarial nematodes, Leucocytozoon, Multiplex PCR, Trypanosomatids, Vector-borne pathogens

Introduction

Black flies (Diptera: Simuliidae) are a significant group of biting insects, widely recognized as the third most important arthropod vectors of disease agents worldwide (Adler and McCreadie, 2019). They include over 2400 formally named species, though only about 10–20% of these are considered as important pests affecting humans and animals (Adler, 2025). Several species (≥ 26 species) are regarded as vectors of Onchocerca volvulus, the causative agent of human onchocerciasis or river blindness, the greatest public health problem associated with black flies (Adler and McCreadie, 2019). Black flies also transmit blood protozoans of the genus Leucocytozoon to avian hosts (domestic ducks, geese, and turkeys), causing leucocytozoonosis, which is known colloquially as turkey malaria, duck malaria, or gnat fever (Adler and McCreadie, 2019). Additionally, other pathogens of veterinary importance, such as the protozoans Trypanosoma spp., vesicular stomatitis virus, and filarial nematodes (Splendidofilaria fallisensis and Dirofilaria ursi), are transmitted by certain species of simuliids (Smith et al., 2009; Adler and McCreadie, 2019). Detection of pathogens in wild-caught female black flies thus plays a key role in identifying natural vectors and understanding disease epidemiology (Reeves et al., 2007).

The black fly fauna of Thailand is well-documented with at least 149 named species, all belonging to the genus Simulium Latreille (Thongsahuan et al., 2025). To date, seven species have been reported as feeding on humans: S. asakoae, S. chamlongi Takaoka & Suzuki, 1984, S. doipuiense Takaoka & Choochote, 2005 (complex), S. nigrogilvum Summers, 1911, S. nodosum Puri, 1933, S. tenebrosum Takaoka, Srisuka & Saeung, 2018 (complex), and S. umphangense Takaoka, Srisuka & Saeung, 2017 (Takaoka et al., 2017a, 2019). Recent molecular studies on host blood meal sources further confirmed additional species that bite humans, including S. chumpornense Takaoka & Kuvangkadilok, 2000, S. khelangense Takaoka, Srisuka & Saeung, 2022, and members of the S. striatum species group (Gomontean et al., 2024; Namtaku et al., 2025). Furthermore, three black fly species, namely S. asakoae, S. nodosum, and S. nigrogilvum, have been reported as natural vectors of various species of filarial and non-filarial nematodes (Fukuda et al., 2003; Takaoka et al., 2003; Ishii et al., 2008; Aupalee et al., 2020; Saeung et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2021). More recently, molecular detection of blood protozoan parasites found that S. chumpornense, S. khelangense, and members of the S. asakoae species group are potentially natural vectors of Leucocytozoon spp. and Trypanosoma avium (Jumpato et al., 2019, 2024; Thaijarern et al., 2019; Pramual et al., 2020; Stangarm et al., 2024). Given the role of black flies in transmitting various pathogens, fast and correct species identification is essential for understanding the epidemiology of vector-borne diseases and developing effective vector control strategies (Yssouf et al., 2016).

Simulium asakoae, originally described from Peninsular Malaysia, belongs to the S. asakoae species group comprising at least 71 valid species of the subgenus Gomphostilbia (Takaoka and Davies, 1995; Adler, 2025). Due to its medical and veterinary importance as biting humans and animals (e.g. chickens) and serving as a potential vector for the transmission of various pathogens (unidentified filarial nematodes and blood protozoans Trypanosoma spp. and Leucocytozoon spp.), S. asakoae has gained significant attention in recent years (Fukuda et al., 2003; Choochote et al., 2005; Ishii et al., 2008; Thaijarern et al., 2019; Aupalee et al., 2020; Pramual et al., 2020; Gomontean et al., 2024; Izwan-Anas et al., 2024; Jumpato et al., 2024; Stangarm et al., 2024; Wannasingha et al., 2026). The identification of female S. asakoae is particularly difficult due to the overlap of certain morphological characteristics with other members of the same species group. Furthermore, recent studies in Thailand (Aupalee et al., 2024) and Vietnam (Putt et al., 2023) have revealed that the cox1 barcode is not an effective genetic marker for species identification of S. asakoae. Consequently, alternative identification methods need to be developed and implemented in the vector control programmes, especially in the areas where other species members of the S. asakoae species group are known to coexist with S. asakoae (Low et al., 2020).

In addition to molecular identification based on the cox1 barcode, multiplex PCR is a powerful tool that has been used successfully to identify insect vector species in several groups (Jangra et al., 2020; Wilai et al., 2020; Dähn et al., 2023; Hrazdilova et al., 2023). This technique relies on the simultaneous amplification of two or more target sequences using multiple primers, enabling the detection of several species in a single reaction (Markoulatos et al., 2002). Although multiplex PCR is simple, fast, inexpensive, and reliable compared with DNA sequencing (Wilai et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2023), it has unfortunately never been used in the identification of black fly species.

In this study, a single-step multiplex PCR assay based on the cox1 gene was developed for the first time to differentiate S. asakoae from other related species in the S. asakoae species group in Thailand. Additionally, important groups of pathogens (filarial nematodes and blood protozoan parasites) circulating in wild-caught female black flies of the S. asakoae species group were molecularly detected.

Materials and methods

Development of multiplex PCR

Black fly samples and morphological identification

Larvae, adults reared from pupae, and wild females of the S. asakoae species group collected from various locations in Thailand were used in this study (Supplementary Table S1). Wild females were captured using an insect net while flying around a human bait. They were then killed instantly by immersion in a 15-ml centrifuge tube containing 80% ethanol. Larvae and pupae were manually collected from available substrates (e.g. trailing grasses, fallen leaves, plant roots, stones) in streams using fine forceps. The larvae were fixed immediately in 80% ethanol after the collection, while the pupae were reared to adults and were later fixed in 80% ethanol. Due to the high morphological similarity among species within the S. asakoae species group, identification to species level is extremely difficult, and in some cases, impossible. Thus, all preserved specimens were morphologically identified to the species group level under a stereomicroscope Olympus SZ51 (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) using the standard identification keys for Thai black flies (Takaoka et al., 2019). Subsequently, they were stored in a freezer (−20 °C) until further molecular analysis.

DNA extraction and cox1 gene amplification

Either the thoraxes of adult black flies or abdominal segments 1–5 of larvae were used for DNA extraction. Briefly, the specimens in 200 μl lysis buffer containing Proteinase K were homogenized using a sterile pestle. The ground specimens were digested by incubating in a hot box incubator at 65 °C for at least 2 h. Then, the lysate was purified using GF-1 Nucleic Acid DNA Extraction Kit (Vivantis Technologies Sdn. Bhd., Selangor, Malaysia), according to the manufacturer’s protocol with some modifications. The purified DNA obtained from each sample was stored at −20 °C until use.

A 658-bp cox1 gene fragment was amplified using the universal primers LCO1490 and HCO2198 (Folmer et al., 1994). Each PCR reaction with a total volume of 20 μl contained 2 μl of DNA template, 1 U of Taq DNA polymerase, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.2 mM dNTPs, 0.2 μM of each primer, and nuclease-free water to reach the final volume. The PCR cycling conditions included: an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 2 min followed by 40 cycles (94 °C for 30 s, 50 °C for 45 s, and 72 °C for 45 s), with a final extension step at 72 °C for 5 min.

After PCR amplification, the amplified products were subjected to electrophoresis on 1.5% agarose gel, pre-stained with RedSafe (Intron Biotechnology, Seongnam, South Korea), and 100 bps DNA marker (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used as standard. All PCR-positive samples with an expected band of ∼700 bp were sent to First BASE, Selangor, Malaysia for purifying and sequencing.

DNA sequence and phylogenetic analysis

Black fly specimens morphologically assigned to the S. asakoae species group were further identified to the species level (closest species) using cox1 barcoding, followed by phylogenetic analysis. The specimens were tentatively divided into two main groups: true S. asakoae and non-S. asakoae. Specifically, specimens were classified as S. asakoae when their cox1 sequences clustered within the reference S. asakoae clade and exhibited an intraspecific divergence of less than 2.27% (Low et al., 2020). The remaining specimens that met the following criteria were classified as non-S. asakoae: (i) they showed identical or high sequence similarity (≥ 99%) to reference sequences of the S. asakoae species group, excluding S. asakoae; and (ii) they fell into a clade containing reference sequences for species of the S. asakoae species group but not S. asakoae.

Raw sequence data from a Sanger sequencing comprising both forward and reverse reads were assembled and manually edited to generate consensus sequences using Geneious Prime v.2025.0.2 (Kearse et al., 2012). Each consensus sequence generated was compared with available sequences deposited in GenBank using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST), available at https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi.

Newly generated sequences and others retrieved from GenBank were aligned with MUSCLE 3.7 under default parameters (Edgar, 2004) and trimmed to remove ragged ends in Geneious Prime v.2025.0.2 (Kearse et al., 2012). Then, genetic distances were estimated using the Kimura 2-parameter (K2P) model, implemented in MEGA 12 (Kimura, 1980; Kumar et al., 2024).

Phylogenetic analysis was performed using the neighbor-joining (NJ) and maximum likelihood (ML) methods. The NJ tree based on the K2P model was built in MEGA 12 with 1000 bootstrap replicates (Saitou and Nei, 1987; Kumar et al., 2024). The ML tree was constructed using IQ-TREE v.3.0.1 (Nguyen et al., 2015) with nodal support assessed through 10,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates (Hoang et al., 2018). The optimal substitution model for ML analysis (indicated in figure legends) was determined based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) using the ModelFinder integrated in IQ-TREE (Kalyaanamoorthy et al., 2017). All the trees generated were visualized and rerooted with FigTree version 1.4.4, freely available to download at http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/.

Primer design

Allele-specific primers were designed using the validated cox1 sequences from previous studies (Low et al., 2015, 2020; Saeung et al., 2017; Takaoka et al., 2017b, 2020; Srisuka et al., 2019, 2021, 2022), deposited in the NCBI GenBank database. In total, 155 cox1 sequences of 35 species of the S. asakoae species group from Thailand and neighboring countries (Malaysia and Myanmar) were used for the primer design (Supplementary Table S2). Unique haplotype sequences of all species were aligned using the MUSCLE algorithm (Edgar, 2004), and then primers were manually designed within Geneious Prime v.2025.0.2 (Kearse et al., 2012). To enhance specificity and reduce nonspecific amplification, the mismatch technique (Okimoto and Dodgson, 1996) was employed by positioning the mismatches in the allele-specific primers at the third nucleotide from the 3′-end. Additionally, the melting temperatures of each primer designed were kept similar to each other so that they could be combined easily in a multiplex PCR.

Multiplex PCR and validation

Initially, the multiplex PCR was developed following the standard protocol used for the cox1 gene amplification as described in Section 2.1.2 above. Subsequently, the multiplex PCR was further optimized to achieve robust amplification. The optimization involved adjusting several parameters, including salts of PCR buffer, primer concentrations, annealing time and temperature, and MgCl2 concentration. Results of the newly developed multiplex PCR were validated by comparison with the cox1 barcoding using specimens previously identified as members of the S. asakoae species group (Supplementary Table S1). These specimens included multiple life stages (larvae, adult males, and adult females) collected from various locations across Thailand.

Molecular detection of pathogens

Black fly collection and morphological identification

Field collection of adult female black flies was conducted at Pang-Bong village, Thepsadej sub-District, Doi Saket District, Chiang Mai Province, Thailand (18°59′4″N 99°20′6″E, 990 m above sea level) on 4 September 2023. This village is situated in a mountainous area adjacent to a forest, where several breeding sites for immature black flies are observed and where a variety of potential blood-meal hosts for adult females are available, including domestic animals (chickens, cattle, cats, dogs, and pigs) and wildlife (birds, deer, and boars). All field-collected black flies were captured, killed, identified, and preserved as described in Section 2.1.1 above.

DNA extraction and molecular pathogen screening

Due to the high abundance of black flies collected during the study, a subset of flies was randomly selected for genomic DNA extraction. Only the head and thorax of each fly were used for DNA isolation, either as pooled samples of 10 specimens (n = 20; AS10-P1 to AS10-P20) or individual samples (n = 100; AS1-F1 to AS1-F100). DNA isolation was performed following the procedures described in Section 2.1.2 above.

The extracted DNA from each black fly sample was then used for screening of medically and veterinary important pathogens, including filarial nematodes and blood protozoan parasites (Leucocytozoon spp. and trypanosomatids). The screening of each pathogen was performed using the specific primer sets listed in Table 1.

PCR amplification and cycling conditions were performed following the protocols described previously with some modifications (Fukuda et al., 2010; Jumpato et al., 2019; Songumpai et al., 2022).

First, all samples were screened for filarial nematode DNA using the primers COIintF and COIintR, targeting the mitochondrial cox1 gene (Casiraghi et al., 2001). PCR amplification was conducted using PCR master mix (20 μl) containing 1 μl of DNA template, 1 U of Taq DNA polymerase, 3 mM of MgCl2, 0.2 mM dNTPs, and 0.2 μM of each primer. Thermal conditions were as follows: an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by five cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min and 37 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 48 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min (Fukuda et al., 2010).

For detection of Leucocytozoon spp. DNA, all samples were screened using nested PCR, targeting the mitochondrial cytochrome b (cytb) gene (Hellgren et al., 2004). The first PCR was conducted in a final volume of 20 μl containing 1 μl of DNA template, 1 U of Taq DNA polymerase, 3 mM of MgCl2, 0.2 mM dNTPs, and 0.2 μM of each primer (HaemNFI and HaemNR3). The thermal profile was composed of denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of 94 °C denaturation for 30 s, 50 °C annealing for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s, and a final extension step at 72 °C for 10 min. Then, the second PCR was performed using the primers HaemFL and HaemR2L-mod. PCR master mix and thermal profile are identical to the first PCR. Each 2 μl of the first PCR was used as a template for the second PCR. Notably, in this study, the modified reverse primer HaemR2L-mod was used in combination with the original forward primer HaemFL for the second PCR. The modification was required due to the failure of the original primer pair (HaemFL and HaemR2L) to amplify positive DNA of Leucocytozoon sabrazesi during optimization of the second PCR, despite producing a positive band in the first PCR. Examination of the primer pair used in the second PCR identified a mismatch at the 3′-end of the reverse primer (HaemR2L) with the sequence of L. sabrazesi, which was determined as the cause of amplification failure. To resolve this issue, the original reverse primer of the second PCR was modified and subsequently employed for the detection of Leucocytozoon DNA in this study.

For detection of trypanosomatid DNA, all samples were screened using nested PCR, targeting 18S rRNA gene (Noyes et al., 1999). The first PCR was performed in a total volume of 20 μl, comprising 1 μl of DNA template, 1 U of Taq DNA polymerase, 1.5 mM of MgCl2, 0.2 mM dNTPs, and 0.2 μM of each primer (TRY927F and TRY927R). The cycling profile consisted of denaturation at 94 °C for 10 min, followed by 35 cycles (94 °C denaturation for 30 s, 57 °C annealing for 1 min, and 72 °C for 1 min), and a final extension step at 72 °C for 10 min (Songumpai et al., 2022). Then, the second PCR was performed using the primers SSU561F and SSU561R (Cai et al., 2019). PCR master mix and thermal profile are identical to the first PCR. Each 2 μl of the first PCR was used as a template for the second PCR.

Each PCR run included both negative (nuclease-free water) and positive controls to ensure the validity of the PCR results. DNA templates of known parasites consisting of Brugia malayi, L. sabrazesi, and T. avium were used as the positive controls for the detection of filarial nematodes, Leucocytozoon spp., and trypanosomatids, respectively.

After PCR amplification, the amplified products were checked, purified, and sequenced following the methods described in Section 2.1.2 above.

Black fly species identification via multiplex PCR and cox1 barcoding

Initially, all 100 individual black flies of the S. asakoae species group used for molecular pathogen detection were identified as either S. asakoae or non-S. asakoae using the newly developed multiplex PCR, following the protocol described in Section 3.1.3 below; cox1 barcoding was then performed to definitively confirm the species identity of each infected fly. The same DNA samples from the previous step were subsequently used in this analysis. The cox1 gene fragment was amplified, checked, purified, and sequenced following the methods described in Section 2.1.2.

Molecular analysis of detected pathogens and infected black flies

The detected pathogens and infected black flies were molecularly analyzed following the detailed procedures described above (Section 2.1.3). Briefly, newly generated consensus sequences of each pathogen and infected black fly were compared with reference sequences deposited in GenBank. Sequences obtained in this study and those retrieved from the database were then aligned, trimmed, and used for phylogenetic tree reconstruction based on the NJ and ML methods. Details of the phylogenetic models and outgroups used for rooting the trees were specified in each figure legend.

Results

Development of multiplex PCR

Black fly specimens, BLAST search, and phylogenetic analysis

In total, 120 specimens of the S. asakoae species group collected from various locations in Thailand were used for validating the newly developed multiplex PCR in this study (Supplementary Table S1). These specimens were molecularly identified based on cox1 barcoding. One hundred specimens were obtained in this study, while twenty specimens were retrieved from previous studies (Srisuka et al., 2021, 2022; Aupalee et al., 2024), including members of the S. asakoae species group (n = 10), S. chiangdaoense (n = 4), S. kiewlomense (n = 3), and S. mokroense (n = 3).

Sequence analysis identified 52 cox1 sequences as unique haplotypes (HT1-HT52). BLAST search found that 13 haplotypes (45 specimens) were identical or similar to S. asakoae, while the remaining 39 haplotypes (75 specimens) showed high similarity to several members of the S. asakoae species group (Supplementary Table S3).

Phylogenetic analysis recovered the 52 cox1 haplotypes as several different species of the S. asakoae species group, which were tentatively divided into two main groups including S. asakoae and non-S. asakoae based on the criteria defined previously (Section 2.1.3) (Fig. 1). Sequences for isolates of S. asakoae did not form a monophyletic clade, as they clustered with other species of the S. asakoae species group from Vietnam, namely S. chaudinhense, S. hongthaii, and S. vinhphucense (Fig. 1).

Primer design

Two specific forward primers, including ASK-F1M and ASK-F2M were newly designed based on the cox1 alignment (586 bp in length), which were specific to S. asakoae of the S. asakoae species group (Fig. 2). A single mismatch at the third nucleotide from the 3′-end primers was also applied to both newly designed primers to enhance specificity. Additionally, the universal cox1 primers, LCO1490 and HCO2198 (Folmer et al., 1994), were included as internal control for the multiplex PCR. Multiplex PCR

The two newly designed specific forward primers combined with the universal cox1 primers enabled simultaneous differentiation of S. asakoae from other related species in the S. asakoae species group in Thailand. This multiplex PCR generated one or two specific bands and one common band (internal control) in gel electrophoresis based on species identity of black fly specimens (S. asakoae or non-S. asakoae). Specifically, S. asakoae showed specific bands of 262 bp, 463 bp, or both, while other related species in the S. asakoae species group (non-S. asakoae) showed only a common band of 709 bp (Fig. 3). For the optimized multiplex PCR assay, each 20 μl reaction contained 1 μl of DNA template, 1× Taq Buffer with (NH4)2SO4, 1 U of Taq DNA polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), 2 mM MgCl2, 0.2 mM dNTPs, and 0.05–0.3 μM of each primer (0.3 μM LCO1490, 0.2 μM HCO2198, 0.05 μM ASK-F1M, and 0.1 μM ASK-F2M). The optimal PCR cycling parameters consisted of an initial 2-min denaturation at 94 °C, followed by 40 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s (denaturation), 52 °C for 15 s (annealing), and 72 °C for 30 s (extension), with a final 5-min extension step at 72 °C.

Validation

The multiplex PCR was validated using a total of 120 specimens of the S. asakoae species group (Supplementary Table S1). These specimens were molecularly identified through DNA barcoding and phylogenetic analysis as S. asakoae (n = 45) and S. asakoae species group (non-S. asakoae; n = 75). All specimens were successfully amplified by the multiplex PCR, producing distinct bands as shown in Fig. 3, which were fully consistent with the cox1 sequencing analysis. Most S. asakoae specimens (37/45) yielded PCR products with three bands (709 bp, 463 bp, and 262 bp), while a few specimens produced two bands (709 bp and 262 bp or 709 bp and 463 bp). Furthermore, only a 709 bp fragment of the cox1 gene was obtained for the S. asakoae species group (non-S. asakoae). These results demonstrate high sensitivity (100%) and specificity (100%) of the newly developed multiplex PCR.

Molecular detection of pathogens

Pathogens detected in the S. asakoae species group

The infection rate of each pathogen in pooled and individual samples of the S. asakoae species group varied, with Leucocytozoon showing the highest detection rate, followed by trypanosomatids and filarial nematodes, as summarized in Table 3. Among 20 pooled samples (10 specimens/pool) analyzed, 5, 8, and 17 pools were positive for filarial nematodes, trypanosomatids, and Leucocytozoon, respectively. Minimum infection rate (MIR) of the pooled samples, calculated by dividing the number of positive pools by the total number of specimens tested (Cowling et al., 1999), was 2.5% for filarial nematodes, 4% for trypanosomatids, and 8.5% for Leucocytozoon.

Discussion

Development of multiplex PCR

To date, multiplex PCR has been successfully applied for species identification of several insect vectors, such as mosquitoes (Wilai et al., 2020; Karan et al., 2024), ticks (Varv et al., 2017; Hrazdilova et al., 2023), biting midges (Dähn et al., 2024), sand flies (Giantsis et al., 2017), and thrips (Jangra et al., 2020). To the best of our knowledge, the present study developed the first multiplex PCR to differentiate S. asakoae from other related species within the S. asakoae species group in Thailand, addressing the lack of a rapid and accurate tool for identifying this black fly species. The newly developed multiplex PCR demonstrated great performance with 100% sensitivity and specificity, producing distinctive banding patterns that clearly distinguished S. asakoae (262 bp, 463 bp, or both specific bands) from non-S. asakoae species (709 bp common band only). This assay offers several advantages over traditional identification methods, including being rapid, cost-effective, and requiring minimal equipment compared to DNA sequencing, making it suitable for routine use in vector control programmes and entomological research (Edwards and Gibbs, 1994). The perfect sensitivity and specificity achieved during the validation demonstrate the robustness of the primer design and the effectiveness of the mismatch technique, with deliberately introduced mismatches at the antepenultimate (−3) positions from the 3′-end employed to enhance specificity (Newton et al., 1989; Cha et al., 1992; Wilkins et al., 2006). The comprehensive validation using specimens from multiple life stages, collection sites, and specimens with high genetic variation further strengthens confidence in the assay’s reliability. The strategic design incorporates universal cox1 primers as internal control, allowing for the monitoring of the DNA extraction and amplification process, as well as helping to rule out false negative results (Chanu et al., 2022). The inclusion of universal cox1 primers in the multiplex PCR also enables direct sequencing for specimens that are identified as members of the S. asakoae species group (non-S. asakoae). Given the high genetic diversity observed within S. asakoae, two allele-specific reverse primers were designed and included in the multiplex PCR to reduce amplification failure caused by primer-template mismatches at the 3′-end, which are known to strongly impair DNA polymerase extension (Kwok and Higuchi, 1989; Bru et al., 2008). This design strategy ensures successful amplification across genetically variable populations of S. asakoae, thereby enhancing both the reliability and diagnostic utility of the assay.

Unfortunately, at the time the allele-specific primers were designed, no cox1 sequences of the S. asakoae species group from Vietnam were available in the NCBI GenBank database for inclusion in the primer design process. Consequently, the multiplex PCR developed in this study may have limited applicability for identifying S. asakoae populations from Vietnam. Several species of the S. asakoae species group reported from Vietnam (S. chaudinhense, S. vinhphucense, and S. hongthaii) exhibit very low interspecific genetic divergence compared with S. asakoae and even share identical bases at the 3′-ends of the two allele-specific forward primers, which could result in non-specific primer binding to the target sites (Putt et al., 2023). Future primer design should therefore incorporate Vietnamese sequence data to improve the assay’s specificity and broader regional applicability.

Molecular detection of pathogens

This study represents a comprehensive investigation combining pathogen detection, phylogenetic analysis, and vector identification to better understand the complex relationships between black flies and their associated pathogens. The molecular detection of pathogens revealed important insights into the diversity and prevalence of filarial nematodes and blood protozoa Leucocytozoon and trypanosomatids, all of which are potentially transmitted by certain members of the S. asakoae species group in Chiang Mai Province, northern Thailand. These findings are consistent with the broad host-feeding behavior of the members of the S. asakoae species group, which have been reported to utilize both avian and mammalian hosts (Gomontean et al., 2024). Furthermore, the presence of these blood parasites in the S. asakoae species group is supported by previous parasitological studies near the study area (Chiang Mai Province), which detected microfilaria, Haemoproteus spp., Leucocytozoon spp. (including L. sabrazesi and L. caulleryi), and Trypanosoma spp. in backyard chickens, wild birds, and captive birds (Takang et al., 2017; Buranapim et al., 2019).

Detection of pathogen DNA in the head and thorax of black flies provides stronger evidence of vector-pathogen association than detection from whole-body or abdominal samples, as these body regions exclude most recently ingested blood meals and may contain parasites that have migrated from the midgut to other tissues (Khanzadeh et al., 2023). However, in black flies and other hematophagous insects, successful transmission generally requires parasite survival and development within the vector, often involving migration through the midgut and subsequent localization in the thoracic tissues, mouthparts, or salivary glands (Adler and McCreadie, 2019). Therefore, detecting pathogen DNA in the head and thorax of black flies in this study implies that the pathogens persisted beyond the initial blood-feeding event and may have undergone at least partial development within the flies. Although molecular detection alone does not confirm vector competence or transmission, these findings support the hypothesis that certain members of the S. asakoae species group may serve as potential vectors for the detected pathogens. Furthermore, the S. asakoae species group comprises multiple closely related species that differ in their geographical distribution, ecology, host preferences, and potentially their vector competence (Takaoka et al., 2020; Adler, 2025). Consequently, while parasites may be acquired by several species during blood feeding, successful development and transmission may occur only in specific members of the species group. A comparable pattern has been documented in the S. damnosum complex, which comprises more than 80 valid and distinct cytoforms in Yemen and Africa (Adler, 2025). Members of this complex exhibit substantial variation in their vectorial capacity for transmitting O. volvulus across different endemic regions (Hendy et al., 2018; Ayisi et al., 2025). More research, including dissection, experimental infections, and vector competence studies, is needed to prove the natural transmission capacity of potential members in the S. asakoae species group.

Based on cox1 barcoding followed by phylogenetic analysis, at least four members (S. nanthaburiense, S. myanmarense, S. asakoae, and S. pitasawatae) of the S. asakoae species group were identified in this study as potential vectors of filarial nematodes and/or blood protozoans. Similarly, previous studies using cox1 barcoding have shown that several members of the S. asakoae species group in Thailand often co-exist in the same areas (Doi Saket District), with some species naturally infected with both filarial and non-filarial nematodes (Aupalee et al., 2020, 2024). This observation is not unexpected, as females of most members of the S. asakoae species group share certain morphological characteristics that make species-level identification difficult or even impossible using morphology alone (Takaoka et al., 2020). Thus, an integrated approach is necessary to determine the species identity of black flies in this species group.

In this study, higher detection rates of pathogens were obtained from individual black fly samples compared with pooled samples, suggesting that pooling may have reduced the sensitivity of the PCR-based assays used. The most likely explanation is the dilution effect, in which pathogen DNA present in a single infected black fly became diluted when mixed with DNA from multiple uninfected individuals, making low-level infections more difficult to detect. Previous studies have shown that pooling can reduce PCR sensitivity, particularly when target concentrations are low or close to the detection threshold (Furstenau et al., 2020; Fracasso et al., 2023). Moreover, DNA isolated from pooled samples may concentrate more host tissue components and environmental contaminants, which can inhibit amplification and further reduce assay performance (Furstenau et al., 2020). Another explanation is the method used to calculate the infection rate of pooled samples in this study. Because MIR assumes only one infected individual per positive pool, it estimates the lower bound of prevalence. The higher the true prevalence, the more likely positive pools are to contain multiple infected individuals, causing the MIR to increasingly underestimate the actual prevalence (Fracasso et al., 2023). However, in vector surveillance, pathogen detection using pooled samples remains a highly useful and cost-effective approach for estimating and monitoring transmission in large sample sets, because it is primarily designed for population-level screening rather than for maximizing detection in each individual specimen (Guevara et al., 2003; Doherty et al., 2023).

Leucocytozoon was the most prevalent among the detected pathogens in the S. asakoae species group in this study. This finding aligns with previous studies in Thailand documenting Leucocytozoon as the most commonly detected blood protozoans in black fly vectors (Jumpato et al., 2019, 2024; Stangarm et al., 2024). Leucocytozoon diversity detected in this study was remarkable, with 14 unique haplotypes representing at least four distinct species, including L. sabrazesi, L. schoutedeni and two unidentified Leucocytozoon spp. Notably, L. sabrazesi, the most prevalent species, was detected for the first time in the S. asakoae species group, whose members primarily feed on avian hosts (ornithophily) (Gomontean et al., 2024). This result aligns with molecular detection of L. sabrazesi in chickens in Thailand, where 80.5% (252/313) of chickens tested positive for the parasite (Khumpim et al., 2021). Several previous studies have investigated the prevalence and diversity of Leucocytozoon in Thai black flies, including in the same study areas where L. sabrazesi was found, but no infections with this parasite were identified (Jumpato et al., 2019, 2024; Pramual et al., 2020; Stangarm et al., 2024). These earlier negative results were likely false negatives caused by amplification failure during the second round of nested PCR due to a mismatch at the 3′-end of the reverse primer, HaemR2L. By using a modified reverse primer, HaemR2L-mod, L. sabrazesi was successfully detected in this study. Due to the high genetic diversity among Leucocytozoon spp., molecular detection poses significant challenges, particularly in designing universal primers capable of amplifying DNA across diverse lineages. Therefore, primers used for detecting Lecocytozoon spp. need to be carefully chosen and validated for each host group when studying parasite diversity (Valkiūnas and Iezhova, 2023).

Among trypanosomatids detected, T. avium, a species known to be transmitted by black flies to avian hosts, was the most prevalent in this study. Likewise, previous studies found that several ornithophilic species in Thailand, including members of the S. asakoae species group, S. chumpornense, and S. khelangense, can potentially transmit T. avium (Thaijarern et al., 2019; Stangarm et al., 2024). These findings are consistent with experimental evidence showing successful transmission of T. avium by ornithophilic black flies, where parasites develop in the hindgut and are transmitted through ingestion or conjunctival contamination rather than direct bite transmission (Votýpka and Svobodová, 2004). Additionally, Leptomonas spiculata and one unidentified species within the subfamily Leishmaniinae were molecularly identified for the first time in black flies in this study. Leptomonas spiculata is a monoxenous trypanosomatid originally isolated from the intestines of Neotropical insects (Heteroptera) (Jirků et al., 2012). While no concrete evidence supports Leptomonas as a primary human pathogen, several studies over the past decade have demonstrated that Leptomonas seymouri can be detected in clinical samples from patients infected with Leishmania donovani in visceral and post-kala-azar dermal leishmaniasis (PKDL) (Thakur et al., 2020). This finding highlights an urgent need to elucidate the biology of Leptomonas co-infection with L. donovani and its possible role in disease outcomes. Additionally, detecting Leptomonas parasites in insects is intriguing because certain species of Leptomonas can influence the morphology, physiology, reproduction, and lifespan of the hosts (Vasconcellos et al., 2019). For an unidentified trypanosomatid, it is molecularly identical to Trypanosomatidae n. sp. 3 detected in the squash bee Eucera (Peponapis) pruinose (Jones et al., 2022), suggesting that this trypanosomatid can infect multiple species across host genera.

In this study, diverse species of filarial parasites were detected in both pooled and individual samples of members of the S. asakoae species group. In the pooled samples, all detected filarial nematodes were genetically identical to Filarioidea sp., previously reported from the S. asakoae species group collected near the present study site, indicating that this filarial parasite continues to circulate in local black fly populations (Aupalee et al., 2020). Likewise, a recent molecular study of filarial parasites in black flies from Laos supported the role of S. asakoae and S. khelangense as potential vectors of Filarioidea species (Wannasingha et al., 2026). Furthermore, this study molecularly detected Onchocerca sp. Type I for the first time in S. asakoae species group, suggesting a new potential vector of this parasite in Thailand. Onchocerca sp. Type I is an undescribed filarial nematode, firstly reported from Japan in S. arakawae, S. bidentatum, S. daisense and S. oitanum and later from Thailand in S. nodosum and S. nigrogilvum (Takaoka et al., 1992, 2003; Takaoka, 1994; Aupalee et al., 2020; Saeung et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2021). Current findings support that Onchocerca sp. Type I has a broader distribution and can be transmitted by various locally black fly species, implying that for controlling diseases like zoonotic onchocerciasis, multiple black fly species must be targeted, rather than focusing on a single vector. In addition to Onchocerca sp. Type I, another unknown Onchocerca sp. was molecularly detected from one specimen of the S. asakoae species group. Sequence comparisons using BLAST search followed by phylogenetic analysis supported that this filarial parasite represents a putative new species. Currently, there is no information on the definitive mammalian hosts of the detected Onchocerca, but phylogenetic evidence showing its close relationship to O. ramachandrini, originally described from the warthog (Phacochoerus aethiopicus) in Cameroon, suggests a possible association with suid hosts (Bain et al., 1993). It is possible that wild boars and domestic pigs may serve as reservoir and/or definitive hosts, as they are present in and around the study area. Several Onchocerca species associated with suids have been reported from Asia, including O. japonica, a parasite of wild boars in Japan that is transmitted by black flies and occasionally infects humans (Uni et al., 2001, 2020; Okazaki et al., 2022). Apart from suid-associated species, black flies are also recognized vectors of several Onchocerca spp., including O. lupi, primarily infecting domestic dogs and wild canids in Europe, North America, Africa, and Asia (Cambra-Pellejà et al., 2020). Since Onchocerca can infect diverse hosts including species of the Bovidae, Camelidae, Cervidae, Suidae, Canidae, Felidae, and Hominidae, the associated hosts of species within this genus should be inferred with caution and cannot be determined based solely on phylogenetic relationships (Kulpa et al., 2025). Further studies are required to clarify the definitive hosts and the adult stage of the parasites.

Additionally, co-infection with both Trypanosoma and Leucocytozoon were observed in two individual black flies, providing evidence for simultaneous transmission of multiple blood parasites by single black fly vectors, as previously reported (Thaijarern et al., 2019). Such co-infections have epidemiological significance as they may influence pathogen transmission dynamics and disease risk in host populations (Soares et al., 2016).

Overall, the findings of this study highlight the necessity of implementing epidemiological surveillance for pathogens based on molecular screening of putative black fly vectors (Verocai et al., 2018). Vector surveillance would help to identify pathogens carried by black flies, improving understanding of potential zoonotic threats and facilitating timely public health responses before zoonotic diseases emerge.

Conclusion

This study presents a significant advancement in black fly vector identification, providing a practical tool that addresses current limitations in morphological and DNA barcoding approaches for the identification of S. asakoae. The time and cost savings by the multiplex PCR compared to DNA sequencing make large-scale studies more feasible, potentially accelerating research progress in simuliid studies. Molecular detection of diverse pathogens circulating among certain black fly species within the S. asakoae species group emphasizes the need for continued epidemiological surveillance of these medically and veterinary important vectors in Thailand and other countries.
