Bridging the representation gap in the surgical workforce: a scoping review protocol of programmes and interventions to support surgical careers for underrepresented minority learners
Authors: Aljeena R Qureshi, Negeen Halabian, Armaan K Malhotra, Meerab Majeed, Vidhi Bhatt, Ajibola Anifowose, Armaghan Alam, David-Dan Nguyen, Betel Yibrehu, Kennedy Ayoo, Adom Bondzi-Simpson, Savtaj Brar, Andrea Covelli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDespite increasing proportions of underrepresented minority (URM) medical school graduates, their progression into surgical training and leadership remains disproportionately low. Barriers such as financial constraints, limited mentorship and implicit bias contribute to this disparity, creating a disconnect between the diversity of patient populations and those providing care. While interventions such as mentorship programmes and pipeline initiatives have been implemented, their overall effectiveness has not been systematically evaluated. The primary aim of this scoping review is to map the current landscape of interventions, programmes and policies designed to enhance access to surgical careers for URM learners.Methods and analysisSearches will be conducted on EMBASE, Web of Science and OVID MEDLINE. Three independent reviewers will screen references, extract data and perform analyses with disagreements adjudicated by a fourth reviewer. This review will include studies conducted across all levels of training: secondary (high school or secondary school), postsecondary (undergraduate, medical school) and postgraduate (residency, fellowship), with no geographical restrictions. The definition of URM will be accepted as reported within each individual study, allowing for variability in racial, ethnic, gender, socioeconomic or other criteria. The review will include any structured interventions, programmes or policies aimed at increasing URM representation in surgical education. Data on the nature, duration and target population of each intervention will be extracted. The primary outcome will be the reported impact of interventions on URM representation or participation in surgical education. Secondary outcomes will include characteristics of the study participants, definitions of URM status and any qualitative or quantitative evaluations of intervention effectiveness.Ethics and disseminationResearch ethics approval is not required under University of Toronto policy. Study results will be reported according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews guidelines. Results will be disseminated to relevant stakeholders at conference presentation(s) and submitted for publication in a peer-reviewed journal.

Introduction

Background

Surgical fields lag significantly behind other healthcare professions in diversity, with persistent gaps in the inclusion of underrepresented minorities (URMs). These disparities are particularly pronounced in leadership and training pipelines, creating barriers to equitable representation at multiple levels of the profession.1 While URMs continue to make up an increasing proportion of medical school graduates, their transition into surgical residencies and advancement into leadership roles remains disproportionately low.2 3 These barriers, often stemming from socioeconomic disparities, implicit biases and a lack of mentorship opportunities, create an enduring disconnect between the diversity of patient populations and those providing care.4 Additionally, this may culminate in a self-fulfilling prophecy wherein continued implicit workplace barriers stifle the pursuit of surgical careers among URM premedical and medical learners. Although many diversity initiatives across healthcare disciplines have demonstrated measurable progress, comparable efforts within surgery have been fewer in number and less effective, leaving the field lagging behind other areas of medicine.5

In principle, representation in surgery should span diverse identities, including but not limited to gender, ethnicity, race and socioeconomic background, but in practice these groups remain underrepresented. Underrepresentation can be framed through the lens of race, ethnicity, gender and sex, with women and gender minority (LGBTQIA+) individuals also facing barriers in a traditionally male-dominated field, though gender diverse groups have historically been underexamined in the literature. Moreover, intersectionality compounds these inequities, as individuals who hold multiple marginalised identities (eg, race, gender and socioeconomic status) often experience overlapping barriers that further restrict access to surgical training and advancement. This disparity is marked not only by the low numerical representation of URM individuals but also by limited inclusion, access and advancement due to factors such as implicit bias, financial constraints and a shortage of role models. Ultimately, true representation means fostering an inclusive environment where all individuals can thrive and shape the field.6

Beyond representation, diverse healthcare teams have been shown to improve patient outcomes, foster culturally sensitive care and drive innovation through the integration of varied perspectives.3 Moreover, URM physicians are more likely to serve in underserved communities, directly addressing healthcare disparities and improving access to care.1 The lack of diversity in surgery thus undermines the profession’s ability to meet the needs of an increasingly diverse patient population. Addressing these disparities requires a focused effort to identify and evaluate the initiatives aimed at improving diversity within surgical training and practice.

The unique perspectives and lived experiences of URM colleagues enhance collaborative decision-making and provide more nuanced approaches to patient care.7 Despite these benefits, surgical programmes continue to struggle with recruiting and retaining URM learners, resulting in a surgical workforce that does not look like the patients they serve.8 While targeted interventions like mentorship programmes and pipeline initiatives (structured programmes designed to offer mentorship, academic support and early exposure to surgical careers) have been explored in individual studies, there is a lack of systematic evaluation of these efforts within the field of surgery. Importantly, such a systematic evaluation could identify effective strategies, priority areas for ongoing work and potential barriers faced by URM learners. This knowledge gap hinders the development of comprehensive, evidence-based solutions tailored to the unique challenges faced by URMs in surgical fields.

Rationale

Although previous literature1 6 7 9 has highlighted the challenges faced by URM learners and proposed individual solutions, no comprehensive review has systematically examined interventions designed to support their progression toward surgical careers. The persistent lack of representation among URM learners not only perpetuates workforce disparities but also may limit the field’s ability to address health inequities faced by patients effectively. Addressing these challenges requires a detailed understanding of existing interventions, including the populations targeted, programme objectives and measurable outcomes.

This scoping review seeks to characterise the landscape of interventions targeted at supporting URM learners in surgical fields. By describing these initiatives, this review will provide potentially actionable insights into best practices, identify areas for further development and support ongoing efforts to create more inclusive and equitable pathways for URM individuals.

Objectives

The primary objective of this scoping review is to characterise the landscape of interventions designed to support URM learners in pursuing surgical careers. Specifically, the review aims to address the following central questions:

How are URM learners defined in the surgical education literature, and what criteria are used for identification?

What are the structures, content focus and delivery methods of existing initiatives (eg, mentorship programmes, institutional policies, educational interventions)?

What outcomes are measured to evaluate these initiatives?

Among studies that measured the effect of interventions, what are the reported impacts of these interventions on URM representation in surgical fields?

Methods and analysis

The proposed scoping review will be reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) and Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews.10 11 This protocol was drafted according to JBI Best Practice Guidance and Reporting Items for the Development of Scoping Review protocols.12 Any post hoc deviations from the protocol will be explicitly detailed in the final manuscript. The planned study dates are from 1 October 2025 to 1 April 2026.

Eligibility

Table 1 outlines the inclusion and exclusion criteria for this study. Structured interventions targeting URM learners at any stage of the surgical training pipeline will be included, including high school or secondary school students, premedical programmes, medical students, residents or fellows. Since URM definitions may vary across different contexts, such as Black, Indigenous, Hispanic, Latinx, Pacific Islander, sexual and gender minorities (including but not limited to lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer, non-binary and gender non-conforming individuals), and those from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds (such as low-income or first-generation students), articles must clearly state the inclusion of an URM population or formal URM designation used by the authors (self-identification, census-based methods or institutional criteria). We intentionally do not specify the method of URM identification since an objective of this work is to characterise existing definitions of this population in surgical education literature.

Concept

The primary concept we aim to capture includes any interventions, programmes, policies or initiatives aimed at enhancing URM representation in surgical careers (ie, recruitment, retention and progression). Our definition for URM learners is agnostic to training level and encompasses trainees in high school or secondary school, undergraduate studies, medical school, residency programmes or fellowship training. The definition of URM was adapted by the Association of the American Medical Colleges and included those who are identified as racial, ethnic, gender, sexuality and socioeconomic populations that are underrepresented in the medical profession relative to their numbers in the general population.

Studies that characterise barriers without describing interventions or programmes aimed to mitigate barriers will not be included (such as focus group interviews in isolation). Relevant interventions may include mentorship, pipeline outreach, curriculum redesign, funding or scholarship programmes, policy initiatives or partnerships specifically targeted towards supporting URM learners in surgical training programmes or pathways.

Studies must also report outcomes related to URM representation, such as recruitment or retention rates, career progression, satisfaction with interventions or mentorship impact. Studies without specific interventions, measurable outcomes or those unrelated to surgical careers will be excluded. In the event, a study outlines an intervention in an undifferentiated medical or premedical cohort, we will include the study if a surgery-specific subgroup is presented or if there is follow-up for future surgical engagement or training outcomes.

Context

To ensure breadth, we will not limit by geographic location, healthcare system or surgical specialty or subspecialties. Given the evolution of modern surgical practices and diversity efforts, our search range will be from 1 January 2000 to present day to capture relevant interventions and policy developments in the context of surgical training in the 21st century.

Types of sources

We will include primary peer-reviewed articles (quantitative, qualitative and mixed-method studies) and grey literature (organisational or policy reports, technical documents). Pure commentaries, opinion pieces, book chapters or sources not describing or evaluating specific interventions will be excluded. We seek to include studies reported in English due to translation resource limitations and concerns that relying on automated or ad hoc translation may compromise the accuracy and consistency of data extraction and synthesis.

Search strategy

A systematic search will be conducted across OVID MEDLINE, Embase and Web of Science. Additional sources will include organisational reports, grey literature and consultation with experts in surgical education and diversity initiatives. The search strategy will incorporate both free-text terms and controlled vocabulary (eg, MeSH terms) to ensure comprehensive coverage. A librarian was consulted in the development of the search strategy on 5 March 2025 (see online supplemental appendix I). The following approach was used to search MEDLINE, with similar strategies adapted for Embase and Web of Science (online supplemental appendix I). Key concepts included ‘underrepresented minority groups’, ‘students’ and ‘surgery’. Search filters were used when available.1315 Reference lists of all included sources of evidence will also be screened for additional studies. Grey literature will comprise preprint servers, institutional repositories, policy statements or position papers and websites from regional or national surgical societies.

Study/source of evidence selection

All retrieved citations will be imported into reference management software for deduplication. Two reviewers will independently screen titles and abstracts using Covidence software (Melbourne, Australia) against the inclusion and exclusion criteria outlined. Full texts of potentially eligible articles will be collated for further review by the same reviewers for study inclusion. A third reviewer will resolve disagreements as needed. Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage will be recorded and reported in the scoping review. We will present the overall selection process in a PRISMA-ScR flow diagram.

Data extraction

Data from included studies will be extracted using a standardised charting form (online supplemental appendix II). Key variables to be extracted include the criteria used to identify URM learners, sample size, URM learner demographics such as average age, sex ratio, reported race, ethnicity and training stage. Additional intervention data will be collected, such as the type of intervention (eg, mentorship, policy), content focus, mode of delivery (eg, in-person, virtual), and outcome metrics such as URM representation rates, participant satisfaction and career progression. Data extraction will be conducted independently by two reviewers to ensure accuracy and reliability. In the case of missing information, corresponding authors will be contacted where applicable.

A draft extraction form is provided in online supplemental appendix II of the supplementary material. This may be revised during the process of data extraction from each included evidence source depending on the reporting styles encountered. Modifications will be explicitly outlined in the scoping review.

Data analysis and presentation

Extracted data will be summarised in tables and figures; additional thematic analysis will be employed to synthesise findings, grouping interventions by type and focus. A descriptive summary will be used to characterise variables such as the frequency of intervention types, geographical distributions, URM definitions and characteristics. Barriers, facilitators and lessons learnt will be coded and categorised in a thematic analysis. Figures will be used to highlight key trends and gaps. All findings will be contextualised within existing social frameworks to highlight the implications for policy and practice.16

Expected outcomes

This scoping review will provide a comprehensive overview of existing interventions aimed at supporting URM learners in the pursuit of surgical careers. It will identify effective strategies, common barriers and gaps in the literature, offering valuable insights for educators, policymakers and institutions committed to enhancing diversity in surgery. Where appropriate, we will also provide recommendations to inform future programme development and policy.

Patient and public involvement

While patients and the public were not involved in the conception or design of this protocol, we plan to engage relevant stakeholders, such as surgical trainees and professional societies, in the final stages of knowledge dissemination. This may include gathering feedback on our findings and co-developing strategies to implement recommendations. Further, this protocol and future scoping review study will be a product of the UpSurge research programme. UpSurge is a resident-led collaborative that aims to enhance representation of surgical training through mentorship, research and education. The UpSurge research group is composed of a diverse group of trainees and faculty, some of whom may identify within the target population outlined in this review protocol.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethics approval and patient consent are not required for this scoping review, as no primary data from human subjects will be collected. Dissemination strategies include publication in a peer-reviewed journal, presentation of findings at national and international conferences, and sharing insights with key stakeholders, including academic institutions, professional societies and diversity committees aiming to enhance URM representation in surgical fields.
