Mineral trioxide aggregate obturation quality with two obturation techniques in severe curved root canals – a micro-CT study
Authors: Alireza Farhad, Amir Hossein Saderi, Masoud Saatchi, Abbasali Khademi, Parisa Soltani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe existence of voids within the mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA) composition is one of the factors that can influence the treatment outcome. The primary objective of this study was to quantitatively assess and compare the MTA orthograde obturation quality in severe curved root canals using two different MTA compaction techniques: manual compaction with K-file, or Auger technique using micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) imaging.MethodsFor this study, 26 mandibular first molar teeth with severely curved mesiobuccal root canals were selected. These samples were randomly divided into two groups. All root canals were instrumented using ProTaper Gold rotary files up to the F3 file at the working length. In one group, OrthoMTA was compacted using a stainless steel K-file, while in the other group, the Auger technique was employed for compaction into the root canals. Once the MTA had completely set, the filled root canals were subjected to scanning using a high-resolution micro-CT scanner. The porosity volume was determined as a percentage in relation to the overal volume of the canal, and the collected data were subjected to analysis using SPSS software, with the significance level set at P < 0.05.ResultsThe two techniques had no significant difference in open, closed, and total mean porosity. In both groups, the mean of open porosity was significantly more than closed porosity.ConclusionsAccording to the results of the present study, neither of these two techniques is preferred to the other, and factors such as working time, etc., can be considered to choose the more appropriate clinical technique.

Background

Mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA) was initially introduced to the realm of endodontics by Torabinejad et al. in 1993 [1]. Subsequently, it has been effectively employed as a vital pulp capping agent, utilized as an apical plug during single-visit apexification procedures, and applied as a root-end filling material. Furthermore, it has been employed to repair lateral root and furcal perforations [2]. Some researchers have proposed utilizing MTA to obturate the entire root canal system due to its efficacy as a material for filling root-ends [3–5]. Employing MTA as the material for root canal obturation offers several advantages, such as its excellent sealing ability [6], enhanced resistance to fractures [7], its support for tissue repair [8] and resolution of resorptive defects [9]. Nonetheless, handling mixed MTA can prove challenging in limited anatomical regions, especially in the apical area, where voids often form [10]. Also, the degree of root canal curvature can impact the root canal filling [11]. Avoiding the formation of voids while filling narrow and confined root canal spaces is challenging [10]. Voids within the MTA structure can result in leakage from the root canal space, potentially resulting in an adverse endodontic outcome [12]. Micro cracks may form due to structural weakness foci caused by porosity and also other imperfections in the microstructure of MTA [5, 13].

Several obturation techniques have been proposed to reduce the risk of voids in MTA structures. These methods encompass the hand compaction technique involving hand files and pluggers [14, 15], delivery via a lentulo spiral [16], ultrasonic activation [15], the MTA pellet technique [17], the canal projection technique [18], and the Auger technique [19]. Each technique has its own set of advantages and disadvantages: Hand compaction technique involves using hand files and pluggers to manually compact MTA. This method allows for controlled placement but can be time-consuming and technique-sensitive [14, 15]. Lentulo spiral uses a rotary device to deliver MTA into the canal. It is less technique-sensitive and faster but may not achieve uniform compaction in curved canals [16]. Ultrasonic activation uses ultrasonic energy to enhance MTA flow and compaction. This technique improves MTA adaptation to canal walls but requires specialized equipment [15]. MTA pellet technique involves forming MTA pellets that are placed into the canal. It simplifies handling but may not fill irregular spaces effectively [17]. Canal projection technique utilizes a custom-fitted cone to project MTA into the canal. This method ensures accurate placement but is complex and may not be suitable for all canal anatomies [18]. The Auger technique is relatively new, utilizing NiTi rotary files for MTA compaction. This method employs rotational motion to convey MTA along the root canal, with the spiral flutes of the rotary instrument ensuring uniform delivery and packing of the material. This motion not only compacts the MTA but also reduces the risk of air entrapment, which can cause voids. The process involves using conventional 0.04 and 0.06 taper NiTi rotary files in reverse mode, combined with circumferential and pecking actions to compact the MTA. After the MTA is delivered to the canal with a carrier gun, the rotary instrument pushes the cement to the apical limit using these combined actions [19]. The proposed advantages of this technique include uniform compaction and ease of use, moreover this technique is potentially safer than ultrasonic placement as less energy is involved [19]. Previous studies have explored various obturation techniques aimed at reducing void formation in MTA structures. For instance, Studies by El-ma’aita et al. and others have shown that manual compaction can achieve dense root fillings, though with varying effectiveness depending on the operator’s skill and technique specifics [20]. Studies by Yeung et al. and others have shown inconsistent outcomes on the ability of ultrasonic activation to reduce voids, indicating that results can vary significantly depending on the specific study design and methods used [21]. Some studies suggest that the Auger technique enhances MTA compaction quality, resulting in fewer voids and better adaptation to canal walls. For example, An et al. [12] found that the Auger technique significantly reduced void volume compared to traditional hand compaction methods, supporting the theoretical benefits of using a rotary instrument for more uniform material distribution. However, comprehensive studies comparing the Auger technique with established methods are still limited.

The literature surrounding root canal obturation techniques with mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA) highlights the absence of definitive support for an optimal obturation technique, particularly in severely curved root canals. While various techniques have been proposed, including manual compaction with hand files, ultrasonic activation, and rotary file compaction, studies comparing these techniques in severely curved canals are limited. Additionally, the impact of these techniques on the quality of MTA obturation, specifically in terms of void formation and sealing ability, remains unclear. Addressing these research gaps is essential for optimizing endodontic treatment outcomes and enhancing the longevity of root canal therapy. the purpose of this study was to assess and compare the quality of orthograde MTA obturation in severely curved root canals by employing two distinct placement techniques: manual compaction with a K-file or the Auger technique, evaluated using micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) imaging.

Methods

Sample selection

The first mandibular molars were extracted for periodontic reasons and collected at the Department of Periodontology at the Isfahan University of Medical Science. These recently extracted teeth underwent a cleaning process to eliminate debris, calculus, and granulation tissues before being placed in a 5.25% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) (Chloraxid 5.25%, CERKAMED, Wojciech Pawlowski, Poland) solution for 30 min. The first mandibular molars were extracted for periodontic reasons and collected at the Department of Periodontology at the Isfahan University of Medical Science. These recently extracted teeth underwent a cleaning process to eliminate debris, calculus, and granulation tissues before being placed in a 5.25% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) (Chloraxid 5.25%, CERKAMED, Wojciech Pawlowski, Poland) solution for 30 min. A power analysis was conducted to determine the appropriate sample size for the study. We calculated that a minimum of 13 teeth per group would be necessary to achieve a power of 0.80 at a significance level of 0.05. Therefore, a total sample size of 26 teeth was deemed adequate for the study. 26 mandibular molars were selected based on inclusion criteria: First mandibular molars with a solitary and distinct mesiobuccal canal within the mesial root with fully matured roots, no resorption, perforation, cracks, fractures, or root restorations and the presence of an apical curvature falling within the range of 25º to 30º according to Schneider’s classification [22]. To determine the degree of curvature, radiographs were taken in buccal and mesial directions for all samples. The curvature of the mesiobuccal canal was calculated using Schneider’s method. On the radiograph, a line parallel to the long axis of the mesiobuccal canal was drawn, and a second line was drawn from the apical foramen to the point where it intersected the first line at the point where the canal deviates from the long axis. The acute angle formed was measured using a protractor [22]. The exclusion criteria included teeth where the mesiobuccal canal did not have severe curvature according to Schneider’s classification, where the mesiobuccal and mesiolingual canals merged in the mesial root, where files separated or the canal was blocked during preparation, or where any transportation, perforation, or other adverse events occurred during preparation. A total of 26 mandibular first mesiobuccal canals were randomly assigned to two groups based on the obturation techniques employed: hand compaction using stainless steel K-files [group K] or the Auger technique involving the reverse rotary motion of Ni-Ti files [group A]. Root canal preparation

The teeth crowns were reducted using a high-speed handpiece and diamond bur (Jota Diamond #837L, JOTA AG, Rüthi, SG, Switzerland) under continuous water spray, standardizing the remaining tooth length to 20 mm. A #10 stainless steel K-file (FKG Dentaire, La Chaux-de-Fonds, Switzerland) was carefully inserted into the mesiobuccal root canal until the file tip became visible at the apical foramen. The working length was established by subtracting 1 mm from this measurement and was subsequently confirmed through radiographic assessment. The instrumentation of all root canals was carried out using Protaper gold Ni-Ti rotary files (PTG, Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland). These files were operated with a torque-limited endodontic motor (VDW Silver Reciproc Motor; VDW GmbH, Munich, Germany) at a speed of 300 rpm, applying a torque of 5.10 Ncm for SX and S1 instruments, 1.5 Ncm for S2 and F1 instruments, and 3.10 Ncm for F2 and F3 instruments until the F3 file reached the established working length. To ensure patency, a #10 K-file was inserted into the apical foramen between each phase of instrumentation. Additionally, each root canal received irrigation with 1 mL of a 5.25% NaOCl solution, administered using a 30-gauge needle (Endo Irrigation Needle Tip, Tribest, Jiangsu, China) between instrumentations. Following the instrumentation process, the canal was subjected to a rinsing sequence that included:

To complete the procedure, the canal was dried using #30 paper points (Millimeter-Marked Paper Points, Diadent, Cheongju, Korea). This protocol was consistently applied across all samples to ensure standardization.

Root canal obturation

OrthoMTA (BioMTA, Seoul, Republic of Korea) was prepared according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Using an MTA carrier (MAP System, PD, Vevey, Switzerland), the mixed MTA was systematically delivered into the prepared canal in incremental portions. In group K, the MTA was manually compacted using a #25 stainless steel K-file (FKG Dentaire, La Chaux-de-Fonds, Switzerland) until two-thirds of the root canal was filled. Any excess moisture from the compacted MTA was absorbed using #45 paper points.

In group A, the MTA was compacted using an F2 ProTaper Gold (PTG, Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) Ni-Ti rotary file inserted into the canal. It was rotated at 300 rpm with a torque of 3.10 Ncm in a reverse rotary motion until two-thirds of the root canal was filled. Excess moisture from the compacted MTA was also removed with #45 paper points.

All teeth were maintained at a temperature of 37 °C with 100% humidity for a week to facilitate the thorough setting of the MTA. To ensure consistency and eliminate potential bias, the entire canal preparation and obturation procedures were performed by a single endodontist who was blinded to the group allocations. An independent researcher, not involved in the instrumentation and obturation processes, assigned a unique code to each tooth. This coding system ensured that the operator was unaware of the specific obturation technique being applied to each sample. This blinding protocol was designed to minimize any bias that could arise from the operator’s knowledge of group assignments.

To ensure consistency and reliability in technique execution, the endodontist performing the canal preparation and obturation underwent a calibration process prior to the commencement of the study. This process involved multiple training sessions to standardize the techniques used for both the manual compaction with a K-file and the Auger technique. Additionally, a series of pilot tests were conducted to assess the reproducibility of the techniques. The intra-operator reliability was evaluated by having the endodontist perform the procedures on a set of sample teeth multiple times under identical conditions, and the outcomes were consistent across trials. This calibration aimed to minimize variability and enhance the reliability of the results.

Micro-CT analysis

A high-resolution micro-CT scanner (LOTUS inVivo, Behin Negareh Co., Tehran, Iran) with a pixel size of 25 μm was utilized to scan the filled root samples. The X-ray tube settings were configured to 70 kV for tube voltage, 50 µA for tube current, and 2 s for frame exposure time. The acquired images from the scanner were reconstructed using the LOTUS inVivo-REC software (Behin Negareh Co., Tehran, Iran) through a standard Feldkamp, Davis, Kress (FDK) algorithm. Subsequently, three-dimensional models and cross-sections of the internal structure were created to perform volumetric analysis (Figs. 2 and 3).

The volume of interest (VOI), also known as the total canal volume, was defined at a distance of 5 mm from the apical seat, typically situated 1 mm from the apex. This total canal volume encompassed both the MTA filling and any voids present. Voids were categorized into two types: open porosities, which were voids located between the canal wall and the MTA filling, and closed porosities, which were voids within the MTA filling itself. An automatic segmentation threshold was used to separate root dentin, filling material and voids using LOTUS inVivo-REC software (Behin Negareh Co., Tehran, Iran). Through this process, the images produced by the micro-CT scanner were initially in grayscale, but they were reconstructed in different shades to represent the various components: gray for dentin, green for filling material, red for open porosity, and blue for closed porosity. Voids in contact with dentin were classified as open porosity, while voids surrounded by filling material were classified as closed porosity. For each slice, regions of interest were chosen to calculate the volume of filling and voids. The calculation of porosity volume was conducted as a percentage relative to the total volume of the canal. To eliminate potential bias, the entire procedures were performed by a single operator who was blinded to the group allocations.

Analytical statistics

To ensure the validity of our findings, we considered potential confounding variables such as the age of the teeth and the experience of the operator. All teeth used in this study were recently extracted first mandibular molars, collected under similar conditions to minimize variability due to age or prior condition. The entire canal preparation and obturation process was performed by a single experienced endodontist to eliminate variability due to operator differences.

The statistical analysis was carried out using SPSS 21.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics, New York, NY, USA). Descriptive statistical analysis of the total canal volume and the percentage volume of closed, open, and total porosity was expressed in terms of the mean and standard deviation (SD). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was employed to assess the normality of the data distribution. Since the data followed a normal distribution, independent t-tests were used to compare the percentage volumes of total, open, and closed porosity between the two groups. Additionally, paired t-tests were conducted to compare within-group differences. The significance level for all tests was set at P < 0.05.

Before conducting the independent t-tests and paired t-tests, we assessed the assumptions underlying these tests to ensure their appropriateness. The homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. The results indicated that the assumption of equal variances was met for all comparisons (P > 0.05). Additionally, the data were checked for normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, which confirmed that the data followed a normal distribution.

To provide a more comprehensive understanding of the magnitude of differences between groups, we also calculated effect sizes. The effect sizes for the independent t-tests were determined using Cohen’s d, which provides a standardized measure of the difference between means. Effect sizes were interpreted according to Cohen’s guidelines, where 0.2 indicates a small effect, 0.5 indicates a medium effect, and 0.8 indicates a large effect.

The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test confirmed that the data in both groups followed a normal distribution. Therefore, independent t-tests and paired t-tests were employed to compare the data between groups. The analysis revealed no significant differences in the percentage volumes of closed, open, and total porosities between groups A and K (closed, P = 0.100; open, P = 0.386; total, P = 0.190).

Open porosities displayed a greater percentage volume compared to closed porosities in both groups (P < 0.001).

The effect sizes (Cohen’s d) for the differences in the percentage volumes of closed, open, and total porosities between the two groups were as follows: for closed porosities, d = 0.45; for open porosities, d = 0.30; and for total porosities, d = 0.40. These effect sizes indicate small to medium effects, suggesting that while there were differences between the groups, they were not large.

Discussion

Voids within filling materials can act as breeding grounds for microorganisms, potentially resulting in microleakage and the long-term failure of endodontic treatments. Various factors can influence the occurrence of these voids, including the clinician’s expertise, the filling technique, root canal preparation methods, material properties, and the anatomical complexity of the root canal system [23]. Considering the challenge of MTA placement in apical 2–3 mm of severely curved root canals, this study evaluated the quality of MTA obturation in these root canals by quantifying the volume of porosities present in condensed MTA structures. In this study, the assessment specifically focused on the apical 5 mm of MTA obturation. This length has been previously demonstrated to effectively prevent bacterial leakage [24]. Furthermore, an apical 5 mm of MTA provides the flexibility for post-placement if required or for backfilling the canal with materials such as thermoplastic gutta-percha or resin-based materials [25].

Indeed, porosity and sealing ability assessment is commonly carried out using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). However, this approach necessitates the destruction of specimens for internal examination, and the resulting cross-sectional images can only support two-dimensional analysis. The process of specimen sectioning and preparation introduces potential artifacts and defects that can significantly influence the results. In contrast, micro-CT analysis offers numerous advantages. It is non-destructive, preserving the integrity of specimens. Moreover, it allows for image analysis in various slice planes, providing high-resolution images. Additionally, it presents a three-dimensional visualization of internal structures. Micro-CT analysis is more time-efficient and demands less labor [20, 26]. Given these advantages, micro-CT was considered a superior tool for observing and quantifying both closed and open porosities within the MTA obturation. The capability to visualize three-dimensional images is particularly beneficial for assessing the distribution of porosities throughout the filling material [27, 28]. Potential sources of measurement error, such as image segmentation discrepancies and operator variability, were minimized through the use of automated software functions and manual corrections by a single trained operator. Regular calibration of the micro-CT scanner ensured consistent image quality, further enhancing the reliability of our measurements.

Most previous studies have primarily reported the occurrence of voids within the filling material without placing emphasis on their specific locations. However, this study made a distinct effort to differentiate and quantify the percentage volume of both open and closed porosities. Open porosities, characterized as voids found at the interface between MTA and the dentin wall, can serve as pathways for the growth and migration of microorganisms towards the periapical region [29]. This can lead to periapical inflammation and increase the risk of treatment failure. Open porosities are particularly concerning as they can harbor bacteria, allowing for the growth and migration of microorganisms towards the periapical tissues, potentially resulting in apical periodontitis and compromised long-term success of endodontic treatments. Closed porosities, on the other hand, represent isolated unfilled spaces within the MTA with significantly lower potential for bacterial growth and migration. However, their presence still signifies potential weaknesses in the obturation material that could affect its structural integrity and long-term performance. Given the distinct impact of each type of porosity and its clinical relevance, it becomes imperative to differentiate and assess the location of porosities within a filling structure. The findings of this study revealed that the percentage of closed and open porosities in both MTA condensation techniques were statistically different. The percentage of open porosities in the groups K and A were 17.96% and 20.24%, respectively, and the percentage of closed porosities were 6.18% and 8.07%, respectively, which indicates that most of the porosities were created between the MTA filling material and dentin wall. This finding was similar to the An et al. study [12]. They investigated the incidence of open and closed porosities in different MTA condensation techniques, and they reported that the open porosities were significantly more than closed porosities. Interestingly, some previous studies have indicated that MTA failed to provide a proper sealing ability when condensed into a root canal [16, 30]. This may be due to open porosities created between MTA and the root canal wall. These open porosities can contribute to apical periodontitis [31].

Several studies have investigated the different methods of canal obturation with MTA. The effect of ultrasonic activation on MTA compaction has been investigated with controversial results. Yeung et al. conducted a study which illustrated that hand compaction of MTA, followed by subsequent indirect ultrasonic activation, resulted in a 10.07% increase in mean MTA weight in curved canals and a 9.1% increase in straight canals [21]. However, these results contrast with those reported by El-ma’aita et al., whose findings indicated that manual compaction of MTA resulted in more compact root fillings compared to those achieved with ultrasonic activation [20]. In addressing the conflicting results reported in previous studies on the effect of ultrasonic activation on MTA compaction, it is essential to explore potential explanations for these discrepancies. Differences in study design, sample characteristics, and methodological approaches likely contribute to the varied findings. Firstly, variations in study design play a significant role. For instance, Yeung et al. reported a 10.07% increase in MTA weight in curved canals and a 9.1% increase in straight canals with ultrasonic activation following hand compaction, suggesting enhanced compaction due to ultrasonic activation. In contrast, El-ma’aita et al. found that manual compaction alone resulted in more compact root fillings compared to those achieved with ultrasonic activation. These differences could stem from variations in the protocols for ultrasonic activation, such as the duration and intensity of ultrasonic application, which can significantly impact the outcome. Secondly, sample characteristics, such as the type of teeth used (e.g., human vs. extracted teeth, molars vs. premolars), the degree of canal curvature, and the condition of the root canals, can influence the results. Studies utilizing teeth with different anatomical complexities may yield inconsistent results due to the varying challenges posed by different canal morphologies. For example, highly curved canals might respond differently to ultrasonic activation compared to straighter canals, potentially explaining some of the observed discrepancies. Methodological differences also contribute to conflicting findings. These include variations in the type of MTA used, the preparation and obturation techniques, and the methods for evaluating obturation quality. Different brands or formulations of MTA may have distinct physical properties affecting their response to compaction techniques. Furthermore, the criteria for assessing the quality of MTA compaction, such as the definition and measurement of voids and porosities, can vary between studies, leading to differing conclusions. Additionally, operator experience and technique sensitivity are critical factors. Ultrasonic activation requires precise control and skill, and slight variations in operator technique can significantly impact the results. Studies conducted by operators with different levels of expertise may thus produce inconsistent findings.

Few studies have investigated the two MTA compaction techniques in the currents study. An et al. compared the MTA orthograde filling obturation quality using three distinct techniques: manual compaction alone, compactor activation, and the reverse rotary motion of Ni-Ti files. Their micro-CT analysis indicated a significant reduction in void volume following orthograde MTA filling when additional activation was combined with hand condensation. Notably, the obturation achieved through the reverse rotary motion of Ni-Ti files exhibited the least open porosity and superior filling quality [12]. In a study by Mathew et al., the density of MTA as a root canal filling material in the apical 5 mm of artificial curved root canals was compared across four different techniques: hand file, gutta-percha points, Auger technique, and plugger technique. Their findings suggested that the hand file, Auger, and gutta-percha point techniques yielded comparable results in MTA obturation in curved canals. Conversely, pluggers were more prone to having voids and could not reach the apical end of the canal, with the hand file technique demonstrating the fewest voids [32].

The present study employed manual condensation with K-file and Auger technique to assess the quality of MTA orthograde obturation. One of the advantages of these two methods is that MTA compaction is achieved using the same instruments utilized for canal shaping, eliminating the need for additional instruments or devices, resulting in enhanced efficiency, convenience, and cost-effectiveness. Also, the presumption was that the vibration caused by the rotating motion of the Ni-Ti file may play an important role. This vibration can cause a sequence of rapid compressive impulses, that reduce surface friction between cement particles. When MTA mixture is subjected to this vibration, individual particles within the mixture rotate as it becomes unstable, leading to the flow of the cement. Consequently, the cement particles may align into a denser mass, while any trapped air is more likely to escape to the surface [20].

The configuration of the ProTaper Gold Ni-Ti rotary file in this study was designed to facilitate the movement of debris toward the coronal area. Consequently, when applying the reverse rotary motion of the ProTaper Gold during MTA obturation, it was expected to compress more MTA particles towards the canal’s apical region, resulting in a denser mass. However, the findings of our study indicated that there was no discernible distinction in the percentage volumes of closed, open, and total porosities between the two groups. Clinically, this suggests that endodontists have flexibility in choosing either technique based on practical considerations without compromising the obturation quality. When deciding between these techniques, clinicians should consider factors such as the time required for obturation, the ease of use, and the availability of instruments. For instance, while the Auger technique might offer faster obturation due to the efficiency of Ni-Ti rotary files, the manual compaction technique with a K-file might be preferred in situations where rotary instruments are not available or when the clinician is more comfortable with traditional methods. By understanding that both techniques can achieve similar outcomes, clinicians can make informed decisions that align with their skills and the clinical setting, ultimately improving patient care.

The lack of significant differences in porosity volumes between the manual condensation technique (group K) and the Auger technique (group A) may be attributed to several factors. Firstly, both techniques were performed by a single experienced endodontist, which minimized operator variability and ensured a consistent application of each method. Secondly, the inherent properties of OrthoMTA, such as its particle size and flow characteristics, may contribute to similar compaction outcomes regardless of the technique used. Additionally, the anatomical complexity of the root canal system and the meticulous preparation process may have reduced the impact of the obturation technique on the final porosity distribution. These factors collectively suggest that both techniques are equally effective under the controlled conditions of our study.

While this study provides important insights into the obturation quality of MTA using two different techniques, it is essential to acknowledge its limitations and consider how they might have influenced the results and interpretations. Firstly, the variability in Ni-Ti file systems is a significant limitation. Different file systems have unique characteristics such as geometric cross-sectional design, helical angle, and cutting angle, which can affect the efficiency of MTA compaction. The ProTaper Gold files used in this study were chosen for their known properties and clinical popularity. However, other Ni-Ti systems might yield different results, and future studies should explore a range of file systems to provide a comprehensive understanding of their impact on MTA obturation quality [33]. Secondly, the characteristics of MTA, including particle size and shape, can influence its compaction quality and the formation of porosities. This study used OrthoMTA, but other MTA formulations or bioceramic materials might behave differently under similar conditions. Variability in material properties could affect the generalizability of our findings, highlighting the need for further research using diverse materials to validate our results.

Additionally, potential sources of bias must be considered. The operator’s expertise and familiarity with the techniques could introduce bias, even though efforts were made to standardize the procedures and blind the operator to group assignments. The use of a single experienced endodontist aimed to minimize operator variability, but it also limits the study’s generalizability to practitioners with varying levels of experience.

Confounding factors such as the anatomical complexity of the root canal system, root canal dimensions and the condition of the extracted teeth might have also influenced the outcomes. We recognize the potential for selection bias, as the teeth were collected from a specific department, which may not represent the general population. Despite efforts to standardize tooth selection and preparation, inherent anatomical variations could affect the results. Moreover, the in vitro nature of the study does not fully replicate clinical conditions, which could impact the applicability of the findings to actual patient scenarios.

Methodological constraints, such as the sample size and the focus on a specific apical region of the root canal, also limit the study. A larger sample size and analysis of the entire root canal system could provide more robust and generalizable results. Furthermore, the selection of only mandibular molars with specific criteria may limit the generalizability of our findings. Furthermore, while micro-CT imaging offers detailed and non-destructive analysis, it cannot fully capture the clinical implications of voids and porosities, such as their impact on bacterial leakage and treatment success. By addressing these limitations and potential sources of bias, we provide a more balanced assessment of our findings. Future research should consider these factors to enhance the reliability and applicability of results, ultimately informing better clinical practices in endodontics. We have utilized nearly all available relevant references in our paper to provide the most comprehensive analysis possible. However, we acknowledge that more comparative studies are needed to draw robust conclusions.

While our study utilizes micro-CT imaging to accurately quantify voids and porosities within the root canal filling material, it is important to consider the clinical relevance of these findings. Voids within the filling material may serve as potential pathways for microbial penetration and fluid leakage, which could compromise the long-term success of endodontic treatments. Future studies should incorporate additional outcome measures, such as leakage tests and assessments of bacterial penetration, to more comprehensively evaluate the clinical implications of these porosities.

Conclusions

Taking into account the limitations of this study, micro-CT analysis indicated that both the Auger technique and manual compaction with a K-file yielded similar results in MTA obturation for severely curved root canals. There was no significant difference in obturation quality between the two techniques. Other factors, such as obturation time, operator preference, equipment availability, and patient-specific considerations, can be taken into account when selecting the appropriate MTA compaction technique in a clinical setting. Future research should explore these practical considerations further to provide a more comprehensive understanding of their impact on clinical outcomes.
