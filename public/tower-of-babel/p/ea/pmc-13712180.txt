Recent advances in loco-ablative therapies for liver cancers
Authors: R. Orr, B. Chan, E.Y.J. Lo, K.S.H. Chok
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most pervasive form of liver cancer worldwide, with its incidence rising due to lifestyle trends. The majority of HCC cases cannot be treated surgically; hence, loco-ablative therapies are required. Classically, radiofrequency ablation (RFA) has been the loco-ablative therapy most often selected for nonresectable HCC. However, due to technological advances and a better understanding of HCC’s pathophysiology, new loco-ablative therapies have been developed for HCC. These novel techniques offer an alternative to surgery and RFA depending on each patient’s clinical presentation, such as the number and size of nodules. Moreover, they present different benefits and potential clinical complications; therefore, knowledge of available techniques is important to allow clinicians to select the most suitable therapy for their patients.Materials and methodsA narrative review was carried out to summarize the existing loco-ablative therapies for HCC, as well as to investigate emerging loco-ablative therapies for HCC. The benefits, drawbacks, indication for, and mechanism of each technique are outlined.ConclusionsThe frontier of locoregional HCC treatment is shifting toward histotripsy and combinations of existing locoregional therapies with immunotherapy. The importance of a multidisciplinary team is highlighted. Nonetheless, there remains a role for surgical resection of HCC and classical locoregional therapies such as RFA, as there is a lack of large-scale randomised controlled trials investigating the role of novel locoregional techniques for treating HCC patients.

Introduction to locoregional therapies for hepatocellular carcinoma

The disease burden of liver cancer is unmistakable, with cases estimated to exceed 1 million worldwide by 2025.1 Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains the most prevalent form of liver cancer, accounting for ∼90% of all cases of liver cancer.1

According to the updated Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) guidelines for HCC treatment, surgical resection is only preferred for early-stage, unifocal liver nodules with preserved liver function.2 For multinodular HCC, locoregional or systemic therapies are preferred. Globally, only 10%-37% of HCC cases are treated with surgical resection; hence, locoregional therapies play an important role in the treatment of HCC.3 Moreover, locoregional and systemic therapies also play an important role in the downstaging of HCC, enabling HCC cases previously classified as unresectable or ineligible for liver transplantation to be reclassified after the tumour burden is reduced.4

Current locoregional therapies for HCC

Local ablation

Local ablation has long been considered a first-line nonsurgical curative option for early-stage, unresectable HCC.5 The most common types of local ablation include chemical ablative techniques such as percutaneous ethanol injection (PEI); hyperthermic ablative procedures such as radiofrequency ablation (RFA) and microwave ablation (MWA); and hypothermic ablative procedures such as cryoablation.

PEI

It involves the injection of ethanol into the HCC tumour mass. The diffusion of alcohol into tumour cells results in immediate coagulative necrosis of the tissue as the ethanol causes protein denaturation and cellular dehydration.6

In current clinical practice, PEI has been replaced by RFA as a loco-ablative therapy for HCC, as it has been demonstrated that RFA outperforms PEI with a higher proportion of complete necrosis of target HCC nodules, and therapeutic outcomes were achieved with a lower number of treatment sessions.7 Moreover, for HCC >2 cm, patients treated with RFA had a higher survival rate and a lower rate of local recurrence of HCC as compared with the cohort treated with PEI.

Nevertheless, PEI still has a niche role in treating HCC patients with tumours immediately adjacent to vital structures such as major blood vessels, biliary ducts, and other abdominal organs.8 There is a risk of thermal damage to these structures if treated with RFA; hence, PEI is a viable alternative locoregional therapy for this category of patients.

Radiofrequency ablation

RFA uses alternating radiofrequency waves from an electrode to heat the tumour tissue, inducing coagulative necrosis and tissue destruction.9 RFA has been shown to be effective in ablating 96% of primary HCC tumours with limited tumour recurrences and patient morbidity.10

However, there are limitations to RFA, with a retrospective cohort study in 2019 showing that there was recurrence of HCC beyond the Milan criteria in 21.6% and 35.1% of HCC patients size ≤2 cm and >2 cm, respectively.11 Moreover, there is a ‘heat sink’ effect of RFA where large vascular structures near the target lesions in the liver help dissipate the thermal energy delivered during RFA via vascular blood flow, potentially resulting in incomplete ablation and tumour recurrence.12 Nevertheless, the extent and clinical significance of the heat sink effect remains disputed and a topic of investigation to date.

Studies have shown RFA results in higher complete necrosis rates than PEI and requires fewer treatment sessions for small-sized tumours (<3 cm), leading to the increasing preference for RFA as a first-line locoregional ablative therapy for HCC in place of PEI.13

Microwave ablation

MWA works similarly to RFA but instead uses an electromagnetic field to generate heat to achieve coagulative necrosis of the tumour tissue, in contrast to the electrical current used by RFA.14 A key advantage of using MWA over RFA is that MWA is less prone to the heat sink effect than RFA, potentially because MWA has been shown to reach higher temperatures for more effective tumour ablation (>100°C).15 Hence, MWA may have an advantage over RFA for HCC tumours located near major vascular structures.

Nevertheless, across multiple studies, it has been shown that MWA and RFA have similar local recurrence and complication rates, with generally comparable postprocedural 1- to 4-year survival rates.14

Cryoablation (cryotherapy)

Cryoablation utilises extreme cold, via liquid nitrogen or argon–helium systems, to destroy tumour cells through ice ball formation, causing direct cellular damage and vascular-related injury.16 Compared with RFA, cryoablation provides larger ablation zones, clearer visualisation of the ice ball margin, less pain, and reduced damage to nearby large blood vessels and the gallbladder. It can be carried out percutaneously, intraoperatively, or laparoscopically, under the monitoring of imaging tools such as computed tomography (CT) or ultrasound. Combination with transarterial chemoembolisation (TACE) has shown higher efficacy of 100% effectiveness compared with 47% using TACE alone, whereas the combinations of immunotherapy and PEI have also shown improvement in survival compared with monotherapy. However, cryoablation does carry a high risk of adverse events, with up to 40% of patients undergoing liquid nitrogen cryoablation experiencing postprocedural complications such as cryoshock and liver parenchyma fractures. Cryoshock, in particular, is a rare complication specific to cryoablation seen in ∼6% of HCC patients undergoing the procedure, resulting in varying degrees of renal failure, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and adult respiratory distress syndrome.

High-intensity focused ultrasound

High-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU) is a noninvasive ablative technique that relies on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-guided high-intensity ultrasound to induce coagulative necrosis in the targeted tissue without damage to surrounding structures.17 In a Chinese study of HIFU ablation for 42 tumours of sizes 7.4 ± 4.3 (1.5-22) cm located within 1 cm of major blood vessels in 39 patients, 21 tumours were completely ablated, while the remaining 21 were ablated by more than 50% of volume.18 There was no major blood vessel injury seen in any of the patients, showing that HIFU can be safely used to ablate liver tumours close to major blood vessels.

Compared with RFA, the usage of HIFU to treat recurrent HCC is promising, as a study by Chan et al.19 found little statistical difference between the two methods, with similar procedure-related morbidity rates (HIFU, 7.4%; RFA, 6.5%;); 1-, 2-, 3-year disease-free survival rates; and 1-, 2-, and 3-year overall survival rates. The main benefit of HIFU compared with other local ablative therapies is that HIFU can be carried out extracorporeally, without the need for inserting an ablative needle or instrument into the body.

However, when HIFU was used to treat larger tumours >5 cm, the complete tumour response rate was found to be 50%, suggesting incomplete ablation.20 Moreover, HIFU as a locoregional therapy carries the risk of thermal injury to internal organs and tissues along the pathway of the focused ultrasound beam if the focus point is misplaced.21 Other reported complications include first-, second-, and third-degree skin burns (in 37.2%, 31.7%, and 2.1% of patients, respectively) as well as ablation of the surrounding small bowel requiring emergency laparotomy to repair the damage.

Transarterial techniques

Transarterial techniques are a category of locoregional therapies indicated for HCC patients with large tumours that are unsuitable for local ablation or surgery and as a bridging therapy to downstage HCC before liver resection or transplantation.22 HCC tumours are mainly supplied by the hepatic artery, whereas healthy liver parenchyma receives most of its blood supply from the hepatic portal venous system.23 Hence, a catheter inserted into the hepatic artery, carrying embolic agents or other tumour-targeting therapies, would preferentially be transported to the HCC tumour tissue instead of the healthy liver parenchyma, resulting in a therapeutic effect while limiting the damage to the noncancerous tissue.24

Transarterial embolisation

Transarterial embolisation (TAE) involves injecting embolic agents, such as gelatine sponge or microparticles, into the hepatic artery to block blood flow to the tumour, causing ischemia and tumour necrosis.22 In TAE, no chemotherapy is used. The occlusion of arterial blood supply cuts off the tumour’s oxygen, leading to tumour necrosis via hypoxia.

Transarterial chemoembolisation

TACE combines chemotherapy, such as doxorubicin, epirubicin, mitomycin, or cisplatin, with embolisation. TACE is recommended as the first-line treatment for intermediate-stage HCC by the BCLC staging system. Chemotherapy is first injected into the hepatic artery, followed by the infusion of embolic agents, such as lipiodol, to trap the drug in the tumour to induce ischemia.25 However, the provision of local chemotherapy together with occlusion by embolic agents, as in the case of TACE, has not been proven to have an additive antitumour effect, with studies showing a similar survival and morbidity profile for TAE and TACE.22 Moreover, as HCC is a relatively chemoresistant type of cancer, the role of embolisation in local therapy may be more salient than chemotherapy, and TACE may not result in statistically better patient outcomes than TAE.26 Hence, there is still debate regarding TAE versus TACE in the treatment of HCC, and higher-quality randomised controlled trials (RCTs) are needed to clarify the usage of TAE versus TACE in the treatment of HCC.

Portal vein (PV) thrombosis remains an absolute contraindication for TACE. Under the already compromised blood supply to the liver due to PV obstruction, TACE will further compromise arterial supply, potentially resulting in hepatic infarction or acute hepatic failure.27

Drug-eluting bead chemoembolisation

Drug-eluting bead chemoembolisation (DEB-TACE) is a refined version of TACE using drug-eluting beads, i.e. beads loaded with chemotherapy drugs, to deliver chemotherapy and embolise the tumour simultaneously.28 The beads slowly release chemotherapy, providing sustained local drug exposure while occluding tumour blood supply, enhancing tumour necrosis. This method is similar to TACE but is often preferred for patients with larger tumours or those requiring more precise drug delivery. Furthermore, DEB-TACE has a comparatively lower adverse event rate and milder postembolisation symptoms than TACE; hence, DEB-TACE may be advantageous for patient safety.29 Moreover, a study found that DEB-TACE resulted in a higher overall patient survival rate and complete objective response rate than TACE.30

Hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy

Hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy (HAIC) uses a catheter technique to directly administer anticancer drugs to tumours through the hepatic artery without the use of embolising agents.31 HAIC is normally reserved for treating advanced HCC. Although there is no consensus in the medical community on its widespread adoption, there is an increasing body of clinical evidence pointing towards its survival benefit for patients with advanced HCC.

Transarterial radioembolisation

Transarterial radioembolisation (TARE) is a catheter-based liver-directed therapy in which micron-sized embolic particles loaded with a radioisotope (usually yttrium-90) are injected using percutaneous transarterial techniques.32 These particles carry a high dose of lethal radiation that targets tumour cells while sparing healthy hepatocytes. TARE has several advantages over TACE in the treatment of intermediate HCC tumours, as it has a more favourable safety profile with a lower incidence of adverse events and postprocedural hospitalisation, as well as requiring less frequent treatments than TACE.33 Moreover, TARE is often indicated in patients with occlusive PV thrombosis where TACE is contraindicated. This is because TARE’s microspheres do not fully occlude the microvasculature, potentially reducing the risk of hepatic ischemia and allowing for outpatient treatment with fewer hospitalisations.34 However, TARE is a relatively more expensive and technically complex procedure than TACE, thus limiting its widespread adoption.

Radiotherapies

Recent advancements in the field of radiotherapy (RT) for HCC, such as breathing motion management and image-guided RT, have made RTs a viable option for HCC, as they provide safe delivery of higher-dose external beam RT to the HCC tumour.35 RTs have a potential role across all stages of HCC. They are an option for HCC patients with unresectable tumours that are also considered unsuitable for the other locoregional therapies, such as RFA and TACE. RTs are also a potentially curative treatment for liver-confined HCC and are associated with good tumour control for small tumours <5 cm in diameter.36

However, RTs do carry a slight risk of radiation-induced liver disease (RILD), but this can be largely avoided by strictly abiding by the published liver dose constraints. However, patients with underlying liver diseases or poorer liver function are at higher risk of developing RILD, which could be a possible contraindication for the use of RTs. The risk of RILD prevents the use of conventional RT techniques, which rely on treating extensive areas of the carcinoma-affected organ, as the healthy liver tissue surrounding the tumour site has low radiation tolerance.37 Hence, RTs must have tightly focused treatment fields to spare the uninvolved liver tissue.

Charged particle therapy

Charged particle therapy (CPT) is a type of liver-directed RT that makes use of charged particles such as protons and carbon ions, which have a steeper dose fall-off, in contrast to traditional photon RT.38 The steeper dose fall-off results in a maximised dose of radiation to the HCC tumour while ensuring uninvolved tissue is spared, limiting the risk of RILD in recipients of CPT. However, there is currently a knowledge gap regarding the role of CPT over other locoregional treatments for HCC, as there is a lack of RCTs investigating the benefits of CPT.39 Nevertheless, CPT holds promise as a therapy for specific HCC patients with nonmetastatic large HCCs >5 cm and those with solitary or clustered multifocal HCC lesions.

Stereotactic body radiotherapy

Stereotactic body RT (SBRT) delivers large doses of radiation compared with conventional RT, which not only causes direct cell necrosis but also vascular damage, reducing tumour blood supply, which precipitates indirect cell death.40 The high-dose radiation may also stimulate antitumour immunity by releasing tumour antigens and activating immune cells, enhancing tumour control. There exists a dose-response effect of SBRT, with an increased dose of SBRT resulting in a higher rate of local tumour control.41 SBRT is currently indicated for patients with early-stage HCC; HCC patients with macroscopic vascular invasion of the PV, hepatic vein, or inferior vena cava; and to downstage HCC as a bridging therapy for liver transplantation.42

Intraoperative radiotherapy

Intraoperative RT (IORT) is an intraoperative locoregional RT that precisely delivers a large dose of ionising radiation to the tumour or tumour bed of an HCC patient undergoing surgery. IORT allows direct visualisation of the tumour bed, permitting the displacement of normal tissues from the tumour, which allows maximisation of the dose to the tumour while minimising the dose to normal tissues nearby.43 Compared with traditional external beam RT, IORT allows for the delivery of a higher total effective dose to the tumour bed and facilitates dose escalation without significantly increasing normal tissue complications. Additionally, it is important to note that IORT is a locoregional therapy that is carried out during surgery for resectable HCC tumours with the aim of lowering future HCC recurrence and improving prognosis, in contrast to most other locoregional therapies that are indicated for unresectable HCCs.

Patients who underwent liver resection combined with IORT have an improved prognosis and lowered early HCC recurrence rates compared with patients who underwent liver resection without IORT.44

Recent advancements in locoregional HCC therapies

Histotripsy

Histotripsy is a noninvasive, nonthermal ultrasound-based method for tumour ablation,45 primarily studied for HCC but also applicable to conditions such as benign prostatic hyperplasia and breast cancer.46 It uses focused, high-amplitude, short-duration ultrasound pulses to create inertial acoustic cavitation, forming a bubble cloud that mechanically disrupts tissue by rapidly expanding and collapsing. This process destroys targeted tissue with precise, predictable boundaries, leaving an acellular liquid homogenate that is quickly reabsorbed. Real-time visualisation via diagnostic ultrasound and a software-controlled micropositioning system ensures accurate targeting of tumours of varying sizes and shapes.47 Preclinical animal studies confirmed its efficacy in destroying tumours and soft tissue with controlled energy delivery, leading to a first-in-human trial to evaluate its safety and effectiveness for primary and secondary liver tumours.

Key advantages of histotripsy

Histotripsy offers several key advantages over traditional thermal ablation methods for liver tumour treatment. It creates highly precise and well-defined ablation cavities with a narrow transition zone, minimising damage to surrounding healthy tissue.45 The real-time visualisation of the cavitation bubble cloud via ultrasound enhances treatment accuracy, clearly distinguishing treated from untreated tumour areas. Histotripsy is particularly promising for anatomically challenging cases, as it selectively targets parenchymal tissue while sparing critical structures like bile ducts and blood vessels due to their higher collagen content. It can treat larger tumours (up to 4 cm or more with overlapping zones) compared with the 3 cm limit of current ablation devices. Additionally, the rapid resorption of the ablation cavity within 1-2 months simplifies the detection of residual or recurrent tumours. Most notably, histotripsy’s nonthermal mechanism produces a slurry of nondenatured proteins, triggering a robust abscopal effect that stimulates both innate and adaptive immune responses, potentially enhancing systemic antitumour immunity at distant sites.

Comparison of the THERESA and #HOPE4LIVER trials

Histotripsy, a noninvasive, nonthermal ultrasound-based method for tumour ablation, was first used in humans in a phase I clinical trial (THERESA Study) in 2019 at the Hospital Clinic de Barcelona, Spain, targeting liver tumours in eight patients. Another well-known trial, the #HOPE4LIVER trial, was conducted from 2021 to 2022 across 14 sites in the United States and Europe. Both trials evaluated the safety and technical efficacy of histotripsy for liver tumours, with THERESA focusing on feasibility in a smaller cohort and #HOPE4LIVER assessing a larger group of 44 patients with primary and metastatic liver tumours, achieving a 95% technical success rate. Currently, histotripsy is primarily approved for liver tumour treatment, with applications being explored for other cancers like kidney and pancreatic tumours, as well as conditions such as benign prostatic hyperplasia and breast cancer. As of July 2024, it has been used in 18 centres across multiple countries, treating 295 patients for 510 liver tumours, including HCC and metastases from colorectal, neuroendocrine, pancreatic, and breast cancers. Leading institutions like Johns Hopkins, the University of Chicago Medicine, and NYU Langone in the United States and Newcastle Hospitals in the UK are among the early adopters.

Limitations and unresolved problems

Histotripsy faces several limitations and barriers that affect its clinical application. Visualising certain lesions using transabdominal ultrasound can be difficult, even with the integration of pretreatment CT or MRI images, potentially reducing treatment accuracy.45 Organs with high gas content, such as the lungs or gastrointestinal tract, are prone to unintended cavitation, which could lead to significant off-target tissue damage if histotripsy is applied in or through these areas. Additionally, ultrasound attenuation increases with tissue depth, making treatment less effective for patients with obesity (body mass index ≥30 kg/m2), as the energy may not penetrate adequately. Although animal studies suggest vascular thrombosis caused by histotripsy is typically nonocclusive, quickly resorbed, and clinically insignificant, this remains a potential risk. Furthermore, the lack of comprehensive, long-term data on efficacy, safety, and durability in humans is a significant barrier. Despite promising early results, extensive clinical trials are needed to establish histotripsy’s reliability and support its widespread adoption as a standard liver-directed therapy. Recent studies also highlight challenges such as the need for precise patient selection and limitations in treating tumours near critical structures due to potential collateral damage, underscoring the necessity for further technological refinements.

Combination therapies for HCC

Combination approaches for locoregional treatment of HCC have shown notable improvement in survival and tumour response compared with standard monotherapy. Nevertheless, adverse effects vary by regimen. Therefore, a careful balance of efficacy versus risk is important in deciding treatment plans.

Benefits of combining different locoregional treatments

A study by Iezzi et al.49 in 2016 demonstrated that balloon-occluded RFA immediately followed by DEB-TACE was effective at achieving prolonged local disease control in a single large HCC (>3 cm), with a sustained complete response in 62.5% of the treated lesions, a 2-year cumulative HCC recurrence rate of 48.1%, and an overall survival rate of 91.1%, which is significantly better than that achieved in a comparable control group of patients treated with DEB-TACE alone. A systematic review by Galanakis et al.50 in 2018 also revealed that a combination of TACE and RFA/MWA is associated with higher overall and recurrence-free survival, especially for intermediate and large tumours, and should be considered as a therapeutic option in early-stage HCC.

Benefits of combining locoregional treatments with immunotherapy

In a study evaluating the effectiveness of combining TACE and sorafenib in Asian regions, combination therapy significantly improved time to progression (hazard ratio 0.66, 95% confidence interval 0.48-0.89, P = 0.006) and overall survival (hazard ratio 0.57, 95% confidence interval 0.45-0.72, P < 0.001).51 Another retrospective study supported the enhanced benefits of anti-PD-1 therapy, i.e. camrelizumab or sintilimab, combined with RFA. Patients have shown significantly better outcomes, with longer overall survival (32.5% compared with 10.0%) and prolonged recurrence-free survival (51 weeks compared with 47.6 weeks) relative to the RFA-only group.52 Combination of TACE and lenvatinib has also shown significantly longer median overall survival compared with lenvatinib monotherapy (17.8 months versus 11.5 months), and a higher response rate according to the modified RECIST assessment (54.1% versus 25%). Such benefits might be due to the administration of TACE therapy, which serves to debulk intrahepatic tumour burden, potentially improving the efficacy of subsequent lenvatinib administration and positively impacting patient survival.53

In recent years, clinical trials on administering nivolumab after selective internal RT have shown an acceptable safety profile. The inclusion factors for this trial included: (i) tumours in the BCLC B2 substage, (ii) unilobar tumours with segmental or lobar PV invasion, (iii) no extrahepatic spread, and (iv) preserved liver function. These patients received selective internal RT followed 3 weeks later by the PD-1 inhibitor nivolumab, for up to 24 doses or until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity.54 Although the study was limited by its small sample size, such promising results warrant further investigation.55

Limitations of combination therapies

Nevertheless, adverse effects should be considered when applying combination therapies. In a study combining TACE and sorafenib, despite the benefits seen among Asian populations, the disease control rate also favoured combination therapy, which simultaneously caused higher incidences of adverse effects such as hand-foot skin reaction and haematologic effects.51 In a systematic review, immuno-onocology monotherapies have shown a superior safety profile, whereas combination therapies caused more toxicity in general.56

Conclusion

Locoregional therapies hold great promise for the treatment of unresectable HCC and as a bridging therapy to downstage HCC for liver resection or transplantation. It is an evolving field, with recent innovations in RTs, histotripsy, and combination therapies having promising preliminary results. Moreover, locoregional therapies are increasingly being considered as first-line therapies that can be offered as an alternative to surgery, as they have an excellent safety profile, are less invasive, and have a good prognosis with less chance of HCC recurrence.

Nevertheless, more clinical research and RCTs are needed to investigate breakthrough technologies such as histotripsy to provide supportive evidence for improving current guidelines for the treatment of HCC.
