Eosinophilia as a diagnostic window for strongyloidiasis: seroprevalence insights from an endemic region
Authors: Bahareh Basirpour, Bentolhoda Ahmadi, Zahra Abbasi, Shahabeddin Sarvi, Mitra Sadeghi, Shirzad Gholami, Seyed Ali Shariatzadeh, Seyed Abdollah Hosseini, Ahmad Daryani, Sargis A. Aghayan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionEosinophilia is a recognized hematological marker associated with helminthic infections, including strongyloidiasis which is caused by Strongyloides stercoralis. This case-control study, conducted in Mazandaran province from 2022 to 2023, aimed to determine and compare the seroprevalence of S. stercoralis infection between individuals with eosinophilia and healthy controls without eosinophilia. The ELISA test by a commercial kit was used for serological detection of anti-S. stercoralis IgG antibodies, and data were analyzed using SPSS software (version 23).ResultsThere was analyzed 184 samples, divided into two groups, with and without eosinophilia, with 92 individuals each. In these groups, 36.96% (34/92) and 7.61% (7/92) were positive for anti-S. strongyloides IgG antibodies, respectively, which revealed a strong association (OR = 7.12, 95% CI [3.82, 13.27], P < 0.001) between S. stercoralis antibodies as eosinophilia, confirming eosinophilia as a important predictor of S. stercoralis exposure. Subgroup analyses revealed that males, farmers, individuals aged > 41 years, those with a history of soil contact, and residents of rural areas exhibited higher seroprevalence rates of S. stercoralis. The findings suggest that screening individuals with eosinophilia for anti-S. stercoralis IgG antibodies may improve diagnosis in endemic area.

Introduction

Strongyloidiasis is an intestinal infection caused by Strongyloides stercoralis, a nematode parasite that is found worldwide but is most common in tropical and subtropical areas [1]. Infection occurs when the parasite’s filariform larvae penetrate the skin. In most cases, the infection may occasionally present non-specific symptoms include cutaneous, gastrointestinal (such as mild abdominal pain and diarrhea.), and pulmonary manifestations, which arise due to the parasite’s migratory pattern in humans [2]. However, there may be severe clinical manifestations under-immunocompromised conditions like the use of corticosteroids, hematologic malignancies, HTLV coinfection, alcohol abuse, AIDS, organ transplantation, or malnutrition, which may progress to dissemination of infection [3].

S. stercoralis diagnosis is challenging due to intermittent and low-level larval shedding in stool samples. Therefore, traditional stool-based methods, such as Baermann-Moraes method, direct smear, culture, and concentration techniques, often yield low sensitivity and may produce false-negative results. Serological tests, particularly ELISA, offer higher sensitivity and can monitor antibody titters during treatment, though cross-reactivity can affect specificity. Avidity-ELISA techniques help differentiate between acute and chronic infections by assessing IgG avidity, reducing false-positive results. Molecular methods like Polymerase chain reaction(PCR) are not routinely employed in in laboratory practice due to their high cost but provide high specificity and sensitivity but the would be useful in confirming infections, especially when traditional methods are inconclusive [4, 5].

The prevalence of this helminthic infection is particularly high in tropical and subtropical regions, where warm temperatures, high humidity, and poor sanitation favor the parasite’s free-living cycle [6]. In northern Iran geographical conditions, climate, socio-cultural factors, and population density create a suitable environment for the transmission and spread of various parasites, particularly S. stercoralis [6]. Based on previous studies, the highest infection rates of this intestinal nematode are observed among residents of the Caspian Sea coastal areas, where adequate heat and humidity facilitate disease transmission [7, 8]. Research has reported infection rates in northen Iran ranging from about 0.95% to over 4% in general and institutionalized populations, and higher seroprevalence rates (up to 32%) in specific groups such as patients with leptospirosis or diabetes [9, 10].

The challenges in diagnosing strongyloidiasis underscore the importance of simple, initial screening markers to guide clinical suspicion. In this context, the presence of eosinophilia as a well-recognized indicator of possible parasitic infection can effectively flag patients for targeted S. stercoralis testing, especially in regions where the parasite is endemic. This study aims to determine the seroprevalence of S. stercoralis infection among individuals with eosinophilia in an endemic area in northern Iran, comparing these findings with a non-eosinophilic healthy control group.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

This project was approved by the ethics committee of Mazandaran University of Medical Sciences (IR.MAZUMS.REC.1402.14731). The purpose and procedures of the study were explained to all participants, and a written informed consent was obtained from all of them.

Study design and setting

A case-control study was conducted between January 2022 and December 2023 at Mazandaran University of Medical Sciences, Sari, Iran, to assess the seroprevalence of Strongyloides stercoralis infection among individuals with eosinophilia compared to individuals without eosinophilia. The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Study population

A total of 184 participants were enrolled, comprising 92 individuals with eosinophilia (cases) and 92 without eosinophilia (controls). Participants were recruited from diagnostic laboratories and medical centers in Sari, Iran.

Subjects were divided into two groups according to complete blood count (CBC) results: eosinophilia group consisted of patients with more than 500 cells/µL eosinophils and control group consisted of subjects with normal eosinophil counts (< 500 cells/µL). Inclusion Criteria: (i) Cases: Individuals aged ≥ 18 years with an absolute eosinophil count > 500 cells/µL. (ii) Controls: Age- and sex-matched healthy individuals with normal eosinophil counts (< 500 cells/µL). Exclusion Criteria: (i) Recent use (within the past 6 months) of anti-parasitic medications. (ii) History of hematologic or dermatologic disorders, asthma, atopy, drug sensitivity, or hematologic conditions (iii) Immunosuppressive therapy or conditions. (iv) Inadequate or hemolyzed serum samples unsuitable for ELISA. (v) Declined informed consent. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are summarized in Table 1.

Sample collection and processing

Demographic data were recorded using questionnaires. From each participant, 5 mL of blood sample was collected in vacuum tubes without anticoagulant (red-top tubes). The blood tubes were centrifuged at 1300 ×g for 10 min. The sera were carefully separated using a 1000 µL micropipette and transferred to another tube for serological testing, then stored at − 20 °C until further analysis. To hematological analysis, the blood samples were collected in an EDTA tube and complete blood cell count was done using an automated blood cell analyser (Sysmex KX-21 N, Kobe, Japan).

Serological analysis

For the serological detection of anti-S. stercoralis IgG antibodies, an indirect ELISA was used (Although parasitology methods are still the conventional and commonly used approaches for diagnosing this infection). The test was carried out using the NOVATEC kit (Novalisa, NovaTec, Germany), which has a reported sensitivity and specificity of 89.47% and 94.12%, respectively. All the laboratory steps, including incubation and washing cycles, were carefully adhered to as instructed by the manufacture manual.

For result quantification, the OD in each well was determined at a wavelength of 450 nm using a micro plate reader. The OD values obtained were then converted into units of concentration for the antibodies in NovaTec units (NTU), via the calibration curve.

Serological results were interpreted using the following NTU cut offs: Positive: ≥ 11 NTU, Borderline: 9–11 NTU, Negative: < 9 NTU.

Data analysis

For sample size calculation, we considered 95% confidence level for confidence interval (CI), power of 80%, ratio of cases and controls of 1:1, reference seroprevalence of 45.4% as the prevalence of exposure in controls, and odds ratio of 2.06 [11]. The sample size calculation gave a value of 92 cases and 92 controls.

Data were analyzed using SPSS software version 23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics. The chi-square test was employed to subgroup analysis. ORs with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to assess the association between eosinophilia and S. stercoralis infection. Among all associated risk factors, a multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to adjust for potential confounders. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Seroprevalence of S. stercoralis in individuals with and without eosinophilia

In this case-control study, 184 participants were enrolled, comprising 92 individuals with eosinophilia and 92 individuals without eosinophilia. Serological testing for anti-S. stercoralis IgG antibodies revealed a significantly higher prevalence in the eosinophilic group (36.96%; 34/92) compared to the control group (7.61%; 7/92). Based on Table 2, this corresponds to an odds ratio (OR) of 7.12 (95% CI: 2.83–20.14), indicating a markedly increased likelihood of S. stercoralis infection among individuals with eosinophilia (P < 0.001).

S. stercoralis seroprevalence based on associated risk factors

Subgroup analyses identified several factors associated with higher seroprevalence rates of S. stercoralis infection (Table 3). Among eosinophilic individuals, those aged 21–40 years exhibited a seroprevalence of 40.48%, while those over 41 years had a seroprevalence of 45.45%. A significant association between age and seropositivity was observed (χ² = 7.50, P = 0.023). Farmers demonstrated the highest seroprevalence in the eosinophilic group (59.09%), followed by self-employed individuals (50.00%). The association between occupation and seropositivity was statistically significant (χ² = 3.09, P = 0.048). Analysis of environmental exposures demonstrated that individuals with reported soil contact (n = 39) had significantly higher seropositivity rates (48.72%, 19/39) than those without exposure history (28.30%, 15/53; χ² = 4.02, P = 0.045). Similarly, in the control group, individuals with soil contact exhibited a higher seroprevalence (15.62%) than those without (5.55%) (χ² = 4.48, P = 0.034).

The strongyloidiasis prevalence was higher in males than in females in case (43.75% vs. 33.33%) and control (10.53% vs. 5.55%) groups, but no significant difference was found (p = 0.32 vs. p = 0.38). Also, the prevalence of S. stercoralis was higher in rural areas than in urban areas in case (38.89% vs. 34.21%) and control (7.84% vs. 7.32%) group, but statistical analysis did not show a significant difference (p = 0.15 vs. p = 0.92).

Discussion

Strongyloidiasis is a globally significant yet underdiagnosed helminthic infection, with severe consequences in immunocompromised hosts [12]. Its unique auto infective cycle allows silent chronic infection for decades, suddenly turning fatal during immunosuppression (Hyperinfection syndrome and disseminated disease) [13]. The infection disproportionately affects tropical regions but spreads through migration, requiring non-endemic countries to maintain clinical suspicion. Diagnosis is challenging due to intermittent larval excretion, necessitating advanced methods like ELISA or PCR for reliable detection [14, 15]. Although these methods would be helpful in confirming S. stercoralis infection, it is important to note that parasitological methods are still the most commonly used for S. stercoralis diagnosis, which makes this parasitic disease a significant public health problem. However, in the present study, we employed ELISA to detect anti-S. stercoralis IgG antibodies for seroprevalence assessment among eosinophilic and non-eosinophilic populations.

Serological diagnosis based on the detection of anti–S. stercoralis IgG antibodies has become an important tool for the diagnosis of strongyloidiasis, particularly in situations where conventional parasitological methods show limited sensitivity due to the low and intermittent excretion of larvae in stool samples [8]. Early evaluations, including that of Anderson et al. [16], assessed the performance of the InBios Strongy Detect IgG ELISA in comparison with other serological platforms such as the SciMedx ELISA and the luciferase immunoprecipitation system (LIPS) assay [16]. Subsequent comparative and systematic studies have further confirmed that recombinant-antigen-based IgG ELISA assays provide high sensitivity and acceptable specificity for the detection of S. stercoralis infection and represent reliable tools for screening and diagnosis, particularly in endemic areas and in high-risk populations [14, 17, 18].

The data of the current study showed a higher prevalence rate of S. stercoralis seropositivity in the eosinophilic individuals (36.96%) than in the control (7.61%), indicating a high correlation between eosinophilia and Strongyloides infection. This observation validates that a high prevalence rate of S. stercoralis infection may be a major causative factor for eosinophilia in this region. When compared with the original work done in Iran by Ashrafi et al. [7], which demonstrated a 42% prevalence rate using a coprological examination, this study reflects a good alignment, with a minor variation [7]. The possible reason for this variation could largely be associated with the differences in the methods used. Moreover, it consistent with Clark et al. [19], which reported elevated eosinophil counts in S. stercoralis-seropositive transplant recipients compared to seronegative individuals [19].

S. stercoralis infection elicits a classic T helper type 2 (Th2) immune response. This is defined by the cytokines IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13, which promote the production of IgE and the expansion, recruitment, and activation of eosinophils [20]. Also, larval migration through cutaneous, pulmonary, and gastrointestinal tissues elicits localized eosinophilic inflammation. These inflammatory foci correlate with episodic peripheral eosinophilia. In immunocompetent hosts, chronic autoinfection maintains low-level antigenic stimulation, sustaining Th2 activation and moderate eosinophilia (≥ 500 cells/µL) in 70–75% of cases [12, 13]. Asymptomatic carriers often exhibit higher eosinophil counts than symptomatic patients, suggesting eosinophils contribute to parasite containment through antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) and granule protein-mediated larval damage.

Hyperinfection syndromes, typically precipitated by corticosteroid therapy or human T-lymphotropic virus 1 (HTLV-1) coinfection, demonstrate paradoxical eosinopenia despite escalating larval burdens [12, 21]. Glucocorticoids induce eosinophil apoptosis and suppress IL-5 transcription, while HTLV-1 skews immunity toward interferon-γ (IFN-γ)-dominant Th1 responses, dismantling the eosinophilopoietic milieu [21]. This collapse of eosinophil surveillance correlates with disseminated larval migration and increased mortality, as evidenced by studies showing hyperinfection patients with normal eosinophil counts exhibit 2.3-fold higher fatality rates than those with persistent eosinophilia [12].

While peripheral eosinophilia remains a sensitive screening marker for chronic strongyloidiasis in immunocompetent populations, its absence does not exclude infection-particularly in high-risk immunosuppressed individuals [13, 22]. Clinicians should consider serological testing (e.g., IgG ELISA) for patients with epidemiological exposure, regardless of eosinophil counts. Post-treatment eosinophil normalization often indicates successful parasite clearance, whereas persistent eosinophilia may suggest ongoing autoinfection or reinfection [22]. Conversely, abrupt eosinopenia in immunosuppressed patients warrants urgent evaluation for disseminated disease [23].

Our study identified agricultural workers with eosinophilia having the highest seroprevalence of S. stercoralis. This is consistent with reports from countries like Rwanda and Cambodia, where frequent soil contact, often barefoot, increases risk. In our sample, participants with a history of soil exposure showed higher seroprevalence (48.7%) than those without (28.3%), highlighting cutaneous contact as the main transmission route. Warm, humid agricultural environments and poor sanitation further support the parasite’s survival and its ability to cause lifelong auto-infection [7]. Limited healthcare access in rural areas further contributes to underdiagnoses and ongoing transmission. Preventive measures include wearing shoes and gloves, improving sanitation, and targeted ivermectin treatment in high-risk communities.

Age and gender distribution also played significant roles in infection rates. We observed a peak in seroprevalence among individuals over 41 years old, a trend that aligns with findings from Rajavel et al. [24]. This age-related susceptibility likely reflects a combination of factors, including immunosenescence, prolonged occupational exposure, and age-related comorbidities that compromise immune function. Additionally, males, who were more likely to engage in agricultural work, showed a slightly higher seroprevalence compared to females, a finding consistent with reports from Cambodia. The rural/urban comparison revealed comparable seroprevalence rates, suggesting that the widespread nature of agricultural practices and soil exposure in both settings might contribute equally to infection risk [7].

Overall, our data suggests that routine screening for S. stercoralis in patients with unexplained eosinophilia, especially in endemic areas, could allow earlier detection and treatment. Indeed, our findings may support considering targeted screening in these populations. Although our study used serology rather than stool diagnostics, the elevated seroprevalence among eosinophilic individuals hints that local prevalence may warrant targeted interventions. At minimum, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for strongyloidiasis in patients from this region with eosinophilia or before initiating steroids or other immune-modulating therapies.

Limitations

The study also includes various critical limitations, and all should be taken into consideration within the process of analysis and interpretation of results acquired. Firstly, the chosen diagnosis method used solely the results of serologic testing, and because there is no molecular analysis, such as PCR, within the noted antigens and/or DNA of S. stercoralis within the observed stool specimens, it eliminates the ability to correctly demonstrate and quantify within the actual active infection. A further consideration to be taken into note lies within the fact that the results attained within the utilization of the ELISA technique are under the threat of cross-reactivity within the antibodies generated by other types of infection, potentially hampering the specificity within the results attained A further potentiality selection bias should be noted, as the subjects were chosen in diagnostic laboratories and medical facilities, and such could possibly lack a random selection, and over-represent symptomatic individuals as well as individuals with access to medical facilities, possibly lacking within a true portrayal of Northern Iran.

In view of these limitations and building upon the present findings, it was proposed that future studies should include the implementation of integral diagnostic methods, aiming at combining serologic, molecular, and parasitologic examinations carried out on stool specimens. Further, it was proposed that the authors should propose future studies carried out on subjects recruited from the general population with the aim of precisely determining the prevalence of infection.
