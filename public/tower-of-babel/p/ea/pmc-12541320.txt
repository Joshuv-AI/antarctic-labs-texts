The autophagy-specific exocyst subcomplex contributes to phagophore assembly site integrity by promoting phagophore expansion
Authors: Ruchika Kumari, Titikhya Nath, Jeremy Pflaum, Inchara R, Sannannagari Vinay, Florian Wilfling, Ravi Manjithaya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThis study significantly enhances our understanding of membrane expansion during autophagy by decoding the role of the tethering complex exocyst during autophagosome biogenesis. Using yeast genetics, we show that the autophagy-specific exocyst subcomplex is essential for preserving the integrity of the phagophore assembly site (PAS) during autophagosome biogenesis. Since the autophagy pathway is involved in maintaining cellular health and is dysregulated during diseases such as neurodegeneration, cancer, and aging, this study has a broad impact on fundamentally understanding these conditions.

Macroautophagy (subsequently autophagy) is one of the principal autophagy pathways that deliver cytoplasmic cargoes to the lysosomes/vacuoles for degradation. It involves sequestration of the cargo inside double-membrane vesicles called autophagosomes, which fuse with the lysosomes/vacuoles. Autophagosome biogenesis is a multistep process involving initiation, membrane expansion, pore closure, and maturation (1–6). In yeast, autophagosome biogenesis occurs at a specific perivacuolar site called the phagophore assembly site/preautophagosomal structure (PAS) (1, 3, 7–9). On induction of autophagy, proteins involved in autophagosome biogenesis are recruited to the PAS, including autophagy-related (Atg) 1 complex, PI3K complex I, Atg9 vesicles, and associated proteins (8, 10–13). Interlinking of several Atg1 complexes results in a supramolecular assembly, which further recruits downstream proteins, facilitating autophagosome biogenesis (14–18). This assembly is a phase-separated structure, and the liquidity of the structure is regulated by Atg1 kinase activity (19, 20). Subsequent recruitment and fusion of Atg9 vesicles results in the formation of a seed membrane, which further expands to form the cup-shaped phagophore (21, 22). Other vesicles, such as coat protein II (COPII) vesicles, have been implicated to play a critical role at this stage (23, 24). Once the cup-shaped phagophore is formed, the Atg2–Atg18 complex mediates a bridging contact between the edge of the phagophore and the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) (25, 26). Lipids from the ER are delivered to the growing phagophore via the Atg2 protein (27). Working in tandem, Atg2 and Atg9 mediate the expansion of the phagophore (25, 28). The expanded spherical phagophore is then sealed to form a closed autophagosome through a membrane scission process (29). Once formed, closed autophagosomes mature by recruiting fusion machinery, enabling them to fuse with the vacuolar membrane and release the autophagic body into the vacuolar lumen for degradation (30).

Currently, the field is moving toward a unified membrane expansion model where Atg9 and secretory vesicles are critical for early membrane precursor expansion, followed by ER-driven phagophore expansion. However, definitive proof for such a model is lacking. There is still a possibility that both processes may occur simultaneously for efficient membrane expansion. As mentioned, membrane expansion during autophagosome biogenesis is poorly understood. Mobilization of membranes, particularly for fusion, involves large protein complexes known as tethering complexes (31). While having unique subunits, the tethering complexes also share common protein components, allowing the cell to repurpose the same proteins for different functions (31, 32). Autophagy involves several membrane fusion events carried out by tethering complexes that conventionally participate in other vesicular trafficking pathways. For example, the homotypic fusion and vacuole protein sorting (HOPS) complex is critical for fusing autophagosomes to lysosomes/vacuoles (33, 34). The conserved oligomeric Golgi (COG) complex required for intra-Golgi trafficking was demonstrated to be involved in autophagosome biogenesis (35, 36). Another set of tethering complexes, the transport protein particle III (TRAPPIII) and TRAPPIV have been shown to participate in autophagosome biogenesis by tethering Atg9 and COPII vesicles (37–41). TRAPPI and TRAPPII complexes, having the same core complex subunits, are important for anterograde and retrograde trafficking between the ER and Golgi (37).

The tethering complex, exocyst, has been identified to be involved in autophagosome biogenesis in yeast, plants, and mammals (42–44). The exocyst complex has been extensively studied for its role in the secretory pathway (45–49). It is an evolutionarily conserved hetero-octameric complex composed of subunits Sec3, Sec5, Sec6, Sec8, Sec10, Sec15, Exo70, and Exo84 (50, 51). In yeast, all exocyst subunit genes exist as single copies and are essential for growth except SEC3, which is nonessential (49, 52). The assembly of the octameric complex is mediated by the formation of two tetrameric subcomplexes, Sec3-Sec5-Sec6-Sec8 and Sec10-Sec15-Exo70-Exo84 (50, 51). Multiple pieces of evidence suggest that the exocyst complex has moonlighting roles in autophagy. In mammalian cells, the exocyst complex regulates the autophagy pathway by forming RalB-mediated activating and inactivating subcomplexes (42). In plants, it was found that this complex delivers membranes for autophagosomal membrane expansion (43, 53, 54).

A previous study from our laboratory showed that in yeast, the exocyst complex functions as a subcomplex during autophagy by regulating Atg9 trafficking (44). However, the exact stage at which it participates in the pathway is unknown. Here, due to the hierarchical nature of Atg protein recruitment and function during autophagosome biogenesis, we used yeast genetics to delineate the exact step in which the exocyst acts in the autophagy pathway. We systematically analyzed the contribution of exocyst subunits in autophagy using temperature-sensitive mutants of exocyst genes. We provide proof that an autophagy-specific exocyst subcomplex, excluding Exo70, is required for autophagosomal membrane expansion. Epistasis analysis revealed that the exocyst subcomplex functions downstream of the Atg1 complex but upstream of the PI3K complex I, Atg2–Atg18 complex, and Atg12–Atg5–Atg16 complex. However, its function is not required for the localization of these complexes to the PAS. Additionally, while characterizing the role of the exocyst complex, we identify a unique phenotype of accumulation of multiple Atg-enriched perivacuolar structures in membrane expansion mutants. Imaging and molecular studies revealed that these structures are abortive dysfunctional PAS, compromised in their ability to expand membranes. Thus, this study suggests that the exocyst subcomplex is critical for phagophore expansion and PAS integrity.

Results

The Exocyst Subcomplex Is Involved in Autophagy.

A previous study from our laboratory demonstrated that the exocyst subcomplex is essential for autophagy. To evaluate and identify the autophagy-specific subcomplex, we probed for the involvement of exocyst subunits in autophagy and secretion. In yeast, several exocyst genes are essential for growth. Therefore, using temperature-sensitive (ts) mutant strains of these genes, we tested whether growth is affected in exocyst ts mutants. While all the strains grew at permissive temperature (PT), only WT and atg1Δ cells grew at nonpermissive temperature (NPT), and the exocyst ts mutants showed delayed or no growth (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). We further tested the role of exocyst genes in the canonical secretory pathway by employing the invertase secretion assay. As expected, invertase secretion was affected in exocyst ts mutants sec3-2 and exo70-38 at NPT (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). Having established that the exocyst ts mutants showed compromised growth and secretion at NPT in nutrient-rich conditions, we tested whether these mutants were defective in the autophagy pathway. Classically, autophagy mutants are unable to recycle cytoplasmic cargo and, therefore, die during nitrogen starvation. We employed Phloxine B staining to differentiate between live and dead cell populations and measured the survival of exocyst ts mutants with respect to nitrogen starvation. While all the mutants showed good survival at PT (25 °C), we observed that within 24 h of nitrogen starvation, all the exocyst ts mutants except Exo70 did not survive at NPT (37 °C) (Fig. 1 A–C). To test the involvement of the exocyst complex in autophagy flux, we performed the GFP-Atg8 processing assay. Log phase cells expressing GFP-Atg8 were shifted to nitrogen starvation at PT and NPT. To measure the recovery of free GFP levels post–NPT treatment, the cells were shifted to PT after keeping them at NPT for 2 h. Temporally, we saw the accumulation of free GFP in all the strains at PT. However, the free GFP release was severely hampered in exocyst ts mutants sec3-2 and sec5-24 compared to WT and exo70-38 at NPT and did not show significant recovery even after shifting back to PT (Fig. 1 D and E). These results were further validated using the auxin-inducible degron (AID) strains of exocyst subunits. Protein degradation and delayed growth were observed on the addition of plant hormone 3-Indoleacetic acid (IAA) in strains expressing degron fusion exocyst subunits and auxin signaling F-box 2 (AFB2) (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 C and D), validating the utility of this approach in studying loss-of-function of the exocyst complex. Using this assay, upon exocyst depletion, the GFP-Atg8 processing assay was carried out. While Exo70 depletion did not markedly alter autophagic flux, loss of subunits Sec3, Sec5, Sec6, Sec8, Sec15, and Exo84 resulted in perturbed autophagic flux (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 E–K). This suggests that the loss of the functional exocyst complex, excluding Exo70, critically affects autophagy flux. Next, to test whether the autophagy block observed is due to affected vacuolar function, we examined the maturation of the vacuolar protease carboxypeptidase Y (Prc1). This protease is transported to the vacuole independent of autophagy (55, 56). While the Prc1 maturation was affected in the sec18-1 mutant at NPT, we observed no defect in Prc1 maturation in exocyst ts mutants at PT and NPT (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 D and E). In conclusion, these results suggest that an autophagy-specific exocyst subcomplex, excluding Exo70, participates in autophagy.

Loss of Functional Exocyst Subunits Affects Autophagosomal Membrane Expansion.

In our previous study, we showed perturbations in Atg9 trafficking in exocyst ts mutants, and protease protection analysis hinted at defects in autophagosome biogenesis in these mutants (44). Autophagosome formation has distinctive steps, such as initiation, membrane expansion, and pore closure (1). The autophagosomes formed in yeast are approximately 500 nm in diameter (57). Thus, resolving the different steps using standard fluorescence microscopy is difficult. To overcome this drawback and identify the stage at which autophagosome biogenesis is affected, we performed the giant Ape1 assay (58). Aminopeptidase 1 (Ape1) is delivered to the vacuole via the cytoplasm-to-vacuole targeting (Cvt) and bulk autophagy pathways (59, 60). Overexpression of Ape1 leads to the accumulation of Ape1 dodecamers in the enlarged Ape1 complexes. Autophagosome formation can then be tracked by labeling Atg8, a marker for autophagic membranes, with GFP (58). Cells were transformed with plasmids expressing GFP-Atg8 under an endogenous promoter and Ape1 under the copper-inducible promoter CUP1. As expected, in WT, upon overexpression of Ape1 through CuSO4 induction and treatment with rapamycin, a cup-shaped GFP-Atg8 structure of approximately 1 µm in length was observed (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and G). This suggests that membrane expansion is unperturbed, which was also the case in mutants vps21Δ and ypt7Δ, since they are blocked in steps after membrane expansion (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 D, E, and G). The mutants atg1Δ and atg2Δ showed the presence of a single punctum on the giant Ape1 cargo, suggesting loss of the initiation and/or membrane expansion ability. (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 B, C, and G). Next, the membrane expansion ability in exocyst ts mutants was examined. These mutants showed the presence of a cup-shaped GFP-Atg8 structure around the giant Ape1 cargo similar to WT at PT, suggesting unaffected membrane expansion (Fig. 2 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). However, at NPT, the exocyst ts mutants, except for Exo70, showed the presence of spot-like GFP-Atg8 structures of lengths less than 0.5 µm, suggesting affected membrane expansion or initiation. The mutants exo70-38 and exo70-29/37 showed unperturbed membrane expansion at NPT (Fig. 2 A and B). Thus, the defect in autophagosome biogenesis seen in exocyst ts mutants is at an early stage before membrane expansion. Additionally, we observed that the exocyst ts mutants defective in membrane expansion exhibited ≥3 (multiple) dot-like GFP-Atg8 structures at NPT (Fig. 2 A and C). Such multiple puncta were only seen in exocyst ts mutants, not in core autophagy mutants (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and C). Interestingly, moving the cells from NPT to PT led to the appearance of a single functional PAS with progressive restoration of autophagy flux (Fig. 1D and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 I and J). Hence, investigating the identity of these structures will help elucidate the specific role of the exocyst subcomplex in autophagy.

Loss of Functional Exocyst Subunits Results in the Accumulation of Multiple Atg Puncta.

Since we observed a unique phenotype of multiple GFP-Atg8 puncta in exocyst ts mutants, we further investigated the composition of these structures. We conducted extensive live cell colocalization analysis of these structures with core autophagy proteins. We tagged expansion markers (Atg2 and Atg18) and initiation markers (Atg13 and Atg17) in WT and exocyst ts mutants sec3-2, sec5-24, and exo70-38. WT and exo70-38 strains showed unperturbed autophagy and were used as control strains. Log-phase cells expressing an Atg protein tagged with 2xGFP (Atg2, Atg18, Atg13, or Atg17) and RFP-Atg8 were treated with rapamycin and shifted to PT and NPT. WT and exo70-38 cells showed ≤2 Atg puncta for the expansion and initiation markers (SI Appendix, Figs. S4 A–D and S5 A–D). In WT and exo70-38 cells, a diffused vacuolar RFP signal was observed, suggesting unperturbed autophagy (Fig. 3 A–D). In exocyst ts mutants sec3-2 and sec5-24, both expansion and initiation markers accumulated as multiple puncta at NPT (SI Appendix, Figs. S4 A–D and S5 A–D). Upon colocalization analysis with Atg8, multiple colocalization events were observed in exocyst ts mutants sec3-2 and sec5-24 with expansion markers (Atg2 and Atg18) and initiation markers (Atg13 and Atg17) at NPT (Fig. 3 A–E). Therefore, it is evident from these results that multiple Atg8 puncta are not mere mislocalization of Atg8, since several core autophagy proteins localize to these sites. These core autophagy proteins are involved in initiation and expansion during autophagosome biogenesis, raising the possibility that the exocyst proteins, except Exo70, may be involved in adequately localizing the autophagy machinery to the PAS.

PAS-Like Structures Appear in Exocyst ts Mutants.

Buoyed by these observations, we wanted to investigate further the nature of these multiple core Atg proteins containing puncta seen in exocyst ts mutants, except Exo70. Based on Differential Interference Contrast (DIC) and live cell fluorescence microscopy images, these protein clusters seemed to localize near the vacuole. To evaluate whether the multiple puncta have perivacuolar localization akin to the PAS, we labeled the vacuolar membrane and lumen in exocyst ts mutant strains expressing tagged Atg proteins. Cells expressing 2xGFP-tagged proteins were stained with CMAC blue dye and visualized using live cell fluorescence microscopy. CMAC blue dye is transported to the vacuole via the endocytosis pathway and is used to stain vacuoles (61). While the WT and exo70-38 cells showed ≤2 Atg puncta at the perivacuolar region (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A), the exocyst ts mutants sec3-2 and sec5-24 showed ≥3 Atg puncta (Atg2, Atg18, Atg13, or Atg17) clustered around the vacuole (Fig. 4A). We confirmed this observation by labeling the cells with the vacuolar membrane marker Vac8 tagged with GFP and examining the colocalization of Vac8 and Atg8. Upon rapamycin treatment, in WT and exo70-38 cells expressing Vac8-GFP and RFP-Atg8, Vac8 localized to the vacuolar membrane, forming a ring surrounding the free RFP signal in the vacuolar lumen, indicating that the autophagy flux was unaffected (Fig. 4 B and C). In the sec5-24 mutant, multiple RFP-Atg8 puncta were observed colocalized with the vacuolar membrane signal (Fig. 4 B and C). Additionally, in exocyst ts mutants at NPT, multiple Vac8 signal-enriched puncta were present on the vacuolar membrane, which were also in proximity with multiple RFP-Atg8 puncta (Fig. 4 B and C). The PAS has also been shown to be in contact with the ER and vacuole tethered by Vac8 on the vacuolar membrane (4, 25, 26, 62, 63). To test whether these multiple puncta are in the vicinity of the ER and vacuole, we measured the colocalization of multiple Atg8 puncta with the ER and vacuole in exocyst ts mutants. Upon rapamycin treatment at NPT in exocyst ts mutants, ≤2 Atg8 puncta localized in contact with the ER and vacuole, and others surrounded the vacuolar membrane (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 B–D). Thus, multiple Atg8 puncta observed in exocyst ts mutants localize to vacuolar membranes. This molecular signature resembles the PAS, suggesting that these multiple Atg puncta in exocyst ts mutants might be PAS-like structures. Recent studies have shown that the PAS is a biomolecular condensate, dispersed upon treatment with 1,6-hexanediol (19, 20). If these multiple puncta are PAS-like structures, they should also exhibit sensitivity to 1,6-hexanediol. Thus, we tested the sensitivity of these multiple puncta to 1,6-hexanediol by performing live-cell microfluidic experiments. Log phase cells expressing Atg13-2xGFP were pretreated with rapamycin at NPT and mounted on a microfluidic device. Cells were continuously imaged during treatment with 1,6-hexanediol, followed by wash-off and recovery. Upon rapamycin treatment, WT and exo70-38 cells formed a single Atg13 punctum. As expected, upon treatment with 1,6-hexanediol, the punctum dissolved and was recovered after washing off the compound (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A–F). However, upon rapamycin treatment, mutant sec5-24 cells showed an accumulation of multiple Atg13 puncta at NPT. These puncta were lost upon treatment with 1,6-hexanediol, and following wash-off and recovery, the multiple puncta reappeared (Fig. 4 D–F), suggesting these structures have a similar recovery pattern as the proteins at the PAS. Additionally, the recovery pattern of Atg13-2xGFP punctum in exocyst ts mutant sec5-24 postphotobleaching was similar to WT cells, suggesting that the liquidity of these PAS-like structures is similar to WT PAS (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 G and H). In conclusion, the multiple Atg puncta observed in exocyst ts mutants share several properties with PAS.

Epistasis Analysis of the Exocyst Subcomplex with ATG Genes Reveals Its Hierarchical Role During Autophagosome Biogenesis.

Autophagosome biogenesis in yeast follows a hierarchical map of Atg proteins, with Atg17 acting as the scaffold (8). To identify the stage at which exocyst is involved, we performed epistasis analysis between exocyst and autophagy genes. If exocyst function were crucial for a particular autophagy gene, the double mutant would show multiple Atg17 puncta phenotype, helping us to place the exocyst within the hierarchy of Atg proteins. To conduct the epistasis analysis, we created autophagy knockouts in the background of WT (control), sec5-24 (blocked in autophagy), and exo70-38 (not blocked in autophagy) strains. The autophagy genes ATG18, ATG5, ATG14, and ATG13 were chosen as candidates for creating autophagy knockouts representing expansion complexes (ATG18 representing Atg2–18 and ATG5 representing Atg12–Atg5–Atg16) and initiation complexes (ATG14 representing PI3K complex I and ATG13 for Atg1 kinase complex). Together, these genes encompass nearly the entire map of the hierarchical autophagosome biogenesis (8). We tagged Atg17, a PAS scaffold protein with 2xGFP, in the single (autophagy mutant) and double mutant (exocyst and autophagy mutant) strains and scored for the number of Atg17 puncta. We deduced that if double mutants with ≤2 Atg17 puncta are observed, then exocyst is involved downstream of the autophagy gene, and if ≥3 Atg17 puncta are observed, exocyst functions upstream of that gene. Thus, log-phase cells of single and double mutant strains were treated with rapamycin, and the culture was split into two and shifted to PT and NPT. As expected, most single mutants of autophagy genes showed ≤2 Atg17 puncta at PT and NPT (Fig. 5 A–H and SI Appendix, Figs. S8 A–D and S9 A–D). The double mutants with exo70-38 strain background showed similar results to single mutants of autophagy genes since autophagy flux is unaffected in exo70-38 cells (Fig. 5 A–H and SI Appendix, Figs. S8 A–D and S9 A–D). In the sec5-24 strain at PT, expectedly, autophagy mutants of genes ATG18, ATG5, ATG14, or ATG13 showed ≤2 Atg17 puncta (SI Appendix, Figs. S8 A–D and S9 A–D). At NPT, sec5-24 double mutants with ATG18, ATG5, or ATG14 showed accumulation of ≥3 Atg17 puncta (Fig. 5 A–C and E–G). This suggests that exocyst is required upstream of ATG18, ATG5, and ATG14 during autophagosome biogenesis. However, in the case of double mutant sec5-24 atg13Δ, ≤2 Atg17 puncta were observed at NPT (Fig. 5 D and H), suggesting that the exocyst is required downstream of ATG13. Thus, our epistasis analysis reveals the exocyst complex functions between the Atg1 kinase complex and PI3K complex I, indicating its possible role alongside Atg9 vesicles in phagophore formation and expansion (Fig. 5I).

Recruitment of Atg2 on Multiple PAS-Like Structures Follows the Hierarchy of Atg Proteins.

Epistasis analysis of exocyst and autophagy genes revealed that the exocyst complex is required upstream of PI3K complex I, Atg12–Atg5–Atg16 complex, and Atg2–Atg18 complex. However, Atg2 and Atg18 proteins were accumulated on PAS-like puncta in exocyst ts mutants. Two possibilities may explain this phenomenon. First, multiple Atg2 and Atg18 puncta could be mislocalized due to the loss of exocyst function. Alternatively, Atg proteins required for autophagosome biogenesis may be recruited to PAS hierarchically, later mobilizing to different locations based on their roles. To determine which explanation holds true, we tagged Atg2 with 2xGFP in the background of sec5-24 strain and created double mutants for ATG18, ATG5, and ATG13 genes. We hypothesized that if Atg2 is mislocalized upon loss of exocyst, we should observe multiple puncta irrespective of the double mutant. However, if Atg2 recruitment follows the hierarchical map, Atg2 puncta will only be observed in the double mutant sec5-24 atg5Δ cells as Atg2 recruitment is independent of Atg5 (8). To test this, cells in the log phase were treated with rapamycin at PT and NPT. We did not observe distinct puncta in sec5-24 atg18Δ and sec5-24 atg13Δ, strains at NPT, suggesting that loss of exocyst subunit Sec5 was unable to influence the localization of Atg2 in the absence of Atg18 and Atg13 (Fig. 6 A and B). Furthermore, a single Atg2 punctum was observed in sec5-24 atg5Δ at PT, and multiple Atg2 puncta were observed at NPT, suggesting that recruitment of Atg2 to multiple PAS-like puncta, seen in exocyst mutant, remained independent of Atg5 (Fig. 6 A and B). These results highlight that multiple Atg puncta observed in exocyst mutants are unlikely to be mislocalized, and they do follow the reported hierarchical assembly at PAS (8) (Fig. 6 A and B). To further confirm the hierarchy-dependent localization of Atg2, we tagged Atg8 with RFP in the above strains. Atg8 is a bonafide autophagy marker present in all stages of autophagosome biogenesis (8, 64–69). Atg8 puncta are observed in all autophagy mutants except in mutants of genes involved in Atg8 conjugation, such as ATG5 (8). As expected, in test strains sec5-24 atg18Δ, sec5-24 atg13Δ, sec5-24 atg5Δ, no colocalization events were observed between Atg2 and Atg8 (Fig. 6 C–E). While a single Atg8 punctum was observed in sec5-24 atg13Δ at NPT (Fig. 6C), multiple Atg8 puncta were observed in sec5-24 atg18Δ cells (Fig. 6D), further confirming that exocyst functions upstream of Atg18. No Atg8 puncta were observed in sec5-24 atg5Δ as conjugation of Atg8 depends on Atg5 (Fig. 6E). Thus, our results imply that the observed multiple Atg2 puncta are not mislocalized due to loss of exocyst function. Additionally, our results indicate that upon autophagy induction, Atg proteins localize to the PAS hierarchically and later relocate to the growing phagophore according to their specific functions. This further signifies that the exocyst is responsible for a single functional PAS rather than recruiting Atg proteins to this site.

Discussion

Tethering complexes are critical for bringing two membrane compartments into proximity and facilitating membrane fusion. They are involved in many vesicular trafficking pathways inside the cell, including autophagy (31, 34, 37, 54). An interesting feature of several multisubunit tethers is that they not only share many core components but also have distinct proteins that determine specificity (31, 32, 37, 70). Exocyst, a tethering complex that has an indispensable and well-characterized role in the secretory pathway, has also been shown to play a role in autophagy in yeast, mammals, and plants (42, 44, 53). However, the exact mechanism and composition of the exocyst complex involved in autophagy remain elusive. Our study showed that in yeast, autophagosomal membrane expansion was severely perturbed in the ts mutants of all exocyst subunits, excluding Exo70. Conventionally, the exocyst complex is important for tethering secretory vesicles to the plasma membrane (45, 49). Therefore, exocyst may function similarly by tethering vesicles/membranes to the growing phagophore and participating in membrane expansion. For successful membrane expansion and subsequent autophagosome formation, hierarchical participation of core autophagy machinery involving the Atg1 complex, PI3K complex I, Atg2–Atg18, and Atg12–Atg5–Atg16 complexes is important (8). Based on the results of our epistasis analysis, the exocyst is required downstream of the Atg1 complex and upstream of the PI3K complex I. This suggests that the exocyst complex may function along with Atg9 vesicles for phagophore assembly and subsequent expansion.

Interestingly, upon losing the functional exocyst subcomplex, structures enriched in core Atg proteins surrounded the entire perivacuolar region. This phenotype differed from the core autophagy mutants for membrane expansion, wherein only one defective PAS was observed (8). Upon further investigation, we found that while multiple PAS-like structures having core Atg proteins accumulated, they were incapable of generating autophagosomes. When exocyst function was restored, these structures disappeared, and the cells eventually retained a single functional PAS, as seen in WT cells. Other studies have also reported visualizing similar phenotypes. Studies have shown that loss of both COPII coat components and the COG complex, which are required for the early steps of autophagosome biogenesis, resulted in multiple mislocalized Atg8 puncta (23, 35, 71). One possible explanation is that, since the PAS is a liquid–liquid phase-separated structure (19), the multiple Atg assemblies observed may represent more gel-like states that exhibit reduced liquidity. However, our FRAP results suggest that the liquidity of these structures is similar to WT PAS. Possibly, upon loss of exocyst function, since membrane expansion is affected, PAS becomes dysfunctional, and Atg protein turnover is compromised. Thus, multiple PAS-like structures are formed due to affected Atg protein turnover, which can again be utilized once exocyst function is restored. Another possibility is that these structures are initiation hubs (72) that are unable to form a mature PAS in the absence of the exocyst subcomplex. These results hint that the exocyst complex provides a key component for forming autophagosome-generating functional PAS. However, it remains to be seen what the exact role of the exocyst subcomplex is in ensuring the formation of a single PAS.

Another critical function of the exocyst complex in autophagy is its role in promoting phagophore membrane expansion. Interestingly, other tethering complexes and secretory pathway components also contribute to autophagosome formation through seemingly nonredundant functions with the exocyst. For example, the COPII coat subunit Sec24 is activated by the Hrr25 kinase, enhancing its interaction with Atg9 and subsequent recruitment to PAS (73, 74). A tethering complex, TRAPPIII, activates the Rab GTPase Ypt1, promoting Atg9 trafficking (39, 40). Additionally, the tethering complex COG involved in intra-Golgi trafficking affects the anterograde trafficking of Atg9 to PAS (35, 36). Loss of exocyst phenocopied loss of COPII coat and COG complex components, suggesting that these complexes are required at the same stage of autophagosome biogenesis, most likely for initial phagophore formation and expansion. One important point is that loss of either COPII vesicles, COG complex, or the exocyst subcomplex is enough to block autophagosome biogenesis, suggesting they do not have redundant functions. Further experimentation is needed to uncover the specific and common functions of these complexes in autophagosome biogenesis. Atg9 and COPII vesicles must undergo fusion to form the phagophore. However, the fusion machinery required for this process is uncharacterized. The exocyst complex seems to be a good contender for tethering these vesicles and facilitating fusion. This theory is supported by the evidence that the exocyst is involved downstream of the Atg1 complex when phagophore formation occurs. Additionally, our previous study suggests that colocalization of the exocyst with Atg9 and the PAS is transient and that the exocyst may constantly oscillate between the PAS and secretory compartments (44). Hence, the exocyst may bring more membranes or fusion machinery derived from the secretory pathway toward the PAS to facilitate membrane expansion. Either way, the exocyst is important for the proper functioning of the PAS and membrane expansion.

Furthermore, our results showed that the expansion markers Atg2 and Atg18 are recruited to the PAS independent of exocyst function. This could mean that the Atg proteins required for biogenesis are initially recruited with the help of Atg9 vesicles to the PAS hierarchically and then relocalize on the growing autophagosome based on their function. This is similar to the case wherein Atg8 is seen on the PAS, even in the Atg1 mutant (8). Taken together, our study places the role of the exocyst subcomplex at a stage after the Atg1 complex during phagophore formation and expansion (Fig. 6F). Furthermore, it indicates that failed phagophore formation or expansion results in the loss of PAS integrity and the formation of multiple abortive PAS-like structures. Interestingly, the subunit Exo70, a member of the canonical exocyst complex, was dispensable for autophagy. In the secretory pathway, Exo70 has been shown to interact directly with the plasma membrane via basic residues on its surface, serving as a landmark for the recruitment of exocyst-positive vesicles (75). Hence, this indicates the possibility of a similar protein that might direct exocyst-positive vesicles for autophagosome biogenesis. Further work will shed light on the composition of the autophagy-specific exocyst complex.

Materials and Methods

Yeast Strains and Media.

The details of the strains, plasmids, oligos, and reagents used in this study have been provided in the SI Appendix, Tables S1–S4.

Yeast cells were grown in SD-complete medium (0.17% yeast nitrogen base, 0.5% ammonium sulfate, 2% dextrose, 0.002% uracil, 0.02% histidine, 0.02% methionine, 0.015% lysine, and 0.01% leucine) or YPD medium (2% dextrose, 2% peptone, and 1% yeast extract). Strains with selection markers were grown in dropout medium SD-X (0.17% yeast nitrogen base, 0.5% ammonium sulfate, 2% dextrose, 0.002% uracil, 0.02% histidine, 0.02% methionine, 0.015% lysine, and 0.01% leucine), where X is the amino acid which has not been added for the selection. Autophagy was induced by nitrogen starvation (SD-N) (0.17% yeast nitrogenous base and 2% dextrose) or rapamycin treatment (0.4 µg/mL for 3 h or 6 h). All the strains were maintained and grown at 25°C. Assays were conducted at PT (25°C) and NPT (37°C). Transformation of linear and plasmid DNA was performed using the lithium acetate method (76).

Survival Assay Using Phloxine B Staining.

Yeast cells were grown overnight at 25 °C. A 10 mL secondary culture was inoculated with 0.05 A600 and allowed to grow until it reached the log phase (0.8 to 1 A600). The cells were then washed with sterile water and resuspended in 10 mL nitrogen starvation medium, and an aliquot (0 h time point) was collected. Following this, the culture was then split into two tubes, one was incubated at PT and the other at NPT. Cells were collected at 12 h, 24 h, 36 h, 48 h, 60 h, and 72 h and pelleted. The cell pellet was washed and resuspended in fresh medium containing 2 µg/mL of Phloxine B and mixed by inverting 2 to 3 times. After staining, the cells were centrifuged and washed twice with sterile water to remove the dye background. Then, they were mounted on a 2% agarose bed and imaged using the FITC channel of the fluorescence microscope.

GFP-Atg8 Processing Assay.

Cells expressing GFP-Atg8 (transformed with pRS316-GFP-Atg8, a kind gift from Prof. Yoshinori Ohsumi) were grown in SD-Ura medium. Log phase cells were pelleted and washed twice with sterile water. Following this, cells were resuspended in a nitrogen starvation medium at 3 A600/mL, and an aliquot (0 h time point) was collected. The culture was then split into 3 tubes: PT (25 °C), NPT (37 °C), and Recovery (37 °C for 2 h followed by shifting to 25 °C). Cells were collected at the mentioned time points (Fig. 1D), and trichloroacetic acid (TCA) precipitated. The processed samples were subjected to SDS-PAGE followed by western blotting.

Western Blotting.

Cell pellets (3 A600) were resuspended in 12.5% TCA and stored at −80 °C overnight. After thawing, pellets were washed with 80% acetone, dried, and resuspended in NaOH+SDS and Laemmli buffer. Proteins (0.6 A600 equivalent) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membrane. Immunoblotting was performed using anti-GFP (#11814460001 Merck, Roche Applied Sciences), anti-CPY (kind gift from Prof. Daniel Klionsky), and anti-Myc (#11667149001 Merck, Roche Applied Sciences) antibodies with appropriate secondary antibodies.

Giant Ape1 Assay.

Cells expressing GFP-Atg8 were transformed with plasmid pDP105/pRS315-CUP1pr-BFP-APE1 (CEN plasmid expressing BFP-Ape1 under the control of the CUP1 promoter, a kind gift from Prof. Claudine Kraft). Transformed cells were grown in SD-Ura-Leu medium. The secondary culture was inoculated with 0.05 A600 and allowed to grow up to 0.3 A600. Once the A600 reached 0.25 to 0.35, cells were treated with CuSO4 (250 µM) for 5 h, followed by rapamycin treatment (0.4 µg/mL) for 3 h. Cells were collected at 0 h, 3 h (PT and NPT) time points, and imaged using live cell fluorescence microscopy (58).

1,6-Hexanediol Experiment.

Cells expressing Atg13-2xGFP were grown to log phase and treated with rapamycin (0.4 µg/mL) for 2 h at NPT. Following this, cells were loaded onto the microfluidic device (CellASIC ONIX2 microfluidics system, Microfluidic plates: Y04C-02-5PK), which was prewarmed to 37ºC. The cells were imaged every 1 min for 14 min. For each time frame, 12 z-stack images were collected with a DIC reference image. For 1 min, the media stream was kept at normal flow rate. Subsequently, the stream was switched to media with 10% 1,6-hexanediol (H11807-1 KG, Sigma-Aldrich) and 10 µg/mL digitonin (19550.02, Serva) with a high flow rate (rapid exchange) for 1 min, followed by normal flow rate for 7 min. The media stream was then switched back to media without 1,6-hexanediol and digitonin, with 1 min high flow rate followed by 5 min normal flow rate. The images were deconvoluted using Huygens compute engine 24.04.0p0 64b and were processed in Fiji with a Gaussian Blur with Sigma (Radius): 1.00.

Fluorescence Microscopy and Image Preparation.

Cells were mounted on 2% agarose beads and imaged immediately using the DeltaVision Elite microscope (Leica Microsystems). The images were captured using the 60x objective and a cool SNAP HQ camera. Z-stack images (25 to 30 of 0.2 µm thickness) were captured for every field otherwise specified. Deconvolution and maximum intensity projection were performed using softWoRx software (Leica Microsystems). Representative images were created using softWoRx software (Leica Microsystems) or Fiji software (NIH). Brightness and contrast were adjusted for the entire panel. Brightness and contrast were separately adjusted for the RFP-Atg8 images (Figs. 3 A–D, 4B, and 6 C–E) due to low signal intensity. Similarly, brightness and contrast were separately adjusted for visualization of Atg13 puncta in zoom images for 1,6-hexanediol experiments due to high background in untreated images (Fig. 4E and SI Appendix, Fig. S7 B and E). Blinded manual image analysis was carried out using Fiji (NIH) or softWoRx software (Leica Microsystems) (For additional details, refer to the SI Appendix, Extended Methods).
