Inflammation-resolution signalling in cardiac repair, remodelling, and heart failure
Authors: Ganesh V Halade, Magnus Bäck, Vasundhara Kain
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic unresolved inflammation is a major driver for the genesis of cardiovascular disease, originating from unhealthy lifestyle interactions with a network of metabolic genes impacting overall immune fitness. Acute inflammation is a host defence response overlapping with safe clearance of inflammation, termed as resolution of inflammation, co-ordinated by nutritionally originated fatty acid's interaction with immune-responsive enzymes. Especially processing of polyunsaturated fatty acids by immune-responsive lipoxygenase and cyclooxygenase orchestrates the biosynthesis of specialized proresolving mediators. In contrast, dysregulation due to an imbalanced lifestyle, such as an unhealthy diet, lack of sleep, and exercise/low physical activity, drives non-resolving inflammation. Overall, the quality of fatty acids, enzymatic processing, on-time biosynthesis of SPMs, and precise activation of SPM-specific receptors operate cardiac repair in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction; however, the dysfunction of specific receptors, such as FPR2, drives obesogenic ageing and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction. Thus, the overlapping inflammation–resolution signalling pathways that are essential for cardiac repair and the prevention of cardiac damage are highly relevant to cardiometabolic disorders and the subsequent development of heart failure. Therefore, future research is warranted to study lifestyle factors that maintain the balance of inflammation-resolution signalling in cardiac health and develop new therapeutic targets for resolution medicine.

Introduction—gene vs. environment

Cardiac health is a cornerstone of overall human well-being. Since 1921, it has remained a persistent public health burden globally. Each year, cardiovascular diseases (CVD) claim nearly 17.9 million lives worldwide. In the twenty-first century, significant lifestyle changes and increased reliance on digitalization have contributed to a 2% annual rise in the risk of myocardial infarction (MI) among individuals under 40 years of age.1 This review focuses on the two fundamental and intertwined determinants of cardiovascular health: Genetic predisposition (‘intrinsic’) and environmental exposure (‘extrinsic’). These elements interact through shared pathways involving immune fitness and the resolution of inflammation, both of which hinge critically on the functional competence of circulating leucocytes to mount appropriate immune responses and engage resolution pathways (quality of circulating leucocytes) (Figure 1). Together, these intrinsic and extrinsic determinants converge on pathways that govern cardiovascular risk, with genetic variation providing the baseline susceptibility on which environmental factors act. Genetic susceptibility to CVD arises from both rare monogenic and common polygenic variants. Although less common, monogenic variants show high penetrance and are strongly associated with arrhythmogenic and cardiomyopathic disorders such as ARVC, HCM, and LQTS, often leading to sudden cardiac death. Mutations in LDLR, APOB, and PCSK9 are well-established causes of familial hypercholesterolaemia, which significantly increase the risk of early-onset MI.2–4 Similarly, variants in GCK are linked to diabetes, KCNQ1 to atrial fibrillation, and F5 to venous thrombo-embolic disease. The contribution of discrete genetic lesions to cardiovascular and metabolic diseases.5 Notably, the TTR p.Val142Ile mutation is prevalent among African Americans but remains underdiagnosed despite its association with transthyretin amyloidosis.6 Moreover, truncating mutations in TTN are now recognized as significant contributors to dilated cardiomyopathy.7 In contrast to monogenic variants, polygenic variants are present in at least 1% of the population and individually exert modest effects. Over the past 15 years, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have revealed the polygenic architecture of cardiometabolic diseases, implicating numerous risk-associated single-nucleotide variants (SNVs). Although individual SNVs confer modest risk, polygenic scores enable stratification of individuals at significantly elevated CVD risk, even without traditional clinical factors.8–10

Immune and metabolic gene (nature) interaction with environment (nurture) regulates cardiac health and disease: gene-guided intrinsic factors (damage-associated molecular patterns, pathogen-associated molecular patterns, homeostasis-associated molecular patterns) influence leucocyte quality and inflammatory tone, while environmental factors such as diet, exercise, and sleep modulate immune fitness. The interaction of inherited predisposition and lifestyle choices determines long-term outcomes in heart health and disease.

Along with the monogenic and polygenic variants, epigenetic mechanisms such as DNA methylation, histone modifications, and non-coding RNAs modulate gene expression without altering DNA sequence, thereby reprogramming immune and stromal cells in response to environmental cues.11,12 Direct experimental evidence now underscores their functional relevance to inflammation resolution process actively governed by gene networks that terminate inflammation, restore tissue homeostasis, and promote repair. In macrophages, genetic deletion of HDAC3 lifts an ‘epigenomic brake’ on alternative activation, promoting anti-inflammatory and pro-resolving programmes.13 In post-MI models, pharmacologic inhibition of the chromatin reader BRD4 (BET bromodomain inhibitor JQ1) suppresses maladaptive inflammatory and fibrotic gene networks, improves ventricular remodelling, and enhances survival.14–16 Similarly, BRD4 occupancy in cardiac fibroblasts has been shown to orchestrate profibrotic transcriptional programmes after myocardial injury, positioning fibroblast epigenetic rewiring as a central driver of remodelling.17 Beyond chromatin modifications, microRNAs also act as epigenetic regulators of resolution; for instance, miR-146b, induced by IL-10 in human monocytes, suppresses TLR/NF-κB signalling to dampen inflammation and reinforce resolution.18 Together, these findings highlight epigenetic mechanisms as a dynamic interface between environment and gene regulation, shaping macrophage plasticity, fibroblast activation, and post-MI healing, while also opening avenues for therapeutic modulation in CVD.

Beyond genetics, the concept of immune fitness is driven by ‘leucocyte quality’ and plays a central role in cardiovascular health by modulating inflammation and influencing the resolution process. This balance is profoundly influenced by environmental factors through molecular signalling cues, including homeostasis-associated molecular patterns (HAMPs), damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), lifestyle-associated molecular patterns (LAMPs), age-associated molecular patterns (AAMPs), and stress-associated molecular patterns (SAMPs). HAMPs promote leucocyte functionality and immune equilibrium through favourable inputs from diet, physical activity, and sleep, key pillars of preventive cardiology first emphasized by Paul Dudley White, the father of the field. These patterns regulate the composition of bioactive lipids that shape leucocyte phenotypes, supporting inflammation resolution and preserving leucocyte quality, thereby maintaining ‘immune fitness’.1,19,20 However, an imbalance of lifestyle pillars drives progression from atherogenesis to atherosclerosis. This shift is associated with the activation of DAMPs, particularly following MI, or PAMPs in the context of infections such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa. In recent years, sedentary lifestyles and consumption of ultra-processed diets promote LAMPs, which impair leucocyte calibration, hinder inflammation resolution, and contribute to chronic immune dysfunction.21,22

DAMPs and PAMPs are key activators of innate immunity in CVD. DAMPs are released following tissue injury or cell death and upon recognition by macrophages, trigger proinflammatory signalling through Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and inflammasomes. These signals originate from extracellular matrix proteins (biglycan and tenascin C), intracellular molecules (high-HMGB1, histones, S100 proteins, and HSPs) and plasma-derived proteins (fibrinogen, Gc-globulin, and SAA).23–25 In contrast, PAMPs are exogenous molecular signatures derived from pathogens, including lipopolysaccharides (Gram-negative bacteria), teichoic acids (Gram-positive bacteria), zymosans (yeast), glycolipids (mycobacteria), and double-stranded RNA. Recent evidence indicates cardiac PRRs also detect host-derived danger signals from necrotic, necroptotic, or apoptotic myocardial cells, activating robust sterile inflammation through both extracellular and intracellular pathways.

HAMPs are receiving increasing attention due to their regulation by lifestyle factors, particularly diet quality, physical activity, and sleep. These lifestyle inputs influence the quantity and composition of bioactive lipids that shape leucocyte phenotypes and immune responses (Figure 1—left panel).1,26–28 Although HAMPs reflect leucocyte abundance and function, they are strongly modulated by the environmental influences that ‘nurture’ health, including LAMPs, AAMPs, and SAMPs. LAMPs are driven by behavioural factors such as poor diet, physical inactivity, and sleep deprivation, conditions often exacerbated by prolonged screen time. These factors negatively impact immune health by impairing leucocyte quality and resolution of inflammation. Ageing and obesity further amplify these disruptions, contributing to the generation of AAMPs and increasing susceptibility to cardiovascular and other chronic diseases. The adverse effects of co-medications, such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and chemotherapeutic agents like doxorubicin, are intensified in older adults, compounding age-related cardiovascular vulnerability.29–31

The psychological stress has now been formally recognized by the American Heart Association as an independent risk factor for CVD and is categorized under SAMPs. Acute stressors like grief and trauma contribute to 3–4% of cardiac events, while chronic stressors such as financial or interpersonal difficulties further increase cardiovascular risk. The impact of SAMPs is exacerbated by health-risk behaviour such as smoking, alcohol consumption, and narcotic drugs.32 Notably, frequent use of opioids significantly increases the risk of CVD by promoting inflammation, oxidative stress, and vascular dysfunction (Figure 1—right panel—Environment).1,33 Collectively, these environmental and behavioural factors modulate cardiac metabolism and directly influence immune fitness, a key driver of inflammation-resolution and long-term cardiometabolic health. By affecting leucocyte quality and the body's capacity to respond to damage or infection, lifestyle factors play a pivotal role in preserving cardiovascular and metabolic well-being.

Lifestyle associated molecular patterns and genesis of chronic inflammation: linking lifestyle to immune dysfunction and cardiovascular risk

LAMPs are the endogenous cellular signals triggered by poor diet, lack of exercise/physical activity, or insufficient sleep. These signals activate inflammatory pathways and disrupt ‘immune fitness,’ a key determinant of the body's ability to resolve inflammation. Diet, exercise, and sleep are the core factors of lifestyle medicine that regulate LAMPs activity. While immune function is influenced by both non-modifiable factors, such as ageing34 and sex,35 and modifiable factors such as sleep, physical activity, stress, smoking, and nutrition, that present opportunities for clinical intervention.27,36–41 Chronic exposure to adverse LAMP signals contributes to low-grade, persistent inflammation, which, over time, may transition into overt cardiometabolic disease. Diet/nutrition represents the first and most influential modifiable LAMP factor, as food intake directly shapes the gut microbiome and immune responses, thereby linking nutrition to immune fitness and cardiovascular risk. Nutritional patterns alter the production of proinflammatory mediators (PIMs) and SPMs.40 The essential dietary components are the n−3 and n−6 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), from which PIMs and SPMs are derived. The n−3 and n−6 fatty acids cannot be synthesized endogenously and therefore must be obtained through the diet. Within the human body, these PUFAs include arachidonic acid (AA, n−6), eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA, n−3), and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA, n−3), each of which plays a central role in maintaining physiological homeostasis. Their downstream metabolites include bioactive lipid mediators such as eicosanoids, which exert potent local signalling effects. Generally, AA metabolism generates pro-inflammatory eicosanoids except lipoxins, whereas EPA- and DHA-derived products give rise to anti-inflammatory lipid mediators, together shaping the balance between inflammation and resolution signalling. Dietary intake of PUFA is largely skewed towards n−6 fatty acids, as most commonly consumed crop seeds and vegetable oils such as soybean, corn, safflower, and sunflower, which are rich in linoleic acid (LA) but provide only limited amounts of the n−3 fatty acid α-linolenic acid (ALA). In contrast, dietary sources of plan-based n−3 PUFAs are relatively scarce; notable exceptions include chia, perilla, and flax seeds, as well as green leafy vegetables that are enriched in ALA. Animal-based foods such as fish (mackerel, salmon), fish oils, and marine animal products provide long-chain n−3 PUFAs (EPA, DPA, DHA) and AA, which can be directly utilized for physiological functions. Importantly, wild marine and cold-water fish contain higher levels of n−3 PUFAs compared to farmed or warm-water species, reflecting their natural diets and environmental adaptations.42,43 In the USA, omega-6 intake has doubled44 due to seed oils like soybean, corn, and safflower, pushing omega-6/3 ratios up to 60:1–77:1.45 This dietary shift has coincided with increased accumulation of LA in adipose tissue up to 2.5-fold and a rise in inflammatory, autoimmune, and allergic diseases.46 Omega-6-derived PIMs like 12-HETE, prostaglandins, and thromboxane exacerbate chronic inflammation and metabolic dysfunction in CVD.41,47,48 Notably, such chronic inflammatory and metabolic effects of excess omega-6 intake extend to cardiovascular health. The studies have shown that prolonged intake of n−6 PUFAs is implicated in several CVD.41,49 From a diet perspective, the CARDIA (Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults) study shows that sustained exposure to cholesterol-raising diets in early adulthood, which are often rich in saturated fats and omega-6-dominant seed oils, leads to elevated LDL-C levels that accelerate atherosclerotic changes. Even if dietary habits improve and LDL-C levels are lowered later in life, the vascular injury initiated earlier cannot be fully reversed, leaving a persistent risk for CVD after age 40. This highlights the importance of adopting heart-healthy dietary patterns early in life, emphasizing fruits, vegetables, fibre, and omega-3–rich foods such as fish, flaxseed, and chia as a foundation for sustained LDL-C control and long-term cardiovascular protection and lipid management.50

The second modifiable LAMP factor is exercise, which confers broad pro-resolving and cardiometabolic benefits. It lowers the risk of coronary artery disease, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and peripheral artery disease, and improves outcomes across these conditions.51,52 Mechanistically, exercise promotes Tregs (Foxp3+CD4+), an anti-inflammatory T lymphocyte subset that suppresses excessive immune activation, while simultaneously limiting the production of interferon-γ (IFN-γ), a pro-inflammatory cytokine that drives metabolic and mitochondrial dysfunction.53 Human studies reveal that higher physical activity levels reduce the recruitment of pro-inflammatory CD16⁺ monocytes, particularly in individuals with central obesity.54 In preclinical models, exercise blunts obesity-induced cardiac inflammation, suppressing pro-inflammatory bioactive lipid mediators.27 However, these benefits are modulated by exercise mode, intensity, frequency, and patient-specific factors such as age and disease status.

The third modifiable LAMP factor is sleep, a crucial lifestyle and metabolic factor influencing cardiovascular health. Chronic sleep deprivation and irregular or fragmented sleep are strongly associated with increased risks of hypertension, dyslipidaemia, and atherosclerosis, particularly among night-shift workers and firefighters, an occupational group well recognized for chronic sleep disruption and elevated cardiovascular risk.55,56 The MESA Sleep Ancillary Study found that variability in sleep duration (standard deviation >120 min) and sleep timing (standard deviation >90 min) correlated with elevated coronary artery calcium and abnormal vascular indices independent of sleep apnoea.57 Mechanistically, sleep modifies the epigenetic landscape of haematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs), enhancing proliferation of lineage−Sca1 + cKit+ (LSK) cells. This expansion elevates circulating Ly6Chi monocytes, reduces clonal diversity, and increases inflammatory priming, underscoring sleep's critical role in preserving haematopoietic integrity and immune balance.36 Sleep also strengthens innate immunity through increased classical monocyte activity, ROS production, and pathogen clearance by circadian clock-dependent mechanisms involving the clock gene Arntl.58 In obese murine models, sleep fragmentation after MI reduced pro-resolving mediators (RvD2, RvD3, RvD5, LXA4, PD1, MaR1) and elevated inflammatory mediators (PGs: PGD2, PGE2, PGF2α, 6k-PGF1α) and cytokines, impairing cardiac healing and exacerbating systemic inflammation.26Thus, healthy and consistent sleep is essential for cardiovascular and immune homeostasis, with disruptions accelerating inflammation, impairing resolution pathways, and increasing the risk of adverse cardiac outcomes.

The fourth and fifth modifiable LAMPs are psychological stress and smoking, both of which amplify LAMP signalling, heighten inflammatory burden, and significantly contribute to CVD risk. A neuroimaging study in The Lancet linked emotional stress to cardiovascular events via increased activity in the salience network and bone marrow, driving arterial inflammation.59 Smoking is a well-documented contributor to both cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. Logistic risk models from the 2024 American Heart Association Heart Disease and Stroke Statistics Report incorporate eight major risk factors: age, sex, systolic blood pressure, total cholesterol, HDL-C, diabetes, treatment for hypertension, and cigarette smoking to estimate cardiovascular risk. Among these, cigarette smoking emerges as a particularly strong and modifiable determinant, with effect sizes showing a substantially elevated risk for both coronary heart disease and stroke in current smokers compared with non-smokers.1 Importantly, passive smoking has also been shown to induce endothelial dysfunction, further broadening its adverse effects on coronary flow velocity reserve.60 Studies quantify that smoking is a lifetime risk for cardiovascular subtypes, accounting for non-cardiovascular deaths. Furthermore, smoking has been directly linked to fatal events as the initial manifestation of clinical CVD, emphasizing its role in severe and acute outcomes.61,62

Thus, modifiable lifestyle factors, including diet, exercise, sleep, stress management, and smoking cessation, play a critical role in shaping immune fitness and cardiovascular health, thereby potentially adding an additional decade to lifespan.63 Addressing these factors through targeted interventions offers a promising strategy to decrease chronic inflammation, support immune homeostasis, and reduce the burden of CVD and systemic diseases.

Leucocyte-mediated inflammation and resolution in cardiac injury and repair

The immune response, which is part of host defence, drives both initiation and resolution of inflammation, crucial for clearing harmful stimuli and restoring homeostasis in heart failure. Although leucocytes constitute <1% of the total blood volume, they are central to cardiac and total systemic health by regulating inflammation, clearing cardiac injury or pathogens, and promoting myocardium tissue repair.64 While inflammation is essential, a resolution must occur in a timely and co-ordinated manner to ensure optimal cardiac health, given overlapping phases of inflammation-resolution signalling.65,66

Acute inflammation facilitates the clearance of necrotic cells and triggers reparative pathways. However, the lack of an appropriate resolving response can exacerbate cardiac injury. Observational studies and randomized clinical trials, such as the PRECISION trial and CLASS trial, have shown an increased incidence of cardiovascular events in NSAID users, especially in those with pre-existing heart failure or high cardiovascular risk.29,67 Similarly, RENEWAL trial targeting TNF-α in heart failure was halted for lack of efficacy, underscoring the limitations of non-specific immunosuppression. Instead of blocking inflammation, promoting endogenous resolution pathways may offer a more effective and safer alternative.68 Resolution of inflammation involves a co-ordinated leucocyte response across three phases: entry (initiation), time-in-transit (repair), and exit (termination). During cardiac healing, distinct leucocyte subsets, including neutrophils, monocytes, and macrophages, execute a tightly regulated, temporally co-ordinated overlapping inflammation-resolution programme essential for effective tissue repair and functional recovery.

Neutrophils are the first/primary responders in an ischaemic injury and myocarditis, where they play a key role in clearing necrotic myocyte debris and countering invading pathogens.69 However, their clinical significance in MI remains complex. The trial CLARITY-TIMI 28 (randomized) reported conflicting results regarding the association between peripheral neutrophil counts and 30-day outcomes in ST-elevation MI patients.70 Another fibrinolysis trial in patients with ST-elevation MI found no significant association between neutrophil counts and clinical outcomes.71 In contrast, elevated neutrophil counts at presentation have been shown to independently predict higher mortality risk up to three years post-MI.72 In pathogen-free healthy mice (C57BL/6 J), neutrophil infiltration peaks immediately after MI and remains elevated until Day 3, subsiding by Day 5. Clinically, a high neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (N/Lmax) is strongly associated with major adverse cardiac events and long-term mortality.73,74 For instance, Nunez et al. showed that an N/Lmax hazardous risk ratio of 2.58 persisted even after adjusting for standard risk factors.74 Elevated neutrophil numbers within 12 h of acute MI have been linked to subsequent heart failure.75 Preclinical and clinical studies further reveal that MI incidence peaks in the morning due to the overactivation of neutrophils, influenced by circadian rhythms.76 Notably, while excessive neutrophil activity contributes to adverse remodelling, experimental neutrophil depletion in MI models worsens cardiac function, increases fibrosis and mortality, and accelerates heart failure progression.77

Timely recruitment of monocytes/macrophages, alongside neutrophils, serves as an effective ‘engineer’ of the immune response. Immune cells leverage their strong chemotactic abilities and phenotypic plasticity to co-ordinate inflammation and repair. These cells communicate dynamically with neutrophils to activate the mononuclear phagocyte system, orchestrating both the initiation and resolution of inflammation. Depending on environmental cues, monocytes/macrophages adopt pro-resolution or anti-inflammatory phenotypes, influencing cardiovascular outcomes. Following MI, monocytes are primarily mobilized from bone marrow and blood, supplemented by angiotensin II-driven recruitment from splenic reservoirs.78 These monocytes contribute to cardiac repair and convert PUFA (AA, DHA, and EPA) to SPMs (E-and D-series Resolvins, Lipoxins, and Maresins). Monocyte/macrophage depletion disrupts this enzymatic activity, reducing SPM production and impairing cardiac repair, resulting in higher rupture rates and mortality.65,79

Macrophages further regulate cardiac regeneration by balancing fibrotic repair and promoting pro-regeneration through Arg1 expression and modulation of Ly6Clo and Ly6Chi monocyte populations. In zebrafish, csf1ra⁺ and mpeg1⁺ macrophages transition from tnfa⁻ to tnfa⁺ states during injury, co-ordinating resolution signals at the epicardial-myocardial niche and upregulating Vegfaa to promote cardiomyocyte proliferation.80 Emerging evidence shows that macrophages facilitate extracellular matrix (ECM) remodelling by depositing collagen; GFP-tagged collagen⁺ monocytes infiltrate injured myocardium to form fibrotic scars. Similarly, zebrafish macrophages expressing col4a3bpa localize collagen at injury sites, and genetic disruption of this phenotype reduces scarring.81

Following MI, damaged cardiomyocytes release DAMPs and alarmins, recruiting leucocytes. CCR2⁺ macrophages amplify inflammation by secreting chemokines and cytokines, hindering resolution.82 Therapeutically, targeting inflammatory pathways, such as IL-1β inhibition in the CANTOS trial, reduced cardiovascular events and heart failure hospitalizations in patients with elevated C-reactive protein but increased fatal infections and sepsis risks.83,84 These findings highlight the necessity for targeted immunomodulation strategies rather than broad immune suppression.

Macrophages, as key regulators of cardiac repair, engage in dynamic crosstalk with fibroblasts to shape post-MI remodelling and fibrosis. Macrophages are highly plastic immune cells that adopt distinct activation states in response to local cues. They are broadly divided into two categories: (i) Classically activated M1 macrophages (proinflammatory), characterized by markers such as iNOS, CD80, CD86, and TNF-α, which display a pro-inflammatory phenotype that promotes pathogen clearance and tissue injury. (ii) Alternatively activated M2 macrophages (proresolution), expressing Arginase-1, CD163, CD206 (MRC1), and IL-10, support tissue repair, resolution of inflammation, and extracellular matrix remodelling.85,86 While this M1/M2 paradigm provides a functional framework, advances in single-cell RNA sequencing and genetic fate mapping have revealed a more nuanced spectrum of macrophage subsets in the heart. In cardiac tissue, TIMD4+, LYVE1+, and FOLR2+ macrophages represent embryonically derived, self-renewing tissue-resident populations with homeostatic roles, whereas CCR2 and MHC-II⁺ monocyte-derived macrophages dominate in the setting of injury and contribute to inflammatory and fibrotic remodelling.87,88 Among these recruited subsets, SPP1+ macrophages have gained attention as profibrotic regulators. These cells often co-express FABP5, GPNMB, CD9, and TREM2, and have been identified in scRNA-seq datasets and validated in models of lung and liver fibrosis, where they drive TGFβ1-induced collagen I production.89,90 In the infarcted heart, SPP1⁺ macrophages cluster in fibrotic regions near activated fibroblasts, underscoring their direct immune–stromal crosstalk. Through paracrine signalling, they amplify fibroblast activation, collagen deposition, and extracellular matrix accumulation, thereby contributing to maladaptive remodelling and impaired scar stabilization.89,91 The spatial and functional interaction highlights how macrophage heterogeneity critically shapes post-MI outcomes, balancing repair with pathological fibrosis.

Macrophages contribute to cardiac repair in part through efferocytosis, the clearance of apoptotic cells that prevents secondary necrosis and uncontrolled inflammation. Efferocytosis is the highly regulated process by which phagocytes, predominantly macrophages, recognize, engulf, and degrade apoptotic cells to maintain tissue homeostasis. The term derives from the Latin effero (‘to take to the grave’), reflecting its role as the physiological burial of dying cells. Unlike necrosis, apoptosis is an immunologically silent form of cell death, and efficient efferocytosis ensures that cellular debris is removed without triggering inflammation. Mechanistically, efferocytosis involves three co-ordinated steps: (i) recognition of apoptotic cells, mainly through exposure of ‘eat-me’ signals such as phosphatidylserine; (ii) tethering and engulfment, mediated by phagocytic receptors such as MerTK, TIM-4, and stabilin-2 via bridging ligands including Gas6, Protein S, or MFG-E8; and (iii) intracellular processing, which activates anti-inflammatory and pro-resolving programmes MerTK recognizes phosphatidylserine on dying cells (via bridging ligands like Gas6 or Protein S), which triggers engulfment of the apoptotic cell and activates anti-inflammatory programmes.92,93 In MI, MerTK-dependent efferocytosis drives engulfment of apoptotic cardiomyocytes and neutrophils, leading to macrophage reprogramming and the release of IL-10, TGF-β, and SPMs.94 Loss of MerTK impairs efferocytosis in atherosclerotic lesions, leading to apoptotic cell accumulation and necrotic core formation. In a 16-week ApoE−/− atherosclerosis model, Ang II exposure reduced MerTK expression on lesional macrophages through ADAM17-mediated ectodomain shedding, leading to impaired efferocytosis and increased necrotic core formation.95

MerTK also plays a key role in post-MI cardiac remodelling. Mertk−/−Mfge8−/− mice exhibit impaired efferocytosis, diminished VEGFA release, and worsened cardiac function and remodelling.96 In STEMI patients, elevated serum soluble MerTK (solMER) suggests defective phagocytic function, while murine ischaemia/reperfusion (I/R) studies demonstrate that MerTK deficiency limits recruitment of reparative MHCIIloCCR2− macrophages.97 These approaches reveal the myeloid system's pivotal role in inflammation, repair, and disease progression, highlighting its potential for therapeutic targeting.

In summary, leucocytes, particularly neutrophils and macrophages, play central roles in orchestrating inflammation and its resolution during cardiac injury and repair. Neutrophils initiate debris clearance and pathogen defence, whereas macrophages regulate inflammation, fibrosis, and regeneration via phenotypic plasticity and context-specific responses. Advanced technologies like CyTOF and scRNA-seq have revealed distinct leucocyte subsets and dynamics in resolution programmes across disease models. Targeting macrophage-driven inflammatory and pro-resolving pathways, and their crosstalk with stromal and immune cells, offers promising therapeutic avenues in inflammatory and fibrotic heart diseases.

Specialized pro-resolving mediator axis—lipoxygenase-mediated biosynthesis of resolution mediators in cardiac inflammation and repair

SPMs are bioactive lipids enzymatically biosynthesized from essential PUFA (Figure 2). SPMs actively orchestrate the resolution of inflammation and the restoration of tissue homeostasis. They serve as key regulators that balance the initiation of inflammation with its timely resolution and are generated through the actions of lipoxygenases (LOXs), cyclooxygenases (COXs), and cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes, primarily in immune cells. In cardiac pathology, these mediators counteract chronic low-grade inflammation, often driven by DAMPs, pathogens, ageing, and lifestyle-related stressors. While leucocytes are frequently implicated in heart failure progression due to their pro-inflammatory responses, they also contribute to repair by producing SPMs.98 These mediators actively suppress ongoing inflammation, promote tissue healing, and support the return to physiological balance.99 Among these enzymatic pathways, LOXs are immune-responsive enzymes that operate at a critical junction between inflammation and resolution. By acting on PUFAs such as AA, DHA, EPA, and DPA, LOXs generate a wide spectrum of bioactive lipid mediators, ranging from pro-inflammatory leukotrienes to SPMs including lipoxins, resolvins, protectins, and maresins (Figure 2).100 These iron-dependent enzymes oxygenate PUFAs, producing bioactive lipid mediators that orchestrate key cellular processes in both animals and plants. In humans, six functional LOX genes, ALOX15, ALOX15B, ALOX12, ALOX12B, ALOXE3, and ALOX5, encode distinct isoforms, each with unique substrate preferences and biological roles.101 Notably, ALOX5, the gene that resides on chromosome 10, while the other LOX family members are clustered on chromosome 17, hinting at shared regulatory mechanisms. In mice, this genomic arrangement is conserved, with alox5 located on chromosome 6 and the remaining isoforms on chromosome 11.102 Functionally, LOXs help shape the inflammatory landscape by catalysing the initial steps in the biosynthesis of leukotrienes, lipoxins, and SPMs. Their activity has been implicated in a wide range of inflammatory and immune-related diseases, including arthritis, psoriasis, asthma, and CVD.103–105 As our understanding deepens, these enzymes are now recognized as both markers and regulators of resolution, highlighting their potential as therapeutic targets in chronic inflammation and heart failure.

Lipid mediators biosynthesis pathways from polyunsaturated fatty acids in inflammation and resolution. Distinct lipid mediator pathways shape inflammation and resolution. Arachidonic acid generates prostaglandins, thromboxane, and leukotrienes that amplify inflammation except lipoxins. In contrast, docosahexaenoic acid and eicosapentaenoic acid metabolize to specialized pro-resolving mediators, including resolvins, protectins, maresins, and lipoxins from arachidonic acid that resolve inflammation and restore tissue homeostasis.

In the context of cardiac injury and heart failure, LOXs contribute to tissue repair and immune homeostasis by regulating immune kinetics and phenotypes. Both 12-/15-LOX and 5-LOX act on distinct fatty acids to generate mediators with diverse and sometimes opposing biological effects. For instance, lipoxins A4 and B4, derived from AA via 12- and 15-LOX activity, serve as key modulators of inflammation. They inhibit neutrophil chemotaxis, reduce vascular adhesion, and actively promote the resolution phase by enhancing the macrophage-mediated clearance of apoptotic neutrophils.106

Importantly, these lipoxins counterbalance the effects of leukotrienes B4 and C4, which are potent drivers of vascular injury, vasoconstriction, and neutrophil-endothelial interactions. Through this regulatory axis, LOXs modulate inflammation and protect against immune overactivation, underscoring their therapeutic relevance in heart failure and inflammatory heart disease.

5LOX plays a pivotal role in orchestrating inflammation resolution and tissue repair following MI. It catalyses the production of SPMs, including D-series resolvins, PD1, maresin-1, and LXA4, that are essential for clearing inflammation and supporting cardiac healing.

In the experimental MI models, global deletion of ALOX5 reduced survival to 36% markedly increased the incidence of ventricular rupture (65%) compared with WT, which typically has 56–58% survival and 28–37% rupture rates (Figure 3A, upper panel). Following MI, mechanistically, 5-LOX deficiency reduced SPM biosynthesis while enhancing COX expression and prostaglandin F1α levels. This imbalance hinders neutrophil clearance, prolongs inflammation, disrupts repair, and increases myocardial rupture risk in acute heart failure (Figure 3A, lower panel).107 In humans, ALOX5 promoter variants are associated with greater atherogenesis and carotid thickening, especially with high n−6 PUFA intake, while n−3 PUFA-rich diets offer protection.106 A specific 5-LOX variant associated with coronary stenosis (>50%) in a smaller cohort (n = 3770),108 was not confirmed in larger studies, including ARIC (n = 13 152)109 and MESA (n = 6153),110 even after adjusting for PUFA intake. Beyond resolution, 5-LOX promotes inflammatory initiation via LTB4 production, essential for early immune responses but potentially pathogenic if dysregulated. Elevated 5-LOX activity increases cytokines (IL-6, MCP-1, TNF-α), driving atherogenesis and chronic cardiovascular inflammation.111 In essence, 5-LOX sits at the crossroads of inflammation and resolution, and its activity must be tightly regulated to support cardiac recovery without tipping the scale towards pathological inflammation.

12/15-LOX is widely expressed across multiple tissues, including eosinophils, dendritic cells, alveolar macrophages, airway epithelium, vascular cells, and cardiomyocytes, where it influences both local and systemic inflammation.112,113 In the context of myocardial injury, 12/15-LOX emerges as a critical driver of cardiac inflammation, fibrosis, and adverse remodelling. Overexpression of 12/15-LOX in cardiomyocytes promotes heart failure by upregulating MCP-1/CCL2, enhancing macrophage infiltration, and accelerating fibrotic remodelling, leading to systolic dysfunction.114 In contrast, genetic deletion of 12/15-LOX significantly improves post-MI outcomes, increasing survival to ∼85% and reducing rupture incidence to just 7% in mice (Figure 3B, upper panel). Mechanistically, loss of 12/15-LOX shifts eicosanoid biosynthesis towards CYP-derived epoxy fatty acids (5,6-, 8,9-, 11,12-, and 14,15-EETs) while lowering 12(S)-HETE levels. This biochemical switch promotes anti-inflammatory macrophage and neutrophil polarization, delays heart failure progression, and supports tissue repair (Figure 3B, lower panel).104 The role of 12/15-LOX in immune cell plasticity is particularly striking. Macrophage-specific 12/15-LOX deletion enhances immune plasticity, improves post-MI survival to 88–91%, and reduces rupture rates to under 1% (Figure 3C, upper panel). These mice show increased Ly6Clo macrophages and Tregs across the splenocardiac axis, indicating a co-ordinated systemic response promoting cardiac repair (Figure 3C, lower panel).115 Interestingly, the role of 12/15-LOX is context-dependent. While its inhibition is protective in heart failure, deletion in arthritis models worsens inflammation and tissue injury, likely due to reduced LXA4 levels and increased p38MAPK activation in macrophages.105 These findings underscore the importance of tissue- and disease-specific targeting when considering 12/15-LOX as a therapeutic axis.

Both 12/15-LOX and 5-LOX are central to the biosynthesis of resolution mediators that facilitate the resolution of inflammation and promote cardiac healing. A prime example is the D-series resolvins, derived from DHA, which are synthesized through the co-ordinated actions of 15-LOX and 5-LOX. Among these, Resolvin D1 (RvD1) stands out as a potent mediator that enhances resolution by limiting neutrophil swarming, suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, CCL2, IL-1β), and recruiting quiescent monocytes to sites of injury.99 These combined actions lead to improved MI outcomes and provide renoprotective effects in heart failure.116 Maresin, another class of DHA-derived SPMs generated via the 12-LOX pathway, contributes to cardiac healing by promoting apoptotic cell clearance, dampening inflammation, and encouraging macrophage polarization towards a reparative, pro-resolving phenotype.117,118 However, ageing disrupts this balance: infarcted hearts from aged mice exhibit reduced mRNA expression of 5-LOX, 12-LOX, and 15-LOX, leading to diminished D- and E-series resolvin production and impaired resolution capacity.34

Together, 12/15-LOX and 5-LOX play complementary roles in cardiac recovery, while 12/15-LOX modulates macrophage plasticity and promotes resolution, and 5-LOX is essential for generating resolvins. By integrating their activity, the dual LOX system helps restore immune homeostasis and tissue integrity after myocardial injury.

Molecular determinants of cardiac injury repair and inflammation resolution

Beyond LOX enzymes, a network of molecular players co-ordinates inflammation resolution, scar formation, and myocardial structural repair following infarction. Disruption of these pathways often leads to impaired healing and increased risk of cardiac rupture. For instance, Fibulin-3 (EFEMP1), an extracellular matrix glycoprotein, is essential for stabilizing collagen in the infarct zone. Its deficiency compromises scar integrity, predisposing to myocardial rupture in murine models.119 Similarly, loss of ALKBH5, an m6A RNA demethylase, disrupts fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transition, weakening fibrotic repair.120 The cytokine Interleukin-22 (IL-22) supports extracellular matrix remodelling after injury; its deletion intensifies inflammation and reduces tissue strength, increasing rupture susceptibility.121 Intercalated disc proteins—PKP2, DSG2, CTNNA1, and JUP maintain cardiomyocyte adhesion and coupling; knockout models reveal their loss leads to desmosomal failure, mechanical instability, and rupture under stress.122–125 From an immuno-regulatory standpoint, Par4 deletion delays inflammation resolution and impairs infarct healing, increasing the risk of rupture. On the metabolic axis, HIF-1α promotes p53-driven apoptosis and infarct thinning, whereas balanced p53 deletion limits border-zone apoptosis and reduces rupture risk.126 Transcriptional control of macrophage function is also crucial. Klf9 deletion disrupts resolution by altering IFN-γ–STAT1 signalling, leading to greater rupture incidence and impaired healing.127 Similarly, MuRF3 (Trim54), a muscle-specific ubiquitin ligase, though dispensable in normal physiology, is required for post-MI mechanical stability; its absence results in significantly higher rupture rates.128

In addition to acute infarct repair, chronic low-grade inflammation also contributes to emerging heart failure phenotypes, particularly obese HFpEF.129 In contrast to heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), which arises from various aetiologies including ischaemic injury leading to maladaptive remodelling and maladaptive remodelling, obese HFpEF arises from systemic metabolic stress, visceral adiposity, and immune–stromal crosstalk. In this setting, adipose-derived cytokines such as IL-6, TNF-α, and leptin recruit and activate innate immune cells, promoting endothelial dysfunction, fibrosis, and myocardial stiffening. Recent work has demonstrated that inflammation is highly prevalent in obese HFpEF and correlates with adverse remodelling and clinical symptoms.130 Circulating immune profiling shows a heterogeneous and pro-inflammatory signature in HFpEF patients compared with HFrEF, marked by expansion of pro-inflammatory monocytes and T cells.131 Multi-omics analyses further confirm that immune and inflammatory pathways are enriched in HFpEF plasma proteome and metabolome, reinforcing the role of immune activation.132 Importantly, impaired resolution programmes, such as reduced SPM generation or defective macrophage polarization, may perpetuate this inflammatory milieu, accelerating diastolic dysfunction and fibrosis. Together, these genes orchestrate cardiomyocyte survival, immune modulation, and matrix remodelling—core elements of infarct healing. Considering LOX pathways regulate inflammation resolution through SPMs, it is worth exploring whether these genes intersect with or influence SPM biosynthesis. Further research should also investigate how external modulators, including common co-medications like NSAIDs or anthracyclines (doxorubicin), might impact this resolution machinery.

SPM–GPCR axis in HFrEF and HFpEF

In HFrEF, the spleen acts as a key reservoir for leucocytes that are mobilized to the injured myocardium following infarction. Once recruited, these immune cells contribute to local SPM production, helping to resolve inflammation and support tissue repair.65 Unlike broad immunosuppressants, immunoresolvents like SPMs reprogramme inflammation without impairing immune competence. They enable the clearance of inflammation while preserving the body's ability to respond to pathogens and maintain tissue integrity.

Resolution mediators act through G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), which co-ordinate resolution signalling in HFrEF, atherosclerosis, and endothelial dysfunction. Key resolution-associated GPCRs include DRV1/GPR32, FPR2/ALX, LGR6, BLT1 (proinflammatory), and ERV1/ChemR23 (Figure 4).133–135 These receptors are expressed across diverse immune and stromal cell types and are essential for fine-tuning inflammatory responses (Table 1). As our understanding of these GPCRs expands, so does the therapeutic potential of targeting resolution pathways, offering a new frontier in the treatment of heart failure that emphasizes restoration over suppression.

Dietary fatty acids, arachidonic acid, docosahexaenoic acid, and eicosapentaenoic acid are converted by LOX enzymes into lipoxins, resolvins, protectins, and maresins. Acting through G protein-coupled receptors such as FPR2/ALX, GPR32/DRV1, LGR6, and ERV1/ChemR23, SPMs enhance macrophage efferocytosis, limit neutrophil infiltration, and support tissue repair. By restoring immune balance and preventing fibrosis, specialized pro-resolving mediator pathways provide therapeutic potential in cardiovascular disease.

RvD1–FPR2/ALX and MaR1–LGR6 pathways: dual modulators of cardiac remodelling and resolution

Resolvin D1 (RvD1), a DHA-derived lipid mediator, resolves inflammation by limiting neutrophils, suppressing cytokines, promoting M2-like macrophages, and enhancing phagocytosis. RvD1 signals through two GPCRs, FPR2/ALX and GPR32 (DRV1)—both essential for mediating its pro-resolving effects. RvD1 binds GPR32 with high affinity, enhancing macrophage phagocytic activity at picomolar concentrations. This effect is abolished by GPR32 silencing.151 In atherosclerotic plaques, reduced GPR32 expression has been observed. In vivo, human GPR32 expression in Fpr2−/− × ApoE−/− mice protected against high-fat diet-induced atherosclerosis, confirming the anti-inflammatory role of GPR32.140 In post-MI models, RvD1 activates the FPR2/ALX receptor, reduces neutrophil influx, and enhances efferocytosis, leading to lower expression of inflammatory markers (Cxcl5, Ccl2) and fibrosis-related genes (Fn1, Icam, CD44, Col1a1, Mmp9), ultimately improving cardiac and renal function.99,116 In STEMI patients, higher plasma RvD1 levels correlate with healing plaques, while lower levels are associated with inflammation, thrombus burden, and poor cardiac function.152,153 Targeted RvD1 delivery using platelet-mimicking liposomes enhances infarct localization, promotes clearance of apoptotic cells, increases endogenous SPMs (RvD2, RvE1, LXA4), and improves angiogenesis in the healing myocardium.154

RvD1 also benefits models of HFpEF. In an Ang II–II-infusion model, RvD1 reduced blood pressure elevation, cardiac hypertrophy, and fibrosis.155 In pulmonary hypertension, RvD1 suppressed atrial fibrosis and arrhythmias by reprogramming macrophages and reducing profibrotic gene expression (Col1a1, Col3a3, Tgfb1, Mmp2, Pdgf).156 In human studies, lower RvD1 levels are associated with increased HFpEF risk.157

Formyl Peptide Receptor 2 (FPR2), also known as FPRL1 or ALX/FPR2, was identified in the early 1990s and later cloned based on its sequence homology to FPR1, sharing 69% amino acid identity.158,159 FPR2 exhibits dual ligand specificity, responding to both pro-inflammatory signals (serum amyloid A, bacterial peptides) and pro-resolving mediators such as LXA4 and RvD1.99,160,161 The dual ligand affinity of FPR2 enables it to act as a molecular switch between inflammation and resolution in CVD. In atherogenesis, FPR2/ALX plays a complex role: while it supports resolution, its expression on macrophages within atherosclerotic plaques also promotes leucocyte recruitment and inflammation. In hyperlipidaemic mouse models (LDLR−/− or ApoE−/−), FPR2 deletion reduces plaque size, underscoring its contribution to lesion development and growth.162,163 Tipping FPR2 signalling towards its pro-resolving arm shows therapeutic potential in atherosclerosis. In hyperlipidaemic mice, treatment with Annexin A1 or its mimetic peptide Ac2–26 significantly reduced plaque size and inflammation, an effect dependent on FPR2/ALX signalling.164,165 FPR2/ALX−/− mice exhibit impaired resolution post-MI, with reduced SPM production, excessive neutrophil accumulation, and worsened remodelling.134 In contrast, selective FPR2/ALX agonists like BMS-986235 improve cardiac function and reduce ventricular dilation in MI models. In skeletal muscle ischaemia, FPR2/ALX is also essential for reparative macrophage programming and angiogenesis.166 Like RVD1, FPR2/ALX has an affinity towards LXA4. Patients with coronary atherosclerosis have been found to have lower circulating levels of SPMs (including aspirin-triggered LXA4) compared to healthy individuals.167 A prospective cohort study found that elevated plasma LXA₄ levels in AMI patients are associated with reduced MACE risk, largely by lowering recurrent ischaemic events. Notably, patients with both high LXA₄ and low high-sensitivity C-reactive protein had the lowest risk of recurrent ischaemia.168 Dysregulation of FPR2/ALX critically impairs inflammation-resolution process, particularly in the context of obesity and ageing—two major comorbidities of HFpEF. FPR2/ALX−/− develop spontaneous obesity, impaired macrophage function, kidney fibrosis, and systemic multiorgan abnormalities characteristic of HFpEF. These findings establish FPR2/ALX as a central regulator linking systemic metabolic inflammation to cardiac dysfunction. Mechanistically, FPR2 deficiency disrupts the biosynthesis of SPMs, including lipoxins and resolvins, which are essential for terminating inflammation and promoting tissue homeostasis. The resulting deficit in SPM production leads to persistent, non-resolving inflammation within the heart and peripheral organs. Moreover, macrophages from FPR2/ALX−/− mice exhibit impaired inflammation-resolution and reparative capacity, contributing to diastolic dysfunction and maladaptive cardiac remodelling. Additionally, loss of FPR2/ALX signalling perturbs the immune co-ordination between the spleen and heart—the splenocardiac axis—further amplifying metabolic and inflammatory derangements. Collectively, these findings position FPR2/ALX as a pivotal mediator in the resolution of inflammation and as a potential therapeutic target in HFrEF and HFpEF driven by metabolic dysfunction and ageing.134,169

In mouse models, the ApoE⁻/⁻ mice, treatment with 15-epi-LXA₄ markedly slowed atherosclerotic progression and reduced plaque inflammation.163 In MI models, exogenous 15-epi-LXA4 accelerated inflammation resolution and improved cardiac outcomes by enhancing neutrophil clearance.161 Similarly, in diabetic cardiomyopathy (HFpEF), LXA₄ reduced cardiac inflammation, limited pro-inflammatory macrophage polarization, and mitigated adverse remodelling.170 Maresin 1 (MaR1), another DHA-derived SPM, signals through leucine-rich repeat-containing G-protein coupled receptor 6 (LGR6), a GPCR with emerging relevance in cardiac injury and immune regulation. LGR6 expression is down-regulated after myocardial ischaemia–reperfusion. Mice lacking LGR6 exhibit worsened necroptosis due to disrupted STAT2/ZBP1 expression via Wnt signalling.171 In diabetic models, cardiomyocyte-specific LGR6 overexpression improves cardiac function and remodelling by limiting ferroptosis and enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis through STAT3/PGC-1α activation.172 LGR6 is also known to modulate the immune response. A frameshift mutation in LGR6 impairs neutrophil and monocyte phagocytosis and increases LTB4 production.173 Through unbiased screening of more than 200 GPCRs, MaR1 was identified as a stereoselective ligand that activates LGR6.147 MaR1 or RvD2 delivery in ApoE−/− mice reduces necrotic core size, limits macrophage infiltration, and enhances reparative macrophage phenotypes.174

Emerging clinical evidence links MaR1 to atherosclerotic protection in humans. In a large community-based cohort in China (n ≈ 2800; 8-year follow-up), higher baseline MaR1 levels correlated with fewer major cardiovascular events.175 Experimentally, MaR1 improves post-MI healing by activating the Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant pathway and inhibiting TLR4/NF-κB inflammatory signalling in the heart.176 Beyond LGR6, MaR1 partially antagonizes BLT1,177 suppressing LTB₄-driven pro-inflammatory signalling in human BLT1⁺ cells. In addition to LGR6, MaR1 interacts with BLT1, acting as a partial antagonist to suppress LTB₄-driven inflammation. This mechanism reduces leucocyte recruitment and improves outcomes in cardiovascular injury models.178 Together, the RvD1–FPR2/ALX and MaR1–LGR6 signalling axes represent powerful endogenous pathways that reprogramme inflammation towards resolution, promote cardiac repair, and offer promising therapeutic targets across heart failure phenotypes and atherosclerotic CVD.

RvE1–ChemR23 axis: a translational target for cardiovascular repair

ChemR23 (also known as ERV1 or CMKLR1) was first identified in 2003 as a GPCR for the chemoattractant chemerin.179 Beyond its immune surveillance role, ChemR23 is now recognized as a high-affinity receptor for the SPM RvE1, linking it to active resolution pathways in inflammation.180 In ApoE−/− and LDLR−/− models, ChemR23 deletion exacerbates atherosclerosis, marked by larger lesions, increased macrophage and foam cell content, defective efferocytosis, and expanded necrotic cores.181 These findings support its protective role in vascular inflammation. RvE1 activates ChemR23-dependent intracellular pathways such as AMPKα/Nrf2, inhibiting NF-κB and downstream cytokines and chemokines. This leads to reduced immune cell recruitment and vascular remodelling in models of hypertension.182 RvE1 also interacts with BLT1, antagonizing LTB₄ signalling,183 further dampening inflammation. In models of periodontitis, RvE1 reduced systemic C-reactive protein and local inflammation, highlighting its role in resolving peripheral and vascular inflammation.184 EPA-fed ApoE⁻/⁻ mice demonstrate an increased RvE1 precursor (18-HEPE) and reduced lesion size. However, loss of ChemR23 in these models negates this benefit, accelerating atherosclerosis and promoting plaque inflammation.181 Further, patients with established atherosclerosis have a significantly lower RvE1 to LTB4 ratio compared to healthy individuals. In murine MI models, the timing of RvE1 delivery proved critical. Early treatment reduced the infiltration of Ly6Chi monocytes, neutrophils, and pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6), improving cardiac repair, while delayed administration was less effective.185 RvE1 also exerts direct cardioprotective effects, with studies showing an 86% reduction in H-FABP leakage in injured cardiomyocytes.186 Indirect clinical evidence comes from omega-3 trials like REDUCE-IT and JELIS, which show that high-dose EPA lowers cardiovascular events, likely by boosting RvE1 production and ChemR23 activation.187,188

RvE1 can modulate key immune cells involved in HFrEF. Beyond innate immunity, RvE1 modulates adaptive immune responses by reprogramming T cells and dendritic cells towards a less inflammatory phenotype. In vitro, RvE1 inhibits Th17 activation, suppressing IL-17, IL-21, and CCR6 expression on CD4+ T cells.189 In an Ang II–induced hypertension model, RvE1 prevented vascular damage by blocking the phenotypic switch of vascular smooth muscle cells into a proliferative, pro-inflammatory state, thereby preserving vascular structure.182 A growing body of translational evidence supports a protective role for SPMs and their GPCRs, including FPR2/ALX, GPR32, LGR6, ChemR23, and BLT1 in regulating chronic inflammation in both HFrEF and HFpEF. Circulating SPMs (RvD1, LXA₄, MaR1) are reduced in patients with HF and acute coronary syndromes, correlating with elevated inflammatory markers and worse outcomes. Crucially, these effects are receptor-dependent, as genetic deletion of their GPCRs abrogates SPM-driven benefits. Unlike broad immunosuppressants, resolution-based therapies preserve host defence while accelerating the return to immune homeostasis. Collectively, this positions the SPM–GPCR axis as a promising translational target to modulate inflammation and remodelling in heart failure and related cardiovascular conditions (Figure 5).

Following myocardial infarction, an acute neutrophil-driven inflammatory response may either progress towards resolution of inflammation or failure of resolution of inflammation. Resolution of inflammation, mediated by specialized pro-resolving mediators, promotes macrophage transition to a reparative phenotype, efferocytosis, and extracellular matrix remodelling, leading to myocardial recovery. Conclusion

CVD arises from the asynchronous interplay between genetic and environmental factors, compounded by defective inflammation-resolution signalling. Genetic variants shape cardiometabolic risk, but lifestyle factors—diet, exercise, sleep, stress—modulate immune responses via molecular signals like DAMPs and SAMPs. SPMs, including RvD1, RvE1, LXA₄, and MaR1, act through GPCRs such as FPR2/ALX and LRG6 to resolve inflammation, promote efferocytosis, and support healing. Deficiencies in SPMs or receptor dysfunctions are linked to heart failure, atherosclerosis, and hypertension. Unlike immunosuppressants, resolution therapies restore immune balance, offering a precision approach to CVD management, thus warrants validation in clinical trials.

Future perspectives and current knowledge gaps

Knowledge gap: The impact of lifestyle dysregulation, especially diet, sleep, and physical activity, on resolution signalling is underexplored.
