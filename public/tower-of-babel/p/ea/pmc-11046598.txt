Framing the shot: a surprise finding of multiple foreign bodies on coronary angiography
Authors: Ali Al-Hadithi, Farhana Ara
Published: 2023

Introduction

This case describes a patient evaluated for chest pain who was incidentally found to have multiple radio-opaque lesions on coronary angiography. These lesions were subsequently confirmed as shotgun pellets.

Case presentation

An 82-year-old man was transferred to our tertiary cardiology centre after presenting to his local hospital with chest pain followed by sudden collapse and loss of consciousness lasting around 5 seconds.

His transfer notes recorded a past medical history of splenectomy, hypertension and type 2 diabetes mellitus.

Relevant investigations at his referring hospital included the following:

•ECGs on presentation: inferolateral T wave inversion.•Blood tests: mildly raised troponin level (initial troponin 22, repeat troponin 26).•Transthoracic echocardiogram: moderate to severely impaired left ventricular systolic function with mild global hypokinesis.

Discussion

The radio-opaque lesions were consistent with multiple gunshot pellets. These gunshots pellets were subsequently confirmed with the patient as having been sustained 40 years ago in an accident involving a shotgun that resulted in his splenectomy.

Cases of finding incidental gunshot pellets on imaging have previously been described in the literature.1, 2, 3 While the pellets did not contribute to the patient's current clinical presentation, their presence is an important piece of information when considering the suitability and safety of imaging modalities, particularly MRI imaging, and highlighted the importance of obtaining a full medical history from patients. In addition, they may impact surgical access when considering cardiothoracic surgery.

Given the presence of multivessel disease on angiography, this case was discussed with the cardiothoracic team. The patient was deemed suitable for surgery and underwent coronary artery bypass surgery. He was subsequently discharged following an uneventful postoperative recovery.

Conclusion

The surprise finding of gunshot bullets when performing coronary angiography highlighted the importance of taking a full medical history from patients. This would inform the evaluation of investigation results and planning future management.

Learning points

•A complete medical history should be obtained from all patients, including in-depth inquiries into any previous procedures and their indications.•In cases where a foreign body is suspected, clinicians should enquire about previous trauma history.•The presence of metallic foreign bodies, including bullets, is important when considering the safety of MRI imaging and planning access for surgical procedures.
