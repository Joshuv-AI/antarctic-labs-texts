Vafidemstat: a lysine-specific demethylase 1A inhibitor that shows promise for treating neuropsychiatric disorders
Authors: Jacob Peedicayil, Dennis R Grayson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe enzyme KDM1A encoded by KDM1A and also known as lysine-specific demethylase 1 (LSD1) plays important roles in the development and function of the brain. Vafidemstat is a KDM1A inhibitor that has shown beneficial effects in preclinical and clinical trials for the treatment of neuropsychiatric disorders, including borderline personality disorder (BPD), autism spectrum disorder (ASD), and attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). This article reviews available preclinical and clinical data on the use of vafidemstat in the treatment of these disorders. Vafidemstat appears to be a potential new and safe drug for the treatment of these disorders.Keywords: KDM1A, lysine-specific demethylase 1, neuropsychiatric disorder, vafidemstat

Introduction

Nucleosomes, the building blocks of chromatin, are affected by a very high number of post-translational histone modifications, which include acetylation and methylation of lysines (K) and arginines (R), phosphorylation of serines (S) and threonines (T), and ubiquitylation and sumoylation of lysines and ADP-ribosylation [1,2]. The majority of these post-translational modifications occur on the aminoterminal and carboxy-terminal tail domains. However, modifications to the central domains of histones are also known to occur [1,2].

Histone methylation

Histone methylation was discovered in the 1960s by Vincent Allfrey and colleagues [3,4]. In addition to histone acetylation, histone methylation is the most studied histone modification [2]. Methylation of histones involves the covalent addition of a methyl group obtained from the methyl donor S-adenosylmethionine (SAMe) to the side chain nitrogen atoms of all basic amino acid residues [4]. A pair of general categories of methylating enzymes have been identified. These are the histone lysine methyltransferases or HKMTs, which methylate lysine amino acid residues, and the histone arginine methyltransferases (HRMTs), which methylate the amino acid arginine [5,6]. Compared to methylated arginines, methylated lysines appear to have greater stability [6]. Histone methylation regulates developmental gene expression beginning before fertilization and continuing into the postnatal period [7]. The regulation of histones is carried out along with proteins containing methyl-binding domains, including PHD fingers (sequence-specific histone recognition Zn2+ binding domain modules), WD40 repeats (a common and interacting protein domain in eukaryotes terminating in tryptophan-aspartic acid), CW domains (zinc-binding domains), PWWP domains (Pro-Trp-Trp-Pro domain, a chromatin methylation reader), and ankyrin repeats (short amino acid sequences found in many proteins) [4].

Histone lysine methylation and histone arginine methylation

All the currently known HKMTs possess a SET domain comprising 130 amino acids [6]. The SET domain was initially identified as a shared sequence motif in three Drosophila proteins, namely suppressors of variegation, enhancers of zeste, and homeobox gene regulators of triothorax. Homologues of the Drosophila Su(var) 3–9 protein, SUV39H1 in humans and Suv39h1 in mice, were the first elucidated HKMTs involved in H3K9 methylation. Since then, more than 50 SET domain-containing proteins with a definite or anticipated enzymatic role in catalysing lysine methylation on histones have been identified in humans. Most SET-containing HKMTs have at least one additional protein module in their protein sequence. The HKMTs that contain SET are classified into six sub-families according to sequence homology within and around the catalytic SET domain, homology with other protein modules, as well as their structures [6].

Histone arginine methylation is a common post-translational histone modification [6] and there are two important types of histone arginine (R) methyltransferases (HRMTs) which transfer a methyl group from SAMe to the guanidine group of arginines in proteins, namely, type I and type II HRMTs. The HRMTs differ in size from 353 amino acids (HRMT-1) to 637 amino acids (HRMT-5). Two well-investigated HRMTs, HRMT-1 and HRMT-4, methylate histones H2B, H3, and H4 in addition to several non-histone proteins which include the carboxyl-terminal domain of RNA polymerase [6].

Histone demethylation

In general, methyl groups are thought to have a longer half-life than many other histone post-translational modifications [8]. The half-lives of histone methylation marks can be influenced by the type of histone involved [9], and the activity of histone demethylating enzymes [10,11]. Shi et al. [10] at Harvard University showed that histone methylation is reversible with the discovery of the first histone demethylating enzyme [8,9]. Two families of histone demethylases are known [11,12]. These are the flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD)-dependent amine oxidase family and the jumonji C (JmjC)-domain-containing iron- and α-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenase family [13]. The first family of histone demethylases consists of two members, namely, LSD1 or KDM1A, and LSD2 or KDM1B. The second family of histone demethylases is much more extensive and includes 30 members in humans. The JmjC family can be further classified based on amino acid sequence similarity. These comprise KDM2, KDM3, KDM4, KDM5, KDM6, and Jumonji clusters [14].

KDM1A or histone lysine-specific demethylase 1

KDM1A, previously called lysine-specific demethylase 1 (LSD1) [2], was discovered as part of a multi-protein co-repressor complex that has both histone deacetylase-1 or -2 and demethylase activities. KDM1A is a riboflavin-dependent H3K4-specific mono- and dimethyl-lysine demethylase [15]. The enzyme is highly conserved in organisms ranging from Schizosaccharomyces pombe (a fission yeast) to humans. It is a homologue of monoamine oxidase (MAO) and contains 852 amino acids [16]. It comprises three important domains: an N-terminal SWIRM domain, a C-terminal AO domain, and a central protruding Tower domain [16]. KDM1A catalyses the demethylation of H3K4 histone lysine residues by cleaving the α-carbon bond of the substrate to form an imine intermediate. The catalytic activity of KDM1A and its homologue LSD2 is located in the AO domain and depends on its co-factor FAD [16]. In addition, it can also demethylate some additional non-histone substrates to regulate their biological functions [17]. To improve our understanding of the mechanism of histone demethylation performed by KDM1A, the crystal structure of KDM1A at 2.9 Å resolution was elucidated in 2006 [18], and more recently at 2.25 Å resolution [19]. KDM1A was first isolated as an interaction partner for histone deacetylase 2 (HDAC2) in HeLa cells and was found to have sequence homology with polyamine oxidase [18]. This discovery led to the identification of the nucleosome remodelling and deacetylating (NuRD) complex that, in addition, contains CHD3 and CHD4 domains, as well as helicase/ATPase domains [20]. Later studies found a protein complex comprising LSD1, RCOR1 (also called CoREST), HDAC1, HDAC2, ZNF217, PHF21A, and HMG20B, frequently called the CoREST transcription repressor complex [16]. Recently, the crystal structure of the KDM1A/CoREST histone demethylase bound to its nucleosome substrate was elucidated, which explains why CoREST is needed for KDM1A to demethylate nucleosomes [21].

Post-translational modifications (PTMs) of proteins permit single proteins to perform multiple actions involved in different biological processes. Hence, PTMs increase biological complexity and greatly increase the encoded repertoire of biological phenomena beyond simple gene expression. In this context, KDM1A goes through several PTMs, including phosphorylation, ubiquitination, methylation, and acetylation [22]. KDM1A PTMs regulate diverse physiological conditions and exert control over pathological conditions such as cancer and sepsis. Hence, it is thought that there is a need to develop drugs that target not only KDM1A, but also drugs that can modulate KDM1A PTMs [22].

Many studies have shown the physical interaction of KDM1A/CoREST with transcription regulators to promote repression. For instance, KDM1A binds to a proline-rich domain of the transcription factor PRDM1, also called BLIMP1, which contributes to the suppression of the mature B-cell gene expression programme during plasma cell differentiation [16]. Another example of the transcriptional repressor action of KDM1A is its physical interaction with members of the SNAG domain transcription factor family, such as SNA11, SNA12, GF11, GFI1B, and INSM1, which play prominent roles in transcriptional regulation, formation of a repressive chromatin structure, cell signalling, and developmental processes [23].

KDM1A has pleiotropic effects [24] and inhibits many non-histone proteins [25]. It regulates the methylation levels of DNMT1 and E2F1 during DNA damage [24]. It suppresses p53 function by inhibiting the interaction between p53 and 53BP1. In addition, it controls angiogenesis, chromatin remodelling, proliferation and differentiation of cancer cells by demethylating HIF-1α, STAT3, and growth factor independent 1.

KDM1A and the brain

KDM1A has several important functions in the brain (Figure 1). There is evidence that this enzyme has played a role in the development of increased brain complexity during the evolution of the human brain [26]. KDM1A is an important regulator of neural stem cell proliferation and neuronal development [16,27]. The recruitment of KDM1A and the full CoREST complex to RE1 sites by the REST/NRSF transcription factor controls the changes from pluripotent to neural stem cells and from progenitor to mature neurons [16]. Based on studies in different animal species, including humans, chimpanzees, monkeys, and rodents, it has been learnt that KDM1A is a key player in the development of the cerebral cortex [28]. The enzyme contributes to the regulation of specific programs of gene expression in post-mitotic, fate-committed neurons, and plays a role in neuronal differentiation, plasticity, and disease [29]. KDM1A is involved in the effect of stress on the brain. In this regard, it has been shown in mice to be an environmental stress-sensitive negative modulator of the glutamatergic synapses [30]. Jeyaraj et al. [31] showed that KDM1A is involved in the altered expression of molecules regulating glutamate signalling that act epigenetically via the glucocorticoid receptor in Indian field mice. Serum response factor (SRF) acts as a stress transducer by promoting inherent experience-induced immediate early genes (IEGs) expression in neurons. Gerosa and co-workers [32] showed that KDM1A is required for the action of SRF in response to environmental stress in 10-week-old C57BL/GN mice. They showed that KDM1A is involved in the modified structural neuroplasticity and response to environmental stress in mice. Although it is required throughout development, KDM1A can also be present in terminally differentiated cells throughout the brain. To determine whether KDM1A is needed for terminally differentiated cells in the brain, Christopher et al. [33], in a preclinical study, deleted Kdm1a in adult mice by crossing floxed Kdm1a mice (to the Cagg-Cre tamoxifen-inducible Cre transgene to produce Lsd1CAGG mice. It was found that the loss of KDM1A in Lsd1CAGG mice caused widespread hippocampal and cortical neuronal death. The authors inferred that loss of KDM1A in hippocampal and cortical neurons is able to cause neuronal death.

Schematic showing some of the functions of KDM1A in the brain.

Engstrom et al. [34], in another preclinical study, used the P301S tauopathy mouse model to show that pathological tau is able to remove KDM1A from the nucleus in neurons. Moreover, the authors showed that decreasing KDM1A in these mice is enough to greatly increase tau-induced neurodegeneration and tau-induced changes in gene expression. Lastly, the authors showed that over-expressing KDM1A in the hippocampus of tauopathy mice is adequate to significantly delay neurodegeneration and oppose tau-induced expression changes. In addition, the pharmacological inhibition of KDM1A by inhibitors such as vafidemstat has shown promising results in the treatment of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [35]. There is also evidence that KDM1A takes part in the regulation of immunity and inflammation in the body [36].

KDM1A inhibitors

Due to the structural homology between KDM1A and MAO, KDM1A has catalytic activity similar to that of MAO [23]. Hence, known MAO inhibitors (MAOi) were tested for their ability to inhibit KDM1A. Further work suggested that tranylcypromine, phenelzine, and pargyline show potent inhibition of MAO and KDM1A. Hence, these drugs have become the basis for the development of further KDM1A inhibitors [22]. With the knowledge that tranylcypromine is a potent inhibitor of KDM1A, many small-molecule inhibitors of KDM1A have been developed. Vafidemstat and its precursor and analogue, ORYZON’s ORY-1001, were first investigated for the treatment of oncological and neurodegenerative disorders [37]. It was noted that these drugs, by inhibiting KDM1A, increase H3K9me2/1 and H3K4me2/1 methylation. ORY-1001 was the first selective irreversible inhibitor of KDM1A to enter clinical trials [37].

Vafidemstat is showing promising results in the treatment of various neuropsychiatric disorders. The current article reviews the pharmacology of vafidemstat with relevance to these disorders. The article is a narrative review based on articles obtained from PubMed and Google Scholar. Only peer-reviewed and published articles were chosen. Poster presentations at conferences were included if the work had not been published in a journal. Articles published from January 2020 to date were selected. Only articles dealing with neuropsychiatric disorders were selected.

Vafidemstat, a KDM1A (LSD1) inhibitor

Vafidemstat, which has the company code name ORY-2001 [35], is being developed by the biopharmaceutical company ORYZON Genomics, which is based in Barcelona and uses epigenetics for developing drugs for diseases with a strong unmet need. Vafidemstat is a small molecule that can be administered orally and has been optimized for disorders of the central nervous system [35]. Its chemical formula is C19H20N4O2 and its molecular weight is 336.4 [38].

Pharmacodynamics of vafidemstat

Vafidemstat is a dual inhibitor of KDM1A and monoamine oxidase-B (MAO-B). As mentioned above, MAO is structurally related to KDM1A. In vitro, vafidemstat inhibits the enzymatic activity of KDM1A and MAO-B by irreversibly binding to their FAD co-factors [35]. In vivo, it mainly inhibits KDM1A and at therapeutic doses it does not significantly inhibit MAO-B [35]. The comparison of vafidemstat with selective inhibitors of KDM1A or MAO-B shows that KDM1A inhibition is crucial for the efficacy of this drug. Within the brain, transcription factors recruit KDM1A, including serum response factor, and vafidemstat is involved in behavioural changes such as the response to stress [32]. In addition, vafidemstat improves the expression of immediate early genes [38]. Vafidemstat also has anti-inflammatory effects in the brain, which could help explain its efficacy in animal models of multiple sclerosis [39]. It has been shown to be an irreversible inhibitor of KDM1A [35].

Pharmacokinetics of vafidemstat

Based on single ascending dose (SAD) and multiple ascending dose (MAD) studies (Table 1), it has been learnt that vafidemstat is rapidly absorbed following oral administration, with a tmax (the time it takes for a drug to reach its maximum (peak) plasma concentration) of about 30 min to 2 h, and a relatively long half-life of 20–30 h [38]. PK modelling suggests that a steady-state level in the blood occurs between about days 5 and 7. Vafidemstat crosses the blood–brain barrier and is highly able to penetrate the brain [35]. The mean apparent distribution volume was found to be 150 L, suggesting a low-to-moderate distribution to tissues [38]. The drug was shown to cross the human blood–brain barrier, with quantifiable concentrations from 2 h after dosing [38]. All these PK data were obtained from studies on humans [38].

Adverse effects of vafidemstat

Vafidemstat has been shown to be a safe drug. In seven phase II trials, the same number (that is, 1.0%) of serious adverse effects (AEs) were reported among subjects who received vafidemstat and those who received placebo [40]. There was no weight gain, sedation, sexual dysfunction, and extrapyramidal effects [40]. The maximum tolerated dose was 2.5 mg/d. In the PORTICO study [39], a phase IIb study in patients with borderline personality disorder (BPD), regarding treatment-emergent AE among 106 subjects who received 1.2 mg of vafidemstat for 14 weeks, and 104 subjects who received placebo, there were fewer treatment-emergent AE in the vafidemstat group (57.5%) than in the placebo group (65.4%).

Preclinical studies of vafidemstat for neuropsychiatric disorders

Maes et al. [41] showed that vafidemstat inhibits brain KDM1A at doses suitable for long-term treatment and corrects memory deficits, as studied by novel object recognition testing in the senescence accelerated mouse prone 8 (SAMP8) model for accelerated aging and AD. The comparisons of vafidemstat’s actions with those of a selective LSD1 or MAO-B inhibitor shows that the inhibitory effect of vafidemstat on KDM1A is the major reason for its efficacy. Vafidemstat was also found to correct changes in behaviour, including aggression and defects in social interaction, in a rat rearing isolation model. It was found to increase the responsiveness of immediate early genes, induce the expression of genes needed for cognitive function and decrease the neuroinflammatory signature in SAMP8 mice. Several genes affected by vafidemstat are differentially expressed in late-onset Alzheimer’s disease (LOAD). Importantly, the gene S100A9 is highly expressed in LOAD and in the hippocampus of SAMP8 mice and down-regulated by vafidemstat [41].

Cavalcanti and colleagues [42] studied the effects of vafidemstat on immune-mediated and virus-induced encephalomyelitis, two experimental models of multiple sclerosis and neuronal damage. In the immune-mediated model, the authors subcutaneously immunized 8-week-old C57BL/6 mice and weighing 25–30 g with 100 μg of the myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein peptide MOG35–5 emulsified in complete Freund adjuvant containing 4 mg/ml Mycobacterium tuberculosis H37 RA. The mice also received intraperitoneal injections of 200 ng of pertussis toxin on days 0 and 2. For the virus-induced model, the cerebral tissue of susceptible 6-week-old SJL/J mice, previously anaesthetized with isoflurane, was inoculated with 2 × 106 plaque-forming units of Daniel’s strain of Theiler’s murine encephalomyelitis virus (TMEV), which was subsequently diluted in 30 μL of Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 5% foetal bovine serum.

In the immune-mediated model, the mice were randomly assigned to different groups. The study group received vafidemstat (1, 0.5, and 0.05 mg/kg), or vafidemstat-KDM1A (0.18, 0.09, and 0.06 mg/kg). For the virus-induced model, treatment comprised the administration of vafidemstat by oral gavage at a dose of 0.3, and 1.0 mg/kg starting at the onset of clinical signs (day 72 post-infection), once a day for 5 consecutive days from day 72 to day 76 and from day 79 to day 83 post-infection.

The oral administration of vafidemstat reduced clinical signs, decreased lymphocyte egress and infiltration of immune cells into the spinal cord, and prevented demyelination. The drug also reduced the inflammatory gene signature typical of experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis in the central nervous system (CNS) of mice. Moreover, vafidemstat caused changes in gene expression, suggesting a neuroprotective effect in the CNS of mice and decreased neuronal glutamate excitotoxicity-caused damage in explants. The authors did not measure KDM1A inhibition but found that the efficacy of vafidemstat was dependent on such inhibition. The authors inferred from these data that vafidemstat is a promising epigenetic drug that acts in the CNS to treat neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative disorders and that it could be moved forward to clinical trials.

KMT2C is a gene that codes for one of the six known histone H3 lysine 4 (H3K4) methyltransferases and was identified as a gene whose heterozygous loss-of-function variants are associated causally with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and the Kleefstra syndrome phenotypic spectrum. However, little is known about how KMT2C haploinsufficiency produces neurodevelopmental defects or how these conditions may be treated. In this context, Nakamura and co-workers [43] developed and investigated genetically engineered mice with a heterozygous frameshift mutation of kmt2c as a disease model with a high etiological validity. In many behavioural studies, the mutant mice exhibited autistic-like features, including impairments in social interactions, flexibility, and working memory, demonstrating face validity as a model of ASD. To study the molecular basis of these anomalies, the authors carried out a transcriptomic analyses of the bulk adult mouse brains. The authors observed that identified risk genes for ASD were specifically enriched in the up-regulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs), whereas kmt2C peaks detected by ChIP-seq were significantly co-localized with the down-regulated DEGS, suggesting a major role for the indirect effects of Kmt2c haploinsufficiency. Clinical studies of vafidemstat for neuropsychiatric disorders

Antonijoan and colleagues [40] conducted a first-in-human single-centre, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 1 clinical trial to determine the safety, tolerability, and pharmacokinetic (PK) and pharmacodynamic (PD) properties of the vafidemstat in healthy volunteers. The primary outcomes were safety, and tolerability, while secondary outcomes included the PK and PD properties of the drug. A total of 110 volunteers took part, 87 of whom were treated with vafidemstat and 23 with placebo.

The study comprised an SAD study in healthy young male volunteers (each subject in five study cohorts with eight subjects each) who received one dose of vafidemstat ranging from 0.2 mg per oral to 4.0 mg per oral. Each cohort received the drug for a duration of 5 d (Table 1). The MAD study of healthy young and older adult male and female adults included five cohorts with eight subjects in each cohort. Each subject received ascending doses of the drug from 0.2 mg per oral agent in the first cohort to 2.5 mg in the fifth cohort (Table 1).

A third component of the study was a sub-study involving cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). The maximum recommended starting dose (MRSD) was 0.2 mg, as calculated in the most sensitive species, that is, the rat. The MRSD was based on the US Food and Drug Administration and the European Medicines Agency Guidelines based on preclinical data. The dose-increase regimens were 0.2, 0.6, 1.5, 2.5, and 4 mg for the SAD study and 0.2, 0.6, 1, 1.5, 2.5 mg, and an additional dose level of 4.0 mg for the MAD study. Regarding the CSF sub-study, the doses administered were 2 and 4 mg. The findings of this trial were as follows: There were no significant differences in AE between the active treatment and placebo groups. There was no change in the biochemical, haematological, ECG, and vital signs data with increasing dose, except for a transient decrease in the platelet count at an extra dose used for that purpose. Vafidemstat showed fast absorption, approximate dose-proportional exposures, and moderate systemic accumulation after treatment for 5 d. The CSF-to-plasma unbound ratio showed CSF penetration by the drug. Vafidemstat bound KDM1A in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) in a dose-dependent manner. Inhibition of MAO-B was not detected.

Bullock et al. [44] conducted a single-centre, open-label, one-arm study to evaluate the efficacy, safety, and tolerability of vafidemstat with regard to reducing aggression in an adult population with moderate to severe AD. Twelve patients were enrolled, although one dropped out. The median age was 75 y. The study group comprised 58% females and 42% males. The drug was noted to be safe and tolerable. Compared with baseline, vafidemstat significantly reduced agitation–aggression, as measured by the Clinical Global Impression Scale, the Cohen-Mansfield Agitation Inventory and the Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NP1) four item sub-scale. There was a global improvement in caregiver burden, as measured by the Zarit Caregiver Burden Interview and both NPI-related emotional distress scores. In addition, there was a global improvement in the total NPI. Based on these data, the authors concluded that vafidemstat controls agitation and aggression in AD patients and is a safe drug.

Ferrer et al. [45] conducted REIMAGINE, a phase IIa, single-centre, open-label, one-arm basket trial that investigated the safety and efficacy of vafidemstat on aggression in adult patients with BPD, attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and ASD. The study subjects were administered 1.2 mg of vafidemstat orally per day of vafidemstat for 8 weeks. It was found that vafidemstat is safe and well tolerated, with no significant drug-induced AEs reported. All the neuropsychiatric scales used showed notable efficacy signals. These included agitation–aggression, overall patient functioning, and disease-specific features related to BPD and ADHD. Based on these data, the authors inferred that vafidemstat deserves continued development as a new treatment option for these psychiatric disorders.

Ropacki et al. [39] of ORYZON conducted PORTICO, a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled adaptive 14-week phase IIb clinical trial of vafidemstat in BPD with two primary efficacy endpoints, decreases in agitation and aggression. The secondary endpoints included a decrease in anger severity and expression. Additional secondary end points were decreases in depression and anxiety, and safety (the subjects’ ability to receive the drug without developing AE The workers noted that although the primary endpoints measuring overall BPD disease severity and agitation–aggression were not statistically significant, secondary measures of agitation–aggression and overall disease reached nominal significance and clinically meaningful decreases. All primary and secondary efficacy measures statistically favoured vafidemstat over placebo and there was a global treatment effect favouring vafidemstat. In addition, the authors determined that treatment with vafidemstat was safe and well tolerated. It was found that there is full KDM1A occupancy by vafidemstat at a dosage of 1.2 mg/d. ORYZON has received approval from the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to conduct a phase III clinical trial of vafidemstat in patients with BPD [46].

Phelan-McDermid syndrome (PMS) is a genetic disorder caused by the loss of the terminal portion of chromosome 22 or by pathogenic or likely-pathogenic variants in the SHANK3 gene. This gene encodes a protein that is vital for the structure and function of synapses, especially in the brain. Preclinical studies have shown that SHANK3 abnormalities can cause abnormal synaptic transmission, which can be corrected by KDM1A and/or HDAC inhibitors [47,48]. Patients with PMS have varying degrees of intellectual disability, delay or absence of speech, reduced muscle tone, motor delay epilepsy, and features of autism. Nevado et al. [49] performed a pilot study to clinically characterize the presentation of 30 patients with PMS. The patients were tested using different psychometric scales, including the Repetitive Behavior Questionnaire (RBQ), the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales (ADOS), the Battelle Developmental Inventory Screening Test, and the Behavior Problems Inventory (BPI). Of the 30 patients, 19 were chosen for the pilot study and 11 in the validation study. The collected psychometric data showed three groups of patients, with different cognitive and behavioural profile scores. Statistically significant variations in deletion sizes in chromosome 22 were detected when comparing the three groups, with the size of the deletion positively correlated with ADOS and negatively correlated with Vineland-A and C-scores. No correlation was found between deletion size and BPI and RBQ scores. The authors stated that this study presents new data on the best possible endpoints for a future clinical study of the use of vafidemstat for treating SHANK3-associated psychiatric disorders such as PMS. Conclusions

KDM1A has several important functions in the brain, such as the regulation of neural stem cell proliferation and neuronal development; regulation of the development of the cerebral cortex; regulation of gene expression in neurons; regulation of neuronal differentiation, plasticity, and disease; taking part in the response of the brain to environmental stress; taking part in the control of neurodegeneration, and the regulation of immunity and inflammation. In this light, it is not surprising that the inhibitor of KDM1A, vafidemstat, is showing beneficial results in preclinical and clinical studies of neuropsychiatric disorders.
