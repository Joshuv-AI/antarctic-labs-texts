Establishing the minimum clinically important difference of the Quality of Life in Childhood Epilepsy Questionnaire
Authors: Mariela Leda, Klajdi Puka, Karen Bax, Joel J. Gagnier, Karina Tassiopoulos, Kathy Nixon Speechley
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo estimate the minimum clinically important difference (MCID) for the parent‐reported 55‐item Quality of Life in Childhood Epilepsy Questionnaire (QOLCE‐55) and its shortened 16‐item version, QOLCE‐16.MethodsData came from 74 children with epilepsy (CWE) (ages 4–10, mean age = 8 [SD = 1.8]) enrolled in the Making Mindfulness Matter in Epilepsy (M3‐E) trial, a pilot, parallel randomized‐controlled trial of a mindfulness‐based intervention. Both anchor‐based and distribution‐based methods were used to estimate MCID values for the QOLCE‐55 and QOLCE‐16. For the anchor‐based approach, the Patient Centered Global Ratings of Change (PCGRC) scale and linear regression analysis were used to estimate the MCID. For the distribution‐based approach, .5 SD of the health‐related quality of life (HRQOL) change score distribution was used to estimate the MCID.ResultsFor the QOLCE‐55, the MCID obtained using an anchor‐based approach was 10 points and using a distribution‐based method was 6 points. For the QOLCE‐16, the MCID obtained using an anchor‐based method was 13 points and using a distribution‐based method was 7 points.SignificanceThis is the first study to estimate MCID values for the QOLCE‐55 and the QOLCE‐16. It has been well documented that CWE are at risk of experiencing psychological, behavioral, and cognitive impairments, which can negatively impact their HRQOL. Reporting MCID values for the QOLCE‐55 and QOLCE‐16 is important in determining whether changes in HRQOL observed are meaningful to CWE themselves, as a key factor in shaping the nature of epilepsy care delivered.

INTRODUCTION

Health‐related quality of life (HRQOL) is a concept defined as “those aspects of self‐perceived well‐being that are related to or affected by the presence of disease or treatment.” 1 Measuring HRQOL in children with epilepsy (CWE) is crucial, as this population is at risk for psychological, behavioral, and cognitive comorbidities. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 In a study of CWE recruited from the community, 26% had a psychiatric diagnosis of depression, anxiety, attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), conduct disorder, and/or oppositional defiant disorder. 2 Furthermore, 39% of the CWE had a neurodevelopmental spectrum disorder, which included language delay, language problem, developmental delay, autism, and/or dyslexia. 2 In another community‐based study, 80% of CWE had cognitive impairment (IQ <85) and/or a behavioral disorder outlined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition, Text Revision (DSM‐IV‐TR). 6 The presence of any comorbidity was identified as a significant predictor of poor HRQOL in a sample of 103 CWE. 8 These findings demonstrate the importance of considering HRQOL in the management of childhood epilepsy.

The Quality of Life in Childhood Epilepsy Questionnaire (QOLCE‐55) is a commonly used parent‐reported measure of HRQOL in CWE. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 A shortened‐version of the QOLCE‐55, the 16‐item parent‐reported QOLCE (QOLCE‐16), was subsequently developed and validated to further reduce respondent burden. 10 The minimum clinically important difference (MCID), which is the minimum amount of change in a score that is considered meaningful to a patient, 13 has not yet been estimated for these outcome measures. MCID is an important concept because statistical significance alone does not indicate clinical relevance. 14 Estimating MCID values for the QOLCE‐55 and QOLCE‐16 can help clinicians and researchers ascertain whether any changes in HRQOL observed are meaningful to the CWE themselves.

The objective of this study was to estimate the MCID for two commonly used measures of HRQOL in CWE: the QOLCE‐55 and QOLCE‐16. Both anchor‐based and distribution‐based approaches were used to quantify the MCID for these outcome measures.

METHODS

Data source

Data were obtained from the Making Mindfulness Matter in Epilepsy (M3‐E) trial. 15 This trial was approved by the Western University Health Sciences Research Ethics Board (#113896). M3‐E was a pilot, parallel, partially nested randomized controlled trial designed to evaluate the feasibility of implementing a program, Making Mindfulness Matter (M3), as an intervention for CWE. M3 is a mindfulness‐based program that delivers lessons on mindful awareness, social–emotional learning, neuroscience, and positive psychology. The program was delivered over the span of 8 weeks by non‐clinician staff from a community epilepsy agency. Children received a 1‐h session each week while their parents received a 1.5‐h session each week. Facilitators leading the sessions were given the same script, protocol, and presentation slides to ensure standardization across groups. One research assistant was present for each session to facilitate the completion of participant questionnaires. Child–parent dyads were randomized 1:1 into the intervention or waitlist control group. The HRQOL of CWE in the intervention group was measured three times: 0–2 weeks before the first session (week 0; baseline), 1 week after the last session (week 9; immediate follow‐up), and 10 weeks after the last session (week 18; extended follow‐up). For the waitlist control group, HRQOL was measured two times: 0–2 weeks before the first session (week 0; baseline) and 1 week after the last session of the intervention group (week 9; immediate follow‐up). Extended follow‐up was not conducted with the waitlist control group in an effort to maximize retention rates. Participants in the waitlist control group were enrolled in the next scheduled M3 session following the completion of their immediate follow‐up questionnaire.

For MCID estimation, HRQOL scores of participants from the intervention and control arms were merged to generate one variable for analyses. Because extended follow‐up (week 18) was only conducted with participants from the intervention group, all analyses were conducted using data obtained at immediate follow‐up (week 9), referred henceforth as “follow‐up.”

Participants

M3‐E participants were recruited from a population of CWE being treated for epilepsy by the Division of Pediatric Neurology, Children's Hospital‐London Health Sciences Center (London, Ontario, Canada) and one pediatric neurology clinic in Windsor, Ontario, Canada. Recruitment was conducted between December 2, 2019, and December 12, 2022. In addition, passive recruitment strategies were utilized through social media posts and e‐posters. Children met the following inclusion criteria: (1) age 4 to 10 years old; (2) diagnosed with epilepsy for at least 6 months, according to the 2014 International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) operational definition 16 ; (3) child and parent had adequate understanding of English; (4) child and parent had ability to follow verbal instructions; and (5) child and parent were willing to attend all scheduled program sessions. Because the intervention was delivered online, families were required to have reliable access to a computer or mobile device and the internet. In addition, families were required to be residents of Ontario so that session materials could be mailed to participants in a timely manner. Families were excluded if (1) child was diagnosed with other progressive or degenerative neurological disorders; (2) had any other major non‐neurological comorbidities (e.g., cystic fibrosis, Crohn's disease, diabetes, renal failure); (3) was scheduled for epilepsy surgery during the trial period; (4) child or parent routinely participated in complementary health interventions (e.g., meditation); or (5) was concurrently enrolled in other intervention trials. 15

Measures

The outcome measures used in this study are the 55‐item and 16‐item versions of the Quality of Life in Childhood Epilepsy Questionnaire (or QOLCE‐55 and QOLCE‐16). 10 , 11 The QOLCE‐55 originated from the original 77‐item QOLCE, whereas the QOLCE‐16 is a shortened, validated version of the QOLCE‐55. The QOLCE‐55 and QOLCE‐16 have been validated for parent report in children between 4 and 18 years old and young adults between 18 and 29 years old. 10 , 12 , 17 The QOLCE‐55 and QOLCE‐16 comprise four subscales assessing the child's physical, cognitive, emotional, and social functioning. Both measures generate an overall HRQOL score and a score for each of the four subscales. The total HRQOL score and subscale scores range from 0 to 100, with a higher score indicating better HRQOL. 11 , 12 Both the QOLCE‐55 and QOLCE‐16 have demonstrated sound psychometric properties and the QOLCE‐55 has been highlighted as one of two leading measures for assessing HRQOL in CWE. 9 , 11

In our study, parents completed either the QOLCE‐55 or the QOLCE‐16. The QOLCE‐55 was offered to all participants, whereas the QOLCE‐16 was offered at follow‐up to participants who withdrew from the program or found the QOLCE‐55 too long to complete. Because the QOLCE‐16 is a subset of the QOLCE‐55, it is possible to calculate QOLCE‐16 scores from partially filled or completed QOLCE‐55 questionnaires. Mean overall scores generated from the QOLCE‐16 were previously found to be comparable to those obtained from the QOLCE‐55. 10 Therefore, scores were generated for both the QOLCE‐55 and the QOLCE‐16 for any participant who completed the QOLCE‐55. For participants who completed the QOLCE‐16 at follow‐up, only their QOLCE‐16 scores were generated.

Handling of missing data

A QOLCE‐55 subscale was considered missing and excluded from total score calculation if more than 20% of the items were left unanswered. 11 , 18 If only one or no subscale was missing, the total QOLCE‐55 score was determined by calculating the mean of the remaining subscales. If more than one subscale was missing, the total score for a participant was considered missing and excluded from analyses. 11 , 18

A QOLCE‐16 subscale was considered missing if more than two items were missing. The total score was calculated by taking the unweighted mean of the four subscales. If more than one subscale was missing, the total QOLCE‐16 score was considered missing. 10

Methods for MCID estimation

There are two main types of methods used for MCID estimation: anchor‐based and distribution‐based methods. 19 The anchor‐based approach uses an external measure of change in the outcome, which acts as the “anchor,” and compares it to the score generated by the primary outcome measure. 20 The anchor is typically a scale that patients use to rate the change they experienced in an outcome and is administered at follow‐up after the completion of an intervention. 21 , 22 One anchor commonly used is the global ratings of change scale. 23 The Patient Centered Global Ratings of Change (PCGRC) scale is a 15‐point global rating scale ranging from −7 (a very great deal worse) through 0 (no change) to +7 (a very great deal better). 21 The PCGRC asks parents to report any changes their child experienced since baseline in five areas assessed by the QOLCE‐55: overall HRQOL, physical function, emotional function, cognitive function, and social function. 21 Participants first rated the five areas as worse, about the same, or better compared to 9 and/or 18 weeks earlier. The scale offered no further follow‐up questions if a participant responded “About the same” in an area on the PCGRC. If a participant reported improvement, the scale offered seven response options from +1 (a tiny bit better) to +7 (a very great deal better). If a participant reported deterioration, the scale offered seven response options from −1 (a tiny bit worse) to −7 (a very great deal worse). The PCGRC was used as the anchor in a previous study estimating the MCID of HRQOL measures for adults with epilepsy. 21 , 24 This study identified a global rating of 3 (somewhat better) as the MCID and used linear regression analysis to calculate the change in HRQOL score associated with this rating. 24 This cutoff was obtained from a sample of patients with medically refractory epilepsy who were asked to identify what they perceived as a minimally important change on a seven‐point scale ranging from 1 (no change at all) to 7 (a very great deal of change). 25 For consistency with past methodology used to estimate the MCID for epilepsy‐specific HRQOL measures, we used the same anchor and cutoff score to estimate the MCID for the QOLCE‐55 and QOLCE‐16.

The distribution‐based approach uses the statistical properties of an outcome score distribution to establish the MCID. 26 Statistical features of the score distribution such as the effect size and standard deviation (SD) can be used to represent the MCID. 19 A systematic review of studies estimating the MCID cited the Multiples of Standard Deviation as the most frequently used distribution‐based approach. 23 Specifically, .5 SD units of the mean change score is the specific Multiples of Standard Deviation approach authors commonly use. 23 Another systematic review of MCID studies supports the use of this method, stating that most meaningful changes in HRQOL approximate .5 SD units of the score distribution. 27

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS Version 16.1. Both anchor‐based and distribution‐based approaches were used to obtain a range of estimates for the MCID of the QOLCE‐55 and QOLCE‐16. For the anchor‐based method, the relationship between the QOLCE change score (follow‐up score minus baseline score) and the parent‐reported PCGRC rating was evaluated. First, the correlation between the PCGRC ratings and the QOLCE‐55 and QOLCE‐16 mean change scores was evaluated using the Spearman rank correlation coefficient. Cohen's conventions state that a correlation of .1 is considered a small correlation, a correlation of .3 is considered a moderate correlation, and a correlation of .5 is considered a large correlation. 28 Based on these conventions, it is suggested that the correlation between an anchor and a primary outcome measure should be at least .30 for the anchor to be considered appropriate for MCID estimation. 29 MCID estimation was conducted for an outcome measure only if the PCGRC met the requirements for anchor suitability (correlation coefficient ≥.30). Next, following the methods outlined by Wiebe et al., 24 the MCID values were calculated using the formula: Estimated QOLCE score change = β1 × PCGRC rating. The estimated QOLCE score change associated with a global rating of 3 on the PCGRC was used to represent the MCID. For the distribution‐based approach, .5 SD units of the mean change QOLCE score was used to estimate the MCID.

RESULTS

Sample characteristics

A total of 85 parent–child dyads were randomized in M3‐E. Of the 85 dyads, 71 completed the QOLCE‐55 at baseline and follow‐up and two dyads completed the QOLCE‐16 at follow‐up. An additional two parent–child dyads did not finish the entire QOLCE‐55 at follow‐up but completed enough questions to contribute a QOLCE‐16 score. One parent–child dyad was excluded from the analyses given that the length of time the parent took to complete the QOLCE‐55 at follow‐up (42 s) was determined to be too short to be considered as valid data. QOLCE‐55 or QOLCE‐16 data were available for 74 parent–child dyads at baseline and follow‐up and used for the analyses.

Of the 74 CWE, more than half were male (58%) and the ages ranged from 4.2 to 10.9 years old. Seizure frequency varied considerably across the sample at baseline, with one‐fifth (19%) of parents reporting that their child had seizures every day, whereas almost half (39%) of parents reported their child had not had a seizure in the past year. The remainder of the children in the sample were reported to have had seizures weekly (14%), monthly (8%), every 3 months (9%) or every 6 months (11%). Parents were also asked to report the formal diagnosis of seven specific comorbidities in their children: developmental delay, learning disability, depression, ADHD or ADD, autism, oppositional defiant disorder or conduct disorder, and anxiety. The most common comorbidities present in the CWE at baseline were learning disability (30%), developmental delay (28%), ADD or ADHD (19%), and anxiety (18%).

Among parent participants, the majority was female (95%), had a college or university‐level education (66%), was currently working (65%), and living with a spouse or partner (89%). Mean PCGRC ratings

A total of 73 participants completed the PCGRC at follow‐up. The mean (SD) PCGRC rating was .7 (1.5). Based on the PCGRC responses, 23 parents (32%) reported that their child's overall HRQOL had improved since baseline (>1 on the PCGRC), 44 parents (60%) reported that their child's overall HRQOL had not changed or changed minimally (−1, 0, and 1 on the PCGRC), and 6 parents (8%) reported their child's overall HRQOL had worsened (<−1 on the PCGRC). Six parents (8%) reported that their child's overall HRQOL improved meaningfully (≥3 on the PCRGC) with a mean QOLCE‐55 change of 15.9 points, whereas no parents reported that their child's overall HRQOL worsened meaningfully (≤−3 on the PCGRC).

QOLCE‐55

The correlation between the QOLCE‐55 change scores and the PCGRC ratings was .33 (p = .007). Because the coefficient is greater than .30, the PCGRC was considered an appropriate anchor for the QOLCE‐55. Using the anchor‐based approach, the MCID estimate obtained for the QOLCE‐55 was 9.8 points (95% CI: 5.1–14.5). The regression coefficient describing the relationship between the QOLCE‐55 change scores and the PCGRC ratings was 3.3 (p < .0001). QOLCE‐16

The correlation coefficient between the QOLCE‐16 change scores (baseline to follow‐up) and the PCGRC ratings was .36 (p = .002), indicating that the PCGRC is a suitable anchor for the QOLCE‐16. Using linear regression analysis, the MCID obtained for the mean change in QOLCE‐16 score between baseline to follow‐up was 13.3 points (95% CI: 8.3–18.3). The regression coefficient depicting the association between the PCGRC ratings and the QOLCE‐16 mean change scores was 4.4 (p < .0001). DISCUSSION

The purpose of this study was to estimate the MCIDs of the QOLCE‐55 and QOLCE‐16 using anchor‐based and distribution‐based approaches. Using the anchor‐based approach, the MCID obtained for the mean change in QOLCE‐55 score between baseline to follow‐up was 10 points. Using a distribution‐based approach, the MCID for the change in QOLCE‐55 score from baseline to follow‐up was 6 points. The MCID obtained for the mean change in QOLCE‐16 score from baseline to follow‐up using an anchor‐based approach was 13 points. Using a distribution‐based approach, the resulting MCID for the mean change in QOLCE‐16 score from baseline to follow‐up was 7 points.

There is a lack of consensus regarding which approach is more ideal for MICD estimation, but a case has been made for prioritizing anchor‐based over distribution‐based methods, since distribution‐based methods tend to overlook the key concept behind MCID as they do not directly ask participants whether they have experienced an important change in the outcome and by how much. 30 , 31 , 32 Therefore, it has been argued that distribution‐based methods are less patient‐centered and should not be used as the primary approach to estimate the MCID. 30 , 31 , 32 Given these findings, it may be advisable to give priority to interpreting changes in HRQOL based on the MCID values obtained using the anchor‐based approach.

When presented with a plausible range of MCID values for a given measure, two factors should be considered to help decide which value to use for interpretation. First, investigators should consider the implication of false negatives and false positives when using varying MCID estimates. If the higher MCID value is chosen, the false‐negative rate may increase, whereas the false‐positive rate may increase if the lower MCID value is chosen. 33 Thus it is important to consider the consequences of choosing each MCID value along with the context of the study. Here are two scenarios to help illustrate this point. In one example, investigators are conducting a pilot randomized‐controlled trial to assess the impact of an educational program on the HRQOL of CWE. The investigators decide to analyze their results using the lower MCID value reported, which would likely increase the false‐positive rate. Interpreting the HRQOL results using the lower MCID value is appropriate in the context of this example because an educational program would have minimal to no consequences on participants’ health, and subsequent trials would be required to replicate this finding. In another example, investigators are conducting a large‐scale randomized controlled trial to compare the effect of two medications on the HRQOL of CWE. In this trial, the interventions used could have serious effects on participants’ health. Therefore, it would be more appropriate to interpret the trial findings using the larger MCID value to conduct a conservative assessment of the medication's effectiveness. The second factor to consider is the level at which change in HRQOL is being assessed, whether at the individual or group level. When considering a meaningful change at the individual level, a greater change in HRQOL score (i.e., higher MCID value) is required to be considered a meaningful change due to the possibility of intra‐individual variability and measurement error occurring. 33 At the group level, a smaller change in HRQOL (i.e., the smaller MCID value) can be considered meaningful because measurement error is better controlled for in‐group analysis. Given these findings, investigators should consider the context in which HRQOL change will be assessed when deciding which value of the MCID range to use.

This study has several strengths. Both distribution‐based and anchor‐based methods were used to estimate the MCID for the QOLCE‐55 and QOLCE‐16, which is typically recommended. 22 , 23 , 34 , 35 In addition, this sample is representative of the psychiatric, cognitive, and behavioral comorbidities commonly present in CWE (Table 1), thereby increasing the applicability of our findings to our target population. Using linear regression analysis and the PCGRC for our anchor based‐method is a strength because it maintains consistency with past methods used to estimate MCID values for epilepsy‐specific HRQOL measures. 24 The SD method used was highlighted as the most commonly used distribution‐based method. 23 , 36

This study also has some limitations. The sample size used in the analyses was relatively small (n = 74); therefore replication of the MCID estimates with larger sample sizes is important. The cross‐sectional nature of the PCGRC is another limitation, as parents were asked to describe the HRQOL change their child experienced over the previous 9 weeks. Recall bias may result in an inaccurate reporting of the PCGRC ratings, which might bias the regression coefficients obtained in the analyses. Furthermore, the MCID estimates provided can apply only to meaningful improvement in HRQOL and not meaningful worsening. 37 , 38 The MCID for worsening HRQOL was not estimated in this study due to the small sample that reported worsening HRQOL on the PCGRC (n = 6). This is consistent with our expectation that an effect of worsened HRQL was unlikely given the non‐invasive nature of the intervention. Finally, generalizability of the findings may be limited. The sample included CWE between 4 and 10 years old, and compared to the 2021 census, our sample had higher proportions of parents living with a spouse or partner (89% vs 84%) and educated at the college or university level (66% vs 58%). 39 As such, our findings may not extend to CWE older than 10 years old and CWE with less educated parents or single‐parent households.

CONCLUSION

This is the first study to report MCID values for the QOLCE‐55 and the QOLCE‐16. Although using both distribution‐based and anchor‐based methods is recommended, some authors argue that anchor‐based methods should be primarily used to estimate the MCID. 30 , 31 , 40 , 41 Therefore, the MCID values obtained through anchor‐based methods may be considered the primary estimates. Using the anchor‐based approach, the MCID estimate obtained for the QOLCE‐55 was 10 points, and for the QOLCE‐16 it was 13 points. Further research is required to create guidelines on interpreting differential MCID estimates. Ultimately, the MCID values should be used with caution awaiting replication in studies with larger samples.
