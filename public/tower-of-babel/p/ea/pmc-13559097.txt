Risk factors and construction of prediction model for plastic bronchitis in children with Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia
Authors: Rui Xin, Xinhui Yuan, Juntao Jin, Jing Shu, Xinhua Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildren’s health is at serious risk due to the possibility of developing life-threatening plastic bronchitis (PB) from severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (SMPP). It is critical to detect PB early. A risk prediction model was developed and evaluated in this study to enable early warning and intervention for high-risk pediatric patients.MethodsA total of 180 children with SMPP were the subject of a retrospective analysis. Patients were divided into a PB group (60 cases) and a non-PB group (120 cases) based on the results of fiberoptic bronchoscopies. Imaging results, laboratory data, and clinical traits were compared between groups. Candidate predictors of PB were initially screened using univariable analysis and the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression. Independent risk factors were subsequently identified through multivariate logistic regression, and a prediction model was constructed. Internal validation was performed on the model, and its discriminatory power, calibration, and clinical value were assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC), calibration, and clinical decision curves.ResultsIndependent risk variables for PB in children with SMPP were history of intensive care unit (ICU) admission [odds ratio (OR): 4.32; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.41–13.82; P=0.01], fever duration (OR: 1.26; 95% CI: 1.09–1.47; P=0.002), neutrophil percentage (OR: 1.05; 95% CI: 1.01–1.09; P=0.03), and lactate dehydrogenase (OR: 1.01; 95% CI: 1.01–1.01; P<0.001). The predictive model built using these parameters had a sensitivity of 81.7% and a specificity of 85.0%, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.871 (95% CI: 0.813–0.929). The model’s strong consistency and practical applicability were validated using calibration curves and clinical decision-support analysis.ConclusionsThe goal of the study was to develop and validate a nomogram for predicting PB in children with SMPP, thereby facilitating early identification of PB in clinical practice.Keywords: Children, severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (SMPP), plastic bronchitis (PB), nomogram, risk factors

Highlight box

Key findings

• This study demonstrates that history of intensive care unit (ICU) admission, high neutrophil percentage (NEUT%), elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) values, and prolonged fever duration are independent predictors of plastic bronchitis (PB) in children with severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (SMPP). These four markers can be used to build a composite predictive model that shows excellent accuracy, discriminating power, and clinical utility while successfully identifying high-risk populations for PB.

What is known and what is new?

• Prior research has mostly focused on individual risk factors for the progression of SMPP to PB; however, multidimensional predictive models that incorporate laboratory results, disease course characteristics, and a history of critical care treatments are lacking.

• This study identified and validated a multivariate predictive model incorporating a history of ICU admission, NEUT%, LDH, and fever duration, highlighting the synergistic effects of early critical care alerts, systemic inflammatory response, and disease duration and providing a new quantitative approach for early identification.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• According to these findings, pediatric patients with SMPP who have a history of ICU admission, elevated peripheral blood NEUT% and LDH levels, and persistent fever should be closely monitored by clinicians and regarded as being at high risk for developing PB. This model improves the prognosis by enabling earlier bronchoscopic screening and intervention. Future therapeutic treatment should include a combined evaluation of multidimensional markers, as well as further validation in larger, multicenter cohorts.

Introduction

Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP), a respiratory infectious disease caused by Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) infection in children, poses a substantial disease burden. Globally, epidemics occur periodically every 3–7 years (1). MP accounts for 29–41% of community-acquired pneumonia cases, with a higher incidence observed in school-aged children (2,3). Following the implementation of non-pharmaceutical interventions, including mask-wearing and disinfection, during the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, the infection rates of MP and other respiratory pathogens declined substantially (4). Since early 2023, China has experienced a nationwide resurgence of MP infection, and this epidemic pattern has deviated markedly from previously observed seasonal trends (5). Most MPP cases have a self-limiting course with a short duration of illness and mild clinical manifestations. Chest imaging findings reveal mild interstitial infiltrates or patchy opacities, and the prognosis is generally favorable without significant complications. However, a subset of patients experience rapid disease progression, predisposing them to the development of severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (SMPP). Approximately 12% of hospitalized children with MPP require transfer to the intensive care unit (ICU) for further management (6). SMPP is characterized by severe illness, rapid progression, prolonged clinical course, and extensive pulmonary parenchymal involvement. It may be accompanied by extrapulmonary complications and multiple organ dysfunction. The potential structural and functional pulmonary sequelae resulting from SMPP adversely affect growth and development in children and have garnered increasing clinical attention.

Plastic bronchitis (PB) is a serious intrapulmonary complication of SMPP, characterized by the formation of tough, dendritic casts in the tracheobronchial tree. These casts can partially or completely obstruct the airways, leading to atelectasis, progressive dyspnea, and potentially life-threatening respiratory failure. Long-term pulmonary sequelae, such as bronchiolitis obliterans and bronchiectasis, may also ensue. MP is one of the primary pathogens responsible for PB (7). The time pattern of bronchial casts (BCs) in children with PB is unknown, and the disease lacks distinctive clinical symptoms and imaging characteristics. As a result, it is susceptible to being overlooked or misdiagnosed, which can result in missing the optimal therapeutic window. Current studies have predominantly focused on exploring risk factors for PB in SMPP through conventional univariate and multivariate regression analyses, whereas risk scoring systems remain relatively scarce. Moreover, some existing prediction models have not corrected for variable collinearity. In the present study, we summarize the clinical characteristics, laboratory findings, and radiological features of children with SMPP, and analyze the early risk factors for PB development. We aimed to develop and validate a visualized nomogram model based on simple clinical indicators, thereby providing a basis for early risk prediction, diagnosis, and therapeutic decision-making in children with SMPP who are at risk of progressing to PB. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2026-0316/rc).

Methods

Patients

In this study, a total of 180 pediatric patients clinically diagnosed with SMPP who underwent flexible bronchoscopy (FB) between October 2023 and October 2025 were analyzed; among them, 60 were diagnosed with PB. According to the number of final model variables, the sample size met the generally accepted criterion of at least 10 outcome events per variable for logistic regression. All patients were hospitalized at the Department of Pediatrics, the First Hospital of Lanzhou University. The inclusion criteria were (I) age ≤16 years; (II) meeting the diagnostic criteria for SMPP; and (III) informed consent obtained from guardians for the performance of FB examination and treatment. The diagnostic criteria for SMPP were based on the Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Mycoplasma pneumoniae Pneumonia in Children (2025 Edition) (8). The diagnostic criteria for PB included expectoration or bronchoscopic removal of casts with a bronchial branching structure. The exclusion criteria were (I) a history of chronic lung disease, immunodeficiency disorders, autoimmune diseases, or hematological diseases; (II) bronchopulmonary dysplasia; and (III) incomplete clinical data. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Hospital of Lanzhou University (No. LDYYLL2026-449). Written informed consent for the performance of FB examination and treatment was obtained from guardians. As this study involved a retrospective analysis of previously collected clinical data, the requirement for additional individual consent specifically for research use of the data was waived by the ethics committee.

Data collection

Clinical data of all enrolled children were retrospectively collected from electronic medical records. The collected data encompassed the following categories: (I) Baseline information: age, sex, body weight and history of preterm birth. (II) Clinical manifestations and signs: peak body temperature (℃), fever duration (days), presence of cough, wheezing, dyspnea (including hypoxemia, nasal flaring, supraclavicular and intercostal retractions, and tachypnea), digestive system complications (abdominal pain, diarrhea, vomiting, nausea), cardiovascular complications (myocarditis, pericarditis, heart failure), neurological complications (encephalitis or meningoencephalitis), rash (maculopapular rash, urticaria, erythema multiforme), diminished breath sounds, and moist crackles. (III) Laboratory parameters: white blood cell (WBC) count, neutrophil percentage (NEUT%), lymphocyte percentage (LYM%), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), red blood cell (RBC) count, hemoglobin (Hb), platelet (PLT) count, C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), interleukin-6 (IL-6), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB), D-dimer and immunoglobulin E (IgE). (IV) Imaging findings: presence of unilobar or multilobar pulmonary consolidation, atelectasis, pleural effusion, and pericardial effusion. All findings were derived from computed tomography (CT). (V) Treatment-related variables: frequencies of FB interventions, duration of glucocorticoid therapy (days), dosage of glucocorticoids (mg/kg/day), requirement for oxygen therapy. (VI) Clinical course and outcomes: length of hospital stays (days), time from symptom onset to definitive diagnosis (days), history of ICU admission, and history of admission to other hospitals prior to presentation. In addition, throat swabs, sputum, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid or blood samples were collected from the pediatric patients. Nucleic acid detection, isothermal amplification chip assay or serum immunoglobulin M (IgM) antibody testing were performed to identify concurrent bacterial or viral co-infection.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software. The outcome variable was defined as the occurrence of PB. Initially, univariate analysis was conducted using independent two-sample t-tests, non-parametric rank-sum tests, and chi-square tests, as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as n (%), continuous variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and those with non-normal distribution were expressed as median [interquartile range (IQR)]. Missing data were almost nonexistent. Variables with a P value <0.1 in the univariate analysis were selected for inclusion in the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression model for further variable screening. Subsequently, multivariable logistic stepwise regression analysis was performed to identify statistically significant independent risk factors and to establish a risk prediction model. The discriminatory ability of the model was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Calibration was assessed using a calibration plot and the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Clinical utility was evaluated through decision curve analysis (DCA). A nomogram was constructed to provide a visual representation of the prediction model. Finally, internal validation was performed using the Bootstrap resampling method (with 1,000 resamples) to assess overfitting and to correct the performance estimates.

Results

This study included 180 children with SMPP who fulfilled the inclusion requirements and underwent FB. Among these, 60 patients were assigned to the PB group, while 120 patients were assigned to the non-PB group. The median body weight was 26.00 (21.00–33.10) kg, and the median age of onset was 7.00 (6.00–9.00) years overall. Regarding sex, age and history of preterm birth, there were no statistically significant differences between the two groups (P>0.1). Regarding clinical manifestations, the PB group exhibited significantly higher maximum temperature, longer fever duration, and higher incidences of dyspnea, diminished breath sounds, digestive system complications, and rash onset compared to the non-PB group (P<0.05) (Table 1).

Laboratory findings revealed that the PB group had significantly higher levels of NEUT%, PLR, NLR, CRP, PCT, IL-6, LDH, AST, ALT, and D-dimer compared to the non-PB group (all P≤0.001). Co-infections were found in both groups, including both single- and multi-pathogen infections in the PB group. In the PB group, 26 patients had co-infections with a single pathogen, including 16 viral co-infections [adenovirus (n=7), rhinovirus (n=3), parainfluenza virus (n=3), respiratory syncytial virus (n=1), influenza A virus (n=1), and influenza B virus (n=1)] and 10 bacterial co-infections [Streptococcus pneumoniae (n=7), Haemophilus influenzae (n=1), Klebsiella pneumoniae (n=1), and Pseudomonas fluorescens (n=1)]. In the non-PB group, the detection rate of bacterial co-infection was 17.5% (21/120), with Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus pneumoniae being the most commonly detected (4.2%, 5/120 each). The incidences of multilobar consolidation (P<0.001), pleural effusion (P<0.001), and pericardial effusion (P=0.004) were significantly higher in the PB group than in the non-PB group. Furthermore, compared to the non-PB group, the PB group demonstrated significantly higher frequencies of FB interventions, longer duration of glucocorticoid therapy, higher proportion of glucocorticoid dosage >2 mg/kg/day, increased need for oxygen therapy, prolonged hospitalization, and higher rate of ICU admission history (P<0.001) (Table 1).

LASSO analysis

To mitigate the risk of multicollinearity and overfitting, LASSO regression was employed for multivariate variable selection (Figure 1). Guided by the clinical principle of prioritizing etiological exposure factors over treatment outcome indicators, the analysis was based on the predictive variables that demonstrated statistical significance in the preceding univariate analysis. The regularization parameter λ was determined via 10-fold cross-validation, yielding a value of 0.07239. At this λ value, the LASSO regression model identified eight variables with non-zero coefficients. In descending order of the absolute values of their coefficients, these variables were history of ICU admission, fever duration, pleural effusion, oxygen therapy, LDH, LYM%, dyspnea, and NEUT%. Among these, LYM% emerged as a negative predictor, while the remaining variables served as positive predictors for the outcome (Figure 2).

Based on clinical considerations, the eight variables with non-zero coefficients identified by LASSO regression were incorporated into a multivariate logistic regression model for stepwise regression analysis. Ultimately, four independent risk factors for the development of PB were identified: history of ICU admission, fever duration, NEUT%, and LDH level (Table 2). A nomogram for predicting the risk of PB in children with SMPP was subsequently constructed based on these factors (Figure 3). The nomogram allows clinicians to input the patient-specific values of each predictive variable to obtain a total risk score, which corresponds to the predicted probability of PB occurrence.

Model performance evaluation and validation

The predictive value of the aforementioned risk factors and the combined model was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The results demonstrated that the AUC for the model was 0.871 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.813–0.929]. At the optimal cut-off value of 0.319, the model yielded a sensitivity of 0.817 and a specificity of 0.850, indicating moderate to good predictive performance (Figure 4).

ROC curve of the predictive model. AUC, area under the curve; ROC, receiver operating characteristic.

The predictive model was calibrated using a calibration curve and the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. The calibration curve demonstrated excellent fit for both the prediction model and the validation set. The Hosmer-Lemeshow analysis revealed a high degree of concordance between the predicted probabilities and the observed outcomes (Figure 5). DCA indicated a positive net benefit across a range of high-risk probability thresholds (Figure 6).

Calibration plot of the predictive model.

Decision curve analysis of the predictive model.

Internal validation of the prediction model was performed using the Bootstrap resampling method (with 1,000 resamples) to assess the degree of overfitting and to correct the performance estimates. The validation results showed that the bias-corrected C-index (equivalent to the AUC) was 0.860, representing a minimal reduction of 0.011 compared to the original C-index of 0.871, with an optimism proportion of 1.3%. This indicates an extremely low degree of overfitting in the model’s discriminative ability. Following correction, the model maintained excellent discriminative performance, with a C-index exceeding 0.85.

Discussion

PB is a rare respiratory disease with an insidious onset, with severity determined by the degree of airway obstruction, and carries a potentially fatal risk. Asthma, congenital heart disease, respiratory infections, cystic fibrosis, sickle cell anemia, cancer, and primary nephrotic syndrome have all been implicated in the complex etiology of PB (9). The mortality rate of PB ranges from 28% to 60% among patients with congenital heart disease, and from 6% to 7% among those with other etiologies (10). The recent surge in PB cases associated with MP infection may be partly attributable to the high population density in Asia and the epidemiological characteristics of MP. Early use of fiberoptic bronchoscopy and bronchoalveolar lavage has been shown to relieve clinical symptoms, atelectasis, and airway obstruction (11). As fiberoptic bronchoscopy technology has advanced and become widely applied, the diagnostic and therapeutic rates for PB have steadily improved.

The exact mechanism by which MPP leads to PB remains unclear. Previous studies have indicated that MP directly impairs mucociliary clearance and airway epithelial cells through adhesion and release of community-acquired respiratory distress syndrome toxin, hydrogen peroxide, and reactive oxygen species, thereby promoting mucus plug formation (12). Multiple pattern recognition receptors are also activated by MP infection, thereby triggering excessive host immune-inflammatory responses and the release of inflammatory cytokines, exacerbating secondary damage (13). It has been reported that IL-6 is a crucial prognostic biomarker in severe cases, directly reflecting the inflammatory status of lung tissue and showing a strong correlation with MPP severity (14). In the present study, the PB group had significantly higher IL-6 levels, suggesting that overexpression of inflammatory cytokines may play a role in the pathogenesis of PB in children with MPP. However, this variable was not retained in the final model, owing to the penalized selection mechanism of LASSO regression and its collinearity with NEUT% and LDH in the model.

Laboratory parameters are widely used in clinical diagnosis because they offer considerable accuracy, sensitivity, and efficiency for dynamic monitoring of disease progression and evaluation of therapeutic response. Previous studies have suggested that children with MPP exhibit increased neutrophil counts in peripheral blood and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, indicating that MP infection triggers excessive inflammatory responses associated with disease severity (15,16). In the present study, elevated neutrophil levels were identified as a risk factor for PB and also demonstrated substantial independent predictive value for its diagnosis. Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) released by neutrophils can trap pathogens in specific areas, thereby limiting the spread of inflammation. However, excessive release of NET components causes immune dysregulation and directly damages tissue (17). These processes can induce apoptosis and ferroptosis in alveolar epithelial cells, which are implicated in airway hypersecretion and PB development. In children with SMPP, this pathological process is more prominent (18). Furthermore, during the acute phase of infection, a sharp increase in neutrophils can damage the airways through the release of proteases, reactive oxygen species, and inflammatory cytokines, thereby promoting PB development (19). Consistent with our results, other studies have shown that LDH, a non-specific inflammatory marker released into the blood after tissue and cellular damage, has substantial independent prognostic value (20,21). Several studies have proposed LDH >462.65 U/L and LDH ≥451.0 U/L as high-risk warning thresholds for PB (22,23), although the specific cutoff values may vary according to age and assay techniques. Additionally, LDH can be used to assess disease severity and serve as a marker to determine whether MPP patients require corticosteroids and the optimal dosage (24). In addition to causing substantial damage to the airway mucosa, these inflammatory mediators also impair ciliary clearance, ultimately leading to the formation of BCs that obstruct the airways.

In this study, children in the PB group were more likely to present with respiratory distress symptoms, including hypoxemia, supraclavicular and intercostal retractions, and tachypnea, compared with those in the non-PB group, indicating that PB-induced airway obstruction severely impairs pulmonary ventilation and gas exchange. Furthermore, the PB group had a significantly higher incidence of diminished breath sounds over the affected lung than the non-PB group, which is consistent with the findings of Yang et al. (25). Nevertheless, the incidence of wheezing did not differ significantly between the two groups. Wheezing is primarily caused by turbulent airflow impinging on the airway walls during passage through narrowed airways; this discrepancy may be related to the location of airway obstruction caused by BCs in PB patients. Persistent high fever suggests a sustained hyperinflammatory response. Elevated insensible water loss during high temperatures may accelerate the formation of BCs by promoting mucus thickening. Owing to their high viscosity and elasticity, BCs are difficult to clear completely by mucociliary clearance or the cough reflex. A fever duration of 12 days was identified by Zhong et al. as an independent risk factor for PB in patients with MPP (26). According to Zhao and Cheng, peak fever temperature may also be an independent risk factor for PB (20,27). In our study, both peak temperature and fever duration were greater in the PB group than in the non-PB group, with fever duration demonstrating robust independent predictive capacity. Fever duration more accurately reflects the duration of inflammation and the severity of illness than a single peak temperature reading.

Notably, our research demonstrates that, in addition to conventional inflammatory markers, ICU admission history serves as a significant clinical exposure factor and independent predictor of PB development, reflecting overall disease severity. In the early disease stage, children admitted to the ICU present with severe clinical manifestations, including persistent fever, severe cough, extensive pulmonary consolidation, and rapidly progressive dyspnea. These symptoms are frequently accompanied by hypoxemia or even respiratory failure, which necessitates advanced life support. These characteristics are associated with a more pronounced hyperinflammatory response, immune dysregulation, and coagulation-fibrinolysis imbalance, indicating the development of a pathophysiological state conducive to PB formation in these patients.

Furthermore, airway mucus plugs can promote microbial colonization and impair immune clearance, frequently leading to coinfection. In this study, children with MPP complicated by PB had a coinfection rate of up to 50%, with adenovirus being the most common viral pathogen. Although the overall coinfection rate did not differ significantly between the two groups, the PB group demonstrated a higher rate of concomitant bacterial infection (20.00% vs. 17.50%), though this difference did not reach statistical significance (P=0.68). Therapeutically, bronchoscopic intervention with repeated lavage using normal saline at 37 ℃, local instillation of acetylcysteine solution diluted 1:1, and mechanical removal of casts using disposable biopsy forceps effectively relieved bronchial obstruction and helped prevent sequelae including atelectasis and bronchiolitis obliterans. Regarding clinical management, systemic corticosteroids were administered to suppress excessive inflammation, control fever, and slow radiographic progression. Compared with the non-PB group, children in the PB group required longer duration and higher doses of corticosteroid therapy. They also had prolonged hospitalization, reflecting greater disease complexity and severity, as well as the need for repeated bronchoscopic interventions. Notably, three sessions were required in 16 children before complete removal of BCs, underscoring the persistent formation of casts over time and the difficulty of achieving clearance in a single procedure. Following bronchoscopic and lavage therapy, all children in the PB group showed substantial improvement in both clinical manifestations and radiographic findings.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center retrospective investigation based on children with SMPP who underwent bronchoscopy. Future external validation in broader SMPP populations, as well as multicenter, large-sample, and prospective studies, is warranted. Second, the absence of blinding may introduce information bias; thus, prospective validation with blinding is needed in subsequent work. Third, based on clinical relevance and to avoid multicollinearity and model overfitting, we excluded several variables that were significant in univariate analysis when constructing the multivariable model. Although this approach enhanced parsimony and interpretability, it also implies that the variable selection strategy may have influenced model performance. Fourth, given the ongoing epidemiological evolution, model calibration may be affected by future shifts in disease phenotypes or treatment paradigms; therefore, prospective time-series validation across different epidemic periods is required.

Conclusions

In summary, this study developed and validated a predictive model for the formation of PB in children with SMPP. By incorporating fever duration, NEUT%, LDH levels, and a history of ICU admission, we established a robust clinical tool for risk stratification. The model demonstrated favorable performance in terms of discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility, thereby providing a foundation for the early identification of high-risk patients. This enables timely intervention and treatment, ultimately reducing the incidence of adverse prognostic outcomes.
