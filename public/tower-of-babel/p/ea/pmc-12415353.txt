Monoclonal Antibody 5F1 Modulates Formyl Peptide Receptor 1 Conformation for Transmembrane Signaling
Authors: Yue Wang, Yezhou Liu, Yixin Chang, Richard D. Ye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFormyl peptide receptor 1 (FPR1) is a G protein‐coupled receptor (GPCR) that mediates chemotaxis and bactericidal activities in phagocytes. The monoclonal antibody 5F1 is generated against full‐length FPR1 and used widely for detection of FPR1 expression. This study aimed to characterize 5F1 for its functions. We found that 5F1 is highly selective for human FPR1 over the homologous FPR2. Epitope mapping led to the identification of extracellular loop 2 (ECL2) as a major epitope, and the synthetic peptide of ECL2 interfered with 5F1 binding to FPR1. Using a NanoLuc Bioluminescence Resonance Energy Transfer approach, we found that 5F1 binding induced FPR1 conformational changes. Although less potent than fMLF, 5F1 binding induced FPR1 internalization, Gi protein dissociation, and β‐arrestins membrane translocation. Alanine substitution of F110 and R205 markedly reduced 5F1 binding without affecting FPR1 cell surface expression, suggesting that 5F1 is sensitive to conformational changes in FPR1 as these residues are not present in ECL2. Altogether, mAb 5F1 can alter FPR1 conformation and modulate transmembrane signaling, features that may be explored for potential use beyond the detection of FPR1 expression.

Introduction

G protein‐coupled receptors (GPCRs) constitute a superfamily of cell surface receptors that play vital roles in physiology [1, 2]. GPCRs are characterized by their 7 transmembrane‐domain structure with functional coupling to heterotrimeric G proteins. Formyl peptide receptors (FPRs) form a subfamily of class A GPCRs with three gene products in humans, namely FPR1, FPR2, and FPR3. Among the FPRs, FPR1 is mainly expressed in neutrophils and macrophages and is well characterized for its functions [3, 4]. As an important component of host defense, FPR1 serves as a pattern recognition receptor that detects bacterial and mitochondrial peptides bearing an N‐terminal formyl‐Met group [5, 6, 7]. Binding of formyl peptides induces conformational changes in FPR1 that enable transmembrane signaling through both G protein‐dependent and ‐independent pathways. Furthermore, FPR1 plays a pivotal role in regulating numerous physiological and pathobiological processes, making it a potential pharmacological target for therapies of cardiovascular diseases, obesity‐related metabolic diseases, and inflammatory diseases [8, 9, 10, 11].

Over the past decades, the rapid development of antibody engineering technology has promoted the generation of antibody agonists for GPCRs [12]. The anti‐human FPR1 antibody, 5F1, is a mouse IgG1 monoclonal antibody (mAb) generated against intact FPR1 expressed on the cell surface and is widely used as a reagent for the detection of FPR1 expression [13]. Antibodies produced against native proteins often recognize multiple epitopes [14, 15], although the epitope for the 5F1 mAb has not been mapped. Some of these antibodies also recognize conformational epitopes, resulting in agonistic activities by alteration of GPCRs conformations [16]. Successful examples include the work by Pardon et al. that used an antibody targeting β2‐adrenoreceptor (β2‐AR) to lock the receptor in an active‐state conformation [17], and Ma et al. engineered the antibody JN241 to be an agonist based on the active conformation of the human apelin receptor [18]. In this work, we sought to determine whether 5F1 is sensitive to FPR1 conformation with receptor activation potential.

To investigate whether 5F1 binding can induce FPR1 conformational changes and whether these changes can translate into receptor‐mediated transmembrane signaling, we first determined 5F1 specificity for FPR1 as opposed to the homologous FPR2, and then mapped the 5F1 epitope on FPR1 using flow cytometry with competitive peptides derived from FPR1. We then determined 5F1‐induced FPR1 internalization and conformational changes. Using the NanoLuc Bioluminescence Resonance Energy Transfer (NanoBRET) approach, we investigated receptor conformational changes induced by 5F1. Finally, the 5F1‐induced Gi protein activation and β‐arrestin membrane translocation were determined. Our results indicate that 5F1 mainly recognizes an epitope in the extracellular loop 2 (ECL2) of FPR1, and 5F1 binding induces conformational changes in FPR1 that translate into receptor signaling. These findings suggest the possibility of using 5F1 for modulation of FPR1 conformation and functions, with potential implications in developing FPR1‐targeting therapeutic antibodies.

Materials and Methods

Reagents

Human BD Fc Block Reagent, mouse anti‐human FPR1, and Alexa Fluor 647‐labeled mouse anti‐human FPR1 (Clone 5F1, RUO) were purchased from BD Biosciences (San Jose, CA, USA). eFluor 450‐labeled mouse anti‐human CD16 monoclonal antibody (eBioCB16) was purchased from eBioscience (San Diego, CA, USA). FLAG M2‐FITC mouse monoclonal antibody (Clone M2) and fMet‐Leu‐Phe (fMLF) were obtained from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Coelenterazine H was purchased from Promega (Madison, WI, USA). TC‐FlAsH TC‐ReAsH II In‐Cell Tetracysteine Tag Detection Kit was from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA, USA). G418, HBSS buffer, Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM), HEPES (1 M), Opti‐MEM, and Lipofectamine 3000 were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Fetal bovine serum (FBS) was purchased from HyClone Laboratories (Logan, UT, USA). PolymorphPrep was purchased from Axis‐Shield (Oslo, Norway). Red blood cell lysis buffer was purchased from Biolegend (Fell, Germany). LANCE Ultra cAMP Detection Kit and HTRF IP‐One Gq Kit were purchased from Revvity (Shelton, CT, USA). Forskolin was purchased from Merck (Rahway, NJ, USA). DAPI was purchased from Beyotime (Jiangsu, China).

Plasmids

The cDNAs encoding FPR1‐SmBiT, β‐arrestin1‐LgBiT, β‐arrestin2‐LgBiT, FPR1‐Clover, β‐arrestin1‐mRuby2, and β‐arrestin2‐mRuby2 (with LgBiT at the N terminus, and SmBiT, Clover, mRuby2 at the C terminus) were synthesized by QYAOBIO (Shanghai, China) and inserted into the pcDNA3.1 vector (Invitrogen). Plasmids expressing mutant FPR1 were generated by molecular cloning of PCR products followed by DNA sequencing for verification.

Cells

HEK293T (CRL‐3216), HeLa (CCL‐2), and RBL‐2H3 (CRL‐2256) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA). HEK293T and HeLa cells were cultured at 37°C in 5% CO2 in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (1% P/S). RBL‐2H3 was transfected with an expression vector pcDNA3.1 (+) containing either the FPR1 cDNA or FPP2 cDNA. Stable transfectants were selected with 600 μg/mL of G418. The FPR1‐RBL and FPR2‐RBL stable cell lines were maintained at 37°C in 5% CO2 in DMEM supplemented with 20% FBS and 600 μg/mL G418.

Human neutrophils were isolated from peripheral venous blood. About 10 mL of peripheral blood was collected by venipuncture using procedures approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen. Neutrophils were isolated by density gradient centrifugation with PolymorphPrep (Axis‐Shield, Oslo, Norway). The erythrocytes were removed by hypotonic lysis with red blood cell lysis buffer (Biolegend, Fell, Germany). The purified neutrophils were 89% CD16+ with a viability of ≥ 95% as determined by trypan blue exclusion. In testing samples containing Alexa Fluor 647‐labeled mouse anti‐human FPR1, DAPI (500 ng/mL) was added to determine cell integrity to avoid overlapping emission spectrum with propidium iodide.

FPR1 Endocytosis

HeLa cells were seeded on a glass coverslip placed in a 24‐well plate, incubated for 12 h, and then co‐transfected with FPR1‐Clover by Lipofectamine 3000 for 24 h at 37°C. The cells were independently exposed to fMLF, 5F1, or HBSS at 37°C for 30 min, then gently rinsed with PBS, and ultimately fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. The coverslips in the 24‐well plate were mounted on glass slides with ProLong Diamond Antifade Mountant and visualized by confocal microscopy (LEICA TCS SP8, Buffalo Grove, IL, USA). The images were taken with a 63× oil objective. The excitation wavelength of Clover was 488 nm, and the emission wavelength was 500–525 nm.

Flow Cytometry

Cells were collected, resuspended in ice‐cold 1× HBSS, and subsequently incubated with Fc Block for 10 min, subsequently incubated with antibodies, such as Alexa Fluor 647‐labeled mouse anti‐human FPR1, Alexa Fluor 488‐labeled anti‐human FPR2, FITC‐labeled anti‐FLAG, and eFluor 450‐labeled mouse anti‐human CD16 monoclonal antibody. DAPI, employed as a viability dye, was added to all tubes 5 min prior to analysis at a final concentration of 500 ng/mL. Fluorescence signals were collected using the PB480 channel, wherein live cells were characterized as DAPI‐negative and dead cells as DAPI‐positive. DAPI was added to all tubes 5 min before flow cytometry analysis at a final concentration of 500 ng/mL. Compensation for fluorescence was performed using single‐positive controls, specifically staining of cells with either Alexa Fluor 647‐labeled 5F1 antibody or FITC‐labeled M2 antibody. The spillover matrix was generated within the FlowJo software (v10.8, BD Biosciences) to correct for spectral overlap.

For the titration experiment, the antibody working concentrations were serially diluted by half, starting from 4 ng/μL down to 0.5 ng/μL. Cells expressing the target gene were mixed at a 1:1 ratio with non‐expressing cells. Each tube contained 1 × 106 cells and was incubated with Fc Block for 10 min, then with the antibody at room temperature for 1 h. Cells underwent 2 washes with 1× HBSS and were filtered through 70 μm cell strainers (ThermoFisher). Flow cytometry (CytoFLEX, Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) analysis was subsequently performed. DAPI was added to all tubes 5 min before flow cytometry analysis at a final concentration of 500 ng/mL.

Competitive binding was conducted as previously described [19] with modifications. Briefly, cells were co‐incubated with exogenous competitive agents, including fMLF and synthetic ECL peptides of FPR1, along with Human Fc Block and anti‐FPR1 antibody (5F1, 6.7E‐9 M). After incubation on ice for 1 h in the dark, the cells underwent 2 times washes with 1× HBSS. Following filtration through 70 μm cell strainers, the expression levels of CD16, FPR1, and FPR2 were quantified using flow cytometry. The dissociation constant (Kd) of 5F1 binding was calculated according to the equation of Cheng–Prusoff [20]: Kd=IC501+competitorKdcompetitor where 5F1 is the competitor with a concentration of 6.7E‐9 M, and Kd of fMLF is 1E‐9 M [21], and IC50 of fMLF was taken from the curve (Figure 1C, around 1.77E‐9 M).

NanoBRET Measurement

For measurement of ligand‐induced conformational changes using the FPR1 biosensor, HEK293T cells were transfected with FPR1‐ICL1‐NanoLuc. After 24 h, cells were loaded with 1 μM FlAsH‐EDT2 mixed with 2 μM 1,2‐Ethanedithiol (EDT) for 1 h at 37°C, followed by PBS washing 3 times. Cells were dissociated with trypsin and plated in a 384‐well white opaque plate at 2 × 104 cells/well. Then coelenterazine H (NanoLuc substrate) was added to the wells at the final concentration of 5 μM for a total volume of 20 μL/well. After incubation for 5 min at 37°C, the BRET ratio was recorded using an Envision plate reader (Perkin Elmer, Boston, MA, USA). Then the ligand was added in wells to stimulate the cells. The 5F1 antibody came at a stock concentration of 200 ng/μL, which was lower than desired. To avoid further dilution, the stock solution was used without adjusting concentration to the exact molar scale. As a result, the 5F1 concentrations in some figures were expressed as 6.7E‐(n) molar concentrations.

After stimulation with different concentrations of fMLF and 5F1, the BRET ratio was recorded again. The equation defined the BRET ratio: BRET ratio = Emission reading at 525 nm/Emission reading at 460 nm. The change of BRET ratio (ΔBRET) was calculated: ΔBRET = BRETafter stimulation − BRETbefore stimulation.

G Protein Dissociation Assay

HEK293T cells were seeded in a 24‐well plate, incubated for 16 h, and co‐transfected with plasmids coding for FPR1, Gαi1‐LgBiT, Gβ and SmBiT‐Gγ at a ratio of 2:1:5:5 (totaling 598 ng/well) by Lipofectamine 3000 for 24 h at 37°C. Cells were harvested and seeded on white 384‐well plates at 2 × 104 cells/well. Coelenterazine H was added to the plates at a final concentration of 10 μM, and the plate was measured for baseline luminescence using an Envision multimode plate reader for 20 min. Subsequently, fMLF and 5F1 were added separately, and the luminescence was detected for 1 h.

cAMP Measurement

FPR1‐RBL cells and neutrophils were harvested and seeded on white 384‐well plates at 3000 cells/well. Different concentrations of fMLF and 5F1 were applied separately with 2.5 μM forskolin to the cells for 30 min. Accumulation of cAMP was detected with the LANCE Ultra cAMP Kit (PerkinElmer) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The measurement was conducted by using an Envision multimode plate reader.

IP1 Measurement

FPR1‐RBL cells and neutrophils were seeded on white 384‐well plates at 3000 cells/well. Different concentrations of fMLF and 5F1 were applied separately to the cells for 1 h. Accumulation of IP1 was detected with the IP‐One‐Gq Kit (Cisbio) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The measurement was conducted by using the Envision multimode plate reader.

β‐Arrestin Membrane Translocation

For the NanoBiT‐based assay [22], HEK293T cells were seeded in a 24‐well plate, incubated for 16 h, and then co‐transfected with FPR1‐smBiT (400 ng/well) and LgBiT‐β‐arrestin1 or LgBiT‐β‐arrenstin2 (200 ng/well) by Lipofectamine 3000 for 24 h at 37°C. Cells were harvested and seeded on white 384‐well plates at 2 × 104 cells/well. The substrate of NanoLuc, coelenterazine H, was added to the plates at a final concentration of 10 μM. After 25 min of incubation at 37°C, the plate was measured for baseline luminescence using the Envision multimode plate reader, and then different concentrations of fMLF and 5F1 were added separately, and the luminescence was detected four times with a time gradient (0, 1, 2, and 3 h).

For confocal microscopy, HeLa cells were seeded on a glass coverslip placed in a 24‐well plate, incubated for 12 h, and then co‐transfected with FPR1‐Clover and β‐arrestin1‐mRuby2 or β‐arrestin2‐mRuby2 by Lipofectamine 3000 for 24 h at 37°C. The cells were treated with different concentrations of fMLF and 5F1 for 5 min at 37°C, washed with PBS, and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. The coverslips in the 24‐well plate were mounted on glass slides with ProLong Diamond Antifade Mountant and visualized by confocal microscope (LEICA TCS SP8, Buffalo Grove, IL, USA). The images were taken with a 63× oil objective. The excitation wavelength of Clover was 488 nm, and the emission wavelength was 500–525 nm. For mRuby2, the excitation wavelength was 552 nm, and the emission wavelength was 565–650 nm.

Results

5F1 Is Highly Specific for FPR1 and Recognizes ECL2 as a Major Epitope

There are two structurally homologous (69% identical amino acid sequence) formyl peptide receptors in human neutrophils, namely FPR1 and FPR2 [3]. To determine the specificity of 5F1, we first compared 5F1 with another mAb produced against the FPR2 protein (Catalog Number FAB3479G, R&D Systems) for their selectivity for the respective formyl peptide receptors. Human neutrophils prepared from peripheral blood express both FPR1 and FPR2 that were detected by the Alexa Fluor 647‐labeled 5F1 mAb and the Alexa Fluor 488‐labeled anti‐FPR2 mAb against isotype controls (Figure 1A, and also Figure S1). Since the same population of cells expresses both FPR1 and FPR2, we next used individually transfected RBL‐2H3 cell lines expressing either FPR1 or FPR2 to further determine the specificity of 5F1. Figure S2A showed that 5F1 staining of FPR2‐RBL (red, MFI = 94.3 ± 2.4) was indistinguishable from that of vector‐RBL (gray, MFI = 119 ± 2.7). Therefore, 5F1 demonstrates high specificity for FPR1 over FPR2. In comparison, the anti‐FPR2 mAb reacted weakly with the FPR1‐expressing cells (blue, MFI = 5496 ± 296 compared with vector‐RBL, gray, MFI = 2747 ± 178.7). Due to its low stock concentration (0.2 mg/mL), the commercially sourced 5F1 antibody was unsuitable for classical affinity determination via direct receptor binding assays, as these require higher antibody concentrations to achieve receptor saturation. We therefore established a competitive binding assay using flow cytometry (Figure 1B) with formyl tripeptide fMet‐Leu‐Phe (fMLF) as a competing ligand. The affinity of 5F1 to FPR1 was determined to be 3.79 × 10−9 M according to the formula of Cheng–Prusoff (Figure 1C) [20]. The binding affinity at this level was expected for an antibody and close to the binding affinity of fMLF (Kd approximately 1 nM [21]).

5F1 is a mouse IgG1 generated against full‐length FPR1 expressed in a cell line [13]. It is therefore likely that 5F1 recognizes one or more epitopes in the extracellular region of FPR1, including the N‐terminus and the extracellular loops (ECLs). A schematic representation of 5F1 binding to FPR1 is shown in Figure 2A. For epitope mapping, we synthesized a series of peptides representing amino acid sequences of the ECLs and the N‐terminus of FPR1 (Figure 2A). These peptides (10 μM) were added to the cells together with 5F1 (6.7E‐9 M) and FPR1 binding was determined. In the first group, the Alexa Fluor 647‐labeled IgG1 isotype control was applied. Group 2 contained 5F1 without the competing peptides. The value of mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) from Group 2 (maximal binding) minus Group 1 (background, isotype control) was set as 100% of binding, against which the effects of various peptides were compared. As shown in Figure 2B, the ECL2 peptide (Group 5) and all peptides mixing group (Group 7) interfered with 5F1 for FPR1 binding and reduced the MFI (p < 0.01). It was found that Group 7, which contained all the synthetic peptides, did not produce a more inhibitory effect than Group 2 (ECL2 peptide) alone. Subsequently, 4 different concentrations of ECL2 (from 100 nM to 100 μM) were co‐incubated with Alexa Fluor 647‐labeled 5F1, and the 5F1 fluorescence was reduced progressively with increasing concentrations of the ECL2 peptide (Figure 2C). This result suggests that the ECL2 peptide contains a major epitope for 5F1 recognition. To identify critical binding residues within ECL2, we performed alanine substitution of four selected amino acids (P168A, K170A, T173A, P182A; Figure S3). These sites were chosen based on their structural and physicochemical properties, as they are among the few amino acid residues that do not overlap with the ECL2 sequence of FPR1. Of the four residues chosen, the prolines are expected to hold the epitope structure, and their substitution could result in substantial structural changes of the epitope, allowing assessment of the necessity of ECL2 among other extracellular domains. All mutants were readily expressed on the cell surface, as determined by flow cytometry (Figure S3B–G). Flow cytometric analysis revealed that all four mutations significantly impaired 5F1 binding (Figure 2D,E), establishing their collective role in epitope formation in ECL2. The fact that the K170A and T173A substitutions abrogated 5F1 binding suggests that the ECL2 epitope is compact and delicate. The proline substitutions also revealed that no other extracellular domain could replace ECL2 for 5F1 binding.

5F1 Induces FPR1 Endocytosis and Conformational Changes

With the identification of the 5F1 epitope on FPR1, we next sought to investigate whether 5F1 could activate FPR1. FPR1 exhibits low basal activity but can be activated by bacterial and mitochondrial peptides containing the formyl‐Met group. We performed a receptor endocytosis assay by treating transfected cells expressing an FPR1‐Clover fusion protein with 5F1 at room temperature for 30 min and then subjecting the cells to confocal microscopy. Our results demonstrated that a high concentration of 5F1 (6.7E‐8 M) treatment led to FPR1 endocytosis (intracellular green fluorescence; Figure 3A) that was comparable to endocytosis induced by fMLF, the full agonist that activates FPR1 [23]. At 1/10th of the concentration used (6.7E‐9 M), 5F1 failed to induce FPR1 endocytosis (Figure S4A). Using flow cytometry, the 5F1‐bound and internalized receptor was detected after removing the cell surface‐bound 5F1. The 5F1‐induced endocytosis of FPR1 was obvious at 10 min after antibody addition, peaked at 30 min (Figure S4B) and continued to 60 min (not shown). Much less endocytosis was observed with 5F1 at 6.7E‐9 M; hence, the 5F1‐induced 5F1 endocytosis is concentration‐dependent.

To further elucidate the impact of 5F1 on FPR1 beyond receptor binding, we employed an FPR1‐ICL1‐NanoLuc biosensor for detection of conformational changes in FPR1 upon 5F1 binding (Figure 3B). This approach is based on the NanoBRET technology [24] and involves fusion of the C‐terminus of FPR1 with a NanoLuc protein with concurrent insertion into the intracellular loop 1 (ICL1) of a FIAsH binding motif (Cys‐Cys‐Pro‐Gly‐Cys‐Cys) that functions as a fluorescent biosensor [25]. The NanoBRET ratio change reflects the distance between ICL1 and the C‐terminus of FPR1, serving as an indication of conformational changes in FPR1. Prior research using this approach has shown that varying concentrations of the full agonist fMLF lead to distinct and biphasic conformational changes in FPR1 [26]. Our experimental findings with 5F1 demonstrated its capacity to induce similar changes in a concentration‐dependent manner [26] (Figure 3D compared with Figure 3C).

5F1 Binding Preferentially Activates Gαi Over Gβγ

To further elucidate the roles played by 5F1 binding to FPR1, experiments were conducted to investigate G protein‐dependent signaling pathways. NanoBiT technology [22] was used to monitor the dissociation of Gαi from the Gβγ subunits following FPR1 activation (Figure 4A). HEK293T cells were co‐transfected with 4 plasmids coding for FPR1, LgBiT‐tagged Gαi, Gβ, and SmBiT‐tagged Gγ. Successful complementation of LgBiT and SmBiT produced luminescence upon substrate addition, while dissociation after G protein activation caused the two complementary fragments to separate and their luminescence to cease. Using this model, we measured G protein dissociation by luminescence changes against the negative control (buffer, with full complementation) and positive control (1E‐5 M fMLF) after the addition of 5F1 (6.7E‐8 M). 5F1 induced a significant reduction in luminescence intensity (p < 0.05) (Figure 4B,C), suggesting that, like fMLF, 5F1 can induce the dissociation of Gi proteins through FPR1.

The dissociation of Gαi from Gβγ upon GPCR activation allows each of them to interact with downstream effector molecules for cellular activities. Gαi can bind to adenylate cyclase (AC) to reduce the production of intracellular cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP). The intracellular level of cAMP was measured in cells expressing FPR1, and results are depicted in Figure 4D (FPR1‐RBL cells) and Figure 4E (human neutrophils). The fMLF‐treated group exhibited a significant decrease in cAMP accumulation at ligand concentrations from 10−11 M to 10−5 M (about a 30% decrease). In comparison, the 5F1‐treated group showed less inhibition of cAMP accumulation, with a decrease of less than 20% even at the highest concentration of 5F1 used. These results indicate that 5F1 is less potent than fMLF in Gαi protein activation, which is consistent with results from the Gαi dissociation experiments.

Gβ and Gγ typically work together as a dimer for intracellular signaling. Gβγ can activate phospholipase C‐β (PLC‐β), leading to the hydrolysis of membrane phosphatidylinositol 4,5‐bisphosphate (PIP2) that generates inositol 1,4,5‐trisphosphate (IP3) and diacylglycerol (DAG) as second messengers [27]. IP3 releases Ca2+ from intracellular stores and is ultimately converted to IP1, the accumulation of which can be detected. As shown in Figure 4F (FPR1‐RBL cells) and Figure 4G (neutrophils), fMLF significantly increased IP1 accumulation at concentrations of 10 nM and above, while 5F1 had no effect on IP1 accumulation even when used at 6.7E‐8 M. This result indicates that the 5F1‐induced signaling is biased towards Gαi over Gβγ pathways.

5F1 Participates in β‐Arrestin Recruitment via FPR1

In addition to the G protein‐dependent signaling pathways, G protein‐independent signaling pathways such as β‐arrestin recruitment have been characterized for FPR1. β‐arrestin is a multifunctional protein that plays a critical role in GPCR internalization and desensitization [28, 29]. To determine whether 5F1 engages in the recruitment of β‐arrestins through FPR1, we performed confocal microscopy on HeLa cells cotransfected to express Clover‐tagged FPR1 and mRuby2‐tagged β‐arrestin1 or β‐arrestin2, respectively. The transfected cells were stimulated for 5 min with fMLF (1E‐5 M, positive control) or 5F1 (6.7E‐8 M). As expected, β‐arrestin1 translocated to the nucleus as well as the plasma membrane [30] upon fMLF stimulation (Figure 5A), while β‐arrestin2 translocated from the cytoplasm to the plasma membrane upon stimulation (Figure 5B). Under these experimental conditions, 5F1 induced translocation of β‐arrestin1 (Figure 5A) and β‐arrestin2 (Figure 5B; pseudo‐colored images for color‐blind reader can be found in Figure S5) in the same profile, suggesting that 5F1 can activate the β‐arrestin pathway.

5F1 Binding Is Sensitive to FPR1 Conformational Changes

Recent reports have shown that ECL2 plays a role in self‐activation of certain Class A GPCRs [31, 32]. In the present study, we have shown that 5F1 recognizes ECL2 as a major epitope (Figure 2). However, because 5F1 was raised against unstimulated FPR1 present on intact cells as an immunogen [13], it may be sensitive to the overall conformation of the immunogen. Indeed, the ECL2‐derived peptide only partially blocked 5F1 binding, and fMLF, which occupies a binding pocket surrounded by the transmembrane domains, can interfere with 5F1 binding (Figure 1C). These observations suggest that 5F1 is sensitive to the conformation of FPR1. To further confirm that 5F1 is a conformation‐sensitive antibody, we identified several amino acid residues in the transmembrane pocket of FPR1 that are critical to fMLF binding based on recent structural studies [33, 34, 35] and substituted them with alanine (Figure 6A). In prior studies mentioned above, D106, R201, and R205 were found to form a “triad” for critical interaction with the formyl‐Met in fMLF [33, 34, 35]. Moreover, the V105A and D106A mutants apparently altered the overall conformation of FPR1, resulting in compromised cell surface expression [33, 34, 35]. We added a FLAG tag to the N‐terminus of these FPR1 mutants for detection of cell surface expression of the receptor mutants. HeLa cells transfected to express the WT and FPR1 mutants were subject to flow cytometry analysis using vector‐transfected cells as a negative control. After subtraction of background fluorescence from the vector‐transfected cells (Figure S6A), MFI of both the anti‐FLAG M2 (FITC‐labeled) and 5F1 (Alexa Fluor 647‐labeled) from the transfected cells were plotted (Figure 6B). Compared with the WT FPR1, the impact of alanine substitution on antibody binding of FPR1 fell into three categories: The first category (M2high5F1high) is represented by the R201A mutant, showing its normal cell surface expression (black bar of R201A) and near WT conformation for recognition by 5F1 (white bar of R201A), indicating that R201 is not critically involved in 5F1 binding or cell surface expression of the receptor. In the second category (M2high5F1low), the mutant receptors (F110A, R205A) were expressed normally on the cell surface, but 5F1 binding was markedly reduced. This change reflects a preserved overall structure of the receptor but altered binding for 5F1, suggesting that substitution of F110 and R205 caused conformational changes in the receptor that affected the epitope on ECL2. The third category of mutants displayed significantly reduced cell surface expression and either proportionally reduced binding of 5F1 (V105A, M2low5F1low) or markedly reduced 5F1 binding (D106A, M2low5F1null). In the former (V105A), improper folding of the receptor could compromise its cell surface expression, with a corresponding reduction in 5F1 binding. D106A is uniquely important to the overall structure of FPR1, as a previous study showed that the D106A mutant was unable to express on the cell surface or bind fMLF [34]. Based on the results, the ratio of 5F1 binding over M2 binding was calculated to offset the effect caused by the level of receptor expression, hence focusing on 5F1 binding (Figure 6C). It appears that two of the three amino acids (D106, R205) that are critical to fMLF binding also play a role for 5F1 binding. Altogether, these experimental results demonstrate that 5F1 is a receptor conformation‐sensitive antibody and its recognition of the FPR1 epitope is influenced by the overall structure of the receptor.

Discussion

In the present study, we investigated the functional consequence of 5F1 binding to FPR1, including receptor internalization, receptor conformational changes, and receptor signaling through Gi proteins and β‐arrestin pathways. Collectively, our results indicate that 5F1 binding to FPR1 induces receptor conformational changes for Gi protein activation and β‐arrestin recruitment. ECL2, which connects TM4 and TM5, harbors a major epitope. In many Class A GPCRs, an altered ECL2 conformation can induce receptor conformational change towards an active conformation, further activating the receptor [36, 37]. This extracellular loop has also been reported in some viral GPCRs to fold back into the binding pocket for auto‐activation of the receptors [31, 32, 38, 39]. It is likely that 5F1 binding to ECL2 triggers conformational changes in FPR1 for its activation.

Agonists binding to GPCRs stabilize the receptor in an active state by altering receptor conformations that favor transmembrane signaling [40]. Previous research using the NanoBRET approach demonstrated distinct FPR1 conformational states induced by low and high concentrations of fMLF. These conformational states result in different signaling outcomes, implying that the agonist concentration‐dependent signal transduction of FPR1 could shift phagocytes from homeostasis to bactericidal activity [26]. In this study, we observed that 5F1 could induce conformational changes in FPR1, indicating that 5F1 has agonistic activity similar to formyl peptide fMLF, although much less potent than fMLF in transmembrane signaling. To note, the affinity of 5F1 to FPR1 was determined to be 3.79 × 10−9. This nanomolar affinity is very close to the binding affinity of fMLF. However, 5F1 as an antibody is bivalent and is much larger than fMLF. Therefore, it is unlikely to occupy the orthosteric binding pocket surrounded by transmembrane domains. In addition to the much weaker agonistic activity compared with fMLF, the 5F1 antibody displays notable distinctions in activating the signaling pathways downstream of FPR1, showing bias in its agonistic activity towards the Gαi pathway (cAMP reduction) over the Gβγ pathway (IP1 accumulation). This signaling property of 5F1 sets it apart from most other peptide agonists of FPR1 [4, 41, 42]. Of note, the functional assays conducted in the FPR1‐RBL stable cell line were reproduced in peripheral blood neutrophils with highly similar results.

Studies of GPCRs for biased signaling primarily focus on the determination of the bias between G protein activation and β‐arrestin recruitment. However, the bias between Gαi and Gβγ‐mediated downstream pathways by receptor agonists remains poorly understood. Qin and coworkers previously identified Compound 17b as a biased agonist that selectively avoids FPR1/2‐mediated calcium mobilization (Gβγ downstream effects) while maintaining survival signals via Gαi‐mediated pathways including the phosphorylation of ERK1/2 and Akt [9]. This distinct signaling profile allows Compound 17b to exert anti‐inflammatory effects without activating pro‐inflammatory pathways, thus protecting mice against ischemia–reperfusion injury. In other GPCRs, such as the opioid receptors, Dembla and coworkers demonstrated that the Gβγ function can be inhibited by forming a complex with TRPM3 [43]. Additionally, various intracellular effectors interacting with Gβγ may in turn inhibit its activation, causing biased activation of Gα [44]. Because the structure of a 5F1‐bound FPR1 has not been solved, it is difficult to visualize how 5F1 binding triggers different conformational states and how these conformational states are linked to biased signaling pathways. Recent structural studies have provided valuable information for FPR1 recognition of formyl peptide ligands as well as small molecule agonists [33, 34], yet cryo‐EM structures did not capture different conformational states, particularly because a Gi‐bound receptor secures the receptor conformation in an active state with high affinity for its agonist [45]. Given the concentration‐dependent nature of biased signaling through FPR1 [26], it is possible that allosteric ligand binding sites exist in FPR1 as shown in a previous report with a biphasic allosteric model of FPR2 [46]. Future studies combining structural determination and functional characterization may be necessary for a better understanding of the promiscuous nature of FPR1, with the aim of identifying biased antibody agonists and small molecule agonists.

The 5F1 is an antibody with a molecular mass much larger than that of the tripeptide fMLF, which exhibits full agonistic activity. It is therefore interesting to know how 5F1 binds to FPR1 and whether this interaction is sensitive to the receptor conformation. Based on high‐resolution structural models of FPR1 [33, 34, 35], fMLF binding to the transmembrane pocket involves a triad of amino acids, D106, R201, and R205. In the present study, our site‐directed mutagenesis has allowed us to examine the functional impact of 5 alanine substituents including D106A, R201A, and R205A. By measuring mutant receptor expression and the impact of the site mutants on 5F1 binding in parallel, we have identified D106, F110, and R205 as key amino acid residues that negatively impacted 5F1 binding. All three of these residues are buried in TM3 and TM5, respectively, which play an important role in the ligand binding pocket of FPR1. In our previous study, we have shown that mutations of these three residues could alter the overall conformation of FPR1 that affects agonist binding [34]. Therefore, the observation that these mutations affected 5F1 binding provides further evidence that 5F1 binding to FPR1 is highly dependent on the conformation of the receptor. Our results demonstrate that 5F1 binding to FPR1 can induce receptor conformational change and, at the same time, it is sensitive to receptor conformational change. Unlike formyl peptides such as fMLF that use the N‐terminal formyl group for strong binding and receptor activation, 5F1 recognizes primarily the epitope on ECL2. As such, it may induce conformational changes of FPR1 that differ from the changes induced by formyl peptide agonists. Moreover, the bivalent antibody may induce receptor dimerization, a possibility that has not been tested in this study. In this regard, it is interesting to note a recently developed agonist anti‐ChemR23 mAb that produces conformational changes in the receptor CMKLR1/Chem23 leading to distinct macrophage phenotypes [47, 48], bringing the possibility of using such antibodies for the treatment of inflammatory diseases and even cancer through their influence on the tumor microenvironment.

FPR1 plays a crucial role in regulating inflammatory responses and sensing cell damage, and its modulation has attracted considerable attention for the treatment of inflammatory diseases [49]. To date, only a few small molecule agonists have progressed to clinical trials [50]. Large molecules such as antibodies may induce biased signal transduction through FPR1, thus representing a promising direction in GPCR research as well as clinical application [51]. The characterization of 5F1 as a highly specific antibody with biased agonistic activity at FPR1 may provide an additional option for the development of therapies for inflammatory diseases.
