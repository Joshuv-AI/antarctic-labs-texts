Macular choriocapillaris perfusion area: a potential biomarker of mild cognitive impairment in patients with cerebral small vessel disease
Authors: Weitao Yu, Zeqi Shen, Weifen Zhang, Mengmeng Yue, Shouxuan Gao, Jiawei Ye, Wanmao Ni, Panpan Shen, Lujie Han, Shunyuan Guo, Jie Zheng, Liang Yu, Faliang Gao, Yu Geng, Chaoyang Hong, Sheng Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo develop and validate retinal vascular biomarkers for detecting mild cognitive impairment (MCI) in cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) using swept-source optical coherence tomography angiography (SS-OCTA).MethodsParticipants with MCI and normal cognition were prospectively enrolled from two ongoing cohorts (Dream-10 and FRESH-CSVD; NCT06164262 and NCT06431711). All participants underwent SS-OCTA and structural MRI (S-MRI). Individuals with Alzheimer’s disease were excluded based on plasma biomarkers. Participants were split into development (January–August 2024) and temporal validation (September 2024–January 2025) cohorts. Feature selection was conducted using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression, followed by receiver operating characteristic analyses.ResultsA total of 209 participants were included, with 48.8% (102/209) diagnosed with MCI. In the development cohort (n=136), the 3–6 mm macular choriocapillaris perfusion area (CCPA) of the left eye (oculus sinister, OS) showed superior diagnostic accuracy for MCI (AUC=0.906), outperforming S-MRI markers (all p<0.05). Temporal validation confirmed diagnostic accuracy (AUC 0.902; sensitivity 88.6%, specificity 81.3%) with minimal performance drift (ΔAUC 0.002). Adding S-MRI markers did not significantly enhance diagnostic performance (p>0.05). Both 0–3 and 3–6 mm OS macular CCPA were significantly associated with cognitive decline (Mini-Mental State Examination, Montreal Cognitive Assessment and Clinical Dementia Rating Sum of Boxes; all p<0.01), and mediation analyses suggested partial effects through white matter hyperintensity volume and right choroid plexus volume ratio.ConclusionSS-OCTA-derived macular CCPA, especially in the 3–6 mm OS region, may serve as a promising and non-invasive biomarker for CSVD-related MCI. Further multicentre studies are needed to establish its clinical applicability.Keywords: Cerebrovascular Disorders, Cognitive Dysfunction

Introduction

Cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) is among the most prevalent neurological disorders, affecting the small arteries and veins in the brain. Its prevalence increases markedly with age, ranging from approximately 5% of individuals aged 50 to nearly 100% of individuals over 90 years old.1 CSVD is a major cause of vascular cognitive impairment, which typically progresses insidiously. Many patients initially present with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), which is a critical stage for intervention. However, the diagnosis of CSVD-related MCI currently relies on comprehensive neuropsychological assessments and multimodal MRI, both of which can be time-consuming and resource-intensive. Given the high prevalence of CSVD and the often subtle nature of cognitive decline, there is a pressing need for novel, efficient screening tools.

The retina and cerebral vasculature share common embryonic origins, anatomical characteristics and physiological features.2 Retinal imaging thus provides a unique, non-invasive window into cerebral microvascular health. Swept-source optical coherence tomography angiography (SS-OCTA) enables high-resolution, rapid and cost-effective visualisation of retinal and choroidal vasculature. Prior OCTA studies have reported that the vascular density of the deep vascular complex (DVC), superficial vascular complex (SVC) and radial peripapillary capillary (RPC) in patients with CSVD is significantly reduced compared with the healthy controls.3 4 Other studies have also reported that these parameters can act as markers for Alzheimer’s disease (AD), MCI or preclinical AD.5–7 Despite these advances, it remains unclear whether OCTA-derived parameters can effectively serve as diagnostic markers of MCI specifically in the context of CSVD. Furthermore, while structural MRI (S-MRI) features, including CSVD imaging markers8 and focal brain atrophy,9 have been linked to cognitive decline, their diagnostic accuracy for early-stage MCI in CSVD and their relationship with retinal microvascular changes are still not well established.

Here, we aimed to explore and validate the diagnostic value of SS-OCTA in detecting MCI among patients with CSVD, and to assess its potential as a screening tool compared with S-MRI-based methods. Additionally, we examined the associations between retinal microvascular parameters derived from SS-OCTA, cerebral lesions identified through S-MRI and cognitive performance assessed by standardised neuropsychological testing. Through this integrative approach, we sought to elucidate the potential links among changes in retinal vascular alterations and cognitive decline, providing new insights into the mechanisms of CSVD-related cognitive impairment.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective observational study included participants from two ongoing longitudinal cohorts: the Dream-10 cohort (NCT06164262) and the FRESH-CSVD cohort (NCT06431711). Participants were consecutively recruited between January 2024 and January 2025.

All participants underwent comprehensive cognitive assessments, structural S-MRI and SS-OCTA. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age 50–85 years, (2) diagnosis of CSVD based on clinical history and MRI findings (eg, white matter hyperintensities (WMH), lacunes or cerebral microbleeds (CMBs)) and (3) completion of both imaging and cognitive testing.

Exclusion criteria included the following: (1) history of major neurological disorders unrelated to CSVD (eg, AD, Parkinson’s disease, multiple sclerosis), (2) significant psychiatric illness or systemic disease that may confound cognitive assessment, (3) poor-quality OCTA or MRI images and (4) evidence of Alzheimer’s pathology based on plasma biomarkers (see online supplemental methods).

To allow temporal validation, the cohort was chronologically divided into a development set (January to August 2024) and a validation set (September 2024 to January 2025).

Clinical and cognitive assessments

Demographics and medical history data were obtained through face-to-face interviews and review of clinical records. The presence of diabetes mellitus, hypertension and hyperlipidaemia was determined based on clinical history.

Cognitive function was assessed using Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). For MoCA, a one-point correction was applied for individuals with 12 or fewer years of education, following recommendations for the Chinese population, and a cut-off of 25 was used to identify cognitive impairment.10

Experienced neuropsychiatrists administered the Clinical Dementia Rating Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB), covering six domains: memory, orientation, judgement, community affairs, home and hobbies, and personal care. Cognitive status was classified as follows: normal cognition (NC) (CDR-SB=0), MCI (CDR-SB=0.5–4), mild dementia (4.5–9), moderate dementia (9.5–15.5) and severe dementia (16–18).11 In addition, a composite CDR domain score (range 0–6) was calculated by summing the number of domains with abnormal ratings to quantify domain-specific impairment.

Plasma Alzheimer’s disease biomarker testing

To exclude participants with AD pathology, Plasma biomarkers (Aβ42/40, p-tau181, p-tau217) were detected following standardised protocols using the COMET Multiplex Flow Cytometry Kit (Jiangxi Cell Gene Biotech Co., China). Participants with biomarker positivity (predefined thresholds) were excluded to minimise the confounding effects of AD. Detailed protocols are provided in online supplemental methods.

SS-OCTA acquisition and parameters

A SS-OCTA imaging system (VG200I; Intalight, vangogh v3.0.188) was utilised to evaluate the retinas of both eyes in all participants. All participants underwent bilateral scanning of the macular and optic disc regions (ONH) under standard ambient lighting. Only images with a Signal Strength Index≥6 and free from motion artefacts or segmentation errors were included. This study evaluated the RPC, SVC, DVC and choriocapillaris (CC) in the macular and ONH regions. Scans were acquired using an SS-OCTA protocol with automated motion correction, an axial resolution of 3.8 µm, and a scan area of 6×6 mm. Details regarding scan parameters and image processing protocols are provided in the online Supplementary Methods.

S-MRI analysis

All participants underwent S-MRI scans using an eight-channel phased array head coil on a 3.0 T whole-body MRI scanner (Discovery MR750 3.0T; GE Healthcare). The detailed MRI scan parameters in the present study are presented in online supplemental table 1. Detailed MRI preprocessing protocols and CSVD rating criteria are provided in online Supplementary Methods. All MRI data were reviewed by two board-certified neuroradiologists blinded to cognitive status and OCTA findings.

Sample size calculation

We calculated the sample size based on the events per variable (EPV) metric.12 13 The incidence of MCI was 0.52 in the development and validation cohorts. We included 4–6 predictor variables and set the EPV to 10; thus, the required sample size for the development cohort was calculated to be 125. Besides, we predefined an acceptable performance difference (Δ) of less than 0.05 for the AUC between the development and validation cohorts. These parameters ensured adequate statistical power to evaluate the model’s generalisability and supported the validity of the temporal validation approach.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are summarised as mean±SD (normally distributed) or median (IQR) (non-normally distributed). Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s χ2/Fisher’s exact tests; continuous variables were analysed with Mann-Whitney U/t-tests. A logistic regression model with feature selection via least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) was used to identify optimal SS-OCTA predictors of MCI. This procedure allowed unbiased selection without predefining eye or region. The discrimination ability of MCI markers was assessed using the area under the ROC curve, net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI). Multivariable linear regression analyses were conducted to assess the relationship between SS-OCTA parameters and cognitive scores (MMSE, MoCA and CDR-SB), adjusting for age, gender and vascular risk factors. Subgroup analyses tested OCTA marker specificity across CSVD subphenotypes. Spearman correlation analyses were conducted to evaluate the correlation between MCI markers, CSVD imaging features and cognitive status. Mediation analyses were conducted to assess the mediation effect of S-MRI markers between OCTA markers and cognitive status. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method, with adjusted p values<0.05 considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using R software, V.4.2.2 and MSTATA software (www.mstata.com).

Results

Participants’ characteristics

Development cohort

A total of 136 participants (mean age=60 years ± 10 (SD); 67 male) were enrolled in the development cohort (figure 1), including 73 (53.7%) participants with MCI and 63 (46.3%) participants with NC. Compared with the NC group, MCI patients were older (61±10 vs 58±10 years, p=0.032), had a higher prevalence of diabetes mellitus (28.8% vs 11.1%, p=0.011) and exhibited more severe CSVD imaging burden (periventricular hyperintensity, PVH grade≥2: 38.4% vs 7.9%, p<0.001) (table 1).

Validation cohort

In total, 73 participants (mean age: 58 years±11 (SD); 41 male), with 35 (47.9%) diagnosed with MCI and 38 (52.1%) with NC (figure 1). MCI patients were older (60±10 years vs 56±11 years, p=0.034), were more likely to have hypertension (48.6% vs 26.3%, p=0.049) and exhibited greater deep WMH burden (DWMH grade≥2: 32.0% vs 6.25%, p=0.014) compared with participants with NC (table 1).

Selection of MCI markers based on OCTA and S-MRI in the development cohort

LASSO regression analysis (online supplemental figure 1) was used to screen 138 OCTA-derived parameters (69 per eye) and 112 MRI parameters (106 structural features, 6 CSVD markers). The model identified two key OCTA predictors (0–3 and 3–6 mm oculus sinister (OS) macular choriocapillaris perfusion area (CCPA)) and seven S-MRI variables, including WMH volume, left hippocampal (L-HPC) volume ratio and right choroid plexus (R-CP) volume ratio.

Multivariable logistic regression, adjusted for age and diabetes mellitus, confirmed that 3–6 mm OS macular CCPA remained the strongest predictor of MCI (OR=0.196, 95% CI=0.110 to 0.349, FDR-adjusted), along with WMH volume (OR=2.270, 95% CI=1.369 to 3.763), L-HPC volume ratio (OR=0.867, 95% CI=0.789 to 0.953) and R-CP volume ratio (OR=1.934, 95% CI=1.218 to 3.072) (table 2).

Validation cohort performance of MCI markers

In the temporal validation cohort (n=73), the 3–6 mm OS macular CCPA maintained high diagnostic accuracy for MCI, with an AUC of 0.902 (95% CI=0.831 to 0.973), sensitivity of 88.6% and specificity of 81.3%. The ΔAUC between the development and validation sets was 0.002, well below the predefined acceptable threshold of 0.05, indicating minimal performance drift (figure 2B).

By contrast, S-MRI markers such as WMH volume (AUC=0.675), L-HPC (AUC=0.692) and R-CP (AUC=0.571) demonstrated significantly lower diagnostic performance (all p<0.05 vs CCPA markers; table 3). The NRI and IDI also confirmed the superior clinical value of OCTA markers over S-MRI markers (both p<0.05; table 3).

Notably, integrating S-MRI markers into the model did not significantly enhance the diagnostic value beyond either the 0–3 or 3–6 mm macular CCPA alone (AUC=0.909 vs 0.864 and 0.902, respectively; all p>0.05). Furthermore, neither the 0–3 nor 3–6 mm macular CCPA from the right eye (OD) outperformed the OS 3–6 mm CCPA in either the development or validation cohorts (both all p>0.05; Online supplemental table 2).

Subgroup analysis for the diagnostic value of 0–3 and 3–6 mm OS macular CCPA in two cohorts

In the development cohort (n=136), compared with 0–3 mm zone, the diagnostic efficacy of 3–6 mm macular CCPA was significantly improved in three subgroups. In the non-diabetic subgroup, the 3–6 mm CCPA demonstrated higher AUC (0.891 vs 0.825; p<0.01), with a 27.0% increase in absolute sensitivity (92.3% vs 65.3%). Patients without CMBs exhibited a marked increase in AUC (0.903 vs 0.834, p<0.05) and a 25.5% increase in sensitivity (90.9% vs 65.4%). Additionally, patients without lacunes revealed the highest diagnostic accuracy (AUC 0.927 vs 0.869; p<0.01) with a 26.8% increase in sensitivity (94.6% vs 67.8%). These patterns of superiority of 3–6 mm OS macular CCPA were consistent in the validation cohort (n=73), although the differences between subgroups were not statistically significant (p>0.05; table 4). Association between OS macular CCPA, S-MRI markers and cognitive measures

A pooled analysis of all participants (n=209) revealed that reduced 0–3 and 3–6 mm OS macular CCPA values were significantly and inversely correlated with age, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, stroke history and CSVD neuroimaging markers, including periventricular and deep white matter hyperintensity (PWMH/DWMH grades), presence of lacunes and CMBs, and enlarged perivascular spaces (EPVS) grades (all p<0.05; Online supplemental figure 2).

In multivariable linear regression models adjusted for age (model 1), both 0–3 and 3–6 mm OS macular CCPA were significantly associated with PWMH and DWMH grades, the presence of lacune and R-CP volume ratio (all p<0.05). Additionally, 0–3 mm OS macular CCPA was correlated with EPVS grade and L-HPC volume ratio, while 3–6 mm CCPA was linked to WMH volume (all p<0.05). After further adjustment for vascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes mellitus and stroke history; model 2), lacunes remained independently associated with both CCPA measurements (p<0.05). Notably, 3–6 mm OS macular CCPA retained significant associations with WMH volume and R-CP volume ratio, whereas the associations for 0–3 mm CCPA were attenuated (all p>0.05; figure 3).

After adjusting for age, hypertension, diabetes mellitus and stroke history, both 0–3 and 3–6 mm OS macular CCPA remained significantly associated with MMSE (0–3 mm: β=2.524; 3–6 mm: β=0.938), MoCA (0–3 mm: β=2.074; 3–6 mm: β=0.733) and CDR-SB (0–3 mm: β=−0.487; 3–6 mm: β=−0.221) (online supplemental table 3). In domain-specific cognitive analyses, decreased OS macular CCPA was correlated with worse CDR memory (β=−0.240 for 0–3 mm; β=−0.091 for 3–6 mm) and judgement/problem-solving scores (β=−0.122 for 0–3 mm; β=−0.049 for 3–6 mm). Furthermore, each one-unit reduction in OS macular CCPA increased the number of impaired CDR subdomains by 1.151 (0–3 mm) and 0.440 (3–6 mm) (both p<0.001; Online supplemental table 3, CDR affected domains section).

Mediation analyses showed that the association between OS macular CCPA and CDR-SB was partially mediated by WMH volume and R-CP volume ratio. For the 0–3 mm zone, mediation proportions were 10.1% (WMH) and 6.2% (R-CP). For the 3–6 mm zone, 9.4% (WMH) and 6.6% (R-CP). Similar proportions were observed for CDR-affected domain scores (WMH: 10.8% vs 10.0%; R-CP: 6.4% vs 6.7%). Additionally, both CCPA zones exhibited indirect effects on WMH volume through R-CP volume ratio, with mediation proportions of 9.1% (0–3 mm) and 13.3% (3–6 mm) (figure 4). No significant interaction effects were detected among independent variables (all interaction p>0.1).

Discussion

We identified macular CCPA measured by SS-OCTA, especially within the 3–6 mm zone of the left eye, as a promising biomarker for detecting MCI in patients with CSVD. In both the development and validation cohorts, this parameter demonstrated higher diagnostic accuracy compared with conventional S-MRI-derived markers. Subgroup analyses further supported its generalisability across CSVD subtypes. Reduced macular CCPA was correlated with increased WMH burden, R-CP enlargement and multidomain cognitive impairment. Mediation analyses suggested that its association with cognition may be partially explained by shared neurovascular mechanisms involving cerebral white matter lesions and CP dysregulation.

SS-OCTA facilitates high-resolution quantification of CCPA through improved signal penetration into the choroid. In our study, macular CCPA (specifically 3–6 mm zone) emerged as a robust biomarker of microvascular dysfunction in CSVD, supporting a link between choroidal hypoperfusion and cognitive impairment. While prior studies have primarily associated choroidal changes with AD,14–18 our findings highlight their potential diagnostic relevance in CSVD-related MCI. Although both eyes were initially included in the LASSO-based feature selection process, the model identified 3–6 mm macular CCPA from the left eye (OS) as the most predictive variable, with right-eye parameters consistently showing lower diagnostic performance (online supplemental table 2). Notably, CCPA outperformed conventional MRI-derived markers such as WMH volume and hippocampal atrophy. Adding S-MRI markers did not significantly improve diagnostic performance, suggesting overlapping vascular information captured by both modalities. This questions the necessity of multimodal imaging and underscores the potential of SS-OCTA as a cost-effective alternative. Macular CCPA remained diagnostically valuable even in patients without lacune (AUC=0.889/0.906 for 0–3/3–6 mm macular regions) or with mild CSVD burden (AUC=0.873/0.880 for CSVD score=0), suggesting that choroidal perfusion deficits may precede overt brain lesions in the disease course.

The potential utility of macular CCPA as a biomarker for CSVD-related MCI is supported by the unique anatomical and physiological characteristics of the choroid, which exhibits one of the highest vascular densities per unit tissue mass in human body.19 The CC, arranged in hexagonal lobules, lacks conventional autoregulation and constriction in response to oxygen tension,20–23 making it particularly susceptible to perfusion deficits. Alterations in choroidal haemodynamics may thus signal early microvascular injury. Although the central macular region (0–3 mm) is relatively avascular, we observed that the peripheral macular zone (3–6 mm) provided greater diagnostic value for MCI, suggesting heightened vulnerability in the peripheral macula. To the best of our knowledge, this spatial distribution pattern has not been previously reported in CSVD. However, analogous peripheral susceptibility has been observed in AD, where pathology is preferentially localised to the outer retinal layers17 and Aβ deposition is more pronounced in the 3–6 mm region.24 We propose that CSVD may share a similar spatial pattern of microcirculatory vulnerability with AD, particularly in the outer macular region.

Our mediation analysis revealed that the association between reduced macular CCPA and cognitive impairment was partially mediated by WMH volume and the R-CP enlargement. R-CP enlargement also mediated the relationship between macular CCPA reduction and WMH expansion, implicating it as a key driver of cognitive decline. Physiologically, the CP exhibits functional and anatomical asymmetry, with left-sided dominance in healthy individuals.25 26 A transcriptomic study further suggests lateralised specialisation, with the left CP enriched in immune-related and adhesion-related genes, and the R-CP expressing higher levels of cilia-related transcripts.27 Given that ependymal cilia regulate perivascular cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow, essential for glymphatic clearance and neuroblast migration,28 R-CP enlargement may signify ciliary dysfunction and CSF dysregulation.29 Combined with prior evidence implicating glymphatic dysfunction in WMH pathogenesis,30 31 this suggests CP asymmetry, particularly R-CP enlargement, may indicate glymphatic dysfunction in CSVD.

These findings support a brain–eye glymphatic connection.32–34 CSF originating from the CP enters the optic nerve sheath via the subarachnoid space, then circulates through retinal perivascular spaces and lamina cribrosa.32 33 Concurrently, metabolic clearance of Aβ and other neurotoxic wastes occurs through aquaporin-4 (AQP4), which is dependent on pathways involving Müller cells and astrocytes. This process is facilitated by pressure gradients across the lamina cribrosa, promoting the mixing of aqueous humour and CSF into interstitial fluid, which ultimately drains via venular routes into dural lymphatics.34–36 Our findings suggest a link between cerebral glymphatic dysfunction and retinal waste accumulation and microvascular damage,37 highlighting ocular pathways as a potential window into CSVD pathogenesis. Supporting this, Wu et al found that R-CP enlargement was associated with impaired retinal haemodynamics and that retinal vascular morphology was a predictor of CP volume, WMH and peak skeletonised mean diffusivity, with CP volume accounting for 36.9%–51.8% of retinal vascular variance.38 We propose that reduced macular CCPA on SS-OCTA may serve as an early, non-invasive biomarker of glymphatic impairment in CSVD. This impairment may precede WMH progression and cognitive decline. Future multicentre, longitudinal studies are warranted to validate these associations and clarify the underlying causal pathways.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a cross-sectional, single-centre study with a limited sample size. Although 3–6 mm OS macular CCPA exhibited good diagnostic value for MCI in both cohorts, further multicentre, longitudinal studies are required for validation. Second, while plasma biomarkers were used to exclude AD patients, the absence of additional confirmatory biomarkers (eg, CSF testing or Aβ/tau-PET) introduces a residual risk of misclassification. Future studies should incorporate these tools to ensure more precise aetiological differentiation. Third, the mechanisms linking ocular and cerebrovascular pathology remain unclear. Experimental studies in animal models or long-term human imaging studies are needed to elucidate causal pathways and clarify the role of CC dysfunction in CSVD-related cognitive decline.

In conclusion, OS macular CCPA, particularly in the 3–6 mm zone, may serve as a promising non-invasive biomarker for MCI in patients with CSVD. It exhibited higher diagnostic performance than conventional MRI markers and was associated with cognitive decline, partially mediated by WMH and R-CP enlargement. These findings suggest a shared microvascular and glymphatic contribution to cognitive impairment in CSVD. However, given the single-centre nature of this study, these results should be interpreted with caution. Future multicentre, prospective studies are warranted to confirm its clinical utility and generalisability.
