Knowledge and awareness factors affecting kidney function and chronic kidney disease among the general population in the West Bank, Palestine: an online cross-sectional study
Authors: Mohammad Yaser Hasan Awad, Haya Jebreen Mohammed Warasna, Bashar Yaser Hasan Awad, Mohamed E A Shaaban, Suzana Sultan, Iyad Yaser Hasan Awad, Ahmad J Warasna, Tarek A Owais
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic kidney disease (CKD) poses a growing burden in Palestine’s West Bank; however, public awareness remains unassessed. This study evaluated the knowledge of kidney function, CKD risk factors, symptoms, prevention, and treatment among Palestinians and identified the predictors of awareness.MethodsAn online cross-sectional survey of 592 adults (≥18 years) was conducted in 11 West Bank cities (August–September 2024). A validated Arabic questionnaire was used to assess sociodemographic characteristics and CKD knowledge. Reliability was confirmed through pilot testing. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were used to identify predictors of high knowledge (≥70% score threshold).ResultsOnly 35.135% of the respondents had attended CKD seminars. Attendees exhibited significantly higher overall knowledge than non-attendees (70.673% vs. 38.021%, p < 0.001). Key gaps included knowledge of kidney metabolic functions (31.250% vs. 17.708%), CKD stages (36.058% vs. 10.677%), and medication-based treatment (75.962% vs. 62.760%). Regression revealed higher odds of high knowledge among seminar attendees (aOR = 4.00, 95% CI: 2.82–5.67), adults >50 years (aOR = 5.56, 95% CI: 3.12–9.91), and females (aOR = 1.65, 95% CI: 1.05–2.59). Social media (25.636%) and academic materials (28.390%) were the primary information sources.ConclusionCKD knowledge gaps are prevalent in the West Bank, particularly regarding early symptoms and non-dialysis treatment. Educational seminars significantly improve awareness, but their reach remains limited. Future interventions should prioritize accessible community-based education, digital campaigns targeting youth, and screenings for high-risk groups. Further research should explore barriers among older populations and evaluate targeted awareness initiatives to address these issues.Keywords: Awareness, chronic kidney disease, public health, risk factors, West Bank

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is defined as kidney damage or a glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of less than 60 ml/min/1.73 m2 for at least 3 months [1]. CKD affects over 800 million people globally, with a rising age-standardized mortality rate of nearly 37% between 2005 and 2013 [2,3]. In the West Bank, the burden of CKD has quadrupled since 2006, with the number of patients undergoing dialysis increasing from 391 to over 1550 by 2021 [4]. Despite the increasing demand, the West Bank’s dialysis capacity remains inadequate, with only 11 units, each serving over 140 patients. These overburdened facilities provide just 2–3 weekly sessions per patient, totaling 147,494 annual hemodialysis (HD) sessions in 2016 (Palestinian Ministry of Health, 2017) [5]. Compounding these challenges, fewer than 20 practicing nephrologists serve the entire region, creating severe disparities in access to renal care, particularly in rural populations [6]. A local study [7] estimated that the average annual cost of HD in Palestine is approximately US$16,085 per patient, covering dialysis sessions, medications, laboratory tests, and outpatient services. In comparison, the average first-year cost of a kidney transplant was reported to be around US$16,277. The Palestinian government funded nearly all HD and transplantation expenses. While comprehensive data from laboratory sources to estimate the prevalence of non-dialysis CKD in the West Bank are limited, the available studies provide valuable insights. A cross-sectional study of primary health care centers in the northern West Bank indicated that 23.6% of 386 patients with type 2 diabetes had CKD, with 19.7% at stage 3, 2.6% at stage 4, and 1.3% at stage 5. In addition, a multicenter study of Palestinian elderly patients showed a 30.2% rate of renal impairment (eGFR <60 ml/min/1.73 m2) [8,9].

Kidney transplantation is uncommon, and preventive nephrology is underdeveloped in this region. There is an absence of a national CKD registry and coordinated screening initiatives, and CKD awareness campaigns are nearly absent. Insufficient early detection initiatives and a scarcity of qualified healthcare workers result in numerous patients arriving late, with dialysis as the sole alternative. Raising public awareness and understanding of CKD is central to its prevention and management. Low awareness is associated with late diagnosis, inadequate disease control, and an elevated progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [10]. From an international perspective, knowledge surveys have been essential in establishing gaps in public knowledge and guiding effective education and intervention programs [11–13].

Currently, no continuous, large-scale campaigns focus on creating public awareness specifically about kidney health or CKD in the West Bank. The importance of carrying out a knowledge survey of CKD in the West Bank is to fulfill the pressing need to define public knowledge regarding kidney function and the early detection of kidney injury in a setting where the disease burden is rising, but preventive care and awareness campaigns are low. In the West Bank, where the CKD burden is growing and nephrology service delivery is limited, conducting a survey of knowledge is an imperative step towards filling this gap. Moreover, specific areas of knowledge deficit will enable more efficient use of limited healthcare resources and perhaps slow CKD progression by enabling earlier identification and lifestyle modification, which are essential factors in limiting the long-term economic burden of ESRD.

While developing educational programs for healthcare professionals is vital, understanding public CKD knowledge is equally crucial for designing population-wide preventive strategies. Surprisingly, no previous study has assessed the public awareness of kidney health in the West Bank. This study addresses this critical gap through a survey of the factors affecting kidney function and CKD knowledge and awareness in this high-risk population. The findings will inform targeted interventions to optimize the use of limited resources.

Materials and methods

Study design, period and setting

This online cross-sectional study targeted Palestinian people aged ≥ 18 years. Data were collected in August and September 2024. Participants were sampled from 11 West Bank cities: Hebron, Jerusalem, Ramallah, Jenin, Tolkarm, Bethlehem, Nablus, Salfeet, Qalqeliah, Tubas, and Jericho. The West Bank, with a population of approximately 3.250 million (Palestinian Central Bureau of Statistics (PCBS), 2023) [14], is a geographically diverse region consisting of urban areas, villages, and refugee camps. It measures approximately 5,655 km2 in area and has a Mediterranean to semi-arid climate with hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters—climatic conditions that could possibly influence public health practices and access. The questionnaire was sent over WhatsApp, Facebook, Instagram, and email, media commonly used in Palestine, to achieve high reach and response rates. The media were selected because of their availability, popularity, and potential for accessing various groups of the population immediately. Participants were requested to share the link of the questionnaire on social media and with their networks, a snowball sampling, to facilitate a broader scope and involvement than from the immediate social network of the research team. This was found to be especially useful concerning the logistic and political limitations of gathering data in the field within the region, as well as the need to provide a diverse sample from localities. All responses were made anonymously via an online questionnaire.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants were considered eligible for participation if they met the following criteria: (1) aged 18 years or older, to guarantee legal ability to give informed consent and respond to questions about personal health and behavior; (2) living in the West Bank, since the study specifically sought to recruit participants from the population residing within this geographic area; (3) able to read and comprehend Arabic, the language in which the questionnaire was made available, to limit survey item misinterpretation; and (4) had internet access and the capability to independently self-complete an online questionnaire, since the study was conducted solely online. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) individuals less than 18 years, out of concern for the ethical process of obtaining children’s consent; (2) individuals who do not reside in the West Bank since they fall outside the given population to be studied; (3) not fully completed questionnaires that might compromise data validity; and (4) inconsistent or duplicate answers. These stipulations were made to ensure data quality, offer a well-defined sample, and maintain ethical standards of human subject research.

Study instruments

The study recruited 592 responses, determined through a sample size calculation using the Raosoft online calculator [15]. The calculation was based on a 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, and a 50% response rate. The population size, estimated at approximately 3,250,000 on July 11, 2023, was obtained from the PCBS [14]. The survey questionnaire was adapted from a prior study conducted in Lebanon [16]. The questionnaire was originally developed for a Lebanese study and was linguistically adapted for this study. First, a certified translator translated the English version into Arabic. Healthcare professionals evaluated each item for clarity, relevance, and cultural appropriateness in the Palestinian setting. Through this systematic process, we ensured that the instrument remained true to the original constructs while resonating accurately and sensitively with our target population. To validate the clarity and reliability of the questionnaire, a pilot study was conducted with 50 randomly selected members of the public. These participants were excluded from the final sample to avoid potential biases. The pilot study confirmed the questionnaire’s internal consistency, with standard alpha values of 0.79, as illustrated in the Supplementary file (see Annex 1). The questionnaire had two parts: the first assessed sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, and the second assessed kidney function and CKD awareness.

The first section contained general sociodemographic characteristics on age, sex, residential area, education level, employment, marital status, monthly income, and usage of tobacco products. Additionally, the clinical characteristics of the current health and family medical histories of renal, metabolic, and cardiovascular illnesses were assessed through yes/no and multiple-choice items.

Basic kidney function questions

These questions were designed to assess participants’ knowledge of the basic physiological functions of the kidneys, such as waste filtering, fluid management, and blood pressure maintenance. These were generic knowledge questions that would check existing familiarity with how healthy kidneys function.

CKD-related questions

These questions were aimed at assessing respondents’ knowledge of the causes and risk factors, symptoms, complications, and prevention of CKD and diseases such as diabetes and hypertension that have the potential to cause CKD. The questions also addressed the knowledge of the early signs of kidney damage and the importance of screening and early diagnosis.

A cut-off score of 70% correct answers was used to categorize participants into high knowledge (≥70%) and low knowledge (<70%) categories. This cut-off point was selected based on earlier studies in similar settings where a 70% mark was considered a reasonable score for adequate health-related knowledge [17].

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol received reference number EA/2024/1 and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Institutional Review Board at Palestine Polytechnic University. Before answering, all participants were prompted to view an electronic informed consent form at the beginning of the questionnaire. Participants were presented with the option to voluntarily decline to participate by selecting ‘No,’ which immediately closed them out of the survey. Participants were also informed of their right to withdraw at any time during the study without consequence. Full details were provided on the purpose of the study, confidentiality of the data, and anonymity of the responses. For confidentiality, no identifying data were collected, and all responses were confidentially preserved within an Excel sheet. The data were examined only in the aggregate form.

Data management and analysis

Data were collected via an online survey and downloaded to an Excel sheet. The Analysis was performed using R version 4.1.1. Participants’ sociodemographic characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were represented as percentages and numbers. A comparison was made between people who answered the questions about knowledge correctly and those who did not, with a 70% cut-off used to categorize high versus low knowledge. The p value was calculated using logistic regression and Wald’s test. While multivariate analysis was corrected for confounding variables, as shown by the Adjusted Odds Ratio (aOR), univariate analysis yielded Odds Ratios (OR) to show the direction of the relationship. A 95% Confidence Interval (CI) and p value of 0.05 were used to calculate the strength of the association.

Results

Baseline

A total of 592 participants were included in this study. Most participants were female (76.014%, n = 450). Participants aged between 18 and 29 composed the highest percentage of the sample (79.223%). Most participants were from Hebron (35.811%). The majority of the participants were single, composing (58.446%) of the sample. Most of the participants were in university or had completed it (84.966%). A significant proportion of the participants were students (45.608%). Only (16.554%) of the participants were smokers. The largest proportion of participants (44.764%, n = 265) had a family income of 1880–5000 ILS. Most participants (59.122%, n = 350) had never undergone renal function tests, and 35.135% attended workshops related to CKD. As shown in Table 1.

Knowledge by going to seminars

Regarding knowledge level, there was a significant difference between those who participated in CKD seminars and those who did not. A total of 592 participants responded to the knowledge section: 208 attendees and 384 non-attendees. People who attended seminars were more likely to have higher levels of knowledge about CKD. As 70.673% of seminar attendees had a high knowledge level compared to only 38.021% of those who did not attend seminars (p < 0.001). A higher percentage of people who participated in CKD seminars (77.404%, n = 161) knew that urine production is one of the kidney functions compared to people who did not attend any of these seminars (64.844%, n = 249), with a p value of 0.006. A total of 51.442% of the attendees knew that the kidney contributes to healthy bones, compared to only 25.781% of non-attendees (p < 0.001). Additionally, more attendees knew that maintaining blood pressure is one of the kidney functions (81.250%, n = 169), than non-attendees (58.073%, n = 223) (p < 0.001). Surprisingly, a higher percentage of attendees (31.250%, n = 65) than non-attendees (17.708%, n = 68) thought that breaking proteins is one of the kidney functions (p < 0.001). A higher percentage of attendees (80.769% and 50.962%) knew that kidney health could be assessed using blood test, and blood pressure, respectively. In comparison, 71.094% and 31.510% of non-attendees answered yes to the same question (p = 0.036, p < 0.001). Regarding the number of CKD stages, 36.058% of the attendees answered 5, compared to only 10.677% of the non-attendees (p < 0.001). Similarly, a higher percentage of attendees (77.885%) answered that adults over 50 years of age were at a higher risk of CKD, compared to only 66.406% of non-attendees. Regarding the organs affected during CKD, a higher number of attendees (86.058%, 58.173%, and 54.327%) knew that the heart, skin, and brain were affected during CKD, while only (70.573%, 39.063%, and 35.677%) knew the same (p < 0.001), respectively. Regarding the leading cause of death, 44.712% of attendees reported cardiovascular diseases, whereas only 27.865% of non-attendees reported the same (p < 0.001). Surprisingly, more seminar attendees (45.192%) thought that excess stress was a risk factor for CKD than non-attendees (35.156%) (p = 0.034). Compared to non-attendees, a higher proportion of attendees (62.500%) were aware that vomiting is a common symptom of CKD, compared to only 48.177% of non-attendees (p = 0.001). A higher proportion of attendees (69.712%) were aware that loss of appetite is a symptom of CKD compared to only 57.552% of non-attendees (p = 0.002). A higher percentage of seminar attendees were more aware of the treatment options. Of the seminar attendees, 75.962%, 87.500%, and 86.538% knew that drugs, dialysis, and transplantation could be used to treat CKD, respectively, compared with 62.760%, 82.031%, and 79.948% of non-attendees, with p values of 0.003, 0.200, and 0.001, respectively. Knowledge model

Regarding the regression model, females were more likely to have higher knowledge levels (aOR: 1.65, p = 0.031). Participants older than 50 years had significantly higher odds of having higher knowledge of CKD (aOR:5.56, p = 0.001) than those in the other age groups. Employment status, family income, and renal function tests did not show statistical significance regarding the knowledge level about CKD. Participants who did not attend any seminars or workshops had lower odds of having a high level of knowledge (OR:0.25, p < 0.001) (Table 3).

Discussion

This study investigated the knowledge and awareness of CKD among the general population in the West Bank, Palestine, focusing on the factors influencing CKD awareness. While similar assessment studies have been conducted in neighboring countries such as Lebanon [16], Jordan [18], and Saudi Arabia [19], this is the first CKD awareness study in the West Bank, Palestine.

Our sample consisted of 592 respondents, predominantly female (76.014%), aged 29 years or younger (79.223%), and mostly students (45.608%). This could be attributed to the largely young demographic profile of Palestine [20] and their wider access to online platforms on which the questionnaires were disseminated. Moreover, females’ willingness to respond to research surveys was reported to be higher than that of males’, hence their higher representation [21]. This young distribution might further explain why 58.446% of participants were single. However, the limited digital literacy among older adults may have affected their access to online-administered surveys, leading to the underrepresentation of their awareness gaps, especially since a considerable fraction of older adults are at high risk for CKD [22].

CKD-related seminar attendance was a key determinant of knowledge. We found that seminar attendees had significantly better knowledge than non-attendees in areas related to kidney function, symptoms, risk factors, and preventive measures. With good overall knowledge (70.673%) among the one third of participants who reported attending previous seminars on CKD, while the remaining two thirds of respondents did not report attending such seminars and had limited overall knowledge (38.021%). This difference in awareness between seminar attendees and non-attendees in our study seems to align with a Malaysian study [23], which found that any previous familiarity with CKD among respondents translated into better knowledge scores. This is a good indicator of the effectiveness of educational interventions in raising CKD awareness.

The most frequently reported information source by participants were study materials (28.390%), followed by social media (25.636%). This is expected from the young student majority in our sample, who generally refer to academic and digital platforms for information.

Our study found that older age and being single were associated with higher knowledge levels than other age groups and being divorced or widowed. Similar associations between older age and better knowledge were found in studies from Lebanon [16] and Saudi Arabia [19], which were believed to stem from older people’s tendency to be more concerned about their health [16]. Studies from Singapore [24] and Malaysia [23] found the opposite, as younger participants displayed better awareness. An Australian study [25] found that single respondents were more likely to have lower knowledge, whereas a Saudi study [19] reported better knowledge among married participants, possibly because living with others might expose people to healthier attitudes [25]. These contrasting findings emphasize the need to tailor local health promotion strategies for CKD to different sociocultural contexts across age groups.

However, we found that employment status, income, and having previously done renal function tests were not significantly associated with CKD knowledge. Other similar studies have reported significant results. In Jordan [18], low income and educational status translated to low knowledge, and vice versa in Saudi Arabia [19], Singapore [24], Malaysia [23], and India [26]. In Lebanon [16], better knowledge scores were found among participants who had previously undergone kidney assessment tests, probably because of their active attitude toward healthier living. The lack of significant associations in our study may reflect sample homogeneity or reporting bias due to self-administered surveys and should be investigated further in future studies.

A difference between seminar attendees and non-attendees was observed in their knowledge of kidney function. The most identified kidney function by attendees was ‘maintaining blood pressure’ (81.250%), which was poorly identified as a kidney function by participants in Saudi Arabia [19] and Australia [25]. Additionally, Saudi [19] and Australian [25] participants had poor knowledge of the kidneys’ function in bone health and metabolism. Not surprisingly, urine production was the most identified function by non-attendees in our study (64.844%). However, the least identified functions by both groups were ‘breaking down’ fat (31.250% and 22.396%) and proteins (31.250% and 17.708%), which is much lower than the Indian [27] public’s knowledge of this function. This highlights a lack of understanding of the vital role of the kidneys in metabolic waste elimination, a critical function that threatens systemic toxicity if disrupted. Addressing this knowledge gap is essential for effective application of CKD preventive measures among high-risk populations.

The majority of both attendees and non-attendees knew that blood tests could assess kidney health, but only half of the attendees and approximately one-third of the non-attendees knew that blood pressure (BP) measurements could be used for the same purpose. Similar knowledge levels were found in Saudi Arabia [19], whereas lower levels (20–35%) were reported in Australia [25]. Moreover, the majority of participants of both groups, especially non-attendees, were unaware of the stages of CKD. These findings suggest a pressing need for public education on the early detection and monitoring of CKD, particularly the role of BP as a simple and accessible CKD assessment tool.

Overall, seminar attendees were more knowledgeable about CKD risk factors and symptoms than non-attendees.

Family history was the most frequently listed risk factor for both attendees and non-attendees (88.942% and 71.094%, respectively). This was followed by hypertension (81.731% and 59.635%), which was higher than that in Jordan [18], Malaysia [23], Saudi Arabia [19], Singapore [24], Hong Kong [28], Nigeria [29], and India [26].

Diabetes was identified as a risk factor by a slightly lower percentage of seminar attendees and non-attendees (79.808% and 61.198%, respectively). However, the knowledge rate of this risk factor was still higher than that reported in most other studies [19,23,24,26]. Similar to Jordan [18], 77.885% of attendees and 66.406% of non-attendees correctly reported being older than 50 years as another risk factor. Heart diseases were the least identified risk factor by both groups (73.077% and 48.177%), and almost half of the seminar attendees and one-third of the non-attendees correctly identified cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) as the leading cause of death in patients with CKD. This highlights the importance of targeted education on the connection between CVDs and CKD progression, which remains under-recognized.

Both groups were well aware of the fluid overload symptoms of CKD, which was the most identified symptom (91.346% and 87.500%, respectively). Vomiting, a common symptom of CKD, was the least identified by respondents, followed by loss of appetite. This indicates the presence of a knowledge gap among the West Bank population regarding the earlier and less specific symptoms of CKD. In Jordan [18], the public was far less knowledgeable about CKD symptoms, whereas in Iran [30], most participants listed pain and difficult urination as early symptoms of CKD.

We found that respondents in both groups had approximately similar good levels of knowledge about CKD preventive lifestyle modifications. The results were close in Jordan [18]. In our study, maintaining blood sugar level was the most correctly reported (89.904% and 86.458%). This was followed by maintaining blood pressure (88.942%, and 78.646%), which was higher than that in Hong Kong [28], and lastly, maintaining body weight (86.538% and 76.042%). However, good knowledge alone does not guarantee good adherence to healthy preventive attitudes [20]. Therefore, educational programs must not only convey facts but also build self-efficacy and social support, as these elements have been shown to enhance long-term adherence.

Our study found overall good knowledge of CKD treatment options. However, both groups were far more aware of dialysis as an option than transplantation and medications, which was the least identified option (75.962% for attendees, 62.760% of non-attendees) but was still higher than that in Saudi Arabia [19] and Australia [25], where only about half of the respondents knew the same. This knowledge gap suggests the need to promote awareness of early CKD management interventions that could potentially delay or spare patients from costly renal replacement therapies.

This is the first study in the West Bank to assess diverse CKD knowledge domains, ranging from kidney function and CKD stages to prevention and treatment. This provides a picture of public understanding and its implications for educational interventions. However, this study has a few limitations. First, females (76.014%), young age groups of 18–29 years (79.223%), single social status (58.446%), and university students (84.966%) were overrepresented in our sample. While this largely aligns with the national demographic profile of the West Bank, it could introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings to the general population. Additionally, online-self administration of questionnaires could result in response bias due to responders overestimating or underestimating their knowledge. We relied on simple language when building the surveys to mitigate this bias. Furthermore, older populations have less experience and access to online media, which might have contributed to the selection bias. However, we tried to minimize this by disseminating the questionnaires across different regions of the West Bank, as representative of the general population. As a cross-sectional design, the study can only identify associations, not causal relationships, between knowledge levels and demographic factors [31].

This study identified key CKD knowledge gaps among the general population in the West Bank, particularly among non-attendees of awareness seminars, who constituted the majority of respondents. Addressing these gaps requires a multifaceted approach from the public health perspective. Although seminars have proven effective for those who attended, their limited reach poses a challenge. A comprehensive public health model must also leverage mass media campaigns, integration into school and university curricula, community health workers, policy-driven screening programs, and utilize traditional media (e.g. TV, radio) to reach older and rural populations with limited internet access. Each of these strategies can help reach harder-to-engage subgroups (e.g. older adults and lower-literacy populations) who may not attend seminars. Since social media was among the top most-listed sources, coordinated, evidence-based digital campaigns on CKD prevention can maximize reach and impact while considering the risks of relying solely on digital tools in excluding older adults and those without internet access [32]. Given the unavailability or unaffordability of renal replacement therapy for ESRD patients in developing countries [33], public health policies should prioritize CKD screening and prevention programs to facilitate early diagnosis and management, which has been documented to reverse the disease before it progresses to more advanced stages [26]. The relatively limited knowledge about the use of medications for CKD management should prompt tailored awareness campaigns about this available option compared with dialysis and renal transplantation. Future awareness studies should consider the influence of socioeconomic disparities, such as digital literacy and age-related barriers, to ensure better representation of high-risk populations.

Conclusion

This study on CKD awareness in the West Bank reveals critical knowledge gaps, especially among non-attendees of educational seminars (64.865% of participants). Seminar attendance strongly predicted higher knowledge; attendees were four times more likely to achieve adequate awareness than non-attendees (aOR = 4.00), highlighting the effectiveness of structured education. However, significant deficits persisted in the recognition of CKD stages, metabolic kidney functions, and non-dialysis treatments. Older adults (>50 years) and women demonstrated better awareness, while the underrepresentation of high-risk groups (e.g. rural and elderly) limits generalizability. To mitigate CKD’s growing burden of CKD, policymakers should expand accessible seminars, leverage social media for youth engagement, and integrate CKD screening into primary care. Future studies should address socioeconomic barriers to healthcare access, employ mixed-methods designs to explore cultural perceptions, and evaluate the impact of community-led interventions in high-risk areas.
