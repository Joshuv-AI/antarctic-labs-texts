Polyelectrolyte-coated nanoporous carbon nanoparticles as pH-sensitive nanocontainers for controlled release of corrosion inhibitors
Authors: Mohammad Reza Roshan, Ali Akbar Kazemi Asl, Mansour Rahsepar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Nanoporous carbon spheres with varying pore size distributions were created using a modified Stöber technique. The study examined how altering the carbonization heating rate affected the pore size and microstructure of the synthesized particles. The synthesized nanoporous carbons were considered as smart carriers for loading 2-mercaptobenzothiazole (MBT), and were encapsulated by coating with two different pairs of polyelectrolyte layers, PEI/PeAA and CS/PeAA. The characteristics of the synthesized and encapsulated samples were investigated by nitrogen adsorption, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy. The total loading of MBT on nanoporous carbons with different pore size distributions was investigated via thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). The MBT release of the encapsulated samples under different pH conditions was assessed via UV‒Vis spectroscopy. Characterization of the pore size indicated that as the carbonization heating rate was increased from 5 to 10 and then to 15 °C/min, the pore diameter grew from 2.25 nm to 3.86 nm and 5.14 nm, respectively. The maximum loading capacity was achieved by the mean pore size distribution of 3.86 nm. Both of the applied polyelectrolyte shells exhibit favorable performance and provide controlled release under acidic and basic conditions. Nanoporous carbon with a suitable pore size distribution is a promising candidate for use as a smart carrier of corrosion inhibitors. Additionally, chitosan could be considered as an effective alternative to existing polyelectrolytes for creating smart anticorrosion particles.

Introduction

Coatings are widely used to prevent corrosion by preventing corrosion substances from reaching the substrate surface. However, any damage or local defects in the coating cause severe corrosion. Traditionally, corrosion inhibitors are incorporated into coatings by direct mixing. The main disadvantage of directly adding corrosion inhibitors to coatings lies in the constant leaching of the corrosion inhibitor, which in turn considerably reduces the protection lifetime. Consequently, great interest has been given to preventing this drawback, extending the protection lifetime and reducing side effects on the environment through the encapsulation of corrosion inhibitors, which is based on loading an inhibitor into a container and incorporating these capsules into the coating. The container should be small enough to maintain the coating integrity. Cellulose microfibers1, inorganic nanocontainers2,3, halloysites4–6, and mesoporous particles7–11 have been used as containers for the encapsulation of inhibitors. The main role of these containers is to load a corrosion inhibitor into them, and the release is performed by diffusion. More recently, encapsulation systems that are sensitive to different internal or external factors (pH changes, concentration, redox charges, etc.) have been widely studied. It is important that the container be capable of storing enough inhibitors and can release them when needed. Therefore, spontaneous leaching of the loaded inhibitor is prevented, and a higher efficiency of corrosion inhibition is attained. When the coating is damaged, the pH will change in the corrosion location. One possible way to create sensitivity in the container is to use pH-responsive shells deposited by layer-by-layer (LbL) assembly12, in which cationic and anionic polyelectrolyte layers are deposited on the surface of the container particles. When the corrosion process occurs, the pH changes at the anodic and cathodic sites, leading to dissociation of the polyelectrolyte coating. Thus, the loaded corrosion inhibitor will be released immediately and locally to hinder corrosion.

Porous particles have received particular interest in recent years due to owing mechanical stability, high loading capacity, and prolonged release duration. Skorb et al.13 produced inhibitor-loaded mesoporous silica coated with oppositely charged polyelectrolyte layers using the LbL assembly method. The authors illustrated that the release of the loaded inhibitor occurs due to a pH trigger caused by the corrosion process. Since then, several researchers have used mesoporous silica particles as smart carriers for corrosion inhibitors7–11,14–16. Borisova et al.17 and Hollamby et al.18 loaded mesoporous silica particles with BTA and studied the corrosion resistance of coatings containing the loaded particles. However, the produced particles were not encapsulated, and inhibitor release was not controlled. Falcon et al.19 produced a mesoporous silica container loaded with dodecylamine and evaluated the release of the inhibitor at different pH values. However, in the absence of encapsulation, controlled release could not be achieved. By installing nanovalves sensitive to pH17,20–22, light23, or mechanical damage24, smart particles with controllable release of corrosion inhibitors can be generated. Carbon particles with nano-sized pore sizes have been investigated recently as strong absorbents for bulky dyes25, surfactants26, and drugs27–31 due to their high mechanical, thermal, and chemical stability, biocompatibility, large surface area, and adjustable pore size. Therefore, it is reasonable to consider nanoporous carbon as a promising adsorbent for corrosion healing agents.

The loading and release performance of nanoporous carbon are highly dependent on the pore size of the particles. Although small pores have larger surface areas, however, there are several challenges including the collapse of porous microstructures at elevated carbonization or graphitization temperatures32, limited molecular mobility due to constrained space arising from small pore dimensions, and limited use in the absorption of large molecules. On the other hand, a very large pore size is not favorable because of the low surface area and pore volume, which result in a decreased amount of adsorbed material, in addition to short-lived corrosion protection due to the accelerated release of the inhibitor attributed to increased mass diffusion rates. Nanoporous carbons were synthesized by two methods. Hard templating techniques rely on porous or colloidal materials, such as silica and metal oxides, to create pores. Consequently, adjusting the pore size is possible by varying the template’s diameter or pore dimensions33,34. For instance, Choma et al.35 demonstrated nanoporous carbon creation with pore sizes of 12.4 and 24.2 nm by using colloidal silica nanoparticles measuring 12 and 24 nm in diameter, respectively. Though the hard template method allows for precise pore size adjustments, it requires the synthesis of various templates. Conversely, soft templating methods utilize surfactants or block copolymers, where manipulating the size or quantity of these agents can influence pore dimensions. Although it is possible to modify pore sizes between 5 and 37 nm by changing the agent concentration36–38, this method can be challenging to manage39. Consequently, there is a pressing need for an effective and straightforward technique to produce nanoporous carbon materials with adjustable pore sizes. The product could be efficiently used as corrosion inhibitor container in smart coatings.

In this study, Phenol–formaldehyde resin spheres were synthesized through a hydrothermal reaction of phenol and formaldehyde under modified Stöber conditions, using ammonia as a catalyst and the triblock copolymer Pluronic F127 (PEO-PPO-PEO) as a soft template. The pore sizes were varied by adjusting the carbonization heating rate. The resultant nanoporous carbon exhibited a spherical shape with tunable pore sizes and a partially graphitic structure. Mercaptobenzothiazole, a corrosion inhibitor, was loaded into the nanoporous carbon powders by applying a vacuum. The inhibitor-loaded nanoporous carbons were surface-modified by LbL-coated polyelectrolytes to create pH-sensitive containers. The use of polyethyleneimine (PEI) as a cationic polymer for smart inhibitor release has been reported1,40,41. PEI, due to the presence of positively charged nitrogen, which promotes adsorption on negatively charged metals, has also been considered an inhibitor1,42. Chitosan (CS) is another polycation that has been used as an inhibitor43. In this work, two types of pH-sensitive particles were produced using CS/PeAA and PEI/PeAA shells. The characteristics of the synthesized particles as well as the loaded and coated particles were investigated by using a series of instruments. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was utilized to assess the amount of the loaded inhibitor, and the pH-triggered release of MBT was evaluated via UV‒Vis spectrometry at different pH values. This study develops favorable carbon-based smart containers for effective use in corrosion protection coatings.

Materials and methods

Materials

The Pluronic F127 triblock copolymer (Mw = 12,600, EO106PO70EO106), phenol, 2-mercaptobenzothiazole (MBT), chitosan (Mw = 190–310 kDa), polyethyleneimine (Mw = 25,000), polyethylene-co-acrylic acid and ammonia solution (25 wt%) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Formaldehyde (37 wt%), hydrochloric acid (HCl), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), sodium chloride (NaCl), acetone, and tetrahydrofuran (THF) were purchased from Kimia Mavad, and ethanol was purchased from Zakaria Jahrom. All chemicals were used as received without further purification, and deionized water was employed throughout the experiments.

Synthesis of nanoporous carbon with different pore sizes

Phenolic resin, recognized for its three-dimensional network structure, was chosen as a carbon precursor due to its affordability, high thermal stability, and suitability for conversion into carbon materials. The modified Stöber method was utilized to synthesize phenolic resin spheres44,45. In a typical synthesis procedure, 3.36 g of F127 was dissolved in 30 ml of ethanol and 30 ml of deionized water and stirred for 30 min at 40 °C until a clear solution formed. Subsequently, 2.1 g of phenol, 12 ml of formaldehyde, and 8 ml of ammonia solution were added dropwise and mixed for an additional 30 min. The resulting solution was placed in a sealed stainless steel autoclave lined with Teflon and heated at 180 °C for 6 h to complete the hydrothermal reaction. The final yellow phenol–formaldehyde (PF) product was retrieved through centrifugation and rinsed with deionized water three times before being dried at 80 °C for 12 h. The PF powder underwent carbonization at 700 °C under a high purity nitrogen atmosphere with three different heating rates (5, 10, and 15 °C/min), and the samples were designated as MC5, MC10, and MC15, respectively.

Loading of MBT

MBT was loaded on nanoporous carbon materials under reduced pressure. To this end, 0.5 mg of MBT was dissolved in 25 ml of acetone, and 0.5 g of the synthesized MC powder was added to the solution. The mixture was exposed to vacuum for 10 min followed by 10 min of sonication. The mixture was subsequently placed under reduced pressure for 10 min. Finally, the loaded powders were collected by centrifugation, washed with deionized water to remove excess MBT, centrifuged again and dried in an oven at 60 °C for 3 h. The above process was repeated to ensure the complete loading of the samples. The samples are named LMC5, LMC10, and LMC15 for the loaded synthesized carbons carbonized at rates of 5, 10, and 15 °C/min, respectively.

Coating by polyelectrolyte

To prepare pH-sensitive containers, two pairs of polyelectrolyte shells were coated on the loaded samples. 1- PEI solution was prepared by adding 50 mg of PEI to 25 ml of 0.15 M NaCl solution. PeAA was dissolved in THF (2 mg/ml), and chitosan was dissolved in a solution of 0.15 M NaCl at pH = 5 and a concentration of 2 mg/ml. The pH was reduced by adding an adequate amount of HCl to the solution. A total of 0.5 g of each loaded MC was immersed in the prepared PEI solution for 15 min. Then, the solution was centrifuged and washed with deionized water. The obtained particles were immersed in PeAA solution for 15 min and then centrifuged, washed, and dried at 60 °C for 3 h to provide a PEI/PeAA coating. The samples were named LMC5-PEI/PeAA, LMC10-PEI/PeAA, and LMC15-PEI/PeAA. 2- To apply the CS/PeAA coating, the loaded MCs were added to the chitosan solution, centrifuged and washed after 30 min of immersion. The obtained samples were poured into the PeAA solution, centrifuged and washed after 15 min. Subsequently, the samples were dried and named LMC5-CS/PeAA, LMC10-CS/PeAA, and LMC15-CS/PeAA.

Characterization

To assess the adsorption properties of the synthesized carbon samples, nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms were collected using a Belsorp Mini II at liquid nitrogen temperatures. The specific surface area (SBET) was determined using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method. Mean pore sizes were calculated from the adsorption branches of the isotherms using the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) method, while total pore volumes were evaluated based on the N2 adsorption volume at a relative pressure of p/p0 = 0.98. The samples’ morphology and structure were analyzed using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) images taken with a TESCAN Mira 3 instrument, which was also equipped for chemical analysis using energy dispersive spectroscopy. Before SEM analysis, the samples were sonicated in acetone for 10 min. Fourier transform (FT-IR) spectroscopy was performed with a Bruker spectrometer.

Loading and release behavior

The zeta potential of the MCs was determined using zeta potential equipment (Horiba. SZ-100). The presented potentials are obtained by averaging three runs of the instrument, and each run contains at least 10 measurements. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed from room temperature to 700 °C with a heating rate of 5 °C/min under a N2 atmosphere (BAHR STA 53 instrument). To investigate the pH response, 0.5 mg of each loaded/encapsulated MC powder was immersed in 25 ml of 0.1 M NaCl solution at pH 2, 7, and 12, which were adjusted with HCl or NaOH. The suspensions were stirred continuously for certain durations and then filtered through 0.1 µm pore size membranes. The amount of MBT released from the LMCs was determined by a LAMBDA 365 UV‒Vis spectrophotometer (Perkin Elmer) as a function of time for each pH.

Results and discussion

Nanoporous carbon synthesis

Nanoporous carbon spheres with diverse pore sizes were successfully synthesized via the modified Stöber procedure. A schematic representation of the synthesis routes is shown in Fig. 1.Fig. 1Schematic illustration of the synthesis steps for the nanoporous carbon samples.

Schematic illustration of the synthesis steps for the nanoporous carbon samples.

PF resin served as the carbon precursor, while Pluronic F127 acted as a soft template, with ammonia serving as a catalyst and ethanol and deionized water as solvents. Upon dissolving F127 monomers in an alcohol-water solution, F127 micelles form and encourage hydrogen bonding between the PEO chains of F127 and the hydroxyl groups (-OH) present in the PF chains45, resulting in composite emulsion droplets. Influenced by the catalytic role of ammonia44, PF resin polymer spheres develop through the crosslinking of emulsion droplets at the high temperature of 180 °C. The reaction time is brief due to ammonia’s catalytic effects and the elevated hydrothermal temperature. Ultimately, nanoporous carbons are produced via carbonization at heating rates of 5, 10, and 15 °C/min. Mesopores arise from the aggregation of small spheres and result from the removal of the Pluronic F127 template during carbonization. Meanwhile, micropores develop on the surfaces of the aggregating spheres owing to gas released during the decomposition of the PF resin.

The nitrogen adsorption/desorption isotherms and their corresponding BJH (Barrett-Joyner-Halenda) pore size distribution for the synthesized carbon powders are depicted in Fig. 2. The N₂ adsorption–desorption isotherms of all MC samples exhibit a pseudo-type-I profile with features of type IV isotherms, including an H1-type hysteresis loop over a wide range of relative pressures (P/P₀ = 0.3–1.0), indicating the coexistence of micropores and mesopores. A steep N₂ uptake at low relative pressures (P/P₀ < 0.1) is attributed to micropore filling. The broad and poorly defined capillary condensation step in the range of P/P₀ = 0.30–0.90 suggests the presence of mesopores with a broad size distribution. Additionally, the pronounced hysteresis at higher pressures (P/P₀ = 0.90–1.00) may result from interparticle voids or macropores formed by the agglomeration of carbon spheres. Therefore, the MC5 sample, carbonized at a lower heating rate, demonstrated more micropores than the MC10 sample, with MC10 showing a greater micropore quantity compared to MC15. The average pore size of the MC5 sample was 2.25 nm, which expanded to 3.86 nm and 5.14 nm with an increase in heating rate from 5 to 10 and 15 °C/min, respectively. During the high-temperature carbonization process, polymer decomposition occurs, and the created micropores and mesopores account for the vacant spaces formed due to the escaping gases. The F127 template is removed between 300–400 °C during pyrolysis46,47, directly producing mesoporous structures47–49. Furthermore, the evolving gases such as CO, CO2, and H2O contribute to the generation of micropores50. Additional mesoporosity develops between the agglomerated nanospheres35. Thermogravimetric analysis of phenolic-formaldehyde spheres demonstrated heating-rate-dependent carbonization kinetics, with onset temperatures progressively increasing from 204 °C (5 °C/min) to 254 °C (15 °C/min) while the final process temperature remains unchanged (Figure S1 in the Supplementary Information (SI)). This 51% compression of the carbonization window at faster heating rates accelerated volatile release via narrowed decomposition timeframe, shifting pore architecture from microporous to mesoporous regimes, as confirmed by BJH pore size distribution analysis (Fig. 2b). Hence, with increasing carbonization heating rates, the ratio of micropores diminishes while mesopores become preferential, resulting in larger mean pore diameters and reduced specific surface area due to decreased micropore numbers.

Loading and release behavior

TGA was performed under a N2 atmosphere to assess the amount of corrosion inhibitor loaded into the MCs. The TGA curves (Fig. 6) demonstrated that MBT was degraded in the range from 200 to 550° C, and the amount of the inhibitor in the carriers was 35% for the MBT-loaded MC5, 38% for the LMC10, and 33% for the LMC15, calculated according to the weight loss.Fig. 6TGA curves of the as-produced MC10 and MBT-loaded nanoporous carbons synthesized with carbonization rates of 5 (LMC5), 10 (LMC10) and 15 °C/min (LMC15).

TGA curves of the as-produced MC10 and MBT-loaded nanoporous carbons synthesized with carbonization rates of 5 (LMC5), 10 (LMC10) and 15 °C/min (LMC15).

The greater amount of loaded MBT in the MC10 sample than in the MC5 sample could be attributed to its larger pore size. Larger pores provide suitable pathways for the inhibitor to diffuse inside the particles. Increasing the carbonization heating rate resulted in an increase in the pore size. Therefore, the inhibitor can diffuse and load into the particles more easily and effectively. However, by increasing the heating rate from 10 to 15 °C/min, the loading capacity decreased, although the pore diameter increased. This can be explained by the lower pore volume of MC15. Therefore, the MC10 sample had the highest loading capacity among the synthesized samples.

First, the polyelectrolyte shell should close tightly under neutral conditions to prevent unwanted leakage of the inhibitor. Second, the shell must be opened, and the corrosion inhibitor must be released rapidly under acidic or alkaline conditions. It is visible from the release curves that both of the polyelectrolyte shells properly satisfy the first requirement. At pH = 7, the neutral shrunken polyelectrolyte shell is similar to a collapsed monolayer and does not permit the fast penetration of substances. The existence of free space between the polymer chains allows limited leakage of the inhibitor. Therefore, less than 4% of the MBT leaked after long immersion times. In addition, under acidic or alkaline conditions, both polyelectrolyte shells exhibit satisfactory performance. After 3 h of dispersing the particles at pH = 12, 71.33, 80.31, and 83.20% of the MBT was released from the samples coated with the CS/PeAA polyelectrolyte shell, and 68.21, 75.12 and 80.13% were released from the samples coated with the PEI/PeAA polyelectrolyte shell, with mean pore sizes of 2.25, 3.86 and 5.14 nm, respectively. Under acidic conditions (pH = 2), 59.79, 62.11, and 65.52 were released from the samples encapsulated by CS/PeAA layers, and 58.14, 60.17, and 63.02 were released from the samples coated with PEI/PeAA layers, with mean pore sizes of 2.25, 3.86 and 5.14 nm, respectively. Under both acidic and alkaline conditions, the amount of MBT released in solutions increased, indicating that the condition of the polyelectrolyte shell changed. At high pH values, the PeAA polyanion layer deprotonates by adsorbing OH- ions in the solution. Thereafter, the polyelectrolyte shell swells, and the inhibitor quickly releases. Similar to the PeAA in an alkaline solution, PEI or CS polycations protonate by adsorbing H+ ions in an acidic solution. Therefore, at low pH values, the protonated polyelectrolyte shell swells, and the inhibitor diffuses out. Figure 9 shows that the amount and speed of MBT release were greater in alkaline solution than in acidic solution. Due to the greater concentration of Na+ ions in the sodium hydroxide alkaline solution, MBT release is accelerated by the formation of MBT-Na. protonated or deprotonated polyelectrolytes with similarly charged chains repelling each other. On the other hand, the particles coated with CS/PeAA exhibited slightly more accelerated release than the PEI/PeAA-encapsulated particles. The rate of dissociation of polyelectrolytes depends on environmental factors (temperature, pH, etc.) and the strength of the polyelectrolyte layer. It seems that the CS/PeAA shell is to some extent more flexible in stretching than the PEI/PeAA shell is. The reason might be the lower number of amine sites on the CS chains compared with the number on the PEI chains, resulting in weaker bonds between the CS and MC particles than between the PEI and MC particles. Therefore, the CS/PeAA shell dissociates more easily than does the PEI/PeAA shell, and MBT release is more accelerated. Furthermore, according to the release curves (Fig. 9), the MBT release rate from the MC15 samples was greater than that from the MC10 samples, and the release rate from the MC10 samples was greater than that from the MC5 samples in both acidic and alkaline solutions and for both the CS/PeAA and PEI/PeAA encapsulants. The cause lies in the greater mean pore size of the MC15 particles compared to that of the MC5 particles. A greater mean pore size results in quick leaching of MBT, and the release accelerates. Therefore, the encapsulated MC15 particles with a mean pore size of 5.14 nm exhibited accelerated release among the synthesized nanoporous carbon spheres. Notably, the amount of MBT loaded into the MC15 particles was less than that loaded into the MC5 and MC10 particles, and the amount of MBT loaded on the MC5 particles was less than that loaded on the MC10 particles. Therefore, the net amount of MBT released into the solution was greater for the MC10 sample. Manipulating the carbonization heating rate is a facile method for producing nanoporous carbon spheres with the desired mean pore size. The particles with a mean pore size of approximately 5 nm exhibited the best loading and release performance. It is well known that corrosion generally leads to local alkalization, due to cathodic reactions69,70. Sometimes, the corrosive environment is acidic by nature. Hence, the synthesized LMC-CS/PeAA and LMC-PEI/PeAA particles are expected to be sensitive to local alkalization or acidic circumferences and the corrosion inhibitors release rapidly to prevent corrosion from spreading. Therefore, the synthesized pH-sensitive particles have potential for use in smart anticorrosion coatings.

Conclusion

In summary, nanoporous carbons with different mean pore sizes were successfully synthesized via a modification of the Stöber method and used as smart containers for loading an inhibitor (MBT). The pore size was altered by manipulating the heating rate of the carbonization process. By simply modifying the carbonization heating rate, pore sizes can be effectively tuned from 2.25 to 5.14 nm. The nanoporous carbon spheres achieved pore sizes of 2.25, 3.86, and 5.14 nm at carbonization heating rates of 5, 10, and 15 °C/min, respectively. All samples maintain a spherical shape with an accessible pore structure, and the chemical compositions of the synthesized samples remain consistent. MBT was loaded properly into the synthesized samples. pH sensitivity was induced by the deposition of CS/PeAA or PEI/PeAA shells on the loaded particles. TGA analysis revealed the effective loading of the particles. The loading capacity of the sample with a mean pore size of 3.86 was the highest among the synthesized carbon particles. UV‒Vis analysis revealed the time- and pH-dependent release of the loaded inhibitor. At pH = 7, the samples had negligible release amounts. Acidic and alkaline environments promote rapid inhibitor release due to protonation or deprotonation of the shells, resulting in swelling and permeability. Additionally, the CS/PeAA shell, which was used for the first time for pH-controlled inhibitor release, exhibited rapid and appropriate pH-dependent release. The results indicate that the proposed containers are promising candidates for the formulation of smart anticorrosion coatings.
