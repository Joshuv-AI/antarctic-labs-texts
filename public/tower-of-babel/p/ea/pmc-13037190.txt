Clinical and molecular characterization of 18p deletion syndrome and a novel case with reproductive disorder
Authors: Xia Cheng, Liang Xu, Jiatao Wu, Li Zhang, Xueting Wei, Shengping Min, Yaping Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract18p deletion syndrome (OMIM #146390) displays a variable clinical spectrum due to differences in deletion size, position, and penetrance. While several classical phenotypes have been linked to specific genomic regions, many genotype-phenotype correlations remain elusive. Here we present a 30-year-old female patient with the classical features of 18p deletion syndrome, along with reproductive endocrine abnormalities, including elevated prolactin levels and diminished ovarian reserve. Chromosomal microarray revealed a 13.85 Mb deletion at 18p (arr[GRCh37]18p11.32p11.21(136,228_13,986,303)x1). To better delineate the clinical and molecular cytogenetic findings associated with monosomy 18p and explore genotype–phenotype correlations, we also reviewed 41 reported cases (including the present case) with confirmed genomic deletions indexed in the PubMed database between 2010 and 2025. The results showed that terminal deletions were most common (66%), with interstitial deletions accounting for 32%. Among cases with breakpoint data, 71% had the proximal breakpoint at 18p11.21, and 63% of deletions exceeded 10-Mb. Reverse phenotyping identified candidate intervals for multiple phenotypes: a 1.2-Mb region (chr18:1,852,498_3,039,186, hg19) was linked to white-matter abnormalities and ptosis, and a 5.9-Mb region (chr18:5,416,774_11,286,578, hg19) was associated with white-matter abnormalities, ptosis, and autoimmune susceptibility. PIEZO2 and SMCHD1, located within the 5.9 Mb and 1.2 Mb regions respectively, are potential candidate genes for ptosis. PTPRM in 5.9 Mb region is a potential candidate gene for causing autoimmune disorders. The genitourinary abnormalities presented here constitute a useful addition to the phenotypic spectrum of 18p deletion syndrome. However, their definitive inclusion in the syndrome’s clinical profile requires confirmation by future studies. Introduction

18p deletion syndrome (OMIM #146390), also known as monosomy 18p, is a rare chromosomal disorder with an estimated prevalence of 1 in 50,000 live births [1]. First described by the French geneticist Jean de Grouchy in 1963, the condition has been well-documented in the literature, with more than 300 patients reported to date [2–7]. Nevertheless, clinical recognition remains challenging due to the subtlety of characteristic features at birth, their frequent emergence later in life, and the considerable phenotypic heterogeneity among affected individuals. Although the syndrome has been well characterized in terms of both phenotypic spectrum and genotypic profile, abnormalities of the reproductive system have been less thoroughly discussed in published studies [1, 2].

Here, we described a female patient with 18p deletion syndrome who exhibited classic features such as short stature, a short neck, and ptosis, while also presenting with notable reproductive problems. This case enhances our understanding of the clinical spectrum and genotype-phenotype correlations associated with this disorder. In addition, to better delineate the clinical and molecular cytogenetic findings associated with this condition, we reviewed 41 cases reported since 2010 (including the present case), all confirmed by molecular cytogenetic testing, and identified several meaningful observations.

Materials and methods

Case presentation

The patient was a 30-year-old woman referred for cytogenetic analysis following two first-trimester miscarriages. She measured 151 cm (< 5th centile) in height and weighed 60 kg (> 30th centile). Her medical history included patent ductus arteriosus, developmental delay, and mild intellectual disability noted in early childhood, with her parents reporting considerable learning difficulties during her youth. On physical examination, she presented with a broad and expressionless face, ptosis, strabismus, telecanthus, a broad nasal root, large and relatively low-set ears, downturned corners of the mouth, a wide mouth, and a short neck. No other dysmorphic features, such as syndactyly, cleft palate, hip dislocation, or scoliosis, were observed. Although clinical observation and communication revealed no obvious motor or language impairments, a formal neurodevelopmental assessment was not performed. Neuroimaging of the central nervous system was not performed due to the absence of significant neuropsychiatric symptoms.

Menarche occurred at 16 years of age, with regular menstrual cycles thereafter. Laboratory investigations revealed the following hormone levels: anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH), 0.63 ng/mL (normal range: 0.576–8.13 ng/mL); testosterone, 0.14 ng/mL (normal: 0.029–0.481 ng/mL); prolactin, 44.60 ng/mL (normal: 4.79–23.3 ng/mL); luteinizing hormone (LH), 4.22 mIU/mL; follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), 6.24 mIU/mL; and estradiol, 62.50 pg/mL. Although the AMH level fell within the normal range, it was notably low. Prolactin was elevated to nearly twice the upper limit of normal. Pelvic ultrasonography showed an antral follicle count (AFC) of 3 in the left ovary (diameters: 4–7 mm) and 2 in the right ovary (diameters: 7 mm and 9.5 mm). Uterine fibroids were also identified. Collectively, these findings indicate diminished ovarian reserve.

Karyotyping and Chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA)

Metaphase chromosomes were harvested from cultured peripheral blood lymphocytes following a standard 72-hour incubation. Chromosome preparation and GTG-banding were performed according to established protocols. Karyotype analysis was conducted using an Olympus BX41 microscope, and metaphase images were captured with VideoTesT-Karyo software (Meta systems, Altlussheim, Germany). Genome-wide copy number profiling was performed using the Affymetrix CytoScan® 750 K array platform (Affymetrix, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA). All procedures, including DNA amplification, labeling, and hybridization, adhered to the manufacturer’s instructions. Data analysis was carried out with the Chromosome Analysis Suite (ChAS) software, version 4.3. The pathogenicity of any identified copy number variations (CNVs) was interpreted based on the guidelines from the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Clinical Genome Resource (ClinGen) [8].

Literature review

A comprehensive search of the PubMed database was performed for the period from January 2010 to October 2025, utilizing the keywords ‘18p deletion syndrome’, ‘Monosomy 18p’, and ‘18p- syndrome’. The inclusion criteria required that cases had a confirmed deletion by molecular techniques such as array-CGH, SNP-array, CNV-seq, or whole-genome sequencing to accurately define the deletion boundaries. Only English-language articles were considered. Exclusion criteria encompassed cases verified only by traditional cytogenetics or clinical presentation, prenatal cases resulting in termination (due to unavailable postnatal phenotypes), and cases with unbalanced translocations or additional chromosomal abnormalities. Following this screening process, 41 cases, including the present case, were included in the final review.

Custom genome browser tracks

Analysis data from 36 subjects with 18p deletions (of the 41 cases, five reports did not specify the precise boundaries of the deleted region) were mapped to the UCSC Genome Browser (https://genome.ucsc.edu) via custom track generation. For specific operation methods, refer to the following URL: https://genome.ucsc.edu/goldenPath/help/customTrack.html.

Determination of candidate intervals

CMA and CNV-seq data from all cases were converted into custom tracks for visualization in the UCSC Genome Browser (https://genome.ucsc.edu) to facilitate genomic analysis [Liao et al., submitted]. Candidate intervals associated with each phenotype were delineated following the methodology of previous studies [2, 9, 10]. Briefly, individuals exhibiting a specific phenotype were first identified. Genomic coordinates of chromosome 18p hemizygosity across these individuals were then compared to define the maximal overlapping region. For each phenotype, proximal and distal candidate interval boundaries were established by the respective breakpoints in two distinct individuals.

Results

Chromosome karyotype analysis

The G-banding chromosomal analysis in peripheral blood cells revealed a hemizygotic deletion of 18p. The karyotype of the patient was defined as 46,XX, del(18)(p11.2) (Fig. 1), combined with the typical clinical features suggestive of 18p deletion syndrome. The karyotypes of her parents are normal.

Chromosomal microarray analysis

CMA identified a de novo, approximately 13.85 Mb terminal deletion at 18p11.32-p11.21 (Fig. 2A), corresponding to the ISCN karyotype 46,XX, del(18)(p11.21). The deleted region (arr[GRCh37] 18p11.32p11.21(136,228_13,986,303)x1) encompasses 62 OMIM genes. Analysis using the UCSC Genome Browser (GRCh37/hg19) identified 14 genes within this interval with established roles in human disease: THOC1, TYMS, SMCHD1, LPIN2, TGIF1, LAMA1, NDUFV2, APCDD1, PIEZO2, GNAL, TUBB6, AFG3L2, PSMG2, and MC2R (Fig. 2B). Clinical data from literature

Our review identified 41 eligible cases (Supplementary Table S1). Based on the SNP array results reported in the literature, the case with a ring chromosome 18 was confirmed to have a deletion restricted to the short arm, with no detectable loss from the long arm. Therefore, this case was included in our analysis. Among them, 36 cases with precisely defined deletion boundaries were included in the subsequent genotype-phenotype analysis. Among the reviewed cases, 57% were female. Among the 19 cases with available inheritance information, 79% (15/19) were de novo and 21% (4/19) were inherited (Table 1). Terminal deletions predominated (66%, 27/41), followed by interstitial deletions (32%, 13/41) and one case of ring chromosome 18 (2%). Deletion sizes were categorized as > 10 Mb (63%, 26/41), < 5 Mb (27%, 11/41), or 5–10 Mb (10%, 4/41). The proximal breakpoint was most frequently identified in band 18p11.21 (71%, 29/41), with the remainder mapping to p11.22-p11.31 (Table 2; Fig. 3).

Candidate intervals

A review of clinical features from the 36 analyzed cases identified nine individuals with white matter abnormalities (Fig. 4A). Among these, four carried interstitial deletions. By determining the overlapping regions between these interstitial deletions and the terminal deletions in the remaining five cases, we delineated two non-contiguous candidate intervals on 18p. The first is a 1.2 Mb segment in 18p11.32-p11.31 (chr18:1,852,498-3,039,186, hg19; defined by case 12). The second is a 5.9 Mb segment in 18p11.31-p11.21 (chr18:5,416,774 − 11,286,578, hg19; defined by cases 32, 33, and 34). Potential haploinsufficient genes within the 1.2 Mb region include NDC80, SMCHD1, and EMILIN2. The 5.9 Mb region contains twenty candidate genes (Fig. 4A, D).

Ptosis was observed in fifteen cases. Analysis of the overlapping regions from both interstitial and terminal deletions defined two non-overlapping critical intervals, measuring 1.2 Mb and 5.9 Mb, respectively (Fig. 4B). These regions contain 5 and 20 known potential haploinsufficient genes each (Fig. 4D).

The candidate interval for autoimmune disorders was refined to a 5.9 Mb interval on 18p, based on interstitial deletions identified in three of the eleven affected individuals. This region contains 20 potential haploinsufficient genes implicated in pathogenesis (Fig. 4C, D).

Discussion

Genitourinary abnormalities in 18p deletion syndrome patients

Although most individuals with 18p deletion syndrome undergo typical pubertal development and fertility has been documented (primarily in females) in familial cases, reproductive tract anomalies occur in a subset of patients and have received relatively little attention [2, 11, 12]. In males, reported abnormalities are dominated by cryptorchidism; in females, external genital anomalies are most frequently described. Our patient presented with reproductive issues that expand the phenotypic spectrum of 18p deletion syndrome. The laboratory and ultrasonographic findings—a low-normal AMH level, elevated prolactin, a diminished antral follicle count, and the presence of uterine fibroids—collectively indicate a state of diminished ovarian reserve. The findings described in our case add to the other abnormalities described in patients with 18p deletion syndrome. New reports, especially those that include assessment for these features, will be very important for a better understanding and their potential incorporation into the clinical spectrum of the syndrome. While the specific genetic mechanisms underlying these reproductive abnormalities remain to be elucidated, this case emphasizes the need for reproductive health evaluation in women with 18p deletion syndrome. Although coincidental hyperprolactinemia is possible, the presence of multiple concurrent reproductive abnormalities in this case suggests a potential syndromic link. Assessment of more cases is needed to elucidate this association.

To better define the reproductive spectrum, we reviewed 10 previously published cases and present case with genitourinary anomalies (Table 3). In females, abnormalities clustered into (i) Müllerian/structural findings—primary amenorrhea; vaginal atresia or a small introitus with immature mucosa; hypoplastic cervix and uterus or uterine absence with a blind-ending vaginal pouch; and non-palpable or streak ovaries consistent with gonadal dysgenesis—and (ii) ovarian/endocrine features—polycystic ovary syndrome, estrogen deficiency, elevated prolactin, oligomenorrhea, and diminished ovarian reserve. One patient also had left renal agenesis. In males, reported findings included micropenis, small testes, and unilateral or bilateral cryptorchidism. Collectively, these observations indicate that while reproductive complications are uncommon in 18p deletion syndrome, they span structural and endocrine domains in both sexes; targeted reproductive and endocrine evaluation may therefore be warranted in affected adolescents and adults. While structural or functional pituitary abnormalities, which are occasionally associated with 18p deletion syndrome, could theoretically lead to reproductive issues via hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, the reported cases show that pituitary defects do not consistently co-occur with reproductive phenotypes [13]. This discrepancy suggests that haploinsufficiency of specific genes on 18p may have a more direct, pituitary-independent role in the development of the reproductive system.

Establishing robust genotype-phenotype correlations for reproductive abnormalities remains challenging due to their low prevalence, clinical heterogeneity, and insufficient molecular characterization in historical cases. A study by Hasi-Zogaj et al. proposed a ~ 5.5 Mb region from 18pter to p11.31, encompassing the SMCHD1 gene, as potentially associated with cryptorchidism [2]. The relevance of SMCHD1 is particularly compelling given that its pathogenic variants cause Bosma arhinia microphthalmia syndrome (BAMS), which includes hypogonadotropic hypogonadism and gonadal abnormalities such as cryptorchidism [21, 22]. Notably, heterozygous SMCHD1 mutations can result in milder BAMS manifestations, suggesting that SMCHD1 haploinsufficiency in 18p deletions might contribute to reproductive phenotypes in some patients.

Characteristics of the reviewed cases and molecular findings

Our analysis of 41 cases confirms several established characteristics of 18p deletion syndrome: female predominance (57%), predominantly de novo occurrence (79%), and generally normal life expectancy evidenced by a significant proportion of adult patients (65%), including one 67-year-old male. Our findings regarding female predominance and the high frequency of de novo deletions are consistent with previous large-scale studies [1, 2]. The 21% inheritance rate indicates the possibility of transmission, which, in this series of cases, occurred primarily through maternal lineage, with one exception. Our findings indicated that the 18p interstitial deletion could be transmitted paternally.

While terminal deletions represent the majority of reported cases, interstitial deletions were identified in a substantial proportion (32%) of cases in this series, ranging from microdeletions to larger segments [23]. Furthermore, approximately 71% of breakpoints cluster in the pericentromeric region at p11.21. The application of advanced molecular techniques such as chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) and CNV-seq has enabled more precise characterization of deletion types and breakpoint localization, significantly facilitating genotype-phenotype analyses and identification of potential disease-associated genes and candidate intervals [24, 25].

Refined candidate regions and genes

Following the approach established by Hasi-Zogaj et al. [2], we analyzed 13 cases with interstitial deletions ranging from 1.2 to 5.9 Mb. This enabled delineation of smallest regions of overlap among individuals sharing specific clinical features, thereby refining candidate intervals and prioritizing candidate genes for various phenotypes. In light of the previously established genetic causes of Facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy type 2 (FSHD2) and dystonia [26–31], our discussion focuses solely on the candidate regions and possible disease-associated genes for the following phenotypes.

White matter abnormalities (WMA)

Reported in 10 of 41 patients [32–35], with the lower prevalence potentially reflecting incomplete MRI screening. Our analysis delineated two non-overlapping genomic regions associated with WMA: a 1.2 Mb segment at 18p11.32–p11.31 and a 5.9 Mb segment at 18p11.31–p11.21 [20, 36]. While the 1.2 Mb region partially overlaps with a previously reported ~ 5.4 Mb terminal region [2], the 5.9 Mb interstitial region represents a novel candidate locus. These findings suggest that WMAs in 18p deletion syndrome may result from haploinsufficiency of multiple genes distributed across distinct regions rather than a single critical region.

Ptosis

Mapping identified two non-contiguous regions: a 1.2 Mb segment (1,852,498_3,039,186, hg19) within Hasi-Zogaj et al.‘s defined critical region (pter_2,931,532) and a non-overlapping 5.9 Mb segment [2, 20, 36]. The 1.2 Mb region contains SMCHD1. Recent studies have shown that the epigenetic regulator SMCHD1 can act as a transcriptional co-activator necessary for maintaining the expression of genes required for muscle progenitor expansion, thereby influencing muscle homeostasis [37, 38]. Furthermore, studies have reported that mutations in SMCHD1 cause Bosma syndrome, in which ptosis is a classic feature, suggesting a potential role for SMCHD1 in the development of ptosis [22, 39]. The 5.9 Mb region contains PIEZO2, whose pathogenic variants can cause syndromic forms of ptosis, suggesting both genes may contribute to this classic feature [40]. However, the exact relationship between these genes and ptosis still requires support from direct experimental evidence. It should be noted that the genetic etiology of ptosis is complex. Several genes on 18p may contribute to its development, some of which may lie outside the candidate intervals identified here. For phenotypes controlled by multiple genes with incomplete penetrance, and particularly in studies with limited sample sizes such as this one, reverse phenotyping analysis has inherent limitations.

Autoimmune disorders

Autoimmune disorders occur in approximately 10% of individuals with 18p deletion syndrome, primarily manifesting as hypothyroidism, Graves’ disease, and rheumatoid arthritis [20, 41–43]. Hasi-Zogaj et al. previously mapped a candidate critical region for autoimmunity to a 12.3 Mb segment [2]. In our reviewed cases, a set of monozygotic triplets with a 5.9 Mb interstitial deletion located within this broader region exhibited Graves’ disease along with chronic urticaria and morphea manifestations. This finding allows refinement of the critical region for autoimmune susceptibility to this narrower 5.9 Mb interval. Interestingly, although the PTPN2 gene has been implicated in the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases, it lies outside this refined region, suggesting the involvement of other dosage-sensitive genes within the 5.9 Mb segment in immune dysregulation [2]. In this region, PTPRM exhibited a high predicted haploinsufficiency score (> 0.86). STAT3 is a key factor in the generation of Th17 cells and the onset of specific autoimmune manifestations. Herlin et al. found that PTPRM haploinsufficiency leads to an increase in cells with phosphorylated STAT3 and elevated levels of Th17 cells in the patients, thereby suggesting it may be a contributing factor to autoimmune susceptibility in 18p deletion syndrome [20]. The evidence from these findings suggests PTPRM is a potential candidate gene for causing autoimmune disorders.

It should be noted that a limitation of this study is that the lack of specific evaluations—such as neuroimaging (e.g., MRI) and hormone level measurements—in many historically reported cases may restrict more in-depth analysis of candidate regions and genotype-phenotype correlations, and even introduce difficulties.

In summary, despite the limitations inherent in this case report and literature review, our study expands the clinical spectrum of 18p deletion syndrome—particularly regarding reproductive endocrine manifestations—and refines candidate genomic intervals for multiple phenotypes through the integration of recent cases and advanced molecular characterization. These findings enhance our understanding of genotype-phenotype correlations and provide valuable insights for genetic counseling, clinical management, and future research into molecular mechanisms.
