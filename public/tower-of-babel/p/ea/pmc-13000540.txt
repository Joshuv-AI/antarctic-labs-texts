Tribological behavior of laser-cladded CoCrFeNi system high-entropy alloy coatings before and after immersion in 3.5 wt. % NaCl solution
Authors: Fangyan Luo, Hongtao Jin, Wenqing Shi, Jiang Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryIn marine engineering, steels are prone to corrosion after long-term seawater immersion, while the tribological properties of coatings post-immersion—which are critical to engineering reliability—remain insufficiently investigated. To address this gap, three coatings (CoCrFeNi, CoCrFeNi-Mn, and CoCrFeNi-Al) were fabricated on Q235B steel substrates via laser cladding (laser power: 1200 W, scanning speed: 500 mm/min). Systematic tribological tests were performed before and after 90 h of immersion in 3.5 wt % NaCl solution at 17°C (simulated seawater conditions). Key findings show that CoCrFeNi and CoCrFeNi-Mn coatings possess a single face-centered cubic (FCC) phase, whereas CoCrFeNi-Al transforms into a body-centered cubic (BCC) phase, with grain size ranking as CoCrFeNi > CoCrFeNi-Mn > CoCrFeNi-Al. Before immersion, CoCrFeNi-Al exhibits optimal hardness (494.29 HV) and wear resistance (wear rate: 4.88 × 10−5 mm3 N−1 m−1). After immersion, CoCrFeNi-Mn achieves the best wear resistance (wear rate: 1.23 × 10−5 mm3 N−1 m−1). This work provides theoretical support and guidance for studying the tribological properties of metallic coatings after long-term immersion in marine engineering.

Introduction

In marine engineering, steel materials are susceptible to corrosion-induced failure after long-term immersion in seawater. Therefore, protective coatings are required to significantly enhance their surface corrosion resistance.1,2,3,4 Among various surface modification technologies, laser cladding (LC) has garnered considerable attention owing to two key advantages: first, its high cooling rate facilitates the formation of stable solid solutions within the coating and second, it enables efficient grain refinement, thus comprehensively optimizing the overall performance of the coating.5,6,7

As reviewed by Luo et al.,8 high-entropy alloys (HEAs) have established themselves as a research frontier in materials science owing to their multi-principal-element design, which features high configurational entropy and a suite of exceptional properties, including high strength, corrosion resistance, and thermal stability. HEAs represent a category of alloy systems composed of four or more metallic elements in equiatomic or near-equiatomic proportions. These materials are predominantly characterized by solid-solution structures, with their crystal structures mainly classified as body-centered cubic (BCC), face-centered cubic (FCC), and hexagonal close-packed (HCP) phases.9,10 Among these materials, CoCrFeNi system HEAs exhibit substantial application potential in marine engineering, with their core advantage lying in the synergistic effect between Co and Cr elements, which facilitates the formation of a highly protective passive film on the coating surface.11,12

Extensive research has been conducted on the modification of CoCrFeNi system HEA coatings (HEACs). Zhang et al.13 fabricated a 6FeNiCoSiCrAlTi HEAC with a BCC solid-solution phase via LC, reporting that the microhardness of this coating could reach 780 HV. Cheng et al.14 explored the effect of Nb on the mechanical properties of CoCrCuFeNi coatings, revealing that the wear resistance of Nb-containing coatings was approximately 1.5 times that of Nb-free counterparts. Du et al.15 employed a hybrid process combining extremely high-speed LC (EHLC) and laser shock peening (LSP) for the preparation of CoCrFeMnNi HEACs. Their studies demonstrated that, after four cycles of LSP treatment, the microhardness of the coating was enhanced by 51.9%, reaching 341.8 HV. Xu et al.16 prepared a series of CoCrFeNiCux HEACs using high-speed LC, finding that, with increasing Cu content, the corrosion resistance of the coatings decreased gradually at first and then declined sharply, whereas the antifouling performance was significantly improved and ultimately maintained at a stable and excellent level. Jin et al.17 fabricated CoCrFeNiMox coatings with varying Mo contents via LC. They found that Mo could not only promote the formation of the σ phase and realize grain refinement but also significantly enhance the coating hardness with increasing Mo content, with the peak value reaching 469.4 HV, while the impact energy decreased to 16 J. Kong et al.18 investigated the effect of theTi/Mo content ratio on the properties of CoCrFeNiMnTixMo1.5-x coatings, concluding that, when x = 0.25, the microhardness and wear resistance of the coating were 3.18 times and 10.9 times those of the substrate, respectively. When x = 1, the coating tended to form a passive film, and the corrosion current density (Icorr) was reduced to a minimum of 6.36308 × 10−7 A/cm2. Yue et al.19 further investigated the role of Ti in CoCrFeNiAl coatings, showing that Ti could induce the phase composition of AlCoCrFeNi coatings to transform from a single BCC phase to a BCC1+B2 dual-phase structure. Meanwhile, it could also facilitate the formation of passive films and inhibit the propagation of corrosion pits.

Existing studies have shown that most research on the modification of CoCrFeNi system HEACs focuses on regulating the mechanical properties and corrosion resistance of coatings by adding metallic elements. However, in the marine engineering field, equipment is not only subjected to long-term severe seawater corrosion but also exposed to erosion under extreme climatic conditions, including strong winds, alternating high and low temperatures, high salinity, saturated oxygen, and intense ultraviolet radiation. Metallic materials are prone to surface damage under the combined effects of corrosion, friction, and extreme climates, which has become a major failure mode of moving components in marine engineering equipment.

In recent years, several studies have focused on the tribocorrosion performance of coatings. Zhang et al.20 fabricated CoCrFeNi-WC HEACs via LC and systematically investigated their tribocorrosion behavior, reporting that the W35 coating, with 35 wt % WC, exhibited the optimal tribocorrosion performance, with an average friction coefficient of 0.329 and a wear rate as low as 8.09 × 10−6 mm3/(N·m). Liu et al.21 systematically explored the microstructure, corrosion-wear performance, and immersion corrosion properties of the CoCrFeNi-Mo (Al, Ti) coating series, noting that the CoCrFeNi-Al coating possessed the best mechanical properties, with a hardness of 825 HV and a wear rate of 1.88 × 102 μm3 s−1 N−1. Furthermore, the dominant corrosion mechanism for all three coatings in this series was pitting corrosion.

It is worth noting that all the aforementioned studies focus on the coupling effects of friction and corrosion. Nevertheless, in practical marine engineering applications, metallic materials often encounter the typical service condition of friction occurring after long-term immersion corrosion. Typical application scenarios are as follows: ship propellers undergo long-term seawater erosion during mooring and then experience friction-erosion with seawater and suspended particles during high-speed rotation after sailing; mooring chains of offshore platforms experience frictional wear with connectors due to platform displacement induced by wind and waves following long-term immersion corrosion; and subsea pipeline supports are corroded when immersed in seawater and sediments while simultaneously undergoing friction with pipeline walls as a result of ocean currents and pipeline displacement. In summary, existing research has mostly concentrated on investigating the individual wear resistance, individual corrosion resistance, or simultaneous tribocorrosion performance of coatings, and there is a lack of targeted research on the specific service condition of friction occurring after long-term immersion corrosion. Therefore, dedicated research on the wear resistance of CoCrFeNi system HEACs after long-term immersion corrosion holds considerable practical engineering value.

Our research group has previously conducted a systematic investigation on the corrosion resistance and passive film characteristics of three HEACs: CoCrFeNi, CoCrFeNi-Mn, and CoCrFeNi-Al.22 Building on this, the present study prepared these three CoCrFeNi system HEACs on Q235B steel substrates via LC, with three key objectives: to analyze the microstructural characteristics of the coatings, to explore their conventional tribological behavior and tribological behavior after immersion, and to reveal the variation in the coatings’ wear resistance before and after immersion. The innovation of this study resides in focusing on the “tribological behavior of coatings after immersion,” aiming to provide theoretical support and a technical reference for the practical application of CoCrFeNi system HEACs in marine engineering and to accelerate their engineering adoption.

Results

Macroscopic morphology

Figure 1 presents the macroscopic surface morphologies of CoCrFeNi, CoCrFeNi-Mn, and CoCrFeNi-Al coatings. The CoCrFeNi and CoCrFeNi-Mn coatings exhibit relatively smooth and uniform surfaces, free of obvious cracks and pores, in contrast to the CoCrFeNi-Al coating, which shows distinct surface crack defects. Crack initiation and propagation during LC constitute complex physical phenomena, essentially ascribed to the coupled effects of residual tensile stress, solidification shrinkage, and molten pool backfilling. The underlying mechanism governing this phenomenon can be elucidated from two aspects: thermal characteristics and intrinsic material properties. Specifically, the temperature gradient and the migration rate of the solid-liquid interface serve as critical factors regulating crack formation in terms of thermal characteristics, while the coefficient of thermal expansion, melt viscosity, and the thermodynamic and kinetic properties associated with intermetallic compound formation all exert a pronounced influence on crack initiation and propagation in terms of intrinsic material properties.Figure 1Macroscopic surface morphology of HEACs, reproduced with permission from Luo et al.22 Copyright 2026 CHINESE LASER PRESS(A) CoCrFeNi coating.(B) CoCrFeNi-Mn coating.(C) CoCrFeNi-Al coating.

Al serves as a core variable governing the crack sensitivity of CoCrFeNi-Al coatings, with its influence manifested in two key aspects. Specifically, Al content indirectly modulates the liquid backfilling capacity of the molten pool by altering the alloy viscosity. Liquid backfilling, defined as the ability of liquid or semi-solid materials to refill newly formed openings (porosities or cracks) below the surface, is a critical factor in suppressing crack formation and is closely associated with the kinematic viscosity of the alloy: materials with higher viscosity exhibit reduced flowability into these openings, resulting in elevated crack sensitivity. Pure aluminum possesses extremely low viscosity near its melting point, so alloy viscosity initially decreases with Al addition but rises sharply as temperature drops. Further increases in Al content, however, lead to increased alloy viscosity near the melting point, rendering the alloy more susceptible to tearing at high temperatures and thereby aggravating crack generation.23 This conclusion is consistent with the findings of Sun et al.,23 who demonstrated that, when the Al atomic ratio reaches 1.0, both the crack density and size of CoCrFeNi-Al coatings increase significantly. Furthermore, the Al content modulates the phase composition of the coating, thereby influencing its brittleness and crack propagation propensity. Yang et al.24 and Kao et al.25 have reported that the critical Al atomic ratio inducing the phase transformation of CoCrFeNiAlx samples from FCC to a single BCC phase is approximately 0.9. Furthermore, complementary studies by Yue et al.19 and Zhou et al.26 have demonstrated that the equiatomic CoCrFeNi-Al coating exhibits a single BCC phase. Increasing the Al concentration suppresses the formation of the FCC phase. This phase transformation facilitates the development of the BCC phase, which enhances the strength of the alloy while reducing its ductility.27 Given the inherent high brittleness of the BCC phase, its dominance in the coating significantly impairs material toughness, creates preferential sites for crack initiation, and accelerates subsequent crack propagation. This constitutes a key intrinsic factor underlying the high crack susceptibility of CoCrFeNi-Al coatings.

Combining the XRD fitting and EBSD phase map results, it can be seen that both the CoCrFeNi and CoCrFeNi-Mn coatings exhibit a single FCC phase, which is consistent with results reported in the existing literature.33,34 Notably, the addition of Mn leads to a leftward shift of the characteristic diffraction peaks of the FCC phase. This occrs because Mn atoms, having a larger atomic radius, dissolve into the FCC lattice, causing lattice distortion; such distortion increases the lattice constant of the FCC phase, thereby resulting in the leftward shift of the characteristic diffraction peaks.35 In contrast, the CoCrFeNi-Al coating exhibits a single BCC phase (Figure 2D). This result corroborates the conclusion from the aforementioned macromorphology analysis that the CoCrFeNi-Al coating contains cracks. It further demonstrates that the addition of 1.0 at.% Mn does not alter the phase composition of the CoCrFeNi HEA, whereas the addition of 1.0 at.% Al can completely transform the phase structure of the HEA from FCC to BCC,36 which is consistent with the findings of Li et al.32 and Zhao et al.,37 who prepared CoCrFeNi-Al coatings via LC.

The underlying reason is that the addition of Al atoms, having a larger atomic radius, not only changes the phase composition of the coating but also induces lattice distortion.38 As the Al content increases, the crystal structure of the coating gradually transforms from FCC to BCC. This occurs because, under the same LC process parameters, the energy of the BCC lattice structure is significantly lower than that of the FCC lattice structure.39 The addition of Al increases the distortion energy and strain energy within the FCC lattice. When such energy rises to a threshold that the FCC lattice cannot withstand, the metastable FCC lattice transforms into a stable BCC lattice to maintain the energy balance of the system.40

Microscopic morphology

To investigate the microstructures of the three coatings, EBSD characterization was performed. It should be noted that the EBSD detection area in this study was selected from the top to the middle region of the coatings, based primarily on the following practical experimental considerations: first, the laser-cladded samples need to undergo grinding and polishing before the wear resistance test, during which the extremely thin surface layer at the top of the coatings is removed, making the effective area for actual performance characterization extend from the top to the middle; second, the EBSD test uses cross-sectional samples, and the top surface area of the coatings is not subjected to grinding treatment, so selecting the region from the top to the middle for detection is more consistent with experimental reality.

Figures 3A, 3B, and 3C displays the inverse pole figures (IPF) and pole figures (PF) of the CoCrFeNi, CoCrFeNi-Mn, and CoCrFeNi-Al coatings. It is clearly observed that the CoCrFeNi coating has the largest grain size among the three, with an average of 65.58 μm, followed by the CoCrFeNi-Mn coating at 39.76 μm, while the CoCrFeNi-Al coating exhibits the smallest grain size, averaging only 17.90 μm. Smaller grain sizes correspond to higher structural compactness of the coating, and enhanced compactness contributes to improving the coating’s microhardness.41 Compared with the CoCrFeNi coating, the addition of Mn or Al elements refines the coating grains, mainly due to the lattice distortion induced by the introduction of these elements. The atomic radius of Mn differs from those of the elements in CoCrFeNi, leading to atomic size mismatch in the coating and subsequent lattice distortion, which refines the grains. Additionally, the introduction of distinct electronic structures into the coating generates a large number of heterogeneous nucleation sites. The increase in heterogeneous nucleation sites reduces the critical nucleation energy required for the coating, improves the heterogeneous nucleation rate, thereby accelerating nucleation efficiency and promoting grain refinement. Compared with Mn, Al has a greater difference in atomic radius from the elements in CoCrFeNi, resulting in more pronounced grain refinement.Figure 3EBSD characterization results of HEACs(A) Inverse pole figure and pole figure of CoCrFeNi coating.(B) Inverse pole figure and pole figure of CoCrFeNi-Mn coating.(C) Inverse pole figure and pole figure of CoCrFeNi-Al coating.

Interestingly, the CoCrFeNi-Al coating exhibits a distinct boundary between fine and coarse grains. The fine grains are mainly composed of equiaxed grains, while the coarse grains consist of columnar grains. This phenomenon stems from the unique cooling process of LC: according to the undercooling principle, the crystal structure of the laser-cladded coating is influenced by the direction of maximum heat flux dissipation. The heat dissipation direction at the top of the coating is closer to horizontal, resulting in a relatively low G/R ratio (where G is the temperature gradient and R is the solidification rate), which favors the formation of equiaxed grains in the top region. In contrast, the heat dissipation direction at the bottom of the coating is perpendicular to the substrate, and the crystal growth direction aligns with this perpendicular orientation. The higher G/R ratio in this region reduces undercooling, thereby promoting the formation of coarse structures (i.e., columnar grains).34 This unique grain distribution in the CoCrFeNi-Al coating can effectively enhance its wear resistance. The fine equiaxed grains at the top provide strong grain-refinement strengthening, while the robust columnar grains at the bottom support the top grains and help share the applied load.

Furthermore, as indicated in Figures 3A, 3B, and 3C, the grain orientation of the CoCrFeNi-Mn coating is mainly concentrated in the {100} crystal plane family, which is consistent with our previous research findings.34 However, compared with the CoCrFeNi-Mn coating, the CoCrFeNi and CoCrFeNi-Al coatings exhibit a higher proportion of grain orientation distribution in the {111} crystal plane family, which is conducive to enhancing coating performance and improving coating anisotropy. The close-packing mode and high binding energy of Co, Cr, Fe, and Ni atoms are the primary reasons for the improvement in the macroscopic mechanical properties of the alloy.42 Additionally, from a kinetic perspective, grains with the (111)<111> crystal orientation exhibit the strongest passivation tendency.43

Figures 3D, 3E, and 3F present the grain boundary (GB) distribution maps of the CoCrFeNi, CoCrFeNi-Mn, and CoCrFeNi-Al coatings. In the figures, blue lines denote low-angle grain boundaries (LAGBs, 2–15°), while red lines represent high-angle grain boundaries (HAGBs, >15°). The proportions of HAGBs in the CoCrFeNi, CoCrFeNi-Mn, and CoCrFeNi-Al coatings are 77.4%, 79.8%, and 95.0%, respectively. Studies have shown that HAGBs exhibit a stronger dislocation-blocking effect. When more LAGBs transform into HAGBs, the GBs tend to stabilize and impede dislocation motion, leading to increased dislocation accumulation. This consequently enhances the mechanical properties of the materials.44,45

Figures 3G, 3H, and 3I present the Schmid factor distribution and corresponding statistical results of the CoCrFeNi, CoCrFeNi-Mn, and CoCrFeNi-Al coatings, with the Schmid factor values of all three coatings ranging from 0.3 to 0.5. Specifically, the Schmid factor of the CoCrFeNi coating is 0.47, that of the CoCrFeNi-Mn coating is 0.46, and that of the CoCrFeNi-Al coating is 0.45. These values can be analyzed with reference to(Equation 3)σ = τc/(cosφcosλ)maxof the critical shear stress law governing the deformation of metal single crystals.46

Here, σ represents the yield strength of the crystal, τc denotes the critical shear stress, φ is the angle between the deformation direction and the normal to the {111} primary slip plane of FCC metals, and λ is the angle between the deformation direction and the <110> slip direction. The term (cosφcosλ) corresponds to the Schmid factor, with its maximum value representing the highest Schmid factor along the slip direction. This relationship indicates that the Schmid factor of a crystal is inversely proportional to its deformation resistance. Consequently, the CoCrFeNi-Al coating is expected to exhibit superior wear resistance.

Macromorphology and micromorphology after immersion

In order to better analyze the wear resistance of the three coatings after immersion, we refer to our previous work regarding the surface morphology, element distribution, and phase composition of the three coatings after immersion, which are presented in Figures 4, 5, and 6, respectively. To simulate the corrosive environment in marine settings, the CoCrFeNi, CoCrFeNi-Mn, and CoCrFeNi-Al coatings were immersed in a 3.5 wt. % NaCl solution at 17°C for 90 h. Figures 4A, 4B, and 4C present the macromorphologies of the CoCrFeNi, CoCrFeNi-Mn, and CoCrFeNi-Al coatings after 90 h of immersion, respectively. It can be observed that the surface of the CoCrFeNi coating exhibits no obvious corrosion traces. In contrast, the surface of the CoCrFeNi-Mn coating shows severe corrosion traces, while the CoCrFeNi-Al coating suffers the most severe corrosion at the crack sites. These results indicate that the CoCrFeNi coating possesses the best immersion corrosion resistance, whereas the CoCrFeNi-Mn coating exhibits relatively poor immersion corrosion resistance. Figures 4D, 4G, and 4J present the micromorphologies of the CoCrFeNi coating after immersion. It can be observed that the corroded surface is relatively flat and covered with white crystals, which can reasonably be inferred to be NaCl crystals. This indicates that the passivation film formed on the surface of the CoCrFeNi coating exhibits strong resistance to NaCl, thereby inhibiting the penetration of Cl− into the coating. EDS surface scanning was performed on the most severely corroded area of the CoCrFeNi coating (Figure 5A). The results demonstrate that Na is mainly distributed at the coating interface, suggesting that the corrosive medium initiates corrosion primarily from the interface. Na+ accumulates on the surface and combines with oxygen to form a surface barrier.47 Furthermore, Cr and Fe are barely detected in the corroded areas, indicating that during the immersion process, corrosion preferentially occurs in Cr-depleted and Fe-depleted regions of the CoCrFeNi coating.

Figures 4E, 4H, and 4K present the micromorphologies of the CoCrFeNi-Mn coating after immersion. It can be observed that obvious corrosion pits formed on the coating. Further magnification of the interior of these corrosion pits reveals the presence of smaller, deeper pits with reduced volume. This indicates that the corrosion pits expand outward from the center, and their size increases with prolonged immersion time. Due to the height difference between the corrosion pits and the coating surface, oxygen has difficulty diffusing within the narrow pits, thus creating an oxygen concentration gradient. Owing to this difference gradient—lower oxygen concentration inside the pits and higher oxygen concentration outside—an oxygen concentration cell is formed between the interior (anode) and exterior (cathode) of the corrosion pits, thereby accelerating the corrosion process.48 EDS surface scanning results (Figure 5B) demonstrate that the corrosion products within the pits are iron oxide.

Figures 4F, 4I, and 4L present the corrosion micromorphologies of the CoCrFeNi-Al coating after immersion. The corrosion morphology of the CoCrFeNi-Al coating exhibits corrosion pits, which are shallower than those observed on the CoCrFeNi-Mn coating. Small corrosion products are scattered on the surface of these pits. Notably, blackened areas appear inside the corrosion pits, suggesting that the CoCrFeNi-Al coating surface contains numerous defects where corrosion is extremely severe. EDS surface scanning results (Figure 5C) demonstrate that the corrosion products formed within the pits are iron oxide, while in areas outside the pits, the element distribution is uniform.

Immersion corrosion products

Figure 6 presents the XRD patterns of the CoCrFeNi, CoCrFeNi-Mn, and CoCrFeNi-Al coatings after 90 h of immersion. Diffraction peaks corresponding to chromium oxide (CrO PDF#08–0254), iron oxide (Fe2O3 PDF#16–0653), cobalt oxide (Co2O3 PDF#02–0770), and nickel oxide (NiO PDF#47–1049) appeared for the CoCrFeNi coating. For the CoCrFeNi-Mn coating, the formed peaks included CrO, Fe2O3, Co2O3, and manganese oxides (MnO PDF#04–0326, MnO2 PDF#12–0141, Mn3O4 PDF#24–0734). As for the CoCrFeNi-Al coating, peaks of Fe2O3 and aluminum oxides (Al2O3 PDF#47–2192, PDF#46–1131) were detected. These results indicate that all the immersed HEACs generated stable oxidation products.

Prior to immersion, the CoCrFeNi-Al coating exhibited the lowest friction coefficient and wear rate, whereas the CoCrFeNi coating showed the highest values for both parameters. Figure 8 presents the microhardness test results of the three coatings. The results indicate that CoCrFeNi-Al coating has the highest microhardness, while the CoCrFeNi coating has the lowest. According to the Archard wear law,49 there is a negative correlation between the wear loss of a material and its hardness. Therefore, the variation trend of the microhardness of the three coatings is completely consistent with that of their wear resistance. Specifically, the improvement in the wear resistance of the CoCrFeNi-Al coating is closely related to the increase in its microhardness. The enhanced wear resistance of the CoCrFeNi-Al coating is primarily attributed to the addition of Al, which facilitates the formation of a hard BCC phase that effectively enhances the hardness of the HEA.25 As revealed by the phase analysis (Figure 2), the CoCrFeNi-Al coating has a single-phase BCC structure, and the inherent high hardness of the BCC phase contributes to its superior wear resistance. Additionally, the grain size of the coatings exerts a significant influence on their wear resistance. Grain refinement strengthens the “barrier effect” of the coating, increases the number of grain boundaries, and intensifies the degree of grain boundary distortion; these effects collectively lead to an improvement in the coating’s microhardness.50,51,52,53 As shown in Figure 3, the CoCrFeNi-Al coating has the smallest grain size, followed by the CoCrFeNi-Mn HEAC. Consequently, both coatings demonstrate better wear resistance than the CoCrFeNi coating. After immersion in the corrosive solution, the friction coefficients of all three coatings exhibited an upward trend. Among them, the CoCrFeNi-Al coating still exhibited the smallest coefficient of friction. However, its coefficient of friction fluctuated greatly, and the wear rate was the largest among the three coatings. The wear rates of both the CoCrFeNi HEA and CoCrFeNi-Mn coatings decreased significantly. Among them, the CoCrFeNi-Mn coating exhibited the lowest wear rate. It is concluded that the CoCrFeNi-Mn HEA has the best wear resistance after 90 h of immersion.

It is noteworthy that the coefficients of friction of the immersed CoCrFeNi and CoCrFeNi-Mn coatings are smoother and less fluctuating (Figures 7D, 7E, and 7F). This indicates that the friction process of the immersed CoCrFeNi and CoCrFeNi-Mn coatings is more stable. This phenomenon can be attributed to the stable passivation film formed on the coating surface during the immersion process. Specifically, during immersion, a corrosion-induced oxide layer of a certain thickness is generated on the coating surface. During the friction process, this oxide layer functions as a lubricating medium, thereby enhancing the frictional performance of the coatings.54,55 The formation of this oxide lubricating layer prevents direct contact between the steel ball and the coating substrate, which in turn reduces the friction coefficient and wear rate of the coatings. As the wear process progresses, the oxide layers undergo damage and flaking; however, they also rapidly flatten and re-adhere to the wear area.47 The compaction and deformation of the oxide layer are accompanied by plastic straining in the coating material; this straining facilitates the formation of a mechanical mixed layer (MML), thereby contributing to the reduction in wear losses.56 In addition, detailed observation of the coefficient of friction graph of the CoCrFeNi coating reveals that the immersed coating shows a similar friction trend to the pre-immersion coating at 35 min. This indicates that the steel ball is in contact with the oxide layer of the coating before 35 min, while after 35 min, the steel ball contacts the surface of the coating. A similar phenomenon was observed for the CoCrFeNi-Mn coatings. However, the variation trend of the friction coefficient of the CoCrFeNi-Al coating differs from that described above. This is because Al2O3 is present in the passivation film of the CoCrFeNi-Al coating, and the coating itself contains numerous defects, with its cracks filled with iron oxide, which is also the reason for the significant fluctuations in the friction coefficient of the coating after immersion. Al2O3 is a high-hardness oxide that tends to induce stress concentration, thereby exacerbating the adhesive wear of the coating and leading to local spallation. The detached hard oxides are trapped to form a three-body wear system together with the steel ball and the coating, further aggravating the abrasive wear of the coating. Ultimately, the wear rate of the coating increases, and its wear resistance is greatly reduced.

Figure 9 presents the EDS surface scans of the wear scars of CoCrFeNi, CoCrFeNi-Mn, and CoCrFeNi-Al coatings before and after immersion. Analysis of the wear scars of the coatings prior to immersion reveals that the CoCrFeNi coating exhibits the largest wear scar width and depth, while both the CoCrFeNi-Mn and CoCrFeNi-Al coatings show smaller wear scar widths, with the CoCrFeNi-Al coating displaying the shallowest depth. This is consistent with the above analysis of the coefficient of friction and wear profile. Observation of the elemental distribution of the coatings reveals that oxides are generated within the wear profiles of each coating, and the most uniform distribution of oxides is found in the CoCrFeNi-Mn coating. This indicates that during friction, the CoCrFeNi-Mn coating reacts with oxygen in the air at a rate higher than the wear rate of the oxides and coatings. This is an important reason why the wear resistance of CoCrFeNi-Mn coating is superior to that of CoCrFeNi coating. This phenomenon occurs due to the strong oxidizing properties of manganese oxides.57 At the high temperature generated during the friction process, Mn elements in the CoCrFeNi-Mn coating form manganese oxides, which exhibit strong oxidizing properties and act as oxidants to accelerate the oxidation rate of metals on the coating surface.Figure 9EDS surface scanning of wear morphology of HEA coatings before and after immersion(A) CoCrFeNi coating.(B) CoCrFeNi-Mn coating.(C) CoCrFeNi-Al coating.

Comparison of the elemental distribution of the coatings before and after corrosion shows that the three coatings before immersion have almost no oxygen elements in the outer wear region. After immersion, the outer wear area is covered with oxygen elements. This indicates that, after immersion, passivation films were generated on all three coatings. Notably, the oxygen content in the out-of-wear regions of the CoCrFeNi and CoCrFeNi-Al coatings is significantly higher than that of the CoCrFeNi-Mn coating, indicating that the former two coatings have a higher amount of oxides generated during immersion and a superior degree of densification of the passivation film compared to the CoCrFeNi-Mn coating. This is mainly due to the presence of Mn element, whose detrimental effect on corrosion resistance is very pronounced.58 In the passivation film of the Mn-containing coating, Mn typically exists in the valence states of Mn2+, Mn3+, and Mn4+, among which the relative content of Mn3+ is usually the highest. Mn3+ exists in the passivation film as Mn2O3 and Mn(OH)3; compared with MnO, these two compounds have a looser structure and lower density, thus exerting a negative impact on the corrosion resistance of the CoCrFeNi-Mn coating. However, precisely due to the presence of Mn3+, during the friction process of the immersed coating, it acts as an oxidant.57 Under the high temperature generated by friction, it accelerates the oxidation of metals on the coating surface, thereby maintaining the existence of the coating’s MML and ultimately enhancing the coating’s wear resistance.

The Co, Cr, and Ni elements in the wear contour of CoCrFeNi coating are significantly reduced. The main components reduced in the wear contour of the CoCrFeNi-Mn coating are Fe and Mn, and the main components reduced in the wear contour of the CoCrFeNi-Al coating are Cr and Fe. This reflects that the main metal elements of the passivation film generated by the CoCrFeNi coating are Co, Cr, and Ni; the main metal elements of the passivation film of the CoCrFeNi-Mn coating are Fe and Mn; and the main metallic elements of the passivation film of the CoCrFeNi-Al coating are Cr and Fe. Notably, the reduction in Fe content is remarkably significant across all three coatings. This is because Fe is highly susceptible to reacting with oxygen during the immersion process, forming Fe2O3. With its relatively high hardness, Fe2O3 tends to induce stress concentration and cause adhesive wear, ultimately leading to local spallation of the coatings.

This observation can be attributed to the alternating stresses generated during the friction process, which induce high localized stress concentrations. These stress concentrations subsequently initiate the formation of surface microcracks. To analyze the valence states of metal oxides in the passivation films formed by corrosion on the surfaces of the CoCrFeNi, CoCrFeNi-Mn, and CoCrFeNi-Al coatings, high-resolution peak scanning was employed. Figure 12 presents the spectra of the main constituent elements (Co 2p, Cr 2p, Fe 2p, Ni 2p, Mn 2p, Al 2p, and O 1s) fitted using Avantage software. It can be seen from the fitting results that oxygen atoms in the passivation films of the three coatings exist in the forms of O2− and OH−. This indicates that both metal oxides and metal hydroxides are present in the passivation films generated by the coatings in the 3.5 wt. % NaCl solution. In addition, the passivation films of these three coatings exhibit identical valence states of Co, Cr, Fe, and Ni. Specifically, Co atoms exist in the forms of Co0, Co3+, and Co2+; Cr atoms exist in the forms of Cr0, Cr2+, and Cr6+; Fe atoms exist in the forms of Fe0, Fe2+, and Fe3+; and Ni atoms exist in the forms of Ni0, Ni2+, and Ni3+. The differences lie in the following aspects: in the passivation film of the CoCrFeNi-Mn coating, Mn atoms exist in the forms of Mn0, Mn2+, Mn3+, and Mn4+; whereas in the passivation film of the CoCrFeNi-Al coating, Al atoms exist in the forms of Al0, Alox3+, and Alhy3+.

The mechanical properties of a passivation film are generally determined by the metal oxides that constitute the film. Compared with metal hydroxides, metal oxides possess higher density and greater hardness, thereby reducing the wear rate of the coating. Therefore, the relative contents of O2− and OH− in the coating passivation film exert a significant influence on the wear resistance of the coating. Figure 13 presents the relative contents of O2− and OH− ions in the passivation films of CoCrFeNi, CoCrFeNi-Mn, and CoCrFeNi-Al coatings. Specifically, the CoCrFeNi and CoCrFeNi-Mn coatings have a higher content of O2− and a lower content of OH− in their passivation films, whereas the CoCrFeNi-Al coating has a higher content of OH− and a lower content of O2−. The main reason for the significant increase in the relative content of OH− in the CoCrFeNi-Al coating is that Al atoms in the coating form Al(OH)3 during immersion, thereby increasing the relative content of OH−.

First, for the CoCrFeNi coating (Figure 14A), the content of metal oxides in its passivation film is higher than that of metal hydroxides, and the high-hardness Cr2O3 accounts for a relatively high proportion, which significantly enhances the overall strength of the passivation film. During the wear process, the high-hardness Cr2O3 acts as a skeleton, providing structural support for the oxide film while also playing a fixing role within the wear contact profile, thus effectively mitigating abrasive wear.60 In addition, no obvious corrosion defects are observed on the surface of the coating after immersion, which provides a favourable lubrication foundation for the wear process.61 However, the formation of such high-hardness oxide films increases the roughness of the worn surface, resulting in fluctuations and an overall increase in the friction coefficient of the coatings. This is the primary reason for the increased friction coefficient of the three coatings after immersion.

Next, for the CoCrFeNi-Mn coating (Figure 14B), consistent with the CoCrFeNi coating, the content of metal oxides in its passivation film is also higher than that of metal hydroxides, but there are significant differences between the two coatings. In the passivation film of the CoCrFeNi-Mn coating, in addition to the high content of hard Cr2O3, the content of hard Fe2O3 is further increased, which enhances the strength of the passivation film. In addition, studies by Liang et al.62 have confirmed that the composite structure of soft and hard phases can shorten the contact expansion time of friction contact pairs and alleviate the problem of stress concentration. Compared with the CoCrFeNi coating, the content of metal hydroxides in the passivation film of the CoCrFeNi-Mn coating shows a slight increase, which can be attributed to the presence of Mn elements. Among the Mn elements contained in the passivation film, Mn3+ is the dominant valence state, existing in the form of two compounds, namely, Mn2O3 and Mn(OH)3. These two compounds have loose structures and low hardness and are distributed in the film as soft phases; the high-content hard phases bear the friction load directly, while the low-content soft phases play a supporting role for the hard phases. When the hard phases slide relatively, the soft phases can generate plastic flow to fully transfer and disperse the stress from the hard phases, thereby reducing friction resistance.63 In addition, under the high temperature generated by friction, Mn3+ acts as an oxidant to oxidize the metal elements or low-valent metal oxides in the passivation film, continuously maintaining and enhancing the stability of the passivation film, thereby improving the wear resistance of the coating. Furthermore, compared with the CoCrFeNi coating, obvious pitting corrosion pits are observed on the surface of the CoCrFeNi-Mn coating after immersion. Hu et al.64 prepared similar pit-shaped micro-dimple textures on the surface of CoCrFeNiMoW HEACs, and their study showed that such textures can reduce the direct contact area during wear, effectively trap wear debris, and form an elastic transition zone at the edges of the pits. Therefore, although the pitting corrosion pits themselves are corrosion defects, they can function as micro-dimple textures during the wear process, further inhibiting abrasive wear and reducing the coating wear rate through chip storage and contact area reduction. In summary, the CoCrFeNi-Mn coating exhibits the optimal wear resistance after immersion.

Third, for the CoCrFeNi-Al coating (Figure 14C): the content of metal hydroxides in its passivation film is higher than that of metal oxides, resulting in lower strength of the passivation film compared with the CoCrFeNi and CoCrFeNi-Mn coatings. Unlike the CoCrFeNi-Mn coating, the CoCrFeNi coating has an extremely low Mn content in its passivation film, so the increase in the content of metal hydroxides in its passivation film is extremely limited. In contrast, the passivation film of the CoCrFeNi-Al coating has a relatively high Al content; thus, the significant increase in hydroxide content in the passivation film is attributed to the formation of Al(OH)3. Notably, the cracks present in the macromorphology of the CoCrFeNi-Al coating also affect its tribological properties after immersion. Due to the presence of these cracks, the passivation film formed on the coating surface during immersion exhibits discontinuity, and the passivation film at the cracks has poor stability, which makes Al(OH)3 tend to preferentially nucleate and grow in these regions. However, during the friction process, such a discontinuous and unstable passivation film will increase the probability of wear debris detachment under the action of stress concentration.65 Therefore, a large amount of soft Al(OH)3 phase is consumed and converted into wear debris during wear. The detached Al(OH)3 wear debris, due to its extremely poor stability, will be oxidized to form high-hardness Al2O3 during subsequent friction processes, whose hardness is significantly higher than that of the steel ball. These high-hardness Al2O3 particles are easily trapped between the steel ball and the coating, inducing the three-body wear effect and thereby increasing the wear loss of the coating.66

Limitations of the study

This study fabricated three CoCrFeNi system HEACs (CoCrFeNi, CoCrFeNi-Mn, and CoCrFeNi-Al) on the surface of Q235B steel via LC. Targeting the typical service condition of “friction after long-term immersion corrosion” for metallic coatings in marine engineering, this study systematically investigated the microstructures and tribological properties of the three coatings, revealed their wear-resistant characteristics after immersion, and clarified the performance regulation effects of Mn and Al elements on CoCrFeNi system coatings.

This work fills the research gap regarding the tribological behavior of such coatings after immersion under actual marine service conditions, laying a theoretical foundation for the engineering application of relevant coatings. However, the performance tests are mainly based on short-term laboratory experiments, lacking simulation of actual marine engineering conditions and support from field test data. Furthermore, the tribocorrosion performance of the coatings after long-term immersion was not investigated. Therefore, the practical marine engineering application of CoCrFeNi system HEACs and their tribocorrosion performance after long-term immersion remain to be explored.

Method details

Laser cladding coating preparation

The experimental substrates were fabricated from Q235B steel, with dimensions of 100 mm (length)×50 mm (width)×3 mm (height). The substrate surface was ground sequentially with 60#, 240#, 800#, 1000# and 2000# sandpapers. Prior to the experiment, the substrate surface was rinsed with alcohol and dried in a drying oven. CoCrFeNi, CoCrFeNi-Mn, and CoCrFeNi-Al HEA powders was preplaced on the substrate surface using a standard mold with a dimension of (2±0.1) mm for the experiments, respectively. Subsequently, the coatings were fabricated using an XL-F2000 W fiber optic processing system. Laser parameters were optimized via preliminary experiments and set as follows: laser power of 1200 W, scanning speed of 500 mm/min, defocus distance of +5 mm, spot diameter of 2.5 mm, overlap rate of 50%, number of cladding tracks of 17, and cladding length of 45 mm. Argon gas with a flow rate of 5 L/min was employed as the shielding gas, which was introduced 20 minutes prior to the experiment and maintained until the experiment was completed.

Sample preparation for characterization

Samples perpendicular to the cladding layer surface were cut using an electric discharge wire cutting machine, with two different sizes prepared: Size 1: 10 mm (length) × 10 mm (width) × 4 mm (height) and Size 2: 10 mm (length) × 5 mm (width) × 4 mm (height). For Size 1 samples, the surface was subjected to gradual grinding with 80∼5000 grit sandpaper using an HYM-1 grinder, followed by polishing with diamond suspension on an HYP-1 metallographic polisher. These samples were prepared for phase composition characterization and wear resistance characterization before and after immersion. For Size 2 samples, the surface was also ground gradually with 80∼5000 grit sandpaper using an HYM-1 grinder, and then polished with diamond suspension on an HYP-1 metallographic polisher. These samples were prepared for microstructure characterization.

Immersed testing

To simulate the seawater corrosive environment, the samples were immersed in a 3.5 wt.% sodium chloride (NaCl) solution at 17 °C until distinct corrosion features appeared on the sample surfaces. A fixed corrosion area of 10 mm×10 mm was designated for each sample, while the remaining surface was sealed with phenolic resin to avoid undesired corrosion. The immersion duration was set at 90 h, a parameter determined based on preliminary corrosion tests.

Coating microstructure and composition testing

The microstructure and elemental distribution of the coatings were characterized in this study. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) were utilized to obtain micrographs of the samples after immersion and friction-wear tests, as well as to analyze their elemental distribution. In addition, electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) was adopted to investigate the grain orientation, phase composition, and Schmid factor of the coatings. The EBSD tests were performed at an accelerating voltage of 20.00 kV and an acquisition rate of 480 Hz. High-resolution X-ray diffraction (XRD) was performed with Cu-Kα radiation (λ=0.1540598 nm) over a 2θ range of 20°-90°, an accelerating voltage of 40 kV, and an emission current of 40 mA. This technique was utilized to characterize the phase structures of the coatings both before and after immersion. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to analyze the properties of the passivation films on the immersed samples. The detailed test parameters were set as follows: a monochromatic Al Kα source (E=1486.68 eV) as the excitation source, an accelerating voltage of 12000.00 V, a power of 72 W, a vacuum level better than 5×10-9 mBar, a pass energy of 100 eV for survey spectra and 50 eV for high-resolution spectra, and a work function coefficient of 4.20 eV.

Microhardness testing of the coating

The microhardness of the coatings was measured using a Vickers hardness tester. Tests were conducted along the cross-section of the sample from the top of the cladding layer to the substrate, with a 200 gf load applied and a dwell time of 10 seconds. Measurements were arranged at 0.10 mm intervals in the vertical direction, and three parallel tests were performed at 0.10 mm intervals in the horizontal direction. The average of these three measurements was recorded as the microhardness value for that horizontal position. The final microhardness value of the sample was determined by averaging all microhardness values within the cladding layer region.

Tribological testing

The wear resistance of the coating surfaces was evaluated using a pin-on-disk friction and wear tester integrated with a displacement sensor. A silicon nitride (Si3N4) ball with a diameter of 5 mm was selected as the counterbody for the wear tests. The test parameters were configured as follows: applied load of 20 N, rotating radius of 2 mm, rotational speed of 200 r/min, and total test duration of 60 min.

Quantification and statistical analysis

This study clarifies some statistical terms involved in the research. Figure 7 presents the experimental data related to the friction coefficient. Figures 7A and 7B show the variation trends of the coating friction coefficient with sliding time before and after immersion, while Figure 7C compares the average friction coefficients of the three coatings. The average friction coefficient of each group was calculated as the mean of all data points on the corresponding friction coefficient curve. Figure 7I illustrates the wear rates of the three coatings before and after immersion. The wear rate was derived by measuring the wear volume of the coating with a displacement sensor and calculating via L=V×(N×d)-1 (Equation 4). The wear volume of each coating was measured at three different positions, and the average value and standard deviation of the wear rates (calculated from the wear volumes at these three positions) were determined. Results indicate that before immersion, the CoCrFeNi-Al coating exhibited the lowest wear rate of 4.88×10-5 mm3·N-1·m-1. After immersion, the CoCrFeNi-Mn coating exhibited the lowest wear rate of 1.23×10-5 mm3·N-1·m-1.

Figure 8A illustrates the microhardness distribution characteristics of the cladding layer cross-section, which can be divided into three distinct regions: the cladding layer, Heat-Affected Zone (HAZ), and substrate. This curve intuitively reflects the variation law of microhardness at different positions. Experimental data are presented as mean ± standard error (SE). The average hardness value and standard deviation (SD) corresponding to each vertical position were calculated from these three sets of test data.
