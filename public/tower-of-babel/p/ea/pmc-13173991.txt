Study protocol: feasibility of a hearing program in primary care for underserved older adults
Authors: David R Friedmann, Leah N Diminich, Emily R Spitzer, Saima Ajmal, Barbara Weinstein, Victoria Dickson, Keith S Goldfeld, Joshua Chodosh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAge-related hearing loss is highly prevalent, underrecognized, and consequently, undertreated. Hearing loss can have a substantial negative impact on communication and biopsychosocial health. We hypothesize that offering validated hearing assessments and a point-of-care counseling program in an older, underserved population will be more accessible and acceptable than the traditional pathway for audiology care, particularly in marginalized communities.MethodsThis convergent mixed methods feasibility study will assess the implementation of a hearing program embedded in a quality improvement initiative within the Geriatric Clinic of New York City Health and Hospital’s Bellevue Hospital Center and the feasibility of recruiting for a future efficacy trial to test the intervention. Background

Hearing loss affects greater than 30 million Americans [1], including more than 70% of those over age 75. Hearing loss is often progressive, and when untreated, leads to social isolation and loss of independence [2]. Access and utilization of treatment for presbycusis or age-related hearing loss (ARHL) have broad implications for healthy aging. Untreated hearing loss has additional negative effects on mental [3] and physical well-being [2, 4, 5]. Implicated in declining neurocognitive function [4, 5], hearing loss is also associated with depression [6, 7], functional decline [8], and decreased quality of life [9, 10]. Moreover, hearing loss, as a fundamental barrier in communication, is likely to increase health service use [11, 12], may decrease quality of care and opportunities to participate in healthcare-related shared decision-making, and may further exacerbate the effects of low health literacy and impaired cognitive function on healthcare communication.

Only 39.5% of adults ages 70 years and older report having a hearing test in the previous 4 years [13]. When hearing-impaired older adults were asked whether their primary care providers had ever inquired about hearing loss, 85% reported they had not been asked [14]. Providers cite time limitations and lack of reimbursement among the reasons for not screening [15], in addition to the lack of evidence that hearing screening impacts health outcomes, as reinforced by the 2021 US Preventive Task Force [16]. This report highlighted insufficient evidence to endorse screening for hearing loss in adults, given the lack of studies to evaluate its impact. Despite minimal attention to hearing loss, several studies indicate that older adults are amenable to hearing screening [17, 18]. Hearing screening may be integrated in to the Medicare Annual Wellness Visit in geriatric care by including questions about subjective hearing difficulty and result in a referral to audiology, but data are not available on how often this is completed. At Bellevue Hospital in New York City, only 15% of a geriatric clinic population complete this annual visit based on administrative health records, and hearing screening is not a routine part of regular care visits.

Prior studies confirm that there are low rates of screening in primary care, but perhaps just as important, low rates of follow-up when hearing loss is suspected from screening. In a recent study, only 50% of individuals who failed hearing screens in primary care followed up to schedule an audiology visit for diagnostic testing, despite facilitation by the research team [19]. Our proposed intervention attempts to overcome this challenge by completing a validated hearing assessment including individual ear measures rather than a mere screening. This facilitates the delivery of a rehabilitative intervention at the same time rather than necessitating an audiology referral for further consideration of rehabilitation.

The proposed approach is warranted given that the current delivery model lacks efficiency and scalability to meet the burden of this chronic condition limiting access to care. Presently, multiple visits to an audiologist are required for diagnostic testing, hearing aid evaluation, dispensing, and fitting (typically 4–6 separate visits over several months). Medicare and most commercial insurance cover diagnostic evaluations but not hearing aids or their fittings [20], leaving many patients to face out-of-pocket costs, which can be as much as several thousand dollars. This financial burden is particularly challenging for older adults in public hospital-based clinics, who may have limited income, mobility impairments, or reliance on care partners for transportation [13].

While treatments for hearing loss are effective [21], they remain underutilized. In the USA, only one in seven adults with hearing loss wears a hearing aid [22]. Among those who do have hearing aids, non-adherence is common due to stigma, dexterity challenges, discomfort, and perceived need/disability [23]. Unsurprisingly, hearing aid ownership is lowest among socioeconomically disadvantaged groups and minorities [24].

Public healthcare settings offer an opportunity to engage these underserved populations, who have historically been understudied. Prior work suggests environmental exposures [25], socioeconomic status [26] and race [27] may contribute to a higher prevalence of hearing loss in these communities. Additionally, incorporating hearing loss management into primary care could be especially beneficial, as minoritized populations are less likely to seek audiology services [28].

Traditionally, audiologists perform diagnostic audiograms using specialized equipment in controlled settings. More recently, self-administered and automated portable tests have been validated in a quiet environment [29]. While these tests assess the degree of hearing impairment, they do not capture functional impact. Social and psychological factors play a crucial role in hearing disability and treatment-seeking behavior, which can be assessed using the Hearing Handicap Inventory screening version (HHI-S) [30]. This 10-item self-report measures perceived communication difficulties and its psychosocial impact.

Population-based studies have shown that HHI-S scores correlate with measured hearing loss [31, 32], highlighting the need to assess both perceived and measured hearing disability. Importantly, perceived hearing disability is more predictive of rehabilitation uptake than the pure tone audiogram. While hearing loss worsens with age, perceived disability does not necessarily follow the same trajectory [33]. The HHI-S has also been validated as a measure of subjective benefit following hearing rehabilitation [34], making it a valuable tool for identifying patients most likely to engage in treatment [35].

Primary care hearing screening has historically focused on identifying patients for audiology referrals, but in prior studies [19, 36, 37] hearing loss is not typically assessed or addressed in that setting. The proposed study is unique in that our hearing assessment is not limited to screening but provides a validated measure of the severity of hearing loss in the individual ear. Given this information, we will offer audiology referral but also offer access to point of service rehabilitation strategies as part of the intervention.

A range of hearing screening approaches exists, but this study proposes combining an objective and validated measure of hearing acuity with a subjective assessment of functional impact. Given the importance of perceived need and motivation in hearing rehabilitation, factors such as demographics, subjective disability and severity of impairment, social support and available resources may influence treatment-seeking behavior. This rationale is grounded in the adapted Stages of Change Model [38], which tailors the intervention to the individuals’ perceived hearing disability.

In this feasibility study, patients who test positive for hearing loss are invited to participate in a pilot study and randomized to one of two approaches: (1) a counseling session on alternative hearing rehabilitation strategies, or (2) usual care with a referral for formal audiologic evaluation. This study aims to first evaluate the feasibility of integrating a hearing screening/assessment into primary care. Then, with the trial phase, we explore the acceptability of counseling on alternative strategies to improve hearing care access for underserved older adults in preparation for a future efficacy trial to test the intervention.

Purpose and methods

This convergent mixed methods study will test the feasibility of integrating a hearing screening/assessment and rehabilitation program for older adults into primary care and conducting a future efficacy trial. Specifically, we will (1) offer on-site self-administered hearing screening/assessment using both subjective (survey-based) and objective (computer-assisted) evaluation and (2) introduce a counseling session on alternative hearing health and communication strategies at the same setting. We will assess acceptability, recruitment and retention rates, and adherence to screening and counseling, and the feasibility of collecting outcome measures.

Key participant interviews with staff and patients may provide insight into aspects of acceptability not otherwise measured from quantitative data. In this convergent study, quantitative and qualitative data will be integrated using joint display to examine qualitative results across types of providers to more fully understand acceptability within this setting juxtaposed with project process measures.

Findings will inform the design of a future study evaluating the intervention’s impact on hearing care access for underserved older adults. We will test whether a patient-centered approach that promotes widely available communication strategies may be more feasible and acceptable than the current pathway for hearing care.

Protocol overview

Older adults will be screened for hearing loss in a large urban public safety-net primary care clinic serving a diverse population. We focus on feasibility both in our ability to integrate a hearing program into primary care and our ability to conduct a future efficacy trial of the intervention as determined from the proposed mixed methods. We will measure feasibility for screening procedures by the proportion of approached patients who are willing to complete screening/assessment and have hearing loss. The feasibility of a larger efficacy trial of the intervention will depend on measures related to recruitment and study completion among participants who enroll. Patients who screen positive will be counseled and referred to audiology, and their providers will be notified of the results. Eligible patients with objective hearing loss will be invited to enroll and randomized to one of two approaches: (1) a counseling session on alternative hearing rehabilitation options or (2) referral to audiology alone. We will tailor counseling to patient preferences and resources, including a tutorial on using smart phones as assistive listening devices, with a focus on their built-in amplification and real-time captioning features, as well as over-the-counter hearing aids. Designed for ease of implementation, the counseling session will require minimal training and can be delivered by a medical assistant. This approach offers patient-centered, accessible options for individuals in primary care unlikely to seek specialty care traditional pathways.

We will measure the proportion of participants who follow through with recommendations accounting for specific actions as counts (numerator) over the number of recommendations (denominator), and we will report subjective benefits (Table 1). Participants will receive $25 upon completion of the 3-month follow-up. These findings will support a larger efficacy trial measuring this intervention’s impact on hearing, communication, and psychosocial well-being. The hospital’s Institutional Review Board approved this study, which is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05943509.

Feasibility measures and targets

Setting

This single-site study will take place at the Bellevue geriatric clinic, which serves adults aged 60 years and older. Bellevue Hospital, the oldest US public hospital, is also the largest in its health system. The patient population served by New York City’s public hospitals is highly diverse, predominantly low-income, and largely uninsured (37%). Among patients served in the system, 45% are Hispanic, 40% African American, 10% Asian American, and 5% Caucasian. The clinic operates Monday through Friday with two 3-h sessions per day, each accommodating 8–20 patients, including both new and return visits. On a typical clinic day, six providers see patients who speak a dozen or more languages. A research assistant will be present in the clinic for 4–6 sessions per week, depending on space availability, as a dedicated room is required for the testing procedures.

Eligibility

To be eligible for hearing screening in this study, individuals must be 60 years or older, speak English or Spanish, and not currently use hearing devices (hearing aids or cochlear implants). As the screening procedure is a quality improvement initiative, informed consent is not required. To be eligible for the clinical trial portion, participants must have objective hearing loss, defined as a four-frequency pure tone average (4F-PTA) greater than 25 dB HL at 500 Hz, 1000 Hz, 2000 Hz, and 4000 Hz in the better-hearing ear (representing at least mild hearing loss). Additionally, they must not have any red flag conditions (Table 2), and must provide consent to research study participation. This study flow is depicted in Fig. 1.

Recruitment

Sample size

The proposed sample size has been determined based on the need to estimate each feasibility outcome with an acceptable level of uncertainty. We will calculate a point estimate and a 95% confidence interval for each target proportion. If the lower bound of the confidence interval is within 10 percentage points of the target (e.g., exceeding 40% for a target of 50%), the threshold for that target will be considered met. Using simulation methods, and based on the current volume of patients in the clinic, we estimated that the feasibility study will have 80% power to meet all the targets if we approach 600 patients over the study period, with 80% agreeing to be screened, and assuming that half of those screened will have measured hearing loss (N = 240) and half of those will be enrolled (N = 120) (Fig. 1).

Approach

A bilingual research assistant (English and Spanish) will approach eligible patients in the waiting area after they have checked in and offer hearing screening/assessment as part of a quality improvement initiative. The research assistant will confirm the patient meets eligibility requirements, explain the process, and address any questions about the testing. While patients are approached prior to their visit, tests will typically be conducted at the conclusion of the patient’s scheduled appointment. For patients who agree to screening, the research assistant will send an electronic chat message to the patient’s provider in the electronic health record (EHR). This message will (1) notify the provider that the patient has agreed to screening, so that they can remind the patient to complete it after their appointment and (2) request that the provider notes in the EHR schedule that the patient requires a screening. This process will help alert clinic staff about pending tests, such as phlebotomy or X-rays, ensuring that the patient does not leave before completing their hearing screening.

Screening/assessment

Patients who agree to the hearing assessment will be directed to an unoccupied exam room after their clinic appointment. No identifiable personal health information will be collected at this point. First, a subjective hearing assessment, the 10-item Hearing Handicap Inventory Screening (HHI-S), is administered electronically on a tablet. Responses are securely recorded in REDCap. While patients will be encouraged to self-administer the questionnaire, the research assistant is available to assist in cases of vision or manual dexterity issues. Next, a validated tablet-based measure of hearing sensitivity (Shoebox™) will be administered using noise-cancelling headphones to calculate the 4F-PTA for each ear. Participants will be encouraged to self-administer the test, which features a drag-and-drop motion on the iPad, though the research coordinator will be available to help as needed.

Confirming eligibility

Hearing loss is defined as a score of 8 or greater on the HHI-S or a 4F-PTA greater than 25 dB HL in the better hearing ear, indicating at least mild hearing loss. Participants who have a positive result on the subjective screener but do not meet the criteria for objective hearing loss are provided with a referral to audiology but are not recruited for the study. Regardless of their subjective report, patients with objective hearing loss will be offered both study enrollment and an audiology referral. The procedure takes less than 10 min to complete. If hearing loss is confirmed, the research assistant performs video otoscopy to check for any ‘red flag conditions’ that would exclude participation, such as occluding cerumen, ear drainage, a foreign object, middle ear fluid, or a perforated eardrum (Table 2). A photograph of each tympanic membrane will be captured using a wireless video otoscope (Depstech), viewable on the tablet and saved to REDCap for review by the study team.

Enrollment and randomization

Eligible participants will be informed about the study procedures and invited to enroll. After obtaining informed consent, participants will be randomized in a 1:1 ratio to either (1) an intervention arm offering counseling on alternative hearing rehabilitation strategies or (2) a usual care arm consisting of an audiology referral alone. Randomization will be stratified by HHI-S score to account for subjective hearing disability and conducted using permuted block randomization with randomly varying block sizes of 2 and 4.

All participants will complete self-administered questionnaires on a tablet, with responses securely saved in REDCap. In addition to demographic information, assessments include the De Jong Gierveld Loneliness Questionnaire [39], the Patient Health Questionnaire 9 (PHQ-9) [40] to depressive symptoms, and questions about prior hearing care experiences, smartphone use, and socioeconomic characteristics (Table 3).

For participants randomized to the usual care control arm, hearing results will be reviewed with the participant and provided to their primary care provider, who submits a referral to the audiology clinic. The research team will monitor the electronic health record (EHR) to confirm placement of the referral.

Participants randomized to the intervention arm will receive a personalized counseling session on hearing rehabilitation. The research assistant will provide education on hearing loss, communication strategies, and hearing conservation (see Additional file 1). Each participant will be offered selections from a menu of alternative communication tailored to their needs, with sessions lasting between 10 and 60 min, depending on participant need and interest. Counseling will be available in English or Spanish, with all intervention materials also provided in Spanish.

Smartphone accessibility tools can be demonstrated with participants’ personal devices, though smartphone ownership is not required. If of interest, these features will still be demonstrated by the research team on devices dedicated for this purpose. Written tutorials will be provided for later reference. Accessibility features on Apple smartphones to be demonstrated include Live Caption, Sound Recognition, Real Time Text (RTT), Subtitles and Close Captions, Live Listen, and Notification Settings (LED Flash) (see Additional file 2). Accessibility features on Android smartphones to be demonstrated include Live Transcription and Notification, Live Caption, RTT/TTY, and Sound Amplifier (see Additional file 3). Participants will be guided through enabling these features and given hands-on practice with the research coordinator. Additionally, participants will be offered a demonstration of a personal sound amplifier (SuperEar SE5000) and a tutorial on proper setup and use if they wish to take the device home (Table 4).

Follow-up

At enrollment, participants will be asked the preferred day time and method of contact, along with any alternate contact information (e.g., care partner phone number, cell phone). Phone numbers provided will be cross-checked with EHR records for accuracy.

One week after enrollment, all participants will receive a follow-up phone call to address any questions or concerns regarding their audiology referral, counseling session, or provided resources. This follow-up reinforces key strategies, clarifies any uncertainties, troubleshoots equipment issues, and ensures participants remain engaged with the intervention.

At 3 months—the study’s designated endpoint—all participants will complete a comprehensive follow-up assessment. While the primary endpoints of the current study relate to the feasibility of conducting a larger trial, participants will be queried for any actions taken as a result of their participation in this trial, such as whether the audiological exam was scheduled and, if so, if it was completed by the 3-month follow-up time point. For participants randomized to the intervention, we will also inquire about the use of smart phone accessibility features or an assistive listening device as reviewed during the counseling session in the subsequent 3 months. We will also repeat the HHI-S, De Jong Gierveld Loneliness Questionnaire, and PHQ-9 measures. We will also query whether any rehabilitative action was taken since joining the study. Participants in the intervention arm of the study also complete the International Outcomes Inventory for Alternative Interventions [41] (IOI-AI) to assess their experiences with the counseling session and with any resources taken home.

Three months was chosen as the follow-up period to allow adequate time for participants to pursue audiology referrals and potential hearing aid fitting, while minimizing the impact of comorbid conditions that could dampen the effect of the intervention. At this time, we also review EHR records to document completed audiology appointments, recommended treatments, and any patient actions (e.g., hearing aid acquisition). Participants from both arms will provide feedback on barriers and facilitators to implementation, and audiology referral completion rates are assessed using EHR data in both study arms as a pragmatic measure of intervention efficacy. Participants will receive a $25 incentive upon completing the 3-month follow-up.

To further explore implementation feasibility qualitatively, we will also conduct key informant interviews with clinic staff (including nurses, physicians, and administrative personnel). We will use purposive sampling, ensuring a range of provider and clinical roles and experience to understand the acceptability of the hearing program in primary care and continue sampling until data saturation occurs. Staff will be informed that the study is focused on hearing impairment and is to evaluate the utility of counseling about alternative options for hearing rehabilitation in primary care settings. Additionally, we will conduct semi-structured interviews with participants at the 3-month follow-up to gather qualitative insights into the effectiveness of alternative approaches to hearing rehabilitation. If patient participants provide verbal consent for recording, they will receive an additional $25 for their participation. With permission, conversations will be audio recorded, de-identified during transcription, and deleted afterwards. We will use content analysis to analyze the data and understand the barriers and facilitators to acceptance. Since the intervention is guided by the Stages of Change model, we will also include codes that reflect new practice and can be used to elicit perspectives from providers on how the program and intervention are acceptable and useful within the framework.

Data analysis

Feasibility

We plan to assess the feasibility both of the proposed screening and rehabilitation program (intervention) integrated into the primary care setting as well as the feasibility of conducting a future larger efficacy trial of the intervention. We will assess the feasibility of the hearing screening using the following measures: number of patients approached, number of patients willing to be screened, and number of patients screened. For the feasibility of a future efficacy trial, we will also measure the proportion of screened who are eligible to participate, the number of patients enrolled, the time required to screen, the time required for counseling, number of patient refusals, number of dropouts, and retention at each follow-up time point. Frequencies, proportions, rates, means/medians, standard deviations, and other measures of variability will be used to summarize feasibility outcomes.

To evaluate feasibility thresholds, we will establish targets for each outcome and calculate point estimates with 95% confidence. A target will be considered met if the lower bound of the confidence interval falls within 10 percentage points of the target (e.g., exceeding 40% for a target of 50%). These quantitative data will be integrated with the qualitative interviews of key staff and patient participants.

Fidelity

The screening procedures and study intervention will be delivered by a research assistant, who ensures intervention fidelity, consistency in the referral processes, and adherence to follow-up protocols.

Data and safety monitoring plan

The Principal Investigator (PI) will oversee study safety, including adverse event monitoring, protocol adherence, and data integrity. A site-specific Data and Safety Monitoring Plan (DSMP) will be implemented to ensure compliance with ethical and regulatory standards.

Adverse events will be systematically monitored and reported in accordance with Institutional Review Board (IRB) requirements. Any serious adverse events related to study participation will be documented and reported promptly to the IRB and, if required, to the study sponsor or regulatory bodies.

To protect participant confidentiality, no identifying information will be collected during screening. Only HHI-S results and 4F-PTA data will be recorded on a tablet and securely stored in REDCap. All data will be de-identified before analysis.

Feasibility endpoints, including completion of counseling and audiology referrals, will be monitored throughout the study. Electronic health record (EHR) data will be used to assess whether participants who received the counseling intervention in addition to the audiology referral are more likely than those in the control arm to complete the referral. EHR data will also be used to track whether hearing rehabilitation was recommended and dispensed by the audiologist.

All protocol modifications will be submitted for IRB review and approval prior to implementation. The research team will ensure compliance with study procedures and data collection protocols through regular oversight.

Discussion

Interventions for sensory disabilities, including age-related hearing loss, are increasingly recognized as a modifiable strategy for promoting healthy aging [16, 42]. While the World Health Organization (WHO) endorses hearing screening after age 50 using both subjective and objective components, the US Preventative Task Force [16] does not currently endorse routine screening due to insufficient evidence of its impact. Prior hearing screening studies in primary care settings have typically referred individuals with hearing loss to audiology for formal diagnostic testing and treatment recommendations [19, 36]. However, these referrals often fail to address significant barriers to care, including limited access to audiology services, the burden of additional specialty visits, and the cost and acceptability of rehabilitation options. As a result, many participants in past studies did not follow through with recommended care, even when research teams facilitated follow-up appointments [19]. These challenges are particularly pronounced in underserved communities, where other pressing health concerns often take priority.

The recently enacted Over the Counter Hearing Aid Act of 2017 aims to improve access to hearing devices by allowing direct consumer purchase of hearing devices at a reduced cost and without requiring audiology specialty care [43]. However, the affordability of these devices remains a concern, and technological challenges may limit their successful adoption. Alternative low-cost solutions, such as personal amplifiers and smartphone-based accessibility features, may offer more practical and accessible options. According to the American Association of Retired Persons (AARP), smartphone adoption is on the rise: 81% of Americans age 60–69, and 62% of those 70 and older own smartphones [44]. The availability of devices across various price points may help reduce the stigma associated with hearing rehabilitation and require less manual dexterity than hearing aids—an important consideration for some older adults. Understanding patients’ preferences and receptiveness to these options is essential for improving hearing accessibility.

Our pilot study introduces a novel approach to hearing loss identification by integrating tailored rehabilitation strategies within primary care using a patient-centered communication counseling model designed for underserved older adults who might otherwise lack access to hearing rehabilitation. This feasibility trial will assess the practicality and acceptability of the intervention, as well as the feasibility of conducting a study in this primary care setting using mixed methods. These findings will inform a future study evaluating the intervention’s impact on improving access to hearing healthcare.
