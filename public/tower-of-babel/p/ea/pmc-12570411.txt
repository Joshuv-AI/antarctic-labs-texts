Global and regional burden of kidney cancer from 1990 to 2021: analysis and future predictions based on global burden of disease study
Authors: Deng Li, Haochen Zhao, Siqi Fan, Jingxing Bai, Linfen Guo, Jia You, Wei Li, Xuewen Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe incidence and mortality of kidney cancer vary widely globally. This study aims to examine the global burden and trends of kidney cancer from 1990 to 2021 and its associations with various factors.MethodsData on kidney cancer cases and age-standardized rates (ASRs) of incidence, prevalence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) from 1990 to 2021 were collected and analyzed globally. The age-period-cohort (APC) and Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) models were used to track disease burden dynamics and predict future trends.ResultsGlobally, the incidence of kidney cancer increased from 1.6 × 105 in 1990 to 3.9 × 105 in 2021. China had the most cases, deaths and DALYs in 2021, followed by the United States. Cabo Verde had the fastest growth in ASRs, whereas Southern Latin America had the highest ASRs. Forty-nine countries or regions, mainly in Western Europe and North America, had a decreasing trend in ASR for mortality over the period. Overall, the burden of kidney cancer increased with a higher sociodemographic index (SDI). The APC and BAPC models predicted a steady decline in the ASRs for incidence, prevalence, mortality and DALYs for kidney cancer over the next 20 years. High body mass index (BMI) and smoking were major risk factors, with high BMI being prominent in low-SDI countries.ConclusionsFrom 1990 to 2021, the global burden of kidney cancer represented a significant health threat. Policies to reduce exposure to risk factors could be beneficial amid global aging trends.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15130-8.

Introduction

Kidney cancer is one of the most prevalent malignant neoplasms of the genitourinary system and has a poor prognosis, ranking as the sixth and ninth most common malignant tumor in adults in men and women, respectively [1, 2]. Clear cell renal cell carcinoma represents the majority of primary kidney malignancies, accounting for approximately 80% of cases [3]. In 2019, there were 3.9 × 105 cases of kidney cancer worldwide, with a notably elevated age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) observed in both males and females [4]. In particular, the incidence of kidney cancer is approximately twice as high in males as in females [5]. A review of data from 1996 to 2004 indicated that kidney cancer had the highest mortality rate among all genitourinary cancers in the United States; however, there has been an overall trend toward a decrease in the mortality rate of kidney cancer since the mid-1990s [6]. Similarly, mortality rates for kidney cancer in European countries have also stabilized or declined since the late 1980 s or early 1990 s [7]. This downward trend may be attributed to better accessibility to healthcare, increased rates of early diagnosis, and advanced treatments [8, 9]. In addition, the National Cancer Institute reported that the five-year survival rate for patients with kidney cancer has reached a high level in the United States, with an overall five-year survival rate of approximately 78% [10]. Notably, patients with early-stage kidney cancer have higher survival rates. In Asia, the prevalence of kidney cancer is increasing. A study of the cancer burden revealed a 317.2% increase in the incidence of kidney cancer in Asian countries from 1990 to 2019 [11]. These findings indicate that Asian countries are facing significant challenges in preventing and treating kidney cancer. The wide global variation in incidence rates suggests that exogenous factors and geographic differences play important roles in genetic risk [12].

Kidney cancer in the early stages lacks typical symptoms, and the majority of patients are already in the middle to late stages of the disease at the time of diagnosis [12]. This results in a relatively low overall survival rate for patients with kidney cancer [13]. However, with the continuous advancement of medical technology, notable advancements have been made in the treatment of kidney cancer. The application of targeted drugs and immune checkpoint inhibitors significantly improved the prognosis and prolonged the survival of patients with advanced kidney cancer [14]. For example, treatment regimens such as Cabometyx in combination with Opdivo and Keytruda in combination with Lenvima have been approved by the FDA for the first-line treatment of advanced kidney cell carcinoma and have demonstrated remarkable efficacy [15, 16]. In addition, certain lifestyle-related risk factors, such as obesity and tobacco use, have been identified as contributing factors to an increased risk of kidney cancer [17, 18].

Globally, there is considerable variability in both the incidence and mortality rates of kidney cancer, with less developed countries exhibiting the highest mortality-to-incidence ratios (MIR) [19]. Even within the same country, the disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) associated with kidney cancer are observed to be greater in low-income groups than in high-income groups [20]. In the United States, trends indicate that individuals with the lowest education levels, which serve as a proxy for socioeconomic status and exhibit a cancer death rate that is 2.6 times greater than that of those with the highest education levels [21]. This focus is driven by the understanding that sociodemographic index (SDI) levels, including indicators such as income, education, and fertility, play a pivotal role in shaping the health outcomes of a population [22]. The objective of this study was to analyze the burden of kidney cancer across SDI regions to gain insight into the influence of socioeconomic factors on disease trends. This analysis may prove valuable for policymakers in the allocation of healthcare resources.

Despite the considerable global health threat posed by kidney cancer, a comprehensive evaluation of its incidence, mortality, and DALYs has been lacking until recently. By employing data from the GBD 2021 database, we estimated the global, regional, and national burdens of kidney cancer from 1990 to 2021. This updated dataset allows us to offer more accurate and timely projections of kidney cancer trends, thereby addressing a deficiency in previous studies that employed data from an earlier period. The age-period-cohort (APC) and Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) models were used to forecast future trends in incidence and mortality, offering unique insights into the dynamic changes in kidney cancer burden. These findings have the potential to inform the development of global health policy, particularly with respect to targeted interventions and resource allocation strategies for different SDI regions.

Several analyses have recently leveraged the GBD 2021 platform to examine the epidemiology of kidney cancer. Han et al. quantified the smoking-attributable burden across 204 countries and 21 regions, documenting pronounced male–female disparities and SDI-related gradients, but their scope was limited to a single risk factor [23]. Zhou et al. focused on adults aged ≥ 65 years and showed that age-standardized rates rose with SDI and that East Asia experienced the fastest increase among older adults; BAPC projections suggested a decline in the older population by 2040, and high BMI together with smoking were identified as the leading modifiable drivers, with trichloroethylene contributing minimally [24]. In addition, Qiao et al. contrasted China with the rest of the world, using the (1-MIR) as a survival proxy and inequality indices to characterize disparities, and reported rising incidence and mortality in China despite global improvement in (1-MIR) [25]. Building on these GBD-based reports, the present study adds value by providing an age-inclusive, global–regional–national portrait of incidence, prevalence, mortality and DALYs from 1990 to 2021; by jointly applying classical APC and Bayesian APC models to forecast trends over the next two decades; and by offering SDI-stratified estimates of risk-factor–attributable burden, thereby enabling cross-country comparisons and policy-relevant targeting.

Preliminary results of this work were presented at the 2025 ASCO Genitourinary Cancers Symposium [26].

Methods

Data source

The Global Burden of Disease 2021 (GBD 2021) database, published by the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx), served as the primary source of data for this study. This database contains comprehensive information on the global burden of 371 diseases and injuries, including kidney cancer, across a range of geographic regions and 204 countries and territories. From this resource, data on the incidence, prevalence, mortality, DALYs, and corresponding age-standardized rates (ASRs) of kidney cancer were extracted.

The GBD framework integrates data from multiple sources including cancer registries, vital registration systems, household surveys, censuses, verbal autopsies, and administrative databases. It employs standardized statistical models such as the Cause of Death Ensemble model (CODEm) for mortality estimation and DisMod-MR 2.1 for non-fatal outcomes in order to harmonize these disparate inputs and ensure internal consistency across incidence, prevalence, and mortality metrics [27]. Uncertainty intervals (UIs) are derived from 1,000 posterior draws, enabling quantification of estimation uncertainty, particularly in data-sparse settings. It is important to acknowledge that registry coverage and data reliability vary considerably across regions. Countries with high SDI generally maintain comprehensive cancer surveillance systems, whereas many low- and middle-SDI countries rely on sparse or indirect data sources. As a result, estimates for these regions tend to have wider uncertainty intervals, reflecting greater statistical uncertainty in these settings [28].

Statistical analyses

First, we used multiple statistical models and analytical techniques to evaluate the burden of kidney cancer. The principal metrics analyzed were the numbers of incidence cases, mortality, and DALYs. To account for variations in population size and age distribution, ASIRs, age-standardized prevalence rates (ASPRs), age-standardized mortality rates (ASMRs), and age-standardized DALY rates (ASDRs) were calculated. These standardized rates enable comparisons across different populations and over time. Subgroup analyses were then conducted on the basis of sex, country, region, and SDI to investigate the current status and time trends from 1990 to 2021.

Descriptive time trends in disease burden were analyzed using joinpoint regression to estimate annual percent changes. The estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) was subsequently employed to monitor the evolving disease burden. This was calculated via the following formula: EAPC = 100*(exp(β) − 1), where β represents the slope coefficient derived from a linear regression model that fits the natural logarithm of the ASRs over time. The EAPCs and their 95% confidence intervals were used to determine whether the trends were increasing, decreasing, or stable.

Furthermore, both APC and BAPC models were employed for projections. These models incorporate age, period, and cohort dimensions to generate forecasts of incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALY rates. The model-generated age- and cohort-specific estimates were aggregated and age-standardized to produce the overall projections. However, the APC model is vulnerable to identifiability issues due to multicollinearity among age, period, and cohort. To address this limitation, we employed the BAPC model, which uses Bayesian inference to provide stable estimates by incorporating prior information [29]. Both models can be used to forecast future trends in the burden of kidney cancer, given their capacity to accommodate intricate temporal and demographic variations. Finally, we analyzed kidney cancer mortality attributable to major risk factors across SDI subgroups. Risk-factor attribution was based on the GBD 2021 comparative risk assessment (CRA) framework. Briefly, the CRA identifies risk–outcome pairs supported by sufficient evidence, estimates exposure distributions by age, sex, location, and year, specifies relative-risk functions, defines the theoretical minimum risk exposure level (TMREL), and then computes population-attributable fractions (PAFs) and the corresponding attributable deaths and DALYs [24]. For kidney cancer, the GBD 2021 CRA provides estimates of burden attributable to high body-mass index, tobacco smoking, and occupational exposure to trichloroethylene. We extracted annual attributable estimates for these three risk factors from 1990 to 2021 directly from the GBD results.

R software (version 3.6.0) was used for data visualization. World maps and other graphical representations were created to highlight the distribution and trends of kidney cancer burden across different countries and regions.

Results

Burden of kidney cancer in 204 countries or territories

Table 1 shows the ASRs and absolute numbers of kidney cancer incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs in 2021, along with the temporal trends in EAPC values from 1990 to 2021 across 204 countries or territories. Globally, the incidence of kidney cancer has risen sharply from 1.6 × 105 in 1990 to 3.9 × 105 in 2021. Concurrently, the number of deaths increased from 7.7 × 104 to 16 × 104, and the number of kidney cancer-related DALYs rose from 2.3 × 106 to 4.0 × 106. The ASRs for all measures of kidney cancer burden were higher for men than for women. Specifically, China had the highest number of incident cases (65,799) and DALYs (663,805) in 2021. The United States had the second highest number of incidence cases after China (64,574) but had a higher prevalence of cases than China did (376,137 vs. 344,280). In 2021, the highest ASRs for both incidence (15.60) and prevalence (89.01) were observed in Argentina, whereas the lowest ASIR was in Côte d’Ivoire (0.47). The number of deaths was markedly greater in China (24,867), followed by the United States (18,026). In 2021, Uruguay had the highest ASMR (6.47) and ASDR (170.16) (see Additional files 1 ~ 5).

Burden of kidney cancer in different GBD regions

In terms of geographical distribution, Western Europe had the highest number of incidence cases (81,903) and mortality cases (32,575) among all GBD regions in 2021. High-income North America had the most prevalent cases (412,186), and East Asia had the most DALYs (699,887) (see Additional files 2 ~ 5). The highest ASIR (13.44), ASPR (73.93), ASMR (4.55), and ASDR (120.33) were observed in Southern Latin America, whereas Oceania presented the lowest ASIR (0.64) and ASMR (0.41). In general, almost all GBD regions had growing ASIRs and ASPRs, whereas East Asia had the fastest growing ASIR (EAPC: 2.4, 95% CI 2.22–2.59) and ASPR (EAPC: 3.42, 95% CI 3.2–3.64). The fastest growing ASMR was observed in Southern Sub-Saharan Africa (EAPC: 1.41, 95% CI 1.23–1.58). Conversely, some economically developed regions, including High-income North America, Australasia, and Western Europe, had declining ASMRs. Southern Sub-Saharan Africa has the fastest growing ASDR (EAPC: 1.28, 95% CI 1.13–1.44), whereas high-income North America has the fastest declining ASDR (EAPC: −0.81, 95% CI −0.93–0.68) among the six GBD regions with declining ASDRs (Table 1).

Burden of kidney cancer in regions with different SDIs

As SDI values decreased, the ASRs and absolute numbers of incidence, prevalence, mortality and DALYs in 2021 showed a clear downward trend (Fig. 2). From 1990 to 2021, middle-SDI and low-middle-SDI countries had the fastest growing burden of kidney cancer, with incidence and prevalence increasing fastest in middle-SDI countries, and mortality and DALYs increasing fastest in low-middle-SDI countries. Notably, high-SDI countries had negative EAPCs for mortality (EAPC: −0.33, 95% CI −0.42–0.24) and DALYs (EAPC: −0.62, 95% CI −0.72–0.51), followed by high-middle-SDI countries. This indicates a downward trend in mortality and DALYs in these countries (Table 2).

APC and BAPC models for predicting trends in kidney cancer incidence from 2022 to 2046

The APC (Fig. 3) and BAPC (Fig. 4) models were employed to project the future global burden of kidney cancer, covering absolute numbers and ASRs for incidence, prevalence, mortality and DALYs up to 2046. The negative slope of the curve indicates a projected downward trend in the future. Both models demonstrated that over the next few decades, the age-standardized incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs will gradually decrease in both men and women. However, while the APC model projected that continued increases in incidence, prevalence, mortality and DALYs will continue to increase in the future, the BAPC model indicated that the above indicators will either increase slightly or remain stable in men and even decrease moderately in women (see Additional files 6 ~ 7).

Risk factors associated with kidney cancer

In countries with varying SDI values, high BMI, smoking, and occupational exposure to trichloroethylene were identified as the primary attributable risk factors for kidney cancer mortality and DALYs. Among the identified risk factors, high BMI and smoking were the primary causes of kidney cancer-related mortality and DALYs. Conversely, occupational carcinogen exposure to trichloroethylene especially in industrializing economies was found to contribute a negligible proportion to these outcomes. High BMI was particularly prominent in low-SDI countries (Fig. 5).

Discussion

In this study, we analyzed the burden of kidney cancer and associated risk factors globally and in different countries and GBD regions between 1990 and 2021, building upon and updating the findings of previous studies in this field. The results revealed a notable increase in the global burden of kidney cancer in 2021, particularly in developing countries, which presents a significant challenge to global health.

In 2021, cancers accounted for approximately 14.6% of global deaths and 8.8% of all DALYs [30]. Although ranking only sixth among men and ninth among women in incidence, kidney cancer contributes disproportionately to mortality and disability. The ASMR for all cancers in 2021 was 125.31 per 100,000, whereas kidney cancer accounted for 2.04 per 100,000, corresponding to approximately 1.6% of cancer deaths. Likewise, the ASDR was 3209.97 per 100,000 for all cancers and 50.90 per 100,000 for kidney cancer, also representing about 1.6% of the total cancer-related DALYs. These figures reflect kidney cancer’s relatively poor prognosis, with a MIR of 0.42 [19]. Substantial geographic and socioeconomic disparities further compound this burden, as lower-SDI regions tend to exhibit higher MIRs. Taken together, these findings emphasize that, despite its moderate incidence ranking, kidney cancer constitutes a significant component of the global cancer burden.

In some high-income developed countries, such as the United States, Western European countries, and Australia, mortality rates remain high but have recently begun to decline from their historical peaks. In contrast, the incidence of kidney cancer is relatively low in Africa and most Asian countries but has increased rapidly in recent decades. This finding is consistent with that of a previous cancer epidemiology study [9]. There is considerable variation in the incidence and mortality rates of kidney cancer worldwide, with the highest mortality-to-incidence ratios observed in less developed countries. Previous studies have indicated that Uruguay had the highest ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR in 2017 [4] and the highest ASDR in 2019 [31]. Nevertheless, our study, on the basis of the most recent data, revealed that, in 2021, Argentina had the highest ASIR, whereas Uruguay still had the highest ASMR and ASDR. These findings merit further investigation, as they suggest significant discrepancies in healthcare quality, accessibility, treatment options, and diagnostic procedures between developed and developing countries [22]. Further research is needed to determine whether the mortality-to-incidence ratio decreases as development progresses.

Our study also revealed a significant correlation between kidney cancer burden and SDI. The burden of kidney cancer was greater in high-SDI countries than in low-SDI regions, including North Africa and the Middle East. These findings suggest that socioeconomic development may contribute to the burden of kidney cancer but is not the only factor involved. Other potential factors, such as environmental factors, lifestyle, and genomics, may also play a role [32, 33]. In the context of global aging, the increasing burden of kidney diseases is likely to continue in the future [34]. The elderly population has a greater risk of developing kidney cancer as well as chronic kidney disease, which is also associated with a substantial economic burden globally [35–37]. As an increasing number of countries face an aging population, ensuring high-quality medical services for the elderly is crucial. In addition, projections of the future burden of kidney cancer derived from both APC and BAPC models suggest that although ASRs for incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs are likely to decline gradually over the coming decades, the continued increase in absolute numbers will exert a non-negligible impact on global public health. This underscores that, despite the anticipated decline in incidence rates, prevention and management of kidney cancer remain crucial. Notably, the consistency between APC and BAPC projections supports the robustness of these forecasts, while the Bayesian approach provided smoother and more stable long-term trajectories, particularly for countries with sparse data.

Finally, this study identified high BMI, smoking, and occupational exposure to trichloroethylene as major risk factors for renal cancer deaths and DALYs. It aligns with previous reports indicating rising global kidney cancer incidence, particularly in high SDI regions, likely linked to improved diagnostics and lifestyle factors, emphasizing the importance of weight control and smoking cessation for the prevention of kidney cancer [20, 38, 39]. The mechanisms by which high BMI contributes to the development of kidney cancer include insulin resistance, inflammation, and the presence of excess adipose tissue [40]. In 2021, 20.07% of kidney cancer deaths were attributable to high BMI on a global scale [39]. Notably, high BMI is particularly pronounced in low-SDI countries, suggesting that these countries should prioritize the prevention and control of obesity and its associated diseases. A previous study revealed that the number of kidney cancer deaths associated with high BMI has been increasing in developing countries [41]. Another study suggested that this trend has remained relatively stable in recent years among adults in China but has gradually declined in the United States [42]. Another significant risk factor for mortality from kidney cancer is smoking. Nicotine has been demonstrated to increase the mRNA and protein levels of UTX and decrease H3K27me3, thereby potentially activating the expression of specific genes and contributing to kidney cancer [43]. Additionally, nicotine-induced tumorigenicity promotes the progression of kidney cancer via AIMP2-DX2 [44]. The considerable disparity in smoking prevalence between men and women in certain regions may further contribute to the observed sex-based variations in kidney cancer incidence [45]. Therefore, emphasizing a healthy lifestyle for the public will help reduce the burden of kidney cancer in the future. In examining the regional disparities in kidney cancer incidence, it is imperative to consider factors beyond high BMI and smoking, particularly in low-SDI regions where the burden has significantly increased. Genetic predispositions and lifestyle habits, including hypertension and alcohol consumption, may contribute to these disparities [46]. Chronic kidney disease and end-stage kidney disease, which are more prevalent in developing countries, have also been identified as risk factors for kidney cancer [47]. Comprehensive investigations into environmental toxins, genetic variations, and societal behaviors could provide deeper insights into the complex interplay of factors influencing the incidence and mortality of kidney cancer across different SDI levels.

Our findings are broadly consistent with prior GBD-based assessments in showing male predominance and higher burdens in high-SDI settings, while mortality has begun to decline in several high-income regions. Compared with analyses limited to smoking, our SDI-stratified estimates advance the evidence base by delineating the joint contributions of high BMI and smoking, thereby identifying dual prevention targets across varying stages of socioeconomic development. Moreover, our age-inclusive estimates place the trends among those aged ≥ 65 years in the context of the whole population and reveal additional heterogeneity at national level—for example, Cabo Verde exhibited the fastest growth across ASMR, ASPR and ASDR since 1990 whereas Southern Latin America had the highest ASRs in 2021. Finally, our integrated APC/BAPC framework delivers transparent, age-structured projections through 2046, providing actionable insights for healthcare capacity planning and prioritization of prevention strategies across SDI settings. At the same time, the inequality perspective introduced by the China-focused analysis offers a complementary lens that our burden-and-risk-oriented forecasts can be integrated with in future work [25].

However, it is essential to acknowledge the constraints of the data employed in this analysis. The GBD 2021 database, while comprehensive, may underestimate the true burden of kidney cancer in certain developing countries because of insufficient diagnosis or low enrollment, which could impact the accuracy of the data. The accuracy of our predictions may be affected by data limitations, particularly in regions with constrained healthcare resources. To address this issue, we have interpreted our findings in light of these limitations and emphasized the necessity for ongoing efforts to improve data quality and enhance surveillance. The disparate burden of kidney cancer across different SDI regions requires independent predictions for each region. Further research involving more detailed data and region-specific models would be beneficial. Finally, we note that not all etiologic factors for kidney cancer are represented in GBD 2021’s modeled risk set (for example, hypertension, chronic kidney disease and certain genetic predispositions are relevant but were not included as attributable risk-outcome pairs in GBD 2021); these omissions may lead to underestimation of the full modifiable burden in some settings.

Based on the above findings, we suggest that governments and health organizations collaborate to develop country-specific strategies. High-SDI countries can prioritize early detection and advanced treatment, whereas low-SDI countries focus on building healthcare infrastructure. Moreover, the impact of risk factors varies across different regions, demonstrating the necessity for differentiated interventions. In high-SDI regions, smoking cessation and obesity prevention campaigns may prove effective, whereas in low-SDI regions, a multifaceted approach is required to combat prevalent risk factors. Continuous monitoring of epidemiologic trends and changes in renal cancer risk factors provides a scientific foundation for policy formulation, contributing to global health equity in genitourinary cancers.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive assessment of the global and regional burden of kidney cancer from 1990 to 2021. Our findings reveal a significant increase in the incidence, mortality, and DALYs associated with kidney cancer globally, particularly in developing countries. A notable reduction in the burden of kidney cancer was accompanied by a decrease in the SDI values. The identification of high BMI and smoking as key risk factors highlights the importance of targeted interventions aimed at modifying these modifiable behaviors. This study underscores the urgent need for global health policies that address the rising kidney cancer burden, particularly in view of the global aging trend and the increasing prevalence of modifiable risk factors. Our findings contribute to a deeper understanding of kidney cancer epidemiology and offer insights for developing effective strategies to mitigate this health threat worldwide.
