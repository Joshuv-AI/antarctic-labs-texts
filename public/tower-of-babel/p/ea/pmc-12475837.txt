Dietary exposure to lambda-cyhalothrin induces intestinal damage in chickens via oxidative stress and gut microbiota dysbiosis☆
Authors: Wen Zhang, Qiaochu Sun, Huayu Zhang, Tianning Luo, Yixin Zhang, Fucheng Wang, Haoran Zhang, Runxiang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Lambda-cyhalothrin (LCT), a widely used pyrethroid insecticide for ectoparasite control in poultry and sanitation pest management, poses health risks to humans and animals despite its broad application. While LCT’s intestinal toxicity has been underexplored, this study innovatively highlights dose-dependent differences in its effects through a novel chicken exposure model. Employing histological analysis, gene expression profiling, oxidative stress assays, and 16S rRNA sequencing, we systematically assessed varying LCT doses on chicken intestines. Results demonstrated pronounced dose-dependent impacts on the jejunum: LCT caused irregular, shortened, fractured, and fused villi; reduced goblet cells; induced inflammation; and downregulated barrier genes (Occludin, Claudin-1, Zonula Occludens-1 (ZO-1)). Notably, low-dose LCT activated the antioxidant system without significant oxidative stress, enabling partial mitigation of damage through adaptive responses. In contrast, high-dose LCT elevated reactive oxygen species, disrupted antioxidant balance, altered gut microbiota structure, decreased the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio, and reduced microbial richness, leading to severe dysbiosis and intestinal injury. This first comprehensive evaluation of LCT’s dose-specific mechanisms—revealing adaptive resilience at low doses versus profound disruption at high doses—provides critical insights for safer pyrethroid use and mitigating poultry health risks.

Introduction

Pyrethroid insecticides, known for their low toxicity, broad-spectrum efficacy, and high efficiency, have largely replaced organophosphorus, organochlorine, and carbamate pesticides, becoming the dominant insecticides in use (Elser et al., 2022). As a Type II pyrethroid, lambda-cyhalothrin (LCT) is frequently employed to control ectoparasites in poultry and manage sanitation pests by disrupting the nervous system of insects, leading to paralysis and death. LCT formulations are typically diluted in water and sprayed onto poultry houses, feathers, and skin surfaces (Pan et al., 2009). However, sprayed liquids can easily disperse to the ground, cages, or feed, leading to unintended ingestion by poultry. This not only results in wastage of the insecticide but also poses potential risks to poultry health and the farming environment. The widespread use of LCT has also raised concerns about environmental contamination. Studies have shown that LCT residues persist in soil, water, and agricultural products for extended periods (Alaoui et al., 2024). High concentrations of LCT have been detected in soil, surface water, and agricultural product surfaces in regions such as Kenya, Togo and Spain (Chaka et al., 2023; Fernandez-Alvarez et al., 2010; Mawussi et al., 2014). These contaminants not only threaten ecosystems but also pose potential risks to human and animal health.

The intestine, as a digestive organ, is responsible for nutrient and water absorption, as well as the elimination of waste and harmful substances. As the first barrier to orally ingested exogenous substances, the intestine is also the primary target of their adverse effects (Zhang et al., 2022). Pyrethroid insecticides exhibit certain intestinal toxicity. Studies have shown that deltamethrin induces intestinal damage in various animals, causing inflammatory infiltration, degeneration, necrosis, and goblet cell hyperplasia in fruit bats, leading to epithelial shedding of intestinal villi in laying hens, altering intestinal wall thickness, villus height, and crypt depth, and accumulating in the mouse colon (Liu et al., 2022; Ma et al., 2023; Oliveira et al., 2022). Fenpropathrin (FEN) damages the intestinal barrier, exacerbates the effects of harmful bacterial toxins, and induces inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis (Xiu et al., 2024). Bifenthrin increases reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and inflammatory gene expression in the intestines of zebrafish embryos, thereby elevating embryo mortality (Park et al., 2020). Cypermethrin impairs intestinal barrier function and reduces the expression of tight junction proteins (Zhao et al., 2023). LCT significantly increases micronucleus formation in colon crypts, induces oxidative stress and inflammation in the zebrafish intestine, and affects intestinal immunity (Celik et al., 2005; Zhao et al., 2024). Additionally, studies have demonstrated that both deltamethrin and LCT induce apoptosis in Caco-2 cells (Ilboudo et al., 2014). Given the frequent use of LCT for ectoparasite control and pest prevention in poultry, understanding its impact on poultry intestines is critical for ensuring animal health and maintaining economic benefits. However, little is known about LCT’s effects on the chicken intestine.

The intestinal barrier plays a vital role in maintaining gut and systemic health by protecting the body from harmful substances and pathogens through physical, chemical, immune, and microbial defense systems (Kayama et al., 2020). Tight junction proteins, composed of transmembrane proteins (e.g., Occludin, Claudin family) and intracellular adaptor proteins (e.g., ZO family), are critical functional components of the physical barrier. When the expression or function of tight junction proteins is disrupted, intestinal permeability increases, widening intercellular gaps and allowing harmful substances from the intestinal lumen to penetrate the barrier, thereby inducing intestinal inflammation and immune responses (Kuo et al., 2022). Dextran sulfate sodium, a common inducer of colitis, disrupts intestinal epithelial barrier function, activates immune responses, and promotes the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, leading to colonic mucosal inflammation (Chen et al., 2025). Studies have shown that FEN exposure causes pathological intestinal damage, alters intestinal ultrastructure, elevates levels of Bacteroides fragilis enterotoxin, modifies tight junction content, impairs the intestinal barrier, and induces intestinal inflammation and oxidative stress (Xiu et al., 2024). The gut microbiota barrier, another critical component of the intestinal barrier, is a defense system established by symbiotic gut microbiota through various mechanisms. The gut microbiota barrier, can inhibit pathogen colonization, maintain gut microbial balance, and indirectly enhance physical, chemical, and immune barrier functions. Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota leads to excessive proliferation of pathogenic bacteria, triggering intestinal oxidative stress and inflammation. Furthermore, microbiota dysbiosis can weaken tight junction protein expression, compromise intestinal barrier function, and exacerbate intestinal inflammation and injury (Wang et al., 2024b). Studies have shown that LCT exposure causes gut microbiota dysbiosis in zebrafish, reducing the relative abundance of Fusobacteriota and inducing intestinal damage (Zhao et al., 2024). Chronic deltamethrin exposure increases the abundance of Proteobacteria and Verrucomicrobiota while decreasing Fusobacteriota, leading to altered intestinal physiological parameters and disrupted microbiota function (Wu et al., 2022).

However, the specific mechanisms by which LCT induces intestinal damage in chickens and its impact on the gut microbiota remain poorly understood. This study simulates strategies used in poultry farms to control ectoparasites and sanitation pests by establishing a model of LCT exposure at varying concentrations. By investigating LCT-induced intestinal damage in chickens and its underlying mechanisms, the present study was conducted to shed some light to how the broad-spectrum insecticide LCT impacts intestinal health.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals and treatments

The Animal Care and Use Committee in Northeast Agricultural University approved the experimental protocol, with the project number NEAUEC20210235. The sampling procedures complied with the “Guidelines on Ethical Treatment of Experimental Animals” (2006) No. 398 set by the Ministry of Science and Technology, China. Eighty one-day-old chicks (Hy-Line Pink, Harbin, China) were randomly assigned to four groups: control group, low-dose LCT group (L group, 5 mg/kg/d body weight), medium-dose LCT group (M group, 50 mg/kg/d body weight), and high-dose LCT group (H group, 200 mg/kg/d body weight). LCT doses and administration methods were based on our previous studies (Zhang et al., 2024). In order to better control the chicks' intake of LCT during the experiment, due to the lack of specific LD50 data for chicks, we referred to the ecological risk assessment guidelines of the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) and selected 1/20 of the mallard duck LD50 (3,950 mg/kg) as the highest exposure level (200 mg/kg/d) (ECHA, 2011). Then, 1/4 and 1/40 of the 200 mg/kg/d b.w. were selected as the medium and low doses, respectively, and the low dose was set to approximate lower potential environmental exposure levels. Chicks were housed in environmentally controlled cages under pollution-free conditions with appropriate temperature, humidity, and lighting. Standard feed and water were provided ad libitum, and chicks were exposed (or not exposed) to LCT for 6 weeks. After a 12-hour fasting period, chickens were euthanized, blood samples were collected and centrifuged, and jejunum and cecal contents were harvested. Jejunum tissues were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde or glutaraldehyde, prepared as tissue homogenates, or snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for subsequent experiments.

Histological and ultrastructural observations

Jejunum tissues were examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Fixed tissues were washed with PBS, post-fixed in 1 % osmium tetroxide for 90 minutes, dehydrated in a graded ethanol series, and substituted with ethanol and acetone. Fully dehydrated samples were infiltrated and embedded in resin, sectioned into 50 nm slices using an ultramicrotome, and mounted on copper grids. Sections were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate and observed using a transmission electron microscope (GEM-1200ES, Japan).

For scanning electron microscopy (SEM) of jejunal villi, fixed intestinal tissues were washed with PBS, dehydrated in a graded ethanol series, and substituted with tert‑butanol. Dried tissues were sputter-coated with gold (HITACHI E 1010, Japan) and observed using a scanning electron microscope (SU8010, Hitachi, Japan). All TEM and SEM observations were conducted on samples taken from the mid-jejunum to ensure sampling consistency across groups.

For hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining, jejunum tissue samples were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde, dehydrated in a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned for H&E staining. Sections were observed under a light microscope. Image J software was used to analyze the villus height and crypt depth of the jejunum.

For Alcian Blue (AB) and Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) staining, paraffin sections were dewaxed and rehydrated, treated with Alcian Blue (Servicebio, G1049) for 8 minutes, and rinsed with water. Sections were then treated with 0.5 % periodic acid solution (Servicebio, G1049) for 10 minutes, stained with Schiff’s reagent (Servicebio, G1049) for 25 minutes in the dark, counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, mounted, and observed under a light microscope. For H&E and AB-PAS staining, three sections per sample (n = 3) were randomly selected, and five fields of view were observed and analyzed per section to ensure comprehensive evaluation.

Immunofluorescence analysis

Paraffin sections of jejunum tissue were dewaxed, subjected to antigen retrieval, and incubated with AutoFluo Quencher A solution for 30 minutes to suppress autofluorescence. Sections were treated with 3 % H2O2 for 25 minutes at room temperature, blocked with 3 % BSA for 30 minutes, and incubated with primary antibodies (Occludin, IL-1β) overnight at 4°C. After PBS washing, sections were incubated with secondary antibodies for 50 minutes, followed by TSA-488 staining for 10 minutes in the dark. Sections were counterstained with DAPI, treated with AutoFluo Quencher B solution for 5 minutes, rinsed in running water for 3 minutes, and observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Determination of oxidative stress indices

For ROS staining, jejunum tissue sections were incubated in DHE at 37°C for 40 minutes in the dark, washed with PBS, and counterstained with DAPI for 10 minutes. ROS levels were evaluated using a fluorescence microscope.

Ten percent jejunum tissue homogenates were prepared in saline, and the activities/contents of catalase (CAT), total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD), reduced glutathione (GSH), and malondialdehyde (MDA) in jejunum tissue were measured using kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) to assess antioxidant capacity.

16S rRNA sequencing analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from chicken fecal samples using a commercial DNA extraction kit and stored at −20°C. DNA quality and concentration were assessed via UV-Vis spectrophotometry and agarose gel electrophoresis. The V3-V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified by PCR using primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) with sample-specific barcodes. The PCR reaction (25 µL) included buffer, DNA polymerase, dNTPs, primers, template DNA, and nuclease-free water, with cycling conditions of 98°C for 5 min, 25 cycles of 98°C for 30 s, 53°C for 30 s, 72°C for 45 s, and a final extension at 72°C for 5 min. Amplicons were purified, quantified, pooled, and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform (2 × 250 bp paired-end). Sequences were demultiplexed, merged, and quality-filtered. Chimeras were removed, and denoising was performed to generate feature tables. Relative abundances were normalized using the SILVA (release 132) database. Alpha diversity (Chao1, Faith_pd, Shannon, Observed_species) and beta diversity were analyzed with QIIME2 (version 2024.5). Differential abundance was evaluated using LEfSe (LDA > 2), and taxonomic assignments were made via BLAST against the SILVA database. Statistical significance was denoted as: P < 0.05: *, P < 0.01: **, P < 0.001: ***.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from jejunum tissue using the Trizol method (TaKaRa, Japan) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a commercial kit. qRT-PCR was performed using the Light Cycler® 480 system (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). GAPDH was used as the reference gene, and primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Primer sequences are listed in Table 1. Western blot (WB)

Proteins were extracted from jejunum tissue using lysis buffer with a protease inhibitor (Beyotime, China). Extracts were separated by SDS-PAGE (80 V for 30 min, then 120 V for 60 min) and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5 % skim milk in TBST for 2 h and incubated overnight with primary antibodies targeting Glyceraldehyde-3-Phosphate Dehydrogenase (GAPDH), Occludin, Claudin-1, Nuclear Factor-kappa B (NF-κB), and Interleukin-6 (IL-6). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using a western blot imaging system (G:BOX Chemi XX9, China). GAPDH served as the reference protein, with the control group used for relative quantification. Antibody details are provided in Table S1.

Bioinformatics analysis

Spearman’s correlation coefficient (https://www.omicstudio.cn) was used to analyze the relationships between ROS levels, expression levels of inflammation-related and intestinal barrier-related genes, and the relative abundance of gut microbiota at the genus level. A significance threshold of P < 0.05 was applied, with correlations considered significant only when P < 0.05. Significance levels were denoted as follows: P < 0.05: *, P < 0.01: **, and P < 0.001: ***. This correlation analysis aimed to explore the degree of association between ROS levels, gene expression, and gut microbiota abundance to evaluate their synergistic or interactive effects under experimental conditions.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Prism 9.0.0 software (GraphPad, USA), with results derived from at least three independent experiments. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All data passed normality tests and were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. Different letters indicate significant differences between groups (P < 0.05), while groups with the same letter were considered not significantly different (P > 0.05).

Results

LCT induces jejunum damage in chickens

To evaluate the effects of LCT on the chicken jejunum, jejunal morphology was assessed using H&E staining (Fig. 1A). The control group exhibited normal jejunal villi structure with uniform arrangement and distinct brush borders at the apical surface of epithelial cells. In the LCT-treated groups, villi displayed irregular morphology, with fracturing and fusion (blue arrows). Villi became wider and shorter, some fusing into plate-like structures. The brush border at the villi tips was unclear (yellow arrows), with reduced microvilli, potentially impairing absorption function. Additionally, LCT exposure led to varying degrees of inflammatory cell aggregation and infiltration in the jejunal submucosa (red arrows). Compared with the control group, LCT treatment significantly reduced the villus height of the jejunum in each group (P < 0.05), and increased the crypt depth in the L group (P < 0.05), indicating that low-dose LCT induced compensatory repair effects in the intestine (Fig. 1B, C).Fig. 1Histomorphological observation of jejunum tissue. To further investigate LCT-induced jejunal damage, the morphology of intestinal villi and cells was examined using SEM and TEM. Under SEM (Fig. 1D), the LCT-treated groups showed irregular and rough villi surfaces, with damage severity increasing in a dose-dependent manner. Microvilli appeared deformed, and severely damaged villi exhibited fracturing or fusion, with some villi completely absent or fused into plate-like structures. Under TEM (Fig. 1E), LCT-treated groups displayed discontinuous cell membranes, with unclear boundaries between the cytoplasm and peripheral membranes. Nuclear membranes were irregular, with increased chromatin aggregation indicated by darker nuclear staining areas (red arrows). Cytoplasmic vacuoles (green arrows) were observed, along with swollen mitochondria, disrupted or absent cristae, and altered matrix density (yellow arrows).

Glycoproteins, stained blue, were observed on the mucosal surface and within goblet cells. Immunofluorescence, qRT-PCR, and WB analyses were performed to evaluate the expression of intestinal barrier-related genes. Compared to the control group, the LCT-treated groups exhibited a dose-dependent reduction in Occludin protein fluorescence intensity (Fig. 2E, F). Additionally, mRNA expression levels of Occludin, Claudin-1, and ZO-1 were significantly decreased (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2G), and protein expression levels of Occludin and Claudin-1 were significantly reduced (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2H, I). These findings indicate that LCT induces intestinal barrier damage in the chicken jejunum.

LCT exposure disrupts gut microbiota

The composition and function of the gut microbiota can be modulated by various exogenous substances. We investigated the impact of LCT on the gut microbiota, and the results showed that after 6 weeks of LCT exposure, principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) revealed differences in microbial community structure (Fig. 3A, B). Ellipses of different colors indicated sample clustering within groups, with distinct distributions across treatment groups, suggesting that different LCT doses influenced the microbial community structure. Alpha diversity indices (Chao1, Faith_pd, Shannon, and Observed_species) are shown in Fig. 3C. The Chao1 index in the H group was significantly different from the control group (P = 0.015), indicating a marked difference in microbial richness. Compared to the control and M groups, the Faith_pd index in the H group was significantly reduced, reflecting a notable decline in microbial phylogenetic diversity. The Shannon index showed an increasing trend in the L group compared to the control group, suggesting that low-dose LCT treatment enhanced gut microbial community diversity, whereas high-dose LCT significantly reduced community diversity (P = 0.017). The Observed_species index in the H group was significantly lower than in the control group, indicating that LCT treatment significantly reduced the number of gut microbial species (P = 0.014). LEfSe analysis bar plots identified differentially abundant taxa (Fig. 3D) with an LDA threshold of 2, showing that Ruminococcaceae and Faecalibacterium dominated in the control group. LCT induces cellular damage and oxidative stress in chicken jejunum

DHE probe experiments were conducted on chicken jejunum tissue to assess ROS levels (Fig. 5A), with relative fluorescence intensity of ROS shown in Fig. 5B. The results revealed that, under LCT exposure, the fluorescence intensity in the L group was not significantly different from the control group. However, in the M and H groups, DHE fluorescence intensity in jejunum tissue increased, indicating a significant rise in ROS levels (P < 0.05). Additionally, changes in oxidative stress markers (CAT, GSH, MDA, and T-SOD) were measured, as shown in Fig. 5C, D, E, F. Compared to the control group, the L group showed a trend toward increased CAT activity (P = 0.69), significantly elevated GSH content (P < 0.05), and no significant changes in T-SOD or MDA activity/content (P > 0.05). In the M group, CAT and T-SOD activities were significantly reduced (P < 0.05). In the H group, CAT, GSH, and T-SOD activities/contents were significantly decreased (P < 0.05), while MDA content was significantly increased (P < 0.05). These results suggest that LCT induces jejunal damage in chickens, with the severity of damage increasing in a dose-dependent manner. LCT induces inflammation in chicken jejunum

Histomorphological observations revealed that treatment with LCT triggered the aggregation and infiltration of inflammatory cells in jejunal tissues. To further assess the impact, immunofluorescence assays were conducted to determine the expression level of the inflammatory cytokine IL-1β in the jejunum. As depicted in Fig. 6A and 6B, the intensity of red fluorescence in the LCT-treated groups was significantly higher than that in the control group (P < 0.05). Fig. 6C illustrates the influence of LCT treatment on the mRNA expression of inflammation-associated genes, including NF-κB, Tumor Necrosis Factor-α (TNF-α), Interleukin-1β (IL-1β), IL-6, and Interleukin-10 (IL-10). qRT-PCR results showed that LCT treatment significantly upregulated the mRNA expression of NF-κB, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 (P < 0.05) or caused an upward trend. In contrast, the mRNA expression of IL-10 in the H group was significantly lower compared to the control group (P < 0.05). The effects of LCT treatment on the protein expression of NF-κB and IL-6 (two key inflammation-related genes) are presented in Fig. 6D and 6E. These results indicated that the protein levels of NF-κB and IL-6 in the LCT-treated groups were significantly higher than those in the control group (P < 0.05). Collectively, these results suggest that exposure to LCT induces inflammatory responses in jejunal tissues. Additionally, Fig. 6F displays the Spearman correlation analysis among ROS levels, the expression of intestinal barrier-related and inflammation-related genes, and the relative abundance of gut microbiota at the genus level. Discussion

Pyrethroid insecticides, a class of synthetic chemicals, have increasingly replaced other insecticides to become the mainstream choice, widely used for controlling ectoparasites and sanitation pests in poultry (Pan et al., 2009). However, the potential harm caused by these insecticides to poultry during ectoparasite control cannot be overlooked. This study innovatively established a chicken model of LCT exposure to investigate its detrimental effects on the chicken intestine and the underlying mechanisms, representing the first comprehensive evaluation of LCT's dose-specific mechanisms in poultry intestinal health. The results demonstrate that LCT disrupts the intestinal barrier, induces oxidative stress and inflammatory damage in the jejunum, and exacerbates intestinal injury by causing gut microbiota dysbiosis, revealing stark dose-dependent differences: adaptive resilience and partial mitigation at low doses versus severe disruption at high doses.

Mounting evidence suggests that intestinal health is a critical determinant of overall animal health (Bröer, 2023), with the intestine being particularly sensitive to pyrethroid insecticide exposure (Li et al., 2022; Slevin et al., 2023). Although previous studies have indicated that pyrethroids can induce intestinal damage, reports on the specific effects of LCT on the intestine are scarce. It is well-established that mucosal morphological features, such as mucosal thickness, villus structure, and goblet cell numbers, are key indicators of intestinal barrier function (Li et al., 2025). The expression of tight junction proteins influences mucosal permeability and integrity. Studies have shown that LCT significantly increases micronucleus formation in colon crypts, induces oxidative stress and inflammation in the zebrafish intestine, and affects intestinal immunity (Zhao et al., 2024). Inflammatory responses activate various cellular signaling pathways, such as the NF-κB pathway, leading to the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β), which disrupt tight junction proteins between intestinal epithelial cells through multiple mechanisms, further reducing their gene expression levels (Zeng et al., 2024). Additionally, excessive ROS can directly damage intestinal epithelial cells, impairing their structure and function, leading to downregulated expression of tight junction protein genes and weakened intestinal barrier function. Consistent with these findings, our study shows that LCT exposure results in irregular, shortened, widened, fractured, and fused intestinal villi, reduced mucopolysaccharides and glycoproteins, increased ROS production, enhanced expression of pro-inflammatory genes, and decreased expression of intestinal barrier-related genes (Occludin, Claudin-1, and ZO-1), ultimately causing intestinal damage.

The gut microbiota plays a crucial role in maintaining host health, with its composition and diversity closely linked to intestinal barrier function, immune regulation, and metabolic homeostasis (Chen et al., 2021). This study further explored the effects of LCT exposure on the chicken gut microbiota and its role in intestinal damage. 16S rRNA gene sequencing revealed significant impacts of LCT exposure on the gut microbiota, demonstrating that it exacerbates jejunal damage by inducing microbiota dysbiosis in a dose-dependent manner. PCoA showed distinct microbial community structures between LCT-treated groups and the control group, suggesting that LCT disrupts microbiota homeostasis through direct toxicity or alterations in the intestinal microenvironment. This aligns with previous studies indicating that exogenous chemicals can perturb microbial community structures through direct toxicity or environmental changes (Xie et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023). The α diversity analysis further revealed that high-dose LCT (H group) significantly reduced Chao1, Faith_pd, and Observed_species indices, reflecting a marked decline in microbial richness and phylogenetic diversity. In contrast, low-dose LCT slightly increased the Shannon index, suggesting that low-dose exposure may trigger adaptive changes in the microbiota. Such adaptations may result from the initial stimulation of tolerant microbial groups (e.g., Lactobacillus) or slight alterations in the intestinal microenvironment (e.g., pH or mucus layer secretion), promoting a dynamic balance in microbial metabolism. However, high-dose LCT significantly reduced the Shannon index, indicating disrupted microbial evenness and severe dysbiosis, which further aggravated intestinal damage.

Moreover, LCT exposure significantly altered the relative abundances of Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes at the phylum level. At the family level, Ruminococcaceae abundance progressively decreased with increasing LCT dose, with genera such as Faecalibacterium, key producers of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) like butyrate, being notably affected. SCFAs play critical roles in maintaining intestinal barrier integrity, suppressing inflammation, and providing energy to epithelial cells (Mann et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024a). Additionally, SCFAs and medium-chain fatty acids can improve production traits, meat quality, and gut microbial attributes in poultry, serving as alternatives to antibiotic growth promoters (Szabó et al., 2023). The combined effects of microbially derived cecal SCFAs and host genetics also enhance feed efficiency in broilers by influencing microbiota and lipid metabolism (He et al., 2023). Thus, the reduction in Ruminococcaceae likely exacerbates LCT-induced intestinal damage by lowering SCFA levels. Conversely, the significant enrichment of Bacteroidaceae in the H group may be associated with increased endotoxin production, as lipopolysaccharides (LPS) can activate the TLR4/NF-κB pathway, inducing the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) and further disrupting tight junction protein expression. At the genus level, the significant reduction in Faecalibacterium and Oscillospira likely weakens the intestine’s anti-inflammatory capacity and mucosal protection, as these genera are closely linked to SCFA production and gut homeostasis (Chollet et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2021). The increased abundance of Bacteroides and Blautia in the LCT-treated groups may reflect complex microbial responses. The proliferation of Bacteroides likely promotes the accumulation of LPS and other harmful metabolites, thereby inducing oxidative stress and inflammatory responses (Vatanen et al., 2016). Although Blautia is generally considered beneficial due to its role in SCFA production, its elevated abundance in the LCT-treated groups, particularly in the H group, may indicate an adaptive or compensatory response to the disrupted gut environment. Spearman correlation analysis further confirmed that the abundance of beneficial bacteria, such as Faecalibacterium, was positively correlated with intestinal barrier gene expression and negatively correlated with pro-inflammatory cytokines and ROS levels. Conversely, genera such as Lactobacillus and Blautia were positively correlated with ROS and inflammatory cytokines and negatively correlated with barrier gene expression. These findings suggest that LCT-induced microbiota dysbiosis, through reduced SCFA production, increased LPS levels, and elevated pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, synergizes with oxidative stress and inflammation to aggravate jejunal damage.

The adaptive changes observed under low-dose LCT exposure contrast sharply with the dysbiosis induced by high doses. Low-dose LCT caused only mild intestinal barrier damage and localized inflammation without significantly inducing oxidative stress, and it even stimulated GSH production, likely reflecting activation of the antioxidant system or adaptive metabolic adjustments in the microbiota. It is well-known that the gut microbiota possesses a certain degree of stability. This suggests that under low-dose LCT exposure, the intestine may maintain a degree of homeostasis through microbial and microenvironmental adjustments. However, high-dose LCT exposure led to severe microbiota dysbiosis, significantly reducing beneficial bacteria, increasing LPS and other harmful substances, and inducing inflammatory responses, which, in synergy with oxidative stress, disrupted the intestinal barrier and tissue structure. These dose-dependent differences indicate that LCT’s toxic effects are closely related to exposure levels. This study provides critical evidence of the potential risks of LCT exposure to poultry intestinal health, highlighting the need to optimize the use of pyrethroid insecticides in agriculture and poultry farming. The adaptive changes in the gut microbiota under low-dose LCT exposure suggest that, at specific doses and exposure durations, LCT may have minimal impact on chicken intestinal health, providing a basis for its rational use. However, the significant toxicity of high-dose LCT underscores the need to strictly control its dosage and frequency of application. Future studies should employ metabolomics and functional gene analysis to further elucidate the regulatory mechanisms of LCT on microbial metabolic pathways and explore whether probiotics or prebiotics can mitigate high-dose LCT-induced microbiota dysbiosis and intestinal damage.

In summary, the dose of LCT determines its impact on the intestine. Low-dose LCT exposure triggers adaptive microbial changes (e.g., slight Lactobacillus proliferation and increased GSH production), suggesting that the intestine may maintain some degree of homeostasis through microenvironmental regulation. In contrast, high-dose LCT causes severe microbiota dysbiosis, synergizing with oxidative stress and inflammation to impair intestinal health. These mechanisms reflect the complex regulatory processes of the body in response to varying intensities of external stimuli. These findings indicate that short-term, low-dose LCT exposure may be relatively safe for chickens, with the intestine partially mitigating LCT-induced damage, which has important implications for the safer use of LCT in poultry production.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that LCT exposure significantly impairs the intestinal barrier function in chickens. Low-dose LCT activates intestinal antioxidant activity and induces adaptive microbial changes, resulting in mild intestinal damage and localized inflammatory responses. In contrast, high-dose LCT severely disrupts the intestinal barrier and induces gut microbiota dysbiosis. This study not only provides new insights into the toxicological mechanisms of LCT in poultry but also offers a theoretical basis for assessing its potential environmental impacts.
