Background

Malaria and dengue are endemic infectious diseases in tropical and subtropical regions, transmitted by mosquitoes of the Anopheles and Aedes aegypti genera, respectively. Both of these conditions represent major public health threats due to their high disease burden and potential to cause severe complications. While their clinical presentations are usually distinct in uncomplicated infections, severe disease—and especially coinfection—can produce substantial clinical overlap and multiorgan dysfunction, with prominent vascular and neurological involvement.

Coinfection with Plasmodium vivax and dengue virus has been sporadically reported in areas where both vectors coexist, particularly in Southeast Asia and South America [1, 2]. This condition poses a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge due to overlapping clinical symptoms and potential pathogenic synergy, which may increase the risk of rare adverse events such as ischemic stroke [3, 4].

Although isolated reports have documented stroke associated with either dengue or malaria individually [5, 6], its occurrence in the context of coinfection is exceedingly rare. The low incidence may be explained by the distinct geographical distribution of the vectors, limiting co-occurrence of the diseases. However, the recent dengue epidemics across the Americas—reaching record numbers in 2023 and 2024—have increased the likelihood of unusual clinical encounters, including coinfection and its severe vascular complications [7].

Herein, we report the case of a young woman who developed an acute ischemic stroke associated with dengue and Plasmodium vivax coinfection.

Case presentation

A 23-year-old woman with no relevant medical history, residing in Cali, Colombia, presented with a 7-day history of intermittent fever, malaise, myalgia, and arthralgia. She had returned five weeks earlier from the Chocó region, an endemic area for vector-borne diseases. Twenty-four hours before hospital admission, she experienced sudden onset monoparesis of the right lower limb.

Upon admission, she was febrile (38.5 °C), hypotensive (BP 82/48 mmHg; MAP 59 mmHg), with a heart rate of 98 bpm, respiratory rate of 19 bpm, and oxygen saturation of 93%. Physical examination revealed conjunctival jaundice and monoplegia of the right lower limb, with signs consistent with upper motor neuron involvement.

Initial laboratory tests showed lymphopenia, anemia, severe thrombocytopenia, hyperbilirubinemia, and elevated liver transaminases (Table 1). Thick blood smear was positive for Plasmodium vivax with a parasitemia of 8,200 parasites/μL. Dengue infection was confirmed using a combined rapid diagnostic test detecting NS1 antigen and total IgM/IgG antibodies, which showed NS1 antigen positivity with an IgM-positive and IgG-negative serologic profile, consistent with an acute dengue infection. Other coinfections, as well as metabolic, hematological, and immunological causes of stroke, were ruled out (Table 1).

Brain MRI revealed an acute ischemic infarct in the territory of the left anterior cerebral artery (ACA) (Fig. 1), with no evidence of spinal cord involvement. In the absence of vascular risk factors or structural abnormalities, the cerebrovascular event was attributed to complications arising from dengue and P. vivax coinfection.

She was treated with intravenous artesunate for 3 days, followed by oral primaquine (15 mg/day for 14 days) after excluding G6PD deficiency. Supportive care included guided fluid resuscitation and platelet monitoring. Dengue management included close hepatic and hematological monitoring. Once platelet count exceeded 100,000/μL, aspirin 100 mg/day was initiated as secondary stroke prevention. The patient underwent intensive physical rehabilitation and achieved progressive neurological recovery, confirmed by follow-up imaging at 4 weeks.

Discussion

We report the case of a 23-year-old woman with coinfection by Plasmodium vivax and dengue virus who developed an acute ischemic stroke in the left anterior cerebral artery territory, presenting with focal motor deficit and severe systemic involvement. The rarity of this neurological complication, particularly in the context of viral coinfection, raises the possibility of an unrecognized pathogenic synergy.

To date, only three cases of ischemic stroke associated with P. vivax have been reported internationally, involving patients aged 16 to 52 years [6, 8, 9].

Although our patient falls within this age range, her clinical course was more atypical and aggressive, progressing from intermittent fever to shock, jaundice, and monoplegia within just seven days. This multisystemic evolution contrasts with prior cases, where fever was the predominant and often sole initial symptom, suggesting a more severe pathophysiological behavior in the setting of coinfection.

Neurologically, our patient exhibited right-sided monoplegia, differing from the hemiparesis or diplopia reported in earlier cases. MRI revealed multifocal ischemia, including severe stenosis of middle cerebral artery branches, unilateral frontal infarction, and multiple ischemic lesions in the left basal ganglia, with predominant involvement of the left ACA territory. Treatment with intravenous artesunate followed by primaquine, along with hemodynamic support and intensive rehabilitation, led to complete neurological recovery within four weeks—longer than previously reported durations (5–15 days) but with a favorable outcome.

Dengue virus has been significantly associated with both hemorrhagic and ischemic stroke, with a relative risk increase of 4.13 and 3.80 times, respectively [5]. This relationship is mediated by viral binding to platelet receptors such as DC-SIGN, CLEC-2, and FcγRII, triggering the release of proinflammatory mediators (IL-1β, CXCL8, CCL5, CXCL4) [10–12]. The resulting immune activation induces endothelial dysfunction, blood–brain barrier disruption, increased vascular permeability, and a prothrombotic state conducive to microvascular thrombosis and ischemic infarcts [13, 14].

Although historically considered less virulent than P. falciparum, P. vivax can also cause endothelial damage and vascular events. Its glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) and histamine activate endothelial TLR-2 and TLR-4 receptors, stimulating the release of Weibel–Palade bodies (WPBs) containing Ang-2, ICAM-1, and E-selectin, which promote leukocyte adhesion and microthrombosis [8, 9]. In addition, reduced ADAMTS13 activity increases circulating levels of large von Willebrand factor (vWF) multimers, further enhancing thrombotic risk [13, 14]. In this context, coinfection with dengue and P. vivax may exert synergistic effects, intensifying endothelial dysfunction, cytokine storm, and microthrombosis—potentially explaining the unusual clinical presentation observed in our patient (Fig. 2). The interaction between both pathogens amplifies oxidative stress, capillary leak, and hypercoagulability, favoring rapidly progressive cerebral ischemia.

Proposed integrative pathophysiological model of ischemic stroke in Plasmodium vivax and dengue virus coinfection. Synergistic effects include glycocalyx degradation, immune-mediated endothelial activation, release of Weibel–Palade bodies, and formation of cerebral microthrombi leading to acute ischemia

In Colombia, where dengue, Zika, and chikungunya co-circulate, laboratory confirmation of dengue can be challenging because of serologic cross-reactivity. Current recommendations favor NS1 antigen detection or molecular testing during the acute phase, as isolated IgM positivity may yield false-positive results in up to 30% of cases in co-endemic settings [15]. In this patient, dengue infection was confirmed by a combined rapid test showing NS1 antigen positivity with an IgM-positive and IgG-negative profile, supporting an acute dengue infection.

This case underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion in young patients with recent exposure to multiple endemic regions. Our patient had traveled from a malaria-endemic area to a city experiencing an active dengue outbreak, facilitating the coinfection. It also highlights the need for strict neurological and hemodynamic monitoring in rapidly progressing symptoms and timely initiation of antiparasitic therapy. Furthermore, it encourages exploration of adjunctive therapies aimed at preserving endothelial integrity.

Finally, this case illustrates how global ecological changes and the increasing circulation of arboviruses and blood-borne parasites can lead to novel coinfection scenarios, whose pathogenic interactions remain poorly understood [1].

Conclusions

Coinfection with Plasmodium vivax and dengue virus, although rare, should be recognized as a potential cause of severe neurological complications in endemic regions. This case illustrates how synergistic interactions between both pathogens may intensify endothelial dysfunction—via glycocalyx degradation, immune overactivation, and cerebral microthrombosis—leading to ischemic stroke even in young, healthy individuals. These findings highlight the need for systematic coinfection screening in febrile patients with neurological or hemodynamic compromise, early targeted therapy, and consideration of endothelial-protective strategies, especially amid expanding vector distribution and ecological change.
