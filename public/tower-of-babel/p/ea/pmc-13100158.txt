Dynamic characteristics of frozen ballast beds in cold-region railways under cyclic train loading
Authors: Jianxing Liu, Yong Cao, Aiping Chen, Wei Qi, Lei Kou, Mykola Sysyn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDue to the freezing influences in alpine regions, an in-depth study of the dynamic characteristics of frozen ballast beds is urgently needed. This paper established discrete element method (DEM) models incorporating ballast-ice bonding, validated through laboratory cyclic loading tests, uniaxial compression tests and vehicle-track coupled dynamics (VTCD). Using the validated models, the effects of varying ice contents on cumulative settlement, bearing stiffness, particle movement, coordination number, number of ice bonding keys, and force chain evolutions were investigated. The results show that high ice content significantly inhibits cumulative settlement, with stabilization typically after 200 loading cycles. The average coordination number, number of ice bonding key, and bonding failure rate all exhibit pronounced segmented changes, with ice content 20% as a key value. Freezing drives the transfer mechanism of internal load from “particle force chains” to “ice-bonded blocky aggregates”. The average coordination number increases markedly with rising ice content, reflecting a structural evolution from a loose granular skeleton to a dense frozen network. These mesoscopic mechanisms directly control the macroscopic dynamic responses by elevating the ballast bed stiffness, localizing particle motion beneath the sleeper, and suppressing overall settlement.Keywords: Railway track, Ballast bed, Freezing environment, Dynamic characteristics, Ice contentSubject terms: Engineering, Physics

Introduction

The ballasted track is an essential component of railway infrastructure, primarily comprising rails, sleepers, fastening systems, and a ballast bed formed by coarse crushed rock particles (Fig. 1). The ballast bed plays a vital role in supporting the train loading transferred through sleepers, attenuating and distributing stresses, and facilitating drainage, making it essential for ensuring the stability and safety of the railway track system1. As railway transportation worldwide advances rapidly towards high-speed and heavy-haul operations, the ongoing escalation in train speeds and axle loads has markedly amplified the dynamic vehicle-track interactions2. This intensified interaction subjects the ballast bed to higher dynamic stresses and more complex deformations, thereby not only accelerating the abrasion and breakage of ballast particles but also potentially leading to track geometry deterioration, substantially increased maintenance costs, and even posing safety risks such as derailment3,4. Therefore, investigating the dynamic behaviors of the ballast bed under train loading is crucial for ensuring track stability, reducing maintenance costs, and enhancing operational safety5. Furthermore, certain railways traverse cold regions, where ballasted tracks must withstand the challenges posed by low temperatures and freezing conditions. These railways are primarily distributed in countries such as Norway, Sweden, Switzerland, Finland, Russia, and Japan, as well as in the Alaska Railroad in the United States and the Qinghai-Tibet Railway in China1,6,7.

Typical ballasted track structure.

The discrete element method (DEM) has gained prominence for modeling the mesoscale mechanics of ballast aggregates, with investigations focusing on vibration characteristics and attenuation in the ballast bed8,9, lateral resistance and shear strength10–12, particle dynamics under train-induced loads13–15, breakage and permanent deformation under cyclic loading16,17, degradation caused by unsupported (hanging) sleepers and fouled ballast18,19, and the influence of tamping maintenance on ballast bed performance4,20. In addition, railways in cold regions face severe challenges to their ballast beds due to the extreme climatic conditions inherent to their unique geographical environments. Indeed, with the long-term operation and ongoing construction of ballasted tracks in high-altitude and frigid areas, the in-service performance of ballast beds under low-temperature and freezing conditions has increasingly attracted the attention of researchers and engineers. Nurmikolu21,22 and Guthrie22 discussed the detrimental impacts of seasonal frost action in Finland’s cold climate on high-speed ballasted tracks, emphasizing that understanding and accounting for frost action mechanisms is crucial for the successful construction and maintenance of high-speed railways. Sheng et al.23 observed several Chinese railway ballasted tracks and found that freezing of the ballast layer commonly occurs in clean ballast beds with low moisture content. Akagawa et al.24 examined railway tracks in northern Japan and discovered that track heave in severe cold regions arises not only from subgrade frost heave but also from frost heave within the ballast layer. Guo et al.25 identified freezing and frost heave phenomena in the ballast layer and, through laboratory image analysis, tracked and qualitatively assessed ice growth and ballast particle movement during the cooling process. Ižvolt et al.26,27 constructed an outdoor 1:1 full-scale ballasted track test rig to observe and comparatively analyze the freezing conditions of ballasted tracks in winter across Slovakia and Lithuania; subsequently, they performed numerical simulations of frozen ballast beds using SoilVision software and proposed a design of a modified nomogram to determine the required thickness of subgrade surface layers susceptible to frost action. Liu et al.28 combined uniaxial compression tests on frozen ballast with the discrete element method (DEM) to explore the compressive performance of ballast aggregates in frozen environments, revealing that the compressive strength of ballast aggregates is closely correlated with variations in temperature and freezing bond strength. Furthermore, Li et al.29 employed the same experimental approach to investigate the effects of ice content and temperature on the compressive behavior of frozen ballast layers. Their results indicated that, with increasing ice content, the ductility of frozen specimens improved, and their failure modes transitioned from brittle failure to axial splitting failure. To qualitatively investigate the influence of freezing environments on ballast bed resistance, Liu et al.30 constructed a full-scale ballasted track model within a large environmental chamber and conducted longitudinal and lateral resistance tests on frozen ballast beds under various low-temperature conditions. The results showed that, at small sleeper displacements, the freezing environment increases the resistance of the ballast bed; however, once the displacement exceeds a certain threshold, the resistance decreases. Subsequently, Li et al.31 employed the discrete element method combined with a bond model to further examine the mesoscale resistance behavior of frozen ballast beds, sequentially analyzing the effects of shoulder height, shoulder width, and slope angle on the performance of frozen ballast beds.

Most studies on railway ballast beds have focused on the effects of vibration and cyclic loading on the dynamic performance of ballast beds under normal and fouled conditions, as well as the increasingly recognized freezing environments in plateau and mountainous regions. However, investigations into the dynamic behavior of frozen ballast beds under cyclic train loading, particularly the internal stress and movement states of ballast particles, remain scarce. Nevertheless, as a prevalent railway track component worldwide, the ballast bed in freezing environments undergoes significant changes in stiffness, damping characteristics, and inter-particle contact mechanisms. The ice formed by frozen moisture not only alters the force chain structure among particles but may also induce crack propagation and stress concentration under train dynamic loads, thereby affecting the overall response characteristics of the track structure. Therefore, there is an urgent need to investigate the dynamic properties of frozen ballast beds to elucidate the evolution mechanisms of internal ballast particle movement patterns under train dynamic loading and their influence on the mechanical performance of the ballast bed. To this end, this study develops DEM simulation models for ballast beds that incorporates ice particles’ intrusion and freezing by introducing a bond contact model. Subsequently, using calibration parameters obtained from uniaxial compression tests on ballast-ice composites, the influence mechanisms of varying ice content on particle movement, force transmission patterns, and accumulated settlement behavior induced by cyclic train loading in frozen ballast beds are investigated. Furthermore, the correlation between ice content and the dynamic performance of frozen ballast beds is analyzed. The results of this paper have direct practical applications in the design, frost damage mitigation, and operational maintenance optimization of ballast beds for railways in cold regions. Specifically, the key value of ice content serves as a critical reference for engineering control of ballast ice (or moisture) content, thereby preventing localized stress concentrations and crack propagation under high ice contents, and reducing the risk of track instability. The analysis of mesoscopic mechanisms provides a foundational basis for on-site monitoring, such as prioritizing stress assessments in the region beneath sleepers, facilitating the development of targeted tamping and de-icing protocols, and ultimately enhancing the safety and economic efficiency of cold-region railways.

Train loading types and discrete element models of frozen ballast beds

In this chapter, a series of DEM models of ballast beds incorporating sleepers are established. The forces applied to one of the sleepers are obtained from the classical vehicle-track coupled dynamics (VTCD) model32. The applicability of ballast bed parameters in the DEM models is verified by comparing the velocities and accelerations of sleepers under loading between the VTCD and DEM models, supplemented by validation against settlement results of ballast beds under cyclic train loading executed in laboratory. Subsequently, ice particles are added into the DEM models along with a bond contact model to simulate the frozen states of ballast beds. Finally, the dynamic characteristics of frozen ballast beds with varying ice contents are investigated.

DEM model validation

To validate the applicability of the ballast bed DEM model under high-frequency dynamic train loading conditions, the vertical velocity and acceleration of tested sleeper in the DEM model were compared with the modelling results from the VTCD model, as shown in Fig. 4. Furthermore, to verify the settlement accumulation of the ballast bed DEM model under cyclic loading, a full-scale ballast bed was constructed in the laboratory in accordance with the ballast particle size distribution curve presented in Fig. 5, ensuring consistency with the DEM model. The ballast bed was established with a density of 1750 kg/m³ through layered filling and vibration compaction. The sleepers were the Chinese Type IIIa concrete sleepers. Cyclic loading tests on the laboratory ballast bed were then conducted using a servo-controlled loading apparatus (Fig. 6(a)). The loading mode was sinusoidal loading, and the number of cycles was 1000 times. The loading amplitude matched that of the simulation model, set at 2 × 27 kN = 54 kN, with a frequency of 3 Hz. During the test, the displacement sensors were used to record the settlement data at both ends of the tested sleeper, and the average values of the data from both ends was taken as settlements. Figure 6(b) presents the settlement curves of the laboratory and DEM ballast bed after 1000 loading cycles.

Cyclic loading tests of ballast beds.

As shown in Fig. 4(a), under the loading excitation from a single railway vehicle carriage, the time-history curve of the sleeper vertical velocity exhibits four peaks and four troughs in both the VTCD model and the DEM model. The peaks in the DEM model are generally lower in magnitude, while the troughs are higher, and the times at which these extrema occur are slightly shifted compared to those in the VTCD model. As evident from Fig. 4(b), the peak and trough values of sleeper acceleration differ to some extent between the two track models. The DEM model exhibits more intense fluctuations in sleeper acceleration compared to the VTCD model. Nevertheless, the overall trends of the acceleration time-history curves from both models remain consistent. The results presented in Fig. 6 indicate that the settlement evolution patterns of the ballast bed in the DEM model and the laboratory experiment are consistent, although some numerical differences exist. Specifically, after 1000 loading cycles, the settlement predicted by the DEM ballast bed model was 6.84% higher than that measured in the laboratory test. The aforementioned validation results demonstrate that the established DEM model is suitable for investigating the mechanical behavior of ballasted tracks under cycle train loading.

DEM models of frozen ballast beds

In this section, fine spherical particles (along with the clumps formed by their mutual bonding) are employed to simulate the intrusion of ice in the ballast bed. Concurrently, the parallel bonding model is used to model the freezing effects between ballast–ice and ice–ice interfaces. This discrete particle approach effectively captures the non-uniform and pore-filling nature of ice in frozen granular materials while maintaining a balance between computational efficiency and cost34,35. Furthermore, uniaxial compression tests on ice specimens and ballast–ice specimens are conducted to calibrate the relevant parameters of the parallel bonding model.

Parallel bonding model

To simulate the bonding effects between particles, the parallel bonding model36 was incorporated into the DEM models of frozen ballast beds. This bonding model functions by preventing relative displacement and rotation between particles within a specified strength envelope, thereby achieving a bonded state. When the forces on the particles exceed the predefined strength limits, the parallel bonds break, and the interaction between the particles reverts to the basic contact model. Uniaxial compression tests on ice specimens and ballast–ice specimens

In previous studies, the authors proposed a preparation method for a ballast-ice combination28. A detachable mold with an inner diameter of 200 mm was constructed using five tempered glass plates, each measuring 210 mm in length, 200 mm in width, and 10 mm in thickness. The glass plates were supported and connected by brackets, and the inner walls of the mold were lined with an extremely thin plastic film to isolate the specimen from the mold while preventing water leakage, as shown in Fig. 7.

Mold for making ballast-ice combinations.

During the preparation of ice-bonded ballast specimens, the ballast aggregates were firstly placed into the mold cavity and compacted by vibration to achieve a dense packing (the packing density was calculated as the ratio of the aggregate mass to the occupied volume. If the ratio did not meet the required density of 1.70 g/cm³ for the Class I railway ballast bed in China37, the filling process would be repeated). Then, one mold was filled with water, and the volume of added water was recorded. Finally, the specimens were transferred to a temperature-controlled environmental chamber, cooled to -20 °C (a typical freezing temperature along railways in China’s high-plateau mountainous regions30, and maintained at constant temperature for 36 h. Ice specimens with dimensions of 100 mm × 100 mm × 100 mm were prepared using the same method.

Prior to the uniaxial compression tests, the mass of each ballast-ice specimen was measured to facilitate subsequent calculation of the ice content within the ballast layer. Uniaxial compression tests were then carried on both the pure ice specimens and the ballast-ice specimens. The compression machine had a load precision of 0.001 kN, and it was calibrated and verified by a certified precision instrument calibration agency prior to testing to ensure the reliability and accuracy of the applied loads. The loading process described in Reference28. That is, a constant stress rate of 0.03 MPa/s was applied. This corresponded to a constant load rate of 0.3 kN/s for the ice specimens (calculated as 0.03 MPa/s×(0.1 m×0.1 m) = 0.3 kN/s) and 1.2 kN/s for the ballast-ice specimens (calculated as 0.03 MPa/s×(0.2 m×0.2 m) = 1.2 kN/s). Liquid nitrogen was employed to maintain low-temperature conditions for the specimens and their surroundings throughout the compression process. The testing procedure is illustrated in Fig. 8.

Results and analysis

Cumulative settlement patterns of frozen ballast beds with different ice contents

Figure 11 presents the cumulative settlement curves of frozen ballasted beds with varying ice contents under cyclic train loading. As evident from Fig. 11, the unfrozen ballast bed exhibits continuous accumulation of settlement throughout the loading process, and the rate of accumulation shows a decelerating trend in the later stages. In contrast, the cumulative settlement of frozen ballasted beds is significantly reduced and consistently exhibits a distinct two-stage settlement characteristic. That is, rapid accumulation occurs within the initial 0–50 loading cycles, followed by a gradual deceleration in settlement rate, with stabilization generally achieved after approximately 200 cycles. The freezing effect suppresses the development of deformation in ballast beds. Furthermore, the magnitude of settlement is strongly dependent on the ice content. For low ice contents (5%-10%), the stabilized settlement values range between approximately − 0.35 mm and − 0.45 mm. At middle ice contents (10%-20%), settlement is further reduced to about − 0.20 mm to -0.30 mm. When ice content reaches higher levels (20%-30%), cumulative settlement is minimized (approximately − 0.10 mm to -0.15 mm), and the stabilization is achieved earlier compared to other groups. It can be concluded that the resistance to settlement of the ballast bed progressively increases with rising ice content.

Bearing stiffnesses of frozen ballast beds with different ice contents

Stiffness is one of the key factors reflecting the load-bearing capacity of the ballast bed under external forces. According to the Chinese standard “Testing method of railway ballast bed parameters TB/T 3448 − 2016”, vertical forces P1 = 7.5 kN and P2 = 35 kN were separately applied at the tested sleeper. After the sleeper stabilizing, the corresponding vertical displacements D7.5 and D35 were recorded, and the bearing stiffness ks of the ballast bed was calculated using Eq. (4). The bearing stiffnesses of frozen ballast beds with different ice contents are shown in Fig. 12.

Bearing stiffnesses of frozen ballast beds with different ice contents.

As shown in Fig. 12, the bearing stiffness of the ballast bed increases gradually with rising ice content. However, this increase exhibits a pronounced nonlinear trend. Especially when the ice content reaches 20%, the bearing stiffness experiences a sharp jump, rising from about 180.29 kN/mm to 310.16 kN/mm. After this jump, further increases in ice content result in a progressively slower rate of stiffness enhancement. This trend is mutually consistent with the results in Fig. 11, where cumulative settlement is significantly reduced at high ice contents, essentially reflecting the suppression of deformation due to the enhanced bearing stiffness. Based on the results from Sect. 3.1 and Sect. 3.2, it can be concluded that the capacity of frozen ballast bed to resist settlement gradually improves with increasing ice content. The formation of a ballast-ice combination following freezing enhances the overall stability of the system, thereby limiting the movements of relative particles (detailed analysis is in Sect. 3.3).

The mesoscopic dynamic characteristics of frozen ballast beds with different ice contents

This section further investigates the mesoscopic influences of ice particles and freezing effects on the dynamic performances of ballast beds, with elucidating the mechanisms responsible for the substantial reduction in settlement observed in frozen ballast beds under cyclic train loading. (Fig. 12).

Movement trends and coordination numbers of particles

Figure 13 presents the velocity vectors and the average coordination numbers of particles within the ballast layers at the 500th loading cycle. The settlements can be recognized almost stabilized at the 500th loading cycle. As shown in Fig. 12, the movement trends of ballast particles under cyclic loading are more uniformly distributed in the unfrozen conventional ballast bed, especially directly beneath the sleeper. Meanwhile, the potential particle velocities remain relatively low (generally not exceeding 10 mm/s). The distribution of ballast particle movement tendencies exhibits local concentrations amid overall dispersion with ice content ω increasing, and the potential velocities in these concentrated regions all exceed 12 mm/s. Further mesoscopic analysis reveals that this phenomenon arises from the intrusion of ice particles, whereby ballast particles and ice particles form integrated larger aggregates in the “ballast-ice” configuration, with the freezing effect between particles becoming more pronounced as ice content increases. Therefore, the regions with more ice particle distribution and sufficient contact with ballast particles are more prone to the formation of large “ballast-ice” aggregates, which effectively bear and transmit external loads, and coordinate overall motion. On the other hand, as the ice content ω increases, the average coordination numbers among particles within the ballast layers gradually rises (this increase is evident since ice is simulated using fine spherical particles). Ice bonding states between particles

Figure 14 shows the variation in the number of ice bonding keys within the frozen ballast beds between the initial state and after 500 cyclic loading cycles. As shown in Fig. 14, with ice content increasing, the number of ice bonding keys exhibits an overall upward trend, while the breakage rate decreases significantly, indicating a clear inverse relationship between the two. In the original state, the number of ice bonding keys increases approximately linearly with ice content increasing, suggesting that higher ice content promotes the formation of more ice bonding keys between ballast particles, thereby enhancing the integrity and stiffness of the ballast bed. After 500 times of cycle loading, the numbers of ice bonding keys decrease to varying degrees across all ice content levels, and the magnitudes of these decreases diminish with the ice content increasing, indicating that higher ice content is beneficial in resisting the ice bond breakage and the structural degradation induced by cyclic loading. Furthermore, the breakage rate curve reveals that at lower ice contents (ω < 20%), the ice bond breakage rates are relatively high, reaching a maximum of 11.72%. That reflects there are relatively few ice bonding connections between particles currently, and the brittle failure characteristic is obvious. As the ice content increases (ω ≥ 20%), the breakage rates drop below 2%, indicating that the dense ice-bonded networks which form at higher ice contents can effectively withstand external loading. Thus, greater structural stabilities can be maintained.

The distribution of force chains

Figure 15 presents the internal force chain distribution characteristics of the ballast beds with varying ice contents under cyclic loading. As shown in Fig. 15, variations in ice content exert a significant influence on the morphology and distribution pattern of force chains. Under ice-free and low-ice conditions (0% and 5%), the force chain networks are predominantly composed of weak force chains, with strong force chains sparsely distributed and failing to form continuous load-bearing paths. At ω = 0%, the overall force chain distribution is relatively uniform, yet the load-bearing zones lack dense bands of strong force chains. It indicates that in the absence of freezing, ballast particles rely on conventional contacts for force transmission, which leads to a limited capacity to resist concentrated loading. When the ice content increases to 10% or 15%, the proportion of medium-strength force chains (40–80 N) expands substantially. Meanwhile, the numbers of strong force chains increase markedly, and distinct vertically penetrating columnar force chain zones form beneath the sleeper. These observations suggest that suitable ice contents enhance the role of ice bonding keys between particles, enabling the assembly of local particle groups into relatively stable load-bearing units, thereby improving the overall continuity and stiffness of force chains within the ballast layer and exhibiting typical characteristics of a “load-bearing from skeleton-ice composite”. When ω > 20%, weak force chains occupy a higher proportion in the side regions. While strong force chains become further concentrated beneath the sleeper, forming distinct “concentrated load-bearing zones.” This occurs because high ice contents cause ice bonding keys between particles to be dominated, then gradually transform the “ballast-ice” system from a typical granular force chain network toward a “frozen monolithic load-bearing”, which enhances the overall stiffness of the ballast bed while inducing localized stress concentration. Under repeated cyclic train loadings, the localized stress concentrations may induce brittle failure of ice bonding keys and subsequent crack propagation. These damage processes will lead to the gradual accumulation of microscopic internal damage within the frozen ballast bed. Over the long term, the sudden catastrophic failure of the ballast bed may occur.

Conclusions

This paper focuses on the dynamic characteristics of frozen ballasted beds in cold regions under cyclic train loading. A systematic investigation was conducted from macroscopic response to mesoscopic mechanical mechanism by comprehensively adopting laboratory tests combined with DEM numerical simulation methods. The main conclusions are as follows.

(1) The effectiveness of the parallel bonding model in simulating the freezing and failure processes of ice bonding keys is validated, thereby ensuring overall consistency between the simulation models and the physical processes.

(2) Freezing significantly alters the settlement pattern of ballast bed, exhibiting a two-stage characteristic with rapid initial settlement followed by a basically stable phase. The stable phase typically occurs after about 200 loading cycles.

(3) The average coordination number, number of ice bonding keys, and breakage rate of bonding keys all exhibit pronounced segmented variations, with ω = 20% serving as a key value.

(4) Freezing makes a transition in the load-bearing mode to one dominated by “ice-bonded blocky aggregates” in ballast beds. This shift results in enhanced overall stiffness of the ballast bed but simultaneously intensifies localized stress concentrations.

(5) As the ice content increases, an evolutionary transition of the structure from a loose granular skeleton to a dense frozen network occurs.

This paper established DEM simulation models for frozen ballast beds, and revealed the dynamic characteristics of frozen ballast beds from a multi-scale perspective. Nonetheless, there is still a need for further in-depth study. That is, ice intrusion and freezing within the ballast bed constitute a progressive, layer-by-layer process. One of the future efforts is to conduct further research on the relationship between the ice content, the bearing stiffness of ballast bed, and the movement characteristics of ballast particles. The other future effort is planned to integrate DEM simulations of gradual ice freezing effects, which can examine the evolution of mechanical properties and underlying mesoscopic mechanisms in frozen ballasted beds. So that the approach can be more in line with the actual needs of the cold regions.
