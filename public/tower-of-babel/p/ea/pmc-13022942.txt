The Effect of Abdominal Massage and In‐Bed Exercise on Gastrointestinal Complications, Pain and Comfort in Palliative Care Patients: A Randomised Controlled Trial
Authors: Serap Gungor Unal, Ismaıl Dusak, Sumeyye Akcoban
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEnteral nutrition helps maintain nutritional status and sustain quality of life in palliative care patients. In this context, enteral nutrition supports nutritional intake, while nursing care aims to enhance comfort and prevent related complications.ObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate the effects of abdominal massage and in‐bed exercise on gastrointestinal complications, pain and comfort in enterally fed palliative care patients.MethodsA randomised controlled trial was conducted at a palliative care centre with 105 patients assigned to three groups: abdominal massage, in‐bed exercise, and control (n = 35 each). In the abdominal massage intervention, superficial and deep effleurage, petrissage, vibration and repeated superficial effleurage manoeuvres were applied sequentially. The in‐bed exercise intervention consisted of passive exercises designed to maintain the range of motion of the upper and lower extremity joints. Interventions were administered twice daily for 3 days, with 15‐min sessions. Data were collected using the Patient Information Form, Comfort Behaviours Checklist, Wong‐Baker Faces Pain Rating Scale, and Gastrointestinal Complication Form.ResultsIn the abdominal massage group, constipation and abdominal distension significantly decreased over time (p < 0.05). In the in‐bed exercise group, constipation, nausea, vomiting, and distension significantly decreased (p < 0.05). Pain scores were significantly lower in both the abdominal massage (1.97 ± 0.70) and in‐bed exercise (2.05 ± 0.80) groups compared to the control group (p < 0.001). Comfort scores were significantly higher in both intervention groups (71.60 ± 7.89 and 71.25 ± 10.05, respectively) than in the control group (p < 0.001).ConclusionsAbdominal massage and in‐bed exercise are effective, safe, and independent nursing interventions that reduce gastrointestinal complications and pain, while improving comfort in palliative care patients receiving enteral nutrition.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov: NCT07125482

Introduction

Palliative care is a specialised medical approach that focuses on providing support to individuals facing life‐threatening illnesses and on improving quality of life and comfort. Palliative care is not limited to end‐of‐life and comfort care alone but encompasses all stages of the disease [1]. Patients receiving palliative care frequently experience multiple symptoms, including pain, nausea and vomiting, fatigue, malnutrition, cachexia, musculoskeletal problems and mood disorders [2, 3].

Providing appropriate supportive nutritional interventions for patients with malnutrition is of great importance [4]. To support nutrition, enteral and parenteral nutrition methods are used, with enteral nutrition being the preferred option in patients with a functioning gastrointestinal system [2]. However, during enteral nutrition, gastrointestinal complications (nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea and constipation), mechanical complications (aspiration pneumonia, tube dislodgement or obstruction) and metabolic complications (hyperglycemia and fluid–electrolyte imbalances) may occur [5]. Both pharmacological and non‐pharmacological approaches may be used to prevent or reduce these complications [6].

Among non‐pharmacological approaches, abdominal massage and in‐bed exercise applied to intensive care patients have been reported to have positive effects on abdominal distension, constipation, gastrointestinal residual volume and blood glucose levels [7]. Similarly, abdominal massage administered to hospitalised elderly orthopaedic patients has been shown to improve constipation severity and stool form [8]. In addition, abdominal massage applied to postoperative patients was found to reduce gastrointestinal symptoms and enhance patient comfort [9]. Furthermore, a meta‐analysis demonstrated that abdominal massage is an effective intervention for patients with chronic constipation, significantly improving straining, abdominal discomfort/pain, bloating, sensation of incomplete evacuation and anorectal obstruction [10]. Moreover, a meta‐analysis focusing on enterally fed patients reported that abdominal massage, when applied as a nursing intervention, significantly reduced feeding intolerance symptoms, including gastric residual volume, incidence of gastric retention, vomiting, abdominal distension, diarrhoea, constipation and abdominal circumference difference [11].

In this context, nursing care for enterally fed patients plays a critical role in facilitating feeding, enhancing patient comfort and reducing complications [12]. Abdominal massage, one of the non‐pharmacological treatments, is frequently used in patients with gastrointestinal dysfunction [13]. During abdominal massage, intra‐abdominal pressure is altered and directed towards the rectum, thereby mechanically stimulating the intestines. The effect of massage is to stimulate peristalsis, shorten the time for food to pass through the gastrointestinal tract, and accelerate bowel movement [14]. Abdominal massage applied to different groups, including enterally fed patients, appears to improve gastrointestinal function, lower the incidence of gastrointestinal complications and relieve pain [7, 13, 14, 15, 16].

Mobilisation is a non‐invasive, practical, safe and cost‐effective procedure that can be independently managed by nurses and is widely preferred for the prevention of complications associated with hospitalisation [17, 18]. In palliative care patients, who are at high risk for immobility‐related complications, mobilisation plays a critical role in maintaining functional capacity, relieving pain, alleviating multiple symptoms and improving comfort and quality of life [19]. Previous studies have reported that exercise training and passive exercise practices for the lower extremities and trunk increase intestinal motility and reduce gastrointestinal complications in bedridden patients for various reasons and in patients followed up in the intensive care unit after cardiovascular surgery [20, 21, 22]. In addition, a pilot study conducted with a group of bedridden patients compared thermotherapy, passive exercise for the lower extremities and trunk, and different physiotherapy methods in which both were combined and revealed that all methods increased bowel sounds [23].

Palliative care nurses aim to improve the comfort and quality of life of patients by managing their symptoms and meeting the psychological, social, emotional and spiritual support needs of patients and their relatives [24]. Although there are studies in the literature evaluating abdominal massage and exercise practices separately or together, no study has examined the effect of abdominal massage and in‐bed exercise on gastrointestinal complications and patient comfort specific to palliative care patients. This study aims to bring an original perspective to the literature on the effectiveness of nursing interventions in palliative care patients.

Aim

This study aimed to evaluate the effect of abdominal massage and in‐bed exercise on gastrointestinal complications and patient comfort in palliative care patients.

Research Hypotheses

Abdominal massage and in‐bed exercise have similar effects on gastrointestinal complications and patient comfort in palliative care patients. Abdominal massage is more effective on gastrointestinal complications and patient comfort in palliative care patients. In‐bed exercise is more effective in gastrointestinal complications and patient comfort in palliative care patients.

Abdominal massage and in‐bed exercise have similar effects on gastrointestinal complications and patient comfort in palliative care patients.

Abdominal massage is more effective on gastrointestinal complications and patient comfort in palliative care patients.

In‐bed exercise is more effective in gastrointestinal complications and patient comfort in palliative care patients.

Method

Study Design

This study was a randomised, controlled, experimental trial with repeated measurements on three groups. The study was conducted based on the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials Statement (CONSORT) checklist and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov database (No:NCT07125482).

Study Setting and Sample Size

It was carried out at the Palliative Care Centre of a training and research hospital after the necessary permissions were obtained. The Palliative Care Centre, where the study was conducted, accommodates 20 beds.

Patients received enteral nutrition via a nasogastric tube or percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG), depending on clinical indication; the distribution of feeding routes did not differ between the study groups. The enteral nutrition of the patients was planned by the nutrition team, in collaboration with the nutrition nurse and dietitian, and was managed and monitored by the clinic nurses. The selection of enteral nutrition formulas was determined by the nutrition team based on each patient's individual clinical characteristics and nutritional needs (e.g., presence of diabetes mellitus, laboratory findings and metabolic requirements). Therefore, enteral nutrition products were individualised according to patient needs rather than using a single standardised formula for all patients.

Enteral nutrition was administered continuously via a feeding pump in all groups by the clinic nurse, in accordance with the recommendations of the nutrition team, between 08:00 AM and 02:00 AM, to ensure standardisation of the feeding method. All patients reached their predetermined enteral nutrition targets as planned by the nutrition team, and no differences were observed between the groups in terms of achieving nutritional goals.

Patients received routine medical treatments as prescribed by the attending physicians, including prokinetic agents when clinically indicated. However, the use of prokinetic medications was not recorded or analysed as a study variable. The patient follow‐up was recorded in the section on nutrition in the patient follow‐up form. No documented guidelines on enteral nutrition were available at the Palliative Care Centre.

Population and Sample

This study was completed over a six‐month period between March 1, 2025 and August 20, 2025.

The population consisted of patients hospitalised in the Palliative Care Centre of a training and research hospital. The sample size was calculated using the G*Power 3.1.9.7 software based on the f‐test (ANOVA) to analyse the mean differences between the three groups. The power analysis was based on the effect size value (η = 0.02) reported in a similar study [7] and was set as f = 0.14. In the analysis, the significance level was set as 0.05, the power level as 0.90, the number of groups as 3 and the number of assessments as 4. Accordingly, it was predicted that at least 90 individuals would be required to achieve 90% power in the study. Considering the possible data loss of 15% and drop‐out from follow‐up, the study included a total of 105 patients in order to reduce safety.

Randomisation

A total of 105 patients included in the study were randomised into three groups in a 1:1:1 ratio. Each group was evenly distributed with 35 patients. A computer‐aided spreadsheet software (Microsoft Excel 365) was used for randomisation. Each group label (“Abdominal Group,” “Bed Group,” “Control Group”) was entered 35 times, and a total of 105 labels were randomly ranked (using the = RAND() function). The list of mixed groups was assigned to patients in the order of patient record, respectively. Randomisation was carried out by a researcher on the team, independent of the study. This method allowed for even distribution between the groups and avoided selection bias. Due to the nature of the abdominal massage and in‐bed exercise procedures, neither the participants nor the researcher could be blinded.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Patients who were hospitalised and received care in a palliative care centre were older than 18 years, had a Glasgow Coma Score (GCS) > 3, started enteral nutrition at least 24 h ago, had no wounds in the extremities or abdominal area, had no restrictions on abdominal massage (having had radiotherapy or abdominal surgery, blockage, or an open wound in the abdominal area in the last 6 weeks), and whose clinical condition allowed participation in in‐bed exercise (defined as having no contraindications to passive range‐of‐motion exercises), and consented or whose relatives consented were included in the study.

Patients in whom enteral nutrition was discontinued during the study and who manifested signs of infection due to VIP or other infections during or before the study were excluded from the study.

Outcomes

Patient Information Form

This form was prepared by the researchers after a literature review, including the patient's age, gender, height, weight and history of chronic diseases [14, 15, 25].

Gastrointestinal Complication Form

This form was prepared by the researchers based on the literature that examines the condition of nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea and constipation from gastrointestinal complications of the patient before, during, and after the intervention throughout enteral nutrition [5, 7, 14].

Wong‐Baker Faces Pain Rating Scale

Wong and Baker developed the scale in 1988. This is a practical and valid assessment tool for pain assessment. The pain levels of the patients are assessed by enumerating their facial expressions. This is scored by enumerating the facial expressions and as the number value rises, the facial expression changes, and this indicates a rise in the pain level. Scoring is assessed as follows: 0—no pain, 2—minimal pain, 4—slightly more pain, 6—much more pain, 8—considerably more pain, 10—the worst pain [26].

Comfort Behaviours Checklist (CBCL)

Kolcaba developed the Comfort Behaviours Checklist (CBCL) in 1989 and designed it to be used for patients who are unable to complete a questionnaire or have cognitive limitations [27]. Unutkan and Balcı Yangın conducted its Turkish validity and reliability study. The CBCL includes 30 behavioural indicators, pain and comfort scores. Questions 2, 3, 5, 7, 8, 9, 11, 12, 13, 19, 20, 21, 22, 24, 25 and 27 on the CBCL are reverse‐coded. The list is scored on a 4‐point Likert scale. As soon as the checklist is filled in, a score of 0 is given to the question that is deemed inappropriate for the individual. The total number of questions answered is multiplied by 4, and the possible score is calculated. The reverse questions are reversed, and the scores of all questions are summed, and the score of raw comfort is calculated. Finally, the raw comfort scores are divided by the possible comfort scores to obtain a decimal number. The decimal part of the resulting score is expressed in two digits. High scores indicate a high comfort level. If all questions are answered, a respondent gets a minimum score of 25 points and a maximum score of 100 points. The Cronbach's alpha value of the scale was found to be 0.91 [28]. There are also two more open‐ended questions in the CBCL and these questions are used when the CBCL is the only assessment tool. Since a different pain assessment tool was used in this study, open‐ended questions were not used. In this study, its Cronbach's alpha value was found to be 0.87–0.91.

Intervention

After randomisation, baseline data were collected prior to the initiation of the interventions. The Patient Information Form, Wong–Baker Faces Pain Rating Scale, and Comfort Behaviours Checklist were completed using information obtained from patient files, patients and their relatives. The gastrointestinal complications were recorded daily for three consecutive days.

The interventions were applied for three consecutive days, twice daily for 15 min, in the morning between 07:00 and 08:00 and in the evening between 19:00 and 20:00. To ensure standardisation, the same researcher applied the interventions and recorded the measures. The interventions were applied under the same environmental conditions (e.g., time of day, room privacy, timing before enteral nutrition), and the duration and method of the application were kept consistent among all patients.

Post‐intervention outcome measures (pain and comfort) were collected at the end of the third day, while gastrointestinal complications were assessed throughout the intervention period. The hospital physiotherapist was consulted during the development and implementation of the intervention protocols.

Abdominal Massage Procedure

The researcher who received the training on abdominal massage applied the abdominal massage. Before the patients started to be enterally fed, the researcher applied abdominal massage for 15 min between 07:00 and 08:00 in the morning and between 19:00 and 20:00 in the evening, twice a day for 3 days at 24‐h intervals. Before starting abdominal massage, the patient was placed in the supine position and kept in the same position throughout the procedure. For abdominal massage, superficial effleurage, deep effleurage, petrissage, vibration and superficial effleurage manoeuvres were applied, respectively. Each technique was performed for equally 3 min, resulting in a total massage duration of 15 min for each patient. No moisturiser was used during the abdominal massage. After the application, the patient was brought to the semi‐fowler position and was allowed to stay in a comfortable position. The timing and frequency of massage application were decided according to the studies in the literature and expert opinions [13, 15] and were designed to be feasible and applicable within routine palliative care practice.

In‐Bed Exercise Procedure

The researcher who received the training on in‐bed exercises applied the in‐bed exercise. Before starting the passive range‐of‐motion in‐bed exercise procedure, the patient was placed in the supine position and kept in the same position throughout the procedure. In‐bed exercises included movements such as extending and contracting the wrist and fingers inward and outward, extending and contracting the elbow and arm, swinging the arm left and right at the head height, rotating the ankle up and down and left and right, bending and releasing the hip and knee, opening and closing the leg laterally, pushing and pulling the hip and knee 90°, and extending and contracting the leg up and down. Each movement was repeated three times using slow and controlled motions, and no resistance was applied during the exercises. After the application, the patient was brought to the semi‐Fowler position and was allowed to stay in a comfortable position. The researcher applied in‐bed exercise for 15 min between 07.00 and 08.00 h in the morning and for 15 min between 19.00 and 20.00 h in the evening for 3 days, and repeated it twice a day for 3 days at 24‐h intervals before starting enteral nutrition.

Procedure of Control Group

The measurements of the control group were realised simultaneously with intervention groups without any intervention on the patient.

Data Collection

Data were collected by a researcher who served as the responsible nurse at the palliative care centre.

Abdominal distension was assessed by the researcher through abdominal palpation. The presence or absence of abdominal distension was evaluated by considering abdominal findings together with the patient's facial expressions and behavioural responses.

During the first follow‐up, the Patient Information Form, Comfort Behaviours Checklist, Wong–Baker Faces Pain Rating Scale and Gastrointestinal Complication Form were completed before the intervention and prior to enteral nutrition. The intervention was then applied twice daily for 15 min in the morning (07:00–08:00) and evening (19:00–20:00).

During the second and third follow‐ups, the Gastrointestinal Complication Form was completed before the intervention, and the same intervention protocol was continued.

During the fourth follow‐up, data were collected using the Comfort Behaviours Checklist, Wong–Baker Faces Pain Rating Scale, and Gastrointestinal Complication Form before morning nutrition (07:00–08:00), after which the study was terminated (Figure 1).

Study flow diagram (CONSORT).

Statistical Analysis

The data were analysed using the SPSS 25.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normal distribution of the data was assessed according to whether the skewness (−0.763–0.722) and kurtosis (−0.078–1.151) values were within ±1.5 [29]. Parametric tests were run for normally distributed data, while non‐parametric tests were run for non‐normally distributed data. The Chi‐square test and one‐way ANOVA were applied to compare demographic and medical characteristics between groups. Repeated measures ANOVA and two‐way mixed ANOVA were run for over‐time and group‐time interaction. Cochran's Q test and post hoc analyses were applied for the comparison of non‐parametric categorical data. Multiple comparisons were made using Bonferroni correction in post hoc analyses. The statistical significance level was accepted as p < 0.05.

Ethical Considerations

Prior to conducting the study, approval from the Mustafa Kemal University Non‐Interventional Research Ethics Committee (dated: 21.05.2024, meeting number: 05, decision number: 27) and necessary written permissions from the institution where the study was conducted (decision dated 06.08.2024) were obtained. All stages of the study adhered to the research principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and publication ethics principles. The data were collected between the dates of the study after the patients and their relatives who met the inclusion criteria and were voluntary to participate granted their written informed consent. The study was registered in the clinical trial database (No: NCT07125482).

The frequency of diarrhoea in the control group was significantly higher in the 2nd follow‐up compared to the 3rd and 4th follow‐up (p < 0.05) (Table 2).

The frequency of constipation in the abdominal massage group was significantly higher in the First follow‐up than in the third and fourth follow‐up (p < 0.001). The frequency of distension was higher in the first and second follow‐up than in the third and fourth follow‐up (p < 0.05) (Table 2).

The frequency of constipation in the in‐bed exercise group was higher in the first follow‐up than in the third and fourth follow‐up (p < 0.05). The frequency of vomiting was higher in the first follow‐up than in the third and fourth follow‐up (p < 0.05). The frequency of nausea was higher in the first follow‐up than in the fourth follow‐up (p < 0.05). Discussion

This study concluded that abdominal massage was found to reduce the frequency of constipation and abdominal distension, relieve pain and improve comfort. In‐bed exercise reduced the frequency of constipation, nausea, vomiting and abdominal distension, reduced the number of vomiting episodes, relieved pain and improved comfort. The literature has reported that abdominal massage stimulates bowel movements by relaxing the abdominal muscles, relieves pain caused by cramps and flatulation, and improves quality of life [30]. A meta‐analysis study reported that abdominal massage applied to enterally fed patients would reduce the frequency of constipation [11]. Furthermore, similar findings were reached in studies conducted with different patient groups. Abdominal massage has been found to reduce the frequency of constipation in adults with constipation [31], patients with hip fractures [32], palliative care patients [33], stroke patients [34], patients after operation [8], individuals in neonatal intensive care units [35], those in adult intensive care units [36], intubated patients in intensive care units, and enterally fed patients [7, 16]. Contrary to these findings, abdominal massage applied to intensive care unit patients has been detected to have no effect on constipation [37]. Overall, these studies highlight abdominal massage as a non‐invasive, low‐cost and safe non‐pharmacological intervention [30].

The study also found that both abdominal massage and in‐bed exercise reduced the frequency of constipation. Physical activity [38] and in‐bed exercises [7] have been found to have no impact on constipation. While these findings corroborate some results in the literature, they contradict some others. It was thought that these contradictions may be due to differences in the patient group and protocol differences, such as the frequency and time of massage.

The study revealed that abdominal massage had no effect on vomiting, whereas in‐bed exercise reduced its frequency. Vomiting is one of the serious clinical symptoms in enterally fed patients [39]. A meta‐analysis study reported that abdominal massage reduced the frequency of vomiting [11]. Abdominal massage applied to intubated patients in the intensive care unit [15, 40] and enterally fed patients [34, 37] has been found to reduce the frequency of vomiting. In contrast to these findings, a systematic review [41] and a study involving enterally fed patients in the intensive care unit [7, 14] reported no significant effect of abdominal massage on vomiting. Furthermore, neither in‐bed exercise [7] nor early bedside exercise training [22] applied to enterally fed patients in the intensive care unit had any effect on vomiting.

The study revealed that abdominal massage and in‐bed exercise reduced abdominal distension. Abdominal massage reduces abdominal distension by affecting the skin directly, stimulating the skin reflex vagus nerve, promoting gastric secretion and accelerating gastrointestinal peristalsis [11]. It has been reported that abdominal massage applied to adults [14] and intubated patients hospitalised in the intensive care unit reduces abdominal distension [7, 16, 40]. In‐bed exercise applied to enterally fed patients hospitalised in the intensive care unit reduces abdominal distension [7]. Contrary to these results, abdominal massage applied to patients in the intensive care unit has been found to have no effect on abdominal distension [37]. While some studies relied on the patient's self‐report to determine the distension, others determined it by measuring the abdominal circumference, which may have resulted in differences due to the lack of standardisation in measurements.

The study found that abdominal massage and in‐bed exercise relieved pain. Abdominal massage relieves pain by stimulating oxytocin release with analgesic and anxiolytic effects [42]. Exercise also has the effect of alleviating pain perception [43]. When examining the studies of abdominal massage with different patient groups, it has been found to be effective in relieving the pain of patients hospitalised in the intensive care unit [7, 44] after colonoscopy procedures [45] and undergoing orthopaedic and traumatology surgery [9]. Physical activity relieves neck pain [38], while mobilisation under nurse supervision after open cholecystectomy has no effect on pain [18].

The study indicated that abdominal massage and in‐bed exercise elevated the comfort level. The importance of physiotherapy for improving the comfort of palliative care patients has been emphasised [46]. Exercises practised on cancer patients in the palliative care phase enhance the quality of life of the patients [47]. In‐bed exercises practised on patients hospitalised in the intensive care unit improve the comfort level of patients [7]. Abdominal massage is another non‐pharmacological method that has been examined for its effect on comfort. Abdominal massage practised on different patient groups has been found to improve the comfort level in patients after colonoscopy [45], after orthopaedics and traumatology surgery [9], after abdominal surgery [48] and in the intensive care unit [7]. These findings are compatible with the literature and support the effectiveness and applicability of these non‐pharmacological methods in improving patient comfort.

Strengths and Limitations

One of the strengths of this study is that, to our knowledge, it is the first to evaluate the effects of abdominal massage and in‐bed exercise on gastrointestinal complications and patient comfort in enterally fed palliative care patients. The single‐centre design is not considered a limitation, as it contributes to the homogeneity of the study population.

This study has several limitations. It was conducted over a relatively short follow‐up period, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Blinding of patients and researchers was not possible due to the nature of the interventions. Gastric residual volume was not measured, and gastrointestinal complications were assessed based on clinically observable symptoms. Additionally, the use of medications affecting gastrointestinal motility was not systematically recorded, and enteral nutrition formulas were individualised according to patients' clinical characteristics and nutritional needs, which may have influenced gastrointestinal outcomes. Moreover, abdominal distension was evaluated subjectively by palpation and observation of patients' behavioural responses rather than through objective measurement methods, which may affect the accuracy of this assessment. Furthermore, the amount of nutrition and caloric intake received by patients was not quantified, limiting the objective evaluation of whether enteral nutrition targets were achieved.

Although inclusion criteria were based on participation in‐bed exercises, patients' functional and mobility status was not assessed using standardised quantitative tools. This is a methodological limitation that may have affected patient group homogeneity and the interpretability of the findings.

Conclusions

Based on the findings, the null hypothesis (H0) that abdominal massage and in‐bed exercise have similar effects on gastrointestinal complications and patient comfort was rejected. Based on the findings, it is recommended that abdominal massage and in‐bed exercises, which nurses can independently apply, are recommended as supportive interventions for enterally fed palliative care patients. Although information about these practices is provided in undergraduate nursing education, it is recommended that it be updated and reinforced with in‐service training and collaboration with physiotherapists be established.

This study applied abdominal massage and exercises twice daily, with each session lasting 15 min. However, the optimal duration, frequency and technique of these interventions have not yet been clarified. Therefore, their effects and application protocols should be standardised in future studies with larger samples and controlled studies.

Relevance for Clinical Practice

Enteral nutrition is a critical component of care in palliative care patients; nurses play a key role in managing potential complications. It is essential for nurses to be familiar with and capable of implementing non‐pharmacological methods independently to reduce complications and improve patient comfort. These results demonstrate that abdominal massage and in‐bed exercises interventions are practical, safe, low cost, and effective non‐pharmacological approaches that can be independently implemented by nurses in clinical practice to support patient well‐being in palliative care settings.
