Visit‐to‐Visit Systolic Blood Pressure Variability Predicts Long‐Term Cardiovascular Events After Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting
Authors: Pujuan Liu, Shang Wang, Huili Liu, Jing Wang, Man Zhang, Hong Shen, Sha Liu, Beibei Xing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis prospective observational study investigated whether systolic blood pressure variability (SBPV) during the transitional care period predicts long‐term outcomes after coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG). We enrolled 120 consecutive patients undergoing isolated CABG between 2020 and 2024 in a structured Transitional Care Service System. Office blood pressure was measured at scheduled visits. SBPV was quantified using the coefficient of variation (CV), and patients were stratified by median SBP‐CV (8.4%) into low‐ and high‐variability groups. The primary endpoint was major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (MACCE). During a median follow‐up of 46 months (corresponding to 34 months post‐landmark), 19 MACCE events occurred in the landmark cohort of 116 patients (4 early events were excluded). The high‐variability group experienced significantly more events than the low‐variability group (26.3% vs. 6.8%, p = 0.005), with markedly reduced event‐free survival (log‐rank p = 0.005). After multivariable adjustment for age, diabetes mellitus, number of measurements, and mean systolic blood pressure, high SBPV remained an independent predictor of MACCE (hazard ratio 2.85, 95% confidence interval 1.12–7.24, p = 0.028). Sensitivity analyses using alternative metrics confirmed these findings, whereas mean systolic blood pressure was not predictive. In conclusion, high visit‐to‐visit SBPV during postoperative transitional care independently predicts long‐term cardiovascular events after CABG. Monitoring blood pressure stability may enhance risk stratification in this high‐risk population.

Introduction

Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) remains the gold standard revascularization strategy for patients with multivessel coronary artery disease (CAD), particularly those with diabetes mellitus or complex anatomical involvement [1, 2, 3]. Despite significant advancements in surgical techniques, perioperative anesthesia, and cardiopulmonary bypass technologies, the long‐term efficacy of CABG is fundamentally limited by the durability of the bypass conduits [4]. While the left internal mammary artery (LIMA) exhibits excellent long‐term patency with rates exceeding 90% at 10 years, the saphenous vein graft (SVG)—which constitutes the majority of conduits used in multivessel revascularization—is prone to failure [5]. Contemporary data indicate that SVG failure rates can reach 10%–20% within the first year and approximately 50% by the tenth year [6]. Consequently, optimizing secondary prevention strategies, including aggressive antiplatelet and lipid‐lowering therapy, to preserve graft patency is a paramount objective in modern cardiac surgery [7].

Hypertension is a well‐established risk factor for graft failure and adverse cardiovascular events [8]. Current clinical guidelines, including the 2021 ACC/AHA Joint Committee Report on Coronary Artery Revascularization, emphasize strict blood pressure (BP) control, typically targeting levels below 130/80 mmHg [9]. However, these guidelines predominantly focus on “mean” BP levels derived from static, cross‐sectional measurements. Accumulating evidence suggests that this approach may be insufficient, as BP variability remains primarily a research topic in contemporary guidelines [10]. Visit‐to‐visit variability (VVV) of BP—defined as fluctuations in BP readings across multiple clinical visits over time—has emerged as a distinct, independent predictor of stroke, myocardial infarction, and all‐cause mortality [11].

The pathophysiological relevance of BP variability is increasingly recognized and may be particularly pronounced in the post‐CABG population [12]. Unlike native coronary arteries, which possess a robust muscular media and inherent autoregulatory capacity, venous grafts are thin‐walled conduits suddenly exposed to high‐pressure arterial hemodynamics, a transition that induces significant transcriptional and inflammatory changes [13]. Experimental models have demonstrated that erratic fluctuations in BP generate oscillatory shear stress (OSS) on the endothelium, a key hemodynamic disturbance that drives endothelial dysfunction [14]. This mechanical instability triggers a cascade of maladaptive responses, including the upregulation of adhesion molecules such as vascular cell adhesion molecule‐1 (VCAM‐1) and intercellular adhesion molecule‐1 (ICAM‐1), processes that are actively modulated by inflammatory pathways [15]. Furthermore, OSS promotes reactive oxygen species production and inflammatory cell infiltration, ultimately accelerating intimal hyperplasia and atherosclerosis in vein grafts [16]. Therefore, we hypothesize that a patient with a “stable” BP of 130 mmHg may have a significantly better prognosis than a patient whose BP fluctuates wildly around the same mean.

Despite this theoretical plausibility, clinical evidence linking BP variability to post‐CABG outcomes remains sparse [12]. A major barrier to such research is the logistical difficulty of obtaining high‐quality, longitudinal BP data in the “real‐world” setting, as accurate calculation of variability metrics often requires a minimum of multiple BP measurements over time [17]. Following hospital discharge, patients often enter a fragmented healthcare environment where follow‐up is sporadic, posing significant challenges for continuous data collection [17]. This period, known as the “Transitional Phase,” is a critical window of physiological adaptation, medication titration, and lifestyle modification [17]. Traditional retrospective studies relying on administrative databases often lack the granularity required to calculate these variability metrics accurately.

To bridge this knowledge gap, our center established a comprehensive, grant‐funded Transitional Care Service System. This nurse‐led initiative was designed to provide structured, longitudinal surveillance for post‐cardiac surgery patients. Leveraging the high‐fidelity data generated by this system, the present study aims to evaluate the prognostic value of visit‐to‐visit systolic BP variability (SBPV) on mid‐to‐long term major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (MACCE) in a cohort of CABG patients followed for up to 6 years.

Methods

Study Design and Ethical Considerations

This was a single‐center, prospective observational cohort study conducted at the Department of Cardiac Surgery of our hospital. The study design adhered to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) statement guidelines [18]. The protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee. All patients provided written informed consent for their data to be used for research purposes upon enrollment in the Transitional Care Service System.

Study Population

We screened consecutive patients who underwent isolated CABG surgery between January 1, 2020, and January 31, 2024. The recruitment window was selected to align with the initiation of the provincial research grant. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Age ≥ 18 years; (2) Successful isolated CABG (on‐pump or off‐pump) with LIMA and/or SVG conduits; (3) Survival to hospital discharge; (4) Formal enrollment in the Transitional Care Service System; (5) Availability of at least three valid outpatient BP measurements during the first 12 months post‐discharge to allow for variability calculation. It is important to emphasize that this final cohort represents CABG survivors who successfully integrated into the transitional care program, rather than the unselected, broader CABG population.

Exclusion criteria were rigorously applied to minimize confounding factors: (1) Concomitant valvular, aortic, or congenital heart surgery; (2) Emergency or salvage surgery; (3) History of persistent or permanent atrial fibrillation (AF), as irregular heart rhythms can significantly interfere with the accuracy of automated oscillometric BP devices; (4) End‐stage renal disease requiring hemodialysis, given the extreme hemodynamic fluctuations associated with dialysis; (5) Patients who were lost to follow‐up or withdrew from the transitional care program within the first 3 months.

The Transitional Care Service System

A unique feature of this study is the implementation of a structured Transitional Care Service System, led by a specialized team of cardiac nurses and overseen by the project leader. This system was developed to ensure continuity of care from the inpatient setting to the home environment. The intervention consisted of: Predischarge Education: Intensive counseling on medication adherence, lifestyle modification (salt restriction, smoking cessation), and self‐monitoring skills. Standardized Follow‐up Schedule: Patients were scheduled for outpatient clinic visits at 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 12 months, and annually thereafter. Telephone Support: A dedicated hotline was available for patients to report symptoms or clarify medication instructions. Data Registry: All clinical data, including BP measurements, medication changes, and adverse events, were prospectively entered into a dedicated digital database.

BP Measurement Protocol

To ensure data consistency and minimize measurement error, office BP was measured according to a strict protocol derived from the 2018 ESC/ESH Guidelines for the Management of Arterial Hypertension [19]. Measurements were performed by trained nurses using calibrated, automated oscillometric devices (Omron HEM‐7200, Omron Healthcare, Dalian, China). Specifically, BP was assessed at scheduled outpatient visit intervals of 1, 3, 6, and 12 months post‐discharge. While the formal protocol mandated these 4 scheduled visits, patients frequently had additional routine clinic visits during this first year. All valid BP measurements recorded strictly within this 12‐month window were included to enhance the reliability of the variability calculation [20]. The specific protocol was as follows [21]: Patients were asked to abstain from caffeine, smoking, and exercise for at least 30 min prior to measurement. They were seated in a quiet room with their back supported and feet flat on the floor for at least 5 min of rest. The cuff was sized appropriately for the patient's arm circumference and placed on the arm at heart level. Three consecutive readings were taken at 1‐min intervals. The first reading was discarded, and the average of the second and third readings was recorded as the visit BP. If the difference between the second and third readings exceeded 10 mmHg, a fourth measurement was taken.

Definition of BP Variability

The primary exposure variable was visit‐to‐visit Systolic Blood Pressure Variability (SBPV), calculated exclusively using BP readings obtained during the first 12 months postoperative window. While several metrics exist to quantify variability, we selected the Coefficient of Variation (CV) as our primary metric. CV is calculated as the standard deviation (SD) of SBP across visits divided by the mean SBP (CV = [SD / Mean] × 100%). This method was chosen because SBP variability is often positively correlated with the mean SBP level; utilizing CV allows for normalization, ensuring that variability is assessed relatively independent of the absolute BP magnitude [22]. For sensitivity analysis, we also calculated the SD and Variation Independent of Mean (VIM) [23].

Outcome Measures and Follow‐Up

The primary endpoint was MACCE (Major Adverse Cardiac and Cerebrovascular Events), defined as a composite of: (1) All‐cause Mortality: Death from any cause. (2) Non‐fatal Myocardial Infarction (MI): Diagnosed based on the Fourth Universal Definition of MI. (3) Stroke: New focal neurological deficit lasting >24 h with imaging confirmation (CT or MRI). (4) Repeat Revascularization: Any unplanned percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or redo‐CABG driven by graft failure or native disease progression. Only the first occurring event for each patient was included in the composite endpoint for the time‐to‐first‐event survival analysis.

Clinical follow‐up was conducted continuously through the Transitional Care Service System. The follow‐up period extended from the date of surgery until December 31, 2025. Outcome events were adjudicated by an independent clinical events committee blinded to the patients' BP variability status. Data sources included hospital electronic medical records, the transitional care database, and telephone interviews with patients or their next of kin.

Statistical Analysis

Sample size estimation was based on the preliminary data from our department, assuming a 2‐year MACCE rate of 15%. To detect a Hazard Ratio of 2.5 with 80% power and a 5% alpha error, a minimum of approximately 110 patients was required. We recruited 120 patients to account for potential attrition.

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median (interquartile range, IQR) depending on normality, assessed via the Shapiro–Wilk test. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. The cohort was dichotomized into “Low Variability” (Low‐CV) and “High Variability” (High‐CV) groups based on the median SBP‐CV value of the entire cohort.

Kaplan‐Meier survival curves were constructed to estimate the cumulative probability of event‐free survival, with differences compared using the log‐rank test. To avoid immortal time bias—since the calculation of BPV requires multiple visits over the first year post‐discharge—we employed a landmark analysis approach [24]. The follow‐up period for the time‐to‐event survival analysis strictly commenced at the 12‐month landmark after surgery. Patients who experienced a MACCE or were lost to follow‐up before this landmark were already excluded from the final cohort. Specifically, 4 patients experienced a MACCE prior to the 12‐month mark and were thus excluded from the survival analysis, yielding a final analytical cohort of 116 patients. To identify independent predictors of MACCE, multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were utilized. Covariates were selected based on a combination of clinical relevance and univariate significance (P < 0.10). Specifically, age and mean SBP were forced into the model due to their established biological role in cardiovascular risk, while diabetes mellitus was included due to its baseline distributional difference (p = 0.023). Additionally, to account for the potential effect of visit density, the total number of BP measurements was included as a covariate in the adjusted models. Given the limited number of events, we strictly adhered to the “rule of thumb” of 10 events per variable to prevent overfitting.

To verify that the association between BPV and adverse outcomes was not dependent on the specific metric used to quantify variability, we performed rigorous sensitivity analyses using SD and VIM. While SD is the simplest measure of dispersion, it can be correlated with the mean. VIM, calculated as k × SD / Meanx (where x is derived from the regression of log SD on log Mean), is theoretically constructed to be uncorrelated with mean BP levels.

A two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY) and R software version 4.3.1.

After applying the exclusion criteria—including 8 concomitant surgeries, 5 emergency procedures, 3 in‐hospital deaths, and 22 patients with insufficient data or loss to follow‐up—the final analysis cohort comprised 120 patients. The study population had a mean age of 64.5 ± 8.2 years, and 72.5% were male. Hypertension was prevalent in 68.3% of the cohort, and 35.0% had Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus. The median SBP‐CV was 8.4% (IQR: 6.5%–10.2%), which served as the cutoff for group stratification. Comparative analysis revealed that patients in the High‐CV group were significantly older (66.8 ± 8.0 vs. 62.1 ± 7.9 years, p = 0.002) and had a higher prevalence of diabetes (45.0% vs. 25.0%, p = 0.023). Notably, there were no statistically significant differences in body mass index (BMI), left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), or baseline medication usage (including beta‐blockers, statins, and antiplatelet agents) between the two groups. Crucially, the mean systolic BP during follow‐up was comparable between the Low‐CV and High‐CV groups (130.1 ± 9.8 vs. 132.4 ± 10.5 mmHg, p = 0.21), indicating that the High‐CV group represented a phenotype of “instability” rather than uncontrolled hypertension per se.

BP Trajectory

Patients underwent a median of 6 (IQR: 4–8) outpatient BP measurements strictly within the first 12 months post‐discharge. BP readings obtained after the 12‐month landmark were not used for the calculation of baseline BPV to avoid incorporating post‐event data. An analysis of the temporal trends showed that while the mean BP levels in both groups generally trended towards the target (<130/80 mmHg) over time, the High‐CV group exhibited distinct oscillatory patterns.

Clinical Outcomes (Mid‐to‐Long Term Follow‐Up)

The clinical follow‐up was completed for 100% of the enrolled cohort until the study end date of December 31, 2025, or the time of death. The median follow‐up duration was 46 months from the date of surgery, which corresponds to a median of 34 months (IQR: 24–46 months) from the 12‐month landmark, with a maximum follow‐up of 72 months. During this period, a total of 22 MACCE events were recorded overall from the date of surgery. However, as per the 12‐month landmark protocol, 4 early events were excluded. In the landmark cohort of 116 patients, 19 first MACCE events occurred, yielding a post‐landmark incidence of 16.4%. The breakdown of these 19 events was: 2 cardiovascular deaths (which accounted for all of the all‐cause mortality cases), 6 non‐fatal myocardial infarctions, 4 strokes, and 7 cases of repeat revascularization.

Stratification by variability status revealed a striking disparity in outcomes. The incidence of MACCE was significantly higher in the High‐CV group (15 events, 26.3%) compared to the Low‐CV group (4 events, 6.8%) (p = 0.005). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis (Figure 2 ) demonstrated that the event‐free survival curves began to diverge approximately 6 months post‐landmark and continued to separate throughout the 4‐year follow‐up period. The cumulative survival probability at 48 months post‐landmark was 91.5% in the Low‐CV group versus 73.2% in the High‐CV group (Log‐rank p = 0.005).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to utilize granular, longitudinal data from a dedicated Transitional Care Service System to investigate the prognostic value of visit‐to‐visit BP variability in patients after CABG. Our findings, based on a rigorous 4‐year recruitment and up to 6‐year follow‐up (2020‐2026), provide compelling evidence that high systolic BP variability is an independent and potent predictor of mid‐to‐long term MACCE. Patients with high variability faced nearly a three‐fold increased risk of adverse events compared to their stable counterparts, a risk that persisted even after adjusting for mean BP and traditional risk factors like diabetes.

A critical strength of our study lies in the consistency of our findings across multiple metrics of variability. While CV was our primary measure, the sensitivity analyses utilizing SD and VIM yielded congruent prognostic signals. This consistency is clinically significant. SD is the most widely understood measure of dispersion but can be heavily influenced by the absolute level of BP. In contrast, VIM is mathematically constructed to be uncorrelated with mean BP, ensuring that the observed association is driven by fluctuation itself rather than the severity of hypertension. Our observation that SBP‐VIM remains a strong predictor of MACCE (HR 2.92, p = 0.024) effectively rules out the possibility that the risk associated with BPV is merely a surrogate for uncontrolled mean BP. This aligns with the findings from the ASCOT‐BPLA trial and other large‐scale registries, reinforcing the concept that BP instability represents a distinct pathophysiological entity—possibly reflecting autonomic dysfunction or arterial stiffness—that requires independent clinical attention beyond standard hypertension management.

The Pathophysiological Link: Why Variability Matters in CABG

The results of this study align with the emerging concept of “residual vascular risk” [25]. While traditional secondary prevention focuses on lowering lipid levels and mean BP, our data suggest that hemodynamic instability is a silent contributor to graft failure [26]. The mechanism linking BP variability (BPV) to adverse outcomes in CABG patients is likely multifactorial but centers on the unique biology of the SVG [27].

Native coronary arteries are adapted to pulsatile flow. In contrast, saphenous veins are evolved for steady, low‐pressure flow. When a vein is harvested and grafted into the arterial circulation, it undergoes a process of “arterialization,” characterized by medial thickening and remodeling [28]. However, this adaptation is imperfect. Experimental studies have shown that OSS—which is exacerbated by fluctuating BP—is particularly deleterious to the venous endothelium [29]. OSS downregulates the expression of anti‐atherogenic genes (such as KLF2 and eNOS) and upregulates pro‐inflammatory adhesion molecules [29]. In patients with high visit‐to‐visit BPV, the graft endothelium is subjected to a “battering ram” effect of constantly changing wall stress, which may accelerate the development of neointimal hyperplasia and subsequent graft thrombosis.

Furthermore, high BPV is often a surrogate marker for increased arterial stiffness and autonomic dysfunction [30]. Patients with stiff arteries lack the “Windkessel” effect to buffer changes in stroke volume, leading to wider swings in BP. This creates a vicious cycle where stiff arteries cause high BPV, and high BPV further damages the vascular wall.

The Role of Transitional Care: From Measurement to Management

A pivotal aspect of our study is the context in which it was performed: a nurse‐led Transitional Care Service System. In routine clinical practice, BP variability is often overlooked because different clinicians see the patient at different times, and no one connects the data points to discern fluctuation patterns. Our funded system addressed this gap by ensuring continuity of data collection and monitoring. The nursing team played a crucial role not only in measuring BP but also in identifying the underlying causes of its variability. Supported by evidence, nurse‐led interventions have been shown to improve long‐term BP control and patient self‐management by addressing behavioral factors [31, 32]. While formal data on adherence were not the primary focus of this analysis, we qualitatively observed that BP fluctuations in the high‐variability group were often temporally associated with periods of medication non‐adherence or seasonal variations. This implies that BP variability is not merely a physiological parameter but can serve as a “behavioral biomarker” indicative of daily self‐management and adherence challenges.

This perspective has profound implications for nursing practice and healthcare policy. It suggests that the goal of transitional care should evolve from simply “achieving target BP” to “achieving smooth and sustained BP control.” Nurses are uniquely positioned to detect these fluctuations and intervene through personalized education and adherence monitoring, a core component of effective transitional care programs [33, 34]. Structured interventions such as tele‐monitoring or guided home BP monitoring (HBPM) could be integrated into future transitional care models to provide denser, more actionable data points [35]. Implementing such nurse‐led, technology‐supported models holds promise for improving patient outcomes and facilitating the transition from hospital to home [34].

Clinical Implications

For the clinician, our findings suggest that a patient with a “normal” average BP but high VVV should be flagged as “high risk,” as such variability is an independent predictor of adverse cardiovascular outcomes even in normotensive individuals [36]. Management strategies might need to be tailored for this phenotype. Pharmacologically, Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs) like amlodipine have been shown to reduce visit‐to‐visit BPV more effectively than other antihypertensive classes, as demonstrated in post‐hoc analyses of major trials [37]. Among antihypertensive agents, long‐acting CCBs and diuretics are considered the most effective for BPV control and should be prioritized for this patient profile [38]. In our cohort, CCB usage was numerically higher in the high‐variability group, likely reflecting an attempt by clinicians to control difficult hypertension. However, robust prospective trials are needed to test whether specifically targeting BPV reduction (using these agents) translates into improved hard endpoints, such as graft patency, as current evidence remains largely hypothesis‐generating [39].

Strengths and Limitations

The primary strength of this study is the high quality of the longitudinal data, facilitated by the dedicated research grant and nursing team. Unlike administrative database studies, our BP measurements were standardized, and our follow‐up was rigorous (median 46 months, up to 6 years), allowing us to capture mid‐to‐long term events. Third, the robustness of our results was rigorously tested using multiple variability metrics (CV, SD, and VIM), confirming that the prognostic value of BPV is not an artifact of a specific calculation method. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the sample size (n = 120) is relatively modest, which limited the number of covariates we could include in the multivariable model to avoid overfitting. Nonetheless, we acknowledge that with only 19 post‐landmark outcome events, our multivariable Cox model may still be prone to overfitting and estimate instability, limiting the robustness of the hazard ratios. Second, this is a single‐center study in Hebei, China, which may limit generalizability to other ethnic groups. Third, we did not have angiographic follow‐up for all patients, so we used clinical events (MACCE) as the endpoint rather than graft patency per se. Furthermore, by excluding in‐hospital deaths and those lost early to follow‐up, our cohort represents a specific survivor population engaged in transitional care, which may introduce selection bias.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this prospective cohort study demonstrates that high visit‐to‐visit SBPV during the transitional care phase is a significant and independent predictor of mid‐to‐long term MACCE in patients after CABG. These findings challenge the traditional “mean‐centric” view of BP management and highlight the critical value of structured, nurse‐led transitional care systems. Future strategies for secondary prevention should incorporate BP stability as a therapeutic target to improve the long‐term prognosis of this vulnerable patient population.
