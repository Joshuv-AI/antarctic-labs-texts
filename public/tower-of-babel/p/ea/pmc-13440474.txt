High-fidelity nanoflares: design strategies, response mechanisms, and biomedical applications
Authors: Yibo Zhou, Aoshuang Xu, Ke Quan, Yanli Lei, Xiaohan Zhang, Juewen Liu, Zhihe Qing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nanoflares, built from a nanoparticle core and a functional nucleic acid shell, have been widely used in various fields, such as programmable DNA assembly, biosensing, drug delivery and therapy. However, rigorous physiological environments (e.g. pH, polarity, biothiols and nucleases) can affect the stability of nanoflares, causing false positive signals, side-effects and off-target responses, thereby severely limiting their biological applications. Thus, the improvement of signaling accuracy is critical to breaking through the barriers for practical applications of nanoflares. In recent years, various methods including signal transduction, surface passivation of nanoparticles, replacement of the anchoring group, and spatial isolation of nanoflares, were proposed to improve the signal fidelity in biosensing and therapy. This review summarizes the development of high-fidelity nanoflares, systematically elaborates the design principles and sensing mechanisms, and outlines the obstacles remaining in this field, aiming to improve practical applications of nanoflares in biological systems.

Introduction

The concept of nanoflares was proposed by Mirkin's group in 2007,1 and involves fluorescent oligonucleotide-functionalized nanoparticles that can detect intracellular RNA with a turn-on signal. Gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) are the most commonly used core material to load functional nucleic acid probes because of their unique chemical and physical properties, such as controllable particle size and morphology,2,3 resistance to oxidation,4 low toxicity,5 ease of DNA conjugation,6,7 strong surface modification capabilities,8,9 elevated fluorescence extinction efficiencies and excellent biocompatibility.10–13 The most striking and exciting feature is the facile uptake of nanoflares (which belong to spherical nucleic acids) by various types of cells and tissue penetration without the need of any transfection agents.14,15 In recent years, significant advancements have been made for nanoflares by expanding the nucleic acid shell to aptamers and DNAzymes,16,17 extending the application to bio-detection of critical intracellular small molecules,18 metal ions,19 and proteins.20,21 Over the past two decades, nanoflares have attracted widespread attention as a versatile nanomaterial platform for biosensing and bioimaging, as evidenced by the rapidly growing body of literature on their design and applications.

A nanoflare is composed of two basic elements: a nanoparticle core and a nucleic acid shell.22 The interaction force between the core and the shell affects the stability of nanoflares, especially in complex biological systems.23 After the initial proof-of-concept work, critical issues have been identified for the application of nanoflares. For example, high concentrations of endogenous biothiols (glutathione (GSH),24,25 cysteine (Cys),26,27 and homocysteine (Hcy)) and nucleases (TREX1, DNase II, and DNase I) are present inside cells,28–32 which can cause the dissociation or degradation of nucleic acids, resulting in unavoidable false positive signals, side-effects and off-target responses. Thus, enhancing signal accuracy is pivotal to overcoming the bottlenecks in the application of nanoflares.

To overcome the limitations of conventional nanoflares, several new nanoflares, including enveloped nanoflares, fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET)-based nanoflares, surface-passivated nanoflares and anchoring group-substituted nanoflares, collectively named high-fidelity nanoflares, were developed leveraging distinct design architectures. The term “high-fidelity nanoflares” refers to nanoflare systems that are rationally engineered to improve signal reliability and analytical accuracy in complex biological environments by minimizing nonspecific activation, enhancing structural stability, and faithfully transducing target-recognition events into detectable signals. We herein review the design strategies and response mechanisms of these high-fidelity nanoflares, and the application of high-fidelity nanoflares in biosensing, drug delivery and bioimaging is discussed (Fig. 1). In addition, the existing challenges and future prospects in the field are also discussed.

Functional nucleic acid probes

Nucleic acids play fundamental roles in life, and not only carry genetic information but also orchestrate a multitude of cellular events, such as transcriptional regulation, cell proliferation, and lineage commitment.33 Nucleic acids have predictable single- or double-stranded structures according to the Watson–Crick principles.34 Thus, the function of nucleic acids can be rationally designed by tuning nucleotide sequences, immobilization density and incorporating chemical modifications, which enhance the stability and integrity of nucleic acids and can be used for biosensing,35,36 drug transport and therapy.37–40

Functional nucleic acid probes comprise aptamers, DNAzymes, ribozymes, triplex DNA, DNA origami and other self-assembled DNA nanostructures.41,42 By regulating the complementary base pairing, these molecular probes have been widely utilized for monitoring a broad range of biomarkers associated with diseases, including metal ions,43 nucleic acids (mRNA and miRNA), proteases,44–46 and microenvironmental changes.47 Meanwhile, by virtue of high programmability and specificity, functional nucleic acid probes exhibit excellent biocompatibility and sensitivity,47–49 making them particularly suitable for sensing low abundance of targets, such as miRNA, through target-induced structure transformation.50,51

Traditional nanoflares

Nanoflares have emerged as a promising platform to overcome the limitations of nucleic acid probes. Conventionally, traditional nanoflares were constructed from AuNPs and a fluorochrome-labelled nucleic acid probe via Au–S bonding,1,52 and the fluorescence signal of the nucleic acid probe was quenched by AuNPs.53 When the nanoflares enter live cells, the target can bind with the captured nucleic acid strand to liberate the complementary strand, and the separation of fluorophores from AuNPs will restore the fluorescence.54–58 In contrast to typical free nucleic acid sequences, each nanoflare contains a large amount of nucleic acid probes, and the high local density facilitates superior detection sensitivity.59,60 More importantly, the DNA corona on the surface of AuNPs generates significant steric hindrance and high local salt concentrations against nucleases,61–64 protecting the nucleic acid probes from destruction by nucleases. Additionally, the high-density surface nucleic acid architecture of nanoflares facilitates multivalent interactions with receptors on the cytomembrane. This multi-site binding drives receptor clustering and subsequent sequestration within lipid rafts or caveolae-like membrane domains, thereby triggering efficient receptor-mediated endocytosis into live cells and ultimately reinforcing the translational potency of such nucleic acid nanoprobes in practical application.65–67

However, as noted in the previous section, biological systems are substantially more hostile than in vitro experimental environments. For instance, the concentration of GSH ranges from 0.5–1 mM in normal cells to as high as 1–10 mM in tumor cells.68,69 Similarly, cysteine (Cys) levels are typically 0.1–0.2 mM in normal cells but are elevated to 0.5–1 mM in tumor cells.70 Furthermore, nuclease activities vary significantly across different cell lines. Such highly heterogeneous microenvironments pose formidable challenges for the reliable application of nanoflares in complex biological systems. Firstly, the Au–S bond plays a pivotal role in nanoflares, which will be destroyed by the high concentrations of biothiols, inducing unavoidable off-target activation. Secondly, although the dense DNA corona provides steric protection for nucleic acid probes, it remains insufficient to completely shield the sequences from nucleases.71 Lastly, while employing the AuNPs as an integrated nanocarrier maximizes the advantages of nucleic acid probes, it also poses potential biosafety risks and causes side effects.72 Hence, improving the ability of conventional nanoflares to address the existing issue is urgent.

High-fidelity nanoflares

In recent years, several strategies, including signal transduction, AuNP surface passivation, anchoring group replacement, and spatial isolation of nanoflares, have been developed to enhance nanoflare stability (Fig. 2). FRET-based nanoflares generate a ratiometric fluorescence signal exclusively upon specific target nucleic acid recognition. This behavior clearly distinguishes target-specific binding from signals induced by non-specific degradation of the nucleic acid probes, effectively preventing false-positive responses (Fig. 2A). Meanwhile, both surface modification coatings on AuNPs and anchoring group substitution at the nucleic acid probe termini can significantly enhance the interaction force between the probes and AuNPs (Fig. 2B and C). This shielding effect effectively prevents the degradation of nucleic acid probes by biological interferents, thereby improving signal accuracy. Finally, enveloped nanoflares leverage the protective properties of the nanocarrier, which creates a stable operational environment for the probes (Fig. 2D), and thus a higher signal fidelity can be achieved. In the following sections, a detailed discussion of these high-fidelity nanoflares will be presented.

FRET-based nanoflares

To address the insufficient stability of traditional nanoflares in intracellular applications, the FRET strategy was introduced into the signal generation process by Wang's group.73 Different from the single-dye-labelled nanoflares, two fluorophores were labelled on a functional nucleic acid probe with the FRET effect. Target interaction triggers the self-hybridization of the FRET-based nanoflares, facilitating efficient non-radiative energy transfer between the fluorophore pair (donor and acceptor). Consequently, a ratiometric fluorescence response is generated, providing a robust signal for target quantification. In this case, even if the nucleic acid probes are displaced from the AuNPs by endogenous biothiols, the FRET process remains inactive, thereby effectively avoiding false-positive signals. As shown in Fig. 3A, a hairpin nucleic acid probe labelled with a donor (fluorescein, FAM) at the 5′ end and an acceptor (tetramethylrhodamine, TAMRA) at the 3′ end was assembled on AuNPs. Without the target, the recognition sequence remains bound to the AuNPs, separating FAM from TAMRA and resulting in low FRET efficiency, thereby preserving the fluorescence of FAM. Upon target binding, the recognition sequence is displaced and folds into a hairpin structure, thereby bringing FAM and TAMRA into close proximity. The shortened fluorophore distance facilitates efficient FRET, leading to enhanced TAMRA fluorescence emission. Therefore, the fluorescence emission ratio of TAMRA to FAM can serve as a reliable signal for quantifying the target content. The fluorescence results demonstrated that this method effectively evaded the false-positive signal generated by GSH and DNase I, thus the signal stability was enhanced compared to conventional nanoflares.73 Additionally, by changing the functional nucleic acid probes with i-motif sequences, this FRET-based nanoflares can be used to sense the intracellular pH fluctuations (Fig. 3B).74

Following this study, this team further refined the FRET strategy by introducing a functional nucleic acid probe onto the AuNPs.75 As illustrated in Fig. 3C, the miRNA target can bind with nanoflares to generate the fluorescence signal similar to previous work. Meanwhile, endogenous mRNA can also bind with nanoflares to liberate the hybridized single strand (R*), which will bind with the functional nucleic acid (R) to liberate the miRNA target for the cycling reaction. Thus, the upgraded nanoflare system achieved signal amplification and enhanced detection sensitivity, making it particularly suitable for the detection of low-abundance targets. To further realize the on-demand sensing, a photo-responsive group was integrated into the functional nucleic acid probe by this team (Fig. 3D).76 This strategy enables the precise activation of nanoflares at predefined spatiotemporal coordinates, thereby facilitating highly controllable target detection with excellent spatiotemporal resolution.

In addition to nucleic acid targets, Wu's team developed a FRET nanoflare for sensing endogenous triphosadenine (ATP).77 A two-structured domain DNA, comprising a poly (A) domain and an ATP-adapted recognition domain, was designed to hybridize with a two-dye-labelled single strand to form a functional DNA duplex. In the absence of ATP, the fluorescence of the acceptor (Cy5) was quenched by AuNPs and the FRET between Cy3 and Cy5 was restrained because of the far distance. Upon binding to ATP, the recognition sequence dissociates from the ATP aptamer and forms a hairpin structure, bringing the two fluorophores into proximity and subsequently modulating the fluorescence signal. Notably, active proteases have emerged as prominent biomarkers for various diseases. Xu and colleagues developed a FRET-based nanoflare for sensing telomerase for the first time (Fig. 3E).78 The recognition sequence of the functional nucleic acid probe was changed as the telomerase primer, which can be extended by telomerase to hybridize with the complementary sequences, leading to the formation of a hairpin structure and subsequent fluorescence activation. In contrast, Peng and co-workers developed a FRET-based nanoflare with telomerase as fuel to realize the signal amplification.79 The recognition of the target microRNA exposes the telomerase primer in the capture probe, and the hexamer telomeric repeats (TTAGGG) can be extended with endogenous telomerase. In this case, the endogenous catalytic strand (CS) can hybridize with the formed sequence to liberate the target microRNA for the next circular reaction in the system, resulting in high sensitivity for miRNA detection. During the progress of recognition of target, the FRET design minimizes non-specific signals from biothiols, thereby enhancing the precision and sensitivity of the assay.

Similarly, this analytical scope of nanoflares can be extended to the detection of free ions. Wang and co-workers developed a nanoflare for the detection of K+ ion.80 As displayed in Fig. 3F, FAM and TAMRA were grafted onto the G-quadruplex sequences, which hybridized with a short single-stranded oligonucleotide to adhere to the AuNPs. In the absence of K+, the G-quadruplex sequence remains in an unfolded state, thereby suppressing FRET between the fluorophore pair. Following cellular internalization, K+ can specially bind with the G-quadruplex sequence to form a stereochemical structure, which detached from the anchoring strand to emit fluorescence from TAMRA. By virtue of the G-tetrahedral sequences, this nanoflare has high selectivity for K+ and can detect fluctuations in K+ levels in cells.

Despite the progress achieved in the above studies, the extinction ability of individual AuNPs is inadequate. Attenuated fluorescence can be detected for nanoflares even in the absence of a target, resulting in a high background signal and a low signal-to-background ratio (SBR), which limit their applicability for the detection of low-abundance targets. To address this issue, Huang and co-workers proposed a nanoflare integrated with an organic quencher (BHQ-2) for sensing ampicillin (AMP).81 The sulfhydryl-modified oligonucleotides were first immobilized on the surface of AuNPs and hybridized with a TAMRA-labelled complementary DNA strand (TAMRA-cDNA). Subsequently, BHQ-2 quenchers were conjugated to the AuNP surface. By means of the double quenching effect by AuNPs and BHQ-2, the fluorescence of TAMRA was thoroughly quenched in the absence of AMP. Upon addition of AMP, the TAMRA-cDNA is liberated from AuNPs and the fluorescence was restored. This strategy effectively suppresses the background signal and enhances detection sensitivity compared to traditional nanoflares. Furthermore, this design can be adapted to detect not only AMP but also other antibiotics.

Compared with conventional nanoflares, FRET-based nanoflares can effectively minimize the false positive signal generated from non-specific cleavage (biothiols, nucleases, etc.). However, this design inherently involves a trade-off in absolute sensitivity. The introduction of a FRET pair partially decouples the probe from the AuNP quenching field, reducing the maximum achievable quenching efficiency and thus elevating the background fluorescence. As a result, ratiometric FRET nanoflares typically exhibit limited detection limits. Moreover, although FRET-based nanoflares rely on ratiometric signal variations for target detection, the differential photobleaching between the donor and acceptor fluorophores remains a primary source of ratiometric signal drift. This discrepant photostability constitutes one of the most significant practical limitations of FRET nanoflares, as it potentially introduces confounding artifacts and compromises the fidelity of long-term quantitative imaging in complex biological matrixes. Concurrently, FRET-based nanoflares are incapable of preventing interferents from degrading or disrupting the peripheral nucleic acid probes, which severely compromise their response sensitivity toward the target. Additionally, this strategy necessitates two different fluorophore labels, which increases the overall cost of the experiment. Therefore, there is a need to develop more advanced strategies to enhance the ability of high-fidelity nanoflares.

Anchoring group-substituted nanoflares

The Au–S bond serves as a classic way to functionalize AuNPs, and they have been widely utilized in fluorescence, photoacoustic, Raman, and electrochemical biosensors.82–84 However, the inherent instability of the Au–S bond remains a significant challenge for practical in vivo applications; therefore, replacing the terminal thiol group of the nucleic acid probe has emerged as a promising developmental direction. Tang and co-workers proposed a new strategy that replaced the Au–S bond with an Au–selenium (Se) bond, based on the understanding that selenium possesses even stronger stability than sulfur.85 In comparison to the Au–S bond-based control nanoflares, the fluorescence intensity of Au–Se nanoflares remained stable for long time in the presence of GSH. Conversely, the fluorescence intensity of control Au–S nanoflares enhanced significantly, demonstrating that the Au–Se bond provides improved stability for nucleic acid immobilization compared with the Au–S bond (Fig. 4A). On the basis of this research, matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP2) was accurately detected,86 and this team changed the single-stranded nucleic acid into a functional molecular beacon, further confirming the feasibility of this strategy.87,88 The mitigation of biothiol interference in Au–Se-based nanoflares enables high-fidelity imaging in live cells with improved stability and selectivity.

However, detecting a single biomarker is insufficient to precisely monitor pathological changes. For instance, certain biomarkers that are overexpressed in cancerous environments are also present in healthy cells.89–95 Therefore, Tang and co-workers developed an Au–Se-based nanoflare that simultaneously responds to two targets for precisely diagnosing the cancer cells.96 As shown in Fig. 4B, Se-modified molecular beacons were immobilized on AuNPs. Since the Au–Se bond has higher stability against biothiol interference compared with the conventional Au–S bond, the fluorescence of this nanoflare is effectively quenched in the presence of GSH. Concurrently, this nanoflare enables simultaneous detection of two kinds of miRNAs that are highly expressed in cancer cells and emit distinct fluorescence signals. This strategy not only minimizes biothiol interference but also allows the discrimination of multiple nucleic acid targets, enabling precise differentiation of cancer cells from normal cells and high-fidelity fluorescence imaging of the tumor tissue in complex physiological environments. Analogous to this approach, the combination of two biomarkers (urokinase-type plasminogen activator (uPA)/MMP2, uPA/matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9), and MMP-1/MMP-12) can be synchronously detected with one Au–Se-based nanoflare in live cells.97–99

Given that miRNAs are also expressed in normal cells, Tang and co-workers integrated the unique APE1 enzyme-activated site into the molecular beacon to construct nanoflares for accurately sensing miRNAs in cancer cells.100 As shown in Fig. 4C, the Se-modified hairpin nucleic acid probe (Cy3-H2) and Se-modified functionalized nucleic acid containing APE1-recognized site (blocked H1) were bound to AuNPs to prepare the nanoflare. Following cellular uptake, the AP site is first cleaved by APE1, exposing the H1 site for miRNA-21 binding and triggering a catalyzed hairpin assembly (CHA) cascade. The resulting CHA product hybridized with Cy3-H2, generating a fluorescence signal. Because the APE1 is particularly expressed in tumor cells and the Au–Se-modified nanoflare has great capacity for resisting interference, this strategy enables precise discrimination between normal cells and cancer cells.

Meanwhile, to monitor the progression of apoptosis, Tang's group developed a two-site activated nanoflare based on Au–Se for imaging Cyt c and caspase-9 during apoptosis (Fig. 4D).101 A Cy5-labeled Cyt c aptamer was first immobilized on the AuNPs through hybridization with a complementary single strand, which was partially anchored to the AuNPs via Au–Se bonds. Subsequently, a caspase-9 cleavable peptide chain, labeled with FITC and the selenol moiety, was also attached to AuNPs. This nanoflare exhibited negligible fluorescence due to the efficient quenching effect of the AuNPs. In the presence of cytochrome c (Cyt c), the aptamer binds with Cyt c to recover the fluorescence signal of Cy5. Meanwhile, the caspase-9 secreted from cells during apoptosis will cleave the peptide to lighten the fluorescence of FITC. The experimental results indicated that Au–Se-based nanoflares were capable of real-time monitoring of changes in Cyt c and caspase-9 levels during apoptosis while effectively minimizing interference from intracellular biothiols. Also, the liver is a vital organ for metabolism and detoxification, capable of chemically modifying both endogenous and exogenous substances to facilitate their excretion. However, hepatocytes contain high levels of GSH, which may alter the surface properties of AuNPs and affect their retention, targeting, and clearance in the bloodstream.102 This special environment prompted Jiang and co-workers to compare the hepatic biotransformation pathways of Au–S and Au–Se bonds.103 Specifically, as shown in Fig. 4E, Au–S bonds undergo ligand exchange in high concentrations of GSH, and the AuNPs cannot enter into the cells but are excreted via the renal pathway in urine. In contrast, owing to their excellent stability, Au–Se-based nanoflares can target the liver via IgG, undergo internalization within live cells and finally are excreted via the hepatic pathway. This strategy provides a pathway for the targeting and transport of nanoparticles, offering new opportunities for the design and optimization of nanoflares in therapy.

To further enhance the stability of nanoflares in rigorous cellular environments, Huang and co-workers proposed a diselenol-modified peptide strategy to stabilize the nanoflare.104 As shown in Fig. 5A, the peptide was terminally substituted with diselenide, and the other side was modified with the fluorophore, which conjugated with AuNPs to form the nanoflare. Experimental results demonstrated that the diselenol-modified nanoflares exhibited significantly enhanced resistance to biothiols compared to the control nanoflare modified with a single Au–S/Se bond. Furthermore, fluorescence imaging results demonstrated that this nanoflare could accurately image enterokinase levels in HeLa cells under physiological conditions.

Apart from the use of selenium, Lu's group developed a fluorine (F)-substituted single-stranded sequence to functionalize AuNPs, enabling high-fidelity imaging (Fig. 5B).105 As a highly electronegative element, F forms a strongly polarized C–F bond with carbon and can interact with other functional groups through electrostatic/polarization or stereoelectronic effects. Consequently, the terminal fluorination of nucleic acids confers enhanced stability upon their assembly with AuNPs. Experimental results indicated that the Au–F nanoflares exhibited greater resistance to biothiols compared to conventional nanoflares, achieving high-fidelity imaging, improved sensitivity, and accurate separating capacity between normal and cancer cells.

Besides, Xiong and co-workers employed a rapid method to synthesize AuNPs and immobilize peptide chains on the surface via the alkynyl–gold bond (Au–CC), which is used for detecting caspase-3 in cancer cells (Fig. 5C).106 With theoretical calculations, the binding energy of Au–CC (−3.67 eV) is found to be higher than that of Au–S bonds (−2.16 eV) and Au–Se bonds (−2.24 eV), thus the nanoflare constructed from Au–CC exhibited excellent stability in the system containing high content of salt; it can also shield against interference from pH changes, temperature fluctuations and biothiols. Additionally, the loading density of acetylenic-modified peptides on the surface of AuNPs is greater than that of thiol- and selenol-modified peptides, enhancing the sensitivity of the assay. In the intracellular environment, Au–CC-based nanoflares can effectively resist ligand exchange of GSH, Cys, and Hcy, ensuring high fidelity of the fluorescence signal in complex physiological environments. Leveraging this platform, Kong and co-workers constructed a furin detection platform,107 which can be used for robust detection of furin in non-invasive patient samples such as urine or blood, thereby broadening the biomedical applications of Au–CC nanoflares.

Changing the terminal anchored group of functionalized molecular tools can dramatically enhance the stability of nanoflares, making them more robust for practical applications. Nevertheless, certain limitations remain to be addressed. For instance, selenol is more prone to oxidation than thiol, posing a substantial obstacle for the long-term storage of nanoflares. Meanwhile, although F modification successfully enhances nuclease resistance, its complete non-biodegradability in vivo poses risks of accumulative biotoxicity. And the synthesis process is demanding, and most of such probes are not commercially available. Moreover, the reported studies have primarily concentrated on in vitro and short-term cellular experiments, while the long-term stability metabolic pathways and potential toxicity of nanoflares in vivo were not investigated.

Surface-passivated nanoflares

In addition to enhancing the stability of nanoflares through modification of the ligand, another approach is developed to replace the nanoparticle.108 Aside from Au, silver (Ag), platinum (Pt), and palladium (Pd) also exhibit similar optical properties. Based on this consideration, our group synthesized AuNP@Pt by reducing the amount of chloroplatinic acid (H2PtCl6) on the surface of AuNPs.109,110 Compared with conventional AuNPs, core–shell AuNP@Pt not only exhibits significantly enhanced resistance against biothiols but also retains localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) properties comparable to bare AuNPs, rendering it a highly promising delivery vector. The stability of Pt–S was significantly enhanced due to its low ligand exchange rate and greater stability to ligands compared to Au (Fig. 6A). To further confirm this point, the Au–S- and Pt–S-based nanoflares were prepared to evaluate the stability. Experimental results indicated that the fluorescence signal-to-background ratio of Pt–S nanoflares was higher than that of Au–S nanoflares after reaction with the target (miRNA-21) in the presence of biothiols. Also, this superior nanoflare can distinguish the cancer cells from normal cells in the mixed system. This study proposed a stable coating on AuNPs, offering a novel approach to enhance the stability of nanoflares.

Owing to the thiol resistance of Pt–S bonds and their much slower ligand exchange rate compared to Au–S bonds, Au@Pt NPs enable accurate biochemical analysis in complex environments relative to AuNPs. By incorporating DNAzymes into nanoflares, an Au@Pt-Dz nanosensor was fabricated to detect l-histidine in infant formulas.111 As shown in Fig. 6B, in the absence of l-histidine, the fluorescence of this nanosensor is quenched. Upon target recognition, the DNAzyme undergoes self-cleavage to restore fluorescence, and the fluorescence intensity correlates with the concentration of l-histidine. Compared with the Au-Dz sensor, the Au@Pt-Dz nanosensor exhibits a significantly higher signal-to-background ratio in environments containing whey protein (rich in thiols). It successfully achieves high-fidelity biosensing in complex samples and enhances the practical applicability of the Au@Pt NP-based DNAzyme nanosensor. Xu's group further developed a portable miRNA detection platform with Au@Pt NPs.112 The integration of a photonic crystal into the Au@Pt DNA nanomachine significantly enhances fluorescence intensity, making the system highly suitable for solid-phase fluorescence detection chips. Harnessing the synergistic effects of Au@Pt NPs and photonic crystals, miRNA can be sensitively detected with a portable detection platform.

AuNPs possess excellent biocompatibility and significant payload capacity, making them more appropriate for drug delivery and therapy. However, high levels of biothiols will cause non-specific leakage of drugs in normal cells, causing undesirable off-target effects. Building upon previous studies, our group communicated a therapeutic platform leveraging the unique properties of AuNP@Pt, which can minimize false-positive signals and reduce side effects on non-target cells.113 As shown in Fig. 6C, the photosensitizer is fixed to the surface of the AuNP@Pt via thiol groups, which will be liberated by miRNA21 to activate the phototherapy in cancer cells. By virtue of the exceptionally low ligand exchange rate of the Pt–S bond, nonspecific leakage of photosensitizers is effectively suppressed in the GSH environment. Therefore, Pt–S bond mediated nanoflares exhibit reduced cytotoxicity toward non-target cells compared to traditional Au–S-based nanoflares. The surface of AuNPs can be coated not only with Pt shells but also with various other passivation materials. He and co-workers uniformly deposited SiO2 coatings on the surface of AuNPs to create a core–shell structure, denoted as Au@SiO2 (Fig. 6D).114 SiO2 is an excellent passivated material that effectively prevents interaction with GSH. Au@SiO2 not only mitigates the interference of GSH but also preserves the fluorescence bursting ability of AuNPs. Au@SiO2 was synthesized and functionalized with fluorophore-labeled DNA recognition sequences. The fluorescence was quenched via an energy transfer mechanism originating from the potent quenching capability of Au@SiO2. In comparison to conventional Au–S nanoflares, Au@SiO2-based nanoflares can sensitively image miRNA21 in live cells.

Coating the AuNPs with a passivated layer (e.g., Pt, SiO2, etc.) is an effective method to improve the performance of AuNPs, which can suppress nonspecific activation caused by active molecules like GSH, improving the detection sensitivity and accuracy. However, the surface deposition on AuNPs still faces several challenges. For instance, the enzyme-like activity of Pt can catalyze the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which interferes with the fluorophores labeled on the nucleic acid probes. Meanwhile, the SiO2 coating undergoes accelerated degradation in acidic environments, leading to the detachment of nucleic acid probes and subsequently false-positive signals. More importantly, such designs leave the nucleic acid probes continuously exposed to harsh biological environments (e.g., nucleases), failing to shield them from destruction by other competitive interferents. In addition, deposition of other materials on AuNPs leads to unpredictable changes in size and shape, so the reproducibility of experimental results will be a problem, especially in vivo.

Enveloped nanoflares

Apart from biothiols, endogenous nucleases will also affect the exposed functional nucleic acid, causing a false positive signal and side effects. Compared with the flexible nucleic acid target, the nuclease possesses a large size, thus developing an advanced strategy to minimize the interference from nuclease is necessary. In view of the differences between nuclease and nucleic acid targets, physically insulating the nuclease from the nucleic acid target with an effective tool can shield the negative effects of nuclease. Adopting this concept, our group proposed a physically masked strategy to separate the large-sized nuclease from nanoflares.115 In our previous work, we enhanced the stability of conventional nanoflares using Pt–S bonds, resolving signal distortion issues in high concentrations of biothiols. However, the exposed functional nucleic acid was susceptible to nucleases within biological systems. In this work, a polymeric micelle fabricated from hyaluronic acid was used to encapsulate the AuNP@Pt nanoflares. TEM characterization reveals that the pore size of the protective shell does not exceed 4 nm, whereas the diameters of the nuclease and single-stranded miRNA are approximately 5 nm and 1.5–2 nm, respectively. Consequently, the spatial confinement effect of the protective shell blocks the passage of bulkier nucleases, thereby shielding the internal nanoflares from enzymatic degradation. Conversely, the target miRNA can freely traverse the porous shell to react with the encapsulated nanoflares and generate a fluorescence signal (Fig. 7A). Concurrently, the AuNP@Pt nanostructure of the nanoflares enhances their resistance against interference from small molecules such as biothiols, ultimately ensuring high-fidelity signal output.

Liu and co-workers constructed a Trojan horse-like delivery system based on a hydrogel platform for high-fidelity miRNA imaging (Fig. 7B).116 The shell of the Trojan horse nanoflare was made from amphiphilic phosphorylcholine and an acid-degradable crosslinker, which encapsulated the nanoflare inside to provide protection. Compared with conventional nanoflares, the Trojan horse-protected nanoflares exhibited remarkable stability upon exposure to GSH and nucleases. The amphiphilic phosphorylcholine facilitates cellular uptake of the Trojan horse-protected nanoflares through multivalent interactions. And the lysosomal acid environment can degrade the shell via a proton sponge effect to liberate the internal nanoflares into the cytoplasm, which undergo a chain reaction to generate fluorescence signal upon interacting with the nucleic acid target. Experimental results demonstrated that the polymer-functionalized nanoflares effectively avoided false-positive signals induced by DNase I and DTT compared to conventional nanoflares, thereby improving detection accuracy. Huang's group utilized metal coordination to develop a dense metal shell, termed SNA@Fe NPs, on the outer layer of the nanoflares.117 The shell was attached to the surface of AuNPs through coordination and electrostatic interactions. As shown in Fig. 7C, the SNA@Fe NPs exhibited significantly reduced release of free fluorophores in the presence of GSH (10 mM), indicating that shielding of nanoflares by SNA@Fe can enhance the resistance to biothiols and reduced competitive binding for DNA segregation. Moreover, the dense shell structure conferred resistance to nuclease degradation, addressing the long-standing limitation of conventional nanoflares. Also, SNA@Fe NPs have a wide range of applications in biology. For instance, they can selectively target cancer cells, leading to improved therapeutic outcomes. This innovative design enables SNA@Fe NPs to serve as promising candidates for drug delivery and biosensing, offering new opportunities for precision medicine.

In practical applications, nanoflares are susceptible to interference from biothiols and nucleases, as well as nonspecific protein adsorption, which compromises their delivery efficiency in physiological environments.118–120 To address the problem, Liu's group designed a gel-encapsulated nanoflare to minimize protein crown formation.121 As shown in Fig. 7D, fluorescent peptide chains with caspase-3/7 cleavage sites were attached to AuNPs via Au–S bonds, and then several monomers bearing terminal vinyl groups were introduced to form a hydrogel shell through polymerization on the AuNP surface. To improve the targeting ability of the integrated nanoflare (Au NP@gels), folic acid was covalently linked to the hydrogel shell via vinyl groups. Experimental results demonstrated that this strategy maintains the responsiveness of nanoflares while preventing the formation of protein corona, thereby improving the cellular delivery efficiency and offering new insights for future nanodrug development.

Inorganic nanoparticles can also serve as protective shells for nanoflares. Chen and co-workers constructed a nanoflare with the metal–organic framework (ZIF-8) as the shell to image the miRNA in live cells (Fig. 7E),122 where a DNAzyme was encapsulated within the system. Upon cellular uptake, significant amounts of Zn2+ were generated in the cytoplasm through the degradation of ZIF-8 in an acidic environment, then the DNAzyme was activated to hybridize with miRNA subsequently. The walking strand can move along the designated trajectory to facilitate the DNA walking reaction, resulting in the cleavage and displacement of the terminal fragment of the reporter probe fluorophore (FAM) from the AuNPs, thereby amplifying the fluorescence signal. Compared with conventional nanoflares, this strategy effectively protects the nanoflare from nuclease degradation and mitigates the interference of GSH, thereby providing a signal-amplifying nanoplatform with high fluorescence fidelity.

Wu and co-workers established a core–shell nanostructure composed of an AuNPs core and a nucleic acid shell.123 As shown in Fig. 8A, an anchored strand was attached to the AuNPs via Au–S bonds, enabling siRNA immobilization through hybridization with the anchored strand. To protect the nanoflare, Y-shaped DNA was loaded to form the protective outer layer because of the rigid triangular backbone. In this case, the stability of siRNA was enhanced, and this nanoflare can target the tumour cells; this rational design effectively prevents in vivo degradation of nanoflares and significantly enhances the therapeutic efficacy. Experimental results showed that the nanoflare remained stable in 10% FBS and exhibited strong resistance to nucleases. In addition, the cellular experiments confirmed that the nanoflare can specifically recognize cancer cells with a strong fluorescence signal.

(A) Schematic synthesis of siRNA/Ap-CS and its application in cancer cells. (B) Nucleic acid shell-protective nanoflares for rapid imaging of endogenous miRNA. (C) Small sized AuNP-protective nanoflares for high-fidelity and sensitive detection of biomarkers.

Taking the same approach, Chen and co-workers engineered a nanoflare with a nucleic acid shell, which can avoid false positive signals and improve the reaction efficiency.124 As shown in Fig. 8B, the fluorophore-labelled double strands were fixed on the AuNPs, then a stable TDN tetrahedral structure was grafted as the outermost layer for nanoflares. The TDN tetrahedral structure not only provides a protective effect for nanoflares but also closes the distance between the functional double strands and the AuNPs, thereby increasing the reaction rate. The results showed that this nanoflare can specifically enter tumour cells due to the targeting ability of TDN. Thus, this work presents a strategy that minimizes interference from nucleases, reduces false-positive signals, and enhances the reaction efficiency, providing a promising platform for high-fidelity intracellular imaging.

The traditional DNA-Au NP probes suffer from two core challenges: nuclease-induced degradation and background fluorescence interference.125 The fluorescence signal can be triggered even in the absence of the target complementary strand, indicating that non-specific signal leakage occurs within complex physiological environments. Aiming at this limitation, Liu's group developed a core–satellite structured Au NP nanoprobe.126 As shown in Fig. 8C, Cy5-labeled ATP aptamers (target-binding sequences) were used as linkers to assemble DNA-modified 30 nm AuNPs (cores) and 13 nm AuNPs (satellites) into a superstructure. The embedded DNA probes, protected by this superstructure, exhibited exceptional enzymatic stability. Meanwhile, the superstructure achieved a fluorescence quenching efficiency of over 98% for the labelled Cy5. Upon recognition of ATP, the superstructure disassembled into individual AuNPs, leading to rapid fluorescence recovery and a significant enhancement in the signal-to-background ratio. This probe successfully enabled accurate monitoring of ATP levels in live cells, thereby providing a novel strategy for precise in vivo biosensing.

Encapsulating nanoflares within artificial nanostructures provides a physical barrier against nuclease-induced degradation, significantly improving both detection sensitivity and diagnostic accuracy. However, this design still faces several critical challenges. For instance, shielding the nanoflares with an inert porous protective shell successfully evades enzymatic degradation of the nucleic acid probes, but it inevitably increases the hydrodynamic size of the nanoprobes. Such dimensional alterations, spanning from expanded single units to multi-nanoflare nano-aggregates, pose a major constraint that dictates and alters their endocytic trafficking mechanisms. Also, the surface functionalization and covalent modification of nanoparticles may increase the cytotoxicity, potentially leading to adverse reactions within the organism. Thus, developing multifunctional nanoflares that can resist nucleases, biothiols, proteins and other interferents is crucial for practical applications.

Other strategies

Beyond the four major categories discussed above, several alternative strategies have also been exploited to enhance the stability of nanoflares. Luo and co-workers developed a new strategy that covered the AuNPs with graphene (AuG NPs) to covalently link the nucleic acid sequence via an amide reaction (Fig. 9A).127 What was special about this nanoflare was that the anchored nucleic acid was covalently grafted onto AuG NPs, facilitating the hybridization of dye- or drug-labelled recognition DNA (rDNA) probes, ultimately leading to the formation of covalent nanoflares with high stability. In the presence of the target, the rDNA will form a stable double-stranded structure, resulting in the release of dye and drug molecules for imaging and therapy. This nanoflare displayed excellent resistance to highly concentrated biological agents, such as nucleases, DNA, and GSH. And they can be utilized for intracellular target detection and targeted photodynamic therapy (PDT). This strategy offers a novel solution for intracellular target monitoring and tumour therapy, accompanied by improved imaging precision and specificity, which is crucial for both basic research and medical applications.

(A) Schematic illustration of covalent amide-bonded nanoflares for high-fidelity fluorescence sensing and targeted therapy. (B) Schematic diagram of the design and detection principle of SNS-LNF for highly specific KRAS mutation detection.

To address the challenge of precisely detecting low abundance circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA, femtomolar range) and susceptibility to interference from wild-type and homologous sequences, Xue and colleagues reported the construction of lipidic nanoflares (SNS-LNFs) with single-nucleotide specificity.128 As shown in Fig. 9B, this system is assembled from a single cholesterol-conjugated multifunctional molecular beacon (MMB) through hydrophobically driven self-assembly. The detection mechanism of SNS-LNFs relies on toehold-initiated self-priming DNA polymerization (TPP). When the target ctDNA binds to the toehold domain of the MMB (a region that serves both as the recognition site and reaction initiation site), it triggers the dissociation of MMB and self-primed DNA polymerization. This process releases the target ctDNA to initiate a cascade signal amplification. The sensor can differentiate the single-nucleotide mutations from a normal sequence. Moreover, it achieves accurate detection of KRAS G12D in serum samples containing colorectal cancer patients, which provides a robust tool for clinical diagnosis.

Conclusions and outlook

In this article, we reviewed the design of high fidelity nanoflares along with a summary of their applications for precise intracellular detection and therapy. The strategies developed for improving the performance of nanoflares include: signal transduction, replacement of the anchoring group, surface passivation of AuNPs and spatial isolation of nanoflares. In comparison with the conventional nanoflares, these superior high fidelity nanoflares possess strong resistance to endogenous interfering substances, including biothiols, nucleases, proteins and saline ions, which gave them the unique ability to obtain the high fidelity fluorescence signal or improve the therapeutic accuracy. Furthermore, the application scope of nanoflares will be broadened by these enhanced capabilities, thereby facilitating their potential for clinical transformation.

Despite the significant progress they had made in biomedical applications, several challenges still existed and needed to be considered: (1) modifying the terminal units with various chemical groups can strengthen the binding force, but the potential mechanism has yet to be fully elucidated. For instance, the underlying reasons for the superior stability of the Au–F interaction compared with Au–S have yet to be fully understood. Thus, deeper analysis and rigorous side-by-side comparisons should be made for this point. Also, the potential activities and toxicity of new groups need to be carefully studied (e.g. alkynyl and –SeH). (2) While utilizing signal transduction to avoid false positive signals can enhance detection accuracy, it cannot prevent the degradation of the nucleic acid probe in a complex environment. This degradation ultimately compromises the sensitivity of nanoflares by reducing the grafting density of the nucleic acid probe. Thus, maintaining the integrity of the nucleic acid probe remains an enduring challenge for TRET-based nanoflares. (3) Depositing a coating on AuNPs can preserve their unique optical properties of AuNPs and enhance the modification stability, but the underlying biotoxicity of covered AuNPs needs to be further explored. (4) For enveloped nanoflares, the pore size of nanocarriers is a critical factor in excluding large-sized nucleases, but the detailed morphological characterization of the nanocarriers is absent in most reported studies. Thus, developing the protective shell with well-defined channels for target molecules is pivotal for ensuring signal fidelity. (5) Indeed, an ideal strategy for advancing nanoflares is to engineer a protective shell encapsulation that simultaneously shields against non-specific degradation by nucleases and interference from biothiols. The above discussion has been focused on the signalling stability of nanoflares, and intracellular trafficking and other detailed intracellular fate of these probes also need to be understood.

Concurrently, although all the designs discussed in this review exhibit excellent stability, the lack of standardized stability assessment protocols remains a critical barrier to rational advancement in high-fidelity nanoflare design. We therefore propose a standardized benchmarking protocol that encompasses: (i) nuclease resistance in 10% FBS over 24 h; (ii) stability under reductive thiol gradients (0.1–10 mM); (iii) protein corona resistance in 50–100% human serum; (iv) photostability under defined laser illumination parameters. The standardization of these core parameters will significantly facilitate a direct cross-comparison of the stability profiles of diverse nanoflares within complex biological matrices.

In conclusion, although there is still room for improvement in high-fidelity nanoflares, the development of systems with strong resistance to endogenous interferents holds great promise for clinical diagnosis and therapy. Nanoflares exhibit considerable merits for in vivo applications; their highly dense nucleic acid shell significantly amplifies the delivery efficiency and facilitates navigation across intricate biological barriers, establishing them as a promising platform for precision oncology, molecular imaging, and gene regulation. Despite these advantages, systemic safety remains a formidable bottleneck impeding clinical translation. Particular scrutiny must be directed toward the gold nanocore, where critical parameters, including biodistribution, long-term tissue retention, protein corona formation, and potential toxicity, are heavily dictated by particle size, morphology, and surface chemistry. From a translational perspective, the nucleic acid therapeutic market is expanding exponentially. Driven by escalating investments in clinical development and manufacturing, the commercial potential of nanoflares is robust. Nevertheless, successful clinical implementation of nanoflares will require coordinated advances in biosafety assessment, scalable and reproducible manufacturing, quality control, and regulatory compliance. Addressing these challenges will be essential for realizing the full potential of nanoflares in precision medicine and clinical diagnostics. In summary, the synergistic integration of molecular recognition, advanced nanotechnology, and structural physical design will serve as a robust platform for optimizing nanoflare performance in complex biomedical applications.
