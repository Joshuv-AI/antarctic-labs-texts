Depression severity and cardiovascular risk in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus: insights from the framingham risk score
Authors: Soghra Aliasgharzadeh, Neda Lotfi Yagin, Nazli Namazi, Sajjad Haji, Naimeh Mesri Alamdari, Samira Pourmoradian, Ziba Ghorbanverdi, Majid Mobasseri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of death among individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus(T2DM). Beyond physiological complications, individuals with T2DM frequently experience psychological comorbidities, with depression being one of the most prevalent. Although Framingham Risk Score (FRS) is shown to predict CVD in people without diabetes, its performance in those with T2DM who experience depression is not well established. This study examines depressive symptoms in individuals withT2DM and explores how these symptoms relate to the Framingham risk score in this group. In a cross-sectional study of 400 T2DM patients, cardiovascular disease risk was assessed using the FRS scoring system. Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) was applied to evaluate depressive symptoms in participants. In 400 T2DM patients, nearly half of the studied patients (49.8%) exhibited no depression, while 19.0% experienced mild depression, 21.5% had moderate depression, and 9.8% diagnosed with severe depression. There is a significant difference in depression severity distribution between the poor–controlled and well-controlled diabetes patients (p = 0.033). In 40.6% of patients, the FRS for 10-year cardiovascular disease is <10%. About 34.1% of patients had a moderate FRS (10-20%), and 25.3% were categorized as being at high risk for FRS (>20%). A significant difference in cardiovascular risk score distribution was observed between patients with poorly controlled and well-controlled diabetes (p = 0.006). According to multivariate linear regression analysis, subjects with mild, moderate, or severe depression had higher Framingham cardiovascular risk scores. A similar finding was observed for both well-controlled and poorly controlled T2DM patients. There is a potent relationship between depression severity and the 10-year CVD risk score in both well-controlled and poorly controlled T2DM patients. Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) poses a major global health burden, with an estimated 536.6 million people affected in 20211. Forecasts suggest that by 2045, roughly 12.2% of the world’s population will be living with diabetes, marking it as one of the fastest-growing health issues of the 21 st century2. Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the top cause of morbidity and mortality among those with T2DM, driving rising healthcare costs and greater demand on resources3,4. A thorough understanding of the key CVD risk factors in people with diabetes and the adoption of strategies to mitigate these risks are essential steps to lessen the burden of non-communicable diseases. Beyond physiological complications, individuals with T2DM frequently experience psychological comorbidities, with depression being one of the most prevalent5,6. Research shows that individuals with diabetes have about double the likelihood of experiencing depression compared with those without the condition7. In addition, psychosocial stress ranks third among modifiable risk factors for acute myocardial infarction in adults with diabetes, behind only smoking and lipid abnormalities, and it accounts for roughly 30% of the attributable risk8. Depression in patients with T2DM is associated with a 47.9% increase in cardiovascular mortality relative to those without depression2. This elevated risk stems in part from both biological and behavioral pathways, with depression frequently linked to unhealthy lifestyle behaviors such as physical inactivity and smoking. Evidence suggests that psychological distress in people with type 2 diabetes mellitus individuals correlates with poorer glycemic control and suboptimal self-management practices, which can lead to vascular complications and worse health outcomes9,10.

Mechanistically, depression may exacerbate diabetes-related complications through pathways involving chronic hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia resulting from prolonged stress and elevated cortisol levels, which can affect synaptic plasticity and immune functioning11,12. Furthermore, increasing evidence points to depression-associated alterations in circulating metabolites, including systemic inflammatory markers, renal function indicators like C-reactive protein and triglycerides, which are recognized as predictors of adverse vascular events13–15. A study by Hazuda et al. demonstrated that both the use of antidepressant medications and the presence of depression independently increase the risk of cardiovascular events in people with type 2 diabetes mellitus16. Similarly, a systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by Farooqi et al. reported a 47.9% increase in cardiovascular mortality, a 36.8% rise in coronary heart disease, and a 32.9% elevation in stroke risk among individuals with diabetes and concurrent depression17.

The Framingham Risk Score (FRS) is a commonly used method to estimate the 10-year risk of cardiovascular disease, derived from both modifiable and non-modifiable factors, including age, lipid profiles, blood pressure, smoking status, and use of antihypertensive therapy18. Nevertheless, depression and other psychological factors are not explicitly incorporated into the FRS model. While the FRS has been extensively validated in predicting cardiovascular risk in population without T2DM, its applicability to depressed T2DM patients remains underexplored19. Given that this subgroup tends to exhibit a higher baseline risk, understanding the impact of depression on FRS-derived risk estimates could enhance prognostic accuracy, inform personalized interventions, and facilitate earlier preventative measures.

Therefore, this study seeks to determine how common depressive symptoms are among individuals with T2DM and to investigate the relationship between these depressive symptoms and the 10-year Framingham cardiovascular risk score, with the goal of enhancing risk stratification and informing targeted clinical interventions for this high-risk population.

Methods and materials

Study participants

This research was designed as a cross-sectional investigation involving 400 individuals with T2DM who sought treatment at the outpatient clinic affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences in Tehran, Iran, between 2022 and 2024. The sample size was calculated based on mean serum triglyceride levels20, which was the primary dependent variable, with a mean ± standard deviation of 6.11 ± 2.88. Using an alpha level of 0.05, a minimum of 356 participants was required—where “d” represented 6% of the average triglyceride level—and this was adjusted for a design effect of 1.5. To account for an anticipated 10% rate of participant dropout or exclusion, the total sample was increased to 400 to ensure sufficient statistical power.

Participants eligible for this study were those aged 18 years or older, diagnosed with T2DM within the past five years, currently on insulin or medication therapy, and capable of providing informed consent. Exclusion criteria included recent significant life events potentially affecting psychological health, severe psychiatric disorders aside from depression, cognitive impairment, cancer, renal disease, and autoimmune conditions. Ethical approval was obtained from the Tabriz University of Medical Sciences Ethics Committee (IR.TBZMED.REC.1404.230). All participants signed an informed consent form. Data collection involved a structured questionnaire capturing demographic variables (such as age, gender, education level, smoking status, socioeconomic status) and clinical information (including diabetes duration, medical history, and medication use).

Laboratory assessments

After an overnight fast of 12 h, morning blood samples were drawn from all participants. The samples were centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 15 min within an hour of collection and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, and fasting plasma glucose were determined using enzymatic colorimetric assays (Pars-Azmoon, Tehran, Iran)21 Blood pressure was measured twice on the same arm after a 10–15 min rest using a mercury sphygmomanometer, and the average of the two readings was used for both systolic and diastolic values.

Cardiovascular risk assessment

The 10-year CVD risk was estimated with the Framingham general CVD risk score. This sex-specific multivariable model, developed by D’Agostino et al., incorporates factors such as age, sex, diabetes status, smoking status, blood pressure (both treated and untreated), total cholesterol, and HDL cholesterol22.

Participants were then grouped by risk level according to their calculated percentages: low risk (< 10%), intermediate risk (10–20%), and high risk (> 20%).

Depression evaluation

Depressive symptoms were evaluated utilizing the Persian version of the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), a validated and widely used psychological assessment tool23. The short, 13-item version of the BDI was employed, with each item scored from 0 to 3, reflecting the intensity of depressive feelings and thoughts24. The severity of depression was classified as follows: minimal (0–4), mild (5–7), moderate (8–15), and severe (> 16)25.

Statistical analysis

We examined the extent and pattern of missing data before conducting the analyses. The proportion of missing values for the main variables of interest (depression, cardiovascular disease status, and key covariates) was very low (< 1%). Given this minimal level of missingness and the absence of any apparent systematic pattern, all 400 participants were retained in the analyses, and no imputation methods were applied.

Data were analyzed with SPSS version 26 (IBM, Chicago, USA). Normality of distributions was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD for normally distributed data or as medians with interquartile ranges for non-normal data. Categorical variables are described by frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons employed Chi-square tests for categorical variables and either independent-samples t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables, depending on distribution.

To explore the link between depression status and the 10-year cardiovascular risk score, both univariate and multivariate linear regression analyses were conducted across different diabetes control levels. The multivariate models adjusted for potential confounders, including fasting blood sugar (FBS), family history of diabetes, duration of diabetes, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and waist circumference which were statistically different between well-controlled and poorly controlled diabetes population (Table 1). A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The initial demographic and clinical profiles of the 400 individuals with T2DM, categorized according to their glycemic control status (well-controlled defined as HbA1c < 7%, and poorly controlled as HbA1c ≥ 7%), are summarized in Table 1. A small amount of missingness (< 1%) was observed in a few covariates, which was handled by retaining all cases in the analyses given the negligible proportion.

Notably, there was a significant difference in gender distribution, with males constituting a larger proportion in the poorly controlled group (53.5%) compared to the well-controlled group (42.6%) (p = 0.036). Patients in the well-controlled category exhibited an average HbA1c of 6.23 ± 0.7%, whereas those in the poorly controlled group had a higher mean HbA1c of 8.43 ± 1.21% (p < 0.001). Furthermore, levels of systolic blood pressure, triglycerides, fasting blood sugar (FBS), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) were elevated in participants with poor glycemic regulation compared to their well-controlled counterparts (p < 0.05).

As illustrated in Table 2, nearly half of the cohort (49.8%) showed no signs of depression, while 19.0% exhibited mild depression, 21.5% had moderate depression, and 9.8% experienced severe depression. A higher proportion of individuals with poorly controlled diabetes displayed severe depression (13.7%) compared to those with well-controlled diabetes (5.9%). Statistical testing confirmed a significant difference in depression severity distribution between the two groups (p = 0.033).

Discussion

This research evaluated the 10-year likelihood of CVD through the Framingham Risk Score and examined how it relates to the severity of depression in patients with T2DM. The findings showed that depressive symptoms were quite common among T2DM patients. Additionally, the level of depression was significantly greater in those with inadequate glycemic management compared to individuals who maintained better control.

The results show that a notable proportion of individuals with T2DM bear a substantial 10-year cardiovascular disease risk, especially among those with poor glycemic control, with many categorized as medium- to high-risk. Additionally, regression analyses reveal a strong association between depression severity and the 10-year CVD risk score in both well-controlled and poorly controlled T2DM groups, after adjusting for potential confounders. More severe depressive symptoms were linked to a significantly higher likelihood of cardiovascular events, indicating that greater depression intensity corresponds to an elevated cardiovascular risk in individuals with T2DM.

In recent years, numerous researchers have emphasized the psychosocial factors associated with diabetes and proposed links between mental health issues and diabetes26–29.

Consistent with the results of this study, Jung et al. found that individuals with T2DM and coexisting depression had an elevated risk of developing cardiovascular complications compared to those with diabetes alone. This observation was made in a comprehensive Korean nationwide cohort study involving 2,228,443 patients with type 2 diabetes30.

Hazuda et al. reported that depressive symptoms were significantly associated with various cardiovascular outcomes in overweight and obese adults with T2DM. These outcomes included cardiovascular disease death, nonfatal heart attacks, nonfatal strokes, hospitalization for angina, congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular conditions, as well as procedures such as coronary artery bypass surgery and carotid endarterectomy16.

A meta-analysis of nine cohort studies found that depression is significantly linked to a higher risk of cardiac events in people with T2DM compared with those who have T2DM without depression. The combined hazard ratios showed that individuals with both T2DM and depression experienced a 47.9% higher cardiovascular mortality, a 36.8% higher risk of coronary heart disease, and a 32.9% higher risk of stroke17.

An updated meta-analysis of 17 cohort studies, encompassing more than one million participants, demonstrated a robust link between depression and an increased risk of both nonfatal and fatal cardiovascular disease in individuals with T2DM7. The findings indicated that those with depression faced a higher stroke risk, with HRs of 1.20 (95% CI, 1.15–1.26) for transient depression and 1.54 (95% CI, 1.46–1.63) for persistent depression. Likewise, the risk of myocardial infarction was raised, with HRs of 1.25 (95% CI, 1.18–1.31) for transient depression and 1.38 (95% CI, 1.29–1.49) for persistent depression.

As shown, prior research has largely concentrated on cardiovascular outcomes through cohort designs, given that cardiovascular disease is a leading source of morbidity and mortality in individuals with diabetes. In contrast, this study uniquely assesses depression status and computes the 10-year cardiovascular risk with the Framingham Risk Score specifically in patients T2DM. We also examined the relationship between depression and cardiovascular risk within this population.

Risk factors are conditions or characteristics that influence the likelihood of developing a disease. They may be associated with the disease and, if modified, can potentially lead to health benefits. Currently, clinical guidelines for preventing CVD mainly focus on assessing a person’s overall risk rather than targeting individual risk factors31,32. The Framingham Risk Score exemplifies this approach by estimating the probability of developing any form of cardiovascular disease, whether fatal or non-fatal. It incorporates factors such as age, gender, smoking status, total cholesterol, and systolic blood pressure to provide an overall risk assessment33.

The precise mechanisms linking depression and diabetes are not yet fully understood. There is a likely bidirectional relationship between T2DM and depression: while T2DM may contribute to the onset or exacerbation of these mental health issues, depression can also increase the risk of developing T2DM34. Additionally, evidence suggests that diabetes and depression may interact synergistically, collectively influencing the outcomes of cardiovascular disease35.

Several hypotheses have been proposed to explain the adverse health outcomes observed in patients with T2DM, who also suffer from depression. One explanation is that behavioral changes linked to mood disorders can deteriorate blood sugar management36,37. Depressive symptoms often correlate with unhealthy lifestyle choices, such as disordered eating patterns leading to higher calorie consumption, decreased physical activity, and smoking, all of which contribute to less effective glycemic control38. Patients with both T2DM and depression are more likely to experience functional impairments than those with only one of these conditions. Additionally, poor adherence to diabetes treatment regimens and engaging in unhealthy behaviors may lead to worse glycemic control and a heightened risk of cardiovascular issues39. Depression often leads to poor medication adherence and unhealthy lifestyles, increasing the risk of hyperglycemia and hyperinsulinemia, which can promote blood clotting issues.

Over activation of the HPA axis leads to excess cortisol production, which can harm the vascular endothelium and worsen insulin resistance. Alterations in the sympathetic nervous system and a pro-inflammatory state are central to the mechanisms linking depression and diabetes40–42 Both conditions are also associated with increased platelet aggregation, higher inflammatory markers, and endothelial dysfunction9. These biological changes may contribute to cardiovascular disease development, as levels of inflammatory biomarkers such as CRP, TNF-α, and IL-6 are typically elevated in affected individuals43,44. Along with hyperglycemia and insulin resistance, these factors contribute to endothelial damage, inflammation, and coagulation abnormalities, which accelerate atherosclerosis and the progression of cardiovascular disease. Some research further indicates a genetic overlap among depression, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease, suggesting shared genes that influence multiple pathways, including corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), and serotonin (5-HT) signaling45,46.

Given the relatively high occurrence of depressive symptoms among patients with T2DM, these findings can inform the development of intervention strategies aimed at preventing depression and subsequent cardiovascular disease in this group. Enhancing prevention efforts for cardiovascular issues in these patients is essential. Programs such as cardiac rehabilitation and exercise can help alleviate depression and decrease mortality rates in type 2 diabetes mellitus patients with CVD. Additionally, screening for psychological factors like anxiety and hostility is important, as these elements can impact the development and progression of cardiovascular conditions and influence outcomes after cardiac rehabilitation, potentially through their effect on depression. Further approaches, including stress management training, may also enhance the overall effectiveness of cardiac rehabilitation programs.

There are some limitations to our study. Primarily Given the cross-sectional nature of the present study, causality and temporal directionality cannot be inferred between depression and cardiovascular disease in individuals with type 2 diabetes. Because exposure (depression) and outcome (cardiovascular disease) were measured at the same time point, it is not possible to determine whether depressive symptoms preceded cardiovascular disease, whether pre-existing cardiovascular disease and its related symptoms contributed to the development or worsening of depression (reverse causation), or whether both conditions arise from shared underlying factors such as inflammation, lifestyle behaviors, or comorbidities. Future longitudinal and prospective cohort studies are required to clarify the temporal sequence and to more robustly evaluate whether depression predicts incident cardiovascular disease in this population.

although more refined cardiovascular risk prediction tools are available for people with diabetes in European and North American populations, the Framingham risk score remains the most appropriate and widely used model for the Iranian population. Also, confined sample size prevents generalizing the findings to other populations.

Finally, although missing data can potentially introduce bias and reduce precision, this is unlikely to have materially affected our findings because missingness in the main exposure, outcome, and key covariates was very low (< 1%) in the present study.

Conclusion

In summary, this research indicates an association between depression and the presence of cardiovascular disease among individuals with type 2 diabetes, without establishing causality or temporal sequence. These findings underscore the importance of routinely assessing depressive symptoms in patients with type 2 diabetes who have, or are at clinical suspicion of having, cardiovascular disease, while recognizing that prospective studies are needed to determine whether depression contributes to future cardiovascular events.

Our findings are best understood as hypothesis-generating regarding the interplay between depression and cardiovascular disease in type 2 diabetes, rather than as proof of a causal pathway or risk effect.
