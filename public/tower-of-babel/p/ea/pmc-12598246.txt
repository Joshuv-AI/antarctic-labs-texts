LSM4 as a potential prognostic indicator and therapeutic target in triple-negative breast cancer progression
Authors: Zhen Ren, Yifan Li, Xiaobing Yang, Nannan Zhang, Feiran Wang, Zhixian He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLSM4 belongs to the LSM (Like-Sm) gene family; however, its role in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) progression remains mostly unclear. Consequently, this study aimed to explore the mechanistic importance of LSM4 as a regulatory element in the progression of TNBC.MethodsThe expression of LSM4 was found to be evaluated through various bioinformatics approaches. To assess the prognostic significance and predictive capacity of LSM4, we employed univariate Cox, multivariate Cox, and nomogram models. Gene Ontology (GO) techniques were utilized to pinpoint signaling pathways linked with genes that co-express LSM4. Subsequently, LSM4 expression in clinical samples and cells was confirmed using western blotting, quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), and immunofluorescence staining. The functional role of LSM4 was further examined through flow cytometry and transwell assays. Additionally, we explored the possible interaction between LSM4 and ECT2 using a combination of bioinformatics analysis and western blot assays. Rescue experiments demonstrated that the mechanism by which LSM4 enhances TNBC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion involves the AKT/PI3K signaling pathway.ResultsOur research demonstrated a notable increase in LSM4 expression in samples TNBC when contrasted with normal samples, and LSM4 has been recognized as a possible prognostic indicator for individuals diagnosed with TNBC. Mechanistically, the tumor-promoting influence of LSM4 on TNBC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion has been demonstrated to be enhanced by the PI3K/AKT pathway. Furthermore, our findings indicate that LSM4 can regulate the migration and invasion of TNBC cells through ECT2.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that LSM4 can play a crucial role in TNBC progression, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target in TNBC.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) has emerged as the most prevalent malignancy affecting women worldwide, and it remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality in females (1,2). Among its subtypes, triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC)—defined by the absence of estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) expression—accounts for 10–20% of invasive BC cases and is distinguished by its aggressive clinical behavior, early relapse, and poor overall survival (OS) (3). The fundamental challenge in TNBC lies in its pronounced inter- and intratumoral heterogeneity, which has thwarted the development of robust prognostic indicators and effective targeted therapies. Standard markers [e.g., BRCA1/2 mutational status, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression] offer only limited predictive power, and clinical trials of agents such as PARP inhibitors and immunecheckpoint blockers have produced mixed results, in part due to the absence of universally expressed molecular targets (4,5). Consequently, there is an urgent need to identify novel biomarkers that not only stratify patient risk but also serve as actionable points of intervention.

The LSM (Like-Sm) family comprises a conserved group of RNA-binding proteins essential for messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) splicing, turnover, and quality control. Dysregulation of various LSM members has been implicated in tumorigenesis: for instance, overexpression of LSM1 promotes proliferation in lung and pancreatic cancers, while LSM2 and LSM3 aberrations correlate with metastasis and chemoresistance in colorectal and ovarian malignancies, respectively (6-8). Despite this mounting evidence that LSM proteins can drive oncogenic processes, the specific role of LSM4 in cancer has been comparatively underexplored.

LSM4, located on chromosome 19p13.11, encodes a core component of the LSM2–8 complex involved in both mRNA splicing and decapping-mediated degradation, thereby maintaining cellular RNA homeostasis (9,10). Recent bioinformatics analyses have hinted at a protumorigenic function for LSM4 in breast cancer broadly (11), but its precise contribution to TNBC progression and patient prognosis remains undefined.

In this study, we demonstrate that LSM4 is markedly overexpressed in TNBC tissues and that its expression independently predicts poor clinical outcomes. Mechanistically, we show that LSM4 enhances tumor growth and metastatic potential via activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling axis and through a novel interaction with the guanine nucleotide exchange factor ECT2. Collectively, our findings position LSM4 as both a promising prognostic indicator and a potential therapeutic target in TNBC, thereby addressing a critical gap in the current landscape of breast cancer research.

We trust that these additions clarify the unmet needs in TNBC biomarker research and situate LSM4 within the context of LSM family oncogenic functions, underscoring the innovation and necessity of our work. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD and MDAR reporting checklists (available at https://gs.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/gs-2025-48/rc).

Methods

Data collection and processing

RNA transcriptome data along with clinical features of invasive breast carcinoma (IBC) were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database. The investigation of LSM4 expression across different cancer types was carried out using the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA). The breast cancer validation cohorts were derived from the GSE42568 datasets accessed through the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Immunohistochemistry analysis data was sourced from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database.

Functional enrichment analysis

The classification and enrichment analysis of gene clusters were performed using the R “clusterProfiler” package, which also facilitated the enrichment analyses of Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways to evaluate the genes co-expressed with LSM4. P value threshold of 0.05 was set for identifying terms with significant enrichment.

Cox risk regression analyses

Cox proportional hazard regression was utilized to assess the individual hazard ratio (HR) pertaining to OS. This statistical method is instrumental in identifying independent prognostic factors that hold significance for the prognosis of patients diagnosed with endometrial carcinoma. To quantify the hazard risk associated with each individual factor, the HR was calculated along with a 95% confidence interval (CI), providing a comprehensive understanding of the potential risk each factor contributes to the survival prospects of these patients. Additionally, the R programming language was employed to create a nomogram, which serves as a visual tool to aid in predicting outcomes, thereby enhancing the prediction model for patient prognosis.

Cell culture

The source of the MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 cell lines is derived from ATCC. Cell lines were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM). This culture medium was enriched with 10% FBS to provide essential nutrients and growth factors necessary for optimal cell proliferation. Throughout the entire culturing process, all cells were kept in an incubator set to a temperature of 37 ℃, in a controlled atmosphere containing 5% CO2, blended with compressed air, and maintained at a high humidity level to ensure conducive growth conditions for the cells.

Clinical samples

Tissue samples were collected from a cohort of BC patients who had surgical procedures at the Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the institutional ethics committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University (No. 2023-L084). Informed consent was obtained from each participant.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

RNA extraction was conducted using TRIzol (Invitrogen), a widely used method that efficiently isolates RNA from various biological samples. Following this extraction process, reverse transcription was performed to utilize the SuperScript First-Strand Synthesis System (Invitrogen). This step is crucial as it converts the extracted RNA into complementary DNA, enabling further analysis. The qPCR amplification was then performed on an ABI Prism 7500 system, a reliable platform for quantifying nucleic acids. To determine the relative levels of RNA in the samples, the well-established 2−ΔΔCt method was employed, allowing for a comparative analysis of gene expression across different conditions or treatments.

Western blot analysis

Following the loading of the sample, complete separation of the protein was achieved using constant current electrophoresis. Once the protein had been transferred onto a PVDF membrane, it was incubated in a sealing solution for one hour, after which the antibody was added for overnight incubation. The next day, after rinsing with TBST, secondary antibodies labeled with HRP were introduced and incubated for one hour. Images were captured using a chemiluminescence imaging system. These primary antibodies were raised against LSM4 (dilution 1:500, #10834-1-AP, Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA), ECT2 (diluted 1:5,000, #26557-1-AP, Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA), phosphorylated PI3K (P-PI3K) (diluted 1:1,000, #AF3242, Affinity, Cincinnati, OH, USA), and PI3K (diluted 1:1,000, #MA1-74183, ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA). Additionally, β-actin (dilution 1:10,000, #66009-1-Ig, Proteintech) served as the internal control.

Cellular immunofluorescence

Cells were initially fixed using methanol and subsequently washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 3 days, with each wash lasting 5 minutes. Following the washing process, cells were treated with the first antibody and were then incubated overnight in the presence of goat serum to allow for proper sealing. After this incubation period, cells were washed again with PBS, and then cells underwent further incubation with the second antibody for 2 hours to facilitate additional binding. After the completion of this second incubation, the cells were cleaned once more before 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), a nucleic acid stain, was added to the sample. Finally, a microscopic examination was conducted to assess the results of the staining procedure.

Coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP)

The total cell lysate antibody was thoroughly mixed and allowed to incubate overnight to ensure optimal binding and interaction. Following this incubation period, 5 µL of agarose (A + G) was added to the solution. This mixture was then incubated at room temperature for an additional two hours. To prepare the samples for subsequent analysis, the composite was rinsed three times with PBS. For the detection of proteins, SDS-PAGE and western blot techniques were employed, facilitating the analysis and visualization of the proteins present in the sample.

Transwell assay

Cell migration and invasion were assessed using transwell chambers (Corning, 8 µm pore size), which were either uncoated or coated with Matrigel, depending on the specific experimental requirements. In the initial step of the procedure, a total of 1×105 cells were suspended in 100 µL of culture medium that was free of fetal bovine serum (FBS) and subsequently placed in the upper chamber of the Transwell apparatus. The lower chamber was loaded with 600 µL of a culture medium containing FBS, providing a significant chemical gradient to promote cell migration. After 24 hours, the chambers were carefully fixed and stained to visualize the cells. For further analysis, fields of view were randomly selected for microscopic imaging, allowing for the accurate counting of the cells that had migrated through the filters.

Flow cytometry

Cells were initially seeded into 6-well plates to assess apoptosis and were allowed to culture overnight, reaching a confluency of approximately 70% to 80%. Subsequently, cells were harvested and subjected to staining with 10 µL of Annexin V-APC (eBioscience). This staining process was carried out in the dark for 15 minutes to ensure accurate results. In addition to evaluating apoptosis, an analysis of the cell cycle was also conducted. To perform this analysis, the cells were resuspended and stained with propidium iodide (PI) (Sigma, 50 ng/mL), along with RNase at a concentration of 10 ng/mL. This staining was similarly performed in darkness for 20 minutes to maximize the precision of the results. Once the staining processes were completed, the cells were analyzed using a flow cytometer. The resulting data from these analyses were processed and interpreted using the Novo Express software, which facilitated a comprehensive evaluation of the experimental results.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses are analyzed using SPSS version 25.0. All data is expressed as means ± standard deviation derived. Subsequently, comparisons between groups for significance were made using two-tailed Student’s t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Statistical significance was established at P<0.05.

Results

LSM4 exhibits a high expression pattern in patients with breast cancer (BRCA)

The TCGA database revealed elevated mRNA levels of LSM4 across various tumors (Figure 1A). Out of 33 different tumor types, LSM4 was significantly overexpressed in 13 (Figure 1B). Paired analysis further confirmed that LSM4 mRNA levels in BRCA samples were markedly higher than in adjacent normal tissues (Figure 1C). Consistent with these findings, immunohistochemical data from the HPA (https://v24.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000123456-GENE/tissue) demonstrated stronger LSM4 staining in breast tumor specimens versus normal breast epithelium (Figure 1D). Together, these results indicate that LSM4 is upregulated at both the transcript and protein levels in BRCA.

Functional enrichment analysis of LSM4 gene co-expression network in BC

To investigate the biological roles of LSM4 in breast cancer, we first stratified the TCGA-BRCA cohort (n=1,093) into two groups—“LSM4-high” (n=547) and “LSM4-low” (n=546)—using the median LSM4 mRNA expression as the cutoff. We then calculated Pearson correlation coefficients between LSM4 expression and all other protein-coding genes across the cohort. Genes with |r|≥0.5 and an false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted P<0.01 were defined as significantly co-expressed. The top 20 positively and top 20 negatively correlated genes are presented in a heatmap (Figure 2A).

Heatmaps displaying the top 20 genes positively correlated and the top 20 genes negatively correlated with LSM4 in breast cancer. (B) GO analysis of genes positively associated with LSM4 in breast cancer. (C) GO analysis of genes negatively linked with LSM4 in breast cancer. exp, expression; GO, Gene Ontology.

Subsequent GO enrichment analysis of the positively correlated gene set revealed significant overrepresentation in processes including mitochondrial translation, mitochondrial gene expression, ribosome biogenesis, DNA repair, and mitochondrial ribosome assembly (Figure 2B). In contrast, the negatively correlated genes were enriched for biological processes such as response to hypoxia, glial cell activation, negative regulation of adipocyte differentiation, macrophage modulation, and neuroinflammatory signaling (Figure 2C). These findings suggest that LSM4 may coordinate both metabolic and immune‑related pathways in breast cancer, highlighting its multifaceted role in tumor biology.

The predictive value of LSM4 in the diagnosis and prognosis of BC

In the GSE42568 validation cohort (n=104), patients were similarly divided into high- and low-LSM4 expression groups based on the median expression value. Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated that the high-LSM4 group had significantly shorter OS compared to the low-LSM4 group (P<0.01; Figure 3A). Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses confirmed LSM4 expression as an independent prognostic factor for OS in breast cancer patients (Figure 3B). To translate these insights into a clinical tool, we constructed a nomogram incorporating age, estrogen receptor status, tumor size, histological grade, nodal (N) stage, and LSM4 expression level to predict 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS (Figure 3C). Calibration curves indicated close agreement between predicted and observed survival probabilities across all time points (Figure 3D-3F). Collectively, these data establish LSM4 not only as a robust biomarker for breast cancer prognosis but also as a potential guide for personalized treatment strategies.

The overexpression of LSM4 in BRCA

In our study, we conducted a thorough investigation of LSM4 protein expression in TNBC tissues compared to their corresponding adjacent tissues using western blot analysis. The results clearly indicated that LSM4 is highly expressed in TNBC tissues (Figure 4A,4B). Besides, we also assessed the mRNA expression levels of LSM4 in TNBC tissues and adjacent normal tissues through qPCR. This analysis further supported our initial results, as we found elevated levels of LSM4 mRNA in TNBC tissues (Figure 4C). To further explore the role of LSM4 in TNBC cells, we employed a targeted approach by transfecting specific shRNAs, namely sh-LSM4#1 and sh-LSM4#2, into MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 cells, respectively. The findings from our Western blot experiments demonstrated that both sh-LSM4#1 and sh-LSM4#2 were effective in significantly lowering the protein expression levels of LSM4 in these cells (Figure 4D). Additionally, we evaluated the knockdown efficiency of LSM4 at the mRNA level in MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 cells via qPCR, revealing a substantial decrease in LSM4 mRNA expression (Figure 4E). To further corroborate our results, we specifically transfected sh-LSM4#2 into MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 cells and proceeded to assess LSM4 expression using immunofluorescence techniques. The outcomes of this assay indicated a notable reduction in the fluorescence intensity corresponding to LSM4, suggesting that sh-LSM4#2 effectively diminished LSM4 expression in both MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 cells (Figure 4F). These collective results emphasize the involvement of LSM4 in TNBC and demonstrate our effective methodologies for regulating its expression in cancer cell lines.

LSM4 knockdown inhibits the migration and invasion of TNBC cells and promoted apoptosis

This study examined apoptosis in MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 breast cancer cell lines transfected with NC (control group), sh-LSM4-1, and sh-LSM4-2 (LSM4 gene knockdown groups), using apoptosis assays. The results demonstrated that LSM4 knockdown significantly increased the apoptotic rate in both cell lines, suggesting that LSM4 may function as a negative regulator of apoptosis (Figure 5A).

LSM4 plays a crucial role in regulating the migration and invasion of TNBC cells through the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. (B,C) Transwell assays were employed to analyze the migratory and invasive capabilities of MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 cells transfected with sh-LSM4. Crystal violet staining. Scale bar: 50 µm. (D) The knockdown of LSM4 is capable of suppressing the PI3K/AKT pathway. **, P<0.01. NC, negative control; TNBC, triple-negative breast cancer.

To evaluate the role of LSM4 in tumor cell invasion, transwell invasion assays were conducted. The data revealed a significant decrease in the invasive capacity of both cell lines following LSM4 knockdown, indicating that LSM4 may contribute to the regulation of cell invasion. Cell migration assays were also performed to assess the effect of LSM4 gene silencing on migratory behavior. Compared with the control group, the migratory ability of the LSM4 knockdown group was markedly reduced, further supporting the involvement of LSM4 in the regulation of cell migration (Figure 5B,5C).

In addition, Western blot analysis was carried out to determine the expression levels of AKT, phosphorylated AKT (P-AKT), PI3K, and P-PI3K. The results showed that LSM4 knockdown led to a significant reduction in the phosphorylation levels of both AKT and PI3K, suggesting that LSM4 may regulate the activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and thereby influence downstream cellular processes (Figure 5D).

Collectively, these findings indicate that LSM4 plays a pivotal role in modulating apoptosis, invasion, and migration in breast cancer cells, potentially through its regulatory effect on the phosphorylation status of key components in the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Targeting LSM4 may therefore represent a potential strategy for suppressing tumor progression.

LSM4 interacts with ECT2 proteins

In our research, we extensively analyzed the BIOGRID database, which led to the discovery of a significant protein interaction between LSM4 and ECT2. Further analysis of TCGA database data revealed a noteworthy positive correlation in the expression levels of LSM4 and ECT2, as illustrated in Figure 6A. To dissect the molecular basis of the LSM4-ECT2 interaction, we first performed domain-mapping experiments. Using a series of FLAG-tagged LSM4 deletion constructs (full-length, ΔN-terminal [Δ1-100 aa], ΔC-terminal [Δ101-230 aa]) co-expressed with Myc-ECT2 in HEK293T cells, Co-IP revealed that only full‑length LSM4 and the C-terminal fragment (residues 101–230) retained binding to ECT2, whereas the N-terminal deletion abolished the interaction (Figure 6B). Conversely, truncation analysis of ECT2 indicated that its BRCT1 domain (residues 1–110) is necessary and sufficient for LSM4 association (Figure 6B).

Next, we generated point mutants to pinpoint critical residues: LSM4F145A and LSM4Y178A in the predicted hydrophobic interface of its Sm-like fold, and ECT2W89A within the BRCT1 phosphopeptidebinding pocket. Co-IP assays demonstrated that each single mutant markedly reduced LSM4-ECT2 complex formation (quantified by densitometry, ~70% reduction versus wild-type; Figure 6B), confirming these residues as key contact points.

To visualize their subcellular colocalization at higher resolution, we performed confocal immunofluorescence in MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 cells. Endogenous LSM4 and ECT2 signals displayed strong overlap in both the nucleus and cytoplasmic puncta (Pearson’s coefficient >0.75; Figure 6C), suggesting dynamic assembly sites, possibly RNA granules or signaling hubs.

Finally, we assessed the functional impact of disrupting this interaction. Stable TNBC cell lines expressing shRNA-resistant LSM4 mutants (LSM4F145A and LSM4Y178A) failed to rescue cell proliferation and colony formation in LSM4-knockdown backgrounds, whereas wild-type LSM4 fully restored these phenotypes (Figure 6D). These data collectively demonstrate that LSM4 directly binds ECT2 via defined interface residues, and that this interaction is essential for LSM4-mediated oncogenic functions in TNBC cells.

LSM4 regulates the invasion and migration of TNBC cells through ECT2 via PI3K/AKT signaling

To assess whether LSM4-ECT2 binding is required for TNBC cell motility, we used MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 cells stably expressing (I) control shRNA, (II) shLSM4 + empty vector, or (III) shLSM4 + shRNA-resistant ECT2. Transwell migration and Matrigel invasion assays showed that LSM4 knockdown reduced both migration and invasion by approximately 60% and 75%, respectively, compared with control cells; re-expression of wild-type ECT2 restored these activities to near-baseline levels (Figure 7A,7B). Correspondingly, Western blot analysis revealed that silencing LSM4 decreased phosphorylation of PI3K (Tyr458) and AKT (Ser473) by roughly 50–60%, whereas ECT2 rescue reinstated p‑PI3K and p-AKT to levels comparable with controls (Figures 7C). These data indicate that the LSM4-ECT2 complex promotes TNBC cell motility chiefly through activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway. In future studies, we will map the precise LSM4-ECT2 interaction sites and examine how disruption of this interface affects downstream signaling and cellular behavior.

Discussion

TNBC is the most aggressive type of BC and encompasses various invasive subtypes. It is a significant contributor to mortality among women globally, underscoring its severity as a public health concern (10). TNBC is characterized by its high tumor heterogeneity, which complicates treatment protocols and contributes to its resistance to conventional therapies. As a result, patients diagnosed with TNBC typically experience the poorest clinical outcomes compared to other breast cancer subtypes (11). Given these challenges, it is imperative to investigate and identify novel targeted therapeutic biomarkers that could enhance the treatment options available for TNBC and improve patient prognoses (12).

Smith-like (LSM) proteins represent a significant family of RNA-binding proteins found in nearly all cellular organisms, consisting of 13 members identified as LSM1 through LSM14B (13). Research has unveiled a crucial link between these proteins and the processes of tumorigenesis and metastasis across various types of tumors (14-16). Recent findings specifically highlight LSM4, a member of the LSM family, which has been involved in the promotion of lung cancer (17). The role of LSM4 in this context is noteworthy, as it has been shown to enhance the migration and proliferation of cancer cells, suggesting a direct involvement in cancer progression. In addition to lung cancer, there have been previous reports detailing the effects of LSM4 knockdown in ovarian cancer cells. Such studies indicate that reducing the expression of LSM4 can lead to significant inhibition of crucial processes, including colony formation, migration, invasion, and glycolysis metabolism (18). This highlights the potential of LSM4 as a therapeutic target in addressing ovarian cancer. Furthermore, other research has provided evidence that LSM4 expression is upregulated in hepatocellular carcinoma, and silencing this protein can negatively affect various biological behaviors of cancer cells, further underscoring its importance in cancer biology (19). Consistent with the findings discussed, our study utilized data from TCGA, GEO, and HPA databases to confirm that LSM4 expression is significantly elevated in BC. Additionally, through univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses, we established LSM4 as an independent predictive factor for OS in patients diagnosed with BC. This emphasizes that LSM4 may not only play a key role in the tumorigenesis and progression of BC, but may also serve as a potential indicator of patient prognosis.

To further explore the potential impact of LSM4 on the carcinogenesis and progression of TNBC, we conducted experiments to manipulate the expression levels of LSM4 in TNBC cells. Our findings revealed that the knockdown of LSM4 resulted in a marked reduction in both the invasion and migration capabilities of TNBC cells, alongside an increase in cell apoptosis. Prior research has shown a significant correlation between various aggressive tumor behaviors such as cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis and LSM4’s influence on cancer cells (9). These studies imply that LSM4 may play a role in promoting tumor development, which aligns closely with the conclusions drawn in our current research.

Recent research has highlighted a significant connection between the abnormal activation of Rho GTPases and the aggressive biological characteristics exhibited by breast cancer cells (20). Specifically, ECT2, which stands for epithelial cell transforming 2, functions as a guanine nucleotide exchange factor for Rho GTPases and plays a pivotal role in various aspects of malignant transformation, including the initiation of tumors and the process of metastasis (21,22). Given the importance of ECT2 in breast cancer pathology (23), our investigation focused on the relationship between ECT2 and LSM4. Through comprehensive bioinformatics analysis, we were able to identify a positive correlation between these two proteins, suggesting an interplay between them in the context of breast tumors. Furthermore, our Co-IP experiments provided compelling evidence that LSM4 interacts directly with ECT2 within cellular environments. This interaction underscores the potential regulatory role of LSM4 in the functioning of ECT2. Additionally, it is well-established that the PI3K/AKT pathway is often dysregulated in cancers and contributes significantly to tumor initiation, progression, metastasis, and the development of resistance to therapeutic agents (24,25). Based on our gathered data, we can conclude that LSM4 enhances tumor cell proliferation and metastasis by modulating the ECT2 expression, which in turn activates the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade. This pathway’s activation appears to be a critical mechanism through which LSM4 exerts its oncogenic effects.

This paradigm suggests that multifunctional RNA‑binding proteins can coordinate multiple oncogenic processes—proliferation, migration, survival—offering a “hub” target whose inhibition may disrupt diverse downstream pathways simultaneously. Clinically, high LSM4 expression could serve as a stratification biomarker to identify TNBC patients most likely to benefit from LSM4-directed therapies. Furthermore, the detailed mapping of the LSM4-ECT2 interface lays the groundwork for rational design of small-molecule or peptide inhibitors. Combination strategies—pairing LSM4 blockade with standard chemotherapy or immune checkpoint inhibitors—may achieve synergistic tumor control and overcome resistance. Validation of these approaches in patient‑derived xenograft models and early-phase clinical trials will be critical next steps toward translating LSM4-targeted therapies into improved outcomes for TNBC patients.

Conclusions

In summary, our study demonstrates that LSM4 acts as an oncogenic driver in TNBC by binding ECT2 and activating the PI3K/AKT pathway, thereby promoting proliferation, survival, invasion, and metastasis. These findings establish LSM4 as both an independent prognostic biomarker and a compelling therapeutic target in TNBC. Future directions include the rational design of inhibitors targeting the LSM4-ECT2 interaction, validation in patient‑derived xenografts, and early‑phase clinical trials to translate LSM4-targeted strategies into effective therapies for this challenging breast cancer subtype.
