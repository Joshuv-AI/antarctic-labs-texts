Identification of signaling pathways induced by porcine parvovirus in porcine kidney-15 cells based on weighted correlation network analysis
Authors: Tingting LU, Xia MA, Xinghui SONG, Li ZHAO
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPorcine parvovirus (PPV) is an important trigger of reproductive issues in pigs. Infection of the porcine kidney-15 (PK-15) cells with PPV induces cell death and inflammation. To explore the impact of PPV infection on gene expression in PK-15 cells and to identify the associated signaling pathways, we performed weighted correlation network analysis (WGCNA) on both PPV-infected and uninfected cells. We identified the blue and brown modules, with the blue module demonstrating decreased gene expression and the brown module showing increased gene expression at 48 hr post-PPV induction. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that genes in the blue module were predominantly associated with cellular components, while those in the brown module were enriched in biological processes, including the immunological response to PPV infection. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis of the modules indicated that the “Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction” was linked to PPV. Furthermore, we screened 124 target genes involved in more than 100 pathways that interact with C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 8 (CXCL8), which plays an essential role in regulating numerous biological processes. The signaling pathways we identified facilitate a comprehensive examination of the mechanisms of interactions initiated by PPV infection.Keywords: C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 8, porcine kidney-15 cell, porcine parvovirus, weighted correlation network analysis

Porcine parvovirus (PPV), which can cause reproductive disorders in pigs, brings losses to the pig business worldwide [4]. The viability of the porcine kidney-15 (PK-15) cells was inhibited by PPV infection [17]. Several scholarly investigations have explored the impact of PPV on intracellular signaling mechanisms. Notably, PPV has been observed to activate the nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) signaling pathway, a process that is mediated by toll-like receptors (TLRs), particularly TLR2 and TLR9 [3, 9]. In addition, PPV infection has been shown to induce apoptosis in PK-15 cells through the activation of the p53 protein and the subsequent activation of mitochondrial signaling pathways [17]. This activation highlights the complex interplay between PPV and the host’s immune response. However, the full extent of the intricate and reciprocal effects of PPV infection on the signaling pathways within PK-15 cells remains elusive. The full impact of PPV on these pathways in infected cells has yet to be comprehensively elucidated.

In our previous research, it was discovered that PPV infection leads to cell death. We analyzed the signaling pathways and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with cell death, as well as the interactions among these DEGs. Building on these findings, in this study, we utilized weighted correlation network analysis (WGCNA) to cluster PPV-associated genes identified through transcriptome sequencing. Our analysis focused on the “Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction” pathway, which is prominently featured in our WGCNA modules and is essential for modulating immune responses to environmental challenges. Cytokines, including interleukins (IL), tumor necrosis factor (TNF), interferons (IFN), lymphokines, and chemokines, are crucial for coordinating immune reactions to challenges like viral infections. Upon binding to their specific receptors on the cell membrane, cytokines initiate a cascade of events within target cells, leading to a wide spectrum of biological responses [15].

The C-X-C motif (CXC) subfamily of chemokines is closely associated with both tumors and inflammatory diseases, including lung inflammation, arthritis, liver inflammation, kidney inflammation, and others [19]. The cytokine C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 8 (CXCL8), also identified as IL-8, which belongs to the CXC chemokine family, is typically not expressed under normal conditions but is rapidly induced by various stimuli, including environmental factors like reactive oxygen species, hypoxia, and pathogens. It can also be stimulated by cytokines like TNF, CXCL12, IL-1, and IL-6 [7]. CXCL8 mediates signaling primarily through its binding to the C-X-C motif chemokine receptors (CXCRs), CXCR1 and CXCR2. This interaction plays a significant role in modulating various disease processes, including cancer, cardiovascular, and neurological diseases, with a notable impact observed in infectious diseases such as Corona Virus Disease 2019 (COVID-19), where an increase in CXCL8 levels has been reported [2]. Our study explores the potential regulatory role of CXCL8 in PPV infection, providing a promising avenue for understanding the underlying mechanisms and identifying novel therapeutic targets.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data source

In our previous study, PK-15 cells were infected with PPV at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1, each experiment was repeated three times. The transcriptomes of both uninfected and infected cells were sequenced at 24 and 48 hr post-infection. To ensure the reproducibility and transparency of our findings, the raw sequencing data have been deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database under the accession number PRJNA1066973. These data are publicly accessible and available for validation and further analysis by the scientific community, in compliance with NCBI’s data usage policies.

WGCNA

Utilizing the R package WGCNA, we constructed gene co-expression networks [11]. The WGCNA algorithm is based on the assumption of a scale-free gene network distribution, which enables the determination of a correlation matrix for gene co-expression. It also facilitates the calculation of neighborhood functions and correlation coefficients for the constituent nodes. This process culminates in the generation of a hierarchical clustering tree. For our study, parameters were meticulously adjusted to an expression power threshold of 0.8, a module similarity threshold of 0.25, and a minimum module size of 30 genes, ensuring the network’s robustness and biological relevance.

Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) classification analysis

The genes within distinct modules were mapped to terms in the GO database (https://www.geneontology.org/), and the frequency of genes in each term was counted to generate a list of genes associated with specific GO functions with gene count statistics. Subsequently, hypergeometric assays were employed to identify GO terms that were significantly enriched in the modules relative to the genomic background [1].

As a primary public resource for biological pathways, the KEGG database provides integrated metabolic pathway queries, making it a powerful tool for metabolic analyses and studies of metabolic networks in organisms [10]. The KOBAS 3.0 tool (http://kobas.cbi.pku.edu.cn/) was utilized to perform a KEGG categorization enrichment analysis for genes within different WGCNA modules, aiding in the identification of pathways that are significantly associated with these gene sets.

Analysis of protein-protein interactions (PPI)

STRING is a database that contains the PPI data from multiple species, which are either predicted or have been experimentally verified [5]. From this database, we extracted relevant genes from the brown module, focusing on interactions with a confidence weight greater than 0.4, as recommended by STRING’s guidelines for reliable interactions. Then, the PPI network was visualized using Cytoscape software (https://cytoscape.org/) to identify hubs and clusters, aiming to uncover key regulatory genes and functional modules within the network.

Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) validation

RT-qPCR was utilized to validate the accuracy of the RNA-seq data for five randomly selected genes, with glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) serving as a stable internal reference gene. Primers for these genes were designed using the TBtools software (http://github.com/CJ-Chen/TBtools/releases) (Table 1). Total RNA was extracted using TRNzol Universal Reagent from TIANGEN Biotech Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and reverse transcribed into cDNA using HiScript II Reverse Transcriptase from Vazyme (Nanjing, China). qPCR was carried out using the ABI 7500 Fast Real-time PCR system from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA), utilizing qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix from Vazyme (Nanjing, China). The qPCR was run using the following parameters: 95°C for 5 min, 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 sec, 60°C for 30 sec. Then entered the machine’s default dissolution curve acquisition program: 95°C for 15 sec, 60°C for 60 sec, and 95°C for 15 sec. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate to ensure the reproducibility and statistical reliability of the results.

RESULTS

WGCNA

To explore the signaling pathways associated with PPV infection, we used the WGCNA method to build a gene co-expression network. The results of the hierarchical cluster analysis of the samples showed good clustering among the three replicates of each sample, with no significant outliers (Fig. 1A). The dendrogram provided a visual representation of the hierarchical clustering of these modules. From an initial pool of 25,722 genes, a total of 2,604 were selected through screening and categorized into three principal modules: the blue module, comprising 291 genes; the brown module, with 259 genes; and the turquoise module, containing 2,050 genes. Additionally, 4 genes were assigned to the grey module. Utilizing heatmaps, we examined the gene expression profiles across the three dominant modules. Our findings indicated that within the blue module, gene expression levels exhibited a decline at 48 hr after PPV infection. In contrast, the brown module demonstrated an upregulation of gene expression at the same point following PPV infection. Furthermore, gene expression in the turquoise module changed over time, which was not caused by PPV infection (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Table 1). This comprehensive assessment sheds light on the distinct responses within these genetic clusters in relation to PPV infection. In module-trait correlation analysis, we found the brown module had a higher correlation with PPV, followed by the blue module (Fig. 1C). The selected modules were analyzed to determine the correlation between module membership and gene significance. The scatter plot revealed that the blue module (cor=0.36, P=2.5 × 10−10) and the brown module (cor=0.44, P=1.1 × 10−13) exhibited significantly stronger correlations than the turquoise module, and these correlations were highly significant (Fig. 1D).

(B) A dendrogram of all expressed genes was clustered based on a topological overlap matrix. Each branch in the clustering tree represents a gene, while co-expression modules are depicted in different colors. (C) Module-trait heatmap showing the correlation between the clustering gene module and PPV. (D) The relationship between gene significance and module membership.

GO functional enrichment analysis

We performed a GO functional enrichment analysis on genes clustered in the blue and brown modules. The GO terms within the blue module exhibit P-values consistently below the threshold of 0.01, indicating statistically significant enrichment. In contrast, the brown module demonstrated even greater significance, with P-values all falling below 10−7, underscoring the profound biological relevance attributed to these terms. It was found that genes in the blue module were predominantly associated with “cellular component” functions, indicating that a 48-hr PPV infection may inhibit the functionality of cellular structural components (Fig. 2A). Additionally, the functional classification of the genes clustered in the brown module mainly belonged to the category of “biological process”. One of the most notable processes was “complement activation”. Furthermore, a multitude of immune-related processes were identified, suggesting an upregulation of specific immune-related genes in response to PPV infection (Fig. 2B).

Gene Ontology functional enrichment analysis in the blue and brown modules. (A) Gene Ontology functional enrichment analysis in the blue module. (B) Gene Ontology functional enrichment analysis in the brown module.

KEGG enrichment analysis

KEGG enrichment analysis was conducted on genes clustered in the blue and brown modules. It was discovered that genes in these two modules were involved in certain common signaling pathways, implying that PPV infection was closely related to these pathways. These pathways included the “Hypoxia-inducible factor-1 (HIF-1) signaling pathway”, “Ras signaling pathway”, “TNF signaling pathway”, and “Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction” within “Environmental information processing”, the “Peroxisome” pathway within “Cellular processes”, and the “Chemokine signaling pathway” and “Toll-like receptor signaling pathway” within “Organismal systems” (Fig. 3A, Fig. 3B, Supplementary Table 2, and Supplementary Table 3). Each module also had distinct pathways. For example, eight genes in the blue module participated in the “mTOR signaling pathway”, six in the “PI3K-Akt signaling pathway”, and six in the “Rap1 signaling pathway” (Fig. 3A). In the brown module, twelve genes were involved in the “Complement and coagulation cascades” pathway, nine in the “NOD-like receptor signaling pathway”, five in the “MAPK signaling pathway”, and five in the “NF-kappa B signaling pathway” (Fig. 3B).

The “Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction” in the brown module

Among the seven co-enriched signaling pathways mentioned above, we specifically focused on the “Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction” pathway. This pathway, pivotal for immune regulation, is integral to cell communication during immune responses. It was particularly prominent in the brown module, boasting the richest gene enrichment, and showed a notably strong association with immune response mechanisms. We found that the expression of these genes increased significantly at 48 hr after PPV infection (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 4). Notably, CXCL8 exhibited a remarkable increase of over 10 times 24 hr post-infection, and its expression increased to more than 100 times its original levels upon PPV infection at 48 hr post-infection, underscoring its important role in the immune response. Other genes, such as Interleukin 19 (IL-19), TNF Superfamily Member 14 (TNFSF14), and Interleukin 1 Receptor Type 2 (IL1R2), showed a similar pattern of upregulation, transitioning from undetectable levels to significant expression post-infection. Numerous genes were upregulated by more than tenfold at 48 hr post-infection, including CXCL10, CXCL11, Interleukin 1 Alpha (IL1A), TNFSF18, C-C Motif Chemokine Ligand 20 (CCL20), CCL2, CCL4, and so on (Supplementary Table 4). The significant increase in expression levels of genes involved in this signaling pathway within the brown module after PPV induction suggests that they play a vital role in the immune response to PPV infection.

“Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction” associated with the brown module. Genes in red boxes are from the brown module.

The targets of CXCL8 in the brown module

By combining the gene expression data from the brown module with interaction pairs in the STRING database, we identified 124 genes as targets of CXCL8. The expression of these genes was induced by PPV and they were implicated in more than 100 KEGG signaling pathways, including “Ferroptosis”, “cAMP signaling pathway”, “IL-17 signaling pathway”, “FoxO signaling pathway”, “Necroptosis”, “Complement and coagulation cascades”, “Phagosome”, among others (Fig. 5, Supplementary Table 5). One of the targets, Ceruloplasmin (CP), involved in “Ferroptosis”, showed significantly induction by PPV at both 24 and 48 hr post-infection. Additionally, the target genes S100 Calcium Binding Protein A8 (S100A8) and S100A9, involved in the “IL-17 signaling pathway”, were not expressed in uninfected cells but were significantly induced post-infection, with expression levels increasing over the infection period. Similarly, PPV significantly induced the expression of S100A12. While multiple target genes did not exhibit changes at 24 hr, they showed a significant increase at 48 hr post-infection. Notably, the expression levels of Z-DNA Binding Protein 1 (ZBP1), TNF Superfamily Member 15 (TNFSF15), TNFSF18, Vascular Cell Adhesion Molecule 1 (VCAM1), and ATP Binding Cassette Subfamily A member 3 (ABCA3) were significantly elevated at 48 hr post-infection (Supplementary Table 5). The abundance of CXCL8 target genes within the brown module and their participation in numerous signaling pathways highlights the critical role of CXCL8 in the immune response to PPV infection.

The targets of C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 8 in the brown module.

RT-qPCR validation

To ensure the reliability of our transcriptome profiling, we randomly selected five genes whose transcriptional levels varied significantly post-PPV infection (fold change ≥1.5 and false discovery rate <0.05). These genes were then subjected to rigorous validation through RT-qPCR analysis. It was shown that the gene expression patterns of interleukin-6 precursor (IL-6), cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 1 (CDKN1A), signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), Colony Stimulating Factor 1 (CSF1), and NFKB Inhibitor Alpha (NFKBIA) were consistent with the transcriptome data (Fig. 6).

DISCUSSION

In our WGCNA analysis, we identified three distinct gene clusters with varying expression patterns. The turquoise cluster’s gene expression was notably influenced by the cell culture duration, while the blue and brown clusters demonstrated significant alterations in response to PPV infection. The latter two clusters, due to their high correlation with PPV infection, were the central focus of our investigation. Transcriptomic data analysis led to the identification of seven signaling pathways enriched in these modules, which are closely linked to immune response and apoptosis.

Considering the blue module’s limited participation in the “Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction” signaling pathway with only five genes from various families, and the brown module’s richer composition of 15 genes characterized by their interactive dynamics, our research prioritizes an in-depth examination of the brown module to uncover its intricate relationships and valuable insights. Cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions, especially those within the CXC and CC chemokine superfamilies, IL-10, IL-1, and TNF families, were found to be intimately associated with PPV infection. The dramatic change in CXCL8 expression post-infection is of particular interest, as this gene is a known regulator of multiple signaling pathways, including NF-κB, toll-like receptor, NOD-like receptor, IL-17, and chemokine pathways. The significant upregulation of CXCL8 revealed its pivotal role in mediating cellular responses within the intricate network of pathways activated by PPV infection.

Existing research indicates that the CXCL8-CXCR1/2 axis may play a significant role in tumor progression and metastasis by regulating the proliferation and self-renewal of cancer stem cells (CSCs). Consequently, several small-molecule CXCR1/2 inhibitors, CXCL8 release inhibitors, and neutralizing antibodies targeting CXCL8 and CXCR1/2 are considered promising for their potential therapeutic effects in various inflammatory diseases [7]. Our findings indicate a similar expression pattern for CXCL8 and CXCR2 at 48 hr post-infection, although CXCR2 is not within the brown module. This suggests a crucial role for the CXCL8-CXCR2 axis in cellular inflammatory responses and cell death.

S100 proteins, characterized by their small size and calcium-binding dimeric structure, regulate processes such as proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and inflammation by interacting with other proteins or receptors [8]. Our study identified S100A8, S100A9, and S100A12 as target genes of CXCL8 in the brown module, which were significantly upregulated in response to PPV infection. S100A9, in particular, has been implicated in modulating inflammatory responses by triggering the release of IL-8, also known as CXCL8, in periodontal ligament cells through NF-κB, extracellular-regulated kinase (ERK) 1/2, c-Jun amino-terminal kinase (JNK) 1/2, and p38 signaling pathways [6]. The co-expression trend of S100A9 and CXCL8 under PPV induction hints at a potential synergistic role in the immune response to viral infection. Furthermore, the upregulation of S100A gene expression across various diseases underscores their multifaceted role in pathological processes [14, 18, 20]. The identification of numerous proteins interacting with CXCL8 within the brown module further supports the intricate immune network’s response to PPV. In addition, an in-depth examination of the signaling pathways within the brown module revealed activation of “Complement and coagulation cascades,” “Chemokine signaling pathway,” and the “NOD-like receptor signaling pathway” following PPV infection. These pathways, known to be triggered by viral infections, play integral roles in immune responses [12, 13, 16]. This discovery has paved the way for a more profound understanding of PPV’s interaction with PK-15 cells.

While our study sheds light on the molecular mechanism of PPV infection, it has limitations. The in vitro nature of our experiments may not fully capture the in vivo complexity of the host-pathogen interaction. Additionally, the study’s focus on specific gene clusters and pathways may not encompass the full spectrum of PPV-induced changes in host cells. Future research should aim to bridge these gaps by incorporating in vivo models and adopting a broader systems biology approach. Moreover, the functional validation of the identified gene clusters and their role in PPV pathogenesis requires further investigation. Understanding these limitations will be crucial for the subsequent studies that can expand upon our findings.
