A cell type-specific surveillance complex represses cryptic promoters during differentiation in an adult stem cell lineage
Authors: Neuza R Matias, Lorenzo Gallicchio, Dan Lu, Jongmin J Kim, Julian Perez, Angela M Detweiler, Chenggang Lu, Benjamin Bolival, Margaret T Fuller
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this study, Matias et al. show that the zinc finger protein KMG and its binding partners fine-tune the transcriptional activity of spermatocyte-specific master regulator tMAC. Together, these factors counteract tMAC binding at weak or cryptic promoters and restrict aberrant transcription while permitting robust transcription from highly expressed tMAC/Aly-dependent promoters, reflecting a sophisticated cell type-specific surveillance system that functions during spermatocyte differentiation.Keywords: transcriptional repression, cryptic promoters, chromatin regulation, NuRD complex, spermatogenesis, Drosophila

During both embryonic development and cellular differentiation, pioneer transcription factors play key roles in cell state transitions due to their ability to convert previously silent chromatin from closed in precursor cells to open for productive expression of new, cell type-specific transcription program(s). Pioneer transcription factors can scan chromatin, recognize and bind target DNA sequences wrapped around nucleosomes, and locally alter the structural relationship between nucleosomes, DNA, and nearby H1 linker histones, making local genomic regions accessible to downstream effectors of transcription (for review, see Barral and Zaret 2024). However, the ability of pioneer factors to recognize target sites in closed chromatin raises two outstanding questions. First, given that these relatively short DNA motifs occur frequently in the genome, what mechanisms direct and restrict pioneer factor binding and action to the appropriate enhancer and promoter regions? Second, once pioneer factor binding has initiated the opening of local chromatin, what mechanisms regulate whether transcription indeed initiates at those sites? Some pioneer factors have been shown to bind many genomic sites that do not become transcriptionally active (for review, see MacQuarrie et al. 2011). For example MyoD, a pioneer factor that orchestrates differentiation of skeletal muscle, binds 30,000–60,000 sites in different muscle cell types, the majority of which do not overlap with genes regulated by MyoD (Cao et al. 2010).

Analysis of how a novel robust transcription program turns on as male germ cells differentiate from proliferating spermatogonia into spermatocytes in Drosophila has uncovered a cell type-specific master regulatory complex required to open chromatin at thousands of promoters for spermatocyte-specific transcription, as well as a surveillance mechanism that restrains this action at cryptic promoters (Fig. 1A). In the Drosophila male germline stem cell lineage, transit-amplifying spermatogonia stop dividing, complete a final S phase, and enter meiotic prophase as spermatocytes. During this stage of differentiation, >1800 genes are strongly upregulated, many of which are only expressed in spermatocytes. In addition, another ∼1200 genes expressed in spermatogonia become transcribed from an alternative, spermatocyte-specific promotor (Lu et al. 2020). Genetic analysis revealed that much of this novel, spermatocyte-specific transcription program requires promotor-proximal action of tMAC, a cell type-specific protein complex that shows many of the hallmarks of a pioneer transcription factor, though this has not yet been assessed by biochemical tests.

tMAC is a spermatocyte-specific version of the conserved, generally expressed MuvB complex (MMB/DREAM) (Beall et al. 2007), which works with a variety of partners such as E2F, Rb, or Myb to repress and/or activate key cell cycle and developmental genes (Sadasivam and DeCaprio 2013; Fischer and Müller 2017). The MuvB complex has been shown to bind and remodel nucleosomes, exposing nucleosomal DNA, consistent with its function as a pioneer transcription complex (Koliopoulos et al. 2022). The spermatocyte-specific tMAC complex has two subunits also in the MuvB complex (p55-Caf1 and Mip40 [LIN37]) but has replaced several other MuvB subunits with paralogs expressed only in spermatocytes. For example, in place of Mip130 [LIN9], the tMAC complex contains Aly, a Mip130 paralog first expressed in early spermatocytes (White-Cooper et al. 2000; Jiang and White-Cooper 2003; Beall et al. 2007; Jiang et al. 2007; Doggett et al. 2011).

Function of tMAC is required to turn on a following wave of spermatocyte-specific transcription that endows the male germ cells with hundreds of gene products needed for the meiotic cell cycle and postmeiotic spermatid differentiation (Fig. 1A). ChIP-seq for Aly indicated binding in a peak near target promoters, with action of Aly required for a small stretch of local chromatin to convert from closed in spermatogonia to open and accessible to ATAC-seq in spermatocytes (Lu et al. 2020). Expression from many tMAC-dependent promoters is upregulated by action of Mediator and the tTAFs, spermatocyte-specific homologs of components of TFIID (Hiller et al. 2001, 2004; Lu and Fuller 2015).

We have discovered a developmentally controlled genetic regulatory system that keeps the powerful but potentially promiscuous tMAC complex from turning on transcription from thousands of inappropriate promoters across the genome while allowing robust transcription from the normal slate of Aly-dependent spermatocyte-specific promoters. We had previously shown that function of a spermatocyte-specific, multiple zinc finger protein, Kumgang (Kmg), first expressed in very early spermatocytes, is required to prevent the tMAC component Aly from binding to and activating cryptic promoters associated with ∼400 genes (Kim et al. 2017). Because changes in expression were assessed by microarray, with probe sets only at the 3′ ends of then annotated genes, Kim et al. (2017) focused on the loss of Kmg function causing misexpression of genes normally expressed in other tissues but not male germ cells. Surprisingly, ChIP-seq from wild-type testes showed the Kmg protein localized along the gene bodies of highly expressed genes rather than enriched at the cryptic promoters (Kim et al. 2017), leaving the mechanism by which Kmg functions to repress transcription from cryptic promoters mysterious. Kim et al. (2017) suggested that Kmg might prevent expression from cryptic promoters indirectly by concentrating tMAC at its normally bound promoters, thus limiting tMAC's availability to bind and activate cryptic promoters.

Here we present new data that suggest that Kmg acts locally at Aly-bound sites to dampen transcription from weak promoters and, with partner proteins, repress transcription, close chromatin, and reduce tMAC binding at cryptic promoters. We identified binding partners of Kmg, including Dany, a spermatocyte-specific pipsqueak domain protein also required for normal transcription in spermatocytes (Trost et al. 2016); the chromatin remodeler Mi-2 (also identified by Kim et al. 2017); and the NuRD complex component Simj. Using a promoter-centric analysis combining full-length PacBio RNA sequencing (Iso-seq), short-read RNA-seq, and cap analysis gene expression (CAGE; which identifies transcription start sites), we found that loss of either Kmg, Dany, Mi-2, or Simj function in spermatocytes allowed expression from thousands of cryptic promoters not annotated as used in testes or any other Drosophila tissues, resulting in expression of many abnormal transcripts predicted to encode aberrant proteins. Loss of function of Kmg, Dany, or Simj also resulted in upregulation of normally low-expressed Aly-dependent promoters. ChIP-seq for Kmg showed enrichment at these promoters, suggesting that Kmg may act locally to dampen transcription from weak Aly-dependent promoters, consistent with prior findings that Kmg (CG5204) acted as a powerful repressor when artificially tethered next to a core promoter (Stampfel et al. 2015). At cryptic promoter sites, ChIP-seq revealed Kmg bound when function of Mi-2 or Dany was knocked down in spermatocytes. This suggests that Kmg may act transiently at cryptic promoters in wild-type testes, with Kmg/Dany/Mi-2 effectively repressing and closing chromatin and evicting tMAC (Aly). Strikingly, Kmg, Dany, Mi-2, and Simj did not appear to repress transcription from very highly expressed Aly-dependent promoters. ChIP-seq from wild-type testes revealed that Dany and Mi-2 colocalized with Kmg along the gene bodies rather than at the promoters of highly expressed genes. Based on recent findings by Ullah et al. (2022) that binding of Mi-2 to RNA antagonizes its ability to bind and act on chromatin, we discuss a model for how the Kmg/Dany/Mi-2 cell type-specific surveillance machinery may provide a mechanism to enhance differences in output between highly expressed and weak or cryptic promoters acted on in the same cell type by the tMAC complex.

Results

Kmg keeps thousands of cryptic promoters repressed genome-wide

Full transcriptome analysis revealed that Kmg is required in spermatocytes to keep transcription repressed from thousands of cryptic promoters across the genome. To gain a full picture of the effects of loss of Kmg on transcription in spermatocytes, we used PacBio long-read RNA sequencing (Iso-seq) coupled with short-read RNA-seq, CAGE, and 3′ end seq to generate a full and accurate transcriptome from wild-type testes and testes lacking Kmg function (Supplemental Fig. S1). Kmg loss-of-function testes were from flies expressing a UAS-RNAi against Kmg with spermatocyte-specific knockdown induced by the bamGal4 driver. Control testes were from flies carrying the bamGal4 driver without the RNAi construct and grown under the same conditions as the knockdown. Because the function of Kmg seemed to be to control which promoters were expressed in spermatocytes, promoter expression was quantified by summing the expression of every transcript starting from a given promoter region, as defined by CAGE cluster (Supplemental Fig. S1). In Kmg knockdown testes, 5490 promoters were upregulated more than fourfold compared with control testes (Fig. 1B, light pink). Applying more stringent criteria, 2065 promoters were upregulated >16-fold and expressed among the top 75% of promoters in the Kmg knockdown (higher than quantile 0.25). Of these, only 84 promoters overlapped with transcription start sites (TSSs) previously annotated in the Drosophila genome. Removing these from the list resulted in a total of 1981 stringently defined cryptic promoters that fired in Kmg knockdown testes (Fig. 1B, dark pink). These 1981 cryptic promoters mapped all over the major autosomes, except to pericentromeric regions. Cryptic promoters were less represented on the X chromosome and mostly absent from the Y and the fourth chromosomes (Supplemental Fig. S2).

Expression from cryptic promoters may profoundly alter the male germ cell proteome

The >3000 polyA+ transcript isoforms expressed in Kmg knockdown testes from the 1981 stringently defined cryptic promoters very often shared downstream exons and 3′ ends with annotated genes, a feature that may contribute to proper termination, polyadenylation, and/or stability of the cryptic transcripts. Most (86%) of the cryptic transcripts were predicted to encode polypeptides, and of those, 90% shared exons with annotated genes (Fig. 1C). In 25% of the cases where cryptic transcripts shared downstream exons with annotated genes, the host genes were not expressed in wild-type testes (360 genes) (Fig. 1D). For example, although CG6296 mRNA was not detected in control testes, in Kmg knockdown testes, two different cryptic promoters downstream from the annotated promoter fired, driving expression of two different cryptic transcripts (Fig. 1E). Both were predicted to encode polypeptides in-frame with the host gene, resulting in N-terminally truncated isoforms of the CG6296 protein. RNA FISH using probes for exons common to the normal and the cryptic transcript isoforms confirmed that CG6296 mRNA was not detected in control testes but was abundantly expressed specifically in spermatocytes when Kmg was knocked down (Fig. 1F,G). For the majority (75%) of cryptic transcripts that shared exons with annotated genes, the host gene was expressed in wild-type testes (1008 genes) (Fig. 1D). Of the 14% of cryptic transcripts (461) not predicted to encode a polypeptide, only 22% matched with annotated genes, whereas the majority (78%) were scored as novel (Fig. 1C). Of these, roughly half mapped to the antisense strand of annotated genes, and the other half mapped to intergenic regions.

Of the cryptic transcripts that spliced or fused into exons of annotated genes, analysis of their protein-coding potential compared with their host gene predicted 1416 cryptic protein isoforms (Fig. 1H). Most of these (97.6%) were in-frame with the host protein-coding sequence and thus were related to proteins encoded by 1136 annotated genes. In most cases (77%), the cryptic proteins were truncated compared with the proteins encoded by the host locus. Analysis by Western blot confirmed several cases of expression of in-frame truncated proteins from cryptic transcripts expressed in Kmg knockdown testes. In control testes, Taf1 was expressed from two different promoters, one of which was not previously annotated in the Drosophila genome (Fig. 1I, gray transcripts). In Kmg knockdown testes, firing of a cryptic promoter located much further downstream resulted in expression of two much shorter transcripts that spliced into downstream exons from the Taf1 locus (Fig. 1I, pink transcripts). These cryptic transcripts contained an AUG near the 5′ end in-frame with the Taf1 protein sequence, predicting expression of smaller proteins of 102 and 98 kDa containing the C-terminal domains of Taf1. Probing Western blots of control testis extracts with anti-Taf1 detected the expected large band of ∼250 kDa. Additionally, a smaller band the size of the predicted truncated proteins was detected in Kmg knockdown testis extracts (Fig. 1J). Similar analysis with antibodies against Chromator (Chro) and Half pint (Hfp) also detected truncated proteins of the predicted size expressed in Kmg knockdown testes but not in control testes (Fig. 1J; Supplemental Fig. S3).

Kmg interacts with Dany, Mi-2, and other NuRD complex components to keep cryptic promoters repressed

Immunoprecipitation followed by mass spectrometry (IP-MS) identified proteins that work with Kmg to block expression from cryptic promoters. Immunoprecipitation of Kmg from Drosophila testes recovered the large chromatin remodeler Mi-2 (homolog of mammalian Chd3/4/5) as expected from previous work (Kim et al. 2017). Anti-Kmg IP-MS also identified other components of the nucleosome remodeling and deacetylase (NuRD) complex, including Simj (p66/Gatad2a/b), CG15142 (a testes-specific paralog of CDK2AP1/DOC-1), and, to a lesser extent, HDAC1 (Rpd3) (Fig. 2A; complete list in Supplemental Table S1). In addition, the anti-Kmg IP-MS also recovered peptides from Dany, previously implicated in regulation of gene expression in Drosophila spermatocytes (Trost et al. 2016). Dany, like Kmg, is expressed only in spermatocytes, with onset of Dany protein expression starting very early in the spermatocyte stage (Fig. 2B). Dany and Kmg proteins colocalized in spermatocyte nuclei along with the Mi-2 protein (Fig. 2B), which is expressed in spermatogonia as well as in spermatocytes. Coimmunoprecipitation confirmed that Mi-2 binds Dany and Kmg. Immunoprecipitation of Mi-2-GFP from testes expressing a Dany allele tagged with V5 at the endogenous locus recovered both Kmg and Dany proteins (Fig. 2C).

The function of Dany and the NuRD complex components Simj and Mi-2 was required to block expression from the same cryptic promoters that require Kmg. Knockdown of either Dany, Simj, or Mi-2 in spermatocytes by UAS-RNAi under the control of bamGal4 (Supplemental Fig. S4) resulted in a meiotic arrest phenotype similar to knockdown of Kmg (Supplemental Fig. S5). Analysis of unfixed testis squashes by phase contrast microscopy and Hoechst (DNA) staining showed that in either case, knockdown testes lacked spermatids and were filled with spermatocytes with rounded up and partially condensed chromosomes, indicating that they were arrested just prior to G2/M transition of meiosis I (Supplemental Fig. S5G–L). Testes in which Mi-2 was knocked down were smaller than the others and had fewer spermatocytes and more debris, indicating cell death soon after arrest (Supplemental Fig. S5E). Analysis of promoter expression in Dany, Simj, or Mi-2 knockdown testes revealed that all three proteins were needed to repress the same cryptic promoters (Fig. 2D–F; Supplemental Fig. S6). These cryptic promoters were highly upregulated in Dany knockdown testes (128×), as in Kmg knockdown (256×). Expression of cryptic promoters was also elevated though to a lesser extent in Simj (56×) or Mi-2 (17×) knockdown testes. Indeed, the genomic coordinates of cryptic promoters defined using Kmg knockdown samples also marked initiation of cryptic transcription in Dany, Simj, and Mi-2 knockdown testes (Fig. 2E). For example, expression of the Notopleural (Np) gene was not detected by RNA-seq in control testes. However, testes in which the function of Kmg, Dany, Simj, or Mi-2 had been knocked down in spermatocytes showed expression from the same cryptic promoter, considerably downstream from the annotated promoter for Np (Fig. 2F). RNA FISH with probes recognizing the cryptic transcripts of Np detected expression in spermatocytes of the different loss-of-function testes but not in control testes (Fig. 2G–K).

In Kmg knockdown testes, cryptic promoters are bound by Aly and are Aly-dependent

In control testes, expression from cryptic promoters either was not detected or was at extremely low levels (Fig. 1B). Analysis by ChIP-seq of testes from flies containing a copy of the transgene with epitope-tagged Aly (Aly-HA) showed that in control testes very little Aly protein (a component of the tMAC activator complex) was detected at cryptic promoters (Fig. 3A,B). ATAC-seq in control samples enriched for mid-stage spermatocytes (ctrl72 h) and CAGE in control testes showed that the chromatin at these loci was largely not accessible and that little transcription initiation occurred at the cryptic promoter sites (Fig. 3A,C–E). In contrast, in Kmg knockdown testes, ChIP-seq for Aly-HA revealed strong enrichment at cryptic promoter sites, which were accessible to ATAC-seq and had robust transcription initiation revealed by CAGE (Fig. 3A–E). Images of two genomic regions with cryptic promoters illustrate the increase in Aly ChIP-seq and ATAC-seq signal at cryptic promoter sites in Kmg knockdown testes. At the CG10628 locus, loss of function of Kmg resulted in firing of a cryptic promoter upstream of the promoter normally used in testes (Fig. 3F). A cryptic promoter at the krotzkopf verkehrt (kkv) locus drove the expression of a cryptic transcript in the antisense direction of the host gene in Kmg knockdown testes (Fig. 3G). In both cases, binding of Aly at the cryptic promoter site increased, new CAGE signal appeared, and the cryptic promoter became more accessible to ATAC-seq in Kmg knockdown testes.

Analysis of promoter expression showed that transcription from cryptic promoters depended on the function of Aly and the testis TAF Sa for maximal transcript production. Expression from cryptic promoters was considerably lower in testes lacking both Kmg and Aly than for loss of Kmg function alone (Fig. 3H). Expression from cryptic promoters was also lower in testes lacking both Kmg and Sa (Fig. 3H), a testis-specific TAF (tTAF) known to boost expression of tMAC-dependent genes in wild-type testes (Hiller et al. 2004). Together, these data showed that in Kmg knockdown testes, cryptic promoters were bound by Aly and dependent on Aly and Sa function for full expression.

Kmg is enriched at cryptic promoters in Mi-2 and Dany knockdown testes

Although Kmg did not accumulate at cryptic promoter sites in control testes, Kmg protein was enriched at cryptic promoters in spermatocytes lacking the function of Dany or Mi-2 (Fig. 4A,B) in which the cryptic promoters were actively producing transcripts. Plots of ChIP-seq, RNA-seq, and CAGE data at two example genes with cryptic promoters showed no accumulation of ChIP-seq signal for Kmg at the cryptic promoters in control testes but substantial enrichment of Kmg when the function of either Mi-2 or Dany was knocked down in spermatocytes (Fig. 4C,D). Loss of function of Kmg resulted in increased signal from ChIP-seq for the tMAC component Aly at these loci (Fig. 4C,D), as shown for other cryptic promoters in Figure 3. As discussed below, we suspect that Kmg acts transiently to repress cryptic promoters in wild-type testes, with a dynamic process depending on Kmg, Dany, and Mi-2 action, eventually ejecting Aly and closing the local chromatin. Our data suggest that although Kmg can be recruited to cryptic promoters in the absence of its binding partners Dany or Mi-2, transcription repression is not established, Aly remains bound, and the chromatin remains accessible for transcript initiation.

Kmg function was also necessary to prevent Aly from binding at thousands of loci genome-wide that did not overlap with productive promoters. In addition to the novel Aly peaks that appeared at cryptic promoters in Kmg loss-of-function testes, 6712 Aly peaks (peak q-value < 10−10) that did not overlap with promoters were called from Aly ChIP-seq data from Kmg knockdown testes but not in control samples (e.g., see Fig. 4D, green arrow; Supplemental Fig. S7A). Chromatin dynamics at these nonproductive Aly peaks closely resembled that at cryptic promoters. While these sites showed little enrichment by ChIP-seq for Aly and little ATAC-seq signal in control testes, they became bound by Aly and accessible to ATAC-seq in Kmg knockdown testes (Supplemental Fig. S7A,B). Although production of polyA+ transcripts was not detected from these loci in Kmg knockdown testes, a very low enrichment for CAGE signal was detected. (Supplemental Fig. S7A,C). Also, as seen for the cryptic promoters, the Aly peaks that appeared at these nonpromoter sites upon loss of function of Kmg marked regions that became enriched for Kmg in testes lacking function or Mi-2 or Dany (Supplemental Fig. S7A,D).

Together, these results showed that loss of function of Kmg in spermatocytes resulted in increased binding of the tMAC component Aly at many sites scattered across the genome. Some of these new Aly-bound sites produced polyA+ transcripts from normally silent (cryptic) promoters, whereas many other sites with novel Aly peaks did not substantially produce transcripts. However, both the productive and the nonproductive novel Aly peakswere enriched for Kmg protein in spermatocytes lacking Mi-2 or Dany.

Analysis of the chromatin landscape at sites that gain Aly peaks but did not produce transcripts in Kmg knockdown showed a lack of the H3K27me3- and H3K9me2-repressive marks in addition to a lack of H3K4me3 and elongating RNA Pol II (phospho-ser2) (Supplemental Fig. S8; Anderson et al. 2023), indicating that these loci are not likely to be located in repressive chromatin. It is possible that transcription weakly initiated but was not successful at these open chromatin regions because the DNA lacked the sequences required for robust recruitment of Pol II, Mediator, testis TAFs, or other effectors needed for transcription elongation (Hiller et al. 2001, 2004; Lu and Fuller 2015). Alternatively, transcripts initiating from these loci could be prematurely terminated, fail to be polyadenylated, or be rapidly degraded.

Kmg is enriched at loci bound by Aly in wild-type testes

In wild-type testes, the tMAC component Aly also bound to thousands of sites across the genome, many of which did not initiate appreciable levels of polyA+ transcripts. ChIP-seq for Aly identified 14,400 peaks strongly enriched by Aly in control testes (peak q-value < 10−10), of which 4786 Aly peaks (33%) overlapped with promoters expressed in control testes. For 1952 of these, expression from the promoters was strongly Aly-dependent (downregulated >16-fold in aly mutants compared with control) (Fig. 5A, top; Supplemental Fig. S9). Surprisingly, Aly was enriched at many more sites on the genome than the promoters it regulates. One-thousand-seventy-nine Aly peaks mapped to promoters that were expressed in testes but did not require Aly for expression (Fig. 5A, middle; Supplemental Fig. S9). In addition, Aly was robustly enriched at 9585 loci (67% of the total Aly peaks) that did not overlap with promoters detected as expressed in testes (in neither control nor Kmg knockdown testes) (Fig. 5A, bottom). Comparing ATAC-seq data from aly mutant testes versus testes samples enriched for mid-stage spermatocytes (ctrl72 h) showed that Aly was needed to open chromatin at both the Aly-dependent promoters (Fig. 5A,D) and the thousands of loci that had Aly peaks but lacked productive transcription (Fig. 5A,F). In contrast, the function of Aly was not required for chromatin to be open at Aly-bound but Aly-independent promoters, which expressed similar levels of transcripts in aly mutant testes as in wild type (Aly-independent promoters) (Fig. 5A,E). As expected, CAGE signal was detected at loci where productive elongation was detected (Fig. 5A,G,H). Aly peaks that did not overlap with productive promoters had very low detectable CAGE signal (Fig. 5A,I).

Similar to the novel nontranscribing Aly-bound loci observed in Kmg knockdown testes (Supplemental Fig. S7A,B), these Aly peaks observed in wild-type testes lacked H3K27me3- and H3K9me2-repressive marks (Supplemental Fig. S8) and may lack important promoter information for transcription initiation or elongation or produce unstable RNAs.

Kmg colocalized with peaks of Aly throughout the genome in wild-type testes whether or not those Aly peaks overlapped with promoters. ChIP-seq for Kmg protein showed enrichment at most of the loci robustly bound by Aly in control testes (Fig. 5A,C), with the level of Kmg ChIP-seq signal seeming to correlate with the level of Aly ChIP-seq signal (Fig. 5A–C). The coincidence of genomic regions bound by Aly and by Kmg raised the possibilities that Aly or other components of tMAC either directly or indirectly recruit Kmg or that the Aly-dependent opening of chromatin at Aly peaks is required for Kmg to bind. Consistent with these possibilities, immunofluorescence analysis showed the Kmg protein present in aly mutant testes but diffused throughout the nucleoplasm rather than concentrated on chromatin as observed in wild type (Supplemental Fig. S10A,B). In addition, ChIP-seq in aly mutants revealed that Kmg was no longer enriched at Aly-dependent promoters or other sites bound by Aly in wild-type testes (Supplemental Fig. S11A,B).

Kmg, Dany, and Mi-2 localize along the transcribed region downstream from highly expressed Aly-dependent promoters

Although ChIP-seq for Kmg enriched for sequences near the transcription start site of most Aly-dependent promoters, the results were different for very highly expressed Aly-dependent promoters, where Kmg colocalized with Dany and Mi-2 along the gene body (Fig. 6A–C). The set of promoters that bound Aly (ChIP-seq) and required Aly function to be accessible (ATAC-seq) and initiate transcription (CAGE signal; 1952 Aly-dependent) (Fig. 5A, top) was transcribed at various levels, from very high to low (RNA-seq signal) (Fig. 6A). For example, promoters for the CG4836 and S-Lap7 genes were bound by Aly and were among the highest-expressed Aly-dependent promoters transcribed in spermatocytes. Analysis of ChIP-seq at these loci showed a peak of Aly at the promoter regions, whereas ChIP-seq for the Kmg, Dany, or Mi-2 proteins enriched for sequences along the gene body (Fig. 6B). These binding profiles were general features of the top 5% highest-expressed Aly-dependent promoters (Fig. 6C).

In contrast, for the 5% lowest-expressed Aly-bound and Aly-dependent promoters in wild-type testes, the ChIP-seq signal for Kmg was much lower overall but was nevertheless more focused at sequences near the TSS and not along the gene body. Comparison of Kmg ChIP-seq signal at the 5% most highly expressed, 5% mid-expressed, and 5% lowest-expressed promoters showed a gradual shift of Kmg distribution from stretched out along the gene body at highly expressed genes to a peak near the promoter in lower-expressed promoters (Fig. 6D).

Strikingly, binding of Kmg along the gene body downstream from highly expressed Aly-dependent promoters required the function of the Kmg-interacting proteins Dany and Mi-2. When expression of Dany or Mi-2 was knocked down in spermatocytes, the Kmg ChIP-seq signal was much reduced along the gene bodies of CG4836 and S-Lap7 (Fig. 6B). A metaplot of Kmg ChIP-seq signal at the 5% most highly expressed Aly-bound and Aly-dependent promoters shows that knockdown of Dany or Mi-2 resulted in loss of Kmg ChIP-seq enrichment from along gene bodies, though some enrichment near the promoters remained (Fig. 6E), similar to the level of Kmg ChIP-seq signal observed at lowly expressed Aly-dependent promoters (Fig. 6F). At the lowest-expressed Aly-dependent promoters, enrichment by ChIP-seq for Kmg was focused at the TSS in control testes in a pattern that remained largely the same upon loss of function of either Dany or Mi-2 (Fig. 6F).

Mi-2 loss of function results in downregulation of highly expressed Aly-dependent promoters

Although Kmg, Dany, and Mi-2 colocalized at gene bodies downstream from highly expressed Aly-dependent promoters, loss of function of these proteins had different effects on promoter expression. Highly expressed Aly-bound, Aly-dependent promoters were still expressed at very high levels in Kmg knockdown or Dany knockdown compared with control testes (Fig. 6H, dark green dots; Supplemental Fig. S12A,C). Expression from the promoters appeared slightly lower than in control testes, in part due to the higher complexity of the Kmg and Dany loss-of-function RNA-seq libraries compared with control (Supplemental Fig. S13). ChIP-seq for Aly showed slightly less enrichment at these promoters in Kmg knockdown compared with control testes (Supplemental Fig. S12E), also possibly in part due to the vast increase in Aly-occupied sites in Kmg knockdown.

In contrast, loss of function of Mi-2 in spermatocytes resulted in substantial downregulation of the highly expressed Aly-dependent promoters (Fig. 6I; Supplemental Fig. S12C). Although ChIP-seq for Mi-2 enriched along the gene body of highly expressed Aly-dependent loci in control testes, enrichment of Mi-2 was greatly reduced in Kmg knockdown testes (Fig. 6B,G), suggesting that Kmg function was necessary for recruitment of Mi-2 to chromatin at these sites. Despite the lack of local Mi-2 along the gene bodies downstream from highly expressed promoters in Kmg knockdown testes, expression from the promoters remained high in Kmg knockdown. We speculate that Mi-2 may be required at highly expressed loci to prevent repression by Kmg (see the Discussion).

Kmg acts to dampen transcription of low-expressed Aly-dependent promoters

In contrast to highly expressed promoters, the function of Kmg and Dany in spermatocytes was required to keep expression from weakly expressed Aly-dependent promoters low. As the level of expression from Aly-bound, Aly-dependent promoters decreased in control testes (Fig. 6H, X-axis), their level of expression in Kmg knockdown testes increased (Fig. 6H, Y-axis) compared with control testes. A similar gradual shift was observed for Dany knockdown testes (Supplemental Fig. S12A). The Aly-bound, Aly-dependent promoters expressed at low levels in control testes (Fig. 6H, light green dots) were strongly upregulated in Kmg knockdown and Dany knockdown compared with control testes (Fig. 6H; Supplemental Fig. S12A,D). Kmg and Dany appeared to have similar but more extreme repressive effects at cryptic promoters (Fig. 6H, pink dots), which were even less expressed in control testes and strongly upregulated upon loss of function of Kmg or Dany (Fig. 6H; Supplemental Fig. S12A). Indeed, some Aly-bound, Aly-dependent promoters expressed at very low levels in control testes became so highly upregulated in Kmg knockdown testes that they reached the threshold to be classified as cryptic promoters (Fig. 6H, yellow dots). For the Aly-bound, Aly-dependent promoters expressed at low levels in wild type, as well as the cryptic promoters, chromatin accessibility and expression from the promoter required the function of Aly (Figs. 3, 5).

Analysis of promoter regions for motifs and AT/CG content revealed differences between robustly expressed and weak or cryptic promoters. The highly expressed Aly-bound, Aly-dependent promoters were enriched for the Inr element (a canonical core promoter element) at their TSSs (Supplemental Fig. S14A). These promoters were also enriched for the previously characterized Aly ChIP and Achi/Vis binding site motifs upstream of the TSSs and two downstream motifs that had been observed to correlate with efficient transcription in spermatocytes: ACACA and CNAAATT (Supplemental Fig. S14A; Lu et al. 2020). In addition, the highly expressed Aly-bound, Aly-dependent promoters had high local AT content (Supplemental Fig. S14E), as noted by Lu et al. (2020) for the class of genes that turned on in spermatocytes. Interestingly, although both the lowly expressed Aly-bound, Aly-dependent promoters and cryptic promoters were enriched for the Inr at their TSSs, the positions enriched for the Aly ChIP and Achi/Vis motifs tended to overlap the Inr and lacked the ACACA and CNAAATT motifs (Supplemental Fig. S14B,C); in addition, the promoters showed no AT enrichment (Supplemental Fig. S14E,F).

Discussion

Our results show that the transcription program that drives differentiation in the Drosophila male germline adult stem cell lineage set up by the master regulator of spermatocyte differentiation tMAC is restrained by a cell type-specific mechanism that downregulates weak promoters while allowing robust transcription from highly expressed, tMAC-dependent genes. Based on ChIP-seq for its subunit, Aly, the spermatocyte-specific tMAC complex binds thousands of sites in the genome, where in most cases Aly function is required for chromatin to become accessible from a characteristically closed state in spermatogonia. Robust transcription initiates from a subset of these Aly-bound loci, in many cases triggering genes only expressed in spermatocytes and in others firing alternative, spermatocyte-specific promoters in genes already being expressed in previous developmental stages or other cell types (Lu et al. 2020; this study).

We found that Kmg and Dany, two proteins expressed at the onset of spermatocyte development, interact with the nucleosome remodeler Mi-2 and possibly other components of the NuRD complex to dampen productive transcription from normally low-expressed Aly-dependent promoters (Fig. 7A). Transcription from these promoters was upregulated upon loss of function of Kmg, Dany, or the NuRD component Simj. ChIP-seq for Kmg enriched for Aly-bound, Aly-dependent, low-expressed promoters, suggesting that Kmg could act locally to repress transcription at weak Aly-bound promoters in spermatocytes. This is consistent with the previous finding that Kmg scored as one of the most powerful repressors when artificially tethered to different core promoters or enhancers (Stampfel et al. 2015).

Proposed model. (A,A′,A″) Diagram of an Aly-bound, Aly-dependent promoter expressed at low levels in wild type. (A) In wild-type testes, Aly (tMAC) binds to the promoter and opens chromatin, allowing expression (1). The Kmg/Dany complex at the promoter site dampens transcription, keeping expression low (2). We propose that Mi-2, a chromatin remodeler, is not successful in closing chromatin at these sites (3). (A′) In Kmg knockdown testes, the Kmg complex is not recruited or not functional, chromatin is open, and the promoter is upregulated (1). (A″) We propose that in Mi-2 knockdown testes, chromatin does not close, and expression level of the promoter stays low, as Kmg/Dany can still repress transcription (2). (B,B′,B″) Diagram of an Aly-bound, Aly-dependent promoter expressed at very high levels in wild type. (B) We speculate that in wild-type testes, Mi-2 associates with nascent RNAs transcribed from the very active promoters (yellow star). This antagonizes Mi-2 binding to chromatin and allows Kmg/Dany to be pulled away from the promoter, which is thus protected from repression and highly active (1). (B′) In Kmg knockdown testes, the Kmg complex is not recruited or not functional, and the promoter is expressed at high levels (1). (B″) We propose that in Mi-2 knockdown testes, Kmg/Dany are not pulled away from the promoter where it can dampen transcription (2). (C,C′,C″) Diagram of a cryptic promoter. (C) We speculate that in wild-type testes, Aly initially binds to chromatin (1), the Kmg/Dany complex represses transcription (2), and Mi-2 closes chromatin (3), evicting Aly (and the Kmg complex). The binding of these players at cryptic promoters is thus transient. (C′) In Kmg knockdown testes, the surveillance function is lacking, Aly binds and opens chromatin unopposed, and promoters are highly expressed (1). (C″) We propose that in Mi-2 knockdown testes, chromatin does not close (due to the loss of the chromatin remodeler), Aly is not evicted, and cryptic promoters are transcribed (1). However, Kmg binding at the promoter site keeps expression at lower levels (2) compared with loss of Kmg.

The action of Kmg, Dany, Simj, and Mi-2 is also required to block transcription in spermatocytes from hundreds of cryptic promoters scattered across the major autosomes. These cryptic promoters normally showed very little Aly ChIP-seq enrichment and remained largely inaccessible to ATAC-seq in wild-type spermatocytes, but in the absence of Kmg function, Aly bound to the promoter region, a small stretch of local chromatin became accessible, and productive transcription initiated. In spermatocytes lacking the function of either Dany or Mi-2, ChIP-seq revealed Kmg enriched at the cryptic promoters. We hypothesize that under normal conditions, both the activating tMAC complex and the Kmg complex may bind transiently at cryptic promoters, with Kmg and Dany effectively repressing transcription, and Mi-2 closing chromatin and evicting tMAC (Fig. 7C). We speculate that in Dany or Mi-2 loss of function, Kmg is successfully recruited to cryptic promoters but is not sufficient to repress transcription in the absence of its partner proteins.

Strikingly, Kmg, Dany, and Mi-2 were not enriched at Aly-dependent promoters that were highly expressed in wild type but instead colocalized along the gene bodies of the highly transcribed genes. No components of the RNA polymerase II complex were detected in our IP-MS for proteins that interact with Kmg. We speculate that the Kmg/Dany/Mi-2 complex may be drawn away from promoters by binding of Mi-2 to nascent RNA, a property of Mi-2 demonstrated by Ullah et al. (2022). The investigators showed that Mi-2 cross-linked with thousands of mRNAs at sequences toward the 3′ end and away from promoters, with a higher propensity to highly expressed transcripts. In addition, in vitro experiments by Ullah et al. (2022) showed that RNA directly inhibited binding of Mi-2 to nucleosomes and Mi-2 nucleosome remodeling activity. The investigators proposed that binding of Mi-2 to nascent transcripts at highly expressed genes could provide a mechanism to remove the repressive NuRD complex away from promoters, sparing highly expressed genes from repression (Ullah et al. 2022). We hypothesize that Mi-2 plays a similar role in the spermatocyte-specific transcription program, clearing the Kmg–Dany complex away from highly active promoters and thus protecting them from repression (Fig. 7B). In agreement with this model, when the function of Mi-2 was knocked down in spermatocytes, the Kmg protein was no longer enriched along the gene body of highly expressed Aly-dependent genes, and the promoters were strongly downregulated. Our data suggest that Mi-2 is not required to actively induce expression from the highly expressed, Aly-dependent promoters because in spermatocytes lacking Kmg function, Mi-2 protein was lost from the loci, but the promoters were still expressed at very high levels. We suspect that ChIP-seq detected Kmg, Dany, and Mi-2 along very highly expressed gene bodies because the levels of nascent RNA at these loci were so high that the complex could be cross-linked to RNA and adjacent chromatin.

We propose that the same players exist in a dynamic battle at all Aly-bound and Aly-dependent promoters, with the outcome of the battle between chromatin opening and activation by tMAC and repression by the Kmg complex depending on how robustly the promoter is able to drive expression of elongating transcripts. In this framework, the ability of Mi-2 to bind nascent transcripts and so pull the Kmg/Dany-repressive complex away from highly active promoters may provide a mechanism to amplify differences in promoter expression levels, suppressing minimal promoters while allowing expression from robust promoters to proceed (Fig. 7).

Failure of the Kmg surveillance system results in the firing of thousands of cryptic promoters genome-wide, generating aberrant transcripts that frequently share downstream exons with known genes, leading to proteomic chaos. Productive elongation from cryptic promoters is predicted to result in the expression of potentially hundreds of N-terminally truncated proteins, as well as completely aberrant polypeptides and proteins not normally expressed in spermatocytes. We wonder whether similar mechanisms are at play in other key differentiation pathways and whether their failure may contribute to abnormal differentiation or cancer.

Materials and methods

Fly strains and husbandry

All flies were grown on standard molasses media. Fly crosses were kept for 3 days at 25°C. Adults were then flipped to a new bottle, and the eggs and early larvae were shifted to 29°C. For RNAi-induced knockdowns (KDs), virgins from bamGal4 (Chen and McKearin 2003) were crossed with males from UAS-Kmg RNAi (Vienna Drosophila Resource Center [VDRC] 107395), UAS-Dany RNAi (VDRC 105571), UAS-Simj RNAi (VDRC 100285), or UAS-Mi-2 RNAi (VDRC 107204). For aly mutants, virgins from aly5p/TM6 were crossed with males from aly2/TM6, and aly2/aly5p males were dissected (White-Cooper et al. 2000). For sa mutants, virgins from sa1/TM6 were crossed with males from sa2/TM6, and sa1/sa2 males were dissected (Hiller et al. 2004). For Kmg KD and aly rescue, virgins from aly2,bamGal4/TM6 were crossed with males from UAS-Kmg RNAi/CyO;aly5p/TM6, and UAS-Kmg RNAi/+;aly2,bamGal4/aly5p males were dissected. For Kmg KD and sa rescue, virgins from sa1,bamGal4/TM6 were crossed with males from UAS-Kmg RNAi/CyO;sa2/TM6, and UAS-Kmg RNAi/+;sa1,bamGal4/sa2 males were dissected. For co-IP experiments, flies from the following stocks were dissected: V5-Dany/V5-Dany;Mi-2-GFP/TM6 or V5-Dany/V5-Dany and Mi-2-GFP (Flytrap CA06598). For ChIP-seq of Aly-HA, testes were dissected from flies bearing one copy of the Aly-HA genomic rescue transgene generated by Kim et al. (2017).

N-terminal V5-tagged Dany (V5-Dany) was created by CRISPR–Cas9-mediated V5 sequence insertion at the endogenous Dany genomic location. The guide RNA sequence [ACTCGGGGACGCTTACCACG(TGG)] was cloned into the pU6B plasmid as described by Ren et al. (2013). Dany (CG30401) genomic sequences from 1253 bp upstream of the ATG to 307 bp downstream from the stop codon were PCR-amplified from Nanos-Cas9 (third chromosome) flies (Ren et al. 2013) as two fragments and stitched together at the start codon ATG with a SmaI site introduced in the primer. The DNA sequence of V5 (GGTAAGCCTATCCCTAACCCTCTCCTCGGTCTCGATTCTACG) was inserted right after the start ATG in the PCR primer. The resulting 2485 bp V5-Dany genomic donor sequence was cloned into the SalI and NotI sites of pBS-SK+ using restriction sites introduced in the PCR primers. Two silent mutations were then introduced to the CRISPR seed sequences in this donor plasmid by site-directed mutagenesis to prevent Cas9 cutting of the donor. pBS_V5-Dany donor plasmid (200 ng/µL) was mixed with 150 ng/µL pU6B plasmid containing the guide sequences and injected into embryos of Nanos-Cas9 flies. Injected flies were allowed to mate to TM3/TM6b;Sp/CyO double-balancer flies and produce progeny before screening by PCR for genomic insertion of the V5 sequence. Twenty CyO virgin female progeny from each PCR-positive parent were single-clone-mated to double-balancer males and allowed to lay eggs for 1 week before being screened by PCR again for germline clones. Testes from 20 males of each balanced line were dissected and stained with an anti-V5 antibody to confirm expression of V5-Dany specifically in spermatocytes. Positive lines were made homozygous for further characterization.

Immunofluorescence staining

Testes from 0–2 day old males were dissected in 1× PBS on a cyclops dissecting dish for <30 min at room temperature. Immunostainings were performed following the protocol described by Gallicchio et al. (2024). The primary antibodies used were rabbit anti-Kmg (1:10,000; serum AA; used as described by Kim et al. [2017]), chicken anti-GFP (1:10,000; Abcam 13970), and mouse anti-V5 (1:200; Invitrogen 46-0705). The secondary antibodies used were donkey or goat antirabbit, antimouse, or antichicken, all used at 1:500 and conjugated with either Alexa 488, Alexa 568, or Alexa 647 (all from Thermo Fisher). Imaging was performed on a Leica SP8 confocal microscope.

HCR RNA-FISH

Testes were dissected from 0–2 day old males in 1× PBS on a cyclops dissecting dish for <30 min at room temperature, fixed, and permeabilized as done for immunofluorescence. The protocol for “generic sample in solution” (Rev9) from the Molecular Instruments website (https://www.molecularinstruments.com) was followed with alterations to the protocol as described by Gallicchio et al. (2024). Probes were designed as described by Bedbrook et al. (2023) and were obtained from Integrated DNA Technologies as oligo pools (50 pmol/oligo; oPools). Hybridization buffer, wash buffer, amplification buffer, and hairpins (conjugated with either 488 or 546 fluorophores) were obtained from Molecular Instruments (HCR RNA-FISH bundle). Probe sequences are listed in Supplemental Table S1. Imaging was performed on a Leica SP8 confocal microscope.

Western blots

Testes from 15 males 0–2 days old were quickly dissected into 1× PBS in a cyclops dissecting dish at room temperature and then transferred to a 1.7 mL Eppendorf tube containing 1× PBS. The 1× PBS was then removed, and the tube was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C. The Western blots were performed as described by Gallicchio et al. (2024) with the following alterations: The lysis buffer used was JUME buffer (10 mM MOPS, 10 mM EDTA, 8 M urea, 1% SDS). The primary antibodies used for Western blots were mouse anti-Taf1 (1:200; 30H9 hybridoma supernatant; a kind gift from Bob Tjian), mouse anti-Chro (1:10,000; 6H11), mouse anti-Hfp (1:200; 6G10-12), rabbit anti-GFP (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology 2956S), rabbit anti-Kmg (1:100,000; serum AA; purified for use as described by Kim et al. [2017]), and mouse anti-V5 (1:1000; Invitrogen 46-0705). The secondary antibodies used for Western blots were peroxidase AffiniPure goat antrabbit IgG (H+L; 1:10,000; Jackson ImmunoResearch AB_2313567) and peroxidase AffiniPure goat antimouse IgG (H+L; 1:10,000; Jackson ImmunoResearch AB_10015289).

Immunoprecipitation

Protein A Dynabeads (Thermo Fisher Scientific 10002D) were used with rabbit anti-Kmg (Kim et al. 2017), and pan-mouse IgG Dynabeads (Thermo Fisher Scientific 11041) were used for anti-GFP (mouse; Millipore Sigma 11814460001). Testes were dissected into 1× PBS in a cyclops dissecting dish in batches of 50 flies for <30 min at room temperature and transferred to a 1.7 mL Eppendorf tube with 1× PBS. The 1× PBS was removed, and the tube was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C. For immunoprecipitation followed by Western blot (co-IP), 150 pairs of testes per sample were used. The protocol used was as described by Baker et al. (2023) with the following alterations: Neither RNase A nor SUPERase were added to the lysis buffer. The supernatant from the IP step was kept to run as a control lane. Proteins from the input, IP, and supernatant samples were precipitated (protocol described below) prior to Western blot (protocol described above). For immunoprecipitation followed by mass spectrometry (IP-MS), the protocol used was as described by Baker et al. (2023). One-thousand pairs of testes per sample were used. Testes from bamGal4 served as the wild-type sample (labeled NM_WT_1_171009 in Supplemental Table S2), and Kmg knockdown testes served as a negative control sample (labeled NM_KD_2_171009 in Supplemental Table S2). Samples were processed and analyzed by the Stanford University Mass Spectrometry Core. Complete IP-MS data are in Supplemental Table S2.

Protein precipitation

Four volumes of methanol (ice-cold) was added to 1 vol of protein sample (in a 1.7 mL Eppendorf), . The mixture was vortexed, and then 1 vol of chloroform was added. After vortexing again, 3 vol of H2O was added, and the mixture was vortexed and then centrifuged at maximum speed for 5 min at 4°C. The upper liquid layer was removed, 3 vol of ice-cold methanol was added, and the mixture was vortexed. The tube was centrifuged at maximum speed for 10 min at 4°C. All liquid was removed, and the protein pellet was air-dried and resuspended in 40 µL of JUME buffer (10 mM MOPS, 10 mM EDTA, 8 M urea, 1% SDS).

PacBio library preparation and sequencing

Testes were dissected from 0–2 day old males in 1× PBS as batches of 50 flies in a cyclops dissecting dish for <30 min at room temperature. Each batch was transferred to a 1.7 mL Eppendorf tube containing 1 mL of 1× PBS; the PBS was then immediately removed, and the testes were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C. RNA was extracted from 150 pairs of testes using the RNeasy Plus mini kit (Qiagen 74104). Frozen tissue was dissociated using a 1 mL syringe with a 27 gauge needle aspirating up and down ∼10 times in 300 µL of lysis buffer (from the RNeasy kit) supplemented with 1:100 β-mercaptoethanol. The quality of the extracted RNA was assessed via Bioanalyzer, and the concentration was measured by Qubit RNA HS assay kit (Life Technologies Q32852). Iso-Seq SMRTbell libraries were made following the “procedure and checklist – Iso-Seq Express template preparation for Sequel and Sequel II systems” protocol from PacBio. Reagents from the following kits were used for library preparation and sequencing: SMRT Bell Express template express kit 2.0 (100-938-900), Iso-Seq Express oligo kit—PacBio (PN 101-737-500), NEBNext single-cell/low input RNA library preparation kit for Illumina (E6420S), Sequel II binding kit 2.1 (101-843-000), and Sequel II sequencing kit 2.0 (101-820-200). Each sample was sequenced on one SMRT Cell 8M (101-389-001) on a Sequel IIe system (24 h run time) at the Chan Zuckerberg Biohub SF Genomics Platform. Circular consensus sequencing (CCS) and Iso-Seq analysis were performed in SMRT Link v11.0.

Two biological replicates were performed per condition. Sequencing was done with 75 bp PE using a HiSeq 4000 at the Stanford Functional Genomics Facility,.

ChIP-seq library preparation and sequencing

Testes from 0–2 day old males were dissected in 1× PBS in batches of 50–100 flies in a cyclops dissecting dish for <30 min at room temperature. Each batch was transferred to a 1.7 mL Eppendorf tube containing 1 mL of 1× PBS and then immediately fixed by adding 28 µL of 37% formaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich 252549), and the sample was incubated for 10 min at room temperature with rotation. The reaction was quenched by adding 52.6 µL of 2.5 M glycine and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. The liquid was removed, and 1 mL of 1× PBS was added. After repeating this wash, the PBS was removed, and the tube was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C. ChIP-seq was performed using the low-cell ChIP kit (Active Motif 53086). Protein G agarose beads were prepared following “session D” of the low-cell ChIP kit protocol. While incubating the “preclearing” beads, the chromatin sample was prepared as follows: We pooled 350 pairs of frozen testes in 130 µL of ChIP buffer mix (from the low-cell ChIP kit). The tissue was dissociated using a 1 mL syringe with a 27 gauge needle aspirating up and down ∼10 times. The sample was transferred to microTUBE-130 (microTUBE AFA fiber preslit snap cap 520045), kept on ice, and sonicated using Covaris (E220 Evolution) with the following treatment for 600 sec, peak power: 75, duty factor: 10, cycles/burst: 200, average power: 17.5 and using intensifier. The sample was then transferred to new a 1.7 mL Eppendorf tube and centrifuged for 5 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was transferred to new tube. The volume of the transferred sample was brought to 200 µL by adding ∼70 µL of ChIP buffer mix, and 5% of input (10 µL) was removed and saved at 4°C until needed. Preclearing of the chromatin and immunoprecipitation, washing, and elution of IP reactions were done as described in “sessions E–G” of the low-cell ChIP kit protocol. Antibodies used for the antibody mixture were rabbit anti-Kmg (Kim et al. 2017), mouse anti-V5 (Invitrogen 46-0705), rabbit anti-HA (Cell Signaling C29F4), or rabbit anti-H3K4me3 (abcam ab8580). The eluted ChIP DNA (end of section G) was transferred to a 250 µL PCR tube, and 2 µL of Proteinase K and 5 µL of 5 M NaCl were added. The tubes were vortexed to mix and kept overnight at 65°C in a thermocycler to reverse cross-links. For the input samples, 90 µL of TE and 2 µL of RNase A were added, and the mixture was incubated for 30 min at 37°C. Next, the input samples were transferred to PCR tubes, 2 µL of Proteinase K and 5 µL of 5 M NaCl were added, and the mixtures were incubated overnight at 65°C. A DNA purification protocol alternative to that proposed by the kit manufacturers was used as follows: ChIP samples and input samples were moved to new 1.7 mL Eppendorf tubes, and 1.8× volume of SPRIselect beads (Beckman Coulter B23318) were added, mixed by pipetting, and incubated for 15 min at room temperature. The beads were magnetically separated and washed twice with 80% ethanol, and DNA was eluted with 50 µL of TE. The libraries were made using NEBNext Ultra II DNA library preparation kit for Illumina NEB (E7645L). Note that due to low concentration, input DNA was not quantified; instead, all 50 µL of eluted DNA was used. Adaptor was diluted 1:25. We skipped “3A size selection of adaptor-ligated DNA.” Two biological replicates were performed per condition. Sequencing was performed by Novogene on a NovaSeq PE 150 Illumina platform.

PacBio data analysis

Processing of each library of PacBio HiFi reads was done following the IsoSeq pipeline (ccs, lima, and IsoSeq 3.4.0). Data were mapped to Drosophila genome assembly BDGP6.32 (GCA_000001215.4) using pbmm2 (1.12.0). Tama (b0.0.0) collapse was used to collapse redundant transcript models (Kuo et al. 2020) using the following parameters: -x capped (though our libraries are not 5′-capped, this avoids overcollapsing), -a 78 (78 bp interquantile width that includes 90% of CAGE clusters from parallel analysis), and -z 80 (80 bp interquantile width that includes 90% of 3′end clusters from parallel analysis). See the analysis pipeline in Supplemental Figure S1.

RNA-seq data analysis

RNA-seq data were used independently in two pipelines: (1) RNA-seq data were used as input files to run SQANTI3, which used Kallisto to map reads to transcriptome and quantify transcript isoforms (details described below). (2) Adapters and low-quality bases were trimmed with trimGalore (0.6.6) (Martin 2011), reads were mapped to Drosophila genome assembly BDGP6.32 (GCA_000001215.4) using STAR (2.7.9a) (Dobin et al. 2013), and deepTools (3.3.0) was used to generate normalized coverage tracks for visualization and plot heat maps (Ramírez et al. 2016).

CAGE data analysis

Low-quality bases were trimmed using TrimGalore (0.6.6) (Martin 2011) and mapped to Drosophila genome assembly BDGP6.32 (GCA_000001215.4) using STAR (2.7.11b) (Dobin et al. 2013). The most 5′ nucleotide of each mapped read was counted using BEDTools coverage (2.27.1) (Quinlan and Hall 2010) and used as input into CAGEr to build CAGE clusters (1.20.0) (Haberle et al. 2015).

3′ end sequencing data analysis

3′ end raw data for control samples (bamGal4) used here were published previously by Gallicchio et al. (2024), and genomic data are available under NCBI GEO (GSE272579). Kmg knockdown samples were generated for this study. Low-quality bases, polyA tails, and adapters were trimmed using bbmap (39.01), and reads were mapped to Drosophila genome assembly BDGP6.32 (GCA_000001215.4) using STAR (2.7.11b). The most 3′ nucleotide of each mapped read was counted using BEDTools coverage (2.27.1) and used as input into CAGEr to build 3′ end clusters (1.20.0).

ATAC-seq data analysis

ATAC-seq raw data for control (ctrl72 h) and aly mutant samples used here were published previously by Lu et al. (2020), and genomic data are available under NCBI GEO (GSE145975). Kmg knockdown samples were generated for this study. Analysis was done exactly as described by Lu et al. (2020).

ChIP-seq data analysis

ChIP-seq data for HA-Aly and Mi-2 samples used here were published previously by Kim et al. (2017), and raw data are available under NCBI GEO (GSE89506). All other samples were generated for this study. Adapters and low-quality bases were trimmed using trimGalore (0.6.6), and reads were mapped to Drosophila genome assembly BDGP6.32 (GCA_000001215.4) using bwa aln (0.7.18) (Li and Durbin 2009). deepTools (3.1.0) was used to generate normalized coverage tracks for visualization and plot heat maps. MACS2 (2.1.1) was used for peak calling (Zhang et al. 2008). Replicates and input data for all ChIP-seq experiments are presented in Supplemental Figures S15–S17.

Build and quantify transcriptome

Transcriptome models from each PacBio library were merged into one single transcriptome with Tama (b0.0.0) merge (Kuo et al. 2020) using the following parameters: -a 78 (78 bp interquantile width that includes 90% of CAGE clusters from parallel analysis) and -z 80 (80 bp interquantile width that includes 90% of 3′ end clusters from parallel analysis). SQANTI3 was used to integrate our transcriptome model with the Drosophila annotation BDGP6.32.109, predict the ORF for each transcript isoform, map short reads to the transcriptome model, and predict RT switching artifacts (Pardo-Palacios et al. 2024). To focus on full-length transcripts, the transcriptome model was then filtered to keep only transcripts that met the following criteria: Their TSS overlapped with CAGE consensus clusters, their TTS overlapped with 3′ end consensus clusters, all splice junctions had RNA-seq coverage, and the transcript had no RT switching artifacts. To quantify transcript isoform expression, we used RNA-seq data to run SQANTI3 (with Kallisto). tappAS was used for transcript and gene expression analysis and expression of predicted protein isoforms (de la Fuente et al. 2020). See the analysis pipeline in Supplemental Figure S1.

Quantification of promoter output

To define promoters, coordinates of the transcription start site (TSS) for each transcript isoform were intersected with coordinates of CAGE consensus clusters (from CAGE analysis) using BEDTools (2.27.1). The ID of the CAGE consensus cluster was considered the promoter ID. To quantify expression from a given promoter, the expression from all transcripts starting at that promoter was summed. DESeq2 was used for differential promoter expression analysis (Love et al. 2014).

Definition of cryptic promoter and cryptic protein

Cryptic promoters were defined as having an expression level in Kmg knockdown sample (from promoter output analysis) higher than quantile 0.25, being upregulated ≥16-fold in Kmg KD compared with control, and having a Padj-value <0.05; in addition, the promoter (defined by CAGE cluster coordinates) did not contain any annotated TSS (from BDGP6.32). Cryptic proteins were defined as being derived from cryptic promoters, having an expression level in Kmg knockdown sample (from tappAS analysis) higher than quantile 0.25, being upregulated ≥16-fold in Kmg knockdown compared with control, and having a Padj-value <0.05.

Analysis of Aly ChIP-seq peaks

To define one TSS per Aly peak, we intersected Aly ChIP-seq peak coordinates with promoter coordinates using BEDTools. We selected the highest expressed promoter that overlapped with each Aly peak. From the selected promoter, we chose the highest-expressed transcript and got its TSS coordinate, which was used for downstream analysis (described below). Classification of Aly peaks, relative to which type of promoter Aly binds to (Aly-dependent, Aly-independent, or cryptic promoter), was done based on the highest-expressed promoter with which it overlapped.

Downstream analysis

deepTools was used to generate matrixes used for graphs of enrichment profiles and to plot heat maps (3.3.0) (Ramírez et al. 2016). Graphs were generated using ggplot2 (Wickham 2016). Integrative genomics viewer (IGV) was used for genomic data visualization (Robinson et al. 2011). SRplot was used to plot the chromosome distribution of cryptic promoters (Tang et al. 2023).
