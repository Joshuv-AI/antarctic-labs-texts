Epigenetic Control of Mammalian Cardiomyocyte Proliferation
Authors: Francesca Butera, David A. Elliott, Enzo R. Porrello
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose of ReviewPostnatal shutdown of the mammalian cardiomyocyte cell cycle underpins limited regenerative capacity of the adult heart. An epigenetic programme regulates a switch at birth from a proliferative state to functional maturity. Identification of the molecular components regulating this switch has revealed therapeutic opportunities for heart regeneration that have started entering the clinic.Recent FindingsGenome-wide analyses reveal that histone modifications and DNA methylation remodel chromatin in adult cardiomyocytes, downregulating cell cycle activation genes while upregulating maturation genes. Epigenetic regulation of upstream transcriptional pathways (e.g. YAP, WNT and Notch) prevents cell cycle activation in the adult heart. Upregulating glycolysis or suppressing oxidative metabolism enhances cardiac repair following injury, with hypoxia demonstrating safety in humans. Gene therapy techniques enabling myocardial targeting have enhanced the translation of cardiac regenerative therapies, with YAP upregulation paving the way for future trials.SummaryThe cardiomyocyte cell cycle is regulated by CDKs/cyclins controlled by transcriptional networks that are epigenetically suppressed in the postnatal period. Adult cardiomyocytes also harbour structural barriers and a metabolic state preventing proliferation. Pharmacological and genetic manipulation of these mechanisms can re-activate adult cardiomyocyte proliferation. However, regenerative therapies are challenged by the intrinsic link between proliferation and epigenetics, metabolism, and ultimately cardiac function.

Introduction

Heart failure is a leading cause of death affecting over 60 million people worldwide. The prognosis for heart failure remains poor even for those receiving first-line medical therapy [1]. The only effective intervention for patients with end-stage heart failure is transplantation, which is constrained by a shortage of healthy donor organs [2]. A common cause of heart failure is ischaemic heart disease, which is defined by extensive cardiomyocyte death [3]. Due to the low intrinsic regenerative capacity of the adult mammalian heart [4], ischaemic tissue is not replaced by new cardiomyocytes but rather by fibrotic scar tissue. In addition to acute (< 24 h) cardiomyocyte death during a major ischaemic event, heart failure itself is associated with a low level of cardiomyocyte death over months to years, surpassing the baseline age-related rate of cardiomyocyte death in the healthy human heart [5, 6]. Therefore, understanding the signals controlling endogenous regenerative capacity, in particular cardiomyocyte cell cycle control, is a major research area.

Endogenous regulation of cardiomyocyte proliferation in immature cardiomyocytes, such as those present in the fetal human heart, is centred on canonical CDKs and cyclins controlled in expression by transcription factors and co-regulators (e.g. RBPJ, YAP, β-catenin) as a part of larger signalling networks (e.g. Notch, Hippo, WNT) crucial for cardiac development [7–9]. While these networks drive cardiomyocyte proliferation during development [10], the same networks are re-activated during regeneration following myocardial injury in the neonatal heart [11]. However, the cardiomyocyte cell cycle is rapidly shutdown postnatally, preventing re-activation of these pathways if injury occurs in the mature heart, recognised as from 1 week after birth in mice [12]. These mechanisms, as discussed in this review, include 1) structural barriers such as polyploidisation, multinucleation and sarcomere maturation; 2) epigenetic marks altered directly at genomic loci associated with cell cycle genes; 3) broader epigenetic rewiring in the mature heart suppressing cell cycle transcriptional networks active in immature cardiomyocytes; and 4) increased oxygen exposure simultaneous with a shift in metabolic substrate utilisation that favour a non-proliferative state.

Mammalian Cardiomyocytes Terminally Exit the Cell Cycle in the Postnatal Period

Dynamics and Timing of Postnatal Cell Cycle Exit in the Healthy Mammalian Heart

While the embryonic heart grows by proliferation (hyperplasia), cardiomyocytes lose the ability to proliferate shortly after birth and heart growth is thereon driven by cell enlargement (hypertrophy) [13]. One of the earliest studies to demonstrate the lack of cell cycle activity in adult mouse cardiomyocytes identified a single nucleus undergoing DNA synthesis from 180,000 ventricular cardiomyocyte nuclei, according to 3H-thymidine incorporation [14]. Therefore, DNA synthesis was estimated at 0.0005% of adult mouse cardiomyocytes [14]. Cell cycle activity declines postnatally, with Bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) incorporation decreasing from ~ 23% on embryonic day 14.5 (E14.5) to 10% on postnatal day 3 (P3) then 0.94% on P7 [15]. Similar cell cycle withdrawal kinetics are observed using Ki67 staining, with post-G1 cardiomyocytes decreasing from 50% at E14.5 to 6% at P14 then 0% at P21 [15]. Therefore, cell cycle activity is observed in under 1% of adult cardiomyocytes.

Consequently, most cardiomyocytes present in the adult mammalian heart are established by the early postnatal period. For example, cardiomyocyte number in rat hearts increases by 68% in the three days post-birth then remains constant [13], while mouse cardiomyocyte frequency increases from P2-P11 then plateaus [16]. In humans, carbon dating using the decay constant of radioactive 14C isotope [17] has been instrumental for cardiomyocyte proliferation quantification. Two studies by Bergmann and colleagues used carbon dating to demonstrate that human cardiomyocytes slowly regenerate throughout life, with 39% of left ventricle cardiomyocytes present in old age generated postnatally [4, 18]. Cardiomyocyte turnover is also age dependent: in the left ventricle, cardiomyocyte turnover is highest in the first decade of life, declines to 0.8% by the age of 20, and lowers to 0.3% by age 75 [4]. Further, phospho-histone H3 (pH3) staining identified low-level proliferative activity of left ventricular cardiomyocytes across the life course but with a decline in the second decade [19]. Thus, postnatal heart growth is predominantly driven by physiological hypertrophy, while cardiomyocyte cell division is a rare occurrence after birth.

Mechanisms of Postnatal Cell Cycle Exit

Cardiomyocyte cell cycle progression is controlled by sequential activation of cyclins in complex with cyclin dependent kinases (CDKs). CDK4/6-Cyclin D regulates S-phase entry via hyperphosphorylation of the retinoblastoma (RB) protein family (RB1/RB, RBL1/p107 and RBL2/p130), which releases RB protein binding to E2F transcription factors [20]. Meanwhile, the CDK1-Cyclin B complex regulates G2 progression to mitosis [21]. In mice, the expression of the major cyclins and CDKs in cardiomyocytes peaks embryonically around E16 then declines before birth [22]. Cardiomyocyte proliferation is also linked to chromosome number (ploidy) and nucleation. In the left ventricle, most human cardiomyocytes are diploid during the first year of life then DNA content increases by 1.7-fold in the second decade of life, after which ploidy remains constant [4]. In humans, this change in DNA content does not involve multinucleation, since around 70% of adult cardiomyocytes remain mononucleated [18, 19]. In contrast, adult cardiomyocytes in rodents adopt multinucleation due to impaired karyokinesis [41], progressing to 80–90% binucleation by postnatal day 12 [13, 16]. Pigs are commonly used large animal models for translational studies to assess regeneration post-myocardial infarction [42, 43]. Pigs adopt even more striking multinucleation, with up to 16 nuclei per cardiomyocyte and no mononucleated cardiomyocytes at 6 months of age [44]. Ploidy and multinucleation are relevant to heart regeneration because adult cardiomyocyte proliferation in rodents following growth stimulation or injury is higher in mononuclear and diploid cardiomyocytes compared to binucleated cells [45]. A key illustration comes from analysis of 120 mouse strains showing baseline variation in the frequency of mononuclear cardiomyocytes (2–17%) correlated to the heart’s ability to recover following coronary artery ligation, suggesting that mononuclear diploid cardiomyocytes are needed for heart regeneration [45]. Furthermore, treatment with the growth factor neuregulin 1 (NRG1) [46], or the extracellular matrix protein periostin [47], results in higher proliferation of mononucleated compared to binucleated rat ventricular cardiomyocytes in the same population. These findings suggest that multinucleation suppresses the efficacy of proliferative signals and that newly formed cardiomyocytes during regeneration arise from mononucleated cardiomyocytes.

However, the relationship between ploidy/nucleation and proliferation is complex, since murine cardiomyocytes harvested post-infarct have similar proliferation rates irrespective of nucleation [48]. Moreover, ablation of the E2f7 and E2f8 transcription factors – linked to cardiomyocyte binucleation [49] – to raise the proportion of mononuclear diploid cardiomyocytes does not improve heart regeneration post-infarction in mice [50]. There are also conflicting reports on whether mono and multinucleated murine cardiomyocytes are transcriptionally distinct [49, 51]. As such, the biological consequences of multinucleation remain poorly understood and species differences in nucleation status between rodents, pigs and humans should be carefully considered when evaluating therapeutic strategies for induction of cardiomyocyte proliferation.

Another proliferation barrier in mature cardiomyocytes is the contractile unit itself: the sarcomere. The sarcomere enlarges with maturation and ultimately takes up over half the cytoplasm, resulting in a physical barrier to proliferation [52] (Fig. 1A). Prior to cell division, sarcomere breakdown is observed in mammalian cardiomyocytes in culture [53] and in vivo during neonatal heart regeneration following apical resection [12] or myocardial infarction [54]. In addition, genetic expression of a degradation-resistant form (YAPS5A) of yes-associated protein (YAP) results in sarcomere disassembly that aids cell cycle progression [55]. Conversely, disrupting the sarcomere may provide a permissive state for cardiomyocyte proliferation [56]. For example, siRNA knockdown of Actn2, encoding the z-disc protein alpha-actinin-2, increases Ki67 incidence in cardiomyocytes. Even if bona fide cell division could be achieved through sarcomere disruption, a caveat is the trade-off between cardiomyocyte proliferative capacity and contractile function. Indeed, proliferative compounds, in particular those linked to the glycogen synthase kinase-3 (GSK3) pathway, decrease contraction force or prolong relaxation time in stem cell-derived cardiac organoids [58]. However, this approach may be achievable by controlling the duration of cell cycle activation or by targeting the appropriate signalling networks to limit adverse contractile effects [58]. For example, induction of cardiomyocyte mitosis by inducible overexpression of the cytokinesis regulators Plk1 and Ect2 results in only transient contractile dysfunction in the adult mouse heart [59]. Altogether, it is likely that controlling sarcomere disassembly and reassembly will be a critical step for ensuring the safety of therapies enhancing cardiomyocyte proliferation.

Mammals have Some Capacity for Heart Regeneration

There is a transitional developmental window spanning the first few days after birth when regeneration following partial cardiac injury is possible. This phenomenon was initially demonstrated using surgical resection of ~ 15% left ventricular apical myocardium in 1-day-old mice. Full restoration of cardiac structure and function was observed by 21 days post-resection [12]. Cell cycle analysis one week after resection showed that cardiomyocyte proliferation occurred following injury. Importantly, neonatal heart regeneration is lost within the week after birth, with apical resection in postnatal day 7 mice leading to significant fibrosis [12]. Regardless of the form of heart injury, fate mapping in mice confirms that de novo cardiomyocytes following myocardial injury arise through expansion of pre-existing cardiomyocytes via cell division, both in normal ageing and following injury [12, 54, 60, 61].

Myocardial infarction is modelled in the mouse heart using ligation of the left anterior descending (LAD) artery to block blood supply, leading to substantial cell death and non-viable myocardium [54, 62]. Analogous to resection, complete cardiac regeneration marked by cardiomyocyte proliferation, lack of permanent fibrosis, and restoration of heart function occurs when myocardial infarction is induced in postnatal day 1 but not postnatal day 7 mice [54, 62]. Transient regenerative capacity after LAD ligation is also observed in neonatal pigs younger than three days old [42, 43]. In sheep, heart regeneration occurs following myocardial infarction during late gestation but not after birth [63, 64], reflecting the earlier in utero switch to a mature, terminally differentiated state in sheep cardiomyocytes [65]. In humans, there are a handful of published case studies describing neonatal heart regeneration [66, 67]. For example, a newborn infant suffering from severe acute myocardial infarction presenting with significantly impaired left ventricular function and extensive cardiomyocyte cell death had complete functional recovery within weeks and remained healthy at 3 years of age [67]. More recently, a neonate with congenital heart disease showed completely restored heart function after an ischaemic injury within eight days after birth [66]. Thus, mammalian hearts appear to have a highly conserved regenerative window during early postnatal life.

Cell Cycle Machinery is Epigenetically Blocked in the Postnatal Period

Key Epigenetic Modifications Associated with Euchromatin versus Heterochromatin

DNA is organised in the nucleus as chromatin, which can exist in an accessible (euchromatin) or closed (heterochromatin) state [68]. Chromatin consists of nucleosomes with a histone octamer core around which DNA is wrapped [69]. Chromatin state is controlled by post-translational modifications, including acetylation and methylation, of histone N-terminal tails. Histone acetylation occurs at lysine residues to neutralise positive charge, weakening histone-DNA interactions and opening chromatin. Histone deacetylases (HDACs) reverse lysine acetylation and close chromatin by restoring the lysine’s positive charge. Acetylation ‘readers’ are the bromodomain and extra-terminal (BET) proteins, which contain a bromodomain that identifies acetylated lysine residues on histone H3 and H4 [70]. Unlike histone acetylation, histone methylation does not alter histone charge and can open or close chromatin depending on the modification. Histone methylation marks associated with gene activation include trimethylation of lysine 4 on histone 3 (H3K4me3) and di/trimethylation of lysine 79 on histone 3 (H3K79me2/3), while modifications linked to closed chromatin include H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 [71–74]. Polycomb repressive complex 1 (PRC1) and 2 (PRC2) are associated with transcriptional repression via histone methylation. The PRC2 complex includes the enhancer of zeste homologue 1/2 (EZH1/2) that provides histone methyltransferase activity [75].

Large-scale Analyses of the DNA and Chromatin State of Fetal versus Adult Cardiomyocytes

The postnatal switch in cell cycle gene regulation is associated with extensive epigenetic changes. In humans, 4,707 regions across the genome have differential chromatin states between gestation and adulthood, mostly in intronic and intergenic regions [10]. These changes primarily occur between mid-gestation and below 4 years of age, demonstrating that the epigenetic profile of cardiac cells is established early in life. Importantly, the genomic regions associated with the cell cycle transcription factors E2F1, E2F2 and TEA domain family member 1 (TEAD) become less accessible with age [10] and are correlated with cell cycle gene downregulation in adult compared with fetal hearts [10]. Similarly in mice, transcriptional activity and open chromatin in neonatal cardiomyocytes are linked to cell cycle transcription factors, including E2f1, forkhead box protein m1 (Foxm1) and Myc. However, these genomic regions become less accessible by postnatal day 56 [11]. The implication for cardiac regenerative therapies is that adult cardiomyocytes would have to adopt a neonatal epigenetic profile to efficiently re-express proliferative genes.

DNA Methylation Changes Enable Postnatal Cell Cycle Shutdown and Sarcomeric Maturation

The mechanisms underlying changes in chromatin accessibility during postnatal heart maturation have been revealed by epigenetic profiling of neonatal and adult hearts as crosstalk between DNA methylation and histone post-translational modifications [76, 77]. In the mouse heart, DNA methylation globally drops across the genome after postnatal day 14, in line with decreased expression of the DNA methyltransferases Dnmt1, Dnmt3a and Dnmt3b [78]. Prior to this, DNA methylation during the first week after birth enforces cardiomyocyte cell cycle exit, since chemical inhibition of DNA methylation in mice from P2 markedly increases heart size, doubles the percentage of pH3 + cardiomyocytes, and reduces the proportion of binucleated cardiomyocytes at P7 [78]. Site-specific DNA methylation changes are also observed in human hearts at genomic regions associated with sarcomeric proteins, aiding the switch to adult isoforms needed for cardiomyocyte maturation. For instance, the gene body associated with the fetal isoform of troponin I (Tnni1) postnatally adopts de novo methylation mediated by DNMT3A/B. In parallel, the adult troponin I isoform (Tnni3) is demethylated and increases in expression after birth [79]. Therefore, a mature gene expression profile is enforced in adult cardiomyocytes by dynamic DNA methylation at cell cycle and sarcomeric genes.

Histone Post-translational Marks Associated with Postnatal Cell Cycle Shutdown

Euchromatin around cell cycle genes in proliferating fetal cardiomyocytes is characterised by low H3K9me3 levels [37] and hyperacetylation at H3K27, H3K18 and H3K9/14 [80–83] (Fig. 1B). In contrast, terminally differentiated cardiomyocytes accumulate heterochromatin at cell cycle activation genes, marked by hypoacetylation and high levels of the repressive histone mark H3K9me3 [37]. Trimethylation of H3K27 by the EZH1/2-containing PRC2 complex is another repressive mark associated with heterochromatin, but is more linked to cell cycle progression since H3K27me3 forms at genomic loci associated with CDK inhibitors [84–87].

The mechanisms enhancing heterochromatin during heart maturation via HDACs have been well described [88]. Cardiomyocyte-specific Hdac3 overexpression in mice results in ventricular myocardial thickening due to cardiomyocyte hyperplasia, mediated by HDAC3-inhibition of multiple CDK inhibitors in the mouse heart, including p21, p27 and p15 [80]. Conversely, HDAC2 can promote cardiomyocyte cell cycle exit in mouse embryos by acetylation of GATA4, an essential cardiac transcription factor [81]. In mammalian cardiomyocytes, HDAC1 suppresses cardiomyocyte proliferation via deacetylation of MBD3, in the HDAC1-containing nucleosome remodelling and deacetylase complex, which inhibits cardiomyocyte proliferation through STAT3 deacetylation [89].

RB proteins primarily function to inhibit cell cycle progression by binding E2F transcription factors [90, 91]. However, RB proteins also inhibit cell cycle activity by epigenetic mechanisms. In multiple cell types, RB facilitates HDAC1 recruitment to the promoters of E2F-regulated genes, including CCNE1, enhancing gene silencing and inhibiting cell cycle progression [92]. In post-mitotic mouse cardiomyocytes, RB and the structurally similar p130 enhance heterochromatin through recruitment of Heterochromatin protein 1 (HP1) that binds to methylated H3K9 to initiate or maintain heterochromatin [37]. Accordingly, adult cardiomyocytes with RB/p130 deficiency have reduced heterochromatin and increased expression of cytokinesis and G2/M transition genes [37], while RB/p130 double knockout mice have threefold higher heart-to-body weight ratio [93]. Therefore, RB proteins function in both genetic and epigenetic regulation of cardiomyocyte cell cycle progression.

Heterochromatin formation around CDK inhibitor loci in the mammalian heart is regulated by the PRC2 components EZH1 and 2 [85–87]. In mice, Ezh2 inactivation under the Nkx2-5 promoter, which is active early in cardiomyocyte differentiation, causes lethal congenital heart malformations including myocardial hypoplasia [85]. This is due to EZH2 regulation of the Ink4a/b (Cdkn2a/b) locus, since Nkx2-5-mediated Ezh2 inactivation upregulates the CDK inhibitors p16 and p15 [85]. Similarly, conditional Ezh2 knockout under the Myh6 promoter, also active in immature cardiomyocytes, reduces cardiomyocyte proliferation via the Ink4a locus [87]. In contrast, Ezh2 inactivation at a later stage of cardiomyocyte differentiation under the cTnT promoter does not cause an overt phenotype [85]. Therefore, EZH2 regulates cardiomyocyte proliferation during early heart development. Interestingly, cardiomyocyte-specific Ezh2 deletion, driven by cTnT-CRE, is lethal with severe heart malformations when combined with homozygous Ezh1 knockout [85, 86], suggesting at least one of EZH1 and EZH2 is required for cardiomyocyte proliferation during heart development.

In addition, the PRC2 complex regulates cardiomyocyte proliferation during neonatal heart regeneration. Myh6-mediated Ezh2 knockout decreases cardiomyocyte proliferation following apical resection in neonatal mice [87]. However, the neonatal regenerative response to myocardial infarction induced by LAD ligation appears more dependent on EZH1 than EZH2, since Ezh1-knockout mice have extensive fibrosis and reduced heart function following MI. In contrast, Ezh2-knockout mice have minimal fibrotic scarring [86]. Thus, the PRC2 complex is important for cardiomyocyte proliferation during heart development, while EZH1 is critical for neonatal heart regeneration.

The KDM4 subfamily of demethylases play a key role in euchromatin formation in proliferating cardiomyocytes via H3K9. Cardiomyocyte-specific Kdm4d overexpression increases cardiomyocyte proliferation by binding cell cycle gene promoters and depleting H3K9 methylation [94]. This leads to activation of genes including Ccnb1/2, Cdk1, and Aurkb, indicating that H3K9 methylation regulates progression through several cell cycle checkpoints. Importantly, Kdm4d overexpression in mice results in increased heart mass by postnatal day 14 and until 9 weeks of age [94]. KDM4D also interacts with the Hippo/YAP pathway [95], a pathway known to regulate cardiomyocyte proliferation [96]. Kdm4d overexpression while knocking down the YAP inhibitor Sav1 by shRNA increases the proportion of cardiomyocytes in the mouse heart positive for cell cycle markers [95]. This combinatorial treatment may more effectively upregulate the cell cycle compared to single pathway interference due to the Hippo/YAP signalling cascade and KDM4D targeting distinct cell cycle genes, with KDM4D regulating E2f1 and Foxm1 and the Hippo pathway regulating Myc [95].

Epigenetic Mechanisms Prevent Proliferative Signals Effective in Fetal Cardiomyocytes from Stimulating Adult Cardiomyocyte Proliferation

Signal transduction pathways driving cell cycle progression in fetal cardiomyocytes are suppressed postnatally. Reactivation of these signalling networks has the potential to activate the cell cycle in adult cardiomyocytes, as demonstrated for YAP [97], NRG/ERBB2/PI3K [46], and MYC [98, 99] signalling. However, cell cycle reactivation in adult cardiomyocytes despite forced activation of these pathways is constrained by epigenetic mechanisms discussed below.

WNT/β-catenin

WNT signalling has a biphasic role during heart development: WNT activation is required for induction of the mesodermal germ layer, while subsequent WNT inhibition aids cardiomyocyte differentiation [100]. β-catenin is a multifunctional protein that can act as a downstream transcriptional co-activator in canonical WNT signalling depending on its phosphorylation status [101]. In the absence of the WNT ligand, cytoplasmic β-catenin is phosphorylated and targeted for proteasomal degradation by a complex including GSK3-β [102] (Fig. 1C). In the presence of WNT, this destruction complex localises to the plasma membrane where it is inactive, leading to higher nuclear β-catenin levels. Nuclear β-catenin associates with transcription factors including T-cell factor and lymphoid enhancer factor [103, 104] to activate cell cycle genes including those encoding Cyclin D1 and MYC [105, 106].

WNT/β-catenin is downregulated postnatally in mice, with nuclear β-catenin protein undetectable in cardiomyocytes by postnatal day 14 [107]. Postnatal cardiomyocyte cell cycle exit corresponds to downregulation of the β-catenin dependent gene network that includes cell cycle machinery [107]. Moreover, pharmacological inhibition of GSK3-β by CHIR99021 [108] or BIO [109] leads to increased nuclear β-catenin and proliferation in cultured mammalian cardiomyocytes. However, metabolic maturation of cardiomyocytes in human cardiac organoids limits proliferation in response to GSK3-β inhibitors [110], suggesting that WNT signalling requires a permissive cell state to promote proliferation. Furthermore, expression of constitutively active β-catenin in an adult mouse myocardial infarction model reduces scarring but does not enhance proliferation [107]. Notably, the cell cycle genes present in neonatal cardiomyocytes are not upregulated by constitutively active β-catenin in adult cardiomyocytes, indicating that β-catenin does not restore a fetal-like gene programme in mature cardiomyocytes [107]. These findings suggest that cardiac maturation causes insensitivity to WNT/β-catenin induction of proliferation. Although the promoters of CHIR99021-enhanced regenerative genes have unaltered chromatin accessibility with postnatal maturation in the mouse heart [107], WNT/β-catenin signalling is a cardiac development pathway postnatally shutdown by CpG hypermethylation [78]. Therefore, rewiring of the Wnt/b-catenin signalling is postnatally repressed by CpG hypermethylation during cardiac development.

Notch

The Notch pathway is important during development but epigenetically repressed in adult cardiomyocytes [7]. Canonically, Delta or Jagged ligands bind to Notch receptors, releasing the Notch intracellular domain (‘activated Notch’) that complexes with the transcription factor RBPJ (Fig. 1C). This complex activates Cyclin D1 among prototypic target genes [111]. Notch signalling is active in the embryonic heart and, like WNT/GSK3-β signalling, temporally precise Notch activation is required for normal heart development [112, 113]. While Notch pathway inhibition or Notch1 deletion results in myocardial hypoplasia [7], Notch1 activation in the earlier mesodermal cell lineage leads to abnormal heart morphogenesis [114]. Experiments in embryonic stem cells suggest that Notch signalling is active in most progenitor cells but Notch signalling is downregulated with differentiation [115].

Activated Notch induces Ccnd1 expression and Cyclin D1 nuclear translocation in neonatal rat ventricular cardiomyocytes in culture, resulting in cell cycle progression based on Ki67 incidence [116]. However, activated Notch can only increase cardiomyocyte mitosis (marked by pH3) in postnatal day 2 hearts but not those from day 5 [116]. While activated Notch does cause some cell cycle activity in this latter population of older cardiomyocytes, they arrest at the G2/M checkpoint, therefore Notch activation is insufficient to override the proliferation barrier in mature cardiomyocytes [116].

Simultaneous cardiomyocyte cell cycle exit and decreased Notch signalling in postnatal mouse hearts are associated with repressive epigenetic marks at Notch-dependent target genes. Specifically, increases in EZH2 occupancy and the heterochromatin mark H3K27me3 are observed around these target loci [84]. In line with this, Notch pathway stimulation does not induce cell cycle re-entry of adult rat cardiomyocytes and is ineffective in stimulating cardiac repair following injury in post-infarct mice [84]. The insensitivity of adult cardiomyocytes to Notch signalling is mediated by DNA methylation at Notch target loci, since treatment with the DNA methylation inhibitor 5-Azacytidine permits cardiomyocyte cell cycle activation by activated Notch [84].

Notch signalling regulation of the cardiomyocyte cell cycle is also dependent on acetylation of activated NOTCH1 itself, which extends the half-life of NOTCH1 and so controls the amplitude and duration of Notch signalling [117]. Accordingly, expression of activated Notch1 fused to the catalytic domain of the HAT p300 promotes cardiomyocyte proliferation and enhances myocardial regeneration [117]. Therefore, the efficacy of the Notch pathway to stimulate cardiomyocyte cell cycle progression is regulated epigenetically at multiple levels, including acetylation of NOTCH1 and methylation of Notch-dependent transcriptional targets.

Hippo/YAP

The evolutionarily conserved Hippo pathway regulates epigenetic mechanisms that mediate cardiomyocyte proliferation. In mammals, Hippo signalling regulates organ size and involves a core cascade with mammalian STE20-like kinase 1/2 (MST1/2), salvador family WW domain-containing protein 1 (SAV1), and large tumor suppressor kinases (LATS1/2) [9]. The best studied Hippo pathway effector is the transcriptional cofactor YAP [118], although the closely related protein TAZ has overlapping functions [119]. YAP is inhibited by phosphorylation by LATS1/2 [120], while Hippo pathway inhibition enables YAP activation and association with nuclear TEAD transcription factors [121] (Fig. 1C).

YAP upregulation enhances immature and mature cardiomyocyte proliferation in vitro [122], while YAP upregulation during embryogenesis in vivo in mouse models causes heart overgrowth [8]. The latter is reversed by β-catenin heterozygosity, suggesting that YAP-mediated cardiomyocyte proliferation partly functions by upregulation of WNT signalling [8]. However, YAP and β-catenin do not completely overlap in their roles in driving cardiomyocyte proliferation, since compounds that activate YAP or β-catenin individually are insufficient to drive proliferation of human stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes [58]. Meanwhile, dual activation of YAP and β-catenin by the compound 6.28 does push metabolically mature cardiomyocytes into a proliferative state [58].

The ability of YAP activation to potentiate proliferation in adult cardiomyocytes is constrained epigenetically via AT-rich interaction domain 1A (Arid1a), a component of the SWI/SNF complex required for normal heart development [123]. ARID1A mediates binding of YAP/TAZ to the SWI/SNF complex, thereby sequestering YAP/TAZ from TEAD [124]. In mice, Arid1a is expressed neonatally and ARID1A protein interaction with YAP suppresses YAP/TEAD-regulated cell cycle activity [125]. Consequently, Arid1a-knockout upregulates YAP targets and Arid1a-knockout mice have increased proliferation of border zone cardiomyocytes following ischaemic injury [125]. Thus, ARID1A promotes epigenetic changes that prevent YAP/TEAD from driving adult cardiomyocyte proliferation or injury-induced regeneration in adult mice. Interestingly, ARID1A also functions in the postnatal shift from proliferative capacity to functional maturation, through the repression of cell cycle genes (e.g. Ccnd2, Ccne1, Ccnb1) and activation of mature sarcomeric genes (Myl2, Myl3 and Tnni3) [125].

Furthermore, the degree of YAP activation and its ability to enhance heart regeneration in the post-infarct mouse heart is dependent on the method of YAP upregulation – namely, the specific gain-of-function YAP variant overexpressed or method of upstream Hippo pathway inhibition. Initially, Human YAP-S127A cDNA expression by adenovirus was shown to stimulate postnatal cardiomyocyte proliferation in the mouse heart in a TEAD-dependent manner [122]. S127A represents a phosphorylation-resistant mutation in a 14–3-3 binding site of the YAP protein that, when phosphorylated, inhibits YAP nuclear translocation [122]. Cardiac regeneration following myocardial infarction in the postnatal heart was demonstrated in a transgenic mouse model overexpressing YAP-S127A, the mouse homologue of human YAP-S112A [97], and induced YAP-S127A expression following myocardial infarction was sufficient to stimulate cardiac repair [126]. Nonetheless, the recognition of YAP-S112A/S127A as a ‘constitutively active’ form of YAP due to enhanced nuclear translocation was challenged by the finding that mice with YapS112A at the endogenous Yap locus are grossly normal despite increased nuclear YAP localisation [127]. This was explained by reduced overall YAP levels, since YAP-S112A expression triggers YAP phosphorylation on a separate residue that enhances degradation [127]. To more fully abolish Hippo pathway activity and upregulate YAP, another YAP variant was engineered with five serine to alanine mutations blocking LATS1/2-mediated phosphorylation, promoting both nuclear translocation and degradation resistance [128]. YAPS5A increases adult cardiomyocyte cell cycle re-entry through chromatin remodelling [128] and metabolic rewiring [129] that promote a fetal state. However, the efficacy and safety of YAPS5A as a heart regeneration strategy are undetermined, since YAPS5A has not been reported in an adult myocardial infarction animal model, while conditional YAPS5A expression in cardiomyocytes is lethal in unstressed mice due to decreased heart chamber size [128].

An alternative strategy for YAP overexpression is inhibition of the upstream Hippo pathway, which is a more promising translational approach. For this, robust preclinical evidence has shown that Sav1/Salv or Lats1 interference enhances heart regeneration, either by postnatal genetic deletion using tamoxifen-regulated cre activity followed by apical resection or myocardial infarction [130], or by short hairpin RNA (shRNA)-knockdown after myocardial infarction in an ischaemic heart failure mouse model [131]. The latter strategy using shRNA to precisely modulate YAP signalling following myocardial infarction, resembling a clinical setting, also enhanced cardiac repair in a porcine model [131]. These findings prompted the first in-human trial targeting Hippo pathway inhibition to treat ischaemic heart failure (SALVADOR-HF; ID: NCT06831825) using YAP101, an adeno-associated virus (AAV)-based gene therapy delivering shRNA targeting SAV1 in cardiomyocytes. Although efficacy has not yet been reported, the Phase 1 trial involving a one-time injection of YAP101 in adults with ischaemic heart failure and reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) demonstrated acceptable safety and tolerability. Therefore, Hippo pathway inhibition using precise genetic modulation is a promising strategy for treating heart failure post-myocardial infarction.

The Intrinsic Link between Metabolic Changes and Epigenetic Modifications Regulates Postnatal Cell Cycle Exit

Metabolism in the Fetal versus Adult Heart

In addition to changes in DNA and histone modifications, the inability of the mature mammalian heart to regenerate is linked to metabolism and oxygen exposure—environmental and therefore inherently epigenetic regulators of cell state. The fetal heart grows in relative hypoxia and relies on anaerobic glycolysis in the cytosol for ATP production, whereby pyruvate from glycolysis is fermented into lactate. After birth, increased blood oxygen levels and fatty acid availability from breast milk enable a shift from anaerobic glycolysis to fatty acid β-oxidation and oxidative phosphorylation [132, 133] (Fig. 1D). The main energy source for the heart therefore switches postnatally from glucose to fatty acids, which is well characterised in mammalian models. In rabbit hearts, 44% ATP is derived from glycolysis at postnatal day 1 (P1) versus only 7% by day 7. Conversely, 13% ATP is derived from palmitate oxidation at P1, which triples to 39% by P7. In addition, measurement of the in vivo uptake and oxidation of lactate, a byproduct of glycolysis, reveals high reliance on lactate in the fetus and newborns that steadily declines after birth [132]. Thus, birth marks a transitional checkpoint where the mammalian heart reduces use of glucose and lactate for energy production while increasing reliance on fatty acids.

How do Metabolism and Epigenetics Co-regulate the Cardiomyocyte Cell Cycle?

Metabolism regulates the cell cycle in two major ways. Firstly, oxidative metabolism generates high levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [134] that activate the DNA damage response and trigger cardiomyocyte cell cycle arrest [135]. Secondly, glycolysis intermediates provide building blocks for biosynthesis for cell growth [136]. These include glucose-6-phosphate for nucleotide synthesis; fructose-6-phosphate for glycoprotein synthesis; 3-phosphoglycerate for conversion into amino acids; and dihydroxyacetone phosphate for phospholipid synthesis.

Cardiomyocyte cell cycle regulation by metabolism is inherently ‘epigenetic’ since it involves environmental non-genetic cues regulating gene expression. Furthermore, metabolites provide intermediate substrates for epigenetic processes that regulate cardiomyocyte cell cycle. Notably, global acetylation levels are determined by metabolism since the acetyl group is synthesised from the processing of glucose, fatty acids and amino acids [137]. Certain HDAC classes also rely on the coenzyme NAD, which functions in all major metabolic pathways [138], while certain demethylases utilise TCA cycle components FAD and α-ketoglutarate [139, 140]. Finally, methylation of histone and DNA both use the universal donor SAM generated by one-carbon metabolism [141].

In some cases, regulation of the cardiomyocyte cell cycle by metabolic intermediates has been empirically tested. Recently, α-ketoglutarate was identified as the most drastically decreased metabolite in neonatal mouse hearts from P0.5 to P7 [142]. When α-ketoglutarate is injected into mice, or α-ketoglutarate degradation is inhibited, heart regeneration post-infarction is promoted via increased postnatal cardiomyocyte proliferation [142]. α-ketoglutarate regulates the cell cycle by decreasing H3K27me3 deposition at the promoters of multiple cyclins and CDKs [142]. Therefore, metabolite levels can regulate heart regeneration by affecting chromatin accessibility.

Pivoting Fatty Acid Oxidation towards Glycolysis Upregulating Cardiomyocyte Proliferation

Genetic, pharmacological or dietary alteration of fatty acid oxidation and the TCA cycle can enhance adult cardiomyocyte proliferation and improve cardiac function following injury (Fig. 1D). For example, knockout of the enzyme required for fatty acyl-CoA mitochondrial transport Carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1B (Cpt1b) enhances hyperplastic postnatal cardiomyocyte growth [143]. Consequently, Cpt1b-knockout mice have double the number of cardiomyocytes at 10 weeks of age [143]. Consistent with increased proliferative capacity, Cpt1b-mutant hearts have improved contraction and a reduced infarct area 24 h after myocardial infarction. The underlying protective mechanism is epigenetic, since Cpt1b inactivation raises α-ketoglutarate levels and activity of the α-ketoglutarate-dependent demethylase KDM5, resulting in removal of the euchromatic mark H3K4me3 at loci associated with cardiomyocyte maturation [143].

Similarly, the fats in breast milk may reduce cardiomyocyte proliferation since neonatal mice fed fatty-acid-deficient milk have prolonged postnatal cardiomyocyte proliferation [144], though differences in proliferation are only temporary [144]. This ability to transiently modulate cardiomyocyte proliferation through dietary changes could present a feasible method to temporally control cardiomyocyte state clinically. In addition to dietary interventions, metabolism can be shifted from fatty acid oxidation to glycolysis by cardiomyocyte-specific deletion of Pdk4, an enzyme that reduces pyruvate conversion, which reduces ROS production while increasing cardiomyocyte proliferation [144]. Furthermore, Pdk4 deletion induced one week after myocardial infarction in mice improves left ventricular function six months post-injury [144]. Therefore, cardiomyocyte proliferation can be controlled by modulating fatty acid availability and metabolism.

The TCA cycle and its metabolites are also linked to heart regeneration. Analysis of metabolite changes following ischaemia–reperfusion identified succinate as the only metabolite altered in a range of tissues, suggesting that succinate accumulation is a characteristic of the injury response [145]. Pharmacological reduction of succinate promotes cardiomyocyte proliferation and extends the neonatal proliferative window following myocardial infarction in mouse models [145, 146]. Since succinate accumulation and reoxidation by succinate dehydrogenase contribute to elevated ROS production, while excess succinate post-infarction attenuates cardiomyocyte proliferation [146], succinate inhibition of heart regeneration may be mediated by ROS production.

In addition to blocking the TCA cycle and fatty acid oxidation, overexpression of glycolytic enzymes upregulates cardiomyocyte proliferation through ROS depletion, enhancement of anabolism, and direct activation of cell cycle activators. For instance, cardiomyocyte-specific expression of the glycolytic enzyme Pkm2 in adult mice increases cardiomyocyte cell division and survival following myocardial infarction. Pkm2-mediated cardiac regeneration involves 1) direct physical interaction of PKM2 with β-catenin; 2) upregulation of β-catenin transcriptional targets, including Ccnd1 and Myc; and 3) increases in the anabolic enzyme glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase [147]. Likewise, overexpression of the glucose transporter GLUT1 (encoded by Slc2a1) results in increased cardiomyocyte proliferation [148]. However, a role for GLUT1 in cardiomyocyte proliferation has only been shown for neonatal mice following cryoinjury at postnatal day 1, so whether GLUT1 promotes adult cardiomyocyte proliferation remains unclear. In contrast, overexpression of the pyruvate-to-lactate conversion enzyme Lactate dehydrogenase A (Ldha) increases adult cardiomyocyte proliferation and improves cardiac function 21 days post-myocardial infarction [149]. LDHA reduces ROS production and intracellular levels of the TCA cycle metabolite succinyl-CoA [149]. Therefore, increased glycolysis promotes cardiomyocyte proliferation and improves the heart’s injury response.

Maturation and cell cycle suppression of cardiomyocytes by metabolic reprogramming is also observed in stem cell-derived cardiac cells. Exploration of the optimal conditions for maturation of stem cell-derived cardiac models, including metabolic substrate utilisation, led to the development of a ‘maturation medium’ containing low glucose and high palmitate (a fatty acid source) [110]. iPSC-cardiomyocytes grown with maturation medium have reduced cell cycle activity and are insensitive to the GSK3-β inhibitor CHIR99021, which enhances proliferation in immature cardiomyocytes [110].

Overall, manipulating metabolism by suppressing fatty acid oxidation or activating glycolysis can drive an immature state in cardiomyocytes permissive to cell cycle progression, aiding regeneration following injury. Alternatively, utilisation of fatty acid oxidation promotes cardiomyocyte maturity. The latter strategy could enhance the quality of iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes for cell therapies and improve iPSC-cardiac cells as models for screening novel therapeutics. Taken together, the studies presented provide a compelling rationale for altering epigenetic profiles through metabolic reprogramming of cardiomyocytes.

Promoting Cell Cycle Progression Involves Metabolic Reprogramming

As modulating metabolism drives postnatal cardiomyocyte proliferation, activating the cardiomyocyte cell cycle reciprocally results in, largely glycolytic, metabolic changes. Genetic and pharmacological manipulation of immature cardiomyocytes have been key in demonstrating this. For instance, a screen identified a cocktail of five molecules that activate the cell cycle in vitro and enhance in vivo heart regeneration following myocardial infarction in rats [150]. One molecular target of this cocktail is Monocarboxylate transporter 1 (MCT1), which mediates the switch from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis. Metabolic analysis revealed that the cocktail of cell cycle activators increased glycolysis, decreased oxidative phosphorylation, and doubled intracellular lactate concentration in neonatal rat cardiomyocytes. Moreover, directly increasing lactate concentration in culture increased the percentage of post-G1 cardiomyocytes [150]. Similarly, rewiring mitosis-refractory adult mouse cardiomyocytes into an immature state using YAP5SA [129], combined Cdk1/Ccnb1/Cdk4/Ccnd1 (‘4F’) [32, 151] or combined Oct4/Sox2/Klf4/Myc [152] induction promotes proliferation while downregulating fatty acid metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation. In parallel, YAP5SA increases glucose catabolism [129], Cdk1/Ccnb1/Cdk4/Ccnd1 upregulates genes associated with biosynthetic pathways branching from glycolysis [151], and Oct4/Sox2/Klf4/Myc upregulates gene expression of glycolytic enzymes in adult cardiomyocytes [152]. Therefore, reactivation of the cell cycle in adult cardiomyocytes involves a shift in metabolism to a state seen in proliferating fetal cardiomyocytes with increased reliance on glycolysis.

Enhanced glycolysis in proliferating cardiomyocytes is also driven by the receptor tyrosine kinase ERBB2/HER2 in mouse hearts. Induction of ERBB2 signalling enhances cardiomyocyte proliferation in both juvenile and adult mice following myocardial ischaemic injury [153], while constitutively activated Erbb2 upregulates glycolytic enzymes (Pkm2, Pdk3, and Pfkp) as well as glucose and lactate receptors [153]. Furthermore, ERBB2-mediated cardiomyocyte cell cycle activation is reversed by small-molecule inhibition of glycolysis [154]. Therefore, shifting metabolic state does not appear to be a collateral effect of cardiomyocyte cell cycle activity but an essential step for proliferation to occur.

In addition to glycolysis, small-molecule screening in human stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes identified the mevalonate pathway as a proliferation driver [58]. The mevalonate pathway involves the conversion of acetyl-CoA conversion for the synthesis of isoprenoids like cholesterol. Proteomic analysis of iPSC-cardiac models revealed that 2 of the 11 proteins ubiquitously upregulated by pro-proliferative molecules are related to the mevalonate pathway. Further, postnatal downregulation of several mevalonate pathway enzymes is observed in the human and mouse heart [58]. Therefore, mevalonate pathway shutdown appears to be a characteristic of cardiomyocyte cell cycle exit.

Oxygen-sensitive Epigenetic Mechanisms Regulate the Cardiomyocyte Cell Cycle

Another environmental factor linked to metabolism and the regulation of heart regeneration is oxygen exposure. The human fetus was coined ‘Everest in utero’ by physiologist Sir Joseph Barcroft in the early 1900s, alluding to the low oxygen environment in the womb, since quantified as under 5% O2 [155, 156]. In contrast, post-birth humans are exposed to atmospheric ~ 20% O2. Oxygen deprivation during fetal development can cause growth defects or perinatal lethality [157] and insufficient oxygen generally underpins heart failure. However, the fetus has physiological adaptations enabling survival in hypoxia, and experimental evidence in mice suggests that hypoxia confers a regenerative advantage following heart damage. Cardiomyocytes from mice exposed to progressive hypoxia (20.9 to 7% O2 over two weeks) followed by sustained hypoxia (7% O2 for two weeks) have an eightfold increase in BrdU incorporation and higher pH3 expression compared to normoxic cardiomyocytes [158]. Grossly, hypoxic mice have higher heart-to-body mass ratio, attributed to a doubling in cardiomyocyte frequency [158]. Importantly, mice exposed to the same hypoxic protocol following LAD ligation show increased cardiomyocyte proliferation and greater functional recovery [158]. However, further experiments suggest that hypoxia-induced cardiomyocyte proliferation is restricted to the right ventricle while left ventricular mass increase is due to hypertrophic growth [159].

One concerning observation for experiments in mice is increased mortality after exposure to sustained hypoxia following myocardial infarction compared to other oxygen regimens [158], highlighting safety concerns that could limit clinical utility. Nonetheless, a feasibility study involving five healthy subjects in a hypoxic tent for five days, progressively reaching 11% oxygen then maintained for 24 h, reported no adverse effects to low oxygen exposure [160]. Furthermore, a clinical trial with patients recovering from myocardial infarction showed that 4.5-day exposure to hypoxia improved left ventricular function. Therefore, initial data in human studies indicate both feasibility and clinical utility for hypoxia treatment post-myocardial infarction [161].

Mechanistically, the transcriptional response to oxygen exposure is through hypoxia inducible factors (HIFs), which are stabilised by low oxygen [162] and support cellular adaptation to hypoxia by downregulating oxidative metabolism [163] and suppressing the DNA damage response [164]. Consequently, myocardial Hif1a (HIF1α) deletion in the developing mouse heart attenuates cardiomyocyte proliferation and causes embryonic lethality [164]. In the adult mouse heart, overexpression of the forkhead box genes Foxk1 and Foxk2 enhances cardiomyocyte proliferation and cardiac repair following myocardial infarction by upregulation of glycolysis via enhanced HIF1α expression [165]. Hif1a also appears to be stably expressed in the adult mouse heart in a rare population of hypoxic cardiomyocytes [166], which resemble proliferative neonatal cardiomyocytes in cell cycle activity and low oxidative DNA damage. Fate mapping by Kimura et al. (2015) suggests that these hypoxic cardiomyocytes act as precursors for newly formed cardiomyocytes that distribute throughout the myocardium [166], although more recent genetic lineage tracing analysis suggests that most cycling cardiomyocytes are located within the subendocardial layer [167].

Another mediator of hypoxia-induced cardiomyocyte proliferation is Uncoupling Protein 2 (UCP2), which uncouples oxidative phosphorylation from ATP synthesis. Hypoxia-induced cardiomyocyte proliferation and glycolysis in neonatal rat ventricular cardiomyocytes is reversed by Ucp2 loss, while oxidative DNA damage is increased [168]. Interestingly, hypoxia also increases acetyl-CoA levels and histone acetylation in a Ucp2 expression-dependent manner [168], indicating that UCP2 may affect cell cycle and metabolic gene expression, however, this has not been directly addressed.

Together, these studies demonstrate the growing interest in cardiomyocyte cell cycle activation by oxygen tension. The evidence that acute hypoxia enhances myocardial recovery following ischaemic injury in vivo [158] and the more recent clinical findings that short-term hypoxia is well tolerated in humans provide a solid case for this approach.

Conclusions

In mammals, cardiomyocytes in the fetal heart harbour a proliferative state supported by high CDK/cyclin expression and active upstream transcriptional signalling (including YAP, Notch, and WNT/β-catenin) [7, 8, 22, 107]. This cell cycle activity enables myocardial repair in the immature heart with limited fibrosis following injury, including infarction [12, 54]. Nonetheless, the mammalian cardiomyocyte cell cycle is rapidly shutdown in the postnatal period, as a trade-off for enhanced cardiac function via cardiac maturation [12, 78]. Recognised mechanisms underlying this trade-off include genetic and epigenetic suppression of proliferative transcriptional networks active in fetal cardiomyocytes, by genome-wide chromatin remodelling and gene body-specific chromatin accessibility changes [10, 78, 107]. Furthermore, mature mammalian cardiomyocytes rely on fatty acid-dependent and oxidative metabolism that limits cell cycle activity [132, 135], as well as structural barriers that block cell division such as polyploidisation in humans, multinucleation in animal models, and sarcomere maturation [4, 14, 56].

Promisingly though, identification of the molecular players involved in the transcriptional and epigenetic shutdown of the cardiomyocyte cell cycle has revealed therapeutic opportunities. One of the most translationally advanced approaches is activating YAP via shRNA knockdown of Sav1, which has demonstrated remarkable pro-regenerative properties in mouse and large animal models [131], in addition to tolerability in humans in early clinical trials. Another approach progressing in the clinic is the use of a low oxygen environment to stimulate repair following myocardial infarction [161]. Areas of research with strong preclinical evidence that may be explored in the clinic include directly manipulating cardiomyocyte metabolism towards a glycolytic state [169], or disrupting the sarcomere to temporarily remove this structural barrier to cell division [52]. While the latter has not been tested in large animals in the context of heart regeneration, the cardiac myosin inhibitors Mavacamten and Aficamten are FDA-approved to reduce hypercontractility in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy [170]. A similar concept is that mechanical unloading (e.g. with a left ventricular assist device) increases cardiomyocyte proliferation in both human patients and rodent models [171, 172]. Therefore, repurposing therapeutics for heart failure may be a feasible method for heart regeneration in the clinic.

General limitations in the discovery of novel approaches for heart regeneration include the need for improved measurements for cell proliferation and bona fide cell division, with current reliance on fixed assays of cell cycle state or low-throughput live imaging of cell cycle progression combined with manual quantification [173]. Furthermore, the identification of targetable signalling pathways has been largely based on mouse and large animal models with differences in ploidy and nucleation to human cardiomyocytes [45]. The use of human stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes will aid in the identification of signalling modalities relevant to human biology and reveal potential toxicities [58, 110] but these models are still limited by cardiomyocyte immaturity.

Finally, therapeutics aiming to enhance cardiomyocyte proliferation collectively face key challenges: 1) specifically targeting cardiomyocytes without driving cardiac fibroblast proliferation, or uncontrolled proliferation of other cell types; 2) avoiding adverse impacts on cardiomyocyte metabolism; 3) ensuring promotion of cardiomyocyte proliferation does not compromise heart function. Specific targeting of cardiomyocytes seems achievable through emerging AAV-based therapies enabling cell-type specific control and nanoparticles targeted to the heart [174, 175]. In addition, precise temporal control of cardiomyocyte division in a manner that does not compromise cardiac contractility and, ultimately, patient safety, will be required to realise the promise of cardiac regenerative medicine.
