Symptom burden in middle-aged women, the role of immigrant status
Authors: Nilam Shakeel, Anne Karen Jenum, Christin Wiegels Waage, Ibrahimu Mdala, Erik L. Werner, Bård Natvig, Anja Maria Braend
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSubjective health complaints (SHCs) are symptoms from various organs that do not have an obvious explanation. Immigrants in Western countries are often exposed to multiple stressors that may trigger SHCs. Increased symptom load may reflect subjective health and functioning in the present and in the future. We, therefore, aimed to identify symptom load and distribution of subjective health complaints among middle-aged women by immigrant status.MethodsThis is a cross-sectional study which uses 11-year follow-up data from the STORK Groruddalen Cohort Study of 823 healthy pregnant women enrolled in primary antenatal care in a multi-ethnic city district in Oslo from 2008 to 2010. 11 years later, 379 women (53% of the eligible) provided questionnaire data on subjective health complaints drawn from the Subjective Health Complaints Inventory (SHCI) and regarding demographics and health issues collected through interviews. Participants were divided into three immigrant status groups: born in Norway by Norwegian parents (reference), born in Norway with one or two foreign born parents, and born abroad.The primary outcomes were the total score and the scores for each of the five domains in the SHCI: musculoskeletal pain, pseudo-neurology and gastrointestinal problems, allergy and flu.In addition, we studied the distribution of the 29 health complaints from the SHCI.ResultsMusculoskeletal symptoms were most severe and prevalent in all three immigrant-status groups.Compared to women born in Norway with Norwegian parents (reference), women born abroad had significantly higher total scores (median: 11.0 (IQR: 14.82–21.45) vs. 7.6 (9.38–12.78) (p = 0.002)). For musculoskeletal pain, the crude median scores were 4.3 (0.92–4.36) for women born abroad and 2.1 (3.81–5.72) for women born in Norway with Norwegian parents (p < 0.001).The following health complaints, headache, migraine, dizziness, pain in feet on exertion, bloating, constipation, chest pain, breathing difficulties and asthma, were more prevalent in women born abroad than in the reference group, before and after adjusting for age and education.ConclusionSHCs were more frequent and severe among women born abroad compared to the reference group. This knowledge is essential to identify women at risk and target appropriate interventions.Trial registrationThe study (STORK G 2) was prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (registration number: NCT03870724), registration date: 08.03.2019.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25093-x.

Introduction

Subjective health complaints (SHCs) are symptoms from various organs or systems, such as the musculoskeletal, neurological, or gastrointestinal system, that do not have an obvious explanation or organic cause and without corresponding findings on medical tests [1]. SHCs are common in the general population and include self-reported symptoms like fatigue, sleep problems, depression, anxiety, dizziness, and pain [2]. SHCs vary in strength and duration but may also be chronic, leading to substantial loss of function and withdrawal from usual activities [3, 4]. Other terms covering similar conditions have been used, such as medically unexplained physical symptoms (MUPS), medically unexplained symptoms (MUS) [3, 5], and recently, also persistent physical symptoms. This paper uses the broader term SHCs focusing on the symptom load. Increased symptom load influences the prognosis [6] and may lead to reduced functional ability [7], poor health, poor quality of life [8, 9], increasing welfare demands [10] and sick leaves, representing challenges for the health care with a significant impact on public health and social costs [11, 12].

Patients with SHCs or MUPS are typical in general practice, constituting around 80% of the general population reporting musculoskeletal complaints [8, 12]. Although few, studies including immigrants also report a higher prevalence of MUS and SHCs among immigrants than the major population [13–15].

Persistent stress may be related to the development of SHCs, and various stressors can be possible triggers [16]. According to the cognitive activation theory of stress, the stress response is normal, healthy, and a necessary alarm [17]. Still, if sustained it may be a risk for the development of SHCs. Immigrants in Western countries are often exposed to stressors before and during migration, but also after resettlement, such as language problems, lack of social integration, exposure to racism and low socioeconomic status, which may have adverse effects on health [18–21]. Further, immigrants may have a different symptom profile and understanding of symptoms compared to the general population [22, 23], representing challenges for health care [24]. Cross-sectional studies have also found female gender, a history of trauma, particularly early life stressful events, low socioeconomic status, and poor parental health as risk factors for stress and for SHCs [25]. We have found studies on SHCs among groups like people on disability benefits [26] and among different occupational groups [27], but few have studied the distribution and burden of SHCs among women with various ethnicities and migration status living in Western countries. Immigrants and Norwegian born by immigrant parents constitute an increasingly larger part of the Norwegian population, about 17% and 4%, respectively, of the total population [28]. The main aim of this study was to identify symptom load and distribution of subjective health complaints among middle-aged women by immigrant status.

Materials and methods

Design, population, and setting

In this cross sectional study of 379 women, we used 11-year follow-up data from the STORK Groruddalen Cohort Study which is a population-based cohort study of 823 healthy pregnant women enrolled in early pregnancy and followed to 14 weeks postpartum in primary antenatal care in three multi-ethnic city districts in Oslo during 2008–2010) [29]. For the planned follow-up in 2019–2022, 729 women were eligible, 385 (53%) met to the examination, and 379 provided questionnaire data on subjective health complaints drawn from the Subjective Health Complaints Inventory (SHCI) [30] through interviews. Those who attended the follow-up had slightly higher age and education at the baseline visit than non-attenders and fewer were ethnic minorities (47% versus 59% at baseline). At the same time BMI and parity did not differ [30]. The recruitment of study participants at the 11-year follow-up was carried out from August 2019 to March 2022 but was stopped during three periods due to the COVID-19 pandemic [30]. This paper addresses subjective health complaints, SHCs, a frequent and possibly disabling conditions in middle-aged women, using these data from the follow-up only.

Ethical approval

We obtained written informed consent from all women at baseline and again from all women participating at the 11-year follow-up. The Regional Committee for Medical and Health Research Ethics for Southeastern Norway approved the protocols for the original and the follow-up studies (ref: 2007/894 and 2018/2517). Relevant institutions have approved new subprojects and amendments.

Exposure variable – immigration status at enrollment

At enrollment, participants were asked about their country of birth and their parents` country of birth if the participant was born in Norway. They were categorized into three groups: (1) born in Norway with Norwegian parents (reference group), (2) born in Norway with one or two foreign-born parents, and (3) born outside Norway, hereafter called born abroad, as our hypothesis was that immigrant status might be a risk factor for SHCs [31].

More than 80% of those born outside Norway were from Asia and Africa, while 11.6% were born in Eastern Europe and 6.7% in Western European countries.

Outcomes

The burden of SHCs was our primary outcome, measured by the SHC Inventory (SHCI), which has been previously used in the literature to address subjective health complaints and has satisfactory validity and reliability [14, 32]. The SHCI registers the occurrence, intensity, and duration of 29 common subjective somatic and psychological complaints experienced during the past 30 days without reference to specific diagnostic categories. The 29 items from the questionnaire are further divided into five domains of symptoms: (1) musculoskeletal pain (headache, migraine, pain in the neck, upper back, lower back, arm, shoulder, and feet), (2) pseudoneurology (extra heartbeats/palpitations, heat flushes, sleep problems, tiredness, dizziness, anxiety, and sadness/depression), (3) gastrointestinal problems (heartburn, stomach discomfort, ulcer/non-ulcer dyspepsia, stomach pain, gas discomfort/bloating, diarrhoea, and constipation), (4) allergy (asthma, breathing difficulties, eczema, allergy, and chest pain) and (5) flu symptoms (colds/flu and coughing).

Each item in the SHCI has four answer options, and the intensity scores for each item ranged from zero to three (0 = not at all;1 = a little;2 = some;3 = severe). We merged the categories “severe” and “some” into one category and ended up with three categories: (1) some to severe, (2) a little and (3) not at all. In addition, we used data from SHCI as a continuous variable and estimated sum scores, further described under statistical methods.

Covariates

Age and education were considered as confounders for our analysis. We used age as a continuous variable. The educational level at enrolment was categorised as “primary school or less” (< 10 years), “high school/secondary school” (10–12 years) or “college/university” (13–16 years).

Parity was divided in three categories (1 child, 2 children and ≥ 3 children).

We also report on “use of general practitioner past 12 months” and “I feel taken care of by my general practitioner” with five response options, merged into three categories: good, neither good nor bad, and bad. “Self-reported health” had four response options merged into two categories: good and poor. We also asked about “reading Norwegian newspapers/watching TV”, which may reflect the level of social integration for immigrant groups in the Norwegian mainstream society.

Statistical methods

Data are presented using the mean with standard deviation (SD), median with interquartile range (IQR), and frequencies with percentages (%). We used analysis of variance (ANOVA) when comparing the mean values for continuous data and the chi-square test for categorical data or the Fisher’s exact test if the expected cell frequencies were < 5. We created one figure for each domain that described the distribution of individual symptoms for the past 30 days based on severity and stratified by immigrant status.

A crude score (continuous data), taking into consideration both severity and duration, was calculated by multiplying the score on a single item (0–3) by duration (number of days), then dividing by 10 to obtain equal weights on both severity and duration [32]. Then, a total and domain score was obtained by summarizing the scores on each single item for each woman giving, a maximum total score of 261 (range 0-261) and maximum subscores for each domain: musculoskeletal pain 72 (0–72), pseudo-neurology and gastrointestinal problems 63 (0–63), allergy 45 (0–45) and flu 18 (0–18). We calculated the total score and the subscore for each of the five domains as explained above and presented the median with interquartile range (IQR) as the scores were highly skewed. Kruskal-Wallis test was corrected for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction, which was used to compare the median scores between the groups.

Data on the sum of scores for each domain and the total sum of scores obtained were bounded from zero and were highly skewed. It is common to log-transform such data before fitting a linear regression model. However, log transforming zeros is not feasible because ln 0 =\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\infty\:}.\:$$\end{document} To solve this problem, researchers commonly add a small number, such as 0.0001, before log-transforming the data. However, this is not optimal because of a lack of consensus on how small this number should be; hence, this may be influential when fitting log-linear regression models. Log-transforming the sum of scores may also yield inconsistent estimates if adjustments for heteroscedasticity and normality of the residuals are not made [33]. This may suggest that inferences drawn from log-linear regression may be misleading. Further, the estimated regression coefficients are not in their natural units and are often retransformed to unlogged terms before drawing inferences.

To correct the issues with the log-linear regression model; we fitted our data with a Poisson regression model with robust standard errors. This model was used to assess independent factors associated with the total/domain scores and each of the 29 items in the questionnaire [34].

Two steps preceded the modelling of the data: first, we fitted unadjusted models to check the association between immigration status and the total and domain scores, second, we adjusted for age and education. We then checked the association between immigration status and each of the 29 items of the SHCI, adjusting for age and education. All analyses were performed using StataSE 18.0 and the significance level was set at 0.05.

One hundred and eighty-two (48%) women were born in Norway with Norwegian parents and constituted the reference group, 33 (8.7%) were born in Norway with one or two foreign-born parents, and 164 (43%) were born abroad. Women in the reference group were slightly older, had fewer children and higher education compared to women in the two other groups.

The burden of subjective health complaint symptoms – overall and by domain

The crude total SHC score was significantly higher among women born abroad compared to women born in Norway with Norwegian parents (median: 11.0 (IQR: 14.82–21.45) vs. 7.6 (9.38–12.78) (p = 0.002)), (Supplementary table 1). For the domain musculoskeletal pain, the crude median scores were 4.3 (0.92–4.36) and 2.1 (3.81–5.72) for the respective groups (p < 0.001). After adjusting for age and education, women born abroad still had significantly higher total scores and domain scores for musculoskeletal pain compared to the two other groups (Fig. 1). From all the 29 individual symptoms, the following individual symptoms were more prevalent and severe in women born abroad compared with the reference group: dizziness (p = 0.017), constipation (p < 0.001), breathing difficulties (p = 0.004), pain in the neck (0.005), shoulder (p = 0.012), upper back (0.029), arm and in feet on exertion (p < 0.001). In contrast, tiredness and sleep problems were more prevalent and severe among the two other groups, however not significant (results not shown).

The burden of individual symptoms

In the crude Poisson regression analysis, scores for neck pain, ulcer and non-ulcer dyspepsia, headache, migraine, dizziness, pain in feet on exertion, bloating, constipation, chest pain, breathing difficulties and asthma were significantly higher for women born abroad compared to women born in Norway with Norwegian parents (results not shown). When adjusted for age and education, the scores for all symptoms, apart from neck pain, ulcer and non-ulcer dyspepsia, remained significantly increased, as shown in the forest plot (Fig. 3). Only the significant estimates are presented in Fig. 3. Further, the scores for migraine, foot pain, dizziness, bloating, and headache were up to 91% higher for women born abroad compared to women in the reference group (Fig. 3). For the group born in Norway with one or two foreign parents, the coefficients were not different from those of the reference group.

Discussion

To our knowledge, our study is the first to describe SHCs in middle-aged immigrant women based on the SHCI. In this multiethnic sample we observed that women born abroad with at least 11 years of residence in Norway had more prevalent and severe individual symptoms, particularly from the musculoskeletal system, compared to the reference group (women born in Norway with Norwegian parents). This translated into a significantly higher total SHC score, and domain score for musculoskeletal pain in women born abroad compared to the two other groups, after adjusting for confounders. Among individual symptoms, headache, migraine, dizziness, pain in feet, bloating, constipation, chest pain, breathing difficulties and asthma remained significantly more prevalent among women born abroad than in the reference group after similar adjustments.

Our well-characterized population-based cohort used study methods adapted to reduce barriers to participation for illiterate women and those that did not speak the majority language [29, 30]. We used the SHCI, a validated and reliable tool enabling us to report on a wide range of symptoms that covers the most common health complaints [8, , 35] and describe the distribution/pattern of symptoms and the total symptom load, known to reflect subjective health and functioning at present and in the future [36]. Our overall findings are in line with studies reporting poorer health among immigrants compared to the native population on most health indicators and even some studies regarding MUPS and somatic symptoms [37]. However, direct comparisons between studies are complex, due to differences in study design, population, and methods to measure health or symptom load. Many studies represent populations from hospital clinics, different employment groups, do not specify the immigrant status, do not use validated measures or focus only on one or few symptoms of symptom domains [12–15].

Our study, therefore, adds to the literature that the symptom burden among middle-aged immigrant women born abroad is more severe and prevalent compared to the majority population, even after long time residence in Norway. Importantly, we found a high prevalence of SHCs, especially for musculoskeletal symptoms among immigrants born abroad. There are few other population-based studies regardless of the instrument used. However, most studies that have included ethnic minorities support our finding of more symptoms among immigrants [37, 38]. Nevertheless, in contrast to our research and most other studies, an American national representative survey study on noninstitutionalized Latinos, Asians and non-Latino whites ≥ 18 years, reported fewer general physical symptoms and less MUPS in Asians and Latinos compared to non-Latino whites [39]. The study suggests that heterogeneity within an immigrant population and cultural factors like valued relationships, links to extended family and more social support could act as protective measures against somatization and reduce general physical symptoms.

Our study confirms the presence of health inequalities within a heterogeneous urban population with a substantial immigrant population, which is in line with Italian research on health-related quality of life, self-rated health and morbidity [40]. This study on immigrants only reported on defined medical conditions rather than symptoms, using questionnaires other than SHCI, and did not compare the prevalence with the majority population as we did. However, they also found that musculoskeletal pathologies were the most prevalent among all somatic pathologies. Most other studies using the SHCI are from the general population only [37, 41]. Further, patients consulting their general practitioner, GP, usually have a high number of SHCs, not necessarily indicating a disease, but still a burden for the patient [42] and feel having poor health, and not taken enough care of by their GP [43] and may tend to seek their GP more often [12]. The latter two points contrast with our findings.

In addition to the frequent reporting of musculoskeletal pain, our study also reports several individual symptoms being more frequent in women born abroad than those born in Norway with Norwegian parents. Regarding frequent symptoms, our study is in line with a Norwegian general practice study that reported that headache, tiredness, lower back pain, sleep problems, and neck and shoulder pain were among the most frequently reported in the general population [42].

Regarding possible underlying factors for the increased burden of SHCs in immigrant women, immigration itself has been postulated as a stressful life event [44] and persistent stress can be associated with the development of SHCs [16]. We have therefore focused on immigrant status rather than ethnic groups. Migration to another country represents a massive change in an individual’s physical, psychological and social life. It can impact the level of perceived stress particularly in the first generation [31]. In line with this, we found that the burden was lower in those born in Norway with one or two foreign-born parents, although this finding must be interpreted with caution as this group was small.

An important strength of the present study is the long-term follow-up of a population-based cohort where we included even “hard to reach” women (about 22% needed a translator at inclusion). Immigration status and education were measured by inclusion. We consider an attendance rate of 53% of eligible women and 47% of the original cohort after 11 years, decent during the COVID-19 pandemic, and with a relatively high number of immigrants with different migration status. We adjusted for age and education as these variables could confound the relation between immigrant status and SHCs. We have used the SHCI to describe the burden of symptoms, which is important for the prognosis.

There are also limitations to report. Heterogeneity is a concern as our grouping strategy may have masked important differences within the groups. Our sample is dominated by women from Asia and Africa and cannot be generalized to all women born abroad. The group born in Norway with one or two foreign-born parents was small which is a further limitation. Our sample represents women with children (and does not include men). Further limitation is that this is an urban population, and we do not have data on subjective health complaints from those who have just migrated as the women in our sample have at least 11 years of residence in Norway.

Further, some question the clinical relevance of SHCI, which cannot be used as a diagnostic tool as there is no cut-off value.

To effectively prevent and treat health conditions that affect the quality of life in multiethnic populations, healthcare professionals should know commonly occurring symptoms and their distribution and recognize them as such in a clinical context. According to the literature, a prognostic tool may be better than a diagnostic tool to gain insight into the patient’s need [6]. The symptom trajectories seem to depend more on other factors than the diagnosis. Knowledge from our study may be used as a prognostic factor leading to stratified care for immigrants, and for planning future public health policy.

Conclusion

Women born abroad reported more subjective health complaints, particularly from the musculoskeletal system, compared to women born in Norway with Norwegian parents, even after more than 11 years of residence in Norway. This knowledge is important to identify women at risk and target appropriate interventions.
