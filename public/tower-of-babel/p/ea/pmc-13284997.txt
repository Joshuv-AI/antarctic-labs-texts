Co-developing implementation strategies to support STEADI (Stopping Elderly Accidents, Deaths, and Injuries) for older adult fall prevention in outpatient physical therapy
Authors: Jennifer L Vincenzo, Sarah K Council, Mariana Wingood, Aaron J Scott, Jamie M Caulley, Emily Kocurek, Jennifer S Brach, Geoffrey M Curran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesEvidence-based quality improvement (EBQI) is an approach to co-develop implementation strategies with implementation partners. This study describes an EBQI approach for co-developing implementation strategies to support the adoption of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s Stopping Elderly Accidents, Deaths, and Injuries (STEADI) falls prevention initiative in outpatient physical therapy.MethodsA multidisciplinary panel (n = 10), representing key partner groups including physical therapists, clinic managers, a referring physician, an older adult with prior physical therapy experience, and a caregiver, collaborated with a research team across 5 outpatient physical therapy clinics within a U.S. health system. Eight virtual EBQI panel sessions conducted over 9 months included meetings, barriers/facilitator ranking, strategy identification/rating, concept mapping, consensus building, and group discussion. Strategies were evaluated on feasibility and importance using Likert-scale surveys and iteratively refined. The main outcome measures were partner-rated rankings of barriers and facilitators to address implementation challenges, rankings of strategy feasibility and importance, and a finalized package of tailored implementation strategies.ResultsTwenty barriers and 20 facilitators were prioritized and ranked by each partner group. Prioritized barriers were matched with prioritized facilitators and/or implementation strategies to address barriers. Of the 78 implementation strategies considered, 39 unique specifications from 22 strategies were identified. Key strategies included clinician education, workflow and electronic health record integration, clinician and older adult-facing materials, clinic champions, and communication templates.Discussion and ImplicationsEBQI effectively engaged implementation partners in identifying and prioritizing barriers, facilitators, and co-developing tailored, actionable implementation strategies to support STEADI adoption in outpatient physical therapy.

This study is innovative in its application of an evidence-based quality improvement approach to co-develop tailored implementation strategies for Stopping Elderly Accidents, Deaths, and Injuries, an evidence-based falls prevention initiative, within outpatient physical therapy settings. By engaging clinicians, physicians, older adults, and caregivers in a structured, data-driven process, the study provides replicable methods to translate evidence into clinical practice. The resulting strategy package supports fall prevention delivery, addressing workflow integration, clinician education, and older adult engagement, and can be used to inform implementation approaches across other health settings.

Background and objectives

Falls are a public health concern among older adults, contributing to morbidity, mortality, and financial burden on the healthcare system.1 To address this, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) created the Stopping Elderly Accidents, Deaths, and Injuries (STEADI) initiative.2 This initiative equips healthcare providers with evidence-based tools and guidelines to systematically identify an older adult’s falls risk, assess modifiable risk factors, and implement preventive interventions. STEADI has been implemented in primary care with varying success due to complications from system- to individual-level barriers.3-5 Casey et al.,3 Eckstrom et al.,4 and Clemson et al.5 demonstrated that provider education and electronic health record (EHR) modifications aided STEADI adoption and fidelity in primary care; however, adoption rates remain low. Outpatient physical therapy is a promising setting for STEADI, with physical therapists demonstrating high rates of screening and physical assessments.6 However, barriers across health systems include workflow inefficiencies, inadequate EHR clinical decision support, and therapists’ limited knowledge and confidence in conducting some STEADI components.6,7 Few studies on falls prevention or STEADI have utilized implementation science to support adoption. Clemson and colleagues5 found that education and academic detailing improved Australian primary care providers’ uptake of and allied healthcare providers’ (including physical therapists) confidence to engage in falls prevention. Differences in care models between countries warrant studies tailored to U.S. outpatient physical therapy. To date, pre-implementation formative work and post-implementation evaluation have only presented barriers and facilitators impacting therapists’ adoption of STEADI.5,7,8 To optimize adoption, implementation partners involved in or impacted by implementation, like management, therapists, referring physicians, older adults, and caregivers, should be engaged in all phases of implementation.9 A collaborative approach can help contextualize challenges and support the co-development of implementation strategies.

Evidence-based quality improvement (EBQI) promotes collaboration between implementation researchers and local partners to identify and tailor implementation strategies to contexts and facilitate the uptake of evidence-based practices.10 EBQI helps align implementation with research and real-world practice, improving sustainability and effectiveness.10 Despite the potential benefits, EBQI has not been used to develop implementation strategies to support the adoption of evidence-based practices, such as STEADI, in outpatient physical therapy. Therefore, we describe our pre-implementation EBQI approach with partners to co-develop implementation strategies for STEADI adoption in outpatient physical therapy.

Research design and methods

Following approval from the university institutional review board (Protocol 274892), partners were invited to participate in the EBQI panel.

EBQI approach

We followed the EBQI methodology outlined by Swindle et al.10

Form a team of local partners and experts

In prior work, we conducted a formative evaluation through surveys and interviews with 44 partners (ie, individuals involved in implementing, delivering, receiving, or supporting STEADI) to identify their knowledge, perceptions, barriers, and facilitators to adopting the CDC’s STEADI initiative in outpatient physical therapy.11 In the current study, these previously identified barriers and facilitators were used as the foundation for prioritization, ranking, and linkage to tailored implementation strategies.

Identified via purposeful sampling, partners included clinic managers, physical therapists and assistants, referring physicians, administrative staff, older adults who had received physical therapy for any indication (not limited to balance or falls), and caregivers of older adults who had received physical therapy for any indication. For the EBQI panel, a subset of these partners was recruited as panelists based on their roles, experience, and participation in the prior evaluation. Panelists were selected to represent key partner groups rather than individual perspectives. Panelists included clinical management of 5 physical therapy clinics in a health system (n = 2), physical therapists or physical therapist assistants representing each clinic (n = 5), a physician who refers older adults to 2 of the clinics (n = 1), an older adult who has had physical therapy for any indication (n = 1), and a caregiver of an older adult who has had physical therapy for any indication (n = 1). We were unable to secure participation from administrative staff and therefore relied on input from the team regarding strategies needed to support implementation for that group. The implementation research team consisted of 2 geriatric physical therapists/implementation scientists, a geriatric physical therapy clinical lead who implemented STEADI in their health system, and an expert implementation scientist.

Activities

The EBQI panel met 8 times via Zoom (approximately once per month) between September 2023 and May 2024 for 1.5 hours each session. Meetings were video-recorded, and notes on panel discussions and decisions were both video and audio-recorded. We utilized multiple approaches and methods to achieve EBQI goals during and between sessions. Descriptions of each panel session and its goals are provided in Table 1 (see Supplementary Table 1 for full details on activities to achieve the goals and activities completed between the sessions).3,10,12–25 Briefly, consensus voting and surveys included input and data from all partners unless otherwise specified. After each EBQI meeting, the research team assimilated the input from the panel, identified actions to be completed between each meeting, and data/information to be analyzed, shared, and acted upon by the panel in the subsequent meeting(s).

Prioritize barriers and facilitators to implementation

The panel was presented with results from the formative work. They confirmed, omitted, or added barriers or facilitators during the meeting. For ranking barriers and facilitators, panel members submitted their “top 5,” ranked in order, that they felt were both important and actionable for each partner group involved in or impacted by implementation. Rankings were submitted by an anonymous chat during the meeting to a research team member (see Supplementary Figure 1).

Identify implementation strategies

The research team used the results of the panel’s barriers and facilitators ranking to identify implementation strategies to address the top 5 barriers in each partner group. The implementation strategies were identified using established methods such as the CFIR-ERIC matching tool,20 StrategEase tool,21 literature,17,25 and expert input. The research team also capitalized on the identified facilitators by matching facilitators that addressed barriers. For example, to address the barrier of therapists’ lack of knowledge about STEADI, there was a facilitator of an available training through an educational platform that the clinics subscribed to.

Select and tailor implementation strategies

Potential implementation strategies to address barriers were presented during panel meetings for discussion and refinement. In post-meeting surveys completed through RedCap,26,27 panel members rated each implementation strategy’s feasibility and importance of addressing a specific barrier (5-point Likert scale; 1 = not at all important/feasible, 5 = very important/feasible) (see Supplementary Figure 2).

Strategies above the mean on both importance and feasibility were recommended for consideration.10 Throughout the EBQI process of implementation strategy development, iterations, and tailoring, strategies could be omitted, revised, or added.

Statistical analysis

For rankings of barriers and facilitators, we calculated a total weighted rank of each item within each partner group by assigning point values to ranked responses and summing across responses. For example, a rank of 1 is assigned 5 points (most important), a 2 is 4 points, a 3 is 3 points, a 4 is 2 points, and a 5 is 1 point (least important). Barriers and facilitators were then ordered by total weighted rank from highest to lowest. Rankings were conducted separately for physical therapists/assistants, physicians, and older adults/caregivers. For concept mapping,19 panelists first completed surveys in RedCap to identify the perceived importance and feasibility of each strategy. One survey was conducted for therapist strategies, and a second survey included physician and older adult/caregiver strategies. We then compiled descriptive statistics on feasibility and importance ratings, followed by plotting that data by importance (x-axis) and feasibility (y-axis).18 Implementation strategies rated above the survey mean for both importance and feasibility were presented to partners in subsequent meetings for discussion and to inform actions. We noticed differences in importance and feasibility responses specific to EHR strategies for clinics that used different EHRs (2 EHRs are used across our sample of clinics), due to the different EHRs having different capabilities. Therefore, we presented the EHR strategy responses by partners associated with the clinics that used each EHR (ie, managers, therapists).

Results

The EBQI process included 8 panel sessions to prioritize barriers and facilitators and develop, tailor, and finalize strategies for STEADI adoption in outpatient physical therapy. Session goals, activities, and between-session work are outlined in Table 1.3,10,12-25 Barriers and facilitators were previously published by Vincenzo and colleagues.6,8

Prioritize barriers and facilitators to implementation

Initial EBQI sessions-oriented panelists to the study and focused on identifying key, actionable barriers and facilitators. Barriers deemed important but not actionable, like limited visit time, scope of practice limitations, and lack of home safety resources, were excluded from strategy development. Twenty barriers and 20 facilitators were prioritized and ranked overall. Of the 20 barriers, 7 related to physical therapists/assistants, 4 to physicians, and 9 to older adults/caregivers; of the 20 facilitators, 8, 6, and 6 related to these groups, respectively (Table 2). Across partner groups, high-priority barriers commonly reflected gaps in knowledge, workflow integration, and communication, while facilitators emphasized alignment with existing clinical roles, perceived value of STEADI, and opportunities to adapt workflows (Table 2). For example, the highest-ranking barrier for physical therapists was limited knowledge about STEADI or evidence-based falls prevention, including medication assessment. The highest-ranking facilitator for physical therapists was STEADI’s uniqueness, comprehensiveness, and lack of complexity.

Identify implementation strategies

The implementation team then identified and presented 78 potential implementation strategies to the panel to address the prioritized barriers. Strategies included the previously identified facilitators, matched to barriers that could be addressed. Panelists refined this list by adding, revising, or omitting strategies based on their perceptions of each strategy’s feasibility and importance. Examples of added strategies included email/listserv announcements to physicians about STEADI implementation in outpatient physical therapy, as well as EHR searches for cognitive screening. Examples of omitted strategies included mandates and EHR reminders.

Panelists then rated each strategy, in a RedCap survey, by its feasibility and importance to address barriers for each partner group (Supplementary Table 2). Of the 78 strategies reviewed, 44 targeted physical therapists/assistants, 14 targeted physicians, and 20 targeted older adults/caregivers. Among these, 26/44 (59.1%) therapist-focused strategies, 7/14 (50.0%) physician-focused strategies, and 7/20 (35.0%) older adult/caregiver–focused strategies were rated as both feasible and important. Across partner groups, highly rated strategies emphasized provider education and training, workflow integration, communication tools, and approaches to support older adult engagement in falls prevention (eg, brief action planning and specific resources).

Select and refine implementation strategies

Through discussion, iterative strategy development, and consensus, the panel selected implementation strategies for STEADI adoption (Table 3 and Supplementary Table 3). While rankings informed deliberation and initial strategies to develop, final decisions and strategies reflected feasibility, workflow burden, and partner preferences. An example of a strategy that was above the threshold but excluded from the final implementation package included EHR flagging of high fall-risk medication. Therapists noted that flags add to burdensome EHR alerts disrupting workflow. Examples of strategies included in the final package despite low rankings were clinic champions and a resource directory for older adults and caregivers. Finally, some strategies that were not in the original group of voted upon strategies were added during the EBQI process and included in the final package of strategies such as strategies targeted to assist administrative staff to implement STEADI and develop a process map workflow for clinics.

Discussion and implications

Using the EBQI approach, we co-developed a tailored package of implementation strategies to support STEADI in outpatient physical therapy. Using our methodological guidance and process, researchers can co-develop implementation strategies with partners to implement innovations, such as STEADI, into clinical practice. Collaborative development of implementation strategies grounded in partner input is important for enhancing feasibility, acceptability, appropriateness, and long-term adoption.28 This study builds on our previous post implementation evaluations of outcomes and barriers and facilitators to STEADI adoption in outpatient physical therapy in a large health system.6,8 A key strategy included partners co-designing and tailoring STEADI to the local context, which supports adoption and sustainability.29 In our study, this included education to address knowledge gaps, integrating STEADI into clinical workflows, the EHR, and developing older adult- and provider-facing educational resources. Although education is a key strategy to address knowledge gaps in healthcare providers’ implementation of evidence-based practices, it alone rarely ensures adoption.30 Therefore, additional strategies are necessary, such as integrating STEADI into the local workflow and EHR to support adoption3,4,31 and sustainment, which aligns with previous STEADI implementation in primary care and more recently, post-implementation evaluation in outpatient physical therapy.6,8 Staggering STEADI assessments and interventions across visits for feasibility align with American Geriatrics Society Workgroup recommendations.32 Notably, our formative work did not reveal barriers at the administrative level; however, the EBQI process identified workflow and EHR barriers requiring implementation strategies. This likely reflects limited administrative participation in earlier data collection, highlighting the need for broad partner representation during the formative phase.33

Audit and feedback emerged as an important strategy to reinforce implementation, particularly at the therapist and administrative levels. Sharing adoption metrics and collaboratively setting adoption goals can improve motivation, accountability, and adoption of clinical initiatives.34 Our prior post-implementation evaluation study on a quality initiative of STEADI in outpatient physical therapy indicated that a mandate was a key implementation strategy to support STEADI adoption.7 However, partners in our study did not recommend inclusion of a mandate. Mandates can contribute to successful strategies that support the adoption of new initiatives19,35 but can be perceived as hindering autonomy and clinical judgment.36

Facilitators guided strategy development for education support and clinician buy-in. Leveraging existing resources and platforms to deliver training and offer continuing education credits (CEUs) was identified as an implementation strategy and addressed therapist engagement. One study found that training that provides CEUs and increases knowledge can significantly increase buy-in and commitment to new geriatric clinical practices.37 Focused training and self-efficacy also influence engagement in physical therapy in addressing medications with patients, which is part of STEADI.38

Physicians’ lack of awareness regarding therapists’ falls prevention training and interdisciplinary communication challenges required implementation strategies. We co-developed announcements to be sent out through existing listservs and newsletters to educate physicians on STEADI implementation and physical therapists’ roles in fall prevention. Standardized language templates were co-developed to improve therapist-physician communication and minimize jargon. Improving healthcare providers’ understanding of team roles and promoting shared language is vital to interdisciplinary collaboration.39,40

We identified several strategies to address older adults’ and caregivers’ commonly cited barriers, such as limited knowledge, low engagement, and lack of awareness of falls risk and prevention resources. Existing literature supports that older adults often underestimate their fall risk and are unaware of available interventions.3,41 Although these strategies were targeted at the older adult, therapists were the primary actors responsible for delivering STEADI education and interventions to the older adult, a common implementation reality in outpatient rehabilitation.42 The majority of the implementation strategies identified through the EBQI process rely on the physical therapist as the central actor. To promote implementation and reduce burden, additional strategies were introduced during EBQI sessions, including the use of clinic champions and assistance by the implementation team to address challenges. These supportive roles are consistent with best practices for implementing complex interventions to optimize adoption and sustainment.9

Study limitations

Our study limitations included our EBQI being conducted in one health system with a small sample of partners, which may limit generalizability. There is potential for selection bias of partners who engaged in EBQI, and we lacked administrative staff representation. Another limitation is the time and resource intensiveness of EBQI. The STEADI itself is a limitation. In this pre-implementation study, we are attempting to implement the “full” STEADI in the physical therapy context. STEADI is a highly complex intervention consisting of several steps—screening, assessment, intervention, several educational components, and the need for communication across clinical teams and older adults/caregivers. Regarding the high initial number of implementation strategies (22, with 39 unique specifications), this includes several pre-implementation activities, for example, adapting and tailoring via EBQI. This number will not be reflected in the active implementation phase. Specific to categories of strategies, we counted each component as a discrete strategy/specification, for example, training and educating stakeholders. There are 8 older adult-facing education strategies, which correspond to multiple steps of the STEADI intervention. Moreover, many of the strategies developed and being tested are embedded and integrated in the EHR and workflow; therefore, they may be more feasible to adapt locally within the same EHR platforms. Applying our iterative approach will allow us to adjust and specify strategies targeting all groups in the physical therapy context to identify an optimal implementation strategy package to test in a future implementation trial where we expect to compare bundles of strategies, likely packaged into a lower complexity bundle (eg, EHR enabled education and decision support) versus the addition of higher complexity strategies (eg, training and deployment of local champions, facilitation). We plan on optimizing strategies for diverse contextual needs. Despite these limitations, the strengths of our EBQI approach are the use of rigorous implementation science methods and the inclusion of multiple partners to co-develop implementation strategies to support local STEADI adoption.

Conclusion

Using a collaborative, iterative EBQI approach, we co-developed a contextually tailored package of 22 implementation strategies with 39 unique specifications to support the CDC’s STEADI initiative adoption in 5 outpatient physical therapy clinics. Strategies targeted actionable and important barriers identified across partner groups, including therapists, physicians, and older adults/caregivers, and aligned with clinic workflows, roles, and resources. Central strategies included education/training, workflow and EHR integration, communication templates, resources, and older adult education tools. Among the 78 potential strategies considered, those most frequently rated as both feasible and important emphasized multi-level approaches, highlighting the need for interventions that address provider education, workflow alignment, interdisciplinary communication, and older adult engagement. The EBQI process facilitated alignment of implementation efforts with the local context, demonstrating that engaging multidisciplinary partners throughout implementation can identify actionable barriers, refine strategy selection, and improve feasibility and acceptability for real-world clinical implementation. Using our methodological guidance and process, researchers and clinicians can co-develop implementation strategies to implement innovations, such as STEADI, to support the uptake of other evidence-based practices in rehabilitation.
