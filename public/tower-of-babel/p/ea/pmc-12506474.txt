Impact of rural residence on clinical outcomes in SLE: a systematic review
Authors: Ryuichi Ohta, Ryu Yoshinori, Chiaki Sano, Kunihiro Ichinose
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSLE is a chronic autoimmune disease with heterogeneous manifestations and variable outcomes. Geographic factors such as rural residence may influence disease severity, access to care and treatment adherence, yet evidence remains fragmented. This systematic review aimed to evaluate the impact of rurality on clinical outcomes in adults with SLE.MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed, Embase and Web of Science for observational studies published up to May 2025 that assessed the association between rural residence and clinical outcomes in SLE. Eligible studies included adult patients with SLE and reported at least one relevant outcome stratified by rurality. Using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, data were extracted on study characteristics, definitions of rurality, outcome domains and risk of bias. Due to heterogeneity in study design and outcomes, a narrative synthesis was conducted.ResultsEight studies, including over 34 000 participants from the USA, Greece, China, Egypt, Puerto Rico and Latin America (Grupo Latino Americano de Estudio del Lupus cohort), met inclusion criteria. Definitions of rurality varied widely, encompassing administrative classifications, demographic thresholds and self-reported residence. Rural residence was often associated with delayed diagnosis, higher disease activity, lower physical quality of life, increased hospital readmissions and poorer medication adherence. Survival findings were mixed, and one study found no rural disadvantage where specialised care was available. Methodological quality was generally moderate to high.ConclusionAcross diverse settings, rural SLE populations frequently experience worse outcomes, although this is not universal and appears to be strongly influenced by structural disadvantages rather than geography alone. Standardised definitions of rurality and multidimensional measurement approaches are needed to improve comparability and guide effective interventions. Targeted strategies—such as telemedicine, outreach programmes and policies addressing healthcare access—may help reduce inequities in SLE care.

Introduction

SLE is a chronic, multisystem autoimmune disease characterised by complex clinical manifestations and variable disease trajectories.1 Despite advances in diagnostics and immunosuppressive therapy, patients with SLE remain at elevated risk for morbidity and mortality due to organ involvement, disease flares, treatment-related complications and barriers to accessing comprehensive care.2 A growing body of evidence suggests that social determinants of health—including geography, socioeconomic status, and health system infrastructure—play a significant role in shaping disease outcomes.3 4

Rurality, defined by population density, healthcare access or geographic isolation, has been increasingly recognised as a critical contextual factor influencing health disparities.5 6 Individuals residing in rural settings often face unique challenges, including limited access to rheumatologists, fewer healthcare facilities, transportation barriers and reduced availability of disease education and social support services.7 These barriers are particularly consequential for individuals with complex and resource-intensive conditions such as SLE, where delayed diagnosis, poor disease monitoring and inadequate management can lead to irreversible organ damage and decreased quality of life.8 9

Prior studies in other chronic conditions have demonstrated poorer health outcomes among rural populations, including increased hospitalisation rates, worse medication adherence and lower health-related quality of life.1012 However, in the context of SLE, the literature remains fragmented and heterogeneous, with studies differing in how rurality is defined and which outcomes are assessed.13 Furthermore, regional differences in healthcare delivery models and insurance structures complicate cross-national comparisons.14

To address these knowledge gaps, this systematic review aims to synthesise existing evidence on the impact of rurality on clinical outcomes in adults with SLE. Specifically, we examine the association between rural residence and five key outcome domains: survival and end-stage renal disease (ESRD) incidence, disease activity, quality of life, adverse events (eg, hospitalisation) and medication adherence. Understanding how rural environments affect disease course and management in SLE may inform strategies for equitable healthcare delivery, particularly in underserved regions.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.15 The review protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews under the registration number (CRD420251069188). The objective was to evaluate the impact of rurality on clinical outcomes among patients with SLE.

Eligibility criteria and search strategy

We included observational studies (cross-sectional, cohort or registry-based) that assessed clinical outcomes in adult patients (≥18 years) diagnosed with SLE according to standardised criteria (eg, American College of Rheumatology or SLE International Collaborating Clinics). Studies were eligible if they compared outcomes between rural and non-rural settings or stratified results by degree of rurality. Exclusion criteria included paediatric populations, interventional studies or trials, reviews, editorials, case reports, studies not stratifying or defining rurality and non-English full texts.

A systematic search was conducted in Embase, PubMed and Web of Science from inception to February 2024. The search strategy incorporated terms related to ‘systemic lupus erythematosus’, ‘rural’, ‘geography’ and relevant clinical outcomes such as ‘survival’, ‘adherence’ and ‘quality of life’. A manual search of reference lists from included studies was also performed to identify additional eligible literature. The complete search strategy is presented in online supplemental table 1.

Data extraction and management

Two reviewers independently screened titles, abstracts and full texts using the Covidence software platform. Data were extracted using a standardised form that captured: study characteristics (authors, year, location, design and sample size), definition of rurality, SLE diagnosis criteria, clinical outcomes (survival, disease activity, adverse events, quality of life and medication adherence) and key findings stratified by rurality. A third reviewer resolved discrepancies through discussion or adjudication. The extracted data were compiled in Microsoft Excel for narrative synthesis and tabulation.

We also extracted detailed information on how each study defined ‘rurality’ and classified geographic residence, including the type of definition (eg, administrative, self-reported, demographic threshold or mixed) and data sources used. These were summarised in a comparative table to facilitate cross-study interpretation.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), which evaluates three domains: selection of study population, comparability of groups and ascertainment of outcomes. A score out of nine was assigned to each study. Studies scoring 7–9 were classified as low risk of bias, 4–6 as moderate and <4 as high risk. Two reviewers conducted the risk of bias assessments independently, with discrepancies resolved through consensus. A summary of NOS scores is provided in online supplemental table 1.

Outcomes and data synthesis

Outcomes were grouped into five categories: survival or ESRD incidence, disease activity (eg, SLE Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI) and Systemic Lupus Activity Questionnaire (SLAQ)), health-related quality of life (HRQoL) (eg, 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36) and Lupus Patient Reported Outcome (LupusPRO)), adverse events and healthcare utilisation (eg, hospital readmission and organ damage) and medication adherence. Given heterogeneity in definitions of rurality, outcome measures and healthcare system structures, meta-analysis was not feasible. Instead, a narrative synthesis was conducted across outcome domains, particularly to variations in definitions of rurality (eg, ZIP code-based, self-reported and population threshold). Sensitivity analysis based on study quality was considered but limited by the few high-quality studies using comparable methodologies.

Results

Study selection

A total of 225 records were identified through database searching: Embase (n=149), PubMed (n=42) and Web of Science (n=34). After the removal of 59 duplicates (57 by Covidence and 2 manually), 166 records were screened. Of these, 142 were excluded based on title and abstract screening. 24 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 16 were excluded for the following reasons: no full text available (n=12), wrong setting (n=2), wrong outcomes (n=1) and wrong patient population (n=1). Ultimately, eight studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final synthesis. A PRISMA flow diagram summarising the selection process is provided in figure 1.

Study characteristics

The eight studies included in this review were published between 2005 and 2019 and conducted across various geographical and healthcare contexts, including high-income and middle-income countries. The geographical distribution of the studies included the USA (n=3), Greece (n=1), China (n=1), Egypt (n=1), Latin America (n=1) and Puerto Rico (n=1). These locations represent urbanised and rural health systems with differing healthcare access and infrastructure levels for managing chronic autoimmune diseases such as SLE.

Study designs encompassed population-based registry analyses (eg, Naleway et al16 and Yazdany et al17) providing epidemiological data on incidence, survival and healthcare utilisation; cross-sectional surveys (eg, Abdul-Sattar et al,18 Xie et al,19 Rodriguez-Rivera et al20 and Gergianaki et al3) evaluating patient-reported outcomes, including disease activity, medication adherence and quality of life; and an ecological analysis21 using aggregated ZIP code-level data to correlate geographic healthcare resource availability with severe SLE outcomes such as ESRD. In addition, a multicentre cohort study by Pons-Estel et al,22 based on the Grupo Latino Americano de Estudio del Lupus (GLADEL) cohort in Latin America, assessed the impact of rural residence on disease manifestations and long-term outcomes.

Sample sizes varied widely, from 80 participants in a single-centre Egyptian study to 31 903 patients in a US hospital readmission database, reflecting marked heterogeneity in scope and analytical power. The GLADEL study enrolled 1480 patients, capturing diverse healthcare contexts across multiple Latin American countries.

Definitions of rurality were operationalised in diverse ways: administrative or geographic classifications based on ZIP code designations or hospital location (eg, Ward21 and Yazdany et al17) aligned with health policy definitions of medically underserved or rural areas; self-reported residence (eg, Xie et al19) relying on participants’ classification of their residential setting; demographic threshold definitions (eg, Gergianaki et al,3 population <10 000); and in the GLADEL study, a combined approach using healthcare centre location and self-reported rural residence (table 1).

Outcome domains evaluated across studies included survival and severe complications, including ESRD (Ward and Naleway et al); disease activity measured by validated indices such as the SLEDAI and the SLAQ (Gergianaki et al, Abdul-Sattar et al and Pons-Estel et al), HRQoL using instruments such as the SF-36 and LupusPRO (Abdul-Sattar et al and Rodriguez-Rivera et al); healthcare utilisation such as 30-day hospital readmission (Yazdany et al) and medication adherence assessed through validated self-report measures (Xie et al).

Risk of bias within studies

The risk of bias across the included studies was evaluated using the NOS, which assesses three core domains: selection of study groups, comparability of groups and ascertainment of outcomes. Overall, the methodological quality of the studies ranged from moderate to high, although notable variability was observed depending on study design and data sources.

Selection bias was minimised in population-based registry studies such as those by Naleway et al16 and Yazdany et al17, which employed large-scale administrative or surveillance data and included well-defined populations. These studies benefited from high external validity and objective outcome measurement (eg, ESRD incidence and hospital readmission), contributing to their robust quality ratings.

In contrast, cross-sectional studies—particularly those relying on convenience sampling or self-reported data—were more prone to selection and measurement biases. For example, Abdul-Sattar et al (2014) and Xie et al19 relied on participant self-reports for rurality classification and outcomes such as disease activity or medication adherence. These approaches increase the risk of information bias due to recall inaccuracies or subjective interpretation.

Confounding control varied across studies. Several studies, such as Gergianaki et al3 and Yazdany et al17 employed multivariate adjustment for significant covariates, including socioeconomic status, education level, comorbid conditions and insurance type. This enhanced the internal validity of their findings. However, inconsistencies in operationalising rurality and healthcare access variables limited direct comparability and may have introduced residual confounding. Cross-sectional studies relying on self-reported data were at moderate risk of bias due to potential recall inaccuracies or subjective classification of rurality.

Furthermore, standardisation of rurality metrics was generally lacking. Some studies used ZIP code or population density proxies, while others relied on self-identification, resulting in potential misclassification bias and hindering meta-analytic pooling. A comprehensive summary of the NOS assessment for each study is provided in online supplemental table 2, highlighting strengths and limitations in study design, comparability and outcome measurement.

Results of individual studies and synthesis

Findings from the eight included studies were synthesised narratively and organised by clinical outcome domain: survival, disease activity, quality of life (QoL), adverse events and medication adherence. Due to substantial heterogeneity in outcome measures, definitions of rurality and healthcare settings, a formal meta-analysis was not feasible. Nevertheless, consistent patterns emerged suggesting a disparity in clinical outcomes among patients with SLE residing in rural or underserved areas.

Survival and severe disease outcomes

Two studies examined long-term survival or disease complications. Ward21, using an ecological design, reported a higher incidence of ESRD in medically underserved ZIP codes in California, indicating a potential association between rural residence and poor renal outcomes. Naleway et al16 found a 10-year survival rate of 76% among a population-based rural cohort in Wisconsin, comparable to urban benchmarks but with a notable incidence of late-onset disease.

Disease activity

Three studies assessed disease activity using validated tools. Gergianaki et al3 identified that rural residents in Crete were diagnosed at a significantly older age and had higher rates of moderate-to-severe disease activity based on physician assessment (adjusted OR 2.17, p=0.011). Abdul-Sattar et al reported significantly elevated scores on the SLAQ among rural Egyptian patients, reflecting greater subjective disease burden. Similarly, Pons-Estel et al22 found that patients from rural areas in the GLADEL cohort had significantly higher SLEDAI scores at diagnosis compared with urban counterparts, indicating more active disease at onset.

Quality of life

Four studies investigated HrQoL using instruments such as the SF-36 and LupusPRO. Abdul-Sattar et al observed significantly lower physical functioning and mental health domains among rural participants, which were associated with perceived neighbourhood disorder. In contrast, Rodriguez-Rivera et al20 found no disadvantage in QoL among medically indigent patients in Puerto Rico, many of whom resided in rural areas but had access to a dedicated lupus clinic. Public insurance patients reported comparable or higher scores in some LupusPRO subdomains than those with private insurance, highlighting the possible mitigating role of specialised care access.

Adverse events and healthcare utilisation

Yazdany et al17 reported a 16.5% 30-day hospital readmission rate across multiple US states, with higher readmission odds among patients from rural hospitals and those covered by public insurance programmes. Gergianaki et al3 additionally found greater prevalence of severe organ manifestations (eg, nephritis and serositis) in rural patients with SLE, although cumulative organ damage was not statistically different. Consistent with these findings, Pons-Estel et al22 reported that rural residence was independently associated with increased risk of organ damage over time, particularly renal involvement, in the Latin American SLE population.

Medication adherence

In a Chinese cohort, Xie et al19 found that rural residence was independently associated with poor medication adherence, with a striking non-adherence rate of 75%. Key contributing factors included low educational attainment, limited understanding of disease and medications and dissatisfaction with treatment.

Synthesis of findings

Overall, rural residence was consistently associated with delayed diagnosis and more severe disease activity, lower physical QoL scores, particularly in the absence of specialised care, higher risk of adverse events, such as hospital readmission or ESRD, and markedly poorer medication adherence. The GLADEL study by Pons-Estel et al further corroborated these trends in a Latin American context, highlighting rurality as a determinant of increased disease activity, organ damage and social vulnerability. Although findings on survival and overall QoL were more variable, the general trend across studies supports a systematic disadvantage for rural patients with SLE. The considerable heterogeneity in how rurality was defined—ranging from ZIP code proxies to self-reported residence and population thresholds—along with differences in health systems, measurement tools and sample demographics, precluded statistical pooling but underscored the need for more standardised and equity-focused SLE research frameworks.

Discussion

Summary of main findings

This systematic review synthesised evidence from eight observational studies examining the impact of rurality on clinical outcomes in adults with SLE. Despite methodological heterogeneity, several consistent themes emerged. Rural residence was associated with delayed diagnosis, increased disease activity, reduced physical aspects of QoL, higher rates of adverse outcomes such as hospital readmission or ESRD and significantly lower medication adherence. Although survival outcomes were variably reported, most studies indicated a pattern of disadvantage among patients living in rural or medically underserved areas. However, this was not universal. For example, Rodríguez-Rivera et al20 found comparable or even improved QoL scores among rural patients with access to a dedicated lupus clinic,20 suggesting that disparities can be mitigated when specialised, comprehensive care is available. This finding underscores the importance of healthcare system structure and access to specialist services as potential modifiers of rural–urban disparities in SLE outcomes.

Comparison with previous literature

These findings are consistent with a broader body of literature in chronic disease populations demonstrating that rurality acts as an independent risk factor for suboptimal healthcare access and adverse outcomes.19 23 24 In conditions such as diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis and chronic kidney disease, rural populations have been shown to experience increased morbidity, delayed diagnosis and lower adherence to evidence-based management protocols.25 26 Geographic isolation, transportation barriers, healthcare workforce shortages and socioeconomic disadvantage often mediate these disparities.

In contrast, the literature addressing SLE and rurality remains sparse and fragmented. Our synthesis contributes to this evolving field by systematically integrating findings across diverse geographical and socioeconomic contexts—including high-, middle- and low-income settings—and highlights the critical role of healthcare system infrastructure in shaping patient outcomes.

Notably, the study by Rodríguez-Rivera et al demonstrated that indigent patients receiving care in a specialised lupus clinic reported comparable or even improved quality of life scores relative to privately insured counterparts.17 This suggests that system-level interventions—such as comprehensive, multidisciplinary care models and equitable access to specialised services—can mitigate, and potentially overcome, the disadvantages associated with rurality or low socioeconomic status.

Multistakeholder collaborations are essential for sustainable improvement in SLE care delivery within rural and underserved settings. Engagement should span patients, healthcare professionals, local community organisations and tertiary referral centres. In particular, tertiary hospitals should take an active leadership role in driving and facilitating these collaborative networks through outreach, capacity building and integration of telemedicine services.27 Strengthening such community–hospital partnerships may prove pivotal in bridging the urban–rural divide in SLE outcomes.

Clinical implications

The observed disparities—while not uniform across all contexts—have important clinical implications for healthcare policy and service delivery. Because structural determinants, rather than rurality itself, often drive these disparities, targeted interventions should address modifiable barriers such as workforce shortages, transportation constraints and affordability of care. Interventions should be targeted not only towards reducing barriers in rural areas but also towards replicating successful care models, such as specialised clinics or integrated multidisciplinary teams, which may offset geographic disadvantage. Geographic location must be formally recognised as a key social determinant of health in SLE management frameworks. In rural and underserved areas, comprehensive interventions are urgently required to address gaps in specialist access, facilitate timely diagnosis, enhance treatment adherence and mitigate the effects of educational and socioeconomic disadvantage.11 28 29

Potential strategies include expanding telemedicine services to deliver rheumatological expertise across geographic barriers, establishing mobile and community-based rheumatology outreach programmes and developing culturally and linguistically tailored patient education initiatives aimed at improving health literacy and empowering self-management.30

Additionally, healthcare systems should consider integrating rurality and social vulnerability metrics into clinical risk stratification tools and electronic health records to identify high-risk patients with SLE systematically.31 This would enable proactive targeting of intensified follow-up, social support services and care navigation interventions.32 Policymakers should also prioritise structural reforms, such as transportation assistance, coverage of ancillary services and equitable reimbursement models, to reduce the urban–rural gap and ensure that all patients with SLE receive equitable, high-quality care regardless of their place of residence.33

This study shows the issue of patients with SLE adherence to treatment and help-seeking behaviours for their symptoms. The delay of their help-seeking behaviours can be caused by the lack of knowledge regarding effective help-seeking behaviours in rural settings.34 35 The following studies should investigate rural patients with SLE’s perceptions of their help-seeking behaviours and how to intervene to improve their medical care usage.36 37

To advance this field, future research should incorporate rurality as a key analytic variable rather than a peripheral descriptor, enabling its independent association with clinical outcomes to be assessed. Importantly, the impact of rurality cannot be adequately captured through simplistic binary classifications (rural vs urban). Adoption of standardised rurality indices—such as the Rural–Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) codes or the Rurality Index for Healthcare Research in Japan—would improve comparability across studies and facilitate robust meta-analytic synthesis.13 38 Moreover, integrating multidimensional approaches, including geospatial mapping and composite indices that reflect healthcare access, deprivation and infrastructure, would allow for more precise identification of high-risk populations and inform the design of tailored interventions.

Limitations

This review has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the included studies employed heterogeneous and often non-standardised definitions of rurality—ranging from ZIP code-level classification to self-reported residence and proxy measures such as insurance status. This variability complicates direct comparisons across studies and may limit the generalisability of findings. Establishing consensus on operational definitions of rurality and healthcare access in SLE research is an important area for future work. Second, there was marked heterogeneity in outcome measures, including varying instruments used to assess quality of life (EuroQol-five dimension and LupusPRO), disease activity (SLEDAI and Systemic Lupus Activity Measure-Revised (SLAM-R)) and medication adherence. Differences in study designs, sample characteristics and healthcare contexts further precluded formal quantitative synthesis (meta-analysis), necessitating a narrative approach. Third, the predominance of cross-sectional study designs limits the ability to draw causal inferences regarding the impact of rurality and socioeconomic status on SLE outcomes. Prospective longitudinal studies are needed to clarify temporal relationships and mechanisms. Fourth, potential publication and language bias cannot be excluded, as the review was restricted to English-language publications and studies indexed in major international databases. It is possible that relevant data from non-English or regional sources, particularly from low- and middle-income countries, may have been missed. Finally, although our synthesis included studies from diverse healthcare systems, representation from certain global regions (eg, sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia) was lacking; future research should address these gaps to ensure a more comprehensive and globally representative understanding of rurality and inequity in SLE care.

Future directions

Future research should move beyond inconsistent and often binary definitions of rurality towards standardised, validated measures that allow robust comparison across studies and facilitate meta-analytic synthesis. Adoption of established indices—such as the RUCA codes or the Rurality Index for Healthcare Research in Japan—should be prioritised so that rurality can be explicitly included as a core variable in SLE research, enabling its independent contribution to risk to be appropriately assessed.13 38 Longitudinal cohort designs are needed to capture disease progression and patterns of healthcare utilisation over time, while intervention trials should evaluate the impact of targeted strategies, such as rural outreach programmes and telehealth-based lupus care, on patient outcomes. Incorporating geospatial mapping, multilevel modelling and multidimensional indices that integrate healthcare access, socioeconomic deprivation and infrastructure measures would allow more precise identification of high-risk populations. Linking these data to national health databases and electronic health records could further guide targeted resource allocation and inform policy responses tailored to specific geographic contexts.

Conclusions

While rural residence is frequently associated with adverse clinical outcomes in patients with SLE—including increased disease activity, poorer quality of life and lower treatment adherence—this pattern is not universal. Evidence from some settings indicates that when specialised, accessible care is available, rural patients can achieve outcomes comparable to their urban counterparts. Addressing rural–urban disparities in SLE care, therefore, requires not only targeted interventions to overcome access barriers but also the broader adoption of successful care delivery models that can mitigate the effects of geographic isolation. Standardised reporting and rurality-aware research frameworks are critical to advancing equitable outcomes in this complex autoimmune disease.
