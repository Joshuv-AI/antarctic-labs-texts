Likely radiation-induced sciatic neuropathy in a dog
Authors: Erica Fox, Cassandra Rodenbaugh, Erin Epperly, Inma Ferrandis, G Diane Shelton, Laura Garrett, Kari Foss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRadiation-induced peripheral neuropathy (RIPN) is a well-known late adverse effect of radiotherapy (RT) in humans, but to date, is not reported in companion animals. An 11-year-old spayed female beagle was presented for evaluation regarding a progressive left sciatic neuropathy. The dog had received stereotactic RT (20 Gy) following excision of a grade 2 soft tissue sarcoma over the proximo-caudal aspect of the left thigh approximately 19 months prior to the onset of signs. Computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging of the left sciatic nerve was consistent with RIPN, and histopathology of the sciatic nerve supported severe nerve fiber loss. No improvement was observed with an anti-inflammatory course of corticosteroids, and the affected limb was subsequently amputated.Keywords: dog, neuroanatomy and neuropathology, neurology, oncology, oncology-treatment, peripheral nervous system disorders

Introduction

Radiation-induced peripheral neuropathy (RIPN) is an uncommon but well-documented late adverse effect of radiation therapy in human patients, characterized by the development of axonal injury and demyelination, extensive fibrosis, and ischemia resulting from injury to local capillary networks, occurring months to years after radiation therapy.1 Risk factors for the development of RIPN include a large total dose (>50-60 Gy),2–4 large doses per fraction (>2.5 Gy),3,5 and a large volume of nerve fibers in the radiation field.6 Diagnosis in humans is based on a combination of compatible signs of neurological disease, electrophysiological findings, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings.1 These findings might mimic neoplastic conditions, primarily peripheral nerve sheath tumors.7,8 Clinically, RIPN manifests as progressive sensory and motor deficits.1 In humans, treatment of late-onset radiation-induced neuropathy is symptomatic.1 No curative treatments have been identified thus far.1

Apart from early experimental reports of peripheral neuropathies after intraoperative radiation therapy (IORT) in dogs,9–12 there is a paucity of veterinary literature examining the effects of modern stereotactic radiation therapy protocols on peripheral nerves.

In this case report, we describe a progressive sciatic neuropathy occurring 19 months after a single 20 Gy dose of stereotactic RT.

Case description

An 11-year-old spayed female beagle was initially presented for evaluation regarding a mass on the proximocaudal aspect of the left pelvic limb. Computed tomography (CT) of the left pelvic limb demonstrated that the mass was limited to the cutaneous/subcutaneous tissues, measuring 2.5 × 2.3 × 1.9 cm. The mass exhibited sharp margins and maintenance of fat attenuation between the mass and semitendinosus and biceps femoris muscles. The mass was greater than 2.5 cm from the sciatic nerve, with the biceps femoris muscle between the mass and nerve (Figure 1). The mass was surgically excised 2 months later at a different veterinary hospital. Pre-operatively, the dog exhibited a normal gait and neurological examination. Histopathology of the mass was consistent with a narrowly excised grade 2 soft tissue sarcoma (French/Kuntz-adapted grading system). No evidence of nerve involvement was described in the histopathology report. Twelve days postoperatively, the dog’s gait and neurological examination was reportedly normal. The dog had external beam radiotherapy to the surgical field to treat microscopic disease 2 months after surgery. For the radiation planning CT, the dog was immobilized and a wire marker delineated the incision scar. Both imaging and treatment were performed with the dog in dorsal recumbency and pre-treatment positioning was verified with a cone-beam CT. Megavoltage radiation was performed using a Varian Trilogy Linear Accelerator with a 120 Millenium multileaf collimator to modulate the plan for Volumetric Modulated Arc Therapy. The treatment was delivered via three 6 MV, coplanar, 270° arcs that spared the quadrant where the spinal cord, colon, and lower urinary tract structures were located. The radiation field administered 20 Gy in a single fraction, with the inverse planning goal of delivering 20 Gy to 95% of the planning target volume (PTV) and a maximum dose of 113%. Heterogeneity correction was not used during radiation therapy planning. There were no contours for gross tumor volume (GTV) or clinical target volume (CTV); instead, the clinical target volume was defined as a PTV with an anisotropic margin from the wire marker. Based on the planning CT and available images from the inverse plan, the PTV was expanded at least 3 cm proximal and distal to the ends of the surgical incision margins, with 5-6 cm lateral margins and a ≥ 2.3 cm depth. The PTV had a total volume of 169.59 cc. Bladder, colon, skin, small intestine, and spinal cord were also contoured as organs at risk and incorporated into the radiation plan with organ-specific dose constraints. The sciatic nerve was not contoured as an organ at risk in the original radiation plan and had no associated spare structures. On an available image of the radiation plan, the left sciatic nerve was within the 20 Gy field (Figure 2). It is possible that the nerve received a higher dose due to proximity to 105% and 110% isodose regions but the maximum dose to the sciatic nerve could not be determined definitively since the original radiation plan was not available for review or additional contouring. Information regarding the treatment immobilization method, organ at risk definitions and dose constraints, and planned PTV margins were not specified in the available records and were unable to be retrospectively confirmed. No acute radiation adverse effects were noted until 42 days after irradiation, at which time the dog had alopecia and hyperpigmentation of the skin.

Approximately 16 months after radiation therapy and 6 weeks before readmission to our hospital, the dog developed left pelvic limb lameness which progressed to knuckling of the digits of the left pelvic limb. Before presenting, conservative management was attempted, comprising of exercise restriction for 6 weeks, carprofen (3.73 mg/kg by mouth once daily for 6 weeks), gabapentin (14.9 mg/kg by mouth every 12 h for 3.5 weeks), and methocarbamol (9.3 mg/kg by mouth every 8 hours for 2 weeks), which resulted in no significant improvement. Neurological examination at the time of readmission was consistent with a left sciatic neuropathy. Specifically, the dog was ambulatory with monoparesis of the left pelvic limb, characterized by strong advancement of the limb at the level of the hip and extension of the stifle but minimal to no flexion of the stifle and frequent knuckling of the digits. The left pelvic limb exhibited an absent placing response, pseudo-hyper-reflexive patellar reflex, and absent withdrawal reflex. Moderate atrophy of the musculature on the proximocaudal aspect of the left pelvic limb was appreciated. Nociception was absent on the lateral aspect of the left pelvic limb (digits III, IV, V). Additionally, marked discomfort was elicited with pressure of the proximocaudal aspect of the left pelvic limb, just cranial and slightly proximal to the surgery site and field of irradiation.

A CT of the left pelvic limb at the time of readmission revealed diffuse thickening of the left sciatic nerve and atrophy of the left pelvic limb musculature (Figure 3). An MRI of the lumbosacral spine, including the lumbosacral plexus and proximal aspect of the pelvic limbs, revealed similar thickening of the left sciatic nerve (4 mm diameter) extending from ischiatic notch to the mid-thigh (Figure 4). Extension into the vertebral canal was not appreciated. The thickened segments were hyperintense on T2W and STIR sequences and contrast enhancing on T1-weighted (T1W) post-contrast sequences (best observed on delayed sequences). The musculature of the pelvic limb was globally decreased with mild hyperintensity on short-tau inversion recovery (STIR) sequences and faint contrast enhancement on T1 post-contrast sequences.

A fascicular biopsy of the proximal aspect of the left sciatic nerve distal to the greater trochanter was performed, which was approximately 3 cm proximal and cranial to the original surgical scar. Grossly, the left sciatic nerve appeared thickened with irregular exterior margins. The nerve sample was fixed in 10% buffered formalin, resin embedded and evaluated in semi-thin (1 μm) sections. Resin sections were stained with toluidine blue basic fuchsin for general changes (Figure 5A) and with paraphenylenediamine (PPD) for myelin (Figure 5B). Severe nerve fiber loss resulting from axonal degeneration and endoneurial fibrosis was observed in the nerve fascicle with axons undergoing various stages of axonal degeneration: from acute axonal degeneration with darkly stained axons (Figure 5A arrow with short tail), a later stage of axonal degeneration with swollen axons and dissolution of organelles (Figure 5A, arrowhead), and the final stage of myelin ovoids (Figure 5A arrow with long tail). Using the PPD stain for myelin, most nerve fibers showed demyelination, myelin splitting, and myelin loss (Figure 5B). The arrows point to small thinly myelinated fibers that are possibly nerve sprouts, indicative of attempted axonal regeneration that is not successful. Neoplastic cells were not observed.

The dog was prescribed a tapering course of prednisone (1.12 mg/kg/day for 7 days, followed by 0.75 mg/kg/day until otherwise directed). No functional improvement was observed after one month of steroid therapy, and the left pelvic limb was subsequently amputated due to persistent discomfort and ongoing abrasions and self-traumatization of the paw. The amputated limb was not made available for histopathological evaluation as the surgery was performed by the referring veterinarian.

Discussion

In this report, we describe a suspected radiation-induced peripheral neuropathy involving the left sciatic nerve of a dog. Although RIPN is a well-known adverse effect of radiation therapy in human patients, to our knowledge, it has not yet been described in dogs following stereotactic radiation therapy protocols.

Despite its efficacy in the treatment of various tumor types, radiotherapy is associated with numerous acute and late adverse effects. The success of radiotherapy in eradicating a tumor depends primarily on the dose of radiation delivered, but the tolerance of normal tissues surrounding the tumor limits the dose. There is considerable variation in radiation tolerance between tissues, with late effects typically occurring in more slowly proliferating tissues, such as the kidney, heart, and central nervous system.13

RIPN is a localized, delayed, and irreversible late radiation adverse effect affecting mature nerve tissue, and is attributed to a combination of microvascular injury, radiation-induced fibrosis, axonal degeneration, and demyelination.14 Early descriptions of RIPN lesions were based on experimental data using single high-dose IORT.9–12 Histopathological evaluation of nerve lesions in these studies revealed increased endoneurial, perineural, and epineurial fibrosis and decreased numbers of axons and myelin.9–12 The histopathology from the left sciatic nerve of the dog in our case report exhibited similar features, including severe nerve fiber loss, axonal degeneration, demyelination, and endoneurial fibrosis. Given the dog’s history of having received a single, large dose of stereotactic RT over the region of the left proximocaudal thigh 19 months prior, a diagnosis of RIPN was strongly suspected. Unfortunately, pathological changes in nerve biopsies often do not reveal what is causative but only reflect axonal degeneration, demyelination, hypomyelination or dysmyelination, and resulting nerve fiber loss. In our case, the pathological changes support severe nerve damage.

Clinical signs associated with RIPN in human patients begin several months to years after RT, including progressive sensory (paresthesia, dysesthesia, hypoesthesia, anesthesia) and motor (weakness, amyotrophy, fasciculations) deficits.1 The dog in our case report exhibited signs compatible with RIPN involving the left sciatic nerve, including loss of sensation over the autonomous zones of the various branches of the sciatic nerve, severe hyperesthesia with pressure over the region of the sciatic nerve just proximal and cranial to the previous surgical site, and loss of motor function of the muscles innervated by the sciatic nerve and its branches.

MRI findings consistent with radiation-induced brachial plexopathy in human patients include marked thickening of the affected nerves which are hyperintense on STIR sequences and exhibit uniform contrast enhancement on T1W post-contrast sequences.15 The dog in our case report exhibited similar findings, including prominent thickening of the left sciatic nerve (observed on CT and MRI) which was hyperintense on STIR and contrast-enhancing on T1W post-contrast sequences.

Treatment of RIPN in human patients is largely symptomatic, including non-opioid analgesics, benzodiazepines, tricyclic antidepressants, and anti-epileptics.1 Vitamins B1-B6 are often advocated for, but clinical data supporting their use is lacking.1 Physical therapy is recommended to maintain function.1 Restriction of stretching or injury to the affected nerve is critical, as sudden neurological deterioration could result.1 Corticosteroids might be beneficial in controlling acute inflammation; however, no efficacy has been observed in the reduction of fibrosis and nerve lesions.16,17 The dog in our report showed no clinical response to anti-inflammatory corticosteroid therapy, and the affected limb was ultimately amputated due to persistent abrasions and self-traumatization of the paw.

In conclusion, we describe the novel recognition of suspected RIPN in a dog after post-excisional stereotactic RT. Although RIPN appears to be rare in dogs, this case highlights the importance of considering total radiation dose and dose per fraction when major peripheral nerves are included in the RT treatment field.
