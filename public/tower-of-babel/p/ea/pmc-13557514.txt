Impact of an antimicrobial stewardship program on antimicrobial resistance patterns, multiple antibiotic resistance indices, clinical outcomes, and cost savings: A pre–post interventional study in the intensive care unit of a tertiary-care hospital in India
Authors: M Bhattacharjee, K Manimekalai, S Bhowmick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) poses a major challenge to patient outcomes, particularly in resource-constrained settings with widespread antimicrobial misuse. Structured antimicrobial stewardship programs (AMSPs) are essential to optimize antimicrobial use and reduce resistance. This pre–post interventional observational study evaluated the impact of AMSP implementation on antimicrobial susceptibility patterns, multiple antibiotic resistance (MAR) indices, stewardship performance indicators, clinical outcomes, and antimicrobial expenditure in the medical and surgical intensive care units of a tertiary-care teaching hospital in India over a 12-month period (pre-AMSP: October 2024–March 2025; post-AMSP: April–September 2025). Standard microbiological methods and Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M100 guidelines were used for organism identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing. Following AMSP implementation, culture positivity decreased from 53.2% to 41.8%, while MAR indices declined by 12–24% across major pathogens. De-escalation rates increased from 49.8% to 77.6% (P = 0.01), guideline adherence improved from 75.2% to 89.1% (P < 0.001), culture turnaround time compliance increased from 68% to 89% (P = 0.01), and days of therapy per 1000 patient-days decreased from 82 to 64 (P < 0.001). Infection-related mortality declined from 5.4% to 3.8% (odds ratio = 0.53, P = 0.011), hospital-acquired infections decreased from 58.2% to 41.8% (relative risk = 0.72, P = 0.018), and antimicrobial expenditure was reduced by 46.4%, corresponding to a monthly saving of ₹9.96 lakh. Implementation of a multidisciplinary AMSP was associated with significant improvements in microbiological, stewardship, clinical, and economic outcomes, supporting the feasibility and effectiveness of structured antimicrobial stewardship programs in tertiary-care hospitals with a high burden of antimicrobial resistance.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has emerged as a critical global health threat, largely driven by inappropriate antimicrobial use, inadequate diagnostic practices, and weak infection prevention measures.[12] The World Health Organization (WHO) and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) have emphasized the urgent need for structured Antimicrobial Stewardship Programs (AMSPs) to optimize antimicrobial prescribing, reduce resistance selection pressure, and improve patient outcomes.

India bears a disproportionately high burden of AMR, with rising prevalence of extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) producers, carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE), and multidrug-resistant non-fermenters.[34] Overuse of broad-spectrum agents such as carbapenems and polymyxins has further worsened local resistance patterns.[5] The Indian Council of Medical Research has repeatedly advocated for institution-specific AMSPs to promote rational antimicrobial use.[6]

Although several studies have assessed the impact of AMSPs on resistance or consumption, integrated evaluations combining susceptibility shifts, multiple antibiotic resistance (MAR) indices, comprehensive clinical outcomes, and detailed cost–benefit analysis remain limited, especially in Indian intensive care unit (ICU) settings. This study addresses this gap by evaluating the effect of a structured AMSP on antimicrobial susceptibility patterns, MAR indices, stewardship indicators, clinical outcomes, and antimicrobial expenditure using standardized WHO and CDC methodologies.

The objective of the present study was to evaluate the impact of AMSP implementation on antimicrobial susceptibility patterns and MAR indices in a tertiary-care hospital setting. The study further assessed key stewardship performance indicators including de-escalation practices, adherence to antimicrobial prescribing guidelines, days of therapy (DOT)/1000 patient-days, and culture turnaround time (TAT) compliance. Additionally, the study evaluated the effect of AMSP implementation on important clinical outcomes such as infection-related mortality, healthcare-associated infections, length of hospital stay, 30-day readmission rates, and adverse drug events. A cost–benefit analysis was also performed to assess changes in antimicrobial expenditure and potential institutional savings associated with reduced antimicrobial utilization and healthcare-associated infections following stewardship implementation.

Materials and Methods

This pre–post interventional observational study was conducted in the medical and surgical ICUs of a 1,200-bed tertiary-care teaching hospital in India. The study spanned 12 months, divided into a pre-AMSP phase (October 2024–March 2025) and a post-AMSP phase (April–September 2025). Formal implementation of a multidisciplinary AMSP began in April 2025, in accordance with WHO and CDC core elements of hospital antimicrobial stewardship programs.

Study population

Inpatients aged 18–70 years admitted to the ICU and receiving high-end antimicrobials (meropenem, imipenem, colistin, vancomycin, ceftazidime-avibactam, or polymyxin B) were included. High-end agents were specifically targeted because they exert the highest selection pressure for resistance and account for the majority of antimicrobial expenditure in Indian ICUs. Patients with only low-grade antibiotic exposure, community-acquired infections managed outside the ICU, outpatients, repeat isolates from the same patient within 14 days, and culture contaminants were excluded. “Other” clinical specimens included pus, wound swabs, ascitic fluid, pleural fluid, and cerebrospinal fluid.

Demographic and clinical characteristics (age, sex, comorbidities, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, site of infection, and mechanical ventilation status) were recorded to assess comparability between phases.

AMSP interventions

The AMSP team comprised an infectious diseases physician, clinical pharmacist, clinical microbiologist, and ICU representatives. Key interventions included prospective audit and feedback, prior authorization for restricted antimicrobials, diagnostic stewardship (promotion of appropriate cultures before antibiotic initiation), de-escalation protocols, intravenous-to-oral switch, and weekly multidisciplinary rounds with real-time feedback.

Microbiological methods

Clinical specimens were processed using standard microbiological techniques as described by Cheesbrough.[7] Organism identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) were performed according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M100 (2024) guidelines using automated systems (VITEK 2) and confirmed by disk diffusion where necessary. The MAR index was calculated using the Krumperman formula: number of antibiotics to which the isolate is resistant divided by the total number of antibiotics tested.[8]

Economic analysis

Direct acquisition costs of high-end antimicrobials (carbapenems, colistin, vancomycin) were obtained from hospital pharmacy purchase and dispensing records. Mean monthly expenditure was compared between phases. Cost savings from prevented hospital-acquired infections (HAIs) were estimated using the average institutional cost per HAI episode (derived from finance department data that included extended length of stay and additional treatment costs). Cost avoided per DOT and per HAI prevented were also calculated.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were compared using independent t-test or Mann-Whitney U test. Statistical significance was assessed using P values, and effect sizes were reported using Cohen’s d. Cohen’s d values were interpreted as small (0.2–0.49), moderate (0.5–0.79), and large (≥0.8) effect sizes. Odds ratios (ORs) and relative risks (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated where appropriate. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and Python version 3.12 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA) with the Matplotlib library for radar chart generation. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legally authorized representatives.

Results

A total of 2,521 clinical specimens were processed during the study period, of which 2,249 clinically significant isolates were analyzed during the study period. Demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable between the pre- and post-AMSP phases.

Microbiological findings

Culture positivity rate decreased from 53.2% in the pre-AMSP phase to 41.8% in the post-AMSP phase. Specimen-wise pathogen distribution is shown in Figure 1. Blood cultures were dominated by Escherichia coli, Klebsiella spp., Staphylococcus aureus, coagulase-negative Staphylococci (CoNS), and Acinetobacter baumannii. Respiratory specimens showed a high proportion of A. baumannii. Urinary isolates were predominantly E. coli and Klebsiella spp. Other specimens [pus, wound swabs, ascitic fluid, pleural fluid, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)] displayed heterogeneous profiles.

Stewardship process indicators

De-escalation rates increased from 49.8% to 77.6% (P = 0.01). Guideline adherence improved from 75.2% to 89.1% (P < 0.001). Culture TAT compliance rose from 68% to 89% (P = 0.01). DOT/1000 patient-days decreased from 82 to 64 (P < 0.001). All other stewardship metrics also showed improvement [Table 3].

Clinical outcomes

Infection-related mortality decreased from 5.4% to 3.8% (OR = 0.53, P = 0.011). Thirty-day readmission rates declined from 18.2% to 13.9% (OR = 0.74, P = 0.022). Discharge rates improved from 76.8% to 93% (P = 0.007). HAI rates fell from 58.2% to 41.8% (RR = 0.72, P = 0.018). Adverse drug events decreased from 4.1% to 2.2% (P = 0.047). Mean length of stay reduced from 7.1 to 6.7 days. These outcomes are summarized in Figure 3 and Table 3.

Economic outcomes

Mean monthly antimicrobial expenditure on high-end agents (carbapenems, colistin, vancomycin) decreased from ₹21.47 lakh to ₹11.51 lakh, representing a 46.4% reduction (P = 0.008). An estimated 70 HAIs were prevented, contributing additional cost savings of approximately ₹59.77 lakh (₹0.85 lakh per HAI prevented).

Discussion

This prospective pre–post interventional study demonstrates that implementation of a structured multidisciplinary AMSP was associated with improvements in antimicrobial utilization, resistance indicators, patient outcomes, and healthcare expenditure in a tertiary-care ICU. These findings support the growing global evidence that stewardship interventions are an essential component of antimicrobial resistance containment strategies and can produce measurable benefits even in resource-constrained healthcare settings.[126]

A distinctive feature of the present study is its multidimensional evaluation of AMSP effectiveness. Unlike many stewardship studies that primarily assess antimicrobial consumption or resistance trends, this investigation simultaneously examined antimicrobial susceptibility patterns, MAR indices, stewardship performance indicators, clinical outcomes, and economic impact. The inclusion of MAR index assessment provides an ecological measure of resistance pressure that is infrequently incorporated into AMSP evaluations in Indian healthcare settings.[8] By integrating microbiological, operational, clinical, and economic outcomes within a single framework, the study offers a comprehensive assessment of stewardship effectiveness that may serve as a practical model for similar institutions.

The reduction in culture positivity observed following AMSP implementation may reflect improved diagnostic stewardship and more rational antimicrobial prescribing. Excessive empirical antibiotic use is known to influence microbiological yield and contribute to selective pressure that favors resistant organisms. Similar improvements have been reported in stewardship programs where enhanced culture-directed therapy and optimization of antimicrobial use improved microbiological surveillance and reduced inappropriate treatment.[910] The predominance of E. coli, Klebsiella spp., and Acinetobacter baumannii among ICU isolates is consistent with findings from Indian AMR surveillance studies, which continue to identify these organisms as major contributors to healthcare-associated infections and antimicrobial resistance burden.[34]

One of the most significant findings of this study was the reduction in MAR indices across major bacterial pathogens. MAR index has been widely recognized as a useful epidemiological tool for assessing antimicrobial exposure and identifying environments with high antibiotic selection pressure.[8] The observed decline suggests that stewardship interventions successfully reduced inappropriate antimicrobial use and limited opportunities for resistance selection. Similar findings have been reported by Ramasamy and Curfs, who demonstrated that stewardship activities can contribute to favorable changes in institutional resistance patterns over time.[11] Although susceptibility to key antimicrobials improved during the post-intervention phase, A. baumannii continued to exhibit substantial multidrug resistance. This observation is consistent with previous reports highlighting the organism’s exceptional ability to acquire resistance determinants, persist within healthcare environments, and withstand antimicrobial pressure despite stewardship efforts.[412] The persistence of resistance in this pathogen emphasizes the need for continued integration of stewardship initiatives with robust infection prevention and control measures.

The improvements in stewardship process measures observed in this study likely represent important mechanisms through which the AMSP achieved its clinical and microbiological benefits. Enhanced adherence to treatment guidelines and greater acceptance of de-escalation practices indicate improved alignment between prescribing behavior and microbiological evidence. Dellit et al.[9] demonstrated that prospective audit and feedback remain among the most effective stewardship interventions for improving antimicrobial prescribing quality and reducing unnecessary broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure. Similarly, Karanika et al.[6] reported that multidisciplinary stewardship programs consistently improve compliance with evidence-based prescribing practices while optimizing antimicrobial utilization. Improved culture turnaround time performance may also have facilitated earlier transition from empirical to targeted therapy, thereby reducing unnecessary exposure to high-end antimicrobials and supporting more individualized patient management.

The favorable clinical outcomes associated with AMSP implementation are biologically plausible and consistent with findings reported in international literature. Appropriate antimicrobial selection and timely optimization of therapy are known to improve infection management while minimizing treatment-related complications. The observed reduction in infection-related mortality, healthcare-associated infections, readmissions, and adverse drug events may therefore reflect the combined effects of improved antimicrobial appropriateness, enhanced diagnostic support, and reduced exposure to unnecessary broad-spectrum agents. Baur et al.,[10] in their systematic review and meta-analysis, demonstrated that stewardship interventions are associated with reductions in antimicrobial resistance, resistant infections, and overall infection burden. Likewise, the Cochrane review by Davey et al.[13] concluded that stewardship interventions improve prescribing practices without compromising patient safety and may contribute to improved clinical outcomes. The reduction in healthcare-associated infections observed in the present study is particularly important because such infections contribute substantially to morbidity, mortality, prolonged hospitalization, and healthcare expenditure.

The economic benefits observed following AMSP implementation further strengthen the case for stewardship adoption in low- and middle-income countries. Hospitals in such settings frequently face competing resource demands, making cost-effective interventions particularly valuable. The reduction in expenditure on high-end antimicrobials suggests that stewardship programs can achieve substantial financial savings by optimizing antimicrobial prescribing. Additional savings generated by preventing healthcare-associated infections further underscore the broader institutional benefits of stewardship initiatives. Similar economic advantages have been reported by Malani et al. and Dik et al., who demonstrated that stewardship programs can generate significant returns on investment while maintaining or improving patient outcomes.[1415] These findings indicate that AMSP should be viewed not only as a clinical quality initiative but also as an important healthcare management strategy.

To our knowledge, few Indian ICU-based AMSP studies have simultaneously evaluated antimicrobial susceptibility patterns, MAR indices, stewardship performance indicators, patient-centered clinical outcomes, and economic consequences within a single intervention framework. The comprehensive nature of the present evaluation provides evidence that stewardship programs influence multiple interconnected dimensions of healthcare delivery. Such an approach may offer a more realistic assessment of stewardship impact than reliance on isolated indicators such as antimicrobial consumption alone.

The study has certain limitations. As a single-center investigation, the findings may not be universally generalizable to all healthcare settings. The pre–post design is susceptible to temporal influences and residual confounding despite comparable baseline characteristics between study phases. Furthermore, the economic analysis primarily focused on direct antimicrobial expenditure and may underestimate the broader financial benefits associated with reductions in morbidity, length of stay, and productivity losses. Nevertheless, the study provides robust real-world evidence supporting AMSP implementation in tertiary-care ICUs with a high burden of antimicrobial resistance. Residual confounding due to unmeasured variables cannot be entirely ruled out despite comparable baseline characteristics. Pathogen-specific analysis of readmissions among resistant infections could not be performed due to sample size limitations.

Barriers encountered during implementation included initial clinician resistance to restriction policies, limited dedicated time for pharmacists, and the need for repeated educational sessions. Sustainability was achieved through institutional policy integration, regular feedback reports, and inclusion of AMSP metrics in ICU quality indicators. The findings are likely generalizable to other tertiary-care ICUs in India and similar low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) settings with high AMR burden.[16]

In conclusion, the implementation of a structured multidisciplinary AMSP was associated with improvements in antimicrobial stewardship practices, reductions in resistance selection pressure, enhanced patient outcomes, and substantial healthcare cost savings. The study further demonstrates the value of incorporating MAR index analysis alongside microbiological, clinical, and economic indicators when evaluating stewardship effectiveness. These findings support wider adoption of comprehensive AMSP frameworks across tertiary-care hospitals in India and other resource-limited settings facing an increasing burden of antimicrobial resistance.
