Diffuse Alveolar Hemorrhage Following Trauma: A Diagnostic Dilemma With Rapid Steroid Response
Authors: Sharadruthi Akula, Swetha Pasupuleti, Arun VS, Anuja Anil, Mokshita Agarwal, Pradip Potdar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diffuse alveolar hemorrhage (DAH) is a rare but potentially life-threatening pulmonary condition that, while classically linked to autoimmune disorders, can occur in the setting of trauma. We report the case of a 20-year-old male who sustained polytrauma following a road traffic accident and initially had a normal chest radiograph. On the fourth day of hospitalization, he developed acute hypoxemia with new bilateral alveolar infiltrates; high-resolution computed tomography revealed diffuse ground-glass opacities with septal thickening. Bronchoalveolar lavage demonstrated progressively hemorrhagic return across aliquots, confirming DAH. Autoimmune markers were largely negative, and in the absence of systemic features, a multifactorial etiology involving trauma-related injury and anticoagulant use was considered. High-dose intravenous corticosteroids produced rapid clinical and radiological recovery. This case underscores the importance of considering DAH in trauma patients presenting with unexplained hypoxemia and bilateral infiltrates, even without hemoptysis, where early bronchoscopy and prompt treatment can be lifesaving.

Introduction

Diffuse alveolar hemorrhage (DAH) is a life-threatening pulmonary syndrome arising from disruption of the pulmonary microcirculation encompassing alveolar capillaries, arterioles, and venules, resulting in intra-alveolar accumulation of red blood cells [1]. The classical clinical triad consists of hemoptysis, new bilateral alveolar infiltrates on chest imaging, and a decline in hemoglobin levels; however, hemoptysis may be absent in up to one-third of patients, rendering early recognition particularly challenging [1,2]. Importantly, an unexplained fall in hemoglobin accompanied by acute hypoxemic respiratory failure and diffuse bilateral infiltrates should always prompt clinical suspicion for DAH, even in the absence of frank hemoptysis [2,3]. Diagnosis is confirmed bronchoscopically by demonstrating progressively hemorrhagic return across sequential bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) aliquots from the same location, the gold-standard approach, which also permits concurrent exclusion of infectious etiologies [1,2].

The pathological substrates underlying DAH are categorized into three major histological patterns: pulmonary capillaritis (the most common, characterized by neutrophilic infiltration of alveolar septa leading to fibrinoid necrosis), bland alveolar hemorrhage (without inflammation or necrosis), and diffuse alveolar damage [4]. Clinically, DAH can be grouped into four broad etiological categories: immune-associated causes (including anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)-associated vasculitis, anti-glomerular basement membrane (anti-GBM) disease, and connective tissue disorders such as systemic lupus erythematosus); congestive heart failure-associated causes; miscellaneous causes (including infection, trauma, coagulopathy, drug-induced lung injury, and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation); and idiopathic DAH [4,5]. Over 100 distinct conditions have been reported to cause DAH, underscoring the breadth of its differential diagnosis [5]. Overall in-hospital mortality from DAH ranges broadly from 7% to over 36% depending on the underlying etiology, with the lowest mortality observed in idiopathic DAH and the highest in congestive heart failure-related cases [5].

Trauma-associated DAH is a particularly rare and underrecognized entity. Concurrent fat embolism syndrome (FES) and DAH have been documented following long bone fractures and orthopedic procedures, which represent likely inciting sources of acute lung injury through mechanical and biochemical microvascular injury [6,7]. FES is most frequently associated with closed long bone fractures of the lower extremities, particularly the femur, with the highest incidence in patients aged 10-40 years [7]. Its manifestations typically develop within 24-72 hours of injury through mechanical occlusion and subsequent hydrolysis of fat into free fatty acids, provoking a pulmonary inflammatory cascade [7]. Beyond FES, severe polytrauma itself triggers a systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) via release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which activate neutrophils, complement, and pro-inflammatory cytokines, culminating in endothelial dysfunction and increased microvascular permeability that can extend to the pulmonary circulation [8]. Anticoagulation with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH), routinely administered for venous thromboembolism prophylaxis in orthopedic trauma patients, represents an additional potential contributor, as it may unmask or exacerbate alveolar bleeding in the setting of pre-existing microvascular injury [1,2]. Distinguishing trauma-associated DAH from other post-traumatic pulmonary complications, including FES, aspiration pneumonitis, and anticoagulant-related hemorrhage, is clinically essential, as their management strategies differ significantly.

Early initiation of corticosteroid therapy is a cornerstone of treatment in immune-mediated DAH and has been shown to be associated with improved outcomes when not delayed pending serological results [9,10]. Pulse-dose methylprednisolone (500-1000 mg/day for three days) is widely employed in severe presentations, including those with active respiratory failure [10]. However, steroid responsiveness alone is not pathognomonic for immune-mediated DAH, and clinical context must guide interpretation [5,9].

We report a case of a young male who developed DAH following polytrauma sustained in a road traffic accident (RTA), with a diagnostic dilemma between trauma-related inflammatory injury, anticoagulant-associated hemorrhage, and a possible immune-mediated component, with a dramatic clinical and radiological response to pulse corticosteroid therapy.

Case presentation

Patient demographics and presenting complaint

A 20-year-old male student with no prior comorbidities, addictions, or known drug allergies presented to the casualty department following an RTA, in which he was riding a two-wheeler and was struck by a bus. He sustained injuries to the right upper limb, left lower limb, and scrotum. At presentation, he denied any chest pain, dyspnea, or hemoptysis.

Initial vital signs and examination

On arrival, the patient was conscious and oriented with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 15/15. He was afebrile, with a heart rate of 98 beats per minute, blood pressure of 110/70 mmHg, respiratory rate of 20 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation of 96% on room air. Cardiovascular examination revealed normal S1 and S2 heart sounds without murmurs. Respiratory auscultation demonstrated bilateral air entry with no added sounds. Abdominal examination revealed tenderness in the right inguinal region. No clinical evidence of chest wall trauma was identified.

Head-to-toe survey revealed a contused lacerated wound over the right eyebrow, right inguinal swelling, right wrist swelling with deformity, and left thigh swelling with deformity.

Operative management (day 0)

The patient underwent orchidopexy by the Surgery department on day 0 for a right testis ascended in the right inguinal canal. He was subsequently admitted to the Orthopedics ward for definitive management of the following:

-Left distal shaft of femur fracture

-Right distal radius and distal ulna fractures.

Pharmacological management initiated included IV meropenem, IV clarithromycin, enoxaparin 0.4 mg subcutaneous, and analgesics.

Acute respiratory deterioration (day 4)

On post-trauma day 4, the patient developed sudden-onset dyspnea, high-grade fever, and a dry cough, with oxygen desaturation to SpO₂ 84% on room air, prompting urgent re-assessment.

Repeat chest radiograph and arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis were obtained. The chest radiograph demonstrated new bilateral alveolar infiltrates consistent with alveolar opacification (Figure 2). ABG analysis confirmed acute hypoxemia with a pH of 7.50, pCO₂ of 32 mmHg, pO₂ of 47 mmHg, and HCO₃ of 25 mmol/L (Table 2). Electrocardiography revealed sinus tachycardia, and computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) ruled out pulmonary embolism. High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) of the chest demonstrated diffuse ground-glass opacities with interlobular septal thickening in a crazy-paving pattern.

At referral to Respiratory Medicine (day 5)

On post-trauma day 5, the patient developed frank hemoptysis with worsening oxygen requirements. The PaO₂/FiO₂ (PF) ratio was 223, consistent with mild acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) by Berlin criteria. Clinical examination revealed bilateral coarse crackles on auscultation. Chest imaging confirmed diffuse bilateral pulmonary infiltrates. There were no clinical features suggestive of deep vein thrombosis (DVT).

Differential diagnosis

At this stage, the differential diagnosis included ARDS secondary to aspiration or trauma, FES, anticoagulant-associated pulmonary hemorrhage, DAH, and infectious etiologies including pneumonia. The presence of a crazy-paving pattern on HRCT further broadened the differential to include acute interstitial pneumonia, cardiogenic pulmonary edema, Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, and viral pneumonia.

Bronchoscopy (day 6)

Flexible bronchoscopy revealed blood clots at the carina and in both main bronchi. On navigation through the right main bronchus and intermediate bronchus, active bleeding was noted in all segmental bronchi. BAL was performed using 60 mL of normal saline instilled in three sequential aliquots in the right middle lobe; the return fluid remained uniformly hemorrhagic without clearing across aliquots, a finding pathognomonic of DAH (Figure 3). Specimens were sent for cytological and microbiological analysis. BAL cytology demonstrated a predominance of red blood cells with few foamy histiocytes and an absence of epithelial cells, supporting the diagnosis of alveolar hemorrhage.

Immunological workup

Autoimmune evaluation revealed a weakly positive antinuclear antibody (ANA) with a speckled pattern at a titer of 1:100, without clinical correlation. Both cytoplasmic ANCA (C-ANCA) and perinuclear ANCA (P-ANCA) were negative. Anti-GBM antibody testing was not performed due to resource constraints. Serum complement levels were not obtained.

The following investigations were not obtained due to patient stability considerations and resource constraints: two-dimensional echocardiography, D-dimer, lower limb DVT scan, serum CD4/CD8 counts, antiphospholipid antibody profile, anti-GBM antibody, serum complement, and specific extractable nuclear antigen (ENA) panel.

Treatment and response

Following confirmation of DAH, enoxaparin was immediately discontinued. Pulse methylprednisolone 1.5 g intravenously once daily was administered for three consecutive days, followed by a transition to oral prednisolone 60 mg once daily with planned tapering. This dose was selected based on the severity of respiratory failure and institutional clinical judgment, exceeding the standard 500-1000 mg/day range typically cited for pulse-dose therapy.

The clinical response was striking. By day 3 of pulse therapy, the PF ratio improved from 223 to 450, indicating normalization of oxygenation. Serial chest radiographs demonstrated progressive bilateral clearing of alveolar infiltrates (Figure 4). A comparison of chest imaging at the onset of respiratory deterioration and at follow-up on day 20 demonstrated near-complete radiological resolution (Figure 5). The patient was discharged in a stable condition with tapering oral corticosteroids and instructions for close outpatient follow-up. Complete radiological resolution was confirmed on final imaging.

Discussion

DAH represents a clinical syndrome rather than a single disease entity, and its etiology demands systematic evaluation to identify potentially treatable underlying causes [1,2]. In the present case, a young polytrauma patient developed DAH in the absence of classic autoimmune features, presenting a genuine diagnostic challenge between trauma-related inflammatory injury, anticoagulant-associated hemorrhage, and a possible immune-mediated component.

The reported incidence of DAH in the general population remains low, and trauma-associated DAH is particularly rare and underreported in the literature [3,4]. De Prost et al., in a retrospective study of 54 immunocompetent patients with DAH, identified diverse etiologies including idiopathic, drug-induced, and coagulation-related causes, with an overall in-hospital mortality of approximately 25% [5]. Their findings highlight that DAH in immunocompetent individuals without systemic autoimmune disease carries a distinct clinical profile and prognosis compared to immune-mediated DAH, as observed in our patient.

FES was a primary consideration in the differential diagnosis, given our patient's long bone fractures. FES is a well-recognized complication following closed long bone fractures, particularly of the femur, with the highest incidence in patients aged 10-40 years [6]. It classically presents within 12-72 hours of injury with the triad of respiratory distress, neurological impairment, and petechial rash [6]. Pulmonary involvement in FES can occasionally manifest as alveolar hemorrhage through mechanical occlusion of pulmonary capillaries by fat globules, followed by hydrolysis into free fatty acids that provoke a local inflammatory cascade [6,7]. However, in our patient, several features argued against FES as the sole etiology: symptom onset occurred on post-trauma day 4, beyond the typical window; neurological features and petechiae were absent; and no fat globules were identified on BAL analysis. These findings are consistent with published diagnostic criteria for FES, wherein the absence of the classic triad substantially reduces its probability [6].

Anticoagulant-associated alveolar hemorrhage represented another plausible contributor. The patient was receiving enoxaparin for venous thromboembolism prophylaxis in the perioperative orthopedic setting -- a clinically appropriate but potentially risk-amplifying intervention in the context of concurrent microvascular pulmonary injury [2,7]. LMWH has been reported to exacerbate or unmask alveolar bleeding by impairing hemostatic plugging at sites of disrupted pulmonary microcirculation [2]. The prompt discontinuation of enoxaparin following confirmation of DAH was therefore both appropriate and potentially contributory to the subsequent clinical improvement observed.

An immune-mediated cause was considered given the weakly positive ANA with a speckled pattern at a titer of 1:100. However, weakly positive ANA without clinical correlation is a non-specific finding, present in up to 15-20% of healthy individuals and in various non-autoimmune conditions including acute illness and trauma [8]. Both C-ANCA and P-ANCA were negative, making ANCA-associated vasculitis, specifically granulomatosis with polyangiitis and microscopic polyangiitis, unlikely [9]. Anti-GBM antibody testing was not performed, representing a limitation of this case; however, the absence of renal involvement, including normal urinalysis, preserved renal function, and no hematuria, substantially reduces the likelihood of Goodpasture syndrome [9]. The absence of systemic features such as rash, arthralgia, serositis, or lymphadenopathy further reduced the probability of a primary connective tissue disorder. Jennings et al. described a subset of DAH cases with underlying isolated pauci-immune pulmonary capillaritis in the absence of systemic vasculitis, which may represent a forme fruste of immune-mediated injury and could account for the steroid responsiveness observed in seronegative cases such as ours [10].

The pathophysiological mechanism most consistent with this presentation is a multifactorial one: trauma-induced SIRS, mediated by the release of DAMPs, activates neutrophils and complement, leading to pulmonary endothelial dysfunction and increased microvascular permeability [7,11]. This trauma-induced inflammatory state likely created the substrate for alveolar capillary disruption, which was further compounded by concurrent anticoagulation. A possible subclinical immune-mediated component, suggested by the weakly positive ANA and the dramatic steroid response, cannot be excluded.

The rapid and complete clinical and radiological response to pulse corticosteroid therapy is a notable feature of this case. The PF ratio normalized from 223 to 450 within three days of initiating methylprednisolone 1.5 g/day, with near-complete radiological resolution by day 20. This response pattern is consistent with published reports of steroid-responsive DAH in both immune-mediated and non-immune contexts [4,5]. Park emphasizes that early corticosteroid administration is a cornerstone of DAH management regardless of the underlying etiology, as it suppresses the alveolar inflammatory cascade and facilitates resolution of hemorrhage [4]. However, as noted by De Prost et al., steroid responsiveness alone is not pathognomonic for autoimmune DAH and must be interpreted within the broader clinical context [5].

This case also underscores several important diagnostic principles. First, hemoptysis may be absent or delayed in a significant proportion of DAH cases; in our patient, frank hemoptysis did not appear until day 5, two days after the onset of hypoxemia and new infiltrates [1,2]. Clinicians should therefore maintain a high index of suspicion for DAH in trauma patients presenting with unexplained hypoxemia, falling hemoglobin, and bilateral infiltrates, even in the absence of hemoptysis. Second, early flexible bronchoscopy with sequential BAL is indispensable; it not only confirms the diagnosis but simultaneously excludes infectious etiologies and guides the initiation of appropriate therapy without delay [1,2]. Third, the absence of a definitive etiological label should not defer treatment, as the window for effective intervention is narrow and the consequences of delayed therapy are severe [4,5].

Conclusions

DAH should be considered in trauma patients presenting with acute hypoxemia, progressive decline in hemoglobin, and new bilateral infiltrates, even in the absence of classic autoimmune features or hemoptysis. This case illustrates that a multifactorial etiology, including trauma-related inflammatory injury, concurrent anticoagulation, and a possible immune-mediated component, may synergistically precipitate DAH. Early bronchoscopy with BAL is essential for diagnosis, and prompt corticosteroid therapy can result in rapid and complete clinical and radiological recovery. Further prospective studies are needed to better characterize trauma-associated DAH.
