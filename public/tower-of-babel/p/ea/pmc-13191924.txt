Mycobacterium leprae small heat shock protein-18 complements Lon protease in Escherichia coli
Authors: Elengikal A Rehna, Kuppamuthu Dharmalingam, Sankar Natesan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn Mycobacterium leprae (M. leprae), the small heat shock protein Hsp18 has been reported to function as a chaperone in vitro. The stability and activity of Hsp18 protein was increased by stress induced post-translational modification. The significant identity and similarity with a conserved sequence GVDP and proteolytic active site between M. leprae Hsp18 and E. coli and M. smegmatis Lon proteases reveals that, Hsp18 will have additional role in vivo that remains unexplored. In this study, we characterized the protease activity of M. leprae Hsp18 by complementing the Lon protease of Escherichia coli (E. coli). The MG1655Δlon mutant of E. coli, which lack the Lon protease overexpress capsular polysaccharide and form mucoid colonies. Similarly, E. coli SG20780 is the cpsB10::lac fusion that is lac+ and expresses lacZ, and both the strains are sensitive to methyl methane sulphonate (MMS) induced DNA damage. Upon induced expression of M. leprae Hsp18 in MG1655Δlon strain the mucoid phenotype was reversed by downregulating rcsA and in SG20780 mutant, lacZ expression was inhibited. In these E. coli mutants M. leprae Hsp18 expression conferred resistance to MMS in a concentration-dependent manner. In patients with leprosy, the polymorphic variants Hsp18S and Hsp18P have been identified, where the codon 52 encodes serine and proline respectively. Among these variants, Hsp18S showed higher protease activity and MMS resistance than the Hsp18P variant. Induced expression of N- and C-terminal domains of M. leprae Hsp18 in MG1655Δlon and SG20780, restored the Δlon phenotype suggesting that both the terminals act in synergy for the proteolytic activity. Thus, our study demonstrates that M. leprae Hsp18 is a protease which has similar function as that of Lon protease in E. coli. The chaperone activity and the proteolytic activity of Hsp18 is pivotal for the survival of M. leprae in vivo within the macrophages as chaperone by refolding the misfolded proteins and proteolytic cleavage of misfolded and damaged proteins during pathogenesis.Keywords: Mycobacterium leprae, Hsp, RcsA, Lon, E. coli, Mycobacterium smegmatis

Introduction

Mycobacterium leprae (M. leprae), the causative agent of leprosy or “Hansen’s disease,” primarily affects the superficial peripheral nerves, skin, nasopharyngeal tract, nasal mucosa and eyes. Based on the immunity elicited by the infectious M. leprae, the leprosy is classified into lepromatous, tuberculoid and borderline leprosy [37]. However, the etiology is not well defined due to the inability of M. leprae to grow in vitro and studying the structural and functional aspects of the M. leprae genes and proteins poses significant challenges. In vivo studies have indicated morphological similarities between M. leprae and other mycobacteria, which can be grown in vitro [8, 46]. Among them, the non-pathogenic strain M. smegmatis mc2155 serves as a valuable tool for functional characterization of M. leprae genes in vitro. Similarly, M. leprae genes are often expressed in E. coli as an alternative system for functional genomic screening [4, 24]. It has been demonstrated that various M. leprae genes, when expressed in E. coli remain functional, offering insights into the biology of these pathogenic bacteria [3, 4]. For example, the DXS-I gene from M. tuberculosis remains functional when expressed in E. coli [3]. Homologous genes involved in biosynthetic pathways in E. coli [10, 43] are also present in M. tuberculosis and M. leprae.

The M. leprae Hsp18, a 148 amino acid antigenic protein belongs to an alpha-crystallin family of proteins and homologous to the small heat shock proteins [5, 33]. The M. leprae Hsp18 when expressed in E. coli localized to the outer membrane and exhibits chaperone-like activity by folding the thermally denatured restriction enzymes into their active/native states [26]. The expression, temperature-dependent regulation, and chaperone function of M. leprae Hsp18 have been demonstrated in vitro in E. coli [26, 32] and in vivo in macrophages [27]. Similar functionally related counterparts have been identified in M. tuberculosis and other mycobacteria [6], whereas in M. ulcerans, an Hsp18-like protein contributes to biofilm formation [34]. In M. leprae, the Hsp18 is the only small heat shock protein expressed and this could be due to the reduction in the genome size of this organism [15]. We recently demonstrated the environmental stress specific transcriptional regulation of the hsp18 gene, wherein the hsp18 gene promoter activity was significantly increased in M. smegmatis when grown under hypoxic conditions [17]. Moreover, M. leprae Hsp18 is reportedly stabilized by auto-phosphorylation, promoting the multiplication of M. smegmatis during stress conditions [27]. These studies clearly demonstrated that the M. leprae Hsp18 is regulated both at transcriptional and translational level and contribute for the bacterial survival under adverse environmental growth conditions inside the macrophages. Moreover, these studies suggest that the Hsp18 might have other functions in addition to the chaperone activity. However, the precise role of the M. leprae Hsp18 protein in pathogenesis and the underlying molecular mechanism remain enigmatic.

The Lon proteases within the HtrA family functions as an ATP-dependent protease and chaperone [14]. Lon’s protease activity has been identified in various organisms, like Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria [12, 13] yeast, plants [20], and humans. The E. coli Lon protease (EcLon) first identified by [18] is an ATP-dependent protease [42], comprising of four identical subunits. Each subunit contains three functional domains in the N-terminal region [2, 38] however, the specific functions remain largely unknown. EcLon possesses a centrally located ATPase domain and a proteolytic C-terminal domain, including a catalytically active serine residue S679 [1, 39]. EcLon participates in degrading abnormal proteins [19] and short-lived regulatory proteins like RcsA [22, 44] and SulA, the natural substrates of Lon protease in vivo [31]. Substrate recognition by EcLon or any ATP-dependent protease [45] remains incompletely understood, with protease activity being stimulated by cellular stress. While Lon protease is nonessential for cell viability, lon mutants in some bacterial species manifest cellular defects, such as the formation of mucoid colonies [22, 44] and filaments [40] by RcsA and SulA stabilization respectively during DNA damage. In M. smegmatis, Lon is an 84 kDa protein with three domains: an N-terminal domain required for substrate engagement, a central AAA+ domain, and a C-terminal S16 peptidase domain. While Lon homologs have been identified in M. avium and M. intracellulare, they have not been detected in M. leprae [25] suggesting that Lon is broadly conserved across various organisms but is absent in M. leprae. The M. leprae Hsp65 has been shown to have proteolytic activity similar to E. coli ClpY protease, a secondary protease active at higher temperature and target the Lon protease substrates [35]. Since Lon is absent in M. leprae, we hypothesized that, the M. leprae Hsp18 protein might act as a protease with the dual function as a cheparone and protease if complemented in E. coli and target the Lon protease substrates for degradation and contribute to the survival of the bacteria under adverse stress conditions.

Herein, this study examines the protease activity of M. leprae Hsp18 by complementing the Lon protease in E. coli and assessing its functional impact. To demonstrate this we used the ∆lon strains of E. coli MG1655 and SG20780 and studied the phenotypic and molecular changes as regulated by the Lon protease. From patients with leprosy, polymorphic variants of the Hsp18 protein, Hsp18S and Hsp18P were identified [41] and their differential proteolytic activity was validated. The N-terminal and C-terminal regions of Hsp18 expressing the α-crystaline and the proteolytic domains respectively restored the ∆lon phenotype. Overall, our study demonstrates that the M. leprae Hsp18 protein possesses both chaperone and proteolytic activity similar to Lon protease when complementing the in ∆lon E. coli.

The bacterial strains, plasmids and the primers used in this study are provided in Table 1. The pQE31-hsp18 recombinant plasmids (S and P variants) were reported earlier [26, 32] having the isopropyl thio-β-galactoside (IPTG)-inducible T5 promoter but lacks the lacI+ repressor gene. The bacterial strains harbouring the pQE31-hsp18 plasmids were transformed with pREP4 lacI+ plasmid. Ampicillin and kanamycin resistant colonies were selected for further studies. Similarly, the C-terminal region was amplified using 5′- CGCGGATCCGCATGCTCCCGGCCG-3′ (forward) and 5′-CCCAAGCTTGGTTATGGGCGCICGGC-3’ (reverse) primers. The amplicons were cloned in BamH1-HindIII sites of the pQE-31 vector, the inserts were verified by restriction analysis followed by sequencing.

P1 transduction

The bacterial strain containing lon-146::Tn10 was cultured until it reached an OD600 of 0.6 in Luria broth (LB; Tryptone-1%, NaCl-1% and Yeast Extract-0.5%,pH 7.2) supplemented with 2.5 mM CaCl2. Subsequently, the bacterial strain was infected with P1 vir lysate at a multiplicity of infection of <1(0.5). Following gentle mixing, the tubes were placed on a shaker for 4–5 h at 30 °C or until complete lysis occurred. To ensure thorough lysis, a few drops of chloroform were added, and the tubes were vigorously vortexed for 2 min. The P1 lysate was cleared by centrifugation at 5000 x g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was stored at 4 °C after adding a drop of chloroform.

For transduction 1 ml of the overnight-cultured recipient MG1655 (lon+) was infected with, 100 μl of P1 lysate from the donor with 2.5 µl (2.5 mM) of CaCl2, and incubated at 30 °C for 30 min to facilitate infection. Subsequently, trisodium citrate (50 mM) was added to the culture to halt any further infection. Bacterial cells were pelleted by centrifuging at 10,000 rpm for 5 min then washed three times with saline to eliminate any remaining phages. The bacterial pellet was suspended in 1 ml LB broth and incubated at 30 °C for an hour to induce the expression. After allowing for expression for one hour in 1 ml of LB, the final pellets were re-suspended in 100 μl of saline, thoroughly vortexed, plated on LB plates containing tetracyclin (10ug/mL) and 50 mM trisodium citrate, plates were then incubated at 30 °C until transductants emerged.

Phenotypic analysis

The single colonies of the E. coli MG1655 and MG1655Δlon strains harboring the different plasmids were streaked on LB agar plates with amplicilin (100ug/mL) and kanamycin (50ug/mL) with and without IPTG (0.5mM) and incubated at 30℃ for 24–36 h, to observe mucoid phenotype. Similarly, the E. coli SG20780 (Δlon) strain harboring the different plasmids were streaked onto M9 minimal agar plates with vitamin B1 and gluocose containing X-gal (30µg/ml), ampicillin (100 µg/ml), and kanamycin (100 µg/ml) with or without IPTG (0.5 mM). The plates were then incubated at 30 °C for 36 h, to observe the blue/white colonies.

Beta-galactosidase assay

SG20780 carrying either pQE31 or pQE31-hsp18(S/P) was inoculated in 3 mL of M9 minimal medium with ampicillin (100ug/mL) and kanamycin (50ug/mL) and incubated at 30℃ overnight. The overnight cultures were subcultured in 5 mL of M9 minimal medium with the same antibiotics with or without IPTG (0.5mM), and the cultures were grown until it reaches OD600 measured using a spectrophotometer (OPTIMA SP-300). Cells (0.5mL) were pelleted, washed with saline and then resuspended in 0.5 mL of saline and mixed with 0.5 mL of 2X Z-buffer (Na2HPO4.7H2O (0.06M), NaH2PO4.H2O (0.04M, KCl (0.01M), β-mercaptoethanol (0.05M) pH 7.0) and a drop of chloroform was added, The mixure was vortexed, and then O-nitrophenly-β-D-galactopyranoside (ONPG) substrate was introduced to achieve a final concentration of 800 µg/mL and incubated in the dark at 30℃ for 30 min. The reaction was halted by adding 0.5 mL of 1M sodium carbonate. The mixure was centrifuged at 7000 rpm for 5 min to remove debris and chloroform and the supernatant was used to measure OD420. Beta-galactosidase activity was calculated in Miller units and the graph was plotted [30] using Graphpad Prizm software by one way annova.

Semi-quantitative polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was extracted from bacterial cells using TriZol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) as per the manufacturers instruction. About 1ug of total RNA was reverse transcribed using a cDNA synthesis kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) with RT primer mix (supplied). The rcsA gene was amplified using 5′-ACCGTTGA-TGACCTTGC-3′ (forward) and 5′-GCCAAGGATATCGTCGAG-3′ (reverse) primers. Similarly, the rpsG gene was amplified using 5′-GTCGCGTCATTGGTCAG-3′ (forward) and 5′-CGAGAGCTACTTCGAATGCT-3′ (reverse) primers and used as an internal control to normalize rcsA expression. [29]. The amplified products were resolved on a 1.5% agarose gel and the intensity of the bands were quantified using ImageJ software (http://rsbweb.nih.gov/ij/download.html) and plotted using Graphpad Prism software using one way annova.

Immuno-detection of RcsA expression

The log phase E. coli MG1655 and MG1655Δlon strains were grown in LB containing appropriate antibiotics at 30 °C were induced with IPTG (0.5 mM) for 2 h and treated with spectinomycin (100ug/ml) for 15 min to halt protein synthesis. The bacterial cells were pelleted and lysed using the buffer containing 25 mM Tris (pH 7.5) with 0.3% SDS and incubated on ice for 10 min followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was collected and the protein concentration was quantified by Bradford’s method [7]. Approximately 35µg of total protein was resolved on a 12% SDS-PAGE gel, transferred to nitrocellulose membranes and blocked with 5% non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline with 0.5% Tween 20 (TBST). The membranes were then incubated in TBST containing 0.1% milk powder and polyclonal RcsA antibody (1:1000) (Susan Gottesman; NIH; USA), for 16 h followed by HRP conjugated secondary antibody at 1:2000 dilution (Sigma Aldrich; St. Louis, Missouri, United States). The expression of proteins were detected by chemiluminesence (Bio-Rad) followed by autoradiogram.

Results

M. leprae Hsp18 replaces E. coli Lon protease and inhibit mucoid phenotype

The mucoid phenotype in E. coli is caused by increased expression of colanic acid capsular polysaccharide (cps). The cps expression is regulated by the Rcs system, in which RcsA is the auxiliary factor that interacts with RcsB to form a heterodimer and induces cps expression, resulting in the mucoid phenotype. The Lon protease degrades RcsA, inhibiting cps expression in E. coli and preventing the mucoid phenotype. Protein sequence comparison showed that M. leprae Hsp18 has a conserved GVDPD amino acid sequence and proteolytic active site when compared with EcLon and MsLon proteases (Fig. 1A). In M. leprae, the lack of Lon protease and the presence of conserved structural similarity in the proteolytic domain of M. leprae Hsp18 suggest that the Hsp18 will act as a compensatory protease similar to the Lon protease. By modulating the key regulatory proteins under stress, Hsp18 promotes M. leprae survival within macrophages, thereby driving its pathogenicity. To demonstrate this the M. leprae Hsp18 protein was expressed in an E. coli MG1655Δlon mutant using an IPTG inducible promoter (Fig. 1B) and the protein was expressed at a significant level and it is not toxic to the E. coli. The E. coli MG1655Δlon mutant transformed with vector backbone alone showed a mucoid phenotype. Similarly, the Hsp18 expression cassette transformed into the MG1655Δlon mutant showed the mucoid phenotype (Fig. 1C). When Hsp18 expression was induced with IPTG, the mucoid phenotype was reversed, forming normal colonies (Fig. 1C & D) and this was confirmed by the spot test, in which induced Hsp18 expression reduced the mucoid phenotype and increased cell survival at lower concentrations (Fig. 1D). These data suggest that the M. leprae Hsp18 protein acts as a protease and possibly degrade RcsA when replaced the Lon protease in E. coli.

M. Leprae Hsp18 protease regulates cps-lac expression

To corroborate the proteolytic activity of the M. leprae Hsp18 protein by reducing the mucoid phenotype, we utilized an alternative system using the E. coli SG20780 (cps::lac) strain [9] in which Lon protease is absent. This strain expresses the β-galactosidase reporter enzyme when plated on X-gal plates, forming blue colonies upon IPTG induction. When the M. leprae Hsp18 expression cassette was transformed into the E. coli SG20780 strain, the Hsp18 protein was expressed significantly upon IPTG induction without affecting the cell growth (Fig. 2A). When induced with IPTG, the SG20780 strain transformed with the vector alone formed blue colonies on minimal agar plates with X-gal. However, when Hsp18 protein expression was induced with IPTG, the blue colonies were completely reduced (Fig. 2B) compared to un-induced cells. The β-galactosidase activity assay in these cells showed approximately seven-fold reduction in β-galactosidase activity upon induced expression of M. leprae Hsp18 protein compared to uninduced and control cells. (Fig. 2C). These data, in addition to the previous data as shown in Fig. 1B–D, demonstrate that the M. leprae Hsp18 protein has protease activity and possibly degrades RcsA, replacing Lon protease in E. coli and resulting in these phenotypic changes.

M. leprae hsp18 regulates the rcsA expression

The E. coli Lon protease degrades the transcriptional regulator rcsA, further repressing the expression of cps and reversing the mucoid phenotype observed in Δlon mutants. The reversal of phenotypic changes like the mucoid phenotype and inhibition of β-galactosidase activity by M. leprae Hsp18 as a protease suggests that the Hsp18 protein may negatively regulate RcsA. To test this total RNA was isolated from E. coli, MG1655 and MG1655Δlon strains expressing M. leprae hsp18 reverse transcribed using specific primers for the rcsA gene and normalized to rpsG gene expression. The MG1655Δlon transformed with the vector showed increased rcsA expression compared with MG1655 transformed with the vector. In contrast, MG1655Δlon cells transformed with hsp18 and induced with IPTG showed decreased levels of rcsA expression similar to that of MG1655 transformed with the vector when compared with un-induced cells (Fig. 3A). Quantification data showed approximately fivefold increase in rcsA expression in MG1655Δlon cells compared with MG1655 cells transformed with the vector upon IPTG induction. Reduced rcsA expression was observed when M. leprae Hsp18 was expressed upon IPTG induction as equal to that of the control cells, whereas un-induced cells showed higher rcsA expression, similar to MG1655Δlon cells (Fig. 3B). The rcsA is an autoregulator and regulates its own expression by a feedback mechanism. The transcriptional regulation of rcsA expression by M. leprae hsp18 suggests that by acting as a protease, it degrades RcsA protein and inhibits the autoregulation of rcsA expression.

Next, total protein was extracted from these cells and approximately 15 µg was resolved on a 15% SDS-PAGE, followed by western immunoblotting using RcsA-specific antibody. In MG1655 cells transformed with vector alone, RcsA protein expression was not detected, whereas MG1655Δlon transformed with vector showed RcsA protein expression. In MG1655Δlon transformed with M. leprae Hsp18 expression cassette, RcsA protein expression was not detected upon IPTG induction, whereas in un-induced cells, RcsA expression was observed as in the MG1655Δlon strain. These data demonstrate that the M. leprae hsp18 protein acts as a protease and degrades the RcsA protein, resulting in the cps downregulation, followed by the mucoid phenotype reversal in the MG1655Δlon strain. Thus, the M. leprae hsp18 protein replaces the function of Lon protease in E. coli by regulating the Lon protease-dependent regulation of cell survival.

Comparison of the M. leprae patient’s derived Hsp18 variants

Reportedly, the M. leprae Hsp18 gene is polymorphic with a single nucleotide polymorphism across the spectrum of patients with leprosy from the same geographical region [41]. This SNP results in Hsp18 protein in two different variants, Hsp18S and Hsp18P in which codon 52 encodes serine and proline respectively. The Armadillo- derived Hsp18 protein encodes the Hsp18S variant (Fig. 4A). Hsp18 is auto-phosphorylated for its stabilization [27], however, the phosphorylated amino acids have not been identified. Hypothetically, the serine residue in Hsp18S may get phosphorylated and stabilized compared with the Hsp18P variant, conferring proteolytic activity. Herein, we characterized the proteolytic activity of the Hsp18P variant and compared it with Hsp18S. The Hsp18P variant expression cassette was transformed into the MG1655∆lon strain and compared with the Hsp18S variant. Hsp18S and Hsp18P variants expressed the protein equally in the MG1655∆lon strain and showed normal growth (Fig. 4B). When the cells were grown and induced with IPTG as well as spotted at different dilutions, Hsp18P and Hsp18S variants showed mucoid phenotype without IPTG induction (Fig. 4C). However, upon induced expression of the Hsp18, the Hsp18S reversed the mucoid phenotype and the increased the survival of cells as evidenced from the lowed dilutions, whereas the Hsp18P variant showed reduced mucoid phenotype and number of cells. These data suggest that Hsp18P is not much effective in degrading the RcsA protein and results in mucoid phenotype and reduced cell growth compared to the Hsp18S variant.

Hsp18 variants differentially regulate the β-galactosidase activity

To further confirm the efficiency of the Hsp18P and Hsp18S variants for their proteolytic activity, these variants were transformed in E. coli SG20780 and expressed the proteins at similar levels (Fig. 5A). When these cells were plated on minimal agar plates and induced with X-gal and IPTG, the Hsp18S variant showed a minimal number of blue colonies, whereas, the Hsp18P variant showed more blue colonies than Hsp18S variant (Fig. 5B). The quantification data show that there was approximately 90% reduction in β-gal expression in cells expressing the Hsp18S variant, whereas the Hsp18P variant expressing cells showed approximately 55% reduction in β-gal expression, demonstrating that Hsp18S is more efficient in its proteolytic activity than Hsp18P (Fig. 5C).

MMS sensitivity of M. leprae Hsp18 variants

When cells are exposed to DNA-damaging agents like MMS, E. coli cells activate the SOS repair system by activating repressed genes like sulA, a cell division inhibitor that interacts with the cell division apparatus. SulA is a short-lived protein and its stability is regulated by the Lon protease [11, 19, 22, 31, 44]. Degradation of this protein permits the cells to divide, whereas in lon- mutants, the half-life of SulA increases by stabilizing the protein and leading to irreversible filamentation and cell death. To test the role of Hsp18 variants against MMS, the Hsp18 variants Hsp18S and Hsp18P transformed in the SG20780 strain were exposed to different MMS concentrations (0.01–0.05%), and the grown cells were spotted at different dilutions. MG1655, the lon+ cells, showed resistance up to 0.05% compared with the lon- mutant SG20780. Notably, the Hsp18S variant showed resistance to 0.02% MMS at 10−2 dilution compared with the Hsp18P variant (Fig. 6A & B). Similarly, when the Hsp18 variants expression was induced in MG1655∆lon cells, the Hsp18S variant showed resistance to different MMS concentrations, similar to the lon+ cells MG1655. In contrast, the Hsp18P variant showed resistance up to 0.01% MMS, and cells did not survive when the MMS concentration increased (Fig. 6A & C). These data demonstrate that the M. leprae Hsp18 protein replaces Lon protease and helps the cells survive the MMS-induced DNA damage response possibly by degrading the cell cycle inhibitor SulA.

Proteolytic activity of deletion mutants of M.leprae Hsp18 protein

The presence of conserved sequences and the proteolytic active site of the Lon proteases of E. coli and M. smegmatis and the alpha-crystalline domain of the M. leprae Hsp18 protein suggest that the Hsp18 protein may have a similar function to Lon protease. To further characterize whether the N-terminal part of the alpha-crystalline domain alone is sufficient for the proteolytic function of the M. leprae Hsp18 protein or it requires the C-terminal part with the GVDPD motif and proteolytic active site, the hsp18 ΔNT and ΔCT expression constructs were expressed in E. coli and analyzed. The N-terminal (aa 1–69) sequence without the GVDPD motif and the C-terminal (aa 49–148) with the proteolytic active site were sub-cloned separately and induced to express in MG1655Δlon mutant strain. The mutants expressed the expected size of the proteins when induced with IPTG (Fig. 7A) and both the mutants formed mucoid colonies in un-induced and induced cells (Fig. 7B). These data suggests that the M. leprae Hsp18 protein requires both the C-terminal and N-terminal domains, and act synergistically for its proteolytic activity.

Discussion

M. leprae ensure their survival within a host by expressing virulent genes in T1 reactional (T1R) cases of leprosy [16]. In T1R cases, high-level humoral immune responses damage several bacterial proteins, making the degradation of such damaged proteins is essential for the pathogen survival within the host. Herein, we have demonstrated the proteolytic function of the M. leprae hsp18 protein by complementing the E. coli Lon protease.

The M. leprae Hsp18 is an alpha-crystallin, small heat shock protein [33] that exhibits chaperone-like activity. Its expression is regulated in a temperature-dependent manner in M. smegmatis [26, 32] and macrophages [27]. In stress conditions, the protein is stabilized by autophosphorylation [27]. Functionally related proteins have been identified in M. tuberculosis [47], other mycobacteria [6], and in M. ulcerans, in which an Hsp18 ortholog contributes to biofilm formation [34]. Recently, we have demonstrated the transcriptional regulation of the hsp18 gene under induced environmental stress conditions [17]. However, the potential functional roles of the M. leprae Hsp18 protein during stress conditions and the possible underlying molecular mechanisms` during pathogenesis have not been demonstrated.

Multiple sequence alignments revealed that the alpha-crystalline domain of the M. leprae Hsp18 protein has conserved sequence homology in the GVDPD motif and the proteolytic active site of E. coli and M. smegmatis Lon proteases. However, a functional Lon protease has not been reported in M. leprae. Though, the M. leprae Hsp65 has been shown as a protease, which is similar to the E. coli ClpY protease and targets the Lon protease substrates only at higher temperatures [35]. The Lon proteasese have dual function as a chaperone and proteolytic activity. Since, there is no functional Lon protease is present in M. leprae, we characterized the proteolytic activity of the M. leprae Hsp18 protein. The E. coli Lon protease substrates RcsA and SulA are well-studied [40, 44]. RcsA acts as a positive regulator of cps genes involved in capsular polysaccharide synthesis, forming a mucoid phenotype [21]. Similarly, SulA the SOS regulon, is induced during DNA damage response, stabilization and increased half-life of this proteins inhibit cell division [23]. The E. coli Δlon mutants form mucoid colonies and are sensitive to MMS due to the stabilization of rcsA and sulA proteins respectively. In M. smegmatis the Δlon mutant was shown to be not essential when the cells were grown in certain stress conditions, where the ClpA and ClpX proteases will replace Lon. Moreover, the double mutant for Lon and the α and β subunits of 20S proteasome genes showed growth rate and stress tolerance similar to the wild type strain [25]. These results demonstrate that the Lon protease is not essential for stress tolerance and that can be regulated by other proteases in M. smegmatis. Hence, we chose E. coli Δlon strains to characterize the proteolytic activity of the M. leprae Hsp18 protein.

When the M. leprae Hsp18 protein was expressed in the ∆lon E. coli strain MG1655, the mucoid phenotype was reversed and the expression of RcsA was inhibited. Similarly, in the cps::lac mutant strain SG20780, the degradation of the cps-lac fusion protein inhibited lacZ expression. In these E. coli strains, the induced expression of Hsp18 proteins showed resistance to increasing concentrations of the DNA-damaging agent MMS, possibly by regulating the cell division inhibitor SulA. The Hsp18 protein is polymorphic and two different variants Hsp18S and Hsp18P where the codon 52 was Serine and Proline respectively have been identified in patients with leprosy [41]. Among these two variants the Hsp18S is more potent than Hsp18P in regulating RcsA and SulA proteins, followed by the phenotypic changes. The Hsp18 protein is autophosphorylated for its stabilization [27], and there are five serine residues present in the protein and the exact serine residue that is phosphorylated has not been identified. Moreover, it has been shown that compared to the Hsp18S, the Hsp18P variant showed altered secondary and tertiary structure, reduced oligomeric assembly, less stable against thermal and chemical denaturation, more susceptible to tryptic cleavage and had lower chaperone activity [32, 36]. The increased proteolytic activity of Hsp18S compared to Hsp18P suggests both the variants will have chaperone activity and autophosphorylation of the serine residue (S52) in Hsp18S may stabilize the protein and increase its proteolytic activity compared to the non-phosphorylatable Hsp18P variant. These studies clearly demonstrate that, the WT Hsp18S important for the pathogenesis through its chaperone and proteolytic activity. However, the exact molecular mechanism is not known because other amino acid residues like threonine (T), tyrosine (Y), arginine (R), aspartic acid (D), histidine (H), and cysteine (C) can also be phosphorylated in bacteria to regulate protein function. Moreover, when the conserved GVDPD sequence motif and the N-terminal domain were expressed separately in the ∆lon E. coli strain, both expression constructs lost proteolytic activity, suggesting that the whole protein acts synergistically to have proteolytic activity and other unknown mechanisms might be involved.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates the molecular characterization of the M. leprae Hsp18 protein as a proteolytic enzyme. that complements the Lon protease in E. coli. Because the Lon protease is absent in M. leprae, the Hsp18 protein as a chaperone, regulate the expression of regulatory proteins by refolding the misfolded proteins for survival in adverse environmental conditions.. Under persistent environmental stress conditions the Hsp18 will be auto-phosphorylated that increase its proteolytic activity to degrade the misfolded proteins that could not be refolded through its chaperone activity. Thus the dual function of Hsp18 contributing for the pathogenesis of M. leprae inside the macrophages. Overall, this study provides valuable insights into the multifaceted functions of hsp18 in the M. leprae pathogenesis and offers insights for effective drug development for eradicating leprosy. Targeting the Hsp18 protein or its activity using cell permeable small molecules and/or protease inhibitors would be the effective management strategy against leprosy.
