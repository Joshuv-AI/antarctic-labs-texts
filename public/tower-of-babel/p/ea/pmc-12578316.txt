Novel amino acid residues in listerolysin O drive phagosome escape and pathogenicity in Listeria monocytogenes
Authors: Jing Xia, Yuchao Feng, Jiali Xu, Simin Deng, Mianmian Chen, Yue Han, Jing Wang, Jing Sun, Kalliopi Rantsiou, Luca Simone Cocolin, Houhui Song, Changyong Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTListeria monocytogenes, a significant zoonotic foodborne intracellular pathogen, relies on its principal virulence factor, listeriolysin O (LLO), to escape from host cell phagosomes-a critical step for establishing intracellular infection and replication. LLO, composed of four structural domains with amino acids essential for its pore-forming abilities, still harbors many sites with undefined roles. We identified a critical domain within LLO (amino acids 251–255) crucial for bacterial pathogenesis and systematically investigated the individual roles of five amino acid sites within this domain. Our findings demonstrate that LLO K252 is dispensable for LLO functionality, residues F251, Q253, and Y255 are critical for LLO’s hemolytic activity and cytotoxicity. Mutation of I254 to alanine alters hemolytic activity, with effects dependent on environmental pH. Notably, alanine mutations at F251, Q253, and Y255A significantly impair bacterial proliferation in mouse organs and disruption of late phagosome membranes. In particular, mutations F251A and Y255A abolish late phagosome escape, underscoring their importance in host cell infection. This study has expanded our understanding of LLO’s role in Listeria pathogenicity and phagosome escape, offering new insights into the molecular mechanisms driving this pathogens’ virulence.KEYWORDS: Listerolysin O, phagosome escape, virulence factors, pathogenesis mechanisms, hemolytic activity

Introduction

Listeria monocytogenes, a prevalent foodborne pathogen, is responsible for listeriosis, a critical illness in both humans and animals accompanied by a mortality rate of 20–30% [1]. L. monocytogenes can be found extensively throughout the natural environment and frequently isolated from various environments and foods [2]. As a typical intracellular pathogen, L. monocytogenes is capable of proliferating within macrophages and various non-phagocytic cells, establishing persistent infections and posing significant public health risks [3].

Comparative with other well-known intracellular pathogens such as Salmonella and Mycobacterium tuberculosis, which create and maintain their own niches within host-derived vacuoles, L. monocytogenes primarily escapes into the host cell cytosol [4]. While Salmonella modulates the host cell’s endosomal system to evade lysosomal degradation through a sophisticated network of effector proteins [5,6], and M. tuberculosis manipulates phagosome maturation processes to favor its persistence [7,8,] L. monocytogenes directly breaches the vacuolar membrane and escapes into the cytosol [9]. This escape is facilitated by listeriolysin O (LLO) and two phospholipases (PlcA and PlcB), which are encoded within the Listeria Pathogenicity Island 1 (LIP-1) and regulated by PrfA [9]. LLO can efficiently attach to lipid membranes with high cholesterol levels. Following this, LLO monomers come together to form structures and then experience a significant conformational shift, enabling them to penetrate the membrane and create pores. LLO performs optimally in the acidic environment of phagosomes in infected cells, where Listeria is engulfed after entering. The insertion of LLO oligomers into the membrane and the permeabilization of the vacuole containing Listeria enable its escape [10]. This mechanism allows the bacterium to bypass vacuolar confinement, rapidly breaching the phagosome membrane to replicate freely in the cytosol and infect neighboring cells.

LLO, essential for phagosome escape, belongs to the cholesterol-dependent cytolysin (CDC) family and is unique among CDCs for being secreted by an intracellular pathogen [11,12]. As the principal virulence factor in L. monocytogenes, LLO exhibits multifaceted activity in pathogenesis and host interaction during infection, just as the “Swiss army knife of Listeria” that is highly suited for the intracellular lifestyle [13]. The phagosome escape induced by LLO pore-forming activity is the first step for L. monocytogenes to establish an intracellular infection, relying on multiple domains or amino acid residues of the protein.

The molecular structure of LLO, comprising 529 amino acids, includes four domains involved in forming a functional pore [14,15]. The efficiency of this pore-forming activity is further enhanced by the N-terminal PEST-like sequence within Domain 1, which aids in the oligomerization necessary for LLO’s lytic action [16,17]. Structural studies have highlighted that specific amino acid sequences and structural motifs within LLO, such as the highly conserved 11-peptide sequence in Domain 4, are critical for its interaction with cholesterol in the host cell membrane [18,19]. LLO’s activity is optimal at a pH of 5.5, suitable for acidified phagosomes, but is inhibited under the neutral pH conditions of the cytosol [20]. This pH-dependent activity is regulated by specific amino acid residues acting as pH sensors (E247, D208, and D320) within the protein, allowing the toxin to function effectively in its intended acidic environment while limiting damage to the host cell [21].

Despite extensive research, many aspects of LLO’s functionality remain poorly understood. Notably, in our preliminary study, deletion of a specific segment comprising amino acids 251–255 (FKQIY) drastically reduces the pathogen’s virulence [22]. The hly Δ251‐255 aa strain, similar to the Δhly mutant, displayed no hemolytic activity, with almost no bacteria recovered from mouse organs 24 and 48 hours after infection, and completely lost its intracellular growth capability in macrophages, highlighting this region as crucial for the bacterium’s life cycle [22]. Previously, Melton-Witt et al. identified F251, Q253, and Y255 as residues critical for hemolytic activity, and especially smaller plaques on mouse L2 cell fibroblasts for Y255A mutant strain [23]. This also offers insights into the roles of these five amino acid sites. What other roles are these amino acids possibly associated with? Here, this study aims to further elucidate the functions of specific amino acids within this critical domain, examining their role in mediating L. monocytogenes” virulence and ability to escape phagosomes. Our findings are expected to provide deeper insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying L. monocytogenes” pathogenicity and offer potential targets for therapeutic intervention.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains, plasmids, and antibodies

Bacterial strains and plasmids used in this study are the same as those in our previously published paper [24]. L. monocytogenes EGD-e served as the wild-type strain, while E. coli DH5α was utilized for cloning experiments and as the host for plasmids pET-30a (+) and pKSV7. For prokaryotic protein expression, E. coli Rosetta (DE3) was employed. Culturing of L. monocytogenes strains was conducted in brain-heart infusion (BHI) medium (Oxoid, Hampshire, England), and E. coli strains were grown at 37°C in Luria-Bertani broth (LB) (Oxoid). All reagents in our lab were sourced from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), Merck, or Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) and were of the highest purity available.

Purified recombinant proteins were used to immunize New Zealand white rabbits for the production of polyclonal antibodies [25]. Initially, rabbits received a subcutaneous injection of 500 µg protein mixed with an equal volume of Freund’s complete adjuvant (Sigma-Aldrich). After 2 weeks, they were boosted with subcutaneous injections of 250 µg protein in incomplete Freund’s adjuvant (Sigma) three times at 10-day intervals. Blood samples were collected approximately 10 days post the final injection. LAMP1 (D2D11) XP® Rabbit mAb (Cat# 9091) and EEA1 (E9Q6G) Mouse mAb (Cat# 48,453) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology.

Cell lines

HeLa, Caco-2, RAW264.7, and L929 cells, with catalog numbers CRL-3216, CL-0050, CL-0190, and CCL-1 respectively, were sourced from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Macrophages obtained from the bone marrow (BMDMs) of C57BL/6 mice were employed (See “Preparation of BMDMs” part). All cell lines were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The culture of Caco-2 cells was carried out in RPMI 1640 (Gibco) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gemini Bioproducts), while the other cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco) containing 10% FBS.

Construction of LLO mutant strains

The ORF of hly gene was amplified from wild-type L. monocytogenes using primer hly-Fwd/hly-Rev (Table S1). The PCR product was purified and the pKSV7 vector was prepared with BamHI and PstI restriction enzymes for 2 hours, followed by ligation with T4 DNA ligase at 4°C overnight. The resulting pKSV7-hly recombinant shuttle plasmid was confirmed by PCR with M13-Fwd/M13-Rev primers (Table S1) and sequenced. Using the sequenced pKSV7-hly plasmid, point mutations were introduced via PCR with specific primers (Table S1) and the modified plasmid was sequenced for accuracy. The plasmid was subsequently introduced into the competent L. monocytogenes cells via electroporation, and the transformed product was coated in BHI solid medium containing chloramphenicol and incubated at 30°C for 24–48 hours, and single colonies were screened using M13 primers. Positive transformants were grown in chloramphenicol-supplemented BHI medium at 42°C to encourage homologous recombination. Subsequently, these cells were passaged in nonresistant BHI medium at 30°C for 15–20 generations. Final mutant strains were identified and sequenced using A-Fwd/D-Rev primers (Table S1) to confirm the point mutations [24]. This experiment was carried out from around May to July 2022.

Overexpression and purification of recombinant proteins from E. coli

Recombinant proteins were expressed as His-tagged fusion proteins using pET-30a (+) vector. E. coli Rosetta (DE3) served as the expression host. The complete ORF of the target gene was amplified from the L. monocytogenes EGD-e genome using specific primers (Table S1), cloned into pET-30a (+), and transformed into competent Rosetta cells. Cultures were grown in 500 mL of LB medium supplemented with 50 µg/mL kanamycin at 37°C until an OD600 nm of 0.8–1.0 was achieved. Expression was induced with 0.4 mM IPTG for 4 hours under optimized conditions. The cells were collected through centrifugation at 6,500 g for 10 min, resuspended in a lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.5), and lysed using an ultrasonic homogenizer (Scientz Biotechnology, Ningbo). His-tagged proteins were purified with nickel-chelated affinity column chromatography. The purified protein was detected by SDS-PAGE, and the remaining protein was ultrafiltered and stored in the refrigerator at −80°C for later use. The 180 kDa Prestained Protein Marker was purchased from Vazyme (Cat# MP102). The duration of the experiment spanned roughly from May to July 2022.

In vitro growth of L. monocytogenes

According to the previously published study [24], L. monocytogenes wild-type strain EGD-e, Δhly and LLO mutants (hlyF251A, hlyK252A, hlyQ253A, hlyI254A, and hlyY255A) cultured overnight at 37°C were harvested by centrifuging at 5,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The pellets were washed in 10 mM PBS (pH7.4), and the OD600 nm was adjusted to 1.0. Diluted cultures (1:100) were incubated in BHI broth at 37°C, and growth kinetics were monitored by measuring OD600 nm every hour for 12 hours. This experiment was completed in August 2022.

Determination of secretory LLO

Briefly, stationary phase growth of L. monocytogenes and LLO mutant strains were centrifuged, and the culture supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 µm polyethersulfone membrane filter. Proteins were precipitated with trichloroacetic acid on ice overnight and washed with cold acetone. The precipitates were re-suspended in SDS-PAGE sample buffer, separated by 12% SDS-PAGE, and immunoblotted with LLO or P60 antisera, using P60 as the internal control [24]. This experiment was also carried out in August 2022.

Analysis of hemolytic activity

Bacterial and proteins hemolysis assessment were conducted as previously outlined [24]. For bacterial hemolysis, overnight cultures of L. monocytogenes wild-type and LLO mutant strains were adjusted to an OD600 nm of 1.0 before collecting supernatants. Hemolytic activity was assessed by measuring the lysis of sheep red blood cells (SRBCs) induced by secreted LLO. Specifically, 50 µL of culture supernatant was diluted in hemolysis buffer (10 mM PBS, pH 5.5 or 6.5, 150 mM NaCl) and equilibrated at 37°C for 10 min. Then, 50 µL of PBS-washed SRBCs (5%) were added, and samples were incubated at 37°C for 30 min. The samples were centrifuged, and supernatants were analyzed for hemoglobin absorption at OD550 nm. To assess LLO protein hemolysis, purified recombinant LLO or its mutant proteins were serially diluted in hemolysis buffer and mixed with 5% SRBCs. Hemolytic activity was determined as described above, and activities in different supernatants were compared using the dilution reciprocal of culture supernatant required to lyse 50% of SRBCs. Erythrocytes treated with 1% Triton-X-100 or PBS were used to determine maximum (100%) and minimum (0%) hemolytic activity, respectively. This experiment was carried out from around July to August 2022.

Cytotoxicity detection

The level of cytotoxicity was determined by quantifying LDH released from cells post-bacterial infection, utilizing the CytoTox96 non-radioactive cytotoxicity assay kit as per the manufacturer’s guidelines as previously described [24]. The cells were exposed to L. monocytogenes overnight cultures at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10, incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 30 minutes, and subsequently treated with culture medium containing or lacking 50 µg/mL gentamicin. At specific times of infection or after incubating with 5 nM LLO protein, cells underwent centrifugation at 250 × g for 5 minutes, and the supernatant was taken for the LDH assay. The experiment setup featured each triplicate wells containing only DMEM and uninfected BMDM cells, to evaluate background absorption and spontaneous LDH release, respectively. For assessing the peak LDH release, 100 µL of lysis buffer was introduced to three infected wells 30 minutes before taking measurements. Cytotoxicity percentage was calculated post background correction using the formula: cytotoxicity% = [(experimental LDH release – spontaneous LDH release)/(peak LDH release – spontaneous LDH release)] ×100. The duration of the experiment spanned roughly from October to November 2022.

Cell membrane permeability assay

RAW264.7 cells were plated on coverslips coated with poly-lysine and subsequently infected, procedure of infection and MOI ratio refer to cytotoxicity detection. After infection, the cells were washed three times in 10 mM PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 30 min at room temperature. The cells were then rinsed with PBS and treated with propidium iodide (PI) and Hoechst 33,342 (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.) at a dilution of 1:500 for 30 min. Following three washes in PBS, the coverslips were affixed on glass slides. Confocal images were performed using FV4000 confocal microscope to assess cell permeability. The proportion of dead cells is calculated by dividing the number of dead cells by the total cell count in the observed area. The data are presented as means ± SDs based on at least five fluorescence images. This experiment was completed in November 2022.

Adhesion and invasion in Caco-2 cells

Caco-2 cells were plated in 24-well plates with 2 × 10 [5] cells per well and infected with L. monocytogenes at an MOI of about 10 for 30 min. Adhesion: After 30 min of bacterial infection of Caco-2 cells, the cells were washed three times with 10 mM PBS and lysed in sterile water, diluted to the appropriate gradient, and then viable bacteria were counted by plating on BHI agar. Invasion: Caco-2 cells were treated with gentamicin at a final concentration of 50 μg/mL for 1.5 hours. After 1.5 hours, the cells infected with bacteria were washed three times with 10 mM PBS, followed by lysis in sterile water and lysates were serially diluted 10-fold for counting of viable bacteria by plating on BHI agar. Data are shown as means ± SDs, with results representative of at least three independent experiments. The experiment took place approximately between November and December 2022.

Detection and observation of cell-to-cell spread ability

For plaque experiment, L929 cell were plated in 12-well plates with 5 × 10 [5] cells per well and subsequently infected with L. monocytogenes at an MOI of about 10. One hour after infection, 50 μg/mL gentamicin was added for incubating another one hour. The cells were then washed three times with 10 mM PBS, and overlaid with 0.7% low-melting point agarose cell medium containing 10 μg/mL gentamicin. Following an incubation at 37°C for 48 hours, cells in each well were fixed with 600 μL formaldehyde and stained with 600 μL 0.5% crystal violet solution. The number and diameter of plaques were counted using ImageJ. The results are presented as means ± SDs and are representative of at least three independent experiments. The duration of the experiment spanned roughly from May to June 2023.

Preparation of BMDMs

BMDMs were isolated from the tibiae and femurs of 6-week-old specific-pathogen-free (SPF) C57BL/6 male mice. The mice were acquired from the Laboratory Animal Centre of Hangzhou Medical College, Hangzhou, Zhejiang Province, China. Following the lysis of red blood cells, BMDMs were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (Caisson), and murine macrophage colony-stimulating factor (Pepro Tech). The culture period spanned 5–7 days to ensure differentiation into macrophages.

Intracellular bacterial growth in macrophages

RAW264.7 and BMDM cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 2 × 10 [5] cells per well and infected with L. monocytogenes at an MOI of approximately 0.2 for 30 min. Cells were then washed twice with 10 mM PBS before replacing the media, and gentamicin was added at a concentration of 50 μg/mL one-hour post-infection. At 0.5-, 2-, 5-, and 8-hour post-infection, cells were lysed in sterile water, and the lysates were serially diluted 10-fold to enumerate viable bacteria by plating on BHI agar. Data are presented as means ± SDs, with results representing at least three independent experiments. The timeframe for this experiment was about March through April 2023.

Virulence in the Galleria mellonella and mouse model

For the median lethal dose (LD50) determination in G. mellonella model, gradient injection of wild-type L. monocytogenes was performed every 12 hours for 5 days after infection, and LD50 was calculated by survival rates. For animal survival experiments, G. mellonella were injected intraperitoneally with 5 × 10 [5] CFU L. monocytogenes and its mutants, monitored for up to 5 days post-infection. Survival curves were calculated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences in survival were assessed using the Log-rank test. The experiment took place approximately between May and June 2023.

Bacterial recovery in liver and spleen sections was assessed in murine models [24]. A total of 49 female 5-week-old SPF ICR mice were acquired from the Laboratory Animal Centre of Hangzhou Medical College, Hangzhou, Zhejiang Province, China. The mice were randomly divided into seven groups (seven mice per group) and were injected intraperitoneally with 1 × 10 [6] CFU bacteria (EGD-e and each LLO mutant strains). After 48 hours infection, the mice were euthanatized by cervical dislocation, which is acceptable under the American Veterinary Medical Association’s guidelines on the euthanasia of animals. After confirming the death of the mice, the livers and spleens were harvested and homogenized in 10 mM PBS for bacterial enumeration on BHI agar. Bacterial burdens were quantified as Log10CFU per organ and expressed as means ± SDs using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Each group of mice was kept in individual cages, at a controlled temperature of 21°C to 26°C and a humidity level of 50% to 60%. The timeframe for this experiment was about September through October 2023. The study has adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines.

Immunofluorescence microscopy

HeLa cells or BMDM cells were plated on poly-lysine-coated coverslips and subjected to infection as the infection protocol with the specified MOI. Cells were then washed three times with 10 mM PBS and fixed with 4% PFA for 30 min at room temperature post infection. Being permeabilized using 0.5% Triton X-100 for 5 min, cells were blocked with 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min, then incubated with primary antibodies (anti-LLO, anti-Lm, anti-LAMP1, and anti-EEA1, all diluted 1:1000 in 2% BSA) for one hour. Cells were then rinsed with 10 mM PBS and incubated with Alexa Fluor 594 or fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled secondary antibodies (1:1000) for additional hour. Coverslips were mounted on glass slides using 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) Staining Solution (Beyotime). Confocal images were captured using FV4000 confocal microscope and analyzed with ImageJ software. The experiment occurred approximately from April through August 2023 and May to July 2025.

Quantitative image analysis

The colocalizations of L. monocytogenes with LAMP1 or EE1A were also measured with FV4000 (Olympus). Briefly, the phagosome escape rate was calculated by counting the number of bacteria on the fluorescence image minus the number of bacteria co-located with LAMP1 or EEA1, and then dividing the difference by the total number of bacteria. The data are presented as means ± SDs based on at least five fluorescence images. The results are representative of at least three independent experiments.

The colocalizations of LLO proteins with LAMP1 was measured with FV4000 (Olympus). Briefly, a line was drawn along the cell boundary, and the integrated intensity of each channel of interest was measured. The Pearson’s correlation coefficient between the channels was also calculated. The fluorescence intensity of each fluorescent protein of interest was determined using the drawing/selection tool within the same software (ImageJ).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using ordinary one-way ANOVA for evaluating multiple groups under a single independent variable. Statistical computations and graphical representations were performed using GraphPad Prism software. Statistical significance was set as follows: not significant (ns) for p > 0.05, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

Results

Mutations at positions 251, 253, and 255 in LLO resulted in a loss of hemolytic activity and a significant reduction in LDH release from cells

We successfully constructed and purified five mutant protein variants of LLO each containing a single mutated residue (Figure 1(A)), and subsequently tested the hemolytic activity of each mutant. Previous studies have established that LLO’s hemolytic activity is pH-dependent, with peak activity at pH 5.5, and that LLO predominantly functions within the acidic environment of the phagosomes [26]. In our experiments, the hemolytic function of LLO and mutant proteins was tested under two pH conditions. At pH 6.5, only the LLOK252A mutant exhibited hemolytic activity (Figure 1(B, C)). However, upon lowering the pH to 5.5, the LLOI254A mutant regained hemolytic activity, while other LLO mutants remained non-hemolytic (Figure 1(D, E)). The data presentation of hemolytic activity for LLOQ253A and LLOI254A proteins have been previously published [27], data are presented here for keeping a complete display of the related results.

To evaluate the cytotoxic effects of these LLO mutants, we treated RAW264.7 macrophages with wild-type LLO, LLON478AV479A (partial hemolytic activity) [24], LLOT515AL516A (no hemolytic activity) [28], and the 251–255 amino acids mutant proteins at a concentration of 5 nM. We measured LDH release at 0.5-, 1-, and 1.5-hour post-incubation. Initially, all proteins induced a minor LDH release at 0.5 hours. As the intoxication progressed, cells exposed to proteins with hemolytic activity (wild-type LLO, LLON478AV479A, LLOK252A, and LLOI254A) demonstrated an increase in LDH release (Figure 1(F)). Conversely, cells treated with LLOT515AL516A, LLOF251A, LLOQ253A, and LLOY255A showed significantly less LDH release compared to those treated with wild-type LLO, suggesting a positive correlation between the cytotoxicity of LLO proteins and their hemolytic activity.

Strains carrying mutations at positions 251, 253, and 255 in LLO exhibited no hemolytic activity

To further explore the impact of these mutations on LLO’s function, we successfully engineered LLO mutant L. monocytogenes strains: hlyF251A, hlyK252A, hlyQ253A, hlyI254A, and hlyY255A. We analyzed the growth capabilities of these mutants in vitro, comparing them to the wild-type strain EGD-e. As illustrated in Figure 2(A), there were no significant differences in the growth rates between the five LLO mutant strains and the wild-type. Considering LLO is a secretion protein essential for L. monocytogenes infection, we also examined the secretion profiles of each mutant using Western blot analysis. The results, shown in Figure 2(B), confirmed the presence of LLO protein in the culture supernatants of all LLO mutant strains, contrasting with its absence in the LLO deletion strain.

Furthermore, we assessed the hemolytic activity of each mutant strain under pH conditions of 6.5 and 5.5. Consistent with the protein-level data, only hlyK252A exhibited hemolytic activity at pH 6.5 (Figure 2(C, D)). At the lower pH of 5.5, the hemolysis capacity of hlyI254A was comparable to that of EGD-e (Figure 2(E, F)), highlighting the pH-dependent characteristics of LLO’s hemolytic activity and the specific effects of individual amino acid mutations. The hlyF251A and hlyY255A mutations reduced membrane permeability during L. monocytogenes infection

To evaluate the cytotoxic effects of the five LLO mutant strains on host cells, RAW264.7 cells were infected, and LDH release were measured at 5- and 8-hour post-infection. As shown in Figure 3(A,B), regardless of gentamicin presence, LDH release by mutant strains hlyF251A, hlyQ253A and hlyY255A was considerably less than of the wild-type strain EGD-e. Specifically, LDH release from hlyF251A-infected cells was akin to that from Δhly, while hlyI254A-infected cells exhibited the minimal difference in LDH release compared to EGD-e. Moreover, hlyK252A-infected cells released less LDH than EGD-e but more than the strains hlyF251A, hlyQ253A and hlyY255A. Non-gentamicin-treated RAW264.7 cells released more LDH post-infection compared to those treated with gentamicin, likely due to increased cell membrane permeability which allows gentamicin entry, facilitating bacterial killing. In untreated cells, ongoing stimulation by extracellular L. monocytogenes persists, while gentamicin eradicates extracellular bacteria in treated cells.

Additionally, PI staining was used to assess membrane integrity in infected RAW264.7 cells. As shown in Figure 3(C,D), the membrane damage in hlyF251A and hlyY255A-infected cells mirrored that of the Δhly strain, displaying minimal lysis. In contrast, the integrity damage in cells infected with hlyK252A and hlyI254A resembled that seen in EGD-e infections, whereas hlyQ253A-infected cells exhibited less severe membrane lysis compared to EGD-e but more than Δhly. The findings align with the LDH release results, suggesting that mutations at amino acid positions F251 and Y255 of LLO reduce membrane permeability, consequently diminishing the cytotoxicity of L. monocytogenes to host cells.

The hlyF251A and hlyY255A mutations impaired invasion and intracellular proliferation of L. monocytogenes

Adhesion and invasion are essential initial steps for L. monocytogenes to infect host cells and are crucial for completing the infection process. We assessed the adhesion and invasive capabilities of five LLO mutant strains in Caco-2 cells. As demonstrated in Figure 4(A,B), mutations at amino acid positions 251–255 in LLO did not affect the adhesion of L. monocytogenes. However, the invasive abilities of mutant strains hlyF251A, hlyQ253A, hlyI254A and hlyY255A were significantly reduced compared to the wild-type strain EGD-e, indicating that mutations at amino acid positions 251, 253, 254, and 255 of LLO primarily impair the bacterium’s ability to invade host cells.

In contrast, the proliferation of mutant strains hlyK252A and hlyI254A mirrored that of the wild-type strain EGD-e, while the mutant strain hlyQ253A exhibited a slightly reduced proliferation capacity compared to EGD-e. Consequently, these findings demonstrate that mutations at amino acid positions 251 and 255 in LLO significantly compromise the intracellular proliferation ability of L. monocytogenes.

F251 amino acid of LLO is pivotal for the cell-to-cell ability of L. monocytogenes

As an intracellular bacterium, the efficiency of cell-to-cell spread is crucial for the infection process of L. monocytogenes. This was assessed using L929 cells, where a larger plaque size indicates a higher efficiency of cell-to-cell spread (31). As shown in Figure 5(A–C), the plaques formed by each LLO mutant strain in L929 cells were significantly smaller than those formed by the wild-type strain EGD-e. Notably, the hlyF251A strain did not form any plaques, mirroring the results of the Δhly strain. Furthermore, regarding the number of plaques, hlyQ253A and hlyY255A formed significantly fewer plaques, while the hlyI254A strain formed significantly more plaques compared to the wild-type strain (Figure 5(B)). The data presentation of plagues formed in L929 cells and corresponding analysis for hlyQ253A and hlyI254A have been previously published [27], the data are shown here to provide a comprehensive overview of the related results. These results indicate that mutations at amino acids 251–255 of LLO significantly impair the cell-to-cell spread ability of L. monocytogenes in L929 cells, with the amino acid F251 playing a pivotal role.

To further substantiate these results, different LLO mutant strains were utilized to infect Caco-2 cells for 6 hours. Following this, bacteria were labeled with anti-Listeria antibodies, and the cell nucleus and cytoskeleton were visualized using DAPI and phalloidin stains, respectively. As shown and analyzed in Figure 5(D,E), hlyK252A and hlyI254A strains exhibited fewer intracellular bacteria compared to the wild-type strain EGD-e but significantly more than those of hlyQ253A and hlyY255A. Consistently, the hlyF251A mutant strain had a similar number of intracellular bacteria as the deletion strain Δhly, aligning with the results from the L929 plaque assay. These observations confirm that the F251 amino acid of LLO is pivotal for the cell-to-cell spread ability of L. monocytogenes.

hlyF251A and hlyY255A display virulence attenuation in G. mellonella and mouse models

G. mellonella serves as an ideal model for studying bacterial pathogenicity, including that of Listeria [29,30]. Due to variability in different batches of G. mellonella, it is necessary to determine the LD50 for the wild-type L. monocytogenes strain EGD-e. As illustrated in Figure 6(A), G. mellonella infected with varying doses of bacteria exhibited different survival states. The LD50 for L. monocytogenes in G. mellonella was calculated to be approximately 5.511 × 10 [5] CFU (Figure 6(B)). To assess the impact of mutations at amino acids 251–255 of LLO on virulence, we infected a dose of 5 × 10 [5] CFU into the G. mellonella. Results shown in Figure 6(C,D) reveal that the survival rate for hlyI254A was comparable to that of EGD-e. The hlyK252A strain demonstrated reduced virulence relative to EGD-e, while hlyF251A and hlyY255A strains exhibited virulence levels akin to Δhly deletion strain. This suggests that amino acids F251 and Y255 of LLO are critical for the virulence of L. monocytogenes in G. mellonella.

In addition, ICR mice were injected with the above strains. After 48 hours, statistical analysis of bacterial counts in the mice’s organs (livers and spleens) revealed that the strains hlyF251A, hlyQ253A and hlyY255A showed significantly reduced proliferative capacities compared to EGD-e. The hlyK252A strain exhibited a notable decrease in proliferative ability in the liver but not in the spleen. No significant difference in proliferative ability was observed in organs infected with hlyI254A compared to EGD-e (Figure 6(E,F)). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that amino acids F251, Q253, and Y255 of LLO are essential for the intracellular proliferation of the bacteria in host tissues.

hlyF251A and hlyY255A lost late phagosome escape ability

The pore-forming toxin LLO is essential factor for the bacteria to escape from the phagosome. Given the impact of various LLO amino acid mutations on the pathogenicity of L. monocytogenes in cellular and animal models, we sought to determine whether these mutations affect phagosome escape. We specifically analyzed the phagosome escape abilities of different LLO mutant strains in HeLa cells. Two hours post infection, we observed the colocalization with the early endosomal marker EEA1 using immunofluorescence. As illustrated in Figure 7(A,B), the escape rates from early phagosomes were quite comparable across all strains. This aligns with previous findings that L. monocytogenes can escape from early phagosomes with the aid of phospholipase PC-PLC even in the absence of LLO in some nonphagocytic human cell lines [31,32].

hlyF251A and hlyY255A lost late phagosome escape ability. Representative images of co-localization of each strain of L. monocytogenes with the early endosomal marker EEA1 (A) or late phagosome marker LAMP1 (C) in HeLa cells. Early (B) or late (D) phagosome escape rate of each strain was statistically analyzed using ImageJ. * means p < 0.05, ** means p < 0.01, *** means p < 0.001, ns means no significance.

For late phagosome escape, which relies on LLO’s pore-forming activity, our results revealed that mutant strains hlyF251A, hlyQ253A, and hlyY255A have lost their hemolytic activity. As demonstrated in Figure 7(C,D), the late phagosome escape rate of hlyQ253A was significantly reduced compared to the wild-type strain EGD-e, and the rates for hlyF251A and hlyY255A were akin to those of the Δhly strain. Conversely, the escape rates for hlyK252A and hlyI254A were comparable to EGD-e. In summary, the amino acids F251, Q253, and Y255 of LLO critically influence the capacity of L. monocytogenes to escape from late phagosomes, with F251 and Y255 having a more pronounced impact.

In BMDM cells, almost all strains are unable to escape the early phagosomes (Figure S1A and B). For late phagosome escape, the hlyF251A and hlyY255A mutant strains, like those in HeLa cells, were found to have nearly lost their capacity to escape late phagosomes, similar to the hly-deficient strain (Figure S1C and D). Consequently, by merging the results from the two cell types, we can infer that F251 and Y255 of LLO are essential amino acid sites for L. monocytogenes to escape phagosomes.

LLO protein secreted by hlyF251A, hlyQ253A and hlyY255A unable to lyse late phagosome membranes

LLO’s membrane perforation ability is necessary for the escape from late phagosome of L. monocytogenes. Therefore, can mutant LLO proteins lacking hemolytic activity still break through the phagosome membrane and promote bacterial escape? We extended our investigations to evaluate the pore-forming ability of LLO on late phagosome membranes, using prokaryotic expression of LLO proteins as controls. Figure 8(A,B) illustrate that wild-type LLO, which exhibits complete hemolytic activity, along with LLOK252A and LLOI254A, demonstrated obvious punctate clustering and a diffuse distribution without clear boundaries. In contrast, mutant recombinant proteins LLOF251A, LLOQ253A and LLOY255A, lacking hemolytic activity, did not exhibit punctate clustering and had clear boundaries. This means that mutations in LLO at F251, Q253 and Y255 render it unable to lyse late phagosome membranes.

(B) the co-localization of LLO and LAMP1 was analyzed by ImageJ. Further, after intoxicating HeLa cells for 3 hours, the localization of LLO secreted by mutant strains hlyF251A, hlyQ253A, and hlyY255A was predominantly intracellular, without diffuse outside the cells, contrasting with LLO secreted by hlyK252A, hlyI254A, which was similar to wild-type strain EGD-e, and spread beyond the cytoplasm (Figure 8(C,D)). This outcome underscores that LLO proteins secreted by hlyF251A, hlyQ253A and hlyY255A lack the necessary activity to lyse late phagosome membranes, thus impeding the escape of L. monocytogenes from late phagosomes.

Discussion

L. monocytogenes has developed a sophisticated system to regulate the expression of LLO, balancing its crucial role in facilitating intracellular survival against the potential for excessive activity that could harm the host cell and expose the bacteria to immune defenses [33]. The PEST sequence within the D1 region of LLO ensures timely degradation via the proteasomal pathway, preventing premature lysis of the host cell membrane and maintaining the intracellular infection and proliferation niche [34]. In this study, the generation of LLO mutants F251A, K252A, Q253A, I254A, and Y255A provided insights into the specific roles of these residues. Notably, F251A, Q253A, and Y255A mutants lack hemolytic activity, highlighting their importance in membrane interaction and pore formation. Contrastingly, the I254A mutation retains hemolytic functionality under low pH conditions, similar to the wild-type strain EGD-e. This suggests a nuanced role of the amino acid environment in modulating LLO activity, which is pivotal for understanding how L. monocytogenes adapts to the acidic phagosome environment.

Interestingly, despite its lack of hemolytic activity, the hlyQ253A mutant still exhibited certain levels of cytotoxicity and cellular proliferation. Previous studies have shown that modifications in LLO protein concentration can adjust its activity levels, suggesting that L. monocytogenes has evolved complex mechanisms to finely tune LLO expression, mitigating damage to host cells and enhancing bacterial survival during infection [15,24,26]. In assays measuring adhesion and invasion, only the hlyK252A mutant displayed an invasion capability similar to that of the wild-type strain EGD-e, while the other four mutants showed varying degrees of decreased invasion ability on Caco-2 cells (Figure 4A, A). The reduced invasion ability of the mutant strain hlyI254A may be due to the lack of the optimal pH environment necessary for it to exert its pore-forming activity during the process. These findings suggest that LLO plays a role in invading intestinal epithelial cells of L. monocytogenes, but this role is not entirely dependent on LLO’s ability to lyse cell membranes. Other virulence factors in L. monocytogenes may compensate for the role played by LLO in this process.

Building on our findings of key amino acid residues (F251, Q253, and Y255) in LLO’s mediation of L. monocytogenes pathogenicity and phagosome escape, we conducted an in-depth structural analysis using SWISS-MODEL (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/) and PyMOL (https://pymol.org/) to simulate and visualize LLO mutant proteins [35]. This analysis, depicted in Figures S1 and S2, focused on the pore-forming activity crucial for L. monocytogenes infection [13,36]. LLO, a key virulence factor, consists of two subunits: LLO-A, responsible for membrane disruption, and LLO-B, which interacts with cholesterol to form pores on cell membranes [37,38]. Our analysis revealed that mutations at F251A do not alter hydrogen bonding or the distance from pH sensors compared to wild-type LLO (Figure S2A-E), yet result in a loss of hemolytic activity. This suggests that factors beyond simple structural alterations, such as pH sensitivity or the capacity of LLO to attach to cell membranes, might influence LLO’s functionality. Specifically, mutations like Q253A disrupt hydrogen bonds between LLO’s β8 and β9, potentially destabilizing the structure necessary for pore formation (Figure S3A-B). Moreover, while the K252 mutation disrupts a hydrogen bond with Gln-301, a remaining bond between Ser-250 and Gln-301 preserves some structural integrity. Research has indicated that K316, located at the interface between LLO’s D2 and D3 regions, plays a role in neutralizing the charge of pH sensor D320, thus impacting the folding of the helical bundles and LLO’s activity under varying pH conditions [39]. The analysis also highlighted that the 251–255 amino acids domain in LLO, which corresponds to the 226–230 domain in perfringolysin O (PFO) (Figure S3C), is crucial for LLO’s interaction with adjacent monomers during oligomerization, critical for maintaining pore-forming activity (Figure S3D). Therefore, mutations in this domain can prevent proper oligomerization, leading to an inability to form functional pores. We also extracted the proteins and performed circular dichroism (CD) experiments. Using online server for further data analysis and comparison, we found that a single amino acid mutation could change the protein’s secondary structure, see Figure S4. The tertiary structure of the protein cannot be predicted by the CD experiment, so the data serves merely as supportive information for better understanding.

L. monocytogenes, a versatile intracellular pathogen, infects both phagocytic and non-phagocytic cells and elicits robust immune responses, making it an excellent candidate for tumor immunotherapy and a valuable model for scientific research [40,41]. More importantly, LLO is an immunogenic protein containing multiple T and B cell epitopes and has provided insights for developing novel anticancer drugs [42,43]. Utilizing the G. mellonella model, we explored the impact of the 251–255 amino acids domain of LLO on the host infection capabilities of L. monocytogenes. The results demonstrated that hlyF251A and hlyY255A increased survival rates in G. mellonella compared to the wild-type strain EGD-e, aligning with Δhly, highlighting their potential as attenuated strains for vaccine development or as antigen delivery systems [29]. Moreover, in murine models, these mutants showed significantly lower proliferation rates in organs compared to the wild-type, further confirming their diminished virulence and suggesting potential applications in immunotherapy and vaccine development.

Meanwhile, in this study, the LLOQ253A and hlyQ253A mutants demonstrated no detectable hemolytic activity but retained essential biological functions such as adhesion, invasion, phagosome escape, and cell proliferation. Despite their reduced virulence in mice and G. mellonella, these traits highlight hlyQ253A‘s potential as an antigen delivery vehicle or vaccine adjuvant, making it suitable for targeted drug delivery or therapy of tumor cells a in specific cellular environments. In addition, the acidic microenvironment of tumors requires treatment that are better adapted to function under acidic conditions [44]. For instance, the LLOY406A mutant preferentially activates at pH 5.7, losing activity at higher pH levels, which is ideal for targeting and treating tumor cells with accumulated drugs in acidic environments [45]. Similarly, our findings show that the LLOI254A mutant exhibits specific activity at pH 5.5, enhancing its suitability for targeted therapy in specific cellular conditions.

In summary, this study identifies key residues within LLO-F251, Q253, and Y255-that are crucial for its hemolytic activity, cytotoxicity, and overall role in the pathogenicity of L. monocytogenes. These insights expand our understanding of LLO’s function in the bacterium’s life cycle, providing a foundation for further research into the molecular mechanisms underpinning L. monocytogenes’ pathogenic strategies and opening new avenues for therapeutic intervention.
